Nanoparticle Drug Delivery Systems: The Future Direction for the Treatment of Tumors
Authors: Yan Zhao, Xin Nie, Hongbo Yan, Jingyuan Ren, Jianxin Zhao, Lage Tong, Lei Zhao, Guoxing Zhang, Haifeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn increasing number of people worldwide are dying from cancer, with metastasis being the main cause of cancer-related deaths, but most of the drugs used to treat these patients have systemic toxicity. Fortunately, with the development and application of nanodrug delivery systems, a large amount of research has been conducted on inorganic nanocarriers represented by metal nanocarriers, as well as organic nanocarriers represented by liposomal nanoparticles and polymeric micelles, as delivery systems for antitumor substances. These nanocarriers can not only increase the solubility and stability of hydrophobic drugs and prolong their circulation time in the blood, but also enable the loaded drugs to have tumor-targeting properties. These characteristics make nanodrug delivery systems synergistically enhance efficacy and reduce toxicity in cancer treatment. In this review, we will introduce the composition of nanoparticle drug delivery system carriers, their advantages in cancer treatment, and focus on the targeting capabilities of nanoparticle drug delivery systems, as well as briefly introduce clinically approved nanodrugs. This provides a theoretical basis and direction for the continuous improvement of drug-loaded nanosystems and the acceleration of clinical translation.Keywords: cancer, nanomedicine, nanomaterials, targeting tumors, subcellular organelles

Introduction

Cancer has become a major public health problem worldwide and a major obstacle to prolonging life.1 Among them, cancer metastasis is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, and it is reported that 66% of cancer deaths are caused by metastasis.2 At present, the treatment of metastatic cancer is still a comprehensive treatment based on systemic therapy. However, chemotherapy and immunotherapy drugs that have anti-tumor effects in systemic therapy produce systemic toxicity while killing tumor cells due to lack of targeting, which in turn affects the patient’s quality of life. In addition to the anti-tumor drugs currently used in clinical practice, there are also some natural active products with anti-cancer effects in nature which can exert anti-tumor effects through a variety of mechanisms, such as curcumin, ginsenosides, quercetin, etc. However, their further clinical application has been limited due to their hydrophobic nature and low bioavailability.3,4

Fortunately, the nano-drug co-delivery system (NDCDS) has shown strong advantages in the delivery of chemotherapy drugs and natural active ingredients with the development of nanotechnology and the wide application of nano-loaded materials in the treatment of diseases, such as improving the water solubility of loaded drugs, prolonging drug cycle time, controlling the slow release of drugs and the release of drugs to target tumors.5 Up to now, a variety of nanodrug carriers have been developed, including inorganic carriers such as metal nanocarriers, carbon nanostructure carriers, and silicon dioxide nanocarriers, as well as organic nanomaterials such as liposomes, polymer nanomicelles, nanogels, and dendritic polymers.6 These nanomaterials can carry a wide range of therapeutic drugs, including cytotoxic drugs, chemosensitizers, anti-angiogenic drugs, and nucleic acids,7 and the loaded substance can be further delivered to the lesion to exert efficacy while maintaining the properties of the drug. In this review, we will introduce the carrier composition of current nanoparticle delivery systems, their advantages in tumor therapy, elaborate on the targeting of nanoparticle drug delivery systems, and briefly introduce the nanoparticle drugs currently applied in clinical practice.

Comparative Analysis of Nanomedicine Delivery Systems for Tumor Therapy

Inorganic Nanocarriers

Inorganic nanocarriers provide a multifunctional scaffold for drug-loaded nanodelivery systems, which can be loaded with a variety of anti-tumor active substances such as small molecule drugs, macromolecular polyproteins and genes, and have the advantages of non-toxicity, biocompatibility, hydrophilicity, structural diversity and high stability, and have been widely used in the field of drug delivery.20 Inorganic nanocarriers mainly include metal nanocarriers, carbon nanostructured carriers and silicon dioxide nanocarriers, which we will introduce in detail below.

Metal Nanocarriers

Metal nanocarriers are a large class of inorganic nanocarriers, including but not limited to gold, silver, copper, platinum, iron/iron oxide, palladium, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, cerium oxide, and magnesium nanocarriers.21–25 Metal nanoparticles (NPs) have a typical core/shell structure, and the core is composed of one or more metal elements, such as gold, silver, copper, platinum, iron, lanthanides, etc., which determines that NPs have fluorescence, optical, magnetic, and electronic properties. The outer shell is made up of substances such as ligands, peptides, proteins, polysaccharides, fatty acids, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), plasmids, or antibodies that not only protect the metal core from interactions with the environment, but also give NPs the ability to bind to certain biomolecules.26 Metal nanoparticles can be used for imaging and various therapeutics due to their optical, acoustic, magnetic, and radioactive properties, such as photodynamic therapy, hyperthermia, and drug delivery, etc.27 In conclusion, metal nanoparticles have been widely used as drug carriers in drug delivery systems due to their unique physicochemical properties.

Gold nanocarriers among metal nanocarriers have attracted extensive attention due to their chemical inertness, good biocompatibility and adjustable morphology (including size and shape),28 it is an inorganic structure that is well suited for genetic detection, imaging applications, disease diagnosis, and drug release, mainly including gold nanoparticles, nanoclusters, nanorods, nanocages, nanoshells, and nanoplates.28 Gold nanocarriers are also easily combined with antibodies, enzymes or cytokines to target cancer cells in addition to the direct cytotoxic effect on cancer cells.29 For example, gold nanoparticles modified with ascorbyl palmitate can enhance the delivery of gemcitabine and its cytotoxicity to 4T1 breast cancer cells in vitro.30 Triphenyl phosphonium-functionalized high-branch gold nanoparticles Dox@TPAu can effectively accumulate at tumor sites, achieve targeted localization to the mitochondria of cancer cells and stromal fibroblasts, and improve the therapeutic efficacy of pancreatic cancer in xenograft mice.31 In addition, the conjugated drug delivery system SAIP@NPs prepared through the amidation reaction between silicon dioxide (SiO2) /gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) and iRGD/ paclitaxel (PTX), can bind to the highly expressed integrin αvβ3 on the MDA-MB-231 cell membrane thereby inhibiting tumor cell growth, and it also showed a longer retention time at tumor sites and stronger anti-tumor effects in animal experiments.32 Like gold nanoparticles, silver nanoparticles can also form specific morphologies for imaging, antimicrobial wound dressings, topical lotions for infection prevention, and anticancer treatments.33,34 The main mechanisms through which silver nanoparticles exert their effects include reactive oxygen species, oxidative stress, and the promotion of apoptosis. In addition, silver nanoparticles can also inhibit the expression of genes involved in angiogenesis (vascular endothelial growth factor A VEGF-A, protein kinase B AKT, and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase PI3K).35 For example, albumin-coated silver nanoparticles can induce apoptosis in human prostate cancer cell lines PC3 and LNCaP, significantly inhibit VEGF-A messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) in both cell lines and lead to a decrease in VEGF-C expression.36 Besides, platinum-based drugs such as cisplatin, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin have been widely used in clinical practice, but adverse effects and resistance increased due to lack of specificity, while platinum nanoparticles can be used to overcome these limitations of free drugs, such as targeting defects and uncontrolled drug release.37 For example, cisplatin-incorporating gelatin-coated gadolinium oxide nanoparticles gelatin-cisplatin-gadolinium oxide NPs exhibit strong cytotoxicity on HeLa cells in vitro by releasing cisplatin, and can efficiently accumulate at tumor sites and significantly inhibit tumor growth in mouse experiments.38 Metallic copper can also be used as a nanocarrie in addition to gold, silver, and platinum nanoparticles. Copper is an essential component of animal and plant metabolism, which is a soft, easily bendable, moldable substance, with high thermal and electrical conductivity, and the cost of preparing copper NPs is lower compared to other metals such as gold, silver, and platinum.25 Not only that, the use of copper can also increase the light absorption of nanoparticles, which has the effect of enhancing biological imaging and photothermal therapy (PTT), in addition, copper nanoparticles can improve the efficacy of anticancer drugs.27 For example, copper sulphide nanoparticles loaded with epirubicin coated by polyarginine and 5TR1 aptamer accumulate more in tumors and have a stronger inhibitory effect on tumors in tumor-bearing mice.39

In addition to the metal nanomaterials introduced above, metal nanoparticles that can be used for drug-loaded nanomaterials include palladium NPs, zinc oxide NPs, titanium dioxide NPs, cerium oxide NPs and nanomagnesium. Among them, palladium nanomaterials have been used as prodrug activators, photothermal agents, anticancer and antibacterial therapies due to their excellent catalytic and optical properties.25 In addition, zinc is recognized as a safe essential nutrient, the particles containing zinc themselves have cytotoxic effects on cancer cells in vitro and on tumors in tumor-bearing mice,40,41 and zinc oxide can produce reactive oxygen species under light exposure thereby damaging cells and inducing cell death.25 In addition to this, titanium dioxide NPs play a therapeutic role in various fields such as anticancer, antibacterial, and antioxidant due to its unique size, shape, and biochemical functions.42,43 At the same time, bio-nano composites and nanoparticle systems of nano magnesium have also been shown to inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells in vitro and tumor growth in tumor-bearing mice.44

Some metal nanoparticles can also release drugs targeted to tumors under the control of a magnetic field, and these magnetic nanoparticles can increase the accumulation of drugs in tumor tissues by “passively targeting” or “actively targeting”, and the functional shells can also be attached to the surface of the magnetic nanocarriers to increase their biocompatibility or targeting. Due to these properties, magnetic nanoparticles have been used in a variety of biomedical fields such as magnetic resonance imaging, magnetic hyperthermia, and tumor treatment.45,46 For example, Iron oxide nanoparticles in magnetic nanoparticles have great potential in diagnostic imaging and cancer treatment due to their biocompatibility, stability, and environmental safety.41 For example, polyacrylic acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticles Fe3O4@PAA-Pt(IV) loaded with the chemotherapeutic Pt(IV) prodrug, not only displayed promising magnetic and relaxometric properties, showing a higher relaxivity than the commercially available NP-based MRI agent Resovist®, but also can accumulate in cancer cells and significantly induce tumor cell apoptosis in vitro compared to free drugs.47 In addition, Fe3O4 magnetic nanoparticles surface-modified with polyethylene glycol and conjugated with caffeic acid phenethyl ester showed significant cytotoxicity to HT29 colorectal cancer cells in vitro and inhibited tumor growth in tumor-bearing mice.48 In summary, metal nanoparticles can serve as nanocarriers for anti-tumor drugs due to their controllable shape and size, ease of synthesis, and low cost.

Carriers of Carbon Nanostructures

Graphene Nanocarriers

In addition to metal nanocarriers, inorganic nanomaterials include carbon nanocarriers such as graphene and carbon nanotubes (CNTs), where graphene is a two-dimensional carbon allotrope with a hexagonal honeycomb crystal structure.49 Graphene has become an ideal vehicle for drug delivery due to its enormous loading potential, mechanical and chemical stability, and excellent optical, electrical, and thermal properties, as well as its ability to respond to a variety of stimuli such as electric, magnetic, pH, and temperature.50 However, due to the hydrophobicity of the original graphene itself, and the accumulation of graphene in solution may lead to cytotoxicity, which hinders its biological application. But fortunately, graphene can improve its solubility and biocompatibility through biomodification.51 For example, graphene derivatives such as graphene oxide (GO), restore GO (rGO), and graphene quantum dots (GQDs) all have good hydrophilicity and have been shown to promote cancer cell death and inhibit cell migration in vitro.52–54 GO is a graphene derivative with a 2D atomic layer, which has excellent water solubility compared to graphene, and can be chemically modified on the surface by forming covalent bonds and non-covalent bonds due to having a hexagonal carbon structure and some functional groups, where the free π electrons in the unmodified region of GO can provide a hydrophobic region and can load hydrophobic drugs by van der Waals force.50 In addition, GO has a larger surface area than other nanomaterials, allowing it to maintain drug stability without altering biological activity.55 At present, it has been applied in the fields of biotechnology and medical research, such as cell imaging, drug delivery, and cancer treatment.56–58 In drug delivery systems, GO is easily functionalized due to its large number of oxygen-containing functional groups and high affinity for metal ions and various compounds, which makes GO a promising nanocarrier in drug delivery systems and gene delivery for tumor treatment.59,60 GO nanogels loaded with etoposide not only enhance drug delivery but also upregulate pro-apoptotic genes and downregulate anti-apoptotic genes in breast cancer cells, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of MCF-7 tumor cells.61 Meanwhile, multifunctional GO loaded with antisense peptide nucleic acid (PNA) and the chemotherapeutic drug doxorubicin (DOX), after further conjugation with folic acid ligands, can not only achieve folate receptor-mediated tumor targeting, but after cellular internalization, the antisense PNA component can effectively reduce human telomerase reverse transcriptase mRNA levels, downregulate telomerase activity, while the pH-responsive release of DOX induces potent cancer cell apoptosis in vitro.62 Nanoparticles composed of GO can also reverse epithelial-mesenchymal transition, significantly downregulate cancer stem cell markers and remodel the tumor microenvironment, showing efficient antitumor effects both in vitro and in tumor-bearing mice in vivo.52

Carbon Nanotubes

In addition to graphene nanocarriers, inorganic nanocarriers include CNTs, which are carbon allotropes with cylindrical nanostructures, and mainly comprise multiple coaxial tubes composed of hexagonal carbon atoms.45,63 Carbon nanotubes exhibit excellent physicochemical properties due to their unique structure, such as high flexibility, good thermal conductivity, good adsorption capacity, and chemically stable.64 Carbon nanotubes also have excellent optical and electrical properties, and currently a large amount of research is being carried out in the field of biomedicine, such as for diagnostic applications, bioimaging, drug delivery, gene delivery, and gene therapy.65 For example, taking advantage of the poor heat resistance of tumor cells, carbon nanotubes can convert infrared light into heat, raising the temperature of the tumor site above 42°C thereby killing tumor cells in tumor-bearing mice while normal cells remain intact.66 In addition, CNTs can be used as an efficient nanodrug targeted delivery system due to its biocompatibility, high drug load, high stability, and release of therapeutic drugs at the target.67 Moreover, functionalized carbon nanotubes also possess non immunogenicity and low toxicity, and these carbon nanotubes can treat cancer with minimal drug doses, higher therapeutic efficacy, and fewer side effects. CNTs can also be modified by bioactive peptides, proteins, nucleic acids, and drugs, and can efficiently transport these substances to the cytoplasm and nucleus.63,64

Silica Nanocarriers

In addition to metallic nanocarriers and carbon nanostructure carriers, mesoporous silica nanocarriers (MSNs) are also commonly used inorganic nanomaterials in medical research,68 which is composed of SiO2, SiO2 is the most abundant compound on earth, usually distributed in large quantities in the earth’s crust in the form of silicate minerals, and also in plants and grains.69 MSNs have been extensively studied in the fields of diagnostic imaging, vaccine development, and as carriers for drugs or genes, etc.70–73 For example, silica has many advantages as a carrier in the field of drug delivery, including low cytotoxicity, uniform distribution in water, good stability, and resistance to decomposition caused by mechanical stress, heat, acidity and organic solvents,69,74 and their inner and outer surfaces can also be selectively functionalized for targeted delivery and controlled release. Moreover, silica as a nanocarrier has a high loading capacity due to its unique porous structure, and it can inhibit the premature release of the loading components.74 In summary, inorganic nanocarriers such as metal nanocarriers (including gold, silver, copper, platinum, palladium, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, cerium oxide, and magnesium nanocarriers), carbon nanostructure carriers (including graphene and carbon nanotubes), and silica nanocarriers have become commonly used nanomaterials in the field of antitumor drug delivery systems due to their biological properties.

Organic Nanocarriers

Organic nanocarriers are generally biocompatible and biodegradable, and have less cytotoxicity than inorganic nanocarriers.75,76 At present, organic nanocarriers have been widely used in drug-loaded nanodelivery systems, which mainly include liposomes, polymer nanomicelles, nanogels, dendritic polymers, etc. and we will introduce in detail the organic nanocarriers commonly used in nanodrug delivery systems as follows.

Liposomal Nanoparticles

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are spherical vesicle nanoparticles, mainly composed of cholesterol and phospholipids, and are the earliest discovered and most widely studied nanodrug carriers in nanodrug delivery systems.77 They are relatively safe in drug delivery systems because they are made of physiological and/or biodegradable lipids, and have characteristics such as high drug loading capacity, good temporal and thermal stability, simple preparation, high yield, and low manufacturing cost. They can be used as carriers for small molecule drugs, nucleic acids, peptides, proteins, and other substances. Therapeutic drugs can be encapsulated into liposomes through electrostatic interactions or by chemical bonding with the liposome surface.77–80 For example, met-oxa(IV) liposomes developed by encapsulating metformin into liposomes constructed from a platinum(IV) oxalate prodrug, can effectively alleviate tumor hypoxia and induce immunogenic cell death in cancer cells thereby inhibiting the growth of colorectal tumors in mice.81 Lipid nanoparticles targeting the long non-coding RNA VPS9D1-AS1 (whose overexpression is associated with sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibition) can efficiently suppress VPS9D1-AS1 expression in tumor cells and patient-derived xenograft tumors, thereby significantly inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis in mice and enhancing the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.82 In addition, LNPs can be loaded with both lipophilic and water-soluble therapeutic drugs due to their hydrophilic and phobic properties, where the hydrophilic drugs can be loaded inside, and the lipophilic drugs can be encapsulated in phospholipid bilayers or adsorbed on the surface of liposomes,83 moreover, such LNPs loaded with therapeutic drugs can increase drug concentrations in tumor cells, reduce therapeutic dose and toxicity, and treatment resistance compared to free drugs. At present, there are many studies on liposome functionalization, and common functionalized liposomes include long-cycle pegylated liposomes, stimuli-responsive liposomes, ligand-functionalized liposomes, and cell membrane-encapsulated biomimetic nanocarriers.84

mRNA-LNP vaccines formed by encapsulating messenger RNA in LNPs, can serve as cancer vaccines for tumor therapy.85 mRNA LNPs induce immune responses by encoding tumor antigens, and their efficient cellular delivery capability makes them a promising technology in nanomedicine delivery systems. For example, LNPs can facilitate the entry of mRNA into antigen-presenting cells, where it is translated into target antigens, then processed and presented on the cell surface, ultimately activating CD8+T cells and CD4+T cells.82 Currently, multiple preclinical models have shown that mRNA LNP-based vaccines can induce strong immune responses thereby inhibiting tumor growth. For example, three neoantigens identified from the mouse gastric cancer YTN16 cell line were in vitro transcribed and encapsulated in LNPs to form neoantigen-mRNA-LNP vaccines, can induce strong CD8+T cell responses in mouse gastric cancer models and show enhanced antitumor efficacy when combined with anti-PD-1 therapy.86 The mRNA-LNP vaccine encoding human papillomavirus 16 E7 antigen combined with immune checkpoint blockade in C57Bl/6J tumor-bearing mice can enhance HPV-specific CD8+ T cells in mice and further promote tumor regression.87 Based on the excellent results obtained from preclinical studies, mRNA-LNP cancer vaccines have entered the patient recruitment phase of clinical trials. Although there are currently no published results, they provide a new option for tumor treatment based on nanomaterials.88

Polymer Nanomicelles

In addition to liposomal nanoparticles, polymeric micelles are also a common form of organic nanocarriers, which are self-assembled from a hydrophobic core and a hydrophilic shell at the critical micelle concentration (CMC), such as low concentrations of polymers in aqueous media present in solution in the form of monomers, and the polymer chains begin to aggregate and self-assemble to form micelles when the polymer concentration increases to CMC.89 This polymeric micelle typically has a diameter of 10 to 100 nm and can accumulate passively in tumor tissue through enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect.90 In addition, nanomicelles are thermodynamically and dynamically stable due to the entanglement of polymer chains within micelles,91 and polymeric micelles also show good biocompatibility in cancer therapy, among them, the hydrophobic core of polymer micelles can be loaded with various components such as hydrophobic drugs, active ingredients of traditional chinese medicine, genes and proteins, while the hydrophilic shell on the surface of the micelle can introduce targeting groups such as glycans, proteins, nucleic acid ligands to actively target tumors.92–95 Among the components that make up the hydrophilic shell, polyethylene glycol forms the hydrophilic shell of most polymeric micelles due to their water-solubility and ability to resist reticuloendothelial system clearance. Besides, the hydrophilic polymers poly(N-vinyl-2-pyrrolidone), polyvinyl alcohol, and polyethylenimine can also be used to prepare micelles.96–98

Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) is one of the commonly used polymers in nanomedicine drug delivery systemic, composed of polylactic acid and polyglycolic acid, has biocompatibility, biodegradability, and the ability to adjust the degradation rate according to specific therapeutic needs, and can be used in medical, pharmaceutical, and other industrial fields.99,100 PLGA is classified as a generally recognized safe polymer by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA). Currently, several PLGA-based drugs have been approved for marketing, for example, PLGA-based microsphere Triptorelin, a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist, has been approved by the FDA for the treatment of advanced prostate cancer and by the European Union for adjuvant treatment of endocrine-responsive, premenopausal, high-risk recurrent breast cancer in combination with tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors.101,102 Another gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist Leuprolide, as a long-acting controlled-release formulation for testosterone suppression, has also been approved by the FDA for the treatment of prostate cancer.103 Despite the good potential of PLGA nanocarriers, their clinical translation also faces some issues, such as the long-term stability of various payloads, aggregation or premature release of nanomedicines, and consistency between production batches.

Nanogels

Nanogels are three-dimensional networks containing hydrophilic polymer chains cross-linked with each other, which can absorb and retain large amounts of liquid but are not dissolved by water which enables hydrogels to be used for tissue engineering and drug delivery.104 Nanogels are hydrogels with a size of approximately 1–1000 nm.105 They can encapsulate hydrophobic or hydrophilic drugs inside and protect the loaded drugs, which allows the loaded substance not to be degraded during blood circulation while reducing systemic toxicity. Nanogels can efficiently load and deliver small molecule compounds, proteins, and nucleic acids, in which the loaded substances can be evenly dispersed in the nanogel, and can also be concentrated in the inner layer of the nanogel, or bind to the gel through covalent interaction.106 When the nanogel reaches the target tissue, the drug can be released by degrading the gel, or by gel expansion and drug diffusion, or by disrupting the interaction between the drug and the nanogel. Nanogels have good biocompatibility and high stability as a drug delivery system, and nanogels can also be surface-modified to give them the ability to target specific tissues.104,107

Dendritic Polymers

Dendritic polymers are also safe and effective anticancer drug carriers in organic nanomaterials in addition to liposomes, nanomicelles, and nanogels, which are a type of highly branched three-dimensional structures with controllable volume and shape, with low viscosity, high solubility and high reactivity. In addition, they also have the advantages of easy modification, low cost, and suitable for large-scale production.108 Up to now, a variety of dendritic macromolecules have been developed, such as polyamidoamines, polypropylenimine dendrimers, poly-l-lysine dendrimers, and carbosilane dendrimers.109,110 The inner layer of the dendritic polymer consists of repeating branching units fixed to the core, while the number of surface groups increases exponentially, showing dendritic branches radiating outward from the central core unit.111 Typically, drug-loaded dendritic polymer-laden drugs can be encapsulated in the cavities of dendritic macromolecules or bind to functional groups on their surface.108 Dendritic macromolecules can also regulate their physicochemical properties in vitro and in vivo by introducing different chemical groups (alkaline, acidic, hydrophobic, hydrogen bonding and electric charge).108 In conclusion, in the field of nanocarriers, in addition to inorganic nanocarriers such as metal nanocarriers, carbon nanostructure carriers, and silica nanocarriers, organic nanocarriers including liposomes, polymeric nanomicelles, nanogels, and dendrimers have also been applied in the field of antitumor drug delivery systems.

Other Nanoparticle Delivery Systems

Albumin-Bound Nanoparticles

Protein nanoparticles have attracted much attention due to their many advantages, including easy access to resources, renewability, reasonable cost, biocompatibility, biodegradability, and the presence of multiple functional groups. Among them, the albumin that composes protein nanoparticles is one of the most important proteins in plasma, with stable structure, good biocompatibility, and degradability, its degradation residues are amino acids which can be used as structural units by surrounding tissues for synthesizing human proteins. Moreover, various functional groups on the surface of albumin nanoparticles, such as thiol, amino, and carboxyl groups, which can bind different ligand molecules to form nanoparticles with different activities and purposes. As a delivery system, albumin can carry anticancer drugs, peptide hormones, growth factors, DNA and RNA.112 Currently, various protein-based nanoparticles have been approved by the FDA and the EMA for tumor treatment.113,114 For example, Abraxane™ and Pazenir™ are albumin-bound forms of paclitaxel (nab-paclitaxel), approved for the treatment of patients with metastatic breast cancer, metastatic pancreatic cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer.14,115–117

Exosome

The lipid bilayer vesicles secreted by cells, namely exosomes and microvesicles in extracellular vesicles, have significant advantages in delivery systems for cancer therapy due to their nanoscale size, good biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and inherent ability to target intercellular communication.118 Currently, engineered exosomes have progressed from preclinical models to early clinical trials. For example, a Phase 1 clinical trial of colorectal cancer treated with exosomes rich in tumor antigens isolated from patients’ malignant ascites combined with the immune adjuvant granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor showed that patients after treatment exhibited tumor-specific T cell responses.119 In addition, autologous dendritic cell-derived exosomes loaded with MAGE tumor antigens for the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer can elicit immune responses in some patients and are well tolerated by patients.120 iExosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (engineered exosomes carrying siRNA against oncogenic KRASG12D) were applied to patients with pancreatic cancer carrying the KRASG12D mutation, the results showed that some treated patients had decreased circulating KRASG12D DNA and their disease remained stable, and the treatment was safe and well tolerated.121 Up to now, although no exosome products have been approved for cancer treatment, the initiation and completion of multiple phase 1 studies indicate that exosomes have potential in cancer therapy and are expected to serve as clinical application formulations for tumor treatment.118

Advantages of Nanoparticle Drug Delivery Systems

Traditional drug delivery systems (tablets, capsules, oral liquids, ointments, etc.) have limitations such as low bioavailability, fluctuating plasma drug levels, lack of targeting, and inability to achieve sustained drug release.122 In contrast, nanomedicine delivery systems, whether inorganic nanocarriers or organic nanocarriers, have advantages such as improving the solubility of the loaded drug, prolonging the circulation time of the drug in the body, increasing drug bioavailability, and reducing adverse drug reactions. In addition, nanomaterials can be surface-modified to target specific cells, or even specific regions within cells (Figure 2). In the following sections, we will provide a detailed introduction to the advantages of nanocarriers.

Advantages of drug-loaded nanoparticles. (1) Improve the solubility of the loaded drug and enhance the stability of the drug; (2) enhance drug permeability and retention effects; (3) facilitate the simultaneous delivery of multiple drugs; (4) Achieve targeted drug delivery.

Improves the Solubility and Stability of the Loaded Drug

There are a large number of botanicals with antitumor activity in nature, which have good antitumor activity but almost insoluble in water, and have a short half-life and are quickly metabolized and excreted in the body when they enter the body, thereby affecting the full exertion of their efficacy, such as curcumin, resveratrol, ginsenosides, etc.123–125 However, with the application of NDCDS in the treatment of diseases, it is found that it not only has the effect of improving the solubility of loaded drugs, but also improves the stability of loaded drugs. For example, nanomaterials can load hydrophobic drugs inside, which not only increases the solubility of hydrophobic drugs but also prevents the loaded drugs from being degraded by proteases or other enzymes, thereby enhancing the stability of drugs. In addition, some bioactive molecules (such as nucleic acids or proteins) are inherently unstable and prone to rapid degradation in vivo, but the application of NPs can protect these substances from degradation, which enhance their stability and increase the half-life of these drugs in the systemic circulation thereby increasing the efficacy of loading drugs.126,127

Enhanced Drug Permeability and Retention Effect in Tumors

In addition to improving the solubility and stability of the loaded drug, NDCDS can also enhanced drug permeability and retention effect of the loaded drug in the tumor. For example, the permeability of tumor tissues is increased due to structural characteristics such as excessive angiogenesis and irregular new blood vessels in tumor tissues, which in turn allows nanomedicines to reach and selectively accumulate in the tumor site. In addition, nanomedicines that penetrate tumor tissues are retained in the tumor tissues due to the lack of an effective lymphatic drainage system, resulting in a higher accumulation of nanomedicines in tumor tissues than in normal tissues, which plays a role in enhancing drug efficacy and reducing toxicity.128 To sum up, based on the structural characteristics of tumor tissues and nanomedicines, drug-loaded nanoparticles compared with free drugs, have increased permeability, enhanced drug accumulation and EPR effect in tumors, thereby allowing the drug to passively deposit at the tumor site and enhance the efficacy of anti-tumor drugs.128

Facilitates the Simultaneous Delivery of Multiple Drugs

NDCDS can also co-deliver two or more substances with different physicochemical properties, pharmacological effects, and therapeutic mechanisms through the same vector,129 including chemotherapy drugs, genes, and other preparations with antitumor activity,76 thereby enabling the simultaneous delivery of multiple drugs into the tumor, improving anti-tumor efficacy and reducing the emergence of drug resistance.1 For example, the nanomedicine co-encapsulating paclitaxel and rapamycin for treating tumor-bearing mice with breast cancer is more effective than nanomedicines loaded with each drug separately.130 In addition, fluorouridine (a hydrophilic therapeutic agent with high antitumor activity) can bind to hydrophobic chemotherapy drugs such as bendamustine or camptothecin through ester bonds, and can self-assemble to form multidrug-loaded amphiphilic nanoparticles, studies have shown that these nanoparticles can quickly break down through hydrolysis inside tumor cells thereby releasing drugs to achieve higher anticancer efficiency.131,132 Nano-co-delivery systems can co-deliver chemotherapeutic agents to tumor tissues with gene therapies in addition to loading multiple chemotherapeutic agents simultaneously, such as microRNAs, small interfering RNAs, or plasmid DNA, where the chemotherapeutic agents can be encapsulated inside the nanocarriers and the genes adsorbed to the surface of the nanoparticles.133–135 This strategy of co-delivering genes and chemotherapeutic agents to tumors can not only increase the efficacy of anti-tumor therapy, but also disrupt the multidrug resistance signaling pathway, thereby helping to overcome the emergence of multidrug resistance in tumors.76

Achieve Targeted Drug Delivery

NDCDS can also enable targeted drug delivery in addition to improving the solubility and stability of the loaded drugs, increasing the high permeability and retention effect of the loaded drugs in tumors, and enabling the simultaneous delivery of multiple drugs.128 For example, due to the rapid proliferation and metabolism of tumors, a unique microenvironment has developed that manifests as low pH, hypoxia, high glutathione, and abnormal expression of certain enzymes (cathepsin B Cat-B, matrix metalloproteinases MMPs, and phospholipase C PLC).136,137 Based on this, NDCDS can be designed as NDCDS with pH response, hypoxia response, redox response, reactive oxygen response and enzyme response, which not only can deliver nanodrugs into tumors but also control the release of drugs at tumor sites, thereby playing a role in passively targeting tumors (Figure 3).138–141 In addition to NDCDS that responds to the tumor microenvironment, NDCDS can also be designed in response to exogenous stimuli, such as light, magnetic, ultrasound, and temperature (Table 2).142–144 Moreover, different types of ligands, such as antibodies, peptides, proteins, nucleic acids, and polysaccharides, can be conjugated to the surface of nanocarriers due to the overexpression of many receptors on the surface of tumor cells (for example, those closely related to cell proliferation include transferrin receptor (TfR), folic acid receptor (FR), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), etc.) thereby improving the specific recognition between nanocarriers and cancer cells and thus actively targeting tumors.145,146 We will elaborate on the targeting of nanodrug delivery systems in the following sections, including passive targeting and active targeting.

Drug-loaded nanodelivery systems in response to internal stimuli. Nanodrugs that respond to physiological stimuli can be designed according to the tumor microenvironment to respond to low pH, hypoxia, high glutathione, highly reactive oxygen species, and abnormal expression of certain enzymes, such as pH response, hypoxia response, redox response, reactive oxygen species response, and enzyme response.

Stimuli-Responsive Nanomedicine Delivery Systems for Tumor Treatment

Passive Targeting of Nanoparticle Drug Delivery Systems

Internal Stimulus Response

pH Response

The pH of extracellular fluid in healthy tissues is tightly regulated between 7.35 and 7.45 in order to maintain normal physiology and cellular metabolism. In contrast, tumor tissue metabolism is significantly increased compared with normal tissues, but cancer cells have increased anaerobic glycolysis due to insufficient blood supply and hypoxia in tumor tissues, resulting in excessive lactic acid production within tumors.156 At the same time, the lactic acid produced is not completely excreted due to the lack of vasculature in the tumor tissue, resulting in a tumor microenvironment with higher acidity and lower pH compared to normal tissue.157 For example, the pH of the extracellular fluid of the tumor is between 6.3 and 7.0,158 this acidic tumor microenvironment provides a suitable internal stimulus for the release of therapeutic drugs. Based on this, a large number of pH-responsive NDCDS have been prepared and developed, and these drug-loaded nanoparticles can release drugs inside tumors, thereby allowing drugs to target tumors to exert efficacy.159 Numerous studies have demonstrated that NDCDS containing ester bonds, carboxyl groups, acetals, imidazole groups, amide bonds, and borate bonds, have the property of responding to pH-releasing drugs.160 These pH-sensitive bonds are structurally stable under physiological conditions but hydrolytic cleavage occurs under acidic conditions, accelerating the release of the drug from the nanoprodrug and resulting in a high local plasma concentration of the drug, which in turn contributes to the full efficacy of the drug.161

Hypoxic Response

50–60% of solid tumors are hypoxic internally due to the rapid proliferation of cancer cells requiring more oxygen and nutrients but the proliferating tumor blood vessels cannot meet the supply. For example, the partial pressure of oxygen (pO2) in healthy cells is 30 to 40 mmHg, while pO2 can be reduced to 0 to 2.5 mm Hg in some areas of the tumor, and different oxygen gradients are formed from the center of the tumor to the periphery of the tumor,162 therefore, NDCDS in response to hypoxic stimuli can be designed based on the low partial pressure of oxygen within the tumor and the hypoxic state of tumor cells.163 At present, it has been found that groups sensitive to hypoxia include hydrophobic nitroimidazole,164 various quinones, nitroaromatic hydrocarbons azobenzene derivatives, etc, among them, 2-nitroimidazole and azobenzene are the most widely used hypoxia responsive molecules,165,166 and these hypoxic responsive groups can be reduced to amino aromatic compounds in the hypoxic tumor microenvironment, but oxygen in normal cells can effectively re-oxidize them. In other words, hypoxia-responsive NDCDS is stable in the normal physiological environment but is activated once extravasation into the hypoxic tumor microenvironment.149 In summary, NDCDS that respond to hypoxia can be designed based on the hypoxic tumor microenvironment, which can respond to the hypoxic tumor microenvironment and control drug release only at the tumor site.167

Redox Response

Cancer cells usually have high levels of antioxidant characteristics in order to maintain intracellular redox homeostasis and to establish strong antioxidant defenses, leading to redox potential gradients between healthy and cancerous tissues as well as between cancer cells and outside cells.164 Among them, glutathione (GSH) accounts for the majority of cell antioxidants and has been observed to be elevated in many tumors, including lung, breast, colon, head and neck, and ovarian cancers. Research has shown that the level of GSH in tumor tissue is about four times higher than that in normal tissue, while the concentration of GSH in tumor cells (2–10 mM) is approximately 100–1000 times that of extracellular GSH (2–20 μM),164 based on this GSH concentration gradient, NDCDS in response to GSH can be designed.168 Ideal NDCDS that response to GSH is characterized by good stability in an environment with low GSH levels and a rapid release of drug in a high GSH environment in tumor tissues or tumor cells.169 The overexpressed GSH exists in the form of a free sulfhydryl group and thus produces a highly reduced tumor microenvironment, so nanocarriers with reductive reactive bonds can be designed,169 such as disulfide and selenium bonds,170,171 and NDCDS with these bonds can be broken in the presence of high levels of GSH in tumor tissues or tumor cells,7 thereby allowing drugs directly deliver to the cytoplasm or nucleus to target tumor release drugs.172

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Response

The partial pressure of oxygen in tumor cells decreases due to the rapid growth of the tumor requiring more nutrients but the corresponding tumor blood vessels cannot meet the required blood supply and oxygen, which in turn leads to the production of more ROS.149 In addition, it is also possible to produce more ROS in tumor cells due to the overexpression of superoxide dismutase in tumor cells, which leads to further elevation of ROS levels in the tumor microenvironment,173 so NDCDS in response to ROS can also be designed based on the high ROS in tumor cells. The ideal ROS-responsive nanomaterial should be “inactive” at normal levels of ROS concentration, but become “active” when stimulated by high ROS in cancer cells and can release loaded drugs.151 The main groups in nanomaterials that respond to ROS include thioethers or sulfides, thione, selenide, diselenide, and aryl borates,160 and the NDCDS in response to these ROS may undergo changes in solubility in the presence of ROS, such as compounds containing thioether groups that can be oxidized in the presence of ROS thus achieving a phase transition from hydrophobic to hydrophilic. In addition to releasing drugs by inducing changes in the solubility of nanodrugs, ROS can also release drugs by disrupting the stability of chemical bonds in nanomaterials, for example, it can lead to the cleavage and decomposition of thioneone bonds.174 In conclusion, NDCDS in response to ROS can be designed based on the high ROS level in the tumor microenvironment to achieve drug release within the tumor, thereby playing the role of targeting tumors.

Enzyme Response

The tumor microenvironment also exhibits abnormal expression of certain enzymes in tumor tissues in addition to low pH, low oxygen, high GSH, and high ROS, for example, Cat-B, MMPs, and PLC are overexpressed in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues.175 Among them, Cat-B is a cysteine protease that is overexpressed in many aggressive or metastatic tumors and in the lysosomes of tumor cells, so Cat-B can be used as an enzyme-responsive stimulator for nanodrug carriers. In addition, matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) in MMPs is also overexpressed in cancer tissues and has been used as a trigger factor in NDCDS, such as the polyethylene glycol (PEG) -GPLGVRGDG-PDLLA micelles prepared by introducing the MMP-responsive peptide GPLGVRGDG into copolymers can be loaded with PTX, and the peptide bonds of the loaded micelles are broken in the presence of MMP-2 thereby releasing the loaded PTX and showing stronger antitumor activity in tumor-bearing mice.160,176 PLC, quinone oxidoreductase isoenzyme I (NQO1), and α-amylase are also highly expressed in tumors, based on which NDCDS responsive to PLC, NQO1, and α-amylase can be developed. For example, phosphoramide (PAD) dendrimites containing phosphorous bonds can be degraded by PLC, so PAD-2-methacryloyloxyethylphosphorylcholine loaded with doxorubicin can release drugs in response to PLC, and the higher the expression of PLC the faster the release of loaded drugs.177 In addition, the expression of phospholipase A2 (PLA2) in PLC has also been significantly increased in cancer cells such as breast cancer and prostate cancer, so PLA2 has also been used as a trigger factor for nanodrug delivery systems.178 For example, PLA2 can specifically hydrolyze the sn-2 ester bonds in phospholipids, which in turn leads to the hydrolysis of liposomes and the direct release of active drugs.179

Response to External Stimuli

In addition to designing nanomedicines that respond to endogenous physiological stimuli based on tumor characteristics such as low pH, low oxygen, high GSH, high ROS, and abnormal enzyme expression, nanomedicine delivery systems that respond to exogenous stimuli can also be designed (Figure 4), such as nanomedicines responsive to light, magnetic fields, ultrasound, and temperature. The following content will describe this in detail.

Drug-loaded nanodelivery systems in response to external stimuli. Nanodrug delivery systems can be designed to respond to exogenous stimuli (light, magnetism, ultrasound, and temperature), such as light response, magnetic response, ultrasound response, temperature response, etc.

Light Response

Light-responsive NDCDS is based on the cleavage of a prodrug with a photosensitive structure that changes in a light-sensitive molecule or has a light-sensitive structure, resulting in the release of a conjugated or encapsulated drug.180 The light stimulus is applied externally and can be localized in time and space, allowing the nanomaterial to release drugs at the target under external light irradiation thereby enabling drugs to target tumors.181 The NDCDS that responds to light stimulation contains a photoreactive portion, which can undergo photodissociation, photoisomerization, or photosensitization under light radiation.182 The photocleavage reaction refers to the cleavage of the chemical bonds of the photoreactive part of NDCDS after photoirradiation, and the photocleaved groups include 2-nitrobenzyl, triphenylmethane, azosulfonate, and phenymethyl ester.183,184 While photoisomerization refers to the change of structure of NDCDS from trans to cis or cis to trans after light irradiation, and photoisomerizable substances include spirooxazines and azobenzenes, which can undergo photosensitive dimerization and the resulting dimers can be photolyzed.185 For example, liposomal hydrogels containing the tumor-targeted photosensitizer IR780 (IR780/lipo/gels) can effectively deliver IR780 to subcutaneous tumors and deep metastatic sites in tumor-bearing mice, under laser irradiation, IR780/lipo/gels can significantly inhibit tumor growth.186 Copper phthalocyanine as a photothermal agent is fused into lipid layers to prepare liposomes encapsulating cationic zinc phthalocyanine ZnPc(TAP)412+ and doxorubicin, followed by the modification of arginine-glycine-aspartate (RGD) peptide on the surface to obtain the final product RGD-CuPc:ZnPc(TAP) 412+:DOX@LiPOs (RCZDL), which exhibits synergistic cytotoxicity after irradiation. Subcellular localization analysis indicates that in light-treated RCZDL, ZnPc(TAP)412+ tends to localize in the mitochondria of tumor cells in vitro. H22 tumor mouse experiments show that RCZDL has excellent tumor-targeting ability, exhibiting significant photothermal effects and synergistic antitumor effects at the tumor site.187 In a word, light-responsive NDCDS is also a promising oncology treatment strategy in drug delivery systems because this light-responsive NDCDS can be localized in time and space due to the ease of control and utilization of external light stimulation, thereby enabling the controlled release of the drug at the target,188 in addition, this light-responsive NDCDS is a low-cost treatment that does not require expensive facilities,189 therefore, light-responsive NDCDS is also one of the strategies for passively targeting tumors in drug delivery systems.

Magnetic Response

Magnetic fields can also be used as external triggers for cancer therapy, allowing magnetic nanocarriers to release drugs in a controlled manner in time and space to target tumors. Magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) can deliver loaded drugs to tumor areas under alternating magnetic fields, and can provide controlled drug release at the tumor site over time, increasing local drug concentrations while protecting healthy tissues and cells from anticancer drugs.154,178 MNPs typically include a therapeutic drug moiety and a magnetic moiety, in which the therapeutic drug loaded in the MNPs can interact with the surface functional groups of the MNPs as part of the nanoparticle coating or can be encapsulated or embedded inside the MNPs. The magnetic moiety of MNPs is usually ferrite, metal alloy, or iron-based magnetic oxide.190 Among them, magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles are the most commonly used magnetic nanomaterials and have good magnetic properties, low toxicity, high biocompatibility, and low price compared with other metal magnetic nanoparticles,191,192 which has also been described in the inorganic nanocarriers introduced earlier.

Ultrasonic Response

In addition to light response and magnetic response, ultrasound is also commonly used for exogenous stimuli in NDCDS response. Ultrasound is a non-invasive type of mechanical wave that can penetrate biological tissues and trigger biological effects. For example, ultrasound can cause the mediated to form gas-filled bubbles and can cause these bubbles to be active, resulting in mechanical and thermal effects thereby improving drug permeability and drug release in NDCDS.178,193,194 The ultrasound parameters determine the strength of the mechanical or thermal effect in ultrasound-responsive NDCDS, and ultrasound as an exogenous stimulus, which can improve the absorption of non-permeable or weakly permeable drugs by cells,195 such as the microbubbles formed by ultrasound can affect the permeability of cell membranes and enhance the absorption of drugs by cells. In addition, high-intensity ultrasound can induce drug uptake by cells even in the absence of microbubbles.164 Moreover, ultrasound can trigger the release of drugs from NDCDS through thermal and mechanical effects,196 for example, the shear forces created by the mechanical effects of ultrasound can promote the release of drugs from liposomes or micelles.197 In addition, ultrasound can cause chemical changes such as bond breakage or recombination, as well as trigger the release of drugs from temperature-sensitive nanocarriers.196 For example, a silica-based nanoplatform formed by hollow silica cores encapsulating 10-hydroxycamptothecin and coated with tumor cell membranes, and embedded with chlorin e6 to form HMSN@TCM/Ce6, which can accelerate nanoparticle internalization and on-demand drug release under ultrasound irradiation, and exhibits prolonged pharmacokinetics, increased drug accumulation in tumors and lymph nodes, and inhibits primary tumors and metastatic lymph nodes both in vitro and in animal models.198 Ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles PIH-NO was constructed with a human serum albumin-based nitric oxide (NO) donor (HSA-NO) to encapsulate perfluorodecalin (FDC) and the sonosensitizer (IR780), can accumulate in cell mitochondria and relieve hypoxia. Upon ultrasound irradiation, O2 and NO are released instantaneously, generating highly oxidative peroxynitrite anions and leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby inhibiting tumor growth both in vivo and in vitro in mice.199

Temperature Response

Temperature is also a commonly used external stimulus for NDCDS, which can trigger the release of loading agents from heat-sensitive nanomaterials. And temperature-responsive NDCDS can respond to temperature changes, such as reversible phase transitions at specific temperatures, altering its structure or water solubility, thereby resulting in the release of the encapsulated drug in the target tissue.200 Temperature-responsive nanomaterials typically have a low critical dissolution temperature (LCST) or a high critical dissolution temperature (UCST).201 For example, therapeutics can be easily encapsulated into polymers in LCST, and the polymers undergo a phase transformation when the external temperature changes, resulting in the release of the therapeutic agents loaded in the polymers at the target lesions.202 The ideal thermally reactive nanocarrier is one that remains stable at normal body temperature but can rapidly release the drug when the lesion site is heated. For example, the core-shell drug delivery system encapsulates hydrophobic drugs inside nanoparticles with heat-responsive materials as shells, and the heat-responsive material changes when the external temperature increases, which in turn releases the encapsulated drug into the target lesion.178 In conclusion, temperature-sensitive drug release system enables the directional release of drugs in target lesions due to the temperature sensitivity of nanomaterials, thereby playing the role of passive tumor targeting. In addition, temperature as an external stimulus of NDCDS also has the advantages of low toxicity and low cost. Therefore, temperature-responsive NDCDS is also a nanodelivery system that is extensively studied in tumor treatment.

Active Targeting of Nanoparticle Drug Delivery Systems

In addition to the EPR effect of drug-loaded nanoparticles and the passive tumor-targeting nanodrug delivery systems designed in response to internal and external stimuli, various ligands can also be conjugated to the surface of nanocarriers based on the overexpression of many receptors on the surface of tumor cells as well as changes in some stem cell markers and subcellular organelles,128,145,189,203,204 (Figure 5) thereby improving the specific recognition between nanocarriers and cancer cells and playing an active role in tumor targeting.146 In the following content, we will provide a detailed introduction to nanoparticle drug delivery systems that actively target tumor cells and tumor subcellular organelles.

Nanoparticle drug delivery systems that actively target tumors. Nanodrug delivery systems that target tumor cells or subcellular organelles can be designed based on overexpression of many receptors on the surface of tumor cells and changes in some stem cell markers and suborganelles. For example, targeting proliferation-related receptors on the surface of tumor cells, targeting glycobinding proteins, targeting cell adhesion molecules, and targeting stem cell markers, and targeting tumor cell subcellular organelles, such as mitochondria, lysosomes, Golgi apparatus, endoplasmic reticulum, nucleus, etc.

Targeting Tumor Cells

Compared with normal cells, the expression levels of certain proteins that are specific to cancer cells or meet the rapid proliferation of cancer cells are increased in tumor cells. These include proteins involved in cell proliferation (such as TfR, FR, EGFR, CD20, integrin-related proteins (CD47), etc.), glycoscopy-binding proteins that increase for glucose and pentose uptake, cell adhesion molecules, and stem cell markers such as CD44, CD133, and CD271.128,145 Based on this, corresponding antibodies or antibody fragments can be used as targeting ligands to NDCDS, which can enter cancer cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis when the nanocarriers bind to cell surface receptors, thereby maximizing the uptake of drugs by cells and actively targeting tumor drug release.146,205 In the following sections, we will provide a detailed introduction to nanodrug carriers targeting tumor cells.

Targeting Cell Proliferation-Related Receptors

TfR has been shown to be expressed in a variety of cancer cells, including breast, colon, and pancreatic cancers,206–208 so TfR can be used as an important target for drug delivery to target tumor cells. While transferrin as a serum glycoprotein, can transport iron from the bloodstream to cells by binding to TfR on the cell surface, and is a widely used targeted ligand.209 In nano-drug delivery systems, transferrin can also be used as a ligand to target highly expressed TfR on the surface of tumor cells, and the specific combination of the two can make drug-loaded nanoparticles enter the cell thereby playing a role in actively targeting tumors to release drugs. And a large number of in vitro and in vivo experiments have also confirmed that transferrin-bound nanoparticles can enhance the inhibitory effect of drugs on tumors by increasing the uptake of nanodrugs by cancer cells.210 For example, transferrin nanomicelles Tf-Mic@SD which encapsulate dihydroartemisinin and sorafenib, can be recognized by transferrin receptors and captured by tumor cells, then undergo lysosomal endocytosis leading to ferroptosis in tumor cells, thereby more rapidly eliminating tumors in vitro and in mice in vivo.211 RhNPs-MSNs-Tf composed of rhodium nanoparticles confined in mesoporous silica nanoparticles and surface-modified with transferrin, can be selectively taken up by cancer cells and show significant tumor growth inhibition without obvious systemic toxicity in vivo tumor models.212 In addition to TfR, FR as a substance necessary for the indefinite proliferation of tumor cells, has also been shown to be highly expressed in some tumors of epithelial origin, including lung cancer, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, ovarian cancer, and bladder cancer, but has little or no expression in most normal tissues, so FR can also be used as a target for nanodrug delivery,146 and facts have also confirmed that folic acid-modified nanoparticles have a good tumor-targeted killing effect. For example, folic acid-modified NDCDS compared with free drugs, which can not only target cancer cells, increase the uptake of drugs by cancer cells, but also enhance the in vitro cytotoxicity of the drugs and their tumor-suppressing effects in tumor-bearing mice, and improve the safety of the drugs.213 In addition, epidermal growth factor receptors (EGFR and human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 HER-2) are closely related to cell proliferation, have also been shown to be overexpressed in a variety of tumors, including head and neck cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, gastric cancer, colon cancer, and ovarian cancer,128 therefore, EGFR and HER-2 can also be used as specific targets for NDCDS. For example, targeted EGFR nanomicelles PLGA-PEG/DOX@Anti-EGFR and trastuzumab-encapsulated polylactic acid-polyethylene glycol carriers all can play a role in actively targeting tumors thereby increasing antitumor efficacy.214,215 Anti-CD47, anti-CD20 and anti-Fas monoclonal antibodies can also be used as targeting agents of nanomedicines to bind to the corresponding receptors on the surface of tumor cells to actively target tumors. For example, nanoparticles loaded with anti-CD47 antibodies can increase drug uptake by cancer cells and show significant tumor inhibition in mice.216 In addition, silver nanoparticles loaded with the anti-CD20 antibody rituximab can rapidly be internalized by chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) cells, and significantly prolong survival in CLL xenograft models compared with unconjugated single agents.217 In short, based on the fact that cell proliferation-related receptors are overexpressed in tumor cells but are not expressed or underexpressed in normal cells, NDCDS can be designed to target the corresponding receptors and then actively target tumors to release drugs.

Targeting Glycobinding Proteins

In addition to the overexpression of receptors associated with cell proliferation in tumor cells, various glucose-binding proteins such as glucose transporters, sialic acid glycoproteins, and lectin receptors have also been found to be overexpressed on the surface of tumor cells, implying that polysaccharides or glycans can also be used as ligands for NDCDS-targeted drug delivery.218–221 For example, glucose-conjugated camptothecin-loaded gliadin nanoparticles (Glu-CPT-Gliadin-NPs) can deliver camptothecin to breast cancer cells MCF-7 by targeting glucose transporters on the surface of tumor cells, thereby targeting tumor cells and increasing the efficacy of anticancer drugs.222 In addition, the increased fructose utilization rate of fructose in tumor cells leads to the high expression of fructose transmembrane transporter in tumor cells, which can also be used to target nanodrug delivery.223 Not only that, sialic acid glycoprotein receptors (ASGPRs) are also overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells, and galactose can specifically recognize ASGPRs, for example, galactose-modified nanocarriers have the effect of increasing drug uptake, increasing cancer cell death, and inhibiting tumor growth in orthotopic xenograft models of liver cancer.224 Furthermore, mannose-based NDCDSs can also be developed based on the high expression of lectins in cancer cells and mannose has a high affinity for lectins. For example, mannose-modified layered double hydroxide nanocomposites can more effectively kill cancer cells in vitro through lectin receptor-mediated drug endocytosis.225

Targeting Cell Adhesion Molecules

Integrins are a class of cell adhesion molecules that mediate cell-cell and extracellular matrix interactions, which are closely related to the proliferation, invasion, and migration of cancer cells,226 and are highly expressed in a variety of tumor cells, including breast, gastric, pancreatic, and prostate cancers.128 The RGD sequence has a strict targeting specificity for integrins and is the most widely studied integrin-targeting ligand. It can selectively recognize integrins on tumor cells, such as RGD-modified NDCDS can specifically target integrin-expressing cancer cells and be recognized by αvβ3, αvβ5, αvβ6, αvβ8, or α5β1 integrins on cancer cells for targeted drug delivery.146,227 In addition to RGD motifs, isoDGR (isoAsp-Gly-Arg) is also an αvβ3 integrin-binding motif, such as gold nanospheres labeled with isoDGR peptide, which are effective carriers for the delivery of pro-inflammatory cytokines to the tumor vasculature, which can achieve targeted drug delivery by targeting αvβ3 integrins on tumor cells, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor activity of the chemotherapy drug doxorubicin and delaying tumor growth in tumor-bearing mice.228

Targeting Stem Cell Markers

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are a major cause of tumor recurrence and resistance to treatment, CD44 is considered a marker for many CSCs,229,230 and which is overexpressed on the surface of lung cancer, breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, ovarian cancer and other tumor cells,231 so CD44 can also be used as a target on the surface of tumor cells for drug targeted delivery.128 Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a readily biodegradable natural polymer that not only binds specifically to CD44 receptors, but also has good biocompatibility and biodegradability,232 and is currently a widely used ligand targeting CD44. It has been confirmed that HA modified NDCDS can specifically bind to CD44 in NDCDS, thereby promoting drug loaded nanoparticles to actively target tumor cells. Not only that, HA-modified NDCDS can also interfere with the binding of endogenous HA to CD44, increasing drug aggregation at tumor sites and further enhancing the tumor inhibitory effect of nanodrugs.128 In addition to CD44, CD133 is also considered a marker for stem cells,233 while A15 is an RNA aptamer that binds to CD133 and potently mediates cellular internalization.234 Lipid polymers conjugated to CD133 have been shown to effectively and specifically facilitate the delivery of drug-loaded nanoparticles to tumor cells, and significantly reduce the survival rate of CD133-positive cancer cells compared with non-targeted nanoparticles.235 In addition, the nerve growth factor receptor CD271 is also considered a specific biomarker for stem cells and has been found to be expressed in various cancers,236 therefore, CD271 can also be used as a target for NDCDS to transport drugs to tumor cells. For example, the results of covalently linking CD271 monoclonal antibody with polyethylene glycol functionalized multifunctional hollow gold nanospheres (HGNs PEG-CD271) for the treatment of osteosarcoma showed that these biomimetic nanoparticles can target osteosarcoma stem cells and be effectively absorbed, and HGNs-PEG-CD271 has a stronger inhibitory effect on tumor cells in vitro compared to non-targeted PEG-modified HGNs.237

Targeting Tumor Organelles

It is also possible to kill tumors by destroying tumor organelles since a variety of organelles play an important role in maintaining the normal physiological function of cells, and nanodrug delivery systems can also exert anti-tumor effects by targeting tumor organelles in addition to targeting the surface receptors of tumor cells. At present, various nanodrug delivery systems targeting tumor organelles have been developed, such as those targeting mitochondria, lysosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and the cell nucleus. And it has been proven that these drug-loaded nanoparticles targeting organelles can effectively kill tumor. In the following content, we will introduce nanodrug carriers that target tumor organelles.

Targeting Mitochondria

Mitochondria are a bilayer of membrane-coated semi-autonomous organelles that play a vital role in various biological processes.145,238 Cancer cell mitochondria show greater transmembrane potential compared to normal cells, and based on this difference nanodrug delivery systems targeting cancer cell mitochondria can be developed.239 At present, the widely used mitochondrial targeting ligands mainly include triphenylphosphine (TPP), dequinoline (DQA), mitochondrial penetrating peptide (MPP), rhodamine, guanidine, and biguanide. Among them, TPP is composed of a positively charged phosphorus atom surrounded by three hydrophobic phenyl groups, and based on this structural characteristics make it easy to pass through the phospholipid bilayer and accumulate into the mitochondrial matrix.240 TPP can induce ROS generation, reduce mitochondrial membrane potential, and induce apoptosis of tumor cells in vitro when conjugation with anticancer drugs, on the contrary, drugs without TPP conjugation have not observed this phenomenon.240 DQA is a lipophilic compound with two positive charges, which can transport drugs or DNA to the mitochondria of cells, such as DQA and pDNA complex nanoparticles (DQAsome-DNA complex) can escape from endosomes and transport pDNA to mitochondrial sites and selectively release pDNA when contact with mitochondria membranes.241 In addition to TPP and DQA, the mitochondrial penetrating peptide MPP with repeating lipophilic and cationic residues also has mitochondria-targeted.242 Furthermore, rhodamine with oleophilic and cationic properties (such as rhodamine 19 and rhodamine 123) can also effectively target mitochondria and disrupt mitochondrial redox electron transport.243,244 Not only that, guanidine and biguanide can also accumulate in mitochondria as photosensitizers and phototoxic agents, and combining with nanomaterials can not only enhance the targeting of mitochondria, but also increase the phototoxicity of cancer cells.239 In addition to the NDCDS targeting cancer cell mitochondria introduced earlier, various nanocarriers such as metal nanoparticles, liposomes, micelles, and dendrimers can also exert antitumor effects by targeting mitochondria.145 For example, gold nanoparticles can accumulate in cell mitochondria and trigger apoptosis after being taken up by tumor cells in vitro, and significantly inhibit tumor growth in mice.245 Silver nanoparticles can induce apoptosis in tumor cells in vitro by damaging mitochondria.246 In addition, zinc oxide NPs can also target mitochondria and exert mitochondrial destruction.145 In short, based on the mitochondrial differences between cancer cells and normal cells, nanodrug delivery systems targeting the mitochondria of cancer cells have become an effective strategy for precision drug delivery for tumor treatment.

Targeting Lysosomes

Lysosomes are known as the endpoints of cellular degradation, which contain a variety of hydrolase enzymes that can break down extracellular substances and intracellular debris and can control metabolism, participate in various physiological processes and cell signaling and cell death.247 Tumor cells have a higher number of lysosomes, more fragile membranes, larger volumes, and higher cathepsin activity than normal cells,248 based on this, lysosomes can also be used as target organelles for nanodrug delivery systems. For example, lysosomes can be an effective trigger for nanodrug delivery systems based on their low pH (4.5–5) properties,249 groups such as disulfide, hydrazone, and dinitroimidazole are highly reactive to acidic environments and can be degraded to facilitate precise drug release in the lysosomal environment, thereby allowing the drug to passively target lysosomes.250 In addition, drug-loaded nanomaterials can also be conjugated to therapeutics with lysosomal localization groups, to drive entire compounds onto lysosomes with the help of lysosomal acidity. Common lysosome-targeting groups include 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)morpholine, N,N-dimethylethylenediamine, and diethylene glycol methyl ether,248 whose weak alkalinity makes them easy to aggregate in the acidic lysosomal environment and thus play a role in targeting lysosomes. Lysosomes are also key sites for various cell death pathways (such as apoptosis, autophagy, and necrosis),251 for example, autophagy is a lysosome-dependent self-destruct method in various cell death mechanisms.252 The lysosomal cell death pathway is typically performed rapidly when altering the lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP)-mediated pathway and the massive discharge of its hydrolyzed contents into the cytoplasm,253 and based on this exogenous stimuli-responsive nanodrug delivery systems can be designed to target lysosomes and induce cell death by altering LMP. For example, photosensitizer-mediated chemiluminescence reactions can lead to partial or complete disruption of the integrity of lysosomal membranes through the production of ROS, which in turn releases cathepsin through the LMP pathway, leading to the activation of endogenous apoptotic stimuli.254 In addition, the alternating magnetic field can also improve the LMP, and can enhance the release of the lysosomal enzyme cascade and the massive cleavage of organelles.255 In other words, the nanodrug delivery system targeting cancer cell lysosomes can increase the accumulation of nanodrugs at tumor lysosome sites and further promote lysosomal disruption, thereby exerting a tumor-killing effect.256

Targeting the Golgi Apparatus

The Golgi apparatus is also a promising target for cancer therapy. It is composed of several layers of flattened vesicles stacked together and is crucial for the processing, sorting, and transport of newly synthesized proteins and lipids.257 While abnormal Golgi dynamics may lead to abnormal morphology and directional changes during trafficking, thereby enhancing the ability of tumor cells to invade and metastasize in the pathological conditions of cancer.248 Based on this, the Golgi apparatus can also be used as a target to achieve targeted delivery and release of drugs,248 such as some Aluminum III, Rhenium I and Iridium III complexes have been reported for Golgi-specific imaging, indicating that metal complexes have the potential to target the Golgi apparatus.258–260 For example, nanomaterials loaded with platinum(II) complex Pt3 can induce a large amount of Golgi stress, which is manifested by Golgi structure fragmentation, Golgi protein downregulation (GM130, GRASP65/55), loss of Golgi-dependent transport and glycosylation, thereby leading to loss of protein homeostasis and cancer cell death.261 In addition, an aggregation-induced emission luminogen (AIEgen) based photosensitizer (TPE-PyT-CPS) can also effectively target the Golgi apparatus via caveolin/raft mediation endocytosis, and Golgi apparatus protein (p115/GM130) exhibits Golgi apparatus fragmentation and cleavage under light irradiation, and further activate the apoptotic pathway, resulting in Golgi apparatus targeting TPE-T-CPS showing better anti-tumor effects in tumor-bearing mice compared to the non-Golgi apparatus-targeting TPE-PyT-PS.262 The Golgi-targeting nanomedicine LM/CS@M can catalyze hydroxyl radical (·OH) generation under ultrasound activation, thereby causing oxidative damage to the Golgi apparatus, disrupting protein processing and secretion, which in turn triggers tumor cell apoptosis and releases immunogenic molecules, activating anti-tumor immune responses.263

Targeting the Endoplasmic Reticulum

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a continuous membrane system and an important organelle that controls a myriad of cellular functions, including protein folding, lipid biosynthesis, and calcium ion homeostasis.264 Moreover, it also plays a crucial role in cell homeostasis and cell survival,265 but in cancer cells, the ER becomes a target for anticancer therapy due to functional dysfunction.264 For example, nanoparticles-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress can enhance apoptosis and autophagy, and many nanoparticles have been found to play a role by targeting ER. For example, silver NPs can induce apoptosis by modulating ER stress, including the activation of protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase, inositol-requiring protein 1, and C/EBP homologous protein.266 In addition, near infrared II fluorescent cascade targeting nanoparticle (NPER/BO-PDT) can also target ER and can generate ROS in the endoplasmic reticulum under near-infrared light irradiation, thereby further stimulating adaptive immune responses and synergistically inhibiting the growth of orthotopic osteosarcoma in mice.267 Moreover, the ER-targeting spherical nano-scale particle, self-assembled from endoplasmic reticulum targeting groups with fluorescent labels, endoplasmic reticulum stress inducers, and nuclear DNA-damaging drugs, can also specifically localize to the endoplasmic reticulum and induce endoplasmic reticulum stress, thereby leading to tumor cell apoptosis.264 Currently, the groups used for targeting the endoplasmic reticulum mainly include methanesulfonamide and its derivatives, such as N-toluenesulfonylethylenediamine (targeted by binding to ER sulfonamide receptors) and phosphoethanolamine (targeting ER through low-density lipoprotein receptor-mediated endocytosis). In addition, peptides with KDEL sequences (binding to KDEL receptors on the ER membrane) or AAKKKA (peptides that interact with specific ER membrane proteins) can also target ER, and these ER-targeting groups can be directly conjugated to the drug through chemical bonds, thereby allowing the drug to target the ER.248

Targeting the Cell Nucleus

The nucleus as a center for the storage, replication, and transcription of genetic material, is considered the ultimate target for many cancer treatments. Some chemotherapy drugs such as platinum, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide exert anti-tumor effects by entering the nucleus and interfering with nucleic acids. If these drugs are delivered to the perinuclear and intranuclear regions of tumor cells, their therapeutic effects can be maximized.268 Among them, the nuclear localization signal (NLS) as a special oligopeptide, has good nuclear targeting properties. Classic NLS promotes cargo entry into the nucleus depending on importin α and importin β,269 and allows nanoparticles with a diameter of smaller than 40 nm or flexible-shaped nanoparticles larger than 40 nm to pass through the nuclear pore complex (NPC), thereby accumulating cargo in the nucleus.270 While TAT peptides can promote the entry of rigid nanoparticles with a diameter of >40 nm into the nucleus, and TAT peptides can not only act as NLS but also as cell penetrating peptides (CPPs). In addition, TAT peptides can transport goods through the cell membrane more efficiently than NLS, thereby promoting the delivery of nanoparticles to the nucleus with higher efficiency.271,272 At present, many nuclear-targeted nanodrug delivery systems have been developed and utilized, such as liposomes and polymeric micelles labeled by NLS (NLS or TAT peptides) have been shown to target drugs to the nucleus of cancer cells to increase the efficacy of anti-tumor drugs, such as liposomes can be directly modified by TAT to deliver loaded substances to the nucleus,273 moreover, polymer micelles modified by NLS can also deliver genes to the nucleus.274 In addition to organic nanocarriers, inorganic nanocarriers can also target the nucleus to release drugs, such as gold nanoparticles can effectively target the nucleus thereby enhancing the therapeutic response.275 In addition, TT-MSN-TAT formed by TAT peptide-modified mesoporous silica-loaded topotecan can also effectively deliver topotecan to the nucleus to improve chemotherapy efficacy.276

Clinical Applications of Nanoparticle Drug Delivery Systems

Currently, there are approximately more than 20 types of nanomaterial-based drugs approved by FDA and EMA for cancer treatment.117 Among them, Doxil® was approved in the United States in 1995 for the treatment of ovarian cancer and AIDS-related Kaposi’s sarcoma, and it is the first liposomal drug approved for clinical use.277,278 Liposomal irinotecan (nal-IRI; Onivyde®), also known as PEGylated liposomal irinotecan, has been approved for patients with metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma whose disease has progressed after gemcitabine treatment.279 MepactTM is liposomal muramyl tripeptide phosphatidylethanolamine (Mifamurtide), which can activate monocytes and macrophages, and has been approved for the treatment of osteosarcoma.280 VyxeosTM is a liposomal formulation of a combination of daunorubicin and cytarabine, approved for the treatment of adult high-risk acute myeloid leukemia.117 Abraxane™ and Pazenir™ are albumin-bound nanoparticle forms of paclitaxel, approved by the FDA and EMA in 2005 and 2008 respectively, for the treatment of metastatic breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer.115–117 Apealea is a paclitaxel polymer micelle, approved by the EMA for the treatment of epithelial ovarian cancer, primary peritoneal cancer, and fallopian tube cancer.278 Another paclitaxel polymer micelle formulation Genexol-PM®, was approved in 2007 for the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and pancreatic cancer.281 Eligard® is a leuprorelin acetate-loaded polymer nanoparticle, approved by the FDA in 2002 for the treatment of prostate cancer.278 In addition, amino-silane-coated iron oxide nanoparticles Nanotherm TD, have been approved by the FDA and EMA for the treatment of glioblastoma and prostate cancer.117 Besides the aforementioned nanoparticle drugs approved for cancer treatment, there are currently more than 60 phase 1–3 clinical trials ongoing.278

Challenges and Future Directions

Although some nanomedicines have been approved for treating cancer patients, their number is still limited. Translating laboratory-effective nanomedicines into clinical drugs still faces many difficulties, including but not limited to the following: (1) The mononuclear phagocyte system is considered the main eliminator of nanomedicines such as mRNA lipid nanoparticles, clearing these particles from their intended targets and redirecting them, thereby reducing their delivery efficiency.282 (2) Limitations of the EPR effect: Currently, the understanding of EPR-mediated tumor-targeted accumulation mainly comes from xenograft mouse models, and this model cannot accurately replicate naturally occurring human solid tumors. In addition, due to the heterogeneity and complexity of human tumor types, locations, sizes, stages, microenvironments, vascular density, and blood perfusion status, the EPR effect varies significantly among different patients, tumor types, and even between primary tumors and metastatic tumors in the same patient. Moreover, the EPR effect is not static and exhibits transient characteristics over time, these features also lead to insufficient accumulation of nanoparticles in tumors, which in turn prevents EPR-based nanoparticles from achieving the ideal passive targeting of tumors, thereby leading to an overestimation of their therapeutic efficacy.283 (3) Nanoplatforms responsive to the tumor microenvironment are challenged by intratumoral pH, ROS levels, redox gradients, oxygen gradients, as well as the concentration and activity of specific enzymes, and photoresponsive systems also face the problem of limited tissue light penetration. (4) Even if nanomedicines bind to tumor cells with high selectivity and specificity, the internalization efficiency is still affected by ligand density, receptor expression, and the size, shape, and surface charge of the nanomedicine. In addition, biomolecules adsorb onto their surface to form a biomolecular corona when nanoparticles come into contact with biological fluids (such as blood or interstitial fluid), which can cover the targeting ligands and thereby reduce their ability to bind to specific receptors on tumor cells.283 (5) Although organelle-targeted nanomedicines can achieve subcellular localization of drugs, organelles vary depending on cell type and physiological state, which makes it difficult to develop a single targeted nanomedicine strategy that can accurately locate specific organelles and cover multiple cellular environments.283 (6) Nanoparticles or their degradation fragments may cause harmful nano-bio interactions, leading to nanotoxicity, including genotoxicity, immunotoxicity, neurotoxicity, pulmonary toxicity, and vascular dysfunction. Nanoparticles can also cause mitochondrial and lysosomal damage, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and Golgi apparatus rupture. Additionally, some immunotoxicity can still be observed six months to one year after administration.284,285 In addition, some nanomedicines have immunogenicity and can induce the production of anti-drug antibodies. For example, the use of PEG-based nanomedicines may lead to the generation of anti-PEG IgG and IgM as well as hypersensitivity reactions, thereby resulting in reduced efficacy and safety issues.286 (7) Most inorganic nanomaterials are relatively stable under physiological conditions, but in vivo degradability is the most important issue, such as the toxicity caused by metal ions contained in inorganic nanoparticles, which is a key limiting factor for their clinical development.287 (8) The preparation process is usually complex, performing well in small-scale laboratory production, but maintaining quality consistency in large-scale manufacturing is challenging, especially for nanocarriers with more complex structures and manufacturing processes.278,288 (9) Cancer tissues in animal models cannot replicate the characteristics of human malignant tumors, resulting in differences between the therapeutic effects observed in preclinical studies and clinical efficacy. (10) Currently, global regulatory policies for nanomedicine are still not perfect, and the process from drug manufacturing to approval is very complex and time-consuming, which affects the progress of translating nanomedicine into clinical applications. Although drug-loaded nanosystems face many challenges in clinical translation, stimulus-responsive nanomedicines and targeted tumor nanomedicine delivery systems have great potential for precise cancer therapy. Looking to the future, using artificial intelligence in the design of nanocarriers will accelerate the development of nanomedicines for tumor treatment. Combining multi-responsive systems with emerging models (developing novel animal models that simulate human tumor heterogeneity and specific physiological characteristics) will help further improve therapeutic outcomes. With advances in biocompatible materials and scalable manufacturing technologies, these nanomedicines are expected to overcome existing limitations and provide safer and more effective cancer treatment options.

Conclusion

At present, NDCDS has become an effective solution to the delivery barriers of various substances such as anticancer drugs, anticancer natural products, and anticancer genes. In this review, we describe the common carriers in nanodrug delivery systems, introduce the advantages of using nanocarriers for drug delivery, elaborate on the targeting of nanodrugs in detail, and introduce nanodrugs currently applied in clinical settings. Although drug-loaded nano systems have many advantages in disease treatment, only a few nanodrugs have been approved by the FDA for tumor treatment, and many nanodrugs face issues such as chemical instability, consistency of quality, complexity in scale-up, nanotoxicity, and overestimation of efficacy during clinical translation. However, based on the advantages of drug-loaded nano systems in improving efficacy and reducing the toxicity of tumor treatment, and with the continuous improvement of drug-loaded nano systems and the perfection of related policies, nanodrug delivery systems are bound to become the development direction of tumor therapy.
