Reducing unmeasured confounding from non-compliance with randomization in surgical RCTs: a simulation study
Authors: Zhenzhen Lu, Wenwen Li, Mengyao Wang, Lihong Huang, Feng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-compliance with randomization is pervasive in surgical randomized controlled trials (RCTs), introducing unmeasured confounding that undermines valid causal inference. Conventional methods, such as intention-to-treat (ITT), per-protocol (PP), as-treated (AT) and instrumental variable (IV) analysis, exhibit significant limitations.MethodsWe conducted a simulation study to compare the performance of negative control methods (NCM), including difference-in-differences (DiD) and double negative controls (DNC), with traditional approaches (ITT, PP, AT, IV). Parameters from influential lumbar discectomy trials informed realistic crossover scenarios with crossover rates ranging from 10% to 40%. Background

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are considered as the gold standard for intervention evaluation, yet treatment crossover frequently undermines their validity. In surgical RCTs, non-compliance rates tend to exceed those in pharmacological studies due to procedural complexity, surgeon expertise, and patient-related factors [1]. Recent meta-analyses have reported crossover rates of 7–13% in cardiac surgery RCTs [2], a mean of 12.6% (range 0–45%) in gastrointestinal cancer trials [3], and 8–42% in spine surgery trials (Supplemental Table S1) [4]. In the Spine Patient Outcomes Research Trial (SPORT), 43% of patients crossed over from conservative treatment to surgery and 42% vice versa within 24 months [5]. Similarly, in the anterior cruciate ligament surgery necessity in non-acute patients (ACL SNNAP) trial, 41% of patients allocated to rehabilitation crossed over to surgical reconstruction within 18 months, and 28% assigned to surgery did not receive their allocated intervention [6].

Conventional approaches to non-compliance with randomization exhibit critical limitations [7, 8]. Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis preserves randomization integrity but attenuates effect estimates by diluting results with non-compliant participants. Per-protocol (PP) and as-treated (AT) analyses introduce bias by violating randomization principles. While methods like regression adjustment, propensity score matching, or inverse probability weighting can mitigate bias from measured confounders, they require complete confounder measurement and correct model specification. Instrumental variable (IV) methods leverage randomization to address unmeasured confounding under strict assumptions (Fig. 1), yet often yield imprecise estimates in surgical settings where these assumptions are frequently violated [9].

Negative control methods (NCM) offer a promising alternative by leveraging a priori null associations to detect and adjust for unmeasured confounding [10, 11]. These approaches utilize two proxy variables: negative control outcomes (NCOs), which are unaffected by treatment, and negative control exposures (NCEs), which lack causal effects on the outcomes of interest (Fig. 2). Such methods are increasingly adopted in epidemiology and observational studies for bias detection [10, 12, 13], with growing recognition of their utility in RCTs [14]. The double negative control (DNC) framework integrates both NCEs and NCOs to enable non-parametric identification of treatment effects [15]. By modeling a confounding bridge function, DNC isolates causal effects from pervasive unmeasured confounders [16]. Notably, difference-in-differences (DiD), a specialized form using baseline outcomes as NCOs, adjusts for time-invariant confounding when the assumption of additive equi-confounding holds [17]. Both approaches are well-suited for surgical RCTs where temporal proxies (e.g., preoperative outcomes as NCOs, delayed exposures as NCEs) are naturally available.

Given their abilities to detect and correct bias with fewer parametric constraints, we conducted a simulation study to compare the performance of negative control methods (including DNC and DiD) with standard approaches (including ITT, AT, PP, IV) under settings of realistic surgical trials.

Methods

The section begins with a brief overview of the SPORT trial, which informed the simulation design. It then describes the simulation framework, which emulates key features of surgical RCTs with treatment crossover. Next, details of the six analytical methods compared are provided: ITT, PP, AT, IV, DiD, and DNC. Finally, the performance metrics used to evaluate bias, precision, and coverage are outlined.

SPORT study

Our simulation framework was inspired by the SPORT trial, a landmark multicenter RCT conducted between 2000 and 2004 across 13 spine clinics in America [8]. The study enrolled 501 surgical candidates with imaging-confirmed lumbar intervertebral disk herniation and persistent radicular symptoms lasting ≥ 6 weeks despite nonoperative care. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either standard open discectomy or individualized nonoperative treatment (including physical therapy, education, medications). Despite its rigorous design, substantial non-compliance occurred: only 57% of participants assigned to surgery underwent surgery within 24 months, while 42% of those assigned to nonoperative treatment crossed over to surgery. This high two-way crossover introduced significant unmeasured confounding, limiting the interpretability of the ITT analysis. Although both groups improved in the primary outcomes, between-group differences in SF-36 bodily pain and physical function scores were small and not statistically significant.

Performance measures

Because the treatment effect is homogeneous across all values of U, the CACE equals the overall ATE, implying that ATE = CACE = τ. For each method m, the estimation performance of was evaluated using bias, empirical standard error (SE), mean squared error (MSE), and coverage probability (CP). Bias was defined as the average deviation of estimated effect from true effect across simulations and SE was empirical standard deviation of estimates. MSE was calculated as the average squared difference between the estimated effect and the true effect. CP was defined as the proportion of 95% confidence intervals (CI) containing the true effect.

Conventional approaches exhibited increasing bias as the crossover rate increased. At n = 100 (Table 2), the ITT estimator’s bias changed from − 1.019 to − 4.018 as the crossover rate increased from 10% to 40%. Bias was larger for the AT and PP estimators, ranging from − 2.491 to − 6.611 and − 1.504 to − 5.955, respectively. The IV estimator showed smaller bias (0.008–0.549) than AT and PP, with SE increasing 4.4-fold from 1.118 to 4.916 as the crossover rate increased. Under the equal confounding assumption, the DiD estimator had negligible bias (|bias| ≤ 0.003). When this assumption was violated, bias increased, reaching − 2.200 in the scenario with a 40% crossover rate and n = 100. In contrast, the DNC estimator showed bias close to zero across the settings presented in Table 2 (|bias| ≤ 0.415). As sample size increased, the variability of the IV estimator decreased, whereas ITT, AT, and PP exhibited persistent bias (Fig. 4).

Regarding MSE, the ITT, AT, and PP estimators exhibited consistently large values (a maximum of 44.037), consistent with their large bias (Table 2). The IV estimator had smaller bias but showed an increase in MSE at a crossover rate of 40% (MSE = 25.612 at a crossover rate of 40% with n = 100). The DiD estimator maintained low MSE under equal confounding (MSE ≤ 0.239) but showed pronounced deterioration when this assumption was violated (MSE = 5.099 at a crossover rate of 40% with n = 100). The DNC estimator achieved consistently low MSE across settings (MSE ≤ 2.515), including scenarios with high crossover. As sample size increased, MSE decreased for the IV estimator, but it remained higher than that of DNC across sample sizes; ITT, PP, and AT continued to exhibit large MSE with limited improvement (Fig. 5).

Conventional methods (ITT, AT, PP) showed poor CP, which declined toward 0% as the crossover rate increased (Table 2; Fig. 6). The IV estimator maintained CP close to the nominal 95% level; however, confidence intervals became wider as the crossover rate increased. Under the equal confounding assumption, the DiD estimator achieved CP ≥ 94.3%. When this assumption was violated, CP decreased to 4.6% in the scenario with a 40% crossover rate and n = 100. In contrast, the DNC estimator maintained generally near-nominal coverage across all simulated settings.

Type I error and statistical power further distinguished the methods (Figs. 7 and 8). Under the null treatment effect, ITT and IV generally showed type I error closer to the nominal 0.05 level, whereas AT and PP showed inflated type I error across settings. DiD maintained low type I error under equal confounding but type I error increased when the equal confounding assumption was violated. DNC maintained type I error close to the nominal 0.05 level in most settings, although it was slightly conservative when the crossover rate was 40% and the sample size was 100, with type I error around 0.02. For statistical power, ITT, PP, AT, and IV generally showed lower power, with the lowest values observed at a crossover rate of 40% and a sample size of 100. DiD retained high power across settings. DNC also showed power exceeding 80% in most settings, falling below 80% only when the crossover rate was 40% and the sample size was 100.

Sensitivity analyses under randomization with exact final 1:1 group sizes yielded consistent results (Supplemental Figures S3–S7). Across Scenarios B–F, the overall ranking of methods remained broadly similar to that in Scenario A, with DNC generally showing greater robustness than ITT, PP, AT, and IV, and DiD remaining sensitive to violation of the equal-confounding assumption. However, the direction of bias under the non-null setting varied across scenarios. In Scenarios A, C, and F, PP and AT showed estimates that tended to fall below the true positive treatment effect, together with inflated type I error and lower power. In Scenarios B, D, and E, PP and AT showed estimates that tended to exceed the true positive treatment effect, together with inflated type I error and correspondingly higher power. These scenario-specific differences are shown in Supplemental Figures S8–S32.

We also performed an additional repetition-stability analysis under the most challenging setting of Scenario A (n = 100, crossover rate = 40%) to evaluate whether 2000 simulation repetitions were adequate. Across independent reruns, the variability of the estimated bias, MSE, coverage probability, type I error, and statistical power decreased as the number of repetitions increased (Supplemental Figures S33–S37). The distributions were visibly more concentrated at larger repetition counts, with comparatively limited changes beyond 2000 repetitions.

Discussion

This simulation study comprehensively evaluates methods for addressing unmeasured confounding due to non-compliance with randomization in surgical RCTs. Consistent with prior literature, conventional approaches (ITT, AT, PP) yielded severely biased estimates and inadequate CP under the non-compliance settings evaluated in this study. ITT estimates were diluted towards the null due to non-complier outcome averaging, risking false-negative conclusions. Despite this bias, ITT analyses better reflect real-world clinical scenarios and remain widely recommended as the primary analytic approach in randomized trials [19]. Naive PP and AT analyses, ignoring randomization, also produced bias because protocol-deviating patients differed systematically from adherers.

IV methods used to estimate CACE exhibited lower bias than AT and PP in the presence of unmeasured confounding, provided their assumptions (relevance, exclusion restriction, and monotonicity) were met. This aligns with recent simulation results by Papazoglou et al. (2025) [20], showing IV recovers the CACE without bias under standard assumptions, whereas PP estimators are unbiased only under completely random non-compliance. However, our study and others [3, 21] highlight important limitations of IV. First, the local effect (CACE) is restricted to compliers and may not generalize. Second, IV estimates suffer from imprecision and wide CIs in moderate sample sizes or with weak instruments (e.g. low compliance). Inflated SEs and reduced coverage were observed when compliance dropped or sample size was limited, consistent with IV’s large-sample power requirement. Third, IV assumptions may be violated in surgery trials: for example, if randomization (the instrument) has any direct effect on outcomes beyond via treatment received (for example, due to patient or provider behavior changes upon knowing the assignment), or if there exist always-takers/never-takers outside the complier strata, standard IV estimators become biased. While novel estimators relaxing these assumptions exist, they are uncommon in clinical research [22].

An additional finding from our simulations is that type I error and statistical power must be interpreted jointly, because both depend on the direction and magnitude of estimator bias under treatment crossover. In our study, PP and AT frequently showed inflated type I error under the null, indicating that these estimators were often shifted away from 0 even when no true effect was present. However, this did not imply uniformly high power under a non-null effect. Instead, the relationship between type I error and power was scenario-dependent. In Scenarios A, C, and F, the estimates from PP and AT tended to underestimate the true positive treatment effect under the alternative (Table 2; Fig. 4, Figure S13, Figure S28), so although these estimators were shifted away from 0 under the null, they were pulled back toward 0 under the non-null setting, resulting in limited power. In Scenarios B, D, and E, by contrast, the estimates tended to overestimate the true positive treatment effect under the alternative (Figure S8, Figure S18, Figure S23), and the corresponding power was therefore more consistent with the inflated rejection behavior observed under the null.

In contrast to the inconsistent performance of ITT, PP/AT, and IV methods, the NCM demonstrated more robust performance across the evaluated settings. The DiD method performed well under the ideal condition that the equal confounding assumption held. When valid, DiD produced unbiased estimates with improved precision. Its advantage lies in canceling time-invariant confounders influencing baseline and follow-up equally by analyzing within-patient change. Baseline measurements, often available in RCTs, serve as patient-specific controls. However, DiD performed poorly when the equi-confounding assumption was violated. In simulations, modest deviations caused greater DiD bias than IV in some cases. This aligns with theory: DiD is not robust to time-varying confounding or differential confounder changes [17]. Practically, baseline outcomes cannot always capture all confounding relevant for post-randomization compliance, especially in longer trials where new confounders may arise. Thus, while performed well when the equi-confounding assumption held, DiD can be misleading if violated.

Findings reinforce the conclusion that no single conventional method is universally reliable for addressing non-compliance. A 2023 systematic review of simulation studies [2] noted that while IV methods are commonly employed, they often show bias under certain settings, such as in the presence of an increasing hazard or unmeasured confounding, highlighting the need for further studies on “G-methods” (e.g., inverse probability weighting, g-estimation) in realistic trials. Earlier overviews similarly concluded that CACE and IV methods, which adjust for non-adherence, rely on strong and potentially vulnerable assumptions [1, 23]. For example, the exclusion restriction is a particularly strong assumption and is often difficult to verify in practice, and the monotonicity assumption is also strong and may not hold in many real-world settings. As Bang and Davis famously noted, neither ITT nor IV are perfect solutions, particularly in complex surgical trials [21]. The persistent risk of bias/imprecision with ITT, PP, AT, and IV methods highlights the need for new approaches. Zhu et al. [24] proposed augmented inverse probability weighting for partial compliance with ordinal outcomes, showing improved performance relative to ITT, PP, and IV under selection bias. These studies underscore active research on mitigating non-compliance bias. Within this landscape, the present evaluation adds new evidence: to our knowledge, this study provides one of the first simulation-based evaluations showing that the DNC framework can reduce bias relative to ITT, AT, PP, and IV under crossover-induced unmeasured confounding in RCT settings.

The DNC method showed robust performance across the simulated non-compliance setting. Compared with the other approaches, it generally achieved lower bias and better precision, particularly in settings where the competing methods performed poorly. Across settings including crossover rates up to 40%, DNC produced nearly unbiased estimates with correct or near-correct CI coverage. Its performance remained favorable even in challenging settings, such as a sample size of 100 and a crossover rate of 40%, where DNC reduced MSE by more than 90% relative to IV while maintaining coverage of at least 95%. In contrast, PP, AT, and ITT were mainly limited by bias, whereas IV and DiD were mainly limited by imprecision.

DNC capitalizes natural proxies: (1) pre-randomization outcome (baseline) as an NCO, and (2) randomization assignment (assuming no direct outcome effect) as an NCE. By simultaneously modeling the relationships between unmeasured confounding, these controls and the primary outcome, DNC “differences out” confounding more flexibly and nonparametrically than IV or DiD. DNC requires weaker assumptions and remains accurate even when “equal confounding” was violated—a condition causing DiD breakdown. Practically, DNC leverages more information than a single instrument or baseline adjustment, enhancing robustness to complex confounding.

DNC’s resilience aligns with proximal causal inference literature on negative controls [25]. Originally developed for observational studies, these techniques nonparametrically identify causal effects under unobserved confounding given valid negative control pairs. Our results affirm their utility in RCTs with post-randomization deviations. In surgical trials, common unmeasured factors (e.g., patient frailty, health-seeking behavior) influence both adherence likelihood and outcomes. DNC effectively accounts for these proxies: pre-surgery functional scores might act as NCOs reflecting frailty, and random assignment (or a delayed treatment offer) can serve as an NCE. By incorporating these, DNC isolates the causal effect of surgery itself. Unlike IV, which excludes non-compliers, DNC uses data from all patients and adjusts via the confounding bridge function, achieving bias reduction and greater efficiency. DNC also produced stable estimates under low compliance, scenarios where IV estimates become unstable or non-identifiable. Thus, DNC offers more robust adjustment for crossover bias than conventional methods, contingent on identifying suitable negative controls.

Anticipate and Mitigate Crossovers in Trial Design

Anticipate and Mitigate Crossovers in Trial Design

Recognize during planning that a proportion of patients may not adhere to their assigned treatment. Refine eligibility/protocols to minimize non-compliance (e.g., exclude patients unlikely to accept allocation, enhance surgeon training to reduce conversions, use ethical early switching rules). Adjust sample size calculations to account for expected non-compliance (e.g., inflation based on anticipated compliance rates). However, simply increasing sample size is not a panacea; combining moderate increases with robust analyses (below) is often more cost-effective.

(2)Employ Advanced Methods Beyond Naive ITT/PP

Employ Advanced Methods Beyond Naive ITT/PP

Plan a priori to use methods accounting for non-compliance. Interpret ITT, AT, and PP results with extreme caution when crossover rates are high. At minimum, consider IV analysis to estimate CACE, while explicitly reporting its limitations (wider CIs) and conducting sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of potential violations of the exclusion restriction. Present both ITT and adjusted estimates (e.g., IV or DNC) to provide a comprehensive picture. ICH E9 recommends presenting analyses based on both the full analysis set and the per-protocol set, with any differences explicitly discussed [26]. More recently, ICH E9(R1) emphasizes that non-adherence should be handled through clearly defined estimands and appropriately aligned estimators, rather than naïve per-protocol analyses [27].

(3)Leverage DNC Methods with Thoughtful Data Collection

Leverage DNC Methods with Thoughtful Data Collection

Our study demonstrates the promise of the DNC methods, which is encouraged for use in future surgical trials facing compliance issues, given its fewer parametric assumptions and ability to handle unmeasured confounders. To implement DNC, prospectively collect high-quality proxies: key baseline outcomes or risk scores (candidate NCOs) and variables influencing treatment uptake that are unrelated to outcomes except via confounding (candidate NCEs, e.g., randomization assignment, scheduled delayed interventions). Proactive planning for these proxies is crucial and requires minimal additional effort. Verify the validity of negative controls, ensuring that the NCO remains unaffected by the treatment and that the NCE has no independent effect on the outcome. Additionally, provide clinical justification for these assumptions. Collaborate with biostatisticians familiar with causal inference for proper application; user-friendly software implementations are emerging.

(4)Plan Sensitivity Analyses and Transparent Reporting

Plan Sensitivity Analyses and Transparent Reporting

Accompany all non-compliance analyses with sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of residual bias (e.g., vary assumptions about unmeasured confounder effects, test direct effects in IV, assess different negative controls in DNC). Report the extent of non-compliance in CONSORT flow diagrams and discuss implications in line with CONSORT extensions [28]. Explicitly acknowledge potential primary analysis bias and present adjusted analyses alongside them. Transparent reporting and rigorous sensitivity checks enhance the reliability of conclusions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it focuses on continuous outcomes and specific additive confounding; while the methods are applicable to binary outcomes, their performance in such contexts requires verification. Integrating DNC with survival models is an important extension. Second, the analysis assumed a simple unmeasured confounding structure, whereas real-world treatment compliance may be influenced by multiple unmeasured and time-varying factors. Although DNC performed well in these settings, its performance in more complex settings requires further study. Third, the success of DNC hinges on the availability of valid negative control. Identifying truly valid NCOs and NCEs is challenging and demands substantive clinical knowledge. If controls are invalid (e.g., baseline outcome affected by pre-randomization treatments, NCE has slight outcome effect), DNC estimates could be biased. Investigators must carefully justify the validity of proxies.

Conclusion

Treatment crossover remains a critical threat to causal inference in surgical trials. These findings support the use of DNC to address unmeasured confounding in scenarios where conventional approaches are biased and IV methods perform poorly. In trials at risk of crossover, researchers should consider prospectively collecting high-fidelity negative control proxies (NCOs/NCEs) and report DNC-based sensitivity analyses alongside primary analyses when non-compliance is substantial. Embracing such robust methods will strengthen the evidence base for surgical interventions.
