Niemann-Pick Disease With Bilateral Adrenal Mass
Authors: Sharifah Noor Adrilla binti Long Mohd Noor Affendi, Chin Voon Tong, Nurul Dayana binti Nordin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractNiemann–Pick disease (NPD) is a heterogeneous group of lysosomal storage disorders with autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. There are 4 types of NPD. Patients with NPD type B generally have better prognosis, allowing them to survive into adulthood. They have a widespread clinical presentation, affecting multiple organs but rarely neurological involvement. Here, we describe the case of a 38-year-old woman with unexplained hepatosplenomegaly and young hypertensive intracranial bleed. Evaluation for young hypertension further revealed a nonfunctional bilateral adrenal mass and gross hepatosplenomegaly. NPD was confirmed through dried blood spot investigation. It showed low activity of acid sphingomyelinase and genetic testing also detected 2 pathogenic mutations. She is being managed by a multidisciplinary team for supportive treatment that includes regular symptoms monitoring and genetic counseling.Keywords: adrenal, adrenocortical disorders, adrenal neoplasia/adrenal mass/hyperplasia

Introduction

Niemann-Pick Disease (NPD) is a rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder. The overall prevalence of acid sphingomyelinase (ASM) deficiency (types A and B combined) is estimated to be 1:250 000 (1). Four types of the disease have been recognized; these are types A, B, C, and D. Type A and type B NPD occur from a deficiency of enzyme ASM. This will lead to pathological accumulation of sphingomyelin and other lipids in lysosomes of various organs, predominantly the liver, spleen, and lungs (2). Additionally, progressive accumulation can happen elsewhere, including the bone marrow, lymph node, brain, kidney, and adrenal glands. On the other hand, types C and D are caused by a defect in the transport of intracellular cholesterol (3).

This disease is uncommonly seen and has diverse clinical features, leaving some patients undiagnosed. There is increasing evidence of new disease-modifying therapies that can ameliorate the prognosis of NPD. Hence, we illustrate a patient with NPD who presented with bicytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, and bilateral adrenal mass.

Case Presentation

A 38-year-old, para 4 woman was referred for evaluation of young hypertension. She was first diagnosed with splenomegaly of unknown etiology at 21 years of age. Interestingly, her younger brother also had a similar diagnosis. At 24 years old, she experienced pregnancy-induced hypertension.

She was well until she developed a hypertensive left basal ganglia bleed at age 38 years. She underwent left decompressive craniectomy with evacuation of clot. During her admission, her full blood picture examination revealed normochromic microcytic anemia with thrombocytopenia. Other workups were unremarkable (Table 1).

Diagnostic Assessment

Hormonal workup for secondary hypertension was negative (Table 1).

Renal Doppler ultrasound showed no evidence of renal artery stenosis, but incidental findings of adrenal lesions and hepatosplenomegaly. Computed tomography (CT) scans of the adrenals and abdomen revealed bilateral lipid poor adrenal lesions (Fig. 1A and 1B). Both adrenal lesions showed similar Hounsfield unit (HU) of 32 HU on plain CT, 66 HU on portovenous view, and 40 HU in delayed 15-minute views. Hence, the absolute washout was 76.5% and relative washout was 39%. The adrenal lesions were unchanged on follow-up CT after 8 months. Furthermore, there was gross hepatosplenomegaly with dilated portal and splenic veins, as shown in Fig. 2A and 2B.

Treatment

Currently, she is stable on 1 antihypertensive medication. She is being managed by a multidisciplinary team that includes an endocrinologist, pulmonologist, and geneticist. Her family members were also advised for screening.

Outcome and Follow Up

This patient sustained residual right-sided hemiparesis because of the hemorrhagic stroke. She remained normotensive on a single antihypertensive medication. Despite not starting enzyme replacement therapy for NPD, she was asymptomatic and there was no involvement in other systems. Moreover, she has been on regular follow-up with the endocrinology, respiratory, and genetic team.

Discussion

Our case illustrates a unique case of NPD type B presenting with bicytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, and bilateral adrenal mass. In our case report, adrenal mass refers to bilateral enlargement/growth of her adrenals which was found during investigation for young hypertension. She also has massive hepatosplenomegaly with bilateral adrenal enlargement that points toward a unifying systemic disease that causes infiltrative/storage diseases that affect multiple organs. Subsequently, the dried blood spot test and genetic testing done were compatible with NPD, in which ASM activity demonstrated low activity and 2 pathogenic mutations (a missense mutation and a frameshift deletion) were detected. In view of involvement of other organs such as hepatosplenomegaly with the confirmatory diagnosis of NPD, the most likely cause of her bilateral adrenal enlargement is due to the same pathology based on the principle of Occam's razor (4). Nonfunctioning adrenal adenomas is a less likely diagnosis because they tend to be smaller. The only definitive test to prove that our patient's bilateral adrenal masses/enlargement is related to NPD will be via histopathology examination through biopsy. Our patient has declined this. Although Gaucher disease should be considered in this case, β-glucocerebrosidase activity was normal, thus ruling out this diagnosis.

Thorough urinary and blood evaluations for secondary hypertension were negative. Unfortunately, we were not able to obtain a urinalysis and serum renin levels. Currently, her blood pressure is controlled well with 1 antihypertensive agent; therefore, we have concluded that this patient has essential hypertension. Furthermore, a literature review found no direct association of NPD with hypertension.

NPD is a rare lysosomal storage disorder with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. The buildup of vacuolated lipid-filled macrophages, known as Niemann- Pick cells, leads to the involvement of various organs: liver, spleen, bone marrow, lung, and central nervous system (5). As this case elucidated, this disease may rarely be first recognized in adulthood, albeit that it is classically diagnosed during childhood. There are 4 main forms: NPD type A and B occur as a result of ASMD, whereas NPD type C and D are due to defect in the transport of intracellular cholesterol (6). NPD type A is an infantile-onset disease with accelerated neurodegenerative course leading to early childhood mortality. NPD type B usually has a later onset, with milder heterogenous symptoms and usually no neurological abnormalities. Patients generally have a good prognosis and survive throughout adulthood. On the other hand, NPD type C has an immensely varied age of onset, with multiple organ involvement including liver, spleen, and lung before development of neurological symptoms, which can be debilitating.

This case has demonstrated that it is challenging to diagnose NPD. High index of suspicion with awareness of its presenting forms are the main elements to clinch the diagnosis. When NPD is suspected, insufficient ASM enzyme activity testing should be performed first. Those with NPD type A or B usually have less than 10% of normal ASM activity. Measuring ASM enzyme activity in peripheral blood lymphocytes or cultured skin fibroblasts is a reliable method to establish the diagnosis (7). A simplified screening method to measure the ASM activity using dried blood spots on filter paper has recently been developed to hasten the diagnosis process (8).

In patients with NPD type B, imaging studies have revealed involvement of other organs such as adrenal glands, kidneys, and lymph nodes. Case reports have described incidental findings of adrenal masses in patients with NPD type B with no evidence of malignancy. They are usually recognized incidentally on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) during imaging of liver and spleen (9). Accumulation of sphingomyelin and lipids can occur in any organ; usually, the liver and spleen. In our case, it caused accumulation in the adrenals, resulting in bilateral adrenal masses/enlargement. Organomegaly in NPD is caused by accumulation of lipid-laden macrophages initially but, over time, organ dysfunction caused by the infiltration may lead to other changes pathologically. For example, in the liver, cirrhotic changes will be seen. Adrenal involvement in NPD has been described to have intermediate signal on T1- and T2-weighted imaging in MRI. Therefore, organomegaly in NPD may not show typical lipid-rich features all the time, as seen in our case (6).

For many years, there has been no treatment for NPD to alter the disease onset and neurological progression or to prolong lifespan. Recently, the emergence of Olipudase alfa, a recombinant human ASM, an enzyme replacement therapy for the treatment of nonneurologic manifestations of ASMD has shown promising outcomes. A 30-month follow-up clinical trial showed statistically significant reductions in liver and spleen volumes. Additionally, there was advancement in the pulmonary system, whereby a mean increase in lung-diffusing capacity and improvement in infiltrative lung disease parameters. Lipid profiles and bone mineral density of the spine also shown improvement in a few patients (10).

Extensive infiltration of organs by NPD can lead to organ dysfunction; however, severity of organ dysfunction depends on degree of infiltration. Because therapeutic curative treatment is limited, most patients were provided supportive treatment with regular complications monitoring. Particularly in our patient, in view of the significant bilateral adrenal mass, evaluation for adrenal insufficiency and hormonal replacement is crucial. A multidisciplinary team approach would provide the best management to patients with NPD.

In conclusion, this case describes a patient initially investigated for young hypertension, later diagnosed with NPD type B. The patient presented with bicytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, and bilateral adrenal masses. The diagnosis was confirmed through a dried blood spot test and genetic testing. Despite declining further investigation, including adrenal biopsy and MRI, due to feeling well otherwise, the overall clinical picture suggests NPD type B. Unfortunately, Olipudase alfa, a potential treatment option, is currently unavailable at our center. It is worth noting that patients with NPD type B generally have a good prognosis and can survive into adulthood. The rarity of bilateral adrenal masses in NPD makes this case noteworthy.
