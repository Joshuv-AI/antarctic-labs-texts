ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroinflammation is a key contributor to pathology in many central nervous system (CNS) diseases. While cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomic studies in adult neurologic conditions have identified insightful inflammatory signatures, fewer studies have been conducted in pediatric diseases. Moreover, past studies primarily employed proteomic approaches that are less suited to detect novel, low-abundance inflammatory mediators that may be critical in pediatric CNS pathophysiology. To address this gap, we applied high-sensitivity, multi-targeted proteomic profiling to characterize the neuroinflammatory signatures across three distinct pediatric neurologic diseases: post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus (PHH), N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis (NMDARE), and brain tumor-associated hydrocephalus.MethodsCSF samples from controls (n = 5) and patients with PHH (n = 9), NMDARE (n = 5), and brain tumor-associated hydrocephalus (n = 10) were obtained from a pediatric CSF biorepository. After proteomic profiling using the Olink Explore platform, 641 inflammation-related proteins were retained for analysis. Differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) were identified using limma with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (FDR < 0.05, |log₂FC| >1). Pathway enrichment of DAPs was performed with Reactome via Enrichr, and protein–protein interaction networks were constructed using STRING to identify functional modules and key hub proteins.ResultsPrincipal component and hierarchical clustering analyses revealed separation of PHH and brain tumor samples from controls, while NMDARE partially overlapped. CSF in PHH contained 532 DAPs, with pathway enrichment analysis identifying alternative complement activation, coagulation, and platelet degranulation pathways as top hits. Conversely, CSF in NMDARE showed only 65 DAPs, with the top pathways involving IL-10 and IL-18 signaling, and the top 3 DAPs involving humoral immunity (IGLC2, MZB1, CD79B). DAPs did not meet statistical significance in brain tumor patients. Longitudinal analysis of serial collections from PHH patients suggested a persistence of coagulation- and complement-related neuroinflammation over time. NELL2 emerged as a consistently downregulated protein in PHH for weeks after the initial hemorrhage.ConclusionsPHH and NMDARE revealed distinct neuroinflammatory proteomic signatures compared to our control samples. PHH was marked by a broad increase in detection of the majority of inflammation-related proteins, with highest representation among the alternative complement and coagulation-related pathways. Conversely, NMDARE induced a narrower lymphocyte-driven profile, more consistent with an antibody-mediated autoimmune disease. Background

CNS inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of many neurological diseases [1–4], and significant advancements in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomics have revealed distinct inflammatory signatures across adult CNS disorders including Alzheimer’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and Parkinson’s disease [5–8]. These findings have provided insight into molecular mechanisms underlying disease pathophysiology and may one day inform clinical practice, with aiding diagnosis, predicting prognosis, or guiding treatment. However, unbiased and unsupervised neuroinflammatory profiling efforts are rare for pediatric conditions, as pediatric CSF available for research is scarce, and most studies focus on individual diseases (e.g. brain tumors, meningitis, post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus [PHH]) [9–14]. Furthermore, CSF proteomic studies have been constrained by their reliance on either supervised approaches that target select inflammatory mediators (e.g. IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-12) or mass spectrometry, which preferentially detects the most highly abundant proteins [15–17]. This limitation is particularly noteworthy when attempting to identify novel neuroinflammatory biomarkers, as key drivers of the immune response (e.g. cytokines, chemokines, complement components) may be present at levels too low to be reliably detected by mass spectrometry.

These gaps underscore the unmet need for comprehensive, unbiased, and unsupervised inflammatory profiling that will provide insight in immune system-related CSF changes in pediatric neurologic diseases. We posit that applying this approach simultaneously to distinct neuroinflammatory diseases may identify unique sets of inflammatory molecules that are reflective of the underlying neuroinflammatory pathophysiology in each. Here, leveraging an institutional pediatric CSF biorepository, we performed high-sensitivity, multi-targeted inflammatory proteomic profiling in 3 pediatric neurologic diseases: PHH, N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis (NMDARE), and hydrocephalus associated with malignant pediatric brain tumors (i.e. medulloblastoma, ependymoma, high-grade gliomas). We aim to identify disease-specific differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) that will provide insight into the unique inflammatory signatures in each disease. Through uncovering these inflammatory profiles, we will provide researchers and clinicians with novel biomarkers and pathways to inform future diagnostic/prognostic and therapeutic strategies.

Results

Description of patient populations

The primary analyses of this study were conducted on samples from the following groups: PHH (n = 9, average age = 26.6 wks post-menstrual age [PMA]), NMDARE (n = 5, average age = 9.4 yrs), brain tumors presenting with obstructive hydrocephalus (n = 10, average age = 9.3 yrs), and controls (n = 5, average age = 9.7 yrs). Samples from a “congenital hydrocephalus” group (n = 4, average age = 33.5 wks) were also collected for a secondary analysis with the PHH group, specifically. Clinical characteristics for each of these groups are included in Supplementary Table 1a-e.

Differential CSF protein expression between pediatric CNS disorders

For our primary analyses, one sample from each of the 29 patients comprising PHH, NMDARE, brain tumor, and control groups underwent quantification of 736 possible inflammatory protein candidates: 641 proteins were detected in at least 75% of samples. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) demonstrated clustering patterns between the four groups (Fig. 1a). PHH samples clustered tightly along PC1 (54.2% of variance), relatively separated from all other groups, while brain tumor samples formed a diffuse cluster primarily along PC2 (10.9% of variance); the control and NMDARE samples exhibited slightly overlapping clusters. The sample-to-sample Kendall distance heatmap with hierarchical clustering reinforced these patterns (Fig. 1b), with strong intra-group correlations in the PHH and brain tumor groups, moderate to high correlations in the NMDARE and control groups, and unsupervised dendrogram analysis largely segregating the groups.

We next analyzed differential protein abundances across diagnostic groups to identify disease-specific proteomic signatures. In the analysis of samples from PHH patients, 532 differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) were identified when compared to controls (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Table 2). All of the DAPs were up-regulated with the exception of 3 down-regulated proteins (Neural EGFL Like 2 [NELL2], Agrin [AGRN], and Kallikrein-related peptidase 7 [KLK7]). Conversely, the NMDARE group revealed up-regulation of only 64 DAPs compared to controls (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Table 2), with only one down-regulated protein, Phospholipase A₂ inhibitor and LY6/PLAUR domain-containing protein (PINLYP). Surprisingly, comparison of brain tumor samples to controls (Fig. 1e) identified no statistically significant DAPs.

Compared to the degree of up-regulation of proteins detected in the PHH samples, the analysis of the NMDARE samples identified a more modest degree of fold change increase of proteins compared to controls (Fig. 1c-d), with three proteins demonstrating substantially higher up-regulation than the others (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Table 2): Immunoglobulin lambda constant 2 (IGLC2), Marginal zone B and B1 cell-specific protein (MZB1), and B-cell antigen receptor complex-associated protein beta chain (CD79B). An UpSet plot was generated to visualize the overlap of DAPs between PHH and NMDARE (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Table 2). The majority of DAPs were uniquely associated with PHH, comprising 472 proteins, while 60 proteins were shared between PHH and NMDARE. Only 5 proteins were unique to NMDARE.

Each of the of the diagnoses included in our dataset has previously undergone biased proteomic investigations, with identification of potential CSF biomarkers that were represented in the Olink panel. Our experimental design permitted comparing the relative levels of these proteins between these unique neuroinflammatory diseases (Fig. 2). In evaluating multiple biomarkers previously described in PHH and subarachnoid hemorrhage patients (e.g., TLR4, IL-1α, and IL-4) [15, 16, 18], we found no significant differences between any of the diagnostic groups or controls. Similarly, IL-12B, TNF-α, and IL-17 A which have been shown to have elevated expression in NMDARE patients [19, 20] were either not significantly elevated relative to controls (TNF-α, IL-17 A) or no more elevated in NMDARE than in PHH (IL-12B). In pediatric brain tumor CSF, our data did not find significant increases in APOA-2 and IL-6 compared to controls as seen previously [21, 22], while the PHH group demonstrated the highest levels of APOA-2 among the groups. Furthermore, CSF-1, which when overexpressed has been found to promote high-grade glioma formation [23], demonstrated increased expression in PHH CSF but not in brain tumor or NMDARE CSF. While not a comprehensive list, these analyses support the concept that some biomarkers identified in prior studies may not be among the most highly elevated inflammatory proteins detectable in the CSF or may not be up-regulated in a disease-specific manner. This emphasizes the importance of pursuing an unbiased, large-scale proteomics approach in which disease-specific pathway-level signatures may be identified from a larger breadth of DAPs that is representative of the broader inflammatory context of each disease.

Comparison of top enriched pathways in NMDARE and PHH

In the pathway enrichment analysis using Reactome 2024 Pathway Database, the top pathways in the PHH versus control comparison generally consisted of those involved in coagulation (e.g. “Fibrin clot formation”, “platelet degranulation”, “defects of Contact Activation System and Kallikrein Kinin System”), alternative complement activation, and Interleukin-10 (IL-10) signaling (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table 3). Because the PHH patients (average age = 26.6 weeks post-menstrual age) were generally younger than members of the control group (9.7 yrs), we conducted a secondary analysis between samples from PHH patients and a population of infants that underwent neurosurgical interventions for anatomic causes of hydrocephalus at a much younger age (“congenital hydrocephalus”, average age = 33.6 weeks). The congenital hydrocephalus samples appeared similar to controls in PCA and did not segregate distinctly with hierarchical clustering analysis; only one statistically significant DAP was detected: pentraxin 3 (PTX3) (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 5). The PCA between the PHH and congenital hydrocephalus groups revealed separation of along PC1, which accounted for 68% of the total variance (Supplementary Fig. 2a), but the hierarchical clustering analysis did not appear to segregate the PHH samples from congenital hydrocephalus patients (Supplementary Fig. 2b) as obviously as in the previous multi-group comparison with the older controls (Fig. 1b). Nevertheless, differential expression analysis revealed a similarly broad up-regulation of 499 proteins, with only 3 proteins down-regulated (NELL2, AGRN, and A Disintegrin and Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin Motifs 4 [ADAMTS4]) (Supplementary Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 6 versus Fig. 1c). Additionally, the pathway enrichment analysis of DAPs revealed a similar list of top enriched pathways as when compared to the control condition (Supplementary Fig. 2 d, Supplementary Table 6 versus Fig. 3a). These top pathways are consistent with an inflammatory response driven by intraventricular hemorrhage, with an emphasis on thromboinflammatory mechanisms and complement induction in brain hemorrhage [24, 25], and a potential role for IL-10 signaling in an immune modulating capacity [26, 27].

The top enriched pathways in the NMDARE versus control comparison differed from those in the PHH versus control comparison (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table 3). They shared up-regulation of IL-10 signaling as a top hit, implicating a common molecular signaling mechanism being engaged in both conditions. However, only one pathway was related to coagulation, and it referred to a very specific component of the intrinsic pathway (i.e. Defective F9 activation). Instead, the most prominent pathways represented in NMDARE were IL-18 signaling, which is involved in the innate and adaptive immune responses and implicated in multiple inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [30], and several pathways related to cell apoptosis (e.g. regulation by c-FLIP, dimerization of procaspase-8, caspase activation, FasL CD95L signaling).

Comparison of Protein-Protein network analyses in NMDARE and PHH

We next used proteins from the top 10 identified pathways in either condition to conduct STRING network analysis (version 12.0) and explore functional connectivity among the DAPs [31]. Because the “Immune System” Reactome pathway was very broadly inclusive of the proteins in our panel, and because the analysis of the PHH group produced such a large number of DAPs, we expected that the inclusion of DAPs that were only identified in this pathway without the other 9 would mask more nuanced clustering relationships between the remaining proteins (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 7). Therefore, we opted to proceed only with proteins that were included in the other 9 to pathways (Fig. 3b and e).

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for NMDARE-associated proteins revealed a total of 19 proteins organized into two distinct functional clusters (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Table 3). The primary cluster (7 proteins) was dominated by proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including interleukin-18 (IL18), interleukin-18 receptor 1 (IL18R1), interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL1RN), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10), C-C motif chemokine ligands 3 and 4 (CCL3, CCL4), and killer cell lectin-like receptor subfamily B member 1 (KLRB1). The exhibited interconnectivity is suggestive of a coordinated inflammatory response. A second, smaller cluster (2 proteins) consisted of Fas ligand (FASLG) and tumor necrosis factor ligand superfamily member 10 (TNFSF10), indicating involvement of death receptor signaling pathways. Several additional proteins, including Cluster of Differentiation 22 (CD22), Interleukin-12 subunit beta (IL12B), and Sialic acid-binding Ig-like lectin 1 (SIGLEC1), did not exhibit significant interactions and remained unclustered in the network. GO Biological Process enrichment analysis of differentially expressed proteins of the top cluster in the NMDARE network analysis was consistent with pathways involved in adaptive immunity: lymphocyte chemotaxis, IL-18 mediated signaling pathway, and regulation of T-helper 1 cell cytokine production (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Table 3).

In contrast, the PHH network of 64 proteins revealed a higher degree of network density (average node degree 8.84; average local clustering coefficient 0.63) compared to the network for NMDARE (average node degree 1.16; average local clustering coefficient 0.372) (Fig. 3e versus 3b, Supplementary Table 3). The first cluster (36 proteins) demonstrated a tightly interconnected network of coagulation-related proteins, including fibrinectin (FN1), fibrinogen alpha chain (FGA), and multiple coagulation factors (F2, F10, F11, F12), complement cascade components (C3, C5, complement factor B [CFB], properdin [CFP], clusterin [CLU]), and serine protease inhibitors (SERPINA1, SEPRINC1, SERPINF2) (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Table 3). Gene Ontology Biological Process enrichment analysis of the top cluster for PHH confirmed the significant up-regulation of coagulation-associated pathways (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Table 3). A second prominent cluster (19 proteins) was centered on cytokine and chemokine signaling, with high interconnectivity among key inflammatory mediators (e.g. IL6, TNF, IL10, IL1B, IL18), receptors (e.g. IL10RA, IL10RB, IL1R2, IL1RN), and chemokines (e.g. CXCL8, CXCL10, CXCL1, CCL3, CCL4, CCL20, and CCL22). Platelet-derived factors platelet factor 4 (PF4) and pro-platelet basic protein (PPBP) appeared at the periphery of the cluster but remained connected to the central inflammatory network.

We used ARCHS4 co-expression analysis to identify immune-related kinase signaling patterns underlying the DAPs in the NMDARE and PHH conditions. In NMDARE, Proviral Integration Site for Moloney murine leukemia virus 2 (PIM2) - a cytokine-responsive kinase that promotes lymphocyte survival [32], exhibited the highest kinase co-expression signal (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, PHH was dominated by Receptor-Interacting Serine/Threonine-Protein Kinase 2 (RIPK2), a core regulator of innate immune responses via NOD-like receptor signaling [33] (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Table 3).

Neuroinflammation in PHH persists for weeks

We next conducted an analysis of time-dependent changes among PHH patients who underwent serial CSF sampling. For this analysis, 4 PHH patients from the cohort underwent a second CSF sampling with at least 4 weeks of separation (range: 4–26 weeks) from their first CSF collection (i.e. “PHH First”); this second sampling was the last sample attained prior to implantation of a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt (i.e. “PHH Last”). Similar to the PHH comparisons included in our primary analysis, which was derived only from PHH First samples (Fig. 1a-c), the PHH Last samples demonstrated visible separation from the controls in PCA and hierarchal clustering (Supplementary Fig. 4a-b). Compared to controls, the PHH Last samples also demonstrated a broad up-regulation of 428 inflammatory proteins (Supplementary Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 8) and enriched pathways (Supplementary Fig. 4 d, Supplementary Table 8) similar to the comparisons seen in our primary analyses of the PHH First samples (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table 3). Interestingly, there were no statistically significant differences in any of the detected protein abundances between the two timepoints after false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 4). STRING analysis using proteins from the top 10 Reactome pathways (excluding “Immune System”) enriched in the PHH Last vs. control dataset identified 65 proteins. Of the proteins differentially expressed in PHH First versus controls and PHH Last versus controls, 53 were shared between the two timepoints, while 11 were unique to the first samples and another 12 were exclusive to the last samples (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table 4). The unique proteins in the PHH First samples are characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines Interleukin-6 (IL6), interleukin-1 beta (IL1B), C-C motif chemokine ligand 20 (CCL20); platelet activation markers platelet factor 4 (PF4) and glycoprotein V platelet (GP5); and acute-phase proteins orosomucoid 1 (ORM1) and apolipoprotein A-I (APOA1) (Supplementary Table 4). Proteins unique to the PHH Last samples included TNF receptor superfamily members, including tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 11 A (TNFRSF11A), TNF receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2), and CD40 molecule (CD40), as well as complement components complement component 7 (C7) and complement component 8 beta chain (C8B) (Supplementary Table 4).

Of note, among the consistently downregulated proteins in the PHH First (Fig. 1c) and PHH Last (Supplementary Fig. 4) samples compared to controls, neural epidermal growth factor-like 2 (NELL2) emerged as a top candidate, being significantly decreased in PHH versus all other cohorts (Fig. 4c).

Discussion

In this study, we employed a large-scale, multi-targeted platform to perform unsupervised pathway-level CSF proteomic profiling in pediatric patients with three distinct CNS diseases in which neuroinflammation plays a key role: PHH, NMDARE, and brain tumors with obstructive hydrocephalus. This approach provides a wide context of the neuroinflammatory landscape in each disease from which to identify salient pathways. For instance, many CSF proteins previously suggested in the literature as disease biomarkers were not among the most highly elevated proteins in the CSF or lacked disease specificity. While this does not necessarily preclude the use of these candidates as CSF biomarkers of general inflammatory burden, we posit that candidates isolated from a larger scale approach may more easily identify disease-specific proteins, which would enhance our understanding of the underlying disease pathophysiology as we try to develop biomarkers and therapeutic strategies.

Before interpreting the data from our head-to-head comparisons, we must recognize the major limitation in our study in that it relied on a convenience sampling from a pediatric CSF biorepository. This presented many challenges, chief among which were small sample sizes and the inability to pre-specify certain aspects of sample collection. For instance, the control group was not free from comorbidities (e.g., Turner syndrome, asthma, autism) and may have had conditions with some degree of neuroinflammatory response (e.g. encephalopathy, concussion) that up-regulates CNS inflammation [34–37]. If the control group had a baseline increase in inflammatory protein expression versus truly healthy controls, then comparisons with other groups could either potentially obscure the identification of some shared proteins (i.e. by being elevated in both the diagnostic group and the control group) or even make it seem as if some proteins were decreased in our diagnostic group (i.e. being elevated in the controls, but not elevated in the diagnostic group). As it is unlikely that CSF could ever be obtained from a truly healthy pediatric population not undergoing an evaluation for an illness (e.g. meningitis, status epilepticus), we opted to maintain consistent use of the same control cohort for each comparison (i.e. PHH vs. control, NMDARE vs. control, brain tumor vs. control), positing that the up-regulated DAPs for each diagnosis could at least be qualitatively comparable in that they would each represent a difference from whatever levels may have been expressed in the control cohort.

In PHH, there appeared to be a broad increase in the detection of inflammatory proteins, with approximately 83% of all detected proteins meeting statistical significance. This included nearly all the proteins noted to be up-regulated in NMDARE. This may represent wide-spread immune activation by the disease pathology, either within the CNS in response to extravasated blood products or potentially a “leakage” from the periphery into the CNS during the hemorrhage. However, we must also consider other potential contributors. First, it is possible that a difference in sample collection method (e.g. lateral ventricle sampling from an implanted VAD for PHH versus lumbar puncture for controls) could introduce variability. For instance, the presence of a foreign body (i.e. VAD) may induce an additional pro-inflammatory influence in the PHH group. Conversely, CSF samples collected from an indwelling neurosurgical device with frequent drainage may yield less overall protein detection than concurrent LP samples [38, 39], with an unknown effect on the detection of the specific inflammatory proteins in our panels. It is also important to note that our PHH cohort was comprised of preterm neonates and not age-matched to the school-aged children in our control cohort, therefore age-differences may account for some proteomic differences [40]. However, the comparison between the PHH cohort and a younger cohort undergoing neurosurgery for congenital hydrocephalus (with direct central CSF sampling) also supported the possibility of a broad inflammatory protein up-regulation similar to our initial comparison to controls (with LP sampling). Furthermore, the direct comparison between the congenital hydrocephalus population and our control population differed only in a single protein. While an age-dependent or foreign body induced expression of these pathways can only truly be ruled out with common sampling methods at a similar age (e.g. LP samples both from PHH and non-PHH preterm neonates), the top enriched pathways were at least similar in the two comparisons: PHH patients appear to demonstrate a prominent up-regulation of the alternative complement activation and coagulation/platelet pathways. Enrichment of these pathways is consistent with DAMP-driven innate immune activation, wherein tissue injury promotes activation of complement, coagulation, and platelet pathways to amplify sterile inflammation [41–43]. The data were also suggestive of a high degree of interconnectedness between coagulation factors, complement components, and cytokine signaling molecules, with central nodes such as fibronectin (FN1), complement C3 (C3), and plasminogen (PLG) serving as major hubs, fitting with hemorrhage as causal factor. A broad thrombo-inflammatory response involving both innate immune and vascular pathways in PHH would be consistent with animal models of acute hemorrhagic brain injuries, where the clotting factor fibrinogen and the complement pathway have both been implicated as inciting neuroinflammation [44, 45]. It also may indirectly support an anti-inflammatory effect for the ongoing trials of anti-fibrinolytic therapies in patients with PHH [46, 47].

Surprisingly, the longitudinal analysis of PHH CSF did not reveal dramatic changes in inflammatory protein abundance, as there were no significant differences when comparing the two groups head-to-head. At most, one could suggest that there is a non-significant trend towards a decreasing global inflammatory protein expression, as the majority of proteins appear to be decreasing; conversely, one may could also interpret these data as indicative of persistent inflammatory signaling even at the time of transitioning to internal CSF diversion (VP shunt implantation). Of note, many of the proteins and pathways in the later PHH samples that appear up-regulated relative to the control group were the same as those in the earlier PHH samples relative to the control group. In comparing the proteins comprising the top up-regulated pathways, most were conserved between the first and last samples. The proteins that differed between the two timepoints could indicate some progression from the early expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL6, IL1B, CCL20), platelet activation markers (PF4, GP5), and acute-phase proteins (ORM1, APOA1) towards adaptive immune engagement with up-regulation TNF receptor superfamily members (e.g., TNFRSF11A, TRAF2, CD40) [48], but further investigations are needed (e.g. 1 year post-hemorrhage), as such a progression would have potential therapeutic implications. Because PHH patients are unlikely to undergo additional CSF sampling that long after VP shunt implantation in the absence of an infectious concern or shunt malfunction, we anticipate that further studies using animal models will be best to directly interrogate the chronic inflammation induced by PHH.

By contrast, NMDARE exhibited preferential enrichment of interleukin signaling (IL-10, IL-18) and lymphocyte-associated immunoregulatory pathways. The more prominent activation of these immune processes supports a model of sustained, selective adaptive immune activation. The NMDARE network was also consistent with a more modular configuration, with fewer interconnections between the included proteins. This could be due to a difference in the number of DAPs being identified in the PHH and NMDARE comparisons, which could bias the networks towards greater and lesser density, respectively. Nevertheless, the fragmented network architecture in the NMDARE analysis suggests a more compartmentalized adaptive immune response that appears consistent with historical reports from other clinical studies. For instance, IL-18 signaling has been implicated in the pathogenesis and severity of other autoimmune diseases [30, 49–53] and aligns with reports that IL-18 levels in the CSF of children with NMDARE correlate with disease severity [54]. Additionally, the most highly upregulated proteins in NMDARE were IGLC2, MZB1, and CD79B, all of which are closely associated with B cell activation and antibody production [55–57]. These findings align with the established role of autoantibody-driven pathophysiology in NMDARE, where B cells and plasma cells contribute directly to neuronal dysfunction [58], and B cell depletion through rituximab is a treatment strategy [59]. Furthermore, two of these proteins, MZB1 and CD79B, were also recently identified in a CSF biomarker screen as being highly predictive of diagnosing multiple sclerosis in adults [8]. Therefore, we posit that these biomarkers of B cell activation have a broader generalizability for autoimmune CNS disorders beyond NMDARE, which can be useful when trying to diagnose CNS diseases suspected of having an autoimmune component but lack an identified autoantibody [60]. Quantitatively measuring these proteins (e.g. ELISA) during an initial evaluation for autoimmune encephalitis may help to narrow the initial differential diagnosis, while trending them over time may provide an indication of treatment response. Finally, we also noted pathways involved in the regulation of apoptosis were very prominently elevated in NMDARE. This is surprising because the binding of antibodies to NMDA receptors has most often been described to lead to receptor internalization rather than eliciting neuronal apoptosis [61]. While it is therefore possible that the apoptosis signals are more related to the turnover of B-cells [62], further studies on the source of cell programmed death are required to confirm this.

Differences between PHH and NMDARE CSF were also seen in the kinase co-expression analysis. The PIM2 signaling seen in NMDARE suggested engagement of adaptive immune regulatory pathways, as PIM-2 has been shown to decrease the suppressive function of regulatory T-cells through phosphorylation of Foxp3 [63]. Conversely, RIPK2 signaling predominated in PHH, consistent with innate immune activation likely triggered by danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) following hemorrhagic injury [64]. Interestingly, both the PIM2 and RIPK2 pathways are targetable, with pharmacologic kinase inhibitors currently being investigated in trials for other diseases [32, 33].

We also noted that there were very few proteins significantly downregulated in our disease conditions. From these, the neuron-specific protein NELL2 stood out as being suppressed over multiple PHH comparisons. Because NELL2 has most been implicated in axon development and neuronal survival [65, 66], we would have expected a higher level of expression in PHH cohort by virtue of their younger age. Instead, a down-regulation may be indicative of impaired neurorestorative capacity in the ventricular environment of PHH patients. Further investigation is warranted to determine whether NELL2 in CSF could serve as a biomarker of PHH severity or if restoring NELL2 expression as a therapeutic target could promote neuroprotection or recovery in this setting.

Finally, we were surprised that the brain tumor patients lacked any statistically significant changes in inflammatory protein expression relative to the control group. We would have expected that because all these patients presented with obstructive hydrocephalus necessitating EVD placement, a consistent up-regulation in inflammatory proteins would have been detected as a sequelae of intracranial hypertension. However, comparisons of the controls with the congenital hydrocephalus and brain tumor groups both revealed minimal-to-no differences. Separately, we expected the CSF in the brain tumor group to reflect some of the immune cell infiltration and signaling that are characteristic of the tumor microenvironment [67, 68]. While expanding the cohort could potentially increase the number of DAPs to reach statistical significance, it is more likely that interpatient variability in tumor type and location contributed to the lack of consistent inflammatory signatures observed within the brain tumor group. The cohort included ten patients with a heterogeneous distribution of tumor histologies, including pilocytic astrocytoma (n = 4), low-grade glioma (n = 2), ependymoma (n = 2), diffuse midline glioma (n = 1), and anaplastic ependymoma (n = 1). Tumor locations were similarly variable, involving the third and fourth ventricles, hypothalamic/chiasmatic region, suprasellar space, and posterior fossa. Such anatomical diversity may influence CSF composition through differences in tumor microenvironment, CSF flow obstruction patterns, and distance from the EVD catheter tip, thereby contributing to within-group variability that could obscure statistically significant differences. None of the tumors were described to have hemorrhagic features, so we are not surprised to see an absence of coagulation-related pathways as in the PHH patients. Finally, the collected CSF samples from this group were via EVD that drained continuously, so obtaining samples hours to days after the initial EVD placement could have been influenced by the dilution of CSF intermediates over time. Overall, the data from these patients indicate that inflammatory CSF proteomic evaluation in these patients may benefit from enhancing uniformity of CSF collection among a more homogenous set of patients, which would be better suited for a dedicated, multi-center study.

Conclusions

This study presents a systems-level analysis of the CSF inflammatory proteome in pediatric neurologic diseases. Through multi-targeted, unsupervised proteomic profiling, PHH revealed an immune signature characterized by early activation of innate immunity, alternative complement and coagulation pathways that appeared to persist over time. In contrast, the NMDARE profile exhibited a more modular, lymphocyte-associated immune architecture, while brain tumor-associated hydrocephalus demonstrated limited differential protein expression. Our data shed light on disease-relevant kinases for PHH (RIPK2) and NMDARE (PIM2). Finally, we also provide support for the identification of disease-specific biomarkers, including IGLC2, MZB1, and CD79B for NMDARE, and NELL2 for PHH that may be of clinical utility in the future.

Methods

Patient population and sample collection

CSF samples were obtained from the centralized CSF biorepository at Children’s National Hospital. Enrollment in this study did not require any additional invasive procedures for research purposes; all CSF samples were remnant from routine clinical care. All samples were obtained with written informed consent from a parent or legal guardian, in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) under two protocols: Pro00011582 and Pro00004039. Samples collected from June 2014 to September 2024. After collection, CSF samples underwent centrifugation at 400xG for 10 min; the cell pellet was discarded and the supernatant was stored at − 80 °C in polypropylene cryogenic vials until proceeding with proteomic analysis.

The post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus (PHH) group was comprised of all biorepository patients diagnosed with PHH undergoing CSF diversion via ventricular access device (VAD) prior to eventual need for a VP shunt. Remnant CSF samples were collected from the periodic drainages during the course of routine clinical care. The brain tumor group consisted of all patients with brain tumors presenting to the hospital with hydrocephalus due to CSF outflow obstruction, requiring CSF diversion via external ventricular drain (EVD) and implantation of a VP shunt. Three brain tumor patients were excluded because they either lacked radiographic evidence of hydrocephalus or the CSF sample was taken greater than 1 week after initial CSF diversion. Remnant CSF samples were collected from EVD drip chamber reservoirs that had accumulated within an hour of collection. As some patients in our PHH and brain tumor groups had serial samples available in the biorepository, only the first sample (i.e. the earliest in the hospital course) was used in our primary analysis. The use of serial samples for a secondary analysis of longitudinal changes in inflammatory proteomics in PHH patients is specifically detailed in the Results.

Remnant CSF was also collected from patients undergoing a lumbar puncture evaluation for neuropsychiatric symptoms. Those in which CSF autoimmune antibodies were confirmed were diagnosed with NMDA Receptor Encephalitis [69] while those that did not and lacked a defining neurologic diagnosis (n = 5, average age = 9.7 yrs) served as a source of “control” CSF. CSF samples from young infants who underwent neurosurgical intervention for anatomic hydrocephalus were also collected at the time of the definitive neurosurgical procedure and assigned to the “congenital hydrocephalus” group.

Proteomics profiling

All CSF samples were profiled together using the Olink Explore 384 Inflammation I and Inflammation II panels (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden), which together can quantify up to 736 inflammation-related proteins via Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) technology and next generation sequencing [70]. Protein concentrations are reported as normalized protein expression (NPX) values on a log2 scale, where higher NPX values indicate higher relative abundance.

Quality control and preprocessing

Quality Control (QC) was examined using both internal controls (incubation, extension, and amplification) and external controls (negative and plate controls), as recommended by the manufacturer [71]. QC filtering was applied at both the sample and protein levels. QC evaluation criteria included (i) deviation of control NPX values within ± 0.3 from the median, (ii) a minimum matched count threshold of 500 reads per sample, and (iii) deviation of negative control medians within ± 5 standard deviations of the predefined value. All 88 CSF samples passed Olink quality control, with no samples flagged or removed and 100% of data points meeting QC thresholds. As in a previous study using Olink panels [8], proteins with >75% of NPX values below the assay’s limit of detection (LOD), or with NPX values < −10, were removed. Residual missing NPX values (< 1% of total data) were imputed using column-wise mean substitution prior to principal component analysis to ensure complete data matrices. These preprocessing steps ensured that downstream analyses reflected biologically meaningful variation rather than technical variation.

Principal component analysis

To characterize global variance and assess sample-level clustering, PCA was performed using the prcomp function in R [72] (version 4.2.0) on centered and scaled NPX values. Variance explained by each principal component was computed from eigenvalues and expressed as a percentage. PCA plots were visualized using the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.0), with samples labeled and grouped by diagnostic category using unique identifiers.

Hierarchical clustering and heatmap generation

Sample–sample correlation analysis was performed using Kendall’s tau distance and hierarchical clustering was conducted using the complete linkage method, implemented via the hclust function in R (version 4.2.0). A heatmap was generated using the ComplexHeatmap (version 2.18.0) [73], with both row and column dendrograms reflecting the clustering structure based on Kendall’s distance.

Differential protein abundance analysis

Differential abundance analysis was conducted using the limma package (version 3.52.4) in R, designed for linear modeling of high-dimensional expression data [74]. NPX values were modeled as continuous outcomes across predefined diagnostic groups, adjusting for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Proteins were considered significantly differentially abundant if they met both criteria: FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| >1. Pairwise comparisons were performed for PHH vs. control, NMDARE vs. control, brain tumor vs. control groups, congenital hydrocephalus vs. control, PHH First vs. congenital hydrocephalus, and PHH Last vs. congenital hydrocephalus. The model matrix was constructed using diagnostic group as the primary covariate, and empirical Bayes moderation was applied to improve variance estimation. Volcano plots were generated for each comparison using EnhancedVolcano (version 1.14.0) to visualize effect sizes and significance thresholds [75].

Pathway enrichment analysis

Prior to network analysis, functional enrichment of differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) was performed using Reactome 2024 pathways via the Enrichr platform (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr) [28, 29, 76]. For each group comparison, significant DAPs (FDR < 0.05, |log₂FC| >1) were submitted to Enrichr, and pathway enrichment was assessed using the Fisher exact test. Pathways were ranked by Enrichr combined score (a proprietary score combining statistical significance and Z-score).

Following k-means clustering of STRING-derived PPI networks (below), Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process enrichment analysis was performed independently for the top PHH and NMDARE network clusters. GO enrichment was conducted within STRING using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (FDR < 0.05), and significantly enriched biological processes were analyzed to delineate cluster-specific functional profiles. Additionally, to identify kinases co-regulated with the DAPs, we queried the ARCHS4 Human Kinase Co-expression database through Enrichr using the same list of significant DAPs (FDR < 0.05, |log₂FC| >1). Enrichment significance was evaluated using Enrichr’s combined score metric, and top-ranking kinase associations were visualized for comparative interpretation of kinase-related regulatory patterns.

Protein–protein interaction network analysis

Proteins within top 10 most significantly enriched Reactome 2024 pathways were selected for interpretation and downstream PPI network analysis. Of note, we ran this analysis both with and without inclusion of the proteins that were solely included in the “Immune System” Reactome pathway. A priori, we were concerned that because our Olink panel was designed to detect inflammatory proteins, that the “Immune System” pathway would be broadly inclusive of a large proportion of the proteins in our panel, which would result in more general protein-protein interactions that would mask more nuanced clustering relationships between the proteins included in the 9 other pathways. Nevertheless, both analyses are included for transparency.

PPI networks were constructed using the STRING database (version 12.0) (https://string-db.org) for each diagnostic group’s DAPs [31]. To remain conservative, only high-confidence interactions (minimum interaction score >0.7) were considered. Network topology metrics, such as node degree and clustering coefficient, were used to identify key hub proteins and functional modules relevant to neuroinflammatory processes. One shell of interactors was allowed to enhance network connectivity and reduce isolated nodes, while preserving biological relevance. K-means clustering was applied to the STRING-generated networks to partition nodes into distinct functional modules based on centroid similarity. The number of clusters (k = 4 for PHH and k = 2 for NMDARE) was determined empirically from network topology to identify functionally distinct protein modules.

Comparisons of individual biomarkers

Normalized protein expression (NPX) values for selected inflammatory proteins chosen based on prior reports of elevated expression in neuroinflammatory conditions [15, 16, 18–23, 29] were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4, Dotmatics). Group comparisons across control, brain tumor, PHH, and NMDARE cohorts were performed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test to identify statistically significant pairwise differences.
