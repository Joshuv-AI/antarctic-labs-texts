Comparative Evaluation of the Effect of Different Storage Media on the Hardness and Fracture Resistance of Reattached Tooth Fragments: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Mahesh V Dadpe, Nikita A Chordiya, Yogesh J Kale, Prasanna T Dahake, Shrikant B Kendre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo assess hardness as well as resistance to fracture in the case of a reattached tooth fragment when different storage media are used.Materials and methodsIn this study, 60 permanent incisor teeth that were extracted for therapeutic reasons were enrolled and divided into four groups. Each group had 15 specimens (n = 15). The teeth of all the groups were sectioned, and the incisal third of the fragments were stored in respective storage media: group I (dry), group II [Hanks' balanced salt solution (HBSS)], group III (coconut water), and group IV (contact lens solution) for 24 hours. The fragments were reattached with a simple reattachment technique using a flowable composite. These teeth were then subjected to a universal testing machine for measuring the fracture resistance of the specimen. Then the obtained pieces were used to measure the hardness of the reattached fragment using a Vickers hardness tester. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and an unpaired t-test were used to check statistical significance.ResultsIn group II (HBSS), the mean value of resistance to fracture was maximum, that is, 16.3787 N, which was followed by the coconut water group, that is, group III, with a value of 15.9467 N. Group IV (contact lens solution) had a mean value of 13.1267 N. Resistance to the occurrence of fracture was seen to be very less in the dry environment group, that is, group I, with a mean of 11.7067 N. The maximum hardness was seen with group II (HBSS) at 350.2000 Vickers hardness (HV), followed by group IV (contact lens solution) at 342.5333 HV, and group III (coconut water) with a mean of 342.3333 HV. The least hardness was seen with group I (dry environment) at 295.4667 HV. There was a significant difference in fracture resistance and hardness between these groups.How to cite this articleDadpe MV, Chordiya NA, Kale YJ, et al. Introduction

Dental injury of the anterior tooth is considered the most common issue among children and adolescents as it affects the esthetics of a person.1 The coronal type of permanent incisor fracture affects about 18–22% of total dental hard tissue injuries.2 Despite the availability of advanced techniques concerned with adhesive materials as well as restorative techniques, an ideal restorative material mimicking the esthetic and functional characteristics of natural dental structures is lacking.3 Following this, tooth fragment reattachments (provided the fragment is available) have emerged that can help dentists take the help of a natural tooth fragment for restoring the fractured tooth.4 Fragment/piece reattachment is an easy, low-price method that does not consist of any special preparation and provides better esthetics and resistance, which may further improve function.5,6 Using an autogenous or homogenous fragment/piece is defined as “biological restoration,” which is the most commonly recommended technique as well.7

Sometimes fragments/pieces are not visible or damaged. In this case, a tooth fragment or parts are reattached from a removed tooth, but it must come from a “bank of teeth” or charitable organizations that preserve and provide teeth for scientific, clinical, and educational purposes.8 It is an inexpensive technique maintaining excellent biocompatibility and tooth structure characteristics.9

The way the fragment is stored after the damage is one of the elements that significantly affects the success of fragment/piece reattachment.10 The amount of time that passes between the trauma, restoration, and fragment storage determines how well the fragment/piece reattachment process works, since the broken portion may get less wet with each minute that goes by.11 The best storage media should be antibacterial, have an osmolality similar to body fluids, not cause antigen–antibody responses, have a long shelf life, and work well in various climates.12

Materials and Methods

Sixty permanent anterior teeth extracted for therapeutic purposes were included in the study. The selected teeth were crack-free, caries-free, and without any structural defects. As part of the infection control procedure, the collected teeth were autoclaved for about 40 minutes at 121°C prior to the start of the investigation.13 Using hand scalers, the tissue remnants on the teeth's root surface were eliminated. After that, until the experiment, the teeth were kept in distilled water. Four groups of storage media were randomly assigned to the chosen teeth. There were 15 samples in each group (n = 15).

The study included the following steps:

Intentional fracture of teeth specimen: The labial side teeth were measured from the cervical to the incisal edge using a Vernier caliper and an electronic digital caliper. To detect Ellis class II and III fractures, the tooth was marked one-third from the incisal edge after the measured values were divided by three. The teeth were cut on the specified line perpendicular to their long axis using a low-speed diamond disk and saline as a coolant. At this stage, the teeth that fractured the recovered piece or displayed an unclear fracture pattern were discarded.

Keeping broken pieces in the proper storage medium: Following the fracture, the pieces were kept for 24 hours in distinct, labeled containers in groups I (dry storage), II (HBSS solution), III (coconut water), and IV (contact lens solution), each using the appropriate storage medium. Distilled water was used to preserve the remaining tooth structure until it was reattached.

Reattachment of fractured specimen using flowable composite resin: After 24 hours, the pieces were reattached using a straightforward reattachment process that required no further preparation. To get rid of any last traces of water, the tooth fragment and fracture were treated with 37% phosphoric acid for 15 seconds, rinsed for 10 seconds, and then left to air-dried for 5 seconds. Two coats of the bonding compound (Te-Econom Bond, Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein) were then applied using applicator tips (Ivoclar Vivadent Applicator, Liechtenstein). In order to facilitate solvent evaporation, the surfaces were then dried once more for 5 seconds using an air syringe. The bonding chemical was cured for 20 seconds on the shattered piece and 20 seconds on the tooth remnant using a light-cure unit (Waldent, MaxCure3 LED Curing light) set to 480 nm. The cracked surfaces of the fragment and tooth remnant were covered with the flowable composite (Te-Econom Flow, Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein). After that, the piece was placed back next to the tooth remnant. The broken piece was carried on a gutta-percha stick. Following placement, the labial and lingual halves underwent 40 seconds of light curing, respectively. Following that, the reattached specimens were once more kept in distilled water.

The repaired specimen's fracture was tested using a universal testing machine: Each specimen was then placed on a 20 × 20 × 25 mm self-cure acrylic resin (DPI RR) block, which took around 30 minutes to set. The roots of the specimens were submerged in resin up to the cingulum in order to align the incisal edge with the horizontal line and the specimen's line axis with the line axis of the resin block. The resin blocks and the rest of the tooth structure were once more kept in distilled water. The universal testing machine (UNITEST 10, India) was then used to test each specimen for fracture resistance. The specimens were put into the fixture or modified jig of the universal testing machine. After that, the rod of the universal testing machine was held perpendicular to the long axis of the tooth specimen, which is exactly at 90° to the incisal third on the material surface. To evaluate the impact, the load was applied at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. The value at which the reattached fragment debonded when the stress was gradually increased was recorded using newtons (N). The fracture resistance of the reattached tooth piece was reflected by this load. Every tooth specimen's fracture resistance was noted in a comparable manner.

Measure hardness using a Vickers hardness tester on the fragment obtained after testing fracture resistance: The hardness of the specimen fragment was assessed using the incisal third broken fragment that was obtained from the tooth specimen. Each receiving piece was turned into an enamel slab, which was then implanted in a 2 × 1 cm block of acrylic resin, exposing just the coronal edge of the tooth specimen. To ensure that the exposed tooth specimens' surfaces were free from deformation and damage, the slabs were serially polished using silicon carbide sheets of sizes 300, 600, and 1200 grits and alumina slurry of sizes 0.2 and 0.05 µm. Using a Vickers hardness tester (Reichert, Austria), all groups were assessed for microhardness changes in order to assess surface hardness variations that were ultimately brought on by storage media. A pyramidal diamond indenter was used to test the surface microhardness of each specimen for 5 seconds while 100 gm/f stresses were applied. A diamond indenter with a square base and a 136° angle was employed. Each specimen had indentations made at two distinct sites, and measurements were computed. Two measurements were averaged to provide a single reading.

Results

To determine whether the mean difference between groups was significant, the unpaired t-test and one-way ANOVA were used to compare the mean and standard deviation (SD) between the two groups; a difference is considered significant if p < 0.05 and not significant if p > 0.05. Each variable's descriptive statistics were displayed using the mean, SD, and standard error of the mean.

The average fracture resistance of groups I (dry environment), II (HBSS), III (coconut water), and IV (contact lens solution) is shown in Table 1 and Figure 1. The amount of force needed to shatter the reattached tooth fragment varied statistically significantly (p = 0.000) according to the findings of a one-way ANOVA test used to examine the intergroup variation of fracture resistance. The maximum fracture resistance was seen with group II, with a mean of 16.3787 N, followed by group III with a mean of 15.9467 N, and group IV with a mean of 13.1267 N. The least fracture load was required to fracture the samples stored in group I, with a mean of 11.7067 N.

There was a statistically insignificant difference between the means of group II (HBSS) and group III (coconut water) and group II (HBSS) and group IV (contact lens solution), with p = 0.849 and p = 0.144, respectively. Intergroup comparison between group III (coconut water) and group IV (contact lens solution) revealed a statistically significant difference with p = 0.031. Accordingly, the findings indicate that group II (HBSS) had the highest fracture resistance, followed by group III (coconut water) and group IV (contact lens solution), while group I (dry environment) had the lowest fracture resistance.

Discussion

Of all facial injuries, dental injuries are the most common, with crown fractures and luxations occurring most frequently.14 Teenagers and children are more susceptible to these injuries.15,16 Approximately 18–22% of all dental hard tissue trauma is caused by coronal fractures of the permanent incisors, with maxillary incisors accounting for 96% of these cases (80% of central incisors and 16% of lateral incisors).2 Therefore, in order to conduct the study's experiment, permanent maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth were included.17

There are several ways to repair damaged teeth, including prosthodontic replacement with resin, steel, or ceramic crowns; pin-retained restorations; porcelain veneers; orthodontic bands; and composite resins.18 Each method has varying degrees of success, as do resin composite restorations.19 Since they necessitate the wear-off of healthy tooth structure, these methods are not conservative.20 Other technological challenges include translucence, color tone matching, and teeth sculpting. It takes a lot of time as well.21 Composite restorations' primary disadvantages are uneven wear and color mismatch, limited abrasion resistance in comparison to tooth enamel, minor discoloration and staining, and a lack of marginal integrity.22

When an unbroken fractured fragment is available, the fragment reattachment surgery offers a conservative therapeutic alternative.23 It may be regarded as a physiologically feasible alternative that not only satisfies present standards but is also less invasive.24 Tennery was the first to reveal that fractured fragments may be reattached using the acid etch process.21 Because fragment reattachment preserves the enamel's original form, color, brightness, and surface texture, it has a major esthetic advantage over conventional techniques.25 Furthermore, the patient has a favorable emotional and social response to the operation.26

The existence of supragingival borders is a prerequisite for performing fragment reattachment.27 The way the fragment is stored in the storage media prior to being reattached is a crucial component of its success.28 Storage medium is essential because hydration maintains vitality, visual appeal, and bond strength.10

Storage Media and Reattached Tooth Fragment Fracture Resistance

A successful reattachment method has been found to benefit from keeping the broken portion in a moist or damp environment prior to reattaching.22 The shattered fragment may get dry throughout the extended period between the trauma and the reattachment surgery.29 Some authors claim that when the surfaces are hydrated with storage fluid, reattachment restores around 50% of the native tooth's fracture strength, and no additional preparation is required.30 Therefore, no further preparation was needed in this investigation to evaluate the impact of storage media.31

Lee et al.31 claim that when employed as a medium for storage, remaining chlorine from sodium hypochlorite and regular saline might adversely affect the bond strengths. Because distilled water has a neutral pH and is free of impurities, it works as the universal storage medium.32 Farik et al.20 in their research, found that drying a fragment piece for greater than an hour considerably reduces its resistance to breaking, highlighting the need of maintaining the fragment's moisture content. The International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT) recommends HBSS, which is a widely standardized solution, as an avulsed tooth storage medium.33 As per the Thomas et al.34 study, HBSS is a better storage medium at 15 minutes and similar to coconut water for 15–120 minutes. This is in accordance with the present study, where statistically comparable results were found on pairwise comparison; group II (HBSS) showed maximum fracture resistance (16.3787 N), followed by group III (coconut water) with 15.9467 N.35 Coconut water is a good storage medium because of its high osmolarity, affordability, and ease of supply.36 Only around 25% of the total moisture content of intact, sound dentin is retained after being kept in a dry environment for a whole day.37 Due to the partial loss of dentin moisture and its shrinkage, it seems that the composite surface contact with dentin is reduced.30 This explains why, according to our analysis, fragments kept in dry environments have the lowest binding strength, confirming the findings of Shirani et al.30 and Farik et al.20

In support of Thomas et al.,34 Silva et al.,35 and Thakare et al.,37 it was hypothesized that a higher water content in coconut water allowed for improved dentin wetting, reducing collagen fiber collapse and aiding in the creation of resin tags, as demonstrated in the current work. In a similar vein, Gopikrishna et al.36 discovered that in simulated avulsed teeth, coconut water maintained periodontal ligament (PDL) cells far more alive than HBSS. Given the typical situation, there is a higher chance that coconut water will be easily accessible at the trauma site, making it a practical and accessible substitute. Human dentin has shown signs of brittleness when dehydrated.37 Because coconut water has a greater osmolality than milk and hydrating surfaces recovers around 50% of the original tooth's fracture strength, Shilpa et al.38 also found that coconut water was more effective than milk and regular saline. The findings support the current study's recommendation that coconut water be used as an approachable storage medium. Since HBSS's osmolality, optimal pH of 7.2, and high water content allow for improved rewetting of dentin tubules, Kumar et al.'s39 investigation revealed that HBSS and coconut water had the highest mean. This finding supports the current study. The saline solution with cationic monoesters as a preservative that adversely affects the binding strength had the second-lowest fracture resistance values because it had the lowest osmolality when compared to other media.37

Storage Media and Hardness of Reattached Tooth Fragment

The microhardness of a material is determined by applying a load between 10 mN and 10 N. Materials having an indentation of microns or even nanometers and a load of less than 700 mN are said to have nanohardness. Because the orientation of the crystal array inside the enamel changes, the hardness of the identical enamel rod decreases from head to tail.

Conclusion

The conclusion drawn from the results of the present study was that coconut water showed superior results to the contact lens solution but was slightly inferior to HBSS in terms of the effectiveness of the medium used for storage when resistance to fracture is concerned. Whereas when the effect of the medium used for storage on hardness quality was studied, the contact lens solution showed slightly superior results to coconut water but was inferior to HBSS. As the gold standard, HBSS is not economical and not commonly available; it can be concluded that coconut water and contact lens solutions can be considered more economical, readily available, interim viable storage media. There are very few studies conducted to describe the mechanical properties of available dental hard tissues following their storage in various storage media. Further analysis is to be done on the effect of interaction of other storage media with the dentin, other mechanical properties, and the in vivo effect of storage media and storage techniques.
