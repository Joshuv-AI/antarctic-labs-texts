Impact of stress-induced precipitate variant selection on anisotropic electrical properties of piezoceramics
Authors: Changhao Zhao, Andreja Benčan, Matthias Bohnen, Fangping Zhuo, Xiaolong Ma, Goran Dražić, Ralf Müller, Shengtao Li, Jurij Koruza, Jürgen Rödel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Precipitation hardening has been recently validated as a new mechanism for domain wall pinning and mechanical loss reduction in piezoelectrics. While anisometric precipitates have high pinning strengths, there is limited knowledge about the electrical anisotropy of the precipitation-hardened piezoceramics. In the present work, we successfully orient the precipitates in Li0.18Na0.82NbO3 piezoceramics by applying a uniaxial stress during the aging and studied its electrical anisotropy. Predicted by mechanical simulation and verified by transmission electron microscopy, it is demonstrated that the precipitate variant with its long axis perpendicular to the applied stress is energetically favored. The electrical anisotropy of the stress-assisted aged Li0.18Na0.82NbO3 is studied by applying electrical fields parallel or perpendicular to the stress axis. The domain wall contribution to permittivity is found to vary by more than a factor of two depending on orientation. In addition, the domain walls are more difficult to be activated by increasing the temperature when the electric field is perpendicular to the stress axis. Our work highlights the precipitate variant selection induced by stress-assisted aging and the related electrical anisotropy in piezoceramics. This technique enables the precipitate orientation in piezoceramics and the utilization of its anisotropy, providing fundamental insight into precipitate-domain-wall interactions and setting the ground for leveraging precipitation hardening effect in piezoceramics.

Introduction

Piezoelectric hardening stands as a pivotal strategy aimed at mitigating the mechanical loss in piezoelectric materials during their application. The strategy is particularly crucial for piezoelectric devices operating in close proximity to their resonance frequency, e.g., ultrasonic sensors, motors, and imaging devices1,2. Recently, investigations of cutting-edge two-dimensional infrared imaging equipment have been made pioneering work by Hu et al.3, where the pressing challenge of piezoelectric hardening in such necessitates prompt attention and resolution. The principal objective of piezoelectric hardening is to suppress domain wall (DW) motion, which constitutes the primary source of mechanical loss and self-heating in these devices4,5.

For decades, the prevailing method for hardening piezoelectric materials has been chemical doping, which is extensively adopted in both the commercial and academic spheres6–8. However, the hardening efficacy of chemical doping diminishes at elevated vibration velocities or temperatures due to disruptions in the internal bias field established by mobile oxygen vacancies9, which serve to stabilize the domain structure10–12. These drawbacks of traditional hardening limit the use of hard piezoelectrics in high-power applications, where mechanical loss increases significantly with rising vibration velocity. In response to this limitation, hardening through a composite approach was proposed as an alternative strategy, capitalizing on the superior stability of secondary phases and their ability to effectively pin DWs via mechanical stress at phase interfaces13–15.

A classical hardening technique in metals, precipitation hardening, which introduces precipitates through a thermal treatment of supersaturated alloys16, was recently successfully implemented in Ba0.7Ca0.3TiO3 (BCT) and Li0.18Na0.82NbO3 (LNN) piezoceramics17,18. By establishing a proper aging scheme, precipitates with dimensions of several hundred nanometers were introduced into grains of the abovementioned piezoceramics. The intragranular precipitates directly interact with DWs and result in a more pronounced pinning effect, as compared to secondary phases located at grain boundaries. In situ, high-energy X-ray diffraction (XRD) further confirmed the suppression of DW motion in the presence of precipitates19.

In the case of anisometric precipitates, which were previously observed in LNN piezoceramics17,20, it is intuitively expected that the pinning effect on DWs varies in accordance to the relationship between the applied excitation (electric field or stress) and orientations of the precipitate. The related phenomenon of anisotropy in mechanical properties has been studied in precipitation-hardened metals21–23, underscoring its distinct influences on impeding different slip and twinning modes. Analogously, electrical properties in piezoceramics with precipitates should also exhibit anisotropic characteristics, stemming from two potential sources. One source arises from the crystal orientation: intrinsic electrical parameters, such as the piezoelectric coefficient and permittivity, manifest as tensors with distinct coefficients, thereby producing divergent electrical responses when an electric field is applied along different crystallographic directions24. Milestone advancements have been achieved by Wang et al. in delving into the electrical characteristics25. However, this effect can be largely neglected in non-textured polycrystalline materials where random grain orientation eliminates the anisotropy. The other source of anisotropy is the precipitate orientation, which can persist even in polycrystalline materials when a preferred precipitate orientation exists in the materials. The studies on precipitate-related anisotropy in ceramics, especially on electrical properties, are in its infancy. Similar to single crystals, achieving such anisotropy in polycrystalline materials can give rise to an enhancement in property if used in the proper direction. For example, if the pinning effect from precipitates on DWs is maximized in a certain direction, the mechanical quality factor could be significantly enhanced when applying an electric field in that direction. Hence, it is of great fundamental significance to investigate the anisotropy in precipitation-hardened piezoceramics as a cornerstone for functional anisotropy enticed by a prior application of a separate deviatoric field.

Investigating such precipitate-related macroscopic anisotropy necessitates the sophisticated alignment of precipitate particles at the microscopical level. It has been reported that applying a uniaxial stress during aging can lead to a shape change (“rafting”) or a preferred orientation (“variant selection”) of precipitates in various metallic alloys26,27. For instance, in Ni-based alloys, the application of tensile and compressive stress along [001] direction leads to the formation of plate-like and parallelepiped precipitates, respectively27–30. The effect of uniaxial stress during aging on precipitate morphology has been attributed to elastic effects (e.g., changes in elastic modulus, misfit strain, elastic free energy)26,29–31 and dislocation generation during creep27,28. Stress-driven variant selection of precipitates has also been observed in other alloy systems, e.g., Ti32, Al33, and Ni31 alloys. Song et al. investigated the effect of a uniaxial stress on different aging stages in a Ni–Mo–Cr–W alloy: the precipitates are globular at the early stage (nucleation) where the surface energy weighs in heavily, while during the coarsening stage, the volume energy leads to variant selection to reduce the strain energy of the system31. Through a simulation study, Shi et al. revealed that the variant selection in a Ti alloy is influenced by a competition between externally applied stress and internal stress arising from the precipitation reaction itself32.

However, the effect of uniaxial stress on precipitate formation in ceramics has remained unexplored. Even for the well-studied commercial system of magnesia-partially stabilized zirconia (Mg-PSZ), where interfacial strain and morphology of precipitates have been dealt with in great detail34, experimental endeavors of stress-assisted aging appear missing. Prior research in LNN has revealed that plate-like precipitates exhibit coherence with the matrix lattice along the {110}PC habit plane (the subscript “PC” means the hkl indexes are based on the pseudo-cubic coordinate system with the lattice parameter of ~3.9 Å)17,20. The smallest misfit strain of 0.84% is observed along the habit plane between the matrix phase (NaNbO3-rich solid solution, NNss) and precipitate phase (LiNbO3-rich solid solution, LNss). Notably, the NNss exhibits a cubic structure at the aging temperature, thus the possibility of a precipitate habiting on any one of {110}PC is the same, leading to six variants of the precipitates. However, when a uniaxial mechanical stress is applied, the misfit strain between the matrix phase and precipitate phase changes anisotropically. This suggests that certain precipitate variants with minimal overall misfit strain are favored energetically, and their selection leads to a degree of precipitate alignment when uniaxial mechanical stress is applied.

In the formation of precipitates in solids, three types of free energy changes compete: the energy reduction by replacing the matrix phase with the precipitate phase, the energy increase from the interfacial energy, and the misfit strain energy35. The driving force for the precipitation is the energy reduction, whereas the factors leading to energy increase represent barriers to precipitate nucleation. Together, the net energy change dictates the thermodynamics for precipitation process. Lowering the aging temperature elevates the chemical driving force35 but slows down the diffusion kinetics for precipitate growth. Therefore, static aging by tuning temperature alone may not be capable of achieving desired precipitate distribution and size within a practically feasible period36. Alternatively, one can envision lowering the nucleation barrier by applying a stress field to reduce the misfit strain energy, which effectively facilitates the precipitation nucleation without compromising on diffusion kinetics.

In this work, we aged sintered LNN piezoceramics under a constant uniaxial stress to introduce preferred orientations for precipitates. Initially, a mechanical simulation was performed to examine the energy difference between two precipitates oriented parallel and perpendicular to the stress direction, which substantiated the existence of preferred precipitate orientation in stress-assisted aged samples. Subsequently, cylindrical stress-assisted aged LNN samples were cut with varied orientations of their electroding faces, either parallel or perpendicular to the prior applied stress direction. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed to further evidence the variant selection in stress-assisted aged LNN by examining the relationship between precipitate orientation and applied stress. Electrical investigations, i.e., small-signal Rayleigh measurement and large-signal polarization hysteresis measurement, were conducted to quantify the DW pinning effect when the electric field is parallel or perpendicular to the stress direction. Our study has successfully achieved variant selection of precipitates in ceramic materials and sheds light on the electrical anisotropy in the precipitated LNN piezoceramics. These results provide a fundamental guide and pave the way for enhancing the electromechanical properties of precipitation-hardened piezoceramics.

Results and discussions

Precipitate alignment introduced by applying mechanical stress during aging

Here we address the challenge of aligning precipitates designed by applying a compressive uniaxial stress during the aging process. Quenched single-phase LNN piezoceramics were stress-assisted aged using a setup schematically depicted in Fig. 1a (see “Methods” section and Section 1 in the Supplementary Information). The samples which were aged with the maximum stress of 150 MPa and 350 MPa are denoted by “150 MPa-aged sample” and “350 MPa-aged sample”, respectively. For reference, samples aged with the same temperature but without stress (“stress-free aged sample”) were prepared. From a mechanical perspective, an applied stress can lead to an anisotropic change in the effective misfit strain between precipitate and matrix. For the LNN precipitation system, the matrix NNss phase is stiffer than the precipitate LNss phase, i.e., NNss has larger elastic constants37,38, and the lattice parameter of the precipitate phase LNss is smaller than that of the matrix phase NNss, indicating a negative misfit strain with respect to the matrix. Hence, a compressive stress will compact the LNss lattice more in the stress direction than the NNss lattice, leading to an increased effective misfit strain in the stress direction. As a result, precipitates with their long axis parallel/close to the stress direction are energetically disadvantaged. The possibilities of finding different precipitate variants are depicted in Fig. 1c, d, which are denoted by P1, P2, …, P6. According to the inference above, it can be hypothesized that P1 should be the smallest in the case of <110>PC-oriented grains, since its corresponding precipitate variant has its largest interface parallel to the stress direction, where the effective misfit is increased most by the mechanical stress thus it is least energetically favored.

Simulation results

To verify the abovementioned hypothesis and examine the relationship between the system’s mechanical energy, precipitate orientation, and precipitate size, the overall mechanical energies (sum of elastic, interface energy, and the potential of the external force) of two-dimensional matrix-precipitate systems were calculated as a function of precipitate size. Similar simulation has been performed to study stresses associated with the tetragonal precipitates and the tetragonal to monoclinic transformation in MgO PSZ39,40. The stress-free model is depicted in Fig. 2a, while the models with mechanical stress are schematically depicted in Fig. 2b, c (the explanation of the symbols used in Fig. 2a–c can be found in the “Methods” section). The ellipse represents the cross-section of the plate-like precipitate, where the long axis corresponds to the length direction and the short axis corresponds to the thickness direction. Compared to the stress-free model, the two models with stress are rotated by 45° to have the long axis of a precipitate parallel and perpendicular to the applied stress (corresponding to the P1 and P2 cases in Fig. 1c), representing the two extreme conditions.Fig. 2Mechanical simulation models and results. a–c Simulation models of matrix-precipitate system for stress-free case, and systems under load with precipitate oriented parallel and perpendicular to the stress vector. The calculated overall mechanical energies and the m values as a function of cross-section area of precipitate without and with applied stress are displayed in Fig. 2d–f. The overall mechanical energies and m values were calculated after a complete relaxation ensuring minimal mechanical energy for each given precipitate volume and each scenario (load case and precipitate orientation), thus only reflecting the final states. The kinetics during the precipitate growth can be described by configurational forces as depicted in Fig. S1. For both cases, the mechanical energy naturally increases with increasing precipitate size, as volume and interfacial area increase. In the stress-free case (σ = 0), the energy-size curves for the scenarios where the precipitate long axis is parallel and perpendicular to the stress vector σ coincide, indicating that there is no energy preference for the two precipitate variants (Fig. 2d). With σ = 150 and 350 MPa, the mechanical energy of the system with the precipitate long axis perpendicular to σ is lower than that of the system with precipitate long axis parallel to σ, and the difference increases with increasing precipitate size. This indicates that the precipitate variant with its long axis perpendicular to the applied stress is favored energetically, consistent with the hypothesis in Fig. 1d, e. The more significant difference at larger precipitate size implies that the variant selection due to the stress tends to occur during the precipitate growth stage, which is consistent with the observation from Song et al.31. In addition, the precipitate-orientation-related overall mechanical energy difference is more prominent in systems with larger mechanical load.

In general, the absolute value of the aspect ratio |m| increases with increasing precipitate size39,40. This is related to the reduced contribution of the interfacial energy term as compared to the volume term. As discussed in the beginning of this section, the compressive stress in LNN leads to an increased misfit strain in the stress direction, which renders precipitate elongation in this directionless energetically favored. In the case of a precipitate with its long axis parallel to the stress, the applied stress decreases the difference in misfit strain between the precipitate and matrix along the long axis. Hence, the dimensions in the cross-section are more equiaxed and the |m| value is reduced.

As predicted by the simulation and evidenced by the TEM characterization, precipitates with their long axes perpendicular to the applied stress carry a reduced penalty in mechanical energy in the stress-assisted aged LNN samples. That means, the precipitates tend to have in-plane orientations in the horizontal cut, while they tend to have out-of-plane orientations in the vertical cut. The Rayleigh results indicate that the stress-induced variant selection of precipitates has an influence on the irreversible DW motion driven by small-signal electric fields. The irreversible DW motion is suppressed to a larger degree when the precipitates are oriented out-of-plane regarding to the electroding faces (vertical cut), while the DWs are less impeded when the precipitates are oriented in-plane (horizontal cut). This difference is more significant in the samples aged with larger stress, where the anisotropic misfit strain changes and the variant selection effect is more prominent.

Polarization hysteresis

The pinning effect of DWs by precipitates differs between small- and large-signal electric field, as indicated in a previous study17. Representative room-temperature P–E loops of the 150 MPa-aged and 350 MPa-aged samples with different cuts are featured in Fig. 5a, b. The polarization of all aged samples does not saturate at electric fields below the dielectric breakdown field. Compared to the unaged sample, which exhibits typical ferroelectric P–E loop (Fig. S6), the strong pinning force from the precipitates does not allow the establishment of saturated polarization in the aged samples up to 8 kV/mm, which is consistent with the sample aged stress-free at a similar condition (500 °C-24 h + 600 °C-6 h) in the previous study17. The P–E loops of the horizontal and vertical cuts behave differently for the stress-assisted aged samples, where the vertical cuts exhibit lower remanent and maximum polarizations, indicating a stronger pinning effect on DWs. Unlike the small-signal property, for which the anisotropy differs distinctly in the 150 MPa-aged and 350 MPa-aged samples, the anisotropy in the large-signal P–E loop due to stress change is very small (Fig. 5e). This phenomenon indicates that the polarization under large-signal electric field is less sensitive to the variant selection of precipitates in LNN. It is worth noting that the stronger DW pinning effect in the vertical cut is consistent with the Rayleigh result, where the irreversible DW contribution is more suppressed in the vertical cut, suggesting that the effect of the stress-induced variant selection on DW pinning has the same trend under small- and large-signal electric fields.Fig. 5Anisotropy in polarization hysteresis loops induced by stress-assisted aging.a, b P–E loops of horizontal/vertical cut from the 150 MPa-aged and 350 MPa-aged samples; c, d P–E loops of horizontal and vertical cut from the 150 MPa-aged sample at elevated temperatures; e remanent polarization and maximum polarization of horizontal/vertical cut from stress-free, 150 MPa-aged and 350 MPa-aged samples; f evolution of the maximum polarization of the horizontal/vertical cut from the 150 MPa-aged sample as a function of temperature.

The P–E loops of the 150 MPa-aged samples at elevated temperatures are displayed in Fig. 5c, d. This can be attributed to the prevailing thermal activation of DWs over the spontaneous polarization decrease with increasing temperature. The P–E loops of the horizontal cut are found to open more with increasing temperature, as compared to the vertical cut. The maximum polarization Pm of the horizontal/vertical cut from the 150 MPa-aged sample as a function of temperature is depicted in Fig. 5f, and the normalized Pm is displayed in the inset. The increased Pm with temperature is suggested to relate to the thermally activated DW motion. Thermal activation is more effective in the horizontal cut (Fig. 5f). This mimics the related influence of precipitate orientation with increased electric field (Fig. 4d), where the horizontal cut also reaches higher relative maximum polarization with rising electric field. In contrast, the P–E loops in the vertical cut demonstrate stronger pinning effects as function of temperature (Fig. 5f) as well as electric field (Fig. 4d). This resilience to temperature excursions again demonstrates the strong advantage of precipitation hardening to chemical doping.

In summary, we have synthesized LNN piezoceramics and aged them under a uniaxial stress to introduce precipitates. The significance of this work lies in providing a simple method to introduce electrical anisotropy, which can be utilized to achieve a stronger DW pinning in a certain direction as compared to samples with isotropic electrical properties. Methods

Sample preparation

The LNN ceramics with a nominal composition of Li0.18Na0.82NbO3 were synthesized by solid-state reaction. The starting powders, Li2CO3 (Alfa Aesar, 99%), Na2CO3 (Alfa Aesar, 99.95%), and Nb2O5 (Alfa Aesar, 99.9%), were weighted stoichiometrically, except that 1% excess Na2CO3 was added to compensate for volatilization during the sintering process. The ball milling and calcination processes were reported elsewhere17. The calcined powders were then compacted into cylinders and subjected to cold isostatic pressing with a pressure of 357 MPa. The dimension of the green samples was ~Φ 6 mm × 9 mm. These green samples were sintered at 1300 °C for 2 h with a heating and cooling rate of 4 K/min.

Quenching is necessary for precipitation in LNN ceramics, as revealed by XRD examination of two aged samples, one of which underwent quenching and the other one was furnace-cooled after sintering (Fig. S9). To prevent cracks caused by thermal shock during quenching, the sintered cylindrical samples were lathed and ground to a dimension of ~Φ 4 mm × 8 mm44. Then, these samples were reheated to 1300 °C and held for 2 h, followed by air quenching. The cooling rate was carefully controlled (with ~5 min to cool from the maximum temperature to room temperature) to avoid cracks.

Stress-assisted aging was conducted on quenched cylindrical samples using a load frame (Z010, Zwick/Roell, Ulm, Germany) equipped with a furnace. A two-stage aging consisting of the nucleation stage and growth stage for the precipitates was adopted. The sample was preheated to 300 °C, where the precipitate formation is kinetically suppressed. Then, the desired mechanical compressive stress of either 150 or 350 MPa was applied to the sample at a loading rate of 0.4 N/s (0.032 MPa/s). The sample was heated to the nucleation temperature (550 °C) and held for 24 h, and then to the growth temperature (650 °C) and held for another 8 h. After aging, the mechanical stress was released at a rate of 1 N/s, and the temperature was decreased to room temperature with 5 K/min. Each cylindrical sample was cut into 4~5 horizontal cuts and 4~5 vertical cuts. Platinum electrodes were sputtered onto the largest faces of the cut samples, so that the applied electrical field was parallel and perpendicular to the applied stress in the horizontal cut and vertical cut, respectively.

TEM characterization

Structural and chemical investigations of precipitates were performed with scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) using probe Cs corrected Jeol ARM 200CF (Jeol Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), equipped with a Jeol Centurio 100 mm2 Silicon Drift Detector (SDD) EDS system and a Gatan Quantum ER Dual EELS system (Gatan, Pleasanton, CA, USA). One horizontal cut and one vertical cut from a 350 MPa-aged sample were prepared for TEM examination. The TEM specimens were prepared by grinding with SiC paper of 1200, 2400, and 4000 grit, followed by polishing with diamond paste containing 3 and 0.25 µm particles to a thickness of ~100 µm. The polished discs were then dimple ground to around 10 µm thickness using a dimpler grinder (Model 656, Gatan, Pleasanton, CA, USA) and further thinned to electron transparency by Ar-ion milling (Gatan PIPS Model 691, New York, NY, USA) at voltages ranging from 3.5 to 2.5 kV until a hole appeared, followed by cleaning at 0.8 kV. The samples were cooled during the Ar-ion milling process. In order to retain information regarding the direction of mechanical load, a horizontal cut was processed as a plane view, while a vertical cut was prepared in the cross-sectional view orientation.

Electrical measurement

The large-signal polarization hysteresis measurements were performed with a modified Sawyer–Tower circuit. Three to four samples of each cut and each aging condition were quantified for statistics and reproducibility. The samples were immersed in silicone oil during the measurement. A triangular electric field with a frequency of 1 Hz and amplitude up to 8 kV/mm was applied. High-temperature polarization hysteresis measurements up to 120 °C were realized by heating the silicone oil, and the electric-field parameters were the same as those used in the room-temperature measurement. Cycling test of P–E loops was performed by applying cycling electric field with a frequency of 1 Hz and amplitude up to 8 kV/mm at room temperature. The Rayleigh coefficients were determined by measuring the permittivity with increasing field amplitude. This measurement used a commercial lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research System, SR830 DSP), a voltage source meter (also SR830 DSP), and a voltage amplifier (Trek, Model PZD700A M/S). These instruments were paired with a custom-made resistor box, which includes precision resistors that are controlled by selection and connected in series with the sample. The frequency was 1 kHz and the applied electric field increased from 0 to 1000 V/mm and then decreased back. Before the measurement, the samples were poled at 8 kV/mm at 100 °C for 15 min and cooled down to room temperature with the field on.
