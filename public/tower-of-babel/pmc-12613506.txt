Double-negative NMOSD: from case report to a proposed diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm
Authors: Giuseppe Romano, Giacomo Lus, Mario Cirillo, Cinzia Coppola, Simona Bonavita, Elisabetta Signoriello
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDouble seronegative neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (DN-NMOSD) is a rare autoimmune disease of the central nervous system, typically involving the optic nerve and spinal cord, characterized by negativity for anti-AQP4 and anti-MOG antibodies.Objectives and methodsA 35-year-old man with optic neuritis and recurrent myelitis, negative for AQP4 and MOG antibodies, was diagnosed with double seronegative NMOSD (DN-NMOSD). Initial treatment with Rituximab led to temporary stability, but relapses in 2021 and 2022 prompted a switch to Satralizumab (anti-IL-6R), achieving two years of clinical and radiological stability.Discussions and conclusionsThis case underscores the therapeutic potential of anti-IL-6 drugs for DN-NMOSD, especially when anti-CD20 therapies fail. It highlights the heterogeneity of DN-NMOSD and the need for novel biomarkers, such as GFAP, tau, and IL-6, to better understand disease mechanisms and guide targeted treatments. A structured therapeutic approach, starting with anti-CD20 drugs and progressing to anti-IL-6 agents if ineffective, may optimize outcomes in this poorly understood condition.

Introduction

Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is an autoimmune disease of the central nervous system (CNS) primarily affecting the optic nerves and spinal cord. NMOSD is usually associated with AQP4-IgG or MOG-IgG positivity, but a subgroup of patients remains doubly seronegative (DN) lacking any established diagnostic biomarker [1]. This report discusses a 35-year-old man diagnosed with DN-NMOSD and treated with Satralizumab, achieving clinical-radiological stability over two years. The patient provided informed consent for the disclosure of his clinical information.

Case report

A 35-year-old man with a family history of sarcoidosis presented in June 2020 with decreased visual acuity in the right eye, pain on eye movements, papilledema, and a paracentral scotoma. These symptoms occurred one month after receiving his second dose of the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine. Brain MRI revealed right optic nerve involvement. (Fig. 1). In September 2020, he developed paraesthesia in the left hand and hypoesthesia in the abdominal area. Neurological examination identified left pyramidal syndrome and decreased vibration sense. Cervical and thoracic spine MRI showed longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (LETM) (Fig. 2). Laboratory tests showed a positive ANA titre (1:640). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed an elevated barrier index without oligoclonal bands. Test for AQP4-IgG in serum and MOG IgG in serum and CSF (live CBA) were negative, as were anti-onconeural antibodies panels and infectious screening. Given the family history, a chest CT and a whole-body PET/CT were performed, both yielding negative results. The patient began Rituximab therapy in November 2020 at a dosage of 1000 mg every six months, but experienced a relapse in January 2021. After initial stability, another episode occurred in May 2022, characterised by vision decline in left eye and LETM, following the last Rituximab dose in January 2022. MRI confirmed disease activity (Fig. 3). Lymphocyte subset analysis confirmed effective B-cell depletion. Anti-MOG, anti-AQP4 and anti-GFAP antibodies remained negative during the acute phase of the disease. These findings led to a therapeutic switch to Satralizumab, resulting in sustained clinical and radiological stability over a two-year follow-up.

Discussion

Double seronegative NMOSD includes cases negative for both AQP4 and MOG antibodies, commonly presenting with optic neuritis and LETM. Several studies report a variable prevalence of this spectrum of disease, with a median age of onset ranging from 32 to 43 years [2]. A slight female predominance is noted, without clear racial predilection [3, 4]. Clinically, patients typically experience longitudinally extensive optic neuritis and LETM. Other manifestations, such as area postrema syndrome and involvement of the brain or brainstem, are less common and less characteristic [5]. The clinical course varies from monophasic to relapsing form and disability was similar to or worse than AQP4-IgG + NMOSD. Older patients have poorer outcomes, though milder forms exist, including those with co-occurring ON and LETM [6, 7, 8]. Recent imaging studies suggest four potential neuroradiological patterns: MS-like cortical lesions, spinal MS-like short-segment myelitis, and NMO-like LETM with or without brain involvement [8]. Whether DN-NMOSD involves astrocytopathy, as seen in AQP4 + NMOSD, remains under investigation. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6 in the CSF, have been observed—similar to AQP4 + NMOSD and MOGAD [9]. It is well known that IL-6 has a pleiotropic effect on numerous biological processes, playing a regulatory role in both acute and chronic inflammation [10]. In addition to IL-6, two of the most extensively studied biomarkers in NMOSD and MOGAD are glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and Tau protein. GFAP is an intermediate filament protein expressed by astrocytes and released into the CSF following astrocytic damage. Tau proteins are microtubule-associated molecules involved in maintaining the structural stability of axons and oligodendrocytes [11, 12]. Hyun et al. reported significantly lower CSF GFAP levels in DN-NMOSD compared to AQP4-NMOSD, with levels similar to those in MOGAD, suggesting a distinct underlying pathology [13]. Kim et al. found higher GFAP levels in the AQP4 + patient group at baseline, while during the acute phase of the disease, there was a significant increase in tau and GFAP in the MOGAD and AQP4-NMOSD groups, respectively [14]. More recently, the group of Carta et al. conducted a retrospective study comparing DN-NMOSD and AQP4-NMOSD patients, showing that during the acute phase of the disease, GFAP and tau levels were significantly higher in the AQP4-NMOSD group compared to the DN-NMOSD group [15]. These findings indicate that astrocytic involvement is less prominent in DN-NMOSD, as reflected by CSF GFAP levels comparable to those seen in MOGAD and significantly lower than in AQP4-NMOSD. Similarly, during the acute phase, both MOGAD and DN-NMOSD show reduced serum GFAP levels compared to AQP4-NMOSD, while tau levels vary, being higher in MOGAD and lower in DN-NMOSD. Taken together, these findings suggest that among the biomarkers studied, GFAP is the most informative in distinguishing AQP4-NMOSD from other subtypes. This case illustrates DN-NMOSD unresponsive to Rituximab but stabilised with Satralizumab, an anti-IL-6R monoclonal antibody approved exclusively for AQP4-positive NMOSD [16]. Clinical trials such as SAkuraSky and SAkuraStar reported limited or no benefit from Satralizumab in seronegative patients. However, a key limitation of both studies, as acknowledged by the authors themselves, is the very small number of seronegative patients included. This significantly limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of Satralizumab in this subgroup [16, 17]. The decision to initiate Satralizumab includes the patient’s excellent clinical response to steroid therapy as well as the neuroimaging characteristics. Both characteristics are well-documented in the literature for MOG-associated diseases, as well as the limited efficacy of anti-CD20 therapies despite successful B-cell depletion, in contrast to AQP4-positive NMOSD forms. Secondly, although CSF IL-6 levels were not assessed, the use of anti-IL6R in this case was based on the known pathophysiological role of IL-6 in NMOSD and supported, to a limited extent, by real-world evidence suggesting possible benefit in DN-NMOSD [18]. Fig. 4 presents a potential diagnostic-therapeutic algorithm in cases of suspected NMOSD or MOGAD. For anti-AQP4 antibody, serum testing using fixed CBA provides good diagnostic performance. In the case of anti-MOG antibody testing, fixed CBA in serum should be the first-line test due to its greater availability and ease of use. However, fixed CBA is known to have lower sensitivity, potentially missing up to 14% of MOGAD cases. Therefore, when clinical suspicion for MOGAD remains high despite negative fixed CBA results, live CBA, preferably performed on serum, and optionally on CSF, should be recommended, as it increases diagnostic accuracy and reduces the risk of false negatives [19]. In cases of double seronegativity, measuring serum and/or CSF levels GFAP, along with CSF levels of IL-6, could provide more detailed insights into the underlying pathology and consequently guide therapeutic choices.

Conclusions

Double seronegative NMOSD represents a heterogeneous and poorly understood group of CNS inflammatory disorders. This case highlights the importance of identifying reliable biomarkers for accurate diagnosis and targeted therapy. The successful use of Satralizumab underscores the potential role of IL-6 in DN-NMOSD and supports a personalised therapeutic approaches. Incorporating GFAP and IL-6 analysis into clinical workflows may enhance disease understanding and therapeutic outcomes.
