Spondyloarthritis Research and Treatment Network Recommendations for the Referral of Adults With Chronic Back Pain and Suspected Axial Spondyloarthritis to a Rheumatologist
Authors: Maureen Dubreuil, Abhijeet Danve, Jean W. Liew, Swetha Ann Alexander, Yuliya Afinogenova, Mohamad Bittar, Liana Fraenkel, Alyssa Grimshaw, Anand Kumthekar, Michael P. LaValley, Marina Nighat Magrey, Vikas Majithia, Sali Merjanah, Hillary Norton, Jessica A Walsh, Atul Deodhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn the United States, axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) diagnosis is delayed up to 14 years preventing effective management. We convened a multidisciplinary clinician and patient group to develop recommendations for rheumatology referral of adults with chronic back pain.MethodsSystematic review (SR) and meta‐analysis determined predictive values of clinical, laboratory, and imaging axSpA features for diagnosis. In a Delphi exercise, experts reviewed these test characteristics and voted to gain consensus for inclusion or exclusion in the final recommendations. Features that did not gain consensus were evaluated using discrete choice experiments (DCE). Positive likelihood ratios (+LRs) were used to calculate the probability of axSpA to develop the referral strategy.ResultsThe SR uncovered 28 axSpA features (+LR 0.5–10). There was consensus to include uveitis, elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate or C‐reactive protein, family history of spondyloarthritis, HLA‐B27, inflammatory bowel disease, sacroiliitis by imaging, good response to nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, and psoriasis. DCE allowed exclusion of features with low importance. Experts considered posttest probability of ≥33% appropriate for referral. +LRs were used to generate a scoring system to be used in adults with chronic back pain with onset before age 45 years; a score of ≥3 is considered a threshold for referral to a rheumatologist.ConclusionsOn behalf of the Spondyloarthritis Research and Treatment Network (SPARTAN), we developed data‐driven referral recommendations for adults with chronic back pain to a rheumatologist for evaluation of axSpA. Validation is needed to determine if axSpA is diagnosed in 33% of those referred and if this strategy reduces diagnostic delay.

INTRODUCTION

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is an immune‐mediated inflammatory disease predominantly involving the spine and sacroiliac joints that affects over 1% of U.S. adults and can lead to chronic back pain, stiffness, and substantial disability. 1 AxSpA may be more common than rheumatoid arthritis in the United States (1.4% vs 0.6% of adults), and disability and lost productivity in axSpA are similar to that of rheumatoid arthritis. 2 , 3 , 4 About 5% of patients with chronic back pain have axSpA. 5 , 6

In recent decades, new treatments have been approved for axSpA, including inhibitors of tumor necrosis factor, interleukin‐17A, and JAK, which are highly effective in improving the symptoms and disability attendant to this disease. Unfortunately, patients presenting with chronic back pain may not be evaluated for axSpA until late in its course. 7 , 8 Lack of timely referral to a rheumatologist is a critical reason for delayed axSpA diagnosis. Globally, diagnostic delay averages six to eight years, as compared to four months for rheumatoid arthritis. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Earlier diagnosis and treatment of those with axSpA is associated with greater improvements in disease activity and long‐term outcomes. 14 , 15 , 16

In North America, there are no formal recommendations regarding which patients with chronic back pain should be referred to a rheumatologist for evaluation of axSpA. Although recommendations exist in other locales, these cannot be directly applied elsewhere due to differences in health care systems. Timely referral remains an unmet need in the United States, where average time to diagnosis has been reported up to 14 years. 17 The Spondyloarthritis Research and Treatment Network (SPARTAN), an organization of North American clinicians interested in spondyloarthritis (SpA), undertook development of such recommendations. Here, we report the development of SPARTAN‐endorsed evidence‐based recommendations for referral of adults with chronic back pain at increased risk of axSpA to rheumatologists.

METHODS

Four teams consisted of SPARTAN members and contributors interested in axSpA. The steering committee (AD, AD, and MD) defined the project scope and established methodology. The literature review committee performed systematic review (SR) and synthesized evidence regarding the utility of features suggestive of axSpA. The data analysis team analyzed the data from SR and conducted discrete choice experiments (DCE). A larger contributor team included patients with axSpA, a representative from the Spondylitis Association of America, a primary care physician, an orthopedic spine specialist, a physiatrist, a chiropractor, an occupational medicine specialist, a gastroenterologist, an ophthalmologist with expertise in uveitis, a dermatologist, and a physical therapist.

This project was conducted in four phases, as follows (Figure 1): (1) SR and meta‐analysis, (2) Delphi exercise to achieve consensus on inclusion and exclusion of identified items, (3) DCE to reduce item number, and (4) drafting the recommendations and final voting. Due to the nature of this work, institutional review board approval was not required.

SR and meta‐analysis

We adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) statement for reporting (Supplementary Table 1). 18 The protocol was registered with Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/7a398/).

Data sources and literature search

SR and meta‐analysis were conducted to identify individual SpA features or combinations that predict diagnosis (or classification) of axSpA in patients with chronic back pain. The research questions were designed in the PICO (Patients, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcomes) format, established by the steering committee and endorsed by team axSpA experts. The central theme of PICO questions was: Are patients with chronic back pain (CBP) with SpA feature X more likely to be diagnosed (or classified) as axSpA compared to those who do not have SpA feature X? SpA features included the inflammatory back pain (IBP) construct, individual IBP criteria, 19 , 20 good response to nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), inflammatory arthritis, enthesitis, uveitis, dactylitis, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD; either Crohn disease or ulcerative colitis), family history of SpA (axSpA, reactive arthritis, psoriasis, IBD, or uveitis), presence of HLA‐B27, elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) or C‐reactive protein (CRP), and sacroiliitis on imaging (x‐ray, computed tomography [CT], or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]). Each of these features was considered in a specific PICO question. We included studies that described diagnosis and/or classification of patients having axSpA to accommodate for the heterogeneity of the data in the reported literature.

A systematic literature search was conducted in the Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Ovid Embase, Ovid Medline, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science Core Collection databases to find relevant articles published from inception of each database to April 2023. Databases were searched using a combination of controlled vocabulary, subheadings, and free text for terms including ankylosing spondylitis (AS), axSpA and subtypes (nonradiographic and radiographic), undifferentiated SpA, spondyloarthropathy, screening, referral, diagnosis, classification, risk factors, and referral strategy. References of included studies were reviewed to find additional potential studies. The search was peer‐reviewed by a second medical librarian using PRESS (Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies). 21 The search was not limited by publication type or date. Details of the full search strategy are listed in Supplementary Table 2. 22

Study selection eligibility criteria

Citations from all databases were imported in an Endnote 20 library (Clarivate Analytics). The Yale Reference Deduplicator was used to remove duplicates. 23 Covidence was used for screening the studies. Two independent reviewers from the literature review team performed a title and abstract review with a third reviewer to adjudicate disagreements. Full texts were then reviewed for inclusion by two independent reviewers with a senior reviewer to resolve disagreements.

Original English language publications meeting four criteria were included. First, case‐control and cohort studies in which patients with chronic back pain with suspicion of axSpA were referred to rheumatologists were included. Second, the report had to include a description of both an axSpA group and a control group. Patients in the axSpA group included those with both radiographic or nonradiographic axSpA (including the historical term AS) and undifferentiated SpA. Patients without axSpA or related diseases in these studies, as well as studies which included nonspecific back pain patients, served as controls. Third, the axSpA group was defined by diagnosis of axSpA or fulfillment of AS or axSpA classification criteria. 24 , 25 Fourth, clear description of prevalence of one or more SpA features among the axSpA and control groups, or description of axSpA prevalence among both a risk factor exposed and unexposed group was required. Abstracts, case series, case reports, and reviews were excluded.

Data collection process

Two reviewers from the literature review team independently extracted data using a standardized form. A third resolved any conflicts. Extracted data included study characteristics (first author, study design, year of publication, country of origin, study duration, sample size, and study sponsor), baseline participant characteristics (primary population and controls, age at back pain onset, duration of back pain, and frequency of SpA features in cases and controls), information about the strategy for referral to rheumatology and diagnostic outcomes (final diagnosis or classification), the number of individuals in the axSpA and control groups, and the prevalence of each SpA feature within each group.

Risk of bias in individual studies

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS‐2) tool for the quality assessment of diagnostic accuracy. 26 The QUADAS‐2 tool consists of four domains that include patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing.

Data analysis

We pooled data from individual studies to generate test characteristics including sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratios (+LRs), and positive predictive values for each SpA feature. We then used the +LR to calculate the probability of axSpA in the presence of each axSpA feature (“posttest probability”), assuming pretest probability of 5%, which is the estimated prevalence of axSpA among adults with chronic back pain. 5 , 27 We also calculated posttest probability of axSpA for combinations of axSpA features using their likelihood products. We multiplied LR for individual SpA features to generate likelihood products, because there were not sufficient data in the literature to estimate correlation or conditional dependence between features. This approach may lead to an overestimate of the resulting posttest probabilities. Given that the primary motivation for this work was to increase the number of adults with chronic back pain who are referred to a rheumatologist for evaluation, this limitation was found to be acceptable.

Delphi exercise

A Delphi exercise was conducted at the SPARTAN 2022 annual meeting to achieve consensus about inclusion or exclusion of the SpA features of interest, informed by the SR and meta‐analysis. The goal at project outset was to develop the most practical and simple set of recommendations consisting of no more than 10 features for consideration by referring clinicians.

In a hybrid virtual or in‐person session at the SPARTAN 2022 annual meeting, full members (experts in axSpA) were asked to vote on the question: “How many adults with chronic back pain would you be willing to evaluate in order to diagnose one new case of axSpA?” which ascertained the lowest probability of an adult having axSpA that would be appropriate for rheumatologist referral. This question assessed the feasibility and willingness of rheumatologists interested in axSpA to evaluate such referrals. Options were 2:1 (50% probability axSpA), 3:1 (33%), 5:1 (20%), 10:1 (10%), and 20:1 (5%). Results of this vote were used to determine the minimal +LR necessary to achieve a probability of axSpA before referral based on an individual's characteristics.

SPARTAN members were asked to review feature characteristics and vote on its inclusion or exclusion in the referral recommendations using a Likert scale (definitely not include, probably not include, neutral, probably include, or definitely include). A priori, the steering committee designed two questions to address the combination of related features into single items within the referral recommendations or keeping items separate; (1) family history of axSpA or related conditions and (2) imaging (x‐ray and MRI) findings of sacroiliitis. Features with >70% votes for either inclusion or exclusion were considered to have achieved consensus and were carried forward as such.

DCE

Features not achieving inclusion or exclusion consensus were further considered using DCE, which quantify individuals’ preferences among competing choices. This method has been used to study patient preferences in a variety of treatment scenarios. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 We presented axSpA experts with a series of scenarios, each comparing two hypothetical patient cases. Each case was described in terms of eight features that did not achieve consensus in the Delphi and one feature that achieved perfect consensus (uveitis; 100% include) to serve as an anchor for interpretation (Supplementary Figure 1). Each scenario was designed to differ on two or three features but not more to avoid cognitive overload. Combinations of differing features were presented with orthogonal frequencies across 25 versions of the questionnaire, each with 20 patient case comparisons. The output was the relative importance value for each feature, describing its importance to a diagnosis of axSpA considering all other features in the exercise, across the respondent sample. The questionnaire was designed, administered, and analyzed using Sawtooth software.

Drafting the final recommendations and endorsement by SPARTAN Board of Directors

Draft recommendations were developed through a series of biweekly steering group meetings, virtual contributor meetings and input from other SPARTAN members or axSpA experts in the literature review, and data analysis committees. The draft recommendations were reviewed by the SPARTAN Board of Directors and presented to the SPARTAN membership at the 2023 annual meeting for feedback and voting.

Acceptable prereferral probability of axSpA

Of the axSpA experts voting on the minimal prereferral probability of axSpA diagnosis needed for adults with chronic back pain to be referred to a rheumatologist, 27 (90%) indicated that a probability of ≥33% was acceptable.

Delphi exercise

AxSpA experts voted to combine individual items comprising family history of axSpA and related conditions into a single item (89%), and to combine sacroiliitis by x‐ray and by MRI into a single imaging item (83%) (Figure 3). Items meeting consensus for inclusion were uveitis (100%), CRP (97%), family history of axSpA or related condition (93%), HLA‐B27 positivity (93%), IBD (87%), sacroiliitis by imaging (86%), good response to NSAIDs (74%), ESR (73%), and psoriasis (71%). Two items met consensus for exclusion: neck pain (97%) and thoracic spine pain (83%).

Development of the final recommendations

Features achieving consensus (>70%) for inclusion in the Delphi exercise were considered mandatory for inclusion in the final recommendations, whereas those with consensus for exclusion were considered ineligible for inclusion. Several decisions further reduced the number of items for consideration: (1) combining two laboratory features of inflammation (ESR or CRP) into a single item for pragmatic reasons and feasibility, (2) omitting the “insidious onset” feature from further consideration due to lack of a broadly accepted definition of this concept and its relatively low specificity and +LR, and (3) omitting “morning stiffness” due to its low LR. Following DCE results, the decision was made to omit features that were less than half as important as uveitis from the recommendations.

We further considered combinations of features to minimize time and effort in evaluation by referring clinicians. To this end, features still eligible for inclusion in the recommendations were grouped by their +LR, considering ranges of 1 to 3, 3 to 5, and more than 5 corresponding with their impact on posttest probability. Feature combinations were considered to achieve a prereferral probability of ~33%, based on the expert vote. A point value was assigned to each item corresponding to its LR or product.

The draft referral recommendations were modified with contributor input in early 2023 to improve accuracy and clarity (Figure 5). The SPARTAN Board of Directors approved this version in April 2024.

DISCUSSION

This was a SPARTAN‐sponsored, collaborative effort between leading axSpA experts from North America, clinicians who commonly evaluate adults with chronic back pain and extramusculoskeletal manifestations of axSpA, and patients with axSpA. We provide recommendations for referral of adults with chronic back pain and other features to a rheumatologist for axSpA evaluation.

Although referral recommendations have been developed in Europe, such recommendations were not previously developed for a U.S.‐specific setting. 34 This remained a major unmet need, considering the differences in health care delivery models in the United States, Mexico, and Canada compared to Europe, as well as differences in costs (including copayments), referral patterns, and the rheumatology workforce. These recommendations achieved our goal of including 10 or fewer features and being simple enough to consider as a checklist, rather than a complex calculator. It is important to note that the SPARTAN referral strategy is intended to guide referral for rheumatology evaluation and is not a diagnostic or classification instrument for axSpA.

Chronic back pain is a major public health problem and about 20% of the adult population may suffer from it. 5 About 5% of these patients, or 1% of the total U.S. population, have axSpA. 6 Due to lack of specific examination findings and the absence of a diagnostic biomarker, patients with axSpA may go undiagnosed for years. Poor awareness among nonrheumatologists also contributes to the lack of timely referral of patients with axSpA to rheumatologists. 7 Given the severe shortage of rheumatologists in North America, it is neither feasible nor appropriate for them to see all patients with back pain. These recommendations are critical given that diagnostic delay for axSpA remains 6 to 8 years, with one study showing a delay of 14 years in the United States. 17 Diagnostic delay can further compound reduced quality of life due to axSpA disease activity. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 35 , 36

Our SR and meta‐analysis summarized the predictive value of various SpA features for axSpA diagnosis and synthesized evidence for referral recommendations. +LRs for most SpA features were fair (1.1–3.5 resulting in posttest probability <15%), such that each of these features could not be used as a sole criterion prompting rheumatology referral, except for positive HLA‐B27 or sacroiliitis on imaging.

Being one of the most common symptoms of axSpA, IBP has been included as a criterion in most referral strategy studies to date. 8 , 17 We found that the definitions of IBP and clinician type discerning the presence of IBP (rheumatologist vs nonrheumatologist) were variable across studies. Similar to previous literature, we found that IBP was sensitive (87%) but not specific (58%) for axSpA diagnosis and had a +LR of 1.87, lower than the 3.1 previously reported. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 Results of a more recent (2023) SR and meta‐analysis were similar to ours, finding that IBP had good sensitivity, low specificity, and low +LR (1.7). 39 In another study, expert rheumatologists assessed 20 patients with IBP and suspected SpA, finding that improvement with exercise (odds ratio [OR] 23.1) and nocturnal pain (OR 20.1) had highest ORs for the diagnosis of IBP. 19 Insidious onset of back pain, improvement with exercise, and no improvement with rest were relatively sensitive features while good response to NSAIDs was more specific. In our study, except for good response to NSAIDs (+LR 2.32), the +LR for individual IBP items was less than 2. In general, a +LR of 4 or more is considered meaningful for a diagnostic test and is associated with increased probability of the particular diagnosis. 41 To our knowledge, ours is the first study in which sensitivity, specificity, and +LR of individual IBP items were analyzed for the diagnosis of axSpA. We calculated the predictive values of individual items in order to include only selected IBP items in the referral recommendations so that nonrheumatology clinicians can avoid the step of determining the presence IBP in patients with chronic back pain before referral.

Among the extraspinal musculoskeletal manifestations, peripheral arthritis, enthesitis, and dactylitis had high specificity but poor sensitivity and low +LRs. Extramusculoskeletal manifestations including uveitis, IBD, and psoriasis, had high specificity and +LR for axSpA but poor sensitivity. We observed that the family history of spondyloarthritides has a good +LR for the diagnosis of axSpA. Data about family history of individual SpA conditions were scant in the included publications for this SR, and therefore precision of this association is unclear. We found that only positive HLA‐B27 and sacroiliitis by imaging had +LR of >4, to indicate they would meaningfully change probability of axSpA as independent features. We note that in the daSilva study, +LR was lower for HLA‐B27 positivity and family history compared to our meta‐analysis, possibly driven by differing objectives and inclusion criteria in this study. 39 Overall, the +LRs were higher for objective manifestations of the axSpA including uveitis, IBD, psoriasis, elevated ESR or CRP, positive HLA B27, and sacroiliitis by imaging than other SpA features.

Since 2004, there have been few studies which attempted to find relative weights of SpA features for the diagnosis of axSpA. 39 , 40 In our study, we found that the +LR of SpA features were lower than those previously described. 40 There are severable possible reasons for these differences. Rudwaleit included studies where comparators may have been healthy, had other musculoskeletal diseases, or had back pain controls as opposed to this SR where all comparators had chronic back pain. We included patients with whole spectrum of axSpA as compared to the Rudwaleit study in which the majority of patients had AS. Our SR also included more cohort studies compared to Rudwaleit, which included predominantly case‐control studies that could have overestimated the +LRs of SpA features. 41 Moreover, the data about nomenclature, prevalence, and clinical manifestations of axSpA have evolved over last two decades which may have contributed to the lower +LRs in our SR.

We note that +LRs are context dependent and vary across practice settings. For example, +LR may be higher among adults who have received specialized care for back pain (eg, chiropractic or orthopedic care). It is beyond the scope of this work to assess setting‐specific +LRs. The direction and magnitude of this potential bias across settings such as primary care, physical therapy, chiropractic, or occupational medicine is uncertain; the +LRs derived here may overestimate or underestimate the performance in populations with differing pretest probabilities and disease spectra. Prospective validation across diverse nonrheumatology settings is needed.

There are several strengths to this work. We include data on nonradiographic axSpA, which comprises more than half of axSpA cases. Our methods were intentionally data‐driven in order to avoid guidelines based solely on expert opinion. Our meta‐analysis allowed calculation of characteristics commonly considered in evaluation of diagnostic tests, highlighting the clinically relevant LR+. The use of a Delphi exercise as the first step to reduce the number of features is a common step in guideline development. 42 , 43 , 44 We were able to further quantify axSpA experts’ opinions regarding the value of individual axSpA features through DCE and ultimately reduce the items to a set of 10 features for clinicians to consider in evaluating adults with chronic back pain. Development of these recommendations included input from diverse clinician experts to ensure broad feasibility. Finally, these recommendations are clear, practical, and relevant to physicians as well as advanced practice providers, physical therapists, and other clinicians who care for patients with chronic back pain.

Several features historically considered key features of axSpA were ultimately excluded from the final recommendations as a result of this data‐driven process. Some features were previously reported to have more test characteristics when considered for radiographic axSpA only, due to limited or unavailable data at that time for nonradiographic axSpA. 40 , 45 Our findings represent recommendations specific to the current understanding of the full spectrum of axSpA. We must acknowledge that, in our SR and meta‐analysis, some SpA features were described in a limited number of published studies, and hence the +LR of these features may not be reliable.

It is also important to note similarities and differences between our referral strategy and that published by the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society (ASAS) in 2015. 34 Both strategies address the same population (adults with chronic back pain with onset before age 45 years); however the ASAS strategy recommends referral if any additional item is fulfilled, thereby weighting all included items equally. Our strategy included a weighting for each item based on LR+, and we found that imaging and laboratory results carry greater weight than extra‐articular manifestations, family history, or back pain features.

It is important to not confuse a referral strategy to be used by nonrheumatologists with an axSpA diagnosis strategy for use by rheumatologists, because both processes use the same features. Although rheumatologists may continue to consider SpA features omitted from the referral strategy when diagnosing axSpA, these features were omitted due to test characteristics indicating little or no improvement in prereferral probability of axSpA. Future studies may use more precise feature definitions that may improve test characteristics and garner inclusion in a later revision.

With stringent application of these recommendations, some people who truly have axSpA may not meet referral criteria. Because of resource limitations (rheumatologist workforce), we sought to strike a balance between relatively low sensitivity of the axSpA features and feature combinations with a minimal axSpA probability of 33%. This 33% threshold was determined by axSpA experts, largely from academic practices, and may not reflect the necessary threshold among community rheumatologists. However, these recommendations are an improvement over current practice in increasing the number of adults with chronic back pain who are referred to a rheumatologist for evaluation of axSpA, thereby reducing the average diagnostic delay. This scoring system is designed to identify patients in whom referral should be strongly considered; it should not be used to exclude axSpA or prevent referral when clinical concern remains. Clinicians should use their acumen and refer patients with suspected axSpA, even if they do not fulfill referral criteria. The current work represents the initial steps of North American referral recommendations, but there is additional work required for the recommendations to influence patient outcomes. These steps include dissemination, implementation and adaptation within different health care settings, and evaluation. 46 , 47 The performance of the referral recommendations should be studied within different practice settings, and attention should be given to strategies that facilitate their use and uptake. Given that the scope of this project was focused on North America, our referral recommendations may not be appropriate for other settings, due to variation in axSpA epidemiology, rheumatology workforce, referral patterns, and access to SpA expertise. Even within North America, access to laboratory and imaging investigations required to implement this referral strategy may vary across practice settings and health care systems.

In conclusion, SPARTAN referral recommendations used the best available scientific evidence within a data‐driven approach involving rigorous SR and meta‐analysis. These recommendations will bridge the long term unmet need to reduce axSpA diagnostic delay and will provide nonrheumatology clinicians with a framework for deciding which patients to refer. Implementation of the recommendations will ultimately promote early diagnosis of patients with axSpA, allowing earlier treatment and improved outcomes in axSpA.
