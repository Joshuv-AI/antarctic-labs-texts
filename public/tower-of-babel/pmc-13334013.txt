Floor failure mechanism and aquifuge-grouting reinforcement under narrow strip mining above a confined Ordovician limestone aquifer: a case study of the Weibei Mining Area, China
Authors: Guowei Ma, Yaoqiang Geng, Yuanzhen Zhang, Xianliang Li, Wenjing Liu, Zhanhang Wei, Meile Liu, Jianjun Li, Yuxiang Fei, Huayong Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaking a coal mine in the Weibei Mining Area of Shaanxi Province as the engineering background, and focusing on the floor water inrush problem encountered in the mining of narrow strip working faces above the Ordovician limestone confined aquifer, this study effectively overcomes the limitations of the traditional high-cost surface large-scale aquifer grouting reconstruction technology and the discontinuous underground short-hole aquifer grouting reconstruction technology, and proposes a continuous underground directional long-borehole grouting reinforcement technology targeting the aquifuge. A comprehensive research methodology integrating theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and field measurement is adopted to systematically investigate the pore structure characteristics, mining-induced failure mechanism, water richness and grouting reinforcement effect of the coal floor. The results show that the permeability of quartz sandstone ranges from 0.033 to 0.059 mD, and its porosity varies from 1.926% to 7.776%. Notably, scanning electron microscope (SEM) observations reveal that abundant micropores, mesopores and distinct fractures are developed inside the rock, endowing it with certain seepage capacity. Under the condition of narrow strip mining, the maximum failure depth of the floor is approximately 5.74 m, with shear failure as the main failure mode. The rock strata within the range of -6.5 m to -15.5 m below the floor are basically unaffected by mining activities and exhibit favorable water-resisting performance. Therefore, based on the floor failure depth, water-resisting performance, and pore structure characteristics, the grouting reconstruction horizon is arranged within the quartz sandstone layer. Finally, grouting reconstruction of the target horizon was carried out using underground directional long boreholes, which significantly improved the apparent resistivity of the rock mass. This confirms that the continuous grouting technology can effectively seal fractures and pores and enhance the performance of the target aquifuge. The research results provide a new approach for the prevention and control of water hazards in mine floors under similar conditions.Keywords: Floor, Confined water, Failure characteristics, Water abundance, Pore-fracture structure, Grouting reconstructionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Floor water inrush is an engineering geological hazard in which pressurized water from underlying aquifers, disturbed by mining activities, breaches the floor aquiclude and enters the working face in sudden, gradual, or delayed forms, leading to increased mine water inflow or even mine flooding1. As mining depth increases, the threat of high-pressure confined water becomes increasingly severe2. For example, mining in the Carboniferous–Permian coalfield of the Weibei mining area in Shaanxi, China, is significantly affected by the Ordovician limestone aquifer, which severely constrains the safe and efficient production of coal3. Therefore, studying the failure mechanisms of the floor under confined aquifers and proposing effective sealing and treatment measures are of great significance for preventing floor water inrush and ensuring the safe and sustainable mining of coal resources.

Currently, a relatively comprehensive theoretical and technical system has been established for the research on the mechanism and prevention of coal seam floor water inrush. Regarding the floor failure mechanism, classical mechanics theories have laid the foundation for understanding the mechanism. For example, the limit equilibrium theory has been innovatively applied to analyze floor stability, revealing the mechanism by which the redistribution of mining-induced stress induces floor shear failure4,5. The “lower four-zone” theory6, established based on damage mechanics, has uncovered the amplification effect of initial floor damage variables on mining-induced failure and their influence on failure depth, breaking through the limitation of the traditional “intact floor” assumption. With the development of science and technology, numerical simulation technology has played a key role in revealing the spatiotemporal evolution law of floor failure. The discrete element method7,8 and the continuous-discrete coupling method9 can reproduce the initiation, propagation, and penetration processes of floor fractures during mining, accurately revealing the spatial distribution characteristic of the “inverted saddle-shaped” failure zone. The finite element method has disclosed the penetration mechanism of water-conducting channels under different geological conditions10. Research has generally confirmed that the four corners of the goaf are stress concentration areas with the maximum failure depth, where water inrush channels are prone to form11. Physical simulation experiments and on-site monitoring have provided empirical support for mechanism research. Physical simulation can intuitively reveal the development law of floor fracture networks under the action of high stress and unloading effects12. Meanwhile, on-site monitoring technologies such as microseismic monitoring13 and electrical prospecting14 can realize the real-time capture of rock mass failure and changes in water abundance, providing dynamic data support for the risk assessment of floor water inrush.

In the field of prevention and control technologies, grouting reinforcement technology remains the dominant direction, with its core advantage lying in the effective sealing of water-conducting channels15. Research trends are shifting from “local sealing” to “regional reconstruction” and from “empirical judgment” to “precision control”. The study of grouting mechanism is fundamental to technical optimization. Zhang et al. established a critical grouting pressure model to reveal the diffusion and consolidation mechanisms of grout in fractured rock masses, providing a theoretical basis for the design of grouting parameters16. Meanwhile, innovations in grouting processes have significantly improved floor treatment effectiveness. Among these, technologies such as surface directional long-borehole multi-stage grouting17 and underground hole-cleaning secondary grouting18 have achieved precise reconstruction of aquifers or key geological structures. Collaborative governance strategies may become a future development direction. For instance, combining green mining technologies such as the Non-Pillar Mining with Automatically Formed Roadway (NMAFR) technique19 and the Pre-Cutting Roof (PCR) technique20,21 with grouting reinforcement technology can reduce the depth of mining-induced floor damage at the source, forming a collaborative prevention and control system of “source damage reduction and terminal reinforcement”. In addition, the quantitative risk assessment model for floor failure based on the Rock Engineering System (RES)22 and the new method for waterproof coal pillar design23 have also provided scientific decision-making tools for the engineering design and risk management of floor water inrush prevention and control.

However, the aforementioned studies are limited to analyzing floor failure depth and water inrush risks under conventional longwall mining conditions, with no investigation into narrow-strip mining. While water inrush in conventional longwall faces is dominated by “areal seepage” resulting from large-scale pressure relief and regional fracture networks, in narrow working faces it is primarily governed by “point-type penetration” controlled by localized faults or fractures. The “areal seepage” in conventional longwall mining represents systematic seepage evolution under large-scale pressure relief, whereas the “point-type penetration” in narrow mining essentially stems from localized failure under high-stress gradients. Prevention and control of the former requires “regional regulation,” while the latter focuses on “precision sealing.24”.

Furthermore, the grouting reinforcement methods proposed in existing studies predominantly involve surface directional long-hole grouting or underground short-hole grouting, with underground directional long-hole grouting remaining largely unexplored. Compared to traditional short-hole grouting, the “underground directional long-hole continuous grouting technology” introduced in this study demonstrates significant advantages, including an approximately tenfold increase in grouting radius, over 15% improvement in fracture sealing rate, 40–50% reduction in borehole length, and a roughly threefold reduction in single-face construction cycle, highlighting its clear technical superiority. Moreover, while previous grouting efforts have primarily targeted aquifers, research on grouting applications for aquicludes remains scarce.

Therefore, this study focuses on narrow-strip mining under confined Ordovician limestone karst water conditions in a mine in the Weibei Mining Area of western China. Laboratory experiments were conducted to analyze the permeability and pore structure of the floor strata. A comprehensive approach combining theoretical analysis, field monitoring, and numerical simulation was employed to investigate the failure mechanisms and depth of the working face floor. The parallel network distributed dynamic monitoring electrical method was applied for in-situ assessment of floor water abundance. Based on these findings, appropriate target layers for grouting modification were determined. Finally, the effectiveness of floor aquiclude performance and reinforcement before and after directional long-hole continuous grouting was evaluated and cross-validated using transient electromagnetic method and drilling, thereby ensuring safe and environmentally sustainable mining of the working face.

Overview of the study mine and its hydrogeological conditions

The mine is situated in the central Weibei mining area of the Carboniferous-Permian coalfield, northern Shaanxi Province, China, approximately 3 km northeast of Chengcheng County (Fig. 1a). Its mining area covers approximately 14.6969 km². The ground surface is predominantly covered by loess, with relatively flat topography. The region is characterized by low annual precipitation and high evaporation.

Mine location and working face borehole histogram (Auto CAD 2021 https://www.autodesk.com.cn/).

The mine has a designed production capacity of 1.2 Mt/a and officially commenced production in December 2012. It adopts a single-level development system with main and auxiliary vertical shafts and a central parallel ventilation system. The primary target is the No. 5 coal seam, with a burial depth ranging from 293.65 m to 552.44 m. The recoverable coal thickness varies from 0.95 m to 6.14 m, averaging 3.1 m. The seam inclination is between 3° and 6°, and the lateral pressure coefficient ranges from 1.0 to 2.5. Due to the extensive problem of pressurized mining under Ordovician limestone karst water in the No. 5 coal seam, the narrow strip mining method was selected. Mining panels are arranged along the strike of the coal seam, with alternate extraction and retention of strips. The working face has a dip length of 35 m, and the intermediate coal pillars are 40 m wide. This method employs longwall mining equipment and techniques, and its most significant feature is the use of narrow working faces combined with wide coal pillars to control rock movement. It effectively limits floor failure depth and surface subsidence. Suitable for mining under confined aquifers and in “three-soft” coal seams under complex geological conditions, it offers advantages such as controllable water inrush risk, high stability of coal pillars, minimal disturbance to aquifers, and applicability to structurally complex areas. The mining technology utilizes fully mechanized mining to extract the full seam height in a single pass, with the complete caving method applied to manage the roof.

The Yangzhuang normal fault in the eastern part of the mine and the Xianxihe normal fault in the northwest serve as the boundary faults of the coal mine. These two faults do not establish hydraulic connections between the aquifers inside the mine and those outside the mining area, thus functioning as aquiclude boundaries. The southwestern and northeastern boundaries are artificially defined. The mine is located within the Weibei Ordovician limestone karst water system. The working face is overlaid by multiple sandstone aquifers, while the underlying strata consist of the K2 limestone and the Ordovician limestone karst aquifer (Fig. 1b). The water inrush coefficient of the Ordovician limestone karst aquifer ranges from 0.004 to 0.015 MPa/m, with a permeability coefficient of 0.01987 m/d and a specific capacity of 0.00319–1.490 L/(s·m). The hydraulic pressure at the roof interface is 0.26–0.38 MPa, making it the most critical source of floor water inrush threatening the extraction of the No. 5 coal seam. In the local structurally weak zones of the floor, such as concealed faults and densely fractured zones, there exists a risk of water inrush.

Since the mine was constructed, there have been two incidents of abnormal Ordovician limestone karst water inflow. The first water inflow occurred when a fault was exposed during the mining process. The initial observed inflow rate was 2.5 m3/h, which subsequently increased to approximately 279.19 m3/h and eventually stabilized at 130 m3/h. The second water inflow was from the coal wall during mining operations, with an inflow rate reaching 90 m3/h, caused by the connection of water-conducting fractures from an adjacent working face.

Plastic theory formula

The plastic slip line field theory is commonly used in calculating the floor failure depth. Specifically, when the abutment pressure in front of the coal wall exceeds the strength limit of the floor rock mass, the floor rock mass will undergo deformation and failure, thereby generating plastic slip surfaces (Fig. 5). At this stage, the floor failure depth reaches its maximum value27. The plastic failure zone in the floor can be divided into three parts, namely the active zone (OAB), the transition zone (OBC), and the passive zone (OCD).

Floor failure depth based on field measurement

Monitoring scheme

To investigate the failure depth and characteristics of the floor in the TC05 strip mining face, boreholes ZK-1 and ZK-2 were drilled in the TC03 roadway at a location approximately 150 m from the open-off cut. Borehole water pressure tests were conducted to determine the failure depth. The boreholes were spaced 1 m apart, with a dip angle of 7°, an azimuth angle of 0°, and a diameter of 89 mm. The designed depths were 70 m and 65 m, respectively, and the water pressure test sections comprised 40 and 29 segments. Simultaneously, borehole ZK-3 was drilled in the hoist chamber of the TC03 roadway, approximately 675 m from the open-off cut. Pre-mining water pressure tests were performed 100 m ahead of the mining face, and after completion, the borehole was utilized as a post-mining strain monitoring hole. This borehole had a dip angle of 7°, an azimuth angle of 0°, a diameter of 70 mm, a designed depth of 70 m, and comprised 31 water pressure test segments. The data obtained from these boreholes can be cross-validated to enhance the accuracy of floor failure depth measurements. A schematic diagram of the specific layout for boreholes ZK-1, ZK-2, and ZK-3 is shown in Fig. 6.

Borehole layout diagram.

Borehole leakage monitoring

Taking the TC05 working face as the measurement target, water pressure tests were conducted using an improved double-end packer leakage detection device. The failure depth of the floor was determined by monitoring changes in water inflow in the boreholes. Based on field measurement data, curves were plotted comparing the post-mining water leakage rates of boreholes ZK-1 and ZK-2 with the pre-mining water leakage rate of borehole ZK-3, as shown in Fig. 7. The figure indicates that the pre-mining leakage rate in borehole ZK-3 was relatively low, suggesting that the original fractures in the floor were not well developed. After the working face was mined, continuous leakage sections with significantly higher leakage rates were observed in boreholes ZK-1 and ZK-2. The maximum leakage rates recorded were 2.963 L·min− 1 and 2.135 L·min− 1, with the corresponding vertical height ranges of the continuous leakage sections being 4.07–5.66 m and 4.74–5.61 m, respectively. Thus, the maximum failure depth of the floor, as determined by the water pressure tests, was 5.66 m.

Strain measurement

To further verify the failure depth of the floor, micro-strain testing was employed to conduct in-situ measurements of the deformation in borehole ZK-3 after mining. This method is not limited by the lithology of the borehole and offers high monitoring accuracy, overcoming the limitations of traditional methods that require comparative testing before and after mining28. The coal seam dip angle is 3.4°, and the hoist chamber has a depth of 1.5 m and a height of 0.5 m. A schematic profile of the borehole and sensor layout is shown in Fig. 8.

Numerical simulation of floor failure characteristics

Model establishment

The TC05 working face was selected as the research object, and its floor rock mass failure characteristics during mining were simulated using the FLAC3D software. Given the general elastic behavior with localized plastic yield failure observed in the mine’s rock mass at a large scale, the Mohr-Coulomb constitutive model was adopted. Based on the actual lithology from borehole columns near the TC05 working face and laboratory rock mechanics tests, the roof and floor strata were classified into 24 material layers according to their engineering geological similarities. The thickness and mechanical parameters of each layer are detailed in Table 3. Considering the actual geological conditions of the TC05 working face, the lateral pressure coefficient was set to 1.1, and the coal seam thickness was 3.1 m. The working face has a burial depth of 350.1 m and a dip width of 35 m. The water pressure at the top of the Ordovician limestone aquifer ranges from 0.26 to 0.38 MPa. The model dimensions are 220 m in the strike direction (X-axis), 156 m in the dip direction (Y-axis), and 87 m in height (Z-axis), as shown in Fig. 10. In the model, displacement boundary conditions were applied to the faces perpendicular to the X- and Y-axes and to the bottom face (Z-axis), with roller supports prescribed. The top face was assigned a stress boundary condition. The in-situ stress from the overlying strata acts on the top surface, applying a vertical load equivalent to the gravitational weight of the rock mass extending to the ground surface.

On the X = 141 m slice, the floor is dominated by shear failure, with a maximum failure depth of 4.0 m. Overall, the maximum floor failure depth is located in the sandy mudstone below the return airway on the X = 113 m section, reaching 5.3 m.

Failure characteristics along the dip and strike directions of the working Face

During the mining of the strip working face, the surrounding rock stress redistributes. Due to mining disturbance, the coal masses on both sides along the strike and dip directions of the working face generate abutment pressure. As the working face advances to a certain distance, the immediate roof collapses, filling the goaf. The main roof fractures at the elastoplastic junction at the goaf edge, forming key rock blocks. These blocks rotate and sink into the goaf at a certain angle, creating an articulated structure with adjacent rock blocks. During periodic weighting, the collapse morphology and stress distribution of the stope roof under confined water conditions along the dip and strike directions are illustrated in Fig. 12. Zone Ⅰ is the stress relief zone, Zone Ⅱ is the stress concentration zone, and Zone Ⅲ is the in-situ stress zone.

Stress and zoning diagram of stope floor above confined water: (a) dip direction; (b) strike direction.

Based on the theory of mine pressure and rock strata control, and without considering horizontal stress, the stope floor is subjected to the combined action of abutment pressure, caved gangue, and Ordovician limestone confined water. Under these conditions, the floor strata can be divided into the “lower three zones”29: the failure zone, the protection zone, and the confined water-conducting zone (Fig. 12). The failure zone refers to the spatial distribution of water-conducting fractures formed in the floor strata under abutment pressure. Since the stress state in the central part of the goaf transitions from “high concentrated stress” to “low unloading stress,” and the supporting effect of collapsed gangue in the goaf partially offsets mining-induced disturbances, the depth of plastic failure in the floor strata at the center of the goaf is reduced accordingly. The protection zone represents the thickness of strata that maintains its pre-mining integrity and original water-resisting capacity. The confined water-conducting zone is the upward migration range of confined water within the water-resisting strata above the Ordovician limestone aquifer. Due to the inhomogeneous development of fractures, the boundaries of these zones are irregular. It is noteworthy that in structural zones or areas with fractured floor strata, the variation in water inflow should be monitored to prevent the occurrence of “point-type penetration” water inrush.

Floor water-richness and grouting layer selection

In practical engineering, the use of geophysical monitoring to invert the water abundance of the stope floor remains the most direct and accurate research method30. Therefore, the parallel network distributed dynamic monitoring electrical method was adopted to conduct on-site measurement and analysis of the water abundance in the protective zone and the confined water conducting zone of the TC05 working face. The acquired data will lay a solid foundation for determining the target grouting layers.

Monitoring system and scheme

This detection enables three-dimensional full-space resistivity inversion of the floor using the parallel network distributed dynamic monitoring electrical method. Specifically, receiving electrodes are arranged in one roadway, while power is supplied for excitation from another roadway. Through observing the variation law of electrical parameters of the floor below the stope, the water-rich condition of the floor can be determined. The monitoring system is mainly composed of an electrical method host, electrical method base stations, dual-mode electrodes, and cables (Fig. 13). As shown in Fig. 13, the measurement point 470 m away from the open-off cut in the TC07 return airway was selected as the coordinate origin. Electrical method main cables were laid in the TC05 return airway and TC07 return airway respectively. A certain number of electrodes, numbered from L1 to L61, were arranged 20 cm away from the coal pillar along one side of the roadway, with an electrode spacing of 5 m and a burial hole depth of 1.2 m. Trenches were excavated around the holes, with a width and depth of 150 mm × 600 mm, for laying the cables of each electrode. After the electrodes were inserted into the holes, the holes were backfilled with loess and salt, and the trenches were refilled with crushed slag. After the above steps were completed, a network-based test was conducted to verify the geophysical exploration results. The first test location was 330 m away from the origin of the monitoring range, and the remaining monitoring locations were 280 m, 230 m, 180 m, 130 m, and 30 m away from the origin respectively. The vertical test range spanned from − 6.5 m to -40.5 m below the bottom of the coal seam. The interval between the first and second slices was 4 m, while the interval between the subsequent slices was 5 m.

Results analysis

Different types of rocks and water-bearing strata exhibit distinct resistivity values, thus presenting different colors on contour maps. Dark-colored areas typically indicate low resistivity, suggesting that the rock strata have high water content, high content of electrically conductive minerals, and may contain organic matter-enriched zones. Light-colored areas generally represent high resistivity, indicating that the rock strata are relatively dry, possess good electrical insulation properties, and are dense with low porosity. Through three-dimensional comparative analysis of pseudo-section maps, combined with the hydrogeological data of the mining area and reference to previous exploration experience, an apparent resistivity of less than 10 Ω·m was adopted as the threshold for identifying hydrogeological anomalies.

As shown in Figs. 14 and 15, the geophysical exploration results of each measuring point are presented when the TC05 working face advances to different positions. When the measuring point is at 330 m, the working face advanced 140 m, and coal mining activities have not yet entered the monitoring range at this stage. Within the range of -6.5 m to -10.5 m in the floor, there is no significant low-resistivity anomaly zone, and no signs of water activity have been detected. This indicates that the rock strata in this range are dense, dry, and stable with good structural integrity, capable of forming an effective aquifuge. Within the range of -15.5 m to -35.5 m, fracture development is extremely uneven, and the strata contain a relatively high content of soluble minerals. Consequently, the resistivity is low in areas adjacent to fractures and karst zones, while it is high in regions with underdeveloped fractures. Multiple low-resistivity anomaly zones exist beneath the left end of the working face and the middle part of the coal pillar. This suggests that water-bearing fractures are well-developed in these areas, and two distinct vertical water-conducting channels may have formed — a relationship inferred to be linked to the high water yield of the limestone. At this time, the water-conducting channels within the range of -20.5 m to -30.5 m beneath the coal pillar are suspected to be confined water ascending channels. The depth of -40.5 m is located in the Ordovician limestone, where no significant low-resistivity anomaly zone is observed, indicating that the rock strata at this position have moderate water abundance and a relatively stable structure.

When the measuring point is located at 280 m, the working face advanced 190 m, and coal mining activities have entered the right end of the monitoring range. Within the range of -6.5 m to -10.5 m, a small-scale low-resistivity anomaly zone is observed beneath the working face. No obvious low-resistivity anomaly zones are detected in other areas, where the resistivity is relatively high and uniformly distributed. This indicates that the rock strata in this range have a stable structure and low water content. Within the range of -15.5 m to -40.5 m, the resistivity beneath the working face and the adjacent coal pillars is low, forming a large-scale low-resistivity anomaly zone. This suggests the accumulation of fractured water or the presence of aquifers, which may form potential confined water-conducting channels connected to the Ordovician limestone. However, these channels have not broken through the rock strata at the upper − 10.5 m horizon, implying that there remains an effective aquifuge of a certain thickness beneath the floor.

When the measuring point is at 230 m, the working face advanced 240 m. Within the range of -6.5 m to -35.5 m, affected by coal mining activities and periodic weighting, a large-scale low-resistivity anomaly zone appears beneath the TC05 return airway and the lower left of the working face. This indicates that fractured water or aquifers may exist in this range, which may give rise to multiple potential water-conducting channels and increase hydrogeological risks. In the Ordovician limestone at -40.5 m, the resistivity is relatively high, the structure is relatively stable, and there is no significant hydrogeological risk.

When the measuring point is located at 180 m, the working face advanced 290 m. Within the ranges of -6.5 m to -15.5 m and − 25.5 m to -40.5 m, no obvious low-resistivity anomaly zones are detected, indicating that the rock strata are dense and structurally stable, with no aquifers or fractured water present, and the hydrogeological risk is relatively low. Within the sandy mudstone at -20.5 m, three low-resistivity anomaly zones exist, suggesting that these areas may contain fractured water or aquifers with strong water abundance.

When the measuring point is at 130 m, the working face advanced 340 m, and coal mining activities have entered the middle of the monitoring range. Within the range of -6.5 m to -10.5 m, no obvious low-resistivity anomaly zones are observed in the rock strata, which maintain good water insulation capacity, resulting in high mining safety. Within the range of -15.5 m to -35.5 m, multiple low-resistivity anomaly zones appear beneath the working face coal wall and the right end of the coal pillar. The confined water in these anomaly zones has been activated by the mining disturbance of the working face, forming two distinct vertical water-conducting channels and leading to the ascent of confined water. In the Ordovician limestone at -40.5 m, the resistivity is relatively high, and the water abundance is weak.

When the measuring point is at 30 m, the working face advanced 440 m, the monitoring is drawing to a close, and all expected data have been collected. At this stage, except for one water-rich anomaly zone located at the − 35.5 m horizon directly beneath the working face coal wall, no confined water ascent or water-rich anomaly zones are observed in other areas and horizons. In particular, the rock strata beneath the goaf exhibit generally high resistivity, and the water-rich anomaly zones generated during the advancement have basically disappeared. This indicates that the rock strata between − 6.5 m and the top interface of the Ordovician limestone possess good water insulation properties.

In summary, within the depth range of -6.5 m to -15.5 m, the rock strata are basically unaffected by mining disturbance. During the mining process, the floor maintains good integrity and favorable water-resisting properties. Within the depth range of -15.5 m to -30.5 m, local water-rich anomaly zones occasionally occur in the rock strata, while the overall trend of resistivity variation remains relatively slight. This indicates that the overall ascending height of the Ordovician limestone confined water has not breached the bauxite mudstone. Locally, however, the height of the confined water ascending zone exceeds the bauxite mudstone, which causes Ordovician limestone water to recharge the K2 limestone aquifer and thus leads to the formation of water-rich anomaly zones in the K2 aquifer. These anomaly zones diminish rapidly, suggesting that the ascending channels are mainly dominated by fractures and joints, which are characterized by small sizes and limited recharge capacity. Additionally, the bauxite mudstone contains abundant clay minerals such as kaolinite and montmorillonite. Such minerals swell upon contact with water, which can seal the ascending channels of Ordovician limestone water and thereby restore the excellent water-resisting capacity of the rock strata. For the rock strata below − 30.5 m, the overall resistivity is relatively high, indicating that the top of the Ordovician limestone within the monitoring area has weak water abundance.

Determination of target grouting layer

In general, the selection of grouting horizons should follow these fundamental principles: (1) The target horizon should lie beyond the range of mining-induced disturbance; (2) The horizon must be injectable; (3) Economic and safety factors must be considered.

Based on the aforementioned research results, the maximum failure depth of the floor under narrow strip mining with confined water pressure was determined through theoretical calculations, on-site measurements, and numerical simulation analysis, with the results being 5.74 m, 5.66, 5.27 m, and 5.30 m respectively. Therefore, it is inferred that the maximum development depth of the floor failure zone in the areas of the mine unaffected by fault structures is 5.74 m. However, relevant studies have shown that the maximum development depth of the floor failure zone in fault-affected areas is approximately twice that of the normal areas31. Thus, according to the principles for selecting grouting horizons and the above research, the grouting horizon of the mine should be determined at approximately 11.48 m below the coal seam. This horizon is situated within the quartz sandstone overlying the K2 limestone aquifer, which exhibits a certain degree of water permeability.

Field application and performance of floor grouting reinforcement

Hydrogeological conditions of working face

The test working face TC12 is adjacent to the TC10 working face to the north and the TC07 working face to the east. It has a strike length of 1932.51 m and a dip width of 35 m. The underlying aquifers are the K2 limestone aquifer and the Ordovician limestone karst aquifer. The K2 limestone aquifer is 16.38 m below the No. 5 Coal Seam, with a unit water inflow of 0.000355 L/(s·m) and a permeability coefficient of 0.00146 m/d. The Ordovician limestone karst aquifer is 30.98 m below the No. 5 Coal Seam, and the overlying bauxitic mudstone has a thickness of 7.85 m, indicating mining under confined water pressure conditions. The maximum water inrush coefficient of the K2 limestone aquifer is 0.056 MPa/m, and that of the Ordovician limestone karst aquifer is 0.015 MPa/m. Other geological and mining conditions are basically consistent with those of the TC05 working face.

Drilling design

A drill site was arranged 1030 m inward from the open-off cut of the TC12 return airway. Transient Electromagnetic Method (TEM) and drilling were mutually verified to analyze the water-resisting performance of the floor before and after grouting reconstruction, thereby ensuring the safety of the working face during mining. A total of 2 directional boreholes (1# and 2#) were drilled at the drill site, with their parameters listed in Table 4. The actual completed depth of both boreholes was 306 m, with controlled vertical depths of -11.07 m and − 10.61 m, and controlled rib distances of 29.98 m and 29.11 m respectively (Fig. 16). After the boreholes were drilled to the designed positions, water-stop casings were run and cemented as required, with the casing length exceeding 5 m. No obvious geological structures were encountered during the borehole construction. Until the start of grouting, water continuously flowed out of the boreholes, with a water yield of less than 1 m³/h. The water was clear and odorless.

Grouting scheme

After completing the detection of hydrogeological conditions (e.g., water abundance, structural development, and water inflow) of the quartz sandstone in the floor, grouting reconstruction was conducted on the quartz sandstone via the detection boreholes to enhance its water-resisting performance. The area surrounding the boreholes is basically unaffected by mining disturbance, so the probability of roadway heave induced by grouting is extremely low. In accordance with the Coal Mine Water Prevention and Control Rules, the designed grouting pressure is 2.8 MPa, and the maximum grouting pressure shall not exceed 2–3 times the designed value. The grouting material is 42.5# ordinary Portland cement, with a water-cement ratio ranging from 2.5:1 to 1.5:1. The grouting pump model is 2ZBYSB60/33-18.5, and a pneumatic mixing tank is used for slurry preparation. The specific grouting system is illustrated in Fig. 17. The grouting flow rate is determined based on the fracture development of the grouting horizon and pump pressure: the full pump rate (250 L/min) is adopted when the pump pressure is below 5 MPa; the medium pump rate is utilized when the pump pressure exceeds 5.5 MPa; and the small pump rate (60 L/min) is employed when the pressure reaches or approaches the designed final hole pressure. A total of 31 tons of cement grout was injected into 1# borehole and 27 tons into 2# borehole. The specific grouting volume, water-cement ratio, and final pressure for each work shift are presented in Fig. 18.

Inspection of grouting effect

To verify the grouting treatment effect of the TC12 working face, the Transient Electromagnetic Method (TEM) was employed to conduct comprehensive and multi-angle detection on the floor grouting treatment area. Based on the detection results, the electrical property changes of the floor before and after grouting were analyzed to further evaluate the treatment effect. This method offers advantages including high sensitivity to water-rich zones, flexible detection directions, and excellent lateral resolution32. The detection equipment used is the YCS360A mine multi-channel transient electromagnetic instrument. Three detection points (M1, M2, M3) were arranged on the sidewall of the TC12 return airway, with 13 survey lines deployed at each point to form a complete fan-shaped detection layout. The arrangement angles of the survey lines are 0°, 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 75°, 90°, 105°, 120°, 135°, 150°, 165°, and 180° (Fig. 19a). In addition, six measurement angles were set in the floor direction at each detection point, namely 5°, 10°, 15°, 20°, 25°, and 30° (Fig. 19b).

Measuring line and its measuring angle diagram.

Through the lateral comparative analysis of cross-sectional profiles and with reference to previous exploration experience, an apparent resistivity of less than 10 Ω·m was determined as the threshold for identifying hydrogeological anomalies. If the apparent resistivity values identified before treatment are significantly lower than 10 Ω·m and have a large distribution range, it may be attributed to the presence of water bodies or water-bearing fractures in the strata, which is classified as a strong water-rich zone with potential water inrush risks. Areas with relatively high apparent resistivity (> 10 Ω·m) and no structural defects are designated as relatively weak water-rich zones or water-free zones.

When the inclined distance exceeds 25 m, the rock stratum resistivity gradually decreases, with two low-resistivity anomaly zones (YC1 and YC2) below 10 Ω·m identified, indicating the potential presence of fracture water-bearing zones or aquifers in this region. With the increase in inclined distance, multiple low-resistivity areas emerged, along with one low-resistivity anomaly zone (YC3). In addition, two other low-resistivity anomaly zones (YC14 and YC16) were identified in the quartz sandstone and its underlying rock strata, which may result from aquifers or complex geological structures. At this stage, the water in the aquifers was displaced to the deep part, leaving only several small-scale low-resistivity anomaly zones (YC17, YC18, YC19, YC20) in the deep area. This indicates that the grout effectively filled defects such as fractures and pores, thereby significantly enhancing the water-resisting performance of the quartz sandstone.

After grouting, the resistivity of the shallow rock strata increased significantly, while densely distributed low-resistivity anomaly zones (YC28, YC29, YC30, YC31, YC32) still persisted in the deep rock strata. These zones were densely distributed over a wide range on the right side of the monitoring area, with YC35 essentially penetrating all rock strata overlying the Ordovician limestone water, providing a potential channel for water inrush. After grouting, the resistivity of the shallow rock strata increased significantly, while low-resistivity anomaly zones remained densely distributed in the deep rock strata (YC38, YC39, YC40, YC41, YC42, YC43), indicating that water-bearing bodies are widely distributed in the deep rock strata. Nevertheless, the overall grouting effect on the aquifuge was remarkable, with its water-resisting performance greatly improved.

The detection results above demonstrate that the resistivity of the quartz sandstone increases markedly after grouting. Profiles with different inclination angles reveal consistent trends, indicating uniform diffusion of the grout. This has significantly enhanced the overall water-resisting performance of the floor, effectively preventing the occurrence of “point-type penetration” water hazards.

Conclusion

Through the systematic research on the floor failure mechanism and grouting reinforcement technology of narrow strip working faces in the Weibei Mining Area, the main conclusions of this paper are drawn as follows:

The quartz sandstone in the floor exhibits suitable porosity (ranging from 1.926% to 7.776%) and permeability (0.033–0.059 mD). Moreover, the presence of numerous micropores and fractures on its surface indicates favorable injectability. Theoretical calculations further reveal that the maximum floor failure depth in narrow strip mining is 5.74 m, which is significantly less than that in conventional longwall mining. This result can provide critical parameters for selecting the grouting horizon.

The successful application of the underground directional long-hole grouting reinforcement technology for the floor aquiclude demonstrates the feasibility of constructing underground directional long boreholes and performing grouting in the aquiclude. After grouting, the apparent resistivity of the target quartz sandstone horizon and the overlying rock strata increased significantly, and the original low-resistivity anomaly zones were reduced or eliminated. This confirms that the cement slurry effectively filled the fractures and pores in the quartz sandstone, substantially enhancing the waterproof performance of the floor and effectively mitigating the risk of “point-type penetration” water inrush in narrow strip mining faces. The aquiclude grouting reconstruction technology proposed in this study supplements traditional methods for controlling floor water hazards in confined water pressure mining.
