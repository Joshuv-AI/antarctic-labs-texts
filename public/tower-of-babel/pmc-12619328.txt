The relationship between stroke-related sarcopenia and 3-month neurological outcome in stroke patients: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Aiguo Zhang, Sunwen You, Jianning Xia, Xiangzhi Shao, Xinyu Pu, Jiacheng Zhang, Xinming Wu, Jinyihui Zheng, Dengchong Wu, Bing Chen, Lielie Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia was initially defined as an age-related decline in muscle mass and function. As sarcopenia has been observed at any age and secondary to many diseases, extending the classical meaning of sarcopenia beyond the age-related phenomenon to the ‘disease-related’ sarcopenia has been debated recently. Stroke-related sarcopenia occurs secondary to the brain injury and is age-independent, potentially worsening patients’ neurological outcomes. We aimed to explore the relationship between stroke-related sarcopenia and 3-month neurological outcome in stroke patients, as well as to evaluate the predictive value of the two main indicators of stroke-related sarcopenia—muscle mass and muscle strength—for adverse neurological outcomes.MethodsA single-centre prospective cohort study of 228 individuals with ischaemic stroke who were admitted for routine rehabilitation treatment, followed up for neurological outcome for 3 months following the onset of the stroke. The muscle mass was expressed by the ratio of appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) to height (Ht) (ASM/Ht2), and the muscle strength was assessed by the grip strength. The neurological outcome was assessed using modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores three months after the stroke. Statistical analyses, including correlation coefficient tests, multivariate regression analyses, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, were applied in this study.ResultsThe prevalence of sarcopenia was 28.9% in all included patients. Compared with the good outcome group, the poor outcome group showed a significantly lower level of ASM/Ht2, grip strength, and a higher level of mRS scores (all P < 0.05). Both ASM/Ht2 and grip strength were associated with mRS scores (r = -0.549 and − 0.592, respectively; both P < 0.01). Both ASM/Ht2 and grip strength were independent protective factors for the neurological outcome (OR = 0.525 [0.399–0.690] and 0.684 [0.605–0.773], respectively; both P < 0.01). The AUCs of grip strength and ASM/Ht2 were 0.884 and 0.833, respectively. The optimal cutoff values for grip strength to predict neurological outcome in the total patients, the male patients, and the female patients were 18.66, 26.69, and 18.66, respectively, with sensitivities of 79.10%, 90.48%, and 95.65% and specificities of 90.68%, 72.04%, and 77.94%, respectively. The optimal cutoff values for ASM/Ht2 to predict neurological outcome in the total population, the male patients, and the female patients were 6.01, 6.19, and 6.01, respectively, with sensitivities of 85.07%, 71.43%, and 93.48% and specificities of 73.91%, 86.02%, and 54.41%, respectively.ConclusionsStroke-related sarcopenia is closely related to worse neurological function at the convalescence stage. Higher ASM/Ht2 and grip strength at baseline were independent protective factors for neurological outcomes. ASM/Ht2 and grip strength could be of superior predictive value for neurological outcomes, with different weights and gender differences in their predictive efficacy.

Background

Stroke is the second leading cause of death in the world [1]. With over 2 million new cases annually, stroke has been the leading cause of death in China during the past two decades [2, 3]. With the highest disability rate in adults, stroke affects the quality of life of patients and brings a heavy burden to their families and the public health system [4].

Sarcopenia was first proposed by Rosenberg in 1989 [5], and it was defined as an age-related, progressive, and systemic decline in muscle mass and function according to the International Working Group on Sarcopenia [6]. Viewed from an etiologic perspective, sarcopenia is of multifactorial origins such as altered endocrine function, immobilization, malnutrition, insulin resistance, denervation, and inflammation [7]. These conditions could also be observed at any age, as well as being secondary to quite a few diseases [8]. Therefore, extending the classical meaning of sarcopenia beyond the age-related phenomenon to ‘disease-related’ sarcopenia has always been debated [9].

Adaptive changes in muscle tissue structure, including a decrease in the number of motor units, can occur at 4 h after the onset of stroke [10], and reducing muscle mass can even last for 6 months after stroke [11]. The changes in muscle tissue following a stroke were bilateral and could not be fully explained by cortical defects [12]. Such features distinguish sarcopenia after a stroke from sarcopenia observed in aging, which is known as stroke-related sarcopenia [7, 13]. Moreover, reduced physical activity following stroke - due to hemiplegia, prolonged bed rest, malnutrition, and secondary osteoporosis - further accelerates muscle atrophy, contributing to a vicious cycle of declining physical function and worsening neurological status. Stroke-related sarcopenia occurs secondary to brain injury and may worsen patients’ neurological outcomes [8]. With a prevalence ranging from 14% to 52%, stroke-related sarcopenia appears to have a notable influence on patients’ quality of life and clinical prognosis. Accordingly, it may be increasingly important to consider its role in the development of personalized treatment strategies and comprehensive outcome assessments for stroke survivors [7].

However, few studies investigate the changes in systemic muscle mass after stroke [7, 8]. Most of these studies are currently small-sample surveys, and their findings are inconsistent or even contradictory [7, 13]. In particular, there is a lack of quantitative assessment for the possible impact of stroke-related sarcopenia on neurological function and outcome events after stroke. In this study, we explored the relationship between stroke-related sarcopenia and the 3-month neurological outcome for stroke patients and assessed the predictive value of the two main indicators of stroke-related sarcopenia—muscle mass and muscle strength—for adverse neurological outcomes.

Methods

Study design

A single-centre prospective cohort study was carried out at Wenzhou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine between November 2022 and January 2023. Finally, a total of 228 ischaemic stroke patients who were admitted to the hospital for routine rehabilitation were enrolled in the analysis. Demographic data, clinical data, and laboratory findings were collected at enrolment. The patients were evaluated at the end of the third month following the onset of stroke. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (No.WZY2022-KT-054-01).

Participants

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) met the diagnostic criteria for ischaemic stroke listed in the diagnostic criteria of cerebrovascular disease in China (version 2019) [14]; (3) patients admitted to our rehabilitation department within 2–4 weeks after the onset of stroke; (4) patients with a first unilateral infarction confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging or computerized tomography scans; and (5) patients with normal cognition. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients who died within 3 months after the onset of stroke; (2) patients with tumors, severe diseases, or unstable conditions; (3) patients with cognitive impairment or mental illness who failed to complete the assessment; (4) patients taking any medication that may affect muscle strength within 1 month; and (5) patients with bilateral limb disability. The criteria for elimination or drop-off are (1) aggravation or adverse events during follow-up; (2) failure or incomplete data; (3) interruption due to other unexpected reasons; and (4) loss to follow-up.

There were 355 consecutive ischaemic stroke patients recruited, and 253 eligible patients were enrolled using the aforementioned criteria; 17 patients were eliminated due to loss of follow-up, and 8 patients withdrew due to family reasons. Finally, a total of 228 patients were included in the analysis (114 males and 114 females), ranging in age from 60.1 to 85.6 years. All subjects were divided into two groups according to the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [15] scores, which were evaluated at the 3rd month after the onset of stroke. The patients with mRS ≥ 3 were assigned to the poor outcome group (67 cases), and the patients with mRS < 3 were assigned to the good outcome group (161 cases). The study workfow is shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection

The characteristics of demographic, medical historyand clinical variables, such as age, sex, height (Ht), body weight, smoking history, drinking history, comorbidities, the infarction type, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores [16], the baseline mRS scores, and serum albumin (Alb) level, were obtained as baseline data through the hospital medical record database or inquiry and measurement on admission. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (m2). According to the Oxfordshire Community Stroke Study (OSCP) [17], infarction was divided into total anterior circulation infarct (TACI), partial anterior circulation infarction (PACI), and posterior circulation infarction (POCI). After fasting for at least eight hours, serum samples were tested for serum Alb (g/L).

Assessment for sarcopenia

According to the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS 2019) consensus [6], the main indicators of stroke-related sarcopenia were evaluated on admission as follows: (1) Muscle mass: Muscle mass was determined by measuring appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) with a bioelectrical impedance analyzer (BIA) (InBody 720, Biospace, land Seoul, Korea) and adjusting for subject height. The corrected formula was ASM (kg) divided by the square of the height (m) (ASM/Ht2). (2) Muscle strength: Muscle strength was assessed by the uninjured side hand grip strength (kg) using the following procedure: the patient took the sitting position with the elbow flexed 90°, and the maximum value was taken after three measurements using a hand grip meter (GRIP-D; Takei Ltd., Niigata, Japan).

AWGS 2019 cutoffs for low muscle mass in sarcopenia diagnosis are as follows: < 7.0 kg/m2 in men and < 5.7 kg/m2 in women by BIA. And low muscle strength diagnostic cutoffs of handgrip are as follows: < 28.0 kg for men and < 18.0 kg for women. In the study, the patient with a stroke who meets the two standards above would be diagnosed with stroke-related sarcopenia.

Outcome measurements

The mRS scores: all the patients underwent mRS evaluation at the end of the third month after the onset of stroke, according to the following criteria: The mRS ranges from 0 to 6 points. 0 points: completely asymptomatic; 1 point: symptomatic but no obvious disability; can complete daily work and activities; 2 points: mild disability; unable to complete all daily work and activities but can handle personal affairs without help from others; 3 points: moderate disability, partial assistance in daily life, but assistance without walking; 4 points: severe disability, need for assistance in activities of daily living; cannot walk without assistance; 5 points: severe disability, incontinence, bedriddenness, and continuous care are required; 6 points: death. According to a previous study [18], an mRS of ≥ 3 was considered an indicator of a poor outcome, and a mRS of < 3 was considered an indicator of a good outcome, which was used as the basis for grouping in this study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 26.0. For continuous variables, normally distributed variables were described as means ± SDs while obviously skewed variables were expressed as the median (interquartile range, IQR). The proportion, or prevalence was used to describe categorical variables. The univariate analysis included a t-test or Pearson’s χ2-test, which was used to compare the mean or proportion between the two groups. The Mann-Whitney U test was performed for the comparison between the two groups of skewed variables. Spearman’s partial coefficient was applied to assess the correlations between the main indicators of stroke-related sarcopenia and mRS scores. Then, the significant variables (threshold value < 0.1) in univariate analysis were analyzed by multivariate stepwise logistic regression (backwards stepwise) to identify the significant variables affecting neurological outcomes. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. To evaluate the effect of variables in predicting neurological outcomes, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted with the area under the curve (AUC), and the optimal cut-off values were calculated. The sensitivity and specificity were used to demonstrate the predictive value of the main indicators of stroke-related sarcopenia. ROC curve analysis was performed in the methodology of DeLong et al. with MedCalc version 20.011. P < 0.05 was used to define statistically significant differences.

No statistically significant difference in age, BMI, the number of days from onset of stroke to hospitalization, the proportion of female, education, marital status, thrombolytic therapy, smoking history, drinking history, the distribution of comorbidities, and the distribution of infarct type were found between the two populations (all P > 0.05). The incidence of adverse outcomes in stroke-patients with or without sarcopenia and the distribution characteristics of sarcopenia cases

Overall, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 28.9% in all included patients. Patients with sarcopenia had a poor neurological outcome at a rate of 66.7% (44/66) compared to patients without sarcopenia who had a rate of only 14.2% (23/162); this difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, sarcopenia was found in 65.7% (44/67) of the poor outcome group and 13.7% (22/161) of the good outcome group (P < 0.05).

Spearman’s partial correlations between ASM/Ht2, grip strength, NIHSS scores, BMI, and mRS scores

In the total patients, after adjusting for age, smoking history, drinking history, the number of days from onset of stroke to hospitalization, annual family income, education, marital status, thrombolytic therapy, the baseline mRS scores, the distribution of comorbidities, serum Alb, and the distribution of infarct type, Spearman’s partial correlation analysis showed that both ASM/Ht2 and grip strength were still negatively correlated with mRS scores (r = -0.549 and -0.592, respectively; both P < 0.01), and NIHSS scores were positively associated with mRS scores (r = 0.520, P < 0.01). When stratified by gender, the correlation coefficients r between ASM/Ht2, grip strength, NIHSS scores, and mRS scores were -0.561,-0.657, and 0.509 in the male group, respectively (all P < 0.01), and -0.522,-0.673, and 0.476 in the female group, respectively (all P < 0.01). Logistic regression analyses for poor neurological outcomes

We used multivariate logistic regression (backwards stepwise) to examine the relationship between the main indices of sarcopenia and outcome after controlling for the significant variables in univariate analysis (age, sex, NIHSS scores, BMI, and the baseline mRS). After adjusting for the covariates, sarcopenia was positively associated with poor neurological function and was an independent risk factor for the poor neurological outcome (OR = 11.418 [4.810-27.103], P < 0.01). Meanwhile, both ASM/Ht2 and grip strength were found to be negatively correlated with poor neurological outcomes and to be independent protective factors (OR = 0.525 [0.399-0.690] and 0.684 [0.605-0.773], respectively; both P< 0.01).The details are presented in Table 3. Discussion

Stroke triggers a cascade of physiological changes—including neurogenic atrophy, immobilization, systemic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation—that collectively contribute to muscle loss and weakness. These alterations, irrespective of their individual contributions, manifest as measurable reductions in muscle mass and strength, which are core components of sarcopenia according to current consensus criteria[6–8]. This study aimed to evaluate whether sarcopenia, assessed using standardized, objective, and clinically feasible measures, could serve as a prognostic indicator of neurological recovery at three months post-stroke.

According to previous studies [7], muscle mass and muscle strength, as the two main indicators of sarcopenia, were adopted in the present study. The results showed that the stroke-related sarcopenia was closely related to neurological function at the convalescence stage. ASM/Ht2 and grip strength, as the two important evaluation indices in different dimensions of sarcopenia, both showed a negative relationship with poor neurological outcome and were independent protective factors for neurological function in the 3rd month since the onset of stroke. In addition, the predictive efficiency of ASM/Ht2 and grip strength for poor neurological outcomes was more powerful, compared with NIHSS scores, BMI, and Alb. The indicators of sarcopenia might serve as a promising potential predictor for the neurological outcome of stroke patients at the convalescence stage. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study regarding the directly predictive property of stroke-related sarcopenia for poor outcomes at the convalescence stage.

It has been 30 years since the concept of sarcopenia was put forward [19]. At present, the diagnosis of sarcopenia is still based on muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance indices [20, 21]. It is not clear to what extent the three indexes can be combined in a universal definition for various clinical outcome events [9, 22, 23]. In general, the patients with stroke were unable to complete the items of physical performance due to their neurological defects, so the other two indicators, muscle mass and muscle strength, were selected to quantify the degree of sarcopenia in this study. Such a method was also adopted in similar research [24]. In addition, some guidelines have shown that both the indicators have good reliability and validity to reflect outcomes [6, 21, 25]. For example, one study found that the presence of decreased skeletal muscle index and hand grip strength had negative effects on functional recovery in subacute ambulatory stroke patients [24]. However, unlike the above study, whose participants were limited to mild stroke patients, in this study, the participants had NIHSS scores ranging from 6 to 19, which reflected a moderate-severe severity of stroke. At present, it is believed that such patients have the highest rehabilitation value [26].

A variety of scales can be used to evaluate the prognosis of stroke patients [27]. The mRS is a commonly used indicator to evaluate the outcome of stroke events [28]. Although it has only six grades, the mRS scale was correlated well with the pathological levels of stroke and other stroke scales, with good validity and acceptance [15, 28]. So the mRS is a simple, effective scale that is easily accepted by patients and evaluators. In this study, mRS at the 3rd month after the onset of stroke was selected as the variable of neurological outcomes. According to the previous studies [18, 29], a mRS of ≥ 3 was considered an indicator of poor outcomes, which was the grouping basis in this study.

The prevalence rate of sarcopenia in all patients included in this study was 30.7%, which was nearly the median of the similar studies [7]. The reasons for this great heterogeneity in the prevalence of stroke-related sarcopenia may be due to the differences in population, region, race, etc. and may also be related to the different indicators selected for the assessment of sarcopenia in various studies [7]. Some studies, for example, used the simple five-item questionnaire (SARC-F) in stroke patients, which was thought to be highly specific to adverse clinical outcomes and could accurately identify physical performance impairment[30]. The SARC-F scale was also recommended as a clinical screening and evaluation-implementation tool for sarcopenia in the AWGS 2019 consensus [6]. However, as a retrospective questionnaire, the SARC-F is more suitable for estimating the presence of sarcopenia before the stroke. The previous studies found that the score of the SARC-F was an independent predictor of functional outcomes 3 months after a stroke[7, 31]. However, as previously noted, the non-paretic side can show signs of weakness 1 week after the stroke, so this might be quite different from pre-stroke sarcopenia [7]. Therefore, SARC-F was abandoned in this study to ensure the reliability of the study.

According to the findings of this study, the population with sarcopenia had a higher rate of poor outcomes than the population without sarcopenia. Meanwhile, the poor outcome group had a higher proportion of sarcopenia than the good outcome group, which preliminary suggested a relationship between stroke-related sarcopenia and neurological outcomes. This correlation was further verified by Spearman's partial correlation analysis after correcting covariants. ASM/Ht2 and grip strength were still negatively correlated with mRS scores at the end of the 3rd month. These findings suggested that the evaluation-index of sarcopenia at the early stage after stroke had an influence on the neurological function scores at the convalescent stage (3 months after the onset of stroke). This was similar to the previous study, which found that sarcopenia in patients admitted to an inpatient rehabilitation unit following a stroke was a predictor of worse functional recovery and a decreased likelihood of community discharge [24]. But there was a longer 3-months follow-up in our study, compared with only 3-weeks in the previous study [24].

When poor neurological function was taken as the outcome variable for logistic regression, ASM/Ht2 and grip strength were negatively correlated with the poor outcome, which were independent protective factors for neurological function (OR= 0.525 and 0.684, respectively, both P< 0.01). It was further observed that the contributions of muscle mass and muscle strength to the model were fairly different, which meant that the weight effects of the two indicators of sarcopenia were inconsistent for the risk of neurological dysfunction, and such inconsistency became more obvious after gender stratification. In men, muscle mass may play a greater role for protection on neurological outcome compared with in female. The sex specificity of hormones and the physiological environment may partly explain this gender difference. From the incremental point of view, testosterone and IGF-1 controlled the increase of muscle mass, and the decrease of testosterone with aging affects muscle mass more significantly in men [32]. From the perspective of loss, there were also gender differences at the rate of muscle loss caused by aging. With aging, the absolute level and decline degree of testosterone in women were much lower than those in men, so muscle mass can be relatively preserved [33]. Therefore, under the same age, the relationship between muscle mass and poor outcomes in men is stronger than it is in women. Such phenomena were similar to those observed in the study of the relationship between sarcopenia and osteoporosis [34]. In addition to interacting with gender, It was found that there was an interact-effect between the grip strength and ASM/Ht2 in the regression model. Such indicated that the associations between ASM/Ht2 and neurological outcome were different in different stratified grip strength groups, or the associations between grip strength and neurological outcome were different in different stratified ASM/Ht2 groups. Subgroup comparison showed that the correlation of ASM/Ht2 on poor outcomes seems only to have been founded in the low grip strength subgroup, so did grip strength. The results suggest that there may be a dose-dependent clue between muscle mass and muscle strength when evaluating the effects on neurological outcomes. At present, the interaction between muscle mass and strength has not been reported in literature.

On the basis of logistic regression, this study further analyzed the predictive efficacy of muscle mass and muscle strength in predicting the neurological outcome. It was found that the AUC of grip strength was highest (0.884), followed by the AUC of ASM/Ht2 of 0.833, which were significantly higher than other commonly used predictive indices such as the NIHSS, and BMI. It was also shown that the sensitivity and specificity of the two main indices were significantly superior to other indices in predicting neurologically adverse outcomes. Not only were NIHSS scores usually used to evaluate the changes in stroke patients' conditions, but they were also used as a predictor of neurological prognosis [16]. However, some studies showed that NIHSS scores were inefficient in predicting POCI patients due to the lack of corresponding parameters [26]. The proportion of POCI cases in this study was close to 33%(75/228), which may affect the predictive efficiency of the NIHSS. The study showed that the indexes related to sarcopenia may remedy the deficiency of NIHSS scores in predicting outcomes in patients with POCI stroke. The previous study found that, influenced by the obesity paradox, there was a U- or J-shaped relationship between BMI and stroke-related adverse outcomes of quality of life [35]. These various patterns indicated various pathophysiological mechanisms [36]. This may be one of the reasons for the poor prediction efficiency of BMI in this study. Due to the small sample size of this study, we did not perform further analysis for BMI.

In view of the metabolic and endocrine differences between males and females, this study performed a gender-stratified analysis of predictive efficacy. The female patients had the highest AUC for grip strength with the highest predictive sensitivity, while in the male group, the AUC of ASM/Ht2seemed to be superior with better specificity. The result indicates that muscle mass in the male patients seems to be better for the prediction of adverse neurological outcomes than that in the female patients, which is consistent with the conclusion of the logistic regression in this study. This was also consistent with the results of a recent similar study in China [37]. In addition, the optimal cutoff for grip strength to predict poor neurological outcome was 26.69 kg and 18.66 kg in men and women, respectively, which was close to the AWGS 2019 criterion [6]. The mechanism underlying gender differences in effect on the prediction of neurological outcomes between muscle mass and strength is not fully understood. Currently, most studies focus on the analysis of changes in muscle content with hemiplegia, and further comparative analysis of grip strength and the combined three dimensional indicators is necessary.

It was also difficult to explain the causality between sarcopenia and poor neurological outcome of stroke. Indeed, attempting to isolate the "pure" effect of sarcopenia on mobility, independent of stroke severity, is complicated by the intertwined nature of these conditions, particularly in the early post-stroke phase. Similar to many interrelated clinical pathophysiological statuses, sarcopenia shares many influencing factors with various clinical outcome events in stroke, such as age, metabolic dysregulation, obesity, frailty, and malnutrition [9]. Under the condition, sarcopenia derived from aging and due to stroke may produce synergistic effects, creating a " double burden on the body", which constitutes an adverse pathophysiologic basis that may severely affect the quality of life and the prognosis of the elderly population [7, 9]. In the design of our current study, it is not feasible to definitively distinguish the extent to which the observed muscle loss results from the direct effects of stroke versus the secondary consequences associated with reduced mobility. The cortex defects in stroke itself can markedly affect neurological function, and the presence of sarcopenia may further aggravate the neurological dysfunction, while the neurological dysfunction and its related catabolic signal activation of neurotrophic imbalance, and the combination of multiple mechanisms, including immobilization and dysfunctional atrophy, impaired feeding, inflammation, sympathetic overactivation and denervation, may be involved in accelerating the process of sarcopenia [7, 8]. Both factors may be reciprocally causal and even create a vicious cycle. Nevertheless, this limitation does not dimish the clinical value of the study. The clinical value of our findings lies precisely in the integrative nature of the stroke-related sarcopenia phenotype: by combining muscle mass and muscle strength, we capture a holistic signal of impaired physical reserve that is strongly associated with poor neurological functional outcomes, regardless of the specific contributions of underlying etiological factors. Regardless, taking sarcopenia as an entry point to explore its relationship with neurological function might be helpful for precise interventions to promote recovery in patients with stroke [9].

There are some limitations to this study. First, the generalizability of our findings may be limited due to the single-source nature of the data. Second, constrained by available resources, this study was unable to include several potentially influential variables such as metabolic markers, medication use, dietary intake, and rehabilitation interventions. Additionally, the follow-up period was relatively short, limiting our ability to capture the dynamic changes in relevant indicators over time. Furthermore, as acknowledged, the study design makes it challenging to distinguish between muscle loss directly attributable to stroke and that secondary to reduced mobility. These limitations highlight the need for future longitudinal studies with more refined study designs, comprehensive data collection and extended follow-up periods.

Conclusions

In conclusion, stroke-related sarcopenia is closely related to worse neurological function at the convalescence stage. Both ASM/Ht2 and grip strength at baseline were negatively correlated with mRS scores at the 3rd month after the onset of stroke. Higher ASM/Ht2 and grip strength at baseline were independent protective factors for neurological outcome. ASM/Ht2 and grip strength could be of superior predictive value for neurological outcomes, with different weights and gender differences in their predictive efficacy.

Perspective

According to the conclusions of the study, the important message provided to clinicians is that muscle mass and muscle strength may be two of competitive indicators for predicting neurological outcome in the rehabilitation setting, which could provide reference basis for condition evaluation and rehabilitation strategy formulation.
