Deaf People’s Access to Health Care Services in Kabul City: An Analytical Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Niamatullah Rajabi, Obaidullah Fahim, Rohullah Sakhi, Abdul Malik Habibi, Mohammd Homayun Tawhid, Naseer Ahmad Durrani, Sayed Qais Hussaini, Binafsha Ahmad Parwani, Abdul Bashir Bashari, Sameer Ahmad Yousufzai, Mohammad Musa Shukoor, Mohammad Babur Azizi, Aziz-ur-Rahman Niazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Afghanistan, there are approximately 434,000 individuals who are deaf. The main barriers to access to health services include the lack of sign language interpreters and communication problems, leading to poor health outcomes. This study was conducted to address the challenges faced by deaf individuals in accessing health care services, considering Afghanistan’s specific socio-economic conditions. In this study, access to healthcare refers to the ability to obtain needed healthcare services, while satisfaction of the services received.ObjectiveTo determine the level of access to health care services among deaf people in Kabul City and assess their satisfaction with these services.MethodsThis study employed an analytical cross-sectional design. The study population consisted of 91 teachers and students, selected via a total method from two schools. The research instrument was a structured questionnaire with acceptable reliability, and the data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 software. Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U-tests were performed to find the association factors.ResultsThe results of the study showed that 39.6% reported poor access to health services. Satisfaction with health services was moderate in 53.8% of cases, and statistical analysis revealed significant associations between access and variables such as gender P-value=0.019, education level (P-value=0.003), and marital status (P-value=0.018). No significant relationship was found between satisfaction and most demographic factors (age P-value= 0.519, marital status, P-value=0.701; gender, P-value=0.178; P-value=0.591; and family economic status, P-value=0.414).ConclusionThis research demonstrated that deaf people in Kabul face significant systemic barriers in accessing health services. The Communication barriers, including the lack of sign language interpreters and limited sign language skills among healthcare providers, were identified as major barriers associated with access to healthcare services. To improve the situation, training healthcare workers in sign language, deploying professional interpreters, and developing inclusive health policies are recommended.

Introduction

Disability represents a significant global challenge, affecting a substantial portion of the world’s population. According to the World Health Organization, over one billion people, equivalent to approximately 15% of the global population, live with some form of disability, and between 110 and 190 million adults experience significant functional difficulties. The prevalence of disability is rising due to population aging and the increasing incidence of chronic diseases. Attaining good health and receiving quality healthcare are important for all; however, persons with disabilities face additional barriers. Among this diverse group, individuals who are deaf or hard of hearing and their families are particularly vulnerable and face difficulties in accessing health information, which has diverse negative impacts on their overall well-being.2 Hearing loss, often termed an invisible disability, manifests in varying degrees and types ranging from mild to profound, and conductive, sensorineural, or mixed. It results from a combination of genetic characteristics, health conditions, and behavioral or environmental influences.3,4 The consequences of hearing loss extend far beyond auditory impairment. From childhood, it typically disrupts language acquisition, which negatively impacts overall development, education, and, crucially, the comprehension of health information.5 Due to insufficient access to oral and media-based information, deaf individuals often lack general health knowledge and cannot effectively participate in their own healthcare decision-making.5,6 This situation culminates in limited health literacy within this population.

International studies consistently demonstrate that deaf individuals, especially sign language users, encounter considerable barriers to accessing healthcare services and consequently experience poorer health outcomes compared to the general population.5,7 The most significant factors contributing to this disparity include low health literacy, insufficient awareness among healthcare providers of the needs and culture of the Deaf community, and a critical shortage of qualified sign language interpreters within healthcare systems.5,7 These communication barriers between doctors and deaf patients lead to misdiagnosis, incorrect medication use, and unnecessary medical tests. Many deaf individuals, deterred by the lack of an interpreter or the difficulty of communication, delay seeking care and typically only visit hospitals when their condition is severe.5 Although patients with hearing loss tend to be more frequent users of healthcare services, these services are often not designed to adequately meet their specific needs. Consequently, they face a higher risk of adverse health outcomes and exhibit lower adherence to treatment regimens.7 Research confirms that deaf patients often feel anxiety and uncertainty after consultations with physicians, whereas those with access to dual communication methods demonstrate a better understanding of their diagnosis and treatment. To address this situation, it is imperative that healthcare staff receive training in appropriate communication methods and gain familiarity with Deaf culture. Furthermore, healthcare services must be systematically evaluated from the perspective of deaf patients. A comprehensive evaluation should encompass three dimensions: the quality of the service structure (facilities and staff competencies), the process of service delivery (patient interaction), and final outcomes (impact on patients’ lives). Attending to the experiences and viewpoints of deaf individuals is crucial for the continuous improvement of services and the enhancement of care quality for this community.5 Ultimately, a multi-faceted approach is required: healthcare staff must become more familiar with the culture and language of deaf people, services must be provided with the support of sign language interpreters, and public awareness regarding the specific needs of the Deaf community must be raised.8

Equal access to healthcare services is one of the fundamental principles of health equity and the right to health. However, deaf individuals face numerous barriers in receiving such services due to their hearing impairment. The absence of sign language interpreters and a lack of adequate supportive policies are among the key factors that reduce both the quality and quantity of healthcare services received by this population.9 According to the World Health Organization, more than 5% of the world’s population lives with disabling hearing loss, and this figure is increasing. Moreover, the Global Burden of Disease study identifies hearing loss as one of the leading causes of disability worldwide.10

According to estimates from the Joshua Project conducted in 2025, the number of deaf individuals in Afghanistan reaches 434,000. Based on these figures, a substantial number of healthcare workers have interacted with deaf individuals. However, access to healthcare services for deaf people in Afghanistan, particularly in the capital city of Kabul, requires thorough investigation.11 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), Afghanistan’s health system faces critical constraints in infrastructure, resources, and specialized services, leading to substantial inequities in access to healthcare, particularly for persons with disabilities.12 Within this group, deaf individuals experience even greater barriers, primarily due to communication challenges such as the absence of sign language interpretation and the lack of effective communication systems within healthcare facilities, which significantly restrict their access to timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment.

Furthermore, the HeRAMS Afghanistan reports indicate persistent shortages of trained health personnel and rehabilitation services, which further limit the availability and quality of essential healthcare for persons with disabilities. In addition, the WHO global report on disability emphasizes that communication barriers and insufficient rehabilitation services are major determinants of healthcare inequity in low-income settings. In Afghanistan, these structural deficiencies disproportionately affect deaf individuals, reinforcing systemic exclusion from equitable healthcare access.13

Although advancements in modern technology have enabled more direct communication for deaf individuals with physicians to some extent, and international bodies like the WHO have strived to improve healthcare accessibility, the situation in Afghanistan is markedly different. Given the country’s distinct economic and cultural conditions, limited healthcare resources, and the limited feasibility of implementing modern health technologies, this research, entitled “Assessment of Access to Healthcare Services among Deaf Individuals in Kabul City,” aims to determine the level of access and satisfaction of deaf individuals with healthcare services in Kabul City and assess their satisfaction with these services. And this study is distinguished from similar studies conducted elsewhere by its geographic, temporal, and unique socio-political context. In Afghanistan, given the scarcity of resources, lack of accurate data, and absence of targeted programs for the deaf community, it is essential to assess their access to healthcare services. This research may provide valuable evidence for policymakers, healthcare institutions, and international organizations to develop more effective strategies aimed at improving healthcare accessibility for deaf individuals. Access to healthcare refers to the ability to obtain needed healthcare services, while satisfaction refers to individuals’ overall evaluation of the services received.

Research Methodology

Type of Study

An analytical cross-sectional design was selected because it allows the assessment of healthcare access and satisfaction and their association with participants’ demographic characteristics at a single point in time.

Study Settings and Participants

The study population consisted of teachers and students at the secondary (high school) level in schools for the deaf in Kabul. There are four schools for the deaf in Kabul City. Of these, two schools were selected using a simple random sampling (SRS) method to participate in the study.

Study Duration

The study was conducted from May 4 to September 28, 2025, in Kabul city, Afghanistan.

Sampling Method

A total population sampling method was used in this study, whereby all eligible teachers and students from the selected schools were included. This approach was adopted because the study population was limited in size, making it feasible to include all eligible participants. Both teachers and students were included because they represent the target population of deaf individuals. The selected schools are among the largest and most well-established schools for the deaf in Kabul City, the capital of Afghanistan.

The data collection tool employed in this study was a structured questionnaire was adapted from a previously published English version,14 translated into Dari, and reviewed by three public health experts for content validity and cultural appropriateness. A sign language interpreter assisted participants when needed.

To evaluate the internal consistency and reliability of the questionnaire, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated for each section. The analysis yielded a reliability coefficient of 0.730 for the “Access to Healthcare Services” section and 0.745 for the “Satisfaction with Healthcare Services” section. Both values exceed the commonly accepted threshold of 0.7, indicating satisfactory reliability and internal consistency of the instrument for use in this research.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 26). The data collected through the structured questionnaire were entered into SPSS, and a double-checking process was ensured to maintain data integrity. Both descriptive and inferential statistics were employed to analyze the data. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare two independent groups, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare three or more independent groups because the outcome variables were measured on an ordinal scale.

Descriptive Statistics

Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize the demographic characteristics of the participants, including age, gender, marital status, education level, and family economic status.

Inferential Statistics

The relationship between demographic variables and the two main outcomes—access to health care services and satisfaction with health care services—was examined using non-parametric tests. The Mann–Whitney U-test was applied for variables with two categories (eg, gender, marital status), and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for variables with more than two categories (eg, age groups, education level, and economic status). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reliability Analysis

The internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The access to health services domain showed a reliability coefficient of 0.730, while the satisfaction domain had a coefficient of 0.745, both indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Data Visualization

Tables and graphs were generated using Microsoft Excel and Word to visually represent the findings.

Discussion

The results indicate that 52.7% of participants reported having access to healthcare services, whereas 39.6% indicated poor access. A few participants claimed to have good access to these services. A major issue identified is that doctors’ lack of sign language proficiency complicates interactions with deaf individuals, with 86.8% expressing difficulties related to this problem. Additionally, 75.8% of participants stated that they always need someone to accompany them to the doctor due to communication challenges. About 33% of deaf individuals mentioned that it is not easy for them to reach nearby hospitals or clinics. Thus, deaf individuals face two significant barriers: the lack of sign language knowledge among doctors and difficulties in accessing healthcare facilities. Deaf individuals require doctors who can communicate in sign language and support while interacting with healthcare providers, as they often struggle with communication due to insufficient interpretation services.

Statistical tests, such as the Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests, found that the level of access is associated with factors such as gender, marital status, and education level. Women and married individuals reported better access than men and single individuals. This may be due to women typically taking care of family health needs or being more proactive in seeking healthcare services. The education level of participants also appears to correlate with their access, as indicated by the statistical tests.

Regarding satisfaction with healthcare, most participants expressed only moderate satisfaction, with 53.8% indicating they were reasonably satisfied. On the other hand, 42.9% of respondents reported never being satisfied with the healthcare services received. Furthermore, 55% stated that doctors do not know how to use sign language. Additionally, 52.8% said that doctors fail to clearly explain their health issues. The dissatisfaction stems from issues with healthcare services and provider communication.

Participants shared various experiences, noting that while most felt respected and trusted by their doctors, the significant problem remains ineffective communication. This communication barrier contributes to their dissatisfaction. Analysis reveals that factors such as gender, socioeconomic status, or education level do not significantly influence satisfaction; rather, the systemic issues in healthcare delivery are at the root of the problem.

The findings of this study align closely with research conducted by Amenyeku Akuyi et al15 in Ghana in 2023. Regarding access indicators, 92.3% of participants in the current study reported insufficient access (52.7% moderate and 39.6% poor), while only 35% of deaf individuals in the Ghanaian study had regular access to healthcare services. In terms of satisfaction levels, 42.9% of respondents in Kabul indicated never being satisfied, complemented by 53.8% expressing moderate satisfaction, compared to fewer than 20% in Ghana reporting satisfaction with services. This alignment reflects both studies identifying “communication barriers,” particularly the lack of sign language proficiency among healthcare providers, as the primary challenge—reported by 86.8% of participants in the current study and similarly highlighted in the Ghanaian research The similarity may be explained by common communication barriers, including the limited availability of sign language interpreters and inadequate communication skills among healthcare providers.15

Additionally, the current research reveals a significant connection with the study conducted by Rezende et al16 in Brazil, focusing on qualitative aspects of healthcare services. In terms of satisfaction levels, 96.7% of participants in the present study (including moderate satisfaction and dissatisfaction) expressed discontent with existing services, echoing the Brazilian study’s indication of “very low satisfaction levels with healthcare services.” Regarding communication barriers, 52.8% of respondents in Kabul found medical explanations unclear, while the Brazilian study documented that visits without interpreters led to “diminished trust, misdiagnosis, and ineffective service delivery.” This similarity is particularly noticeable in the call for sign language interpreters within the deaf community, explicitly requested by over 60% of participants in the Brazilian study and implicitly reflected in 75.8% reporting a constant need for companions in the current research. These findings suggest that communication barriers negatively affect both access to healthcare services and patient satisfaction across different healthcare settings.16

Moreover, the findings demonstrate a significant convergence with the study by Witko et al8 in New Zealand, which reveals systemic barriers to healthcare access for the Deaf community. Both studies specifically identify the lack of professional sign language interpreters as a central challenge—75.8% of deaf individuals in Kabul require companions, which is corroborated by numerous reports of unavailable interpreters in the New Zealand study. Furthermore, healthcare providers’ lack of proficiency in sign language has been highlighted as a critical barrier in both contexts (86.8% in Kabul). The consequences of these communication barriers manifest similarly across both studies: significantly diminished patient satisfaction (42.9% dissatisfaction in Kabul) and impaired comprehension of medical information, which leads to reduced treatment adherence. The extensive alignment of findings underscores that improving healthcare access for the Deaf population necessitates fundamental solutions and transformative changes in overarching health policy frameworks. Although the healthcare systems differ, the similar findings indicate that communication barriers are a universal challenge for deaf individuals.8

Similarly, our study reflects findings from research conducted in the United States by Blomstrand et al.17 Both studies highlight that communication barriers are the most significant problem. In Kabul, 75.8% of deaf individuals needed companions for assistance, while in the US study, 33% indicated they did not receive the help they required due to communication issues with healthcare providers. Many participants in both Kabul and the United States reported difficulties understanding their doctors. In Kabul, 52.8% mentioned having unresolved questions about their health, while 52% of participants in the US study reflected similar experiences regarding unanswered medical questions. The emotional consequences of these barriers are consistent: many individuals feel unhappy, frustrated, and exhausted from continually fighting for their basic right to effective communication. This suggests that deaf individuals encounter communication problems within healthcare systems everywhere, primarily due to their rights as a linguistically distinct group being overlooked. These challenges are not dictated by economic factors or geographical location but stem from a fundamental failure to acknowledge deaf individuals’ rights. This indicates that effective communication is a key determinant of equitable healthcare access, regardless of the country’s level of development.17

The results of this study contrast sharply with findings from Aragão et al18 regarding the ease of accessing medical assistance. In this study, only 7.7% of participants reported easy access to the help they needed, significantly lower than the 75% who reported ease of access in the Brazilian study, where 88.9% could access dental assistance. This stark disparity is primarily due to differences in healthcare system infrastructure and socioeconomic conditions between the two countries. Both studies indicate that access to services remains a critical issue needing further exploration. Despite these quantitative differences, both studies converge on a fundamental commonality: communication challenges affected 100% of participants in the Brazilian study versus 86.8% in the current research. Furthermore, dependency on companions was reported by 86.1% in Brazil compared to 75.8% in Kabul. This shared finding underscores that even with improved physical access, healthcare services for deaf individuals remain inadequate and unsatisfactory without addressing core communication. The observed differences may be related to differences in healthcare infrastructure, availability of support services, and socioeconomic conditions between the two countries.18

Conclusion and Suggestions

This study found that the deaf community in Kabul experiences substantial barriers to accessing healthcare services. Communication barriers, including the lack of sign language interpreters and limited sign language skills among healthcare providers, were identified as important barriers associated with healthcare access. The near-universal lack of sign language proficiency among healthcare providers and the consequent dependence on companions severely restricts access and fuels widespread dissatisfaction. These challenges transcend national contexts, highlighting a global issue of linguistic exclusion in healthcare.

To address this, it is imperative to implement structural reforms, including integrating sign language training into medical education, deploying professional interpreters in health facilities, and developing inclusive health policies that explicitly recognize and accommodate the communication needs of the deaf community. Future research should employ mixed-methods approaches to explore these barriers in greater depth across diverse regions of the country.
