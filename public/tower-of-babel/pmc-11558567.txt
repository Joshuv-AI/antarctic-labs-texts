Form Equals Function: Influence of Coacervate Architecture on Drug Delivery Applications
Authors: Chaeyoung Lim, Whitney C Blocher McTigue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplex coacervates, formed through electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged polymers, present a versatile platform for drug delivery, providing rapid assembly, selective encapsulation, and responsiveness to environmental stimuli. The architecture and properties of coacervates can be tuned by controlling structural and environmental design factors, which significantly impact the stability and delivery efficiency of the drugs. While environmental design factors such as salt, pH, and temperature play a crucial role in coacervate formation, structural design factors such as polymer concentration, polymer structure, mixing ratio, and chain length serve as the core framework that shapes coacervate architecture. These elements modulate the phase behavior and material properties of coacervates, allowing for a highly tunable system. In this review, we primarily analyze how these structural design factors contribute to the formation of diverse coacervate architecture, ranging from bulk coacervates to polyion complex micelles, vesicles, and cross-linked gels, though environmental design factors are considered. We then examine the effectiveness of these architectures in enhancing the delivery and efficacy of drugs across various administration routes, such as noninvasive (e.g., oral and transdermal) and invasive delivery. This review aims to provide foundational insights into the design of advanced drug delivery systems by examining how the origin and chemical structure of polymers influence coacervate architecture, which in turn defines their material properties. We then explore how the architecture can be tailored to optimize drug delivery for specific administration routes. This approach leverages the intrinsic properties derived from the coacervate architecture to enable targeted, controlled, and efficient drug release, ultimately enhancing therapeutic outcomes in precision medicine.Keywords: Polyelectrolyte complexes, drug delivery, liquid−liquid phase separation, coacervate architecture

Introduction

A complex coacervate is a polymer-rich liquid droplet formed through liquid–liquid phase separation (Figure 1A).1−3 This phase-separation-driven phenomenon, called complex coacervation, results from the electrostatic interaction between oppositely charged polymers and an entropic driving force from counterion release.4,5 Therefore, this process is spontaneous upon mixing oppositely charged polymers in aqueous solution, leading to phase separation of the mixture into two liquid phases, a polymer-rich phase (coacervate) and a polymer-poor phase (supernatant) (Figure 1B).4,6−9 The absence of a membrane and the liquid-like nature of formed coacervates provide several features, including rapid assembly and disassembly, cargo exchange, selective encapsulation, responsiveness to stimuli, viscoelastic nature, and dynamic behaviors such as coalescence.10−12 These characteristics have led to the widespread use of coacervates for microencapsulation in the food13,14 and cosmetic industries,15,16 as well as in underwater adhesives,17−19 wastewater treatment,20 protein purification,21 and gene/drug delivery.22−26 Notably, extensive research has been conducted on utilizing coacervates as efficient drug carriers, highlighting their potential to improve therapeutic efficacy as an alternative approach to similar platforms such as lipid and polymer nanoparticles and other biomaterials that face some challenges in delivering drugs.17−20

To date, many studies have explored the relationship between coacervate architectures and structural and environmental design factors to predict coacervate morphologies and properties. Structural design factors associated with the chemical properties of component polymers include polymer concentration,4,5 mixing ratio,27−29 polymer structure,30−32 chirality,33,34 and functional groups,4,35 while the environmental design factors in aqueous solution involve salt,3,35−38 pH,29,39 and temperature.40−42 Controlled by these design factors, coacervates exhibit varying morphologies and properties, which can affect the bioavailability and efficacy of drugs.26,43,44 The impact of morphologies and properties on cellular uptake and internalization has already been reported in other drug delivery systems, and it is not only coacervates.45−48 In addition, coacervate morphologies and properties determine the drug administration routes, which are directly related to specific tissues targeted for disease treatment.49,50 Therefore, understanding this relationship is important for tailoring coacervate-based drug carriers to specific administration routes and optimizing treatment by controlling structural and environmental design factors.

In this context, we explore design strategies for coacervate-based drug carriers, emphasizing the relationship between the coacervate architecture and design factors in structural and environmental aspects, with particular attention given to structural design factors. Thus, we begin by discussing the origin and structure of component polymers commonly used in complex coacervation, followed by a discussion of how the assembled coacervate architecture is determined by varying combinations of the component polymers. Finally, this review concludes by examining how the coacervate architecture influences drug administration routes, offering insights into their potential as promising tools for personalized medicine.

Relationship between Component Polymers and Coacervate Formation

The origin and architecture of component polymers used in coacervation are crucial for designing them as drug carriers as these factors directly impact the architecture and material properties of coacervates, which are tunable due to long-range electrostatic interaction between oppositely charged polymers, ultimately determining the success of their application.51−53 This section will discuss the origin and architecture of component polymers used in coacervation and how these variations influence the function and architecture of the resulting coacervates.

Polymer Origin

Charged polymers commonly used in the coacervate formation classified synthetic polymers and biological polymers, such as polysaccharides, nucleic acids, polypeptides, and proteins. The resulting coacervates can exhibit either solid-like or liquid-like characteristics, depending on whether the component polymers are synthetic or biological.54,55 This difference in polymer origin leads to significant variations in the chemical aspect of charges and backbone, which impacts the entropic release of counterions and, as a result, the overall phase behavior of coacervates.56,57 This distinction of polymer origin not only drives the entropic forces but also plays a critical role in determining the strength and nature of polymer associations, as further modulated by additional specific interactions such as hydrophobic forces and hydrogen bonding.54,58

Synthetic Polymers

Coacervation with synthetic polymers began with the pioneering work by Fuoss and Sadek several decades ago. They were the first to describe the coacervation between oppositely charged polymers with high charge density, poly(styrenesulfonate) (PSS) and poly(vinyl-N-butylpyridinium bromide), observing insoluble solid-like coacervates, called precipitates, that were influenced by the mixing ratio.59 Later, Michaels et al. studied the properties of solid-like precipitates resulting from the mixture of PSS with poly(vinyl benzyl trimethylammonium chloride) at the stochiometric ratio and demonstrated how ternary solvents, which consist of a combination of water, organic solvent, and salt, interact and dissolve complexes.60,61 These early studies laid the foundation for extensive research on synthetic polymers in coacervation, leading to many quantitative findings on how phase behaviors of coacervates vary as a function of experimental parameters by using weak polyelectrolytes, not only strong polyelectrolytes.30,62,63 Synthetic polyanions typically feature sulfonates or carboxylate groups, while polycations contain amine or ammonium groups.64−67 Fu et al. studied how the strength of electrostatic interaction between commonly used synthetic polymers with varying charge densities determines the formation of coacervates.67 Systems with weaker interactions, such as those with poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), tend to form liquid-like coacervates even in the absence of salt, whereas stronger interactions, such as the poly(allylamine hydrochloride) (PAH)/PSS system, result in more solid, finely dispersed precipitates that resist agglomeration. Moreover, the authors highlighted that, while charge density is traditionally thought to influence interaction strength in coacervates, the type of ionic group is more critical, with sulfonates and quaternary ammoniums, which are hydrophobic, binding more strongly than carboxylates and primary amines, which are hydrophilic.

Biological Polymers

Following synthetic polymers, incorporating biological or modified biological polymers has further broadened the spectrum of charged macromolecules that can be used to design coacervate-based materials. Biological polymers, such as polysaccharides, nucleic acids, polypeptides, and proteins, offer distinct advantages for therapies and diagnostic applications due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and functional diversity (Table 1).43,49,68,69 For example, naturally derived polysaccharides are widely used because they are less toxic, exhibit antibacterial and antifungal activity, and possess inherent biodegradability.70,71 Native nucleic acids, such as RNA and DNA, are biocompatible, negatively charged polymers that can be engineered to interact selectively with specific cationic biomolecules, facilitating coacervation, mainly for gene therapy applications.69 Naturally derived polypeptides and proteins not only have intrinsic bioactive properties but are also biocompatible and generally non-immunogenic, making them highly relevant for use in food, pharmaceuticals, and biomedical applications.72

Despite the advantages of these biological polymers, several challenges are shown in coacervation due to their chemical nature, molecular structure, and additional driving forces, such as hydrogen bonding, which differs from synthetic polymers that primarily depend on electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged polymers.58 For example, polysaccharides are typically highly polydisperse and often possess complex branching structures, making it difficult to predict and control their phase behavior during coacervation.3,73,74 In addition, their relatively high molecular weight and varying degrees of acetylation or sulfation contribute to inconsistencies in phase behaviors.73 Nucleic acids are prone to degradation by nucleases, both intracellularly and extracellularly, which may reduce their stability in biological environments.75 Moreover, the high charge density and rigidity of nucleic acids lead to intricate precipitation rather than liquid-like coacervate formation.68 Polypeptides and proteins often exhibit rigid and ordered secondary structures, including α-helices and β-sheets, that can lead to strong interchain interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, resulting in the formation of irregular solid precipitates.76,77 Furthermore, due to restricted flexibility in altering side-chain interactions or modulating molecular structure, the ability to control phase behavior and stability during coacervation is limited.78,79 In particular, proteins are more challenging to predict in coacervation due to their heterogeneous surface charge distribution and charge regulation.80−82 Proteins are composed of long polypeptide chains that fold into three-dimensional globular structures driven by secondary intermolecular forces, causing the formation of charge patches on their surface through the clustering of charged amino acids in localized regions.83 Proteins are generally considered weakly charged polymers with a low charge density, and similar to weakly charged synthetic polymers, their net charge is influenced by the pH of the environment.84 However, while synthetic polymers can become fully ionized when reaching specific pH levels, the degree of ionization in proteins is governed by their isoelectric point (pI), at which they carry no net charge.84−86 Proteins carry a positive charge at pH levels below their pI and a negative charge at pH levels above it.84 This mechanism, known as charge regulation, is induced by polymer–protein interaction and implies that the charge of the ionic groups on proteins is not fixed; it varies depending on the local electrostatic environment, which includes the specific proteins involved and the strength of the interacting polyelectrolytes.80,81,86 Typically, complexation is expected between oppositely charged polymers; however, protein-based complexation can also occur beyond the protein’s pI, commonly referred to as the “wrong side”, where the net charge of both polymers is the same.83 Both surface charge distribution and charge regulation contribute to the occurrence of complexation on the wrong side, where protein-based polyelectrolyte complexes form even when both the protein and the polyelectrolyte carry the same overall charge.87−96 Besides, many native proteins often do not undergo phase separation at moderate ionic strength and neutral pH when mixed with oppositely charged polymers, which limits the conditions under which phase separation can occur.93,97

Several studies have focused on chemically modifying these biological polymers to optimize their features and design complex coacervates with desired functions.13,71,98 For example, polysaccharides have been chemically modified through processes such as carboxymethylation, which enhances their solubility in water.99 The modifications allow for more stable and predictable polyelectrolyte complexes, addressing the limitation of naturally derived polysaccharides during their phase separation.99 Complexation of nucleic acids with block copolymers containing both cationic and neutral blocks has been studied, highlighting the formation of stable ordered polyelectrolyte complexes with better control over size, morphology, and long-term stability.100−102 Polypeptides, which can adopt tunable secondary structures by controlling their amino acid side sequence, have garnered considerable interest in directing nanoscale structure formation through intra- or intermolecular interactions in treatment and diagnostic applications.103,104 Moreover, specific sequences can be designed to respond to environmental factors such as temperature, enabling better control of size and structure, and adaptability to varying environments.105 Along with polypeptides, several studies have also explored phase behaviors of protein–polyelectrolyte complexes by chemically modifying proteins to increase net charge, known as supercharging proteins, which not only broadens the range of pH and ionic strengths at which coacervation can occur but also improves the stability of the resulting complexes rather than aggregation.93,106 Recent studies on supercharged protein complexes observed liquid-like phase behaviors that can be tuned by controlling charge patches on proteins across a wide range of salt, pH, and polymer concentration.93

Polymer Architecture

We categorized polymer architectures used in coacervation based on polymer configurations, such as linear and branched polymers (Figure 2). Linear polymers comprise a single, unbranched chain, whereas branched polymers have chains of varying lengths extending from specific points along their backbone.107,108 Along with the polymer origin, polymer architecture is also an important design factor in determining the phase behavior of polyelectrolyte complexes. Thus, we will review the types of polymer architectures used in the formation of coacervates and how they affect phase transitions.

Schematic illustration of various polymer configurations. (A) Linear conformations of homo- and copolymers. (B) A selection of branched polymer architectures.

Linear Polymer

Linear polymers typically include homopolymers and copolymers (Figure 2A). A homopolymer consists of one type of charge species, whereas copolymers, including random, alternating, and block copolymers (Table 2), are composed of two or more distinct monomer units linked by covalent bonds.109,110 A random copolymer arranges monomer units randomly along the polymer chain, while alternating and block copolymers follow a specific pattern.109 For example, alternating copolymers alternate monomer units regularly, and block copolymers link distinct blocks of monomers.111 In block copolymers, diblock copolymers have two distinct blocks and triblock copolymers have three. Decades ago, studies highlighted the assembly of multiblock copolymers into ordered nanoarchitectures, leading to significant interest in not only amphiphilic block polymers but particularly charged block copolymers.62,98,112−114 Using charged block copolymers, groups investigated varying phase behaviors of coacervate formation, and commonly used copolymers include a charged block and a neutral, water-soluble block.115 Some studies have also explored polyzwitterions, which are characterized as ampholytic polymers containing charged groups of opposite signs, typically located on the same side.116,117 The charged state of the two charged blocks within the chain is also altered by the pH of the solution, switching from a polyzwitterion to a polyelectrolyte, which exhibits a uniform charge state.118 The polyzwitterion can self-assemble when their net charge is zero, driven by intra- and intermolecular electrostatic interactions.118 On the other hand, triblock copolymers used in coacervation typically consist of a neutral hydrophilic block flanked by two charged blocks (ABA-type), where the charged blocks induce phase separation through electrostatic interactions.119,120 ABC-type triblock copolymers, where the third block carries a charge opposite to that of the first, have also been studied as zwitterionic triblock copolymers.118 Electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged blocks in multiblock copolymer systems drive the hierarchical assembly of diverse nanostructures in aqueous solution.114 When neutral and charged blocks are combined, the charged blocks form a core with the neutral blocks surrounding it.113 The architecture of these assemblies is influenced by the chemical nature of each polymer block.32 We will further discuss how this chemical nature controls the phase behavior of the assembled complexes in Section 2.3.2.

Branched Polymer

Branched polymers encompass comb/branched, hyperbranched, dendrimer, cross-linked, and star polymers (Figure 2B). Studies on complexation with branched polymers have discussed the impact of branched polymer structures on phase separation behavior by comparing them with linear polymers. For example, Li et al. studied the influence of polymer architecture on polyelectrolyte complexation using linear, 4-arm, and 8-arm star-shaped polymers, poly(dimethyl amino ethyl acrylate), and dendrimers, poly(amidoamine), complexed with a negatively charged block copolymer, poly(N-isopropylacrylamide)-block-poly(styrenesulfonate).32 The complexation resulted in the formation of coacervates with a micellar structure. The author found that the micellar size and aggregation number (total number of polymer chains per micelle) significantly increased with the length of the poly(styrenesulfonate) block, especially as the number of arms in polycation architecture increased, as shown by static and dynamic light scattering techniques. In contrast, the micellar coacervate structure with dendrimers showed consistently low hydrodynamic size (as the sum of core radius and corona thickness) and low aggregation numbers regardless of the PSS length. The difference is attributed to the pH-dependent nature of dendrimers, which allows for easier relaxation and fewer kinetically trapped states.32 Similarly, Johnston et al. utilized polypeptide-based comb polymers to investigate how branching influences the self-assembly and stability of coacervates, employing experimental and simulation approachs.30 They used linear/comb poly(l-lysine) and linear/comb poly(d,l-glutamate) to form complexes and confirmed that comb–linear polymer complexation phase separated at approximately 0.5 charge fraction as indicated by the highest peak in the turbidity graph, although this complexation was less effective in inducing phase separation compared to linear–linear polymer complexes (Figure 3A). Moreover, they observed that comb polymer systems had lower salt stability compared to linear–linear systems (Figure 3B). This reduced stability is attributed to the structural characteristics of comb polymers, which diminish electrostatic interactions and limit counterion release, thereby weakening the driving force for coacervate formation. To better understand predictive phase behavior of comb polymer complexes by controlling comb branch length, they predicted the binary phase diagram using simulations. The phase diagram relied on branch length, and with increasing comb branch length, their phase behavior becomes more like linear systems (Figure 3C).

Coacervate Architecture

Mixtures from various polymer assembly combinations with different origins and architectures undergo liquid–liquid phase separation (coacervation) or solid–liquid phase separation (precipitation), depending on structural and environmental factors.3,4,58,164 These two distinct phases have different properties, making each suited for specific applications. Solid precipitates colloidally remain stable for several days and provide utility as polymeric membranes for separation applications.165,166 In contrast, liquid-like coacervates, with their molecular flexibility, are better suited for injectable drug delivery via invasive routes.167 While many quantitative studies have examined the role of salt in facilitating solid-to-liquid phase transitions by weakening electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged polymers due to screening effects, most works to date have focused on the use of liquid coacervates for drug delivery, rather than unprocessed precipitates.3,58,128,168,169 Accordingly, this section will discuss the formation of liquid-like coacervates, which is central to our goal of developing strategies for coacervate-based drug carriers with different architectures tailored to patient-specific needs in this paper.

Coacervates resulting from liquid–liquid phase separation in aqueous solution can exhibit a range of hierarchical architectures (Table 3) from bulk coacervates, polyion complex (PIC) micelles, and vesicles to cross-linked gels.53,158 The architecture of these coacervates varies significantly, depending on the specific polymer assembly combination used. For example, while the complexation of two oppositely charged homopolymers generally forms bulk coacervates (Figure 4A),12,86 substituting a diblock copolymer for one of these homopolymers produces micellar or vesicular structures, known as PIC micelles or vesicles. (Figure 4B).86,170,171 In addition, using multiblock copolymers with more than two blocks, mainly triblock copolymers, enables the formation of more intricate and robust structures, such as interconnected networks or physically cross-linked gels (Figure 4C).119,172 Assembled from various polymer combinations, polyelectrolyte complexes can undergo changes in their morphologies and properties by regulating structural and environmental design factors.13

Bulk Coacervate

A mixture of two oppositely charged homopolymers, regardless of synthetic or biological polymers, yields bulk coacervates in aqueous solution.86 Liu et al. prepared bulk coacervates using methacryloyl and acryloyl synthetic polymers, indicating how polymer structure and chain length affect their phase behaviors and rheological properties.7 Methacryloyl polymers were more stable in the presence of salt than the acryloyl polymer of the same chain length, as shown by the larger two-phase region in the phase diagram (Figure 5A). This increased stability is primarily attributed to the higher rigidity and hydrophobicity of methacryloyl polymers, which influences their interaction with water and leads to stiffer coacervates with higher viscoelastic moduli. Moreover, longer polymer chains enhance the salt stability of coacervates due to increased charge interactions and higher entropy, resulting in slower relaxation times and increased stiffness in their viscoelastic properties (Figure 5B).

As another case of using assembly combinations with different polymer origins and structures, Zhou et al. prepared bulk coacervates using negatively charged protein bovine serum albumin (BSA) and six different positively charged synthetic polymers, controlling structural design factors of the polycations, including chain length, polymer structure, and charge groups, to observe their effect on phase transition behavior.85 Optimal complexation required an excess of polycation, deviating from charge stoichiometry due to the globular structure of BSA, which caused a geomatic mismatch with the polycation. While maintaining the mixing ratio, longer polymers reinforced electrostatic interactions, resulting in partial precipitation. Polymer structure also affects the coacervation; for example, dendrimers induce steric hindrance, preventing coacervate formation. In addition, polycations with primary amines formed stronger complexes than those with quaternary ammonium groups, although the quantitative impact on coacervation formation was not thoroughly detailed.

For biological–biological polymer complexes, Vieregg et al. investigated the phase behavior of short oligonucleotide–polypeptide complexes, regulating polymer structure (e.g., single-/double-stranded DNA), salt, and temperature.68 While the complexation of single-stranded (ss) DNA led to liquid-like coacervate formation, double-stranded (ds) DNA formed solid precipitates due to the higher charge density and low flexibility of dsDNA, the same phase behavior observed in dsRNA complexes. Upon adding salt, the solid phase behavior of dsDNA complexes transitioned to liquid-like coacervates without denaturation of dsDNA, stabilizing its hybridization. The phase behaviors remained consistent regardless of the mixing ratio. Later, Marras et al. found that the complexation of ssDNA and dsDNA with biological/synthetic homopolymers with different charge density, hydrophobicity, and structure induced macrophase separation, forming either liquid like coacervate or solid precipitates based on certain conditions such as chain length and salt concentration.101

On the other hand, Perry et al. found that chirality affects the phase behavior of polypeptide complexes composed of poly(lysine) and poly(glutamic acid).34 They confirmed that racemic (d, l) polypeptides hinder the formation of rigid secondary structures during complexation, which promotes liquid-like coacervation. This was confirmed by higher turbidity in the racemic system compared to the homochirality (d or l) system in the presence of salt (Figure 5C). While complexes with racemic polypeptides formed liquid droplets (Figure 5D and E), complexes with homochirality were observed as solid precipitates (Figure 5F and G). In protein-based complexes, Cummings and Obermeyer demonstrated how phase separation behaviors of supercharged protein complexes depend on protein surface charge and types of negative charged polymers to interact, indicating that proteins with higher positive charge densities phase separate over broader pH and salt concentrations.93 This indicated that increasing charge density promoted the phase transition of protein-based complexes at higher salt concentration, requiring more salt to dissolve their complexes. Moreover, their complexes with synthetic polymer such as PAA and PSS exhibit higher salt stability compared to those with biological polymers such as DNA and RNA. Building on this, Kim et al. quantitively investigated how surface charge distribution affects the phase behavior of protein-synthetic polymer/polysaccharide complexes, maintaining nearly the same net charge and pI for comparing the charge patchiness of four different proteins.80 The larger negative patches on proteins lead to liquid coacervates, allowing for more flexibility in interacting chains, while the smaller patches lead to the formation of solid precipitates. They also demonstrated that charge regulation impacts phase behavior in protein–polymer coacervation, observing only the weak polyanions such as HA and PAA formed soluble complexes with proteins having the largest charge patches, likely due to their extensive positive charge regions, while the strong polyanions such as PSS did not, likely due to the charge-regulation ability of weak polyanions.

Polyion Complex (PIC) Micelle or Vesicle

Although studies of bulk coacervates have been actively reported, their tendency to coalesce and lose structural stability over time has raised concerns, particularly limiting their potential as drug carriers.198,199 Alternatively, groups have conjugated polymers with neutral blocks to form PIC micelles or vesicles, enhancing stability and utility for drug delivery as a strategy first reported by Harada and Kataoka.63,198,200−203 PIC micelles consist of a core and a corona, similar to micelles assembled from amphiphilic block copolymers.204 The core of PIC micelles formed through the complexation of two oppositely charged blocks, while the corona, made up of swollen neutral hydrophilic blocks, such as PEG, protects the core and influences the solubility and stability of micelles.200,204,205 Since most biological polymers used as drugs, such as proteins and nucleic acids, are ionic and hydrophilic, the core of PIC micelles can effectively encapsulate them by interacting with charged blocks.206 Driven by electrostatic interaction, the core also responds to stimuli, enabling controlled drug release.200,202,207 Many studies have considered the chemical nature of block copolymers (e.g., chain length of charged block and neutral block) as an important structural design factor in creating PIC micelles as well as other established structural design factors (e.g., overall chain length, polymer concentration, polymer structure, mixing ratio), to investigate their impact on controlling the architecture of PIC micelles.32,112,132,200,207

In addition to experimental findings that regulate the formation of PIC micelles, several studies have reported property–structure correlation between component polymers and micelles through scaling theories and empirical works, providing quantitative predictions of their measurable properties, such as morphologies, aggregation number, and hydrodynamic radius as a function of the structural factors.112,204,207−209 Van der Kooij et al. studied the effect of chain length on the formation of PIC micelles composed of negatively charged homopolymers, poly(acrylic acid), and positively charged block copolymers, poly(N-methyl-2-vinylpyridinium)-block-poly(ethylene oxide) (PM2VP-b-PEO).132 They found that the hydrodynamic radius of PIC micelles increased with increasing length of the charged blocks, whereas the length of homopolymers did not significantly affect micelle size until it exceeded a certain critical length, after which it led to size growth. This critical length varies depending on the aggregation number, which is influenced by the chemical structure of the block copolymer. However, when salt was added, homopolymers shorter than the critical length induced a morphological transition from spherical to elongated structures as shorter PAA chains interact with fewer PM2VP chains, making it easier for the micelles to adopt different shapes. Recently, Heo et al. studied how charged blocks influence the morphologies of PIC micelles using two oppositely charged diblock copolymers with identical numbers and distribution of charged groups.185 They proposed a scaling theory that predicts the core radius, corona thickness, and aggregation number of PIC micelles formed, with quantitative fits to experimental data. Their results showed that longer charged blocks lead to more extended core stretching, resulting in larger micelles and the incorporation of more polymer chains, thereby increasing the core radius and aggregation number. Simultaneously, the corona chains become less stretched, causing a slight reduction in thickness, as the larger core size allows the corona chains to relax.

Apart from spherical and elongated structures, Ong et al. precisely calculated varying phase behaviors of diblock copolymer complexes using advanced theory-based simulation, controlling charge-to-neutral block ratio, charged block length, polymer concentration, and salt concentration.210 The authors predicted that a charge-to-neutral block ratio in diblock copolymers with identical chain length and charge distribution induced nanophase separation, forming not only micellar but also ordered hexagonal and lamellar structures of the complexes at certain polymer and salt concentrations. As the charged block increased, these ordered phases transitioned to bulk coacervates (Figure 6A). Beyond two-dimensional diagrams, diblock copolymer complexes with higher charged block ratios exhibited three-dimensional body-centered cubic, hexagonal cylinder, gyroid, and lamellar structures (Figure 6B). In contrast, chain length did not induce phase transition but only affected the overall size of the assembled structure. The studies of varying phase behaviors of hierarchically assembled structures such as body-centered cubic and hexagonal phases can be beneficial to designing more efficient drug delivery systems using coacervates that allow enhanced encapsulation efficiency and enable sustained and controlled release due to their larger surface area, as observed in liquid crystalline nanoparticles when used as drug carriers.211,212 Yet, few studies have been experimentally reported using polyelectrolyte complexes with these structures.

The morphological properties of PIC micelles controlled by design factors occur not only in synthetic–synthetic polymer complexes but also in biological–biological modified polymer complexes. Lueckheide et al. prepared PIC micelles using a negatively charged polymer, ssDNA or dsDNA, and a positively charged polymer, poly(l-lysine)-block-poly(ethylene glycol) (PLys-b-PEG), demonstrating that the hybridization state of DNA also significantly influenced micellar architecture, in addition to chain length.102 PIC micelles with ssDNA exhibited spherical structures, while those with dsDNA formed elongated PIC micelles, as characterized by electron microscopy, multiangle light scattering, and small-angle X-ray scattering measurements. The authors further showed that the micelle size grew larger with increasing length of the charged block, whereas the length of DNA had little effect. Similarly, Marras et al. also demonstrated a spherical structure of PIC micelles typically forms with ssDNA, whereas the elongated structure is more common with dsDNA, particularly when complexed with PLys-b-PEG.213 When using poly((vinylbenzyl)trimethylammonium)-block-PEG (PVBTMA-b-PEG), the architecture formed is more variable in shape and size. While ssDNA with PVBTMA-b-PEG still tends to form spherical micelles, the dsDNA complexes result in shorter, more polydisperse elongated micelles compared to those formed with PLys-b-PEG. This suggests that PVBTMA-b-PEG tends to create less uniform structures, likely due to its higher hydrophobicity and altered interaction dynamics with DNA. They also studied the effect of charge block length on the size and stability of PIC micelles, indicating that longer charged block lengths in polyelectrolyte complexes result in larger and more stable PIC micelles, while shorter lengths can lead to aggregation.

In some diblock copolymer complexes, the vesicular architecture of PIC micelles, referred to as the PIC vesicle or PICsome, was observed. For example, Li et al. confirmed the formation of PIC vesicles through the assembly of poly(styrenesulfonate)-block-poly(ethylene oxide) (PSS-b-PEO) and dendrimer polyamidoamine (PAMAM).194 On the other hand, block copolymers containing zwitterionic blocks have been shown to promote the formation of PIC vesicles. Ohara et al. utilized diblock copolymers composed of a zwitterionic poly(2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine) (PMPC) block to form PIC vesicles.195 Similarly, Kwolek et al. produced PIC vesicles using block copolymers that also contained PMPC zwitterionic blocks.196 Both studies demonstrated that complexation between linear diblock copolymers resulted in the formation of PIC vesicles using the same PMPC block, through which the charge density of the polymer structures differed. Ohara et al., using weak polyelectrolytes, found that pH had an impact on the vesicle morphology, with PIC vesicles remaining stable in basic conditions but disintegrating in acid environments.195 In contrast, Kwolek et al., using strong polyelectrolytes, observed that the vesicle architecture was not affected by pH.196

On the other hand, the assembly of two oppositely charged triblock copolymers forms physically cross-linked gels, which are less toxic and biodegradable compared to covalently cross-linked hydrogels.36,42,158 The electrostatic gels provide adaptability, injectability, and stimuli-sensitivity, making them effective for drug delivery, particularly in the form of injectable hydrogels.158,214 Upon mixing, the oppositely charged blocks form the coacervate core, driven by electrostatic interaction, while the neutral midblocks create the network, resulting in gel formation.181,182 Srivastava et al. demonstrated that triblock copolymer assemblies formed individual flower-like PIC micelles without creating a network, similar to the structures observed in diblock copolymers at low polymer concentrations.184 As the polymer concentration increases, both systems undergo a similar disorder-to-order transition, resulting in dense, ordered architectures (Figure 7A). However, the phase behavior of the two systems diverges noticeably when the neutral midblock length is controlled. Triblock copolymer complexes created interconnected gels even at very low polymer concentrations and become denser with an increasing polymer concentration (Figure 7B and 7C). In contrast, diblock copolymers remained as isolated micelles without forming a network, as confirmed by molecular dynamics simulations (Figure 7B). Furthermore, the authors showed a phase diagram illustrating the disorder-to-order phase transition of triblock copolymer assemblies, which can be tuned by controlling charged block length and polymer concentration (Figure 7D).158

Applications

Several studies have explored the use of biological polymers, such as nucleic acids, polypeptides, and proteins, in disease therapeutics, revealing significant potential in biomedical fields. For example, nucleic acids have been investigated for their ability to selectively control specific gene expression in cancer treatments, while polypeptides and proteins have shown great promise in treating central nervous system diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.215−217 Despite these promising findings, the clinical application of biological polymers faces challenges, particularly concerning stability for polypeptides and proteins, as well as the requirements for and intracellular delivery for nucleic acids.218−220

To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored various polymer-based vehicles, which encompass a diverse range of platforms with different architectures and assemblies, including polymeric micelles,113,221,222 dendrimers,223,224 liposomes,225,226 hydrogels,227−229 and coacervates.12,230 These platforms, known for their advantageous features in drug delivery, can encapsulate small molecules or macromolecules via surface adsorption, electrostatic interactions, and covalent bondings.218 Among these platforms, coacervates stand out due to their ability to spontaneously self-assemble without the need for organic solvents, which could otherwise reduce the bioactivity of encapsulated drugs.231,232 Coacervates can incorporate drugs by forming complexes such as polymer–drug, polymer–drug conjugated polymer, and polymer–drug–polymer assemblies, which facilitate molecular transport and enable administration through various routes based on the disease and desired pharmacokinetics.231,233 The drug delivery efficiency of coacervates relies on polymer assembly combinations, which influence their physicochemical and mechanical properties, and in turn bioavailability and efficacy of drugs.220

This section will explore how the architecture and properties of polyelectrolyte complexes, assembled from diverse polymer combinations, are tailored to different drug administration routes. Furthermore, we examine on-demand release strategies in coacervate drug carriers, highlighting how various triggers, depending on the selected polymer combinations, can be employed for effective drug delivery.

Drug Administration Route

In the past few decades, conventional delivery systems have mainly focused on encapsulating small molecules as the primary therapeutic agents.220 However, with the advent of the next generation of therapeutics, which includes macromolecules such as nucleic acids, polypeptides, proteins, and antibodies, new therapeutic capabilities have emerged.220 These macromolecules offer several advantages such as increased potency, reduced nonspecific binding, and lower toxicity.234 Nevertheless, they face significant challenges such as poor solubility, instability, and short systemic circulation times.235−238 Upon administration, biomacromolecules can be delivered either systemically through the bloodstream or locally to specific tissues.239−242 While systemic delivery is commonly employed, it encounters barriers such as the blood–brain and blood–retina barriers, which limit access to target tissues.217 These barriers, due to the size and charge of macromolecules, hinder effective biodistribution compared to small molecules.243 In contrast, local delivery can bypass these barriers, but it often involves more invasive procedures and intricate techniques, making it better suited for diseases confined to accessible sites, such as solid cancers or traumatic injuries.242,244,245 To achieve effective delivery, drug carriers often require external assistance. However, a significant drawback of many delivery systems is their tendency to accumulate in organs, reducing their effectiveness of drug delivery to target tissues or cells.246 As a result, there is a growing focus on optimizing drug carriers to improve their interaction with the biological environment at cellular and subcellular levels, enhancing cellular uptake and endosomal escape, which directly impact bioavailability.247 It is therefore important to understand how the morphologies and properties of drug carriers influence drug bioavailability and efficacy, as the association of the drug with polyelectrolyte complexes can be adjusted to enhance pharmacokinetics and biological responses, ensuring precise targeting of disease areas while reducing unintended side effects.131,246,248,249

The standard routes of drug administration are categorized as either noninvasive or invasive routes. Representative noninvasive routes include the oral route, where drugs pass through the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, and the transdermal route, where drugs are absorbed through the skin.250,251 In contrast, invasive routes administer drugs systemically through intravenous and intramuscular injections.218,219 We will explore both noninvasive (e.g., oral and transdermal) and invasive routes of drug administration, focusing on the architecture and properties of coacervate-based drug carriers that enhance stability and delivery efficiency in each route. For example, nanoscale particles, often composed of polysaccharides, are suited for oral delivery as they can enhance stability and facilitate absorption in the GI tract.133,252−255 In contrast, gel-like patch and microneedles are more appropriate for transdermal delivery, providing controlled drug release.237,256−260 In addition to nanoparticles, which are commonly used for invasive delivery,131,142,191,261,262 injectable hydrogels with tunable sol–gel properties also can be utilized to regulate drug release and enhance delivery efficiency.167,263−267

Non-Invasive Routes

The oral route is the most common method of drug administration, offering several advantages, such as ease of ingestion, cost-effectiveness, and high patient compliance.236,268,269 However, it also has some significant drawbacks, including poor absorption, gastric irritation, and first-pass effect, where drug metabolism in the GI tract reduces the drug concentration in the GI tract, such as stomach and small intestine, before it reaches systemic circulation.238,268 The primary challenge in drug absorption arises from the variability in pH levels, which range from acidic in the stomach to neutral or slightly alkaline in the small intestine, and the presence of diverse enzymes that can reduce drug stability and availability in the GI tract.270 Orally administered drugs typically reach the small intestine across the physiological barrier of the intestinal epithelium that regulates drug absorption.271,272 Several studies have explored the use of coacervates to preserve drugs against the harsh GI environment.273,274 In particular, pH-responsive coacervates have proven effective in enhancing stability in the stomach and enabling controlled release in the intestine, optimizing the efficacy of oral drug delivery.254 Feng et al. prepared bulk coacervates using chitosan (CS) and o-carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) cross-linked by tripolyphosphate for oral delivery of doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX).133 They evaluated in vitro drug release by stimulating the GI tract and the bulk coacervates slowly release DOX at the pH of the stomach, while quickly disintegrating at the pH of intercellular space between epithelial cells, corresponding to the pH of the tight junctions, which are intercellular adhesion structures in epithelial and endothelial tissues that regulate paracellular permeability.275 The tight junction poses a significant obstacle to paracellular drug absorption in epithelial and endothelial barriers.273,274,276 The pH sensitivity of CS/CMCS coacervates could make them potential candidates as small-intestine-targeted drug carriers.133 Similarly, Li et al. reported CS-based complexes with the hyaluronic acid (HA)-conjugated anticancer drug Paclitaxel (PTX).193 Since the CS shell protects HA-co-PTX, the coacervate maintains its structure without initial burst release at the low pH, while the coacervate rapidly releases PTX when it collapses at pH 7.4.

The transdermal route administers drugs through the skin, typically using patches, microneedles, and topical creams.256,277,278 Unlike the oral route, which is subjected to the harsh GI environment, transdermal administration bypasses first-pass metabolism, allowing for improved selective targeting and controlled drug release, thereby reducing the frequency of dosing.256,257 The human skin is composed of three main layers, which are the stratum corneum, the epidermis, and the dermis.236 Bioactive compounds must traverse these layers sequentially, with systematic absorption occurring through the dermal microcirculation once they reach the dermis.236,279 However, the stratum corneum presents a significant barrier, allowing only molecules that meet specific criteria such as size, charge, and hydrophilicity to pass due to its low permeability.259,279−281 This limitation makes enhancing permeation a primary focus of transdermal delivery systems.257 Sakulwech et al. utilized quaternized cyclodextrin-grafted chitosan (QCD-g-CS) and hyaluronic acid (HA) to form PIC micelles for skin delivery, adjusting the molecular weight of HA (low, medium, and high) at different molar ratios of HA to QCD-g-CS (e.g., 0.01, 0.3, and 1.5) (Figure 8A).137 They demonstrated that smaller PIC micelle sizes with a low molecular weight of HA (LHA_1.5) facilitated deeper skin penetration compared to those with a higher molecular weight HA. Although LHA_1.5 exhibited a penetration depth between 160 and 190 μm, the practical relevance of this improvement remains unclear due to the minor difference in penetration compared to free LHA (Figure 8B). Shigefuji et al. evaluated the skin penetration of PIC micelles composed of poly-l-lysine hydrochloride (HANP) and HA, observing that the micelles significantly enhanced skin permeability compared to free HA.282 While free HA primarily stayed on the skin surface, the PIC micelles were able to penetrate deeper into the stratum corneum through the intercellular route, a result further confirmed by increased adsorption to biological membranes. Moreover, morphological changes in the PIC micelles caused by skin ions suggested that the complexes disintegrate after penetration, enabling HA to function effectively within the skin layers.

Invasive Routes

An invasive route involves delivering drugs directly into the body through techniques, such as intravenous, intramuscular, and subcutaneous injections. These methods bypass external barriers, including the skin or GI tract, resulting in a rapid drug effect. For example, intravenous injection delivers drugs directly into the bloodstream, offering immediate systemic effects and precise dosing control. However, invasive methods also come with risks, including unintended toxic effects on nontarget tissues and patient discomfort, which often lead to insufficient therapeutic outcomes to patient needs.283

Recent studies have utilized injectable hydrogels that can be precisely located at the site of interest and release drugs with minimal invasiveness, thereby reducing the risk of systemic side effects.167,264,266 Moreover, these hydrogels exhibit sol–gel transition properties, enabling them to undergo a transition from liquid to gel phase under physiological conditions.167,266 This characteristic enhances their ability to conform to irregular tissue surfaces and ensure localized, sustained drug release.167 Hydrogels are also designed to enhance bioadhesion, as their mechanical and structural properties mimic the extracellular matrix.167,264 An example of this is the work by Vignesh et al., who developed injectable coacervate-based hydrogels using CS and HA to encapsulate deferoxamine (DFO) loaded nanoparticles (Figure 9A).135 Their study demonstrated that the hydrogel exhibited shear-thinning properties, allowing the gel to flow easily during injection but regaining structure once the stress was removed (Figure 9B). This property is important for maintaining the gel at the target site after administration without it spreading to unwanted tissues.284 Furthermore, the hydrogels exhibited a prolonged release of DFO compared to bare drug-loaded hydrogels (Figure 9C).135 Another example is the work by Awada et al., who proposed composite hydrogels by integrating fibrin gel with heparin/poly(ethylene argininylaspartate diglyceride) (PEAD) bulk coacervates for intraventricular injection.285 The hydrogels were designed for the sequential release of three proteins: fibroblast growth factor (FGF-2), stromal-cell-derived factor (SPF-1α), and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 3 (TIMP-3). FGF-2 and SDF-1α were incorporated into heparin/PEAD complexes, while free TIMP-3 was embedded within the gel (Figure 9D). The high affinity of FGF-2 and SPF-1α for heparin enabled their sustained release over 6 weeks, with a loading capacity of 97 and 98%, respectively, whereas free TIMP-3 showed release within 1 week (Figure 9E). Moreover, the release studies illustrated that the gel systems first released TIMP-3, followed by the other proteins, indicating their potential for the controlled delivery of growth factors and cytokines.

On-Demand Release in Coacervates

Intelligent drug carriers using coacervates have been developed by introducing on-demand drug release strategies, responding to both endogenous and exogenous stimuli.286,287 Endogenous stimuli are typically specific to diseased tissue, enabling the targeted drug action.287 Endogenous stimuli are associated with variations in pH and glutathione (GSH) concentration due to oxidative stress or specifically overexpressed enzymes.287,288 For example, the tumor microenvironment is characterized by reduced pH and elevated enzymatic or redox activity, which has inspired the design of coacervate-based carriers that release anticancer drugs in response to the endogenous triggers.289 On the other hand, exogenous stimuli are associated with temperature, light, ultrasound, or magnetic/electric field.286 With these stimuli-responsive characteristics, the coacervates enable us to improve the bioavailability of drugs at the disease site. Leveraging these triggers for controlled drug release offers significant potential for disease treatment by preventing an initial burst release.290 Stimuli-triggered drug release has emerged as an attractive strategy, with engineered drug carriers capable of responding to specific endogenous or exogenous cues.291 These stimuli induce structural changes in the delivery platform, such as swelling, disassembly, or degradation, which ultimately result in drug release at the desired tissues.286 We will introduce representative release strategies in response to endogenous stimuli, such as pH, redox responsive species, enzymes, and glucose.

pH-Responsive Release

pH-responsive drug carriers have attracted significant interest for cancer treatments, as they target both the slightly acidic extracellular tumor environment and the more acidic endosomes.159,292 In these systems, the surface charge shifts from negative to positive as the pH changes from basic or neutral in the bloodstream to mildly acidic at the drug release site.293 Negatively charged drug carriers extend circulation time in the bloodstream, avoiding uptake by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), while positively charged carriers facilitate rapid drug release and cellular internalization due to their affinity for negatively charged cell membranes.293,294 Wang et al. designed a pH-responsive bulk coacervate composed of antimicrobial peptides, Magainin-I, and a pH-sensitive charged polymer, 2,3-dimethyl maleic anhydride (DA) modified ε-poly(lysine) (EPL-DA). This coacervate disintegrated at slightly acidic pH (pH 5.0–6.0), as found in bacterial-infected tissues, while remaining stable at physiological pH (pH 7.4) (Figure 10A).295 As the pH shifted from 7.4 to 6.0, the surface charge of EPL-DA/Magainin-I complexes flipped from negative to positive, leading to structural collapse and the release of Magainin-I (Figure 10B). Moreover, EPL-DA/Magainin-I coacervates exhibited greater antibacterial activity at pH 6.0 compared with free Magainin-I and pH-insensitive EPL modified by succinic anhydride (EPL-SA) complexes (Figure 10C). Similarly, Wang and co-workers designed pH-responsive PIC micelles using a pH-sensitive diblock copolymer, methoxy poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(N(ε)-((1-carboxy-cis-cyclohexene)-2-carbonyl)-l-lysine) (mPEG-b-PCLL) and an antitumor drug, doxorubicin (DOX).296 The PIC micelles maintained a stable hydrodynamic diameter at pH 7.4 but swelled and rapidly collapsed in the slightly acidic tumor microenvironment (pH 6.8), especially in the more acidic intercellular spaces (pH 5.5). The study highlighted the potential for selective intracellular drug delivery in tumors.

Redox-Responsive Release

Redox-responsive drug carriers have been widely utilized for delivering antitumor agents, leveraging the higher concentration of glutathione (GSH) in cancer cells compared to normal cells.297 Chenglong et al. developed a redox-responsive bulk coacervate using dextran-based copolymers for DNA delivery into the cell nucleus.71 They utilized dextran-graft-poly[ethyl-3(N,N-dimethylamino) acrylate] (Dex-g-P(3-DAASE)) and dextran-graft-poly(2-diethylaminoethyl methacrylate) (Dex-g-PDEAEMA) as polycations. Each copolymer formed a coacervate with DNA, and the drug loading and redox responsiveness of the two dextran-based complexes were evaluated through gel electrophoresis at varying polycation-to-DNA weight ratios. The results showed that Dex-g-P(3-DAASE) complexes showed stable DNA encapsulation with no release, regardless of varying weight ratios (Figure 11A). This stability is attributed to structural differences, as the dimethylamino groups in Dex-g-P(3-DAASE) bind DNA more tightly, leading to encapsulation without release, even under exposure to GSH. In contrast, Dex-g-PDEAEMA complexes progressively released DNA with increasing polymer ratios (Figure 11B). When exposed to GSH, Dex-g-PDEAEMA demonstrated significant DNA release after 24 hours. The weaker diethylamino groups in Dex-g-PDEAEMA facilitate this DNA release in response to GSH, distinguishing it from the Dex-g-P(3-DAASE) system. The study highlighted the potential of Dex-g-PDAEMA coacervates as vehicles for gene therapies targeting cancer.

Enzyme-Responsive Release

Enzyme-responsive drug carriers leverage the fact that certain enzymes are overexpressed in the tumor microenvironment, leading to excessive enzyme secretion compared to normal tissues.298,299 Insua et al. developed enzyme-responsive PIC micelles designed to release the cationic antimicrobial polymer, poly(ethylenimine) (PEI), specifically upon encountering Pseudomonas aeruginosa.130 They used an anionic peptide containing a specific sequence (P1SH) as the polyanion to interact with PEI, which can be cleaved by P. aeruginosa’s elastase (LasB) (Figure 12A). Depolymerization of the PIC micelles was characterized by dynamic light scattering at 37 °C, revealing a decrease in scattering intensity over time upon exposure to LasB, which indicated micelle degradation, whereas those treated with human leukocyte elastase (HLE) or without enzyme initially swelled and then showed a complete decrease in scattering intensity without structural instability (Figure 12B). Furthermore, these PIC micelles were selectively degraded by LasB, produced by the pathogenic P. aeruginosa strain PAO1V, but not by a strain lacking the LasA and LasB genes (ΔlasAB), serving as a LasB-negative control and resulting in the release of PEI (Figure 12C).

Glucose-Responsive Release

In the design of a glucose-responsive drug carrier, glucose-responsive elements such as glucose oxidase (Gox), catalase (CAT), phenylboronic acid (PBA), and glucose-binding proteins play a crucial role in inducing structural changes in response to glucose concentration fluctuations, enabling dynamic, glucose-triggered drug release.300,301 For example, Lim et al. discussed glucose-responsive bulk coacervates using Gox for insulin delivery in diabetes treatment.302 They utilized histidine-rich beak (DgHBP-2) peptides containing pH-sensitive species and encapsulated insulin and Gox within a coacervate formed by the DgHBP-2 peptides. As glucose diffuses into the coacervate, Gox converts it into gluconic acid, creating an acidic environment that dissolves the coacervates, resulting in insulin release (Figure 13A). The presence of Gox and glucose significantly accelerates insulin release compared to other combinations, demonstrating the ability of the coacervate to trigger drug release (Figure 13B).

Conclusion

Complex coacervates offer a promising and versatile platform for drug delivery, distinguished by their rapid and reversible assembly as well as their capacity to selectively encapsulate drugs. This review highlights the critical role of polymer origin, architecture, and assembly combinations in influencing the phase behavior and functional properties of coacervates. First, the origin and structure of the component polymers serve as the foundational framework in designing the hierarchical architecture of coacervates. Synthetic polymers provide precise control over backbone chemistry to better predict phase transition behavior, while biological polymers, including polysaccharides, nucleic acids, polypeptides, and proteins, offer diverse functionalities suitable for therapeutic and diagnostic applications. However, predicting the phase behavior of biological polymers, especially those with complex structures such as proteins, poses challenges. To address this, modifications and conjugation of the biological polymers, such as enhancing solubility and forming block copolymers, have been effective in improving stability and functionality.

Understanding the intrinsic properties of the component polymers and their impact on coacervate formation is crucial for the next step, which determines coacervate architecture by varying the combinations of the polymer assembly. For example, homopolymer–homopolymer combinations typically form bulk coacervates, whereas diblock or triblock copolymer assemblies can create more sophisticated structures, including PIC micelles, vesicles, and physically cross-linked gels. By manipulating structural design factors such as chain length, polymer concentration, and mixing ratios in homopolymer assemblies, as well as adjusting block length and the neutral-to-charged block ratio in multiblock copolymer assemblies, it is possible to achieve phase transitions at the macro-, micro-, and nanoscales, though further research is needed to fully understand the mechanisms behind these nanophase transitions in polyelectrolyte complexes.

The coacervate architectures and material properties produced by various polymer assembly combinations can be tailored to optimize drug delivery for specific administration routes. These carriers can be engineered into forms that match the preferred architecture of drug delivery systems, such as nanoscale particles for oral delivery, gel-like patches for transdermal applications, and injectable hydrogels for invasive routes. This customization enhances cellular uptake, bioadhesion, and circulation time, ultimately improving the bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy.

However, although a few well-known drug delivery routes have been extensively studied, research into other potential routes, such as intranasal, pulmonary, or ocular delivery, remains limited. Therefore, for coacervates to be effective as drug carriers, it is crucial to first meet the specific requirements of each delivery route, which necessitates a deeper understanding of the structural and environmental factors that influence their behavior. By fine-tuning these aspects, coacervates can be tailored to fulfill the demands of diverse delivery systems. This insight holds great promise for precision medicine, suggesting that coacervate-based carriers could leverage nanotechnology to develop smart drug delivery systems, potentially revolutionizing future healthcare.
