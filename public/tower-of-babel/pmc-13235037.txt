Background

Patient and public involvement in research is now an essential component of research activity rather than an optional extra. It is embedded in policy and regulatory frameworks and is often a prerequisite for funding. The UK standards for public involvement offer a template for promoting “good “practice in research [1]. The Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) launched the Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR) to build national capacity for patient and public engagement in research [2]. The Patient Centered Outcomes Research Institute in the United States funds patient -centered comparative clinical effectiveness research and in doing so adds to the body of literature that supports the shift in healthcare research from passive patient engagement to active patient contribution [3].

The Kidney Warriors Foundation (KWF), a non-governmental patient advocacy organisation in India with over 10,000 members initiated a survey on prescription practices and adherence to medications amongst patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Chronic Kidney Disease affects an estimated 10% of the global population in 2021 [4]. India has an increasing CKD prevalence of 13.24% with rural populations being disproportionately affected. There is a regional variation within India with prevalence ranging from 4.1% to 32.7% across different states [5].

Using our experience with a patient led survey conducted by a NGO in India, we share insights and reflections on the opportunities and challenges in patient led research. Whilst these are unique to the Indian context, we believe that there are transferrable lessons for conducting research in other international settings, particularly in the context of ensuring inclusion and equity of access to participation.

Designing the project

The committee

“Who is at the table? “

Patient and public engagement refers to the active participation of patients, caregivers and other members of the public in health-related research to enhance the quality and relevance of the chosen projects. Arnstein proposes a ladder of participation in her seminal work on citizen involvement [6]. The research and scientific committee established in 2023 deliberated research priorities meaningful to patients, with equal representation of patients and physicians, and patient partners selected independently of the physicians. The subject of medication adherence was proposed by a patient representative. The patient representatives led on draft development, revision, pilot testing and expanding language accessibility. The abstracts were coauthored by patient representatives and statistical tests were commissioned by the NGO with an external agency. The corresponding author of this submission is a patient representative.

Priority setting

“Who decides what matters most “

Any collaborative effort requires a clear vision and aims. The World Health Organisation defines “priority setting” as an interpersonal activity to identify research questions and topics with the greatest potential for public benefit [7]. It is widely seen as useful in practical terms but there is a paucity of evidence to support its value in terms of patient centered outcomes. One can argue that even with the most equal of coalitions, there is a bias in the process of priority setting. The value of patient involvement in priority setting has been studied effectively with regards to rare diseases where because of the limited availability of funding for a disproportionately large number of rare diseases, the priorities of research initiatives and industry developments may not always align with patient views [8]. Fleurence et al. published a study that demonstrated that the priorities selected by researchers and patient partners converged after face-to-face discussion i.e. consensus was reached. What it failed to demonstrate were fiscal benefits for research funding or a positive impact on inclusion of more patient centered studies [9]. The James Lind Alliance for Priority Setting Partnerships in the UK aims to bring researchers, patients and carers together on an equal footing to collaborate and influence policy and research and ultimately health related outcomes [10]. Rajendran et al. in their systematic review on stakeholder engagement in healthcare research include 25 published studies that have examined stakeholder engagement in healthcare research. Notably none of these studies have had significant patient partnerships or representation [11].

KWF set up its research and scientific committee in 2023. One of the aims of the research and scientific committee was to develop surveys and questionnaires that could inform research, policy and practice in India. The two proposals submitted by the Chairman of KWF and one of the patient partners, a researcher were about awareness regarding phosphorus metabolism in patients with CKD and on medication adherence amongst patients with CKD. The committee chose a survey on prescriptions and medicines adherence as their first project. The survey on prescriptions and medicines adherence was chosen unanimously by the committee because it was agreed that the findings would have the greatest potential to inform both policy and behaviours. The proof-of-concept arose from a general feeling amongst patients with CKD that medication adherence amongst patients with CKD was not just to do with patient behaviours but also to do with the variations in prescriptions of medicines. The project’s core aspiration was to supplement existing clinical data on prescription practices by capturing the often-overlooked patient experience of medication management. While substantial research exists on how prescriptions are written and clinical outcomes measured, there remains a significant gap in understanding the patient journey- the real-world challenges, decision-making processes, and behavioural factors that lead to medication mishaps, non-adherence, or unintended consequences [12].

Creating the survey

“How do we ask about what matters most “

There is a large evidence base of scientific literature that informs the development of methodologically sound, scientifically accurate and easily comprehensible questionnaire surveys for healthcare research. NHS England has published a guide in 2018 listing the three main elements of a patient questionnaire namely asking the right question, asking the right people the right question and using the data accurately [13]. There are resources such as the Picker Institute toolkits that offer guidance in designing content and emphasize the value of collecting data but also using narratives and experiences [14].

Focus group methodology is supported by a rich evidence base for creating patient surveys [15]. It provides rich contextual detail, facilitates organic growth of ideas and relationships, aligns language with the intent and allows rapid identification of themes. The limitations do include dominance, social desirability bias and the need for a skilled moderator to draw out actionable views and ideas [16].

We used the focus group methodology for our survey, and we arranged three focus groups. One group consisted exclusively patient partners with a non-nephrologist physician as the moderator, the second consisted of the physicians with the chairman of KWF as the moderator and the third was a mixed group with 3 nephrologists and 2 patients with the non-nephrologist physician as moderator. The committee developed an initial draft questionnaire that was then presented in a subsequent focus group discussion involving 8 patients as part of this participatory approach. Modifications were made based on direct patient feedback as they shared their multiple experiences of dealing with prescriptions. It was interesting to note that the key domains identified by the mixed group concurred largely with the themes identified by the patients focus groups. What was a draft document with 10 questions went through many revisions and eventually consisted of 31 questions which were broadly categorised into the following domains i.e. demographic details, purchase and procurement of medicines, features of prescriptions and medicines adherence and behaviours. The content and language were largely guided by the patients focus group discussions with rearrangements and revisions by the physicians to improve extractability of information.

Pilot testing

“What works“

Pilot testing is often recommended before surveys are shared widely. Pilot testing or feasibility testing is a prerequisite for clinical trials because they allow researchers to test feasibility, assess recruitment potential and minimise potential for failure. It allows optimisation of the survey prior to release [17].

There was much discussion around the platforms for distribution, and it was agreed that testing the questionnaire on a small group of respondents would reduce technical glitches, improve usability and support dissemination.

This draft questionnaire was pilot tested, where 31 patients, including those from the focus group discussion, answered the questions. Pilot testing the survey highlighted problem areas (income status, patient behaviour), reduced measurement error, reduced respondent burden (limiting number of questions), helped determine whether respondents were interpreting questions correctly, and ensured that the order of options had no impact on respondents’ choice. We tested practical aspects such as survey duration, platform specific glitches, response rates, and data completeness. This offered us an opportunity to revise the questionnaire before dissemination.

The pilot survey resulted in removing repetitive questions, optimising the income brackets and discarding the question on marital status. We then agreed on the final version of the survey which consisted of 30 questions in 5 domains namely demographic details, procurement of medicines, quality of prescriptions, usage of medicines and adherence.

Distribution of survey

“Who can participate“

Accessibility is an important factor in improving participation by patients. The term language justice recognises the autonomy of all patients to speak and receive information in their primary language [18]. Language justice helps redress the imbalance of power between providers and researchers on one side and patients on the other. The value of language justice goes beyond acknowledgement of human rights to facilitate co-creation and collaboration.

A critical factor was the survey’s availability in five languages (English, Hindi, Marathi, Telugu and Kannada), translated by patients themselves, which extended participation beyond the English-speaking population. The multilingual approach yielded significant results, where 56% of all responses (398 out of 708 total responses) were in the vernacular, demonstrating the importance of linguistic accessibility in healthcare research in India [19]. This approach was consistent with the principle of community embedded knowledge production, recognising that individuals with lived experience and linguistic familiarity are best placed to convey meaning accurately. Translations were informally reviewed by community members prior to deployment; however, a formal back-translation protocol was not employed. The final survey was disseminated through a multi-pronged strategy: nephrologists encouraged patient participation in their clinics, while KWF leveraged its volunteer patient network and social media platforms (Facebook, WhatsApp) to create awareness about the survey’s importance through patient-generated videos and testimonials.

Challenges

“What did not work“

The National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants mandate ethics committee registration for all research including community or participatory research. It offers two pathways for ethics approval, namely the Institutional Ethics Committees (requiring institutional affiliation) or Independent Ethics Committees requiring formal legal registration, governance structures, and Department of Health Research fees [20].

Additionally, patient experience surveys fall into a regulatory grey area. While Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) guidelines acknowledge flexibility for “low or minimal risk survey research,” no clear simplified pathway exists for non-interventional patient-reported studies [21]. This gap forces patient-led research to either proceed without formal ethics approval or remain unconducted, effectively excluding patient voices from healthcare evidence generation.

KWF are in a unique position as grassroots advocacy organisation across many states in India to harness the collective voice of patients with CKD but the lack of an institutional affiliation and the prohibitive costs of setting up an independent ethics committee prevented us from securing ethics approval. We created an ethics checklist to satisfy ourselves that the project was conceived and implement within an ethical framework, albeit informal. Although the study included meaningful engagement efforts, it lacked a formal equity and cultural competence framework in line with internationally accepted standards. Limitations included no structured cultural assessment, insufficiently rigorous translation methods, and a demographically skewed sample reflecting barriers to equitable participation.

Information governance

Information governance is crucial in safeguarding patient data and preventing unauthorised disclosures to third parties. The UK General Data Protection Regulation ( GDPR) ensures that organisations have a data protection policy, and individuals are aware of their right to privacy of their personal data. In India, the governance of patient data in healthcare and research is shaped by multiple regulatory and ethical frameworks. Medical practitioners are guided by the National Medical Commission’s professional conduct regulations, which emphasise informed consent and patient confidentiality, while researchers follow the ICMR’s ethical guidelines for biomedical research. These frameworks establish strong principles of confidentiality and consent but were developed largely in the context of clinical interactions rather than contemporary digital data flows. With the enactment of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, India now has a comprehensive legal framework governing the processing of digital personal data. However, the application of the Act to routine clinical communication and health research remains unclear, particularly with respect to informal digital data sharing for care coordination, expert consultation, and research purposes. The absence of detailed, research-specific operational guidance under the Act increased responsibility on researchers and healthcare organisations to interpret compliance requirements and to adopt robust ethical, consent-based, and governance safeguards when collecting, sharing, and using patient data for research [22].

We obtained informed consent for the survey and have declared that the information will only be used for the purposes of a scientific article.

Funding and resource limitations

Unlike university-based research that typically has access to grants, institutional budgets, and established funding streams, patient-led organizations operate with minimal financial resources. The absence of funding raised barriers at multiple points in the journey of this project ranging from fees for ethics committee approval to charges for professional data management systems to meet privacy and security standards or capacity to train patient researchers in formal research methodologies or for formal legal consultation to establish liability frameworks protecting both participants and researchers, and insufficient resources to cover publication fees for academic journals. These financial constraints not only hinder the research process but also result in the exclusion of valuable patient knowledge from formal healthcare research ecosystems. This study shows that patient-led research is feasible within an Indian NGO setting, with strengths including patient–clinician collaboration and community-driven design. However, developing robust, standardised frameworks—such as GRIPP2-SF or PPEET—and adapting international guidance like PFMD to the Indian context is essential to ensure structured, equitable, and meaningful patient involvement.

Despite the challenges, the results of our survey (708 respondents) were presented by the Chair of KWF at the Indian Society of Nephrology conference in December 2025. The results of the survey confirmed that although overall satisfaction with prescriptions were high, there is potential to improve adherence to medications in patients with CKD by standardising prescriptions and ensuring that duration, cautions and side effects were explained to patients [23].

Conclusion

This study is a maiden attempt by a non-governmental organisation at co-created research where questions, methodology, and dissemination strategies originated from and were shaped by patient priorities, with patients and doctors constituting the steering committee. This truly collaborative approach between patient partners and healthcare professionals aims to create a model for patient-centered research in the Indian CKD community that will benefit all patients. We have moved away from mere patient representation to patient authorship [24]. We expect that by using this model, we will be able to demonstrate a linear and pragmatic road map for conducting similar research for patients with chronic illnesses. We hope that our data will reinforce our understanding of patient behaviours and inform our policies in the areas of public awareness, provider education and digital support. We have demonstrated that by altering the power balance between patients and healthcare professionals, meaningful research can be undertaken.
