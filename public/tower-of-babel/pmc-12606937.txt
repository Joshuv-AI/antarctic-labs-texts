Associations between dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients with the risk of migraine: a prospective study of 202,656 participants
Authors: Zhengming Shan, Mianxue Liu, Lily Zhang, Yu Zhang, Wanbin Huang, Liu Yang, Zheman Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies have indicated that mitochondrial dysfunction contributed to migraine development, yet the links between dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients and migraine risk haven’t been explored in prospective cohort studies.MethodsData from 202,656 UK Biobank participants were used to explore associations between dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients, including magnesium, riboflavin (vitamin B2), thiamine (vitamin B1), niacin (vitamin B3), vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate (vitamin B9), with migraine risk. For analysis, Cox proportional hazards models, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and restricted cubic spline plots were used.ResultsAfter a median follow-up of 13.25 years, 1844 (0.9%) participants developed migraines. Those with migraines had substantially lower intakes of mitochondrial-related nutrients. In multivariable Cox regression, each 1SD increase in niacin intake was linked to a 3% migraine risk reduction (HR: 0.97; 95% CI: 0.94–0.99; p = 0.010*), as was each 1SD increase in vitamin B12 intake showed a 4% risk reduction (HR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.93–0.99; p = 0.040*). Subgroup analyses showed no interactions between nutrient intakes and gender or age. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of these associations. Significant nonlinear and negative associations between magnesium, niacin, and vitamin B12 with migraine were observed.ConclusionsHigher dietary intake of mitochondrial-related nutrients was associated with a reduced risk of migraine, with niacin and vitamin B12 showing the most stable protective effects. Our study suggests that adjusting dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients may offer a promising strategy for the prevention and management of migraine.Keywords: Dietary intake, Nutrient, Mitochondria, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Migraine

Introduction

Migraine is a prevalent and severe neurological disorder affecting approximately one billion individuals globally, imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden [1]. In clinical practice, migraine treatment primarily relies on pharmacotherapy, encompassing acute medication intervention in acute migraine attacks, as well as preventive medication for episodic and chronic migraine. Increasingly, novel drugs such as monoclonal antibodies targeting calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and its receptors, as well as 5-HT1F receptor agonists, are also recommended for use [2]. In recent years, some dietary patterns have been proven effective in preventing migraine, including the ketogenic diet [3, 4], low-fat diet [5], high omega-3/low omega-6 diet [6], Mediterranean diet [7], and Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet [4].

According to clinical reports, migraine can be triggered by a number of factors in daily life, including behavioral factors such as stress, mental fatigue, and changes in sleep habits; physiological factors such as hypoglycemia, hypoxia, infection, and estrogen; environmental factors such as noise and air pollutants; and dietary factors such as alcohol, nitrates, and aspartame [8]. Notably, all of the above predisposing factors are associated with mitochondrial function and oxidative stress [8]. Mitochondrial-related nutrients have been shown to be closely related to the aforementioned processes, and thus, it may be hypothesized that the dietary intake of such nutrients could represent a novel approach for migraine therapeutics.

Some nutrients enriched in the diet have been shown to be associated with mitochondrial function, such as magnesium, riboflavin, thiamine, niacin, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate. Magnesium plays a crucial role in regulating mitochondrial function, influencing biochemical processes like adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA) synthesis, and intracellular calcium ion regulation [9]. Riboflavin is vital for mitochondrial electron transport in respiratory chain complexes I and II and participates in fatty acid oxidation within the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Thiamine is essential for converting pyruvate to acetyl-coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA). Niacin is a precursor of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), which is crucial in the electron transport chain. Vitamin B12 is a key cofactor in forming succinyl coenzyme A (succinyl-CoA), an important TCA cycle metabolite [10]. In mitochondria, vitamin B6 aids in transaminase and decarboxylation reactions and acts as a cofactor for side-chain cleavage enzymes. Folate is involved in the initiation of mitochondrial protein synthesis, serine hydroxymethyl transferase catalyzes glycine synthesis, and serine cleavage forms 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate [11].

Currently, numerous researches have examined the associations between dietary intake of various nutrients, such as magnesium [12], riboflavin [13], thiamine [14], niacin [15], vitamin B6 [16], vitamin B12 [17], and folate [16], with migraine, finding that these nutrients may offer protective benefits against migraine episodes. Nevertheless, these previous investigations are limited by their cross-sectional design and small overall sample sizes. Therefore, it is still necessary to design a prospective study to explore further the associations between dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients and the risk of migraine.

In this study, we analyzed the associations between dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients and the risk of migraine using the data from the UK Biobank, a large prospective cohort study, aiming to provide dietary guidance for migraine patients.

Methods

Study population

The data for this study were acquired from the UK Biobank, with the project application number 143,136. UK Biobank is a large-scale prospective database of biospecimens, which recruited more than 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years between 2006 and 2010 [18]. The data includes sociodemographics (for instance, lifestyle, cognitive function, physical measurements, and biological samples), dietary intake, environmental factors, imaging, genetics, disease diagnosis, health-related outcomes, etc. Some of the data is still being updated on an ongoing basis. All participants have provided informed consent. Researchers using the UK Biobank were not required to provide an additional ethical review (see website for details: https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/).

Baseline information was obtained for a total of 502,366 participants in this study. 291,418 participants missing the data of dietary nutrients intake, 1007 participants missing one or more covariates, 6740 participants with migraine at baseline, and 562 participants missing follow-up data were successively excluded, resulting in a final total of 202,656 participants without migraine at baseline being enrolled in the study, which included 200,812 participants without migraine, and 1844 participants with migraine after end of follow-up (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of inclusion and exclusion criteria

Migraine diagnosis

The ICD-10 (field 41270) is a summary of the distinct diagnosis codes a participant has had recorded across all their hospital inpatient records. In ICD-10, the code for migraine is G43. The corresponding date each diagnosis was first recorded across all their episodes in hospital is given in field ID: 41280. Self-reported noncancer disease was collected by verbal interview in the assessment center, where participants were asked to report the type of illness they had had. To ensure the participants who were included in the prospective cohort study did not suffer from migraine, participants with migraine were excluded based on the ICD-10 (field IDs: 41270 and 41280) and self-reported noncancer disease codes (field ID: 20002) [19, 20].

Since field 20,002 was only collected by UKB when participants were recruited, in the prospective cohort population, migraine was primarily diagnosed based on ICD-10 diagnosis (field ID: 41270) and date of first ICD-10 diagnosis (field ID: 41280).

Assessment of dietary intake

Dietary nutrient intakes were evaluated using the Oxford WebQ method based on 24-hour recall (category ID: 100117) [21]. Over 200,000 UK Biobank participants completed at least one assessment between 2009 and 2012, measuring nutrients like energy, protein, fat, sugars, fatty acids, carbohydrates, fiber, vitamins, and micronutrients like calcium, magnesium, and zinc. In this study, seven nutrients related to mitochondrial function, including magnesium (field ID: 26025), riboflavin (field ID: 26035), thiamine (field ID: 26034), niacin (field ID: 26054), vitamin B6 (field ID: 26020), vitamin B12 (field ID: 26021) and folate (field ID: 26022) were included. In addition, the dietary nutrient intakes of participants who completed two or more dietary assessments were averaged and used for subsequent data analysis to avoid bias. For those who completed only one instance, the value from that instance is used directly to maximize the utilization of all available data.

Selection of covariates

Sociodemographic information, lifestyle, and health status related variables were incorporated as covariates in this study. Sociodemographic information comprised age (field ID: 21022), gender (field ID: 31), race (field ID: 21000), education level (field ID: 6138), and Townsend Deprivation Index (field ID: 22189). Lifestyle and health status included smoking (field ID: 20116), alcohol intake (field ID: 20117), body mass index (BMI, field ID: 21001), sleep duration (field ID: 1160), screen time (field ID: 1070 and 1080), hypertension (field ID: 6150), coronary heart disease (field ID: 6150), stroke (field ID: 6150) and diabetes (field ID: 2443) Other covariates which may influence dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients were also adopted, included the status of using vitamin supplements (field ID: 104670), energy (field ID: 26002), protein (field ID: 26005).

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics of the participants were presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (quartile) for continuous variables (p-values were calculated by the t-test or Kruskal-Wallis) and as percentages for categorical variables (p-values were calculated by chi-square test). The multicollinearity relationship between dietary intake of each nutrient and covariates was calculated according to the variance inflation factor (VIF) for subsequent statistical analyses. If the value of VIF was greater than 10, it was usually considered that there was serious multicollinearity, and VIF close to 1 indicated that there was no collinearity.

In this study, Cox proportional hazards models were used to investigate the associations between dietary intake of mitochondrial-related nutrients and the risk of migraine. First, three Cox proportional hazards models were constructed using the original data as continuous variables. Model 1 was adjusted for age and gender, model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, race, education level, and the Townsend Deprivation Index, and model 3 (the full model) was based on model 2 with the addition of BMI, smoking, alcohol intake, sleep duration, screen time, vitamin supplements, energy, protein, coronary heart disease, stroke, hypertension, and diabetes as the adjusted variables. Second, the original data were log-transformed and standardized by Z-score as continuous variables. Three similar Cox proportional hazards models were constructed on the scale of each SD increase in dietary intake of nutrients, and hazard ratios (HR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values were calculated. Third, Cox regression analysis was performed on the log-transformed and Z-score standardized data, which were divided into categorical variables Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4 based on quartiles, and each category was represented by its median. HR, 95% CI, and p-value were calculated for each SD increase in nutrient intake, and a test for trend was performed with Q1 as a reference.

Subgroup analyses included analyses of subgroups according to gender (males and females) and subgroups according to age (≤ 55 and > 55 years). Full-model Cox regression was constructed with log-transformed and Z-score standardized dietary nutrient intake data as continuous variables, and HR, 95% CI, and p-values were calculated for each SD increment (controlling for FDR by the Benjamini-Hochberg method). Interactions (p for interaction) were calculated by likelihood ratio tests comparing the two Cox regression models before and after the addition of the product terms for dietary nutrient intakes and stratification variables.

To examine the robustness of the results of the association analyses, sensitivity analyses were performed in this study. The analyses included association analyses after excluding participants who used vitamin supplements and association analyses after excluding participants with abnormal dietary energy intake (< 2000 kJ and > 50,000 kJ). For sensitivity analyses, full-model Cox regressions were constructed with the raw data and log-transformed and Z-score standardized data of dietary nutrients as continuous variables, respectively. HRs, 95% CIs, and p-values were computed for each SD increment.

Based on the Cox proportional hazards model, restricted cubic spline plots were used to assess whether there was a linear distribution between dietary intakes of magnesium, riboflavin, thiamin, niacin, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate, with the risk of migraine.

All analyses were undertaken using the R statistical software, version 4.3.1. Two-sided p-values below 0.05 were interpreted as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 202,656 participants who did not suffer from migraine at baseline were included in this study. After a median follow-up time of 13.25 years, a total of 1844 (0.9%) participants were diagnosed with migraine. The mean age of participants with a migraine diagnosis was lower than that of participants without migraine. In participants with migraine, the proportion of females was higher. Additionally, the proportions of white people and participants with a current drinking status were slightly lower. The mean economic level, the level of dietary energy and protein were lower for participants with migraine, and the proportions of participants with short sleep duration (< 7 h), long screen time (> 4 h), vitamin supplements, a history of stroke, and hypertension were higher. These findings were all statistically significant differences between the two groups. However, there were no significant differences in education level, smoking status, co-morbid diabetes, or co-morbid coronary heart disease between the two groups (Table 1).

When the dietary intake was taken as a continuous variable, the associations between mitochondrial function-related nutrients and migraine risk, as shown by the three adjusted Cox proportional hazards models, were shown in the top part of Fig. 2. Subgroup analyses

As shown in Table 4, among female participants, each 1 SD increase in dietary intake of niacin (HR: 0.97; 95% CI: 0.94–0.99; p = 0.025*) and vitamin B12 (HR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.92–0.99; p = 0.048*) contributed to 3% and 4% lower risk of migraine, respectively. Among participants aged > 55 years, each 1 SD increase in dietary intake of niacin contributed to a 4% lower risk of migraine (HR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.93–0.98; p = 0.004*). The associations between dietary intake of the seven nutrients and migraine were generally consistent across the four subgroups: men, women, age ≤ 55 years, and age > 55 years. There was no interaction between dietary intake of each nutrient and gender or age (p for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

This study investigated the associations between dietary intake of mitochondrial-related nutrients, including magnesium, riboflavin, thiamine, niacin, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate, with the risk of migraine based on the UK Biobank. Using multivariate-adjusted full model Cox regression analyses, we found that dietary intake of all seven types of mitochondrial-related nutrients may have some efficacy in reducing migraine risk, with niacin and vitamin B12 showing the most stable results. Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the association results remained consistent across different populations. Magnesium, niacin, and vitamin B12 were found to be negatively and nonlinearly associated with the risk of migraine.

In recent years, the relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and migraine has gained considerable attention. Some drugs that have been used to treat migraine clinically can improve mitochondrial function. For instance, sodium valproate can reduce nitroglycerin-induced migraine in rats by modulating mitochondrial biogenesis and mitochondrial function [22]. Lamiditan, a 5-HT1F receptor agonist, has also been shown to induce mitochondrial biogenesis and enhance mitochondrial function in several studies [23–25]. These results imply that the changes in mitochondrial function may be related to the onset of migraine. Furthermore, a large number of clinical studies and animal experimental results have increasingly indicated that mitochondrial dysfunction may be involved in the pathogenesis of migraine [26–33]. Consequently, improving mitochondrial function may emerge as a promising therapeutic strategy for the management of migraine.

Previously, studies have shown that dietary patterns can influence the composition and metabolic activity of the gut microbiota, leading to changes in metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and bile acid derivatives that are involved in systemic energy regulation and modulation of neural signaling pathways [34]. This suggests that dietary intake can influence mitochondrial function, which in turn may affect neurological disorders such as migraine. A variety of nutrients have been demonstrated to have a significant relationship with mitochondrial function, including calcium, magnesium, and B vitamins (riboflavin, thiamin, niacin, biotin, vitamins B6, B12, folate, etc.), as well as vitamin A and vitamin C. These nutrients play integral roles in mitochondrial metabolic pathways, exhibiting complex and diverse mechanisms of action that collectively contribute to the maintenance of metabolic homeostasis of the internal environment. Notably, the relationship between homocysteine, which is metabolized by vitamins B6, B12, and folate, with the risk of migraine is inconclusive. However, a recent meta-analysis suggests that high plasma homocysteine levels may be a risk factor for migraine [35]. Reactive oxygen species may operate as a mediator in the interplay between homocysteine levels and mitochondrial function and homeostasis, which are intimately linked and have a complicated mechanism of action [36]. According to the findings of a recent study, folate attenuated the elevated homocysteine levels to reduce ethanol-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [37]. Additionally, the epigenetic level, aberrant DNA methylation has been associated with migraine [38–41], and deficiencies in vitamins B6, B12, and folate can lead to hypomethylation, thereby contributing to migraine pathogenesis [42].

Niacin serves as an essential cofactor in mitochondrial energy metabolism, playing an indispensable role in the production of ATP [43]. Insufficient dietary niacin may impair the mitochondrial respiratory chain and reduce ATP production [44], which has been consistently implicated in migraine pathophysiology [45]. Brain imaging studies on individuals with migraines have revealed modifications in energy metabolism within brain regions associated with sensory and pain processing, including the bilateral insula, premotor cortex, and secondary somatosensory cortex [45, 46]. These findings imply that repeated migraine episodes may intensify mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP depletion in these areas. Additional support for a link between mitochondrial dysfunction and migraine comes from comorbidities such as fibromyalgia (FM), which frequently co‑occurs with migraine and is thought to share pathophysiological mechanisms such as increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, accumulation of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage, and impaired energy metabolism [47–50]. For instance, studies have shown that fibromyalgia patients exhibit reduced blood monocyte levels of coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), elevated mitochondrial ROS production, and ATP concentrations approximately 70% of those in healthy controls—findings that correlate with greater headache severity and oxidative stress [51, 52]. Fatigue - a common feature in both chronic and episodic migraine - is also closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction [53, 54]. Proposed mechanisms include alterations in mitochondrial structure, reduced enzyme activity, oxidative/nitrosative stress, and impaired energy metabolism, including both ATP production and fatty acid oxidation [55]. Collectively, these findings reinforce the concept that disrupted mitochondrial bioenergetics, oxidative stress, and insufficient ATP availability are key mechanistic pathways linking niacin and other mitochondria-related nutrients to migraine. This mechanistic evidence raises the hypothesis that optimizing intake of such nutrients could offer a novel preventive or therapeutic approach, potentially with fewer side effects and better patient compliance than conventional migraine medications.

Clinically, several clinical randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been conducted to study the effects of supplementation with mitochondria-related nutrients for the treatment or prevention of migraine. The results of these studies have shown that oral high-dose magnesium is effective in preventing migraine and that intravenous magnesium sulfate is effective in the treatment of migraine with aura [56, 57]. Besides, the combination of magnesium and sodium valproate is significantly more effective than sodium valproate monotherapy in the treatment of migraine [58]. High-dose riboflavin is effective in preventing migraine attacks in adults [59]. However, neither medium-dose nor high-dose riboflavin has a significant effect on migraine in children [59–61]. A systematic review analyzing several clinical trials has shown that niacin is effective in the treatment of migraine [62]. In addition, a case study reported that two women with migraine had lower than normal blood thiamine concentrations and that intravenous thiamine supplementation improved headache symptoms [63]. The results of a recent RCT showed that supplementation with thiamine, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate alone or in combination with these nutrients reduced the frequency and severity of migraine attacks [64]. However, these clinical and observational studies share several limitations: sample sizes were often small, outcome definitions varied, and supplementation trials were primarily short-term interventions among patients with established migraine. Consequently, these findings cannot establish the long-term effects of habitual nutrient intake on migraine risk in the general population. Importantly, for most individuals, requirements for magnesium, riboflavin, thiamine, niacin, and other mitochondria-related nutrients can generally be met through a balanced and varied diet; supplementation should be considered only for populations who are unable to achieve adequate intake through diet al.one (e.g., individuals with malabsorption, restrictive diets, clinically confirmed deficiencies, or increased physiological needs), and always under medical supervision. Therefore, our analysis focus is on dietary intake rather than supplement use.

This study employed a prospective design to investigate the associations between the dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients and the risk of migraine, utilizing data from the UK Biobank. We found that dietary intake of mitochondrial-related nutrients was significantly lower in participants with migraine than in those without migraine. Examining the associations between dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients and the risk of migraine by Cox proportional hazards models, our results showed that for every 1 SD increase in dietary intake of the mitochondria-related nutrients magnesium, riboflavin, thiamine, niacin, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate, there was a 1–4% reduction in the risk of migraine onset. Our results suggest a protective effect of these mitochondria-related nutrients against migraine, which were consistent with previous results of RCT [56–60] and cross-sectional studies [12–15]. Subsequently, we performed stratified analyses according to age and gender to investigate the robustness of the association results across subgroups, which showed that the association results remained stable overall across the four groups: men, women, age ≧ 55 years, and age < 55 years. Sensitivity analyses showed overall consistency with previous results after excluding participants using vitamin supplements or those with abnormal dietary energy intake (< 2000 kJ and >50,000 kJ). The results of the restricted cubic spline plots showed that the dose-effect relationships between magnesium, niacin, and vitamin B12, and the risk of migraine were all nonlinearly distributed. Our results suggest that adjusting dietary intake of mitochondria-related nutrients may be effective in reducing the risk of migraine to a certain extent.

Also of note, a large number of studies have investigated the associations between dietary intake of nutrients and the risk of disease onset in a wide range of diseases. Several recent studies based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) found that dietary intake of nutrients, for instance, magnesium [12], riboflavin [13], thiamine [14], and niacin [15], exerts a protective effect against migraines. However, these studies were limited by small sample sizes, used cross-sectional designs, and imprecise diagnoses, which were simply based on the question “Have you experienced severe headaches or migraines in the past three months?”. Compared with previous studies, this study has the following advantages. First, the sample size of this trial is large, and the results are representative of the UK population of this age group as a whole. Second, a prospective design was used to examine the associations between dietary intake of nutrients and the risk of migraine. Third, in this study, migraine was diagnosed strictly according to a uniform criterion, ICD-10. The UK Biobank database, which incorporates the National Health Service of the United Kingdom, can recognise a wide range of disease outcomes from routine medical records [18]. Fourth, UK Biobank complements the disease records of the health service system by professionally assisting participants in completing self-reported disease status questionnaires, increasing the comprehensiveness of participants’ health status. Fifth, UK Biobank collected dietary intake data multiple times, which was more representative of a participant’s daily diet than the single-collection approach of other databases.

Besides, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the UK Biobank determined the presence of migraine mainly through inpatient diagnostic records, supplemented by self-reported data. This case ascertainment approach may have missed individuals with migraine who did not require hospitalization or formal diagnosis, potentially leading to an underestimation of migraine prevalence. Second, UK Biobank recruits predominantly white people, and the results of this trial may not apply to other ethnic groups. Third, dietary intake was assessed using the Oxford WebQ 24-hour dietary recall method. Although the UK Biobank investigated participants’ dietary intake several times (1–5 times), it did not adequately reflect participants’ daily diets, and the results are still subject to uncontrolled bias. Fourth, the UK Biobank did not collect specific information on migraine, including the frequency of attacks, the severity of symptoms, prophylactic medication therapy, and the specific subtypes of migraine, which precludes more granular analyses of whether nutrient intake associations differ by migraine phenotype. Fifth, although our results demonstrated significant associations between higher dietary intakes of mitochondria-related nutrients and a lower risk of migraine, the observational design precludes causal inference. It remains a long-standing and still relevant question whether such associations represent a true therapeutic effect on migraine pathophysiology, or merely an epiphenomenon of a generally healthier neuro-nutritional lifestyle. Our analyses could not fully disentangle the effects of individual nutrients from broader dietary patterns and lifestyle factors. Future randomized controlled trials and carefully designed intervention studies are warranted to determine whether intentional modification of dietary intake of mitochondrial-related nutrients can directly reduce migraine risk beyond the benefits of an overall balanced diet and healthy lifestyle.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this prospective study based on the UK Biobank investigated the associations between the intake of mitochondria-related nutrients and the risk of migraine. Our findings indicate that dietary intakes of most mitochondrial-related nutrients are negatively associated with the risk of migraine. Specifically, magnesium, niacin, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12 exhibit a nonlinear negative association with migraine risk, with niacin and vitamin B12 offering the most stable protective effect. This study provides epidemiological evidence for adjusting dietary intakes of mitochondrial-related nutrients in the prevention of migraine.
