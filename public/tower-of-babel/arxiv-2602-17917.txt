Interactions that reshape the interfaces of the interacting parties
David I. Spivak

Abstract
Polynomial functors model systems with interfaces: each polynomial specifies the outputs a system can produce and, for each output, the inputs it accepts. The bicategory 𝕆 ​ 𝐫𝐠 \mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg} of dynamic organizations [ Spi21 ] gives a notion of state-driven interaction patterns that evolves over time, but each system’s interface remains fixed throughout the interaction. Yet in many systems, the outputs sent and inputs received can reshape the interface itself: a cell differentiating in response to chemical signals gains or loses receptors; a sensor damaged by its input loses a channel; a neural network may grow its output resolution during training. Here we introduce polynomial trees , elements of the terminal ( u ⊲ u ) (\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}) -coalgebra where u \mathrm{u} is the universe polynomial, to model such systems: a polynomial tree is a coinductive tree whose nodes carry polynomials, and in which each round of interaction—an output chosen and an input received—determines a child tree, hence the next interface. We construct a monoidal closed category 𝐏𝐨𝐥𝐲𝐓𝐫 \mathbf{PolyTr} of polynomial trees, with coinductively-defined morphisms, tensor product, and internal hom. We then build a bicategory 𝕆 ​ 𝐫𝐠𝐓𝐫 \mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr} generalizing 𝕆 ​ 𝐫𝐠 \mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg} , whose hom-categories parametrize morphisms by state sets with coinductive action-and-update data. We provide a locally fully faithful functor 𝕆 ​ 𝐫𝐠 → 𝕆 ​ 𝐫𝐠𝐓𝐫 \mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}\to\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr} via constant trees, those for which the interfaces do not change through time. We illustrate the generalization by suggesting a notion of progressive generative adversarial networks, where gradient feedback determines when the image-generation interface grows to a higher resolution.

Chapter 1
Introduction

Wiring diagrams are a common way to represent information flow, e.g. block diagrams in control theory
[
BE15
,
BSZ14
]
, compartmental models in epidemiology
[
Lib+22
]
, and many other graphical formalisms for compositional systems
[
Sel10
,
Spi13
,
VSL15
]
.

Each box has an
interface
—a collection of input and output ports—and the wiring specifies how outputs of one box flow to inputs of another, and how that whole structure implements a box at a higher level of abstraction.

But these wiring diagrams are
static
, in contrast to many real-world settings, where the interaction pattern is
dynamic
. That is, the stuff that flows between entities can affect the very channels by which they flow: water carves a deeper channel for itself; a “disconnect” signal causes the disconnection of a signal-carrying wire. In training an artificial neural network, the accuracy of the informational transformation cascading through a model causes changes in the connection strengths (weights) constituting that very transformation.

This phenomenon—interaction patterns that reconfigure in response to what flows through them—has been formalized as
dynamic categorical structures
: categories, operads, and monoidal categories enriched in the bicategory
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}
of dynamic organizations
[
SS22
]
. In this framework, each box is assigned a fixed polynomial interface
p\in\mathbf{Poly}
(a functor
\sum_{i:I}{\mathcal{y}}^{A_{i}}\colon\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
, where
I
is the set of
positions
and
A_{i}
is the set of
directions
at position
i
; see
Chapter

2
), and the wiring between boxes is governed by a coalgebra whose state evolves over time. Applications include prediction markets, where trader successes reconfigure the wealth distribution, as well as gradient descent, where prediction error reconfigure a model’s weight parameters.

However, in many situations of interest it is not only the wiring that changes but the interfaces themselves. A neuron can die; a sensor can be damaged, losing an input channel; an organism can gain a prosthetic limb, acquiring new capabilities. In all these cases, the signals flowing through the system modify the very shapes of the boxes they flow between. None of this can be captured by the framework of
[
SS22
]
, in which each box retains its fixed interface for all time.

The present paper develops a more general categorical framework accommodating this phenomenon. We replace each fixed polynomial with a
polynomial tree
: a coinductive tree in which each node carries a polynomial, with a branch for each position and direction of that polynomial. Such a tree encodes an interface that evolves: after each round of interaction, the interface may transition to a different polynomial, and the process continues coinductively. In particular, an interface can die (transition to the zero polynomial) or divide, or undergo any other structural change, all determined by what flows to and from the interface over the course of the interaction.

The technical engine is a
universe polynomial

\mathrm{u}\coloneqq\sum_{I\in\mathbb{U}}{\mathcal{y}}^{|I|}
, where
\mathbb{U}
is a fixed universe of cardinalities (e.g. the finite cardinalities). Its self-substitution
\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}
is a polynomial whose positions encode polynomials with arities from
\mathbb{U}
: a position in
\mathtt{p}:\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}(1)
consists of a
\mathbb{U}
-set of positions together with a
\mathbb{U}
-set of directions for each. Polynomial trees are then elements of the terminal
(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})
-coalgebra, i.e. coinductive trees in which each node carries such an encoded polynomial.

Our main construction is a category
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
whose objects are polynomial trees and whose morphisms are coinductively-defined systems of polynomial maps, one at each node along every possible interaction path. We show that
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
has:

•

a symmetric monoidal product
\otimes
, extending parallel composition of interfaces to the evolving setting;

•

an internal hom
[-,-]
, making
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
monoidal closed;

•

a coproduct
+
distributed over by
\otimes
.

The full subcategory
\mathbf{Poly}_{\mathbb{U}}\subseteq\mathbf{Poly}
of polynomials with arities from
\mathbb{U}
embeds faithfully into
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
via
constant trees
, which repeat the same polynomial at every node. We also develop a
\mathbf{Cat}
-enriched version of
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
, in which a morphism
p\to q
is specified by a state set in
\mathbb{U}
together with a coinductive system of polynomial-map–valued actions and state updates. The resulting bicategory
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}
generalizes
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}
: for constant polynomial trees, the hom categories of
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}
embed fully faithfully into the hom-categories of
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}
. Enrichment in
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}
yields dynamic categories, operads, and monoidal categories in which both the wiring
and
the interfaces co-evolve.

Plan of the paper.

In
Chapter

2
we review polynomial functors and their monoidal structures. In
Chapter

3
we define polynomial trees as elements of the cofree
(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})
-coalgebra and construct the category
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
with its coinductively-defined morphisms, tensor product, and monoidal closed structure. In
Chapter

4
we study the relationship between
\mathbf{Poly}
and
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
, construct the bicategory
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}
generalizing
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}
, and illustrate it with an example from progressive deep learning.

Preliminaries

We briefly review the category
\mathbf{Poly}
of polynomial functors in one variable on
\mathbf{Set}
and some relevant structures; see
[

for a comprehensive treatment and

1
Polynomial functors and their maps

As in the introduction, a
polynomial functor
is an endofunctor
p\colon\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
of the form
\sum_{i:I}{\mathcal{y}}^{A_{i}}
, where
I
is the set of positions and
A_{i}
is the set of directions at position
i
. Since
p(1)=\sum_{i:I}1=I
, we can avoid inventing a variable and use the compact notation
p(1)
for the position set and
p[i]\coloneqq A_{i}
for the direction set at
i:p(1)
, so that

p=\sum_{i:p(1)}{\mathcal{y}}^{p[i]}=\sum_{i:p(1)}\;\prod_{d:p[i]}{\mathcal{y}}.

(1)

As in high school algebra, we use juxtaposition for scalar multiplication:
pq\coloneqq p\times q
, and we write
1={\mathcal{y}}^{0}
. A
monomial
is a polynomial of the form
M{\mathcal{y}}^{N}=\sum_{m:M}{\mathcal{y}}^{N}
; we refer to
{\mathcal{y}}^{N}
as a
representable
and to
M=M{\mathcal{y}}^{0}
as a
constant
.

A morphism
\varphi\colon p\to q
in
\mathbf{Poly}
(i.e. a natural transformation of the corresponding functors) is equivalently a map forward on positions and backward on directions:

\mathbf{Poly}(p,q)=\prod_{i:p(1)}\;\sum_{j:q(1)}\;\prod_{e:q[j]}\;\sum_{d:p[i]}1.

(2)

By the
type-theoretic axiom of choice
,

The type-theoretic axiom of choice states that products distribute over sums,

\prod_{a:A}\sum_{b:B(a)}C(a,b)\cong\sum_{b:\prod_{a:A}B(a)}\prod_{a:A}C(a,b(a)).

That is, a dependent product of dependent sums is equivalently a sum over choice functions of the original dependent product. Unlike the set-theoretic axiom of choice, this is provable in type theory; see
[
Voevodsky:2013a
, Theorem 2.15.7]
.

the outer
\prod_{i}\sum_{j}
yields a function
\varphi_{1}\colon p(1)\to q(1)
, and the inner
\prod_{e}\sum_{d}
yields, for each
i:p(1)
, a function
\varphi^{\#}_{i}\colon q[\varphi_{1}(i)]\to p[i]
. The subscript
\varphi_{1}
records the component of the natural transformation
\varphi
at
1:\mathbf{Set}
. We say
\varphi
is
cartesian
if each
\varphi^{\#}_{i}
is an isomorphism.
2
2

2

The term
cartesian
is surprisingly robust. The map
\varphi
is cartesian if its naturality squares are pullbacks, iff
\varphi
corresponds to a pullback square connecting the associated bundles, iff
\varphi
is cartesian in the sense of the Grothendieck fibration
\mathbf{Poly}\to\mathbf{Set}
sending
p\mapsto p(1)
.

2
Monoidal structures on
\mathbf{Poly}

In this section we recall the monoidal structures on
\mathbf{Poly}
that will be lifted to
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
.

The category
\mathbf{Poly}
has all coproducts and products: for any family
(p_{\alpha})_{\alpha:A}
of polynomials,

\Big(\sum_{\alpha:A}p_{\alpha}\Big)(1)=\sum_{\alpha:A}p_{\alpha}(1),\qquad\Big(\prod_{\alpha:A}p_{\alpha}\Big)(1)=\prod_{\alpha:A}p_{\alpha}(1)

(3)

with directions
(\sum_{\alpha:A}p_{\alpha})[(\alpha,i)]=p_{\alpha}[i]
and
(\prod_{\alpha:A}p_{\alpha})[(i_{\alpha})_{\alpha:A}]=\sum_{\alpha:A}p_{\alpha}[i_{\alpha}]
. The initial polynomial
0
has no positions and the terminal polynomial
1={\mathcal{y}}^{0}
has one position and no directions. In the binary case we write
p+q
and
p\times q
.

Beyond sums and products,
\mathbf{Poly}
carries two additional monoidal structures relevant to the present paper.

Dirichlet product and closure.

For polynomials
p,q
, their
Dirichlet tensor product
is

(p\otimes q)(1)\coloneqq p(1)\times q(1),\qquad(p\otimes q)[(i,j)]\coloneqq p[i]\times q[j].

(4)

The unit is
{\mathcal{y}}={\mathcal{y}}^{1}
. Both
\times
and
\otimes
distribute over
+
. The Dirichlet product is symmetric monoidal and closed: the internal hom is

[p,q]=\sum_{\varphi:\mathbf{Poly}(p,q)}{\mathcal{y}}^{\sum_{i:p(1)}q[\varphi_{1}(i)]}=\prod_{i:p(1)}\;\sum_{j:q(1)}\;\prod_{e:q[j]}\;\sum_{d:p[i]}{\mathcal{y}}

(5)

satisfying
\mathbf{Poly}(r\otimes p,\,q)\cong\mathbf{Poly}(r,\,[p,q])
naturally in
r
.

Note that
[p,q](1)=\mathbf{Poly}(p,q)
, as is immediate from comparing (
5
) with (
2
). A direction at
\varphi:[p,q](1)
consists of a pair
(i,e)
, where
i:p(1)
is a
p
-position and
e:q[\varphi_{1}(i)]
is a
q
-direction. But note that this data also implicitly specifies positions and directions of both polynomials: we have the a
q
-position
j\coloneqq\varphi_{1}(i)
and we also have a
p
-direction
d\coloneqq\varphi_{i}^{\sharp}(e)
.

The cofree comonad

In this section we recall the cofree comonad construction, which will provide the coinductive framework for polynomial trees.

A
\mathbin{\triangleleft}
-comonoid
in
\mathbf{Poly}
is a comonoid for the substitution product: a polynomial
c
equipped with a counit
\epsilon\colon c\to{\mathcal{y}}
and comultiplication
\delta\colon c\to c\mathbin{\triangleleft}c
satisfying coassociativity and counitality. Since the substitution product is composition of endofunctors on
\mathbf{Set}
, a
\mathbin{\triangleleft}
-comonoid is equivalently a (polynomial) comonad on
\mathbf{Set}
. Let
\mathbf{Cmd}(\mathbf{Poly})
denote the category of
\mathbin{\triangleleft}
-comonoids and their morphisms.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
LS24
]
)
.

The forgetful functor
U\colon\mathbf{Cmd}(\mathbf{Poly})\to\mathbf{Poly}
has a right adjoint
\mathfrak{c}\colon\mathbf{Poly}\to\mathbf{Cmd}(\mathbf{Poly})
,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Cmd}(\mathbf{Poly})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Poly}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\scriptstyle\Rightarrow}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{c}}

called the
cofree comonad
. For any polynomial
p
, it is constructed as follows. Define polynomials
p^{(i)}
for

by

p^{(0)}\coloneqq{\mathcal{y}}\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad p^{(1+i)}\coloneqq{\mathcal{y}}\times(p\mathbin{\triangleleft}p^{(i)})

(7)

with projection maps
\pi^{(i)}\colon p^{(i+1)}\to p^{(i)}
, where
\pi^{(0)}
is the terminal map and
\pi^{(i+1)}\coloneqq{\mathcal{y}}\times(p\mathbin{\triangleleft}\pi^{(i)})
. Then
\mathfrak{c}_{p}\coloneqq\lim_{i}\,p^{(i)}
.

Thus the position set

consists of
p

: coinductively, a

-behavior tree

has a

. The direction set

is the set of nodes of

followed by a node of

.

Recall that a

for the endofunctor

is a set

. The set

sending each state to its coinductive behavior tree. This universal property will be used in
Chapter

3
onward.

Universe polynomials

Fix a
universe

\mathbb{U}
of cardinalities, closed under singletons, dependent sums, and dependent products, i.e. a
\Pi\Sigma
-universe
in the sense of Martin-Löf type theory
[
Mar75
]
. Any Grothendieck universe gives such a
\mathbb{U}
, as does the universe of finite cardinalities. For each cardinality
I\in\mathbb{U}
, write
|I|
for a canonical set of that cardinality. Define the
universe polynomial

\mathrm{u}_{\mathbb{U}}\coloneqq\sum_{I\in\mathbb{U}}{\mathcal{y}}^{|I|}

(8)

so
\mathrm{u}(1)=\mathbb{U}
and
\mathrm{u}[I]=|I|
. For example, when
\mathbb{U}
is the universe of finite cardinalities,
\mathrm{u}_{\mathbb{U}}
is the
list polynomial

\mathsf{list}\coloneqq\sum_{N:\mathbb{N}}{\mathcal{y}}^{N}
. We will generally drop the subscript and write simply
\mathrm{u}
.

Since
\mathbb{U}
is closed under singletons and dependent sums,
\mathrm{u}
carries the structure of a
cartesian
monad in
(\mathbf{Poly},{\mathcal{y}},\mathbin{\triangleleft})
: the unit
\eta\colon{\mathcal{y}}\to\mathrm{u}
picks out the singleton, and the multiplication
\mu\colon\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}\to\mathrm{u}
sends a
\mathbb{U}
-set of
\mathbb{U}
-sets to their dependent sum; cartesianness enforces this (see end of
Section

1
). In the finite case, these are singleton list and concatenation of lists, e.g.
\mu
sends a list of natural numbers
(N_{1},\ldots,N_{K})
to their sum
N_{1}+\cdots+N_{K}
, with the canonical bijection on directions. Closure under dependent products will be used starting in
Chapter

3
.

The composite
\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}
will be central to this paper: its positions encode polynomials with arities from
\mathbb{U}
. By (
6
), a position
\mathtt{w}=(I,D):(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})(1)
consists of
I:\mathrm{u}(1)
and
D:\mathrm{u}[I]\to\mathrm{u}(1)
, i.e. a cardinality
I
together with a
\mathbb{U}
-indexed family of cardinalities. This data encodes the polynomial

\llbracket\mathtt{w}\rrbracket\coloneqq\sum_{i:|I|}{\mathcal{y}}^{|D_{i}|}:\mathbf{Poly}.

(9)

The direction set
(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})[\mathtt{w}]=\sum_{i:\mathrm{u}[I]}\mathrm{u}[D_{i}]=\sum_{i:|I|}|D_{i}|
(again by (
6
)) consists of position-direction pairs
(i,d)
of
\llbracket\mathtt{w}\rrbracket
, where
i:|I|
and
d:|D_{i}|
.

We say a polynomial
p=\sum_{i:p(1)}{\mathcal{y}}^{p[i]}:\mathbf{Poly}

has arities from
\mathbb{U}
if
p(1)=|I|
and
p[i]=|D_{i}|
for (necessarily unique) elements
I,D_{i}:\mathrm{u}(1)
, and write
\mathbf{Poly}_{\mathbb{U}}\subseteq\mathbf{Poly}
for the full subcategory of such polynomials. For any
p:\mathbf{Poly}_{\mathbb{U}}
, define its
code

\ulcorner p\urcorner\coloneqq(I,\,D):(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})(1)
where
I
and
D
are the unique elements with
|I|=p(1)
and
|D_{i}|=p[i]
. Then
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
and
\ulcorner\cdot\urcorner
are inverse bijections between
(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})(1)
and
\ob(\mathbf{Poly}_{\mathbb{U}})
:
\llbracket\ulcorner p\urcorner\rrbracket=p
and
\ulcorner\llbracket(I,D)\rrbracket\urcorner=(I,D)
. Every polynomial whose position and direction sets have cardinalities represented in
\mathbb{U}
is (noncanonically) isomorphic to one in
\mathbf{Poly}_{\mathbb{U}}
.

5
Polynomial trees

In this section we define polynomial trees as elements
\mathtt{p}:\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}}(1)
of the terminal
(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})
-coalgebra and describe their coinductive structure.

By (
6
), a position
\mathtt{w}:(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})(1)
has two components:
\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{pos}:\mathrm{u}(1)
and
\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{dir}:\mathrm{u}[\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{pos}]\to\mathrm{u}(1)
, a position set
|\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{pos}|:\mathbf{Set}
and a direction assignment. Together these encode the polynomial
\llbracket\mathtt{w}\rrbracket=\sum_{i:|\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{pos}|}{\mathcal{y}}^{|\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{dir}_{i}|}:\mathbf{Poly}
, cf. (
9
), with direction set
(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})[(\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{pos},\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{dir})]=\sum_{i:|\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{pos}|}|\mathtt{w}.\mathtt{dir}_{i}|
, all directions of
\llbracket\mathtt{w}\rrbracket
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
polynomial tree
is an element
\mathtt{p}:\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}}(1)
, the terminal
(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})
-coalgebra (
Section

3
). Coinductively,
\mathtt{p}
consists of:

•

a
root code

\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}=(\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{pos},\,\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{dir}):(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})(1)
, with
\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{pos}:\mathrm{u}(1)
and
\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{dir}:\mathrm{u}[\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{pos}]\to\mathrm{u}(1)
, encoding the
root polynomial

\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket=\sum_{i:|\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{pos}|}{\mathcal{y}}^{|\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{dir}_{i}|}:\mathbf{Poly}
; together with

•

\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{rest}\colon(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})[\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}]\to\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}}(1)
, assigning a child tree
\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{rest}_{i,d}:
to each direction
(i,d):(\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u})[\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}]
.

A polynomial tree encodes an interface that evolves through interaction. The root polynomial
\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket:\mathbf{Poly}
is the current interface; when position
i:\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{pos}
is selected and direction
d:|\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{dir}_{i}|
is received, the interface transitions to
\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{rest}_{i,d}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket
, and the process continues. The tree terminates along any branch where
\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}=N{\mathcal{y}}^{0}
is constant.

Example 5.2

(Communication protocols)
.

A polynomial tree can specify a communication protocol whose available operations change over time. At each node, positions represent the message types a client may send, and directions represent the server’s possible responses.

For instance, consider a server initially offering
login
(with responses
success
or
failure
) and
quit
(no response): the root polynomial is
{\mathcal{y}}^{2}+1
. After
\texttt{login}/\texttt{success}
, the interface transitions to
\mathsf{String}{\mathcal{y}}^{\mathsf{String}}+\mathsf{String}{\mathcal{y}}^{\mathsf{Int}}+1
: the authenticated session offers
query
(sending a query
\mathsf{String}
, returning a result
\mathsf{String}
),
set
(a write operation sending a
\mathsf{String}
and returning an
\mathsf{Int}
status code), and
logout
(terminating the session). After
\texttt{login}/\texttt{failure}
, it returns to
{\mathcal{y}}^{2}+1
for a retry. The polynomial tree encodes the full protocol, with the coinductive structure capturing unbounded interaction. This perspective is closely related to
session types

[
Hon93
]
.

Example 5.3

(Cell differentiation)
.

A cell’s interface changes as it differentiates. Let
L
be a finite set of concentration levels, large enough to include all relevant amounts of any signaling molecule. Model a stem cell that outputs a concentration
\ell:L
of growth factor and receives signals simultaneously on three receptors, each detecting a concentration in
L
. The root polynomial is
L{\mathcal{y}}^{L^{3}}:\mathbf{Poly}
, with
|\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{pos}|=L
and
|\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{dir}_{x}|=L^{3}
for each
x:L
. A direction
d=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}):L^{3}
is a triple of concentrations, one per receptor. The child tree depends on this input: for example, high
\ell_{1}
might trigger
\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{rest}_{x,d}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket=L{\mathcal{y}}^{L}
(differentiation to a neuron that outputs neurotransmitter, one receptor), high
\ell_{2}
might give
\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{rest}_{x,d}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket=L{\mathcal{y}}^{L^{3}}
(growth signal, interface unchanged), and high
\ell_{3}
might give
\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{rest}_{x,d}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket=0
(apoptosis). The neuron subtree might be constant at
L{\mathcal{y}}^{L}
; the growth subtree could equal
\mathtt{p}
itself coinductively.

Example 5.4

(Chess)
.

A game of chess is naturally modeled as a polynomial tree. At each board state
b
, the current player has a finite set
M_{b}
of legal moves; after playing
m:M_{b}
, the opponent has a set
R_{b,m}
of legal responses. The polynomial at state
b
is thus
\sum_{m:M_{b}}{\mathcal{y}}^{R_{b,m}}
, and the child tree at
(m,r)
is the polynomial tree for the resulting board state. The interface can change quite substantially from move to move: a pawn on
e2
can advance to
e3
or
e4
but can only capture diagonally on
d3
or
f3
if an opponent’s piece occupies that square; castling is available only when king and rook have not moved and no intermediate square is attacked; a pawn reaching the eighth rank must promote, replacing itself with a new piece and altering the move set for all subsequent positions. The polynomial tree for chess encodes the complete game tree, with the changing polynomial at each node reflecting the changing set of legal moves and responses.

The simplest polynomial trees are
constant
: for any
p:\mathbf{Poly}_{\mathbb{U}}
, the
constant tree

\overline{\mathtt{p}}
is defined coinductively by
\overline{\mathtt{p}}.\mathtt{root}\coloneqq\ulcorner p\urcorner
and
\overline{\mathtt{p}}.\mathtt{rest}_{i,d}\coloneqq\overline{\mathtt{p}}
for all
i
and
d
, so that
\llbracket\overline{\mathtt{p}}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket=p
at every node. Constant trees model interfaces that never change; in
Chapter

4
, we will show that
p\mapsto\overline{\mathtt{p}}
extends to a faithful functor
\mathbf{Poly}_{\mathbb{U}}\to\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
.

6
The category of polynomial trees

In this section we define the morphism set
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})
as a limit of finite approximations, making
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}
into a category with composition defined coinductively.

Definition 6.5
.

For polynomial trees
\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q}:\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}}(1)
, define sets
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(n)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})
for
n:\mathbb{N}
by
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(0)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})\coloneqq 1
and

\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(n+1)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})\coloneqq\prod_{i:|\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{pos}|}\;\sum_{j:|\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{pos}|}\;\prod_{e:|\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{dir}_{j}|}\;\sum_{d:|\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{dir}_{i}|}\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(n)}(\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{rest}_{i,d},\,\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{rest}_{j,e}).

(10)

These form a cofiltered diagram of sets: there is a unique function
\pi_{0}\colon\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(1)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})\to 1
and we define the projection recursively by
\pi_{n+1}\colon\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(n+2)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})\to\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(n+1)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})
by
\prod_{i}\sum_{j}\prod_{e}\sum_{d}\,\pi_{n}
. The
morphism set
is taken to be the limit

\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})\coloneqq\lim_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(n)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q}).

Write
h\coloneqq[\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket,\llbracket\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket]:\mathbf{Poly}
for the internal hom of the root polynomials; then
h(1)=\mathbf{Poly}(\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket,\llbracket\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket)
is the set of polynomial maps between them, and
h[\varphi]=\sum_{i:|\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{pos}|}|\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{dir}_{\varphi_{1}(i)}|
is the direction set at a map
\varphi:h(1)
. Since
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(0)}=1
, comparing (
10
) with (
2
) gives
\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(1)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})=h(1)=\mathbf{Poly}(\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket,\llbracket\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket)
.

In general, an element
\varphi:\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(n+1)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})
specifies:

•

\displaystyle\mathbf{PolyTr}_{\mathbb{U}}^{(n+1)}(\mathtt{r}\otimes\mathtt{p},\,\mathtt{q})\;

Relationship to
\mathbf{Poly}
and
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}
; the bicategory

An element

specifies a polynomial map at every node along every interaction path. In practice, morphisms are more naturally described by a state set and a transition rule. This is a familiar pattern: for any endofunctor

, a
p
-coalgebra
(S,\,\beta\colon S\to p(S))
compactly encodes a family of elements of the terminal
p
-coalgebra

; each state

determines a behavior tree by coinductive unfolding. In the bicategory
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}
of
[

]
, this idea is applied to the internal hom polynomial
[p,q]
: a morphism

in

is an

-coalgebra

assigning a successor state to each direction. For instance, modeling gradient descent via a weight-update rule (a coalgebra on the weight space

) is far more practical than exhibiting the full tree of all possible training trajectories, which would be an element of

(
Section

In this section, we make the same move for general polynomial trees: we parametrize the hom tower by a state set

whose objects are state-based descriptions. The set

is recovered using a state set of cardinality

(

embeds fully faithfully into

The parametrized hom

In this section we generalize the hom tower (
10
) by introducing a state set
S
, so that morphisms can be described by an action-and-update rule rather than an explicit tree of polynomial maps.

Definition 9.1
.

For polynomial trees
\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q}:\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}}(1)
and a set

, define sets

for

(17)

The
parametrized hom set
is
H_{S}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})\coloneqq\lim_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,H_{S}^{(n)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})
.

Comparing with (
10
), the parametrized tower wraps the original in
\prod_{s:S}
and inserts
\sum_{s^{\prime}:S}
before the recursive term. Write
h\coloneqq[\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket,\llbracket\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket]:\mathbf{Poly}
as in
Section

6
. An element of
H_{S}^{(n+1)}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})
specifies, for each state
s:S
:

•

an
action

\mathrm{act}(s):h(1)=\mathbf{Poly}(\llbracket\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket,\llbracket\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{root}\rrbracket)
; together with

•

for each direction
(i,e):h[\mathrm{act}(s)]
, letting
j\coloneqq\mathrm{act}(s)_{1}(i)
and
d\coloneqq\mathrm{act}(s)^{\#}_{i}(e)
, a
successor state

\mathrm{upd}(s,i,e):S
and an element of
H_{S}^{(n)}(\mathtt{p}.\mathtt{rest}_{i,d},\,\mathtt{q}.\mathtt{rest}_{j,e})
.

The successor state records which polynomial map to apply at the next interaction step; the recursive element ensures this structure continues through all child trees.

This structure is

: the child tree pair

varies with each interaction, so the recursive data at each node involves a potentially different polynomial hom than at the node’s parent. This is the key difference from

and

are fixed and the map

is always the same.

Definition 9.2
.

For polynomial trees
\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q}:\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}}(1)
, the
hom-category

\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})
is defined as follows.

•

Objects
: pairs
(S,\alpha)
where
S\in\mathbb{U}
and
\alpha:H_{S}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})
.

•

Morphisms

(S,\alpha)\to(S^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
: functions

such that for all

,

(i)

\mathrm{act}^{\prime}(f(s))=\mathrm{act}(s)
;

(ii)

for all directions

;

and the same conditions hold coinductively at all child tree pairs, with

relating the successor states at every node.

Composition and identity in
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q})

Proposition 9.3
.

\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}
is a bicategory, with composition
(S,\alpha)\mathbin{\fatsemi}(T,\beta)\coloneqq(S\times T,\;\alpha\mathbin{\fatsemi}\beta)
defined by composing actions as in
Section

6

is
(1,\,\mathrm{id}_{\mathtt{p}})
.

Proof 9.4
.

Given

and
(T,\beta):\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}(\mathtt{q},\mathtt{r})
, define
\alpha\mathbin{\fatsemi}\beta:H_{S\times T}(\mathtt{p},\mathtt{r})
coinductively. For

and

and

; for

, set

and

. Then

\mathrm{act}_{\alpha;\beta}(s,t)\coloneqq\mathrm{act}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbin{\fatsemi}\mathrm{act}_{\beta}(t),\qquad\mathrm{upd}_{\alpha;\beta}((s,t),i,f)\coloneqq\big(\mathrm{upd}_{\alpha}(s,i,e),\;\mathrm{upd}_{\beta}(t,j,f)\big).

The recursive factor at

. The identity

For morphisms, given

We illustrate the bicategory structure with an example from deep learning. Let

denote the polynomial encoding a single real-valued channel: positions are real numbers, directions are cotangent vectors (gradient feedback). In

Example 9.5

(Progressive generative architectures)
.

In progressive training of generative adversarial networks
[
Kar+18
]
, the image resolution grows during training: the generator begins producing small, coarsely pixelated images and gradually transitions to larger, higher-resolution ones, learning global structure before fine detail. The output polynomial changes at each resolution step, so the generator’s interface evolves in a controlled way during training, exactly what polynomial trees are meant to formalize. Let
N_{0}<N_{1}<\cdots<N_{L}
be pixel counts at increasing resolutions. The polynomial
t^{\otimes N_{l}}=\sum_{x:\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}}}{\mathcal{y}}^{T^{*}_{x}\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}}}
is the interface at resolution
l
: positions are images (
N_{l}
real-valued pixel intensities), directions are cotangent vectors carrying gradient feedback.

The progressive image tree.
Define a polynomial tree
\mathtt{g}_{0}:\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{u}\mathbin{\triangleleft}\mathrm{u}}(1)
with root
\mathtt{g}_{0}.\mathtt{root}\coloneqq\ulcorner t^{\otimes N_{0}}\urcorner
. The direction set
T^{*}_{x}\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}
at each image
x:\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}
is partitioned as

T^{*}_{x}\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}=D^{\mathrm{stay}}_{x}\sqcup D^{\mathrm{grow}}_{x}

(e.g.
D^{\mathrm{stay}}_{x}\coloneqq\{v:\|v\|>\epsilon\}
for a threshold
\epsilon>0
: large gradients indicate continued learning, small gradients indicate convergence at the current resolution). The child trees branch accordingly:

\mathtt{g}_{0}.\mathtt{rest}_{x,v}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathtt{g}_{0}&\text{if }v\in D^{\mathrm{stay}}_{x},\\
\mathtt{g}_{1}&\text{if }v\in D^{\mathrm{grow}}_{x},\end{cases}

where
\mathtt{g}_{1}
is defined similarly with root
\ulcorner t^{\otimes N_{1}}\urcorner
, and so on up to
\mathtt{g}_{L}\coloneqq\overline{\mathtt{t}^{\otimes N_{L}}}
(constant at the final resolution). The polynomial tree encodes all possible resolution trajectories, and the gradient feedback selects among them.

The generator as an
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}
-morphism.
Let
k:\mathbb{N}
be the dimension of the generator’s input space (the “latent space”: a fixed-dimensional space of random vectors from which images are generated). The generator is an object

(W_{G},\,\alpha_{G}):\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}(\overline{\mathtt{t}^{\otimes k}},\,\mathtt{g}_{0})

in the hom-category from the constant tree
\overline{\mathtt{t}^{\otimes k}}
(fixed input interface) to the progressive tree
\mathtt{g}_{0}
(evolving output interface). The state set
W_{G}:\mathbb{U}
consists of the generator’s learnable parameters. At resolution level
l
and state
w:W_{G}
, the action
\mathrm{act}_{\alpha_{G}}(w):\mathbf{Poly}(t^{\otimes k},t^{\otimes N_{l}})
applies the parameterized function
f_{G}(w,-):\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}}
as the forward pass, with backward pass
(Df_{G})^{\top}
carrying gradient feedback from the output back to the input. The successor state
\mathrm{upd}_{\alpha_{G}}(w,z,v):W_{G}
performs gradient
ascent
:
w\mapsto w+\epsilon\,\pi_{W_{G}}(D_{(w,z)}f_{G})^{\top}\cdot v
, where
z:\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the input and
v:T^{*}_{f_{G}(w,z)}\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}}
is the gradient feedback from the discriminator. The sign flip (
+\epsilon
rther than
-\epsilon
) reflects the adversarial objective: the generator ascends the discriminator’s loss. Whether
v
falls in
D^{\mathrm{stay}}
or
D^{\mathrm{grow}}
determines the child tree, hence the next resolution level.

Composition in
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}
.
A discriminator
(W_{D},\alpha_{D}):\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}(\mathtt{g}_{0},\overline{\mathtt{t}})
is defined similarly: at resolution
l
and state
w:W_{D}
, the action applies
f_{D}(w,-):\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}}\to\mathbb{R}
, classifying images as real or fake, with gradient descent update on
W_{D}
. Composition in
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}
gives

(W_{G}\times W_{D},\;\alpha_{G}\mathbin{\fatsemi}\alpha_{D}):\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}(\overline{\mathtt{t}^{\otimes k}},\,\overline{\mathtt{t}}).

This is the end-to-end system with joint parameter space
W_{G}\times W_{D}
: the composed action feeds a latent vector through the generator and then the discriminator, producing a single real output, and both parameter sets update simultaneously via backpropagation. The gradient feedback at the outermost interface
\overline{\mathtt{t}}
is provided by the loss function (e.g. binary cross-entropy against real data), which acts as the environment.

Why
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rgTr}
, not
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}
.
At resolution
l
, the generator acts as a polynomial map
t^{\otimes k}\to t^{\otimes N_{l}}
. Since
N_{l}\neq N_{l^{\prime}}
for
l\neq l^{\prime}
, these are maps between genuinely different polynomials at different nodes of the tree; an
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}
-morphism would require a single fixed hom
[t^{\otimes k},t^{\otimes N}]
for all time. After reaching level
L
, the interfaces stabilize and the dynamics reduce to an
\mathbb{O}\mathbf{rg}
-morphism, consistent with
Section

10
below.

Adaptive vs. fixed schedules.
In
[
Kar+18
]
, resolution growth follows a fixed schedule: train for
N
steps, then grow. This amounts to using the polynomial tree in which
all
directions lead to the same child tree (the branching is trivial and a counter in the state controls the transition). The direction-dependent formulation above is more adaptive: the gradient feedback itself signals when the current resolution has been learned, triggering the transition to a higher resolution only when the training dynamics warrant it. This could reduce wasted computation at resolutions that have already converged and prevent premature growth before convergence is reached. This example is speculative; we have not implemented it.

Remark 10.13

Since
