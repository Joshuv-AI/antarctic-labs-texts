Skein theory for the Links–Gould polynomial Thanks: Key words and phrases: knots, links, Links–Gould invariant, ADO invariant, braid groups, cubic skein theory, R R -matrices, quantum groups, Nichols algebras, Yetter–Drinfel’d modules, Seifert genus. MSC: primary 57K14, 57K16; secondary 17B37, 18M15, 20F36
Stavros Garoufalidis, Matthew Harper, Rinat Kashaev, Ben-Michael Kohli, Jiebo Song, Guillaume Tahar

Abstract
. Building further on work of Marin and Wagner, we give a cubic braid-type skein theory of the Links–Gould polynomial invariant of oriented links and prove that it can be used to evaluate any oriented link, adding this polynomial to the list of polynomial invariants that can be computed by skein theory. As a consequence, we prove that this skein theory is also shared by the V 1 V_{1} -polynomial defined by two of the authors, deducing the equality of the two link polynomials. This implies specialization properties of the V 1 V_{1} -polynomial to the Alexander polynomial and to the ADO 3 \mathrm{ADO}_{3} -invariant, the fact that it is a Vassiliev power series invariant, as well as a Seifert genus bound for knots.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Link polynomials via skein theory

The origin of skein theory as a way to uniquely define and compute invariants of
links goes back to at least Alexander in 1928 and Conway in
1969
[
2
,
7
]
who used it to compute the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{L}(t)\in\mathbbm{Z}[t^{\pm 1/2}]
and the Conway polynomial
\nabla_{L}(z)\in\mathbbm{Z}[z]

of an oriented link
L
in
S^{3}
. These polynomials satisfy the skein relations

\Delta_{L_{+}}(t)-\Delta_{L_{-}}(t)=(t^{1/2}-t^{-1/2})\Delta_{L_{0}}(t),\qquad\nabla_{L_{+}}(z)-\nabla_{L_{-}}(z)=z\nabla_{L_{0}}(z)

(1)

where,
L_{+},L_{-},L_{0}
are three link diagrams that are identical except at a
single crossing, where they differ by being a positive crossing, a negative crossing,
or a smoothing, respectively. Such a relation, together with the fact that any
diagram of a link can be changed to a diagram of an unlink, and the initial condition

\Delta_{\text{unknot}}(t)=1
or
\nabla_{\text{unknot}}(z)=1
, computes
the polynomial on any link diagram. The two invariants are related by a change of
variables.

But a dramatic revival of skein theory came with Jones’s discovery of the Jones
polynomial
J_{L}(q)\in\mathbbm{Z}[q^{\pm 1/2}]
which satisfies the skein-theory

q^{-1}J_{L_{+}}(q)-qJ_{L_{-}}(q)=(q^{1/2}-q^{-1/2})J_{L_{0}}(q),\qquad J_{\text{unknot}}(q)=1\,.

(2)

Soon after Jones’s discovery, it was realized by a group of six people that there
is a 2-variable generalization of the Jones and the Alexander/Conway polynomial,
the so-called HOMFLYPT-polynomial that satisfies the skein theory
[
11
,
33
]

lP_{L_{+}}(m,l)-l^{-1}P_{L_{-}}(m,l)=mP_{L_{0}}(m,l),\qquad P_{\text{unknot}}(m,l)=1\,.

(3)

At this point, skein-theory and skein modules (on surfaces and 3-manifolds) were
introduced and studied independently by Przytycki
[
33
,
32
]
and
Turaev
[
38
]
. At the same time the Kauffman polynomial was introduced, and it satisfies a quadratic skein relation

Q_{L_{+}}(z)+Q_{L_{-}}(z)=zQ_{L_{0}}(z)+zQ_{L_{\infty}}(z)

(4)

for framed unoriented links, where
L_{\infty}
is the horizontal smoothing,

[
25
]
.

An excellent introduction of the history of skein theory and its role in knot theory
is given by Przytycki
[
34
]
, where one may find examples of linear and
quadratic skein theory.

On the other hand, cubic skein theory is a more difficult subject, and in fact it was
only recently shown that the move that replaces three half-twists by
none is not sufficient to untie all knots
[
5
]
.

Our paper gives an example of a cubic skein theory which uniquely determines the
two-variable Links-Gould invariant
[
29
]
and identifies it with the recent

V_{1}
two-variable polynomial of
[
15
]
. This identification will be used
in a subsequent work to provide Seifert genus bounds for the colored Links-Gould
polynomials and of the
V_{n}
-polynomials of
[
15
]
which are independent of
n
,
extending the work of
[
27
]
and confirming some patterns found
in
[
14
]
.

1.2.
Multivariable knot polynomials

Recently, a systematic way to define and effectively compute multivariable knot
polynomials was introduced in
[
15
]
, using as input a finite dimensional
Nichols algebra (or a finite-dimensional Drinfeld–Yetter module of it)
with an automorphism. From such an algebra, one can define a rigid
R
-matrix and
construct a state-sum invariant of long knots by applying the well-known
Reshetikhin–Turaev functor.

Nichols algebras are easy to describe, and
in the simplest case of rank 1, such an algebra is uniquely determined by the
data

\text{basis}(V)=\{x\},\qquad\Delta(x)=x\otimes 1+1\otimes x,\qquad\tau(x\otimes x)=qx\otimes x,\qquad\phi(x)=tx\,.

(5)

In
[
15
]
, it was shown that the corresponding invariants are the ADO
invariants of a knot
[
1
]
, and the colored Jones polynomials of a knot

[
35
,
37
]
.

The next case of a Nichols algebra of rank 2 leads to a family of 2-variable
polynomials
\Lambda_{\omega}(t_{0},t_{1})
at each root of unity
\omega
and a
sequence
V_{n}(t,q)
of 2-variable polynomials, where
n\geq 1
is an integer.

The above polynomials are defined for oriented long knots, but the
construction can often be extended to polynomial invariants of framed, oriented
links in 3-space. Whereas a general theorem is not known for all finite dimensional
Nichols algebras with automorphisms, it was shown in
[
13
]
that the
long knot
V_{1}
,
\Lambda_{1}
, and
\Lambda_{-1}
polynomials do extend
to framed, oriented links (and that they are independent of the framing), and two
of them were identified with the Alexander polynomial and the
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
-link polynomial
of
[
20
]

\Lambda_{1,L}(t_{0},t_{1})=\Delta_{L}(t_{0})\,\Delta_{L}(t_{1}),\qquad\Lambda_{-1,L}(t^{-2},s^{-2})=\Delta_{\mathfrak{sl}_{3},L}(t,s)

(6)

as was conjectured in
[
15
]
. In the following we will identify the
V_{1}
polynomial
of
[
15
]
with the Links–Gould polynomial
[
29
]
, as was conjectured in

[
15
]
.

3.
Basics of the
\mathrm{LG}
,

V_{1}
and

\mathrm{ADO}_{\omega}
link invariants

The three polynomial invariants of links that we study in our paper, namely
the
\mathrm{LG}
,
V_{1}
and
\mathrm{ADO}_{\omega}
come from the well-known Reshetikhin–Turaev
construction
[
35
,
39
]
applied to enhanced matrices given below.

Instead of repeating definitions and notations from previous works and arguments
that we will not use, we comment briefly how these invariants are defined
following
[
13
]
and references therein.

A rigid
R
-matrix leads to invariants of long knots
[
24
]

and Nichols algebras with automorphisms (or suitable finite dimensional quotients
thereof) produce rigid
R
-matrices and hence invariants of long knots
[
15
]
.

All three polynomial invariants come from rigid
R
-matrices, and in fact from
enhanced ones, in the sense of Ohtsuki and Turaev
[
31
,
37
]
.
For a precise definition see
[
13
, Sec.2]
,
where an extension to tangles is given, and
a comparison of the various definitions is also discussed.

A common feature of the invariants of tangles given by the three polynomials
that we study is that they vanish when evaluated to closed links, since in a sense
all three are fermionic invariants.

To overcome this problem and define a nontrivial invariant of oriented links,
one cuts one component to obtain a
(1,1)
-tangle, and then shows that the
invariant of
(1,1)
-tangles is a scalar (so-called property
(P_{1})

in
[
13
, Sec.1.1]
), and then that an invariant of a
(2,2)
-tangle
is unchanged if we close it on one or the other side (property
(P_{2})
).

All three tangle invariants satisfy properties
(P_{1})
and
(P_{2})
and consequently
give well-defined invariants of oriented links. Although these link invariants are
highly nontrivial, they do vanish on split links.

Note that in dealing with the Links–Gould invariant of links, we stick to
conventions used by Ishii for example in
[
23
]
. In doing so,

\mathrm{LG}_{L}(p^{-2},p^{2}q^{2})
with
p=q^{\alpha}
coincides with the Links-Gould
invariant from
[
9
]
.

Having discussed the basic properties of the three invariants of interest,
we give their
R
-matrices in Appendix
A
, and their
enhancements here:

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{LG}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(t_{0}^{-1},-t_{1},-t_{0}^{-1},t_{1})\in\mathrm{End}(W)

(49)

\displaystyle h_{V}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,-1)\in\mathrm{End}(V)

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{ADO}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(t^{2},\omega^{2}t^{2},\omega^{4}t^{2})\in\mathrm{End}(X)\,.

Lemma 3.1
.

(R_{\mathrm{LG}},h_{\mathrm{LG}})
,
(R_{V},h_{V})
and
(R_{\mathrm{ADO}},h_{\mathrm{ADO}})
are enhanced

R
-matrices and satisfy properties
(P_{1})
and
(P_{2})
of
[
13
, Sec.1.1]
.

The statement about enhancement follows by an explicit computation.
Regarding properties
(P_{1})
and
(P_{2})
,
(R_{V},h_{V})
satisfies them as was
shown in
[
13
, Sec.3]
. So do
(R_{\mathrm{LG}},h_{\mathrm{LG}})
and
(R_{\mathrm{ADO}},h_{\mathrm{ADO}})

since they are defined representation theoretically via a ribbon category
and the tangle invariants are colored by a simple ambidextrous object
in the sense of
[
16
]
.

The link invariants can be computed in terms of a braid presentation

\beta\in B_{n}
of an oriented link
L
as stated in
[
13
, Rem.2.3]

and for the convenience of the reader, we reproduce here:

\displaystyle F_{L^{\mathrm{cut}}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}_{2,\dots,n}\left((\mathrm{id}_{V}\otimes h^{\otimes(n-1)})\circ\rho_{R}(\beta)\right)\in\mathrm{End}(V)

(50)

\displaystyle\langle F_{L^{\mathrm{cut}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\dim(V)}\mathrm{tr}\left((\mathrm{id}_{V}\otimes h^{\otimes(n-1)})\circ\rho_{R}(\beta)\right)\,.

As mentioned before, all three link invariants thus defined have the following
common feature.

Lemma 3.2
.

The
\mathrm{LG}
,
V_{1}
and
\mathrm{ADO}_{\omega}
polynomials vanish on split links, and are equal to
1
on the unknot.

Proof.

The vanishing on split links follows from the definition of the invariants
and the fact that the diagonal matrices
h_{\mathrm{LG}}
,
h_{V}
and
h_{\mathrm{ADO}}

have trace zero. The value of the unknot, whose long version is a single
vertical strand, is obvious.
∎
