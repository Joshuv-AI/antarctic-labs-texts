Parents’ and guardians’ attitudes, beliefs and behaviours towards injury and injury prevention within adolescent female rugby union in England
Authors: Gemma Knight, Liam Colbert, Omar Heyward, Ben Jones, Carolyn Emery, Simon Roberts, Keith Stokes, Stephen W West
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objectivesDuring adolescence, parents and guardians can play an important role in shaping their child’s attitudes, beliefs and behaviours. Yet research examining their beliefs and behaviours regarding injury and prevention within adolescent female rugby union is limited. The primary aim of the study was to identify attitudes, beliefs and behaviours towards injury and injury prevention among parents and guardians of adolescent female rugby players, including subgroup differences. The second aim was to examine how attitudes and beliefs aligned among parents and guardians and the players (their child).MethodsParents and guardians of adolescent female rugby players were recruited from under-16 and under-18 rugby schools/colleges, clubs, and regional talent pathways teams and asked to complete an online cross-sectional survey.ResultsTwo hundred and eighty-five parents and guardians responded to the survey; 144 parents and guardians and players (their child) completed their respective surveys. Fifty-four per cent believed it was ‘possible to prevent some rugby injuries’. Understanding of equipment effectiveness varied; 12% agreed that wearing a scrum cap could prevent concussions. Agreement between parent and guardian and player responses to statements regarding injury and injury prevention varied; Gwet’s AC ranged from almost perfect agreement (0.8–1) to fair agreement (0.21–0.4).ConclusionThe study advances understanding of parents’ and guardians’ attitudes, beliefs and behaviours regarding injury and injury prevention in adolescent female rugby, showing generally safe practices while highlighting notable gaps that constrain effective injury management. Addressing these gaps offers a practical route to improving the consistency of parents’ and guardians’ safeguarding players’ welfare.Keywords: Injury, Prevention, Rugby, Adolescent

Introduction

Adolescent female rugby union (hereafter rugby) has experienced substantial growth in participation over recent years, with World Rugby having reported that more than 1.3 million women and girls participated in rugby during 2023.1 Participation offers not only physiological, psychological and social benefits2 but also carries inherent injury risk.3 The reported injury incidence rate within adolescent female rugby in England was 53 injuries/1000 match-hours (95% CI 37 to 76).4 To address injury risk among adolescent female rugby players, a range of injury prevention strategies are available and implemented, including neuromuscular training (NMT) warm-ups5 and protective equipment6 as well as law/policy changes (eg, lowering the tackle height) and education initiatives.7 For injury prevention strategies to be effectively implemented, an evaluation of the context in which they are used is needed, as outlined in the Translating Research into Injury Prevention Practice Framework.8 Investigating the context, in turn, helps to establish the potential barriers and motivators that influence the player’s adoption of injury prevention strategies.8

When considering how injury prevention strategies are implemented, it is essential to account for community partners such as parents and guardians.9 These partners can substantially influence players’ adoption of injury prevention strategies through the attitudes and behaviours they model.10 Parents and guardians can strongly shape adolescent female rugby players’ subjective norms, which refers to the perceived social pressures or expectations from significant others that guide players’ decisions,11 and in turn explain players’ care-seeking behaviours after an injury.12 Previous literature shows that subjective norms influenced adolescent athletes’ intention to report concussion symptoms.13 Although no direct evidence links parents and guardians to these norms, broader literature suggests this influence is plausible.13 Parents and guardians may also directly shape players’ use of injury prevention strategies by acting as ‘providers of experiences’14 by deciding whether their child participates, providing transport and purchasing protective equipment. Their influence is reflected in wider rugby research, as when players across adult and adolescent rugby were asked regarding the reasons they wore shoulder pads, 7.3% cited a ‘recommendation from coaches, friends or parents’ with one player stating, ‘(they) wore (shoulder pads) on the insistence of (their) mum’.15

While emerging research has begun to examine parents’ and guardians’ attitudes, beliefs and behaviours in rugby, little is known of this area within adolescent female rugby. In adolescent male rugby, one study found that 90% of parents viewed reducing injury risk as essential9 and many believed they were responsible for managing concussion recovery. However, it was unclear whether they had received formal concussion education.16 Such education is important, as parents and guardians often provide the initial concussion guidance at home through verbal instructions and reinforcement of safety behaviours.17

The primary aim of this study was to examine parents’ and guardians’ attitudes, beliefs and behaviours related to injury and injury prevention in adolescent female rugby and to determine whether these differed across subgroups (ie, previous rugby experience, concussion education completion and parent type). The secondary aim was to examine whether there was alignment between the attitudes, beliefs and behaviours of parents and guardians and players (their children).

Methods

Design

This was a cross-sectional observational study. An online cross-sectional survey (available from March to August 2024, spanning the end of the rugby season into the off-season) was used to investigate the attitudes, beliefs and behaviours of parents and guardians towards injury and injury prevention. A parallel survey was distributed to players and was reported in another study,18 and matched parent-player responses were compared where available. Participants were recruited from a wider cohort study (Injury Prevention in Female Adolescent Female Rugby Union: a Multisite International Study). All respondents were assured anonymity to optimise honesty and to mitigate any fear of repercussions. Eligibility criteria included being a parent or guardian of a player in under-16 or under-18 female rugby across school/college, community clubs and regional talent pathways in England, called Developing Player Pathways and Centre of Excellence (CoE). Further criteria included consent and assent; informed consent was collected for parents and guardians and players ≥18 years old, and informed assent and consent from a parent or guardian was obtained for players <18 years old before completing the survey.19 Reporting followed the Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS).

Survey tool design, development and validation

The online survey was informed by previous tools examining injury perceptions and reporting behaviours.20–23 Participants provided consent and completed the survey through a bespoke online portal: SHREDConcussions (Surveillance in High School and Community Sport to Reduce Concussion and their Consequences) (https://shred.concussions.co.uk). Connected to the participant-facing platform, REDCap,24 25 where all the data were stored for analysis purposes. The online survey was comprised of five sections designed to capture different aspects of parental attitudes, beliefs and behaviours. Section A focused on demographics; section B examined their attitudes and beliefs towards injury; section C explored their attitudes and behaviours related to injury prevention strategies. Section D captured information regarding concussion education and concussion-related behaviours and, finally, section E focused on Musculoskeletal (MSK) injuries during the 2022–2023 season and factors contributing to non-reporting of MSK injuries (see online supplemental appendix A). Question formats included yes/no, 7-point Likert scales, multiple choice and open-ended responses. To ensure clarity and relevance, the survey underwent face validation by nine researchers, clinicians and practitioners. As the survey was based on previously validated rugby and football instruments, and due to time constraints during the playing season, piloting was not undertaken.

Patient and public involvement statement

The current study did not consult the target population regarding the study design, the survey or commitment levels involved with participation, for reasons outlined above.

Data analysis

For all data analysis, STATA BE V.17 was used. Section A was presented as frequencies and proportions, except for age, which was presented using the mean and SD. Throughout sections B, C, D and E, results were displayed as frequencies and proportions with 95% CI. Differences in parent and guardian subgroups (by previous rugby experience (yes/no), concussion education completion (yes/no) and parent type (mother/father)) responses to 7-point Likert scale questions for attitudes and beliefs towards injury and injury prevention strategies were assessed with Mann-Whitney U tests, Z scores and p values. Statistical significance was reported for p values ≤0.05. Agreement between parents and guardians and the players’ (their child’s) responses to questions exploring their attitudes and beliefs towards injury and injury prevention strategies was assessed using inter-rater reliability. Actual percentage agreement, percentage agreement, percentage agreement coefficient (AC) and Gwet’s AC26 27 were reported for the 7-point Likert scale questions. Gwet’s AC was used for its more stable inter-rater reliability coefficient when compared with Cohen’s kappa.28 Interpretations of Gwet’s AC were in accordance with those reported by Landis and Koch29 (online supplemental appendix B).

Results

Throughout the 2023–2024 rugby season, 1062 parents and guardians were contacted to participate, of which 289 responded, and 285 participants met the eligibility criteria (4 responses were removed due to players, not parents and guardians, having completed the survey: table 1). Although the overall sample size was large, the response rate was relatively low (response rate 27%). Incomplete answers to the survey were included within the data set.

Attitudes and beliefs towards injury prevention strategies

Forty-nine per cent either agreed that they ‘would like (their) child’s team to complete a rugby-specific warm-up programme prior to every game and training session this season’ (table 2). Many parents and guardians (39%) either agreed that ‘lowering the legal height of the tackle to below the base of the sternum can prevent injuries in rugby’ (table 2).

Behaviours and understanding towards injury and injury prevention strategies

The majority (96%) reported that their child wears a mouthguard in both matches and training; however, their understanding of a mouthguard’s effectiveness in preventing concussions varied, as 11% of parents and guardians agreed that wearing a mouthguard could prevent concussions (table 2), yet 28% disagreed. Fewer parents and guardians disclosed that their child wore a scrum cap (24%) in matches or matches and training. 17 per cent of parents and guardians slightly agreed that wearing a scrum cap could prevent concussions, while 30% disagreed with the above statement (table 2).

Injury management and reporting behaviours

Most (n=157, 55%) reported that they had engaged in some form of concussion education, with the majority of parents and guardians (n=123, 78%) having completed the Rugby Football Union’s (RFU’s) Headcase (https://www.englandrugby.com/run/player-welfare/headcase). When asked whether ‘it was the (parent and guardian’s) responsibility to guide (their) child’s gradual return to play (RTP) following a concussion’, 90% (n=262) of parents and guardians responded with yes. Of those parents and guardians who believed that it was their responsibility to guide their child’s RTP, 44% (n=114) had received no form of concussion education. For the parents and guardians who did not believe it was their responsibility (6%), they believed it was either a mix of community partners’ roles (41%) or the coaches’ (30%) responsibility to guide the player’s gradual RTP following a concussion. Parents and guardians were asked when they would ‘recommend a player with a concussion to seek medical attention’: 71% believed ‘when a player has been suspected of sustaining a concussion injury, even if they show no visible signs of a concussion’, while 5% believed ‘only if the concussion is severe, for example, the player is knocked unconscious’, 2% assumed ‘only if (the player is) unable to return to sport for a prolonged period (more than a month)’. Meanwhile, 20% considered only seeking medical attention if the player felt unwell.

Comparisons between previous rugby experience, concussion education and parent type

There were very few differences in attitude and belief responses regarding injury and injury prevention strategies between previous rugby experience (yes, n=182/no, n=103), concussion education completion (yes, n=157/no, n=128) and parent type (mother, n=179/father, n=105). Parents and guardians who had no rugby playing experience demonstrated significantly greater agreement with the statements ‘injuries are not a problem in rugby’ and ‘it is possible to prevent some rugby injuries’ (z=−2.79, p=0.00) (z=−3.02, p=0.00) (figure 2).

Inter-rater reliability between parents’/guardians’ and players’ responses

The inter-rater agreement regarding the statement ‘it is possible to prevent some rugby injuries’ was almost perfect between parents and guardians and the players (their child) (Gwet’s AC: 0.87 (CI 0.81 to 0.94)) (table 3). The agreement regarding the statement ‘limiting contact sessions in training can prevent injuries in rugby’ was fair (Gwet’s AC: 0.16 (CI 0.04 to 029)) (table 3).

Discussion

The current study found that parents and guardians generally held safe and encouraging attitudes and beliefs towards injury prevention strategies. Over half (55%) had completed concussion education, and parent–player responses showed fair to almost perfect agreement across injury-related statements. Many parents and guardians viewed injury risk seriously, with 42% believing injuries could cause long-term physical problems and 35% perceiving players to be at a high risk of suffering an injury (table 2). Similar findings in Australian adolescent rugby have also shown that parents, coaches and players commonly rate injury risk as high, suggesting a consistent perception across adolescent rugby.9

Most parents and guardians (51%) agreed that ‘regularly training tackle skills (could) prevent injuries in rugby’ (table 2). Given the tackle is consistently identified in the wider literature as the leading injury mechanism in adolescent female rugby,3 this represents a key target for injury prevention efforts. In contrast, when the attitudes of rugby players in South Africa towards tackle training were investigated, on average, players reported that it was more important (p=0.0001) to learn proper technique for performance improvements (4.63±0.65 on a scale of 1=not at all important to 5=very important) than for reducing their risk of injury (4.2±1.12).30 The differences in responses may stem from varying demographics and/or may be indicative of improvements made to promote player welfare and safety over performance. Additionally, 49% of parents and guardians agreed that they ‘would like their child’s team to complete a rugby-specific warm-up programme prior to every game and training session’ (table 2). Similar responses have also been reported among Canadian adolescent rugby coaches, with 52% agreeing that they would like their team to complete a rugby-specific warm-up programme.21 Most community partners indicated that they would like their team to complete a rugby-specific warm-up programme, indicating a clear appetite for their use in adolescent female rugby. This is encouraging, given that such programmes are effective injury-prevention strategies in adolescent male rugby in reducing concussion rates.5

In contrast, 11% of parents and guardians disagreed that neck muscle strengthening could prevent concussion in rugby (table 2), despite evidence suggesting such programmes may reduce concussion risk.31 This disconnect may reflect poor dissemination of injury prevention guidance32 or differing parental priorities towards injury prevention. Similarly, 17% disagreed that limiting contact in training could reduce injuries (table 2), yet research in American football showed substantial reductions (64%) in practice-related concussions when collisions were restricted.33 34 Informed by the Health Action Process Approach (HAPA), particularly outcome expectancy, which refers to an individual’s beliefs about the potential consequences of a behaviour. Outcome expectancies play an important role during the motivational phase of the HAPA model, whether an individual decides to engage with a behaviour,35 and are positively associated with intention to perform a behaviour.36 Understanding players’ engagement with injury prevention strategies requires consideration of how parents and guardians influence these decisions and shape players’ outcome expectancies. As the HAPA model highlights, outcome expectancies shape intention; therefore, if parents view certain strategies as ineffective, their child may be less likely to use them. Ultimately, there needs to be a consideration of parental beliefs and targeted interventions to improve potentially harmful parental beliefs through education and communication strategies and support the adoption of evidence-based injury prevention behaviours.

Parents’ and guardians’ understanding of protective equipment (mouthguards and scrum caps) effectiveness varied, as 11% considered mouthguards as effective, and 12% considered scrum caps as effective in preventing concussions (table 2). Although protective equipment has shown benefits in sports like ice hockey,33 rugby research indicates that neither mouthguards nor scrum caps prevent concussion.37 38 These findings suggest a need for education initiatives to address misconceptions about protective equipment. Codesigning such initiatives with community partners may enhance their relevance and impact.32 Recognising what shapes parental perceptions, such as media or manufacturer messaging, would also further support effective education.8 Notably, parents and guardians with previous rugby experience were more likely to believe that protective equipment prevents injuries and concussions (figure 2), possibly reflecting experience bias, where individuals rely on personal playing history to inform their beliefs.

The current study concluded that over half of the parents and guardians had completed some form of concussion education (55%). Of the parents and guardians who had undertaken concussion education, the majority (78%) had completed the RFU’s Headcase e-learning resource, which includes a section on ‘How to reduce the risk of getting a concussion’ covering Activate, protective equipment and tackle technique. Previous literature reported that 39% of parents and guardians of adolescent rugby players had completed a form of concussion education.39 Yet both results are lower than what has been seen in other sports; in adolescent ice hockey, 79% of parents and guardians reported having received a form of concussion education.40 While the differences may stem from the differing sports, the findings imply that lessons may be learnt and applied from different sports to understand how to promote concussion education engagement from parents and guardians of adolescent rugby players. There is also a need to understand why there is a lower engagement of parents and guardians with concussion education resources within rugby in comparison to other sports. One potential explanation includes how the RFU’s Headcase resource is not mandated for parents and guardians.

The secondary aim of the current study was to examine whether the attitudes, beliefs and behaviours of parents and guardians aligned with their child’s (the player’s) attitudes and beliefs. There was almost perfect agreement (Gwet’s AC: 0.91 (0.85 to 0.96) (table 3) between parents and guardians and the player’s response to the statement, ‘regularly training tackle skills can prevent injuries’. Within adolescent female rugby, the tackle has been identified as the most frequent mechanism of injury,3 which makes the tackle a target for injury prevention strategies. The extent to which coaches and adolescent female rugby teams focus their time on tackle training is unknown and will vary between teams, but evidently, well-informed contact training could prove to be an effective form of injury prevention. One such evidence-informed contact training resource coaches/teams could use is World Rugby’s Contact Confident resource, which aims to promote confidence, physical competence and safety during contact rugby. Though ultimately, there appears to be a general buy-in from players and parents and guardians alike that regularly training tackle skills can prevent injuries.

Limitations

Participants were recruited from a wider injury surveillance project, introducing potential selection bias, as they may have been more aware of injuries and prevention strategies than the general population. Likewise, participants may have reported more positive baseline attitudes or beliefs towards injury and prevention compared with those in the wider population. This, along with the low response rate (27%), may limit generalisability. To help improve response rates, future studies could consider launching the survey at the start of the rugby season, when engagement is typically higher. Some survey items required recalling the 2022/2023 season, creating the possibility of recall bias. Social desirability bias may also have influenced responses, although anonymity was ensured to reduce this risk. Additionally, the survey was not piloted by the research team, which may have limited the ability to identify unclear questions; however, this was mitigated by drawing on previously validated surveys.

Research/policy implications

A prominent focus for community partners moving forward is to encourage parents and guardians to engage more with concussion education initiatives. Many parents and guardians believed it was their responsibility to guide their child’s RTP yet had not completed any form of concussion education. To help facilitate greater concussion education engagement among parents and guardians, current education initiatives may need to be adapted and evaluated.

Conclusion

The current study investigated the attitudes, beliefs and behaviours of adolescent female rugby players’ parents and guardians. Overall, they demonstrated positive and safety-oriented views towards injury and injury prevention, though their understanding of protective equipment effectiveness could be strengthened through education. Engagement with concussion education also requires improvement, as 45% reported having not engaged with any concussion education. These findings highlight the need for parent-focused education and improved access to relevant resources.
