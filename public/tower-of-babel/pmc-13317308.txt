Effects of castration on growth performance and cecal microbiota in growing raccoon dogs: an exploratory study
Authors: Guocai Jiao, Yun Jia, Xiao Li, Zhiqiang Han, Liuwei Xie, Xiuli Zhang, Rui Du, Weigang Zhao, Chao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCastration is a common surgical procedure in male farm animals and may affect growth and physiology. However, its effects on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, serum parameters, and cecal microbiota in growing raccoon dogs remain unclear. Therefore, this exploratory study was conducted to evaluate these responses in growing raccoon dogs. Ten healthy raccoon dogs (aged 80 ± 5 days) were randomly allocated to Sham and Cast groups (n = 5 per group).ResultsAfter a 60-day trial, raccoon dogs in the castrated group showed significantly lower final body weight and average daily gain, together with a higher feed to gain ratio (p < 0.05). Castration was also associated with higher apparent digestibility of crude fat and neutral detergent fiber (p < 0.05) and elevated serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels (p < 0.05). In addition, the cecal microbial community structure differed between groups. Compared with the Sham group, the Cast group showed a higher relative abundance of Bacteroidota, a lower Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio, and a higher relative abundance of Prevotella (p < 0.05). Cecal acetic acid and propionic acid concentrations were also higher in the Cast group (p < 0.05).ConclusionsIn this exploratory study, castration was associated with reduced growth performance, altered lipid-related traits, higher apparent digestibility of crude fat and neutral detergent fiber, and shifts in cecal microbiota composition in growing raccoon dogs. The increased relative abundance of Prevotella and higher short-chain fatty acid concentrations may reflect changes in hindgut fermentation and energy utilization following castration. However, given the limited sample size and the lack of direct functional validation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in larger and mechanistic studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05526-4.

Introduction

The raccoon dog (Nyctereutes procyonoides), indigenous to East Asia, is a valuable fur-bearing animal belonging to the order Carnivora and the family Canidae [1]. This species is a typical omnivore with a diverse diet that includes fruits, insects, and frogs [2]. In mammals, gut microbiota composition is shaped by multiple host and environmental factors, including diet, antibiotic exposure, and hormonal status [3–5]. A stable gut microbial community plays essential roles in host physiology, metabolism, reproduction, and overall fitness [6, 7]. Disruption of this ecological balance may lead to alterations in microbiota composition and subsequent physiological changes.

Previous studies in other animal species have shown sex-related differences in gut microbiota composition, possibly because sex hormones shape the intestinal microbial environment [8]. Consistent with this view, castration has been reported to alter gut microbiota composition in both mice and rats. In mice fed either a standard diet or a high-fat diet, castration significantly changed the cecal microbiota and was associated with increased body temperature [9]. In rats, castration significantly reduced serum testosterone levels and markedly altered the structure and composition of the fecal microbiota [10]. As an anabolic steroid hormone, testosterone plays an important role in regulating animal growth and metabolism [11, 12].

In addition to affecting gut microbiota, castration may also influence host metabolism and nutrient utilization. In rats, castration significantly reduced average daily feed intake and body weight while increasing abdominal and subcutaneous fat mass [13]. Under a standard diet, castration also increased serum total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels [13]. Related findings have been reported in cattle, where castration decreased body weight, average daily gain, and dry matter intake and was associated with reduced concentrations of acetate and propionate in cecal contents [14]. Castration also decreased the activities of cecal digestive enzymes, including cellulase, xylanase, pectinase, and β-glucosidase, but increased those of lipase and α-amylase [14]. Moreover, biological sex may influence nutrient digestion and utilization [15]. In goats, castration increased dry matter intake and improved the digestibility of dry matter, crude protein, ether extract, ash, nitrogen-free extract, and crude fiber [16]. In calves, castration did not affect early growth performance but significantly increased the apparent digestibility of organic matter [17]. Together, these findings suggest that serum biochemical traits, nutrient digestibility, and cecal microbial metabolites are biologically relevant indicators for evaluating post-castration responses.

To date, numerous studies have investigated the effects of castration in laboratory animals, livestock, and poultry, providing a valuable foundation for the broader utilization of these species [18–20]. However, although the raccoon dog was included in the Chinese Catalogue for Livestock and Poultry Genetic Resources in 2020 [21], research on the effects of castration in this species remains limited. In particular, the impacts of castration on growth performance, metabolic status, nutrient digestibility, and gut microbiota in raccoon dogs are still poorly understood. Based on evidence from other animal species, we hypothesized that castration would reduce growth performance and alter serum biochemical parameters, nutrient digestibility, cecal microbiota composition, and microbial metabolic products in growing raccoon dogs during the post-castration period. Therefore, the present study was conducted to test this hypothesis by comprehensively evaluating these parameters. The findings of this study are expected to provide a scientific basis for further research on growth and development, as well as for production practices in raccoon dogs.

Materials and methods

Animals experimental design

The feeding trial was conducted at the experimental fur animal breeding base of our research group located in Yutan Town, Nanguan District, Changchun, China. The experimental animals used in this study were bred and obtained from this facility. All animals were healthy and maintained under standard farm management conditions prior to the experiment. A total of ten 80 (± 5) day-old healthy male Jizuo black raccoon dogs with similar body weights (3.38 ± 0.27 kg) were randomly assigned into two groups, with 5 replicates, each consisting of 1 raccoon dog. The two groups were defined as the sham-operated group (Sham) and the castrated group (Cast).

The surgical procedure for the castrated raccoon dogs was performed according to a previously described method [22]. Briefly, raccoon dogs were premedicated intramuscularly with atropine (0.02 mg/kg; Macklin, Shanghai, China) and acepromazine (0.05 mg/kg; Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) 10–15 min before anesthesia induction. Propofol (Macklin, Shanghai, China) was used to induce anesthesia. Anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China) using a CDS 9000 anesthesia machine (SurgiVet, St. Paul, MN, USA). To minimize pain and inflammation, meloxicam (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China) was injected before and after surgery. All surgical procedures were performed under sterile conditions. In the Cast group, a 2-cm ventral midline scrotal incision was made, and both testes were removed. The skin incision was then routinely closed with nonabsorbable suture. In the sham-operated group, the same incision and suturing procedures were performed without removal of the testes.

The experimental diets were formulated to meet the nutrient requirements recommended by the NRC [23]. The ingredient composition and nutrient levels of the experimental diets are presented in Table 1. The formulation and composition of the experimental diet are shown in Table 1. All animals were housed individually and fed twice daily at 07:00 and 14:00, with free access to water. The feeding trial lasted for 60 days following a 7-day postoperative recovery period.

During the 7-day postoperative recovery period, all animals were monitored daily for general health status and recovery. Feed intake, behavioral condition (including appetite, activity, posture, and response to external stimuli), and clinical signs were recorded daily. Wound healing was also assessed by visual inspection of the incision site for swelling, redness, bleeding, exudation, dehiscence, or other signs of infection. Animals were closely observed to ensure adequate recovery before the start of the feeding trial. All procedures were conducted with careful attention to animal welfare, including perioperative monitoring, anesthesia, analgesia, and postoperative care. This study was designed as an exploratory experiment, and the sample size was determined by balancing the study objectives with ethical and practical considerations in accordance with the 3R principles.

Sample collection and preparation

The weights of the raccoon dogs were measured on day 0 and day 60 of the trial before the morning feeding. The amounts of feed offered and residual feed were recorded daily to calculate the average daily gain, average daily feed intake (ADFI), and the ratio of feed to gain (F/G).

A 3-day digestion and metabolism trial was conducted on days 40 to 42 of the experiment. Quantitative total fecal collection was performed for each animal throughout the trial period. Feed offered and refusals were recorded daily, and actual nutrient intake was calculated based on feed offered minus refusals. Feces were collected daily, pooled by animal at the end of the collection period, and thoroughly homogenized. An appropriate amount of 10% sulfuric acid was added to the fecal samples to fix nitrogen. The samples were then dried at 65 °C to constant weight, ground to pass through a 40-mesh sieve, and additionally passed through an 18-mesh sieve for neutral detergent fiber (NDF) analysis before storage until further analysis. Diet and fecal samples were analyzed for dry matter (DM), crude ash, crude fat (CF), crude protein (CP), calcium, and total phosphorus according to the following Chinese national standards: DM according to GB/T 6435 − 2014, crude ash according to GB/T 6438 − 2007, CF according to GB/T 6433 − 2006, CP according to GB/T 6432 − 2018 using the Kjeldahl method, total phosphorus according to GB/T 6437 − 2018 using spectrophotometry, and calcium according to GB/T 6436 − 2018 using the disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate complexometric titration method. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) was determined according to Van Soest et al. [24] using an ANKOM A2000i fiber analyzer (ANKOM Technology, Fairport, NY, USA). Gross energy in diet samples was determined using an oxygen bomb calorimeter (C2000, IKA, Staufen, Germany). Apparent nutrient digestibility was calculated as follows: Apparent nutrient digestibility (%) = (nutrient intake – fecal nutrient output)/nutrient intake × 100. Nutrient intake was calculated as feed intake (feed offered minus refusals) multiplied by the corresponding nutrient concentration in the diet, whereas fecal nutrient output was calculated as total fecal dry matter output multiplied by the corresponding nutrient concentration in feces.

At the end of the trial, blood was collected from the raccoon dogs’ hind limb vein in the morning after fasting, using 5 mL vacuum blood collection tubes without any additives. The blood samples were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min at 4℃ to separate the serum, which was then stored at -80 °C℃ for the analysis of serum parameters. Subsequently, the raccoon dogs were humanely slaughtered by electric shock, and their cecum contents were collected using sterile centrifuge tubes and stored at -80 °C℃ for the analysis of the cecal microbiota and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs).

Measurement of serum parameters

Testosterone (Cat# JL11087) and estradiol (Cat# JL11671) concentrations in the serum were measured using ELISA kits (Shanghai Jianglai Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). Commercial colorimetric kits (Beijing Zhongsheng Beikong Biochemistry Company, Beijing, China) were used to determine the levels of serum triglycerides (Cat# 100020090), cholesterol (Cat# 100020080), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C; Cat# 100020238), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C; Cat# 100020248) using the Beckman AU480 automatic biochemistry analyzer (Vitalab Selectra E, Spankeren, The Netherlands).

Measurement of SCFAs concentrations in cecal contents

The concentrations of the SCFAs (acetic, propionic, isobutyric, butyric, isovaleric, and valeric acids) in the cecal contents were measured by an Agilent 7890 A gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies Inc., California, United States). The column used was an Agilent HP5 silica capillary column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.32 μm; Agilent Technologies Inc., California, United States). The injector and detector temperatures were set at 250 °C, and the carrier gas used was high-purity nitrogen. The initial temperature was 60 °C, which was then increased at a rate of 10 °C/minute to 170 °C, and further raised by 8 °C/minute to 212 °C.

Sequencing and analysis of cecal microbiota

The cecal content samples were sent to Novogene Co., LTD (Beijing, China) for the analysis of cecal microbiota composition. The total genomic DNA of the samples was extracted using the CTAB/SDS method. The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F (5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’). Sequencing libraries were generated with NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit (Cat No. E7645). The quality of the libraries was assessed using the Qubit@ 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Santa Clara, CA, USA). Finally, the libraries were subjected to sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, CA, USA), generating 250 bp paired-end reads for further analysis. The FLASH software (Version 1.2.11) was utilized to assemble the reads of the samples, generating Raw Tags. Quality control of the Raw Tags was performed using the fastp software (Version 0.20.0). The Tags were compared with the Silva database to identify and remove chimeric constructs, ultimately obtaining the Effective Tags. These Effective Tags were denoised using the DADA2 in the QIIME2 software (Version QIIME2-202006) to obtain initial Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs), and ASVs with an abundance less than 5 were eliminated. The QIIME2 software was employed to align the ASVs against a reference database, enabling the assignment of taxonomic information to each ASV. The absolute abundance of ASVs was normalized using a standard of the sequence number corresponding to the sample with the fewest sequences.

The sequencing depth adequacy was evaluated using rarefaction curves based on the Observed OTUs and Shannon index. Good’s coverage was also calculated to further assess the completeness of sequencing. The alpha diversity indices were calculated using the QIIME2 software. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances was conducted to reveal the differences in bacterial communities between the two groups. To study the significance of differences in community structure, the Adonis functions within the QIIME2 software were used. Differences in the relative abundances of microbial taxa between the two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test. Functional profiles of the cecal microbiota were predicted using Tax4Fun, and differences in predicted pathways between the two groups were further compared. In addition, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed using LEfSe software (version 1.0) to identify microbial biomarkers between the two groups, with the LDA score threshold set at 4.0.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to analysis, data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test [25] and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test [26]. For data that met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, differences between the two independent groups were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test [27]. For data that met the assumption of normality but violated homogeneity of variance, a two-tailed Welch’s t-test was applied [28]. Variables analyzed using Welch’s t-test included the F/G ratio, testosterone concentration, the relative abundance of Streptococcus, DM digestibility, crude fat digestibility, NDF digestibility, and the concentrations of acetic acid, propionic acid, and valeric acid. Potential outliers were inspected during data screening and were retained in the analysis unless attributable to clear measurement or recording errors. Each animal was considered the experimental unit. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). In addition, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between cecal microbial taxa and production and metabolic indices [29]. Multiple testing correction for differential taxa, predicted functional pathways, and Spearman’s correlation analyses was performed separately using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method [30]. Adjusted p values (q values) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Results with 0.05 ≤ q < 0.10 were considered suggestive only and were interpreted with caution. For analyses not subject to multiple-testing correction, a two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and 0.05 ≤ p < 0.10 was considered a trend.

Results

Effects of castration on growth performance and serum hormone concentrations

There was no significant difference in initial body weight between the Sham and Cast groups (Sham = 3.35 ± 0.29 kg, Cast = 3.39 ± 0.29 kg, Fig. 1A, p = 0.832). At the end of the experiment, the Cast group had a lower final body weight than the Sham group (Sham = 6.47 ± 0.34 kg, Cast = 5.84 ± 0.35 kg, Fig. 1A, p = 0.019). Average daily gain was also lower in the Cast group (Sham = 52.00 ± 2.25 g/d, Cast = 40.83 ± 3.58 g/d, Fig. 1B, p < 0.001), whereas the F/G ratio was higher (Sham = 4.95 ± 0.22, Cast = 6.35 ± 0.54, Fig. 1C, p = 0.008). In addition, serum testosterone concentration was significantly lower in the Cast group than in the Sham group (Sham = 136.05 ± 32.70 pg/mL, Cast = 58.92 ± 10.52 pg/mL, Fig. 1D, p = 0.008). No significant difference was detected in serum estradiol concentration between the two groups (Sham = 10.54 ± 1.40 pg/mL, Cast = 9.57 ± 1.13 pg/mL, Fig. 1E, p = 0.259).

Effects of castration on cecal microbiota diversity and community structure

Following quality filtering, a total of 634,795 high-quality reads were obtained, with an average sequence count of 63,480 ± 5,088 reads per sample (Table S1). Rarefaction curves for both Observed OTUs and the Shannon index approached a plateau in all samples (Fig. 2A and B), indicating that the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture most of the bacterial diversity. The Venn diagram showed that 527 OTUs were shared between the Sham and Cast groups, whereas 442 and 357 OTUs were unique to the Sham and Cast groups, respectively (Fig. 2C).

Alpha diversity analysis showed no significant differences between the Sham and Cast groups in Observed OTUs (Sham = 370.00 ± 60.36, Cast = 406.20 ± 36.37, Fig. 3A, p = 0.284), Chao1 (Sham = 372.22 ± 60.98, Cast = 413.90 ± 38.38, Fig. 3B, p = 0.232), Pielou’s evenness (Sham = 0.78 ± 0.03, Cast = 0.74 ± 0.07, Fig. 3C, p = 0.270), Shannon index (Sham = 6.65 ± 0.42, Cast = 6.42 ± 0.67, Fig. 3D, p = 0.538), or Simpson index (Sham = 0.97 ± 0.01, Cast = 0.96 ± 0.04, Fig. 3E, p = 0.316). Effects of castration on cecal microbial composition and differential taxa

At the phylum, genus, and species levels, the compositional profiles of the cecal microbiota differed between the Sham and Cast groups (Fig. 4B-D). At the phylum level, Bacteroidota (Sham = 39.65 ± 4.44%, Cast = 53.74 ± 5.82%) and Firmicutes (Sham = 54.02 ± 6.58%, Cast = 39.85 ± 6.29%) were the dominant phyla in both groups (Fig. 4B). After Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, the relative abundance of Bacteroidota remained significantly higher in the Cast group than in the Sham group (Fig. 5B, p = 0.042), whereas Firmicutes showed only a suggestive decrease (Fig. 5A, p = 0.068). Accordingly, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio (Sham = 1.39 ± 0.31, Cast = 0.76 ± 0.18) was significantly lower in the Cast group (Fig. 5C, p = 0.004). At the genus level, Prevotella (Sham = 29.07 ± 5.35%, Cast = 42.06 ± 9.81%), Streptococcus (Sham = 18.99 ± 11.27%, Cast = 4.94 ± 2.97%), Lactobacillus (Sham = 3.15 ± 1.99%, Cast = 4.18 ± 4.79%), Megasphaera (Sham = 3.18 ± 1.30%, Cast = 2.86 ± 3.67%), and Blautia (Sham = 2.61 ± 0.49%, Cast = 4.66 ± 2.42%) were among the predominant taxa (Fig. 4C). Compared with the Sham group, the relative abundance of Prevotella was significantly increased in the Cast group after FDR correction (Fig. 5D, p = 0.047), whereas Streptococcus showed a suggestive decrease (Fig. 5E, p = 0.071). At the species level, Prevotella_copri was the most abundant species in both groups (Sham = 18.99 ± 4.43%, Cast = 29.35 ± 7.19%, Fig. 4D). Its relative abundance was numerically higher in the Cast group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance after FDR correction, although a suggestive difference was observed (Fig. 5F, p = 0.096).

Predicted functional profiles of the cecal microbiota

To investigate functional differences in the cecal microbiota between the two groups, Tax4Fun analysis was performed to predict the top 20 enriched functions at KEGG level 2. As shown in Table S2, the predominant functional categories included carbohydrate metabolism, replication and repair, membrane transport, translation, and amino acid metabolism. Compared with the Sham group, the Cast group showed significantly higher predicted abundances of pathways related to amino acid metabolism (p = 0.038), transport and catabolism (p = 0.033), metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (p = 0.023), and energy metabolism (p < 0.001), whereas pathways associated with membrane transport (p = 0.003) and transcription (p = 0.001) showed significantly lower predicted abundances (Fig. 7).

Effects of castration on apparent nutrient digestibility

The effects of castration on feed intake and apparent nutrient digestibility are shown in Fig. 8. Discussion

Castration in males is a common surgical procedure used in both biomedical research and farm production. Following castration, the removal of the testes, which are the primary source of male sex hormones, results in a rapid decline in circulating testosterone. Previous studies have shown that castration reduced plasma testosterone concentrations in Göttingen minipigs to below assay sensitivity within 10–18 days [31], and increased heart rate and respiratory rate in male dogs by day 14 after surgery [32], indicating that castration can induce physiological changes within a relatively short period. Holstein cattle have also been reported to show reduced body weight after castration [14]. Consistent with previous reports, the present study showed that castration reduced body weight and average daily gain, while increasing the feed to gain ratio in raccoon dogs. Other research found that castration induced the expression of uncoupling protein 1 in the brown adipose tissue and subcutaneous white adipose tissue of mice fed a normal diet, which was associated with elevated body temperature and increased energy consumption, thereby limiting weight gain [33]. This study may provide a possible reference for interpretation, but our data do not directly demonstrate a similar mechanism in raccoon dogs. One possible explanation is that castration may have altered energy metabolism in a manner unfavorable for growth; however, because body composition, skeletal muscle traits, and energy expenditure were not measured in the present study, this interpretation remains hypothetical. However, it has also been reported that castration had no effect on the average daily gain of Awassi lambs fed a high concentrate diet, while the feed to gain ratio was significantly increased [34]. This may have been related to dry matter intake increased in castrated lambs, thereby compensating for their lower feed utilization efficiency. Additionally, a study conducted in mice reported that castration induced body weight gain in mice fed a high-fat diet but not in those on a standard diet [35]. Taken together, these contrasting findings suggest that the growth response to castration may depend on dietary and physiological context. However, because the present study was conducted under only one dietary regimen, the potential interaction between castration and diet could not be evaluated directly. From a practical husbandry perspective, the present findings do not support castration as a strategy to improve growth performance in growing raccoon dogs.

We also observed that castration significantly reduced serum testosterone concentrations but had no significant effect on serum estradiol concentrations. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Rhodes et al. in beagle dogs [22]. The decrease in testosterone is biologically plausible because testosterone is produced primarily by Leydig cells in the testes [36], whereas only a small proportion of estradiol is synthesized in the testes through aromatase activity [37]. Testosterone has well-established anabolic effects on bone and skeletal muscle through androgen receptor-mediated pathways [38]. Previous studies have shown that it can promote protein synthesis while inhibiting protein breakdown in skeletal muscle, thereby supporting growth [39]. Higher testosterone concentrations have also been associated with increased growth hormone release, positive nitrogen balance, and greater calcium and phosphorus deposition in bone [38]. Accordingly, the marked decline in testosterone after castration may be one factor associated with the reduced growth performance observed in raccoon dogs. However, because skeletal muscle traits, growth hormone, nitrogen retention, and body composition were not measured in the present study, the specific biological pathways linking reduced testosterone to poorer growth cannot be determined directly from our data.

Diet, feeding pattern, environment, host phylogeny, and other factors can influence the homeostasis of the gut microbiota [40–43]. Among these, diet is regarded as one of the principal determinants of gut microbial composition and function [44]. In addition to altering sex hormone levels, castration may also have implications for gut microbiota composition and overall host health [9, 14]. In the present study, castration was associated with alterations in the cecal microbial composition of raccoon dogs. At the phylum level, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes were the two dominant phyla, jointly accounting for more than 90% of the total community, which is consistent with the general microbial pattern reported in mammals [45]. After Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, Bacteroidota remained significantly enriched in the Cast group, whereas Firmicutes was only numerically reduced. Consequently, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio was significantly lower following castration. Previous studies have suggested that variation in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio may be associated with gut dysbiosis, immune status, and energy metabolism, although these relationships are not always consistent across studies and species [46–48]. To characterize differences in gut microbial community structure, the term “enterotype” was introduced [49]. In the present study, despite a significant shift in β diversity after castration, the overall cecal microbial community in both groups remained broadly characterized by a Prevotella-dominant pattern.

Prevotella-dominant microbial communities are often associated with the fermentation of plant-derived polysaccharides and the production of SCFAs [50]. Prevotella has been reported to harbor enzymes involved in the degradation of cellulose and xylan, and its abundance is often associated with plant-based diets [51, 52]. Since the diet composition was identical between the two groups in the present study, the higher relative abundance of Prevotella in the Cast group may be compatible with altered fermentation of dietary polysaccharides after castration. However, microbial activity and substrate utilization were not measured directly, so this interpretation remains tentative. In addition, predictive functional analysis using Tax4Fun suggested a higher representation of pathways related to energy metabolism in the Cast group; however, because this approach provides inferred rather than measured functional information, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Streptococcus, a member of the family Streptococcaceae, is a normal component of the gastrointestinal microbiota [53]. Streptococcus was numerically lower in the Cast group, but this difference did not remain statistically robust after multiple-testing, and its biological relevance therefore remains uncertain. Although some previous studies have linked Streptococcus to host developmental or metabolic traits [54–56], the present dataset does not allow us to determine whether the lower abundance trend observed here was related to hormonal changes, altered nutrient availability, or other physiological responses after castration. Accordingly, these findings should be regarded as associative rather than mechanistic.

Nutrient digestibility involves a quantitative assessment of the nutritional and physiological factors associated with digestive capacity and gut function [57]. In the present study, dry matter digestibility showed an increasing trend, and NDF digestibility was significantly higher in the Cast group than in the Sham group. NDF mainly represents plant cell wall components, including cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin [58]. As a carnivorous omnivore, the raccoon dog has a relatively short digestive tract, and the utilization of structurally complex plant polysaccharides may depend to a considerable extent on hindgut microbial fermentation [59]. Consistent with this possibility, acetic acid and propionic acid concentrations in cecal contents were significantly higher in the Cast group. In addition, Spearman’s correlation analysis showed a positive association between acetic acid concentration and Prevotella abundance. These parallel changes are consistent with an overall shift in hindgut fermentation status associated with castration, but they do not demonstrate that Prevotella directly caused the observed differences in digestibility or SCFA production. Given the small sample size and the correlational nature of the analysis, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Further studies are needed to clarify whether Prevotella contributes functionally to fiber degradation and SCFA production in raccoon dogs. At the species level, P. copri was numerically higher in the Cast group, but this difference did not remain statistically significant after multiple-testing correction and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Previous studies in other species have reported associations between Prevotella or P. copri and nutrient utilization or lipid-related phenotypes [60–62]. These reports provide useful context, but they do not demonstrate that the same biological relationships apply in raccoon dogs. In the present study, crude fat digestibility was higher in the Cast group, and serum biochemical analyses showed significantly higher cholesterol and triglyceride concentrations in this group. These findings indicate that castration was accompanied by concurrent changes in microbial composition and lipid-related traits, but the present dataset does not support a specific functional role for P. copri in these changes. Although P. copri showed positive correlations with crude fat digestibility, cholesterol, and triglyceride concentrations, none of these associations reached statistical significance.

One of the most notable findings of this study is that increased apparent digestibility did not translate into improved growth performance. Castrated raccoon dogs showed higher crude fat and NDF digestibility, as well as higher acetic acid and propionic acid concentrations, yet exhibited lower body weight gain and poorer feed efficiency. This pattern suggests that nutrient utilization after absorption may have differed between groups; however, the underlying mechanism cannot be determined from the present dataset. Because body composition, skeletal muscle traits, nitrogen retention, and energy expenditure were not measured directly, it remains unclear whether the observed discrepancy reflected reduced lean tissue accretion, altered maintenance energy requirements, greater lipid deposition, or other changes in energy partitioning. Accordingly, altered energy partitioning should be regarded as a working hypothesis rather than a conclusion supported directly by the present data. This limitation should be kept in mind when interpreting the concurrent changes in digestibility, SCFA concentrations, and growth performance.

From a practical husbandry perspective, the present findings do not support castration as a strategy to improve growth performance in growing raccoon dogs. Although castration was associated with higher apparent digestibility of some nutrients, it also associated with reduced average daily gain and poorer feed efficiency. At the same time, the associated changes in serum lipid indices, SCFA concentrations, and cecal microbial composition suggest that castration may influence host metabolism in ways that could be relevant to animal management. However, the practical significance of these metabolic and microbial changes remains uncertain because the present study did not assess body composition, carcass traits, health outcomes, or longer-term production value. Therefore, if castration is performed for reproductive, behavioral, or management reasons, its potential effects on growth and metabolism should be taken into account rather than assuming a production benefit.

Overall, this study should be regarded as an exploratory investigation of the effects of surgical castration on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, serum lipid-related traits, and cecal microbiota composition in growing raccoon dogs. Castration was associated with reduced growth performance, altered serum lipid indices, higher apparent digestibility of some nutrients, and shifts in the cecal microbiota, including a higher relative abundance of Prevotella. Although P. copri showed a numerical increase, this difference was not robust after multiple-testing correction and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. More broadly, the observed associations among hormone status, microbial composition, and metabolic traits do not establish causality. Several additional limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the small sample size (n = 5 per group) limited statistical power, particularly for microbiota analyses, and increased the influence of individual variation. Second, baseline fecal or cecal microbiota composition was not assessed prior to castration, which limits our ability to determine whether the observed microbial differences were caused by castration or partly reflected pre-existing variation between individuals. Third, the use of a single dietary regimen and a single age range restricts the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, microbiota analysis was based on 16 S rRNA sequencing and predictive functional inference, without direct metagenomic, metabolomic, or experimental validation. Future studies should include larger cohorts, different dietary conditions and developmental stages, direct measurements of body composition and energy metabolism, and functional or interventional approaches to clarify causal mechanisms and practical relevance.

Conclusions

In this exploratory study, surgical castration in growing raccoon dogs was associated with reduced serum testosterone concentrations, poorer growth performance, higher apparent digestibility of crude fat and neutral detergent fiber, altered serum lipid indices, and shifts in cecal microbiota composition. Castration was also accompanied by higher cecal acetic acid and propionic acid concentrations and a higher relative abundance of Prevotella. These findings suggest that castration may affect growth and metabolic status in growing raccoon dogs, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Given the small sample size and the lack of direct functional validation, the results should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger, mechanistic studies.
