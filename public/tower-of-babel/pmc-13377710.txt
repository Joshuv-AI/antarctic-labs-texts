Association between glycemic variability and mortality after coronary stent implantation in critically ill patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xiao-Jun Xiang, Xiao-Long Sun, Dong-Yu Ma, Li Ma, Meng-Yang Liu, Jian-Feng Li, Wen-Bo Zhang, Yin-Di Wang, Ping Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlycemic variability (GV) has been associated with adverse outcomes in critical illness; however, its prognostic relevance in patients undergoing coronary stent implantation remains unclear.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we analyzed 2,427 adult patients who underwent coronary stent implantation and were admitted to the coronary care unit (CCU) or cardiovascular intensive care unit (CVICU) using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database. Glycemic variability (GV) was defined as the coefficient of variation of blood glucose (standard deviation/mean ×100%). The primary and secondary outcomes were in-hospital and 365-day all-cause mortality. Associations were assessed using multivariable Cox models. Nonlinear relationships, survival differences, discriminative ability, and mediation effects were evaluated using restricted cubic splines, Kaplan–Meier analysis, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and mediation analysis, respectively. External validation was performed in the eICU Collaborative Research Database.ResultsPatients in the highest GV quartile had significantly higher risks of in-hospital mortality (HR 2.58, 95% CI 1.30, 5.13) and 365-day mortality (HR 1.77, 95% CI 1.20, 2.60) compared with those in the lowest quartile. A significant trend across GV quartiles was observed (p for trend < 0.01), although excess risk was primarily driven by the highest quartile. These associations were consistent across analyses and were externally validated. Mediation analysis indicated that blood urea nitrogen accounted for 7.7% and 10.3% of the association, respectively.ConclusionsElevated glycemic variability (GV) was independently associated with increased risks of in-hospital and 365-day all-cause mortality after coronary stent implantation. GV may serve as a simple and clinically accessible marker for risk stratification in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06005-2.Keywords: Glycemic variability, Coronary stent implantation, Coronary care unit, Cardiac intensive care unit, Mortality

Introduction

Coronary artery disease remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality [1], with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and stent implantation representing the cornerstone of contemporary management [2]. Despite significant advances in interventional techniques and perioperative care, patients undergoing PCI continue to face substantial risks of adverse outcomes, particularly in high-risk settings such as intensive care [3, 4].

Dysglycemia, encompassing admission hyperglycemia, stress-induced glucose excursions, and glycemic variability (GV), is commonly observed in critically ill patients [5]. Stress hyperglycemia has been associated with poor outcomes in acute coronary syndrome [6], while glycemic variability has been linked to increased mortality in both critically ill and cardiovascular populations [3, 5]. These observations position acute dysglycemia as a clinically relevant manifestation of cardiometabolic stress—the integrated neurohormonal, inflammatory, and metabolic response to acute cardiac injury [7]. In broader critical care settings, stress-related dysglycemia has also been associated with increased mortality, supporting its role as a marker of physiological decompensation [5].

However, static glycemic indices, such as single-point or mean glucose levels, are inherently limited in capturing the dynamic nature of glucose homeostasis during critical illness [8]. Factors such as acute myocardial ischemia, procedural stress, and neurohormonal activation contribute to pronounced glucose fluctuations that static measures fail to reflect [8, 9]. This limitation is clinically relevant, as large randomized trials have shown that strategies focusing solely on lowering mean glucose do not improve survival and may increase the risk of hypoglycemia [10]. Consequently, glycemic variability has emerged as a more integrative marker of acute metabolic instability. Accumulating evidence indicates that glycemic variability is associated with increased mortality in both critically ill populations [4] and cardiovascular populations [3]. This association has also been observed in patients with acute coronary syndrome [6] and those undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention [11].

To evaluate the association between glycemic variability and mortality outcomes in critically ill patients undergoing coronary stent implantation, we conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study. We used data from two large critical care databases, the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU). The objectives of this study were to: (1) examine the association between glycemic variability during intensive care unit (ICU) stay and in-hospital and 365-day all-cause mortality; (2) explore the potential mediating role of renal dysfunction, as assessed by blood urea nitrogen (BUN); and (3) validate these findings in an independent external cohort.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This retrospective multicenter cohort study used data from two large publicly available critical care databases: the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 3.1) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU, version 2.0) [12, 13]. The MIMIC-IV database contains de-identified clinical records of patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022, including demographics, diagnoses, laboratory measurements, procedures, and outcomes. The eICU Collaborative Research Database is a multicenter critical care database comprising data from intensive care units across the United States. Given the retrospective design and the use of fully de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Access to the database was granted to one of the authors (X.J.X.), who completed the required Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) program (certification number: 72621916).

Study population

Adult patients with coronary artery disease were identified using predefined ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM diagnosis code ranges (ICD-9-CM: 410.xx, 411.0–411.89, 412, 413.xx, 414.00–414.11; ICD-10-CM: I20.x, I21.x, I23.x, I24.x, I25.1–I25.2). In the MIMIC-IV database, codes were extracted in their original format without decimal points (e.g., 410xx and I219). Coronary stent implantation was identified based on procedure records. The following exclusion criteria were applied sequentially: (1) non-first hospital admission and non-first ICU stay; (2) ICU stay < 24 h; (3) absence of coronary stent implantation; (4) admission to units other than the coronary care unit (CCU) or cardiovascular intensive care unit (CVICU); (5) metastatic malignancy; and (6) fewer than three blood glucose measurements during the ICU stay, as at least three measurements are generally considered necessary to ensure a reliable estimation of glycemic variability [14].

External validation cohort

External validation was conducted using the eICU database. Patients with coronary stent implantation were identified based on procedure records (Supplementary Fig. S1) (Fig. 1).

Patient selection flowchart from the MIMIC-IV database. CCU, Coronary Care Unit; CVICU, Cardiovascular Intensive Care Unit

Study endpoints

The primary outcome was in-hospital all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcome was 365-day all-cause mortality. In the MIMIC-IV cohort, follow-up was defined from admission until death or up to 365 days. In the eICU cohort, only in-hospital mortality was available, and follow-up was therefore limited to the duration of hospitalization due to the absence of post-discharge data.

Glycemic variability

Glycemic variability (GV) was assessed using all available blood glucose measurements during the ICU stay. Mean glucose was calculated as the average of all recorded values during the ICU stay, and SD was used to quantify glucose dispersion [15]. GV was expressed as the coefficient of variation (CV), calculated as SD divided by mean glucose and multiplied by 100% (GV = [SD/mean] × 100%), a commonly used metric in prior studies [14].

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature. These comprised demographic characteristics (age, sex, and body mass index), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, and renal disease), illness severity assessed by the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [16], and laboratory parameters obtained within the first 24 h of ICU admission, including white blood cell count, platelet count, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and potassium. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for the overall model and all covariates. The functional form of continuous variables was assessed using residual diagnostics (e.g., Martingale residuals), with no major deviations from linearity. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors, all of which were < 5.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0) and Python (statsmodels). GraphPad Prism (version 10.6) was used for figure preparation. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value < 0.05.

Missing data were handled prior to model construction. Variables with a missing rate > 10% were excluded a priori to minimize potential bias and model instability associated with excessive missingness. For the remaining covariates, missing values were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) under the missing-at-random assumption, incorporating all variables included in the analysis into the imputation model [17]. Overall, most variables had low proportions of missingness, with no evident differences across GV groups.

Baseline characteristics were summarized across glycemic variability (GV) quartiles. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and compared using analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Kaplan–Meier curves were generated and compared using the log-rank test. The association between GV and mortality was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models, including unadjusted and multivariable-adjusted models. GV was analyzed as both categorical (quartiles) and continuous variables, and linear trends were assessed by modeling quartiles as an ordinal variable. Restricted cubic spline analyses with four knots were used to assess potential non-linear associations between GV and mortality. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed to evaluate the discriminative performance of GV, with area under the curve (AUC) calculated at multiple time points [18].

Predefined subgroup analyses were conducted with interaction testing. Mediation analysis was performed to evaluate the mediating effect of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), using regression-based models with bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) [19]. Sensitivity analyses included a complete-case analysis and assessment of unmeasured confounding using E-values [20], calculated for the fully adjusted model comparing the highest (Q4) versus lowest (Q1) quartiles of GV.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics stratified by GV quartiles are summarized in Table 1. Glycemic variability was categorized into quartiles: Q1 < 16.35%, Q2 16.35–21.08%, Q3 21.08–27.08%, and Q4 ≥ 27.08%. Patients in higher GV quartiles had a lower proportion of males and a greater burden of cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities, including diabetes, congestive heart failure, and renal disease. Disease severity indicators, such as SOFA score, and laboratory markers, including indicators of systemic inflammation (white blood cell count) and renal dysfunction (serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen), generally increased across GV quartiles. Several additional physiological and laboratory parameters also differed significantly across GV quartiles (Table 1).

In-hospital mortality differed across GV quartiles, with proportions of 1.8% in Q1, 3.0% in Q2, 2.5% in Q3, and 7.1% in Q4. A similar pattern was observed for 365-day mortality. Although formal trend tests indicated a significant association (p < 0.001 for trend), the mortality proportions across the intermediate quartiles were not strictly monotonic, indicating that the excess risk is primarily driven by the highest GV quartile. These findings support a threshold-like pattern, wherein clinically significant risk elevation emerges predominantly at higher GV levels (Q4) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed differences in survival probability across GV quartiles (Fig. 2). For in-hospital mortality (Fig. 2A), patients in the highest GV quartile (Q4) exhibited noticeably lower survival compared with the other quartiles, while the survival curves for Q1–Q3 were relatively similar with some overlap. A comparable pattern was observed for 365-day mortality (Fig. 2B), with the most pronounced decline in survival occurring in the Q4 group. Log-rank tests indicated overall differences among GV quartiles for both outcomes (p < 0.001).

Kaplan–Meier curves for all-cause mortality stratified by glycemic variability (GV) quartiles. A In-hospital mortality; (B) 365-day mortality. Differences among groups were compared using the log-rank test

Association between GV and mortality (RCS analysis)

Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated a positive association between GV and mortality risk (Fig. 3). For in-hospital mortality (Fig. 3A), the overall association was significant (p-overall = 0.001), with no evidence of nonlinearity (p-nonlinear = 0.894). Similarly, for 365-day mortality (Fig. 3B), GV was significantly associated with mortality risk (p-overall < 0.001), and the relationship remained approximately linear (p-nonlinear = 0.194).

Association between glycemic variability (GV) and all-cause mortality in the MIMIC-IV cohort (n = 2,427), modeled using restricted cubic splines with four knots. A In-hospital mortality; (B) 365-day mortality. The red line represents adjusted hazard ratios (HRs), with dashed lines indicating 95% confidence intervals and gray bars showing the distribution of GV. Models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, diabetes, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, renal disease, SOFA score, white blood cell count, platelet count, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and potassium

Notably, while the restricted cubic spline analysis did not detect statistical nonlinearity, the hazard ratio curve exhibited a steeper upward trajectory at higher GV levels, reinforcing the threshold-like pattern observed in the quartile-based analysis.

Cox regression analysis

Higher glycemic variability (GV) was consistently associated with increased risks of in-hospital and 365-day mortality (Fig. 4). In fully adjusted models, patients in the highest GV quartile had a significantly higher risk of in-hospital mortality (HR 2.58, 95% CI 1.30, 5.13) and 365-day mortality (HR 1.77, 95% CI 1.20, 2.60) compared with those in the lowest quartile. Although formal trend tests indicated a statistically significant association across GV quartiles (p for trend < 0.01), the mortality risk elevation was not uniformly graded but was primarily concentrated in the highest quartile, consistent with a threshold-like pattern. When GV was modeled as a continuous variable, it remained independently associated with both in-hospital and 365-day mortality in fully adjusted analyses, though the magnitude of risk increase appeared more pronounced at higher GV levels.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses are presented in Fig. 5. The association between GV and mortality was consistent across subgroups defined by age, sex, diabetes status, myocardial infarction, and renal disease. No significant interactions were detected for either in-hospital or 365-day mortality (all p for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the prognostic impact of GV was robust across clinically relevant subpopulations.

Models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, diabetes, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, renal disease, SOFA score, white blood cell count, platelet count, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and potassium, excluding the variable used to define each subgroup

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis suggested that blood urea nitrogen (BUN) may partially explain the association between GV and mortality, accounting for 7.7% and 10.3% of the observed association for in-hospital and 365-day mortality, respectively (Table S1).

Discriminative ability of GV

Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of GV for mortality outcomes (Fig. 6). The area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.813 at 30 days, 0.810 at 60 days, 0.802 at 180 days, and 0.686 at 365 days, indicating good discrimination at shorter time points, with decreasing discrimination over longer follow-up.

Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for glycemic variability (GV) in predicting all-cause mortality in the MIMIC-IV cohort. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated at 30, 60, 180, and 365 days

External validation using the eICU database

In-hospital mortality varied across glycemic variability (GV) quartiles in the eICU cohort, with proportions of 3.9%, 10.5%, 8.3%, and 18.2% for Q1–Q4, respectively (p for trend < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. S3). In multivariable Cox models, patients in the highest GV quartile had a significantly higher risk of in-hospital mortality compared with those in the lowest quartile, and this association remained consistent across all models. When analyzed as a continuous variable, GV remained independently associated with in-hospital mortality. Overall, findings from the eICU cohort were consistent with those from the MIMIC-IV cohort (Fig. 7).

Association between glycemic variability (GV) and in-hospital mortality in the eICU cohort using Cox proportional hazards models. Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age, sex, and body mass index; Model 3: further adjusted for clinical covariates

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings. Complete-case analysis yielded similar results, suggesting minimal impact of missing data. E-values were calculated for the fully adjusted model comparing the highest (Q4) versus lowest (Q1) quartiles of GV. For in-hospital mortality, E-values were 4.60 for the point estimate and 1.92 for the lower confidence limit; corresponding values for 365-day mortality were 2.93 and 1.70, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). These findings indicate that the observed associations are unlikely to be materially influenced by missing data or unmeasured confounding.

Discussion

In this large multicenter cohort study with external validation, higher glycemic variability during ICU stay (GV) was independently associated with increased risks of both in-hospital and 365-day all-cause mortality among critically ill patients undergoing coronary stent implantation. These associations were consistent across multiple analytical approaches, including continuous and categorical modeling, restricted cubic spline analyses, subgroup analyses, time-dependent ROC analyses, and sensitivity analyses, and were reproduced in an independent external cohort. In addition, mediation analysis was conducted to explore potential pathways underlying these associations. Notably, the observed association appeared to be primarily driven by patients in the highest GV quartile, rather than a strictly monotonic increase across all quartiles, suggesting a threshold-like pattern in which excess risk becomes clinically relevant mainly at higher levels of GV. Collectively, these findings suggest that glycemic variability during ICU stay may serve as a potential prognostic marker in this high-risk cardiovascular population.

Similar associations between GV and mortality have been reported in heterogeneous ICU and cardiovascular populations [4, 6, 21]. The consistency of this association across clinically relevant subgroups—including age, sex, diabetes status, myocardial infarction, and renal dysfunction—underscores the broad prognostic relevance of GV. Notably, the absence of significant effect modification by diabetes status in our study suggests that the detrimental effects of GV extend beyond chronic hyperglycemia and may, at least in part, reflect acute stress-related metabolic dysregulation, a phenomenon widely observed in critically ill populations, including recent evidence from ICU patients with trauma [22].

Emerging mechanistic evidence provides biological plausibility for these findings. Oscillating glucose levels have been shown to induce greater endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory activation than sustained hyperglycemia [23]. They may also impair nitric oxide bioavailability and promote platelet hyperactivation [24, 25], potentially contributing to myocardial injury and adverse structural remodeling through pathways such as AKT/GSK-3β signaling and endoplasmic reticulum stress [26, 27]. In addition, glycemic variability may reflect dysregulation of neurohormonal and autonomic responses under acute stress conditions [28]. However, these mechanistic insights are derived from prior experimental and observational studies and were not directly evaluated in the present analysis; therefore, they should be interpreted as biologically plausible but not causal evidence.

Beyond direct cardiovascular effects, glycemic variability may reflect broader systemic instability involving multi-organ interactions, as conceptualized within the cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome framework [7]. In our mediation analysis, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) partially mediated the association between GV and mortality. Renal dysfunction is a well-established predictor of adverse outcomes in both cardiovascular and critically ill patients [29]. Consistent with recent evidence, stress-related dysglycemia has been associated with peri-procedural renal injury, including contrast-induced acute kidney injury following PCI [30], as well as acute kidney injury in other critical illness settings [31]. The observed mediation effect should be interpreted cautiously, as BUN may represent a marker of systemic illness severity rather than a direct causal pathway linking GV to mortality.

From a clinical perspective, glycemic variability during ICU stay provides incremental prognostic information beyond traditional risk factors and mean glucose levels [3]. Unlike static metrics such as HbA1c or mean glucose, which reflect chronic glycemic exposure, GV captures the dynamic instability of glucose homeostasis under acute stress [8, 9]. This dynamic characterization may enhance risk stratification when integrated with other readily available clinical markers, as suggested by recent evidence supporting the combined assessment of glycemic indices in critically ill cardiovascular populations [3]. Recent studies have also highlighted the prognostic value of emerging risk markers and composite scores in patients undergoing PCI, including the admission EASIX score, the Osaka Prognostic Score, and combined electrocardiographic and clinical risk models, further underscoring the need for improved and integrative risk stratification strategies in this population [32–34]. While these multimodal approaches offer robust predictive performance, they often require complex calculations, multiple data domains, or specialized scoring systems. In contrast, glycemic variability represents a single, routinely available metabolic metric that can be calculated directly from standard laboratory results, offering a more accessible, low-cost, and immediately actionable alternative for bedside risk stratification.

However, several considerations should be taken into account when interpreting these findings. This study has notable strengths, including a relatively large sample size in the primary cohort, the use of two independent multicenter databases, and comprehensive analytical approaches. Nevertheless, several limitations merit consideration. First, the observational design precludes causal inference. Second, glycemic variability (GV) should not be interpreted as a standalone predictive marker but rather as a complementary component within multivariable prognostic frameworks. Third, several clinically relevant factors that may influence GV—such as nutritional support, insulin therapy, and the use of vasopressors or inotropic agents administered in glucose-containing solutions—were not available and may represent unmeasured confounders. Fourth, the temporal relationship between PCI and ICU admission could not be precisely determined; peri-procedural stress rather than the intervention itself may contribute to glycemic fluctuations, suggesting that GV may reflect overall physiological stress burden rather than PCI-specific effects. Fifth, potential misclassification bias may exist in attributing glycemic variability to procedure-related exposures, given the lack of precise temporal information linking PCI and ICU admission. Sixth, GV was derived from intermittently measured glucose values, and variability in sampling frequency may introduce measurement bias. Seventh, the external validation cohort was relatively small (~ 700 patients) and limited to in-hospital outcomes, which may affect the generalizability of long-term findings. Finally, as the study population was derived from ICU databases in the United States, extrapolation to non-ICU or non-Western populations warrants caution. Differences in clinical practice patterns, patient characteristics, and healthcare resources across healthcare systems may further limit the generalizability of these findings.

Taken together, increased glycemic variability during ICU stay was independently associated with higher risks of both short- and long-term mortality among patients undergoing coronary stent implantation, with the observed association primarily driven by higher GV levels, consistent with a threshold-like pattern. These findings support the potential value of incorporating GV into clinical risk stratification in ICU settings, although prospective studies are needed to determine whether targeting GV can improve clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Higher glycemic variability during ICU stay was significantly associated with increased risks of in-hospital and 365-day all-cause mortality among critically ill patients undergoing coronary intervention. These findings suggest that glycemic variability may provide additional value for risk stratification and prognostic assessment in this high-risk cardiovascular population.
