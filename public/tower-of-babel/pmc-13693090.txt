Genome-wide identification and expression profiling of GATA gene family in response to salt stress in sesame (Sesamum indicum L.)
Authors: Sk Shoaibur Rahaman, Jibon Chandro Roy, Khandker Shazia Afrin, Nasar Uddin Ahmed, Md Abdur Rahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
GATA transcription factors (TFs) are characterized by the existence of a type IV zinc-fingered structure. This TF family plays a significant role in growth and development, and in response to abiotic and biotic stresses. The GATA gene family has been well characterized in various plant species but remains unexplored to date in sesame (Sesamum indicum L.). This research aims to conduct a comprehensive genome-wide analysis, characterization, and expression profiling of the GATA gene family in sesame under salt stress, using systematic bioinformatics tools and ‘quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction’ (qRT-PCR). A total of 23 SiGATA genes were identified and categorized into five sub-groups via an extensive phylogenetic analysis. The CDS and intron numbers of SiGATA genes varied from 1 to 13 and 0 to 12, respectively. The length of SiGATA proteins varied from 119 aa to 822 aa. Gene duplication analysis predicted four duplication pairs in the SiGATA gene family. The expression of SiGATA genes was analyzed using both publicly available RNA-seq data (RPKM values) and qRT-PCR data obtained under salt stress in sesame. Five genes (SiGATA2, SiGATA3, SiGATA4, SiGATA6, and SiGATA12) were selected through RNA-seq-based expression to further validate expression pattern using qRT-PCR under salt stress, where all five selected SiGATA transcripts demonstrated strong upregulation at 12 h before their expression levels sharply decreased at 24 h. Among them, SiGATA6 exhibited the highest upregulation at 12 h. This research highlights the potential to develop climate-resilient sesame cultivars with enhanced productivity in saline areas.

Introduction

Sesame (Sesamum indicum L), often referred to as the ‘Queen of Oilseed Crops’, is an ancient oilseed crop of the Pedaliaceae family with 26 chromosomes.1, 2 This crop grows well in tropical and subtropical areas of Asia, Africa, and South America.3 Sesame seeds are enriched in a higher amount of oil content (40%–60%) than other major oilseed crops, such as soybean (20%), rapeseed (40%), sunflower (45%), and groundnut (45%–56%), as summarized by Teklu et al2 It maintains a balanced ratio of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids and contains bioactive compounds, including sesamol, sterols, and tocopherols, which promote human health.1 Despite having such economic and nutritional benefits, very little effort has been made to improve this crop, compared to other crops. Due to this, it is known as an orphan crop.3 Sesame can be cultivated in moderately harsh environments, such as salt stress, which has one of the most adverse effects on crop production as it induces water stress, nutrient imbalance, cell division reduction, and inhibits germination, as summarized by Carillo et al4 So, it is necessary to identify salt-tolerant genes and their expression patterns to design a future salt-tolerant sesame breeding program.

Transcription factors (TFs) play a significant role in key physiological processes, including stress regulation and signal transduction.5 They regulate the plant's growth and development to adapt to its environment.6 Furthermore, they are also playing a significant role in conferring disease resistance7 and in responding to stresses.8 There are more than 60 TF families found in plants.9 GATA TFs refer to a DNA-binding domain, binds to the consensus sequence WGATAR [W, thymidine (T) or an adenosine (A); R, guanidine (G) or adenosine (A)],10 classified by IV zinc-fingered region in the form of ‘C-X2-C-X17–20-C-X2-C' (C - cysteine; X - other residues), followed by a basal sequence,10 but only the C-terminal finger can bind DNA11 and has been discovered in animals, plants, and fungi.12 GATA DNA motifs are involved in nitrate-dependent and light-dependent regulation of transcription.10 It can identify precisely the promoter region of the target genes and bind to it to control transcriptional activity.13 This transcription factor influences flowering time, chlorophyll development,14 and abiotic stress resistance, such as drought and salt stress.15 After investigating the GATA gene family first in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) in 1993,16 it has been reported in several major crops, including Arabidopsis,10 rice,10 soybean,17 tomato, rapeseed,18 cucumber,11 foxtail millet,19 wheat,20 sorghum,21 maize,5 peanut,22 melon,23 eggplant13 and potato.12 The studies on the GATA genes of different species, with their gene number and year, are shown below (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Chronological timeline of genome-wide GATA gene family identifications in major crops.Fig. 1

Chronological timeline of genome-wide GATA gene family identifications in major crops.

In those studies, functional and expression analyses were comprehensively studied to assess the significance of GATA TFs in plants, which may prove crucial for engineering stress-resistant crop cultivars. For example, SbGATA04 of subfamily III was relatively highly expressed in the root under different abiotic stress (cold, drought, salt, etc.), which demonstrates the novelty of this sorghum GATA gene in an adverse environment.21 GATA33, GATA49, GATA71, GATA73, and GATA74 in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) were upregulated in responding against PEG, ABA, and salt stress.20 GATA genes are expressed variably in different tissues because of their specificity. As it is evident that GATA TFs are involved in crop growth and development, conducting a genome-wide analysis of this gene family in unexplored socio-economic crops is scientifically vital. Therefore, for the first time in this study, we conducted an in silico characterization and expression analysis of the GATA gene family in sesame under salt stress. Here, a total of 23 SiGATA genes were identified and classified. Physicochemical properties, evolutionary relationship, syntenic relationship, cis-elements, motif, and gene and protein structures were comprehensively studied by employing various bioinformatics tools. Moreover, the expression profiling of sesame GATA genes under salt stress was analyzed using available RNA-seq data, and by mining some potential SiGATA genes, a qRT-PCR-based expression study was performed to assess their expression patterns under salt stress conditions. This study aims to provide a theoretical framework for SiGATA genes and how they respond to salt stress, which will assist future sesame improvement programs, especially in developing salt-tolerant sesame varieties.

Materials and methods

Identification of putative SiGATA genes in Sesamum indicum L.

At first, we obtained the protein sequences of GATA genes from Arabidopsis thaliana from a previously published source.21 Then, the homologs of GATAs in Sesamum indicum L. were blasted through the ‘BLASTP’ tool on Ensembl Plants (https://plants.ensembl.org/Multi/Tools/Blast; accessed on 3 January 2025) (S_indicum_v1.0; NCBI accession: GCF_000512975.1; cultivar Zhongzhi No. 13), using default parameters. We rechecked the blast results in the NCBI database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi; accessed on 3 January 2025), as well. GATA conserved domains were confirmed using the CD search tool of NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi; accessed on 3 January 2025) and SMART database (https://smart.embl.de/; accessed on 3 January 2025) for the putative SiGATA genes, and genes without confirmed GATA conserved domains were removed, except a special case in SiGATA12, which was retained due to a presence of a structurally divergent and non-canonical GATA motif.

Analysis of the physicochemical properties

The physicochemical properties of all candidate SiGATA genes, including molecular weight, ‘isoelectric point’, and ‘grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY)’, were determined using the ‘ProtParam’ tool of the ‘ExPASy’ platform (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/; accessed on 6 January 2025). The length of each gene (bp), protein (aa), and chromosomal location was estimated from ‘Ensembl Plants’. By employing the available ‘Plant-mPLoc' tool (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/; accessed on 6 January 2025), the subcellular localization of each candidate SiGATA gene using the protein sequence was predicted.

Gene structure, conserved motifs, cis-acting elements

Gene structure of all SiGATA genes was visualized using ‘Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS 2.0)’ (https://gsds.gao-lab.org/; accessed on 6 January 2025).24 Conserved domains of GATAs were predicted with the help of the ‘CD search tool’ (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi; accessed on 3 January 2025), and motif analysis was conducted by using ‘MEME’ (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme; accessed on 7 January 2025). The ‘PlantCARE’ (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/)25 platform was used to analyze cis-acting regulatory elements in the 2 kb upstream of the SiGATA promoter region.

Chromosomal location, gene duplication, Ka/Ks ratio, GO enrichment

Chromosomal locations of putative SiGATA genes were collected from ‘Ensembl Plants’. Here, we retrieved the information on which chromosome and position each gene is located. Gene duplication events were predicted by the all-vs-all BLASTP program among possible gene pairs from the phylogenetic analysis using TB Tools-II.26 Synonymous substitution (Ks) and non-synonymous substitution (Ka) of SiGATAs with CDS sequence were calculated based on pair events using ‘TBtools-II’ (v2.483).27 We also performed ‘Gene Ontology (GO)’ enrichment analysis using an online tool ‘g: Profiler’ (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost; accessed on 1 June 2026). Resulting p-values were multiple-testing corrected using g:SCS algorithm, employing a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Phylogenetic analysis, classification, and multiple sequence alignment

By using ‘MEGA11’,28 a phylogenetic tree was constructed between different species with the amino acid sequence of GATA genes from Arabidopsis thaliana, Sesamum indicum, Sorghum bicolor, Solanum lycopersicum, and Glycine max (Table S2), where amino acid sequences were aligned using the ‘ClustalW’ and ‘Neighbor-Joining’ method with 1000 bootstrap values, followed by default parameters. This circular phylogenetic tree was further polished to enhance visualization by ‘iTOL’ (https://itol.embl.de/). To ensure readability, bootstrap confidence of phylogenetic tree and to avoid overcrowded topology, this 5 species subset was chosen which contains the representation of model dicot, monocot and major eudicots. Multiple sequence alignment was completed using the ‘phylogeny.fr’ website with its ‘MUSCLE’ tool (https://phylogeny.fr/tools/muscle).

Protein structure, protein-protein interaction, and syntenic relationship

The 3D protein structures of the SiGATA genes were predicted using the ‘Alphafold Protein Structure Database’ (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/; accessed on 10 January 2025).29 To obtain the interaction between SiGATA proteins, an online tool ‘STRING’ (https://string-db.org/; accessed on 15 January 2025) was used.30 This protein interaction model was constructed using orthologs of SiGATAs in A. thaliana. For predicting the syntenic relationship of SiGATAs between A. thaliana, S. indicum, S. bicolor, S. lycopersicum, and G. max, the online ‘BAT platform’ (https://bat.infspire.org/; accessed on 15 January 2025) was used, where sesame protein sequences of GATA were placed as a query sequence.

Transcriptomic analysis under salt stress

The ‘Reads Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (RPKM)’ gene expression dataset was downloaded for sesame under salinity conditions from the previous research study.31 While this original dataset included biological replicates, our analysis utilized the published normalized RPKM expression values to visualize gene family expression dynamics. The putative SiGATA genes were identified from that dataset by LOC ID for salt-sensitive and salt-tolerant plants, along with their RPKM values, to understand their expression patterns across different time intervals.

Plant materials, growth, and treatments

‘BARI Til-6’, a popular sesame variety, was collected from the ‘Bangladesh Agricultural Research Institute (BARI)’ and used as plant material. Seeds are planted in pots, containing soil, vermicompost, and cocopeat mixture. Growth conditions: temperature at 28–30°C, RH 70–80% and 11 h of daylight, with ambient outdoors natural sunlight. Proper management of plants was followed to keep the plants healthy by weeding, irrigating, and applying fertilizers when necessary. We used 3-week-old (21 days) seedlings, which were later exposed to salt treatments. For this treatment, NaCl with 150 mM was prepared and applied. Leaf samples were collected at four (4) different time intervals (0 h as control, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h) for RNA extraction.

RNA extraction and cDNA library preparation

To extract total RNA from sesame leaf samples, the ‘RNA simple Total RNA Kit’ (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) was used, followed by the exact manufacturer's instructions. The integrity of RNA was measured by gel electrophoresis (1% agarose gel), and RNA concentration and quality were checked by a ‘Micro Spectrophotometer’ (Nano-100 model, BOYN, China). Then, cDNA was synthesized by using the ‘PrimeScript first strand cDNA Synthesis Kit’ (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan), adopting its developed protocol and preserved for qRT-PCR.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

We chose five SiGATA genes (SiGATA2, SiGATA3, SiGATA4, SiGATA6, SiGATA12) as representatives for the detailed expression analysis under salt stress using qRT-PCR. The selection of five sampled SiGATA genes was based on their expression patterns in the RPKM gene expression dataset under salt stress,31 more specifically on transcript abundance and differential expression patterns (induction or repression) across 4 distinct time points of treatment. Though SiGATA18 showed continuous strong upregulation in RNA-seq dataset, its fluctuation under stress was minimal (about 1.11-fold change from 0 h to 12 h at salt-tolerant genotype) and that's why it was not prioritized. The distribution of those five genes was on different chromosomes (LG7, LG4, LG3, LG6, and LG2, respectively). The gene-specific primers for the five SiGATA genes used for qRT-PCR were designed by the ‘Primer3Plus’ (https://www.primer3plus.com/index.html), and ‘18S rRNA’ was used as an internal reference gene for normalization.32 All the primers prepared for this analysis, including the reference gene ‘18S rRNA’, could be found here (Table S3). The qRT-PCR analysis was carried out using the ‘Bio-Rad CFX96’ system. The reactions were prepared using the ‘TB Green Premix Ex Taq II’ (Tli RNaseH Plus) (2×) kit. Each 25 μl reaction was a mixture of 12.5 μl of ‘TB Green Premix Ex Taq II’ (2×), 1 μl of each primer, 2 μl of cDNA, and 8.5 μl of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling process includes an initial denaturation phase at 95°C for 30s, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (denaturation at 95°Cfor 5 s and primer annealing at 55°C for 30s) and an extension phase at 72°C for 45 s.

For qRT-PCR analysis, 2 biological and technical replicates were used for each sample. The relative expression of selected SiGATA genes was quantified using the comparative CT (2−ΔΔCT) method.33 Statistical analysis was performed using a One-Way ANOVA, followed by Duncan's Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Identification of the SiGATA family and physicochemical analysis

A total of 23 SiGATA genes were identified through blast with reported Arabidopsis thaliana GATA proteins against the sesame genome in ‘Ensembl’ (https://www.ensembl.org) database and named as SiGATA1-SiGATA23 (Fig. 2) (Table 1) according to their gene ID chronology. According to the conserved domain analysis (Fig. 2), all the SiGATA proteins harbored the characteristics of the GATA domain, whereas SiGATA12 lacked this domain, which revealed the structural divergence from the other SiGATA members. Besides the GATA domain, some proteins were accompanied by several specific domains, such as ‘CCT motif’ on SiGATA8, SiGATA12, SiGATA22, and SiGATA23; ‘B-box’ on SiGATA12; and ‘TIFY’ domain on SiGATA22 and SiGATA23 (Fig. 2). The subcellular localization, length of coding sequence (CDS) (bp), gene (bp), protein (aa), molecular weight (kDa), isoelectric point (PI), and gravy were analyzed (Table 1). Notably, in silico analysis using Plant-mPLoc predicted all the SiGATA proteins in the nucleus. SiGATA17 has the highest length of CDS, and the lowest CDS length estimated in SiGATA6. Gene length varied between 468 bp (SiGATA6) and 16,758 bp (SiGATA17), and protein length varied between 119 aa (SiGATA6) and 822 aa (SiGATA17). The heaviest and lightest proteins are SiGATA17 and SiGATA6, respectively, according to their molecular weight. The pattern between molecular weight and CDS length of SiGATAs suggests a near-proportional relationship. Between molecular weight and protein sequences, correlations were deviated due to specific amino acid composition, which hinders obtaining a perfect proportional relation. The isoelectric points of all SiGATA proteins were distributed in the range of 5.37 (SiGATA3, SiGATA22) - 10.19 (SiGATA6). The closer view of this distribution indicates that 12 SiGATA proteins (52.2%) tend to be acidic (PI<7) and 11 SiGATA proteins (47.8%) are enriched with basic amino acids (PI>7), and no neutral proteins (PI = 7) were observed. Phylogenetic analysis, classification, and multiple sequence alignment

To understand the evolutionary relationship, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the GATA protein sequences of A. thaliana, S. indicum, Sorghum bicolor, Solanum lycopersicum, and Glycine max from the previous study (Fig. 3) (Tables S1 & S2). All SiGATA proteins were distributed among 5 subfamilies. The highest number of SiGATA proteins belonged to Subfamily I (7/23), each of the Subfamily II and V contained an equal number of SiGATA proteins (6/23), followed by Subfamily III (3/23). Subfamily IV had only one member of the SiGATA protein (SiGATA15) (Fig. 3) (Table 1).Fig. 3A circular phylogenetic tree between Arabidopsis thaliana, Sesamum indicum, Sorghum bicolor, Solanum lycopersicum, and Glycine max.Fig. 3

A circular phylogenetic tree between Arabidopsis thaliana, Sesamum indicum, Sorghum bicolor, Solanum lycopersicum, and Glycine max.

To figure out the structural domains in all 23 SiGATA proteins, we further aligned the protein sequences and analyzed them by taking 40-amino acid sequences that contain GATA domains (Fig. 4). The result showed that the majority of SiGATA proteins contained the highly conserved ‘C-X2-C-X18-C-X2-C' zinc finger motif while SiGATA12 and SiGATA15 exhibited variations, particularly at the C-terminal cysteine residues, indicating structural divergence within the GATA domain. Multiple protein alignment indicated that SiGATA1 and SiGATA21 proteins were 100% identical strictly within 40 aa, though full-length proteins shared 68.22% sequence identity with BLASTP alignment length 236 aa (E-value threshold is 1e-50). It indicates the possibility of a duplication event between them. Structures and conserved motifs analysis

Gene structures of 23 SiGATA genes were predicted (Fig. 5). There were variations in the number of CDSs and introns in each SiGATA gene. The CDS number ranged between 1 and 13. SiGATA8 and SiGATA20 were constructed with only 1 CDS, but they had no introns, indicating the possibility of the whole gene being translated into protein. SiGATA17 was the most abundant one, with a CDS number of 13, which is the longest gene among the 23 SiGATAs. Most of the genes (15/23) contained only 2 CDSs.Fig. 5Distribution of CDSs and introns in 23 SiGATA genes.Fig. 5

Distribution of CDSs and introns in 23 SiGATA genes.

To understand the structural diversity and functional conservation of GATA genes in sesame, the motif pattern of 23 SiGATA proteins was predicted and analyzed (Fig. 6). The motif discovery revealed five distinct conserved motifs, designated as Motif 1 through Motif 5, confirmed by significant P-values, ranging from 8.41e-20 to 2.78e-104. The representation of Motif 1 was observed in all SiGATA proteins (each with one GATA domain) except SiGATA12. It indicates Motif 1 is the core conserved GATA domain that binds the DNA. Motifs belonging to a similar subfamily also experienced similar kinds of motif patterns: all the proteins of the subfamily I contained 4 (four) motifs except SiGATA20 (only 1 motif); all the proteins of subfamily II contained only 1 (one) motif except SiGATA12; similar things were applicable to subfamily III (a similar kind of two motifs on each); except SiGATA2 and SiGATA3 (each has four motifs), rest of proteins under subfamily V accompanied by 3 (three) motifs on each. Proteins under subfamily I were the most enriched in the number of conserved domains. Differences in pattern and number of motifs among subfamilies reflect the structural diversity of GATA transcription factors in sesame.Fig. 6Conserved motifs present in 23 SiGATA proteins (5 motifs are distinguished by rectangular colored boxes, numbered by 1–5).Fig. 6

To investigate the possible gene duplication events in 23 SiGATA genes, five pairs of genes were identified from the phylogenetic tree as they fall into the same clade (Fig. 3): SiGATA1 and SiGATA21; SiGATA17 and SiGATA18; SiGATA2 and SiGATA3; SiGATA11 and SiGATA13; SiGATA12 and SiGATA6. To predict the gene duplication among them, we ran the ‘all-vs-all’ (SiGATA1, SiGATA21, SiGATA17, SiGATA18, SiGATA2, SiGATA3, SiGATA11, SiGATA13, SiGATA12, SiGATA6) BLASTP program, setting E-value threshold to 1e-50 using TB Tools-II. We found that SiGATA2/SiGATA3, SiGATA11/SiGATA13, SiGATA17/SiGATA18, and SiGATA1/SiGATA21 might undergo duplication events (Fig. 8).

Ka/Ks and GO enrichment analysis

We estimated the Ka/Ks ratio of a duplicated pair of SiGATA genes to understand the selection pressure, which gives information on evolutionary significance (Table 2). Protein structure and interaction analysis

To predict the three-dimensional (3D) structures of 23 SiGATA proteins, the ‘Alphafold Protein Structure Database’ was used with ‘UniProt’ accession (Fig. 11). The majority of SiGATA proteins are highly disorganized by the random coils, interwined with α-helical regions (low confidence regions, e.g. mean pLDDT ~53–55). β-sheets are noticeably very scarce across the SiGATA gene family. Despite these structures, most of the SiGATA proteins (except SiGATA5, SiGATA6, and SiGATA8) exhibit a highly conserved compact cluster, consisting of α-helical regions, which represents the zinc-finger binding domain. In SiGATA5 and SiGATA6, structural variations are seen in both proteins that are predominantly composed of elongated α-helical structures, lacking random coil loops. In contrast, SiGATA8 displays a uniquely dominant β-sheet structure. Discussion

GATA is one of the crucial gene families in plants, contributing to growth, development, morphogenesis, and abiotic stress tolerance, including salt and drought.20 This gene family has been explored in several crops to identify, characterize, and gain insights into possible gene function and expression patterns. However, despite being an enriched oilseed crop, no comprehensive studies have been reported on this gene family in sesame to date. In this study, employing several bioinformatics tools, 23 SiGATA genes were identified and classified (Table 1). Based on the phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 3), constructed from the GATA sequences in Arabidopsis thaliana, Sesamum indicum, Sorghum bicolor, Solanum lycopersicum, and Glycine max, 23 SiGATA genes were clustered in five distinct subfamilies (subfamily I – V). To facilitate cross-study comparisons, our five-subgroup-based phylogeny was mapped to foundational four-subfamily-based classification by Reyes et al10 Our Subgroup-IV corresponds to Subfamily-IV. Notably, Subfamily-I genes were clustered into our 2 distinct subgroups (Subgroups I & V). Similarly, Subfamily-II largely corresponds to our Subgroup-II, though only 1 gene (At3g20750) clustered into our Subgroup-III alongside the entirety of the foundational Subfamily III. To mention, our phylogeny contains 30 GATA TFs from A. thaliana [29 from the investigation by Reyes et al.10], including 1 additional gene (At4g16141).36 Several previous reports have attempted to identify GATA ‘transcription factors’ (TFs) in different plants alongside Arabidopsis and rice, for example, this gene family in cucumber comprises 26 GATA TFs clustered into four subfamilies11; 28 GATA TFs were identified and classified into four subfamilies in eggplant13; 45 GATA TFs into three subfamilies in peanut22; and 49 GATA TFs into five subfamilies in potato.37 These studies signify that the members and subfamilies of the GATA TFs exhibited variation across different crops, which validates our findings in sesame as well. A relationship of nearest positive proportionality was found between molecular weight and ‘CDS’ length, which was reported in the sorghum study.21 From other physicochemical analyses, including the length of ‘CDS’, ‘amino acid’, gene length, molecular weight, and ‘isoelectric point’, variation across the SiGATA genes was noticed (Table 1), which suggests the members of the SiGATA gene family are involved in diversified functions. SiGATA genes predominantly function in the nucleus (Table 1), which was predicted similarly in previous studies, but some also have their presence in chloroplasts, plastids, cytoplasm, and mitochondria.19, 21, 37 According to the ‘theoretical isoelectric point’, SiGATA proteins tend to be slightly more acidic than basic, but the sorghum study reported the enrichment of basic ‘amino acids’ in GATAs,21 suggesting this change could have taken place due to a mutation in sesame GATAs over the evolution period.

Conserved domain analysis revealed the presence of a single ‘GATA domain’ or ‘zinc finger’ structure in each SiGATA, except SiGATA12 (Fig. 2), which was predominant in other studies too, with some exceptions.10, 19 Along with the ‘zinc finger domain’, some important motifs, including ‘CCT’ and ‘TIFY’ domains, were also conspicuous and reported in melon GATA TFs.23 The ‘CCT’ motif regulates flowering with light-signaling factors,38 and the ‘TIFY’ domain plays a crucial role in defense response, mediated by jasmonic acid (JA).39 Another important domain, ‘B-box’, was visible in SiGATA12, which contributes to the light-dependent physiological process, such as flowering, pigment accumulation,40 and also regulates phytohormone-mediated growth and development processes.41 Overall, these extra motifs might work in regulating similar kinds of functions in sesame. In the multiple sequence alignment of 40 aa, a single zinc finger structure (‘CX2CX18CX2C') was seen, except for SiGATA12 and SiGATA15 (Fig. 4). This might be the result of mutations over the evolutionary period, where cysteine was lost; nonetheless, further validation is required. Gene structure analysis revealed the variation in CDS and intron number across the SiGATA gene family (Fig. 5), which is consistent with the previous studies. This variation in gene structure suggests the possibility of a wider role in sesame growth and development. Motif analysis indicates the presence of a highly conserved zinc finger structure, Motif 1, throughout the SiGATA gene family, except SiGATA12 (Fig. 6). Conserved domain analysis, motif analysis and multiple protein alignment confirmed that SiGATA12 lacks the Motif 1, where a non-canonical, degenerated domain lacking the conserved CX2C (lacks the second cysteine residue) zinc-finger motif is present, as evident in multiple protein alignment (Fig. 4). Such structural divergence is a well-documented feature in GATA gene families, which might have a new function; GATA study in Sorghum bicolor and Setaria italica reported SbGATA10 and SiGATA15 with such absence of canonical CX2C structure, respectively.19, 21 SiGATA genes were distributed unevenly across the sesame genome, with their presence in different linkage groups (LG1 through LG15, but no presence on LG5 and LG14) (Fig. 7). This type of variation is common in other plants too,5, 17 which actually suggests the functional enrichment of SiGATA genes, as some chromosomes contain multiple GATA genes belonging to different subfamilies. SiGATA genes experienced possible four pairs of gene duplications (SiGATA17/SiGATA18, SiGATA1/SiGATA21, SiGATA2/SiGATA3, and SiGATA11/SiGATA13) (Fig. 8). Like our investigation, duplication events of GATA genes were also reported in several studies. For example, GATA genes in foxtail millet experienced three tandem and five segmental duplications, 15 pairs of duplication events in eggplant, and cucumber showed one tandem and six segmental duplications,11, 13, 19 which leads to the extension of the gene family42 and contributes to the inclusion of novel traits in plants, including abiotic stress tolerance and biotic stress resistance.43 Synteny analysis from the protein sequences of A. thaliana, S. indicum, S. bicolor, S. lycopersicum, and G. max, which was based on sequence homology, also displayed how SiGATA proteins evolve and maintain a relationship with other SiGATAs, found in different plants (Fig. 9). Furthermore, our estimation of Ka/Ks values (<1) indicated that the SiGATA genes have experienced purifying selection pressure during the entire evolution period to maintain gene functions stable (Table 2). This result aligns with the previous findings in Setaria italica and Cucumis melo.19, 23 We also analyzed the 2 kb promoter region of SiGATA genes to identify cis-acting regulatory elements (Table 3). Our analysis revealed the presence of important elements, which are associated with hormonal, light, and stress/defense response, predicting how functionally enriched SiGATA genes are. Such identification of stress-responsive cis-acting regulatory elements through bioinformatics approach only provides predictive insights into transcriptional regulation of SiGATA genes and can never be an evident of independent gene function. The protein structure (Fig. 11) and protein association network (Fig. 12) were predicted. SiGATA22 and SiGATA19 interacted with maximum proteins, suggesting their crucial regulatory role in the SiGATA gene family.

Understanding the expression of genes in different tissues and environmental stresses is crucial in plant biotechnology. By recognizing this, we have shed light on a few genes of the SiGATA family to determine their expression patterns under salt stress. The transcriptional activity of SiGATA genes in salt stress was assessed using available RNA-seq data (Fig. 13), where the expression pattern varied considerably in salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive genotypes. It provides the basis for GATA genes to contribute to the salt tolerance trait in sesame. Five SiGATA transcripts (SiGATA2, SiGATA3, SiGATA4, SiGATA6, and SiGATA12) were selected based on their up-regulation and down-regulation patterns, and their relative expression was analyzed using qRT-PCR (Fig. 14). Although qRT-PCR analysis validated the salt-stress responsiveness of GATA genes in sesame, some variation in expression patterns occurred between RNA-seq and qRT-PCR data. This is due to the biological and technical confounding factors, which are common in gene expression studies. Technically, RNA-seq algorithm maps read across the whole gene model and could mask the sudden expression pattern, whereas qRT-PCR is highly transcript-specific and better at recognizing sudden spikes of expression. Moreover, differences are also expected between RNA-seq-based screening (shoot tissue; ST/SS)31 and our qRT-PCR-based validation (leaf tissue; BARI Til-6) as they utilized different genotypes and plant materials. Our qRT-PCR results revealed that all five SiGATA genes were expressed at 12 h of salt stress, after initial downregulation at 6 h, and experienced sharp declines at 24 h than their initial expression levels (0 h). Among them, SiGATA4 and SiGATA6 exhibited the highest transcriptional activity at 12 h, suggesting their critical role in thriving under a salt stress environment. As GATA gene expression is highly tissue-specific, and expression is low in leaves compared to root and stem when induced to abiotic stresses (salt, drought, hormone, etc.), it aligns with the previous studies as well.19, 21 So, alongside leaves, investigating the transcriptional activity of SiGATAs in the root and stem would be crucial, where they might function more significantly. Among 8 promising SbGATA genes in sorghum- SbGATA04, SbGATA27, SbGATA28, and SbGATA33 experienced leaf expression at 12 h under salt stress, increased from 3 h, and were repressed at 24 h.21 That means there is a strong possibility that the GATA genes would show higher transcriptional activity in the leaf at 12 h and drop at 24 h. Such fluctuations in SiGATA expression in response to salt stress involve different stress hormones and signaling pathways (Fig. 15). The transcript repression at 6 h could be seen as an effect of primary osmotic and ionic shock,44 which induces acute inhibition of plant growth.45 Due to salt stress, plants start to accumulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and abscisic acid (ABA).44, 46 This increase in ABA level promotes the expression of stress-related genes through ABA-responsive elements (ABRE), which activates a pathway known as AREB/ABF-SnRK2. This signaling pathway acts as a positive regulator in responding to osmotic stress.46 Furthermore, to cope up with extended salt stress, plants adopt several mechanisms, including detoxification of ROS, building up protective proteins like Late Embryogenesis Abundant (LEA), activation of calcium-dependent SOS pathway (salt-overly-sensitive) and expression of NEK6 gene (suppresses ethylene production).47, 48, 49 All these together leads to surge in transcript expression at 12 h. However, plants can't afford running such hyperactivated defense mechanisms indefinitely with the cost of growth-arrest for survival.47 That's why a balance between growth and defense in plants is required, which is achieved by reaching acclimation stage, ensuring steady-state growth. This phase involves reprogramming of energy metabolism and resetting gene expression toward a lower maintenance baseline for obtaining osmotic and ionic homeostasis,44, 47 which could be the reasons of expression attenuation in selected SiGATAs at 24 h. However, variation in expression patterns can occur due to plant-specific factors, such as the growth stage, genetic adaptation mechanism, etc. Though SiGATA4 exhibited strong performance at 12 h in qRT-PCR analysis, it demonstrated continuous repression over the prolonged stress-period in salt-tolerant genotype in RNA-seq dataset. On contrast, SiGATA6 was one of the promising transcripts in RNA-seq data analysis, which proved its potential in qRT-PCR as well, suggesting that this gene could be one of the crucial candidates in enhancing salt-tolerant traits in sesame.Fig. 15Proposed model of GATA-mediated salt response in sesame.Fig. 15

Proposed model of GATA-mediated salt response in sesame.

Though this study strongly focuses on bioinformatics and expression profiling of GATA genes in sesame under salt stress, it lacks functional validation. Therefore, future research attempts should utilize transgenic approaches, for example, overexpression or CRISPR/Cas9 knockout methods to confirm biological functions of candidate SiGATA genes in response to salt stress.

Conclusion

In this study, 23 SiGATA genes were identified and classified into five subfamilies (I – V). The phylogeny, physicochemical properties, gene structure, conserved domain structure, motifs, synteny, chromosomal distribution, gene duplication, protein structure, and protein association network were systematically analyzed and predicted by employing several bioinformatics tools. Expression profiling was done using the available RNA-seq dataset as well as qRT-PCR under salt stress and the proposed SiGATA6 (SIN_1012752) gene, as a promising candidate in striving salt stress conditions for future sesame improvement programs. Overall, this study provides significant insights into the sesame GATA gene family, its characteristics, and expression pattern under salt stress and provides the basis for functional validation of GATA genes in sesame.
