Radiographic subsidence after single-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion; a comparison between blade-anchored zero-profile cages and stand-alone cages
Authors: Shinya Arataki, Akiyoshi Miyamoto, Tadashi Komatsubara, Masato Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCage subsidence is a common radiographic finding after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), particularly with stand-alone cages, and may compromise postoperative alignment and stability. Blade-anchored zero-profile cages have been developed to provide immediate fixation while avoiding plate-related complications; however, their influence on subsidence characteristics has not been fully elucidated. This study aimed to compare the magnitude and direction of radiographic subsidence between blade-anchored zero-profile cages and stand-alone cages after single-level ACDF.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included 76 patients who underwent single-level ACDF between 2017 and 2024 using either a blade-anchored zero-profile cage or a stand-alone cage. The anterior disc height (ADH) and posterior disc height (PDH) were measured preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, and at 6 months after surgery. The mean disc height was defined as the average of the ADH and PDH. Radiographic subsidence was calculated as the difference between the immediate postoperative and 6-month mean disc heights. Anterior and posterior subsidence were analyzed separately. Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed in two steps: Model 1 adjusted for demographic and baseline radiographic variables, and Model 2 further adjusted for disc height recovery to account for the effect of intraoperative disc height restoration.ResultsMean subsidence was significantly greater in the blade-anchored zero-profile cage group than in the stand-alone cage group, and the incidence of subsidence ≥ 3 mm was higher with blade-anchored cages. Directional analysis demonstrated significantly greater anterior subsidence in the blade-anchored group, whereas posterior subsidence did not differ between the groups. In the multivariate analysis, cage type was independently associated with subsidence in Model 1; however, this association was attenuated after adjustment for disc height recovery in Model 2.ConclusionsBlade-anchored zero-profile cages were associated with greater radiographic subsidence after single-level ACDF compared with stand-alone cages, primarily due to increased anterior subsidence. The attenuation of the cage-type effect after adjustment for disc height recovery suggests a potential role of restoration-related factors. Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a well-established surgical procedure for the treatment of cervical degenerative diseases. Despite generally favorable clinical outcomes, radiographic subsidence of interbody cages remains a common postoperative finding, with a wide range of reported incidence rates across studies. A systematic review by Noordhoek et al. demonstrated that cage subsidence is commonly observed after ACDF, although its clinical significance remains controversial [1].

Stand-alone cages were introduced to avoid complications related to anterior cervical plating, such as dysphagia and anterior soft tissue irritation. However, cage subsidence has been recognized as a characteristic radiographic issue associated with stand-alone constructs [1–3].

Zero-profile anchored spacers have been developed to address concerns regarding immediate fixation stability while avoiding plate-related complications. Clinical studies have demonstrated acceptable radiographic and clinical outcomes with these devices, supporting their widespread adoption in contemporary ACDF practice [4–7].

Nevertheless, cage subsidence continues to be reported, even with zero-profile devices. Subsidence has been associated with several biomechanical and surgical factors, including implant design, endplate preparation, and the magnitude of intraoperative disc height restoration.

Most previous studies have primarily evaluated subsidence based on its overall magnitude, with relatively limited attention to its directional characteristics or underlying biomechanical mechanisms [8–12].

Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to compare radiographic subsidence between blade-anchored zero-profile cages and stand-alone cages after single-level ACDF, with specific attention to both the magnitude and direction of subsidence. We hypothesized that blade-anchored zero-profile cages would demonstrate distinct subsidence characteristics compared with stand-alone cages.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Okayama Rosai Hospital (IRB No. 595) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

Consecutive patients who underwent single-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) for degenerative cervical disease were reviewed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: single-level ACDF performed using either a blade-anchored zero-profile cage or a stand-alone cage, and availability of complete radiographic data obtained preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, and at 6 months after surgery. Patients who underwent multilevel fusion, combined anterior–posterior procedures, or revision surgery were excluded.

A total of 76 patients who met the inclusion criteria were included in the final analysis. The patients were divided into two groups according to cage type: the blade-anchored zero-profile cage group and the stand-alone cage group. The choice of cage type was not based on patient-specific clinical factors but was primarily determined by the chronological adoption of implants at our institution. Stand-alone cages were predominantly used in the earlier study period, whereas blade-anchored cages were introduced later and subsequently became the standard device. Therefore, treatment allocation was largely time-dependent rather than randomized.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed using a standard anterior cervical approach. After discectomy and neural decompression, endplate preparation was carefully performed to preserve the subchondral bone as much as possible. The cage size was selected based on an intraoperative assessment to achieve appropriate disc height restoration and segmental stability.

In the blade-anchored zero-profile cage group, anchoring blades were inserted into adjacent vertebral bodies according to the manufacturer’s recommendations to achieve immediate fixation. In the stand-alone cage group, the cage was inserted without additional anterior plating or anchoring devices.

Radiographic evaluation

Lateral cervical radiographs were obtained preoperatively, immediately after surgery, and at 6 months postoperatively. Anterior disc height (ADH) and posterior disc height (PDH) were measured at each time point, as illustrated in Fig. 1. ADH and PDH were defined as the perpendicular distances between the superior and inferior endplates at the anterior and posterior margins of the operated disc space, respectively. The mean disc height was defined as the average of ADH and PDH.

To improve measurement reliability, immediate postoperative disc height measurements were repeated thrice for all patients. During each measurement, the previous values were masked. The average of the second and third measurements was used as the final value for analysis. Baseline disc height was defined as the preoperative mean disc height.

Definition of disc height recovery

Disc height recovery was defined as the difference between the immediate postoperative and preoperative mean disc heights. This variable was used as an indicator of the degree of disc height restoration achieved during surgery and as a surrogate for potential over-distraction.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the mean radiographic subsidence at 6 months after surgery. Secondary outcomes included anterior subsidence, posterior subsidence, and the incidence of subsidence ≥ 3 mm. Subsidence ≥ 3 mm was treated as a categorical outcome for the descriptive and supplementary analyses.

Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with total subsidence (mm). Two models were constructed a priori.

Model 1 (base-adjusted) included cage type (blade-anchored vs. stand-alone), age (per year), sex (male vs. female), lower cervical level (yes vs. no), and baseline disc height (per mm).

Model 2 (mechanism-adjusted) included all variables in Model 1 and additionally incorporated disc height recovery (per mm) to account for restoration-related effects.

Regression coefficients (β) and standard errors are reported. All statistical analyses were performed using R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1.

A total of 76 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 56 in the blade-anchored zero-profile cage group and 20 in the stand-alone cage group. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, sex distribution, operated level, or baseline disc height.

Comparison of radiographic subsidence between groups

Radiographic subsidence outcomes at 6 months after surgery are presented in Table 2; Fig. 2. The mean subsidence was significantly greater in the blade-anchored zero-profile cage group than in the stand-alone cage group (median 2.45 mm vs. 2.15 mm, p = 0.049). In addition, the incidence of subsidence ≥ 3 mm was higher in the blade-anchored group than in the stand-alone group (32.1% vs. 5.0%, p = 0.018).

Directional analysis demonstrated a significant difference in anterior subsidence between the groups. Supplementary observations

Although not included as a primary outcome, reoperation due to progressive subsidence and symptomatic vertebral collapse occurred in two patients in the blade-anchored zero-profile cage group, both of whom required additional posterior fixation. No reoperations related to subsidence were observed in the stand-alone cage group.

Discussion

The present study compared radiographic subsidence between blade-anchored zero-profile cages and stand-alone cages after single-level ACDF, with a particular focus on both the magnitude and direction of subsidence. The principal findings were as follows: (1) blade-anchored zero-profile cages were associated with greater total subsidence and a higher incidence of subsidence ≥ 3 mm compared with stand-alone cages; (2) anterior subsidence was significantly greater in the blade-anchored group, whereas posterior subsidence did not differ between groups; and (3) the association between cage type and subsidence was attenuated after adjustment for disc height recovery, suggesting that restoration-related factors may partially account for this association.

Comparison with previous studies

Subsidence after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) has been widely reported in the literature, with variable incidence rates and uncertain clinical relevance [1, 13]. Although zero-profile anchored spacers were developed to improve fixation stability while avoiding plate-related complications, subsidence has continued to be observed even with these newer devices [8–10]. These findings indicate that subsidence is not fully eliminated by implant design alone.

Most previous studies evaluated subsidence primarily as an overall reduction in disc height without distinguishing between anterior and posterior components [1, 13]. In contrast, the present study demonstrated that the difference in overall subsidence between cage types was mainly driven by increased anterior disc height loss in the blade-anchored group. These findings suggest that the directional assessment of subsidence may provide additional insights beyond total disc height measurements.

Potential mechanisms of anterior-predominant subsidence

The anterior predominance of subsidence observed with blade-anchored zero-profile cages may be explained by a combination of distraction-related and endplate-related biomechanical factors. In the present multivariable analysis, disc height recovery was independently associated with subsidence, and the inclusion of this variable attenuated the association between cage type and subsidence. This finding suggests that the degree of intraoperative disc height restoration may partially mediate the relationship between cage design and postoperative subsidence.

Previous biomechanical and clinical studies have suggested that excessive restoration of disc height during ACDF increases stress at the cage–endplate interface and predisposes the segment to subsidence [11, 14, 15]. This supports the interpretation that disc height recovery may act as a biomechanical contributor to postoperative disc height loss and may partially mediate the relationship between cage design and subsidence. Excessive disc height restoration may increase compressive loading at the cage–endplate interface and alter the segmental load distribution. These biomechanical effects may accelerate cage settling, particularly in the anterior portion of the vertebral endplate.

In addition, the anchoring blades of zero-profile cages inevitably interact with the anterior endplate–vertebral junction, potentially altering the anterior load distribution and stress concentration.

Endplate-related mechanical factors are also likely to influence postoperative subsidence. Previous studies have suggested that implant–bone interaction and endplate loading conditions may contribute to cage settling after ACDF [14, 15]. These observations highlight the importance of meticulous endplate preservation and appropriate cage selection during implantation. Taken together, these findings suggest that a combination of disc height over-restoration and altered anterior endplate stress may have contributed to the anterior-predominant subsidence observed in the blade-anchored group.

Posterior subsidence and load-sharing considerations

In contrast to anterior subsidence, posterior subsidence did not differ significantly between cage types in the present study. This finding may be related to the biomechanical contribution of posterior elements, including facet joints and posterior ligaments, which participate in load sharing and segmental stability. Because posterior structural support is less directly affected by cage anchoring mechanisms and endplate preparation, posterior disc height may be relatively preserved regardless of cage design, resulting in asymmetric subsidence patterns.

Clinical implications

From a clinical perspective, the present findings suggest that blade-anchored zero-profile cages, while advantageous in avoiding plate-related complications, may be associated with a higher risk of anterior subsidence when excessive disc height restoration is achieved. Awareness of this tendency may encourage more cautious distraction strategies and careful endplate preservation during ACDF. Furthermore, directional evaluation of subsidence may offer a more nuanced understanding of implant-specific behavior and postoperative radiographic changes than assessment of total disc height loss alone. These findings highlight the importance of considering not only the magnitude but also the directional pattern of subsidence when evaluating implant performance after ACDF.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, sagittal alignment parameters, such as segmental Cobb angle and global cervical alignment, were not evaluated, although these factors have been reported to interact with subsidence patterns in previous studies [12]. Third, clinical outcome measures were not included in the present analysis. In addition, several potentially relevant factors could not be accounted for. Bone mineral density was not routinely assessed. Given its known association with cage subsidence, this represents an important limitation.

Implant selection was time-dependent rather than randomized, which may introduce selection bias related to temporal changes in surgical technique or patient characteristics.

However, the primary objective of this study was to investigate radiographic subsidence patterns and their potential biomechanical mechanisms rather than clinical outcomes. Previous reports have suggested that radiographic subsidence does not necessarily correlate with adverse clinical outcomes after ACDF [1]; therefore, future studies incorporating both radiographic and clinical assessments are warranted.

Conclusions

Blade-anchored zero-profile cages were associated with greater radiographic subsidence after single-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion than stand-alone cages, primarily due to increased anterior subsidence.

The attenuation of the cage-type effect after adjustment for disc height recovery suggests a potential role of restoration-related factors.

Given the absence of clinical outcome data, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further studies incorporating both radiographic and clinical assessments are warranted.
