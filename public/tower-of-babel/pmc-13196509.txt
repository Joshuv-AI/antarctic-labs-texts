Cognitive Function Has an Inverse Association With Cerebral Blood Flow in Individuals With CKD
Authors: Danya Pradeep Kumar, Nicholas Herrera, Mary Piedad, Nicole Oblon, Jacob Hiss, Rebecca J Lepping, Aditi Gupta
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD) have a high prevalence of cognitive impairment, ranging from mild cognitive impairment to dementia.1 Cognitive impairment in aging and other causes of dementia such as Alzheimer disease is generally associated with low cerebral blood flow (CBF).2 Interestingly, CBF is high in individuals with CKD.2 This raises an important question about whether CBF has the same association with cognition in patients with CKD as it does in aging and Alzheimer disease.

In this single-center, cross-sectional pilot study, we enrolled English-speaking adult participants with an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Individuals who were pregnant/breastfeeding or those with contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging, history of traumatic brain injury, known brain lesions, or conditions such as severe psychiatric illness limiting participation in study assessments were excluded. The study was approved by the institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent. Demographic information and medical history included age, race, sex, education, and comorbid conditions, including atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, seizures, stroke, and history of smoking.

Cognition was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Toolbox Cognition Battery (NIHTB-CB), an iPad-based validated tool that assesses different domains of cognition and has been previously used in individuals with CKD.3 The NIHTB-CB change-sensitive scores have a centered value of 500, which corresponds to the average score of a 10-year-old child. Fluid composite score assesses problem-solving and reasoning (without relying on past experience), crystallized composite score assesses experience and knowledge-based abilities, and total composite score assesses overall cognitive function. Pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling with a 3T whole-body Siemens Skyra magnetic resonance imaging scanner with a 32-channel head receiver coil was used to assess global and regional (across 9 prespecified brain regions) CBF as previously described.4 CBF was also adjusted for hematocrit as described previously,5 because hematocrit can alter blood viscosity and thus CBF. Linear regression analysis was performed to assess the association between NIHTB-CB change-sensitive scores and CBF. Model assumptions were verified via Q-Q plots and residual analysis. Covariates (age, sex, and estimated glomerular filtration rate) were added in sequence to assess confounding, with model comparisons made using the Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria. Our final models included the dependent and independent variables of interest, controlling for age. Our primary objective was to assess the association between CBF in the global gray matter and fluid cognition composite change-sensitive scores.

The final analysis was performed on 13 of the 14 patients enrolled, because 1 could not complete the magnetic resonance imaging because of claustrophobia. The study participants are described in Table S1. The mean age of the 13 participants was 56 ± 10 years; 9 (69%) were women, 9 (69%) White, and 3 (23%) African American, and 9 participants (69%) had a college or graduate school degree. Medical history included diabetes in 5 (39%), hypertension in 11 (85%), dyslipidemia in 7 (54%), smoking in 4 (31%), and cardiac bypass procedure in 1 (7.7%); none had atrial fibrillation, seizures, stroke, or coronary angioplasty. The mean estimated glomerular filtration rate was 46.4 ± 13.1 mL/min/1.73 m2 and the mean CBF in global gray matter was 75.5 ± 12.3 mL/100 g/min. The mean fluid, crystallized, and total cognition composite scores were 511.7 ± 18.2, 534.6 ± 11.7, and 532.2 ± 11.5, and the median scores were 512 (interquartile range [IQR], 499-521), 535 (IQR, 532-541), and 521 (IQR, 520-531), respectively.

Contrary to published data in aging and Alzheimer disease,2 fluid cognition composite score had a strong negative association with CBF in the global gray matter (higher scores were associated with lower CBF, P = 0.01) (Table 1 and Fig 1), as well as the middle frontal, parietal, precuneus, superior parietal, primary motor cortex, and temporal regions (P < 0.05) (Table 1). Crystallized and total cognition composite scores also showed largely negative trends with global and regional CBF, but the associations were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Addition of sex and estimated glomerular filtration rate to the model did not change these associations.

This study shows that cognitive impairment in CKD is not associated with lower CBF. Paradoxically, low CBF, especially in the parietal cortex, may be associated with better cognition. Our data are consistent with previous studies demonstrating high CBF in individuals with CKD6 and those undergoing dialysis.7 The former study in patients with CKD did not report cognition,6 whereas the latter also showed a negative association of CBF with cognition.7 Notably, severe CKD is associated with cognitive impairment and high CBF,5,8 and although cognition improves after kidney transplantation,8 CBF decreases after transplantation,5 indicating that cognition in patients with CKD may be independent of CBF. Although the exact cause for high CBF in CKD is unknown, blood brain barrier disruption,9 altered cerebral autoregulation,10 hypoxia, and endothelial dysfunction have been suggested. Although these data provide proof of concept and novel insights into cognition in CKD, multiple comparison corrections (such as the Bonferroni method) were not performed because of the limited sample size and exploratory nature of the study. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. Larger, adequately powered studies will be necessary to draw definitive conclusions and to allow appropriate statistical adjustments.

To summarize, there is a difference in the association between cognition and CBF in the CKD population versus the general population, and results from studies in aging and Alzheimer disease should not be extrapolated to individuals with CKD. Studies focused on understanding the mechanisms underlying cognitive impairment in CKD are needed to improve diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to manage cognitive impairment in this unique population.
