A computational approach to rational summability and its applications via discrete residues
Carlos E. Arreche, Hari P. Sitaula

Abstract
. A rational function f ⁡ ( x ) f(x) is rationally summable if there exists a rational function g ⁡ ( x ) g(x) such that f ⁡ ( x ) = g ⁡ ( x + 1 ) − g ⁡ ( x ) f(x)=g(x+1)-g(x) . Detecting whether a given rational function is summable is an important and basic computational subproblem that arises in algorithms to study diverse aspects of shift difference equations. The discrete residues introduced by Chen and Singer in 2012 enjoy the obstruction-theoretic property that a rational function is summable if and only if all its discrete residues vanish. However, these discrete residues are defined in terms of the data in the complete partial fraction decomposition of the given rational function, which cannot be accessed computationally in general. We explain how to efficiently compute (a rational representation of) the discrete residues of any rational function, relying only on gcd computations, linear algebra, and a black box algorithm to compute the autodispersion set of the denominator polynomial. We also explain how to apply our algorithms to serial summability and creative telescoping problems, and how to apply these computations to compute Galois groups of difference equations.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field of characteristic zero, not assumed to be algebraically closed, and denote by
\mathbb{K}(x)
the field of rational functions in an indeterminate
x
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
. Given
f(x)\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, the
rational summation problem
originally posed in
[
Abr71
]
asks to construct
g(x),h(x)\in\mathbb{K}(x)
such that

f(x)=g(x+1)-g(x)+h(x),

(1.1)

and such that the degree of the denominator of
h(x)
is as small as possible. Such an
h(x)
is called a
reduced form
of
f(x)
.
The rational summation problem has attracted much attention over the last half century
[
Abr71
,
Abr75
,
Moe77
,
Kar81
,
Pau95
,
Pir95
,
PS95
,
MS95
,
Abr95
,
Pol08
]
. It is easy to see that the problem admits a solution, starting from the trivial solution
(g(x),h(x))=(f(x-1),f(x-1))
and applying the well-ordering principle. Moreover, given a solution
(g(x),h(x))
we obtain another solution
(g(x)-h(x),h(x+1))
since the degree of the denominator of
h(x+1)
is the same as that of
h(x)
, and therefore these solutions are not unique. In the above references, several different choices are made for addressing this non-uniqueness in algorithmic settings – for example, one can ask for the denominator of
g(x)
to be also as small as possible, and/or to compute some (any) solution
(g(x),h(x))
to (
1.1
) as efficiently as possible. The introduction of
[
PS95
]
contains a concise summary and comparison between most of these different approaches.

Every algorithm for solving the rational summation problem also addresses, as a byproduct, the
rational summability problem
of deciding, for a given
f(x)\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, whether (just yes/no) there exists
g(x)\in\mathbb{K}(x)
such that
f(x)=g(x+1)-g(x)
. In this case we say that
f(x)
is
rationally summable
. There are various algorithms for addressing this simpler question, designed to forego the usually expensive and often irrelevant computation of the
certificate

g(x)
, which are presented and discussed for example in
[
Mat00
,
GGSZ03
,
BCCL10
,
CS12
,
CHKL15
,
HW15
,
GHLZ22
]
and the references therein.

The main goal of this work is to explain how to implement effectively and efficiently the residue-theoretic approach to rational summability proposed in
[
CS12
]
, following and expanding upon the preliminary presentation developed in
[
AS24
]
. The
discrete residues
of
f(x)\in\mathbb{K}(x)
(see Definition
3.1
) are certain constants, belonging to the algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
and defined in terms of the complete partial fraction decomposition of
f(x)
, which have the obstruction-theoretic property that they are all zero if and only if
f(x)
is rationally summable. Computing these discrete residues directly from their definition is not possible in general, because neither is the apparently necessary prior computation of the complete partial fraction decomposition.

The algorithms that we propose here avoid expensive/impossible complete factorizations of denominator polynomials into linear factors, and rely
almost
exclusively on gcd computations and linear algebra. We say “almost” because we do require a black box algorithm to compute the
autodispersion set
of a polynomial, for which the most efficient known method to compute it relies on computing irreducible factorizations of denominator polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[x]
. Besides the method of computation, another problem inherent in the original definition of discrete residues is that in general they belong to algebraic extensions of
\mathbb{K}
of arbitrarily high degree. Thus, inspired by
[
BS93
, Thm. 1]
, we propose a
\mathbb{K}
-rational representation of the discrete residues of
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
consisting of pairs of polynomials. For each such pair, the first polynomial encodes in its set of roots
where

f
has non-zero residues, and the second polynomial encodes in its evaluation at each such root
what
the corresponding discrete residue is (see Definition
3.4
for more details).

A key idea that enables all our computations is that iteratively applying classical Hermite reduction to
f(x)
allows us to focus our attention on the special case where
f(x)
has only simple poles. This idea is already suggested in
[
Hor71
, §5]
, but does not seem to be widely utilized in the literature. Thus a secondary goal of this work is to proselytize for this useful trick. Although we have not yet formally computed the theoretical worst-case complexity of this procedure (see Algorithm
1
), we conjecture that it should be asymptotically the same (as a function of the degree of the denominator) as that of applying classical Hermite reduction only once.

Having reduced to the case of rational functions with only simple poles, all the varied approaches to compute reduced forms
h(x)
of
f(x)
as in (
1.1
) seem now to proceed quite similarly to one another. As we remarked in
[
AS24
]
, our own approach is based on similar ideas as that of
[
GGSZ03
, §5]
. But the latter procedure is necessarily more complicated than ours, because it allows more general inputs than we need to, having carried out our earlier reduction to the case where
f(x)
has only simple poles. The relative simplicity of our procedure is precisely what enables us to easily produce variants of our basic procedures that are better adapted to particular purposes.

Once we have computed a reduced form
\bar{f}(x)
of
f(x)
whose denominator is both squarefree and shift-free, the discrete residues of
f(x)
coincide with the classical first-order residues of
\bar{f}(x)
. The factorization-free computation of the latter is achieved by the well-known computation in
[
Tra76
, Lem. 5.1]
.

Our own interest in computing discrete residues is motivated by the following serial variant of the summability problem, which often arises as a basic subproblem in algorithms for computing Galois groups associated with shift difference equations
[
vdPS97
,
Hen98
]
. Given an
n
-tuple
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{n}(x))\in\mathbb{K}(x)^{n}
, we show in Proposition
7.4
how to compute (a basis for) the
\mathbb{K}
-vector space

V(\mathbf{f}):=\bigl\{\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n}\ |\ v_{1}f_{1}+\dots+v_{n}f_{n}\ \text{is summable}\bigr\}.

(1.2)

The adaptability of our procedures enables us also to address in Proposition
7.7
serial differential creative telescoping problems generalizing (
1.2
) by replacing the
v_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
with unknown linear differential operators with
\mathbb{K}
coefficients
\mathcal{L}_{i}\in\mathbb{K}\left[\frac{d}{dx}\right]
. Also analogously, this kind of problem often arises in the computation of differential Galois groups of shift difference equations
[
HS08
,
Arr17
]
. In the computation of this space of differential operators there arises the additional difficulty of having to produce an a priori bound on the orders of the operators that need to be considered. As far as we know, the first ever such bound is obtained in Proposition
7.9
, relying on our algorithmic results on discrete residues.

Our approach based on discrete residues makes it very straightforward how to accommodate several
f_{i}(x)
simultaneously as in (
1.2
), which adaptation is less obvious (to us) how to carry out efficiently using other reduction methods (but see
[
CHKL15
, §5]
). There are also direct analogues of discrete residues for
q
-difference equations
[
CS12
]
, Mahler difference equations
[
AZ22
,
AZ24
]
, and elliptic difference equations
[
DHRS18
,
HS21
,
Bab25
]
. We hope and expect that the conceptual simplicity of the approach that we continue here will be useful in developing analogues in these other related (but much more technically challenging) contexts beyond the shift case.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Basic notation and conventions

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of strictly positive integers, and by
\mathbb{K}
a computable field of characteristic zero. We denote by
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
a fixed algebraic closure of
\mathbb{K}
. We do not assume
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed, and we will only refer to
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
in proofs or for defining theoretical notions, never for computations. We shall impose the following technical assumption on
\mathbb{K}
: either it is feasible to compute integer solutions to arbitrary polynomial equations with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
, or it is feasible to compute irreducible factorizations of polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[x]
(see Remark
5.3
).

We denote by
\mathbb{K}[x]
the ring of polynomials, and by
\mathbb{K}(x)
the field of rational functions, in the indeterminate
x
. For
f(x)\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, we define

\sigma:f(x)\mapsto f(x+1);\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta:f(x)\mapsto f(x+1)-f(x).

Note that
\sigma
is a
\mathbb{K}
-linear field automorphism of
\mathbb{K}(x)
and
\Delta=\sigma-\mathrm{id}
is only a
\mathbb{K}
-linear map with
\mathrm{ker}(\Delta)=\mathbb{K}
. We often suppress the functional notation and simply write
f
instead of
f(x)
,
\sigma(f)
instead of
f(x+1)
, etc., when no confusion is likely to arise.

We assume implicitly throughout that rational functions are normalized to have monic denominator. Even when our rational functions are obtained as (intermediate) outputs of some procedures, we will take care to arrange things so that this normalization always holds. In particular, we also assume that the outputs of
\mathrm{gcd}
and
\mathrm{lcm}
procedures are also always normalized to be monic. We adopt the convention that
\mathrm{deg}(0)=-1
. A
proper
rational function is one whose numerator has strictly smaller degree than that of its denominator. An unadorned
\mathrm{gcd}
or
\mathrm{lcm}
or
\mathrm{deg}
means that it is with respect to
x
. On the few occasions where we need a
\mathrm{gcd}
with respect to a different variable
z
, we will write
\mathrm{gcd}_{z}
. We write
\frac{d}{dx}
(resp.,
\frac{d}{dz}
) for the usual derivation operator with respect to
x
(resp., with respect to
z
).

2.2.
Partial fraction decompositions

A polynomial
b\in\mathbb{K}(x)
is
squarefree
if
b\neq 0
and
\mathrm{gcd}\bigl(b,\frac{d}{dx}b\bigr)=1
. Consider a proper rational function
f=\frac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, with
\mathrm{deg}(b)\geq 1
and
\mathrm{gcd}(a,b)=1
. Suppose further that
b
is squarefree, and that we are given a set
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
of monic non-constant polynomials such that
\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}=b
. Then necessarily
\mathrm{gcd}(b_{i},b_{j})=1
whenever
i\neq j
, and there exist unique non-zero polynomials
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
with
\mathrm{deg}(a_{i})<\mathrm{deg}(b_{i})
for each
i=1,\dots,n
such that
f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}
. In this situation, we denote

\mathtt{ParFrac}(f;b_{1},\dots,b_{n}):=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}).

(2.1)

We emphasize that the computation of partial fraction decompositions (
2.1
) can be done very efficiently
[
KT77
]
, provided that the denominator
b
of
f
has already been factored into pairwise relatively prime factors
b_{i}
, which need not be irreducible in
\mathbb{K}[x]
. Although one can similarly carry out such partial fraction decompositions more generally for pre-factored denominators
b
that are not necessarily squarefree, our procedures only need to compute partial fraction decompositions for pre-factored squarefree denominators, in which case the notation (
2.1
) is conveniently light.

2.3.
Summability and dispersion

We say
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
is
(rationally) summable
if there exists
g\in\mathbb{K}(x)
such that
f=\Delta(g)
. For a non-constant polynomial
b\in\mathbb{K}[x]
, we follow the original
[
Abr71
]
in defining the
dispersion
of
b

\mathrm{disp}(b):=\mathrm{max}\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ \mathrm{gcd}(b,\sigma^{\ell}(b))\neq 1\}.

If
\mathrm{disp}(b)=0
we say that
b
is
shiftfree
. For a rational function
f=\frac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
with
\mathrm{gcd}(a,b)=1
and
b\notin\mathbb{K}
, the
polar dispersion

\mathrm{pdisp}(f):=\mathrm{disp}(b)
.

We denote by
\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
the set of orbits for the action of the additive group
\mathbb{Z}
on
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
. For
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
, we denote

\omega(\alpha):=\{\alpha+n\ |\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\},

the unique orbit in
\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
containing
\alpha
. We will often simply write
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
whenever we have no need to reference a specific
\alpha\in\omega
.

3.
Discrete residues and summability

Definition 3.1

(
[
CS12
, Def. 2.3]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, and consider the complete partial fraction decomposition

f=p+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}}\frac{c_{k}(\alpha)}{(x-\alpha)^{k}},

(3.1)

where
p\in\mathbb{K}[x]
and all but finitely many of the
c_{k}(\alpha)\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
are zero for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
. The
discrete residue
of
f
of order
k\in\mathbb{N}
at the orbit
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
is

\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k):=\sum_{\alpha\in\omega}c_{k}(\alpha).

(3.2)

The relevance of discrete residues to the study of rational summability is captured by the following result.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
CS12
, Prop. 2.5]
)
.

f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
is rationally summable if and only if
\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k)=0
for every
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

As pointed out in
[
CS12
, Rem. 2.6]
, the above Proposition
3.2
recasts in terms of discrete residues a well-known rational summability criterion that reverberates throughout the literature, for example in
[
Abr95
, p. 305]
,
[
Mat00
, Thm. 10]
,
[
GGSZ03
, Thm. 11]
,
[
AC05
, Cor. 1]
, and
[
HW15
, Prop. 3.4]
. All of these rely in some form or another on the following fundamental result of Abramov, which already gives an important obstruction to summability.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
Abr71
, Prop. 3]
)
.

If a proper
0\neq f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
is rationally summable then
\mathrm{pdisp}(f)>0
.

Discrete residues are also intimately related to the computation of reduced forms for
f
, in the following sense. As discussed in
[
CS12
, §2.4]
, every reduced form
h
of
f
as in (
1.1
) has the form

h=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k)}{(x-\alpha_{\omega})^{k}}

(3.3)

for some arbitrary choice of representatives
\alpha_{\omega}\in\omega
. Conversely, for any
h
of the form (
3.3
), Proposition
3.2
immediately yields that
f-h
is rationally summable. An equivalent characterization for
h\in\mathbb{K}(x)
to be a reduced form is for it to have polar dispersion
0
(see
[
Abr75
, Props. 4 & 6]
). By (
3.3
), knowing the
\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k)
is thus “the same” as knowing some/all reduced forms
h
of
f
. But discrete residues still serve as a very useful organizing principle and technical tool, for both theoretical and practical computations.

Definition 3.4
.

For
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, a
\mathbb{K}
-rational system of discrete residues
of
f
is a sequence of pairs
(B_{1},D_{1}),\dots,(B_{m},D_{m})\in\mathbb{K}[x]\times\mathbb{K}[x]
such that:

•

f
has no poles of order higher than
m
;

•

for each
1\leq k\leq m
, the polynomial
B_{k}
is nonzero, squarefree, shiftfree, and has
\mathrm{deg}(B_{k})>\mathrm{deg}(D_{k})
;

•

if
1\leq k\leq m
and
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
are such that no
\alpha\in\omega
is a root of
B_{k}
, then
\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k)=0
; and

•

if
1\leq k\leq m
and
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
are such that
B_{k}(\alpha)=0
, then

\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega(\alpha),k)=D_{k}(\alpha).

Note that we do not insist on the length
m
to be precisely equal to the highest order of a pole of
f
. The notion of
\mathbb{K}
-rational system of discrete residues in Definition
3.4
was not defined explicitly in
[
AS24
]
, and it is somewhat less rigid than what we set out to compute in
[
AS24
, §3]
. We find it useful to introduce this notion explicitly, and with this added flexibility. It may not seem immediately obvious to the non-expert that there always exists a
\mathbb{K}
-rational
system of discrete residues for any
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
. Although it is not logically necessary to formally prove their existence here (since we shall even construct them explicitly in Algorithm
4
, thus they exist!), it may perhaps be useful to offer a more conceptual Galois-theoretic justification.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
there exists a
\mathbb{K}
-rational system of discrete residues of
f
as in Definition
3.4
.

Proof.

The Galois group
\Gamma:=\mathrm{Gal}\bigl(\overline{\mathbb{K}}(x)/\mathbb{K}(x)\bigr)
is canonically identified with
\mathrm{Gal}\bigl(\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{K}\bigr)
via the restriction homomorphism
\gamma\mapsto\gamma|_{\overline{\mathbb{K}}}
. Since the action of
\Gamma
on
\overline{\mathbb{K}}(x)
commutes with the
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
-linear shift automorphism
\sigma:x\mapsto x+1
, we see that
\omega(\gamma(\alpha))=\gamma(\omega(\alpha))
for every
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
and
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Moreover, since
\gamma\in\Gamma
acts trivially on
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, we see that the coefficients in the partial fraction decomposition (
2.1
) of
f
also satisfy
c_{k}(\gamma(\alpha))=\gamma(c_{k}(\alpha))
for every
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\gamma\in\Gamma
. It then follows from Definition
3.1
that
\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega(\gamma(\alpha)),k)=\gamma\bigl(\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega(\alpha),k)\bigr)
for each
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Thus we can construct a monic, squarefree, shiftfree
B_{k}\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}[x]
having precisely one root
\alpha_{\omega}
in each orbit
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k)\neq 0
, and in such a way that
\alpha_{\gamma(\omega)}=\gamma(\alpha_{\omega})
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
, which forces
B_{k}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
. There then exists a unique interpolation polynomial
D_{k}\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}[x]
with
\mathrm{deg}(D_{k})<\mathrm{deg}(B_{k})
such that
D_{k}(\alpha)=\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega(\alpha),k)
for each root
\alpha
of
B_{k}
. Since
\gamma(D_{k}(\alpha))=D_{k}(\gamma(\alpha))
for each such
\alpha
and for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we must have
D_{k}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
.
∎

Our goal is to compute efficiently a
\mathbb{K}
-rational system of discrete residues for any given
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, in place of the discrete residues of
f
as literally given in Definition
3.1
. For such a sequence
(B_{1},D_{1}),\dots,(B_{m},D_{m})
as in our Definition
3.4
, the polynomials
D_{k}
encode all the discrete residues of
f
of order
k
simultaneously, since
D_{k}(\alpha)=\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega(\alpha),k)
for each root
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
of
B_{k}
, and moreover each
B_{k}
contains in its set of zeros a complete and irredundant set of representatives
\alpha\in\omega
for every orbit
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k)\neq 0
. Thus with such a set of data computed from
f
we are able to write down explicitly a symbolic expression for the general theoretical reduced form
h
given in (
3.3
) of
f
as in (
1.1
):

h=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}\ \text{s.t.}\\
B_{k}(\alpha)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{D_{k}(\alpha)}{(x-\alpha)^{k}}.

(3.4)

Remark 3.6
.

The expression in (
3.4
) is analogous to the inspirational result
[
BS93
, Thm. 1]
on symbolic partial fraction decompositions, and provides an answer to the question posed in the Remark at the end of
[
Pau95
, §6.2]
, of what an analogue of the Rothstein-Trager method
[
Tra76
]
for rational integration might look like.

4.
Iterated Hermite reduction

It is immediate from the Definition
3.1
that the polynomial part
p
of
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
in (
3.1
) is irrelevant, both for the study of summability as well as for the computation of discrete residues. So beginning with this section we restrict our attention to proper rational functions
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
.

Our first task is to reduce to the case where
f
has squarefree denominator. In this section we describe how to compute
f_{k}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that, relative to the theoretical partial fraction decomposition (
3.1
) of
f
, we have

f_{k}=\sum_{\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}}\frac{c_{k}(\alpha)}{x-\alpha}.

(4.1)

Of course we will then have by Definition
3.1

\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k)=\mathrm{dres}(f_{k},\omega,1)

(4.2)

for every
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Our computation of the
f_{k}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
satisfying (
4.1
) is based on iterating classical so-called
Hermite reduction
algorithms, originally developed in
[
Ost45
,
Her72
]
and for which we refer to the wonderful modern reference
[
Bro05
, §2.2,§2.3]
.

Definition 4.1
.

For proper
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, the
Hermite reduction
of
f
is

\mathtt{HermiteReduction}(f)=(g,h)

where
g,h\in\mathbb{K}(x)
are the unique proper rational functions such that

f=\frac{d}{dx}(g)+h

and
h
has squarefree denominator.

Algorithm
1
computes the
f_{k}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
satisfying (
4.1
) by applying Hermite reduction iteratively and scaling the intermediate outputs appropriately.

Algorithm 1

\mathtt{HermiteList}
procedure

1:

A proper rational function
0\neq f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
.

2:

A list
(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})
of
f_{k}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
satisfying (
4.1
), such that
f_{m}\neq 0
and
c_{k}(\alpha)=0
for every
k>m
and every
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
.

3:

Initialize loop:
m\leftarrow 0
;
g\leftarrow f
;

4:

while

g\neq 0

do

5:

(g,\hat{f}_{m+1})\leftarrow\mathtt{HermiteReduction}(g)
;

6:

m\leftarrow m+1
;

7:

end

while
;

8:

f_{k}\leftarrow(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\hat{f}_{k}
;

9:

return

(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Algorithm
1
is correct.

Proof.

Let us denote by
||f||=m\in\mathbb{N}
denote the highest order of any pole of
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, i.e., the largest
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that there exists
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
with
c_{m}(\alpha)\neq 0
in (
2.1
). Note that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}(x)
defined by (
4.1
) are uniquely determined by having squarefree denominator and satisfying

f=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\frac{d^{k-1}}{dx^{k-1}}(f_{k}).

(4.3)

Defining inductively
g_{0}:=f
and

\displaystyle(g_{k},\hat{f}_{k}):=\mathtt{HermiteReduction}(g_{k-1})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\qquad g_{k-1}=\smash{\frac{d}{dx}}(g_{k})+\hat{f}_{k}

(4.4)

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
as in Algorithm
1
, we obtain by construction that all
g_{k},\hat{f}_{k}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
and every
\hat{f}_{k}
has squarefree denominator. Moreover,
||g_{k}||=||g_{k-1}||-1=m-k
, and therefore the algorithm terminates with
g_{m}=0
. Moreover, it follows from (
4.4
) that

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{d^{k-1}\hat{f}_{k}}{dx^{k-1}}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{d^{k-1}g_{k-1}}{dx^{k-1}}-\frac{d^{k}g_{k}}{dx^{k}}\right)=g_{0}-\frac{d^{m}g_{m}}{dx^{m}}=f.

Therefore the elements
(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\hat{f}_{k}
are squarefree and satisfy (
4.3
), so they agree with the
f_{k}\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}(x)
satisfying (
4.1
).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

As we mentioned in the introduction, as well as in
[
AS24
]
, we do not expect Algorithm
1
to be surprising to the experts. And yet it is a mystery to us why this trick is not used more widely since being originally suggested in
[
Hor71
, §5]
. We expect the theoretical cost of computing
\mathtt{HermiteList}(f)
iteratively as in Algorithm
1
to be asymptotically the same as that of computing
\mathtt{HermiteReduction}(f)
only once, as a function of the degree of the denominator. This might seem counterintuitive, since the former is defined by applying the latter as many times as the highest order
m
of any pole of
f
. But the size of the successive inputs in the loop decreases so quickly that the cost of the first step essentially dominates the added cost of the remaining steps put together. This conclusion is already drawn in
[
Hor71
, §5]
regarding the computational cost of computing iterated integrals of rational functions.

5.
Simple Reduction

The results of the previous section allow us to further restrict our attention to proper rational functions
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
with simple poles, which we write uniquely as

with

such that

Our Algorithm

Proposition 5.4
.

Algorithm

is correct.

Proof.

As in Algorithm
3
, let us denote by

the denominator of
f
and set

. Then indeed if
S=\emptyset
,
\mathrm{pdisp}(f)=0
, so

is already reduced and there is nothing to compute. Thus let us assume from now on that
S\neq\emptyset
, and let us consider roots of polynomials in
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
. For each
\ell\in S
, the roots of

are those roots
\alpha
of
b
such that
\alpha-\ell
is also a root of
b
. Therefore the roots of
G=\mathrm{lcm}(g_{\ell}:\ell\in S)
are those roots
\alpha
of
b
such that
\alpha-\ell
is also a root of
b
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
(because all possible such
\ell
belong to
S
, by the Definition
5.1
of

). It follows that the roots of

are those roots
\alpha
of
b
such that
\alpha-\ell
is
not
a root of
b
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular,
\mathrm{disp}(b_{0})=0
. We call
b_{0}
the
divisor of initial roots
.

Now the roots of
b_{\ell}:=\mathrm{gcd}(\sigma^{-\ell}(b_{0}),b)
are those roots
\alpha
of
b
such that
\alpha-\ell
is a root of
b_{0}
, i.e., the roots of
b
which are precisely
\ell
shifts away from the initial root in their respective
\mathbb{Z}
-orbits. It may happen that

for some
\ell\in S
, because even though each
\ell\in S
is the difference between two roots of
b
, it might be that no such pair of roots of

involves any initial roots of
b_{0}
. Writing
N:=\{0\}\cup\{\ell\in S\ |\ \mathrm{deg}(b_{\ell})\geq 1\}
, it is clear that

(5.1)

Therefore we may uniquely decompose

into partial fractions as in (
2.1
) with respect to the factorization (
5.1
), as called by Algorithm
3

f=\sum_{\ell\in N}\frac{a_{\ell}}{b_{\ell}},\qquad\text{and set}\qquad\bar{f}:=\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in N}\sigma^{\ell}\left(\frac{a_{\ell}}{b_{\ell}}\right).

Now this
\bar{f}
is a sum of proper rational functions with squarefree denominators, whence
\bar{f}
also is proper with squarefree denominator.
Since
\sigma^{\ell}(b_{\ell})=\mathrm{gcd}(b_{0},\sigma^{\ell}(b))
is a factor of

for each
\ell\in N
and
\mathrm{disp}(b_{0})=0
, we conclude that
\mathrm{pdisp}(\bar{f})=0
. Finally, for each

\frac{a_{\ell}}{b_{\ell}}-\sigma^{\ell}\left(\frac{a_{\ell}}{b_{\ell}}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\sigma^{i}\left(\frac{a_{\ell}}{b_{\ell}}-\sigma\left(\frac{a_{\ell}}{b_{\ell}}\right)\right),

whence

is a sum of rationally summable elements, and is therefore itself rationally summable. By Proposition

for every

and every

.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

As we stated in the introduction, Algorithm

strikes us as being conceptually similar to the one already developed in
[

, §5]
, but its description is made simpler by our restriction to rational functions with simple poles only. Although having a procedure that is easier for humans to read is not always computationally advantageous, it is so in this case, because the relative simplicity of Algorithm

will enable us in §

6.
Computation of discrete residues

Now we wish to put together the algorithms presented in the earlier sections to compute symbolically all the discrete residues of an arbitrary proper
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
, in the sense described in §
3
. In order to do this, we first recall the following result describing the sense in which we compute classical residues symbolically by means of an auxiliary polynomial, and its short proof which explains how to actually compute this polynomial in practice.

Lemma 6.1

(
[
Tra76
, Lem. 5.1]
)
.

Let
f=\frac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
such that
a,b\in\mathbb{K}[x]
satisfy
a\neq 0
,
\mathrm{deg}(a)<\mathrm{deg}(b)
,
\mathrm{gcd}(a,b)=1
, and
b
is squarefree. Then there exists a unique polynomial
0\neq r\in\mathbb{K}[x]
such that
\mathrm{deg}(r)<\mathrm{deg}(b)
and

f=\sum_{\{\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}\ |\ b(\alpha)=0\}}\frac{r(\alpha)}{x-\alpha}.

Proof.

Since the set of poles of
f
(all simple poles) is the set of roots of
b
, we know the classical first-order residue
c_{1}(\alpha)
of
f
at each
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
such that
b(\alpha)=0
satisfies
0\neq c_{1}(\alpha)=a(\alpha)/\frac{db}{dx}(\alpha).
Using the extended Euclidean algorithm we can compute the unique
0\neq r
in
\mathbb{K}[x]
such that
\mathrm{deg}(r)<\mathrm{deg}(b)
and
r\cdot\frac{d}{dx}(b)\equiv a\pmod{b}.

∎

For
f\in\mathbb{K}(x)
satisfying the hypotheses of Lemma
6.1
, we denote

\mathtt{FirstResidues}(f):=(b,r),

(6.1)

where
b,r\in\mathbb{K}[x]
are also as in the notation of Lemma
6.1
. We also define
\mathtt{FirstResidues}(0):=(1,0)
, for convenience. With this, we can now describe the following simple Algorithm
4
to compute a symbolic representation of the discrete residues of
f
.

procedure

1:

A proper rational function

.

2:

.

.

is correct.

Proof.

It follows from the correctness of Algorithm
1
proved in Lemma

that
f

m
. Consider now
\bar{f}_{k}:=\mathtt{SimpleReduction}(f_{k})

f_{k}-\bar{f}_{k}
is summable. We prove the correctness of Algorithm
4
for each

\bar{f}_{k}=0

In case
\bar{f}_{k}=0

)

for every
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
. Thus the output of Algorithm

is correct in this case.

Suppose now that
\bar{f}_{k}\neq 0
. It follows from the definition of

as in (

) that

is the denominator of the proper rational function

, and therefore

is non-constant, squarefree, and has
\mathrm{disp}(B_{k})=0

proved in Lemma

. Let us denote by

at each

(note that

that

, and

for each root

of

. Since

has at most one pole in each orbit

(this is the very meaning of

for every

. To conclude, we observe that

\mathrm{dres}(\bar{f}_{k},\omega,1)=\mathrm{dres}(f_{k},\omega,1)=\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k)

Remark 6.3
.

As we mentioned in §
3
, the knowledge of a reduced form
h
for
f
is morally “the same” as knowledge of the discrete residues of
f
. And yet, the output
\bigl((B_{1},D_{1}),\dots,(B_{m},D_{m})\bigr)
of Algorithm
4
has the following deficiency: it may happen that for some
j\neq k
, we have
\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k)\neq 0\neq\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,j)
, and yet the representatives
\alpha_{j},\alpha_{k}\in\omega
such that
B_{j}(\alpha_{j})=0=B_{k}(\alpha_{k})
may be distinct, with
\alpha_{j}\neq\alpha_{k}
. In many applications, this is not an issue because summability problems decompose into parallel summability problems in each degree component, as we see from Proposition
3.2
. Actually, the systematic exploitation of this particularity was one of the original motivations behind Algorithm
1
. But it is still somewhat unsatisfying that the different
B_{k}
associated to the same
f
are not better coordinated. Moreover, this does become a more serious issue in further applications to creative telescoping, where the discrete residues of different orders begin to interact. We explain how to address this problem in Remark
7.2
, when we have developed the requisite technology.

7.
Generalizations and applications to serial summability and creative telescoping

In this section we collect some modifications to the procedures described in the previous sections to produce outputs that allow for more immediate comparison of discrete residues across several rational functions and across different orders.

7.1.
Serial summability

We begin with the parameterized summability problem (
1.2
) described in the introduction. Let
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\in\mathbb{K}(x)^{n}
be given, and suppose we wish to compute a
\mathbb{K}
-basis for

V(\mathbf{f}):=\left\{(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n}\ \middle|\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\cdot f_{i}\quad\text{is summable}\right\}.

(7.1)

It follows from Definition
3.1
that, for every
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
,
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathrm{dres}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\cdot f_{i},\,\omega,\,k\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\cdot\mathrm{dres}(f_{i},\omega,k).

(7.2)

Therefore, by Proposition
3.2
, we obtain the alternative characterization

\begin{gathered}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in V(\mathbf{f})\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\cdot\mathrm{dres}(f_{i},\omega,k)=0\quad\text{for every}\quad\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{and every}\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.\end{gathered}

(7.3)

This is a linear system that we can solve for the unknown vectors in
V(\mathbf{f})
as soon as we know how to write it down explicitly. The problem is that applying Algorithm
4
to each
f_{i}
separately yields

\mathtt{DiscreteResidues}(f_{i})=\bigl((B_{i,1},D_{i,1}),\dots,(B_{i,m_{i}},D_{i,m_{i}})).

It may happen that many different
\alpha_{i,k}\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
all belong to the same orbit
\omega\in\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{Z}
and satisfy
B_{i,k}(\alpha_{i,k})=0
. In this case, the condition (
7.3
) is
not
equivalent to asking for
\sum_{i}v_{i}\cdot D_{i,k}=0
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
(but see Proposition
7.4
below). This leads to the undesirable bookkeping problem of having to decide, for each fixed
k\leq\max\{m_{i}\ |\ i=1,\dots,n\}
, for which orbits
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}/\mathbb{Z}
it might happen that we have several different
\alpha_{i}\in\omega
which are roots of
B_{i,k}
but are not equal to one another on the nose.

To address this kind of problem, we introduce in Algorithm
5
a generalization of Algorithm
3
that computes reduced forms
\bar{f}_{1},\dots,\bar{f}_{n}
for several
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
simultaneously and
compatibly
, in the sense that if
\alpha_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
is a pole of
\bar{f}_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
is a pole of
\bar{f}_{j}
such that
\omega(\alpha_{i})=\omega(\alpha_{j})
then we insist on having
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{j}
on the nose. Note that this compatibility is succintly captured by the requirement that the product of the denominators of the
\bar{f}_{i}
be shiftfree, which is how this compatibility is expressed in the Output of Algorithm
5
below. As in Algorithm
3
, we may assume that all the
f_{i}
are proper and, thanks to Algorithm
1
, that they all have squarefree denominators.

7.2.
Serial creative telescoping

In

For an

We begin with a computational result.

Lemma 7.5
.

For

\mathrm{dres}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(f),\omega,k\bigr)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{j}\frac{(-1)^{j}(k-1)!}{(k-1-j)!}\mathrm{dres}(f,\omega,k-j).

Proof.

This follows from the elementary computation that

\mathcal{L}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}}\frac{c_{k}(\alpha)}{(x-\alpha)^{k}}\right)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{j}\frac{(-1)^{j}(k-1)!}{(k-1-j)!}c_{k-j}(\alpha)\right)\frac{1}{(x-\alpha)^{k}}

and the Definition
3.1
.
∎

The following differential analogue of (
7.2

Corollary 7.6
.

For

Let
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})

Remark 7.8
.

Note that the range (
7.7
) may be excessive in particular cases. If we have a given
(\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{n})\in\mathbb{K}\left[\frac{d}{dx}\right]^{n}
and we wish to test whether it belongs to

, it is sufficient to verify (
7.7
) only for
1\leq k\leq M
, for

M:=\mathrm{max}\bigl\{\mathrm{ord}(\mathcal{L}_{1})+m_{1},\dots,\mathrm{ord}(\mathcal{L}_{n})+m_{n}\bigr\},

since the highest order of any pole of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}(f_{i})
is bounded above by
M
, and therefore
\mathrm{dres}(\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i}(f_{i}),\omega,k)=0
automatically for any

. In general,
M
may be strictly smaller than the sum of the maxima

.

Since

is the filtered union of the

will eventually succeed in computing a
\mathbb{K}\left[\frac{d}{dx}\right]

. But this is not useful in practice without a way to produce an upper bound

that is guaranteed to be large enough. We accomplish this and a little more in the next result.

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}(x)
be proper and non-zero, let
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
be the highest order of any pole of
f_{i}
, and let

\mathbb{K}

as in (

Proof.

For
\bm{\mathcal{L}}=(\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{n})\in W(\mathbf{f})
as in (
7.6
), let
\beta_{i}:=\mathrm{ord}(\mathcal{L}_{i})

and

Note that

if and only if

already. Thus, supposing

, it suffices to construct
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{n})\in W^{\prime}(\mathbf{f})

M:=M(\bm{\mathcal{L}})
, and write each

\mathcal{L}_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{\beta_{i}}\lambda_{i,j}\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}.

Then we

the desired

with the convention that
\lambda_{i,M-m+j}:=0
for every

such that
\beta_{i}\geq M-m

\tilde{\beta}_{i}:=\mathrm{ord}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\right)
is the largest

such that
\lambda_{i,M-m+j}\neq 0

\tilde{\beta}_{i}=\beta_{i}-(M-m)

\beta_{i}+m_{i}\leq M
by definition (
7.9
), which implies that
\beta_{i}-(M-m)\leq m-m_{i}
. Thus,
M-m+\tilde{\beta}_{i}=\beta_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq n
such that
\beta_{i}\geq M-m

\tilde{\lambda}_{i,\tilde{\beta}_{i}}=\lambda_{i,\beta_{i}}

), we see that

\mathrm{ord}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i}-\frac{d^{M-m}}{dx^{M-m}}\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\right)\leq\beta_{i}-1

for such

. We note there is at least one

\beta_{i}\geq M-m

\beta_{i}+m_{i}=M

Our interest in computing the

The Galois theory of linear difference equations developed in
[
vdPS97

associates with a system of linear difference equations over
\mathbb{K}(x)
a Galois group that encodes the algebraic relations among the solutions to the system. This Galois group is a linear algebraic group, that is, a group of matrices defined by a system of polynomial equations in the matrix entries. Associated with
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\in\mathbb{K}(x)^{n}
is the block-diagonal linear differential system

\sigma(Y)=\begin{pmatrix}1&f_{1}\\
0&1\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&1&f_{n}\\
&&&0&1\end{pmatrix}Y.

(8.1)

It follows from
[
HS08
, Prop. 3.1]
(see also
[
Har08
, Prop. 2.1]
) that the Galois group of (
8.1
) is the subgroup of
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}\simeq\overline{\mathbb{K}}^{n}
consisting of
(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})
such that
v_{1}\eta_{1}+\dots+v_{n}\eta_{n}=0
for every
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in V(\mathbf{f})
.

Remark 8.1
.

We emphasize that the Galois group of (
8.1
) is
not

V(\mathbf{f})
itself, as was incorrectly stated in
[
AS24
, §8]

Generalizing the theory of
[
vdPS97
]
, the differential Galois theory of difference equations developed in
[
HS08
]
associates with a system of linear difference equations over
\mathbb{K}(x)
a differential Galois group that encodes the differential-algebraic relations among the solutions to the system. This differential Galois group is a linear differential algebraic group, that is, a group of matrices defined by a system of algebraic differential equations in the matrix entries (see
[
Cas72
]
, where the study of such groups was initiated). It follows from
[

that the differential Galois group of (

The algorithmic results of the previous section are also relevant to the Galois-theoretic study of diagonal systems

&\ddots&\\
&&r_{n}\end{pmatrix}Y.

As in

9.1.
Implementation comments

Our implementation of
\mathtt{HermiteList}
differs slightly from what is literally written in Algorithm
1
in step 3, where our actual code sets

(g,f_{m+1})\leftarrow\mathtt{HermiteReduction}(g,x)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}(-1)^{m+1}(m+1)&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

which allows us to skip the computation of
m
factorials in step 6 and return the
f_{k}
directly from the loop in steps 2–5. Maple’s implementation of what we have called
\mathtt{HermiteReduction}
is contained in the more general command
\mathtt{ReduceHyperexp}
in the package
\mathtt{DEtools}
.

Our implementation of
\mathtt{SimpleReduction}
uses Maple’s
\mathtt{autodispersion}
command in the package
\mathtt{LREtools}
, then adds
1
to all the operands to the output, to create the set
S
in step 2 of Algorithm
3
. It is unsurprising, and indeed we have also verified experimentally, that this is vastly more efficient than calling on Algorithm
2
. In step 15 we had intended to call on Maple’s conversion to partial fractions
\mathtt{parfrac}
conversion procedure, whose programmer entry point form allows to input a numerator and list of factors of the denominator, and is supposed to return an output equivalent to (
2.1
). This is essential to our implementation of Algorithm
3
(see steps 15–16). However, this conversion algorithm seems to have a bug: as of this writing, Maple returns an error for
\mathtt{convert}\bigl([1,[2x-1,1],[2x+1,1]],\mathtt{parfrac},x\bigr)
, explaining that the factors in the denominator must be relatively prime. In order to work around the unreliability of Maple’s
\mathtt{parfrac}
conversion, we have applied the
\mathtt{ExtendedEuclideanAlgorithm}
command in the package
\mathtt{Algebraic}
to compute the
a_{\ell}
in step 15 of Algorithm
3
as the remainder of
a\cdot d_{\ell}
modulo
b_{\ell}
, where
a
is the numerator of
f
and
d_{\ell}
is the first Bézout coefficient in

d_{\ell}\cdot\frac{b}{b_{\ell}}+\tilde{d}_{\ell}\cdot b_{\ell}=1.
