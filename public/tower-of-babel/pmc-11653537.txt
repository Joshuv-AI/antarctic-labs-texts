Challenges in the implementation of cardio-oncology trials: lessons learnt from investigating statins in the prevention of anthracycline cardiotoxicity
Authors: John Lee, Sean Tan, Satish Ramkumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite advanced in targeted cancer therapies, anthracyclines remain essential in treating various malignancies, albeit with risks of cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD). Out of the myriad of mitigation strategies for CTRCD, statins are an attractive preventive therapy for anthracycline associated CTRCD given their widespread availability, cheap costs and added benefit of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk reduction. Recent trials of PREVENT, SPARE-HF, and STOP-CA investigated atorvastatin’s efficacy in preventing CTRCD, with mixed outcomes. While STOP-CA showed a significant reduction in CTRCD rates (22% vs. 8% in placebo), PREVENT and SPARE-HF found no statistical differences between statin and placebo groups. The trials faced challenges such as selection bias, exclusion of patients with comorbidities, and reliance on imaging-derived outcomes, which may not reflect clinically meaningful benefits. This calls for improved trial designs that prioritize diverse patient recruitment, longer follow-ups, and clinically relevant endpoints to enhance the translational impact of findings. Addressing these challenges collaboratively between oncology and cardiology will be crucial for optimizing patient outcomes in this vulnerable population. Future studies should emphasize both immediate cardioprotective effects and long-term cardiovascular benefits of statins, especially in the context of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in cancer survivors.Keywords: Cardiotoxicity, Anthracycline, Cardioprotection, Statin

Introduction

Despite recent advances in targeted oncological treatments, anthracyclines remain the backbone of numerous chemotherapy regimens in the management of solid organ and haematological malignancies. Seminal studies have demonstrated that anthracyclines are associated with left ventricular dysfunction, with proposed mechanisms of cardiotoxicity involving the inhibition of topoisomerase 2β, resulting in activation of cell death pathways and inhibition of mitochondrial biogenesis [1, 2]. The risk of cancer therapy related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) with anthracyclines is dose dependent and increases with accumulative lifetime doxorubicin equivalent doses of greater than 250mg/m2 [3]. Anthracycline-related CTRCD may manifest acutely during treatment or later in cancer survivorship, contributing to the growing cardiovascular morbidity and mortality observed among cancer survivors [4].

Numerous strategies to mitigate risks of anthracycline cardiotoxicity have been investigated. From a cardiovascular perspective, the prophylactic use of neurohormonal pathway agents (beta blockers, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) conventionally employed in the treatment of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction have yielded conflicting results [3]. Oncological strategies to minimise cardiotoxicity such as the use of dexrazoxane and liposomal anthracycline preparations, have also been studied and may have a beneficial role in cardioprotection [5]. Statins, which inhibit hydroxylmethyl-glutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) within the liver have garnered recent interest in the prevention anthracycline associated CTRCD through their anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant properties and have had a cardioprotective signal demonstrated in retrospective and registry studies [6, 7].

With safe cardiotoxicity profiles, cheap costs, widespread availability and potentially added benefits of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk reduction, statins are an attractive preventive therapy for anthracycline associated CTRCD [8]. Specific to CTRCD, they have anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant properties as the mechanism to their cardioprotective signal and may counteract the oxidative stress from anthracyclines. This cardioprotective signal has been interrogated by three main trials, PREVENT, SPARE-HF and STOP-CA [9–11]. The summary of these trials is depicted in Table 1.

The PREVENT trial recruited 279 patients treated with anthracyclines predominantly for breast cancer (85%) and a smaller cohort of lymphoma patients (15%). The use of atorvastatin 40 mg daily compared to placebo did not result in a statistically significant difference in CTRCD (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] decline of ≥ 10%) at a follow up of 24 months between both groups (both 3% respectively) [9]. The SPARE-HF trial was another double blind randomised controlled trial comparing atorvastatin 40 mg daily to placebo in 112 patients (568 screened) with a combination of solid organ (65% breast cancer) and haematological malignancies (22%). There was no significant difference in the rates of CTRCD (LVEF decline of > 10% by CMR [cardiac magnetic resonance imaging]) or LVEF between both groups [10]. The STOP-CA trial, the most recent to be published investigated the use of atorvastatin 40 mg daily compared with placebo in patients with lymphoma (73% non-Hodgkin). It was a double blind randomised controlled trial which screened 1320 patients, of which 300 were eligible for the study. After 12 months of follow up, there was a significantly higher rate of CTRCD (CTRCD was defined as an LVEF decline of > 10% by CMR) in the placebo arm compared to the statin arm (22% vs. 8%; p = 0.002) [11]. There have been three other trials (Acar et al., Nabati et al. and Kettana et al.), which recruited a low number of patients with haematological malignancies or breast cancer respectively and were randomised to statins or placebo. Their results were similar to PREVENT and SPARE-HF [12, 13]. Meta-analyses of these trials have demonstrated a significantly lower risk of developing CTRCD with statin use vs. placebo [14, 15].

Challenges with cardio-oncology trials

In the emerging field of cardio-oncology, trials like PREVENT, SPARE-HF and STOP-CA have underscored the critical need for systematic exploration of cardio-oncology topics to inform clinical practice [9–11]. Historically, patients with both cardiovascular disease and cancer were excluded from relevant trials in both fields, resulting in insufficient evidence to guide management for this growing and vulnerable patient population. These trials not only provide valuable insights into cardio-oncology research challenges but also emphasise the necessity for improved collaboration to avoid pitfalls in future studies. These challenges extend beyond statins and anthracycline associated CTRCD and could affect future trials investigating various therapeutic agents across tumour types. Addressing these issues early in trial design is crucial for enhancing clinical applicability.

A major issue highlighted in PREVENT, SPARE-HF and STOP-CA was selection bias. For example, in the STOP-CA trial, despite screening around 1600 patients over 4.5 years from 9 centres, only 300 were ultimately recruited with over 1000 patients not randomised due to patients declining participation (46%), having already taken a statin or were statin intolerant (28%) or chemotherapy regimens not containing anthracyclines (23%). The impact of this potential selection bias in recruitment is tangible, with 1 year mortality rates in STOP-CA being 2.6% compared to the approximate 4.2% annualised mortality rate seen in traditional lymphoma studies and registries [16, 17]. Similar difficulties in recruitment were seen in SPARE-HF, which required over 3 years to screen 568 patients in 5 participating centres, ultimately recruiting 112 patients. The PREVENT trial had 31 participating centres and recruited 279 patients however the total number of patients screened and recruitment duration were not reported. The selection bias may also stem from healthier, more health-conscious participants being more likely to enrol, as highlighted by the low rates of hypertension (~ 10%) and diabetes (< 1%) within the trials.

The selection bias observed in the PREVENT, SPARE-HF and STOP-CA trials reflect the challenge of recruiting representative populations into cardio-oncology trials. Patients with cancer who have progressive disease despite conventional therapy preferentially participate in oncological clinical trials to access life-saving treatments. Understandably, cardio-oncology trials with a preventive focus may be prioritised lower by treating oncologists and patients in this setting. These challenges may have resulted in longer recruitment times and the implementation of recruiting combined population of patients with solid organ and haematological malignancies may have been a measure to attain target numbers. What was observed though, was that it created significant heterogeneity in the patient cohorts in regards to their primary malignancy, chemotherapy regime and baseline comorbidities [9, 10]. Future trials addressing the cardioprotective affects of anthracycline mediated cardiotoxicity should aim to recruit patients within a specific tumour stream to homogenise baseline comorbidities, chemotherapy regimes and in turn, better interrogate the treatment effect of statins in the prevention of anthracycline associated CTRCD.

The choice of outcome measures in PREVENT, SPARE-HF and STOP-CA trials also limited clinical applicability. The potential benefits of imaging derived surrogate endpoints such as left ventricular ejection fraction or changes in left ventricular volumes are that they reduce trial follow up duration and required sample size. Furthermore, such an approach could identify early cardioprotective benefits of preventative treatments. The downsides however are that changes in imaging markers may not translate to benefits in clinically meaningful endpoints. In the STOP-CA trial there was a 1.3% difference in the reduction in left ventricular ejection from baseline to follow up (4.1% vs. 5.4%) in the statin group compared to placebo. Although statistically significant, this is unlikely to translate to a clinically meaningful difference. To improve translation to clinical practice, clinically focused outcomes such as heart failure hospitalisations or cardiac death should be considered, but this mandates larger populations or much longer follow up which can be impractical from a trial governance perspective.

Finally, most cardio-oncology trials have thus far had relative short follow up times. The PREVENT trial has the longest follow up of the statin trials with a median follow up of 24 months. Whilst the statin trials were focussed on the reduction of CTRCD during oncological treatment in the short term, there has been limited discussion about the long-term benefits of statins. Statins have a strong evidence base in primary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. As ASCVD contributes to a large burden of cardiovascular disease in cancer survivorship, there could be a potential long term benefit form statins in reducing CVD-related mortality [4, 8, 18]. Therefore, rather than purely focussing on the short-term benefits in reducing rates of CTRCD, the other potential benefits of using preventive therapies such as statins in patients with cardiovascular risk factors should be emphasised in the commentary of future trials.

Future directions

The observations made about recent statin trials in the prevention of CTR-CD highlight real-world challenges in conducting cardio-oncology trials. It is by highlighting these challenges, that we hope the community can come together to refine trial designs in the future to address some of these issues. There are several potential benefits which will result in faster translation of clinical research into clinical practice and therefore may translate into improved patient outcomes. The success of cardio-oncology trials needs ongoing engagement with the oncology community so that these important clinical trials can be conducted in unison rather than having to compete with other oncology clinical trials. Integration of trial interventions into routine clinics where cancer patients already have rigorous pathology and radiology investigations for their cancer care, would help reduce the barrier to recruitment, reduce the total burden of healthcare on the cancer patient and improve patient retainment throughout the duration of the trial. This may have further downstream effects of improving selection bias and obtaining a study cohort more reflective of current clinical practice.

Finally, it would be prudent to have trials that employ clinical endpoints but given they occur less frequently, this would mandate large numbers and longer follow up to interrogate in earnest. Unless the research question is simple enough to allow mass recruitment and long term follow up, this may be difficult to address even with industry sponsorship. Ultimately, trials will require careful and ongoing consultation and leadership from both cardiology and oncology communities to balance out the value of a specific trial design to the resources available at hand.
