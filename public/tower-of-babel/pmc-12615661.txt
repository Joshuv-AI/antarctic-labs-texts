Predictors for avascular necrosis of the femoral head in closed reduction following traction for developmental dysplasia of the hip
Authors: Tomoyuki Nakamura, Ryosuke Yamaguchi, Akifusa Wada, Chinatsu Ishikawa, Haruhisa Yanagida, Kazuyuki Takamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvascular necrosis (AVN) is a serious complication associated with the treatment of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH). In this study, we analyzed 241 affected hips from 232 patients who underwent closed reduction following traction (CR/TR) between 1995 and 2021 to identify risk factors for AVN and establish clinical strategies to reduce its incidence. All patients received four weeks of traction (horizontal, vertical, and gradual abduction) followed by closed reduction. The severity of dislocation was assessed using the International Hip Dysplasia Institute (IHDI) classification and Yamamuro’s distance a (YDA), with lower YDA values indicating more severe dislocation. AVN was evaluated on radiographs taken one to two years after reduction using Salter’s criteria. Patients were divided into AVN( +) and AVN( −) groups for demographic and radiographic comparisons. AVN developed in 15% of hips. Logistic regression analysis identified two significant risk factors: left-sided involvement (odds ratio 3.15) and insufficient horizontal traction (YDA < 8 mm during traction). Among left-sided hips, a YDA < 8 mm was associated with a 32% incidence of AVN. Achieving a YDA of ≥ 8 mm during traction is essential to reduce the risk of AVN, especially in left-sided hips. Introduction

Avascular necrosis (AVN) is one of the most serious complications encountered in the treatment of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH). This iatrogenic condition can occur following various reduction procedures1,2 and may severely affect the proximal growth plate of the femur, leading to deformities of the femoral head and neck. These deformities complicate subsequent treatment3, potentially causing limited range of motion, leg length discrepancy, and acetabular dysplasia, which can result in early-onset osteoarthritis of the hip4.

Two main theories have been proposed to explain the pathogenesis of AVN1,2. The first suggests that excessive adductor muscle tension and excessive external rotation can compress the artery supplying the epiphysis2. The second proposes that a tight reduction increases pressure on the femoral head surface, thereby compressing small blood vessels within the epiphyseal cartilage and leading to ischemia5. In both theories, soft-tissue tension around the hip is considered a key factor in AVN development. To reduce soft-tissue tension, pre-reduction traction has traditionally been used before closed reduction at several institutions, including ours. However, the necessity and effectiveness of traction prior to closed reduction remain controversial6,7. One central issue is that the specific objectives and clinical effectiveness of pre-reduction traction protocols have not been thoroughly evaluated. Accordingly, the aim of this study was to identify risk factors for AVN in patients undergoing closed reduction following traction (CR/TR), with particular attention to assessing the effectiveness of pre-reduction traction and developing strategies to minimize AVN incidence.

Material and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fukuoka Children’s Hospital (Study No. 2023–85). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the Ethics Committee of Fukuoka Children’s Hospital waived the need to obtain informed consent. Informed consent was obtained using an opt-out approach, whereby information about the study (including its purpose, methods, and data usage) was published on the hospital’s official website. Participants were provided the opportunity to decline participation by contacting the research team via the contact information listed on the website. We retrospectively reviewed the records of 573 patients with DDH who underwent initial reduction at our institution between January 1995 and December 2021 (Fig. 1). Indications for closed reduction following traction (CR/TR) included patients aged older than six months and up to three years at diagnosis, as well as those who had failed Pavlik harness treatment. Exclusion criteria included diagnosis at ≥ 3 years, successful treatment with other methods such as a Pavlik harness or open reduction (with or without osteotomy), teratogenic dislocations, and neuromuscular disorders identified during treatment. Until 2006, the upper age limit for CR/TR was < 2 years; from 2007 onward, this limit was increased to < 3 years. Initially, CR/TR was attempted for 286 hips in 273 patients; 277 hips (97%) were successfully reduced, while 9 hips could not be reduced and were excluded. Additional exclusions were: 12 hips in 10 patients with redislocation within two years, 2 hips in one patient requiring conversion to direct traction with a K-wire, 10 hips in 10 patients with follow-up of less than two years, and 12 hips in 11 patients with incomplete data. Ultimately, 241 hips in 232 patients were included in the final analysis. Patients who underwent conversion to open reduction were excluded because they may have developed AVN as a result of the surgery. The final cohort included 28 males and 204 females. Affected sides included bilateral involvement in 11 patients, right-sided in 62, and left-sided in 159. Among those with bilateral involvement, two had one side successfully reduced using a Pavlik harness. The mean age at the start of traction was 0.7 years (standard deviation [SD] 0.5; range 0.2–2.9), and the mean age at reduction was 0.8 years (SD 0.5; range 0.3–3.2). A total of 125 hips (52%) had experienced Pavlik harness failure. There were no missing data for the variables used in the final analysis; therefore, no imputation methods were required. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan.

Traction protocol using the speed trac traction system

Traction was applied using the Speed Trac traction system (ALCARE Co., Ltd. Tokyo, Japan) during a four-week hospitalization. The protocol consisted of three stages: (1) horizontal traction to lower the femoral head and release iliopsoas contracture, (2) vertical traction to relieve posterior capsule and hamstring contractures, and (3) gradual abduction traction to address adductor muscle tightness. Since 2013, Mepilex Transfer (Mölnlycke Health Care, Tokyo, Japan) has been applied to the thigh and lower leg before using the Speed Trac device, followed by elastic bandage wrapping (Fig. 2a,b). A restraining band attached to a metal plate was used to immobilize the trunk during traction (Fig. 3a,b). Horizontal traction began at 1.6 kg per side, with the weight increased daily by 200 g (Fig. 4a). When the traction weight reached 3 kg—usually after one week (Fig. 4b)—an anteroposterior (AP) X-ray was obtained while maintaining traction to assess its effectiveness. If the femoral head was not descended, the weights were increased up to a maximum of 3.5 kg. After two weeks of horizontal traction, vertical traction (Fig. 4c) followed by gradual abduction traction (Fig. 4d) were applied over the final two weeks, progressively increasing abduction to 90°.

Manual reduction and arthrography under general anesthesia

The stability of the reduced hip was assessed before performing arthrography. If reduction was unstable, an additional two weeks of horizontal traction was applied (7 hips in 7 patients). Arthrography was then conducted to determine the optimal limb position for casting. Flexion and abduction angles were carefully adjusted to avoid placing pressure on the femoral head against the thickened labrum (Fig. 5a). Following arthrography, a hip spica cast was applied, maintaining flexion between 90° and 110° and abduction between 70° and 80°, for a period of four weeks (Fig. 5b). Abduction traction effectively relieved adductor muscle contracture in most patients; however, adductor tenotomy was required in two hips of two patients because persistent tightness prevented stable reduction. Four weeks after reduction, the cast was removed, and patients transitioned to wearing an above-knee abduction brace for three months, which allowed free knee movement (Fig. 6a). This was followed by use of a Hoffman-Daimler brace for an additional three months to gradually extend the hip (Fig. 6b).

Evaluations

Pre-traction dislocation status was assessed using anteroposterior hip radiographs and classified according to the International Hip Dysplasia Institute (IHDI) classification system8. Displacement was quantified using Yamamuro’s distances a (YDA) and b (YDB)9. YDA was defined as the vertical distance from the midpoint of the proximal femoral metaphysis to the Hilgenreiner line, and YDB as the horizontal distance from the same point to the extension of the lateral margin of the ischium (Fig. 7). YDA was measured both before and during traction, whereas YDB was measured only before traction. AVN was evaluated on radiographs obtained one to two years after CR/TR, according to Salter’s criteria1. Patients were divided into AVN(+) and AVN(−) groups. The findings in the AVN(+) group included: (1) failure of the appearance of the ossific nucleus of the femoral head to appear for one year or more after reduction, (2) failure of growth in an existing ossific nucleus for one year or more after reduction, (3) broadening of the femoral neck for one year after reduction, (4) increased radiographic density of the femoral head followed by the radiographic appearance of fragmentation, and (5) residual deformity of the femoral head and neck when reossification was complete. Femoral heads without bone resorption or sclerosis, or showing temporary irregular ossification, were classified as AVN(−). Sex, age, affected side, prior treatment with a Pavlik harness, and radiographic findings were compared between the two groups.

IHDI classifications were determined using the anterolateral view of the hip X-ray before traction. The IHDI classification of the left hip was Grade 3. All radiographic measurements were performed by one observer (T.N.) during two sessions at least one month apart. Two independent observers (T.N. and C.I.) also assessed 50 randomly selected hips to assess measurement reliability. Intra- and inter-observer reliabilities were calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Intra-observer reliability was excellent for both YDA (ICC = 0.979, p < 0.001) and YDB (ICC = 0.962, p < 0.001), as was inter-observer reliability for YDA (ICC = 0.992, p < 0.001) and YDB (ICC = 0.980, p < 0.001). Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The R scripts used for these analyses are provided as supplementary data to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Correlation analyses were conducted to examine age-related differences in YDA and YDB. Univariate analyses, including chi-square tests, t-tests, and Fisher’s exact tests, were used to compare categorical and quantitative variables between the two groups. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analyses were included in multivariate logistic regression to identify independent predictors of AVN. Cut-off values for YDA were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Association between YDA/YDB and age

Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed a weak negative correlation between the YDA on the unaffected side and age before traction (r = − 0.25, 95% CI − 0.36 to − 0.12, p < 0.001). In contrast, YDA on the affected side showed a stronger negative correlation with age (r = − 0.53, 95% CI − 0.62, − 0.44, p < 0.001). No significant correlation was found between the YDB on the unaffected side and age before traction (r = − 0.05, 95% CI − 0.18 to − 0.08, p = 0.44). However, a positive correlation was observed between the YDB on the affected side and age before traction (r = 0.63, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.70, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that severe dislocation is associated with increasing age (Table 1).

Univariate analysis to explore candidate factors for AVN

AVN was observed in 36 of 241 hips (15%). Compared with the AVN( −) group, the AVN( +) group demonstrated more severe dislocation and insufficient horizontal traction. However, no differences were observed in sex, laterality, history of failed Pavlik harness treatment, epiphyseal ossification, age at traction start or reduction, counterweight, hospital stay length, follow-up period, or age at final examination (Table 2). AVN was more frequent in left-sided hips (p = 0.03) and in patients who underwent a longer duration of horizontal traction (p = 0.02). The IHDI classifications were as follows: grade 2 (45 hips), grade 3 (136 hips), and grade 4 (60 hips). The distribution of IHDI grades differed significantly between groups (p = 0.01). YDA on the affected side before traction was significantly smaller in the AVN( +) group than in the AVN( −) group, indicating a more severe dislocation (p = 0.01). There was no difference in the YDB on the affected side before traction between the two groups (p = 0.10). During horizontal traction, YDA on the affected side was significantly smaller in the AVN( +) group (p < 0.001), suggesting inadequate reduction. In contrast, there was no statistically significant difference in the YDA on the unaffected side between the two groups during traction (p = 0.10).

Multivariate analysis to identify risk factors for AVN

Multivariate analysis confirmed that left-sided involvement and a smaller YDA during traction were independent risk factors for AVN. Binomial logistic regression was performed using variables found to be statistically significant in univariate analyses (Table 3). Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and all VIF values were below 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity. The VIF for “left-sided involvement” was 1.01, confirming stability of the estimates. Two independent risk factors for AVN were identified: left-sided involvement (odds ratio [OR]: 3.15, 95% CI: 1.13–8.79, p = 0.03) and smaller YDA during traction (OR: 0.78 per mm, 95% CI: 0.67–0.91, p = 0.002). Cut-off values combining these two factors were as follows. For patients with left-sided involvement, the YDA cut-off was 7.9 mm (AUC: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.67–0.86, sensitivity: 0.645, specificity: 0.819). For patients with right-sided involvement, the YDA cut-off was 8.7 mm (AUC: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.25–0.81, sensitivity: 0.600, specificity: 0.716) (Table 4).

YDA threshold analysis

Among 63 hips with YDA < 8 mm during traction, 20 hips (32%) developed AVN, compared to 16 of 178 hips (9%) with YDA ≥ 8 mm (p < 0.001). Although the distribution of right and left hips was similar when stratified by YDA (p = 0.79), all 20 hips with YDA < 8 mm were left-sided (p = 0.01) (Table 5). Overall, 169 hips with left-sided involvement had a higher incidence of AVN (18%) compared to right-sided hips (p = 0.03). However, no significant differences between sides were observed for sex, IHDI grade before traction, age at first consultation, age at the start of traction, age at reduction, YDA and YDB before traction, or YDA during traction (Table 6). A secondary multivariate analysis using dichotomized YDA (< 8 mm vs. ≥ 8 mm) confirmed that YDA < 8 mm was significantly associated with an increased risk of AVN (OR: 3.92, 95% CI: 1.67–9.20, p = 0.002) (Table 7).

Interaction analysis between side and YDA

To assess whether the effect of YDA on AVN risk differed by laterality, a multivariable logistic regression including an interaction term (YDA < 8 mm × affected side) was performed. The interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.08), indicating that the effect of YDA on AVN risk does not differ substantially between right- and left-sided dislocations, even after adjusting for IHDI grade, duration of horizontal traction, and pre-traction YDA (Table 8).

Discussion

Avascular necrosis (AVN) is a serious complication that must be avoided when treating children with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH). AVN most commonly develops during the initial reduction phase and has been reported with various reduction methods3,10–13. Closed reduction (CR) is frequently used for infants and young children with DDH, although age limitations exist. Pre-reduction traction (TR) has been shown to reduce the risk of both re-dislocation and AVN after reduction; however, its specific clinical objectives remain unclear. In this study, we focused on the incidence of AVN in patients undergoing CR with pre-reduction traction (CR/TR). By identifying risk factors for AVN, we aimed to propose optimal traction strategies to minimize its occurrence. Our comprehensive evaluation identified two independent risk factors: left-sided involvement and a smaller YDA during traction. These findings suggest that traction prior to reduction was insufficient in AVN cases, regardless of dislocation severity or age at reduction. For cases with left-sided involvement, a reliable YDA cut-off value was established. The incidence of AVN increased significantly when YDA during traction was < 8 mm in left-sided DDH. These results may help guide clinicians in refining closed reduction strategies and emphasize the importance of achieving adequate femoral head descent before reduction.

Pre-reduction traction is thought to stretch the joint capsule and surrounding tight muscles, thereby reducing post-reduction stiffness14. While some reports suggest that pre-reduction traction may be unnecessary15,16, reduced stiffness is expected to have two important effects: (1) achieving a stable reduction14—in our series, CR/TR following this protocol yielded an initially stable reduction in 93% of patients treated before age three, and (2) preventing AVN by decreasing excessive pressure between the acetabulum and femoral head1. Despite this likely importance, no standardized protocol exists regarding traction duration or counterweight, and the impact of traction effectiveness on AVN incidence has not been fully evaluated. In this study, we assessed traction effectiveness using radiographs obtained during horizontal traction. AVN incidence after CR/TR appeared to be influenced by the degree of femoral head descent, as reflected by YDA. We recommend that when YDA is < 8 mm, additional horizontal traction with increased counterweight should be applied. The maximum counterweight used in our series was 3.5 kg per leg. If sufficient YDA cannot be achieved despite these adjustments, adductor and iliopsoas tenotomies should be considered before further traction.

During closed reduction, we confirmed both reduction stability and limb positioning to minimize impingement between the inverted labrum and the femoral head, using hip arthrography. The final limb position within the cast was determined based on arthrographic findings. The recommended “human position” is typically within the safe zone, with abduction of 40°–45° and flexion of ~ 95°1,17. applying a hip spica cast with minimal abduction risks posterior femoral head impingement against a thickened, inverted labrum, potentially leading to pseudo-reduction or re-dislocation. Therefore, in our CR/TR protocol, abduction traction was used to release adductor contractures, allowing for a cast position with relatively large abduction. While the incidence of AVN is high with positions such as the Lorenz position without pre-traction, the Lange position, or the human position outside the safe zone18,19, it may be possible to reduce AVN risk by applying sufficient traction with adequate abduction before reduction.

Previous studies have identified age at reduction3,20, epiphyseal ossification11,21, and a history of Pavlik harness treatment11,22 as AVN risk factors. However, in our study, none were significantly associated with AVN after CR/TR. Although waiting for aging3,20,23 or for epiphyseal ossification to appear9,11,21,24 has been recommended to reduce AVN risk, some studies report no clear association between AVN and either epiphyseal ossification20,25 or age at reduction26,27, leading to ongoing debate. On the other hand, delayed reduction can worsen joint congruency, increasing the need for corrective surgeries such as Salter innominate osteotomy26,28. Therefore, early reduction may promote better hip joint remodeling. It has been reported that hip stiffness or severe dislocation may occur in patients whom Pavlik harness treatment has failed9. In such cases, applying traction before closed reduction could help relieve stiffness. Our findings suggest that effective traction can overcome these stiffness-related factors and reduce AVN risk, supporting earlier research that underscores the importance of appropriate pre-reduction preparation. Additionally, our data highlight the predictive value of YDA—a factor rarely emphasized in the literature.

Left-sided DDH is more common29, likely due to intrauterine positioning that limits left hip abduction. Whether left-sided dislocation confers higher AVN risk remains uncertain. In our study, although AVN was more frequent in left-sided cases, no demographic differences were found between right- and left-sided patients. Unmeasured factors—such as anatomical, vascular, or mechanical differences, or the severity of periarticular contracture—may contribute. Further research should evaluate YDA during manual traction before treatment and quantify adductor tightness via abduction limitation measurements. In an additional multivariate analysis using YDA dichotomized at 8 mm, hips with YDA < 8 mm had a significantly higher AVN risk (OR: 3.92, 95% CI: 1.67–9.20, p = 0.002). Interaction analysis revealed no significant effect between side and YDA, suggesting that low YDA similarly increases AVN risk in both hips. These findings underscore the importance of confirming sufficient femoral head descent during traction. For borderline IHDI Grade III/IV cases, our results suggest pre-reduction traction remains valuable. YDA measurement during traction could guide clinical decision-making in such cases.

Recent studies have highlighted the utility of advanced imaging techniques, particularly MRI, in evaluating acetabular and femoral head development and in predicting future acetabular morphology and long-term hip function in DDH patients. Tetsunaga et al. demonstrated that age at reduction and initial radiographic parameters are significant predictors of residual acetabular dysplasia after closed reduction30. Furthermore, our own longitudinal MRI study showed that childhood cartilage CE angle measurements can accurately predict acetabular morphology at skeletal maturity, supporting the value of early imaging-based evaluations for long-term hip preservation and informing early surgical decision-making31. Incorporating these imaging findings into clinical practice may further refine traction protocols and optimize long-term outcomes in children with DDH. The higher AVN risk in left-sided involvement may be related to intrauterine positioning, which limits abduction of the left hip, or to subtle side-to-side differences in vascular anatomy. These findings suggest that, in bilateral DDH, traction protocols could be standardized using the same YDA threshold for both hips to avoid asymmetrical outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it focused exclusively on patients who underwent CR/TR, which may have introduced selection bias. We excluded 21 hips that required open reduction (9 due to failed CR/TR, 12 due to redislocation within two years), of which 13 showed femoral head deformities, including AVN in 6 hips and coxa magna in 7 hips. This exclusion may have influenced AVN incidence. Second, AVN assessment bias is possible; longer follow-up often leads to smaller samples due to dropout or relocation. We used Salter’s criteria1, assessed 1–2 years after reduction, which are widely accepted, though not all AVN cases progress to deformities requiring surgery. Third, factors such as periarticular contracture severity (including that of the adductor muscles and capsule), variations in vascular supply following reduction, and the hip abduction angle after reduction may influence AVN development. These factors were not analyzed in this study and warrant further investigation. Fourth, the retrospective nature of this study may have introduced recall or documentation bias, potentially limiting generalizability to other settings with different treatment protocols. Fifth, the relatively low AUC (0.53) for right-sided hips indicates limited discriminatory ability of YDA in this subgroup, suggesting that the protective threshold of YDA ≥ 8 mm may not be equally applicable to all patients and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the relatively short follow-up may not capture late AVN-related deformities; long-term follow-up to skeletal maturity is needed. Despite these limitations, the key finding remains that achieving YDA ≥ 8 mm through sufficient traction is crucial for reducing AVN risk. Regardless of specific protocols, adequately lowering the femoral head to release periarticular contracture should be emphasized. Comparative studies of traction protocols may help determine generalizability.

Future directions

Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate the YDA threshold across different institutions and to examine whether modifications to traction protocols can further reduce AVN risk. Incorporating advanced imaging modalities, such as MRI, into pre-reduction assessment may also refine predictive accuracy.

Conclusion

AVN occurred more frequently in left-sided hips, and YDA < 8 mm during traction was associated with higher AVN risk. Special attention should be given to left-sided involvement when planning traction before CR. Achieving YDA ≥ 8 mm is important for both left- and right-sided DDH. Radiographic YDA assessment during traction is a practical, effective target for ensuring adequate traction and improving long-term hip preservation outcomes.
