Multiplicative self-decomposition of the exponential and gamma distributions
Mohamed Erraoui, José Luís da Silva

Abstract
Multiplicative self-decomposable laws describe random variables that can be decomposed into a product of a scaled-down version of themselves and an independent residual term. Shanbhag et al. (1977) have shown that the gamma distribution is multiplicative self-decomposable (in particular, the exponential distribution). As a result, they established the multiplicative self-decomposability of the absolute value of a centered normal random variable. A limitation of Shanbhag’s result is that the distribution of the residual component is not explicitly identified. In this paper, we aim to fill this gap by providing an explicit distribution of the residual term. In more detail, the residual term follows an M M -Wright distribution in the case of the exponential distribution, whereas for the gamma distribution it follows a Fox H H -function distribution. This, in turn, enables us to identify the distribution of the residual term of the absolute value of a centered normal random variable as a Wright distribution. Introduction

A real random variable
X
with distribution (or law)
\mu
is said to be self-decomposable
(cf.
[
Sat99
, Definition 15.1]
) if for any constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
there exists an independent random variable (called residual term), say
X_{\alpha}
, such
that

X\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\alpha X+X_{\alpha}.

(1)

The symbol
\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}
here and below means equality in law. Specifically, the characteristic function
\hat{\mu}(z):=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zX}]
satisfies

\hat{\mu}(z)=\hat{\mu}(\alpha z)\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}(z),

where
\mu_{\alpha}
is the distribution of
X_{\alpha}
. The notion of self-decomposable distributions goes back to P. Lévy and A. Ya. Khintchine. Classical examples of distributions that are self-decomposable are Gaussian distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, gamma distributions, and Cauchy distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}
. We note that self-decomposable distributions are an important subclass of infinitely divisible laws and have the property of unimodal associated densities; see
[
Sat99
, p. 404]
.

In the paper
[
SS77
]
, the authors established the self-decomposability of
the random variable
\log(Z)
(resp.
\log(|U|)
, where
Z
is a gamma (resp.
U
is a centered normal) random variable.
This is equivalent to considering the “multiplicative” self-decomposability
of
Z
, expressed as follows: for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have

Z\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}Z^{\alpha}Z_{\alpha},

(2)

where
Z_{\alpha}
is an independent residual term. In the particular case where
Z
has an exponential distribution, the random variable
Z_{\alpha}
takes the form

Y_{\alpha}^{-\alpha}
, with
Y_{\alpha}
having a stable one-sided
distribution with parameter
\alpha
; see Eq. (2) in
[
SS77
]
.
One key element missing from Shanbhag’s work is the explicit identification of the distribution of the residual term.

Our contribution in this paper is to fill this gap. To this end, we use the Mellin transform,
a tool adapted to study the product of independent random variables rather than the characteristic function used in Shanbhag’s work.
Indeed, the Mellin transform of a product of two independent random variables is the product of their Mellin transforms; therefore, the Mellin transform of the residual term is simply the ratio of the Mellin transform of the original distribution to its reduced version. The main challenge is identifying the distribution of this resulting function.

First, we show that the density of the residual term
Z_{\alpha}
can be expressed in terms of the
M
-Wright function in the exponential case (cf. Theorem
2.2
), whereas for the gamma distribution, it is expressed as a Fox
H
-function; see Theorem
3.1
below. For the absolute value of a centered normal random variable, the density distribution of
Z_{\alpha}
is expressed using the Wright function; see Theorem
4.1
. Furthermore, we emphasize that the decomposition given in (
2
), involving the aforementioned residual terms, provides a characterization of these distributions; see Propositions
2.4
,
3.2
, and
4.2
below.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the definition and some properties of the Mellin transform that will be necessary in subsequent discussions. In addition, the
M
-Wright density function and its key properties are reviewed. It will be the density of the residual term in the self-decomposition of the exponential random variable; cf. Theorem
2.2
. We show that the self-decomposition (
7
), where the residual term follows an
M
-Wright density, characterizes the exponential distributions; see Proposition
2.4
. Section
3
addresses the gamma case, where we show that the residual term has a density involving a Fox
H
-function; see Theorem
3.1
and Proposition
3.2
. In Section
4
, as a consequence of the result of Section
3
, we express the density of the residual term in the self-decomposition of the absolute value of a centered normal random variable as a Wright function.

2
Multiplicative self-decomposition of the exponential distribution

To start, we recall our basic tool, the Mellin transform, and some of its properties. For any sufficiently well-behaved function
f:\mathbb{R^{+}}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
the Mellin transform of
f
is defined by

(\mathcal{M}f)(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)x^{z-1}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

(3)

whenever the integral converges.
The integral (
3
) defines the Mellin transform in a vertical strip in the
z
-plane whose boundaries are determined by the analytic structure of
f(x)
as
x\to 0^{+}
and
x\to+\infty
.
For any function with the following asymptotic behavior

f(x)=\begin{cases}O\left(x^{-a+\varepsilon}\right)&\text{as }x\to 0^{+},\\
O\left(x^{-b-\varepsilon}\right)&\text{as }x\to+\infty,\end{cases}

for every (small)
\varepsilon>0
and
a<b
, the integral (
3
) converges absolutely and defines an analytic function in the strip
a<\Re(z)<b
. This strip is known as
strip of analyticity
of
(\mathcal{M}f)(\cdot)
.

As discussed earlier, the Mellin transform is a fundamental tool in probability theory. Its primary application lies in characterizing the distributions of products of independent random variables via the Mellin convolution formula

(f\star g)(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)g(y)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{y}=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(y)g\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{y},

(4)

whenever the integral exists. To illustrate, given two independent random variables
X
with density
\rho_{X}
and
Y
with density
\rho_{Y}
, the density
\rho_{XY}
of the product
XY
is given in terms of the Mellin convolution, that is,
\rho_{XY}=\rho_{X}\star\rho_{Y}
, see, for example,
[
Zol57
,
Spr79
]
. This is achieved through the following property: Let
f,g
be given so that
f\star g
is well-defined, then

(\mathcal{M}(f\star g))(z)=(\mathcal{M}f)(z)(\mathcal{M}g)(z).

Let
0<\beta<1
be fixed. A positive random variable
Y_{\beta}
is said to follow an
M
-Wright distribution if its distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, with a density function given by

M_{\beta}(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-t)^{n}}{n!\Gamma(-\beta n+1-\beta)},\quad t\geq 0.

(5)

The density
M_{\beta}
is called the
M
-Wright function (or Mainardi function) and was introduced by Mainardi in
[
Mai93
]
as the Green function of certain types of time-fractional differential equations; see
[
MPG03
,
Pag13
]
and references therein.
The Laplace transform of the density
M_{\beta}
is the Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\beta}
, that is,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-st}M_{\beta}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=E_{\beta}(-s):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-s)^{n}}{\Gamma(n\beta+1)},\quad s\geq 0.

(6)

For certain specific values of the parameter
\beta
, the associated random variable
Y_{\beta}
can be identified:

•

for
\beta=1/2
,
Y_{1/2}
coincides with the one-sided Gaussian random variable with density
M_{1/2}(t)=\pi^{-1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/4}
,

•

for
\beta=1/3
, the density
M_{1/3}
of
Y_{1/3}
is given

M_{1/3}(t)=3^{2/3}\operatorname{Ai}\left(\frac{t}{3^{1/3}}\right),

where
\operatorname{Ai}
denotes the
Airy function
; see for example Eq. (F.17) in
[
Mai10
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

The exponential random variable, denoted by
Y_{0}
, can be regarded as a limiting case of the family
Y_{\beta}
as
\beta\to 0^{+}
. This offers an alternative interpretation of Cressie’s result (see
[
Cre75
]
and also
[
DuM71
]
) and differs from the one presented in Shanbhag et al.
[
SS77
]
. It represents a weak continuity result.

This section provides a multiplicative self-decomposition of the exponential random
variable
Y_{0}
in terms of the random variables
Y_{\beta}
with
0<\beta<1
.

The following theorem yields the desired self-decomposition, which can also be regarded as an extension of Eq. (6.4) in
[
MPG03
]
to the case
\eta=0
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For any
0<\beta<1
, the following self-decomposition holds

Y_{0}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}Y_{0}^{\beta}Y_{\beta}.

(7)

Proof.

It is a well-known fact that
Y_{0}^{\beta}
has a Weibull distribution with parameter
1/\beta
and density
g_{\beta}(x)=\frac{1}{\beta}x^{1/\beta-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{1/\beta}}
,
x\geq 0
. The probability density of the product of two independent random variables is given by the Mellin convolution of the corresponding densities of the two variables. Hence the probability density
\rho_{\beta}
of
Y_{0}^{\beta}Y_{\beta}
is expressed as

\rho_{\beta}(x)=(M_{\beta}\star g_{\beta})(x),\quad x\geq 0.

(8)

It is not difficult to show that, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(z)>0
, the Mellin transform of
g_{\beta}
is

(\mathcal{M}g_{\beta})(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{z-1}g_{\beta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\Gamma(\beta(z-1)+1),

and the Mellin transform of
M_{\beta}
is given by the generalized
moments of
M_{\beta}
, that is,

(\mathcal{M}M_{\beta})(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{z-1}M_{\beta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\Gamma(z)}{\Gamma(\beta(z-1)+1)}.

Thus, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(z)>0
, we have

(\mathcal{M}\rho_{\beta})(z)=(\mathcal{M}M_{\beta})(z)(\mathcal{M}g_{\beta})(z)=\frac{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(\beta(z-1)+1)}{\Gamma(\beta(z-1)+1)}=\Gamma(z).

On the other hand, the Mellin transform of the exponential distribution
is
\Gamma(z)
,
\Re(z)>0
. Therefore, by the uniqueness of the Mellin transform
(cf. Corollary 5 in
[
BJ97
]
), the claim follows.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

An alternative method for proving this is to show that the Laplace transform
of the density
\rho_{\beta}
matches the Laplace transform of the
exponential density with the help of (
6
) and the formula

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x}E_{\beta}(-x^{\beta}t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{1+t},\quad\beta\geq 0,\;t\geq 0,

see, for example, Eq. (E.51) in
[
Mai10
]
.

The following proposition shows that the self-decomposition (
7
) provides a characterization of the exponential distribution.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
Y
be a random variable with a continuous probability density
\rho_{Y}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. If
Y
satisfies (
7
) for some
\beta\in(0,1)
, then

Y\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}Y_{0}.

Proof.

A simple calculation leads to the fact that the Mellin transform
(\mathcal{M}\rho_{Y})
satisfies

(\mathcal{M}\rho_{Y})(z)=\frac{\Gamma(z)}{\Gamma(\beta(z-1)+1)}(\mathcal{M}\rho_{Y})(\beta(z-1)+1),\quad\Re(z)>0.

(9)

Set
h(s):=\dfrac{(\mathcal{M}\rho_{Y})(z)}{\Gamma(z)}
,
\Re(z)>0
. From Eq. (
9
), we infer that
h
satisfies the following functional equation

h(z)=h(\beta z+(1-\beta)),\quad\Re(z)>0.

By iterating the functional equation, we conclude that for any integer
n\geq 2
, we have

h(z)=h\left(\beta^{n}z+(1-\beta)\sum_{k=0}^{k=n-1}\beta^{k}\right),\quad\Re(z)>0.

Given the continuity of
h
, as
n
approaches infinity, we obtain
h(z)=h(1)=1
, for every
\Re(z)>0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

3
Multiplicative self-decomposition of the gamma distribution

Let
r>0
be given and
Z_{r}
be a gamma random variable with density
\rho_{Z_{r}}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(r)}t^{r-1}e^{-t}
,
t>0
.
The multiplicative self-decomposition of
Z_{r}
uses the so-called Fox
H
-density function, which we introduce now. According to a standard notation (see
[
KS04
]
for more details), the
H
-functions were introduced
by Fox in
[
Fox61
]
and are defined by the Mellin–Barnes type integral with the integrand containing products and quotients of the Euler gamma functions. Examples include Wright’s generalized hypergeometric functions,
M
-Wright functions, Meijer
G
-functions, and the exponential function, among others. We consider the particular case of the Fox
H
-functions
H^{1,0}_{1,1}
with representation
is given by

H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(z\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathscr{L}}\mathcal{H}_{1,1}^{1,0}(s)z^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad 0<\alpha<1,

(10)

where

\mathcal{H}_{1,1}^{1,0}(s):=\frac{\Gamma(r-1+s)}{\Gamma(r-\alpha+\alpha s)},

and
\mathscr{L}
is a suitable contour in
\mathbb{C}
,
z^{-s}=\exp(-s\log|z|+\mathrm{i}\arg(z))
,

\log|z|
represents the natural logarithm of
|z|
and
\arg(z)

is not necessarily the principal value.

Since
a^{*}=\Delta=1-\alpha>0
, Theorems 1.2 and 2.2 in
[
KS04
]
assert that the contour
\mathscr{L}
in (
10
) may be chosen as a left-oriented loop within a horizontal strip, beginning at the location
-\infty+\mathrm{i}
and ending at
-\infty-\mathrm{i}
. Moreover, the Fox
H
-function defined in (
10
) is an analytic
function on
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
and is expressed as follows

H_{1,1}^{1,0}(z)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\text{Res}_{s=b_{l}}\left[\mathcal{H}_{1,1}^{1,0}(s)z^{-s}\right]=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\text{Res}_{s=b_{l}}\left[\frac{\Gamma(r-1+s)}{\Gamma(r-\alpha+\alpha s)}z^{-s}\right],

where
b_{l}=-l+1-r
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
, are the poles of the function

\Gamma(r-1+\cdot)
. It is easy to see that, for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

\text{Res}_{s=b_{l}}\left[\frac{\Gamma(r-1+s)}{\Gamma(r-\alpha+\alpha s)}z^{-s}\right]=\frac{(-1)^{l}}{l!}\frac{z^{l+r-1}}{\Gamma\left(r(1-\alpha)-\alpha l\right)}.

This leads to the following series expansion of the Fox
H
-function in (
10
)

H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(z\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right)=z^{r-1}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{l}}{l!\Gamma\left(r(1-\alpha)-\alpha l\right)}z^{l}.

We note that the above series representation can be written in terms of the well-known Wright function
W_{\lambda,\mu}
(see
[
Wri40
]
or
[
Mai10
]
) as

H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(z\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right)=z^{r-1}W_{-\alpha,r(1-\alpha)}(-z).

On the other hand, the Laplace transform of
W_{-\alpha,r(1-\alpha)}(\cdot)
is given by the generalized Mittag-Leffler function (see
[
Mai10
, Eq. (F.25)]
), that is,

\big(\mathcal{L}W_{-\alpha,r(1-\alpha)}(-\cdot)\big)(s)=E_{\alpha,r(1-\alpha)+\alpha}(-s),\;s>0.

(11)

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it was shown by Schneider
[
Sch96
]
that
E_{\alpha,r(1-\alpha)+\alpha}(-s)
is completely monotone. Hence, the characterization of a completely monotonic function implies that
W_{-\alpha,r(1-\alpha)}(\cdot)
is nonnegative on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, which in turn ensures that
H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(s\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right)
is also nonnegative on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
.

The Mellin transform of
H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(s\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right)
can be computed with the help of Theorem 2.2 in
[
KS04
]
, valid on the open right half-plane
\Re(z)>1-r
, as

\mathcal{M}H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(\cdot\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right)(z)=\mathcal{H}_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(z\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(r-1+z)}{\Gamma(r-\alpha+\alpha z)}.

(12)

Taking
z=1
we can see that
H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(s\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right)
is normalized. Thus, we may conclude that
H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(s\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right)
is indeed a density function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
Y_{\alpha,r}
be a positive random variable (independent of
Z_{r}
) with density
\rho_{Y_{\alpha,r}}
given by

\rho_{Y_{\alpha,r}}(t)=H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(t\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(r-\alpha,\alpha)}{(r-1,1)}\right),\quad t>0.

(13)

With this, we state the self-decomposition of the random variable
Z_{r}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the following self-decomposition holds

Z_{r}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}Z_{r}^{\alpha}Y_{\alpha,r}.

(14)

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be given. We show that the Mellin transforms on each side of (
14
) are equal, and by the uniqueness of the Mellin transform (cf. Corollary 5 in
[
BJ97
]
)
the claim is proved. In fact, the Mellin transforms of the densities
\rho_{Z_{r}}
and
\rho_{Z_{r}^{\alpha}}
are well-defined for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(z)>1-r
and are given by

\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z_{r}}(z)=\frac{\Gamma(r-1+z)}{\Gamma(r)}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z_{r}^{\alpha}}(z)=\frac{\Gamma(r-\alpha+\alpha z)}{\Gamma(r)}.

Since the random variables
Z_{r}^{\alpha}
and
Y_{\alpha,r}
are independent, it follows that

\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z_{r}^{\alpha}Y_{\alpha,r}}(z)=\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z_{r}^{\alpha}}(z)\mathcal{M}\rho_{Y_{\alpha,r}}(z)

leading to

\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z_{r}}(z)=\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z_{r}^{\alpha}Y_{\alpha,r}}(z),

on the open right half-plane
\Re(z)>1-r
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We will show that Eq. (
14
) characterizes the gamma distributions.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
Z
be a random variable with continuous probability density
\rho_{Z}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. If
Z
satisfies (
14
) for a pair
(r,\alpha)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,1)
, then

Z\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}Z_{r}.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
\Re(z)>1-r
, this will be an assumption throughout our proof. Computing the Mellin transform
\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z}
we obtain

(\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z})(z)=\frac{\Gamma(r-1+z)}{\Gamma(r-\alpha+\alpha z)}(\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z})(\alpha(z-1)+1).

(15)

Set
h(z):=(\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z})(z)/\Gamma(z-1+r)
. Then, Eq. (
15
) gives rise to the following functional equation

h(1+z)=h(1+\alpha z).

By iterating this equation, we obtain

h(1+z)=h(1+\alpha^{n}z),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Letting
n\to\infty
and using the continuity of
h
, yields

h(1+z)=h(1)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(r)},

and by analyticity
h
is constant on the half-plane
\Re(z)>1-r
. Thus, we conclude that

(\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z})(z)=\frac{\Gamma(z+r-1)}{\Gamma(r)}=(\mathcal{M}\rho_{Z_{r}})(z),\quad\Re(z)>1-r.

This shows that the Mellin transform of
Z
is the same as that of the gamma random variable
Z_{r}
with parameter
r>0
. Therefore, due to the uniqueness of the Mellin transform, the claim of the proposition is proven.
∎

4
On the multiplicative self-decomposition of the Gaussian distribution

Shanbhag et al.
[
SS77
]
showed that the absolute value of a standard normal distribution possesses the property of self-decomposability. However, the specific random variable involved in the self-decomposition was not identified in their work. In this section, we identify the random variable as a consequence of the self-decomposition of the gamma distribution.

Let
U=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
be a centered normal random variable.
Since
U^{2}/2
has a gamma distribution with parameter

1/2
, then according to Theorem
3.1
, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have the self-decomposition (
14
)
for
U^{2}/2
, that is,

U^{2}/2\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\left(U^{2}/2\right)^{\alpha}Y_{\alpha,1/2},

with the density of is given by

\rho_{Y_{\alpha,1/2}}(t)=H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(t\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(1/2-\alpha,\alpha)}{(-1/2,1)}\right),\quad t>0.

Hence, it is easy to see that

\left|U\right|\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\left|U\right|^{\alpha}\,2^{(1-\alpha)/2}Y_{\alpha,1/2}^{1/2}.

Now, with a simple calculation, we deduce that the random variable
X_{\alpha}:=2^{(1-\alpha)/2}Y_{\alpha,1/2}^{1/2}
has the density function

\rho_{X_{\alpha}}(t)=2^{\alpha}\,t\,H_{1,1}^{1,0}\left(2^{1-\alpha}\,t^{2}\,\middle|\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{(1/2-\alpha,\alpha)}{(-1/2,1)}\right),\quad t>0.

The Taylor series of
\rho_{X_{\alpha}}
is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{X_{\alpha}}(t)

\displaystyle=2^{\alpha}\,t\,\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Res}_{t=-l+1/2}\frac{\Gamma(-1/2+t)}{\Gamma(1/2-\alpha+\alpha t)}\left(2^{\alpha-1}t^{2}\right)^{-s}

\displaystyle=2^{\alpha}\,t\,\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{l!}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1/2-\alpha+\alpha(-l+1/2))}\left(2^{\alpha-1}t^{2}\right)^{l-1/2}

\displaystyle=2^{(1+\alpha)/2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{l!}\frac{(-2^{\alpha-1}t^{2})^{l}}{\Gamma(-\alpha l+(1-\alpha)/2)}

\displaystyle=2^{(1+\alpha)/2}W_{-\alpha,(1-\alpha)/2}(-2^{\alpha-1}t^{2}).

We summarize the above results as follows:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
U
be a centered normal random variable, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
be given. The following self-decomposition holds

|U|\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}|U|^{\alpha}X_{\alpha},

(16)

where the random variable
X_{\alpha}
is independent of
U
and has a density function given by

\rho_{X_{\alpha}}(t)=2^{(\alpha+1)/2}W_{-\alpha,(1-\alpha)/2}(-2^{\alpha-1}t^{2}),\quad t>0.

Now we show that (
16
) characterizes the Gaussian distribution. To this end, we need the Mellin transforms of
\rho_{|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|}
and
\rho_{X_{\alpha}}
. Using a change of variable formula, we obtain

\mathcal{M}\rho_{|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|}(s)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\exp\left(-t^{2}/2\right)\mathrm{d}t=\frac{2^{s/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right).

(17)

From this, we deduce the Mellin transform of
|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|^{\alpha}
, that is,

\mathcal{M}\rho_{|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|^{\alpha}}(s)=\mathcal{M}\rho_{|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|}(\alpha(s-1)+1)=\frac{2^{(\alpha(s-1)+1)/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha(s-1)+1}{2}\right).

Therefore, we obtain the Mellin transform of
\rho_{X_{\alpha}}
via (
16
), namely

\mathcal{M}\rho_{X_{\alpha}}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{M}\rho_{|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\rho_{|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|}(\alpha(s-1)+1)}=2^{\frac{s-1}{2}(1-\alpha)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha(\frac{s-1}{2})\right)}.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
U
be a symmetric random variable with
continuous probability density
\rho_{U}
on
\mathbb{R}
. If
U

satisfies (
16
) for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then

U\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Proof.

Using the independence of
|U|
and
X_{\alpha}
in (
16
), the Mellin transform of the density
\rho_{|U|}
satisfies

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}\rho_{|U|})(s)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{M}\rho_{|U|})(\alpha(s-1)+1)(\mathcal{M}\rho_{X_{\alpha}})(s)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{M}\rho_{|U|})(\alpha(s-1)+1)2^{\frac{s-1}{2}(1-\alpha)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha(\frac{s-1}{2})\right)}.

Let us define the continuous function
h
by

h(s):=\frac{(\mathcal{M}\rho_{|U|})(s)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s-1}{2}\right)2^{\frac{s-1}{2}}},\quad s>0,

then the above equality yields

h(s)=\frac{(\mathcal{M}\rho_{|U|})(s)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s-1}{2}\right)2^{\frac{s-1}{2}}}=\frac{(\mathcal{M}\rho_{|U|})(\alpha(s-1)+1)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha(\frac{s-1}{2})\right)2^{\alpha\frac{s-1}{2}}}=h(\alpha(s-1)+1).

Hence, we have

h(s)=h(1-\alpha+\alpha s),\quad s>0,

and by iteration, for any integer
n\geq 2
, we obtain

h(s)=h\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha^{k}(1-\alpha)+\alpha^{n}s\right)\to h(1)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}},\;n\to\infty.

Thus

(\mathcal{M}\rho_{|U|})(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)2^{\frac{s}{2}}.

The right-hand side is the Mellin transform of the standard normal distribution; cf. Eq. (
17
).
Due to the uniqueness of the Mellin transform and the fact that
U

is symmetric, we obtain

U\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\mathcal{N}(0,1).\qed

5
Discussion and conclusion

We have identified the density distributions of the residual terms in the self-decomposition given in Shanbhag et al.
[
SS77
]
, thereby strengthening the results of this work. These densities are expressed through special functions, precisely Wright’s functions. In the special case of the exponential distribution, the density is expressed by an
M
-Wright function. For the gamma distribution and the absolute value of a normal distribution, the density is given by a Fox
H
-function, which can, in turn, be represented using a Wright function. In addition, we show that all self-decompositions characterize the corresponding distributions.
