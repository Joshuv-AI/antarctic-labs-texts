OS odontoideum in an achondroplastic adult – A case report and review
Authors: Reza Mollahouseini, Abolfazl Rahimizadeh, Walter Wiliamson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Achondroplasia is a rare genetic disorder affecting bone development, resulting in disproportionate short stature. The coexistence of os odontoideum (OO) in individuals with achondroplasia is exceptionally uncommon.Case Description: We present the case of a 29-year-old female with achondroplasia who experienced progressive weakness in the extremities, ultimately leading to difficulty in ambulation. Radiographic evaluation revealed an atlantoaxial dislocation secondary to an unstable OO. Surgical stabilization of C1–C2 resulted in gradual yet significant neurological recovery.Conclusion: Since the first reported case in 1974, only a limited number of achondroplastic individuals with atlantoaxial dislocation due to OO have been documented.

INTRODUCTION

Achondroplasia represents the most prevalent form of short-limbed dwarfism, occurring in approximately 1 in 10,000–30,000 live births. It is inherited as an autosomal-dominant trait; however, nearly 90% of cases result from de novo mutations in the fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 gene.[1,3-5,8] Os odontoideum (OO) is defined as an ossicle with smooth circumferential cortical margins representing the odontoid process that has no osseous continuity with the body of the C2 vertebrae.[5,6]

The occurrence of unstable OO in individuals with achondroplasia is exceedingly rare.[1,3-5,8] We report a case exemplifying this unusual association.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 29-year-old female with achondroplasia presented with an 8-month history of gait instability, progressing to complete bedridden status over the preceding 3 months [Figure 1]. Neurological assessment revealed spastic quadriparesis. Plain and dynamic cervical radiographs demonstrated OO with reducible atlantoaxial dislocation [Figure 2]. Computed tomography confirmed the diagnosis [Figure 3]. Surgical intervention involved C1–C2 stabilization using bilateral translaminar C2 screws and sublaminar C1 hooks [Figure 4]. Postoperatively, the patient exhibited steady and substantial neurological improvement.

DISCUSSION

Achondroplasia is the most common skeletal dysplasia leading to short-limbed dwarfism. Clinical features include disproportionate short stature, macrocephaly, limb shortening, saddle nose deformity, and trident hand, all attributable to defective endochondral ossification.[2,7]

OO, defined as a separate ossicle with smooth margins and no continuity with the axis, may present asymptomatically or with severe instability.[6] The coexistence of unstable OO in achondroplastic individuals is exceptionally rare.[1,3-5,8] Putting our case together with those 6 from the literature include four males and three females, averaging 14.8 years of age [Table 1].

Clinical picture

Both conditions can compromise the cervicomedullary junction through distinct mechanisms.[1,2-6,8] In infants and young children with achondroplasia, cervicomedullary compression at the foramen magnum often manifests as sleep apnea or sudden death.[2,5] In adults, progressive quadriparesis is the predominant feature, typically due to foramen magnum stenosis.[2,5] Conversely, unstable OO may lead to atlantoaxial dislocation and subsequent myelopathy.[1,3-6,8] Progressive paraparesis is another specific feature in achondroplastic adults, which is usually due to thoracolumbar stenosis and rarely secondary to ossification of the yellow ligament.[7]

Diagnosis

Diagnostic evaluation includes dynamic cervical radiographs to assess reducibility, computed tomography for detailed bony anatomy, and magnetic resonance imaging to evaluate spinal cord compression and signal changes indicative of myelopathy.[1,3-5,8]

Treatment

Surgical stabilization is imperative in cases of instability.[1,3-6,8] The C1 lateral mass–C2 pedicle screw construct (Harms technique) offers optimal stability.[1,3-6,8] Alternative techniques, such as C2 translaminar screws combined with C1 lateral mass screw or C1 laminar hooks, provide comparatively less biomechanical strength.[1,3-6,8]

CONCLUSION

The coexistence of OO and achondroplasia is an exceptionally rare clinical entity, particularly in adults. Instability associated with OO may exacerbate cervicomedullary compromise in these patients. Comprehensive surgical management, including C1–C2 fixation and, when indicated, foramen magnum decompression, is essential for favorable outcomes.
