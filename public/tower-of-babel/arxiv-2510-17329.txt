The first positive position of a lattice random walk
Claude Godrèche, Jean-Marc Luck

Abstract
The distribution of the first positive position reached by a random walker starting at the origin is central to the analysis of extremes and records in one-dimensional random walks. In this work, we present a detailed and self-contained analytical study of this distribution for symmetric finite-range lattice walks, whose steps are drawn from a distribution supported on finitely many integers.

1
Introduction

We consider a random walker on the one-dimensional lattice,
whose integer position at discrete time
n
is given by

x_{n}=x_{n-1}+\eta_{n}.

(1.1)

The walker is launched from the origin (
x_{0}=0
).
The steps
\eta_{n}
are independent, identically distributed (iid) integer random variables drawn from an even distribution
supported on finitely many integers:

\mathbb{P}(\eta_{n}=k)=\rho_{k}\qquad(k=-K,\dots,K),

(1.2)

with
\rho_{k}=\rho_{-k}
.
The integer
K\geq 1
is called the range of the step distribution.
We assume that
\rho_{1}
is non-zero,
i.e., that steps with unit length are allowed.
This hypothesis will allow us to discard non-generic behavior.
The probability
\rho_{0}
for the walker to stop at every time step may either be zero or not.

The main subject of this paper is the distribution of the first positive position of the walker, denoted by
H
.
This distribution is fundamental for understanding the statistics of successive record positions, or ‘ladder heights’,
in one-dimensional random walks
1
1

1

See
[
1
]
for a review on records for random walks and Lévy flights.
.
The present work is a direct continuation of our recent comprehensive investigation
of the same quantity in continuous symmetric random walks
[
2
]
.
The latter provides a detailed account of the subject, including its history,
and sets key observables in perspective with one another.
Most of the bibliographic references cited therein will not be repeated here.

In the present situation, namely symmetric finite-range lattice walks,
the Wiener-Hopf equations governing the problem can be solved analytically,
without having to resort to the general formalism.
The essence of the method is to factorise the relevant analytic functions over their complex zeros and poles.
This approach was put forward long ago by Wick
[
3
]
and Chandrasekhar
[
4
]

for Wiener-Hopf equations of the kind encountered in continuous random walks (see
[
5
]
for a review).
In that setting, the method works whenever the step distribution is a finite superposition of decaying exponentials,
so that its Laplace transform is a rational function.
The possibility of applying a similar approach for finite-range lattice walks
is mentioned in Feller’s book
[
6
, Ch. XII]
.
The method has been rediscovered and given the name ‘algebraic trick’
in a study of the crossings of a biased simple random walker with a ballistic obstacle
moving at rational velocity
[
7
]
.

In continuity with the above, the case of general symmetric finite-range lattice walks is investigated
in the present work, with the following setup.
Section
2
presents a self-contained derivation
of explicit expressions for all fundamental quantities of interest,
including the distribution of the first positive position of the walker (section
2.5
)
and its moments (section
2.6
).
In section
3
we examine the simplest examples of walks whose range is either 1 or 2.
The case of a discrete uniform step distribution with an arbitrary range
K

is then studied in section
4
.
A special emphasis is put on the regime where the range becomes large.
The asymptotic expressions of various quantities are compared with their counterparts
for continuous random walks with a symmetric uniform step length distribution.
Section
5
contains a discussion
in which analogies and differences between continuous and discrete walks are highlighted,
and the findings of the present paper are set in perspective with those of earlier works
[
2
,
8
]
.
Finally, two appendices are devoted respectively to the detailed derivations of selected key results
and to integer renewal processes.

2
Fundamental observables

In this section we present a self-contained derivation of general results concerning the fundamental quantities,
valid for arbitrary step distributions of the form (
1.2
).

2.1
Preliminaries

The generating series of the step distribution,

\hat{\rho}(z)={\langle z^{\eta}\rangle}=\sum_{k}\rho_{k}z^{k}=\frac{\rho_{K}}{z^{K}}+\cdots+\rho_{K}z^{K},

(2.1)

is a self-reciprocal (or palindromic
2
2

2

The term palindromic refers to the fact that the sequence
of coefficients reads the same forwards and backwards,
just like a palindrome word such as
radar
.
) Laurent polynomial in
z
,
obeying
\hat{\rho}(1/z)=\hat{\rho}(z)
.
The factorisation of the difference

\phi(z)=1-\hat{\rho}(z)

(2.2)

over its complex zeros plays a central role hereafter.
We have

\phi(z)=-D(z-1)^{2}+\cdots

(2.3)

as
z\to 1
, where

D=\frac{{\langle\eta^{2}\rangle}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}k^{2}\rho_{k}

(2.4)

is the diffusion coefficient of the walk.
Besides the above double zero at
z=1
,

\phi(z)
has no other zero on the unit circle.
Setting
z=\rme^{{\rm i}\alpha}
, we have indeed

\phi(\rme^{{\rm i}\alpha})=\sum_{k}\rho_{k}(1-\cos k\alpha)\geq 2\rho_{1}(1-\cos\alpha).

(2.5)

The factorisation of the Laurent polynomial
\phi(z)
therefore reads

\displaystyle\phi(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho_{K}}{z^{K}}(z-1)^{2}\prod_{a}(z+z_{a})(z+1/z_{a})

(2.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{K}(1-z)(1-1/z)\prod_{a}\frac{(z_{a}+z)(z_{a}+1/z)}{z_{a}}.

(2.7)

The second form (
2.7
) makes the invariance under the change of
z
into
1/z
manifest.
Here and throughout the following, the label
a
runs over
1,\dots,K-1
.
The
K-1
complex numbers
z_{a}
are inside the unit circle,
whereas their inverses
1/z_{a}
are outside the unit circle.
The zeros of
\phi(z)
are therefore 1 (double), as well as
-z_{a}
and
-1/z_{a}
.
Henceforth, for the sake of brevity, we simply refer to the
z_{a}
as being ‘the zeros’.
They come in complex conjugate pairs.
For
K
even, there is also a real zero such that
0<z_{a}<1<1/z_{a}
.
In generic circumstances, as the range
K
is increased,
the zeros come closer to the unit circle and become more evenly spread.
Plots of these zeros will be shown in section
4

(see figure
3
) for discrete uniform step distributions.

The leitmotiv of the present approach is that all quantities of interest
can be expressed as symmetric functions of the complex zeros
z_{a}
.
A first example,

D=\rho_{K}\prod_{a}(1+z_{a})(1+1/z_{a}),

(2.8)

is obtained by comparing (
2.3
) and (
2.7
).

2.2
Enhancement factor

The enhancement factor of the walk is defined by

{\cal E}=\exp\biggl(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{P}(x_{n}=0)\biggr).

(2.9)

In the probabilistic literature,
and especially in the classic books
[
6
,
9
]
,

1/{\cal E}^{2}
is denoted by
c
, whereas
D
is denoted by
\sigma^{2}/2
.
For a random walk with a continuous step distribution,
\mathbb{P}(x_{n}=0)
vanishes, and so
{\cal E}=1
.
If the step distribution is discrete, like e.g. for the lattice walks considered here,

\mathbb{P}(x_{n}=0)
may be non-zero, at least for some
n
, and so
{\cal E}\geq 1
.
The enhancement factor
{\cal E}
therefore measures to what extent a given lattice walk
is different from the class of continuous ones.
This quantity enters the expressions of many observables.
To take a characteristic example
[
10
,
11
]
,
we recall that the mean maximum of a long random walk of
n
steps takes the universal asymptotic form

{\langle M_{n}\rangle}\approx 2\sqrt{\frac{Dn}{\pi}},

(2.10)

depending only on the diffusion coefficient of the walk,
whereas the mean number of records, or ‘ladder heights’, of the same walk grows as

{\langle R_{n}\rangle}\approx\frac{2}{{\cal E}}\sqrt{\frac{n}{\pi}}.

(2.11)

We have furthermore
[
10
,
11
]

M_{n}\approx{\langle H\rangle}R_{n}.

(2.12)

The ratio between the estimates (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) therefore reads
{\langle H\rangle}={\cal E}\sqrt{D}
.
This result (see
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
) is recovered in (
2.58
).

The enhancement factor can be evaluated as follows.
From the definition (
1.1
) of the walk, we have
{\langle z^{x_{n}}\rangle}=\hat{\rho}(z)^{n}
, and therefore

\mathbb{P}(x_{n}=0)=\oint\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi{\rm i}z}\,\hat{\rho}(z)^{n},

(2.13)

where the integration contour is the unit circle.
Equation (
2.9
) can thus be recast as

{\cal E}=\exp\biggl(-\frac{1}{2}\oint\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi{\rm i}z}\ln(1-\hat{\rho}(z))\biggr).

(2.14)

The integral above can be evaluated using (
2.7
), together with the identity

\oint\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi{\rm i}z}\ln((y+z)(y+1/z))=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\alpha}{2\pi}\ln(1+2y\cos\alpha+y^{2})=0

(2.15)

valid for complex
y
with
{\left|y\right|}\leq 1
.
We thus obtain

{\cal E}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{\rho_{K}}\prod_{a}z_{a}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(2.16)

Combining this with (
2.8
) yields

{\cal E}\sqrt{D}=\prod_{a}(1+z_{a}).

(2.17)

Two remarks are in order.
First, a similar but different factorisation scheme for finite-range lattice walks
was proposed recently in a work on the survival probability of random walks and Lévy flights
[
13
]
.
Second, in full generality, ‘diluting’ a step distribution,
i.e., introducing a non-zero probability
\rho_{0}
for the walker to stop at every time step,
alters the separate values of
D
and
{\cal E}
, but neither changes the product
{\cal E}\sqrt{D}
,
nor most quantities to be introduced below, including the distribution of
H
, as should be.

2.3
Solution to the homogeneous Wiener–Hopf equation

Let us begin our study with a first fundamental quantity,
namely the solution to the homogeneous Wiener–Hopf equation

G_{k}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho_{k-j}G_{j},

(2.18)

normalised by the boundary condition
G_{0}=1
.
In analogy with the continuous case
[
2
]
,
the preceding equation admits a unique solution, growing linearly with
k
.
In the present setting,
the corresponding generating series,

\hat{G}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}G_{k}z^{k},

(2.19)

reads

\hat{G}(z)=\frac{1}{\displaystyle(1-z)^{2}\prod_{a}(1+zz_{a})}.

(2.20)

A detailed derivation of this formula is given in
A.1
.

The behaviour of
G_{k}
at large
k
is of special interest.
Expanding (
2.20
) as
z\to 1
, using (
2.17
), as well as the identities

\sum_{k}z^{k}=\frac{1}{1-z},\qquad\sum_{k}kz^{k}=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}},

(2.21)

we obtain

G_{k}\approx\frac{k+\ell}{{\cal E}\sqrt{D}}.

(2.22)

The additive constant
\ell
, known as the extrapolation length, is given by

\ell=1+\sum_{a}\frac{z_{a}}{1+z_{a}}.

(2.23)

The corrections to the asymptotic formula (
2.22
) are exponentially small in
k
.

2.4
Survival probabilities

We proceed with the study of the survival probabilities

g_{n,k}=\mathbb{P}(x_{0}\geq 0,\ x_{1}\geq 0,\ \dots,\ x_{n}=k\geq 0),

(2.24)

defined for
n\geq 0
and
k\geq 0
,
and of their temporal sums

g_{k}=\sum_{n\geq 0}g_{n,k}.

(2.25)

Just as in the continuous case
[
2
]
,
the survival probabilities
g_{n,k}
, especially
g_{k}
,
are not only of intrinsic interest but also provide an intermediate step
towards our main objective: the distribution of the first positive position.

Conditioning on the last step of the walk,
and setting
x_{n-1}=j
,
yields the following recursion formula for the survival probability:

g_{n,k}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho_{k-j}g_{n-1,j},

(2.26)

with initial condition
g_{0,k}=\delta_{k0}
.
As a consequence, the quantities
g_{k}
obey

g_{k}=\delta_{k0}+\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho_{k-j}g_{j}.

(2.27)

The above equation is an inhomogeneous variant of the Wiener–Hopf equation (
2.18
).
Again in analogy with the continuous case
[
2
]
, it has a unique bounded solution.
The corresponding generating series,

\hat{g}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}z^{k},

(2.28)

reads

\hat{g}(z)=\frac{{\cal E}^{2}}{\displaystyle(1-z)\prod_{a}(1+zz_{a})}.

(2.29)

A detailed derivation of this formula is given in
A.2
.
Comparing (
2.20
) and (
2.29
) yields the identity

\hat{g}(z)={\cal E}^{2}(1-z)\hat{G}(z),

(2.30)

implying

g_{k}={\cal E}^{2}(G_{k}-G_{k-1})\qquad(k\geq 1),

(2.31)

and especially

g_{0}={\cal E}^{2}.

(2.32)

At large
k
,
g_{k}
approaches exponentially fast the limit

g_{\infty}=\frac{{\cal E}}{\sqrt{D}}.

(2.33)

We also mention the product formula

\hat{g}(z)\hat{g}(1/z)=\frac{{\cal E}^{2}}{1-\hat{\rho}(z)},

(2.34)

which is one form of the Wiener–Hopf factorisation.

To close, we make contact with the theory of arbitrary lattice walks,
whose range is infinite in general (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
).
To do so, we recast the key result (
2.29
) as

\ln\frac{{\cal E}^{2}}{\hat{g}(z)}=\ln(1-z)+\sum_{a}\ln(1+zz_{a}).

(2.35)

Noticing that
z=1
and
z=-z_{a}
are the zeros of
\phi(z)
in the closed unit disk
(see (
2.6
)),
we obtain the following contour integral representation for any regular function
F(z)
:

F(1)+\sum_{a}F(-z_{a})=\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,F(y)\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime}(y)}{\phi(y)}+\frac{K}{y}\right).

(2.36)

The second term inside the parentheses reflects the fact that
\phi(y)
has a pole of order
K
at the origin.
It brings no contribution to the integral whenever
F(0)=0
.
This holds for the case at hand, namely
F(y)=\ln(1-zy)
.
We thus obtain

\ln\frac{{\cal E}^{2}}{\hat{g}(z)}=\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\ln(1-zy)\,\frac{\phi^{\prime}(y)}{\phi(y)}=z\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\ln\phi(y)}{1-zy},

(2.37)

where the second expression is obtained by integration by parts.
Finally, parametrising the unit circle by
y=\rme^{{\rm i}\alpha}
, we get

\hat{g}(z)={\cal E}^{2}\exp\left(z\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\alpha}{2\pi}\,\frac{z-\cos\alpha}{1-2z\cos\alpha+z^{2}}\,\ln(1-\tilde{\rho}(\alpha))\right),

(2.38)

where

\tilde{\rho}(\alpha)=\hat{\rho}(\rme^{{\rm i}\alpha}),

(2.39)

the Fourier transform of the step distribution, is a real, even, and
2\pi
-periodic function of
\alpha
.
Inserting (
2.38
) into (
2.30
), we have

\hat{G}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}\exp\left(z\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\alpha}{2\pi}\,\frac{z-\cos\alpha}{1-2z\cos\alpha+z^{2}}\,\ln(1-\tilde{\rho}(\alpha))\right).

(2.40)

So, for finite-range lattice walks, the key results (
2.29
) and (
2.20
) have been given
the contour integral representation (
2.38
) and (
2.40
).
Equivalent forms are given in
[
12
]
.
The latter representations are discrete analogues of the Pollaczek-Spitzer formulas.
They are far more general than (
2.20
) and (
2.29
),
as they hold for arbitrary symmetric lattice walks,
including those with step distributions falling off as a power law, namely

\rho_{k}\approx\frac{c}{{\left|k\right|}^{1+\theta}}.

(2.41)

For this class of walks, which includes long-ranged diffusive walks for
\theta>2

and lattice Lévy flights for
0<\theta<2
,
the Fourier transform
\tilde{\rho}(\alpha)
is well-defined,
whereas the Laurent series (discrete Laplace transform)
\hat{\rho}(z)
is ill-defined
whenever
z
is not along the imaginary axis.

2.5
Distribution of the first positive position

We now address our main objective, namely the distribution of the first positive position.
Let
N
denote the discrete time at which the first positive position
H=x_{N}
is reached.
The joint distribution of these two random variables is

\displaystyle f_{n,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N=n,\ H=k)

(2.42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x_{0}\leq 0,\ x_{1}\leq 0,\ \dots,\ x_{n-1}\leq 0,\ x_{n}=k),

defined for
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 1
.
Our main interest lies in the marginal distribution of the first positive position
H
,

f_{k}=\mathbb{P}(H=k)=\sum_{n\geq 1}f_{n,k}.

(2.43)

In analogy with the continuous case
[
2
]
,
conditioning on the last step of the walk,
and setting
x_{n-1}=-j
,
yields the following expression for the joint probabilities
f_{n,k}
:

f_{n,k}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho_{k+j}g_{n-1,j}.

(2.44)

As a consequence, the probabilities
f_{k}
obey

f_{k}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho_{k+j}g_{j}.

(2.45)

The corresponding generating series,

\hat{f}(z)={\langle z^{H}\rangle}=\sum_{k\geq 1}f_{k}z^{k},

(2.46)

reads in the present setting

\hat{f}(z)=1-\frac{{\cal E}^{2}}{\hat{g}(z)}.

(2.47)

Using (
2.29
), this can be written as

\hat{f}(z)=1-(1-z)\prod_{a}(1+zz_{a}).

(2.48)

The results (
2.47
), (
2.48
) are derived in detail in
A.3
.
Finally, using (
2.38
), we obtain a general expression for
\hat{f}(z)
,
valid for all lattice walks, namely

\hat{f}(z)=1-\exp\left(-z\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\alpha}{2\pi}\,\frac{z-\cos\alpha}{1-2z\cos\alpha+z^{2}}\,\ln(1-\tilde{\rho}(\alpha))\right).

(2.49)

The right-hand side of (
2.48
) is a polynomial in
z
of degree
K
, vanishing at
z=0
,
as should be, since the probabilities
f_{k}=\mathbb{P}(H=k)
are nonzero for
k=1,\dots,K
only.
These probabilities admit explicit expressions in terms
of the elementary symmetric polynomials
S_{k}
of the zeros
z_{a}
,
defined through the expansion

\prod_{a}(1+zz_{a})=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}S_{k}z^{k}.

(2.50)

We have

S_{0}=1,\quad S_{1}=\sum_{a}z_{a},\quad\dots,\quad S_{K-1}=\prod_{a}z_{a}.

(2.51)

The expression (
2.48
) yields

f_{k}=S_{k-1}-S_{k}\qquad(k=1,\dots,K),

(2.52)

with the convention
S_{K}=0
.
We have in particular

f_{1}=1-\sum_{a}z_{a},\qquad f_{K}=\prod_{a}z_{a}={\cal E}^{2}\rho_{K}.

(2.53)

The formulas (
2.48
) and (
2.52
) are the main results of this work.

Using (
2.47
), the product identity (
2.34
) translates to

(1-\hat{f}(z))(1-\hat{f}(1/z))={\cal E}^{2}(1-\hat{\rho}(z)).

(2.54)

This alternative form of the Wiener–Hopf factorisation
yields the following quadratic sum rules for the probabilities
f_{k}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}^{2}={\cal E}^{2}(1-\rho_{0})-1,

(2.55)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K-m}f_{k}f_{k+m}=f_{m}-{\cal E}^{2}\rho_{m}\qquad(m=1,\dots,K-1).

(2.56)

2.6
Moments of the first positive position

The formula (
2.48
) also provides a route to evaluate the moments of the first positive position,

{\langle H^{m}\rangle}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{m}f_{k}.

(2.57)

The first moment can be obtained by taking the derivative of (
2.48
) at
z=1
.
We thus recover the result (see
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
)

{\langle H\rangle}=\prod_{a}(1+z_{a})={\cal E}\sqrt{D}.

(2.58)

In analogy with the continuous case
[
2
]
,
the evaluation of higher-order moments can be made systematic by rearranging (
2.48
) as

\displaystyle\hat{f}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-(1-z){\cal E}\sqrt{D}\prod_{a}\frac{1+zz_{a}}{1+z_{a}}

(2.59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-(1-z){\cal E}\sqrt{D}\;\exp\Biggl(\sum_{a}\ln\frac{1+zz_{a}}{1+z_{a}}\Biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-(1-z){\cal E}\sqrt{D}\;\exp\Biggl(\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{c_{m}}{m!}(z-1)^{m}\Biggr),

where we have introduced the quantities

c_{m}=(-1)^{m-1}(m-1)!\sum_{a}\biggl(\frac{z_{a}}{1+z_{a}}\biggr)^{m}.

(2.60)

We have in particular

c_{1}=\ell-1,

(2.61)

where the extrapolation length
\ell
is given by (
2.23
).

Setting
z=\rme^{s}
and expanding (
2.59
) as a power series in
s
yields
the following explicit expressions for the first few moments of
H
:

\displaystyle{\langle H^{2}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal E}\sqrt{D}\,(2c_{1}+1)={\cal E}\sqrt{D}\,(2\ell-1),

\displaystyle{\langle H^{3}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal E}\sqrt{D}\,(3c_{2}+3c_{1}(c_{1}+2)+1),

\displaystyle{\langle H^{4}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal E}\sqrt{D}\,(4c_{3}+6(2c_{1}+3)c_{2}+2c_{1}(2c_{1}^{2}+9c_{1}+7)+1).

(2.62)

The reduced variance of
H
reads

V=\frac{{\langle H^{2}\rangle}}{{\langle H\rangle}^{2}}-1=\frac{2\ell-1}{{\cal E}\sqrt{D}}-1.

(2.63)

This quantity is non-negative, hence the inequality
2\ell-1\geq{\cal E}\sqrt{D}
.

2.7
Height renewal process

In analogy with the continuous case
[
2
]
,
the successive upper records
X_{1},\ X_{2},\ \dots
of the walker’s position form a renewal process,
referred to as the ‘height renewal process’.
The first record coincides with the first positive position.
More generally,

X_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}H_{j},

(2.64)

where the integer spatial increments
H_{j}
between consecutive record positions
are iid copies of the first positive position
H
,
with distribution
\mathbb{P}(H=k)=f_{k}
.
We consistently set
X_{0}=0
.

For a given lattice position
x\geq 0
, there is a unique integer
N_{x}
such that

X_{N_{x}}\leq x<X_{N_{x}+1}.

(2.65)

The random integer
N_{x}
represents the number of records up to
x
.

The backward length
B_{x}
and the forward (or excess) length
E_{x}
are defined by

B_{x}=x-X_{N_{x}},\qquad E_{x}=X_{N_{x}+1}-x.

(2.66)

We have thus
B_{x}\geq 0
whereas
E_{x}>0
.
These lengths obey

B_{x}+E_{x}=X_{N_{x}+1}-X_{N_{x}}=H_{N_{x}+1}.

(2.67)

In the present setting of an integer renewal process,
the distributions of
N_{x}
,
B_{x}
and
E_{x}

are less known than their continuous counterparts.
For completeness, a self-contained derivation of these distributions is provided in
B
.

It is worth emphasising various relationships between the above variables and results obtained earlier.
The solution
G_{k}
to the homogeneous Wiener–Hopf equation admits an appealing interpretation,
in analogy with the continuous case
[
2
]
.
Combining (
2.30
) and (
2.47
) yields the alternative expression

\hat{G}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-z)(1-\hat{f}(z))}.

(2.68)

Comparing the formula above with (
2.8
),
we obtain

G_{k}=1+{\langle N_{k}\rangle},

(2.69)

where
{\langle N_{k}\rangle}
denotes the mean number of records up to position
k
.
Substituting into this relation the expansions (
2.22
) of
G_{k}
and (
2.10
) of
{\langle N_{k}\rangle}

at large
k
,
one recovers the expressions of the first two moments of
H
, namely

{\langle H\rangle}={\cal E}\sqrt{D},\qquad{\langle H^{2}\rangle}={\cal E}\sqrt{D}\,(2\ell-1),

(2.70)

in agreement with (
2.58
), (
2.62
).

The distribution of the equilibrium backward length,

B=\lim_{x\to\infty}B_{x},

(2.71)

or, equivalently, of the equilibrium forward (or excess) length
E=B+1
(see (
2.15
)),
is obtained by comparing (
2.59
) and (
2.12
), (
2.14
).
We thus obtain

{\langle z^{B}\rangle}=\prod_{a}\frac{1+zz_{a}}{1+z_{a}}=\exp\Biggl(\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{c_{m}}{m!}(z-1)^{m}\Biggr).

(2.72)

Using (
2.17
) and (
2.50
), we get

\mathbb{P}(B=k)=\mathbb{P}(E=k+1)=\frac{S_{k}}{{\cal E}\sqrt{D}}\qquad(k=0,\dots,K-1).

(2.73)

The first moments of
B
and
E
are

\displaystyle{\langle B\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1},\qquad{\langle B^{2}\rangle}=c_{2}+c_{1}^{2}+c_{1},

\displaystyle{\langle E\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}+1,\qquad{\langle E^{2}\rangle}=c_{2}+c_{1}^{2}+3c_{1}+1.

(2.74)

From (
2.72
) it follows that
the coefficients
c_{m}
, explicitly given in (
2.60
),
are the factorial cumulants of the equilibrium backward length
B
.
Recall (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
15
]
)
that the moments
\mu_{n}
and cumulants
\gamma_{n}
of a real random variable
X
are defined through

{\langle\rme^{sX}\rangle}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\mu_{n}}{n!}\,s^{n}=\exp\,\Bigl(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\gamma_{n}}{n!}\,s^{n}\Bigr),

(2.75)

while the factorial moments
m_{n}
and factorial cumulants
c_{n}

of an integer random variable
N
are defined through

{\langle z^{N}\rangle}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{m_{n}}{n!}(z-1)^{n}=\exp\,\Bigl(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{c_{n}}{n!}(z-1)^{n}\Bigr).

(2.76)

3
First examples: range 1 or 2

3.1
Range
K=1

The simplest lattice random walk has range
K=1
.
This is the so-called simple walk,
where we allow a non-zero probability
\rho_{0}
to stop at every time step.
We set

\rho_{\pm 1}=p,\qquad\rho_{0}=1-2p,

(3.1)

with
0<p\leq 1/2
.
In this case,

\phi(z)=-\frac{p(z-1)^{2}}{z}

(3.2)

has no non-trivial zeros.
We have

D=p,\qquad{\cal E}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}

(3.3)

as well as

{\cal E}\sqrt{D}=1,\qquad\ell=1,\qquad f_{1}=1,\qquad G_{k}=k+1.

(3.4)

The latter quantities do not depend on
p
, in line with the remark below (
2.17
).

The Pólya walk is recovered for
\rho_{0}=0
, i.e.,
p=1/2
.
We have then

D=\frac{1}{2},\qquad{\cal E}=\sqrt{2}.

(3.5)

3.2
Range
K=2

The next case in increasing order of complexity corresponds to the range
K=2
.
For conciseness, we set
\rho_{0}=0
, and define

\rho_{\pm 1}=\frac{1-a}{2},\qquad\rho_{\pm 2}=\frac{a}{2},

(3.6)

with
0\leq a<1
.
We have

\displaystyle\phi(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{(z-1)^{2}}{2z^{2}}(az^{2}+(1+a)z+a)

(3.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{a(z-1)^{2}}{2z^{2}}(z+z_{1})(z+1/z_{1}),

where

z_{1}=\frac{1}{2a}\left(1+a-\sqrt{(1-a)(1+3a)}\right)

(3.8)

increases monotonically from 0 to 1 as
a
increases from 0 to 1.
We have also

D=\frac{1+3a}{2},\qquad{\cal E}=\sqrt{\frac{2z_{1}}{a}}.

(3.9)

The enhancement factor takes its largest value,
{\cal E}=\sqrt{2}
, for
a\to 0
and
a\to 1
,
and reaches its minimum,
{\cal E}=\sqrt{3/2}
, for
a=2/3
, i.e.,
z_{1}=1/2
.
We have as well

{\cal E}\sqrt{D}=1+z_{1},\qquad\ell=\frac{1+2z_{1}}{1+z_{1}}

(3.10)

and

f_{1}=1-z_{1},\quad f_{2}=z_{1},\qquad G_{k}=\frac{k+\ell}{1+z_{1}}+(-1)^{k}\frac{z_{1}^{k+2}}{(1+z_{1})^{2}}.

(3.11)

In particular,

{\langle H\rangle}=1+z_{1},\qquad{\langle H^{2}\rangle}=1+3z_{1},

(3.12)

so that the reduced variance of
H
(see (
2.63
)),

V=\frac{z_{1}(1-z_{1})}{(1+z_{1})^{2}},

(3.13)

vanishes for
a\to 0
and
a\to 1
,
and reaches its maximum,
V=1/8
, for
z_{1}=1/3
, i.e.,
a=3/7
.

4
Discrete uniform distributions

This section is devoted to the family of lattice walks defined by the symmetric discrete uniform distributions

\rho_{k}=\frac{1}{2K+1}\qquad(k=-K,\dots,K),

(4.1)

parametrised by the range
K\geq 1
.
We have

D=\frac{K(K+1)}{6}

(4.2)

and

\hat{\rho}(z)=\frac{1}{2K+1}\sum_{k=-K}^{K}z^{k}=\frac{z^{2K+1}-1}{(2K+1)z^{K}(z-1)},

(4.3)

so that

\phi(z)=1-\hat{\rho}(z)=-\frac{(z-1)^{2}P_{K}(z)}{(2K+1)z^{K}},

(4.4)

where

\displaystyle P_{K}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K(K+1)}{2}z^{K-1}+\sum_{m=0}^{K-2}\frac{(m+1)(m+2)}{2}(z^{m}+z^{2K-2-m})

(4.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{a}(z+z_{a})(z+1/z_{a})

(see (
2.6
))
is a self-reciprocal polynomial with degree
2K-2
,
whose coefficients are ascending and descending triangular numbers.

4.1
The first few values of
K

We begin by investigating the first few values of the range
K
.

•

K=1
.
In this case, the distribution (
4.1
) identifies with that of the simple walk,
studied in section
3.1
, with
p=1/3
.
We have
D=1/3
, as well as

{\cal E}=\sqrt{3},\qquad\ell=1,

(4.6)

and, of course,
f_{1}=1
, hence
{\langle H\rangle}={\langle H^{2}\rangle}=1
and
V=0
.

•

K=2
.
In this case, the distribution (
4.1
) is some ‘diluted’ form of that studied in section
3.2
,
with
a=1/2
.
We have
D=1
and
P_{2}(z)=z^{2}+3z+1
, and therefore

z_{1}=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}=0.381966\dots,

(4.7)

so that

{\cal E}=\frac{5-\sqrt{5}}{2}=1.381966\dots,\quad\ell=\frac{15-\sqrt{5}}{10}=1.276393\dots

(4.8)

and

f_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}=0.618033\dots,\quad f_{2}=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}=0.381966\dots

(4.9)

so that

\displaystyle{\langle H\rangle}=\frac{5-\sqrt{5}}{2}=1.381966\dots,

\displaystyle{\langle H^{2}\rangle}=\frac{11-3\sqrt{5}}{2}=2.145898\dots,

\displaystyle V=\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{10}=0.123606\dots

(4.10)

•

K=3
.
We have
D=2
and
P_{3}(z)=z^{4}+3z^{3}+6z^{2}+3z+1
,
so that there is a pair of complex conjugate zeros,

z_{1}=a+{\rm i}b,\qquad z_{2}=a-{\rm i}b,

(4.11)

with

\displaystyle a=\frac{(1-\sqrt{7})7^{1/4}}{4\sqrt{2}}+\frac{3}{4}=0.276779\dots,

\displaystyle b=\frac{(1+\sqrt{7})7^{1/4}}{4\sqrt{2}}-\frac{\sqrt{7}}{4}=0.386864\dots

(4.12)

We have therefore

\displaystyle{\cal E}=\frac{7+\sqrt{7}}{2\sqrt{2}}-\frac{7^{3/4}}{2}=1.258529\dots,

\displaystyle\ell=2-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,7^{1/4}}=1.565279\dots,

(4.13)

and

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\sqrt{7}-1)7^{1/4}}{2\sqrt{2}}-\frac{1}{2}=0.446441\dots,

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\sqrt{7}}{2}+\frac{7^{1/4}}{\sqrt{2}}=0.327287\dots,

\displaystyle f_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\sqrt{7}}{2}-\frac{(1+\sqrt{7})7^{1/4}}{2\sqrt{2}}=0.226270\dots,

(4.14)

so that

\displaystyle{\langle H\rangle}=\frac{7+\sqrt{7}}{2}-\frac{7^{3/4}}{\sqrt{2}}=1.779829\dots,

\displaystyle{\langle H^{2}\rangle}=\frac{21+5\sqrt{7}}{2}-\frac{(1+4\sqrt{7})7^{1/4}}{\sqrt{2}}=3.792030\dots,

(4.15)

4.2
Scaling behaviour at large
K

We now investigate the regime where the range
K
of the discrete uniform distribution becomes large.
It is to be expected that the first positive position of the lattice walk reads approximately

H\approx K\,H_{c},

(4.16)

where
H_{c}
is the first positive position of the continuous walk
defined by the continuous uniform step distribution

\rho_{c}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\qquad(-1\leq x\leq+1),

(4.17)

whose diffusion coefficient is
D_{c}=1/6
.

Hereafter we use several notations and results from
[
2
]
,
with the addition of a subscript
c
for quantities pertaining to the continuous case.
The distribution of
H_{c}
has a density
f_{c}(x)
on

,
which can be determined by means of Wiener–Hopf techniques.
We have in particular
f_{c}(1)=1/2
, whereas

(4.18)

where

(4.19)

hence

\omega_{c}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}q\ln\left(1-\frac{\sin q}{q}\right)=1.601537\dots

(4.20)

In terms of the probabilities

the asymptotic equivalence (

The offset in the numerator has been introduced in order for the

5
Discussion

The present article completes our investigation
of the distribution of the first positive position of a random walker, denoted by
H
.
The case of continuous random walks was analysed in an earlier work
[
2
]
,
while the present one is devoted to finite-range lattice walks.
In both papers, only symmetric walks are considered for definiteness,
and the number of steps is taken as a discrete time variable.
Recall that the distribution of
H
is the building block
for studying the statistics of all record positions,
or ‘ladder heights’, in one-dimensional random walks,
with these successive positions constituting the ‘height renewal process’.
In what follows, we highlight a few salient analogies and differences between continuous and lattice walks.

We begin with a feature common to both continuous and lattice walks.
In each setting, there are remarkable classes of step distributions for which
the Wiener-Hopf equations governing the problem can be solved analytically by simple means,
without resorting to the general formalism.
As recalled in the Introduction,
the method consists in factorising the relevant analytic functions over their complex zeros and poles.
In the continuous setting, this approach has long been known:
it applies when the step distribution is a finite superposition of decaying exponentials,
so that its Laplace transform is a rational function.
In the discrete setting, the same method is used extensively here for generic finite-range lattice walks.
The resulting expressions agree with those obtained from the full formalism, as they should.
In particular, the key results (
2.20
), (
2.29
), and (
2.48
)
can be recast as the corresponding discrete Pollaczek-Spitzer
formulas (
2.40
), (
2.38
), and (
2.49
).

A marked difference between the two settings concerns dimensional analysis.
For continuous walks, it makes sense to measure the step lengths in centimeters, say,
and to apply dimensional analysis throughout.
For discrete walks, such as the lattice walks considered here,
non-trivial dimensionless quantities naturally enter the expressions of observables.
The first and foremost example is the enhancement factor
{\cal E}
.
This difference between the continuous and discrete settings is manifest in the expressions of the moments of
H
.
The expressions (
2.62
) derived here for lattice walks involve, besides
{\cal E}
,
several different powers of the quantities
c_{k}
.
Their continuous analogues, derived in
[
2
]
,
only involve the homogeneous polynomials of the highest degree,
in line with the above remark on dimensional analysis.

The last important difference between the two settings
that we wish to address concerns the topmost gap between the two largest positions of a very long walk
(see
[
16
,
17
]
, and
[
18
]
for a review).
For continuous walks, the distribution of the topmost gap, denoted by
Z
,
was studied extensively in an earlier paper
[
8
]
.
That work relied entirely on the identity

Z=\min(H_{1},H_{2}),

(5.1)

which states that the gap can be viewed as the smaller of two independent copies of
H
,
associated with the semi-infinite portions of the walk before and after its maximum,
i.e., its largest position.
A key ingredient in deriving (
5.1
) is the uniqueness of the maximum of the random walk.
This property clearly holds for continuous step distributions, but fails for discrete ones.
More precisely, for a formally infinite discrete walk, such as the lattice walks considered here,
the multiplicity
\nu
of the maximum, i.e., the number of times it is reached,
is itself a random variable,
whose distribution can be obtained from a formula for the first return time to the origin
of a walker whose position is constrained to stay non-negative at all times (see
[
6
, Ch. XVIII]
).
We thus arrive at a geometric law,

\mathbb{P}(\nu=k)=(1-q)q^{k-1}\qquad(k=1,2,\dots),

(5.2)

with parameter

q=1-\frac{1}{{\cal E}^{2}},

(5.3)

where
{\cal E}
is again the enhancement factor.
In particular, the mean multiplicity of the maximum reads

{\langle\nu\rangle}={\cal E}^{2}.

(5.4)

This expression equals unity for continuous walks, reflecting the uniqueness of the maximum.
It exceeds unity for discrete walks.
For instance, we have
{\langle\nu\rangle}=2
for the simple Pólya walk.
The non-uniqueness of the maximum invalidates the identity (
5.1
),
thereby making the study of the topmost gap considerably more difficult in the discrete setting.

Appendix A
Detailed derivations of central formulas

A.1
Derivation of (
2.20
)

1.

In the definition (
2.19
) of the generating series
\hat{G}(z)
, i.e.,

\hat{G}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}G_{k}z^{k},

(1.1)

which is expected to be analytic for
{\left|z\right|}<1
,
replace
G_{k}
by the right-hand side of (
2.18
), obtaining

\hat{G}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}z^{k}\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho_{k-j}G_{j}.

(1.2)

2.

Express
\rho_{k-j}
and
G_{j}
as the contour integrals

\rho_{k-j}=\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}x^{k-j+1}}\,\hat{\rho}(x),\qquad G_{j}=\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}y^{j+1}}\,\hat{G}(y),

(1.3)

obtaining

\hat{G}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}z^{k}\sum_{j\geq 0}\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}x^{k-j+1}}\,\hat{\rho}(x)\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}y^{j+1}}\,\hat{G}(y).

(1.4)

3.

Perform the geometric sums over
k
and
j
to obtain

\hat{G}(z)=\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{\rho}(x)}{x-z}\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{G}(y)}{y-x}

(1.5)

for
{\left|z\right|}<{\left|x\right|}<{\left|y\right|}<1
.

4.

The integral over
y
is given by the residue of the pole at
y=x
, namely

\hat{G}(z)=\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{\rho}(x)\hat{G}(x)}{x-z}

(1.6)

for
{\left|z\right|}<{\left|x\right|}<1
.

5.

Shrink the
x
contour to
{\left|x\right|}<{\left|z\right|}
, picking the residue of the pole at
x=z
, i.e.,

\hat{G}(z)=\hat{\rho}(z)\hat{G}(z)+\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{\rho}(x)\hat{G}(x)}{x-z}.

(1.7)

Using the definition (
2.2
) of
\phi(z)
, this reads

\phi(z)\hat{G}(z)=\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{\rho}(x)\hat{G}(x)}{x-z}

(1.8)

for
{\left|x\right|}<{\left|z\right|}<1
.

6.

As a consequence of the form (
2.1
) of
\hat{\rho}(z)
,
the right-hand side evaluates to a linear combination of the monomials
1/z
up to
1/z^{K}
, and therefore

\phi(z)\hat{G}(z)=\frac{P_{1}(z)}{z^{K}},

(1.9)

where
P_{1}(z)
is a polynomial of degree
K-1
that can be determined as follows.
For
z=-z_{a}
, where
z_{a}
is any of the zeros introduced in the factorisation (
2.6
),

\phi(z)
vanishes while
\hat{G}(z)
is finite, and so
P_{1}(z)
vanishes.
We have therefore

P_{1}(z)=C\prod_{a}(z+z_{a}).

(1.10)

The boundary condition
\hat{G}(0)=G_{0}=1
fixes the constant
C
, namely

C=-\frac{\rho_{K}}{\displaystyle\prod_{a}z_{a}}=-\frac{1}{{\cal E}^{2}}

(1.11)

(see (
2.16
)).
We have therefore

\phi(z)\hat{G}(z)=-\frac{\rho_{K}}{z^{K}}\prod_{a}(1+z/z_{a}),

(1.12)

hence, using (
2.6
),

\hat{G}(z)=\frac{1}{\displaystyle(1-z)^{2}\prod_{a}(1+zz_{a})},

(1.13)

which is (
2.20
).

A.2
Derivation of (
2.29
)

1.

In the definition (
2.28
) of the generating series
\hat{g}(z)
, i.e.,

\hat{g}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}z^{k},

(1.14)

which is expected to be analytic for
{\left|z\right|}<1
,
replace
g_{k}
by the right-hand side of (
2.27
), obtaining

\hat{g}(z)=1+\sum_{k\geq 0}z^{k}\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho_{k-j}g_{j}.

(1.15)

2.

Proceed as in
A.1
.
The analogue of (
1.8
) reads

\phi(z)\hat{g}(z)=1+\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{\rho}(x)\hat{g}(x)}{x-z}

(1.16)

for
{\left|x\right|}<{\left|z\right|}<1
.

3.

The integral in the right-hand side again evaluates to a linear combination
of the monomials
1/z
up to
1/z^{K}
, and therefore

\phi(z)\hat{g}(z)=\frac{P_{2}(z)}{z^{K}},

(1.17)

where
P_{2}(z)
is a polynomial of degree
K
that can be determined as follows.
First of all,
P_{2}(z)
is a monic polynomial: the coefficient of
z^{K}
is unity.
For
z=-z_{a}
, where
z_{a}
is any of the zeros introduced in the factorisation (
2.6
),

\phi(z)
vanishes while
\hat{g}(z)
is finite, and therefore
P_{2}(z)
vanishes.
Finally, the
g_{k}
being bounded,
g(z)
only has a simple pole at
z=1
, while
\phi(z)
has a double pole.
As a consequence,
P_{2}(z)
also vanishes for
z=1
.
We have therefore

P_{2}(z)=(z-1)\prod_{a}(z+z_{a}),

(1.18)

hence

\phi(z)\hat{g}(z)=\frac{z-1}{z^{K}}\prod_{a}(z+z_{a}),

(1.19)

and therefore, using (
2.6
) and (
2.16
),

\hat{g}(z)=\frac{{\cal E}^{2}}{\displaystyle(1-z)\prod_{a}(1+zz_{a})},

(1.20)

which is (
2.29
).

A.3
Derivation of (
2.47
) and (
2.48
)

1.

In the definition (
2.46
) of the generating series
\hat{f}(z)
, i.e.,

\hat{f}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}f_{k}z^{k},

(1.21)

which is in fact a polynomial of degree
K
in
z
,
replace
f_{k}
by the right-hand side of (
2.45
), obtaining

\hat{f}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}z^{k}\sum_{j\geq 0}\rho_{k+j}g_{j}.

(1.22)

2.

Express
\rho_{k+j}
and
g_{j}
as the contour integrals

\rho_{k+j}=\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}x^{k+j+1}}\,\hat{\rho}(x),\qquad g_{j}=\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}y^{j+1}}\,\hat{g}(y),

(1.23)

obtaining

\hat{f}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}z^{k}\sum_{j\geq 0}\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}x^{k+j+1}}\,\hat{\rho}(x)\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}y^{j+1}}\,\hat{g}(y).

(1.24)

3.

Perform the geometric sums over
k
and
j
to obtain

\hat{f}(z)=z\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{\rho}(x)}{x-z}\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{g}(y)}{xy-1}

(1.25)

for
{\left|z\right|}<{\left|x\right|}
and
{\left|xy\right|}>1
.

4.

The integral over
y
is given by the residue of the pole at
y=1/x
, namely

\hat{f}(z)=z\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{\rho}(x)\hat{g}(1/x)}{x(x-z)}

(1.26)

for
{\left|z\right|}<{\left|x\right|}
.

5.

Use the identity (
2.34
) to recast the numerator of the integrand as

\hat{\rho}(x)\hat{g}(1/x)=\hat{g}(1/x)-\frac{{\cal E}^{2}}{\hat{g}(x)},

(1.27)

so that

\hat{f}(z)=z\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{\hat{g}(1/x)}{x(x-z)}-z{\cal E}^{2}\oint\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{1}{x(x-z)\hat{g}(x)}.

(1.28)

The first integral vanishes,
as shown by inflating the integration contour to a circle whose radius becomes infinitely large.
The second integral is the sum of the residues of the poles at
x=z
and
x=0
,
which respectively read
1/(z\hat{g}(z))
and
-1/(z\hat{g}(0))=-1/(z{\cal E}^{2})
, using (
2.32
).
Therefore

\hat{f}(z)=1-\frac{{\cal E}^{2}}{\hat{g}(z)},

(1.29)

which is (
2.47
).
Using (
2.29
), this can be recast as

\hat{f}(z)=1-(1-z)\prod_{a}(1+zz_{a}),

(1.30)

which is (
2.48
).

Appendix B
Integer renewal processes

The notations of this appendix are consistent with those of section
2
.
We consider an integer renewal process where events sit at positive integer positions:

X_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}H_{j}.

(2.1)

The increments
H_{j}
between consecutive events
are iid probabilistic copies of some integer random variable
H
,
distributed according to
\mathbb{P}(H=k)=f_{k}
, with generating series

\hat{f}(z)={\langle z^{H}\rangle}=\sum_{k\geq 1}f_{k}z^{k}.

(2.2)

We consistently set
X_{0}=0
.
For a given position
x\geq 0
, there is a unique integer
N_{x}
such that

X_{N_{x}}\leq x<X_{N_{x}+1}.

(2.3)

The random integer
N_{x}
represents the number of events up to the given position
x
.
The backward and forward lengths
B_{x}
and
E_{x}
are defined by

B_{x}=x-X_{N_{x}},\qquad E_{x}=X_{N_{x}+1}-x.

(2.4)

We have thus
B_{x}\geq 0
whereas
E_{x}>0
.
These lengths obey

B_{x}+E_{x}=X_{N_{x}+1}-X_{N_{x}}=H_{N_{x}+1}.

(2.5)

The aim of this appendix is to provide a self-contained derivation of the distributions of
N_{x}
,
B_{x}
, and
E_{x}
.
Special attention will be paid to the equilibrium regime, reached in the limit of a very large observation position
x
.

B.1
Distribution of
N_{x}

The distribution of the number
N_{x}
of events up to position
x
is conveniently encoded
in the double generating series

L_{N}(y,z)=\sum_{x\geq 0}z^{x}{\langle y^{N_{x}}\rangle},

(2.6)

which can be determined as follows.
We have

\displaystyle L_{N}(y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\geq 0}z^{x}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}\dots}f_{k_{1}}f_{k_{2}}\dots

(2.7)

\displaystyle{\hskip 4.0pt}\times\sum_{n\geq 0}y^{n}\;{\bm{1}}(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}\leq x<k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}+k_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}y^{n}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}\dots}f_{k_{1}}f_{k_{2}}\dots

\displaystyle{\hskip 4.0pt}\times\sum_{x\geq 0}z^{x}\;{\bm{1}}(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}\leq x<k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}+k_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}y^{n}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}\dots}f_{k_{1}}f_{k_{2}}\dots(z^{k_{1}}\dots z^{k_{n}})(1+z+\cdots+z^{k_{n+1}-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}y^{n}\,\hat{f}(z)^{n}\,\frac{1-\hat{f}(z)}{1-z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\hat{f}(z)}{(1-z)(1-y\hat{f}(z))}.

The mean number
{\langle N_{x}\rangle}
of events up to position
x

is obtained by taking the first derivative of (
2.7
) at
y=1
, namely

\sum_{x\geq 0}{\langle N_{x}\rangle}z^{x}=\frac{\hat{f}(z)}{(1-z)(1-\hat{f}(z))}.

(2.8)

The growth of
{\langle N_{x}\rangle}
at large
x

can be derived by expanding the formula (
2.8
) for
z\to 1
.
Using

\hat{f}(z)=1+{\langle H\rangle}(z-1)+({\langle H^{2}\rangle}-{\langle H\rangle})\frac{(z-1)^{2}}{2}+\cdots,

(2.9)

as well as (
2.21
),
we obtain

{\langle N_{x}\rangle}=\frac{x}{{\langle H\rangle}}+\frac{{\langle H^{2}\rangle}+{\langle H\rangle}-2{\langle H\rangle}^{2}}{2{\langle H\rangle}^{2}}+\cdots

(2.10)

The unwritten correction falls off more or less rapidly, depending on the distribution of
H
.
In the situation of interest in this work, where
H
is bounded by some integer
K
,
the correction falls off exponentially with
x
.

B.2
Distribution of
B_{x}

The distribution of the backward length
B_{x}
is encoded in the double generating series

\displaystyle L_{B}(y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\geq 0}z^{x}{\langle y^{B_{x}}\rangle}

(2.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\geq 0}z^{x}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}\dots}f_{k_{1}}f_{k_{2}}\dots\sum_{n\geq 0}y^{x-(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n})}

\displaystyle{\hskip 4.0pt}\times{\bm{1}}(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}\leq x<k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}+k_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}\dots}f_{k_{1}}f_{k_{2}}\dots\sum_{x\geq 0}z^{x}y^{x-(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n})}

\displaystyle{\hskip 4.0pt}\times{\bm{1}}(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}\leq x<k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}+k_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}\dots}f_{k_{1}}f_{k_{2}}\dots(z^{k_{1}}\dots z^{k_{n}})

\displaystyle{\hskip 4.0pt}\times(1+yz+\cdots+(yz)^{k_{n+1}-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\hat{f}(z)^{n}\,\frac{1-\hat{f}(yz)}{1-yz}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\hat{f}(yz)}{(1-yz)(1-\hat{f}(z))}.

When the observation position
x
becomes very large,
the backward length
B_{x}
converges to a limit random variable
B
,
the equilibrium backward length,
whose distribution is given by

{\langle y^{B}\rangle}=\lim_{z\to 1}(1-z)L_{B}(y,z)=\frac{1-\hat{f}(y)}{(1-y){\langle H\rangle}}.

(2.12)

B.3
Distribution of
E_{x}

The distribution of the forward or excess length
E_{x}
is encoded in the double generating series

\displaystyle L_{E}(y,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\geq 0}z^{x}{\langle y^{E_{x}}\rangle}

(2.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\geq 0}z^{x}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}\dots}f_{k_{1}}f_{k_{2}}\dots\sum_{n\geq 0}y^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}+k_{n+1}-x}

\displaystyle{\hskip 4.0pt}\times{\bm{1}}(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}\leq x<k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}+k_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}\dots}f_{k_{1}}f_{k_{2}}\dots\sum_{x\geq 0}z^{x}y^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}+k_{n+1}-x}

\displaystyle{\hskip 4.0pt}\times{\bm{1}}(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}\leq x<k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}+k_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{k_{1}k_{2}\dots}f_{k_{1}}f_{k_{2}}\dots(z^{k_{1}}\dots z^{k_{n}})

\displaystyle{\hskip 4.0pt}\times(y^{k_{n+1}}+y^{k_{n+1}-1}z+\cdots+yz^{k_{n+1}-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\hat{f}(z)^{n}\,\frac{y(\hat{f}(z)-\hat{f}(y))}{z-y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{y(\hat{f}(z)-\hat{f}(y))}{(z-y)(1-\hat{f}(z))}.

When the observation position
x
becomes very large,
the forward length
E_{x}
converges to a limit random variable
E
,
the equilibrium forward length,
whose distribution is given by

{\langle y^{E}\rangle}=\lim_{z\to 1}(1-z)L_{E}(y,z)=\frac{y(1-\hat{f}(y))}{(1-y){\langle H\rangle}}.

(2.14)

Comparing (
2.12
) and (
2.14
) yields the identity

E=B+1.

(2.15)

The two lengths coincide for continuous renewal processes at equilibrium.
For integer processes, however, this symmetry is explicitly broken by the definition (
2.3
) of
N_{x}
.
