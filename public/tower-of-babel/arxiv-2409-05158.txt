Derived equivalences for the derived discrete algebras are standard
Grzegorz Bobiński, Tomasz Ciborski

Abstract
. We prove that any derived equivalence between derived discrete algebras is standard, i.e. is isomorphic to the derived tensor product by a two-sided tilting complex.

1.
Introduction and the main result

Throughout the paper
\Bbbk
denotes an algebraically closed field of arbitrary characteristic. All considered categories and functors are assumed to be
\Bbbk
-linear.

For a finite dimensional
\Bbbk
-algebra
A
we denote by
\mod A
the category of finite dimensional left
A
-modules and by
\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod A)
the bounded derived category of
\mod A
. Algebras
A
and
B
are said to be
derived equivalent
if there exists a triangle equivalence between
\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod A)
and
\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod B)
(such equivalences are called
derived equivalences
between
A
and
B
). Rickard has proved
[
20
]
*Theorem 3.3 that if algebras
A
and
B
are derived equivalent, then there exists a complex
X
of
B
-
A
-bimodules such that the derived tensor product
X\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{A}-\colon\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod A)\to\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod B)
is a derived equivalence. A derived equivalence is called
standard
if it is isomorphic to a derived equivalence of the form
X\otimes^{\mathbb{L}}_{A}-
, for a complex
X
of
B
-
A
-bimodules. An open question posed by Rickard is whether every derived equivalence between finite dimensional
\Bbbk
-algebras is standard. This question has been answered affirmatively for some classes of algebras, including the triangular and (anti-)Fano algebras
[
14
,
16
,
17
]
. Moreover, Orlov’s theorem
[
18
]
*Theorem 2.2 stating that every derived equivalence between smooth projective varieties is a Fourier–Mukai transform can be viewed as a geometric analogue of these results.

Derived discrete algebras are algebras introduced by Vossieck
[
22
]
with nontrivial derived categories which are still accessible for direct calculations. This class of algebras is closed under derived equivalences and has been an object of intensive studies. It serves as a test case for verifying and studying homological conjectures and problems (see for example
[
1
,
8
,
5
,
6
,
11
,
9
,
10
,
19
]
). In particular, Chen and Zhang
[
13
]
*Theorem 3.6 have proved that the derived equivalences between derived discrete algebras of finite global dimension are standard. The following main result of the paper extends this result to arbitrary derived discrete algebras.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
A
and
B
are derived discrete
\Bbbk
-algebras, then every derived equivalence between
A
and
B
is standard.

Recall that if
A
and
B
are derived equivalent, then
A
is of finite global dimension if and only if
B
is of finite global dimension (see
[
15
]
*Lemma III.1.5). Consequently, in view of the above-mentioned result of Chen and Zhang (
[
13
]
*Theorem 3.6) Theorem
1.1
follows from the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
A
and
B
are derived discrete
\Bbbk
-algebras of infinite global dimension, then every derived equivalence between
A
and
B
is standard.

Theorem
1.2
has already been proved in some cases by Chen and Ye (see
[
12
]
*Section 7). In this paper we present a unified proof, which also covers the cases studied by Chen and Ye. A crucial fact we use in the proof is Proposition
4.10
, which allows us to use a criterion of Chen and Ye
[
12
]
(see Proposition
2.1
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we present a method developed by Chen and Ye in
[
12
]
which can be used to verify that, for a given algebra
A
, all derived equivalences starting at
\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod A)
are standard. We also give a list of representatives of the derived equivalence classes of the derived discrete algebras of infinite global dimension there. Next, Sections
3
and
4
are devoted to a description of the homotopy categories of projective modules for the derived discrete algebras of infinite global dimension. Finally in Section
5
we prove Theorem
1.2
.

Throughout the paper by
\mathbb{Z}
,

, and

, then

. Similarly, if

We introduce now basic definitions following the exposition in

. A

is a natural transformation from

.

Let

. We can identify

with the full subcategory of

, and

. It follows from

For a finite dimensional
\Bbbk

-algebra such that any pseudo-identity

According to

]

[

*Definition 4.1. By

]
*Theorem 6.1, this implies that the category

*Definition 5.1. Now our claim follows from
[

]
*Theorem 5.10.

3.
Objects and morphisms in the homotopy category of
\proj\Lambda(n,m)

Throughout this section we put
\Lambda:=\Lambda(n,m)
and
Q:=Q(n,m)
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. The aim of this section is to describe the indecomposable objects of the category
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
and the morphism between them.

3.1.
Objects

For
u\in[-m,n-1]
, denote by
P_{u}
the corresponding indecomposable projective
\Lambda
-module, i.e. the one whose top is the simple module concentrated at
u
. Similarly, for a path
\sigma
in
Q
from
u
to
v
, we denote by
P_{\sigma}
the corresponding map
P_{v}\to P_{u}
.

By abuse of language by a path in
\Lambda
we mean a path in
Q
which does not contain any of the zero relations
\alpha_{n-1}\alpha_{0}
,
\alpha_{n-2}\alpha_{n-1}
, …,
\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}
. For
u\in[-m,n-1]
we denote by
\sigma_{u}
the maximal path in
\Lambda
ending at
u
, i.e.

\sigma_{u}:=\begin{cases}\alpha_{u}\cdots\alpha_{0}&\text{if $u\in[-m,-1]$},\\
\alpha_{u}&\text{if $u\in[0,n-1]$}.\end{cases}

By
s(u)
we denote the starting vertex of the path
\sigma_{u}
. In this way we obtain a map
s\colon[-m,n-1]\to[-m,n-1]
which is given by the formula

s(u):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $u\in[-m,-1]$ and $n>1$},\\
u+1&\text{if $u\in[0,n-2]$ and $n>1$},\\
0&\text{if $u\in[-m,0]$ and $n=1$ or either $u=n-1$}.\end{cases}

If
s(u)=s(v)
and
u\leq v
, then there exists a unique path
\varsigma_{u,v}
such that
\sigma_{u}=\varsigma_{u,v}\sigma_{v}
. Note that
\varsigma_{u,v}
is the stationary path at
u
, if
v=u
, and
\varsigma_{u,v}=\alpha_{u}\cdots\alpha_{v-1}
, if
u<v
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of quadruples
(k,u,l,v)
such that
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
u,v\in[-m,n-1]
, and either
v=s^{l}(u)
or
v<s^{l}(u)\leq 0
, where by
s^{0}
we mean the identity map
[-m,n-1]\to[-m,n-1]
. Note that the condition
v<s^{l}(u)\leq 0
can be expanded to: either
l=0
and
v<u\leq 0
or
l>0
and
v<0=s^{l}(u)
.

For
(k,u,l,v)\in\mathcal{C}
, let
C_{k,u,l,v}
be the complex
(C_{k,u,l,v}^{i},d_{k,u,l,v}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of projective
\Lambda
-modules defined by:

\displaystyle C_{k,u,l,v}^{i}:=\begin{cases}P_{s^{i-k}(u)}&\text{if either $i\in[k,k+l]\setminus\{k+l-1\}$}\\
&\quad\text{or $i=k+l-1$, $v=s^{l}(u)$, and $l>0$},\\
P_{s^{l-1}(u)}\oplus P_{v}&\text{if $i=k+l-1$, $v<s^{l}(u)$, and $l>0$},\\
P_{v}&\text{if $i=k+l-1$, $v<s^{l}(u)$, and $l=0$},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle d_{k,u,l,v}^{i}:=\begin{cases}P_{\sigma_{s^{i-k}(u)}}&\text{if either $i\in[k,k+l-3]$}\\
&\quad\text{or $i=k+l-2$, $v=s^{l}(u)$, and $l\geq 2$},\\
&\quad\text{or $i=k+l-1$, $v=s^{l}(u)$, and $l\geq 1$},\\
[P_{\sigma_{s^{l-2}(u)}},0]^{\tr}&\text{$i=k+l-2$, $v<s^{l}(u)$, and $l\geq 2$},\\
[P_{\sigma_{s^{l-1}(u)}},P_{\varsigma_{v,s^{l}(u)}}]&\text{if $i=k+l-1$, $v<s^{l}(u)$, and $l\geq 1$},\\
P_{\varsigma_{v,s^{l}(u)}}&\text{if $i=k+l-1$, $v<s^{l}(u)$, and $l=0$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Following the convention used in
[
2
]
the complex
C_{k,u,l,v}
can be ‘unfold’ as

u\xrightarrow{\sigma_{u}^{*}}s(u)\to\cdots\to s^{l-1}(u)\xrightarrow{\sigma_{s^{l-1}(u)}^{*}}s^{l}(u),

if

, and

u\xrightarrow{\sigma_{u}^{*}}s(u)\to\cdots\to s^{l-1}(u)\xrightarrow{\sigma_{s^{l-1}(u)}^{*}}s^{l}(u)\xleftarrow{\varsigma_{v,\alpha_{s^{l}(u)}}^{*}}v,

if

, where the leftmost module appears in degree

and, if

, the part

u\xrightarrow{\sigma_{u}^{*}}s(u)\to\cdots\to s^{l-1}(u)\xrightarrow{\sigma_{s^{l-1}(u)}^{*}}s^{l}(u)

reduces to

. In the above we write

instead of

and
\sigma^{*}
instead of

. We will sometimes write the above diagrams in a unified way as

The following is the the first step in describing the category

.

Proposition 3.1
.

The complexes
C_{k,u,l,v}
,
(k,u,l,v)\in\mathcal{C}
, form a complete set of representatives of the isomorphism classes of the indecomposable objects in
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)

Proof.

Let
\overline{Q}
be the
double quiver
of
Q
, i.e. the quiver with the same set of vertices as
Q
and with an additional arrow
\overline{\alpha}\colon v\to u
, for each arrow
\alpha\colon u\to v
in

. If
\sigma=\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{l}
is a path in
\overline{Q}
, then we put
\overline{\sigma}:=\overline{\beta}_{l}\cdots\overline{\beta_{1}}
, where
\overline{\overline{\alpha}}=\alpha
, for each arrow
\alpha
of
Q
. Moreover,
\overline{\sigma}:=\sigma
, if
\sigma
is a stationary path.

By a

in

we mean a path in
\overline{Q}
which does not contain a path of the form
\alpha\overline{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is an arrow in
\overline{Q}
, as a subpath. Homotopy strings
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
are called

if
\theta^{\prime}=\theta
or
\theta^{\prime}=\overline{\theta}
. Up to this equivalence, the following is a complete list of homotopy strings in

:

•

stationary paths,

•

\alpha_{u}\cdots\alpha_{u+l}
,
u\in[-m,n-1]
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

\alpha_{u}\cdots\alpha_{u+l}\overline{\alpha}_{-1}\cdots\overline{\alpha}_{v}
,
u\in[-m,n-1]
,
v\in[-m,-1]
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
u+l\geq 0
, and
u+l\equiv n-1\pmod{n}
.

In the above formulas, if
w\geq n
, then
\alpha_{w}
denotes
\alpha_{r}
, where
r
is the reminder of
w
divided by
n
.

For a pair
(k,\theta)
consisting of an integer
k
and a homotopy string
\theta
one defines (see
[
7
]
*Section 3) a complex

in such a way that:

•

, if

is the stationary path at vertex

;

•

X_{k,\theta}=C_{k+1,u+l+1,0,u}
, if

,

,

, and

;

•

X_{k,\theta}=C_{k,u,u+l+1,v}
, where

is the reminder of

divided by
n
, if
\theta=\alpha_{u}\cdots\alpha_{u+l}
,
u\in[-m,-1]
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
, and
u+l\geq 0
,

•

X_{k,\theta}=C_{k,u,l+1,v}
, where
v
is the reminder of
u+l+1
divided by
n
, if
\theta=\alpha_{u}\cdots\alpha_{u+l}
,
u\in[0,n-1]
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

X_{k,\theta}=C_{k,u,u+l+1,v}
, if
\theta=\alpha_{u}\cdots\alpha_{u+l}\overline{\alpha}_{-1}\cdots\overline{\alpha}_{v}
,
u,v\in[-m,-1]
,

,
u+l\geq 0
, and
u+l\equiv n-1\pmod{n}
.

•

X_{k,\theta}=C_{k,u,l+1,v}
, if
\theta=\alpha_{u}\cdots\alpha_{u+l}\overline{\alpha}_{-1}\cdots\overline{\alpha}_{v}
,

,

,

,

, and

Observe that all the quadruples

and (up to the equivalence of the homotopy strings) all the pairs

appear in the above formulas.

Since

]

and the complexes

∎

3.2.
Morphisms

Our next aim is to describe bases of the morphism spaces between the complexes defined in subsection

For a quadruple
(k,u,l,v)\in\mathcal{C}
let

be the subset of

consisting of the quadruples

such that:

•

,

•

(consequently,

•

if

, then

, and

•

if

and

, then

[
2
]
). Note that in all the below diagrams the dashed arrows may not appear.

(L2)

k+l=k^{\prime}+l^{\prime}+1

v<s^{l}(u)

v^{\prime}<v
or
v^{\prime}=s^{l^{\prime}}(u^{\prime})
.

4.
A model of the homotopy category of
\proj\Lambda(n,m)

Throughout this section we still put
\Lambda:=\Lambda(n,m)
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. The aim of this section is to present a model of the category
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
. We will follow the description presented in
[
8
]
*Section 5, given there without a proof. For convenience of the reader, we include the main steps of the proof below. We also prove necessary facts about irreducible morphisms in
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
here.

4.1.
The category

We are now ready to describe the full subcategory
\ind\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
of
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
formed by the indecomposable objects. We will present it as the quotient
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
of the path category
\Bbbk\Gamma
of a quiver
\Gamma
by an ideal
\mathcal{I}
.

First we put

\Gamma_{0}:=\{(i,a,b):\text{$i\in[0,n-1]$, $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$, $a\leq b+\delta_{i,0}\cdot m$}\},

where
\delta_{x,y}
is the Kronecker delta. For each
V=(i,a,b)\in\Gamma_{0}
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{V}:=\{(i,x,y):\text{$x\in[a,b+\delta_{i,0}\cdot m]$, $y\in[b,\infty)$}\}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{V}:=\{(i+1,x,y):\text{$x\in(-\infty,a+\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m]$, $y\in[a,b+\delta_{i,0}\cdot m]$}\},

where
i+1
is calculated modulo
n
. Then

\Gamma_{1}:=\{f_{U,V}\colon V\to U:\text{$V\in\Gamma_{0}$, $U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}$, $U\neq V$}\}\cup\{g_{U,V}\colon V\to U:\text{$V\in\Gamma_{0}$, $U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}$}\}.

Finally, let
\mathcal{I}
be the ideal generated by the relations

\displaystyle f_{W,U}\circ f_{U,V}-f_{W,V},

\displaystyle V\in\Gamma_{0},\,U\in\mathcal{F}_{V},\,U\neq V,\,W\in\mathcal{F}_{U},\,W\neq U,

\displaystyle g_{W,U}\circ f_{U,V}-g_{W,V},

\displaystyle V\in\Gamma_{0},\,U\in\mathcal{F}_{V},\,U\neq V,\,W\in\mathcal{G}_{U},

\displaystyle f_{W,U}\circ g_{U,V}-g_{W,V},

\displaystyle V\in\Gamma_{0},\,U\in\mathcal{G}_{V},\,W\in\mathcal{F}_{U},\,W\neq U,

\displaystyle g_{W,U}\circ g_{U,V},

\displaystyle V\in\Gamma_{0},\,U\in\mathcal{G}_{V},\,W\in\mathcal{G}_{U},

where
f_{W,V}:=0
(
g_{W,V}:=0
) if
W\not\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
(
W\not\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
respectively).

We will denote by
f_{V,V}
the identify morphism
\Id_{V}
, for
V\in\Gamma_{0}
. Obviously
f_{U,V}\circ f_{V,V}=f_{U,V}
=
f_{U,U}\circ f_{U,V}
,
g_{W,V}\circ f_{V,V}=g_{W,V}=f_{W,W}\circ g_{W,V}
, for all
V\in\Gamma_{0}
,
U\in\mathcal{F}_{U}
,
W\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
. If
V\in\Gamma_{0}
and
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
, then we put

g_{U,V}^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}g_{U,V}&\text{if $U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Observe that
n=1
provided
g_{U,V}^{\prime}\neq 0
.

We will often use the following observation about the morphism spaces in
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
without reference.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
V,U\in\Gamma_{0}
.

(1)

If
U\not\in\mathcal{F}_{V}\cup\mathcal{G}_{V}
, then
\Hom(V,U)=0
.

(2)

If
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{V}
, then
\dim_{\Bbbk}\Hom(V,U)=1
, with basis formed by
f_{U,V}
.

(3)

If
U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{V}
, then
\dim_{\Bbbk}\Hom(V,U)=1
, with basis formed by
g_{U,V}
.

(4)

If
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}\cap\mathcal{G}_{V}
, then
\dim_{\Bbbk}\Hom(V,U)=2
, with basis formed by
f_{U,V}
and
g_{U,V}
.

In particular, if
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
, then
f_{U,V}
and
g_{U,V}^{\prime}
span
\Hom(V,U)
.

Proof.

In the paper, we usually do not distinguish between morphisms in
\Bbbk\Gamma
(in particular, arrows in
\Gamma
) and the corresponding morphisms in
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
, since it should not lead to misunderstanding. However, in this proof, for a morphism
\chi
in
\Bbbk\Gamma
we will denote by
[\chi]
the corresponding morphism in
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
.

We introduce a grading in the homomorphism spaces in
\Bbbk\Gamma
by putting
|f_{U,V}|=0
and
|g_{W,V}|=1
, for
V\in\Gamma_{0}
,
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
, and
W\in\mathcal{G}_{U}
. Since the relations defining the ideal
\mathcal{I}
are homogenous with respect to the above grading, we have the induced grading in the homomorphism spaces in
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
. If, for
V,U\in\Gamma_{0}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Hom^{(d)}(V,U)
denotes the space of morphisms
V\to U
in
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
of degree
d
(with respect to the above grading), then we easily see that
\Hom^{(d)}(V,U)=0
, for
d\geq 2
. Moreover,
\Hom^{(0)}(V,U)
is either
0
(if
U\not\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
) or is spanned by
[f_{U,V}]
(if
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
). Similarly,
\Hom^{(1)}(V,U)
is either
0
(if
U\not\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
) or is spanned by
[g_{U,V}]
(if
U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
). Thus in order to finish the proof, we need to show that
[f_{U,V}]\neq 0
and
[g_{W,V}]\neq 0
, for all
V\in\Gamma_{0}
,
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
, and
W\in\mathcal{G}_{U}
.

Fix
V\in\Gamma_{0}
and
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
. Let
\mathcal{P}:=\mathcal{P}_{U,V}
be the set of the sequences
(W_{0},\ldots,W_{l})
such that
W_{0}=V
,
W_{l}=U
, and
W_{p}\in\mathcal{F}_{W_{p-1}}
and
W_{p}\neq W_{p-1}
, for all
p\in[1,l]
. Note that the assumption
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
implies that
W_{p}\in\mathcal{F}_{W_{q}}
, for all
q\leq p
. For
(W_{0},\ldots,W_{l})\in\mathcal{P}
, we put

\omega_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{l}}:=f_{W_{l},W_{l-1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{W_{1},W_{0}}

(in particular, if
V=U
, then
\omega_{V}:=\Id_{V}
). If, moreover,
l\geq 2
and
p\in[0,l-2]
, then we put

\rho_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{l},p}:=\omega_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{l}}-\omega_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{p},W_{p+2},\ldots,W_{l}}.

Let
(\Bbbk\Gamma)^{(0)}(V,U)
the space of morphisms
V\to U
in
\Bbbk\Gamma
of degree
0
and
\mathcal{I}^{(0)}(V,U):=(\Bbbk\Gamma)^{(0)}(V,U)\cap\mathcal{I}
. One easily observes that
(\Bbbk\Gamma)^{(0)}(V,U)
is spanned by the morphisms
\omega_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{l}}
, for
(W_{0},\ldots,W_{l})\in\mathcal{P}
, while
\mathcal{I}^{(0)}(V,U)
is spanned by the morphisms
\rho_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{l},p}
, for
(W_{0},\ldots,W_{l})\in\mathcal{P}
with
l\geq 2
and
p\in[0,l-2]
. In fact, we can find a smaller spanning set of
\mathcal{I}^{(0)}(V,U)
. Namely,
\mathcal{I}^{(0)}(V,U)
is spanned by the morphisms
\rho_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{l},0}
, for
(W_{0},\ldots,W_{l})\in\mathcal{P}
with
l\geq 2
, which follows from the equality

\rho_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{l},p}=\sum_{q\in[1,p+1]}\rho_{W_{0},W_{q},\ldots W_{l},0}-\sum_{q\in[1,p]}\rho_{W_{0},W_{q},\ldots,W_{p},W_{p+2},\ldots,W_{l},0}.

It is an easy exercise in linear algebra to show that

\mathcal{I}^{(0)}(V,U)+\Bbbk\cdot f_{U,V}=(\Bbbk\Gamma)^{(0)}(V,U)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{I}^{(0)}(V,U)\cap\Bbbk\cdot f_{U,V}=0

(we use here, that if we order the elements of
\mathcal{P}
in such a way that longer sequences precede shorter ones, then
\omega_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{l}}
is the leading term of
\rho_{W_{0},\ldots,W_{l},0}
). Now the Second Isomorphism Theorem implies that the canonical map
\Bbbk\cdot f_{U,V}\to(\Bbbk\Gamma)^{(0)}(V,U)/\mathcal{I}^{(0)}(V,U)
is an isomorphism, hence in particular
[f_{U,V}]\neq 0
.

The proof that
[g_{W,V}]\neq 0
, for all
V\in\Gamma_{0}
and
W\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
, is analogous.
∎

The following proposition is the main result of this subsection and describes the aforementioned equivalence between
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
and
\ind\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let

\Theta(i,p\cdot(m+n)+r+i,q\cdot(m+n)+t+i-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)\\
:=\begin{cases}C_{-q\cdot n-t-i,-t,(q-p)\cdot n+(t-r),-r}&\text{if $r\in[-n+1,0]$ and $t\in[-n+1,-1]$},\\
C_{-q\cdot n-i,-m+t,(q-p)\cdot n-r,-r}&\text{if $r\in[-n+1,0]$, $t\in[0,m]$},\\
&\qquad\text{and $(q-p)\cdot n-r>0$},\\
C_{-q\cdot n-i,-m+t,0,-m+t}&\text{if $r\in[-n+1,0]$, $t\in[0,m]$},\\
&\qquad\text{and $(q-p)\cdot n-r=0$},\\
C_{-q\cdot n-t-i,-t,(q-p)\cdot n+t,-m-1+r}&\text{if $r\in[1,m]$ and $t\in[-n+1,-1]$},\\
C_{-q\cdot n-i,-m+t,(q-p)\cdot n,-m-1+r}&\text{if $r\in[1,m]$ and $t\in[0,m]$},\end{cases}

for
i\in[0,n-1]
,
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
,
r,t\in[-n+1,m]
, such that
p\cdot(m+n)+r\leq q\cdot(m+n)+t
. If

\Theta(f_{U,V}):=\varphi_{\Theta(U),\Theta(V)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Theta(g_{W,V}):=\psi_{\Theta(W),\Theta(V)},

for
V\in\Gamma_{0}
,
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
,
W\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
, then
\Theta
induces an equivalence
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}\xrightarrow{\sim}\ind\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
.

Before giving the proof we explain how
\Theta
on vertices of
\Gamma
is constructed. One gets from
[
7
]
*Corollary 6.3 (see also
[
4
]
*Lemma 3.1) that for each
i\in\{-m\}\cup[1,n-1]
there exists a unique (up to scalar and radical square) irreducible map in
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
starting at
P_{i}
(which we identify with
C_{0,i,0,i}
). On the other hand, we will prove (see Corollary
4.9
) that there is a unique (up to scalar and radical square) irreducible map in
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
starting at
V
if and only if
V=(i,a,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
, for some
i\in[0,n-1]
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus we can start defining
\Theta
by putting
\Theta(0,0,-m):=C_{0,-m,0,-m}
. Observe that there are nonzero maps
P_{-m}\to P_{1}
and
P_{i-1}\to P_{i}
,
i\in[2,n-1]
, provided
n>1
. Since
(i,0,0)
and
(i-1,0,-\delta_{i-1,0}\cdot m)
are the only objects of the form
(j,a,a-\delta_{j,0}\cdot m)
with nonzero maps from
(i-1,0,-\delta_{i-1,0}\cdot m)
, by easy induction it follows that we have to put
\Theta(i,0,0):=C_{0,i,0,i}
,
i\in[1,n-1]
.

We will show in Corollary
4.8
that an irreducible map starting at
(i,a,b)
terminates at either
(i,a,b+1)
or
(i,a+1,b)
(note that the latter object may not exist). On the other hand, using
[
7
]
*Main Theorem, Part II we can obtain a similar result about irreducible maps in
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
. More precisely, if
C:=C_{k,u,l,v}
then there is an irreducible map
C\to C^{\prime}
, where

C^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}C_{k-1,u-1,l+1,v}&\text{if $u>1$},\\
C_{k-1,-m,l+1,v}&\text{if either $n>1$ and $u=1$ or $n=1$ and $u=0$},\\
C_{k-1,n-1,l+1,v}&\text{if $n>1$ and $u=0$},\\
C_{k,u+1,l,v}&\text{if $u<0$}.\end{cases}

Moreover,
unless
l=0
and either
u=v\in\{-m\}\cup[1,n-1]
or
v+1=u
, we also have an irreducible map
C\to C^{\prime\prime}
, where

C^{\prime\prime}:=\begin{cases}C_{k,u,l-1,v-1}&\text{if $l>0$ and $v>0$},\\
C_{k,u,l,-m}&\text{if either $l>0$, $v=0$, and $m>0$},\\
&\qquad\text{or $l=0$ and $-m<u=v\leq 0$},\\
C_{k,u,l-1,n-1}&\text{if $l>0$ and either $v=0$ and $m=0$ or $v=-1$},\\
C_{k,u,l,v+1}&\text{if either $l>0$ and $v<-1$ or $l=0$ and $v+1<u$}.\end{cases}

Using the above observations, one can deduce the required formula for
\Theta
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Observe that if
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
,
r,t\in[-n+1,0]
, then
p\cdot(m+n)+r\leq q\cdot(m+n)+t
if and only if
p\cdot n+r\leq q\cdot n+t
. As a first consequence we obtain that
\Theta
is well-defined on the vertices of
\Gamma
. Secondly, we use the above observation to show that, for each
V\in\Gamma_{0}
,

(4.3)

\Theta(\mathcal{F}_{V})=\Phi_{\Theta(V)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Theta(\mathcal{F}_{V})=\Psi_{\Theta(V)},

where
\Phi_{C_{k,u,l,v}}:=\Phi_{k,u,l,v}
and
\Psi_{C_{k,u,l,v}}:=\Psi_{k,u,l,v}
. This implies in particular that
\Theta
is well-defined on the arrows of
\Gamma
. Since obviously
\Theta(\Id_{V})=\Theta(f_{V,V})=\varphi_{\Theta(V),\Theta(V)}=\Id_{\Theta(V)}
, we get a functor
\Bbbk\Gamma\to\ind\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
, which we also denote by
\Theta
. Again by checking the indices we see that
\Theta
is dense. Moreover, formulas (
4.3
) imply that
\Theta
is full.

In order to show that
\Theta
induces a functor
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}\to\ind\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
, we have to verify that the relations defining the ideal
\mathcal{I}
are satisfied, i.e.

\displaystyle\varphi_{\Theta(W),\Theta(U)}\circ\varphi_{\Theta(U),\Theta(V)}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\Theta(W),\Theta(V)},

\displaystyle V\in\Gamma_{0},\,U\in\mathcal{F}_{V},\,W\in\mathcal{F}_{U},\,

\displaystyle\psi_{\Theta(W),\Theta(U)}\circ\varphi_{\Theta(U),\Theta(V)}

\displaystyle=\psi_{\Theta(W),\Theta(V)},

\displaystyle V\in\Gamma_{0},\,U\in\mathcal{F}_{V},\,W\in\mathcal{G}_{U},

\displaystyle\varphi_{\Theta(W),\Theta(U)}\circ\psi_{\Theta(U),\Theta(V)}

\displaystyle=\psi_{\Theta(W),\Theta(V)},

\displaystyle V\in\Gamma_{0},\,U\in\mathcal{G}_{V},\,W\in\mathcal{F}_{U},\,

\displaystyle\psi_{\Theta(W),\Theta(U)}\circ\psi_{\Theta(U),\Theta(V)}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle V\in\Gamma_{0},\,U\in\mathcal{G}_{V},\,W\in\mathcal{G}_{U},

where
\varphi_{C^{\prime},C}:=0
(
\psi_{C^{\prime},C}:=0
) if
C^{\prime}\not\in\Phi_{C}
(
C^{\prime}\not\in\Psi_{C}
, respectively). This is done by simple, but lengthy case by case analysis, so we only give an example here, which will also illustrate that in general these relations hold only up to homotopy.

Take
V\in\Gamma_{0}
,
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
and
W\in\mathcal{G}_{U}
, and write

\displaystyle V=(i,p\cdot(m+n)+r+i,q\cdot(m+n)+t+i-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m),

\displaystyle U=(i,p^{\prime}\cdot(m+n)+r^{\prime}+i,q^{\prime}\cdot(m+n)+t^{\prime}+i-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m),

and

\displaystyle W=(j,p^{\prime\prime}\cdot(m+n)+r^{\prime\prime}+j,q^{\prime\prime}\cdot(m+n)+t^{\prime\prime}+j-\delta_{j,0}\cdot m),

for
i\in[0,n-1]
,
p,q,p^{\prime},q^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
r,t,r^{\prime},t^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime\prime}\in[-n+1,m]
, where
j:=i+1
, if
i<n-1
, and
j:=0
, if
i=n-1
. Assume also that
r,r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in[-n+1,0]
and
t,t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}\in[-n+1,-1]\cup\{m\}
. Put

\displaystyle u:=\begin{cases}-t&\text{if $t<0$},\\
0&\text{if $t=m$},\end{cases}\quad u^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}-t^{\prime}&\text{if $t^{\prime}<0$},\\

4.2.
Irreducible morphisms

As a final step in this section we describe the irreducible morphisms between the objects of
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
. We start with the following easy observation, whose proof is left to the reader.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
V,U\in\Gamma_{0}
and
f\in\Hom(V,U)
. Then
f
is an isomorphism if and only if
U=V
and
f=\lambda\cdot f_{V,V}+\mu\cdot g_{V,V}^{\prime}
, for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk
,
\lambda\neq 0
. ∎

Recall that by the
radical
of a Krull–Schmidt category we mean the ideal consisting of the maps
f\colon X\to Y
such that, for each split monomorphism
\iota\colon X^{\prime}\to X
and each split epimorphism with
\pi\colon Y\to Y^{\prime}
, with
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
indecomposable,
\pi\circ f\circ\iota
is not an isomorphism. As a first immediate consequence of Lemma
4.5
we get the following (recall the we treat the category
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
as a subcategory
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
).

Corollary 4.6
.

The radical of the category
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
coincides with the ideal
\langle\Gamma_{1}\rangle
generated by the arrows in
\Gamma
. ∎

For each arrow
\gamma
in
\Gamma_{1}
we define its
degree

\deg\gamma
in the following way. If
V=(i,a,b)\in\Gamma_{0}
and
U=(j,x,y)\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
(in particular,
j=i
),
U\neq V
, then we put
\deg f_{U,V}:=(x-a)+(y-b)
. Moreover, if
V\in\Gamma_{0}
and
U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
, then
\deg g_{U,V}:=\infty
. These definitions extend naturally to the paths in
\Gamma
(trivial paths having degree
0
). We have the following.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
V\in\Gamma_{0}
. If
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
, then
f_{U,V}\in\rad^{\deg f_{U,V}}(V,U)\setminus\rad^{\deg f_{U,V}+1}(V,U)
. Similarly, if
U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
, then
g_{U,V}\in\rad^{\infty}(V,U)
.

Proof.

In this proof, similarly as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, we will distinguish between morphisms in
\Bbbk\Gamma
and
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
. In particular, for a morphism
\chi
in
\Bbbk\Gamma
we will denote by
[\chi]
the corresponding morphism in
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
.

For
V,U\in\Gamma_{0}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, let
(\Bbbk\Gamma)_{k}(V,U)
be the subspace of
\Hom_{\Bbbk\Gamma}(V,U)
spanned by the paths of degree
k
. Obviously,
\Hom_{\Bbbk\Gamma}(V,U)=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}(\Bbbk\Gamma)_{k}(V,U)
. Observe that the ideal
\mathcal{I}
is homogenous with respect to the above grading, i.e. if we put
\mathcal{I}_{k}(V,U):=(\Bbbk\Gamma)_{k}(V,U)\cap\mathcal{I}(V,U)
, then
\mathcal{I}(V,U)=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}\mathcal{I}_{k}(V,U)
. Consequently,
\Hom_{\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}}(V,U)=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}\mathcal{R}_{k}(V,U)
, where
\mathcal{R}_{k}(V,U):=(\Bbbk\Gamma)_{k}(V,U)/\mathcal{I}_{k}(V,U)
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.

We know from Corollary

that
\rad_{\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}}(V,U)=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}}\mathcal{R}_{k}(V,U)
. Now it is fairly easy to show that
\rad_{\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}}^{d}(V,U)=\bigoplus_{k\geq d}\mathcal{R}_{k}(V,U)

– in order to prove this, we use two easy observations: first, if
\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}
and
\deg\gamma<\infty
, then the map
[\gamma]
is a composition in
\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}
of
\deg\gamma

\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}
and

such that

\deg\gamma^{\prime\prime}=1

If

Let

(1)

(2)

is irreducible with

, where

, then there exists an automorphism
\phi\in\Aut(U)
such that
\phi\circ f=f_{U,V}
.

Dually:

(1’)

If
U=(i,a-1,b)
and
f\colon U\to V
is irreducible, then there exists an automorphism
\phi\in\Aut(U)
such that
f\circ\phi=f_{U,V}
.

(2’)

If
b=a+1-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m
,
U=(i,a,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
, and
f\colon U\to V
is irreducible with
f_{W,V}\circ f=0
, where
W:=(i,a+1,a+1-\delta_{i,0})
, then there exists an automorphism
\phi\in\Aut(U)
such that
f\circ\phi=f_{U,V}
.

Proof.

(
2
) We know from Corollary
4.8
that
f=\lambda\cdot f_{U,V}+\mu\cdot g_{U,V}^{\prime}
, for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk
,
\lambda\neq 0
. We show that the condition
f\circ f_{V,W}=0
implies
\mu\cdot g_{U,V}^{\prime}=0
. Indeed, assume that
g_{U,V}^{\prime}\neq 0
, i.e.
U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
. This means

,
i=0
, and

a+1\leq a+m\qquad\text{and}\qquad a\leq a+1-m\leq a+1,

which implies

. Consequently,

, thus
g_{U,V}^{\prime}\circ f_{V,W}=g_{U,V}\circ f_{V,W}=g_{U,W}\neq 0
. Since

, we get
0=f\circ f_{V,W}=\mu\cdot g_{U,W}
, hence
\mu=0
, thus
\mu\cdot g_{U,V}^{\prime}=0
.

As
\mu\cdot g_{U,V}^{\prime}=0
, we can put
\phi:=\lambda^{-1}\cdot f_{U,U}
, and get
\phi\in\Aut(U)
such that
\phi\circ f=f_{U,V}
.

(
1
) Again
f=\lambda\cdot f_{U,V}+\mu\cdot g_{U,V}^{\prime}
, for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk
,
\lambda\neq 0
, by Corollary
4.8
. We put

\phi:=\lambda^{-1}\cdot f_{U,U}-\lambda^{-2}\cdot\mu\cdot g_{U,U}^{\prime}
. Then
\phi\in\Aut(U)
(by Lemma
4.5
) and by direct calculations (similarly as above we show that
g_{U,U}^{\prime}\neq 0
provided
g_{U,V}^{\prime}\neq 0
) we get
\phi\circ f=f_{U,V}
.

Proofs of statements (
1’
) and (
2’
) are dual.
∎

5.
Proof of the main result

Throughout this section again
\Lambda:=\Lambda(n,m)
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We also fix a pseudo-identity
(F,\omega)
on
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
. Our aim is to show that
(F,\omega)
is isomorphic, as a triangle functor, to the identity functor
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\Id_{\Sigma})
on
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
, where
\Sigma
is (as usual) the suspension functor.

We will freely use notation introduced in Section
4
. In particular, by
\Gamma
we mean the quiver introduced in subsection
4.1
.

5.1.
Construction of the isomorphism

In this subsection we construct automorphisms
\phi_{U}\in\Aut(U)
,
U\in\Gamma_{0}
, which satisfy (as we will show in the next subsection) the condition
\phi_{U}\circ F(f)=f\circ\phi_{V}
, for all
V,U\in\Gamma_{0}
and morphisms
f\colon V\to U
. We will then use these automorphisms to construct a natural isomorphism between
(F,\omega)
and
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})
, for some connecting isomorphism
\omega^{\prime}\colon\Sigma\to\Sigma
.

Before we proceed with the construction, we explain how our method differs from the one used by Chen and Zhang in
[
13
]
. In the cases considered in
[
13
]
, for each indecomposable object
U
of the homotopy category which is not (homotopy equivalent to) a stalk complex, there exists a stalk complex
V
such that
\dim_{\Bbbk}\Hom(V,U)=1
. Consequently, if
f\colon V\to U
is a nonzero map, then
F(f)=\lambda\cdot f
, for some scalar
\lambda
, and they use
\lambda
to define the required automorphism
\phi_{U}
.

In our case, the above property may not hold. Thus in order to construct
\phi_{U}
, we use an inductive procedure based on Proposition
4.10
, which uses a properly chosen irreducible morphism starting or terminating at
U
.

Fix
i\in[0,n-1]
. As mentioned above we construct automorphisms
\phi_{U}
for
U=(i,a,b)
,
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a\leq b+\delta_{i,0}\cdot m
, inductively.

First we put
\phi_{U}:=f_{U,U}(=\Id_{U})
, for
U=(i,0,b)
,
b\in[-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m,0]
. Note that if
i=0
and
j\in[-m,-1]
, then
\phi_{U}\circ F(f_{U,V})\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}=f_{U,V}
, where
V:=(0,0,j)
and
U:=(0,0,j+1)
, since
F(f_{U,V})=f_{U,V}
, as
F
is a pseudo-identity and
V
and
U
are projective modules (by Corollary
4.4
).

Next assume that
b\geq 0
,
U=(i,0,b+1)
, and
\phi_{V}
, where
V:=(i,0,b)
, is already constructed. Since
f_{U,V}
is irreducible by Corollary
4.8
,
F
is an equivalence, and
\phi_{V}
is an automorphism,
F(f_{U,V})\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}
is irreducible. Using Proposition
4.10
(
1
) we find an automorphism
\phi_{U}\in\Aut(U)
such that
\phi_{U}\circ F(f_{U,V})\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}=f_{U,V}
.

Now assume that
a\geq 0
,
U=(i,a+1,a+1-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
, and
\phi_{V}
and
\phi_{W}
such that
\phi_{V}\circ F(f_{V,W})\circ\phi_{W}^{-1}=f_{V,W}
, where
V:=(i,a,a+1-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
and
W:=(i,a,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
, are already known. Similarly as above
F(f_{U,V})\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}
is irreducible. Moreover,

F(f_{U,V})\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}\circ f_{V,W}=F(f_{U,V})\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}\circ\phi_{V}\circ F(f_{V,W})\circ\phi_{W}^{-1}\\
=F(f_{U,V}\circ f_{V,W})\circ\phi_{W}^{-1}=F(0)\circ\phi_{W}^{-1}=0,

as
U\not\in\mathcal{F}_{W}
. Consequently there exists an automorphism
\phi_{U}\in\Aut(U)
such that
\phi_{U}\circ F(f_{U,V})\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}=f_{U,V}
by Proposition
4.10
(
2
).

Finally assume that
a>0
,
b\geq a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m
,
U=(i,a,b+1)
, and
\phi_{V}
, where
V:=(i,a,b)
, is already constructed. In this case we use Proposition
4.10
(
1
) again in order to find an automorphism
\phi_{U}\in\Aut(U)
such that
\phi_{U}\circ F(f_{U,V})\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}=f_{U,V}
.

The automorphisms
\phi_{U}
, for
U=(i,a,b)
with
a<0
, are defined similarly. Namely, for a given
a<0
we first define
\phi_{U}
, for
U=(i,a,a+1-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
, using Proposition
4.10
(
1’
) and that
\phi_{V}
, where
V:=(i,a+1,a+1-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
, is already defined. Next we construct
\phi_{U}
, for
U=(i,a,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
using Proposition
4.10
(
2’
), and finally
\phi_{U}
, for
U=(i,a,b)
with
b>a+1-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m
, using Proposition
4.10
(
1
) and induction on
b
.

5.2.
Verification

We verify now that
\phi_{U}\circ F(f)=f\circ\phi_{V}
(equivalently,
F^{\prime}(f)=f
, where
F^{\prime}(f):=\phi_{U}\circ F(f)\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}
), for all
V,U\in\Gamma_{0}
and morphisms
f\colon V\to U
, where
\phi_{U}
,
U\in\Gamma_{0}
, are the automorphisms constructed in the previous subsection. In fact it is enough to prove the above equalities for
f=f_{U,V}
(if
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
,
U\neq V
) and
f=g_{U,V}
(if
U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
).

By the above construction we know that
F^{\prime}(f_{U,V})=f_{U,V}
in the following cases:

(1)

V=(i,a,b)
and
U=(i,a,b+1)
,

(2)

V=(i,a,a+1-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
and
U=(i,a+1,a+1-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
.

We show first that the above formula holds for the remaining arrows of degree
1
, i.e. for
V=(i,a,b)
and
U=(i,a+1,b)
, where
b>a+1-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m
. Put
W:=(i,a,b-1)
and
V^{\prime}:=(i,a+1,b-1)
. By the above observations and the induction hypothesis

F^{\prime}(f_{V,W})=f_{V,W},\qquad F^{\prime}(f_{V^{\prime},W})=f_{V^{\prime},W},\qquad F^{\prime}(f_{U,V^{\prime}})=f_{U,V^{\prime}}.

Since
F^{\prime}(f_{U,V})\in\Hom(U,V)
,
F^{\prime}(f_{U,V})=\lambda\cdot f_{U,V}+\mu\cdot g_{U,V}^{\prime}
, for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk
. Using that
f_{U,V}\circ f_{V,W}=f_{U,W}=f_{U,V^{\prime}}\circ f_{V^{\prime},W}
, we get

F^{\prime}(f_{U,V})\circ F^{\prime}(f_{V,W})=F^{\prime}(f_{U,V^{\prime}})\circ F^{\prime}(f_{V^{\prime},W}).

Consequently,

\lambda\cdot f_{U,W}+\mu\cdot g_{U,V}^{\prime}\circ f_{V,W}=f_{U,W}.

This immediately implies
\lambda=1
and
\mu\cdot g_{U,V}^{\prime}\circ f_{V,W}=0
. As a result
F^{\prime}(f_{U,V})=f_{U,V}
, provided
g_{U,V}^{\prime}=0
, thus assume that
g_{U,V}^{\prime}\neq 0
. Then
U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
and this implies
U\in\mathcal{G}_{W}
, thus
g_{U,V}^{\prime}\circ f_{V,W}=g_{U,V}\circ f_{V,W}=g_{U,W}\neq 0
. Consequently,
\mu=0
and again
F^{\prime}(f_{U,V})=f_{U,V}
.

Now let
V
and
U
be arbitrary such that
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
,
U\neq V
. We know that
f_{U,V}
is a composition of
\deg f_{U,V}
arrows of degree
1
(see the discussion after Corollary
4.6
), hence
F^{\prime}(f_{U,V})=f_{U,V}
.

Finally we show that
F^{\prime}(g_{U,V})=g_{U,V}
, for all
V\in\Gamma_{0}
,
U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
. Since
g_{U,V}\in\rad^{\infty}(V,U)
, while
f_{U,V}\not\in\rad^{\infty}(V,U)
(provided
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
) by Lemma (
4.7
), there exists
\lambda_{U,V}\in\Bbbk
such that
F^{\prime}(g_{U,V})=\lambda_{U,V}\cdot g_{U,V}
. Our aim is to show that
\lambda_{U,V}=1
, for all possible
V
and
U
. The following will be useful.

Lemma 5.1
.

Fix
i\in[0,n-1]
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. If there exist
b\in[a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m,\infty)
and
x\in(-\infty,a+\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m]
, such that
\lambda_{(i+1,x,a),(i,a,b)}=1
, then
\lambda_{(i+1,x^{\prime},a),(i,a,b^{\prime})}=1
, for all
b^{\prime}\in[a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m,\infty)
and
x^{\prime}\in(-\infty,a+\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m]
.

Recall that as usual
i+1
is calculated modulo
n
.

Proof.

We first show that
\lambda_{(i+1,a+\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m,a),(i,a,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)}=1
. Put
V:=(i,a,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
,
V^{\prime}:=(i,a,b)
,
U^{\prime}:=(i+1,x,a)
, and
U:=(i+1,a+\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m,a)
. Then
g_{U,V}=f_{U,U^{\prime}}\circ g_{U^{\prime},V^{\prime}}\circ f_{V^{\prime},V}
, hence

\lambda_{(i+1,a+\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m,a),(i,a,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)}\cdot g_{U,V}=F^{\prime}(g_{U,V})\\
=F^{\prime}(f_{U,U^{\prime}})\circ F^{\prime}(g_{U^{\prime},V^{\prime}})\circ F^{\prime}(f_{V^{\prime},V})=f_{U,U^{\prime}}\circ g_{U^{\prime},V^{\prime}}\circ f_{V^{\prime},V}=g_{U,V},

thus
\lambda_{(i,a,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m),(i+1,a+\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m,a)}=1
.

Now let
b^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime}
be arbitrary, and put
V^{\prime\prime}:=(i,a,b^{\prime})
and
U^{\prime\prime}:=(i+1,x^{\prime},a)
. Similarly as above
g_{U,V}=f_{U,U^{\prime\prime}}\circ g_{U^{\prime\prime},V^{\prime\prime}}\circ f_{V^{\prime\prime},V}
, hence

g_{U,V}=F^{\prime}(g_{U,V})=F^{\prime}(f_{U,U^{\prime\prime}})\circ F^{\prime}(g_{U^{\prime\prime},V^{\prime\prime}})\circ F^{\prime}(f_{V^{\prime\prime},V})\\
=f_{U,U^{\prime\prime}}\circ(\lambda_{(i+1,x^{\prime},a),(i,a,b^{\prime})}\cdot g_{U^{\prime\prime},V^{\prime\prime}})\circ f_{V^{\prime\prime},V}=\lambda_{(i+1,x^{\prime},a),(i,a,b^{\prime})}\cdot g_{U,V},

thus
\lambda_{(i+1,x^{\prime},a),(i,a,b^{\prime})}=1
.
∎

Fix
i\in[0,n-1]
. We show that
\lambda_{(i+1,x,y),(i,a,b)}=1
, for all
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
b\in[a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m,\infty)
,
x\in(-\infty,a+\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m]
,
y\in[a,b+\delta_{i,0}\cdot m]
, in several steps.

\mathbf{0}^{\circ}
.
a=0=y
.

Put
V:=(i,0,0)
and
U:=(i+1,0,0)
. By the construction,
\phi_{V}=\Id_{V}
and
\phi_{U}=\Id_{U}
. Since
V
and
U
are projective modules by Corollary
4.4
,
F(g_{U,V})=g_{U,V}
. Consequently,
F^{\prime}(g_{U,V})=\phi_{U}\circ F(g_{U,V})\circ\phi_{V}^{-1}=g_{U,V}
, i.e.
\lambda_{U,V}=1
. Now the claim in this case follows for arbitrary
b
and
x
from Lemma
5.1
.

\mathbf{1}^{\circ}
.
a=y
arbitrary.

Assume first that
a>0
. Put
V^{\prime}:=(i,0,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
,
V:=(i,a,a-\delta_{i,0}\cdot m)
,
U^{\prime}:=(i+1,\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m,0)
, and
U:=(i+1,\delta_{i,n-1}\cdot m,a)
. Then
f_{U,U^{\prime}}\circ g_{U^{\prime},V^{\prime}}=g_{U,V^{\prime}}=g_{U,V}\circ f_{V,V^{\prime}}
. By earlier steps

F^{\prime}(f_{V,V^{\prime}})=f_{V,V^{\prime}},\qquad F^{\prime}(f_{U,U^{\prime}})=f_{U,U^{\prime}},\qquad F^{\prime}(g_{U^{\prime},V^{\prime}})=g_{U^{\prime},V^{\prime}}.

Consequently,

g_{U,V^{\prime}}=f_{U,U^{\prime}}\circ g_{U^{\prime},V^{\prime}}=F^{\prime}(f_{U,U^{\prime}}\circ g_{U^{\prime},V^{\prime}})\\
=F^{\prime}(g_{U,V}\circ f_{V,V^{\prime}})=(\lambda_{U,V}\cdot g_{U,V})\circ f_{V,V^{\prime}}=\lambda_{U,V}\cdot g_{U,V^{\prime}},

hence
\lambda_{U,V}=1
. We use Lemma
5.1
again and the claim follows.

The proof for
a<0
is analogous.

\mathbf{2}^{\circ}
.
a
and
y
are arbitrary.

Put
V:=(i,a,b)
,
W:=(i,y,b)
, and
U:=(i,x,y)
. Then
g_{U,V}=g_{U,W}\circ f_{W,V}
. We already know that
F^{\prime}(g_{U,W})=g_{U,W}
and
F^{\prime}(f_{W,V})=f_{W,V}
. Consequently,
F^{\prime}(g_{U,V})=g_{U,V}
, i.e.
\lambda_{U,V}=1
.

The following proposition, which in view of Proposition
2.1
constitutes the first important step in the proof of the main result, summarizes the above calculations.

Proposition 5.2
.

There exists a natural isomorphism
\phi\colon F\to\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)}
and a natural isomorphism
\omega^{\prime}\colon\Sigma\to\Sigma
such that
\phi
is a natural isomorphism between triangle functors
(F,\omega)
and
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We know from the preceding discussion that we have a natural isomorphism
\phi\colon F|_{\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}}\to\Id_{\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}}
. The isomorphism
\phi
extends to a natural isomorphism
\phi\colon F\to\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)}
by
[
12
]
*Lemma 2.4. The existence of
\omega^{\prime}
is a consequence of
[
12
]
*Lemma 2.3.
∎

5.3.
Connecting isomorphism

Let
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})
be a triangle functor. Our aim is to show that the functor
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})
is isomorphic to the identify functor (in fact in most of the cases, i.e. if
n>1
or
m>0
, already
\omega^{\prime}=\Id_{\Sigma}
). We start with the following.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})
be a triangle functor. Then there exist scalars
\mu_{V}\in\Bbbk
,
V\in\Gamma_{0}
, such that
\omega_{V}^{\prime}=f_{\Sigma V,\Sigma V}+\mu_{V}\cdot g_{\Sigma V,\Sigma V}^{\prime}
, for all
V\in\Gamma_{0}
. In particular,
\omega^{\prime}=\Id_{\Sigma}
, if
n>1
.

Proof.

Fix
V=(i,a,b)\in\Gamma_{0}
, and put
U:=(i,a,b+1)
and
W:=(i,b+1+\delta_{i,0}\cdot m,b+1)
. We show there exists an exact triangle

(5.4)

V\xrightarrow{f_{U,V}}U\xrightarrow{f_{W,U}}W\xrightarrow{\nu\cdot g_{\Sigma V,W}}\Sigma V,

for some
\nu\in\Bbbk
,
\nu\neq 0
. Indeed, one easily verifies the following:

(1)

f_{W,U}\circ f_{U,V}=0
,

(2)

if
f\circ f_{U,V}=0
, then there exists
g
such that
f=g\circ f_{W,U}
,

(3)

if
h\circ f_{W,U}=f_{W,U}
, then
h
is an automorphism.

Consequently, there exists an exact triangle
V\xrightarrow{f_{U,V}}U\xrightarrow{f_{W,U}}W\xrightarrow{g}\Sigma V
, for some
g
, by
[
6
]
*Proposition 2.2. Observe that
g\neq 0
, since
U
is not the direct sum of
V
and
W
(see
[
15
]
*Lemma I.1.4). Consequently,
g=\nu\cdot g_{\Sigma V,W}
, for some
\nu\neq 0
, if either
n>1
or
n=1
and
\Sigma V\not\in\mathcal{F}_{W}
. On the other hand, if
n=1
and
\Sigma V\in\mathcal{F}_{W}
, then
g=\xi\cdot f_{\Sigma V,W}+\nu\cdot g_{\Sigma V,W}
, for some
\xi,\nu\in\Bbbk
, such that
\xi\neq 0
or
\nu\neq 0
. Recall that
\Sigma V=(i,a+1+m,b+1+m)
in this case. Consequently,
\Sigma V\in\mathcal{F}_{W}
implies
a=b
, which in turn gives
\Sigma V\in\mathcal{F}_{U}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{U}
. Thus
0=g\circ f_{W,U}=\xi\cdot f_{\Sigma V,U}
, where the first equality is a consequence of
[
15
]
*Proposition I.1.2(a). This implies
\xi=0
, i.e.
g=\nu\cdot g_{\Sigma V,W}
, for some
\nu\neq 0
.

(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})

) and using
[

The above lemma settles the case
n>1
, thus for the rest of the subsection we assume that
n=1
. In order to simply notation, we write
(a,b)
instead of
(0,a,b)
, for
(0,a,b)\in\Gamma_{0}
. The next step is the following.

Lemma 5.5
.

Assume
n=1
and
m>0
. If
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})
is a triangle functor, then
\omega^{\prime}=\Id_{\Sigma}
.

Proof.

Fix
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. By induction on
b
we show
\omega_{V}^{\prime}=\Id_{\Sigma V}
, for each
V=(a,b)\in\Gamma_{0}
. If
b=a-m
, then
\Sigma V\not\in\mathcal{G}_{\Sigma V}
(since
m>0
), hence
\omega_{V}^{\prime}=f_{\Sigma V,\Sigma V}=\Id_{\Sigma V}
by Lemma
5.3
.

Now assume that
b>a-m
and put
U:=(a,b-1)
. Lemma
5.3
implies
\omega_{V}^{\prime}=f_{\Sigma V,\Sigma V}+\mu\cdot g_{\Sigma V,\Sigma V}^{\prime}
, for some
\mu\in\Bbbk
. Moreover,
\omega_{U}^{\prime}=\Id_{\Sigma U}
by induction hypothesis. Put
f:=\Sigma^{-1}f_{\Sigma V,\Sigma U}
. Then

f_{\Sigma V,\Sigma U}=\Sigma f\circ\omega_{U}^{\prime}=\omega_{V}^{\prime}\circ\Sigma f=f_{\Sigma V,\Sigma U}+\mu\cdot g_{\Sigma V,\Sigma V}^{\prime}\circ f_{\Sigma V,\Sigma U},

hence
\mu\cdot g_{\Sigma V,\Sigma V}^{\prime}\circ f_{\Sigma V,\Sigma U}=0
. If
g_{\Sigma V,\Sigma V}^{\prime}\neq 0
, i.e.
\Sigma V\in\mathcal{G}_{\Sigma V}
, then
\Sigma V\in\mathcal{G}_{\Sigma U}
(since
m>0
), thus
g_{\Sigma V,\Sigma V}^{\prime}\circ f_{\Sigma V,\Sigma U}=g_{\Sigma V,\Sigma U}\neq 0
. Consequently,
\mu=0
and the claim follows.
∎

It remains to consider the case
n=1
and
m=0
. This case has already been treated in
[
12
]
*subsection 7.1. For completeness we include a proof, which slightly differently formulates the corresponding idea from the proof of
[
12
]
*Theorem 7.1.

We need the following observation.

Lemma 5.6
.

Assume
n=1
and
m=0
. Let
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})
be a triangle functor,
V,U\in\Gamma_{0}
, and
f\colon V\to U
.

(1)

We have
\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}\circ f=f\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}
.

(2)

If
f
is not an isomorphism, then
\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}\circ f=f=f\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The first part follows from the following sequence of equalities using that
\omega^{\prime}
is a natural transformation of
\Sigma
:

\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}\circ f=\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}\circ\Sigma(\Sigma^{-1}f)=\Sigma(\Sigma^{-1}f)\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}=f\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}.

In order to prove the second part, we use that
\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}=f_{V,V}+\mu^{\prime}\cdot g_{V,V}^{\prime}
and
\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}=f_{U,U}+\mu^{\prime\prime}\cdot g_{U,U}^{\prime}
, for some
\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime}\in\Bbbk
, by Lemma
5.3
. If
U\not\in\mathcal{F}_{V}\cup\mathcal{G}_{V}
, then
f=0
and the claim is obvious. If
U\in\mathcal{G}_{V}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{V}
, then
f=\mu\cdot g_{U,V}
, for some
\mu\in\Bbbk
, and the equalities
\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}\circ f=f=f\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}
follow by easy calculations. Thus we may assume that
U\in\mathcal{F}_{V}
. Then there exist
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk
such that
f=\lambda\cdot f_{U,V}+\mu\cdot g_{U,V}^{\prime}
. Consequently

\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}\circ f=f+(\lambda\cdot\mu^{\prime})\cdot g_{U,U}^{\prime}\circ f_{U,V}.

Since
f
is not an isomorphism, either
\lambda=0
or
U\neq V
by Lemma
4.5
. In the former case, the equality
\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}\circ f=f
immediately follows. In the latter case,
U\not\in\mathcal{G}_{V}
(since
n=1
and
m=0
implies that
V
is the unique element of
\mathcal{F}_{V}\cap\mathcal{G}_{V}
), hence
g_{U,U}^{\prime}\circ f_{U,V}=0
, and the equality
\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}\circ f=f
follows again. The equality
f\circ{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}=f
is proved analogously.
∎

We finish this subsection with following.

Lemma 5.7
.

Assume
n=1
and
m=0
. If
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})
is a triangle functor, then there exists an isomorphism
\eta\colon(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})\to(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\Id_{\Sigma})
of triangle functors.

Proof.

We first define automorphisms
\eta_{V}
, for
V\in\Gamma_{0}
. Let
V=(a,b)
. If
a=0
, then we put
\eta_{V}:=\Id_{V}
. If
a>0
, then we define
\eta_{V}
inductively
by
\eta_{V}:=\Sigma\eta_{\Sigma^{-1}V}\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}
(note that
\Sigma^{-1}V=(a-1,b-1)
). Similarly, we put
\eta_{V}:=\Sigma^{-1}\eta_{\Sigma V}\circ\Sigma^{-1}\omega_{V}^{\prime-1}
, if
a<0
. One easily checks that the formula

(5.8)

\eta_{V}=\Sigma\eta_{\Sigma^{-1}V}\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}

holds for arbitrary
V
.

We show that
\eta_{U}\circ f=f\circ\eta_{V}
for arbitrary
V=(a,b),U=(x,y)\in\Gamma_{0}
and
f\colon V\to U
. Assume first that
a=x
. If
a=0
, then the claim is obvious. If
a>0
, then using Lemma
5.6
(
1
) and the induction hypothesis, we get

\eta_{U}\circ f=\Sigma\eta_{\Sigma^{-1}U}\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}U}^{\prime}\circ f=\Sigma\eta_{\Sigma^{-1}U}\circ f\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}=\Sigma(\eta_{\Sigma^{-1}U}\circ\Sigma^{-1}f)\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}\\
=\Sigma(\Sigma^{-1}f\circ\eta_{\Sigma^{-1}V})\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}=f\circ\Sigma\eta_{\Sigma^{-1}V}\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}V}^{\prime}=f\circ\eta_{V}.

Similarly, the claim follows if
a<0
. On the other hand, if
a\neq x
, then
V\neq U
, in particular
f
is not an isomorphism. Similarly as above we show by induction on
|a|
(
|x|
) and using Lemma
5.6
(
2
) that
f\circ\eta_{V}=f
(
\eta_{U}\circ f=f
, respectively).

In other words, we have just showed that
\eta
is a natural automorphism of
\Id|_{\Bbbk\Gamma/\mathcal{I}}
. By
[
12
]
*Lemma 2.4
\eta
extends to a natural automorphism of
\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)}
. Using
[
12
]
*Lemma 2.3 we obtain a natural automorphism
\omega^{\prime\prime}
of
\Sigma
such that
\eta\colon(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})\to(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime\prime})
is an isomorphism of triangle functors. Observe that

\omega_{V}^{\prime\prime}=\Sigma\eta_{V}\circ\omega_{V}^{\prime}\circ\eta_{\Sigma V}^{-1}=\Sigma\eta_{V}\circ\omega_{V}^{\prime}\circ\omega_{\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma V}^{\prime-1}\circ\Sigma\eta_{\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma V}^{-1}=\Id_{\Sigma V},

for every
V\in\Gamma_{0}
, where the second equality follows from (
5.8
). Consequently,
\omega^{\prime\prime}=\Id_{\Sigma}
and the claim follows.
∎

5.4.
Summary

We summarize now the above considerations.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
\Lambda:=\Lambda(n,m)
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

If
(F,\omega)
is a pseudo-identify on
\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)
, then
(F,\omega)
is isomorphic, as a triangle functor, to
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\Id_{\Sigma})
.

(2)

In particular, if
G\colon\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod\Lambda)\to\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod B)
is a triangle equivalence, for some algebra
B
, then
G
is standard.

Proof.

(
1
) It follows from Propostion
5.2
that there exists a natural isomorphism
\phi\colon F\to\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)}
and a natural isomorphism
\omega^{\prime}\colon\Sigma\to\Sigma
such that
\phi
is a natural isomorphism between triangle functors
(F,\omega)
and
(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})
. Now Lemmas
5.3
,
5.5
and
5.7
imply that there exists a natural isomorphism
\eta\colon(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\omega^{\prime})\to(\Id_{\mathbf{K}^{b}(\proj\Lambda)},\Id_{\Sigma})
of triangle functors. By taking
\eta\circ\phi
we get our claim.

(
2
) This follows immediately from (
1
) and Proposition
2.1
.
∎

5.5.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Let
F\colon\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod A)\to\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod B)
be a derived equivalence between derived discrete algebras
A
and
B
of infinite global dimension. By Proposition
2.2
there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
A
is derived equivalent to
\Lambda:=\Lambda(n,m)
. By
[
20
]
*Theorem 3.3 we know there exists a standard derived equivalence
H\colon\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod A)\to\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod\Lambda)
. Moreover, there exists an equivalence
G\colon\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod\Lambda)\to\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mod B)
such that the functors
F
and
G\circ H
are isomorphic. Now
G
is standard by Proposition
5.9
(
2
). Since a composition of standard derived equivalences is obviously standard, the claim follows. ∎
