Adiposity-lipid-glycemic clusters as potential warning signals of bone mass reduction in Asia’s largest urban communities – based bone health assessment via ultrasound
Authors: Qingqing Zhang, Xiaoyu Zhang, Shanshan Zhang, Guangda lv, Yu Wang, Xiaotian Shi, Yan Li, Lei Ding, Dong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn osteoporotic fragility fracture occurs every three seconds, with a particularly high incidence after the age of 65, reflecting a substantial decline in bone mass. Given the limitations of dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) in early-stage bone mineral density (BMD) assessment, we aim to employ ultrasound-based BMD evaluation within community populations to gain a deeper understanding of the age at which bone mass reduction begins and the associated risk factors.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 15,052 individuals from routine health check-ups at Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital (2017–2024). BMD was assessed through ultrasound, with body composition measured in 4,999 participants using multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis. Key risk factors were identified via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) were used to predict bone mass reduction risk. Models were evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation. Model performance was assessed using the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). SHAP plots were employed for interpretability. The best model was deployed in a Shiny web application for real-time prediction.ResultsAmong 15,052 individuals, 55.7% had normal bone mass, 43.0% had osteopenia, and 1.3% had osteoporosis. Bone mass was significantly associated with gender, age, body mass index (BMI), and metabolic markers (P < 0.001). Age increased with decreasing bone mass: normal (43 years), osteopenia (53 years), and osteoporosis (65 years). In 4,999 participants, osteopenia and osteoporosis were linked to higher fat mass index (FMI) and metabolic markers. Group medians in the osteopenia/osteoporosis fell within reference ranges, yet some individuals had values close to either limit. No associations were found between smoking or drinking status and BMD. Bone mass reduction rose sharply from 27.2% to 53.4% between ages 30–59. ROC analysis showed age as a predictor of bone mass reduction with optimal cutoffs at 47 years for males and 49 years for females. LASSO regression identified age, gender, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides (TG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and FMI as key factors. XGBoost achieved the highest AUC (0.734). Gender-stratified analysis showed that in males, age, HDL-C, FMI, and FBG were significant factors (XGBoost AUC = 0.687), while in females, age, TG, and FMI were key factors (XGBoost AUC = 0.770).ConclusionThis study found a high prevalence of bone mass reduction among Chinese adults aged 30–59 years. FMI and age showed significant associations with reduced bone mass. Furthermore, even when HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and HbA1c were near the reference limits but within normal ranges, their variations were associated with bone mass reduction, which may serve as an early warning indicator. These results underscore the potential utility of community-based bone health monitoring and offer epidemiological insights for comparable aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02788-z.Keywords: Bone mineral density, Ultrasound, Asia’s largest residential cluster, Bone mass reduction, Risk factor

Background

Globally, an osteoporotic fragility fracture occurs every three seconds, underscoring the silent yet pervasive nature of bone loss [1]. In China, over one-third of individuals aged 50 and older are at risk of osteoporosis [2]—a figure expected to rise sharply due to rapid population aging. Although osteoporosis has long been considered a condition of the elderly, emerging evidence indicates that bone mass reduction is increasingly being detected in younger and middle-aged adults [3–5].

This trend is particularly alarming. Studies now report diagnoses of osteopenia and osteoporosis in individuals as young as their 30 s and 40 s [6, 7]. The earlier onset of bone fragility threatens the health and productivity of the working-age population. Premature skeletal decline elevates the risk of fractures, chronic pain, and disability, contributing to increased absenteeism, long-term incapacity, and rising healthcare costs [8, 9]. If left unaddressed, this deterioration—already a major contributor to global disability, dependency, and healthcare burden [10, 11]—may severely strain our country’s health system and labor force [8, 12].

Over the past three decades, China has undergone unprecedented economic expansion and rapid urbanization, accompanied by profound shifts in lifestyle and dietary patterns [13]. Increased reliance on ultra-processed foods, reduced physical activity, and growing sedentary behaviors, and insufficient sun exposure have contributed to a surge in non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [14–17]. As reported in the 2023 Statistics Report of Physical Examination of Beijing, participation rates for screening of major non-communicable diseases were relatively high—78.26% for blood pressure, 58.65% for blood lipids, and 71.82% for fatty liver [18]. In contrast, the screening rate for bone mineral density (BMD) using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) [19], the gold standard for osteoporosis diagnosis, was only 22.17%. However, physical examination data revealed that the detection rate of bone mass reduction and osteoporosis ranked fourth among all assessed conditions. Therefore, bone health has not yet been adequately recognized as a major health concern. Ultrasonography offers a non-invasive, radiation-free, and cost-effective solution for early detection of bone loss [20–22]. The Huitian area—known as Asia’s largest residential clusters and comprising the two massive residential communities of Huilongguan and Tiantongyuan—covers 63 square kilometers and is home to over 800,000 permanent residents in Beijing (Fig. 1). Despite having active general practitioner (GP) teams, this area has yet to implement systematic bone health assessments. Given its scalability and operational ease, ultrasound bone densitometry is ideally suited for large-scale community screening. In light of China’s rapidly aging population and the strategic priorities of Healthy China 2030, integrating early-onset bone loss detection into national preventive frameworks is now imperative.

Methods

Data sources and population

In this retrospective study, the dataset was derived entirely from routine health check-ups conducted at a physical examination center of Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital from 2017 to 2024. All individuals included in the study were Huitian area residents living within a 15-kilometer radius of the hospital and participating in routine health check-ups (Fig. 1). A total of 103,262 individuals underwent health examinations, of whom only 29,056 received BMD assessments—yielding a screening rate of just 28.13%. In cases where individuals had undergone multiple physical examinations, only data from their first visit were retained for analysis. Consequently, a total of 15,052 participants were included in the final study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital, Beijing, China (19182-0−01). The study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of bone mineral density and body composition

BMD was measured using the SPUS quantitative ultrasound device (Human Techpia Co., Ltd., South Korea), which employs a patented longitudinal transmission ultrasound technique. Measurements were taken at the middle phalanges of the non-dominant index, middle, and ring fingers (2nd–4th digits). High-frequency ultrasound waves (~ 2 MHz) were transmitted bilaterally through the phalanges, and the speed of sound (SOS) was recorded. Higher SOS values reflect greater bone density, allowing indirect estimation of BMD. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, BMD is classified based on T-scores as follows: Normal bone mass: T-score ≥ − 1.0, Low bone mass (osteopenia): T-score between − 1.0 and − 2.5, Osteoporosis: T-score ≤ − 2.5 [23].

Of the total participants, body composition was assessed in 4,999 individuals using the GAIA KIKO analyzer (GAIA Inc., Korea) based on multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (5/50/250 kHz) with 8-point tactile electrodes. Participants stood barefoot on the foot electrodes and gripped the hand electrodes with arms abducted at 15°. Height and weight were automatically imported via integrated stadiometer and calibrated scale. Three consecutive measurements were performed, and results with impedance coefficient of variation (CV) < 3% were retained.

Statistical analysis

In order to focus on risk prediction of bone mass reduction in a community setting, we employed a two-step analytical strategy. In the first step, descriptive and comparative analyses were used to examine differences demographic characteristics across bone mass categories. In the second step, key variables were selected, and predictive models were subsequently constructed and evaluated using multiple machine learning algorithms.

General analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; version 4.5.0). Data normality was tested using the One-Sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For normally distributed continuous data, results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD); for non-normally distributed data, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) are provided. Categorical data are reported as percentages. For comparisons across bone mass categories, continuous variables with normal distributions were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), whereas those not normally distributed were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square (χ²) test. To address missing data, multiple imputation was performed under the assumption that data were missing at random based on “mice” package (Version 3.17.0).

Given the limited sample size in the osteoporosis group, participants with osteopenia and osteoporosis were combined into a single “bone mass reduction” category. This binary classification (normal vs. reduced bone mass) was used in all downstream analyses. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the relationship between age and bone mass reduction in 15,052 individuals. Gender-specific cutoff values for age were also identified.

Development of data-driven models for community-level bone screening

Initially, we employed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to identify key risk factors associated with bone mass reduction based on the body composition data obtained from 4,999 individuals. This method was chosen to effectively address multicollinearity among the variables and to enhance model interpretability by selecting the most relevant predictors. The selected predictors were as follows: age, gender, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), hemoglobin (Hb), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting blood glucose (FBG), fat mass index (FMI), 13 C-urea breath test (13 C-UBT), smoking duration, smoking status classification, daily smoking amount, drinking duration categories, drinking status categories, alcohol drinking frequency. To complement the limitations of Body Mass Index (BMI), we employed FMI as a more refined measure of adiposity. Calculated as total fat mass (kg) divided by height squared (m²), FMI isolates fat mass from lean and bone components, offering a more accurate evaluation of body composition. More importantly, FMI facilitates the identification of metabolically at-risk individuals with normal BMI but excess adiposity—a condition referred to as “Metabolically obese normal weight“ [24]. Model performance was evaluated using the 5-fold crossvalidation [25]. Subsequently, A range of statistical and machine learning models were employed to develop the prediction model, such as: Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) to predict the risk of bone mass reduction. Gender-stratified models were also constructed to account for sex-specific differences. To evaluate and compare the impact of risk factors on bone mass reduction, model performance was primarily assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), which quantifies the model’s discriminatory ability. For interpretability, we employed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) plots. SHAP is a versatile and theoretically grounded method for interpreting machine learning models [26]. Based on game theory, SHAP leverages Shapley values to allocate optimal credit among features, thereby quantifying their contribution to model predictions. For global interpretation, SHAP not only ranks feature importance but also elucidates the directionality and magnitude of each variable’s effect on model outputs. Finally, the best-performing model was implemented in a user-friendly Shiny web application, allowing real-time input and personalized risk prediction to facilitate early screening and clinical decision-making.

Results

General characteristics of individuals undergoing health examinations

Table 1 presents the distribution of BMD in the study population: 55.7% had normal bone mass, 43.0% had osteopenia, and 1.3% had osteoporosis. The prevalence of low bone mass differed significantly by gender, with a higher proportion of osteoporosis in males and osteopenia in females (P < 0.001). Median age increased with decreasing BMD (normal: 43 years; osteopenia: 53 years; osteoporosis: 65 years; P < 0.001). BMI was significantly higher in individuals with osteopenia and osteoporosis compared with those with normal BMD (P < 0.001). Significant differences were also observed in metabolic markers (TC, HDL, LDL, TG, and HbA1c) across BMD categories (P < 0.001).

Age-specific prevalence of bone mass reduction by gender

The prevalence of bone mass reduction increased progressively with age across all groups (Fig. 2). The prevalence of bone mass reduction rose sharply from 27.2% to 53.4% between the ages of 30 and 59—a critical window of susceptibility for bone health—and then plateaued after the age of 60. Strikingly, the rate of bone mass reduction began to accelerate markedly from the 30–39 age group. A similar trend was observed in both male and female groups. Moreover, males consistently exhibited a higher prevalence of osteopenia than females across all age groups.

Sex-stratified assessment of age and FMI in relation to BMD

As shown in Fig. 3, ROC curve analysis was used to assess the predictive value of age for bone mass reduction in 15,052 individuals. The AUC was 0.66 for the overall population, 0.65 for males, and 0.70 for females, indicating better model performance in females. The optimal age cut-off for predicting reduced bone mass was 47.0 years in the overall population and in males, and 49 years in females. Sensitivity and specificity at these thresholds were 65.1% and 60.7% (overall), 63.9% and 60.0% (males), and 70.8% and 61.6% (females), respectively. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, consistent findings were also observed in the subpopulation (n = 4,999). In this population, men demonstrated a lower ROC-based FMI cutoff compared with women (6.2 vs. 6.6), as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Risk factors for bone mass reduction using body composition variables

LASSO regression identified the following variables as relevant predictors: age, gender, HDL-C, TG, HbA1c, and FMI. These variables showed a positive association with the outcome, except for HDL-C, which was negatively associated. As shown in Fig. 4A, the AUC values of the four predictive models, in descending order, were: XGBoost (0.734), Logistic Regression (0.713 and SVM (0.703), and Random Forest (0.691), respectively. An online calculator based on the predictive score has been developed and made freely available to the public (https://bone-mass-prediction.shinyapps.io/bone-mass-prediction/). The XGBoost importance matrix plot (Fig. 4B) identifies the top six variables contributing to the model’s predictions: age, FMI, gender, TG, HDL-C, and HbA1c. The SHAP summary plot revealed the importance of each feature in the model prediction and its influence trend as follows: age, FMI, gender, HDL-C, TG, and HbA1c (Fig. 4C). In this plot, the x-axis represents the actual values of the feature, while the y-axis denotes the corresponding SHAP values, which reflect the feature’s contribution to the model’s prediction. This allows us to visualize how the importance attributed to the feature changes across its range. Notably, positive SHAP values indicate that higher feature values are associated with an increased risk of bone mass reduction.

Discussion

Bone health is a critical yet frequently underrecognized dimension, particularly in rapidly urbanizing populations. In this population-based sample of 15,052 community-dwelling, 44.3% were found to have reduced bone mass—exposing a major yet ignore public health challenge. Notably, the rate of bone mass reduction began to accelerate markedly from the 30–39 age group, and males consistently exhibited poorer bone health than females. These findings challenge prevailing assumptions that bone deterioration is primarily a concern of the elderly or of postmenopausal women and highlight the urgent need for earlier and broader screening strategies in China’s urban population.

In addition to the well-established risk factor of age, our analysis identified FMI, TG, and HbA1c as risk factors for bone mass reduction, while HDL-C exhibited a protective inverse association. Importantly, gender-specific differences in risk profiles were observed: FMI emerged as critical influential role in males and females.

High prevalence and sex-specific vulnerability

We found the bone mass reduction occurs at a lower age in males (47 years) than in females (49 years), with consistent findings also observed in the subpopulation [27, 28]. One potential contributing factor is that men are often less likely than women to seek preventive medical care, partly due to the entrenched perception that bone mass reduction predominantly affects older adults and postmenopausal women [29, 30], thereby leading to underestimation of risk in men. Additionally, the ROC-derived FMI cutoff was lower in men than in women (6.2 vs. 6.6), which may reinforce the importance of considering earlier risk screening in men. Furthermore, the number of individuals with abnormal metabolic indicators was substantially exceeded that in male (green line) compared to female (red line), consistently observed in both the total population and the bone mass reduction subgroup (Supplementary Fig. 3 A and Supplementary Fig. 3B). This metabolic burden may be associated with the earlier age-related inflection in bone mass decline observed in men. Existing evidence indicates that male smokers are associated with a higher prevalence of osteoporosis or osteopenia compared with nonsmokers [31]. In addition, when alcohol intake falls within social drinking ranges, alcohol is not associated with whole-body BMD; however, consumption of low-alcohol beer has been reported to be negatively associated with spinal BMD [32]. All individuals included in our study were community-dwelling, generally healthy adults rather than patients. No significant differences in smoking or drinking intensity and frequency were observed among the three groups. Given the limited sample size in the osteoporosis group, participants with osteopenia and osteoporosis were combined into bone mass reduction group. In Supplementary Table 1, using chi-square tests for smoking/drinking history, smoking exhibited a statistically significant among groups (P = 0.038), whereas drinking showed no significant difference (P = 0.144). As shown in Supplementary Table 2, gender-stratified analyses also showed no statistically significant differences. We employed LASSO regression to identify key risk factors for bone mass reduction; smoking and alcohol consumption were not selected by the model. We suspect the reason is that the proportions of current smokers and drinkers were low, which may dilute effect sizes and reduces statistical power to detect associations. Other contributors in our study population may include higher rates of unmeasured lifestyle risk factors, such as lower calcium intake, greater consumption of carbonated beverages, occupational stress, and inadequate physical activity [33, 34].

Consistent with prior studies [6, 35–41], our findings confirm a high prevalence of osteopenia in China, with gender and age remaining dominant determinants of BMD. In our study, the identified peak age for predicting reduced bone mass closely align with findings from large-scale European and North American studies [42]. Moreover, our results are consistent with recent physical examination data in Beijing, encompassing 6,476,589 individuals across 292 medical institutions, which initially demonstrated early-onset bone loss and substantial disease burden [18]. Similar study results also identified age-related bone loss as a nearly universal phenomenon [43].

The role of metabolic and glycemic markers in bone health risk prediction

Aberrant lipid metabolism has been implicated in skeletal deterioration through mechanisms involving chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered adipokine signaling—factors that collectively enhance osteoclastogenesis while suppressing osteoblastic activity [44]. Beyond demographic factors, our study highlights the mechanistic relevance of lipid profiles in bone health. The inverse association between HDL-C and bone mass reduction supports the concept of a metabolic–bone axis, with HDL-C promoting bone remodeling via anti-inflammatory and anabolic pathways [45–47]. Given that HDL-C is positively modulated by aerobic exercise, its inverse association with bone mass reduction may partially reflect underlying physical activity levels. This supports the potential value of integrating aerobic exercise prescriptions into bone health management strategies, reinforcing the dual utility of HDL-C as both a metabolic marker and a proxy for lifestyle-related skeletal protection [48]. TG levels are influenced by dietary patterns—particularly high intake of refined carbohydrates and sugars [49]. Elevated TG levels, reflecting dietary imbalance and metabolic dysfunction, which tightly related to impaired bone remodeling and increased risk of bone mass reduction [50–52]. The strong predictive value of both HDL-C and TG in our model underlines their clinical relevance in bone health assessment. These findings reveal the practical utility of integrating lipid biomarkers into routine osteoporosis risk, offering insights for early identification and management of at-risk individuals in clinical settings.

Consistent with existing evidence, our findings reinforce the strong association between disordered glucose metabolism and compromised bone health. Hyperglycaemia and insulin resistance—reflected by elevated markers such as HbA1c—have been independently linked to reduced BMD and heightened fracture risk [53, 54]. These metabolic disturbances may impair skeletal integrity through several interconnected mechanisms, including increased oxidative stress, accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), suppression of osteoblast function, and disruption of the bone remodeling microenvironment [55–57]. Integrating glycaemic indicators into machine learning frameworks facilitates a more comprehensive and individualized assessment of bone health, particularly in asymptomatic individuals undergoing routine metabolic screening.

Adiposity: coexist risk for cardiometabolic and skeletal health

Excess adiposity is a well-documented risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, linked to hypertension, dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation [58–60]. In particular, visceral fat accumulation exacerbates atherosclerotic burden and metabolic dysregulation [58, 61]. While a higher BMI is sometimes associated with increased BMD due to mechanical loading, central adiposity is concurrently linked to elevated fracture risk [62, 63].

Excess adiposity has been increasingly linked to skeletal fragility, primarily via chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and hormonal dysregulation. These mechanisms promote pro-inflammatory cytokine release, impair osteoblast function, and disrupt the bone–fat endocrine axis [58–61]. While BMI remains widely used, it does not differentiate fat from lean mass and may underestimate adiposity-related skeletal risk—particularly in individuals with normal-weight obesity. To address this limitation, we incorporated FMI, a height-adjusted metric of fat burden [62, 63]. In our study, BMD in males declined markedly from the 30–39 age group, with FMI emerging as very important predictor. These findings call for targeted bone health strategies in young and middle-aged male population—a key labor force cohort often overlooked in skeletal health policies, especially in the context of declining fertility and population aging. Routine inclusion of FMI in bone health evaluations offers a more accurate approach for identifying skeletal risk in urban populations.

Traditional statistical methods often fall short in ranking risk factors. Therefore, our study also leveraged machine learning techniques to identify individuals at risk of early bone mass loss. Among the models tested, XGBoost achieved the highest discriminative performance [64, 65], effectively capturing nonlinear relationships and intricate variable interactions—especially in sex-stratified analyses. It consistently outperformed other approaches such as Logistic Regression [66] and Random Forest [67]. This aligns with previous research demonstrating the superiority of machine learning algorithms in predicting chronic diseases, including osteoporosis [68–70]. To enhance clinical applicability, we translated the XGBoost model into a publicly accessible web-based risk calculator. This tool offers a scalable and practical solution for early screening and individualized prevention in asymptomatic populations [71, 72], particularly within community health settings where access to DXA remains limited.

This study has several limitations. First, bone mineral density was assessed using quantitative ultrasound of the phalanges rather than DXA, the current gold standard. However, ultrasound offers a non-invasive, radiation-free, and widely accessible alternative suitable for large-scale screening. Second, as a cross-sectional study, causal inferences cannot be established. Third, although the lipid and glycemic parameters (e.g., HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, HbA1c, and FMI) differ significantly between groups, their values fall within normal ranges, suggesting that they may serve as subtle indicators of risk rather than clinical diagnostic tools. Finally, external validation in other populations is needed to confirm the generalizability of the predictive model. Despite these limitations, the study benefits from a large sample size encompassing multiple ethnicities and occupations, comprehensive metabolic profiling, and the integration of modern statistical learning methods.

Conclusion

This community-based study reveals a notable burden of bone mass reduction among Chinese adults aged 30–59 years, challenging the common perception that bone health is primarily a concern of old age. Our findings highlight the potential value of incorporating FMI into early bone health risk stratification. Variations in HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and HbA1c—mostly within normal ranges—were associated with bone mass status, indicating potential early susceptibility signals. These results support the necessity for population-level surveillance rather than clinical diagnosis, especially in rapidly aging societies facing low fertility and rising urbanization. As countries undergo similar demographic transitions, our experience may inform scalable strategies for bone health promotion in community settings.
