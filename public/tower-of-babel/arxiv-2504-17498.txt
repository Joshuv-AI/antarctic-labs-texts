Hausdorff dimension of shrinking targets on Przytycki-Urbański fractals
Thomas Jordan, Henna Koivusalo

Abstract
Shrinking target problems in the context of iterated function systems have received an increasing amount of interest in the past few years. The classical shrinking target problem concerns points returning infinitely many times to a sequence of shrinking balls. In the iterated function system context, the shrinking balls problem is only well tractable in the case of similarity maps, but the case of affine maps is more elusive due to many geometric-dynamical complications. In the current work, we push through these complications and compute the Hausdorff dimension of a set recurring to a shrinking target of geometric balls in some affine iterated function systems. For these results, we have pinpointed a representative class of affine iterated function systems, consisting of a pair of diagonal affine maps, that was introduced by Przytycki and Urbański. The analysis splits into many sub-cases according to the type of the centre point of the targets, and the relative sizes of the targets and the contractions of the maps, illustrating the array of challenges of going beyond affine maps with nice projections. The proofs require heavy machinery from, and expand, the theory of Bernoulli convolutions.



1
Introduction

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
T:X\to X
a Borel measurable transformation. A
shrinking target set
is defined by fixing a point
z\in X
and a sequence
(r_{n})\subset(0,\infty)
and setting

\displaystyle R^{*}(z,(r_{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{x\in X\mid T^{n}(x)\in B(z,r_{n})\text{ for infinitely many }n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}T^{-n}(B(z,r_{n})).

Study of sets with recurrence properties such as this are classical in dynamics. The term shrinking target was coined by Hill and Velani
[
14
,
15
]
. Since then, the set
R^{*}
and its variants have since been studied in a variety of settings, including circle rotations, general dynamical systems under mixing conditions, toral automorphisms,
\beta
-transformations, and the Gauss map (see
[
27
,
13
,
10
,
9
,
23
,
20
]
), and by now the literature is so extensive that it would be impossible to give an exhaustive list. In these works, typical questions to ask about
R^{*}
have had to do with its size, for example its measure with respect to some
T
-invariant Borel probability measure on
X
, or, in more geometric set-ups, its Hausdorff dimension. We will focus our attention on the second question for a class of iterated function systems on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which we define next.

Let
f_{0},\ldots,f_{k-1}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be contractions, that is, there exist
c_{0},\ldots,c_{k-1}\in(0,1)
such that for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
|f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(y)|\leq c_{i}|x-y|
. The collection
\{f_{0},\dots,f_{k-1}\}
is called an
iterated function system (IFS)
. It is well known that there exists a unique non-empty compact set
F
such that
F=\cup_{i=0}^{k-1}f_{i}(F)

[
17
]
. If we assume the
strong separation condition
, that for all
i\neq j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}

f_{i}(F)\cap f_{j}(F)=\emptyset
, we can define a dynamical system
E:F\to F
by setting
E(x)=f_{i}^{-1}(x)
for each
x\in f_{i}(F)
. Even in the absence of the strong separation condition, the map
E
can be well-defined for example by picking a canonical choice of
E=f_{i}^{-1}
on any non-empty
f_{i}(F)\cap f_{j}(F)\neq\emptyset
. We consider the shrinking target problem for the dynamical system
(F,E)
.

We will begin with a short sketch of the types of arguments applied in the study of dimension of
R^{*}
in the IFS setting. The result outlined here can be deduced from
[
14
]
(for a translation from the results of Hill and Velani to the IFS context, see
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
, and for related results, also see
[
2
, Section 4]
). For simplicity, assume that the
f_{0},\dots,f_{k-1}
are similarities with contraction ratios
c_{0},\dots,c_{k-1}
. Let
F
be the associated self-similar set. Let
z\in F
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Let
r_{n}=\gamma^{n}
for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For the sets

R^{*}(z,(r_{n}))=\limsup_{n\to\infty}E^{-n}(B(z,r_{n}))

it can be shown that
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(z,(r_{n}))=s(\gamma)
where

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}c_{i}^{s}=\gamma^{-s(\gamma)}.

For general
(r_{n})
the same formula holds with
\gamma=\limsup_{n\to\infty}r_{n}^{1/n}
.
The method of proof is to get an upper bound by finding natural coverings of the sets
E^{-n}B(z,r_{n})
and take unions of these covering for all
n
sufficiently large. For the lower bound, sometimes disguised as a mass transference principle, one has to consider subsets of those points with
x\in E^{-n_{m}}(R^{*}((z,(r_{n_{m}}))))
along some subsequence
(n_{m})
satisfying
\lim_{m\to\infty}n_{m+1}n_{m}^{-1}=\infty
. The argument then relies on building a suitable mass distribution on these subsets.

Now consider an IFSs consisting of affine maps, that is, each
f_{0},\dots,f_{k-1}
is of the form
f_{i}:x\mapsto A_{i}x+\tau_{i}
where
A_{i}\in\text{GL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
is a linear map with
\lVert A_{i}\rVert<1
and
\tau_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a translation. The same basic methods in the study of Hausdorff dimension of
R^{*}
are still used. However, the affine case poses many technical obstacles of both geometric and dynamical nature. Overcoming these problems usually requires some restrictions to the types of maps being considered. Past work in this context has focused on IFSs with generic translations
\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{k-1}
, and with additional restrictions on the matrices
A_{0},\dots,A_{k-1}

[
19
,
8
]
. Perhaps most restrictively, in many past results the returns are not to geometric balls
B(z,r_{n})
, but to subsets called
cylinder sets
for which the pre-images under
E
are easier to characterise. Only limited work has been carried out in the case where the shrinking targets are balls, for self-affine IFSs. Most notably in the case of Bedford-McMullen carpets, a special class of self-affine sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
based on a fixed
n\times m
grid, Bárány and Rams
[
6
]
computed the Hausdorff dimension of the shrinking target set (also see
[
3
]
).

All of the existing literature on Hausdorff dimension of the shrinking target set for affine IFSs has one of two main limitations: either, the IFS has to arise from a rigid grid structure; or the class of IFSs can be somewhat broader as long as a generic translations-condition is satisfied, but the returns are to a contrived dynamical subset rather than a ball. In the current work we are able to bridge the gap between these two set-ups, by computing the Hausdorff dimension of the shrinking target set
R^{*}(z,(r_{n}))
in a representative class of IFSs without grid structure. More precisely, we consider shrinking targets on
PU-sets
, a class of self-affine sets first defined by Przytycki and Urbański
[
22
]
. The affine IFS defining the PU-set consists of two affine maps
f_{0}:(x,y)\to(\lambda x,y/2)
and
f_{2}:(x,y)\to(\lambda x+(1-\lambda),y/2+1/2)
, where
\lambda>1/2
. Notice that the two translations
\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
are specific rather than generic, and that no alignment between images is assumed. We can now informally state our main theorem. The full statement needs more notation and is given as Theorem
2.5
underneath.

Informal version of the main theorem
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
. We compute explicitly the value of
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(z,(\gamma^{n}))
in the following cases:

(1)

For all
z\in F
and for all
\lambda\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2\gamma})
outside of a set of dimension
0
.

(2)

For all
z\in F
satisfying a
unique expansion
condition (see Definition
2.4
), and for all
\lambda\in(\frac{1}{2\gamma},1)
outside of a set of dimension
0
.

(3)

For typical choices of
z\in F
(with respect to a natural measure, see Definition
2.1
), and Lebesgue almost all
\lambda
in the
region of transversality
(see Definition
2.3
).

The PU-family of self-affine IFSs is seemingly simple, but has been one of the main examples driving the intuition in dimension theory of self-affine sets in recent years
[
5
,
4
]
. We will review some of the relevant ideas in Section
2
.

The PU-fractals have intricate local structure, governed by their projections to the two coordinate directions. In particular, it is intimately entwined with the theory of
Bernoulli convolutions
, the distribution of sums of the form
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\pm\lambda^{n}
, with the sign chosen randomly. It has been known since the 1930’s
[
18
]
, that the resulting Bernoulli convolution measure is always either absolutely continuous or singular, and it was proved early on by Erdős
[
12
]
that if
\lambda^{-1}
is Pisot then the corresponding Bernoulli convolution is singular. The question of which other parameters, if any, result in singular measures, has been the object of intense attention over the past several decades, and has inspired new techniques in dimension theory, such as the transversality method in the 90’s
[
26
]
. We now know that the set of parameters for which the resulting measure is singular is of dimension zero
[
24
]
.

The array of methods needed in this article is broad. On the dynamical side of the problem, we extend the theory of shrinking targets for affine IFSs to the geometric target case. On the geometric side, for the dimension analysis, we need to adjust the now-classical transversality techniques
[
26
]
, as well as apply the most recent results on local properties of Bernoulli convolutions
[
24
]
. The bulk of the work will be needed for the proof of part 3 of the theorem, see sections
4
and
7
. As a corollary to the proofs, Theorem
2.7
, we also obtain a result for cylinder targets.

2
Problem set-up and statement of results

Let
\{f_{0},f_{1}\}
be the two-map homogeneous affine iterated function system that contracts by
\lambda
in the
x
-direction and by
\tfrac{1}{2}
in the
y
-direction. More precisely, let
f_{0}:(x,y)\to(\lambda x,y/2)
and
f_{2}:(x,y)\to(\lambda x+(1-\lambda),y/2+1/2)
. Denote by
F
the limit set of the iterated function system, which satisfies

. See Figure 1.

An example of a Przytycki-Urbański fractal for

We will also need to consider another iterated function system, which corresponds to the Bernoulli convolution. Hence, let
g_{0},g_{1}:[0,1]\to[0,1],g_{0}(x)=\lambda x
and
g_{1}(x)=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)
. Notice that in other words,

Use the notation

for the sequence space

and so on for elements of

the shift dynamics on the symbolic space, and also on finite words when appropriate. Furthermore, let
\underline{i}\wedge\underline{j}
be the maximal joint beginning of
\underline{i}
and
\underline{j}
,

the finite sequence obtained by truncating

at the

-th symbol, and

\underline{i}|_{n}^{k}
denote the finite word obtained from the digits of
\underline{i}
between
n
and

. Now let

[\underline{i}]=\{\underline{j}\in\Sigma\mid\underline{j}|_{|\underline{i}|}=\underline{i}\}.

Let
f_{\underline{i}}=f_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{|\underline{i}|}}
in the usual way. Denote by
\pi=\pi_{\lambda}
the natural projection map

\pi:\Sigma\to F,\quad\pi(\underline{i})=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\underline{i}|_{n}}(0).

The map
\pi
is a bijection. Denote by
\pi_{I}:\Sigma\to[0,1]
the natural projection map corresponding to the iterated function system
\{g_{0},g_{1}\}
. We will repeatedly use the notation
\underline{x}
for the point in
\Sigma
with the property
\pi(\underline{x})=x\in F
, and analogously for, say,
z\in F
,
\underline{z}\in\Sigma
. We also define the following measures.

Definition 2.1
.

Denote the Bernoulli measure with equal weights on
\Sigma
by
\nu
, the push-forward to
F
by
\overline{\nu}_{\lambda}=\pi_{*}\nu
, and the push-forward to
[0,1]
by
\nu_{\lambda}=(\pi_{I})_{*}\nu
. Note that, equivalently,
\nu_{\lambda}=(\textrm{proj}_{1})_{*}\overline{\nu}_{\lambda}
, where
\textrm{proj}_{1}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
is the projection to the first coordinate.

Before we look at the shrinking target sets in this setting, let us recall what has been previously shown in terms of the Hausdorff dimension of the set. In Proposition 2 and Theorem 7 of
[
22
]
it is shown that
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}F=2+\frac{\log\lambda}{\log 2}
for those
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
for which the dimension of the Bernoulli convolution
\nu_{\lambda}
is
1
. There is a long history of study of Bernoulli convolutions. Concerning their dimensions some key literature includes:

1.

In
[
26
]
Solomyak showed that
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}\nu_{\lambda}=1
for Lebesgue almost all
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
, using what is known as the transversality technique.

2.

In
[
16
]
Hochman showed that
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}\nu_{\lambda}=1
for all
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
satisfying an exponential separation condition (see
2.2
) which holds for all but a set of packing dimension
0
, and his result was refined by Shmerkin
[
24
]
.

3.

In
[
28

Varjú showed that
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}\nu_{\lambda}=1
for all transcendental
\lambda\in(1/2,1)

It follows that combining

We now turn to the problem this paper is focused on.
Denote by
E:F\to F
the expanding map that has

as its local inverses, i.e. for any

, let
E(x)=f_{i}^{-1}(x)
– since

is defined at every point. For those

, we always take

. Fix

,

, and set the sequence of

to be the sequence of shrinking cubes
(Q_{n}=Q(z,\gamma^{n}))\subset F
. We will want to understand the subset of points

which return to the shrinking targets infinitely often, that is,

R^{*}(z,\lambda,\gamma)=R^{*}=\{x\in F\mid E^{n}(x)\in Q(z,\gamma^{n})\textrm{ for infinitely many }n\}.

We demonstrate in Remark
6.10
that for any sub-exponentially shrinking sequence of targets, the set

has the dimension of

. Hence, the exponential speed of shrinking is appropriate for dimension problems for
R^{*}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let

It is shown in

. Denote

. We say that

satisfy the

. The only property of this set that we use is due to Shmerkin

, and is characterised in Theorem

Definition 2.3
.

There is a value

such that for all

g(\lambda):=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}\bar{\lambda}^{n}\quad\textrm{ with }b_{n}\in\{0,-1,1\}

). The value of

is approximately

, see Theorem 2.6 in
[

. The interval

is known as the

. The only property of

that we will need is due to Peres and Solomyak
[

and is characterised in Theorem

Definition 2.4
.

Given
\lambda\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
, we say that a point

has a unique
\lambda
-expansion if the map
\pi_{I}:\Sigma\to[0,1]

, that is, there a unique

.

Finally, denote the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
by
\mathcal{L}
. We are now ready to state the main theorem.

Theorem 2.5
.

Fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Then the following are true:

(1)

For any
\lambda\in(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2\gamma})\cap\mathcal{E}
, and for all
z\in F
,

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(z,\lambda,\gamma)=\frac{-\log 2}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}.

(2)

For any
\lambda\in(\tfrac{1}{2\gamma},1)\cap\mathcal{E}
, for any
z\in F
such that
\pi_{I}(\underline{z})=\textrm{proj}_{1}(z)
has a unique
\lambda
-expansion,

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(z,\lambda,\gamma)=2+\frac{\log\lambda}{\log 2}-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(\gamma\lambda)}.

(3)

For
\mathcal{L}
-almost every
\lambda\in[\tfrac{1}{2\gamma},\bar{\lambda})
, for
\overline{\nu}_{\lambda}
-almost every
z\in F
,

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(z,\lambda,\gamma)=\frac{2\log 2+\log\lambda}{\log(2/\gamma)}.

The difference between the dimensions in parts (2) and (3) is relating to the different local dimension for the Bernoulli convolution for
\textrm{proj}_{1}(z)
. In fact for part (2) the unique expansion assumption could be strengthened to the assumption that the local dimension of the Bernoulli convolution at
\textrm{proj}_{1}(z)
is
-\frac{\log 2}{\log\lambda}
. It may be the case that a general formula in terms of this local dimension exists, but our arguments rely on the local dimensions being either typical or maximal.

We can also exploit Fubini’s Theorem and the monotonicity in

to obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 2.6
.

For almost every
\lambda\in(\frac{1}{2},\overline{\lambda})

\overline{\nu}_{\lambda}
almost all
z\in F

1.

For
\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2\lambda},1)
we have that

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(z,\lambda,\gamma)=\frac{2\log 2+\log\lambda}{\log(2/\gamma)}.

2.

For
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2\lambda})

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(z,\lambda,\gamma)=\frac{-\log 2}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}.

For
\lambda\in(\frac{1}{2},\overline{\lambda})

\underline{j}\in\Sigma

by

f_{\lambda,\underline{j}}(\gamma)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\log 2}{-\log(\lambda\gamma)}&\text{ if }&\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2\lambda})\\
\frac{2\log 2+\log\lambda}{\log(2/\gamma)}&\text{ if }&\gamma\in[\frac{1}{2\lambda},1)\end{array}\right.

We have by Theorem
2.5
that for all
\gamma\in(0,1)

\mathcal{L}\times\nu(\{(\lambda,\underline{j})\in(\frac{1}{2},\overline{\lambda})\times\Sigma:\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(\pi(\underline{j}),\lambda,\gamma)<f_{\lambda,\underline{j}}(\gamma)\})=0.

Therefore by Fubini for Lebesgue almost every
\lambda
and
\nu

\mathcal{L}(\{\gamma\mid\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(\pi(\underline{j}),\lambda,\gamma)<f_{\lambda,\underline{j}}(\gamma)\})=0.

In our analysis, we will need to consider the sets

. Since the shift map is topologically conjugate to

\ell_{n}=\lceil n\frac{\log\lambda}{\log\gamma}\rceil.

(1)

For all
\lambda\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2\gamma})\cap\mathcal{E}

3
Preliminaries

In the following, the notation
f\lesssim g
means that
f\leq Cg
for some constant
C
.

Definition 3.1
.

Fix
n,r\in{\mathbb{N}}
to set the following notation:

•

\ell_{1}(n)\in{\mathbb{N}}
to be the smallest integer such that
(\tfrac{1}{2})^{\ell_{1}(n)}\leq\gamma^{n}
.

•

\ell_{2}(n)\in{\mathbb{N}}
to be the smallest integer with
\lambda^{\ell_{2}(n)}\leq\gamma^{n}

•

to be the smallest integer with
\lambda^{k(r)}\leq(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}

These will be used repeatedly throughout, with the exception of Section

\ell_{n}:=\ell_{2}(n)
as the parameter
\ell_{1}
is not needed. Further, in Section

\ell_{2}(n,\lambda):=\ell_{2}(n)

Note that as

\frac{\ell_{1}(n)}{n}\to\frac{\log\gamma}{-\log 2},\frac{\ell_{2}(n)}{n}\to\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda},\textrm{ and }\frac{k(r)}{r}\to\frac{\log 2}{-\log\lambda}.

Let
z\in F
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Recall that the targets are given by
Q_{n}=Q(z,\gamma^{n})
. Denote by
R_{n}\subset\Sigma
the set of those symbolic points which return at time

, that is,

R_{n}=\{\underline{i}\in\Sigma\mid\sigma^{n}(\underline{i})\in\pi^{-1}(Q_{n})\}.

Then notice that
R=\limsup_{n\to\infty}R_{n}
, so that for all
N
,

R\subset\bigcup_{n\geq N}R_{n}.

(3.1)

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\underline{i},\underline{j}\in\Sigma

\lambda\in(1/2,1)
. There exists

such that if
|\underline{i}\wedge\underline{j}|=r
then
d(\pi(\underline{i}),\pi(\underline{j}))\geq C2^{-r}
.

Proof.

Fix
\underline{i},\underline{j}\in\Sigma
,
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
and
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
|\underline{i}\wedge\underline{j}|=r
. We will assume that
\underline{i}_{r+1}=1
and
\underline{j}_{r+1}=0
.
Since
\lambda>1/2
we can find
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(1-\lambda)\lambda+\lambda^{n+1}<\lambda^{2}-\lambda^{n+1}

and we set

C_{1}=\lambda^{2}-\lambda^{n+1}-((1-\lambda)\lambda+\lambda^{n+1})>0.

Note that if

\sum_{k=r+1}^{\infty}(\underline{i}_{k}-\underline{j}_{k})2^{k}\geq 2^{-(r+n)}

then
d(\pi(\underline{i}),\pi(\underline{j})\geq 2^{-(r+n)}
. So we now suppose that

\sum_{k=r+1}^{\infty}(\underline{i}_{k}-\underline{j}_{k})2^{k}\leq 2^{-(r+n)}.

In this case we have that
(\underline{i}_{r+1},\ldots,\underline{i}_{r+n})=(1,0,\ldots,0)
and
(\underline{j}_{r+1},\ldots,\underline{j}_{r+n})=(0,1,\ldots,1)
.
Thus

\displaystyle|\pi(\underline{i})-\pi(\underline{j})|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\lambda^{r+2}-\lambda^{r+n+1}-(1-\lambda)\lambda^{r+1}-\lambda^{r+n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{r}(\lambda^{2}-\lambda^{n+1}-((1-\lambda)\lambda+\lambda^{n+1})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C_{1}\lambda^{r}\geq C_{1}2^{-r}

Thus if we let

we have

d(\pi(\underline{i}),\pi(\underline{j})\geq C2^{-r}.

∎

Lemma 3.8

Remark 3.9
.

In the computations, the dimension value

t(\gamma):=2+\frac{\log\lambda}{\log 2}-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(\gamma\lambda)}

will often show up in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\log 2(2+\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda})+\log(\gamma\lambda)}{\log 2(1+\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda})}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{2}{1+\tfrac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda}}+\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(\gamma\lambda)}+\frac{\log\gamma}{\log 2(1+\tfrac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda})}+\frac{\log\lambda}{\log 2(1+\tfrac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda})}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{1+\tfrac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda}}(2+\frac{\log\lambda}{\log 2})+\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(\gamma\lambda)}(1+\frac{\log\lambda}{\log 2})

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{1+\tfrac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda}}(2+\frac{\log\lambda}{\log 2})+\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(\gamma\lambda)}(2+\frac{\log\lambda}{\log 2})-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(\gamma\lambda)}

\displaystyle\quad=t(\gamma).

In this computation we made use of the fact that

\frac{1}{1+\tfrac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda}}+\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}=1.

Finally, we record the following standard result that we will use to give lower bounds for the Hausdorff dimension.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let

be a Borel set, and let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure with
\mu(A)=1
. We have the following:

(1)

If
\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\mu(B(Q(x,r)))}{\log r}\geq s
for all

then
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}A\geq s
.

(2)

If
\iint\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}{|x-y|^{s}}<\infty
then
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}A\geq s
.

This result follows from Theorems 3.3.14 and 3.2.4 in
[

]
. The nature of the measure we will be constructing means we will need a limit inferior as in (1) above, rather than the same result with a limit, that is perhaps more common elsewhere.

4
Upper bounds

In this section we prove the upper bounds for both Theorems
2.5
and
2.7
. We use the standard approach briefly described in the introduction. That is, we cover the shrinking target set by first covering each time
n
return,
E^{-n}(Q_{n})
, by cubes of side length
2^{-r}
, and then take the union of these covers for
n\geq N
; compare to (
3.2
).

For a given
n
, there are three strategies for covering the pre-images
E^{-n}(Q_{n})
that are relevant to the upper bounds at the dimension values of Theorem
2.5
. The very first strategy, which gives the dimension value of (1), is to use a single cube of side
2^{-r_{0}}
, where
r_{0}
is chosen such that
k(r_{0})
is approximately
n+\ell_{2}(n)
(for notation, recall Definition
3.1
). The second strategy gives the dimension value of (3). Here, we cover by cubes of side-length
2^{-r}
where
r=n+\ell_{1}(n)
. The final method is to cover by cubes of side
2^{-r}
where
r=n+\ell_{2}
. This gives the dimension value from case (2) in Theorem
2.5
.
The first two strategies give upper bounds which hold for all centres
z
, whereas the third method only applies in the case when
z
satisfies the unique expansion condition, as it makes use of the fact that there is (almost) no branching between times
n+\ell_{1}(n)
and
n+\ell_{2}(n)
. At the end of the first and third case we will explain how they can be adapted to give the upper bounds in Theorem
2.7
.

4.1
The case
r=r_{0}
:

We fix
\gamma,\lambda
and
\underline{j}
and cover the set
R^{*}=R^{*}(\pi(\underline{j}),\lambda,\gamma)
. Choose
r_{0}
large as possible so that we can use a single cube of side
(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r_{0}}
to cover each of the
2^{n}
pre-images
E^{-n}(Q_{n})
, which are rectangles with the side-lengths
\lambda^{n}\gamma^{n}
and
(\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma)^{n}
. This leads to
r_{0}
the largest integer with
2^{-r_{0}}\geq\lambda^{n+\ell_{2}(n)}
. Hence, we obtain the upper bound

\mathcal{H}^{s}(R^{*})\lesssim\sum_{n\geq N}2^{n}(\lambda^{n}\gamma^{n})^{s},

which sum converges, and hence gives an upper bound to the dimension, when

s>\frac{-\log 2}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}.

This gives the upper bound for part 1 of Theorem
2.5
.

For Theorem
2.7
when
\ell_{n}=\lceil n\frac{\log\lambda}{\log\gamma}\rceil
we have that if
\pi(\underline{j})=z
and
\ell_{n}=\lceil n\frac{\log\lambda}{\log\gamma}\rceil
then

\pi_{\lambda}(\{\underline{j}\in\Sigma\mid\sigma^{n}(i)\in[\underline{j}|_{l_{n}}]\})\subseteq Q_{n}(z,\gamma^{n}).

Thus

\pi_{\lambda}(R(\underline{j},(\ell_{n})))\subseteq R^{*}(\pi(\underline{j}),\lambda,\gamma)
and so it immediately follows that

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R(\underline{j},(\ell_{n}))\leq\frac{-\log 2}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}.

4.2
The case
r=n+\ell_{1}(n)
:

In this case we cover with cubes of side length
2^{-r}=2^{-(n+\ell_{1}(n))}
which, by choice of
\ell_{1}(n)
, corresponds to covering by cubes of side length
2^{-n}\gamma^{n}
, up to constants. We note as in the previous case, that each of the sets
E^{-n}(Q_{n})
is contained in a rectangle of sides
(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n}\gamma^{n}
and
\lambda^{n}\gamma^{n}
. We can cover these by cubes of side-length
\gamma^{n}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n}
. For each of the pre-images, there are (up to constants)

\frac{\lambda^{n}\gamma^{n}}{\gamma^{n}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n}}=\frac{\lambda^{n}}{(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n}}

of them. This means that

\mathcal{H}^{s}(R^{*})\lesssim\sum_{n\geq N}2^{n}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n}}((\tfrac{1}{2})^{n}\gamma^{n})^{s},

which sum converges when

s>\frac{-2\log 2-\log\lambda}{\log(\gamma\cdot\tfrac{1}{2})}.

This gives the upper bound for part 3 of Theorem
2.5
.

4.3
The unique expansion case with
r=n+\ell_{2}(n)
:

In this case we aim to cover with cubes of side length
2^{-r}=2^{-(n+\ell_{2}(n))}
. Let
z=\pi_{\lambda}(\underline{z})
satisfy the unique expansion condition, that is,
\pi_{I}(\underline{z})
has unique
\lambda
-expansion
\underline{z}
. We then have that
\pi_{\lambda}(\underline{i})\in E^{-n}(Q_{n})
implies
|\pi_{I}(\sigma^{n}(\underline{i}))-\pi_{I}(\underline{z})|\leq\gamma^{n}
. Recall that

N_{k}(z)=\#\{\underline{i}\in\Sigma_{k}\mid\pi[\underline{i}]\cap Q(z,\gamma^{k})\neq\emptyset\}.

Thus, since
\ell_{2}(n)
is the smallest integer satisfying
\lambda^{\ell_{2}(n)}\leq\gamma^{n}
, there are at most
N_{\ell_{2}(n)}
choices for
(i_{n+1},\ldots,i_{n+\ell_{2}(n)})
. Thus

can be covered by
2^{n}N_{\ell_{2}(n)}

, Furthermore, each of those can be covered by
\lambda^{n+\ell_{2}(n)}2^{n+\ell_{2}(n)}

, up to constants.

Therefore we have that for

, the Hausdorff measure of

can be estimated

\mathcal{H}^{s}(R^{*}(z,\lambda))\lesssim\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{n\geq M}2^{n}N_{\ell_{2}(n)}\lambda^{n+\ell_{2}(n)}2^{n+l_{2}(n)}2^{-s(n+\ell_{2}(n))}.

For the dynamical shrinking target result Theorem

Similarly to above it is true that

Fix

\overline{\mu}(\mathcal{C})=1

\underline{x}

where

and

thus for

.

. We consider

such that
R\in[C2^{-r_{-1}(n_{m+1})},C2^{-r_{-1}(n_{m})}]
, where

•

r_{-1}=r_{-1}(n_{m})

, and

•

such that

Note that since

. Underneath, we will handle separately three ranges of values

R\in[C2^{-n_{m}},C2^{-r_{-1}(n_{m})}]
,
R\in[C2^{-r_{0}},C2^{-n_{m}}]
and

.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{C}
. Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfy
r_{-1}(n_{m})\leq r\leq n_{m}
. Then for all

we have that

\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))\leq 2^{L(m-1)-r}.

Proof.

Let
\underline{x}\in\Sigma
satisfy that
\pi(\underline{x})=x
. Suppose that
\underline{y}\in\Sigma
with

|\pi(\underline{y})-\pi(\underline{x})|<R.

We have that by Lemma
3.2
for
1\leq i\leq r
,
\underline{x}_{i}=\underline{y}_{i}
. Thus using the definition of
\mu
gives

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(\{\underline{y}\mid|\pi(\underline{x})-\pi(\underline{y})|<R\})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu(\{\underline{y}\mid x_{i}=y_{i}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq r\})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{-r+L(m-1)}

and the result follows.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

We have that for
x\in\mathcal{C}
,
n_{m}\leq r\leq r_{0}(n_{m})
and
R\in[C2^{-(r+1)},C2^{-r})
that

\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))\leq 2^{-n_{m}+L_{m-1}}.

Proof.

Let
\underline{x}\in\Sigma
satisfy that
\pi(\underline{x})=x
. Suppose that
\underline{y}\in\Sigma
satisfies
\pi(\underline{y})\in Q((x,R))
.
We have that by Lemma
3.2
for
1\leq i\leq r
,
\underline{x}_{i}=\underline{y}_{i}
. Thus using that
r\geq n_{m}
we have that

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(\{\underline{y}\mid|\pi(\underline{y})\in Q(x,R)\})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu(\{\underline{y}\mid\underline{y}_{i}=\underline{x}_{i}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq r\})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{-n_{m}+L_{m-1}}.

∎

In the final range we need to make use of the exponential separation condition that
\lambda
satisfies. Note that for some of the range it would be possible to get better estimates but any finer analysis is unnecessary for case (1) of Theorem
2.5
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C_{1}>0
such that for
x\in\mathcal{C}
,
r_{0}(n_{m})\leq r\leq r_{-1}(n_{m+1})
and
R\in[C2^{-(r+1)},C2^{-r})
we have the estimate

\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))\leq C_{1}2^{-n_{m}+L_{m-1}}\lambda^{(k(r)-(n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m}))(1-\varepsilon)}.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\underline{x}\in\Sigma
satisfy that
\pi(\underline{x})=x
. Suppose that
\underline{y}\in\Sigma
satisfies that
\pi(\underline{y})\in Q(x,R).

We have that by Lemma
3.2
for all
1\leq i\leq r

y_{i}=x_{i}
. Moreover since
x\in\mathcal{C}
we have that
x_{i}=z_{j-n_{m}}
for all
r_{0}<j\leq k(r_{0}(n_{m}))=n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m})
. Thus if for some
r_{0}<j\leq k(r_{0}(n_{m}))=n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m})
we have that
y_{j}\neq x_{j}
, and then

\mu([y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}])=0.

Hence, we may suppose that
x_{i}=y_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n_{m}+l_{2}(n_{m})
. Thus we wish to find

V=\#\{(y_{n_{m}+l_{2}(n_{m})+1},\ldots,y_{k(r)})\mid\pi([x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m})},y_{n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m})+1},\ldots,y_{k(r)}])\cap Q(x,R)\neq\emptyset\}.

Since
\lambda\in\mathcal{E}
we can use Corollary
3.5
to bound

V\leq C_{1}\lambda^{(1-\varepsilon)(k(r)-(n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m})))}2^{k(r)-(n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m}))}.

Putting this together we get that

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle V2^{-k(r)+L_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle V2^{-k(r)+n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m})}2^{-n_{m}+L_{m-1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}\lambda^{(1-\varepsilon)(k(r)-(n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m})))}2^{-n_{m}+L_{m-1}}

which is what was wanted.
∎

We now need to consider what each of the above estimates gives as
m\to\infty
. Firstly, for
r_{-1}(n_{m})\leq r\leq-n_{m}
and
R_{m}\in(C2^{-(r+1)},C2^{-r})
we obtain
by Lemma
5.1
that, for any

,

\frac{\log(\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R_{m}))}{\log R_{m}}\geq\frac{\log(2^{-r-L_{m-1}})}{\log C2^{-r-1}}=\frac{r+L_{m-1}}{r+1}.

That is, there is a function
\delta(m)
, approaching
0
with

, for which

r+L_{m-1}\geq r-n_{m-1}\geq r-n_{m}(\delta(m))\geq r-r(\delta(m)\frac{\log 2}{-\log\lambda}).

Therefore in this region we have

\frac{\log(\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R_{m}))}{\log R_{m}}\geq 1+\mathit{o}(1).

In the second part we have
n_{m}\leq r\leq r_{0}(n_{m})
and
R_{m}\in[C2^{-(r+1)},C2^{-r}]
, so that by Lemma
5.2
we have for

\frac{\log(\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R_{m}))}{\log R_{m}}\geq\frac{\log 2^{-n_{m}+L_{m-1}}}{\log R_{m}}\geq\frac{\log 2^{-n_{m}+L_{m-1}}}{\log(C2^{-r_{0}(n_{m})})}.

This gives

\frac{\log(\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R_{m}))}{\log R_{m}}\geq\frac{\log 2}{-\log(\lambda\gamma)}+\mathit{o}(1).

Finally for the third part we have that for a fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
r_{0}(n_{m})\leq r\leq r_{-1}(n_{m+1})
and
R_{m}\in[C2^{-(r+1)},C2^{-r}]
by Lemma
5.3

\frac{\log(\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R_{m}))}{\log R_{m}}\geq\frac{\log(2^{-n_{m}+L_{m-1}})+\log(C_{1}\lambda^{(1-\varepsilon)(k(r)-(n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m})))})}{\log 2^{-r_{0}(n_{m})}+\log(\lambda^{k(r)-(n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m}))})}

and thus in this region

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\frac{\log(\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R_{m}))}{\log R_{m}}\geq\min\{(1-\varepsilon),\frac{\log 2}{-\log(\lambda\gamma)}\}+\mathit{o}(1).

Combining these estimates, letting
m\to\infty
,
\varepsilon\to 0
and using that
\frac{-\log 2}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}\leq 1
gives, by part 1 of Lemma
3.10
, that
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{C}\geq\frac{-\log 2}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}
. Thus combining with Section 4.1 we get that
\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(z,\lambda)=\frac{-\log 2}{-\log\lambda\gamma}
for all
\lambda\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2\gamma})\cap\mathcal{E}
.This finished the proof of part (1) of Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Lower bound for Theorem

In this section we prove the lower bound for part (2) of Theorem

2.4

Recall the dimension value from the statement and denote it by

Let

Let

(6.1)

Hence for lower bounds to

,

Remark 6.1
.

The computations in this section work for any

To show (

Let

(n_{m})
. Denote

grows so fast that

•

, and

•

\frac{L_{m-1}}{n_{m}}\to 0
.

Define a mass distribution

by setting for a finite word

This extends to a Borel probability measure

on

by Carathéodory’s Extension Theorem. Denote

and note that

.

Let

and

. We would like to give upper bounds on

which will enable us to bound below

which will then give a lower bound on the dimension of

by Lemma
3.10
.

Let

for some

. Let

be the closest return to

, that is, assume that

. The arguments presented throughout will depend crucially on the size of

relative to
\lambda^{n_{m}},\lambda^{n_{m}}\gamma^{n_{m}}
. For

•

,

•

,

•

: the smallest integer for which

,

•

r_{-1}\in{\mathbb{N}}
: the largest integer for which

,

•

r_{1}\in{\mathbb{N}}
: the largest integer for which

•

: the constant from Lemma
3.2
,

•

\mathit{o}(1)
is a function that approaches

with

(or

as all of these happen simultaneously).

In the lemmas underneath, we go through the possible relative positions of

to these other quantities. Note that since

we have that

. We will now fix

and cover all the possible cases.

Lemma 6.2
.

There is some
C_{1}>0
such that the following holds: Let
\varepsilon>0
,
x\in\mathcal{C}
,
r_{-1}\leq r<r_{0}
and
R\in[C2^{-(r+1)},C2^{-r})
. Then

\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))\leq C_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{{r-L_{m-1}}}\lambda^{(n_{m}-r)(1-\varepsilon)}.

Proof.

Notice that in order for a sequence
\underline{i}\in\Sigma
to satisfy
\pi(\underline{i})\in Q=Q(x,R)
we have
\underline{x}|_{r}=\underline{i}|_{r}
by Lemma
3.2
. We will have to give an estimate for the number of next
n_{m}-r
symbols in
\underline{i}
which would still let
\pi(\underline{i})\in Q
, and after that until
n_{m}+\ell_{n_{m}}
they will have to agree with
\underline{z}
. By the definition of
\mu
, their mass does not change after this level. Denote

N=\#\{\underline{i}\in\Sigma^{n_{m}}\mid\pi[\underline{i}]\cap Q(x,R)\neq\emptyset\}.

By the above discussion we have the estimate

\overline{\mu}(Q)\leq N(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n_{m}-L_{m-1}}.

The rest of the argument focuses on bounds for
N
. Note that in order for
\pi[\underline{i}]\cap Q\neq\emptyset
, we need

\sum_{j=1}^{n_{m}}(\underline{i}_{j}-\underline{x}_{j})\lambda^{j}\leq C(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}\leq(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r},

but since we know that
\underline{i}_{j}=\underline{x}_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,r
, this translates to

\sum_{j=r}^{n_{m}}(\underline{i}_{j}-\underline{x}_{j})\lambda^{j}\leq(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r},

and further to

\sum_{j=r}^{n_{m}}(\underline{i}_{j}-\underline{x}_{j})\lambda^{j-r}\leq(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}\lambda^{-r}.

Note that since
r\geq r_{1}
, we have
(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}\leq\lambda^{n_{m}}
, and hence for some
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]
,

N\leq\#\{\underline{i}\in\Sigma_{n_{m}-r}\mid\bar{\pi}[\underline{i}]\cap B(x^{\prime},\lambda^{n_{m}-r})\}

To estimate this number, we can apply Corollary
3.5
, and obtain

N\leq C_{1}\frac{\lambda^{(n_{m}-r)(1-\varepsilon)}}{(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n_{m}-r}}.

(6.3)

Together with the argument from the beginning of the proof this implies

\overline{\mu}(Q)\leq C_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n_{m}-L_{m-1}}\frac{\lambda^{(n_{m}-r)(1-\varepsilon)}}{(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n_{m}-r}}.

∎

Remark 6.3
.

We can apply Lemma
6.2
, to obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\log\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))}{\log R}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(n_{m}-r)(1-\varepsilon)\log\lambda+(r-L_{m-1})\log(\tfrac{1}{2})}{(r+1)\log(C(\tfrac{1}{2}))}+\frac{\log C_{1}}{(r+1)\log(C(\tfrac{1}{2}))}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(n_{m}-r)(1-2\varepsilon)\log\lambda}{(r+1)\log C(\tfrac{1}{2})}+\frac{(r-L_{m-1})\log(\tfrac{1}{2})}{(r+1)\log(C(\tfrac{1}{2}))}+o(1)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{n_{m}(1-\varepsilon)\log\lambda}{(r+1)\log(C(\tfrac{1}{2}))}+\frac{r(1-\varepsilon)\log\lambda}{(r+1)\log(2C)}+\frac{(r-L_{m-1})\log(\tfrac{1}{2})}{(r+1)\log(C(\tfrac{1}{2}))}+o(1).

We have that
L_{m-1}=\mathit{o}(r_{-1})
and

as

. Thus we have

\displaystyle\frac{\log\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))}{\log R}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\log 2}{-\log(\lambda\gamma)}\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\lambda}{-\log 2}+\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\lambda}{\log 2}+1+\mathit{o}(1)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\lambda}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}+\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\lambda}{\log 2}+1+\mathit{o}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)+\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\gamma}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}+\frac{(1-\varepsilon\log\lambda}{\log 2}+1+\mathit{o}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2-\varepsilon+\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\gamma}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}+\frac{(1-\varepsilon\log\lambda}{\log 2}+\mathit{o}(1).

Taking

to
0
gives the dimension value

from Theorem
2.5
(2).

Lemma 6.4
.

There exists
C_{2}>0
such that for

,

,

and

\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))\leq C_{2}\lambda^{(-r-\ell_{n_{m}})}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r+r(1-\varepsilon)-L_{m-1}}.

Proof.

We let
\underline{x}\in\Sigma
satisfy that
\pi(\underline{x})=x
and note that as before for
\underline{i}\in\Sigma
to satisfy
\pi(\underline{i})\in Q(x,R)=Q
we need to have

by definition of

. We then need an estimate for the number of
\underline{i}|_{r}=\underline{x}|_{r}
with also
\underline{i}|_{n_{m}}
such that
\pi[\underline{i}|_{n_{m}}]\cap Q\neq\emptyset
. This quantity we have just defined as

and estimated from above in Lemma

, see (
6.3
).

From there, between the times

and

, not all of the mass of the cylinders on level

is inside

. That is, for a projected cylinder
\pi[\underline{i}|_{n_{m}+\ell_{n_{m}}}]

. Fix some such

the first coordinate of

\pi^{-1}(Q\cap\pi[\underline{i}|_{n_{m}+\ell_{n_{m}}}])\leq\bar{\pi}^{-1}(B(x_{1},C(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r})\cap\bar{\pi}[\underline{i}|_{n_{m}+\ell_{n_{m}}}]).

\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R)\cap\pi[\underline{i}|_{n_{m}+\ell_{n_{m}}}])\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}{\lambda^{\ell_{n_{m}}+n_{m}}}\right)^{1-\varepsilon}.

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\tfrac{1}{2})^{n_{m}-L_{m-1}}NC_{1}\left(\frac{(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}{\lambda^{\ell_{n_{m}}+n_{m}}}\right)^{1-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}^{2}\lambda^{(n_{m}-r-\ell_{n_{m}}-n_{m})(1-\varepsilon)}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r+r(1-\varepsilon)-L_{m-1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}^{2}\lambda^{(-r-n_{m})(1-\varepsilon)}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r+r(1-\varepsilon)-L_{m-1}}

\displaystyle C_{2}\lambda^{-r-n_{m}}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r+r(1-\varepsilon)-L_{m-1}}

for

∎

Remark 6.5
.

As before we look at what this means for the local dimension as

approaches infinity. We have

\displaystyle\frac{\log\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))}{\log R}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log C_{2}+(-r-\ell_{n_{m}})(\log\lambda)-(r(2-\varepsilon)-L_{m-1})\log(2)}{\log C-(r+1)\log 2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log(C_{2})}{\log C-(r+1)\log 2}+\frac{(-r-\ell_{n_{m}})(\log\lambda)}{\log C-(r+1)\log 2}-\frac{(r(2-\varepsilon)-L_{m-1})\log(2)}{\log C-(r+1)\log 2}.

The first part of the sum goes to

as

. For the second part we use that
\frac{\log\lambda}{\log 2}<0
along with

\frac{r+\ell_{n_{m}}}{r}\leq\frac{r_{0}+\ell_{n_{m}}}{r_{0}}\to\frac{\log(\lambda\gamma)-\frac{\log(\gamma)\log(2)}{\log\lambda}}{\log(\lambda\gamma)}\text{ as }m\to\infty

and finally for the third part

. This gives the following bound

\displaystyle\frac{\log\overline{\mu}(Q(x,R))}{\log R}

\displaystyle\geq

Remark 6.7

The estimate from Lemma

Remark 6.10
.

As mentioned above the lower bound for this section holds for any choice of centre

,

7
Lower bound in Theorem
2.5
(3): Typical centre

In this section, we consider the third case of Theorem
2.5
. We will consider multiple parameters
\lambda
simultaneously. The dependence on
\lambda
as a parameter will, for example, be relevant to the definition of
\ell_{2}
and hence we will write
\ell_{2}(n,\lambda)
to emphasise the dependence. We consider
\lambda>(2\gamma)^{-1}
. Just as in Section
6
, we need to define a Cantor subset
\mathcal{C}
of

and a corresponding mass distribution
\mu
in order to estimate the dimension from below. The difference between parts (2) and (3) in Theorem
2.5
is that in part (2) the centre of the target

had a unique
\lambda
expansion, but in part (3) we are dealing with a centre
z
, generic for
\overline{\nu}_{\lambda}
on
F
, and which will in general have multiple expansions. Here we need to use both exponential separation and transversality techniques, and consequently, our results hold for Lebesgue almost every

within the region of transversality, that is, for

(see Definition

The strategy is still to consider a Cantor set where the returns are at sparse, predetermined times
(n_{m})
, together with an appropriate well-distributed measure on this set. However, we cannot use the exact same choice of
\mathcal{C}
and the measure
\mu
as we did before. This is a consequence of the following: For points
\underline{i}\in R_{n}
, the choices of digits between times

and

are fixed:
\underline{i}|_{n_{m}}^{n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}

, between

and

there are potentially multiple possibilities for the digits in
\underline{i}
, and indeed, both the value of
\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)
and the exact distribution of branching in this range depend on
\lambda
. These appear as parameters in the definition of the measure below.

\displaystyle\#\{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{k-n_{m}-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)}\mid

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-n_{m}-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)}\omega_{i}\lambda^{i}\in B(x_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)}j_{i}\lambda_{i},C2^{-r})\}

\displaystyle\lesssim(2\lambda^{1-\varepsilon})^{k(r)-n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)}.

This gives that for each
|\underline{j}|=n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)

\mu_{\lambda}(\{\omega\mid\omega\in[\underline{j}]\cap Q(x,C2^{-r})\})\lesssim(\lambda^{1-\varepsilon})^{{k(r)-n_{m}-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)}}\leq(2^{-r}\lambda^{-\ell_{2}-n_{m}})^{1-\varepsilon}.

Putting this together

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{\lambda}(Q(x,C2^{-r}))

\displaystyle\lesssim(2^{-r}\lambda^{-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)-n_{m}})^{1-\varepsilon}2^{r\varepsilon}(2\lambda)^{(n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)-r)(1-\varepsilon)}2^{-n_{m}}2^{r\varepsilon}(2\lambda)^{-\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-\varepsilon\xi n_{m}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{2r\varepsilon}\lambda^{-\varepsilon\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-r-\xi n_{m}}2^{-2r+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)-\xi n_{m}}.

∎

We now are left with only the final case to consider.

Lemma 7.8
.

For
n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)\leq r<r_{1}<n_{m+1}
. Then for
x\in\mathcal{C}
,

\overline{\mu}_{\lambda}(Q(x,C(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r})\lesssim\lambda^{-\varepsilon r}2^{2r\varepsilon}2^{-2r+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)-\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-\xi n_{m}-r}

Proof.

It follows from (
7.2
) that

\displaystyle\mu_{\lambda}[\underline{x}|_{r}]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{r\varepsilon}2^{-r+\ell_{2}(n_{m})}(2\lambda)^{-\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-\varepsilon\xi n_{m}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\lambda^{-\varepsilon r}2^{r\varepsilon}2^{-r+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)-\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-\xi n_{m}}

From here the argument is analogous to the argument in Lemma
6.8
, with the above estimate replacing (
6.4
). Hence we get that

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{\lambda}(Q(x,C(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\lambda^{-\varepsilon r}2^{r\varepsilon}2^{-r+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)-\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{(k(r)-r)(1-\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\lambda^{-\varepsilon r}2^{2r\varepsilon}2^{-2r+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)+\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-\xi n_{m}-r}

which gives what we want.
∎

To complete the proof of Part (3) Theorem

, we need to show that for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
for
\mathcal{L}
-almost every
\lambda\in[\tfrac{1}{2\gamma},\bar{\lambda})
, for
\bar{\nu}_{\lambda}
-almost every

,

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(z,\lambda)\geq\frac{2\log 2+\log\lambda}{\log(2/\gamma)}.

We begin by collecting all the Lemmas in this section together to prove the following statement.
Recall that we have set

\eta(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})=\left(\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log\lambda_{1}}-1\right)+(s+1)\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{1}}-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{0}}\right)

Lemma 7.9
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
. There exists
C_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
there is a set
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\subset[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}]
with
\mathcal{L}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon})\geq\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon

\mathcal{L}
-almost every
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}R^{*}(\pi_{\lambda}{\omega},\lambda)\geq\frac{2\log 2+\log\lambda_{0}}{\log(2/\gamma)}-C_{0}\varepsilon-\eta(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}).

Proof.

We follow the strategy described at the beginning of Section
7.3
, and aim for the energy bound (
7.4
). We fix
\varepsilon>0
and take
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\subset[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}]
as specified at the start of Section
7.3
. We will use the estimate (
7.5

\int_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}\iint|\pi(\underline{i})-\pi(\underline{j})|^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\underline{i})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\underline{j})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}\lesssim\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}2^{rt}M(r).

Recall that Lemmas
7.6
,
7.8
and
7.7
imply an upper bound to
M(r)
via (
7.17
). We are looking for a
t
such that this sum converges, and thus it is sufficient to find
t
such that for all large

\frac{\log M(r)}{\log(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}>t.

Recall the notation

s=\frac{2\log 2+\log\lambda_{0}}{\log(2/\gamma)}.

We will show that there is some
C_{0}>0
such that for all
r
sufficiently large

\frac{\log M(r)}{\log(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}>s-C_{0}\varepsilon-\eta(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}).

(7.18)

Notice that for
\lambda_{0}>1/(2\gamma)
,

s<\min\left\{2+\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log\tfrac{1}{2}},\frac{-\log 2}{\log(\gamma\lambda_{1})},2+\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log 2}+\frac{\log\gamma}{\log(\gamma\lambda_{1})}\right\}.

Let us now consider all possible values of
r
in turn. We start with the case of Lemma
7.4
where
r_{1}<r<r_{0}<n_{m}
, and
M(r)\lesssim 2^{r\varepsilon}(2^{-n_{m}}+2^{-2r}\lambda_{0}^{-r})
. Then one of these terms dominates and either, for large enough
r
,

\frac{\log M(r)}{\log(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}\geq\frac{\log 2+\log 2^{-n_{m}}}{\log 2^{-r}}-\varepsilon\geq\frac{\log 2+\log 2^{-n_{m}}}{\log 2^{-r_{0}}}-\varepsilon\geq\frac{\log 2-\log 2}{\log(\lambda_{0}\gamma)}-\varepsilon>s-\varepsilon,

or

\frac{\log M(r)}{\log(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}\geq\frac{\log 2+\log(2^{-2r}\lambda_{0}^{-r})}{\log 2^{-r}}-\varepsilon=2+\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log 2}-\frac{\log 2}{r\log 2}-\varepsilon>s-\varepsilon.

(7.19)

Now consider the case of Lemma
7.5
,
r_{0}<r<n_{m}
and
M(r)
is bounded from above by

\lesssim 2^{2r\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{n_{m}}((\tfrac{1}{2})^{2r}\lambda_{0}^{-r}+\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}}\gamma^{-n_{m}}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r+n_{m}}).

We write
{\bf M}(r)=((\tfrac{1}{2})^{2r}\lambda_{0}^{-r}+\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}}\gamma^{-n_{m}}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r+n_{m}})
for the main term, and for the error term use the notation
{\bf E}(r)=2^{2r\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{n_{m}}
with
M(r)\lesssim{\bf M}(r){\bf E}(r)
.

Let us first consider the error term
{\bf E}(r)
. For this term for large enough
r
,

\displaystyle\frac{\log({\bf E}(r))}{\log 2^{-r}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-2\varepsilon-\frac{n_{m}}{r}\frac{\frac{\log(\lambda_{1})}{\log(\lambda_{0})}}{\log 2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-3\varepsilon+\frac{\log 2}{\log\lambda_{1}}\frac{\frac{\log(\lambda_{1})}{\log(\lambda_{0})}}{\log 2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-3\varepsilon-\left(\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log\lambda_{1}}-1\right).

As of the two main terms, one of them dominates in the upper bound; if it is the first term, we repeat the computation (
7.19
) to obtain
{\bf M}(r)\geq s
. If the second term is the larger one, we use the estimate
r<n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})
, noting that
2\gamma\lambda>1
to obtain the estimate

\frac{\log{\bf M}(r)}{\log(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}\geq\frac{\log 2+\log(\lambda_{0}\gamma)^{-n_{m}}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r+n_{m}}}{\log(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}\geq 1+\frac{n_{m}\log(2\gamma\lambda_{0})^{-1}}{(n_{m}+\ell_{1})\log(\tfrac{1}{2})}-\frac{1}{r}.

Here, for any
\delta>0
, for large enough
r
,

\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\tfrac{1}{2}}-\delta<\frac{\ell_{1}(n_{m})}{n_{m}}<\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\tfrac{1}{2}}+\delta.

(7.20)

Hence, for large enough
r
,

\displaystyle\frac{-\log 2+\log(\lambda_{0}\gamma)^{-n_{m}}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r+n_{m}}}{\log(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}

\displaystyle\geq 1+\frac{n_{m}}{n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}+\frac{n_{m}}{n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\cdot\frac{\log(\lambda_{0}\gamma)^{-1}}{\log\tfrac{1}{2}}-\varepsilon

(7.21)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2\log 2+\log\lambda_{0}}{\log(2/\gamma)}-\varepsilon

\displaystyle>s-\varepsilon.

Combining with the error term we get that

\frac{\log M(r)}{-r\log 2}\geq s-4\varepsilon-\left(\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log\lambda_{1}}-1\right)\geq s-4\varepsilon-\eta(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}).

The next range of
r
is
n_{m}<r<n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})
, where by Lemma
7.6
,

M(r)\lesssim 2^{2r\varepsilon}(\lambda_{0}\gamma)^{-n_{m}}(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r+n_{m}}.

Again, we first estimate the impact of the error term

\frac{2r\varepsilon\log 2}{-r\log 2}=-2\varepsilon.

We can now repeat the computation (
7.21
), to obtain the same bound for the main term, and hence,

\frac{\log M(r)}{\log(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}}\geq\frac{2\log 2+\log\lambda_{0}}{\log(2/\gamma)}-\varepsilon>s-3\varepsilon.

For the next range, consider
n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})<r<n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})
. Let
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
be arbitrary, and recall from Lemma
7.7
that for
n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})<r<n(n_{m})+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)
and any
x\in\mathcal{C}
we have,

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{\lambda}(Q(x,(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}))

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{-2r+\ell_{2}(\lambda,n_{m})-\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-r-\xi n_{m}}2^{\varepsilon 2r}\lambda^{-\varepsilon\xi n_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\varepsilon 2r}\lambda_{0}^{-\varepsilon\xi n_{m}}2^{-2r+\ell_{2}(\lambda,n_{m})-\xi n_{m}}\lambda_{0}^{-r-\xi n_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\varepsilon 2r}\lambda_{0}^{-\varepsilon\xi n}2^{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}2^{-2r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-r-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\varepsilon 2r}\lambda_{0}^{-\varepsilon\xi n}2^{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}2^{-2r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-r-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}.

This is now independent of
\lambda
and so we can estimate from (
7.17
)

M(r)\lesssim 2^{\varepsilon 2r}\lambda_{0}^{-\varepsilon\xi n}2^{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}2^{-2r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-r-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}.

Thus we can write
M(r)\lesssim{\bf E}(r){\bf M}(r)
where

{\bf E}(r)=2^{\varepsilon 2r}\lambda_{0}^{-\varepsilon\xi n}2^{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}

and

{\bf M}(r)=2^{-2r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-r-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}.

We first have for
r
sufficiently large and using that
\lambda_{0}^{-\varepsilon\xi n_{m}}\leq 2^{r\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\frac{\log{\bf E}(r)}{-r\log 2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-3\varepsilon-\frac{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}{r}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-3\varepsilon-\frac{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}{n_{m}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-4\varepsilon-\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{1}}-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{0}}\right)

For the main part
{\bf M}(r)
we use that
2+\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log\frac{1}{2}}>s
to get

\displaystyle{\bf M}(r)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{-2r+\ell_{2}(\lambda_{0})-\xi n_{m}}\lambda_{0}^{-r-\xi n_{m}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{-n_{m}-r}\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}2^{-r+n_{m}+\ell_{2}(\lambda_{0})-\xi n_{m}}\lambda_{0}^{-r-\xi n_{m}+n_{m}+\ell_{2}(\lambda_{0},n_{m})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(2^{-n-\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}})^{s}2^{-r+n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})+n_{m}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(2^{-n_{m}-\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}})^{s}2^{-2(r-n_{m}-\ell_{1}(n_{m}))}\lambda_{0}^{n_{m}-r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(2^{-n_{m}-\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}})^{s}(2^{-r+n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})})^{2+\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(2^{-n_{m}-\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}})^{s}(2^{-r+n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})})^{s}\leq 2^{-rs}.

Putting this together for large enough

\frac{\log M(r)}{\log r}>s-5\varepsilon-\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{1}}-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{0}}\right)

On the other hand, if
n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})<r<n_{m}+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})

for cubes radius

. This gives

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{\lambda}(Q(x,(\tfrac{1}{2})^{r}))

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{2r\varepsilon}\lambda^{-\varepsilon\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-n_{m}-2\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}2^{-2n_{m}-2\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}

Hence for
r
in this range we can estimate
M(r)\lesssim{\bf E}(r){\bf M}(r)
where

{\bf E}(r)\leq 2^{2r\varepsilon}\lambda_{0}^{-\varepsilon\xi n_{m}}2^{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}

and

{\bf M}(r)\leq\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}-2\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}2^{-2n_{m}-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}.

For the error term, using that
\lambda_{0}^{-\varepsilon\xi n_{m}}\leq 2^{r\varepsilon}
, for sufficiently large

\displaystyle\frac{\log{\bf E}(r)}{-r\log 2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-3\varepsilon-\frac{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}{r}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-3\varepsilon-\frac{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}{n_{m}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-4\varepsilon-\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{1}}-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{0}}\right)

With the main term
{\bf M}(r)

\displaystyle{\bf M}(r)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}-2\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}2^{-2n_{m}-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{-2n_{m}-\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}2^{-2(\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})-\ell_{1}(n_{m}))}\lambda_{0}^{-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}

\displaystyle 2^{s(\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0}))}2^{-rs}.

Hence, for

\displaystyle\frac{\log M(r)}{-2\log r}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle s-5\varepsilon-(s+1)\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{1}}-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{0}}\right)

Finally we have the range
n_{m}+\ell_{2}(\lambda_{1},n_{m})<r<r_{1}
. Here by Lemma

\displaystyle M(r)

\displaystyle\lambda^{-\varepsilon r}2^{2r\varepsilon}2^{-2r+\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda)-\xi n_{m}}\lambda^{-\xi n_{m}-r}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{1})-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}\lambda^{-\varepsilon r}2^{2r\varepsilon}2^{-2r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})-r}

We know that
2+\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log 2}>\frac{2\log 2+\log\lambda_{0}}{\log(2/\gamma)}=s
,

2^{-2n_{m}}\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}}

\lambda_{0}^{\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}

, and where we can choose

\displaystyle{\bf M}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-2r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})+\ell_{1}(n_{m})-r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-2n_{m}-\ell_{1}(n_{m})}\lambda_{0}^{-n_{m}-\ell_{2}(n_{m},\lambda_{0})}2^{-2(r-n_{m}-\ell_{1}(n_{m}))}\lambda_{0}^{-r+n_{m}+\ell_{1}(n_{m})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2^{-n_{m}-\ell_{1}(n_{m})})^{s}(2^{-r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})+n_{m}})^{2+\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log 2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2^{-n_{m}-\ell_{1}(n_{m})})^{s}(2^{-r+\ell_{1}(n_{m})+n_{m}})^{s}\leq 2^{-rs}

Thus in this range we have that

\frac{\log M(r)}{\log r}\geq s-3\varepsilon-\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{1}}-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{0}}\right).

Putting everything together this completes the proof with
C_{0}=5
and

\eta(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})=\left(\frac{\log\lambda_{0}}{\log\lambda_{1}}-1\right)+(s+1)\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{1}}-\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\lambda_{0}}\right).

∎

Proof of Theorem

(3) as a consequence of Lemma

.
