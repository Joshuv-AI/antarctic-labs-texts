Sodium new houttuyfonate affects tumor angiogenesis by suppressing FGF1 expression and the p38/MAPK signaling pathway in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Xin Yu, Lihong Jiang, Xiaoyu Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic cancer is one of the deadliest cancers in the world. Phytochemicals are considered as potential anticancer drugs because of their safety and low cost. Sodium new houttuyfonate (SNH) is derived from Houttuynia cordata Thunb. (HCT), which is a famous edible and medicinal plant in China. Although research findings suggest that SNH possesses anticancer properties, its role and mechanisms in pancreatic cancer remain unclear. Using network pharmacology, we identified 49 potential targets of SNH action in pancreatic cancer. In vitro, we confirmed that SNH inhibited the proliferation and invasion, promoted mitochondrial damage and induce apoptosis of PANC-1 cells. In vivo, SNH promoted tumor apoptosis and inhibited pancreatic cancer development in mice. In addition, RNA sequencing demonstrated that SNH exerted anticancer effects by regulating the MAPK (Mitogen-activated protein kinase) signaling pathway and identified FGF1 (Fibroblast growth factor 1) as a key gene in this process. Moreover, FGF1 is also an important gene affecting tumor angiogenesis, which is an important process in tumor development. Therefore, the study demonstrated that SNH inhibited pancreatic cancer progression by down-regulating FGF1 expression and inactivating the p38/MAPK pathway, thereby affecting tumor angiogenesis. Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is one of the most common tumors of the digestive system and is the seventh leading cause of cancer-related deaths in both male and female populations1. In the past few decades, the global burden of pancreatic cancer has increased dramatically and is expected to become the third leading cause of cancer death in European countries1.

At present, systemic chemotherapy is still the main treatment for pancreatic cancer. Gemcitabine is a first-line therapeutic agent for pancreatic cancer2. In recent years, FORFIRINOX and gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel have been associated with improved survival, as compared with monotherapy3. However, pancreatic cancer is resistant to several chemotherapeutic drugs4,5, and the effects of chemotherapy are relatively poor6. Additionally, chemotherapy drugs often have serious side effects and reduce the quality of life of patients7. Therefore, reducing the toxic side effects of drugs, improving the quality of life of patients, and prolonging survival are important research areas.

Phytochemicals are considered potential alternative or complementary anticancer drugs because of their safety, low-cost, oral bioavailability, and potential anticancer effects8–11. HCT, a member of the Saururaceae family, is a well-known edible and medicinal plant that is widely distributed in China and has complex medicinal ingredients. Although the volatile oil is the active component in HCT, it is unstable and easily degraded. SNH is a compound of sodium bisulfite and dodecanoyl acetaldehyde in the volatile oil of HCT and is relatively stable. Research findings indicate that SNH has anti-inflammatory12,13, anti-fungal14,15, antiviral16,17, and anticancer properties18–21. For example, SNH promoted breast cancer cell apoptosis18 and suppressed non-small cell lung cancer proliferation19 and metastasis22. Collectively, these findings indicate that SNH has potential anticancer effects. However, the therapeutic effect and potential molecular mechanisms of SNH in pancreatic cancer remain unclear.

Angiogenesis is an important process in tumor development. Although human pancreatic tumors are thought to be hypovascular23, pancreatic cancer cells induce angiogenesis by secreting a variety of pro-angiogenic factors, cytokines, and growth factors, thereby promoting tumor blood supply and metastasis24,25. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is considered as a key mediator of angiogenesis in pancreatic tumors. Although preclinical data suggest VEGF-mediated angiogenesis is important in pancreatic cancer26–28, the use of anti-VEGF antibody treatment in pancreatic cancer has been a clinical failure29,30. Therefore, it is also crucial to find therapeutic approaches that inhibit other targets of angiogenesis in pancreatic cancer.

In this study, we preliminarily confirmed that SNH inhibits p38/MAPK signaling. We next found that SNH affects angiogenesis in pancreatic cancer by decreasing FGF1 expression in vitro and in vivo. This is the first report demonstrating the effect of SNH on angiogenesis in pancreatic cancer. Furthermore, our results suggest that SNH is effective in treating pancreatic cancer.

Methods

Network pharmacological analysis

The potential target genes of SNH were predicted through SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/). Two expression profiles related to pancreatic cancer were screened from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and NCBI (‌National Center for Biotechnology Information, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The common expressed genes shared between the two datasets were shown using a Venn diagram by omicshare (https://www.omicshare.com/). An interaction network of these common expressed genes was constructed by using Cytoscape 3.9.1 software (https://cytoscape.org/). String database (https://string-db.org/) is used to analyze interacting proteins and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html) pathways31–33.

Cell culture

Human pancreatic cancer cell lines PANC-1 (CVCL_0480, BNCC339759) and SW1990 (CVCL_1723, BNCC338480) were purchased from the BeiNa Culture Collection. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells HUVECs (CVCL_2959, CL-0675) was purchased from the Procell. PANC-1 and SW1990 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle’s medium (DMEM; C11995500BT, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 04-001-1ACS, Biological Industries). HUVECs were cultured in Complete culture medium (CM-0675, Procell) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 95% air and 5% CO2. Cellular mycoplasma testing was performed once a week.

Cell viability assay

Briefly, PANC-1, SW1990 cells and HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells/well and cultured in the presence of 10% FBS at 37 °C. The next day, the culture medium in each well (final volume, 100 µL) was supplemented with various concentrations of SNH (1847-58-1, MCE) and FGF1(HY-P7001, MCE). Thereafter, 10 µL of CCK-8 (HY-K0301, MCE) was added to each well at 24, 48, and 72 h after drug treatment. After 2–4 h of incubation, absorbance was measured with a microplate reader (FlexA-200, Allsheng) at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Colony formation assay

PANC-1 and SW1990 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (4,000 cells/well), incubated overnight, and treated with SNH (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 µΜ) for 14 days. Thereafter, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (BL539A, Biosharp) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (BL802A, Biosharp) for 30 min at 25 °C. Finally, the stained plates were scanned using a printer.

Cell invasion assay

Briefly, 200 µL of PANC-1, SW1990 cells and HUVECs suspension (5 × 105 cells/mL) were added into 8 μm transwell chambers, followed by the addition of DMEM containing 10% FBS and 45 µΜ (PANC-1 cells and HUVECs), 55 µΜ (SW1990 cells) of SNH to the lower compartment of the chamber (24-well plate). After culturing in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h, upper chamber cells were first fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 20 min before staining with crystal violet and finally photographed by light microscopy (BX43, Olympus).

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was assessed via flow cytometry using Annexin V(AV) and propidium iodide (PI). Briefly, PANC-1 and SW1990 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well, followed by treatment with SNH (45 µΜ/ 55 µΜ) for 72 h starting from the next day. Thereafter, the cells were trypsinized and stained with AV and PI using the FITC Annexin V/Dead Cell Apoptosis Kit (C1062L, Beyotime), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using a flow cytometer (NovoCyte, Agilent).

Transmission electron microscopy

PANC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well, followed by treatment with SNH (45 µΜ) for 72 h starting from the next day. Thereafter, the cells were fixed, dehydrated, permeated, and sectioned. Finally, the sections were stained with uranyl acetate and photographed by a Scanning Electron Microscope (JEM-1400Plus, JEOL) at 100 kV.

Tube formation assay

For the endothelial tube formation assay, 50 µL of Matrigel was added to each well of a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Thereafter, HUVECs (2 × 104) were added to each well, followed by further incubation for 4 h. The degree of tube formation was evaluated using an inverted microscope.

Western blot analysis

PANC-1 and SW1990 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well, followed by treatment with SNH (45 µΜ/55 µΜ) for 72 h starting from the next day. Thereafter, the cells were lysed with RIPA buffer, and the protein concentration was measured using Bradford method with BSA as the standard. Protein samples (20 µg/ 10 µL) were separated using 12% gel electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies targeting GAPDH (1:10000, AF7021, Affinity), MMP2 (1:2000, ab92536, Abcam), Bax (1:5000, ab32503, Abcam), Bcl-2 (1:1000, ab196495, Abcam), p-p38/MAPK (1:1000, AF5887, Beyotime), p38/MAPK (1:1000, AF7668, Beyotime), p-JNK (1:1000, AF1762, Beyotime), JNK (1:1000, AF1048, Beyotime), p-ERK (1:1000, ab201015, Abcam), ERK (1:1000, ab17942, Abcam), and FGF1 (1:1000, ab207321, Abcam), followed by incubation with specific secondary antibodies (1:5000, ab6721, Abcam) at 20 °C for 1 h. Finally, protein bands were quantified using Image J software (https://soft.kafan58.com/soft/133478.html? bd_vid=8246644741271853987).

In vivo experiment

Female BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the Animal Research and Resource Center of Yunnan University. The mice were housed and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions and heedfully reared throughout the study. All mouse experiments were performed following protocols approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Animal Experimentation of Yunnan University, and all experiments and methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Briefly, the mice were subcutaneously injected with PANC-1 cells (1 × 107) to establish a xenograft model. When the tumor volume reached 100–150 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to three treatment groups (n = 4 mice/group): saline (Control), low-dose SNH (15 mg/kg SNH), and high-dose SNH (30 mg/kg SNH). All treatments were administered once every two days via intraperitoneal injection. Tumor size was measured every two days using the same caliper. Tumor volumes were calculated using the following formula: 0.5 × (length × width2). Mice were euthanized on the 12th day of drug administration or when the tumor volume reached 1000 mm3. Mice were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation, consistent with AVMA guidelines. Tumor, kidney, heart, liver, lung, and spleen were harvested and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Manuscripts presenting studies that have be reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Mouse tissue staining

Briefly, tumor and organs were dehydrated after fixing in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and embedded in paraffin. Thereafter, the tissues were cut into 4 μm thick sections, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and observed using an optical microscope. Additionally, tumor sections were used for immumohistochemical (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) assays.

For IHC assay, the tumor sections were dewaxed with xylene and ethanol, soaked in citric acid antigen repair buffer (pH 6.0, G1202, Servicebio), and heated in a microwave oven for antigen repair. After blocking with bovine serum albumin (GC305010, Servicebio) at room temperature for 30 min, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody (p-MAPK: GB153380, Servicebio; FGF1: ab207321, Abcam), washed, and incubated with secondary antibody (GB25303, GB23303, Servicebio) at room temperature for 50 min. Thereafter, the sections were stained DAB (G1212, Servicebio) for color development, and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (G1004, Servicebio). For IF assay, the nuclei were stained with DAPI (G1012, Servicebio) after washing and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10 min. Finally, the stained sections were observed using a microscope. Positive results were quantified using Image J software.

Results

Network pharmacology reveals the potential mechanism of SNH on pancreatic cancer

First, we predicted the potential target genes of SNH using SwissTargetPrediction, identifying 100 key molecular interactions that may underlie its pharmacological effects. Concurrently, we analyzed gene expression profiles from two pancreatic cancer-related datasets and used a Venn diagram to illustrate their overlap, revealing 2305 commonly expressed genes (Fig. 1A). A subsequent Venn analysis between the 100 potential SNH target genes and the 2305 pancreatic cancer-related genes identified 49 of the potential target genes of SNH were associated with pancreatic cancer (Fig. 1B). To further investigate the interaction of these genes, we constructed a map of their interaction network using Cytoscape (Fig. 1C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these 49 genes identified the top 20 potential signaling pathways through which SNH may exert its effects in pancreatic cancer (Fig. 1D).

(B) The common target genes of SNH and two datasets. (C) Interaction network analysis of the common target genes was performed using Cytoscape software. (D) KEGG (www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html) enrichment analyse for common target genes.

Given that over 90% of pancreatic cancers harbor activating mutations in the KRAS gene, and that the MAPK pathway serves as the central signaling axis downstream of KRAS—driving uncontrolled proliferation, survival, and resistance to apoptosis—targeting this pathway is of critical therapeutic importance in pancreatic cancer. Interestingly, the MAPK signaling pathway was among the top 20 potential SNH-affected pathways identified in our analysis (Fig. 1D). Moreover, key hub proteins in the interaction network—including AKT1, TNF, STAT3, and EGFR—are known to play critical regulatory roles within the MAPK pathway. Taken together, these findings lead us to hypothesize that SNH might inhibit pancreatic cancer by acting on MAPK signaling pathway.

SNH exerts anticancer activities in pancreatic cancer cells in vitro

To examine the anticancer effects SNH, the pancreatic cancer cell lines PANC-1 and SW1990 were treated with different concentrations of SNH at various time points, followed by cell viability assay. SNH treatment for 72 h significantly inhibited the growth and proliferation of PANC-1 and SW1990 cells in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2A), with a stronger inhibitory effect observed in PANC-1 cells (50% inhibitory concentration [IC50] = 44.53 µM) than in SW1990 cells line (IC50 = 54.27 µM). Additionally, colony formation assay showed that SNH treatment markedly reduced the colony formation efficiency of the cells (Fig. 2B). PANC-1 cells were selected for subsequent investigations due to their observed higher sensitivity to SNH treatment compared to SW1990 cells. Transwell invasion assay was performed to assess the effects of SNH on the invasive ability of PANC-1 cells. SNH treatment significantly inhibited the invasive ability of PANC-1 cells (Fig. 2C and D). Additionally, SNH treatment downregulated the expression of Matrix Metallopeptidase 2 (MMP2), a critical factor in tumor metastasis (Fig. 2E and F, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, we found that SNH arrested PANC-1 cells in the S and G2/M phases (Supplementary Fig. 1B and C). Thus, SNH significantly inhibited the proliferation and invasion of pancreatic cancer cells.

Moreover, we examined the effect of SNH on cell apoptosis using AV–FITC dual staining assay and found that SNH treatment induced apoptosis in PANC-1 cells (Fig. 2G and H). SNH treatment upregulated the expression of Bax (Bcl-2-associated X protein, pro-apoptotic protein) and downregulated the expression of Bcl-2 (B-cell lymphoma-2, anti-apoptotic protein) in PANC-1 cells (Fig. 2I and J, Supplementary Fig. 1D). Bax and Bcl-2 belong to the Bcl-2 family and initiate the apoptotic process by regulating mitochondrial membrane permeability34. Therefore, we examined mitochondrial morphology in PANC-1 cells using transmission electron microscopy. Compared with that in the control group, abnormalities were observed in the mitochondrial morphology of SNH-treated cells, indicating mitochondrial damage (Fig. 2I). Collectively, these results indicate that SNH induces mitochondrial damage in cells, resulting in apoptosis. Similarly, treatment with SNH resulted in consistent pro-apoptotic and anti-invasive effects in the SW1990 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1E-H).

SNH suppresses pancreatic tumor growth in vivo, without causing significant host toxicity

To investigate the anticancer effect of SNH in vivo, we established a PANC-1 subcutaneous tumor model. The xenograft mice were intraperitoneally injected with saline (Control), low-dose SNH (15 mg/kg SNH), and high-dose SNH (30 mg/kg SNH) once every two days. Tumor growth curves were obtained by measuring tumor volumes every two days (Fig. 3A). Tumor growth was rapid in the control group, but suppressed in the SNH groups (Fig. 3B). SNH treatments, especially the high-dose treatment, significantly decreased tumor cell proliferation, as indicated by the final tumor morphology and weight (Fig. 3C and D).

Meanwhile, IHC assay showed that SNH treatments significantly inhibited Bcl-2 expression (Fig. 3E and F) and upregulated Bax expression (Fig. 3G and H) in tumor tissues, with the high-dose group showing a stronger effect. Meanwhile, tumor tissue sequencing data showed that SNH regulated multiple signaling pathways related to mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B). Overall, these findings were consistent with the in vitro results. Importantly, no distinct abnormalities were observed in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidneys in each treatment group (Fig. 3I).

The MAPK signaling pathway is involved in the anticancer activity of SNH

To investigate the molecular mechanisms by which SNH inhibits pancreatic cancer, we performed RNA sequencing of mouse tumor tissues and PANC-1 cells after SNH and DMSO (Control) treatments. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed a shift in principal component 1 (PC1) and PC2 in the SNH vs. Control (Fig. 4A). Meanwhile, we found that SNH-treated cells and tumor tissues produced many up-regulated or down-regulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 3A-F). Subsequently, these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed by KEGG (www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html) enrichment analysis31–33. Then, we focused on the down-regulated signaling pathways in Control vs. SNH of PANC-1 cells (Fig. 4B) and Control vs. 30 mg/kg SNH (Fig. 4C), 15 mg/kg SNH vs. 30 mg/kg SNH (Fig. 4D) of tumor tissues. Notably, the MAPK signaling pathway was downregulated in both tissues and cells in the SNH group. This is consistent with the results of our network pharmacology analysis, which indicates that the MAPK signaling pathway is involved in the development and progression of pancreatic cancer35. Overall, these results suggest that SNH exerts its anticancer effects by downregulating the MAPK signaling pathway.

Research has established that there are three main components of the MAPK signaling pathway in mammals, including extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK), Jun N-terminal kinases (JNK), and p38/MAPK. JNK and p38/MAPK have similar functions and are involved in inflammation, apoptosis, and growth, whereas ERK is mainly involved in cell growth and differentiation. Western blotting was used to detect the phosphorylation levels of ERK (p-ERK), JNK (p-JNK) and p38/MAPK (p-p38/MAPK) in each group, and the results showed that SNH had a stronger effect on p-p38/MAPK protein than on other proteins examined (Fig. 4E and F). Moreover, SNH treatment downregulated p-p38/MAPK protein expression in the tumor tissues in a concentration-dependent manner compared with that in the control group (Fig. 4G and H). Similarly, the downregulation of p-p38/MAPK by SNH was confirmed in an additional pancreatic cancer cell line, SW1990 (Supplementary Fig. 1I). Collectively, these results suggest that SNH suppresses pancreatic cancer development by inhibiting the p38/MAPK pathway.

SNH affects tumor angiogenesis by suppressing FGF1 expression

To investigate the genes involved in SNH-mediated regulation of the MAPK signaling pathway, we analyzed transcriptomic data related to the MAPK signaling pathway in SNH-treated cells and tumors. Additionally, differential expression analysis was performed to identify DEGs related to the MAPK signaling pathway in the Control vs. SNH groups. Compared with those in the control group, we identified 51 and 12 downregulated DEGs in SNH-treated cells and tumor tissues, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4A and B). Additionally, we identified 8 downregulated DEGs in tumor tissues in the 30 mg/kg SNH group compared with those in the 15 mg/kg SNH group (Supplementary Fig. 4C). A combined analysis of the three datasets revealed that FGF1 and DUSP4 (Dual specificity phosphatase 4) are involved in SNH-mediated regulation of the MAPK signaling pathway in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 5A). FGF1 showed the highest variation in cellular expression and a better trend in animal tumors (Fig. 5B). Therefore, we focused on the role of FGF1 in the anticancer effect of SNH. Further experiments showed that SNH inhibited FGF1 protein expression in the cells regardless of the presence or absence of exogenous FGF1 protein (Fig. 5C and D). Similarly, SNH treatment downregulated FGF1 protein expression in the xenograft tumors (Fig. 5E and F). Similarly, the downregulation of FGF1 by SNH was confirmed in an additional pancreatic cancer cell line, SW1990 (Supplementary Fig. 1I). Collectively, these results suggest that FGF1 may be the key gene responsible for the anticancer effects of SNH.

FGF1 has been shown to exert pro-angiogenic effects on endothelial cells36. A previous study showed that ILT4 (Leukocyte Immunoglobulin Like Receptor B2) activated MAPK phosphorylation and upregulated FGF1 expression, leading to angiogenesis in colorectal cancer37. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that SNH exerts its anti-tumor effects by suppressing FGF1 expression, which in turn inhibits endothelial cell function within the tumor. Expectedly, SNH treatment significantly inhibited the proliferation and migration of HUVECs. Notably, the addition of exogenous FGF1 protein substantially rescued these inhibitory effects (Fig. 6A-C). Furthermore, the compromised tube formation—a key indicator of angiogenic potential—caused by SNH was similarly restored by FGF1 supplementation (Fig. 6D and E). Additionally, IF and IHC assay were performed to examine CD31 expression in tumor blood vessels (Fig. 6F and G). SNH treatment significantly decreased CD31 + area and microvascular density (MVD) in tumors compared with those in the control group (Fig. 6H and I). It can be seen that SNH affects tumor angiogenesis by decreasing FGF1 expression.

Discussion

In this work, SNH significantly inhibited the proliferation and invasive abilities of pancreatic cancer cells while promoting apoptosis in Pancreatic cancer cells. Notably, transmission electron microscopy showed that SNH treatment induced mitochondrial damage in PANC-1 cells, indicating that that the pro-apoptotic effects of SNH are related to mitochondrial function. Our study provides novel evidence linking SNH-induced mitochondrial damage to its anti-cancer activity. To further validate the therapeutic potential of SNH, we generated a xenograft model of pancreatic cancer. Consistent with the in vitro results, SNH treatment suppressed pancreatic cancer development in vivo, underscoring its potential as a promising anti-cancer agent. Moreover, by using cell and tissue transcriptome sequencing, we identified MAPK signaling pathway as the key pathway for SNH to exert its anti-cancer effect. This finding is particularly significant, as the MAPK signaling pathway is a well-established regulator of cancer progression and has been extensively targeted in therapeutic strategies. By further validation, we found that SNH affects tumor angiogenesis by decreasing FGF1 expression, which is the first report. This novel insight not only expands our understanding of SNH’s anti-cancer mechanisms but also highlights its potential to target multiple facets of tumor biology, including proliferation, apoptosis, and angiogenesis.

Pancreatic cancer is a refractory cancer with one of the lowest 5-year survival rates. Chemoresistance and side effects associated with most chemotherapy drugs are critical challenges affecting pancreatic cancer treatment. Research findings indicate that KRAS (KRAS Proto-Oncogene, GTPase)-activating mutations are present in most pancreatic cancers38,39. Mutant KRAS proteins play oncogenic roles by participating in multiple downstream signaling cascades, including the MAPK and PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathways40. The MAPK pathway regulates multiple transcription factors, cell cycle progression, and cell proliferation41,42. Additionally, the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway regulates protein synthesis, cell growth, and survival43. Based on previous findings, the MAPK pathway is considered as one of the most critical therapeutic targets44. Generally, the MAPK pathway includes ERK, JNK, and p38/MAPK in mammals. Our study showed for the first time that SNH inhibited the p38/MAPK pathway in pancreatic cancer. The p38/MAPK pathway is involved in the occurrence, development and drug resistance of pancreatic cancer45–47. Inhibition of p38/MAPK pathway activation is an effective means to treat pancreatic cancer, and also an effective way to reverse the chemoresistance of pancreatic cancer. Therefore, SNH can not only inhibit the growth of pancreatic cancer, but also be a potential drug for combined chemotherapy drugs in the treatment of pancreatic cancer.

Notably, the p38/MAPK pathway is activated by most growth factors, cytokines, and immune receptors as well as by several integrin and chemokine receptors. In the present study, transcriptome sequencing revealed that FGF1 and DUSP4 were potential drivers of SNH-mediated regulation of p38/MAPK pathway in pancreatic cancer. FGF1 is the most important cytokine involved in pancreatic cancer formation and has been shown to interact with the p38/MAPK pathway48,49. Moreover, a growing number of studies have established that FGF family proteins can activate the p38 MAPK pathway in various biological contexts50–53. FGF1 is a member of the FGF family and is involved in cell proliferation and chemotherapy resistance in human pancreatic cancer54,55. Importantly, our experiments confirmed that SNH downregulated FGF1 expression in pancreatic cancer. In addition to playing an important role in tumor cell motility and survival, the FGF family is involved in tumor angiogenesis56,57. Tumor angiogenesis is a key determining step in tumor growth and metastasis58. In the last few decades, anti-angiogenic therapy has become a rational strategy for the treatment of various cancers, including pancreatic cancer. Unfortunately, clinical trials of anti-VEGF-angiogenesis therapy in pancreatic cancer have been unsatisfactory. Therefore, FGF1 is expected to be a new anti-angiogenic target for clinical treatment of pancreatic cancer.

While our study provides evidence supporting the anti-tumor effects of SNH in pancreatic cancer, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the majority of our in vitro functional validation was conducted in a single cell line. Although we have supplemented key experiments in a second cell line to strengthen our conclusions, broader validation across a more diverse panel of pancreatic cancer models would enhance the generalizability of our findings. Secondly, our claim regarding the affection of tumor angiogenesis is primarily supported by in vitro endothelial cell assays and a reduction in microvessel density in vivo. However, more direct evidence of impaired vascular function, such as measurements of key angiogenic factors or advanced in vivo imaging, is lacking and represents an important direction for future investigation. Finally, the precise mechanistic link within the SNH signaling network, particularly the causal relationship between FGF1 downregulation and p-p38 MAPK inhibition, remains to be fully elucidated. Direct genetic rescue experiments will be crucial in future work to definitively establish this pathway as the primary mechanism of action.

Conclusion

In summary, our study confirmed that SNH not only inactivated the p38/MAPK pathway but also affects tumor angiogenesis. Our work reveals SNH inhibits the proliferation and invasive abilities of PANC-1 cells, induces cells mitochondrial damage and apoptosis, and suppresses the growth of pancreatic tumors in vivo without significant toxicity. Meanwhile, SNH affects tumor angiogenesis in pancreatic cancer by suppressing FGF1 expression and phosphorylation of p38/MAPK pathway. Based on our findings, it can be concluded that SNH has the potential to serve as a promising candidate for the clinical treatment of pancreatic cancer.
