Genistein Has Beneficial Effects Against Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease Through Activation of the SIRT1/PPAR-α/PGC-1α and Nrf2 Signaling Pathways
Authors: Fahimeh Zamani-Garmsiri, Golnaz Goodarzi, Sadra Samavarchi Tehrani, ShadiSadat Seyyedebrahimi, Arash Bahramzadeh, Reza Meshkani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of fatty liver has been associated with hepatic metabolic derangements. Genistein has been reported to be a treatment option for Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). In this study, we evaluated the effect of genistein on hepatic steatosis through the silent mating type information regulation 2 homolog − 1 (SIRT1)/peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α)/peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) and Nrf2 signaling pathways in mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD). After 10 weeks of HFD feeding, the mice were randomly divided into the HFD and genistein (0.2%) groups. Genistein treatment significantly decreased liver weight and lipid accumulation in the liver of the HFD group. The mRNA level of PPARα, the acetylation of PGC-1α, mRNA, and protein levels of SIRT1 was increased in the genistein group compared with the HFD group. Genistein supplementation could enhance total antioxidant capacity, total thiol content and decrease protein carbonyl and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in the liver of mice fed HFD. In addition, the mRNA and protein levels of Nrf2, HO-1, and SOD2 were enhanced in the liver of HFD-fed mice. These findings suggest that genistein has favorable impacts against NAFLD via activation of the SIRT1/PGC1α/PPARα and Nrf2 signaling pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10753-025-02396-0.Keywords: Genistein, SIRT1, PPAR-α, PGC-1α, NAFLD, Hepatic steatosis, Nrf2, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) that has been renamed around 2023 metabolic fatty liver disease (MAFLD) are two of the most common consequences of modern lifestyle changes. MAFLD is characterized by the accumulation of fat in more than 5% of hepatocytes and can be considered one of the mildest conditions affecting the liver, but if left untreated, it can progress to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [1, 2]. With a staggering prevalence of 25% in the general population, MAFLD can result in a significant surge of liver disease in the near future [3]. Recent studies have suggested that lipid metabolism is dysregulated in MAFLD [4, 5]. There is evidence that decreased fatty acid β-oxidation due to mitochondrial dysfunction plays a key role in the pathogenesis of MAFLD [6]. Furthermore, in insulin resistance conditions, the inhibition of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα), a transcription factor essential for fatty acid β-oxidation, occurs [7]. Given the important role of dysregulated fatty acid oxidation in MAFLD, an emphasis on therapeutic approaches based on promoting the fatty acid oxidation capacity of the liver may have the potential for suitable treatment of MAFLD.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) are a family of nuclear ligand-activated receptors that act as transcription factors and are essential for energy metabolism [8]. Dietary or de novo-originated fatty acids in particular can activate PPAR-α which generally results in an increased expression of genes involved in fatty acid trafficking, uptake, and oxidation [9]. It has been indicated that PPAR-α efficiency relies upon the functioning of many other transcription factors and coactivators such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α). PGC-1α expression is induced under different conditions such as cold, physical exercise, and fasting and its activation promotes the expression and activation of different transcription factors including PPARs resulting in increased fatty acid oxidation, mitochondrial biogenesis, and oxidative capacity [8]. There is also evidence demonstrating that Silent mating type information regulation 2 homolog − 1 (SIRT1) can affect both PPAR-α and PGC-1α. SIRT1 is a deacetylase protein that plays helpful roles in regulating hepatic lipid metabolism and controlling hepatic oxidative stress in fatty liver diseases [10]. At the molecular level, SIRT1 can directly deacetylate PGC-1α and significantly increase its activity [11]. Moreover, this molecule has demonstrated the ability to increase the expression of PPAR-α, thus improving hepatic liver metabolism [12].

Numerous studies have revealed that hepatic oxidative stress is responsible for progression of fatty liver due to a close link between dysregulated lipid homeostasis and oxidative stress [13, 14]. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) plays a central role in maintaining redox balance in the liver. It has been indicated that SIRT1 has a regulatory effect on Nrf2, so overexpression of SIRT1 as an upstream factor, activates Nrf2 and increases Nrf2/ARE binding [15]. The deacetylation of Nrf2 leads to nuclear translocation, and eventually mediates the transcription of the antioxidant genes, such as glutathione peroxidase, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase [16]. On the other hand, in MAFLD induced by high-fat diet (HFD), the SIRT1-mediated PGC-1α and Fork head box proteins-1 (FOXO1) pathways can enhance the expression of target antioxidant genes, leading to protection against hepatic oxidative stress in fatty liver [17].

Genistein is an isoflavone which is found naturally in legumes. This polyphenol is one of the most extensively studied isoflavone and it has demonstrated numerous therapeutic properties such as anti-oxidative, anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory effects [18, 19]. Furthermore, genistein has shown potent capabilities in ameliorating obesity state through its effects on the adipose tissue [20]. Recent studies have also shown that genistein can be a potent treatment option for MAFLD as it is able to restore serum lipid profile, ameliorate insulin resistance, decrease inflammatory cytokines, inhibit transcription factors and pathways responsible for liponeogenesis and upregulate the transcription factors involved in lipid oxidation [21]. Despite the beneficial effects of genistein on MAFLD, the molecular mechanisms underlying this effect are not clearly understood. In this study we aimed to investigate whether the impact of genistein on MAFLD is mediated through the SIRT1 signaling in HFD fed model of MAFLD.

Materials & Methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from the Pasteur ‎Institute (Tehran, Iran). Animals were acclimatized to laboratory conditions (12 h day/night cycle and controlled temperature) for a period of one week before the beginning of the experiments. For the purpose of this study, the animals were initially divided into two dietary groups: standard chow diet (SCD) (10 kcal% fat diet) (n = 7) and HFD (55.9 kcal% fat diet) (n = 14). After 10 weeks, the mice fed HFD were further divided into two groups: the HFD and HFD plus 200 mg/kg animal body weight genistein (HFD + Genistein). Animals were fed for an additional period of 12 weeks. At the end of the study, the animals were euthanized under general anesthesia (intraperitoneal injection of ketamine-xylazine). Blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture and the yielded plasma after centrifugation was stored at −80 °C for further assessments. Liver tissue samples were immediately stored at −80 °C until further analysis. All the animal experiments were carried out in accordance with NIH guidelines and were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences.

Determination of TG Content of Liver

At the end of the experiment, the lipids of the liver were isolated based on the Folch method by using a chloroform/methanol solution. TG concentration of the liver was determined by using enzymatic assay (Pars Azmun Kit, Iran).

Oxidative Stress Indices Assessment

The thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method established by Ohkawa et al. (1979) was used to evaluate the liver malondialdehyde (MDA) level as an index of lipid peroxidation. In this method, MDA reacts with TBA and gives a pink-colored species that absorbs at 532 nm [22].

The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay was performed to determine the total antioxidant capacity (TAC) of plasma. By detecting the change in absorbance at 593 nm at low pH, the reduction of the ferric tripyridyltriazine (F III -TPTZ) complex to the ferrous form, was evaluated. The combined or “total” reducing power of the electron-donating antioxidants present in the reaction mixture is directly associated with this change.

Protein carbonyl content as a marker of oxidative damage was detected by 2, 4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) assay. Protein carbonyl groups react with reagent DNPH and lead to the formation of 2,4-dinitrophenyl (DNP) hydrazone product, which absorb at 370 nm. Data are expressed as nmol of protein carbonyl formed per mg protein.

Total thiol content was measured by the spectrophotometric method using 5,5’-dithiobis-(2- nitrobenzoic) acid (DTNB) known as Ellman’s reagent. Thiol groups react with DTNB and generate 5-thio2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB), which absorb at 412 nm [23].

RNA Extraction, cDNA Synthesis, and qRT-PCR

Tissue samples were homogenized using TRIzol reagent. Next, total RNA was extracted using a Hybrid-R RNA Purification Kit (GeneAll Biotechnology co. South Korea) and subsequently, the quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific. USA). Afterward, using 1000 ng of the RNA as substrate, cDNA was synthesized using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific. USA). The synthesized cDNA was then used as the template for the RT-qPCR experiment which was performed using an SYBR Green RealQ Plus 2x Master Mix Green (Ampliqon). All the reactions were performed as duplicates and were loaded into the Rotor-Gene Q real-time PCR cycler (QIAGEN Hilden. Germany) using the following cycle parameters: a 30-second stage at 95 °C for the initial denaturation of DNA followed by 40 PCR cycles with 5 s at 90 °C and 20 s at 60 °C in each cycle. The obtained data were analyzed using the 2–ΔCt method. Primers used for the selected genes and β-Actin (as an internal control) are presented in Table 1 of the supplementary file.

Western Blotting

Tissue samples were homogenized using a RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Next, the supernatant was collected and protein concentration was assessed using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, IL, USA). Afterward, the yielded proteins were separated using the SDS-PAGE method (10%) and were subsequently electro-transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, MA, USA). After 2 h incubation period with blocking buffer (5% bovine serum albumin in 0.05% PBS-Tween 20) and subsequent washing with PBS, the membranes were incubated with anti-SIRT1 (sc-74465, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti-PGC-1α (ab54481, Abcam, Cambridge, MA), anti-Nrf2 (sc-365949, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti-HO-1 (sc-136960, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti-SOD2 (sc-137254, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) and anti-β-actin (sc-47778, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After this step, the membranes were washed 4 times with PBS and then were probed with a horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibody (1:8000) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was subsequently determined using an ECL detection system (GE healthcare BioSciences Corp, NJ, USA).

Histological Study

All liver tissue samples were removed, weighed, fixed in 10% formalin, and embedded in paraffin. All tissues were cut into 5 μm thickness and stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for general morphological analysis. All tissue samples were observed under the light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 200× magnification. Finally, to assess the histopathological changes in the liver, the NAFLD activity score (NAS) was determined based on three criteria including steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocyte ballooning by an expert pathologist and researchers blinded during the analysis (Table 1).

Statistical Analysis

The statistical significance was determined by oneway analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. All the results are reported as means ± standard error ‎of mean ‎‎(SEM) and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was done using the IBM SPSS 20.0 software package (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and the presented graphs were developed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Genistein Reduced Body and Liver Weights

Our results indicated that compared with the SCD group, the final body weight (Figure. 1a) and liver mass (Fig. 1b) were increased by HFD. However, genistein could significantly lower body weight and liver mass in mice fed HFD.

Body weight and liver mass in different groups of mice. (a) Body weight and (b) liver mass of mice fed on standard chow diet (SCD), high-fat diet (HFD), and HFD supplemented with genistein. The results are expressed as the mean ± SEM (n = 7). **p <.01, *p <.05, #<0.001 (between genistein group with HFD group), and $ <0.05 (between HFD group with SCD group)

Genistein Ameliorated Hepatic Steatosis

Hepatic steatosis was evaluated in H&E-stained sections by light microscopy. Histopathological observations elucidated fat accumulation in the liver of the mice fed HFD. By contrast, the livers from the mice of HFD group taking genistein had noticeable improvement in hepatic steatosis (Fig. 2a). Moreover, the degree of the lesion and NAFLD activity score (NAS) were determined and computed. We observed that NAS was dramatically increased in HFD group; demonstrating the steatohepatitis. NAS of the mice receiving genistein declined in comparison with the HFD group. Consistent with the histology findings, measurement of triglyceride (TG) content in the liver showed that HFD mice had significantly higher hepatic TG level as compared to SCD mice, whereas treatment with genistein reversed this effect (Fig. 2b-c).

Hepatic steatosis and triglyceride content in the liver of different groups. (a) H&E hepatic staining (b) NAFLD activity score (NAS), and (c) liver TG content. The results are expressed as the mean ± SEM (n = 7). ***p <.001, **p <.01, *p <.05.

Genistein Activated SIRT1/PGC1-α Signaling Pathway in the Liver of HFD-fed Mice

As shown in Fig. 3, HFD feeding resulted in a notably decreased mRNA and protein levels of SIRT1 compared to control group. Importantly, genistein treatment for 12 weeks in HFD-fed mice significantly increased the levels of SIRT1 mRNA and protein. In this study, we found a significant reduction in the PGC-1α expression level in the liver of HFD-fed mice as compared to control mice. Genistein treatment could significantly reverse this effect in the HFD group. PGC1-α acetylation in the liver of mice fed a HFD was significantly increased and genistein treatment was able to significantly reverse this change in HFD fed mice. We also measured the expression of PPAR-α in all experimental groups. Feeding the HFD was followed by a significant decrease in the expression of hepatic PPAR-α, whereas an increase in PPAR-α expression was found in response to genistein administration.

Genistein Attenuated HFD-Induced Oxidative Stress

To evaluate whether the beneficial effects of genistein on hepatic steatosis is mediated through its antioxidant activity, we measured several oxidative stress-related parameters such as MDA, TAC, protein carbonyl, and total thiol content. As shown in Fig. 4, HFD feeding led to lower TAC, lower total thiol content, higher protein carbonyl and greater MDA levels in comparison with the SCD group. Notably, genistein could enhance TAC and total thiol content and decrease protein carbonyl and MDA levels in mice fed HFD.

To gain insight into whether genistein ameliorates hepatic steatosis through activation of the Nrf2 signaling, we measured mRNA and protein levels of Nrf2 and its downstream signaling. In comparison with the SCD group, the expression of Nrf2 was significantly lowered by HFD feeding. After genistein treatment, the Nrf2 expression was significantly elevated. We confirmed these findings by measuring the Nrf2 protein level. Regarding the Nrf2 downstream genes, significant decrease for the mRNA and protein levels of HO-1 and protein level of SOD2 was observed in the liver of mice following HFD feeding (Fig. 5a–f). Finally, treatment with genistein led to significant increase in mRNA and protein levels of HO-1 and protein level of SOD2 in mice fed HFD.

The results are expressed as the mean ± SEM (n = 7) of at least three separate experiments. ***p <.001, **p <.01, *p <.05

The FOXO1 is a transcription factor that regulates several cellular processes such as resistance to oxidative stress, glucose and lipid metabolism [24]. To evaluate the involvement of FOXO1 in the beneficial effects of genistein on hepatic steatosis, we assessed the acetylated protein of FOXO1 in different groups. The results revealed that the acetylation of FOXO1 is significantly increased in HFD-fed mice compared to SCD-fed mice. When the HFD mice were treated with genistein, the FOXO1 acetylation level was significantly decreased.

Discussion

MAFLD is a multifactorial condition, but generally, the pathogenesis of the disease starts with an increased intake of fat through the intestines which result in an increased deposition of triglycerides in hepatocytes [25, 26]. The results of the current study show that the beneficial effects of genistein on hepatic steatosis is mediated through signaling pathways that control fatty acid β-oxidation, and oxidative stress in an experimental mice model of MAFLD. Importantly, the main finding of this study was the involvement of SIRT1 pathway and its downstream molecules in the useful effect of genistein on MAFLD.

It has been suggested that impairment of mitochondrial function and subsequently decrease in fatty acid oxidation have important role in the pathogenesis of MAFLD [4, 5]. Fatty acids are metabolized by β-oxidation in mitochondria through the activation of the PPAR-α and its co-activator PGC-1α. Upon fatty acid binding, PPAR-α and its partner PGC-1α induce the expression of genes related to fatty acid catabolism in the mitochondria. Moreover, SIRT1 is required for the induction of PGC-1α/PPAR-α target genes and oxidative metabolism. In this regard, SIRT1can deacetylate and activate PGC-1α leading to its binding to several transcription factors such as PPAR-α that are involved regulating oxidative metabolism and mitochondrial biogenesis [17, 27]. Therefore,, the impairment in the PPAR-α/PGC-1α signaling and subsequently a decrease in fatty acid oxidation and increment susceptibility to liver steatosis has been reported from liver-specific SIRT1 knockout or adenoviral-mediated liver-specific down-regulation of SIRT1 in mice [28]. While overexpression of SIRT1 reduces PGC-1α acetylation level and ligand-dependent PPAR-α transcriptional signaling as well as the expression of PPAR-α/PGC-1α targeting genes, which leads to increased fatty acid β-oxidation and alleviation of fatty liver [28, 29]. Our results indicated that HFD feeding decreased hepatic PPAR-α expression in mice, whereas treatment with genistein upregulated this gene in the liver of HFD mice. In accordance with our study, it was reported that genistein was involved in the improvement of liver steatosis via enhancing fatty acid oxidation via upregulation of PPAR-α in the liver of high fat-high sucrose diet-treated rats [30]. Similarly, another study demonstrated that genistein promoted the expression level of PPAR-α in mice fed a high-fat–high-fructose diet [31]. By contrast, Lee et al. [32], did not observe any significant changes in hepatic PPAR-α gene expression. In the case of PGC-1α, our data indicated a significant decrease in the PGC-1α mRNA level in HFD-fed mice, while genistein treatment could significantly restore this effect in the HFD group. In addition, genistein supplementation was able to significantly reverse the changes in the level of acetylated PGC-1α protein in the liver. Regarding the function of SIRT1 in our model, we demonstrated that genistein treatment could increase the expression of SIRT1 at both the mRNA and protein levels. Overall, these data suggest that the beneficial effects of genistein on hepatic steatosis is mediated at least in part by induction of the SIRT1/PPAR-α/PGC-1α signaling pathway.

SIRT1 activation has been indicated to protect hepatic cells against steatosis by suppressing oxidative stress. SIRT1 exerts its effects via enhancing the antioxidant capability involving the FOXO1 [33]. To determine whether the effect of genistein on liver steatosis is mediated through activation of the SIRT1/FOXO1 pathway, we assessed the acetylation level of FOXO1 in the liver of our MAFLD model. Our data demonstrated that genistein could decrease the acetylation level of FOXO1 in the liver of HFD fed mice, indicating the key role of SIRT1/FOXO1 pathway in the beneficial effects of genistein in our MAFLD model.

Nrf2 signaling has been reported to be regulated by SIRT1 [34]. The Nrf2/ARE pathway is considered as one of the key cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative stress and impairment of this system is associated with an increased susceptibility to fatty liver in mice [35, 36]. To investigate whether genistein could exert the anti-oxidative stress effects via activating the Nrf2 signaling, we first measured several markers of oxidative stress in the liver of HFD fed mice. Notably, the oxidative stress markers including MDA and protein carbonyl were declined following genistein treatment. We also evaluated the mRNA and protein levels of Nrf2 and its downstream anti-oxidant enzymes such as HO-1 and SOD2. In the present study, reduced hepatic expression levels of Nrf2, HO-1 and SOD2 following HFD feeding was evidently enhanced in genistein–treated mice. In support of above mentioned findings, Wang et al. elucidated that genistein protects against acetaminophen-induced liver toxicity via increasing of SIRT1 followed by induction of Nrf2 signaling [37]. These data imply that the beneficial effects of genistein appear to be mediated through activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway.

In summary, we observed that genistein could improve hepatic steatosis. The beneficial effects of genistein on MAFLD seem to exert through activation of SIRT1/PGC-1α/PPAR-α signaling leading to increased oxidation of fatty acids in the liver. In addition, genistein appears to protect fatty liver by ameliorating the oxidative stress, which is mediated by activation of the SIRT1/FOXO1 and Nrf2/HO-1/SOD2 signaling pathways. Hence, the data of the present study provide the evidence on the critical role SIRT1 in the beneficial effects of genistein against MAFLD.
