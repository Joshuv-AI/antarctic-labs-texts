Abstract
Neural networks are powerful tools for data-driven modeling of complex dynamical systems, enhancing predictive capability for control applications. However, their inherent nonlinearity and black-box nature challenge control designs that prioritize rigorous safety and recursive feasibility guarantees. This paper presents algorithmic methods for synthesizing control invariant sets specifically tailored to neural network based dynamical models. These algorithms employ set recursion, ensuring termination after a finite number of iterations and generating subsets in which closed-loop dynamics are forward invariant, thus guaranteeing perpetual operational safety. Additionally, we propose model predictive control designs that integrate these control invariant sets into mixed-integer optimization, with guaranteed adherence to safety constraints and recursive feasibility at the computational level. We also present a comprehensive theoretical analysis examining the properties and guarantees of the proposed methods. Numerical simulations in an autonomous driving scenario demonstrate the methods’ effectiveness in synthesizing control-invariant sets offline and implementing model predictive control online, ensuring safety and recursive feasibility.

Neural Networks (NN), from early developments in Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP)

Theoretically, NNs serve as universal function approximators, allowing for accurate representation of intricate dynamical systems
[
1
,
4
]
. In practice, the availability of well-established NN training algorithms
[
5
]
and platforms
[

]
facilitates their use for data-driven identification
[
7
]
.
This versatility has enabled their deployment in diverse fields, including autonomous vehicles for human behavior prediction
[
8
]
, finance for stock market prediction
[
9
]
, and biology for protein structure and interaction discovery
[
10
]
.

Building on the predictive power of NN-modeled Dynamical Systems (NNDS), their integration into Model Preditive Control (MPC) and decision-making has significantly enhanced the responsiveness
[
11
]
and adaptability
[
8
]
of control strategies, leading to improved practical performance
[
12
]
.
Those algorithms utilize NNDS to forecast system evolutions while optimizing control actions to achieve task-related objectives and enforce engineering constraints
[
13
]
. Among these constraints, safety is often paramount, ensuring that system states remain within a predefined safe region and avoid potentially hazardous or undesirable conditions. However, the derivation of formal safety guarantees in NNDS-based control remains a critical challenge due to the inherent nonlinearity and lack of interpretability of NNs
[
14
]
.

Literature addressing safety in NNDS spans both open-loop and closed-loop considerations. The safety verification of open-loop NNDS primarily focuses on reachability analysis or output range estimation, which maps a prescribed NNDS input domain to an output range and examines whether it remains within predefined safe regions. Techniques such as interval bound propagation
[
15
]
, mixed-integer programming
[
16
]
and nested optimization schemes
[
17
]
have been developed for this purpose. However, open-loop approaches inherently lack corrective or feedback control mechanisms to regulate the NNDS’s behavior, making their safety guarantees conservative.

To address this limitation, reachability analysis has been extended to closed-loop systems, demonstrating its ability to verify safety under predefined feedback control policies. Studies in this domain analyze the evolution of linear
[
18
]
and nonlinear
[
19
]
dynamical systems governed by fixed control policies, which are often learned NN controllers. Popular methods include interval analysis
[
20
]
, Bernstein polynomials
[
19
]
, and Semi-Definite Programming
[
18
]
. A comprehensive tutorial on reachability analysis, along with its transcription into mathematical programs and numerical algorithms, is provided in
[
21
]
.
However, in this safety verification process, the safety of the NNDS is tied to a fixed control policy within a given horizon, which may limit adaptability to dynamic environments where online optimization for control is required. In contrast, our approach establishes Control Invariant Sets (CISs)
[
22
]
for NNDS, ensuring the safety of the closed-loop system without dependence on a specific control law.

In particular, CIS synthesis
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
involves designing a generalizable control strategy offline that ensures safety indefinitely. This makes CIS synthesis a more general and challenging problem, particularly for NNDS. Researchers have developed function-based methods that prescribe CIS as level sets of control barrier/Lyapunov functions
[
25
]
and safety indices
[
26
]
. Meanwhile, set-based methods have been explored for piecewise affine discrete-time systems
[
27
]
, alongside function-based approaches for NNDS
[
28
]
, where subsequent online optimization for control is not discussed. Since function-based methods might have limited ability to handle multi-horizon prediction while ensuring the recursive feasibility of online optimization for control, our method focuses on set-based developments tailored for NNDS.

Additionally, similar to function-based methods
[
29
,
30
]
, we integrate CIS into MPC design, facilitating online optimization for control while ensuring recursive feasibility in real-time operation. In contrast to
[
29
]
, which requires the formation selection of safety index functions, restricting the representational flexibility. It is also limited to a one-step prediction horizon for control due to computational constraints. Our method introduces an algorithmic framework for CIS synthesis.
This framework accommodates more flexible set representations for the CIS and incorporates feasibility considerations within broader MPC formulations.

In particular, the proposed method synthesizes CIS for discrete-time NNDS, modeled by MLP with ReLU activation, with state and control constraints.
The proposed algorithm has several distinguished features that represent the main contribution of this article:

•

The proposed method synthesizes CIS through a nested set recursion and reduces the set recursion into subproblems handled by interval reachability analysis and mixed-integer linear optimization, ensuring computational efficiency. The individual optimization problem scales linearly with the number of neurons in NNDS and the dimensionality of state and control variables.

•

Theoretical properties of the algorithmic framework guarantee that the set recursion terminates in a finite number of steps, making the CIS synthesis process computationally feasible.

•

The algorithmic framework employs a state-space quantization approach, enabling flexible set representations as unions of hyperboxes, and minimizing tuning complexity by reducing parameter adjustments to a single variable governing quantization resolution. This parameter balances the resulting CIS size with the theoretical maximum number of iterations required for synthesis.

•

The CISs are incorporated into generalized MPC designs as mixed-integer linear constraints, ensuring the closed-loop NNDS stays within the CIS indefinitely. The MPC optimization remains recursively feasible and can be warm-started with feasible solutions from the offline CIS synthesis process, while its complexity scales linearly with the number of neurons in the NNDS, the prediction horizon, and the dimensions of state and control variables.

•

The theoretical results are validated through simulations in a vehicle lane-keeping application, demonstrating the ability to ensure recursive feasibility and maintain computational times suitable for real-time implementation.

II-A

Preliminaries

We consider a dynamical system represented by a discrete-time model,

x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},u_{k}),

(1)

where
x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
is a vector of state variables and
u_{k}\in\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
is a vector of control inputs.

Definition 1
(Forward Invariant Set
[
22
]
)

For an autonomous system
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k})
, a non-empty set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a Forward Invariant Set (FIS) if and only if, for all
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
, the system trajectory satisfies
x_{k}\in\mathcal{S}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 2
(Control Invariant Set
[
22
]
)

For a non-autonomous system
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},u_{k})
, a non-empty set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a CIS if and only if there exists an admissible feedback control law
u_{k}=\pi(x_{k})\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\mathcal{C}
is a FIS for the closed-loop system
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},\pi(x_{k}))
.

Remark II.1

The FIS and CIS for their respective dynamical systems are not necessarily unique. The largest sets that contain all the invariant sets that are constraint admissible are referred to as the Maximal FIS
[
31
]
and the Maximal CIS
[
32
]
, respectively.

Definition 3
(One-Step Returnable Set
[
32
]
)

For a dynamical system
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},u_{k})
, the One-Step Returnable Set
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{T})
is a set of all the states in a non-empty set
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, from which there exists an admissible control that drives the system to a non-empty target set
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{T}):=\left\{x_{k}\in\mathcal{I}:\exists u_{k}\in\mathcal{U},~s.t.\;x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{T}\right\}.

(2)

Definition 4
(One-Step Returnable Subset)

For a dynamical system
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},u_{k})
, a set
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{T})
is a One-Step Returnable Subset of a non-empty set
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
to a non-empty target set
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
if and only if, for all
x_{k}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{T})\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, there exists an admissible control
u_{k}\in\mathcal{U}
such that
x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{T}
.

Definition 5
(
i
-Step Admissible Set

[
33
]
)

For a dynamical system
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},u_{k})
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
, the
i
-Step Admissible Set
\mathcal{A}_{i}(\mathcal{T})
is a set of all initial states in the state space
\mathcal{X}
, starting from which there exists an admissible control sequence of length
i
that keeps the system state trajectory inside the non-empty target set
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
for
i
steps, i.e.,

\mathcal{A}_{i}(\mathcal{T}):=\left\{x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}:\begin{array}[]{c}\exists u_{k}\in\mathcal{U},~k=0,\dots,i-1,\\
s.t.~x_{k}\in\mathcal{T},~k=1,\dots,i\end{array}\right\}.

(3)

Definition 6
(
i
-Step Admissible Subset)

For a dynamical system
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},u_{k})
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
, a set
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(\mathcal{T})\subseteq\mathcal{T}
is an
i
-Step Admissible Subset of a non-empty set
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
if and only if, for all
x_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(\mathcal{T})
, there exists an admissible control sequence of length
i
, i.e.,
u_{k}\in\mathcal{U}
,
k=0,1,\dots,i-1
, such that the system trajectory satisfies
x_{k}\in\mathcal{T}
for all
k\in\left\{1,2,\dots,i\right\}
.

Remark II.2

Any subset of the One-Step Returnable Set
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{T})
and the
i
-Step Admissible Set
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{i}(\mathcal{T})
, including the empty set, is itself a valid One-Step Returnable Subset and an
i
-Step Admissible Subset, respectively. Additionally, a
j
-Step Admissible Subset is always an
i
-Step Admissible Subset for any
j\geq i
.

(4a)

z^{(i)}={\tt activate}^{(i)}(\hat{z}^{(i)}),

(4b)

\hat{z}^{(i)}=W^{(i)}z^{(i-1)}+B^{(i)},~i=1,\dots,\ell-1,

(4c)

(4d)

where

) and (

activation functions

{\tt activate}^{(i)}(x)=\max\{0,x\},\;i=1,\cdots,\ell-1,

. This study addresses the following problems related to constrained control using NNDS.

Problem 1
(Control Invariant Set Synthesis)

Given the NNDS (
4
), synthesize offline a CIS
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{s}
such that the closed-loop system remains within
\mathcal{C}
when starting from an arbitrary initial state
x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
, under some admissible feedback control law
u_{k}=\pi(x_{k})\in\mathcal{U}
. Here,
\mathcal{X}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
represents a safe set in the state space defined by a set of state constraints.

Problem 2

(Model Predictive Control and Recursive Feasibility)
Consider the feedback control law as the solution to the following MPC Problem:

\pi(x_{k})\in\argmin\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{k+n},~x_{k+n+1},\\
n=0,1,\dots,N-1\end{subarray}}K(x_{k+N})+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}l_{k+n}(x_{k+n},u_{k+n}),

(6a)

subject to:

\displaystyle x_{k}=x_{0},

(6b)

\displaystyle u_{k+n}\in\mathcal{U},

(6c)

\displaystyle x_{k+n+1}=f(x_{k+n},u_{k+n}),

(6d)

\displaystyle x_{k+n+1}\in\mathcal{X}_{s},~n=0,1,\dots,N-1

(6e)

where
N
is the prediction horizon length,
K
is the terminal cost,
l_{k+n}
is the running cost, and

is an initial state satisfying the constraints.
The MPC (
6

to regulate the state trajectory, as encoded by the equality constraints (
6d

6c
) and state constraints (
6e

However, the MPC (
6
) may not always be feasible and might lack an admissible control solution. To address this, additional measures are required to ensure the recursive feasibility of the MPC, i.e., if a feasible solution exists at a given time step
k
, it must also exist at every subsequent time step.

III-A

Algorithmic Setup and State Space Quantization

We assume that the state space
\mathcal{X}
, safe set
\mathcal{X}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, and admissible control set
\mathcal{U}
satisfy Assumption
1
.

Assumption 1

We assume that the state space
\mathcal{X}
and the set
\mathcal{U}
are bounded hyperboxes that take the following form,

\mathcal{X}=[\underline{x},\overline{x}]\in\mathcal{H}^{n_{x}},~\underline{x},\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}},~\underline{x}\leq\overline{x},

(7a)

\mathcal{U}=[\underline{u},\overline{u}]\in\mathcal{H}^{n_{u}},~\underline{u},\overline{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}},~\underline{u}\leq\overline{u}.

(7b)

where
\mathcal{H}^{n}
denotes the collection of hyperboxes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We assume the interior of the safe set,
\mathrm{int}\mathcal{X}_{s}
, is not empty.

We now define the following State Space Quantization (SSQ) process that discretizes the state space
\mathcal{X}
into a union of hyperboxes, producing a collection of basis hyperboxes
\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
that support the quantization of the safe set
\mathcal{X}_{s}
into many smaller safe hyperboxes
\mathcal{X}_{s}^{(i)}
,
i=1,\dots,n_{s}
.
In particular, each safe hyperbox
\mathcal{X}_{s}^{(i)}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{s}^{\Delta}
can be represented as a union of basis hyperboxes.

Definition 7
(SSQ Process)

Given Assumption
1
,
\mathcal{X}
can be quantized into a finite union of disjoint
basis hyperboxes

\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
, namely,

\mathcal{X}=\cup_{i=1}^{n_{\Delta}}\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}^{(i)},~\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{n_{x}},~\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}:=\\
\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\left[a,b\right]=\\
\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n_{x}}[a_{j},b_{j}]\end{array}:\begin{array}[]{c}a_{j}=\underline{x}_{j}+kd_{\min},~b_{j}=\\
\min\{\underline{x}_{j}+(k+1)d_{\min},\overline{x}_{j}\},\\
k\in\{0,1,\dots,\lceil\frac{(\overline{x}_{j}-\underline{x}_{j})}{d_{\min}}\rceil\}\end{array}\right\}

(8)

where
n_{\Delta}=\prod_{j=1}^{n_{x}}\lceil\frac{(\overline{x}_{j}-\underline{x}_{j})}{d_{\min}}\rceil
is the number of basis hyperboxes in
\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
,
\lceil x\rceil
is the smallest integer greater than or equal to
x
,
d_{\min}
is the SSQ resolution denoting the side length of the basis hyperboxes,
\bigotimes
denotes the Cartesian product, and the subscript
j
represents the
j
th element in the respective vectors.

Afterward, our discussion focuses on sets that belong to the power set of
\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
under union, denoted as
\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta})
, which contains all possible unions of basis hyperboxes in
\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}):=\{\mathcal{S}:\exists\mathcal{J}\subseteq\left[n_{\Delta}\right],~\mathcal{S}=\cup_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}^{(j)}\}

(9)

where
\left[n_{\Delta}\right]:=\{1,2,\dots,n_{\Delta}\}
represents a set of indices smaller than or equal to
n_{\Delta}
.
In addition, given an non-empty
\mathrm{int}\mathcal{X}_{s}
, the safe set
\mathcal{X}_{s}
can be approximated from within, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}_{s}^{\Delta}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{s}
, by a finite union of safe hyperboxes
\mathcal{X}_{s}^{\Delta}
(see the left side of Fig.
2
) that obeys the following condition,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{X}_{s}^{\Delta}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\mathcal{X}_{s}^{(i)},\text{ s.t. }\mathcal{X}_{s}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{H}^{n_{x}},\mathcal{X}_{s}^{(i)}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{s},\\
\mathcal{X}_{s}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}),~\forall i=1,\dots,n_{s},\end{array}

(10)

where
n_{s}
is the number of safe hyperboxes in
\mathcal{X}_{s}^{\Delta}
. We also note that
\mathcal{X}_{s}^{\Delta}\in\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta})
.

Remark III.1

We note that the inner approximation of the safe set
\mathcal{X}_{s}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}_{s}^{\Delta}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\mathcal{X}_{s}^{(i)}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{s}
, is achieved using a coarser quantization, as described in (
10

8

\mathcal{X}_{s}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta})
can be larger than the basis hyperboxes (see Fig.

This approach is motivated by memory considerations for
\mathcal{X}_{s}^{\Delta}

\mathcal{X}_{s}
using smaller basis hyperboxes, i.e., making each safe hyperbox a basis hyperbox

The coarser quantization (

In addition, due to the basis hyperboxes defined in (

forms an algebra of sets
(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta},\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}))

Proposition III.1

Consider the SSQ process, for all
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta})

\mathcal{J}=\Delta(\mathcal{S})\subseteq[n_{\Delta}]

\mathcal{S}=\cup_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}^{(j)}

\forall\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}),~\exists!\mathcal{J}=\Delta(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\left[n_{\Delta}\right],~\text{s.t. }\mathcal{S}=\cup_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}^{(j)},

where
\Delta(\cdot):\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta})\to\mathbb{N}

Proof:

The proof follows from the fact that the basis hyperboxes
\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
are disjoint.
∎

Theorem III.1

The power set of
\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}
under union,
\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta})
, as defined in (

an algebra of sets

(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta},\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}))

According to definition (
9

\varnothing=\cup_{j\in\Delta(\varnothing)}\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}^{(j)}

\Delta(\varnothing)=\varnothing\subseteq\left[n_{\Delta}\right]
. Meanwhile, for all sets
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta})
, we have
\mathcal{S}=\cup_{j\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})}\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}^{(j)}
. Then, due to the basis hyperboxes defined in (

\mathcal{S}^{c}=\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}\backslash\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}_{\cup}(\mathcal{X}_{\Delta})

\mathcal{S}^{c}=\cup_{j\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}^{c})}\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}^{(j)}

\mathcal{S}_{1}=\cup_{j\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}_{1})}\mathcal{X}_{\Delta}^{(j)}

Corollary III.2

The algebra of sets

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(\mathcal{X}_{s})=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i+1}(\mathcal{X}_{s})=\varnothing
, at which point the algorithm terminates.
∎

Remark IV.3

The resolution of the SSQ process can be adjusted through
d_{\min}
; smaller values of
d_{\min}
result in an SSQ with higher resolution, potentially yielding larger CISs but at the cost of producing a lager
N_{T}
and requiring more iterations.
We also note that, for a fixed
d_{\min}
,
N_{T}
grows exponentially with the state dimensionality. Although the optimization problem (
18
) scales linearly with the state space dimension, the overall CIS synthesis process exhibits worst-case exponential time complexity, even though it is conducted offline.
Nonetheless, we note that the
for
-loops in the CIS synthesis process can be accelerated through parallel computing, allowing multiple hyperboxes to be processed simultaneously. This approach can significantly reduce computation time, when handling high-dimensional state spaces, by leveraging multi-core processors or distributed computing frameworks.

V
Model Predictive Control

In this section, we present the results that address Problem
2

V-A

III

Additionally, to reduce the computational burden, we develop an alternative MPC formulation with fewer decision variables and constraints that preserves the theoretical properties in Sec.
V-B

V-B
.

If a solution to the MILC-MPC (

With the initial condition

If a solution to the MILC-MPC (

Theorem V.3

If the initial state
x_{k|0}=x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
, where

, derived from the CIS synthesis process, is a non-empty set, then the optimization problem (

Corollary V.4

In a receding-horizon optimization scheme, a control sequence
\{u_{k+n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
is optimized at the current time step
k
, the first control action
u_{k}
is applied, and a new control sequence is computed at the next step.
If the initial state

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{s}
, derived from the CIS synthesis process, is a non-empty set, then the optimization problem (

\{u_{k+n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}\in\mathcal{U}
that serves as a solution to (

. Furthermore, this control sequence ensures the subsequent state
x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{C}

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we have proposed an algorithmic framework that addressed critical challenges in ensuring safety and feasibility in control dynamical systems modeled by neural networks. By developing algorithms for synthesizing a CIS via finite-step set recursion, we established theoretical guarantees for closed-loop forward invariance and safety.
In addition, we proposed an MPC framework that integrates these invariant sets using MILCs, enabling robust and recursively feasible online control.
Simulation results in an autonomous driving application demonstrate that the proposed methods effectively ensure closed-loop system safety, maintain recursive feasibility of the MPC, and provide robust performance suitable for safety-critical control applications.
Future research will investigate improvements in the time complexity of the offline CIS synthesis process, particularly for high-dimensional systems, and extend these methods to handle uncertainties.
