Comments on “Comparative Study on the Efficacy of PRP Gel and UC‐MSCs Gel as Adjuvant Therapies in the Treatment of DFU Wounds”
Authors: Mostafa Javanian, Mohammad Barary, Majid Khalilizad, Soheil Ebrahimpour
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Jin et al. [1], titled “Comparative Study on the Efficacy of PRP Gel and UC‐MSCs Gel as Adjuvant Therapies in the Treatment of DFU Wounds.” This valuable research contributes to our understanding of how platelet‐rich plasma (PRP) gel compares with umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UC‐MSCs) gel in managing diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). The authors demonstrated that UC‐MSCs gel was significantly more effective than PRP gel, resulting in quicker wound closure, improved wound healing rates, enhanced control of pain and infection, and better vascular and neurological outcomes. We commend the authors for this insightful work and would like to offer several points of reflection and suggestions for further research.

First, while the study highlights essential laboratory parameters, it could have expanded its analysis by incorporating other biomarkers indicative of inflammation and infection, such as the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR) [2]. These markers have proven valuable in predicting poor outcomes and could have strengthened the study's conclusions on infection activity and inflammation severity.

Second, the inclusion criteria included patients with type 1 or 2 diabetes. However, there was no precise specification of whether patients with type 1 diabetes were actually present in the study cohort. This distinction is critical, given the differences in disease pathophysiology between type 1 and type 2 diabetes, which could affect wound healing responses [3, 4]. Furthermore, it would have been beneficial to indicate whether the study considered the use of insulin therapy versus oral anti‐diabetic agents in type 2 diabetes patients, as insulin has been shown to promote wound healing more effectively than oral medications.

Third, the study mentions that the ulcers were located below the ankle on either the plantar or dorsal surface of the foot. However, a more detailed anatomical classification of wound locations would have been helpful, such as specifying the involvement of the first metatarsal head, heel, or other areas. This could have allowed for a better understanding of how ulcer location might influence healing outcomes in response to the adjuvant therapies used. Additionally, the study did not clarify whether the ulcers were primary or recurrent, nor did it address whether patients had a prior history of amputation, which is relevant when assessing treatment efficacy.

Moreover, the study lacked measurements of foot skin temperature, which could have provided valuable information regarding the ulcers' vascular status and healing potential. Foot temperature is an essential factor in the early detection of infection and inflammation [5].

Lastly, there was insufficient information regarding the frequency of dressing changes with the PRP and UC‐MSCs gels. Dressing frequency and protocols for managing infections during dressing changes are crucial for understanding the overall treatment efficacy and patient care.

In conclusion, while Jin et al. present a well‐conducted study with promising findings, addressing the limitations mentioned above could enhance the study's scientific rigor and clinical applicability. We hope these reflections will contribute to the ongoing discourse on DFU management and encourage further investigation into optimizing treatment strategies.
