Contemporary Management of Clot-In-Transit
Authors: Pipin Kojodjojo, Edgar Tay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: cardiac arrest, catheter thrombectomy, clot in transit, pulmonary emboli

Clot in transit (CIT), which refers to mobile venous thrombi detected in the great veins and right heart en route to the pulmonary circulation, complicates 2% to 4% of patients diagnosed with pulmonary embolism (PE).1 According to registry data, CIT is an independent adverse prognostic marker for patients with PE, with a greater than 2-fold increased mortality risk.2 Because most PE originates from the deep veins, most cases of PE would, at some point in time, have existed as a CIT. However, visible CIT poses a greater risk of hemodynamic decompensation probably due to its larger size, mobility which lends itself to earlier detection on imaging and abrupt increase in right heart afterload with unpredictable consequences when it does embolize to the pulmonary arteries. According to a meta-analysis of 177 cases of CIT described in the literature between 1966 and 2000, survival of patients receiving systemic thrombolysis was sufficiently higher than that of patients receiving no treatment, anticoagulation alone, or surgical embolectomy.3 More recent data from a 2-center patient series of only 35 patients showed that patients who received systemic thrombolysis or catheter-based therapies had significantly lower rates of mortality, hemodynamic decompensation, or cardiac arrest compared with those receiving anticoagulation alone.4 No randomized data exist to guide therapies in this understudied category of high-risk PE. Nonetheless, in the recent 2026 American PE guidelines, there is a Class 2a (Level of Evidence: C-Limited Data) recommendation for advanced therapies to be used in preference to anticoagulation alone for the management of right heart CIT in patients with American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology PE categories C3 to E2, in order to reduce the risk of clinical deterioration.1

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Shavgulidze et al5 report an interesting case of a 96-year-old woman with recurrent syncope, shock, and hypotension due to a large right atrial CIT causing dynamic tricuspid valve obstruction in conjunction with American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology PE category E2 (European Society of Cardiology high-risk PE). Systemic thrombolysis was contraindicated because of a recent ischemic stroke, while her advanced age would render her to be at high surgical operative risk. Therefore, large-bore aspiration catheter thrombectomy guided by echocardiography successfully extracted the large CIT with resolution of hypotension and resolved the need for inotropes. Given such clinical improvement, the team decided to defer further thrombectomy in the pulmonary arteries for her concomitant PE, and the patient remained well 1 year after discharge.

In recent years, there have been sizable advancements in the field of catheter-directed therapies for PE, which can be grouped into smaller-bore catheter-directed thrombolysis and larger-bore mechanical thrombectomy, largely reliant on suction to remove pulmonary arterial thrombi. In addition, 2 catheters (Angiola; AngioDynamics, and FlowTriever; Inari Medical) have received specific Food and Drug Administration approval for CIT extraction. Emerging data from randomized controlled trials and prospective registries have shown that such catheter-based therapies are more effective than anticoagulation in reversing right heart strain (reduced right ventricle/left ventricle ratio, pulmonary arterial pressures), improving hemodynamics (increased cardiac index, reduced sinus tachycardia), and reducing the risk of decompensation requiring rescue therapy and greater symptomatic relief.6, 7, 8 Although longer-term data on survival and freedom from pulmonary hypertension may be lacking (and may never be seen as all contemporary trials must ethically allow for the use of bailout rescue therapy in decompensating control patients only on anticoagulation), the use of advanced therapies in intermediate- and high-risk patients is associated with greater survival compared with historical cohorts treated with anticoagulation and systemic thrombolysis only.9

In the National Inpatient Sample database from 2016 to 2020, there is a substantial rise in patients receiving catheter-directed thrombectomy for CIT and PE, increasing from 203 cases in 2016 to 533 cases in 2020.10 Furthermore, compared with similar patients undergoing surgical embolectomy, patients treated with catheter-directed thrombectomy had a significantly lower in-hospital mortality (12.5% vs 26.6%). The case described by Dr Shavgulidze reflects current clinical practice. As recently as 15 years ago, it would be highly unlikely that successful catheter therapy for such an elderly patient with contraindications to systemic thrombolysis would have been feasible. Most likely, she would have only been treated with anticoagulation alone and be at higher risk of adverse clinical outcomes. It is pertinent to remember that death in PE results not from a failure of oxygenation but from circulatory arrest. Reversing the specific pathophysiology leading to right heart failure is crucial to restore hemodynamics and improve survival. This is nicely demonstrated in this case, where relief from tricuspid obstruction caused by a large CIT was sufficient to improve hemodynamics without the need for further pulmonary arterial thrombectomy. It remains uncertain whether complete pulmonary thrombus clearance can improve long-term cardiopulmonary capacity and prevent chronic pulmonary hypertension. Given the advanced age of this patient and the marked improvement in clinical status, the decision to defer additional thrombectomy in this 96-year-old patient seems prudent.
