Liquid Biopsy-Based Detection and Response Prediction for Depression
Authors: Seungmin Kim, Youbin Kang, Hyunku Shin, Eun Byul Lee, Byung-Joo Ham, Yeonho Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProactively predicting antidepressant treatment response before medication failures is crucial, as it reduces unsuccessful attempts and facilitates the development of personalized therapeutic strategies, ultimately enhancing treatment efficacy. The current decision-making process, which heavily depends on subjective indicators, underscores the need for an objective, indicator-based approach. This study developed a method for detecting depression and predicting treatment response through deep learning-based spectroscopic analysis of extracellular vesicles (EVs) from plasma. EVs were isolated from the plasma of both nondepressed and depressed groups, followed by Raman signal acquisition, which was used for AI algorithm development. The algorithm successfully distinguished depression patients from healthy individuals and those with panic disorder, achieving an AUC accuracy of 0.95. This demonstrates the model’s capability to selectively diagnose depression within a nondepressed group, including those with other mental health disorders. Furthermore, the algorithm identified depression-diagnosed patients likely to respond to antidepressants, classifying responders and nonresponders with an AUC accuracy of 0.91. To establish a diagnostic foundation, the algorithm applied explainable AI (XAI), enabling personalized medicine for companion diagnostics and highlighting its potential for the development of liquid biopsy-based mental disorder diagnosis.Keywords: depression, treatment monitoring, diagnosis, extracellular vesicles, artificial intelligence, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy

Depression is a major mental health issue that mentally and physically affects millions of individuals worldwide.1−3 Although many individuals with depression show improvement with conventional treatments such as antidepressants and psychotherapy, about 40% of patients do not achieve remission after the initial treatment.4−9 Moreover, treatment failures result in a delay of 5–7 weeks, which diminishes the depression remission rate. A study on sequenced treatment alternatives to relieve depression (STAR-D) revealed that the overall remission rates after the first, second, third, and fourth treatment stages were 28, 25, 18, and 10%, respectively.2,7,8 Therefore, early prediction of antidepressant response is crucial for devising effective treatment strategies and enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

Researchers in the field of mental health disorders have expended continuous efforts to access psychiatric information using biomarkers, instead of relying solely on subjective data, such as self-reports or clinical observations.10−14 Although extensive research has been conducted to identify suitable biomarkers for the early prediction of treatment response, a definitive biomarker has yet to be established, thus rendering early prediction using current methods challenging.15−18

Hence, using biomarkers for quantitative analyses to assess the status of individuals with mental health disorders can significantly enhance both diagnostic processes and ongoing monitoring. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), i.e., nanovesicles secreted by every cell, which are abundant in plasma, are crucial in intercellular communication and have emerged as highly promising diagnostic markers for diseases.19−24 Moreover, the ability of EVs to penetrate the blood–brain barrier implies that psychiatric information can be obtained via liquid biopsy without requiring brain biopsy, thus highlighting their promising potential in the field.25

Leveraging the comprehensive characteristics of various EV components is critical in employing EVs to predict treatment response. Raman spectroscopy has been used to consolidate the features of EV markers into a single spectrum.26−33 Raman spectroscopy is a highly sensitive method for obtaining information pertaining to the vibrational and rotational modes of chemical structures, thus offering a molecular fingerprint that identifies the overall information regarding EV components.34−40

Previous studies analyzed extracellular vesicles (EVs) using Raman spectroscopy to explore the diagnosis of various diseases, including major depressive disorder (MDD).25,34−37 This study builds upon our earlier work, which established a foundation for diagnosing MDD using Raman spectroscopic features of plasma exosomes.25 The current study improved the model by including patients with panic disorder in the nondepression group, resulting in enhanced diagnostic accuracy and specificity. Furthermore, the research was extended to predict treatment response in MDD patients, marking a significant step toward personalized medicine. The integration of Explainable AI (XAI) provided clear, individualized insights, enhancing clinical decision-making and making this approach more applicable and tailored to patient-specific needs.

This research presents a approach for detecting depression and predicting treatment response using a combination of deep learning and spectroscopic analysis of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in plasma. Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) signals were obtained using gold nanoparticles (GNPs) uniformly distributed on a substrate, enabling high-sensitivity detection when EVs were attached (Figure 1c). Our artificial intelligence (AI) algorithm, developed through extensive training on these SERS signals, comprises two models: one for detecting depression and the other for predicting treatment response (Figure 1d).

The decision-making process of the AI algorithm was validated using explainable AI (XAI) to identify the key spectral features that underlie its predictions. This makes the technology particularly suitable for clinical applications, where transparent decision-making is essential. By pinpointing the primary spectral characteristics responsible for the predictions, clinicians can gain deeper insights into the biological markers associated with treatment response.41−43 This enhanced understanding facilitates more reliable treatment decisions, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes.

Isolation of EVs from Plasma

A size-exclusion chromatography kit was employed to isolate EVs from plasma. Precleared plasma samples were segregated based on size using a nanoporous bead-filled column. Only the 11th and 12th fractions were obtained to comply with the manufacturer guidelines.

Evaluation of the Isolated EV

Subsequently, the morphology, diameter, and expression of EV markers of the isolated EVs were verified. The cryo-transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image in Figure 2a shows the isolated EV morphology. The images obtained from cryo-TEM analysis provide detailed insights into the morphology and lipid bilayer structure of EVs, serving as crucial evidence for evaluating their structural integrity and quality. Figure 2a offers visual confirmation that the EVs used in this study maintain the expected physical properties, including size and shape.

Furthermore, nanoparticle-tracking analysis was employed to confirm whether the size and concentration of the EVs were within the conventional EV range (Figure 2b). The average size of the EVs isolated from the plasma samples of the nondepression (n = 48) and depression groups (n = 98) were 117.74 ± 4.3 and 118.56 ± 5.2 nm, respectively (Supporting Tables 2, 3, and 4). Additionally, the results from Western Blotting assess the expression levels of specific protein markers (CD63, CD81, TSG101), confirming the successful isolation and characterization of EVs (Figure 2c and Supporting Figure 1). The WB analysis complements the morphological data from TEM by demonstrating that the EVs exhibit the anticipated biochemical features. Consequently, these findings reinforce the reliability of the EV analysis in this study and provide essential foundational data for interpreting the study’s results.

EV Detection

The isolated EVs were deposited onto a uniformly coated SERS substrate containing GNPs and then dried overnight.37 The interface between the dried EVs and GNPs was illuminated using a 785 nm laser to obtain SERS signals (Figure 2d). Figure 2e shows the representative EV SERS signals obtained for the responder and nonresponder groups. Supporting Figure 2 shows representative SERS signals of the nondepression group, including those of the healthy controls (HCs) and patients with panic disorder (PD). A total of 400 (20 × 20) SERS signals, which were acquired at equal intervals, were used for every plasma sample. The laser-acquisition spot was shifted by 3 μm per SERS signal acquisition.36,37

The SERS signals were classified using statistical methods, such as principal component analysis, to assess the signal differences between the groups. Although common peaks were observed at 623, 845, 1111, 1358, 1521–1579, and 1700 cm–1, which are indicative of EV characteristics, minimal differences were observed between the groups, thus rendering them clinically unusable (Supporting Figure 3).30,35,37,44 This was ascribable to the considerable inherent variation (shaded area in Figure 2e) in the EV signals. The signals were classified by AI analysis to address their complexity.34,45−47

Data Distribution

The overall AI algorithm comprises two serially arranged models for detecting depression and predicting the treatment response. Plasma samples (n = 146) from the nondepression and depression groups were used to develop the algorithm. In particular, the nondepression group included HCs and individuals with PD who did not have depression but suffered from similar mental disorders. Among the 146 plasma samples, 78 were used as the algorithm training set (Figure 3).

Flow diagram of classification algorithm. (a) Prototypical standards for reporting diagnostic accuracy (STAR-D) diagram showing participant flow in this study.

The first model for diagnosing depression was trained using the nondepression group (n = 28, labeled 0) and depression group (n = 28, labeled 1) without bias resulting from the sample. The second model for prediction of treatment response was trained using the responders (n = 25, labeled 0) and nonresponders (n = 25, labeled 1). The models were developed via sample-wise analysis, thereby eliminating the redundancy of signals and facilitating the easy identification of outliers or anomalous data.

Prediction Process Using AI

To evaluate the diagnostic algorithm, 68 independent samples, not included in the training process, were utilized to test the trained models (Figure 3). For the first model, the nondepression group was labeled as 0, and the depression group as 1, which the AI model used for training. The model consists of multiple layers for computation and a final prediction output step. The sigmoid function is applied at this final stage, converting the Raman signals into values between 0 and 1. During prediction, the model calculates the probability of whether the signal aligns more with the features of the nondepression or depression group.

A SERS signal map (20 × 20) was obtained using 400 SERS signals from the 68 plasma samples (Figure 4a).25,36,37 The 400 signals in the SERS map generated 400 predicted values ranging from 0 to 1, which allowed the first model to calculate the probability for prediction. When the signal features were more predictive of the nondepression group, the model returned values closer to 0, whereas when the features aligned with the depression group, the values were closer to 1.

Detection and prediction results using SERS and AI. (a) Schematic of the entire algorithm. The colored boxes show representative predicted results per sample using two deep learning models. (b, c) Mean output value of predicted map per sample. The box and circle indicate interquartile range and median, respectively. (d) ROC curve for the algorithm using the entire test set.

The colored box for each sample represents the 400 predicted values as a heat map. A single value for depression diagnosis was obtained by averaging these 400 values, as indicated by a colored bar. Samples with values exceeding a certain threshold in the SERS map were classified as depression patients.

Subsequently, the algorithm used the second model to generate SERS maps for predicting treatment response. If the signal features were more predictive of responders, the model produced values closer to 0; if the features indicated nonresponders, the predicted values were closer to 1. Nonresponders were diagnosed based on the final average value.

Depression Detection

The proposed algorithm successfully distinguished depressive patients from those without depression. Training using 22,400 SERS signals, extracting features from each group, and optimizing the diagnostic model resulted in a final model accuracy of 88.67% (Supporting Figure 4). This model was validated using 5-fold cross-validation (Supporting Table 5).

In this study, we utilized 400 signals per sample to efficiently make use of a small number of clinical samples. Classification performance based on the number of Raman spectra utilized per sample shows a significant improvement in accuracy. When more than 100 Raman spectra are used, the AUC-based accuracy rises from 0.63 to 0.94 (Supporting Figure 5). The coefficient of determination (R2) is 0.98, reflecting a strong correlation between the number of spectra used and the classification accuracy. This approach enhances diagnostic accuracy by combining SERS and deep learning output values, effectively utilizing the clinical samples for more reliable assessments.

Supporting Figure 6 shows the Raman heat maps using 400 spectra per sample, showing the predicted values for the HC and depression signals that were not used for training. Figure 4b depicts the trend for the mean output value. The inner box plot displays the median, interquartile range (Q1 and Q3), and minimum–maximum ranges. As a result, 19 out of 20 patients in the nondepression group were correctly identified as nondepressive, and 44 out of 48 patients in the depression group were accurately diagnosed with depression by the first model. Notably, all patients with panic disorder (n = 3) within the nondepression group were correctly classified as nondepressive. These results indicate that the first model clearly differentiates between nondepressive and depressive groups.

Prediction of Treatment Response

The 44 patients diagnosed with depression by the first model were subsequently transferred to the treatment-response prediction model (second model). The second model predicted that 44 patients exhibited treatment response. The nonresponder prediction model, which was trained to classify the responders and nonresponders, achieved a final accuracy of 82.45% (Supporting Figure 7). This model was validated using 5-fold cross-validation (Supporting Table 6). Supporting Figure 8 presents the Raman heat maps using 400 spectra per sample, showing the predicted status values of the 44 nonresponders. As in the first model, the final output for each sample was calculated by averaging the 400 predicted values. Figure 4c presents the trend of the final output, which shows successful classification based on treatment response. The responders and nonresponders were classified using a threshold value of 0.5, which resulted in an AUC accuracy of 0.92 (Figure 4d, pink).

Finally, our algorithm successfully predicted the nonresponders among the depressive patients, thus enabling the early formulation of treatment strategies to maximize treatment efficacy. The first model selectively classified depressive patients from both healthy individuals and those with other mental disorders, such as PD. Among the 20 patients in the nondepression group, including the HCs (n = 17) and patients with PD (n = 3), 19 were correctly identified as nondepressed, excluding one HC. Among the 48 depressive patients, including the responders (n = 19) and nonresponders (n = 29), 44 were diagnosed with depression (Figure 5a). These 44 patients were then further classified into responders and nonresponders using the second model. Among the 16 responders, 15 were predicted to be responders, while among the 28 nonresponders, 25 were predicted to be nonresponders (Figure 5b). These results shows that the AL algorithm can classify the target groups from control groups with high accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity (Supporting Table 7).

Application of the Explainable AI Algorithm (XAI)

The local interpretable model diagnostic explanation model, which is a technique within XAI, was applied to confirm the basis of our diagnostic outcomes.41 XAI systems are designed to provide explanations for AI predictions. Therefore, employing XAI enhances the credibility of AI-based decisions.45,46 This technology is well-suited to providing the necessary transparent decision-making evidence for clinical applications. By identifying key spectral features that serve as the basis for predictions, it can support more reliable clinical decisions.

This model can identify the main peaks that positively affect the diagnostic decision based on the SERS spectra from the test sets of the two previous models. The key features extracted by XAI from the full-length Raman signals, accumulated from all components of the EVs, are closely related to the biological components of the EVs. Specifically, by identifying the origin of key peaks important for classification, it has become possible to examine whether these peaks are associated with factors linked to depression from a clinical perspective.

Decision-Basis Identification

Ten key values with a higher weight were extracted for each SERS spectrum. Since the diagnostic procedure for each sample used 400 signals, the key values from 400 signals per sample were averaged to derive a final weight. Consequently, a single final weight matrix, which indicates the impact level of each peak across wavelength ranges on the decision, was obtained for each sample. The weight values for each peak were visualized using a bar graph, thus allowing one to directly understand the signal-weight trends in the sample (Figure 6a).

(b, c) Summed feature weights of the test samples using XAI. Final feature weight for (b) first and (d) second models. (c) Table listing weights of the 10 key features of the nondepression and depression groups using the first diagnostic model. (e) Table listing weights of the 10 key features of the responder and nonresponder groups using the second prediction model.

Figure 6b shows the decision-positive weight for the test set of the first model. The summed feature weights of the final weight matrix were averaged in the nondepression (n = 20, green) and depression (n = 48, purple) groups. Supporting Figure 9a shows the entire final weight matrix heat map of the samples. These key weights are enumerated in the descending order of significance in Figure 6c. The nondepression and depression groups exhibited common peaks at 641, 714, 1435, 1443, and 1609 cm–1 and 657, 708, 1441, 1458, and 1616 cm–1, respectively. The uncolored peaks indicate features that represent distinct characteristics of each group, which suggest their significance in differentiating between the two groups.

As shown in the table, the key peaks have origins related to depression: 641 and 1609 cm–1 correspond to tyrosine (a precursor of dopamine),47−49 714 cm–1 to cholesterol involved in serotonin activity,49−51 and 1435 cm–1 to lipid-related features related to inflammatory responses associated with depression.47,49,52 This indicates that the model diagnoses depression by reflecting Raman peaks associated with disease-related biological components.

Figure 6d shows the decision weights of the second model. Both the responder and nonresponder groups exhibited similar key peaks at 1479, 1724, 1735, and 1625 cm–1 (Figure 6e). The high value at 1625 cm–1, as shown in Supporting Figure 9b, is attributed to an unusually high value derived from one patient. Additionally, the peak at 1625 cm–1 corresponded to the norepinephrine Raman peak, which indicates the existence of hormonal differences between the patients in the responder and nonresponder groups.44−46 Nevertheless, the weight of the peak (0.15) was relatively low compared with that of the previous model.

Moreover, owing to the minimal biological differences between the responders and nonresponders within the depressive-patient cohort, the decision-positive values were low and distributed evenly across the entire signal instead of appearing as specific peaks. This underscores the importance of leveraging the full SERS spectral data for predictive analysis instead of the individual peaks.

Discussion

Subjective indicators, such as self-reports or clinical observation, are typically used to diagnose depression. However, the proposed quantitative method, which is based on liquid biopsy, offers objective results, thus enabling a reliable diagnosis while facilitating the early prediction of treatment resistance and personalized treatment strategies. Therefore, our algorithm provides initial evidence supporting the potential for detecting depression and predicting antidepressant response.

In our study, we build upon previous research by advancing the diagnostic capabilities and extending the application to predict treatment response in Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) patients, representing a significant leap toward personalized medicine. While prior studies, including our own earlier work, focused primarily on the detection and diagnosis of MDD, our current study introduces several critical innovations.

First, by incorporating patients with panic disorder into the nondepression group, we achieved greater diagnostic accuracy and specificity, enhancing the robustness of the model in distinguishing MDD from other mental health conditions. Second, our inclusion of a treatment-response prediction model adds a layer of clinical significance, as it enables the proactive identification of patients who are likely to respond to antidepressants. This capability is crucial for tailoring individualized treatment strategies, minimizing unsuccessful treatment attempts, and ultimately improving patient outcomes.

The use of Explainable AI (XAI) in our model further enhances its clinical applicability by providing transparent, interpretable insights into the biological markers that underlie the algorithm’s predictions. This transparency is essential for gaining clinical trust and ensuring that the technology can be effectively integrated into clinical workflows, supporting more reliable and informed treatment decisions. Thus, our study not only advances the field of mental health diagnostics but also sets a standard for the application of AI in personalized medicine.

Moreover, the proposed technique is applicable to actual clinical environments as it offers rapid and accurate diagnoses and predictions within 30 min using only 500 μL of plasma, thereby improving treatment outcomes and monitoring for patients.

However, our study has some limitations that should be considered. First, the small sample size limits the scope of our findings, and larger-scale studies with validation across more diverse populations are needed for a more comprehensive understanding. Second, while we performed diagnostic assignments of key Raman shifts and their association with depression-related substances, further prospective studies should focus on elucidating the bridge between biological analysis and our technique to strengthen the clinical relevance. This would greatly enhance the contribution of the algorithm to the clinical field. Finally, integrating richer data sets that encompass physiological, psychological, and sociological variables would improve the robustness and reliability of the algorithm, ensuring it is more adaptable to different clinical settings.

Conclusions

The results of this study demonstrated the potential of liquid biopsy-based depression detection and prediction of antidepressant response for companion diagnosis. The nondepression (including the HCs and patients with PD) and depression groups were classified with high accuracy (0.95), sensitivity (0.91), and specificity (0.95). Moreover, the responder and nonresponder groups were classified with high accuracy (0.91), sensitivity (0.89), and specificity (0.93). Additionally, the decision the basis of the algorithm was identified using XAI. This method enables precision medicine and companion diagnosis for individuals with unfavorable treatment responses.

Methods

Blood Sampling

Peripheral venous blood samples were obtained from all participants after they fasted for at least 12 h. A total of 8 mL of blood was drawn into additive-free vacutainer tubes. The plasma samples were allowed to clot for 30 min at room temperature and then separated via centrifugation at 1000g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was stored at −80 °C for up to 6 months and thawed on the day of assay. We ensured that blood samples were collected before the initiation of any treatment. However, not all participants were drug naïve. In cases where participants had previously been on medication, we implemented a washout period of more than 2–5 half-lives (exceeding 4 weeks) prior to blood collection and the initiation of alternative treatments to minimize any potential interference from previous medications.53 This approach ensured that all samples were free from the influence of active medication at the time of collection.

Participants

Recruitment Criteria

A total of 98 individuals diagnosed with major depressive disorder (MDD, responder, 44; nonresponder, 54) and 48 nondepression individuals (42 Healthy Control, 6 Panic Disorder (PD)) were included in this study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Korea University Anam Hospital (2022AN0540, 2023AN0363, 2019AN0174, 2024AN0001). All participants were recruited between July 2018 and September 2023 from the outpatient psychiatric clinic of Korea University Anam Hospital in Seoul, Republic of Korea and provided written informed consent to participate in the study. The diagnosis of MDD and PD was determined by two experienced board-certified psychiatrists (K.-M. Han and B.-J. Ham) according to the Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-IV-TR (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, Text Revision)54 Axis I Disorders (SCID-I). The study methodology was in accordance with approved guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criterion for the MDD group was adults aged 19–65 years and the exclusion criteria included (i) comorbidity with other major psychiatric disorders (including personality and substance use disorders), (ii) MDD with psychotic features, (iii) acute suicidal or homicidal patients requiring inpatient treatment, (iv) history of a serious or unstable medical illness, (v) primary neurological illness (i.e., Parkinson’s disease, cerebrovascular disease, or epilepsy), (vi) recent abnormal results on physical examination or laboratory tests, (vii) pregnant or currently nursing, and (viii) any contraindication for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Participants were included in the nondepression group if two board-certified psychiatrists confirmed that they had no ongoing or past history of Axis I or II disorders.

Sociodemographic Characteristics

The sociodemographic information on the participants, including age, sex, education level, and smoking, was collected from both groups. The Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS) was used to measure severity of illness in PD (average 6.83). The Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS),55,56 widely used scale in empirical research involving psychiatric patients, was used as an assessment tool for depressive symptoms. The test, composed of 17 items scored on a scale of 0 to 2 or 0 to 4 was used to classify the patient’s antidepressant response. For responders, we classified patients with more than 50% decrease in the total HDRS score after 8 weeks of treatment from baseline. Detailed information regarding participants is provided in the Table S1.

EV Isolation

EVs were isolated from plasma. Blood samples were initially subjected to centrifugation at 500g for 5 min, followed by a subsequent centrifugation at 2000g for 15 min at 4 °C to eliminate any potential contamination from blood cells or cellular debris. The EVs were isolated using EXO-I isolation equipment (Exopert, Seoul, South Korea). The isolation procedure involved processing approximately 500 μL of plasma in accordance with the precise guidelines provided by the equipment manufacturer.

Isolated EV Evaluation

For the Western blotting, EVs were lysed using 10X RIPA buffer (Millipore). Protein concentrations were determined with a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher). Protein samples were mixed with 5X SDS loading dye and boiled for 5 min at 100 °C. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto a 4–20% precast gel (Bio-Rad) for sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad). The membranes were blocked with 3% BSA in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were Anti-CD9, Anti-CD63, and Anti-TSG101 (Santa Cruz), diluted to 1:1000 for CD9 and CD63, and 1:5000 for TSG101 in 2% BSA solution. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h at room temperature, diluted to 1:10,000 in 1% BSA solution. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence substrate (Bio-Rad) and detected with a bioimaging system (Amersham ImageQuant 800, Cytiva, Germany).57,58

For cryo-TEM analysis, 3 μL of the sample was placed onto a 200 mesh Lacey carbon grid. The grid was then processed using an FEI Vitrobot FP5350/60, with a wait time of 30 s and a blot time of 3 s. After vitrification, the grid was transferred to liquid nitrogen and subsequently mounted on a Gatan 626 cryo holder for observation with a Tecnai F20 G2 microscope.57−59

Preparation of the SERS Substrate

All cover glasses were cleaned using a piranha solution (H2SO4/H2O2 = 3:1). The cleaned glasses were treated with 1% APTES in ethyl alcohol (v/v) for 1 h and then washed with ethyl alcohol.34,37 The GNP solution was concentrated by centrifuging thrice at 1000g for 12 min. Subsequently, the concentrated GNP solution was centrifuged on the surface of the substrates at 1000g for 10 min at 4 °C. After washing with DIW, 8 μL of isolated EVs were deposited on the substrates and dried overnight at room temperature.

SERS Characterization

A spectrometer (Dongwoo Optron Co., Ltd., Gwangju City, Republic of Korea) coupled with a spectrographic detector (Andor Technology Ltd., Belfast, UK) was used to acquire the SERS spectra. A 785 nm laser operating at 1.5 mW was used for excitation. The spectral signals were systematically acquired near the substrate center in a 20 × 20 array configuration using a 50× objective lens for 1 s to ensure data consistency. Signal processing encompassing denoising, baseline correction, and normalization was conducted using MATLAB R2020a (MathWorks).34−36 Corrections were executed using the BEADS toolbox with specified regularization parameters, including a cutoff frequency of 0.05 and an asymmetry parameter of 6.

Deep Learning and Statistical Analysis

Two deep learning models were applied using the Keras library in Python. In the first model, which was a CNN model, cross-entropy was defined, and optimization was performed during the learning process of the primary model using an RMS prop optimizer at a learning rate of 0.35.60−68 A decay rate of 0.9 and a batch size of 128 were used. Meanwhile, the second model was applied using an RMS prop optimizer at a learning rate of 0.003 and a decay rate of 0.4. A batch size of 406 was used to avoid overfitting.
