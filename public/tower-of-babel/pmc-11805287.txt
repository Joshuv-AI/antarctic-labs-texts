Perception of Dental Interns About Intracanal Fracture of Endodontic Instruments in the Central Region of Saudi Arabia: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Abdullah Ahmad A. Aloyouni, Muhammad Atif Saleem Agwan, Saleh Suliman S. Almuzaini, Faris Saleh A. Alqazlan, Abdulaziz Abdulrhman A. Alshumaym, Khalid Abdullah G. Alfuryah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:This study assessed the perceptions among dental interns regarding intracanal fracture of endodontic instruments.Methodology:This cross-sectional observational study was performed among 206 dental interns working in Saudi Arabia. A validated questionnaire encompassing 15 close-ended questions was used. The questionnaire consisted of two components: the first component (Section I) gathered demographic details, and the second part (Section II) examined the causes of EFS during root canal treatment. A Pearson’s Chi-square test was applied to determine the association of perceptions of EFS during endodontic procedures between male and female interns.Results:The study findings showed that out of 206 dental interns, 107 (51.9%) were males and 99 (48.1%) were females. The perceptions regarding intracanal instrument fracture revealed that hand files (71 (34.5%)) were less likely to break than rotary files (132 (64.1%)). Around 152 (73.8%) respondents said that stainless steel was the most frequently broken alloy. Moreover, there was a significant relationship between gender and the belief that operator expertise plays a role in preventing instrument fracture.Conclusion:This study concluded that dental interns were aware of factors contributing to file separation during endodontic treatment. They believed fractures were more common in the apical third of posterior teeth, preferred Nickel Titanium for instruments, and thought hand files were less prone to fracture than rotary files.

INTRODUCTION

Numerous procedural mistakes might happen at any point throughout the root canal procedure in endodontic practice.[1] During root canal treatment, one of the most experienced procedural faults that happens is intracanal file fracture, which presents a significant obstacle for root canal therapy.[2]

Intracanal fractures of endodontic hand instruments occur in 0.25%–6% of cases.[3]

The existence of endodontic file separation (EFS) has a substantial effect on treatment outcomes, influencing both the chemical and mechanical components of root canal therapy. Common variables that contribute to EFS include root anatomy, incorrect instrument use, insufficient access, inexperienced operators, manufacturing defects, restricted material characteristics, and a lack of thorough knowledge about root canal shape and its variations.[4] The materials that make up these discrete instruments range from carbon steel to stainless steel (SS) to nickel–titanium (NiTi). These instruments include Gates-Glidden burs (GG), spreaders, pluggers, lentulo-spirals, and endodontic hand or rotary files.[5]

Although stainless steel hand files have long been the only instruments used for canal preparation, their high rigidity and low elasticity can result in different procedural errors. Rotary NiTi files were brought to the market to reduce these complications and accomplish the anatomical challenges associated with root canal therapy. In comparison with stainless steel files, rotary NiTi files have higher mechanical strength, less tendency to straighten the canals, and greater flexibility and elasticity.[6]

According to reports, the prevalence of fracture of NiTi rotary files ranges from 1.04% to 13.54%, whereas that of SS hand files differs between 2% and 6%.[7]

It is imperative to take preventive action to lower the possibility of EFS. A coronal flare is used to develop access in a straight line, which improves the glide path. Furthermore, coronal flaring gives vision into the anatomical canal and minimizes the torsional stress while the canal width approaches the instrument’s diameter.[8] Calcium-chelating agents such as EDTA soften the walls of the dentin by creating calcium complexes with the non-organic dentin components. Prior research has shown that using these lubricants can lower the chance of file fracture.[9]

There is a lack of research that focuses exclusively on dental interns’ perceptions, despite their large population in dental education and clinical practice. Therefore, this study assessed the perceptions among dental interns regarding the intracanal instrument fracture of endodontic instruments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional observational study was performed among dental interns working in the central region of Saudi Arabia after the approval of the Institutional Review Board of Qassim University, Saudi Arabia (approval number: 23-68-08). The study’s duration was about 3 months (December 2023–February 2024); using a convenient non probability sampling technique. This study included 206 dental interns working in the central region of Saudi Arabia. Undergraduate dental students, students who completed internships, and interns not practicing or volunteering were excluded from the study.

Data collection methods

A validated questionnaire consisting of 15 closed-ended questions was utilized, which was reported and tested in the prior literature.[10] The questionnaire comprised two components: the first component (Section I) gathered demographic details, and the second part (Section II) examined the reasons for EFS during root canal therapy. The questionnaire collected demographic information such as gender and age, as well as the causes of endodontic instrument breakage that occurred during root canal therapy. The questionnaire was distributed to all respondents through Google Forms by using their university email addresses. This study concluded that dental interns were aware of factors contributing to file separation during endodontic treatment. They believed fractures were more common in the apical third of posterior teeth, preferred Nickel Titanium for instruments, and thought hand files were less prone to fracture than rotary files. Interns also believed that using EDTA and coronal flares reduced the likelihood of file separation, demonstrating good awareness and preventive practices.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the (SPSS; Armonk, NY, USA) version 26.0. The demographic details, such as gender and perceptions of EFS during endodontic procedures among interns, were presented as frequencies and percentages. A Pearson’s Chi-square test was applied to determine the association of perceptions of EFS during endodontic treatment between female and male interns. A P value of less than 0.05 was reflected as statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 206 dental interns participated in the study, with 107(51.9%) males and 99(48.1%) females. The mean age was 24.91 ± 1.43 years. Most interns 191(92.7%) believed that endodontic instrument fractures occur more frequently in older permanent teeth, with few thinking they occur in primary or young permanent teeth. The majority 177(85.9%) felt that patient anxiety contributed to fractures. Most respondents 201(97.6%) emphasized the importance of operator expertise in preventing fractures, and 189(91.7%) felt that a “coronal flare” reduces fracture risk. Additionally, 193(93.7%) supported using EDTA Gel, 179(86.9%) believed cleaning instruments prevents fractures, and 95.6% agreed that reusing instruments raises fracture risk, as depicted in Table 1.

DISCUSSION

File fractures are considered to be among the most critical risks that could compromise endodontic therapy and have an impact on the prognosis.[11] This study demonstrated the perceptions about endodontic file fractures during root canal procedures among dental interns in the central region of Saudi Arabia.

One of the prospective studies identified the frequency of NiTi rotary and hand file fractures during endodontic therapy performed by postgraduate, 4th, and 5th-year students. The results of this investigation showed that compared to hand files, NiTi rotary files had a higher fracture frequency.[12] This result is in line with other research findings.[3] According to some reports, excessive apical pressure and instrument turning can cause fractures in hand files, although flexural or torsional loading is typically the cause of breakage in NiTi rotary files.[1213] Furthermore, the breaking of files involves numerous parameters.[14] The present study was partially consistent with the above-reported studies and showed that rotary files (132 (64.1%)) were thought to be more likely to break than hand files (71 (34.5%)). As far as the type of endodontic instrument alloy is concerned, 152 (73.8%) respondents mentioned stainless steel, and 48 (23.3%) interns indicated nickel titanium.

Interestingly, one cross-sectional survey in Pakistan was carried out to explore the perceptions and attitudes of dental experts toward intracanal separation of endodontic instruments and to raise awareness about the management of separated instruments. Out of the 374 participants in this study, 307 (82.1%) thought that operator-related factors, such as misuse or incorrect use of the instrument, were the most frequent causes of instrument separation during root canal treatments. The majority of respondents (241 (64.4%)) stated that endodontic instruments were typically separated during the root canal’s cleaning and shaping. According to the respondents, negotiating the canal (31%) was the second most frequent phase of instrument separation.[15] The present study was consistent with the above study, which indicated the majority (180 (87.4%)) believed that instruments are most likely to break during cleaning and shaping, while 23 (11.2%) interns mentioned negotiating the canal.

Generally, it is recognized that there is a higher chance of endodontic instrument fracture with the more complex root canal morphology. Overall, another study revealed that one important factor influencing endodontic instrument separation is the tooth’s location—anterior or posterior, mandibular or maxillary. The posterior teeth had a higher rate of instrument separation (96.3%) as compared to the anterior teeth (3.7%).[16] These findings showed similarity with the present study, which reported that the majority of interns 187 (90.8%) stated that a tooth’s posterior position contributed to instrument fracture, whereas a smaller percentage mentioned a tooth’s anterior position.

The perceptions of dental interns regarding intracanal fracture of endodontic instruments in relation to endodontic instrument factors in the present study showed that most of the interns (197 (95.6%)) thought that selecting the right endodontic instrument could lower the risk of instrument fracture. Compared to hand files (71 (34.5%)), rotary files (132 (64.1%)) were thought to be more prone to breaking. These findings were inconsistent with the previous study, which found that 545 (56.7%) DHOs thought that hand files broke in the canal more often than rotary files and that 362 (45.2%) participants thought SS was the best alloy for endodontic procedures and 356 (44.5%) thought NiTi was the best instrument.[10] Furthermore, a retrospective study revealed that the occurrence of file separation using a rotary and hand instrument was 1.68% and 0.25%, respectively.[17]

EFS is influenced by several factors, such as frequency of instrument use and the proficiency of operators. According to earlier research, endodontic instrumentation and the necessary techniques for EFS retrieval require dentists to have received the highest level of training and extensive understanding.[18] According to the present study, the majority of interns (201 (97.6%)) agreed that an operator’s expertise is essential in preventing endodontic instrument fracture. In terms of particular procedures, most interns (193 (93.7%)) supported the use of EDTA Gel (glide) as a way to lower the risk of fracture. Similarly, a large majority (179 (86.9%)) believed that cleaning an endodontic instrument during root canal instrumentation helps prevent its fracture.

This study had some limitations. The cross-sectional nature of this study raises the possibility of bias. The study involved a small sample size, which may have limited the validity and applicability of its findings. Consequently, further research is advised involving a variety of variables, including torque, instrument techniques, and sterilization.

CONCLUSION

This study concluded that dental interns were aware of factors contributing to file separation during endodontic treatment. They believed fractures were more common in the apical third of posterior teeth, preferred Nickel Titanium for instruments, and thought hand files were less prone to fracture than rotary files. Interns also believed that using EDTA and coronal flares reduced the likelihood of file separation, demonstrating good awareness and preventive practices.
