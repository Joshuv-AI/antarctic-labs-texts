Association between exposure to ambient particulate matter and aggression among adolescents: a national school-based study in China
Authors: Jie Hu, Wei Hu, Zixuan Xu, Chang Peng, Junhan Cheng, Fajuan Rong, Yan Wang, Nan Zhang, Meiqi Guan, Jing Wei, Yizhen Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParticulate matter (PM) exposure affects the neurobehavioral function. However, the link between particulate matter (PM) and aggression remains incompletely elucidated. This study aimed to explore the associations between exposure to varying-sized PM and the risk of aggression in adolescents.MethodsIn the cross-sectional study, a total of 19852 participants, aged 10 to 18 years, were recruited using a multi-stage cluster sampling method from April to December 2021. Ambient PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 concentrations were sourced from the ChinaHighAirPollutants (CHAP) dataset. The Chinese version of the Buss-Warren Aggression Questionnaire (BWAQ) was used to measure aggression.ResultsAmong the adolescents surveyed, 4995 (25.16%) exhibited aggression. Each 10 µg/m3 increase in PM1 (OR [95%CI]: 1.11 [1.04–1.19]), PM1–2.5 (OR [95%CI]: 1.21 [1.09–1.35], and PM2.5 (OR [95%CI]: 1.07 [1.03–1.12]) concentrations was associated with higher odds of aggression. The generalized weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression model showed a significantly positive association between exposure to different sizes of PM below PM10 and aggression (OR [95% CI]: 1.10 [1.06–1.15]. In the interaction analyses, we observed a stronger correlation between varying-sized PM with aggression among males and senior high school students (P for interaction < 0.01). Additionally, the relationship between PM and aggression was mainly due to PM1 and PM1 − 2.5.ConclusionsPM mixed exposure was positively associated with aggression, with associations primarily observed for PM1 and PM1 − 2.5. These findings suggest that effective control of PM with a focus on smaller particles may be more beneficial in reducing aggression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25023-x.Keywords: Particulate matter, Mixture exposure, Aggression, Mental health, Adolescent

Introduction

Aggression refers to any actions aimed at causing harm, pain, or injury to another person, and it is the most common factor promoting violent crimes [1]. It was reported that interpersonal violence ranked as the fifth leading cause of disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) among adolescents aged 10–24 years [2]. Moreover, aggressive and violent behaviors not only substantially contribute significantly to the global burden of physical health but also result in lifelong impacts on the psychological and social functioning of adolescents [3]. Therefore, identifying the modifiable risk factors for aggression is substantial importance, as it can guide the development of early intervention strategies.

The pervasive issue of air pollution has garnered global attention owing to its significant impact on public health [4–7]. Among the various health effects, the potential influence of particulate matter (PM) exposure on neurobehavioral outcomes, including aggression, has emerged as an important area of investigation [8–10]. Recent evidence has suggested that air pollution, especially PM, was linked with aggression, although inconsistent findings have persisted [10–16]. Moreover, there is limited research documenting the negative impacts of PM exposure on aggression in adolescents [14–16]. Meanwhile, most of the above studies have focused on developed countries, with less attention given to regions with more severe PM exposure and larger adolescent populations, such as China [17]. Additionally, existing studies have focused on the association between PM2.5 (PM with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 2.5 μm) and aggression, and we know very little about the relationship between varying-sized PM exposures and aggression [13]. Exposure to PM1 (PM with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 1 μm) may induce more severe health effects, including neurotoxicity, as it can penetrate deeper into the human brain compared to larger particulates [18]. To our knowledge, there have been no studies reporting the link between PM1 exposure and aggression in adolescents, possibly because PM1 is not routinely monitored on a global scale. In addition, PM1–2.5 (PM with an aerodynamic diameter between 1 μm and 2.5 μm), PM2.5–10 (PM with an aerodynamic diameter between 2.5 μm and 10 μm), and PM10 (PM with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 10 μm) have shown different toxicological responses in previous studies [18–20]. Given that PM with different aerodynamic diameters exhibit varying toxicological effects, it is still necessary to evaluate the associations between PM of different sizes and aggression to identify the most harmful particles.

To address the current literature gap, we estimated the relationship between varying-sized PM exposure and aggression among a large sample (n = 19852) of Chinese adolescents and compared the associations of different aerodynamic diameters of PM with aggression. We hypothesized that elevated PM concentration would be correlated with increased aggression among adolescents and that exposure to smaller aerodynamic diameters would exhibit a stronger association with aggression compared to PM with larger aerodynamic diameters.

Method

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted from April to December 2021. Participants were recruited by a multi-stage cluster sampling method in five provinces. The study areas are provided in Figure S1. The design of the study has been reported previously in detail [21]. In brief, based on the level of economic development and the characteristics of geographical distribution, we selected two cities from each province. Then, we randomly chose two junior high schools and two senior high schools in each city, totaling 38 schools surveyed. Subsequently, random digits were employed to select 4 to 6 classes from each grade (grades 7 to 12). Finally, the study encompassed 20,153 participants, ranging in age from 10 to 18 years old. After excluding 48 participants with manifestly fictitious or inconsistent responses, 213 participants who did not respond to key covariates, and 40 participants for incomplete aggression assessments with more than 15% missing data, we analyzed 19,852 participants. Informed written consent was obtained from every participant and their guardians before the filed. Ethics approval for the current study was acquired from the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (2021-A216).

Measures

Aggression measurement

Aggression was evaluated using the Chinese version of the Buss-Warren Aggression Questionnaire (BWAQ) [22], which has been validated with internal consistency among adolescents in China [14, 23, 24]. The scale comprises of 34 items with a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = “not at all like me” to 5 = “completely like me”. We first calculated the raw aggression scores for each adolescent. Subsequently, we calculated the T scores for total aggression, with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation (SD) of 10 [22]. Using the T score thresholds from prior research on Chinese adolescents, participants were divided into seven categories: very low (at or below 29T), low (30T to 39T), low average (40T to 44T), average (45T to 55T), high average (56T to 59T), high (60T to 69T), and very high (70T and above) [23]. The categories labeled “high average,” “high,” and “very high” were defined as participants with aggression, while the other groups were classified as not exhibiting aggression [14, 23]. The Cronbach α for BWAQ in current study was 0.918.

Particulate matters (PM) measurement

Ambient PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 concentrations were sourced from the ChinaHighAirPollutants (CHAP) dataset, which is a long-term, seamless, high-resolution, and high-quality dataset of ground-level air pollutants (available at https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html). CHAP covers the Chinese mainland, with a temporal resolution of every day and a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km grids [25–27]. A space-time random forest approach estimated the daily mean concentrations of ambient PM1, PM2.5, and PM10. Detailed PM assessment methods have been published before [25–27]. We assigned the daily mean concentrations of PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 to each school’s address, and subsequently computed the average exposure of each adolescent to ambient PM over the 6- and 12-month periods preceding the survey date. Moreover, we further calculated the concentrations of PM1 − 2.5 and PM2.5−10 using the equations “PM1 − 2.5 equals PM2.5 minus PM1” and “PM2.5−10 minus PM10 minus PM2.5”, respectively [28]. All students in a school were allocated uniform levels of PM concentration for coding.

Covariates

We created a directed acyclic graph (DAG; see Figure S2) to choose potential covariates. Based on the DAG and previous literature [14, 24, 29], we selected sex (female or male), region of school (rural or urban), grade (junior high school or senior high school), parents’ education level (primary school or less, junior high school, senior high school, or college or more), single-child family (yes or no), family structure (core family, single parent, combined family, grandparents, or others), family income (average family income per month in RMB: 6000 ~, 4000–5999, 2000–3999, or ~ 1999), smoking (yes or no), alcohol use (yes or no), physical activity (≥ 1 h/day or < 1 h/day), annual precipitation (continuous). Average precipitation was sourced from the Copernicus Climate Change Service https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/#!/home).

Statistical analysis

Before all analyses, the characteristics of the participants and PM concentrations were presented as follows: categorical variables were described as number (percentage), while continuous variables were described using mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range (IQR).

We tested the correlations between concentrations of PM using Pearson correlation analysis. Then, we performed logistic regression models to explore the relationship between PM and aggression. Multicollinearity existed among average precipitation, average temperature, and relative humidity. Furthermore, the variance inflation factor scores for both average temperature and relative humidity were over 4. Consequently, only average precipitation was adjusted for among the meteorological factors. In model 1, no covariates were adjusted. In model 2; we adjusted for grade, sex, region of school, family structure, parents’ education, family income, physical activity, smoking, and alcohol use. In model 3, we further adjusted for average precipitation. Similarly, we performed subgroup analyses by grade, sex, and physical activity. Group comparisons were evaluated using interaction term P values. Moreover, we assessed non-linear associations between single PM exposures and aggression by employing restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. The generalized weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression model was employed to assess the relative contributions of three non-overlapping PM size fractions—PM1, PM1–2.5, and PM2.5−10—to aggression [30]. By analyzing these distinct, non-overlapping size categories, we enhanced the interpretability of the individual effects attributable to each PM fraction below PM10. Each size fraction was entered as a separate variable in the WQS model to evaluate its specific relative weight and association with aggression. Furthermore, we also performed subgroup analyses. The estimated correlations were reported as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each 10 µg/m3 increase for each category of PM.

We conducted a range of sensitivity analyses, which included (1) using average temperature and relative humidity instead of precipitation as confounding variables in the main analysis; (2) utilizing 6-month PM levels instead of the 12-month averages levels employed in the main analysis; and (3) repeating the analysis with the revised version of BWAQ. Detailed descriptions of the revised version of aggression measurements are outlined in the eMethods in Supplement materials.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2), with all statistical tests being two-tailed and significance defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of study population

In the final analyses, the sample comprised 19,852 adolescents, with 9886 males (49.80%) and 9996 females (50.20%). Among all adolescents, 10,804 (54.42%) were enrolled in junior high school, while 9048 (45.58%) were enrolled in senior high school. As for physical activity, 3576 students (17.30%) exercised at least 1 h per day, and 16,276 students (82.70%) took exercise less than 1 h per day. A small proportion of students reported smoking (694 [3.50%]) or alcohol use (1627 [8.20%]). The average annual precipitation is 4.27 mm. Additional characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Correlation between PM exposure and aggression

Figure 2 illustrates the exposure-response relationships between varying-sized PM exposure and adolescent aggression. Significant nonlinear associations were found between PM1, PM1 − 2.5, PM2.5, and PM2.5−10 exposure and aggression. In addition, a significant linear association of PM10 exposure with aggression was observed. After full covariates adjustment, we observed positive associations between PM exposure and aggression, with a 10 µg/m3 increase in PM1 (OR [95%CI]: 1.11 [1.04–1.19], PM1–2.5 (OR [95%CI]: 1.21 [1.09–1.35], and PM2.5 (OR [95%CI]: 1.07 [1.03–1.12] (Table 2). However, no association was found between either PM2.5−10 or PM10 and aggression. The WQS regression showed a significantly positive association between exposure to different sizes of PM below PM10 and aggression (OR [95% CI]: 1.10 [1.06–1.15]; see Fig. 1B). Among the PM fractions, the contribution of PM1 was the most significant, accounting for 97.20% of the mixture index, suggesting its primary role in the observed association with aggression. Additionally, the negative weights were not statistically significant (P = 0.117).

Subgroup analysis for PM exposure and aggression

In the interaction analyses, we found stronger associations between varying-sized PM and aggression among males and senior high school students (P for interaction < 0.01). Although the associations between PM1, PM1 − 2.5, and PM2.5 exposure and aggression were stronger among physically active adolescents, no effect modification was identified for physical activity (P for interaction > 0.05). Detailed OR and 95% CI are given in Table S2.

The WQS regression model demonstrated that PM1 and PM1 − 2.5 were the main contributors to the risk of aggression (Fig. 3). Specifically, Stronger associations were identified for senior high school students (OR [95%CI]: 1.16 [1.10–1.23) than junior high school (OR [95%CI]: 1.06 [1.00–1.12]), and males (OR: 1.12 [1.05–1.19]) than females (OR [95%CI]: 1.02 [0.96–1.08]). Moreover, the associations between PM exposure and aggression differed numerically between physically active (OR [95% CI]: 1.10 [0.99–1.21]) and inactive adolescents (OR [95% CI]: 1.06 [1.01–1.11]), though only the latter reached statistical significance.

Sensitivity analyses

In the sensitivity analysis, using average temperature and relative humidity instead of precipitation as confounding variables in the main analysis yielded results that exhibited the same trends as those of the primary analysis, except for the association between PM2.5−10 and aggression (Tables S3-S4 and Figure S3). Sensitivity analyses in logistic regression models and WQS regression models using changed exposure time (6 months) and measurement of aggression did not materially change the results (Tables S5-S6 and Figures S4 A-C). In the regression models, the trends of the association between exposure to PM with different aerodynamic diameters and aggression were consistent with the main model analysis (Tables S5-S6). Moreover, the results from the WQS regression models indicated a positive association between particulate matter below PM10 exposure and aggression (Figures S4 B-C), driven primarily by PM1.

Discussion

In this large school-based study, we found that the association between PM and aggression were primarily driven by PM1 and PM1 − 2.5, with evidence of a dose-response pattern. Males and senior high school students showed stronger associations with PM exposure and aggression. Overall, our results indicated that PM exposure was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of exhibiting aggression, and that PM with smaller aerodynamic diameters may show stronger associations than PM with larger aerodynamic diameters.

As far as we know, no prior research has explored the link between exposure to PM mixtures and aggression, but numerous studies have investigated the relationship between PM2.5 exposure and aggression among adolescents, with mixed results [14–16]. Our findings are consistent with epidemiological evidence conducted in London and China [14, 16]. Specifically, a cohort study from London demonstrated that exposure to PM2.5 exposure was linked to reduced conduct problems, including aggression, during adolescence [16]. Furthermore, research conducted in rural schools in China revealed that PM2.5 serves as a mediator in the link between surrounding greenness at schools and aggression [14]. However, another recent study conducted in America found no association between PM2.5 and aggression [15]. The inconsistency in results may be due to several factors, such as differences in study design, population demographics, aggression assessment, and the levels of PM2.5 assessment methods. Interestingly, our analysis, employing average temperature and relative humidity instead of average precipitation as confounding variables, found a positive correlation between PM2.5−10 exposure and aggression. This unexpected finding may be explained by the physical properties of larger PM Particles larger than PM2.5 primarily deposit in the respiratory tract, which may stimulate the respiratory system [31]. Changes in respiratory function could increase individuals’ awareness of personal protection, potentially leading to reduced exposure to subsequent air pollution, particularly smaller particles like PM1 and PM2.5, which can more easily enter the bloodstream and even the brain [18, 20]. Therefore, this may represent one plausible explanation for the observed protective trend of PM2.5−10 against aggression.

We found that the relationship between PM and aggression among adolescents was mainly due to PM1 and PM1 − 2.5. Not surprisingly, PM1 and PM1 − 2.5 are components that contribute to the composition of PM2.5. Prior research has estimated that PM1 contributes to over 80% of ambient PM2.5 mass in China [32]. Our WQS analysis identified PM1 as the dominant contributor in the context of PM exposure. However, the stronger associations were observed for PM1 − 2.5 when analyzed as a single pollutant. Both PM₁ and PM1 − 2.5 due to their small aerodynamic diameters, are more likely to accumulate, adsorb, or condense on cell surfaces, thereby enhancing their toxicity [33]. While PM1 can penetrate deeply into the alveoli, PM1 − 2.5 shows higher deposition fractions in the head and proximal airways, resulting in greater effective doses in sensitive respiratory regions [34–36]. Additionally, PM1 − 2.5 typically contains higher concentrations of harmful metals and organic compounds, which are associated with oxidative stress and neuroinflammation [37, 38]. These characteristics may help explain why PM1 − 2.5 could exert a more pronounced impact on aggression compared to larger PM fractions. However, the potential effects of PM1 − 2.5 may partly be masked by its correlation with PM1. This complexity highlights the intricate toxicity profiles of PM and underscores the need for further research on size-resolved PM components to better understand their specific impacts on health.

Our findings for the association between PM and aggression were modified by grade and sex. Specifically, behavioral factors might play a role in the differential health effects of PM between females and males. For instance, males tend to spend more time engaging in outdoor sports compared to females, thereby potentially leading to higher exposure to PM [29]. While existing research has demonstrated the health advantages of physical activity, some experts posit that air pollution may offset or diminish these benefits [39]. Therefore, evidence regarding the aggression induced by the combination of PM exposure and physical activity remains inconclusive. Regarding other factors, we found limited evidence supporting grade as a modifier. We utilized grade level instead of age for subgroup analysis, as grades may be a superior indicator of students’ psychological patterns. We discovered that PM was linked with a higher risk of aggression among senior high school students compared to junior high school students. The grade-based disparities in this association may indicate the cumulative effects of PM on aggression [40].

The mechanisms through which PM may influence aggression are not yet fully understood. However, several possible pathways may explain the relationship between PM and aggression. Firstly, experimental evidence has suggested that PM may augment aggression through a pathway involving increased anxiety [41]. Secondly, PM may interfere with DNA methylation and mRNA expression, thereby leading to aggression [42, 43]. Thirdly, PM such as PM2.5 may induce hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA)-axis action, which may contribute to the onset of aggression [44, 45]. Lastly, airborne PM may lead to aggression through influenced neuroinflammatory responses, oxidative stress, elevated stress hormone levels, and alterations in brain function [46]. Given the potential complexity and multiplicity of pathways involved, additional studies are needed to clarify the exact mechanisms through which PM may induce aggression.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first national school-based study that investigates the association of PM with different aerodynamic diameters and aggression among adolescents. Additionally, we took into account numerous potential confounders in the adjusted models, which enhanced the reliability of the results. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were employed to demonstrate the reliability of our results. Inevitably, our study has some limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish a temporal relationship between PM exposure and aggression. Secondly, the assessment of PM exposure was school-based, which may lead to potential misclassification of exposure. However, it should be noted that a considerable portion of students’ daily lives is spent within the school environment. Additionally, under the school assignment policies in China, the majority of students reside close to their schools. Thirdly, although we focused on medium-term, spatially based PM exposure to better capture the sustained environmental impacts relevant to adolescents, this approach does not account for inter-day variability or potential acute effects, which may also be associated with aggression levels. Fourthly, aggression was assessed through self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to social desirability bias or recall bias, potentially leading to underreporting of aggression. Finally, while our samples were representative, this could potentially limit generalizability to other populations. Future studies should be conducted with diverse samples to validate our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we confirmed that higher ambient PM exposure correlates with aggression among adolescents. The PM mixture effects were mainly driven by PM1 and PM1 − 2.5. Additionally, stronger associations between PM exposure and aggression were observed among males and senior high school students. These findings highlight the important association between PM and aggression. Environmental protection policies and measures should be considered to control PM pollution, and PM1 could be routinely monitored given the stronger associations observed with PM of smaller aerodynamic diameters.
