Synbiotic supplementation leads to gut microbiome communities with functional capacity linked to better brain white matter development in preterm infants

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPreterm birth, a major cause of brain injury, is often linked to dysregulated gut microbiome development. This association underscores microbial metabolic function as a modifiable target to support neurodevelopment. In this secondary analysis of data derived from a randomized controlled trial (Trial Registration: ISRCTN96620855), we tested whether daily nutritional supplementation with Bifidobacterium breve M-16V, short- and long-chain oligosaccharides, and L-glutamine could steer the gut microbiome of very and extremely preterm infants toward communities that support brain maturation. The gut microbiome was profiled with longitudinal shotgun metagenomics at nine time points during the intervention, which started at 48–72 h after birth and continued until 36 weeks postmenstrual age. Additionally, MRI scans were conducted when infants reached term-equivalent age to evaluate brain maturation. Supplementation promoted the early establishment of Bifidobacterium-rich communities in the test group, with enhanced capacity for amino acid biosynthesis and pyruvate fermentation towards acetate and lactate production. Integration of microbiome data with brain developmental markers post hoc showed that the same functions were markedly reduced in infants with delayed white-matter myelination. By integrating microbiome functional capacity profiling and evaluation of brain maturation via MRI, this study demonstrated that early microbial modulation could influence brain development, positioning the preterm gut microbiome as a clinically actionable target.

Introduction

It is estimated that 11% of all births globally are preterm, with two-thirds occurring without an evident risk factor. 1 Despite advances in perinatal and postnatal care, preterm birth remains strongly associated with long-term neurodevelopmental disorders that can translate into behavioral and cognitive difficulties later in life, 2 , 3 as it disrupts a critical in utero developmental period marked by fundamental brain maturation processes. 4-7

For preterm infants, the fundamental brain maturation processes occurring in early life coincide with the assembly of their gut microbiome. Emerging gut microbiome imbalances may disrupt the developmental trajectories of their highly sensitive neural circuits 8-10 and even trigger the onset of severe conditions linked to neurodevelopmental disorders, such as neonatal sepsis and necrotizing enterocolitis. 11-14 Conversely, the timely establishment of a beneficial gut microbiome may support brain maturation through improved metabolic output and gut–brain signaling pathways. 15

Because preterm infants miss key periods of in-utero growth, targeted early nutrition becomes especially important – it not only supports brain development but also helps them “catch up” to the physiological and microbial trajectories typically seen in healthy term infants. In full-term, vaginally-delivered infants, early gut colonization typically originates from maternal microbial communities enriched with beneficial Bifidobacterium spp. 16 In preterm infants, however, the initial gut microbiome communities are primarily associated with gestational age, regardless of delivery mode. 17-21

More specifically, during the first days of life, aerobes such as Staphylococci predominate in the immature preterm infant gut, with Enterobacteriaceae becoming more abundant at later postmenstrual ages. 19 , 20 With gut maturation, decreasing proportions of Bacilli may be accompanied by increasing proportions of strict anaerobes such as Clostridia. 21 The establishment of a Bifidobacterium-dominated community, which is characteristic of healthy neurodevelopment, is significantly delayed, possibly due to high pre-existing levels of Enterobacteriaceae. 21-25

In the postnatal period, breastfed, full-term infants typically receive a continuous supply of maternal microbes and human milk-derived prebiotics that promote the early enhancement of Bifidobacterium spp. 26-28 In contrast, preterm infants often exhibit impaired feed digestion due to immature gastrointestinal function, reduced enzyme activity, and delayed motility. 29 , 30 These limitations can hinder their ability to fully utilize human milk components – especially complex oligosaccharides that normally fuel Bifidobacterium growth – thereby slowing the establishment of beneficial microbial communities. Moreover, preterm infants are frequently exposed to clinical practices associated with neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), such as early exposure to antibiotics, which can further delay microbiome maturation, often because of prolonged carriage of multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae. 31 , 32

Optimizing nutrition that stimulates early gut microbial colonization in preterm infants is a promising therapeutic strategy for accelerating microbiome maturation and ultimately lowering neurodevelopmental risk by influencing gut–brain signaling and energy availability during critical windows of brain development. 33 Recently, it has been suggested that enhanced microbial metabolic capacity may support neuroprotection and improved developmental outcomes in preterm infants. 34 Also, observational and randomized studies have investigated the potential of probiotics to prevent inflammatory diseases that affect neurodevelopment, such as sepsis and necrotizing enterocolitis, with findings ranging from no observed benefit 35 to general risk reduction. 36

Differences in the reported effects of probiotics on preterm infant health likely reflect differences in the probiotic strains used, the simultaneous use or absence of prebiotics, varying sample sizes, and clinical practices or study designs. 37 Furthermore, no randomized trial to date has directly evaluated the effect of controlled enteral supplementation with pro- and prebiotics on gut colonization, microbiome function, and subsequent brain development in preterm infants.

The present study uses longitudinal metagenomic data from a cohort of extremely and very preterm infants derived from a randomized controlled trial (Trial Registration: ISRCTN96620855), 38 that tested the impact of daily enteral supplementation with probiotics, prebiotics, and L-glutamine on brain development assessed at term-equivalent age (TEA). The strain Bifidobacterium breve M-16V was selected for its established safety and prior use in NICUs for preterm and low-birth-weight infants, where it had demonstrated potential benefits for the gut microbiome development. 39 , 40 L-glutamine was included for its role in reducing neonatal infections and gastrointestinal dysfunction, 41 , 42 and a prebiotic mix of short-chain galactooligosaccharides and long-chain fructooligosaccharides (scGOS/lcFOS) for their prebiotic properties. 43 We hypothesized that the nutritional intervention would support the establishment of Bifidobacterium-rich gut communities and would accelerate functional maturation of the gut microbiome in preterm infants. Furthermore, we investigated whether shifts in the microbial metabolic capacity were linked to improved brain developmental markers assessed at TEA. The randomized intervention, implemented within a clearly defined time window, provided a unique opportunity to examine how targeted early nutritional modulation influences gut microbiome assembly during a critical developmental period. Randomization helps minimize potential confounding, while repeated longitudinal sampling enables detailed characterization of microbial trajectories and their potential relationship with brain development at term-equivalent age.

Materials and methods

Objectives

A double-blind, randomized, controlled, parallel-group and single-center intervention study was conducted to test the combined effect of the probiotic strain B. breve M-16V, a prebiotic mix of scGOS/lcFOS, and the amino acid L-glutamine on the postnatal gut microbiome and brain development of infants born between 24 and 30 weeks of gestation. 38 The present study explores secondary objectives of the trial as described in the study protocol, 38 with the main outcome presented here being the composition and temporal development of the gut microbiome in preterm infants assessed with shotgun metagenomics sequencing. Post hoc analyses examined associations of microbiome structure and function with brain injury markers assessed with MRI.

Ethical approval, study design, and participants

Ethical approval was obtained by the Medical Ethical Committee of the University Medical Center Utrecht (UMCU) (reference: 5-213/M NL 49902.041.14). The study was performed in accordance with the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki. Details on trial registration are available online: https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN96620855.

The trial began at the level III NICU of the UMCU and continued in seven Dutch regional hospitals for infants transferred before completing 36 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA). Custodial parents of 338 eligible infants were approached shortly after admission to the obstetric ward or NICU with signs of preterm labor (<30 weeks of gestation). Following written free and informed consent, infants who satisfied the inclusion criteria were randomized 1:1 to either the intervention or control group of the study with permuted block randomization stratified for sex (male and female), using the PLAN procedure of SAS v4.3. From October 2018 to May 2022, 99 infants were randomized to the intervention or control group of the trial. The assessors, parents, and clinical staff remained blinded at least until the last participant reached 24 months of corrected age. The flow of participants through enrollment, randomization, intervention allocation, follow-up, and inclusion in the analyses is demonstrated in Figure S1, and their demographic and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. The number of available samples per time point is summarized in Table S1, and the demographic and clinical characteristics of participants sampled at each time point are summarized in Table S2.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants.

The exclusion criteria included any proven or suspected metabolic disorder, genetic syndrome, congenital central nervous system malformation or infection, gastrointestinal malformation, use of gastric acid inhibitors, poor survival prognosis (at supervising clinician’s discretion), admission to another hospital, and participation in other postnatal intervention studies. Participation was discontinued in cases of health risk (informed at the supervising clinician’s discretion), consent withdrawal by the custodial parents, and suspected or proven inability to comply with the study protocol.

The intervention began within 48–72 h after birth and continued until 36 weeks PMA or discharge home, whichever came first. The intervention group received a daily enteral dose of B. breve M-16V via nasogastric tube, orogastric tube, or syringe. The starting daily dose was 3 × 109 or 1.5 × 109 colony-forming units (CFU), for birth weight higher or lower than 1000 g, respectively. This dosage was increased to 3 × 109 CFU when the daily enteral feeding intake reached 50 ml/kg. The control group received a daily dose of 0.6 or 0.3 g maltodextrin as a placebo, to match the weight of the test product. Additionally, the intervention group received a daily supplement to the regular milk feed with a starting dose of 0.05 g/kg L-glutamine and 0.1 g/kg scGOS/lcFOS (9:1), which increased to 0.3 g/kg and 0.6 g/kg, respectively, at full enteral feeding. The control group received an isocaloric placebo consisting of maltodextrin, casein, and whey protein hydrolysates at corresponding doses (0.5 g/kg/day initially and 3.0 g/kg/day at full enteral feeding). The placebo formulation was designed to match the carbon and nitrogen contents of the intervention supplement.

Microbiome profiling

The first stool samples from infants were collected within 72 h after birth (meconium) and 1, 3, and 6 weeks after birth. Up to five additional samples were collected until hospital discharge or TEA, and up to three more samples were collected at follow-up visits (Table S1). The samples were stored at −70 °C, and whole genomic DNA was extracted with a Maxwell® RSC, Wisconsin, U.S.A., Genomic DNA Kit according to the manufacturer's cell pellet protocol, following sample homogenization, including pellet washing, removal of organic material and lipids, proteinase K digestion, and chemical lysis, at the Laboratory of Microbiology at Wageningen University. The extracted DNA was stored immediately at −20°C. Quality control, library preparation, and metagenomic shotgun sequencing were performed by GenomeScan B.V., Leiden. After quality control, the samples were retained if DNA ≥ 25 μl, OD260/280 ~1.8–2.0, and OD260/230 ~2.0–2.2. Samples of sufficient quality were then included in library preparation and sequenced with an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE150). After quality filtering of the stool DNA, 473 metagenomes from 84 infants were obtained and included in all downstream microbiome analysis.

Brain MRI scans

The infants were scanned at TEA on a 3.0 Tesla MR system (Achieva, Philips Medical Systems, Best, the Netherlands) with a 32-channel SENSE head coil. Diffusion, T1-, and T2-weighted imaging were performed as previously described. 44 Mean fractional anisotropy (FA) was measured in a region of interest in the right posterior limb of the internal capsule (rPLIC) and in a tract segment of the right corticospinal tract (rCST), using whole-brain tractography (FA threshold > 0.1, curvature < 45°). Brain injury and maturation were assessed using Kidokoro scores by UMCU neonatologists. 45 The Kidokoro system includes brain injury and maturation markers for white matter (cystic lesions, focal signal abnormalities, delayed myelination, ventricular dilation, and corpus callosum thinning), gray matter (gyral maturation delay, enlargement of extra-axial spaces, signal abnormalities), and cerebellum (volume reduction and signal abnormalities). Data was entered in an electronic Case Report Form by trained staff in line with data privacy laws. Of the 84 infants with longitudinal microbiome data available, 76 had usable brain MRI scans and evaluated Kidokoro scores for brain development and injury.

Data analysis

Metagenomic sequence reads derived from stool sample DNA were quality filtered and trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.39 (parameters: ILLUMINACLIP:2:30:10, LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, MINLEN:36). 46 Host DNA was removed with Bowtie2 v2.5.4 47 using the publicly available Genome Reference Consortium h38 human genome assembly. B. breve M-16V strain was identified using the available genome (NCBI BioProject: PRJNA482528) and StrainPhlAn v4.1.0 48 with clade-specific marker genes generated with MetaPhlAn v4.1.0. 49 Taxonomic and functional profiling of the metagenomes were conducted with MetaPhlAn v4.1.0 49 and HUMAnN3 v3.9, respectively. 50

Relative abundances of microbial taxa were compared between the control and the intervention group within each sampling point with Wilcoxon rank sum tests with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment for false discovery rate control, and significance cutoff P < 0.05 in the native R stats package. 51 Microbiome beta diversity within each sampling point was visualized using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and tested with non-parametrical ANOSIM 52 to assess the influence of the intervention (intervention/control), sex (male/female), enteral nutrition type (>80% mother milk, >80% preterm formula or combination), delivery mode (vaginal delivery/caesarian section), antibiotics (administration at time or within 1 week prior to sampling/no administration) and gestational age (24–26, 26–28, and 28–30 weeks).

To identify taxonomic balances in each sampling point predictive of the intervention or the control group and also of brain developmental markers (Kidokoro scores 45 ), we used selbal, selecting models with probability AUC-ROC > 0.7. 53 We used indicspecies to measure the specificity of microbial taxa (indicVal) to the same sample groups, using 10,000 permutations to assess confidence and significance cutoff P < 0.05. 54

For exploring differences in microbial metabolic function and in specific metabolic pathways between sample groups, we performed differential abundance analysis using DESeq2 with exclusion of pathways with average abundance lower than 1000 counts per million and prevalence < 10% across all samples, default internal normalization, dispersion estimation, Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment and significance cutoff P < 0.05. 55 To test if FA measurements were associated to relative abundances of microbial taxa, we used Spearman rank correlations and significance cutoff P < 0.05 in stats. 51 Microbiome clustering into community types was done with Ward hierarchical unsupervised method 56 and checked using pvclust with 10,000 bootstrap replicates and acceptance threshold P < 0.05. 57 The independence of the community types to the intervention and to brain developmental markers was tested with chi-squared tests and independence residual-based models using vcd and default settings. 58

Results

Microbiome differences between the control and intervention groups

The relative abundance of B. breve M-16V was higher in the intervention group only at week 1, 3, and 6 after birth (Wilcoxon P = 1.76 × 10−10, for week 1, and P = 0.0002 for week 3 and 6) (Figure S2). The strain was detected (relative abundance > 0.5%) in 97.3%, 100%, and 93.94% of the infants of the intervention group, compared to 9.1%, 44.44%, and 39.39% of the control group infants at the same time points (Supplement 2). Total B. breve relative abundance, regardless of strain, was higher in the intervention group only at week 1, 3, and 6 after birth (Wilcoxon P = 1.1 × 10−8, P = 0.01, P = 0.033) (Figure S2). B. breve detection not attributed to the administered strain was detected in 9,1%, 11,11%, and 24,24% of the control group infants at the same time points, in comparison to 6,1% of the intervention infants only at week 6.

The overall microbial community (Figure S3) differed between groups at week 1, 3, and 6 (R = 0.4, P < 0.001; R = 0.10, P < 0.001, and R = 0.06, P = 0.003, respectively) (Figure 1A). No significant differences in the gut microbiome community structure were attributed to sex (male or female), nutrition (fed with > 80% mother milk, > 80% preterm formula or mix of the two), delivery mode (vaginal delivery or cesarian section) or gestational age (24–16, 26–28, or 28–30 weeks) at any time point; neither for all samples combined nor for samples of each intervention group separately. Antibiotic administration had a significant effect on the microbiome composition only at week 3 (R = 0.23, P = 0.030), with 9 out of 73 infants with available samples receiving antibiotics. At that time point, the relative abundance of Enterobacter was higher in subjects receiving antibiotics (Wilcoxon P = 0.002). The difference was significant for the control (Wilcoxon P = 0.008) but not for the intervention group (Wilcoxon P = 0.055) (Figure S4). Finally, no infant weight development difference was linked to the intervention (Figure S5).

Comparison between the control and the intervention group showed higher Bifidobacterium spp. relative abundance from weeks 1 through 6 in the intervention group (Wilcoxon P = 7.9 × 10−10, P = 0.006, and P = 0.042, for week 1, 3, and 6, respectively), accompanied by lower Staphylococcus spp. (P = 0.017) at week 1 and lower Enterococcus spp. abundance (P = 0.008) at week 3 (Figure 1B). For the same period, microbiome clustering grouped all the microbial metagenomes into six community types (CTs) (Figure S6): CT-1: Bifidobacterium, CT-2: Bifidobacterium-Klebsiella, CT-3: Enterobacter-Enterococcus-Escherichia, CT-4: Klebsiella, CT-5: Enterococcus, and CT-6: Staphylococcus.

Microbiome differences associated with brain development

No significant differences in the assessed brain injury markers were observed between the intervention and control groups (chi-squared analysis, P > 0.05). Two white matter injury markers were associated with differences in the gut microbial composition. The gut communities at week 1 differed between infants with (n = 11) and without (n = 52) white matter thinning of the corpus callosum at TEA (R = 0.20, P = 0.017), and the gut communities at week 3 differed between infants with (n = 20) and without (n = 45) white matter myelination delay at TEA (R = 0.25, P = 0.001). The other Kidokoro scores and FA values were not significantly associated with microbiome differences.

Community type transitions from the 1st to the 3rd week after birth were not independent of the occurrence of white matter thinning of the corpus callosum and white matter myelination delay at TEA (X2 = 1300.9, df = 48, P = 2.2 × 10−18). Transition to CT-3: Enterobacter-Enterococcus-Escherichia associated with white matter myelination delay and grouping under CT-6:Staphylococcus to thinning of the corpus callosum. Also, community type transitions linked to the intervention group were negatively associated with both types of white matter brain injury (Figure S6).

Association of bacterial species to the synbiotic supplementation and to brain development markers

The balance of B. breve to Staphylococcus epidermidis and Escherichia coli for week 1 (AUC-ROC = 0.95) and to S. epidermidis and Klebsiella michiganensis at week 3 (AUC-ROC = 0.79) distinguished the intervention group from the control group (Figure 2), with higher levels of B. breve characterizing the intervention group. Additionally, a higher Staphylococcus haemolyticus to B. breve ratio at week 1 was indicative of white matter thinning of the corpus callosum (AUC-ROC = 0.75), and a higher Enterobacter roggenkampii to B. breve ratio at week 3 was indicative of white matter myelination delay (AUC–ROC = 0.78) (Figure 2).

The box plots represent the distribution of the balance scores for the subject subgroups. The right part of each figure contains the ROC curve with an AUC-ROC value assessing the model’s performance and probability.

Indicator species analysis confirmed the specificity of B. breve to the intervention group for week 1 (IndicVal = 0.67, P = 0.001) and week 3 (IndicVal = 0.30, P = 0.015). It also confirmed the specificity of B. breve for infants not showing white matter injury at TEA for week 1 (IndicVal = 0.37, P = 0.035), as well as for week 3 (IndicVal = 0.42, P = 0.002). Furthermore, it confirmed the association of white matter thinning of the corpus callosum with S. haemolyticus (IndicVal = 0.50, P = 0.001) and of myelination delay with E. roggenkampii (IndicVal = 0.24, P = 0.007) (Table S3) for the same sampling points.

Differences in the microbial functional capacity as a result of the intervention

From the 1st to the 3rd week after birth, several metabolic pathways related to amino acid biosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, and central carbon metabolism and fermentation were more abundant in the intervention group. Other enhanced pathways were related to nucleotide biosynthesis, transcription, cell wall biosynthesis, and housekeeping metabolism (Figure 3). No differences in metabolic pathway abundances were observed in other time points.

Differential abundance of metabolic pathways across conditions and time points. Bars represent the fold change in pathway abundance, and the colors indicate the type of comparison. Only pathways with significant differences are shown, with adjusted P-values shown as labels.

A comparison of the gut microbiome between infants with normal and delayed white matter myelination at TEA showed that white matter myelination delay was accompanied by reduced capacity for pyruvate fermentation in 3-week-old infants. Pathway II for pyruvate fermentation to acetate and lactate (PWY-5100) and pathway I for pyruvate fermentation to acetate and (S)-lactate (P41-PWY) (log2 change: 1.34; P = 4.93 × 10−4 and log2 change: 1.47; P = 0.003, respectively) showed the highest difference between the groups (Figure 3). Both pathways were enhanced in the intervention group: PWY-5100 at week 1 and 3, and P41-PWY at week 1 (Figure 3).

Discussion

The gut microbiome of the preterm infants in the present study progressed through distinct community types during the early postnatal period, with Bifidobacterium-dominated communities representing a more mature stage that followed initial colonization by Staphylococcus-, Enterococcus-, and Enterobacteriaceae-rich communities, in agreement with studies following the preterm gut microbiome development. 18-21 , 23 Supplementation with a mixture containing B. breve M-16V, scGOS/lcFOS, and L-glutamine facilitated the early establishment of the administered probiotic strain and accelerated gut microbiome maturation, similarly to studies investigating targeted probiotic use in preterm infants. 59-62 HMOs were used to promote the colonization and growth of the probiotic strain 43 and glutamine to support intestinal barrier integrity. 41 , 42 Colonization was robust but transient, with significantly higher abundance in the intervention group during the supplementation period until 6 weeks after birth, despite the cross-colonization of the administered strain in a proportion of control infants. The supplementation resulted in higher Bifidobacterium levels accompanied by lower Staphylococcus and Enterococcus levels in the first 3 weeks of life.

Unlike what is commonly accepted for full-term infants, 63 sex and delivery mode did not significantly influence the gut microbiome composition. Preterm infants have been associated with sparse, low-diversity initial gut microbiomes regardless of delivery mode, possibly reflecting clinical practices and physiological intestinal immaturity. 18 , 20 Nutrition type also showed no effect on the microbiome structure, with infants fed with mother milk not showing significantly different microbiome structure compared to the infants fed predominantly with preterm formula. Although weeks of gestation are often linked to differences concerning the preterm gut microbiome, 19 we also found no influence of gestational age, possibly because our cohort included only very and extremely preterm infants. In contrast to studies with a wider range of gestational ages, which often capture differences between moderate, very, and extremely preterm infants, 17 , 19 our cohort included infants with a gestational age of 24–30 weeks, who were hospitalized in the same NICU environment. Similar to other trials with probiotics, intervention was the primary factor influencing the preterm microbiome. 60 , 61

Furthermore, antibiotic administration had a significant effect on the microbiome composition, favoring Enterobacter only at week 3 and only within the control group. Studies have shown that antibiotic treatment likely promotes the growth of opportunistic microbes already resident in the infant gut and that these same microbes can then go on to have adverse health effects. 64 Such opportunists can also be the early colonizers we detected in our study, such as Enterobacteriaceae, enterococci, and staphylococci. 65 The low proportion of infants receiving antibiotics in the present study does not allow us to draw robust conclusions on the effect of antibiotics on the preterm gut microbiome development; however, the fact that antibiotics had an effect only in the control group warrants further investigation to test whether probiotics can prevent opportunistic pathogens from exploiting available niches in the infant gut after antibiotic administration.

Two phenotypes of brain injury diagnosed at TEA, white matter thinning of the corpus callosum and white matter myelination delay, were associated with early-life microbiome differences. White matter injury, which is the most common brain injury pattern in extremely preterm infants, 66 is indicative of disrupted maturation and regional brain tissue volume reduction. 67 Infants whose gut microbial communities were dominated by Staphylococcus, Enterobacter, and Enterococcus exhibited a higher occurrence of those brain injury phenotypes. This aligns with a prior study showing that 3-week-old preterm infants with Enterococcus- and Staphylococcus-dominated gut microbiome communities achieved lower developmental scores later in life. 24 At the same time, we found that infants of the intervention group, as well as infants without signs of such encephalopathy, were characterized by higher B. breve abundances. This observation is consistent with accumulating evidence linking low early-life Bifidobacterium levels to adverse neurological outcomes in preterm infants. 18 , 68 Taken together, these findings reinforce the concept that the first weeks after preterm birth are a critical window during which microbial colonization and key neurodevelopmental processes – such as white matter maturation, occur in parallel. 8 Nutrition that can stimulate early colonization by beneficial microbes such as B. breve may therefore modulate gut–brain signaling, potentially protecting neurodevelopmental trajectories in the vulnerable preterm population.

The intestinal microbiome is increasingly recognized as a modifiable factor influencing the risk of encephalopathy in preterm newborns through the production of metabolites that participate in gut–brain axis signaling. 15 , 34 In the present study, the metabolic shifts accompanying the intervention point to enhanced functional capacity in pathways linked to amino acid production, core carbon metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism – processes that may have important implications for preterm infants by supporting nutrient provision, microbial energy metabolism, and metabolite-mediated gut–brain signaling. On the other hand, other affected pathways primarily related to microbial replication may reflect a microbiome state associated with favorable developmental conditions, rather than a direct effect on the host.

Notably, the metabolic pathways that differed the most between infants with and without white matter myelination delay at TEA were involved in pyruvate fermentation. These energy-producing metabolic pathways involved in the production of acetate and lactate were enriched in both the intervention group and in infants who did not exhibit white matter myelination delay. Pyruvate fermentation plays a central role in anaerobic energy metabolism by regenerating NAD⁺, supporting sustained glycolysis in low-oxygen environments. This is particularly relevant for preterm infants, whose tissues often face metabolic stress due to immature vascularization and a fluctuating oxygen supply. Metabolites derived from pyruvate fermentation, such as acetate, propanoate, and lactate, have been shown to downregulate pro-inflammatory responses in intestinal epithelial and myeloid cells, 69 suggesting a potential mechanism whereby gut microbial metabolism could mitigate systemic inflammation. Acetate and propanoate have also been attributed the potential to affect brain structure and function, 70 through (neuro)immune modulation. 71 Oral administration of acetate in germ-free mice accelerated the maturation of microglia, 72 while lactate has been shown to mediate brain neurogenesis and neuroprotection in mice. 73 , 74 Thus, the enrichment of pyruvate fermentation pathways in both the intervention group and infants without brain injury highlights potential microbiome-mediated mechanisms of neuroprotection and warrants further investigation. 75

The randomized controlled design of this study provided a clearly defined intervention window and minimized confounding related to the nutritional supplementation, facilitating the evaluation of its associations with preterm gut microbiome composition and functional capacity. Also, the intervention utilized a clinically relevant probiotic strain, previously shown to be safe for neonatal populations. Repeated sampling enabled detailed microbiome profiling, and integration of microbiome data with brain development markers provided a multidimensional view of potential gut–brain interactions. Finally, the incorporation of functional metagenomics offered insights into how the microbiome may influence brain development.

However, certain limitations should be noted. This study represents a secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial; 38 consequently, the original sample size calculation was based on the primary endpoint of the parent trial. Attrition between enrolment and data collection lowered the sample size of the initial cohort. In addition, this was a single-centre study. Consequently, our findings may partially reflect local clinical practices and patient characteristics, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other neonatal intensive care units. Also, the cross-colonization of the probiotic strain in the control group, which is often reported in probiotic trials conducted in neonatal units, 34 , 59 , 76 may have masked the extent of the intervention effect. Furthermore, testing of additional probiotic strains within the Bifidobacterium genus, especially of strains that have exhibited beneficial impact on infant health, 60 , 77-79 will provide more insight into whether the observed effects are strain-specific or represent a broader genus-level phenomenon. Finally, future studies integrating additional host and microbial functional data, such as metabolomic profiling of microbial metabolites and host immune markers, may provide greater mechanistic insight into the causal pathways linking the gut microbiome and neurodevelopment.

In conclusion, supplementation with a nutritional product containing B. breve M-16V, scGOS/lcFOS, and L-glutamine accelerated the establishment of gut microbial communities that could be associated with improved health outcomes. These gut communities showed elevated metabolic capacity centered around amino-acid synthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, and core carbon metabolism. Improved white matter development in extremely and very premature infants was associated with pyruvate fermentation pathways that were enhanced in the gut microbiome of the intervention group.
