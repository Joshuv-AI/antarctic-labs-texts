BDFlower: Growth stage flower image dataset for precision agriculture and floriculture
Authors: Aritra Das, Mohammad Rifat Ahmmad Rashid, Md Rakibul Hasan, Karib Shams, Raihan Ul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a comprehensive BDFlower growth stage dataset designed to support research in precision agriculture and floriculture. The dataset encompasses eight common flower species found in Bangladesh: Bush Allamanda, Red Hibiscus, Yellow Bell, Pinwheel Flower, Pink Periwinkle, White Madagascar Periwinkle, Marvel of Peru, and White Hibiscus. Each species is represented across three growth stages—Early, Mid, and Full—resulting in 24 distinct classes. A total of 23,334 colour images are included, comprising 3889 original photographs and 19,445 augmented samples generated with five augmentation techniques. Bush Allamanda contains 499 images, Red Hibiscus contains 489 images, Yellow Bell contains 483 images, Pinwheel Flower contains 497 images, Pink Periwinkle contains 452 images, White Madagascar Periwinkle contains 472 images, Marvel of Peru contains 468 images and White Hibiscus contains 529 images. Each image was collected using smartphone camera at three-time intervals per day, spaced eight hours apart, to capture natural variations in lighting and appearance. The dataset is further organized into training, validation, and testing splits, enabling direct application to machine learning workflows. This is a publicly available dataset specifically curated for flower growth stage classification. In addition to dataset collection, we also conducted a simple experiment using a CNN model to evaluate its performance on this dataset. It is intended to facilitate the development of robust computer vision models that can monitor flower development, with potential applications in automated plant phenotyping, crop monitoring, and digital floriculture systems.Keywords: BDFlower, Flower growth stage, Data augmentation, Image dataset, Floriculture

Background

The global floriculture industry is expanding rapidly across major producing countries [1], with Bangladesh also playing a growing role [2]. Understanding the visual and morphological evolution of flowers is crucial for botanical and computational research. Since flower development depends on environmental, lighting, and genetic factors, this dataset was created to capture key visual stages of growth for use in analytical and machine learning models.

Bush Allamanda [10]: Bush Allamanda (Allamanda schottii) is a robust evergreen shrub prized for its bright green foliage and clusters of golden, trumpet-shaped blossoms that may appear intermittently throughout the year under warm conditions. It is one of the easier allamanda varieties to grow, thriving in full to partial sun, and tolerates moderate salinity—making it suitable for tropical garden settings.

Red Hibiscus [11]: Hibiscus rosa-sinensis ‘Red Dragon’ is an upright, well-branched evergreen shrub famed for its spectacular scarlet-red double blooms. It flowers profusely throughout summer, offering a vivid contrast to its dark green, oval foliage. The plant typically attains a height of 6–8 ft (180–240 cm) with a spread of 3–4 ft (90–120 cm) [11]. It thrives in full sun to part shade, preferring neutral to slightly acidic, moist but well-drained soils. In cultivation, regular watering during the growing season, occasional pruning to encourage branching, and shielding from wind are recommended practices.

Yellow Bell [12]: Allamanda, often called the yellow bell or golden trumpet, is a tropical shrub or vine bearing vivid yellow, trumpet-shaped blooms. The flowers emerge in clusters of two or three from the stem tips, with overlapping petals forming a striking trumpet appearance. Its foliage is evergreen, featuring whorled leaf blades measuring about 4 to 8 inches long, and the plant produces green seed pods that may blend visually with the leaves.

Pinwheel Flower [13]: The young plant will develop smooth stems and begin producing glossy, dark green, oval leaves (about 15 cm × 5 cm) with a somewhat wavy margin [13]. As it matures, branching increases and more foliage fills out. Flower buds form and waxy white, pinwheel-shaped blooms (twisted petals) begin appearing in clusters. In its mature state, Pinwheel Flower may reach up to 0.5–5 m tall and 1–1.8 m in crown width. It becomes free-flowering year-round under favourable light and soil conditions, producing fragrant, terminal clusters of flowers.

Pink Periwinkle [14]: The Pink Periwinkle (Catharanthus roseus) is an evergreen subshrub that typically grows up to about 1 m tall. Its leaves are glossy green, smooth, and oval to oblong in shape, arranged neatly in opposite pairs along the stem. The plant is admired for its attractive flowers, which range in color from pure white with a yellow or red centre to deep pink with a darker red eye. Each flower has five petal-like lobes, a slender basal tube, and measures around 2–5 cm across.

White Madagascar Periwinkle [14]: The White Madagascar Periwinkle (Catharanthus roseus ‘Titan F1 White’) begins its life as a small, tender plant with delicate stems and a few glossies, ovate leaves. As it grows, it develops a more compact and well-branched structure, producing an increasing number of leaves and the first flower buds in the upper leaf axils. In full maturity, the plant reaches about 38 cm in height. Basically, pure white flowers featuring pale yellow centres.

Marvel of Peru [15]: Mirabilis jalapa, commonly known as the Marvel of Peru or Four O’clock plant, begins its growth as a small, tender seedling with delicate stems and a few glossy, ovate leaves. As it matures, it develops a more compact and well-branched structure, producing an increasing number of leaves and the first flower buds in the upper leaf axils. In full maturity, the plant reaches about 1 m in height.

White Hibiscus [16]: The White Hibiscus (Hibiscus rosa-sinensis), specifically the 'Dainty White' cultivar, begins its growth as a small, tender plant with delicate stems and a few glossies, ovate leaves. As it matures, it develops a more compact and well-branched structure, producing an increasing number of leaves and the first flower buds in the upper leaf axils. In full maturity, the plant reaches about 1–3 m in height.

The dataset is organized in a hierarchical structure. There are eight main folders named according to the flower classes. Within each flower folder, three subfolders correspond to the growth stages: Early Stage, Mid Stage, and Full Stage. Each stage folder further contains subfolders named flower_1, flower_2, etc. These subfolders represent individual flowers tracked from their early development until full bloom, with images captured at different times of the day (morning, evening, and night) over consecutive days. The naming convention reflects this sequence (e.g., Morning_Flower1_Day1.jpg, Evening_Flower1_Day1.jpg, Night_Flower1_Day1.jpg), ensuring consistency across the dataset. In addition to the raw dataset, an Augmentation folder follows the same hierarchical structure. The only difference lies in the naming convention of the files. For example, for the raw image Morning_Flower1_Day1.jpg, the augmented versions are stored as Morning_Flower1_Day1_Aug1.jpg, Morning_Flower1_Day1_Aug2.jpg, Morning_Flower1_Day1_Aug3.jpg, Morning_Flower1_Day1_Aug4.jpg, and Morning_Flower1_Day1_Aug5.jpg. This parallel organization ensures easy traceability between raw and augmented images. Fig. 1 illustrates the folder structure.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Our work is structured into four main steps, beginning with data collection and ending with model experimentation. The primary goal of this work is to collect and organize flower images across different growth stages—Early, Mid, and Full Stage—in a systematic and usable format. The experiment was conducted only to demonstrate the applicability of the dataset, while the dataset itself can directly support researchers in training and evaluating their models. Fig. 2 shows a flowchart of the work.

Data collection pipeline

The data collection pipeline of this work was designed to systematically capture flowers across three distinct growth stages—Early, Mid, and Full—to support developmental stage recognition rather than species identification. Flowers were selected at the onset of their growth cycle, and individual buds were tracked continuously until full bloom to ensure authentic temporal progression. Images were captured three times per day (morning, evening, and night) at approximately eight-hour intervals. This schedule enabled consistent monitoring of morphological changes while capturing natural variations in illumination and appearance. For example, when a bud was first observed at the Early Stage, it was photographed at regular intervals until it transitioned to the Mid Stage (visible expansion and partial opening) and subsequently to the Full Stage (complete bloom). This structured approach ensures that the dataset reflects true growth dynamics rather than isolated snapshots. To enhance practical usability and reproducibility, all images were acquired using widely available smartphone cameras— Realme XT (64 MP, f/1.8, wide, 1/1.72″, 0.8 µm, PDAF) and OnePlus Nord CE5 (50 MP, f/1.8, 26 mm wide, 1/1.95″, 0.8 µm, PDAF, OIS). Smartphone-based acquisition was intentionally adopted to reflect real-world agricultural scenarios, where farmers and field practitioners rely on mobile devices rather than specialized imaging equipment. This choice improves the dataset’s applicability for real-world deployment, facilitates reproducibility by other researchers using accessible devices, and ensures that trained models remain robust under practical field conditions.

Data preprocessing and augmentation

After collection, all raw images were resized to a standard dimension of 640 × 640 pixels to maintain uniformity across the dataset. This resizing step ensures consistency during training and allows the model to process images efficiently. However, no random cropping was performed.

To further enrich the dataset and improve model generalization, five augmentation techniques were applied. These augmentations not only increase the dataset size but also simulate real-world variations such as changes in perspective, lighting, and orientation. The applied techniques are summarized below:

•Rotation (90°) [18]: Each image was rotated by 90° either clockwise or counterclockwise. This increases viewpoint diversity and helps the model become more robust to orientation differences.

•Flip [18]: Images were flipped horizontally or vertically, producing mirror versions of the originals. This enhances the dataset with natural variations that may occur during flower growth observations.

Flip [18]: Images were flipped horizontally or vertically, producing mirror versions of the originals. This enhances the dataset with natural variations that may occur during flower growth observations.

•Hue Adjustment (−5°) [18]: A slight hue shift of −5° was applied to simulate changes in color caused by varying illumination conditions. This improves robustness against lighting variations commonly encountered in natural environments.

Hue Adjustment (−5°) [18]: A slight hue shift of −5° was applied to simulate changes in color caused by varying illumination conditions. This improves robustness against lighting variations commonly encountered in natural environments.

•Zoom (30%) [18]: Images were zoomed in by 30%, creating a slightly closer focus on the flower. This technique enables the model to learn finer details and improves performance in scenarios where flowers appear at different scales.

•Shift (20%) [18]: Images were shifted horizontally or vertically by 20%. The blank regions created by shifting were filled using the nearest pixel values (border replication), preserving visual integrity while simulating positional variations.

Final dataset

Finally, the dataset was prepared for experimentation. In total, the dataset consists of 3889 original images and 19,445 augmented images, resulting in a combined set of 23,334 images. This large and diverse dataset provides a strong foundation for training and evaluating deep learning models across multiple flower species and growth stages. The expanded dataset ensures robustness to real-world variations and enables comprehensive experimentation with different architectures.

Model training

Now, the dataset is fully prepared for experimentation. For model training and evaluation, we divided the dataset into three subsets: 80% for training, 10% for testing, and 10% for validation. This ensured a balanced distribution of data across different phases of model development.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed dataset, a comparative analysis was conducted using three deep learning models: ResNet18, Vision Transformer (ViT-S/16), and EfficientNetV2. All experiments were conducted using dual NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs (T4×2) with 16 GB memory each. Models were implemented using Python and PyTorch. The model was trained for 20 epochs with early stopping criteria to prevent overfitting. These models were selected to represent both conventional convolutional neural networks and more recent, computationally efficient architectures. All models were trained under identical experimental settings to ensure a fair comparison. The results indicate that while ResNet18 serves as a reliable baseline, ViT-S/16 and EfficientNetV2 achieve significantly higher classification performance, thereby demonstrating the robustness and scalability of the dataset across different model architectures.
