Polarization contrast neutron imaging enables operando visualization of iron plating in redox flow batteries
Authors: Inmaculada Gimenez-Garcia, Marina Tabuyo-Martinez, Pierre Boillat, Rémy Richard Jacquemond, Adrian Mularczyk, Antoni Forner-Cuenca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVisualizing multiphase (solid–liquid–gas) electrochemical transformations during operation is essential to advancing sustainable energy storage technologies. All-iron redox flow batteries represent a promising candidate due to their low-cost and earth-abundant materials; however, they suffer from non-uniform plating rates, cycling instability, and parasitic hydrogen evolution. Here, we introduce an operando neutron imaging methodology – combining polarized and transmission modes – to simultaneously track iron plating/stripping and hydrogen evolution. We uncover preferential iron deposition near the membrane indicating a non-uniform current density distribution. We find that pH plays a major role in reaction selectivity and cycling performance, where less acidic pH results in inaccessible iron deposits. Our experiments with different flow field geometries show that both reaction selectivity and spatial distribution are strongly influenced by electrolyte flow patterns. This multimodal imaging approach provides critical insights into reactive transport and multiphase behavior in flow batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Chemical engineering, Imaging techniques, Batteries

Introduction

The transition to a renewable energy economy demands cost-effective, scalable energy storage technologies that can be deployed globally. All-iron redox flow batteries (AIRFBs) have emerged as a promising candidate, combining the modularity of redox flow battery (RFB) systems with the advantages of iron chemistry: abundance, low-cost, environmental benignity, and inherent safety1,2. However, their performance remains constrained by the kinetically sluggish iron plating/striping reactions, competition with the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), and mass transport losses in the electrochemical reactor3,4. These challenges also arise in other electrochemical systems, such as metal deposition in lithium and zinc metal batteries5,6, parasitic gas evolution in vanadium RFBs7, and bubble management in water electrolyzers8. Improving these systems requires a deeper understanding of multiphase reactive phenomena. In AIRFBs, controlling iron deposition within porous electrodes can enhance capacity, prolong cycle life, and prevent pore blockage. Yet, linking cell-level metrics (e.g., polarization curves, efficiency) with local material transformations in the reactor remains a fundamental challenge.

Addressing this challenge necessitates operando, multiscale characterization techniques capable of resolving both chemical and morphological changes across the reactor during operation. Iron plating dynamics are highly sensitive to electrolyte composition (e.g., pH, supporting ions), operating conditions (e.g., temperature, flow rate, current density), and reactor architecture (e.g., flow field geometry, porous electrode)9. Mechanistic insights have been provided by tools such as atomic force microscopy10, Mössbauer spectroscopy11, and infrared spectroscopy12, but are not suited to probe reactor-scale processes. Larger-scale techniques, i.e., in situ nuclear magnetic resonance13 and electron paramagnetic resonance14, enabled the characterization of reaction mechanisms and degradation pathways of redox molecules (e.g., anthraquinones), while operando fluorescence microscopy was used to visualize heterogeneous mass transport in all-liquid flow cells15,16. Although powerful, their applicability is often restricted to specific molecules. X-ray radiography provides operando, spatially resolved imaging of electrode wetting17, electrolyte flow18,19, and hydrogen evolution reaction20, but requires specially designed cells, often compromising realistic operating conditions21.

Neutron imaging has been instrumental in advancing electrochemical systems due to its sensitivity to light elements such as hydrogen, lithium or boron—common in energy materials—and its penetration through hardware materials (e.g., metals, carbon), enabling non-invasive imaging under realistic operating conditions. In polymer electrolyte fuel cells, it has been employed to elucidate water management challenges in porous media and flow channels.22–27 In water electrolyzers, it has provided insights into gas transport and bubble dynamics that impact cell efficiency28,29. In lithium-ion, neutron imaging has been used to study degradation mechanisms30,31, lithium transport32,33 and evolution of gaseous byproducts associated with electrolyte decomposition and cell failure34,35. In vanadium RFBs, it revealed correlations between gas evolution and electrode properties36. Furthermore, energy-selective neutron imaging has been recently used to map the concentration of hydrogen-containing organic redox molecules and boron-containing supporting ions during electrochemical operation, providing insights into reactive mass transport in all-liquid RFBs37. Despite these advances, a method capable of visualizing operando metal plating processes during electrochemical operation has remained elusive, limiting our ability to fully understand phase-change electrochemistry in practical cell architectures.

Here, we introduce polarization contrast neutron imaging (Fig. 1a) to visualize and quantify iron plating and stripping dynamics in an operating AIRFB (Fig. 1b). We aim to map plating and stripping patterns, investigate mass transport in flow cells, and correlate local phenomena with macroscopic electrochemical performance (Fig. 1c). Polarization contrast neutron imaging is sensitive to ferromagnetic phases in a sample38,39. The incident polarized neutron beam interacts with the magnetic field distribution in the sample, becoming depolarized and generating imaging contrast40,41. As neutrons possess a magnetic moment, they respond strongly to ferromagnetic materials like metallic iron, allowing high sensitivity and low detection limits42. Hence, this technique has been applied to characterize magnetic domain distribution in bulk samples41,43 and induced magnetic order in non-magnetic samples by applying high pressure39,44. We hypothesize that these capabilities can be leveraged to image and study iron plating and stripping within porous electrodes of AIRFBs during operation, providing spatially resolved insight into phase-transformation and transport phenomena under practical conditions.

This work demonstrates the feasibility of polarization contrast neutron imaging for operando iron deposition/stripping, along with the hydrogen evolution side reaction. We use this approach to examine how electrochemical and transport conditions influence the spatial distribution of iron plating and stripping, and the secondary hydrogen evolution. We first quantify the correlation between iron species content and neutron depolarization. We then use this technique to investigate key operational parameters affecting electrodeposition dynamics and mass transport in an AIRFB, such as current density, pH, and flow field geometry. The results reveal material-level limitations, such as inhomogeneous deposition, formation of inactive species, and gas-induced blockage, and offer guidelines for improved electrode and reactor design. We anticipate that this methodology is broadly applicable to electrochemical systems involving dynamic magnetic phase transformations and gas-evolving electrodes.

Results

Selective imaging of ferromagnetic iron

We first assess the detection capabilities of the method and correlate the neutron response with the content of various iron species: α-Fe, Fe3O4, α-FeOOH, γ-FeOOH, and γ-Fe2O3. To this end, ex situ calibration cuvettes containing α-Fe powders (20–190 μm iron-in-beam thickness, corresponding to 0.2–2 vol%) were prepared using PTFE as non-attenuating matrix (cuvette composition in Tables 1 and S1; transmission data in Fig. S5). Even at the lowest iron content (20 μm iron-in-beam), a measurable depolarization response around 0.4 is observed (Fig. 2a), demonstrating high sensitivity. Magnetic depolarization increases logarithmically with α-Fe content (Fig. 2b), consistent with the theoretical dependence on magnetic phase thickness for ferromagnetic materials (Eq. 3). At higher loadings (>200 μm iron-in-beam), signal saturation occurs (Fig. 2b), thus establishing the practical range of this technique (for the equipment and conditions described in “Methods”) as < 200 μm of α-Fe in-beam. To further quantify the correlation, the natural logarithm of the depolarization is plotted against iron thickness (Fig. 2c). The slope yields the linear depolarization coefficient (η) (Eqs. 3 and 4), which largely depends on the variable parameters magnetic domain size (d) and local magnetic flux density (B). Due to the lower precision of the measured depolarization near the saturation limit, powder inhomogeneity and limited replicates, some deviation from linearity is observed for the higher loadings, especially for 2 vol% iron-in-beam (further discussed in Section S2). Since B is influenced by the particle size, shape and aggregation45, calculating η is non-trivial. Using the experimental η, we estimate long-range magnetic domain sizes between 0.3 and 1.0 μm for α-Fe (Fig. S4). Accurate quantification would benefit from experiments at varying neutron velocities and controlled model systems.

To evaluate selectivity, we measured the depolarization response of oxides and oxyhydroxides (Fe3O4, α-FeOOH, γ-FeOOH and γ-Fe2O3), which may form in aqueous AIRFB operation46,47. These species showed minimal depolarization (Fig. 2d), due to their magnetic properties44: while α-Fe is ferromagnetic, Fe3O4 and γ-Fe2O3 are ferrimagnetic48,49 and α- and γ-FeOOH are antiferromagnetic50,51. Non-metallic iron compounds and pure PTFE cuvettes exhibit negative depolarization values, an artifact caused by PTFE. Nevertheless, the depolarization caused by metallic iron is well above such an artifact (Fig. 2d), confirming the high selectivity of polarization contrast neutron imaging for α-Fe.

Iron plating dynamics under cycling

Polarization contrast neutron imaging enables simultaneous visualization of metallic iron plating/stripping dynamics and changes in neutron transmission, making it well-suited to study multiphase electrochemical systems. We illustrate its capabilities using AIRFBs, where the negative electrode supports iron plating (charge) and iron stripping (discharge), forming and dissolving ferromagnetic α-Fe, respectively, altering the net polarization of the neutron beam (Figs. 1b and 3a). Simultaneously, during charge the parasitic hydrogen gas evolution in the negative half-cell increases neutron transmission due to its lower attenuation compared to the liquid electrolyte. In contrast, the Fe2+/Fe3+ redox reaction at the positive electrode does not alter polarization or transmission37. As such, polarized neutron imaging allows spatially and temporally resolved tracking of key reactions in the performance-determining negative half-cell under operando conditions.

To visualize these phenomena, we performed galvanostatic cycling over 10 charge-discharge cycles (Fig. 3). As shown in Fig. 3b, the system initially exhibits low columbic efficiency (CE) (~10%) that increases to ~80% by cycle 5, indicating a stabilization period. This trend parallels the increment in the depolarization signal profiles (Fig. 3c), which show increasing iron deposition across the electrode thickness over the first five cycles. From cycle 6 onward, the depolarization signal stabilized, marking steady-state operation. The iron thickness across the beam path in the negative electrodes increases with the cycle number, following the same trend as the coulombic efficiency (Fig. 3d). Moreover, the depolarization radiographs (Fig. 3e) enable the visualization of the local distribution of iron and its evolution with increasing cycle number. Thanks to the high sensitivity of polarized neutrons to small amounts of α-Fe (vide supra), iron stripping can also be investigated, and we find full removal of metallic iron by the end of each discharge (see Supplementary Video S1). Once a steady state is reached, the depolarization signal changes significantly during the charge-discharge cycle, shifting from about 0.8 at the end of the charge to about 0 at the end of the discharge (Fig. S6).

The reduced CE during early cycles is attributed to the competing hydrogen evolution. Neutron transmission radiographs (Fig. 3f–h) show increased transmission—indicative of gas presence—particularly in the initial cycles. The larger hydrogen evolution rate in cycle 1 is driven by faster kinetics at lower pH (initial pH ~ 1), and decreases over time due to proton consumption, which raises the negative electrolyte (negolyte) pH. Steady-state conditions are reached around cycle 6, which we hypothesize is driven by the pH increase and the progressive stabilization of pre-plated electrode surfaces (see Effect of negolyte pH). However, the increase of CE correlates with a weak decrease in hydrogen gas volumetric fraction (Fig. 3g), given the combined effect of hydrogen formation and accumulation in the transmission radiographs. Thus, neutron transmission data in this system do not provide direct information on CE. Nevertheless, hydrogen evolution remains non-negligible due to the acidic, aqueous electrolyte.

Beyond temporal tracking, polarized neutron imaging reveals the spatial distribution of plated iron within the electrodes, with an effective pixel size of ~31 μm. Iron initially deposits near the membrane, where local current density is the highest. This preferential plating is consistent across experiments (Figs. 3–7) and is accompanied by increased presence of hydrogen in the same regions. As plating proceeds and CE increases, iron accumulates both in previously plated areas and further into the electrode thickness toward the current collector. However, the plating rate is faster in pre-plated areas, possibly due to residual non-ferromagnetic iron species or to local modifications of the carbon fibers that catalyze metal nucleation52,53 (further discussed below with post mortem X-ray photoelectron spectrometry results). This behavior leads to saturation of the depolarization signal in localized regions (Fig. 3c), typically around 200 μm iron thickness (Fig. 2b). Thus, while the technique exhibits high sensitivity to small iron amounts, signal saturation can limit quantification of total plated iron with the used setup. Signal saturation can be lessened by reducing the electrode dimension in the beam direction or by shortening the wavelength of the incident beam and thus reducing the contrast.

Having validated the diagnostic capability of polarized neutron imaging coupled with electrochemical testing, we proceed to systematically investigate the effects of current density, negolyte pH, flow field geometry, and flow rate on plating dynamics and performance.

Current density determines spatial reaction distribution

Current density is a key performance metric in redox flow batteries, as it determines battery performance and economic feasibility54,55. It is particularly relevant in AIRFBs where capacity is constrained by electrode pore volume, and thus understanding how current density influences plating behavior is essential. We investigated the effect of current density (25–100 mA cm−2) under steady-state cycling. Figure 4 summarizes the electrochemical and neutron imaging response using representative depolarization radiographs and the spatially resolved depolarization profiles during both charge and discharge, as well as metrics capturing plating-front dynamics and hydrogen accumulation. For extended electrochemical and depolarization data, see Figs. S8 and S9.

Galvanostatic voltage profiles (Fig. S8, first column) remain stable for all currents except at 100 mA cm−2, where a voltage rise suggests mass transfer limitations and reactant depletion (Fig. S7)56. However, given that only ∼255 mAh have been consumed (<10% of the negolyte capacity), this cannot be attributed to overall depletion of Fe2+ in the system. Instead, this behavior is indicative of local ion depletion and spatially non-uniform rates within the porous carbon electrode57.

Polarized neutron imaging resolves these local distribution effects. Radiographs captured ~20 minutes into charging (Fig. 4a) show iron deposition consistently initiating near the membrane (complete charge-discharge imaging sequence in Fig. S9 and Video S2). Depolarization profiles (Fig. 4b) confirm that higher currents accelerate plating, increasing iron accumulation per unit time. However, comparison at equivalent charges (Fig. S10a) reveals little difference in total plated iron across current densities. Only when examining the extent of signal saturation (Figure S10b) we can more finely differentiate the effects of current density in the plating distribution. The key distinction emerges from the spatial distribution of the deposit: low current densities yield more uniform iron distribution throughout the electrode thickness, while higher current densities produce more localized plating toward the membrane, with reduced deposition toward the current collector. Analogous effects would be expected for the hydrogen evolution, although gas accumulation effects prevent the deconvolution of gas formation regions (Fig. S11).

This plating distribution behavior is corroborated by the position of the plating front (measured as the full width at half maximum of the depolarization signal as measured from the membrane position), seen in Fig. 4e, which tracks the location of the deposition front as a function of time and current density. At lower currents, the plating front advances more gradually and reaches overall greater penetration distribution across the electrode. In contrast, at higher current densities, the front progresses more rapidly but saturates in a narrower region closer to the membrane, with poorer electrode utilization when approaching the current collector (Fig. S10).

These trends are in agreement with classical porous electrode theory, as first reported by Newman and coworkers in 196258. In porous electrodes, where the electronic conductivity of the solid matrix (σ) greatly exceeds the value of the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte phase (κ), the current density distribution is predicted to concentrate near the membrane-electrolyte interface. In typical systems employing carbon fiber electrodes, this ratio, τ=σκ, is on the order of 102, which causes the reaction current to displace strongly towards the membrane even at moderate applied current densities59. As cycling proceeds, this imbalance is dynamically amplified: the deposition of highly conductive metal iron leads to increased electronic conductivity of the solid phase, while the simultaneous local depletion of Fe2+ ions and partial pore blockage leads to decreased ionic conductivity, progressively increasing the τ ratio and thus exacerbating uneven plating. Therefore, pore size reduction and ultimately clogging are more likely to occur near the membrane, potentially causing battery failure by loss of pathways for ionic flow.

These parameters can help determine the effective reaction penetration depth, Leff, which can describe how much of the electrode thickness actively participates in the iron deposition. When operating under Butler–Volmer kinetics regime at higher current densities, Leff is inversely proportional to λ. Therefore, with increased applied current densities, λ is also expected to increase, with the corresponding reduction in the effective reaction penetration depth, restricting deposition to those areas closer to the membrane. That is, although thicker electrodes would nominally provide greater pore volume for storage, ionic resistance imposes a practical limit on utilization of the electrode in the negative half-cell.

For all current-density experiments (Fig. 4), we estimated porosity changes in the negative felt electrode, aided by the calibration data, across segmented electrode regions (Fig. S12). Even at the lowest applied current density of 25 mA cm−2, spatial porosity gradients develop, which is indicative of non-uniform electrode volume utilization. With increasing current, the membrane regions show sharply declining porosity, while the other regions stabilize, confirming accelerated void-space saturation near the membrane due to localized deposition. These observations highlight a fundamental trade-off in hybrid, phase-change system design: even though thicker electrodes may initially seem desirable to increase metal-based energy storage capacity, the resulting increase in ohmic losses due to ionic resistance imposes a practical limit, thus concentrating plating in preferential regions. This motivates the development of alternative electrode designs with tailored thickness and porosity distributions, for example, with larger pore size near the membrane to level ionic and electronic conductivities and minimize localized clogging. We provide a more detailed discussion of these trends in light of the porous electrode theory in Section S4.1 of the Supplementary Information

During discharge, time-resolved radiographs and depolarization profiles (Fig. S8, fourth and fifth columns; full data in Fig. S9) reveal that the iron stripping front initially appears uniform through the electrode thickness, but soon a preferential reaction front appears, whose location and shape are based on current. We attribute this general pattern to mass transfer being dominated by forced convection and axial diffusion holding a secondary role60, as we further evidence when studying flow field geometries (vide infra). We observe distinct trends for this preferential iron stripping front: at low current densities, major stripping remains near the membrane (high τ), thus creating an increase of the liquid phase in the membrane adjacent region61. At high currents, the ionic conductivity of the system becomes more limiting, therefore also affecting the balance between ohmics and kinetics. This displaces the reaction away from low-conductivity zones and creates a widened front towards the electrode midsection. The resulting patterns reflect the system’s tendency to preserve material in areas of lower ohmic resistance.

These insights demonstrate that the current density distribution for plating and stripping becomes increasingly inhomogeneous at higher operating currents. For instance, at 100 mA cm−2, full stripping is not achieved, as evidenced by a higher discharge overpotentials, earlier reaching of the voltage cut-off, and the presence of residual iron at the end of this cycle. Although such current densities are common in state-of-the-art systems55, our imaging reveals critical utilization limitations, inaccessible with conventional techniques. To unlock the full storage potential of AIRFB (or analogous hybrid systems), strategies beyond traditional cycling may be necessary. Protocols like constant current—constant voltage cycling may improve electrode utilization and mitigate localized pore clogging effects.

Negolyte pH dictates iron accessibility and cell lifetime

The competition between iron plating and hydrogen evolution determines the coulombic and energy efficiencies of AIRFBs. Since the hydrogen evolution kinetic rate is strongly pH-dependent, increasing the electrolyte pH can suppress hydrogen evolution. However, the chemical stability of Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ ions is limited to bulk pH values ~6 and 2, respectively, beyond which insoluble oxides or oxyhydroxides may precipitate, reducing the capacity, as shown in the Pourbaix diagram (Fig. S13). These challenges are further amplified by local pH variations that occur during electrochemical operation. Polarization contrast neutron imaging offers insights into how pH influences plating behavior.

To investigate the influence of pH on performance and durability, galvanostatic cycling was conducted with initial negolyte pH values of ~1 and ~2.5 (Fig. 5; extended data in Fig. S14). The starting low CE efficiency can be attributed to the competing hydrogen evolution reaction, which is more severe for lower pH, as observed by the transmission radiographs in Fig. S15. At pH~1, the depolarization response (Fig. 5b, c) progressively increases with the cycle number and stabilizes after 5 cycles, consistent with the rising CE. Low initial plating efficiency leads to large stripping overpotentials, which decrease as the efficiency increases. At pH~2.5, maximum depolarization is reached in cycle 2 and declines in subsequent cycles, more notably near the current collector area (Fig. S14). This decline likely indicates the onset of iron oxide/oxyhydroxides precipitation, as the cell operated near the Fe2+ local solubility limit (Fig. S13). The observed rise in high-frequency resistance from 0.71 to 0.79 Ω over 10 cycles further supports the formation of non-conductive species. The decay in depolarization signal does not correlate with an observable decrease in dischargeable capacity. Thus, depolarization radiographs reveal capacity fading overshadowed in the electrochemical test as the cycling was not conducted in the 0–100 % state-of-charge range62.

Post-discharge radiographs (Fig. S14) reveal persistent depolarization near the inlet at pH~2.5, particularly after cycle 7. We hypothesize that it is due to the formation of insulating (oxyhydr)oxides around metallic iron, which prevents its stripping during the discharge. Post mortem x-ray diffraction (XRD) after 10 cycles of galvanostatic cycling (see electrode cleaning protocol in “Methods”) shows α-Fe and γ-FeOOH in the electrode for both negolyte pH (Fig. S16), with additional species (α-FeOOH, γ-Fe2O3 and ferrihydrite) detected at pH~2.5. However, determining oxyhydroxide formation rates via α-Fe/γ-FeOOH ratios was inconclusive due to a high background signal coming from the carbon felt. Moreover, the hypothesis of formation of iron (oxyhydr)oxides for initial negolyte pH 2.5 is further supported by the developed analytical model derived from the depolarization data (see details of the model in Supplementary Information). The model shows a significant shift of pH after 10 cycles (up to 3.2, corresponding to a 27% increase in pH) when starting with pH 2.5, in contrast with a minimal change (up to 1.02, a 2% increase in pH) with an initial pH of 1 (Fig. S17). According to the Pourbaix diagram (Fig. S13), Fe2O3 may be formed at pH 3 for potentials above 0.2 V vs. SHE as Fe2+ concentration in the negolyte is above 1 M.

The pH effect on iron plating with respect to hydrogen evolution appears to be both thermodynamic and kinetic. Higher pH (lower proton activity) reduces the thermodynamic driving force, while pH changes to the carbon electrodes (i.e., acidic oxidation) may alter catalytic activity. However, post mortem x-ray photoelectron spectrometry (XPS) analysis (Figs. S19 and S20) shows similar carbon-oxygen bond rations at both pH 1 and 2.5, suggesting comparable catalytic sides53. To clarify the role of kinetics, further studies are needed on in situ electrode modifications and their influence on reaction pathways. The increased Fe–O signals at pH 2.5 also support the formation of more oxidized, non-reversible iron species, which may contribute to capacity fade. Next to pH, we envision that other operating parameters, such as temperature, can be leveraged to accelerate battery stabilization in the earlier cycles. Exploratory experiments (Fig. S18) show that operating at higher temperatures (35 °C) results in a decrease in the number of cycles to reach stabilization, which we attribute to enhanced iron plating kinetics relative to hydrogen evolution63.

Flow field architecture and flow velocity govern gas management and metal plating

The electrolyte distribution inside the porous electrodes is a strongly determining factor in flow batteries, especially in systems with phase-change chemistries, where access to the redox active material and pore space evolves dynamically during operation. Here, we analyze the influence of flow field geometry and flow rate on the electrochemical performance, iron plating and stripping, and gas evolution in an AIRFB. Three flow field configurations were investigated: a flow-through flow field (FTFF) enabling fully convective flow, a parallel flow field (PFF) relying on diffusion, and an interdigitated flow field (IDFF) combining both transport regimes. Figure 6 shows the results for all three studied flow field types in terms of their electrochemical behavior and the neutron response. The experimental data for each configuration is shown under steady state conditions (after ten galvanostatic cycles at 50 mA cm−2, 22.5 mL min−1 and starting negolyte pH of 1). Supplementary Figs. S21 and 22; Videos S1 and S3 provide extended data. All systems display similar charge-discharge profiles: a peak in voltage followed by stabilization during the charge step, and a plateau during discharge of the batteries, before a sharp voltage drop attributed to the removal of all the accessible iron previously plated. Furthermore, for all studied flow fields, representative overpotentials for the charge and discharge steps (calculated as the median voltage for each half-cycle) display high symmetry, showing discrepancies within ±10 mV. That is, for a given flow field, the overpotential for the plating reaction is similar to that of the stripping reaction. However, the diffusive-flow PFF exhibits a significantly higher average overpotential (~410 mV), compared to the fully or partially convective FTFF and IDFF (~310–315 mV), underscoring the impact of flow regime on mass transport. We ascribe these differences to the electrolyte intrusion route: in the FTFF, the convective flow ensures homogeneous electrolyte invasion over the electrode thickness, thus filling small-pore areas and largely preventing the formation of highly differentiated flow pathways19. Neutron imaging further confirms the high electrode utilization, with the greatest degree of depolarization saturation (maximum amount of iron measurable by the technique). With an average saturation value of 77.5% (integrated neutron response in Fig. 6b), iron plating occurs more homogeneously, with a slight bias towards the membrane, attributed to current distribution asymmetry. Beyond the spatial distribution, the temporal, cycle-dependent evolution of the total plated iron, extracted from the computed depolarization signal (Fig. 6d), provides additional insight into the stabilization behavior of the various flow field designs. During the initial cycles, all configurations exhibit a similar, progressive increase in the deposited iron, corresponding to the previously defined stabilization or break-in phase of the system. After this point, the FTFF continues to show a gradual increase in the measured plated iron as a function of cycle number, if at a reduced rate. This behavior is consistent with the progressive, minor improvements observed in the full voltage-time profiles (Fig. S19), and reflects sustained electrochemical accessibility and more effective mass transport throughout the electrode thickness. Contrastingly, the PFF and IDFF configurations, which rely fully or partially on diffusive transport, are lined with an integrated depolarization signal that begins to decrease after the stabilization period, indicating a net loss of electrochemically electroplated iron. This decline is consistent with the full electrochemical data and coincides with the appearance of residual, incompletely stripped regions in the electrode (see Video S3), which we had linked to the local pH degradation and formation of the partially insulating iron (oxyhydr)oxide species. In contrast, the diffusive transport PFF results in severe mass transfer limitations, as demonstrated by the higher overpotentials. The electrolyte preferentially flows through the channels due to a lower pressure drop. We anticipate a reduced velocity field entering towards the electrode and relying entirely on lateral imbibition and wicking of the electrolyte, ultimately characterized by sluggish diffusive transport64. These phenomena are associated with electrolyte fingering, Haines instabilities and gas entrapment due to heterogeneous wetting and pore structure65. This leads to uneven plating, particularly concentrated near the membrane due to the askew solid-to-liquid conductivity ratio, with declining iron content across the electrode and significant pore inaccessibility, resulting in the lowest averaged depolarization saturation value of 56.9%, among all studied systems.

The IDFF offers intermediate performance, as its alternating inlet-outlet channels force fluid through the porous medium, improving species distribution versus PFF while avoiding the higher pressure drop of FTFF66. However, this mixed diffusive-convective regime displays performance limitations when combined with thick felt electrodes and sluggish Fe2+|Fe0 kinetics. Despite a similar electrochemical response of the IDFF versus that of the FTFF, we observe regional inhomogeneities in depolarization distribution. Averaged depolarization reaches 62.5%, an intermediate value in volumetric electrode utilization, although plating is skewed towards the upper half and towards the membrane, mirroring trends seen in the PFF plating.

Upon reaching steady-state operation, the FTFF cell is able to maintain the levels of depolarization with extended cycling and achieve full iron removal discharge. Conversely, both flow fields relying on diffusive mass transport exhibit a decline in depolarization performance after approximately the sixth cycle (Fig. S22, middle and bottom rows), accompanied by incomplete iron removal during discharge. This residual iron is evidenced in Video S3 where persistent orange regions would correspond to unstripped iron likely passivated by a non-conductive layer of (oxyhydr)oxides. As previously discussed, this behavior is attributed to local pH shifts induced by parasitic hydrogen evolution, which promotes the formation of these insulating surface layers.

Next to plating/stripping, hydrogen evolution and gas entrapment are major challenges in AIRFBs. To investigate gas phase distribution, neutron transmission imaging was employed (Fig. 6e–g, extended data in Fig. S22). While the neutron transmission signal does not directly identify the (electro)chemical origin of the gas phase or provide a direct quantification of hydrogen evolution rates, it is highly sensitive to the presence and spatial distribution of gas within the porous electrodes. Accordingly, the measured transmission decrease should be interpreted as a local gas volume fraction rather than a species-specific signal.

The assignment of this gas phase to the evolved hydrogen is, however, strongly supported by electrochemical thermodynamics and extensive prior literature2,67–69. In aqueous, acidic electrolytes, such as those used in this study, the Fe2+|Fe0 couple takes place at a more negative standard reduction potential than that of the 2H+|H2 couple. Furthermore, previous studies have reported direct hydrogen gas generation and collection, with quantitative verification by means of gas collection and gas chromatography under comparable operating conditions70.

Initial operation shows peak transmission values, seen in the radiographs as yellow regions, which indicate preferential regions in the porous electrode for the formation and accumulation of gas bubbles. The presence of gas persists throughout the full charge–discharge cycle. Although gas is not generated during the discharge phase, residual entrapped bubbles remain detectable and are gradually removed from the cell as operation proceeds. Transmission values stabilize after several cycles, except for minor variations due to the stochastic nature of bubble flow, as the HER decreases and a hydrodynamic balance between bubble generation and gas removal is reached71. FTFF maintains a relatively uniform gas distribution (~14.3% gas fraction from the integrated neutron response in Fig. 6e) across the negative electrode, reflecting its effective gas removal capability. In contrast, PFF and IDFF reveal localized gas buildup, particularly near the membrane, where current density –and thus HER– is highest due to the non-uniform current distribution. PFF’s direct in-out channel connection causes flow to bypass the deeper electrode region, leading to poor gas removal, resulting in an averaged 11.1% gas content. Although overall lower than in the FTFF case, the PFF exhibits sharp, localized gas peaks, with major accumulation near the membrane region, leading to increased blockage of ion transport pathways and inaccessible surface area of the electrodes, thus exacerbating current distribution non-uniformities and contributing to increased overpotentials.

In the IDFF cell, the middle channels exhibit pronounced gas accumulation. Owing to the convective-diffusive mass transport nature of this design, flow forced into the carbon felt partially flushes gas from the inner electrode region, resulting in the lowest transmission values across all cells within the 0.17–0.47 normalized thickness range. However, the electrolyte flow penetrates insufficiently to evacuate hydrogen near or within the dead-ended regions of channels, where local velocities are nearly stagnant. This leads to significant gas build-up along the boundaries of the negative half-cell, particularly at the membrane and current collector interfaces, yielding the highest average content (~15.2%) among all studied flow field designs. Furthermore, the elevated gas presence introduces considerable voltage noise during charging (direct voltage comparison observable in Fig. S21), a phenomenon absent in the other configurations. Nonetheless, we hypothesize that the spatial segregation of gas in IDFF leaves broad, unobstructed pathways that allow electrolyte access to active sites, thereby mitigating the mass transport overpotentials observed in the diffusion-limited PFF. The cycle-resolved hydrogen gas fraction, extracted from the integrated neutron transmission signal (Fig. 6g), further corroborates these observations. Across all flow field designs, the gas fraction decreases modestly with continued cycling, consistent with the gradual consumption of available protons and stabilization of HER activity. The IDFF exhibits the highest average hydrogen content, comparable in magnitude to the FTFF but with a markedly more heterogeneous spatial distribution, whereas the PFF displays the lowest overall gas fraction due to electrolyte bypassing of the deeper electrode regions. These trends mirror the single-cycle observations and highlight the strong coupling between flow regime, gas removal efficiency, and long-term electrode accessibility.

To contextualize these results and the validity of neutron transmission and its integral as a measure of hydrogen -or gas-bubble fraction, we have examined the limited existing literature on operando imaging and gas quantification in flow cells. Our observations using operando neutron imaging align with prior studies employing X-ray radiography (e.g., Zeis et al., for vanadium systems20), which report similar bubble fractions (~15–20%vol) and preferential accumulation at the membrane interfaces due to the use of partially hydrophobic polymeric materials, inhomogeneity of electrolyte distribution and heterogeneous electrode surface chemistries. Importantly, we find that even during discharge, where there is no electrochemical production of hydrogen, residual gas remains trapped in the porous structure (Videos S1 and S3), highlighting the persistent impact of the HER on electrode blockage and performance degradation.

With these observations, we can establish that electrolyte delivery and flow uniformity are critical in governing reaction distribution, gas management and ultimately cell performance in AIRFBs. Among accessible control parameters, flow rate offers a practical means to influence species transport and electrode accessibility, thereby reducing losses and improving performance. However, the effects of flow rate vary depending on the dominant transport mechanism -diffusive or convective- imposed by the flow field architecture. We then investigate this relationship across a range of flow rates for the interdigitated cells (IDFF) below (Fig. 7). Complementary data for all flow fields are provided in Figs. S23–25.

Persistent hydrogen evolution and gas entrapment generate voltage noise at all flow rates, although particular disruptions are observed at the lowest tested rate (5.625 mL min−1). Although in-plane iron deposition remains relatively uniform (Fig. 7b, c), these flow conditions lead to sharply rising overvoltages driven by mass transport limitations: (i) depletion of iron species due to weak convective intrusion and poor reservoir mixing, and (ii) hydrogen accumulation, exacerbated by capillary forces overwhelming the viscous flow of electrolyte, thus reducing effective permeability71. Transmission maps (Fig. 7e, f) confirm gas build-up near the membrane and within dead-ended channel regions72. Among all designs, IDFF exhibits the highest gas buildup under low flow, as shown in Fig. S24 where we illustrate the correlation between hydrogen content as a function of flow rate and flow field.

Increasing flow rates improve species transport and alleviate these limitations. At intermediate flow rates (22.5 mL min−1), overpotentials stabilize, eliminating limiting-current regimes and membrane-adjacent gas accumulation drops by ~50%. Higher rates further enhance iron electrodeposition and thus CEs. As shown in Fig. S25, IDFF consistently delivers the highest CE across all flow conditions despite its incomplete stripping, suggesting elevated membrane-adjacent plating. Voltaic and energy efficiencies (VE and EE, respectively) show a marked rise with flow rate, asymptotically reaching ~75% and ~65%, respectively. Notably, a detailed, quantitative analysis of the depolarization signal-derived iron content (Fig. 7d) is only weakly dependent on flow rate across the investigated range. While higher flow rates improve electrode accessibility, the overall iron detection remains comparable once steady-state operation is reached. Contrastingly to the weak flow-rate dependence of imaged iron tracking, the volumetric fraction of gas trapped within the porous electrode exhibits a strong, near-linear dependence on electrolyte flow velocity (Fig. 7g). At the lower flow rates, substantial flow accumulation was observed, consistent with the aforementioned blockage of electrolyte and amplification of overpotentials. Progressive increases in flow rate manage to reduce the accumulated gas volume, underscoring flow rate control as a tool towards more effective bubble detachment, transport and removal.

In contrast, flow-through and parallel architectures exhibit limited flow-rate sensitivity. Their respective convective- and diffusion-dominated transport modes remain largely unaltered, and flow rate adjustments yield only modest gains in voltage profiles or CE (Fig. S23), consistent with prior modelling73. Nonetheless, higher flow rates do aid in gas evacuation, slightly improving VE and EE.

Overall, we find key operating parameters that can aid in increased performance in AIRFBs. Although no optimal cell architecture can be devised independently of the redox chemistry, electrolyte and electrode properties, or operating conditions74, we have shown that predominantly convective flow promotes more uniform metal deposition and lower overpotentials. Nevertheless, FTFF designs suffer from high hydraulic losses75; IDFFs could provide a promising alternative, balancing performance and pressure efficiency, though careful optimization of channel geometry and electrode pairing is essential. Given the challenges associated with gas evolution, strategies for HER mitigation and improved liquid-gas management are necessary. While HER suppression has been widely explored63,76–80, effective management of two-phase flow remains underdeveloped. We envision potential solutions that include modification of electrode surfaces to promote uniform plating and control gas nucleation via the incorporation of functional groups or conductive polymers. Similarly, tailoring flow field architecture, such as optimizing channel-to-rib ratios, integrating tapering, or incorporating static mixers (e.g., pillars or textured surfaces), could enhance electrolyte distribution and gas removal without significantly increasing pressure drops70. Altogether, we find that operating at high electrolyte velocities benefits AIRFB performance, as it can induce sufficient electrolyte flow intrusion. Beyond simply increasing flow, further gains could arise from pulsatile flow strategies that enhance back-mixing and facilitate periodic gas dislodgement81,82.

Practical limitations and opportunities for polarization contrast neutron imaging

Polarization contract neutron imaging is a powerful operando diagnostic technique for investigating phase-change processes, as it enables selective, spatially and temporally resolved visualization of metallic iron deposition and stripping within porous electrodes. However, signal saturation can limit the quantification of total plated iron with the setup used. Local depolarization signal saturation at the end of charge is observed for all the investigated configurations, thus limiting the quantification capabilities of the technique and preventing the full usage of the electrode.

In order to prevent saturation of the depolarization signal, several strategies can be used. Firstly, given that contrast depends on the square of the wavelength, saturation can be reduced by filtering the neutron incident beam in order to employ shorter wavelength neutrons, which would be especially useful for in-plane measurements (as performed herein). Secondly, for in-plane measurements, the use of an alternative cell design with a smaller electrode in the beam direction (i.e., 1 cm instead of 1.5 cm electrode width) would reduce the amount of iron plated across the beam and thus lower the saturation limit. Thirdly, when studying large electrodes, through-plane measurements would enable higher amounts of iron plated before saturation (about 5 times more with a similar cell design as the one used here). Although, through plane imaging does not allow to measure the across-thickness distribution of iron. Fourthly, an alternative approach could be to apply a strong magnetic field to the sample. In this way, the magnetic domains would be more oriented, thus lowering the depolarization coefficient and subsequently the signal intensity. Nevertheless, the external magnetic field might influence the plating process itself, besides other technical issues that may arise from incorporating a strong external magnetic field. By incorporating these strategies, the capabilities of polarization contract neutron imaging can be enhanced, enabling quantification of ferromagnetic reaction products, i.e., iron plated.

Beyond iron-based flow batteries, polarization contract neutron imaging is broadly applicable to electrochemical and non-electrochemical systems involving dynamic magnetic phase transformations and/or gas-evolving electrodes. Specifically, in the field of electrochemical systems in the cell or stack level, this operando methodology can be applied for investigating iron conversions in iron-nickel and iron-air batteries or nickel oxidation and hydrogen accumulation in 3D electrodes in operando alkaline water electrolysis.

Summary and outlook

This study establishes polarization contract neutron imaging as a powerful operando diagnostic technique for investigating phase-change processes in all-iron redox flow batteries. We demonstrated that this method enables selective, spatially and temporally resolved visualization of metallic iron deposition and stripping within porous electrodes. The technique leverages the high sensitivity of polarized neutrons to ferromagnetic α-Fe, with detection limits below 20 μm and signal saturation at ~200 μm iron thickness, while remaining unaffected by non-ferromagnetic species. By combining electrochemical diagnostics with neutron imaging, we uncovered critical limitations in cell performance arising from non-uniform current density distributions, which result in preferential iron deposition near the membrane and localized hydrogen evolution. Moreover, our results revealed an inverse relationship between pH and long-term stability, wherein higher pH accelerated iron (oxyhydr)oxide formation, contributing to capacity fade. Furthermore, we demonstrated that flow field geometries significantly impact reaction selectivity and spatial uniformity. Convective flow fields enhanced electrode utilization and minimized hydrogen accumulation, while diffusive flow regimes exacerbated inhomogeneous deposition and gas accumulation.

By correlating macroscopic cell performance with operando imaging, this work provides a mechanistic foundation for addressing the coupled challenges of metal plating, mass transport, and gas evolution in redox flow systems. Looking ahead, this methodology offers a powerful platform for optimizing AIRFBs across multiple design dimensions—such as electrode architecture and local chemical environment—with particular potential for mitigating parasitic hydrogen evolution and improving plating uniformity. Beyond iron-based systems, the techniques and insights developed here are broadly relevant to electrochemical technologies involving dynamic magnetic or multiphase phenomena. Continued integration of operando imaging with electrochemical and spectroscopic tools will be essential to accelerate the rational design of next-generation energy storage systems.

Chemicals

Iron(III) chloride anhydrous (FeCl3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 98%); iron(II) chloride anhydrous (FeCl2, Sigma Aldrich, 98%); potassium chloride (KCl, Thermo Fisher Scientific, ≥99%); iron(III) oxide, gamma phase (γ-Fe2O3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, ≥99%); iron(III) oxyhydroxide, alpha phase (α-FeOOH, Thermo Fisher Scientific, ≥99%); iron(III) oxyhydroxide, gamma phase (γ-FeOOH, Thermo Fisher Scientific); iron(II, III) oxide (Fe3O4, Sigma Aldrich, ≥98%, particle size <50 nm); carbonyl alpha-iron powder (α-Fe, Sigma Aldrich, ≥99.5%, average particle size 5–9 μm); polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, Sigma Aldrich, 1 μm free-flowing powder); n-hexane (Sigma Aldrich, for analysis ACS Reag.); deuterated water (D2O, ZEOtope, ≥99.9% deuterium content) and deuterium chloride (DCl, ZEOtope, 20%wt DCl/D2O, ≥99.5% deuterium content, 1.19 g cm−3) were used as received without further purification.

Calibration sample preparation

To quantify the effects of the magnetization within the beam path caused by iron or iron-containing species, cuvette calibration measurements were performed to correlate relative neutron transmission and depolarization to the content of various iron species listed in Chemicals, i.e., α-Fe, γ-Fe2O3, α-FeOOH, γ-FeOOH and Fe3O4.

The iron and PTFE powders were mixed in the calculated ratios (see Table 1 for carbonyl alpha-iron samples and Table 2 for iron oxyhydroxide and oxide samples, and for further information regarding sample preparation, see Tables S1 and S2 in the Supplementary Information). It must be noted that species of different particle size were employed, ranging from nano- to micrometer range; to account for the size-related effects on the depolarization behavior, theoretical calculations for local magnetic flux densities were performed as a function of the magnetic domain size, see Fig. S4 of the Supplementary Information. The different powder mixtures were then homogenized in the dry state by use of a vortex for 30 s. Afterward, 15–20 mL of n-hexane were added to the mixture vessel to solubilize the PTFE powder. To ensure complete solubilization and homogeneous mixing of the solution components, each sample was subjected to 3 cycles of ultrasonication in an ice bath to avoid clumping of the iron powder or unwanted oxidation and vortexed for 300 s and 30 s, respectively, until a homogeneous viscous mixture was obtained. The contents of these vessels were transferred to a glass crystallizer, and an additional 20 mL of n-hexane was added to each. The solutions were then carefully stirred until complete homogenization. The solvent was allowed to evaporate at ambient conditions, and the remaining solid was transferred to an ES-type quartz glass cuvette (Portmann Instruments, dimensions: 4 × 10 × 40 mm3, LWH, where the width is the dimension across the beam path, see Fig. S5a). The quartz cuvettes were mounted on a robotized platform controlled remotely, 14 cm in front of the detector.

Electrolyte preparation

The negative electrolytes consisted of a total volume of 50 mL, and were prepared by dissolving 3.73 g KCl (anhy.) [1 M] and 7.13 g FeCl2 (anhy.) [1.125 M] in argon-purged deuterated water (D2O) as a solvent to reduce the contribution of the hydrogen atoms to the neutron attenuation. All the negolytes were titrated with 780 μL of deuterium chloride to reach a pH~1 unless otherwise specified.

For further study regarding the pH influence on the all-iron RFB behavior, including iron electrodeposition and hydrogen evolution and their subsequent effects on neutron depolarization and relative neutron transmission, a negolyte of initial pH~2.5 was prepared analogously without the addition of DCl.

Given the deuterated nature of the solvent and the anhydrous salts employed in these experiments, hydrogen evolution will result in the formation of deuterium gas (D2 or 2H2).

The positive electrolytes (posolytes) for all studies consisted of a total volume of 100 mL, and were prepared by dissolving 7.46 g of KCl (anhy.) [1 M], 12.68 g of FeCl2 (anhy.)[1 M] and 4.06 g of FeCl3 (anhy.) [0.25 M] (equivalent to an initial 30% state of charge)83 in deuterated water and titrated with 3.9 mL of DCl [0.25 M] to improve the kinetics of the Fe2+|Fe3+ couple and avoid the formation of Fe3+-derived precipitates due to their lower pH-dependent solubility83.

All electrolytes were purged with argon prior to experimentation.

Flow cell set-up

Polarization contrast neutron imaging was performed using a laboratory-scale redox flow battery cell as described previously84 with minor modifications for improved imaging, which were all machined in-house. The electrolyte diffusers were made from polypropylene (McMaster-Carr), and graphite current collectors, which also serve as flow fields with different flow designs, were milled from 3.18 mm thick polymer-infused graphite plates (G347B graphite, MWI Inc). The geometrical design of the different flow fields used in this study are: (i) flow-through with one inlet and outlet channel located at the bottom and top of the working area, respectively, (ii) interdigitated, with 7 channels of length 16 mm, where 4 of these are inlet channels, alternating with 3 outlet channels and (iii) parallel, with 7 channels, 16 mm long each, all of which have an inlet and outlet aperture. For all three designs, channels are 1 mm wide and 0.5 mm deep. Further details and schematic representation of the flow field designs can be found in references37,85. The electrodes used for this study were carbon felts with a nominal thickness of 2.8 mm (AvCarb G280, 95% nominal porosity) and a geometric area of 2.55 cm2 (1.5 cm width by 1.7 cm height). For the experiments employing a flow-through flow field design, the felt electrodes were compressed until they reached a thickness of 2.5 mm each with incompressible PTFE gaskets, made in-house, for a total cell thickness of 5 mm. For the experiments employing interdigitated and parallel flow field designs, two additional compressible gaskets were added per side (multidirectional expanded PTFE, GORE®, 500 μm nominal thickness, 56% compressibility), for a total cell thickness of ca. ~6 mm. The flow field and gasket designs were slightly modified by grooving on both sides along the beam path, around the active area, to avoid affecting the electrolyte flow and the different transport phenomena within the cell. The grooving was performed to minimize the attenuation of the neutron beam intensity caused by the cell parts, therefore increasing the sensitivity of the measurement within the electrode volume. All experiments were performed with cells containing a Fumasep FAP-450 anion exchange membrane (FuMA-Tech, 50 μm dry thickness, area of 2 cm by 2 cm), previously stored in a solution of background electrolyte (1 mol L−1 KCl in D2O) for >24 h. After assembly, the cells employing incompressible gaskets were tightened with a torque-controlled wrench in a crisscross sequence, in 0.3–0.4 N m increments until reaching a final torque of 2.2 N m. The cells employing the expanded PTFE gaskets were tightened further until reaching a torque of 6.0 N m, to ensure maximal compression of the additional gasketing. Following this, the cell was mounted 14 cm in front of the detector on a robotized platform controlled remotely. A peristaltic pump (Cole-Palmer MFLX07522-20 pump drive with MFLX77202-60 pump head) was used to pump the electrolyte to the cell with high precision, cured silicone tubing (Masterflex L/S 16 tubing), connected to the 50 mL negolyte and 100 mL posolyte reservoirs, see Fig. S1 for a detailed photograph of the experimental set-up. The pump flow rates were calibrated for the cell assemblies prior to electrochemical and imaging experimentation to account for the intrinsic pressure drop of the pristine carbon felt electrodes. The negolyte reservoir was open to the atmosphere to allow the release of the generated hydrogen and avoid overpressurization, with a constant flow of argon in the electrolyte to avoid unwanted oxidation to Fe3+ ions or Fe3+-containing precipitates. The posolyte reservoir was closed to the atmosphere to limit the availability of oxygen in the system. Flow rates of 5.625, 22.5, and 56.25 mL min−1 were employed in the different experiments for all flow field designs, see below. No climatic chamber was used for these experiments.

Polarization contrast neutron imaging

Polarization contrast neutron imaging was performed at the BOA beamline, at the Spallation Neutron Source facility (SINQ) of the Paul Scherrer Institute (Villingen, Switzerland)86. In the SINQ facility, neutrons are ejected from a lead spallation target by the incidence of a proton beam at 590 MeV of energy and 1.5 mA current. This beamline is characterized by a cold neutron spectrum, achieved by a first moderation step with deuterated water until reaching a mean energy of 25 meV and a final moderation through a liquid deuterium tank at 25 K, decreasing the velocity of the neutron beam until reaching a mean energy of 8.53 meV, with a peak wavelength of 2.8 Å and a mean wavelength of 3.8 Å86,87. Additionally, the beamline is permanently equipped with a multichannel polarizing bender unit between the cold neutron source and the exit from the source monolith. A pinhole of 40 × 40 mm2 was placed at the beam exit to limit the beam divergence at the sample position. The instrument was further equipped with an π-spin flipper, a magnetic guide field and a 40 × 40 mm2 supermirror neutron spin filter used as a spin analyzer. The neutron imaging detector was comprised of a 30 μm thick scintillator screen (Gd2O2S:Tb, from RC Tritec) and an optical Ikon-L CCD camera (from Andor, number of pixel 2048 × 2048) with a Makro-Planar 2/100 ZF.2 T objective lens from Zeiss. The magnification of the lens was such to achieve an effective pixel size of 31.5 μm, therefore resulting in a camera field of view of 63 × 63 mm2.

Polarized contrast neutron measurements were performed for the cuvettes with an exposure time of 10 min (alternating between spin-down and spin-up every minute). For the electrochemical cells, the neutron measurements were taken with 60 s of exposure time and four data points (following the sequence spin-down, spin-up, spin-up, spin-down) were then averaged to obtain the images, making it around 4 min per obtained image.

Electrode physicochemical characterization

For post mortem analysis, after the electrochemical sequence previously described and before the cell disassembly, the electrodes were washed with water for 20 min followed by washing for 15 min with ethanol at a flow rate of 22.5 mL min−1. Both the water and ethanol were deaerated by bubbling argon. Afterward, the cell was disassembled, and the electrodes were dried at about 20 °C under vacuum overnight.

The crystallography and phase composition of selected post mortem samples were investigated via X-Ray diffraction (XRD) spectroscopy using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 X-ray benchtop diffractometer. The samples were analyzed at a power of 600 W, 40 kV acceleration voltage and 15 mA applied current in the 2-θ range of 10–90° with a 0.02° step and 0.5° min−1 sweep rate. A copper Kα (wavelength 1.54056 Å) and a D-teX-ultra silicon strip detector were employed. Background removal and peak identification were performed using the software Profex88.

Surface chemistry was characterized using X-ray photoelectron spectrometry (XPS) on a Thermo Scientific K-alpha instrument. The system features a monochromatic small-spot X-ray source paired with a 180° double-focusing hemispherical analyzer and a 182-channel detector. Spectra were acquired using an aluminum anode (Al Kα, energy 1486.68 eV) operating at 72 W, with a measurement spot size of 400 μm. Broad survey scans were collected at a constant pass energy of 200 eV between 130 and 1300 eV (acquisition time 0.24 s). High-resolution region scans were measured at 50 eV for C 1 s (280–298 eV) and Fe 2p (700–740 eV). The background pressure was set to 2 10−8 mbar (Ar) and to 4 10−7 mbar during measurement due to charge compensation. No calibration for adventitious carbon was performed, given the carbonaceous nature of the samples. Peak deconvolution and fitting were performed using the software CasaXPS®.
