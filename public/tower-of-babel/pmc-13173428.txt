Eastern equine encephalitis—104 horses (1979-2017)

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEastern equine encephalitis (EEE) is a highly fatal neurologic disease of horses, but data describing clinical presentation, epidemiology, and prognostic factors are limited.ObjectivesDescribe the clinicopathologic and epidemiologic features of horses with EEE and identify factors associated with survival.AnimalsOne hundred four horses diagnosed with EEE presented with signs of diffuse brain disease.MethodsRetrospective case series of horses evaluated at a Veterinary Teaching Hospital (1979-2017). Medical records were reviewed for clinical findings, diagnostic results, and outcomes. Logistic regression assessed associations between demographic, epidemiologic, and clinicopathologic variables and survival. Generalized linear models evaluated associations between climatic variables and annual case count.ResultsMedian age was 1.4 years (0.1-13.4), predominantly Thoroughbreds and Quarter Horses. Most horses (66/104) presented during summer. In negative binomial regression adjusted for temperature and year, each 100-mm increase in annual precipitation was associated with a 43% increase in cases (IRR 1.43; 95% CI, 1.22-1.82; P < .001). Common findings included abnormal behavior (96%), hyperthermia (99%), recumbency (51%), and seizure-like activity (46%). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) abnormalities included discoloration (65%), high protein concentration > 80 mg/dL (80%), and neutrophilic pleocytosis (62%). Case fatality rate was 90% (94/104). Recumbency, seizure activity during hospitalization, band neutrophilia, monocytosis, and high CSF protein concentration and neutrophils were associated with death.Conclusions and clinical importanceEastern equine encephalitis is a rapidly progressive, highly fatal neurologic disease of horses, with increased case numbers during periods of higher precipitation. Clinicopathologic findings, particularly recumbency, seizure-like activity, leukogram changes and CSF abnormalities, provide prognostic indicators to guide clinical decision-making and client communication.Keywords: alphavirus, EEE, encephalitides, horses, mortality, vaccination

Introduction

Eastern equine encephalitis (EEE) is an arboviral neurological disease that affects a wide range of mammalian species.1–4 The disease is caused by a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus of the Togaviridae family. The virus is maintained in endemic areas by a sylvatic cycle involving mosquitoes, wild birds, and other reservoir species.5,6 Spillover of the EEE virus occurs to a range of vertebrate hosts, resulting in encephalitis, of which the horse is most severely affected.7,8 Neurologic disease in horses attributed to the EEE virus was first reported in Massachusetts in 1831, and the EEE virus is endemic in Florida and most states along the eastern seaboard and Gulf Coast of North America, and in Texas.7,8 This disease is also endemic in South and Central America and the Caribbean. Sporadic outbreaks have occurred in Michigan, as well as the southeastern USA and Canada.1,9

In the Northeastern US states, virus transmission is seasonal and coincides with increases in mosquito populations in mid-to-late summer.5,10 The ecology of the EEE virus is not well understood, and how the virus survives the winter and is subsequently reintroduced is yet to be determined.10 In contrast, cases of EEE are seen in Florida year-round, signifying virus survival by low-level cycling between wild birds and ornithophilic mosquitoes in freshwater forested swamp habitats.11 EEE virus strain isolates in the Northeastern USA are phylogenetically similar to those circulating in Florida around the same time, suggesting Florida’s probable role as a geographic reservoir.6,10 There is evidence that the North American EEE virus is genetically and epidemiologically distinct from the virus circulating in South America. The latter has been reclassified as the Madariaga virus, a related alphavirus that causes disease in both humans and horses.11 Human cases of EEE in North America have historically been uncommon, but the virus is now considered an emerging threat, with 6-11 annual cases reported in the eastern and southeastern regions of the United States.8,12

Eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEE) is considered the most virulent of the naturally occurring encephalitic viruses in North America, with a reported fatality rate of 75%-90% in equids.7,13 Despite the severity of clinical signs in affected horses, there is limited detailed and comprehensive information regarding the epidemiology of the disease, clinical presentation, disease progression, and outcomes in equine cases. Therefore, the objective of this study was to describe the epidemiological patterns and clinicopathologic features of both adult horses and foals diagnosed with EEE and hospitalized at a single veterinary teaching hospital. A secondary aim was to identify clinical and laboratory variables associated with the survival of horses with EEE.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective case series study.

Criteria for inclusion of study horses

The medical record database at the University of Florida Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital (UF) between June 1979 and October 2017 was reviewed, and records for all horses presenting with signs of diffuse brain disease were evaluated. Horses were included in this study if the following inclusion criteria were met: (1) clinical signs consistent with diffuse brain disease; and (2) serologic or molecular confirmation of EEE, or postmortem findings consistent with a diagnosis of EEE. Cases were excluded if available data were insufficient to confirm a diagnosis of EEE. Serological testing considered consistent with a diagnosis of EEE were a hemagglutination inhibition (HI) titer for EEE ≥ 4-fold higher than that for WEE, or the identification of immunoglobulin M antibody through capture ELISA at a single dilution of > 1:400. Postmortem findings consistent with a diagnosis of EEE was histologic evidence of diffuse meningoencephalitis with neuronal degeneration, marked perivascular cuffing (neutrophilic or mononuclear), and microglial proliferation14–16 Molecular testing (immunohistochemistry, PCR) on brain tissue also provided a confirmatory diagnosis of EEE.

Data collection

Data recorded in the medical record at admission were retrieved and entered a computer database (Microsoft Excel). Not all data were available for all horses. Data collected included: age, sex, breed, body weight (actual or estimated), season (spring, summer, fall, and winter), clinical signs before presentation (including inappetence (yes or no), colic (yes or no), sweating (yes or no) and neurologic abnormalities such as levels of consciousness (normal, obtunded, stuporous, comatose), abnormal behavior, deglutition disorder (yes or no), tongue paralysis (yes or no), vestibular signs (yes or no), abnormal gait (yes or no), tremor (yes or no), duration of clinical signs (≤2 or >2 days), EEE vaccination history (number and timing). Clinical data recorded at the time of admission included: rectal temperature (<99.5°F, ≥99.5 and <101.5°F, or ≥101.5°F; < 37.5 °C, ≥37.5 and <38.6 °C, or ≥38.6 °C), heart rate (≤40 or >40 beats per minute), and respiratory rate (≤12 or >12 breaths per minute), Neurologic variables were based on admission assessment: level of consciousness, abnormal behavior, menace response (left and right, normal or abnormal), pupillary light response (left and right, normal or abnormal), presence of nystagmus (left and right, physiological or pathological), recumbency (yes or no), or seizure-like activity (yes or no).

Clinicopathologic data recorded at admission included: CBC [total white blood cell (WBC) count, PCV, count of myelocytes, metamyelocytes, band neutrophils, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes and eosinophils] and total protein (TP) and fibrinogen concentrations, serum biochemistry profile including alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate transaminase (AST), γ-glutamyl transferase, creatine kinase activities and concentration of total bilirubin, albumin, globulin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, glucose, triglycerides, bile acids, magnesium, sodium, potassium, chloride, total calcium, phosphorus, and blood ammonia. Selected venous blood gas analysis variables (pH and concentrations of bicarbonate) and concentration of lactate were also recorded.

Data related to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis: collection site (atlantoaxial or lumbosacral), color (normal defined as clear or colorless), protein concentration (reference range [rr]: defined as ≤80 mg/dL), count of nucleated cells (rr: ≤6/μL) and total red blood cell (rr: ≤100/μL); as well as proportions of neutrophils (rr: ≤30%), lymphocytes (rr: ≤ 70%), and monocytes (rr: ≤30%). Results of serologic testing (HI and MAC-ELISA) were also included.

Other recorded variables included duration of hospitalization (≤1 day, >1 and ≤2 days, or >2 days), clinical progression (recumbency or seizure-like activity during hospitalization), survival to discharge (yes or no), and results of postmortem examination.

Annual temperature (recorded in Celsius) and precipitation (millimeters for liquid depth) for the study period were obtained from the Florida Climate Center office of the State climatologist.17,18

Statistical methods

Data were summarized using descriptive statistics. A Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess for normality, and the data were reported as a percentage of the total value, or as median and range (non-normally distributed). Signalment, season, historical data, clinical data, and results of laboratory analyses were evaluated for the association with non-survival using logistic regression. No differentiation was made between horses that died and those that were euthanized. Univariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors associated with the survival of EEE horses. Percentages were calculated based on the number of horses assessed per variable because data were occasionally missing from the medical record, and certain diagnostics were not performed on all animals. The cause of missing values was considered “missing at random” because most of the missing data resulted from not measuring a given variable in a particular horse, and the reason for the missing data was unrelated to the outcome. Therefore, analysis was conducted based on the complete cases.19 Factors were excluded if the P-value was >.05. Odds ratios were then calculated for factors significantly associated with survival (P ≤ .05). Odds ratios are presented as the point estimate and 95% CI. Associations between climatic variables (annual mean temperature and annual total precipitation) and annual cases were initially explored using Spearman correlation coefficients. Generalized linear models were used to evaluate associations between climatic predictors and annual case count. Overdispersion was assessed by comparing the variance and mean of the outcome variable and by estimating the dispersion parameter. Negative binomial regression model with a log link function was fitted. The multivariable model included annual mean temperature, annual precipitation, and calendar year to account for temporal trends. Results were reported as incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% CI. For clinical interpretability, IRRs were additionally adjusted per 100-mm increase in annual precipitation. A Kruskal–Wallis test was performed to evaluate differences in duration of clinical signs between horses with mononuclear and neutrophilic pleocytosis. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. Statistical analyses were conducted using a commercially available statistical software program (Minitab, LLC. (2021). Minitab. Retrieved from https://www.minitab.com).

Results

Horses

One hundred and four horses met the inclusion criteria. A total of 51 females (49%), 38 males (37%), and 15 (14%) geldings were included. There were 32 (31%) Thoroughbreds, 18 (17%) Quarter Horses, 13 (12%) Arabs, 8 (7%) American Paints, 5 (5%) Draft or Draft cross (including Clydesdale, Percheron, Gypsy Vanner), 5 (5%) Paso horses (including Paso Fino, Peruvian Paso), 4 (4%) American Miniature Horses, 4 (4%) ponies (including Shetland Pony, Welsh Pony) 3 (3%) Appaloosa, 3 (3%) Tennessee Walking Horses, 3 (3%) Warmbloods, 2 (2%) Haflingers, 2 (2%) Morgans, and 1 (1%) Friesian. The breed was not recorded for 1 case (1%).

Age

The median age at presentation was 1.4 years (range: 0.1-13.4 years), with 78% (81/104) of horses younger than 3 years old (Figure 1).

Vaccination history

Information on vaccination history was available for 74% (77/104) of horses, but data on the actual vaccination schedule were limited. Sixty-five cases (84%; 65/77) had received one or more vaccinations against the EEE virus within the lifetime (including preparturient vaccination of mares). Thirty-three cases (51%; 33/65) had received more than one vaccination within the lifetime. Information regarding the timing of the most recent vaccination against the EEE virus was available for 58% (60/104) of the horses (Figure 2). Based on available data, only 12 cases (24%; 12/50) had received multiple vaccines, with the most recent dose administered within the past 6 months. Vaccination history was not significantly associated with survival (OR 3.21; 95% CI, 0.17-59.86; P = .59).

Vaccination intervals were categorized as ≤1, 1-6, 6-12, and >12 months. Information regarding the most recent vaccination was available for 60 of 104 horses (58%). Data for the years 2006 through 2009 were unavailable.

Horses were presented year-round, with most admitted in the summer (63%; 66/104), followed by spring (17%; 18/104). Cases in fall (11%; 11/104) and winter (9%; 9/104) were less common. The highest number of horses was presented in July. The monthly distribution of cases is shown in Figure 4. Rectal temperature before admission was recorded in 65% (68/104) of cases and ranged from 98.9 to 107.0°F (median: 104.6°F). Duration of clinical signs before admission ranged from 0.1 to 7 days (median: 1 day). Neurologic abnormalities, including altered mentation, abnormal gait, recumbency, vestibular signs, blindness and seizure-like activity, were reported in most cases (Table 1). Clinical signs at admission

Common clinical signs identified at the time of admission are shown in Table 1, with abnormal mentation (96 %; 95/98), tachypnea (94 %; 75/80), and tachycardia (87 %; 78/90) being the most common. Recumbency was reported in 51% (52/101) of cases. The rectal temperature at the time of admission was recorded for 88% (92/104) of the cases and ranged from 96.4 to 107.0°F (median: 101.1°F). Hyperthermia was detected in 46% (42/92) of cases (Table 1).

Hematology, biochemistry profile, and venous blood gas analysis

Hematologic variables were available for 79% (82/104) of the cases; however, several data points were missing (Table S1). Alterations in the PCV (<30% and >44%) and total protein (<6 and >8 g/dL) were identified in 36% (30/82) and 12% (10/82), respectively. An abnormal total white blood cell count was reported in 57% (46/80) of the cases. Among these, 85% (39/46) had leukocytosis (WBC > 11 600 cells/μL), which was mostly attributed to neutrophilia (>5700 cells/μL, 100%; 39/39). Cell count was reported in 96% (77/80) of the horses. Alterations in neutrophil count (<2600 and >6700 cells/μL) were present in most cases (73%; 56/77), largely due to neutrophilia (68%; 52/77) with variable evidence of left shifting (>100 bands/μL; 49%; 38/77). Monocytosis (>700 cells/μL) was also frequently observed (47%; 36/77), and changes in lymphocyte count were relatively rare (15%; 12/77). Hyperfibrinogenemia (>400 mg/dL) was reported in 56% (44/79) of horses.

Serum biochemistry analysis was available for 76% (79/104) of the cases, but missing data points were frequent (Table S1). Most common abnormalities included hyperbilirubinemia (>1.9 mg/dL; 87%; 54/62), high ALP (>228 IU/L; 72%; 46/64) and AST (>228 IU/L; 63 %; 37/59) activities, hyperglycemia (>128 mg/dL; 70%; 48/69), hypertriglyceridemia (>80 mg/dL; 34%; 19/56), and azotemia (>2 mg/dL; 28%; 21/76;). Electrolyte derangements included hyponatremia (<136 mmol/L; 52%; 41/79) and hypochloremia (<96 mmol/L; 37%; 29/79). Blood ammonia concentration was available for 16 horses, with 31% (5/16) having hyperammonemia (>40 μmol/L). Serum bile acids were available for 5 cases, with 2 horses having elevated concentrations (6 mg/dL).

Venous blood gas measurements were available for 33% (34/104) of the horses, but several data points were missing. Of these, acidemia (pH < 7.35) was reported in 24% (8/34) and alkalemia (pH > 7.45) in 26% (9/34) horses. Bicarbonate concentration was decreased (<24 mmol/L) in 61% (17/28) of the cases, otherwise normal in 32% (9/28) of the horses. Hyperlactatemia (>2 mmol/L) was detected in 77% (10/13) of the horses.

Cerebrospinal fluid analysis

Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was available for 87% (90/104) of the horses, but some data points were missing. The site of collection was available for 99% (89/90) of horses. The most frequently sampled site was the atlantooccipital cistern (73%; 65/89) compared to the lumbosacral space (27%; 24/89). Color was typically abnormal (65%; 56/86) and mostly characterized as yellow or xanthochromic (60%; 52/86). Additional variables are presented in Table 2. The most common findings were increased protein concentration (80%, 66/83) and a high nucleated cell count (87%; 78/90). CSF pleocytosis was typically attributed to increased numbers of neutrophils (62%; 48/78), followed by monocytes (22%; 17/78) and lymphocytes (16%; 12/78). There is no statistically significant difference in duration of clinical signs before admission between horses with mononuclear (median: 1, IQR: 1-2) and neutrophilic pleocytosis (median: 1, IQR: 1-2) (P > .05).

Serologic testing

Ante-mortem serologic testing by either hemagglutination-inhibition or MAC-ELISA (IgM capture ELISA) was performed in 61% (63/104) of the horses and yielded positive results in 79% (50/63) of the horses.

Case progression

Duration of hospitalization ranged from 0 to 13 days (median: 1 day). Records regarding cases progressing during hospitalization were available for 94% (98/104) of the horses. Seventy-six percent (74/98) of the horses became recumbent during hospitalization, and 51 (52%; 51/99) cases developed seizure-like activity.

Fatality rate

The case fatality rate was 90% (94/104). Of these, 90% (85/94) were euthanized, and the remaining cases died (10%; 9/94). Ten horses survived hospital discharge, with 4 horses attaining a complete recovery and 6 having residual neurological deficits. However, information of the specific remaining neurological deficits was not available.

Of the survivors, 7 were females, and 3 were males, with a median age of 1.5 years (range: 0.32-3 years). Seasonal distribution at presentation included 2 cases in winter, 1 in spring, 3 in summer, and 4 in fall. Seven of the survivors were vaccinated against the EEE virus, with 5 being vaccinated within one month of disease onset and 2 within the preceding 6 months. The median duration of clinical signs before hospital admission was 2.5 days (range: 1-6 days). At the time of admission, all 10 horses were obtunded, and 8 had abnormal gait. Six horses were febrile, 1 was recumbent, and 2 exhibited seizures. On CBC, 1 horse had a high number of bands, and another 1 had a high number of monocytes. All horses had serum triglyceride concentrations within the reference range on admission. The CSF analysis showed high protein concentrations in 5 horses and neutrophilic pleocytosis in 1. During hospitalization, 2 additional horses developed seizures and 2 became recumbent. The median duration of hospitalization among survivors was 9 days (range: 6-9 days).

Additional positive predictors of non-survival were the presence of band neutrophils or monocytosis on hematology and high concentrations of protein or neutrophils on CSF analysis.

Discussion

The study describes the history, clinical presentation, pathological findings, and outcomes of a large cohort of horses diagnosed with EEE presented to a single teaching hospital in the Southeastern United States. Most of the horses were presented during the summer months, with the highest number of cases in July. These seasonal patterns reflect the activity of mosquito vectors in Florida.20,21 However, the detection of EEE cases throughout the year highlights the year-round risk in subtropical climates.8 Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for EEE in horses presenting with abnormal behavior, hyperthermia, recumbency, and seizure-like activity, even during the less common seasons for this disease. Our findings suggest that young animals, particularly weanlings and yearlings, might be more susceptible to EEE virus infection. In warm regions with high mosquito populations, early initiation of vaccination and biannual booster administration should be strongly considered. Ideally, one dose should be administered in late spring to ensure adequate immunity during peak mosquito activity and the highest-risk month.

The clinical signs reported in this cohort of horses with EEE agree with those previously reported14,22,23 and align with the neuroinvasive behavior of the virus.14 The neurologic findings in this cohort were consistent with diffuse or multifocal intracranial disease, with clinical signs localizing to the forebrain (behavioral changes, seizure-like activity) and brainstem (altered mentation, vestibular dysfunction, deglutition disorder). Gait abnormalities could have been associated with impaired consciousness or involvement of brainstem proprioceptive pathways. Spinal cord disease could have contributed to alterations in gait. Given the retrospective design, the relative contribution of individual central nervous system structures to the observed gait abnormalities could not be determined. In addition, tachycardia and tachypnea, particularly when irregular and observed in recumbent horses without identifiable primary cardiopulmonary disease, might reflect dysfunction of brainstem autonomic centers; however, contributions from hyperthermia, whether infectious or centrally mediated, cannot be excluded. These signs can be attributed to the characteristic neuropathological lesions induced by the virus. These include polioencephalomyelitis, leptomeningitis, meningoencephalitis, vasculitis, perivascular lymphocytic or neutrophilic cuffing, hemorrhage, thrombosis, gliosis, neuronophagia, and microabscessation.14–16,24 These lesions are mostly identified in the cerebral cortex, thalamus, hypothalamus, and mesencephalon, although similar pathological changes have also been documented in the spinal cord.14

Clinically, differentiating EEE from other viral encephalitides can be challenging. Western equine encephalitis (WEE) is typically milder, and horses can recover if they remain ambulatory.8 Venezuelan equine encephalitis (VEE) more often presents with prominent systemic signs, including high fever, diarrhea, colic, and dehydration, in addition to neurologic abnormalities.25 West Nile virus (WNV) encephalitis is commonly associated with neuromuscular signs such as muzzle or lip twitching, asymmetric ataxia, and hyperesthesia.26 In contrast, equine herpes virus (EHV-1) myeloencephalopathy primarily affects the spinal cord, resulting in urinary incontinence, tail hypotonia, and symmetric hindlimb weakness.27 Rabies is distinguished by marked behavioral changes, including aggression or mania, and progressive paralysis.28

In addition to central nervous system involvement, the EEE virus can induce extraneural lesions, with reports describing myocarditis and gastroenteritis contributing to the systemic manifestations of the disease and could, at least in part, explain the tachycardia, colic signs, and high blood ammonia frequently reported in the horses included in our study.14,29,30 Hyperammonemia could have contributed to encephalopathy; however, because of the retrospective design, causality and the independent contribution of hyperammonemia to the observed clinical signs cannot be established.

The CSF analysis provided antemortem insights into the inflammatory response in EEE. Most horses had elevated CSF protein concentrations and nucleated cell count, with a predominance of neutrophils. This neutrophilic pleocytosis, while not specific to EEE, is consistent with rapidly progressive inflammatory encephalitis and can support a diagnosis of EEE in the appropriate clinical and geographic context.8 In humans with EEE, CSF analysis typically demonstrates an inflammatory response characterized by an initial neutrophilic pleocytosis that later transitions to a predominantly lymphocytic pattern.31 Serial CSF analyses were not performed in the horses included in this study, particularly in those with neutrophilic pleocytosis; therefore, it was not possible to determine whether a similar temporal shift in cellular predominance occurs in horses. In our study, no statistically significant association was identified between duration of clinical signs and the type of pleocytosis observed on CSF analysis. Elevated CSF protein and neutrophil count were also associated with non-survival, potentially reflecting more severe or advanced central nervous system inflammation. Histopathologic examination consistently demonstrated hallmark lesions of viral encephalitis, including neuronal necrosis, perivascular cuffing, and microglial proliferation, corroborating the clinical and laboratory diagnosis in 99% of the horses included in this study.13–15

In our study, the median time from onset of clinical signs to hospital presentation was 1 day (range: 0.08-7 days), and the median duration of hospitalization for non-survivors was also 1 day (range: 0-7 days), consistent with previous reports. The rapid progression observed in these horses is similar to that described for VEE,28 which can be even more peracute. Rabies also progresses rapidly once clinical signs develop and is invariably fatal.28 In contrast, WEE generally follows a lengthier course (1-2 weeks),27 WNV progression is variable (days to weeks),26 and EHV-1 typically evolves over several days.27

The case fatality rate in this study was exceptionally high (90%) but consistent with previous reports that describe EEE as one of the most lethal viral encephalitides affecting horses.7,13,32 The few survivors in our cohort demonstrated certain features that could inform prognosis. Most were young animals with a median age of 1.5 years (all <3 years), had received recent vaccination against the EEE virus, presented with a longer duration of clinical signs before hospital admission, and were less frequently recumbent or having seizures at presentation or during hospitalization. These results suggest that vaccination, subacute presentation, and milder neurologic involvement at admission can be associated with improved outcomes, although the small number of survivors limits robust statistical conclusions.

Our study had several limitations, the most notable being the retrospective design, which could have introduced both selection and information bias. Medical histories, clinical evaluations, diagnostic testing, treatments, and decisions regarding euthanasia were not standardized across cases, potentially impacting the consistency and interpretation of findings. In addition, many data points were missing or inconsistently reported. This variability limited the ability to perform more robust statistical analyses and could have introduced bias or reduced the power to detect significant associations. Finally, all horses were presented to a single teaching hospital in Florida, which might not fully represent the spectrum of disease presentation, management, or outcomes in other geographic regions. Therefore, caution should be taken when extrapolating our findings to a different population. The high case fatality rate observed might also reflect a referral bias toward more severe cases.

Despite these limitations, our study highlights the high case fatality rate and characteristic clinical features of EEE in horses. Our findings suggest that milder neurologic presentation and certain CSF parameters might offer prognostic information. Given the rapid progression and limited treatment options, preventive strategies, particularly timely vaccination, remain critical in reducing the burden of EEE in equine populations.
