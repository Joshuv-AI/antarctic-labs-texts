Association of preoperative cognitive frailty with postoperative complications in older patients under general anesthesia: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Jiamin Fang, Hao Liang, Muxin Chen, Yidi Zhao, Lin Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive frailty (CF) is characterized by the coexistence of physical frailty and cognitive impairment, and it is associated with adverse health outcomes. Older adults are particularly vulnerable to CF due to factors such as age-related brain changes and the presence of comorbidities.ObjectiveTo investigate the effect of preoperative CF on postoperative complications in older patients.MethodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted among 253 patients aged 60–85 years, who underwent elective orthopedic and abdominal surgery (with a postoperative hospital stay of ≥ 3 days) at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine from May 2023 to November 2023. CF was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) for the cognitive status and the Fried criteria for five frailty scales. Participants were split into four groups: Group A (neither frailty nor cognitive impairment), Group B (frailty without cognitive impairment), Group C (cognitive impairment without frailty), and Group D (cognitive frailty). The primary outcome was postoperative complications, while secondary outcomes included mobility disability, prolonged hospital stay (PLOS), re-operation and 90-day readmission.ResultsThe median age (interquartile range) of participants was 69 (65–73) years, of which 40.3% were male. The prevalence of CF was 17.8%. The incidence of postoperative complications was 18.2% in Group A, 50.0% in Group B, 37.4% in Group C, and 75.6% in Group D. Multivariate analysis revealed that, compared to the control group (without cognitive impairment or frailty), patients with CF had a significantly higher risk of postoperative complications (OR, 12.86; 95%CI, 4.23–39.08). “Patients with frailty without cognitive impairment” had an increased risk (OR, 6.53; 95%CI, 2.04–20.9), while “those with cognitive impairment without frailty” also showed a higher risk (OR, 3.46; 95%CI, 1.57–7.64).ConclusionsCognitive frailty is significantly associated with an increased risk of postoperative adverse outcomes in older patients undergoing orthopedic and abdominal surgeries with general anesthesia. It indicates that clinicians should pay much attention to these older adults with CF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05431-1.

Introduction

With the aging global population and advancements in surgical techniques, an increasing number of older patients are receiving anesthesia and undergoing surgery. Statistics indicate that approximately 313 million surgeries are performed worldwide each year, with one-third of these patients being older adults [1]. With the continuous development of surgical evaluation methods, many scholars believe that a patient ‘s own physiological status has a great impact on postoperative prognosis than the surgery itself [2]. Older surgical patients face substantial challenges due to physiological changes such as bodily dysfunction, cognitive decline, and diminished stress response capacity [3]. As a result, they are more susceptible to postoperative complications, increased medical burdens, and decreased quality of life compared to younger populations. Consequently, it is crucial to identify modifiable risk factors early and provide supportive interventions to promote healthy ageing.

Frailty is a complex geriatric syndrome recognized as an independent risk factor for postoperative adverse outcomes in older adults [4, 5]. Cognitive frailty (CF), a subtype of frailty, is defined as a geriatric syndrome characterized by the coexistence of physical frailty and cognitive impairment, excluding dementia [6]. Unlike cognitive impairment resulting from neurodegenerative disorders, CF reflects a preclinical cognitive state primarily driven by physical frailty. It is distinct from other cognitive conditions such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI), Alzheimer’s disease, and delirium. Although individuals with CF may show similar cognitive deficits on screening tests, their structural and neurophysiological profiles differ from those with MCI or Alzheimer’s disease [7, 8]. Furthermore, unlike delirium, which is an acute and typically reversible condition, CF is chronic and progressive [9].

Previous studies have demonstrated that CF increases the risk of adverse outcomes, including falls, disability, depression, and mortality, among community-dwelling older adults [10–14]. However, its impact on postoperative recovery in older surgical patients remains unclear. To address this gap, we conducted a prospective cohort study to examine the effect of preoperative CF on short-term postoperative recovery in older patients. The aim is to identify the risk of poor prognosis in older surgical patients with CF as early as possible, providing a basis for healthcare professionals to develop preoperative optimization strategies and reference for early clinical interventions. We hypothesize that older patients with CF are associated with higher risks of postoperative complications and other adverse outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

A prospective cohort design was employed. The participants were recruited from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine from May 2023 to November 2023. Criteria for selecting the participants were as follows: (1) age 60 to 85 years; (2) patients were scheduled for elective orthopedic or abdominal surgery under general anesthesia; (3) the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification is I-III; (4) Mini-Cognitive Assessment (Mini-cog) scores>0; (5) have good language expression ability, communicate with the investigators without obstacles; Voluntary participation in the study and signed informed consent. Elimination criteria were as follows: (1) participants with a diagnosis of dementia; (2) history of severe neurological or psychiatric disease; (3) serious audiovisual impairments that impacted assessment; (4) postoperative hospital stay < 3 days; (5) unable to cooperate with completing the relevant assessment.

We included patients undergoing elective orthopedic surgeries, such as total hip or knee arthroplasty (THA or TKA), femoral head arthroplasty, cervical, lumbar, or thoracic spinal fusion, as well as open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) for fractures. Abdominal surgeries included hepatectomy, cholecystectomy, and gastric or intestinal tumor resection. General anesthesia was administered using either inhalational or intravenous anesthetic agents to induce unconsciousness and muscle relaxation. In some cases, epidural anesthesia or peripheral nerve blocks were incorporated into a multimodal analgesia approach alongside general anesthesia. Patients were categorized into four groups: Group A (neither frail nor cognitive impairment), Group B (frailty without cognitive impairment), Group C (cognitive impairment without frailty), and Group D (cognitive frailty).

The trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry in 17/05/2023, ChiCTR2300071535 and approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medical (BE2022-165‐01). We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment.

Measures

Demographic characteristics and clinical data

Demographic characteristics included sex, age, marital status, educational, pre-retirement occupation, smoking, and drinking. Clinical and physical variables comprised body mass index(BMI), multiple medication (defined as the concurrent use of five or more drugs) [16], History of stress(within 1 year), the age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (aCCI) [17], activities of daily living (ADL, assessed using the Barthel Index, BI) [18], nutritional status (evaluated by the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002, NRS2002) [19], pain (measured by the Numerical Rating Scale, NRS) [20], sleep quality (measured by the Athens Insomnia Scale, AIS) [21], and depression (measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire, PHQ) [22]. Surgery-related data included the ASA score, surgery category, surgery time(min), use of an analgesia pump, preemptive analgesia, and intraoperative blood loss(ml). Demographic, clinical, and physical variables were collected within 24 h of hospital admission through face-to-face interviews in the ward. Surgical data were extracted from electronic medical records within 24 h post-surgery.

Criteria of cognitive frailty

According to the criteria proposed jointly by the International Academy on Nutrition and Aging (I.A.N.A) and the International Association of Gerontology and Geriatrics (I.A.G.G) in 2013, cognitive frailty is defined as the simultaneous presence of both physical frailty and cognitive impairment, without dementia [6]. The assessment methods for frailty and cognitive function are outlined below.

Frailty measures

The frailty status of the older adults was assessed using the FRAIL scale, which includes five domains (Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illness, and Loss of weight) [23, 24]. The Frail scale scores range from 0 to 5, with 1 point assigned per domain (0 = best, 5 = worst). patients could be classified into three groups: frail (3–5), pre-frail (1–2), and robust (0). The Frail Scale was measured at baseline. The details of scales used in the current study can be found in the Supplementary Material Table S1.

Cognitive function measures

Cognitive function was assessed using the Chinese version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-Chinese Beijing version) scale [25]. Patients with a MoCA score of ≥ 26 were classified as cognitively normal, while those with a score of ≤ 25 were classified as cognitive impairment. To counterbalance the effect of lower education, 1 point was added to the final score of individuals with less than 12 years of education. Furthermore, several methods were implemented to enhance assessment quality and methodological reliability, including the following: (1) The researchers conducting the MoCA assessment had obtained certification through the official website; (2) All tests were administered under the supervision of staff who had been trained by neuropsychologists prior to the study; (3) Data collection was performed by the same researcher to minimize measurement bias. The MoCA scale was collected at baseline.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was the occurrence of postoperative complications within (5 ± 2) days after surgery. These complications were assessed using the Clavien–Dindo classification system (CD) [26]. Postoperative complications were defined as any events with a grade ≥ 2. Mild complications, classified as grade II, primarily included postoperative infections, hypoalbuminemia, and electrolyte disturbances. Severe complications, classified as grades 3–5 (CD3 complications), mainly included cardiovascular-related complications (heart failure, arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, angina pectoris), respiratory-related complications (respiratory failure), neurological complications (stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, delirium), thrombotic complications (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism), and ICU admission.

The secondary outcomes included: (1) Mobility disability. Mobility disability was assessed using the Barthel Index (BI) within (5 ± 2) days after surgery, with a BI score ≤ 60 indicating “yes“ [27]; (2) Prolonged length of stay (PLOS). PLOS was defined as a length of stay greater than the 75th percentile of the total average hospital stay (from admission to discharge) [28]. PLOS was defined as more than 14 days, based on the 75th percentile of the total average hospital stay in our cohort. (3) Reoperation, defined as any reoperation occurring within 90 days after surgery; (4) 90-day readmission, defined as any readmission occurring within 90 days postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages (%), and comparisons between groups were made using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between two groups using the independent sample t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For non-normally distributed data, values were expressed as median [M (P25, P75)] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U-test. The association between preoperative CF and postoperative adverse outcomes was analyzed using multivariate logistic regression. Multicollinearity was assessed by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF), with variables having a VIF > 10 removed from the analysis [29] (Supplementary Material: Table S2). Variables with p ≤ 0.1 in univariate analysis, along with those deemed clinically relevant, were included in a multivariate analysis using forward stepwise logistic regression [30] (Supplementary Material: Table S3-S4). The final model identified age, smoking, drinking, ADL, preemptive analgesia, and CF as significant influencing factors. Given that logistic regression typically requires adjustment for one confounder per 5–10 events in the group with fewer occurrences [31], the number of positive postoperative events in our study allowed for the inclusion of only 8 confounders. Therefore, based on the prioritization of confounders, we included three additional key variables—aCCI, NRS2002, and surgery category—that have been identified in previous studies as important factors influencing postoperative complications [32, 33]. Four models were constructed. Model 1 did not adjust for any variables. Model 2 adjusted for general characteristics, including age, smoking, and drinking. Model 3 further adjusted for physical variables, including aCCI, NRS2002, and ADL base on Model 2. In Model 4, We additionally adjusted for surgery-related variables, such as surgery category and preemptive analgesia. Finally, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed to further explore the association between CF and postoperative complications across different surgery categories. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 324 patients were assessed for eligibility, among which 253 patients were finally analyzed. It included 77 patients (30.4%) in Group A (neither frailty nor cognitive impairment), 24 patients (9.5%) in Group B (frailty without cognitive impairment), 107 patients (42.3%) in Group C (cognitive impairment without frailty), and 45 patients (17.8%) in Group D (cognitive frailty). The enrollment flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. The demographic and clinical characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Cognitive frailty was found to be significantly more prevalent among individuals who were older, had lower education levels, higher aCCI scores, higher AIS scores, and higher PHQ scores, as well as lower ADL scores.

Postoperative outcomes

A total of 100 patients experienced complications classified as Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ II, including 18 older patients who experienced CD3 complications (grades ≥ III). Figures 2 and 3 illustrate the frequencies of Grade II and CD3 complications, respectively, among the four groups. There were differences in postoperative complications, mobility disability, and PLOS among the four groups. However, no significant differences were observed in the reoperation and 90-day readmission among the four groups (P = 0.092) (Table 2).

Discussion

This is the first study to explore the association between preoperative CF and adverse outcomes in older patients undergoing orthopedic and abdominal surgery with general anesthesia. Our findings indicate that cognitive frailty, frailty, and cognitive impairment all increase the risk of postoperative complications, with CF posing the highest risk. The robustness of the associations between preoperative CF and postoperative outcomes in different surgery categories was confirmed through subgroup analyses. Furthermore, CF is also an independent risk factor for mobility disability and PLOS.

In clinical settings, the prevalence of CF among older adults is relatively high, likely due to disease-related factors that reduce physiological reserve, immunity, and stress response capacity. In our study, the prevalence of CF among older patients undergoing elective orthopedic or abdominal surgery with general anesthesia was 17.8%, which is similar to the prevalence reported in geriatric day-hospitals for intermediate care and geriatric wards (14.3 -19.7%) [15, 34]. Compared to those in a clinical setting, the prevalence of CF in community-dwelling older adults (1 − 8.7%) is lower [35–37]. It may be attributed to differences in diagnostic criteria and screening tools, as well as the fact that hospitalized patients are often more physically compromised due to illness. Therefore, it is necessary to establish standardized diagnostic criteria for CF and to enhance early identification and intervention in hospitalized older patients to reduce postoperative complications and improve outcomes.

We found that CF was an independent risk factor for postoperative complications in older patients undergoing general anesthesia. Statistics revealed that the older adults with CF were more likely to have postoperative complications than those with either frailty alone or cognitive impairment alone. A review has shown that physical frailty and cognitive dysfunction have similar pathophysiological mechanisms, including mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and endocrine disorders [38]. Frailty is a geriatric syndrome resulting from age-related cumulative decline across multiple physiologic systems, impaired homeostatic reserve, and reduced capacity to resist stress [39]. Prior research has shown that frailty is an important factor in risk stratification and outcome prediction [40, 41], and it was associated with an increased risk for postoperative complications, longer hospital stays, healthcare burdens, and other survival outcomes [42–44]. Cognitive dysfunction refers to varying degrees of cognitive impairment stemming from different causes, and it is also known to be associated with an increased risk of adverse events, complications from coexisting medical conditions, decreased mobility, higher healthcare utilization, and substantial caregiver burden [45, 46]. Cognitive frailty represents the combination of these two conditions. The coexistence of both can accelerate the decline of physical and cognitive functions creating a vicious cycle that results in more serious cumulative effects on the body [47, 48]. This is consistent with the results of our study, which show that older adults with CF had the highest risk of postoperative complications compared to those with frailty alone or cognitive impairment alone.

Different from previous research [46, 49, 50], our study found no significant association between frailty or cognitive impairment with postoperative mobility disability or PLOS. This could be attributed to the categorization of different states of frailty and cognitive impairment into four distinct groups. In our categorization, patients with CF were no longer classified into the Group B or Group C, resulting in a relatively lower incidence of frail and cognitive impairment. Additionally, it may also be influenced by the use of different assessment scales for frailty and cognitive impairment.

A substantial amount of research has demonstrated an association between CF in community-dwelling older adults and various adverse outcomes [51–53], and many communities have incorporated CF into their assessments. However, due to the unclear impact of preoperative CF on postoperative recovery in older adults, many countries have not yet included it in clinical practice assessments. Our study indicates that preoperative CF is associated with adverse postoperative outcomes in older patients. It suggests that healthcare professionals need to pay more attention to CF in older patients. Besides, it also provides clinical evidence for early screening and intervention for CF.

One strength of this study is that the research is the first study to investigate the relationship between preoperative CF and adverse outcomes in older patients undergoing orthopedic or abdominal with general anesthesia. Previous studies on CF have mainly focused on the community, with limited studies on hospitalized patients, particularly those undergoing surgery. Older adults are more vulnerable to stressors due to reduced physiological reserves and a diminished ability to maintain homeostasis. And surgery as a major physical and psychological stressor, can further deplete their limited reserves and lead to decompensation [54]. In addition, we specifically focused on high-risk surgeries, such as orthopedic and abdominal procedures, which are common among older patients. By selecting these higher-risk surgeries, this study provides insights into the impact of CF on postoperative outcomes in older adults undergoing surgery under general anesthesia.

There are some limitations to our study. First, this is a single-center study primarily focused on abdominal and orthopedic surgeries, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, owing to the limited sample size, confounders could not be fully adjusted for, which may potentially affect the robustness of the results. Third, some self-reported parameters are subjective in nature, which may lead to measurement bias. Fourth, Given that different surgeries and anesthesia management can have varying impacts on cognitive function. Delirium, as a significant postoperative cognitive complication, was not included in our analysis due to its occurrence in only three cases. Future research could explore the effect of preoperative CF on postoperative delirium. Lastly, the long term effects of CF on postoperative recovery in older patients were not assessed. Hence, further multi-centre, large-sample trials are needed to investigate the long-term impacts of CF on postoperative recovery in older patients and explore the effective treatment for CF.

Conclusions

Although screening for CF is not currently a routine clinical practice for hospitalized patients, leading to its potential risk being overlooked by medical staff. Our study demonstrates that CF is an independent risk factor for adverse postoperative outcomes in older patients undergoing orthopedic and abdominal surgeries with general anesthesia. This finding highlights the importance for clinicians to pay much attention to older adults with CF. Given that existing assessment tools can be time-consuming, future research should focus on developing a more convenient and efficient tool for identifying CF in clinical settings, enabling early interventions to mitigate the risk of adverse postoperative outcomes.
