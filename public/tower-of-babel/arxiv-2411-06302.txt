Fourier Dimension and Translation Invariant Linear Equations
Angel D. Cruz

Abstract
. We consider a translation invariant linear equation in four variables with integer coefficients of the form: a ​ x 1 + b ​ x 2 = c ​ y 1 + d ​ y 2 ax_{1}+bx_{2}=cy_{1}+dy_{2} . The main result of the paper states that any set on the real line with Fourier dimension greater than 1 / 2 1/2 must contain a nontrivial solution of such an equation.



1.
Introduction

We investigate the following type of equations:

(1.1)

ax_{1}+bx_{2}=cy_{1}+dy_{2}\text{ where }\{a,b,c,d\}\subset\mathbb{N}\text{ obeying }a+b=c+d

and
\{x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Such equations are examples of
translation invariant linear equations
with integer coefficients.
Translation invariant because any
\{x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\}
that satisfies (
1.1
) and any
t\in\mathbb{R}
the collection
\{x_{1}+t,x_{2}+t,y_{1}+t,y_{2}+t\}
remains a solution to (
1.1
) by the condition
a+b=c+d
.
These equations are widely studied in many areas of mathematics including harmonic analysis, combinatorics and number theory; see
[
11
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
2
]
and
[
4
]
.
When
a=b=c=d
, (
1.1
) reduces to
x_{1}+x_{2}=y_{1}+y_{2}
, an equation central to the study of Sidon sets.
Another special case occurs when
y_{1}=y_{2}=y
but
x_{1}\not=x_{2}
, in this case (
1.1
) reduces to
x_{1}+x_{2}=2y
and
\{x_{1},x_{2},y\}
lie in an arithmetic progression.
This type of equation is also a translation invariant linear equation and is studied in
[
8
]
with respect to the
size
of a set that must contain a
nontrivial solution
to (
1.1
).

In our current work, we consider the following question

(1.2)

\text{How ``large" must a set $E\subset[0,1]$ be to ensure it contains}\\
\text{a ``nontrivial" solution to an equation of the form \eqref{4variable equation}?}

The words “large” and “nontrivial” are open to interpretation and we start with the latter.
For a given
\{a,b,c,d\}
obeying
a+b=c+d
and
E\subset[0,1]
we define the set of solutions
\mathcal{S}
of (
1.1
) in
E
as follows

\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{S}(E,\{a,b,c,d\})=\{(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\in E^{4}:ax_{1}+bx_{2}=cy_{1}+dy_{2}\}.

This set is always nonempty because
x_{1}=x_{2}=y_{1}=y_{2}
always satisfies (
1.1
).
Such a solution will be called a trivial solution. Loosely speaking, other types of solutions are
nontrivial
. For example, solutions of (
1.1
) where
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
are distinct are always nontrivial. See subsection
1.1
for a precise definition of nontrivial solutions.

We measure the
size
of a set using the Fourier dimension.

Definition 1

(Fourier Dimension)
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}
be a Borel set.
The
Fourier dimension

E
, denoted
dim
{}_{\mathbb{F}}(E)
, is defined as

\text{dim}_{\mathbb{F}}(E)=\sup\left\{0\leq\beta\leq 1:\begin{aligned} &\exists\text{ a probability measure $\mu$ supported on $E$ and }\\
&\text{$C\in(0,\infty)$ such that }|\hat{\mu}(\xi)|\leq C(1+|\xi|)^{-\beta/2}\ \forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}\end{aligned}\right\}.

Reformulating our question (
1.2
), our objective is to find the best constant
\beta
such that dim
{}_{\mathbf{F}}(E)>\beta
implies
E
contains a nontrivial solution to (
1.1
) for some
\{a,b,c,d\}
.
Our main result in line with this inquiry is displayed below.

Theorem 1
.
1
1

1

A more general version of Theorem
1
holds for multivariate translation-invariant equations. This is currently work in progress
[

]
.

Let
E\subset[0,1]
be a Borel set with
\text{dim}_{\mathbb{F}}(E)>\frac{1}{2}
.
Then there exists an equation of the form (
1.1
) for which
E
contains a nontrivial solution.

In Theorem
1
,
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}
cannot be replaced by Hausdorff dimension. This is originally due to a body of work, authored by Keleti
[
6
]
, Maga
[
9
]
and Mathé
[
10
]
. Let us expand on these results. Keleti calls a collection of points
\{x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
a rectangle if they satisfy
x_{1}-y_{1}=x_{2}-y_{2}
(observe this is a translation invariant linear equation) and shows that there exists a full Hausdorff dimensional set in the real line that avoids (nontrivial) rectangles where
x_{1}\neq y_{1},x_{2}\neq y_{2}
.
These results were expanded by Maga in
[
9
]
to full dimensional sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are able to avoid the vertices of any parallelogram; Maga again defines parallelograms to be a collection of points
\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
x_{2}-x_{1}=x_{4}-x_{3}
.
Such results were considerably generalized by Mathé in
[
10
]
, which provides a construction of a compact set not containing a solution to countably many given polynomial equations.
In particular, a special case of Mathé’s result is the existence of a rationally independent set in
\mathbb{R}
of full Hausdorff dimension, similar to the result of Keleti.
That is, there exists a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
such that for any collection of points
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset E^{n}
satisfying
\sum a_{i}x_{i}=0
with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
i
, it must be that
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{n}=0
.
As is discussed in
[
8
]
, a rationally independent set cannot have positive Fourier dimension; Theorem
1
aligns with this result.

Our next result partially complements theorem
1
.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
\{a,b,c,d\}\subset\mathbb{N}
obeying (
1.1
).
There exists a set
E\subset[0,1]
of Fourier dimension
1/2
that does not contain any nontrivial solutions of (
1.1
).

It is unclear at the moment if the dimensional threshold 1/2 is sharp.
Our methods rely on a transference principle (see section
4.2
) of an avoiding set from the discrete to the continuum, and as such, the Fourier dimension is directly tied to known cardinality bounds for such sets.
The following question is of interest in this regard.

Question 1
.

A set
E\subset[0,1]
is Sidon if there does not exist a nontrivial collection
\{x,y,u,v\}\subset E
obeying
x+y=u+v
.
What is the maximal Fourier dimension of a Sidon set in the real line?

Theorem
2
shows that Sidon sets can have Fourier dimension at least 1/2 but does not rule out the possibility of a Sidon set having Fourier dimension larger than
1/2
.

1.1.
Nontrivial Solutions and Genus

In this subsection we give a precise definition of
nontrivial solutions
to an equation like (
1.1
).
This relies on the notion of
genus
given in
[
11
]
, which we include here.

Let us write (
1.1
) in the following way

ax_{1}+bx_{2}+(-c)y_{1}+(-d)y_{2}=0.

We consider two examples to lead our discussion

\displaystyle(i)\ \{a,b,-c,-d\}

\displaystyle=\{1,1,-1,-1\}

\displaystyle(ii)\ \{a,b,-c,-d\}

\displaystyle=\{2,2,-3,-1\}.

Note that even though both examples satisfy
a+b=c+d
, in
(i)
there is a smaller set of coefficients that sums to zero, namely
a-c=0
and
b-d=0
.
This is not possible for example
(ii)
. We will call
(ii)
to be of genus 1, and
(i)
to be of genus 2.

Definition 2

(Genus)
.

We say that (
1.1
) is of
genus one
if there does not exist a strict subset
T
of
\{a,b,-c,-d\}
such that
\sum\limits_{t_{i}\in T}t_{i}=0
.
Otherwise we say (
1.1
) is of
genus two
. In this case
\{a,b\}=\{c,d\}
.

Definition 3
.

Consider an equation satisfying (
1.1
).

•

If (
1.1
) is of genus one, then a solution
\{x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\}
of (
1.1
) is said to be
trivial
if
x_{1}=x_{2}=y_{1}=y_{2}
.

•

If (
1.1
) is of genus two, a solution is called
trivial
if
x_{1}=y_{1}
and
x_{2}=y_{2}
.

•

All other solutions (in any case) are referred to as
nontrivial solutions
.

2.
Construction of Measures

We construct a class of measures and apply them in the next section to prove theorem
1
.
To this end, fix a closed set
E\subset[0,1]
satisfying
\dim_{\mathbf{F}}(E)>\frac{1}{2}
and for each choice of coefficients
\{a,b,c,d\}\subset\mathbb{N}
the goal is to construct a measure that if nonzero signals the existence of nontrivial solutions to (
1.1
) inside
E
.
To build up to this measure, we start with a functional that acts on the subspace
\mathcal{V}\subset C([0,1]^{3})
of Schwartz functions whose Fourier transform is compactly supported.
We note that
\mathcal{V}
is dense in
C([0,1]^{3})
under the sup norm.
This was also showed in
[
8
]
but we include proof in section
2.5
for completeness.

Given any choice
\{a,b,c,d\}\subset\mathbb{N}
, the associated equation (
1.1
) can be identified with a pair
(s,t)\in(0,1)^{2}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{2}
.
To see this set
S=a+b=c+d
, write equation (
1.1
) as

(2.1)

\frac{a}{S}x_{1}+\frac{b}{S}x_{2}-\frac{c}{S}y_{1}-\frac{d}{S}y_{2}=0\text{ and define }g(x_{1},x_{2};y_{1},y_{2}):=\frac{a}{S}x_{1}+\frac{b}{S}x_{2}-\frac{c}{S}y_{1}-\frac{d}{S}y_{2}.

We use this pair
(s,t)
to construct our desired measure; however, in a later lemma, this identification will become useful.

Recall that
E\subset[0,1]
with
\dim_{\mathbf{F}}(E)=\beta>1/2
and let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on
E
satisfying

|\hat{\mu}(\xi)|\leq C(1+|\xi|)^{-\beta/2}

for some positive finite constant
C
.
We mollify this measure
\mu
with the following approximation to the identity to define a functional
\Lambda_{(s,t)}
.
Take
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
\psi\geq 0
, supp
(\psi)\subset[-1,1]
and
\int\psi=1
.
For all
\epsilon>0
define

(2.2)

\psi_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\psi\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right)\text{ and }\mu_{\epsilon}:=\mu*\psi_{\epsilon}.

Observe that
\mu_{\epsilon}
is supported on an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
E
, denoted by
\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(E)
.

For each pair
(s,t)\in(0,1)^{2}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{2}
define the following limit for functions
f\in\mathcal{V}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\langle\Lambda_{(s,t)},f\rangle

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\langle\Lambda_{(s,t)}^{\epsilon},f\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{3}}f(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1})\mu_{\epsilon}(x_{1})\mu_{\epsilon}(x_{2})\mu_{\epsilon}(y_{1})\mu_{\epsilon}\left(\frac{sx_{1}+(1-s)x_{2}-ty_{1}}{1-t}\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}dy_{1}.

The following Lemma establishes this limit as well defined.

Lemma 1
.

If
f\in\mathcal{V}
, then for every
(s,t)\in(0,1)^{2}
, the limit in (
2.3
) is absolutely convergent and equals

(2.4)

\langle\Lambda_{(s,t)},f\rangle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\hat{\mu}(\xi)\prod_{i=1}^{3}\hat{\mu}(\eta_{i})\hat{f}\left(-\eta_{1}-\frac{s}{1-t}\xi,-\eta_{2}-\frac{(1-s)}{1-t}\xi,-\eta_{3}+\frac{t}{1-t}\xi\right)d\xi d\eta_{1}d\eta_{2}d\eta_{3}.

Even further we can find a constant so that the limit above behaves like a functional over
\mathcal{V}
.

Lemma 2
.

For every
(s,t)\in(0,1)^{2}
, there exists a positive finite constant
C^{\prime}((s,t))
depending only on
C
and
(s,t)
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{V}

(2.5)

\sup_{\epsilon>0}|\langle\Lambda_{(s,t)}^{\epsilon},f\rangle|\leq C^{\prime}((s,t))||f||_{\infty}\text{ and so }|\langle\Lambda_{(s,t)},f\rangle|\leq C^{\prime}((s,t))||f||_{\infty}.

Using Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
we are equipped to show the existence of the measure we are after, this is the content of the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Fix a choice of coefficients
\{a,b,c,d\}\subset\mathbb{N}
satisfying
a+b=c+d
and consider the associated equation as in (
1.1
).
Let
E\subset[0,1]
be a closed set and
\mu
be a probability measure supported on
E
satisfying

|\hat{\mu}(\xi)|\leq C(1+|\xi|)^{-\beta}

for some finite positive constant
C
and
\beta>1/4
.
Then the following conclusions hold:

(a)

The limit in (
2.3
) holds for all
f\in C([0,1]^{3})
. Furthermore
\langle\Lambda_{(s,t)},f\rangle
can be identified with a nonnegative bounded linear functional on
C([0,1]^{3})
obeying

\left|\langle\Lambda_{(s,t)},f\rangle\right|\leq C^{\prime}(s,t)||f||_{\infty}.

In particular,
\Lambda_{(x,t)}
can be identified as integration against a nonnegative Borel measure which we also denote by
\Lambda_{(s,t)}
.

(b)

If
\Lambda_{(s,t)}
is nontrivial, then supp
(\Lambda_{(s,t)})\subset\mathcal{S}
and assigns measure zero to the trivial solutions
\mathcal{T}
, which is given by

\mathcal{T}=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathcal{S}(E,(s,1-s,t,1-t)):(x_{1},x_{2})=(y_{1},y_{2})\ \text{or}\ (x_{1},x_{2})=(y_{2},y_{1})\right\}.

is dependent on
(s,t)
.
To bypass the dependence on
(s,t)
, fix a box such that
(s,t)\in[a,b]^{2}\subset(0,1)^{2}
.
In

, one obtains

D_{\xi}\leq\left\lVert\hat{f}\right\rVert_{\infty}C^{3}(\beta)\left(\frac{(6R)^{3}}{\text{min}\{\frac{a}{1-a},\frac{1-b}{1-a}\}}\right)(1+|\xi|)^{-3\beta}.

Consequently

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{\mu}(\xi)D_{\xi}d\xi\leq\left\lVert\hat{f}\right\rVert_{\infty}C^{4}(\beta)\left(\frac{(6R)^{3}}{\text{min}\{\frac{a}{1-a},\frac{1-b}{1-a}\}}\right)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|\xi|)^{-4\beta}d\xi

which is finite because

.
Thus, the dominated convergence theorem applies and continuity follows with

.
∎

In the proof of Theorem

Proposition 2

The expression

Proof.

The proof follows by showing a decomposition of (

Our first reduction is that

Lemma 4
.

From the expression (
3.5
), the summand involving

We prove the finiteness of this stated integral in two cases: when

When

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\int\limits_{R_{1}}I(\xi,s,t)d\xi ds

\displaystyle\approx\int\limits_{0}^{1/2}\int\limits_{R_{1}}|\xi|^{-2\beta}\left(\frac{1-t}{t}\right)^{-\beta}d\xi ds

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)^{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{1-2\beta}\right)\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{t}{1-s}\right)^{1-2\beta}ds.

Next, we use the coarse approximation that

\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)^{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{1-2\beta}\right)\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{t}{1-s}\right)^{1-2\beta}ds\leq\frac{t^{1-\beta}}{(1-t)^{\beta}(1-2\beta)}2^{-2\beta}.

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\int\limits_{R_{1}}I(\xi)d\xi ds

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{t}{1-s}}\left(\frac{1}{1+|\xi|}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{1+\frac{1-t}{t}|\xi|}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}d\xi ds

\eqref{R2 case for integrability}=\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int\limits_{\frac{t}{1-t}+t}^{\frac{t}{1-t}+2t}\left(\frac{t}{x-\frac{t}{1-t}}\right)^{2}\log xdx+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\log\frac{1-t}{t}.

Plugging (

\eqref{R2 case for integrability}\leq\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}t\log\left(\frac{t}{1-t}+2t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\log\frac{1-t}{t}.

∎

Lemma 5
.

From the expression (

4.
Avoiding Set

In this section, we focus on the construction of a set that avoids nontrivial solutions to a given equation as in (
1.1
).
To do this, we follow a construction in
[
12
]
which, for completeness, we recall the important components in the following subsection.
Briefly, the first step partitions
[0,1]
into
N_{1}
equal length intervals.
Then, let
Q_{N_{1}}
be an avoiding set in the integers, whose existence is established in
[
11
]
, and pick the corresponding subintervals of
[0,1]
.
That is, the union of intervals of the form

\left[\frac{q}{N_{1}},\frac{q+1}{N_{1}}\right]\text{ for all }q\in Q_{N_{1}}.

Then, according to some random shift
l_{1}
, we keep the shifted intervals associated with
u=q+l_{1}\mod N_{1}
for all
q\in Q_{N_{1}}
, i.e., the union of all such intervals of the form
[\frac{u}{N_{1}},\frac{u+1}{N_{1}}]
.
At the
n
th stage of construction, we follow a similar algorithm.
Each of the basic Cantor intervals given by the
n-1
stage of construction are partitioned into
N_{n}
many equal-length pieces.
For
N_{n}
take an avoiding set
Q_{N_{n}}
that initially dictates the children of each basic Cantor interval.
However, for
each
of the basic Cantor intervals at the
n-1
stage, there is an associated shift for its children that survived construction.
That is, the children of each basic Cantor interval do not necessarily have the same orientation.
These random shifts ensures that our set has our desired Fourier decay.

4.1.
Notation & Construction

Let
\{L_{n}\},\{M_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
be fixed sequences of positive integers with
1\leq L_{n}\leq M_{n}
and
M_{n}\geq 2
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Set

\Sigma_{n}:=\{\mathbf{j}:=(j_{1},\dots,j_{n}):j_{k}\in M_{k}\text{ for each }k\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}\text{ and }\Sigma^{*}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\Sigma_{n}.

For each
M_{n}
and multi-index
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in\Sigma_{n}
there is an associated interval in
[0,1]
as follows,

I_{M_{n},\mathbf{j}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{j_{k}}{M_{1}\dots M_{k}}+\left[0,\frac{1}{M_{1}\cdots M_{n}}\right].

The Cantor iterates,
E_{n}
, to be specified consists of a finite union of the intervals
I_{\mathbf{j}}
for
\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma_{n}
; thus,

E_{1}\supsetneq E_{2}\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq E_{n}\supsetneq E_{n+1}\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq E,\quad E=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}.

To specify
E_{n}
, we introduce our random pieces.
Let
\{X_{\mathbf{j}}:\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma^{*}\}
be a family of independent random sets that obey the following properties:

(1)

For each
n\geq 0
and
\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma_{n}
, the set
X_{\mathbf{j}}
is a subset of
[M_{n+1}]
with
\#(X_{j})=L_{n+1}
, almost surely.

(2)

For each
a\in[M_{n+1}]
and
\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma_{n}
,
\mathbb{P}(a\in X_{\mathbf{j}})=\frac{L_{n+1}}{M_{n+1}}
.

Each sequence of random sets
\{X_{\mathbf{j}}:\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma^{*}\}
leads to a sequence of multi-indices
\{\mathcal{J}_{n}\}
.
That is, for every
n\geq 1
define,

(4.1)

\mathcal{J}_{n}:=\{(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in\Sigma_{n}:j_{k+1}\in X_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})}\text{ for all }k=0,\dots,n-1\}\subset\Sigma_{n}.

With this collection of multi-indices, we may define the Cantor iterates
E_{n}
as

E_{n}=\bigcup_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\text{ and hence }E=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}.

Because we are interested not only in a set but also in the types of measures that are supported on it, we include the description of a measure such as that in
[
12
]
which has ideal Fourier decay.
From properties 1 and 2, we have
|\mathcal{J}_{n}|=|L_{1}\cdots L_{n}|
almost surely and that for every fixed multi-index
\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma_{n}
,

(4.2)

\mathbb{P}((\mathbf{j},j_{n+1})\in\mathcal{J}_{n+1})=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{J}_{n}}(\mathbf{j})\frac{L_{n+1}}{M_{n+1}}

for all
j_{n+1}\in\{0,1,\dots,M_{n+1}-1\}
.
On
E_{n}
, define the following probability density function

\mu_{n}(x):=\frac{M_{1}\cdots M_{n}}{L_{1}\cdots L_{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{I_{\mathbf{j}}}(x).

By the Carathéodory extension theorem, there exists a probability measure
\mu
supported on
E
such that

\mu(I_{\mathbf{j}})=\mu_{n}(I_{\mathbf{j}})=\frac{1}{L_{1}\cdots L_{n}}\text{ for all }\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}.

Specifically,
\mu_{n}\rightarrow\mu
weakly.
Even luckier for us, the authors of
[
12
]
prove that such a construction above will lead to a measure
\mu
that has maximal Fourier decay.
This is the theorem below; for proof, we refer the reader to
[
12
]
.

Theorem 3

(
[
12
]
, Theorem 2.1)
.

Suppose that
\{M_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
satisfies

(4.3)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log M_{n+1}}{\log(M_{1}\cdots M_{N})}=0.

Fix
\sigma>0
such that

(4.4)

\sigma<\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log(L_{1}\cdots L_{n})}{\log(M_{1}\cdots M_{n}}.

Then for
\mu
as above there almost surely exists a constant
C>0
such that

|\hat{\mu(k)}|\leq C|k|^{-\sigma/2}\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.

4.2.
Transference Lemma

This section is dedicated to proving the following Lemma, which is then utilized to prove Theorem

.

Lemma 7

Let
N

We show each condition separately.
The first follows by a construction in
[
11
]
while the second follows from some bounds we establish.
Let
N

and
S=a+b=c+d=

To show the first condition, set
M=\lceil N/(S^{2}+abcd)\rceil>4

For the second condition, let

By the construction of the set

Plugging the form (

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
