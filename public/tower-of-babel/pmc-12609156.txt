A high-entropy mixed ionic and electronic conductor for accelerating the cathode dynamics in all solid–state lithium metal batteries
Authors: Xiangkun Kong, Zongzi Jin, Linwang Chen, Xianzhun Huang, Bingzi Feng, Huang Huang, Yifan Xu, Weihao Wu, Wenkan Yang, Shiji Shen, Zhiwen Zhuo, Weiwei Ping, Ranran Peng, Chusheng Chen, Chengwei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Both electron and ion transports determine the dynamics of the cathode in all solid–state lithium metal batteries (ASSLMBs). Traditional composite strategies combining solid electrolytes and electronic conductors cause complex solid-state interfaces that hinder carrier migration. We present a high-entropy mixed ionic and electronic conductor (HE-O-MIEC), Li1/6-x(LaPrNdSrBa)1/6CoO3-δ, based on oxidation-resistant electronic conductors. HE-O-MIEC exhibits an electronic conductivity of 1150 siemens per centimeter and a Li+ conductivity of 2.3 × 10−4 siemens per centimeter at room temperature. The enhanced Li+ conductivity is attributed to the large configurational entropy, promoting multicomponent solubility and increased Li+ concentration. HE-O-MIEC exhibits electrochemical and thermodynamic compatibility with LiCoO2 and stabilizes ion/electron transport in the ASSLMB using the Li6.4La3Zr1.4Ta0.6O12 electrolyte. Without organic electrolyte or additional pressure, the ASSLMB achieves 115–milliampere·hours per gram initial discharge capacity at 30°C and retains 83% capacity after 500 cycles. Homogeneous electron and ion transport in the HE-O-MIEC demonstrates potential to improve active material utilization and address interfacial challenges in ceramic-based ASSLMBs.

INTRODUCTION

All solid–state lithium metal batteries (ASSLMBs) offer a nonflammable alternative to address the increasing demand for high-safety energy storage systems (1, 2). However, ASSLMBs encounter challenges in the slow transport of electrons and ions at the interface between the cathode and the solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) (3, 4), as well as within the cathode (5, 6). A composite conductive network based on SSEs and electronic conductors (ECs) was used to enhance the dynamics of the cathode in batteries (7, 8). Unfortunately, in the absence of liquid electrolytes, the SSEs, ECs, and cathode are separated and form complex solid-state interfaces (5, 9), where the electron and ion transport along the tortuous pathways with slow charge carrier migration dynamics (Fig. 1A). Moreover, the side reactions at the multiple solid-solid interfaces can lead to the high-resistance phases, which also inhibit the utilization of active materials (Fig. 1A). To address these challenges, efforts have focused on designing single-phase mixed ion-electron conductors (MIECs) (6, 10), which can establish a fast and homogeneous ion and electron transport network. Single-phase MIECs are typically achieved by doping SSEs with transition metals and subjecting them to a reduction reaction (R-MIEC) (6, 10, 11). Because of the constraints imposed by thermodynamic stability and the limited tunability of ionic conductivity, R-MIEC materials often face challenges in simultaneously achieving high electronic and ionic conductivity (11, 12). Moreover, the electronic conductivity of R-MIEC is gradually diminished because of the oxidation of the reduced transition metals during the battery charging process (Fig. 1B).

To develop MIEC materials for enhancing the dynamics of cathode in ASSLMBs, the concept of antioxidant MIECs (O-MIECs) and their design criteria are proposed. Criterion A: O-MIECs should exist as a single phase to establish a three-dimensional (3D) ion and electron transport network, minimizing the formation of solid-solid interfaces and facilitating homogeneous migration of carrier (6). Criterion B: O-MIECs should demonstrate an appropriate redox voltage to prevent hindrance of electronic and ionic conductivity, particularly during the charging process (13). Criterion C: O-MIECs should exhibit high electronic and ionic conductivity, ensuring an adequate carrier concentration and effective transport pathways for both electrons and ions (14, 15). The cobalt-based perovskite material exhibits high electronic conductivity and high-voltage stability but lacks Li+ transport capability (Fig. 1C) (14). In contrast, the perovskite material Li3xLa2/3 − xTiO3 (LLTO) demonstrates high Li+ conductivity and superior voltage tolerance (16) yet lacks electronic conductivity (Fig. 1C). Drawing inspiration from the structure and composition of these two materials, we propose to develop the perovskite-type O-MIEC with high electronic and ionic conductivities.

In this work, the general design criteria for O-MIEC are analyzed and summarized, and lithium-doped perovskite oxide Li1/6-x(LaPrNdSrBa)1/6CoO3-δ (HE-O-MIEC) was successfully synthesized using a high-entropy experimental strategy. HE-O-MIEC exhibits an electronic conductivity of 1150 S cm−1 and a Li+ conductivity of 2.3 × 10−4 S cm−1, 107 to 109 and 102 to 103 higher than those of traditional cathode materials [LiCoO2 (LCO), LiNiO2, LiFePO4, and their derivatives] (17–19), respectively. Meanwhile the HE-O-MIEC also has a suitable redox potential of 4.0 V, a favorable electrochemical match with the LCO cathode. By using the ultrafast high-temperature sintering (UHS) method (20), HE-O-MIEC was annealed onto the Li6.4La3Zr1.4Ta0.6O12 (LLZTO) electrolytes to form a 3D ion and electron conductive network throughout the full cell (Fig. 1D). This optimized electron and ion transport pathway enhances the effective utilization of active materials (Fig. 1D). Because of the homogeneous transmission of electrons and ions, the entire battery operated normally at 30°C, achieving a discharge capacity of 115 mA g−1 and a stable capacity retention rate of 96% after 100 cycles. Please note here that no liquid electrolyte or mechanical pressure was applied throughout the battery cycle, further demonstrating that the HE-O-MIEC successfully establishes the ion and electron networks.

RESULTS

O-MIEC material design criteria

Cobalt-based perovskite materials, like La1-xSrxCoO3-δ, are commonly used as cathodes in solid oxide fuel cells, exhibiting high stability under oxidation conditions (14). Moreover, La1-xSrxCoO3-δ exhibits high electronic conductivity (>1000 S cm−1) (21) and excellent structural tolerance, enabling the development of O-MIECs through component design (22). On the basis of La1-xSrxCoO3-δ, the (Li1/8Ln3/8AE1/2) CoO3 composition was designed and a theoretical evaluation was performed to assess its compliance with the design criteria for a mixed conductive framework. In this composition, Li+ is introduced as the ionic conduction carrier at the A site, while lanthanide elements (Ln) and divalent alkaline earth metals (AE) are incorporated to adjust the valence state of cobalt and enhance the chemical compatibility of the component elements.

First, the disordered enthalpy-entropy descriptor (DEED) (23), a thermodynamic criterion for evaluating the stability of multicomponent ceramics, was calculated for (Li1/8Ln3/8AE1/2)CoO3 by density functional theory (DFT) (Fig. 2A, criterion A). A large DEED value indicates a small miscibility gap temperature, facilitating the formation of a solid solution. Among the compositions, most of the Ln-Sr codoped compositions with large DEED values were evaluated to exhibit high synthesizability, while Ln-Ba doping presents challenges in achieving a stable cubic perovskite phase. To validate the DEED results, a solid-phase reaction method was used to synthesize the (Li1/8Ln3/8AE1/2)CoO3 material. X-ray diffraction (XRD) results indicate that La-Sr doping at the A site can introduce lithium carriers in the bulk, forming a stable single-phase perovskite structure at 1000°C (Fig. 2B). However, an impurity phase of Co3O4 is observed under the synthesis conditions when Pr-Sr or Nd-Sr are doped at 1000°C. As the synthesis temperature increases to 1170°C, the impurity phases are eliminated, forming a stable single-phase structure (fig. S1). The absence of a single perovskite phase when the A site is codoped with Nd-Ba at either 1000° or 1170°C (fig. S2) aligns with the calculated results.

The electronic density of states (DOS) for Ln-Sr codoped compositions with varying Li content is presented in fig. S3. All DOS profiles exhibit a similar semimetallic characteristic, with the Fermi level intersecting the band formed by Co3d and O2p hybrid orbitals, suggesting that introducing Li does not alter the electronic structure. In addition, the average redox potentials (24) of Ln-Sr codoped compositions were calculated and compared with those of the reported R-MIECs and common cathodes to assess their suitability for fitting criterion B. For the R-MIEC, such as LLTO (6, 25, 26) and Li5.735Fe0.375La3Zr1.5Ta0.5O12 (LLZTO-Fe) (27), transition metals (Ti3+ and Fe2+) are oxidized when the charging voltage exceeds approximately 2 V (Fig. 2C), leading to decreased electronic conductivity (26). In contrast, Li-doped La1-xSrxCoO3-δ exhibits a high-average redox potential comparable to that of LCO, suggesting that O-MIEC does not induce Li+ transport failure within the operational voltage range of ASSLMB. To evaluate the Li+ conduction capability within the La1-xSrxCoO3-δ framework, configurations with continuous Li+ conducting channels were selected (fig. S4), and the potential barrier for Li+ transport through lithium vacancies was assessed using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CINEB) method (criterion C). When Li+ migrates across the A-O layer, the migration barrier was calculated to 0.47 eV, a value comparable to that of certain oxide-based SSEs (28–30), suggesting that La-Sr codoped compositions have a good capability for Li+ transport (Fig. 2D). Therefore, the Ln-Sr codoped compositions are anticipated to form a continuous and efficient mixed ion and electron conductive network for improving the cathode dynamics in ASSLMB.

Development and structure evolution of Li1/6-x(LaPrNdSrBa)1/6CoO3-δ

A high configurational entropy can promote the concurrent solubility of multiple components and facilitate the optimization of the desired performance (31–33). As a result, to introduce additional Li+ carriers, La, Pr, Nd, Sr, Ba, and Li are likely incorporated into the A site to form a high-entropy structure. XRD results demonstrate that HE-O-MIEC synthesized at 1000°C exhibits a single-phase cubic perovskite structure (Fig. 3A). Under high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) (Fig. 3B), the nearest-neighbor distances in regions I and II were measured at 0.277 and 0.396 nm, respectively, which are consistent with the (110) and (100) crystal plane spacing of perovskite oxides. The electron diffraction pattern reveals that the nearest-neighbor plane spacing of the (110) crystal plane is 0.28 nm, while the nearest-neighbor plane spacing of the (1-11) crystal plane is 0.23 nm (Fig. 3C and fig. S5) and the lattice constant can be determined. These values are consistent with the spacing relationships predicted by the Pm-3m space group, allowing for the determination of the lattice constant. The lattice constant was determined to be 0.396 nm, which agrees well with the results obtained from XRD refinement (fig. S6). Furthermore, HRTEM/energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and inductively coupled plasma (ICP) spectrometer analyses confirm that the molecular composition is La:Pr:Nd:Ba:Sr = 1:1:1:1:1 (tables S1 and S2).

The relative density of HE-O-MIEC pellets was 98% using the Archimedes method (Fig. 3D), and the mean particle size of HE-O-MIEC was 17.55 μm (figs. S7 and S8), indicating favorable sintering activity. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)/EDS analyses show that the elements are uniformly distributed with no observable secondary phases (figs. S9 and S10) and the elemental ratios closely correspond to the molecular formula (table S3). TEM/EDS further confirms the homogeneous element distribution in an individual grain (Fig. 3E). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data suggest that the valence states of the Co element in HE-O-MIEC are present as +4 and +3, with a Co4+/Co3+ ratio of 0.57 (fig. S11). Furthermore, ICP tests were performed on the stable perovskite materials to determine the lithium content of various components (Fig. 3F). According to ICP results, La-Sr doping introduces a lithium content of 0.05, whereas Pr-Sr and Nd-Sr doping result in lithium contents of 0.03 and 0.01 at the A site, respectively. The amount of lithium introduced was closely correlated with the DEED value of (Li1/8Ln3/8AE1/2)CoO3; a higher DEED value corresponds to a greater Li content introduced. The high-entropy perovskite structure has a Li content of 0.09, which is almost two times greater than that observed with La-Sr doping. This further demonstrates that large configurational entropy can enhance the simultaneous solubility of multiple components, thereby providing a sufficient carrier density to increase the Li+ conductivity of HE-O-MIEC.

Ionic and electronic conductivities

To investigate the Li+ transport capacity of HE-O-MIEC, two electrolyte-wetted polypropylene (PP) films were used for electronic isolation and interfacial wetting. Lithium metal was placed on one side of the Li-Cu cell, with Cu foil on the opposite side, allowing for in situ lithium deposition (Fig. 4A). After 10 hours of deposition, lithium metal was observed to grow on the glossy Cu foil (Fig. 4B), with a thickness of 100 μm (fig. S12). A clear difference was observed between the SEM images of the clean Cu foil and the lithium-deposited foil (figs. S13 and S14). The lithium metal particles exhibit dense and uniform growth on the Cu foil (Fig. 4C). At a discharge current of 0.5 mA cm−2, the cell has a pronounced steep slope before deposition (Fig. 4D), which may be attributed to double-layer charging and the lithium present in the thin oxide layer on the Cu surface (Fig. 4D) (34, 35). In addition, the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) curve reveals that the total impedance of the Li-Cu cell before deposition is 100 kilohm·cm2, indicating a blocking behavior (Fig. 4E). After the deposition of Li, the cell reaches a lithium deposition plateau at −80 mV (Fig. 4D), and the Li-Cu cell becomes a Li-Li symmetric cell with a total impedance of 300 ohm·cm2. The Li+ conductivity is calculated to 1.8 × 10−4 S cm−1 (Fig. 4E), which can be regarded as the lower limit of the real Li+ conductivity of HE-O-MIEC due to the presence of interfacial resistance.

To analyze the Li+ conductivity of O-MIEC in an all solid–state system, lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulphonyl)imide (LiTFSI)–poly[di(ethyleneglycol) ethyl ether acrylate (DEGA)]–fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) was used as a separator between the MIEC material and lithium metal to construct an all-solid symmetric cell system (Li|SPE|MIEC|SPE|Li) (fig. S15). EIS results indicate that the type and concentration of A-site cations substantially influence Li+ transport in perovskite. Doping at the A site with Pr-Sr or Nd-Sr results in minimal Li+ conductivity, while La-Sr doping yields an Li+ conductivity of 0.7 × 10−4 S cm−1 (figs. S16 to S18). This observation suggests that a larger ionic radius is favorable to establish efficient channels for Li+ transport. However, the simultaneous doping of La, Nd, Pr, Ba, Sr, and Li at the A site to form a high-entropy perovskite structure results in an increased Li+ conductivity of 2.3 × 10−4 S cm−1 for HE-O-MIEC (Fig. 4F), which is three times greater than that of the La-Sr–doped variant alone. High-entropy metal cation mixtures enhance the Li+ conductivity of the compound, decreasing reliance on specific chemical constituents and improving composability. Measurement of activation energy reveals that the A-site material comprising only La and Sr has an activation energy of 0.488 eV, whereas the A-site material with a high-entropy configuration exhibits a reduced activation energy of 0.417 eV (Fig. 4G), aligning with typical values for oxide ceramic materials (28–30, 36). The local distortions introduced in high-entropy materials overlap with the potential energy distributions of the base ions, thereby facilitating the penetration of Li+ with reduced activation energy.

The electronic conductivity of HE-O-MIEC was measured at various temperatures using a four-probe technique. The electronic conductivity reaches 1150 S cm−1 at 25°C and decreases to 700 S cm−1 at 700°C, indicating distinct metallic properties (Fig. 4H). In Li+ MIEC materials, the electronic conductivity of HE-O-MIEC is approximately twice that of carbon black, substantially higher than that of other MIEC materials, while its Li+ conductivity is comparable to that of conventional solid electrolytes (Fig. 4I) (6, 10, 11, 17, 18, 27, 37, 38). Traditional cathode materials for Li-ion batteries, such as LCO, LiNiO2, and their derivatives, exhibit poor carrier migration capacity. Their lithium-ion conductivities (σLi+) range from 10−7 to 10−6 S cm−1, while the electronic conductivity (σe-) range from 10−6 to 10−4 S cm−1. Consequently, the electronic conductivity of HE-O-MIEC is 109 to 107 times greater than that of conventional cathode materials, while its Li+ conductivity is 103 to 102 times higher. In addition, cyclic voltammetry curves demonstrate that HE-O-MIEC and LCO exhibit favorable electrochemical compatibility (fig. S19). Therefore, the high electronic and ionic conductivities, coupled with the observed electrochemical compatibility, suggest that HE-O-MIEC could serve as an efficient ion and electron network in ASSLMBs.

HE-O-MIEC promotes cathode dynamics processes in ASSLMBs

By using the UHS method, HE-O-MIEC and LCO were annealed onto the LLZTO electrolytes to form a 3D ion and electron conductive network throughout the HE-O-MIEC ASSLMB (HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO|Li). Simultaneously, La0.5Sr0.5CoO3 and LLZTO are used as the EC and ionic conductor in composite cathodes, respectively, to construct composite-ASSLMB (LSC@LLZTO@LCO|LLZTO|Li) with UHS method. At 80°C, the total area impedance of the HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery is 65.8 ohm·cm2, while that of the LSC@LLZTO@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery is 185 ohm·cm2, which is approximately three times higher (fig. S20). Benefit from the highly homogeneous electron and ion transport, the active materials achieve a discharge capacity of 133 at 45 mA g−1 (Fig. 5A), close to the theoretical capacity (140 mA·hour g−1). In contrast, to operate the LSC@LLZTO@LCO|LLZTO|Li, it was tested at a lower current density of 20 mA g−1, resulting in a discharge capacity of only 14.4 mA·hour g−1 (Fig. 5A). The differential capacity versus voltage curves (Fig. 5B) were analyzed to elucidate the impact of the HE-O-MIEC on the electrochemical reactions and discharge capacity. In both the HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery and the LSC@LLZTO@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery, the prominent peaks appear solely around 3.88 V during the charge-discharge process, indicating that the discharge capacity is entirely provided by the LCO cathode. However, during discharge, the peak strength of the reduction reaction in the HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery reaches 1221 mA·hour g−1 V−1, nearly 10 times higher than that in the LSC@LLZTO@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery. This is consistent with the observed difference in discharge capacities between the two batteries and further demonstrates that the homogeneous electron and ion transport provided by HE-O-MIEC can improve the utilization of the LCO cathode materials.

In addition, the single-phase HE-O-MIEC enables the ASSLMB based on all ceramics to operate normally at 30°C, achieving a specific discharge capacity of 115 mA·hour g−1, which is notably higher than 7 mA·hour g−1 discharge capacity of the composite ASSLMB (Fig. 5C). In addition, the total area impedance is only 445 ohm·cm2 at 30°C, nearly four times lower than that of LSC@LLZTO@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery (fig. S21). Under identical preparation conditions, carbon black and LLZTO are used as the EC and ionic conductor in composite cathodes to construct ASSLMB (C@LLZTO@LCO|LLZTO|Li), which has a total area impedance of 6255 ohm·cm2 at 80°C, 100 times higher than that of HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery, and fails to operate (fig. S22). XRD and Raman analysis indicate that HE-O-MIEC and LCO demonstrate good thermodynamic compatibility, with no formation of secondary phases (figs. S23 and S24). SEM/EDS and HRTEM/EDS analyses confirmed that no elemental diffusion occurred during the cosintering of HE-O-MIEC and LCO and that both materials retained their original crystal lattices after the process completion (figs. S25 to S28). The SEM images of the HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO show a conformal interface, which is favorable for carrier transport dynamics (Fig. 5, D and E). No elemental codiffusion was detected under rapid heat treatment by UHS due to the short annealing time of ~3 s (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, the HE-O-MIEC particles form a network structure for electron and ion transport at the interface of HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO, contributing to the higher specific capacity and lower operating temperature of the HE-O-MIEC battery. At 80°C, the HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery undergoes stable cycling for over 200 cycles at a current density of 45 mA g−1, with a Coulombic efficiency of 100% (Fig. 5G). In addition, the HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery can also operate stably for 500 cycles at 30°C, with a capacity retention of 83% (Fig. 5H). Compared with the operating temperature and cycling performance of LLZO-based ASSLMBs reported in the literature, the HE-O-MIEC@LCO|LLZTO|Li battery exhibits notably enhanced cycling stability (fig. S29) (39–51). These results suggest that the robust mixed ion and electron network enabled by the HE-O-MIEC can greatly mitigate the charge transfer problem at the interface and in cathode for the oxide-based ASSLMBs.

DISCUSSION

We introduce an antioxidation perovskite MIEC concept through a high-entropy strategy, achieving a Li+ conductivity of 2.3 × 10−4 S cm−1, comparable to solid electrolytes. The electronic conductivity (1150 S cm−1) is akin to ECs, demonstrating favorable electrochemical and thermodynamic compatibility with the cathode materials. HE-O-MIEC and LiCoO2 cathode materials have favorable chemical stability and electrochemical compatibility. As a result, HE-O-MIEC forms a mixed ion and electron conductive network on the cathode side, facilitating the charge transport within the cathode and enabling the garnet-type ASSLMBs to operate stably at room temperature. This study not only develops mixed ion and electron conductive materials optimized for the cathode side of batteries but also analyzes and summarizes the design criteria for mixed conductive materials tailored to this application, thereby contributing to their further development.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

MIEC synthesis

MIEC precursors, including Li2CO3 (98%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd), Co2O3 (98%, Apinno), Pr6O11 (99.9%, Innochem), Nd2O3 (99.9%, Innochem), La2O3 (98%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd), BaCO3 (98%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd), and SrCO3 (98%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd), were mixed in a stoichiometric ratio. To compensate for the loss of Li, 10 wt % excess Li2CO3 was added. After ball milling for 6 hours with ethanol and ZrO2 balls, the suspension was dried and pelletized in an oven at 80°C overnight. A green pellet of the MIEC precursors was pressed into pellets under cold isostatic (DJYP-100TZ) pressure at 230 MPa.

Dense solid electrolyte pellets

LLZTO (Hefei Kejing, China) powder was prepared by cold isostatic pressing at a pressure of 230 MPa and then sintered for 10 s by UHS technology to obtain dense LLZTO sheets. The sintered LLZTO pellets were stored in the glovebox (Vigor) before further use.

Preparation of SPE

All operations were conducted within a glove box, which was filled with argon. The raw materials, namely DEGA (Macklin), FEC (Anhui Sage Energy Co. Ltd.), LiTFSI (Guotai Chaowei Co. Ltd.), 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (HMPP, Macklin), 2,2′-azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (Sinopharm), and succinonitrile (Sinopharm), were stored in the glove box upon receipt. The precursor solution is composed of 1 M LiTFSI in a DEGA: FEC: HMPP ratio of 80: 20: 2 (v: v: v). FEC serves as a plasticizer and film-forming additive, while HMPP acts as the photoinitiator. Fifty microliters of the precursor solution was deposited onto the substrate and evenly distributed. The liquid precursor was then irradiated with an ultraviolet lamp (365 nm, 1100 mW cm−2) at a distance of 5 cm for 3 min, resulting in its transformation into a solidified polymer electrolyte.

Preparation of sintering aids

B2O3 (Aladin, 99%) and Li2CO3 were initially mixed in a molar ratio of 1:2 and ground for 20 min in an agate mortar. The resulting mixture was subsequently heated in air at 600°C for 10 hours.

Preparation of the cathode composite

The obtained Li3BO3-Li2CO3 powders, HE-O-MIEC, and LCO were mixed in N-methyl-1,2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich) in a mass ratio of 15:20:65 to form a solid solution.

Preparation of the ASSLMBs

The cathode composite was applied onto the dense LLZTO and the resulting double-layer structure. Then the double-layer structure was rapidly heat-treated for 3 s at 800°C using UHS technology to form a conformal cathode|LLZTO interface. The heating and cooling curve of this process is shown in fig. S30. The anode was coated with molten lithium at 220°C on the other side of LLZTO with the cathode to ensure good electrical contact. To coat the cathode particles with a current collector, carbon nanotube solution in NMP (3 mg/ml) was dropped on the surface of the cathode and the excess solvent was evaporated off on a heating plate at 150°C.

The relative density test

The density of the sample was determined using a solid density meter based on Archimedes’ displacement principle. The measurement procedure involved weighing the sample in air ( m1 ), followed by immersion on a stand in ethyl alcohol ( ρe = 0.79 g/cm3) to measure the mass of the displaced alcohol ( m2 ). The sample density ( ρ ) was calculated using the formula ρ=m1ρe/m2 and subsequently compared with the theoretical density to determine its relative density.

Characterizations

XRD measurements were carried out on an U1timaIV (Rigaku Corporation, Japan) x-ray diffractometer operating at 40 kV and 40 mA with a scanning step of 0.02° and scanning speed of 15° and 2° min−1, using Cu K radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). SEM images were captured with an FEI Apreo field-emission scanning electron microscope (5 kV) and EDS spectra at 20 kV. HRTEM images were taken on an FEI Talos F200X at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. XPS measurements were performed on an ESCALAB 250Xi (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) equipped with Mono chromated Al K alpha (energy 1486.68 eV). The Raman spectra were obtained by a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution Raman spectrometer with a laser wavelength of 532 nm.

Electrochemical measurements

EIS spectra of the symmetric Li|PP|MIEC|PP|Li cells were tested using Bio-Logic (SP-200) with a frequency range of 1 to 100 mHz and a temperature range of 25° to 70°C. EIS spectra of ASSLMBs were investigated using BioLogic with a frequency range of 100 to 1 MHz and a voltage of 50 mV. The galvanostatic charge/discharge of the ASSLMBs was tested on a NEWARE (CT-4008Tn-5 V10-mA-164) battery test system at 80°C and room temperature without any organic electrolyte.

First principles computation

All first-principles calculations in this study were performed within the framework of DFT to investigate the structural stability, electronic properties, and ionic transport behavior of a series of A-site codoped (Li1/8Ln3/8AE1/2)CoO3 compositions, where Ln denotes lanthanide elements and AE denotes alkaline earth metals. The initial reference structure is derived from the cubic LaCoO3 phase (mp-510611).

All DFT calculations are performed in the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof generalized-gradient approximation (52), implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (53). The projector augmented-wave (54) method is used to represent the core states. The convergence parameters of all static DFT calculations were set to be consistent with the Materials Project (55).

We used the AFLOW-POCC module to model the chemically disordered (Li1/8Ln3/8AE1/2)CoO3 systems (56). The AFLOW-POCC algorithm constructs an ensemble of supercell configurations accounting for the A site’s substitutional disorder. Starting from the parent cubic LaCoO3 structure, the smallest supercell satisfying the desired occupancies and stoichiometry was determined. All unique configurations were exhaustively generated by considering various supercell shapes and decoration permutations, filtered through the AFLOW-SYM and AFLOW-XtalFinder modules (57, 58) to eliminate duplicates. The DEED, as an ensemble-averaged descriptor, was used to evaluate trends in thermodynamic stability across the different codoped compositions (23).

To gain insights into the lithium-ion transport properties, we evaluated the migration barriers by constructing tailored supercell models incorporating Li-ion diffusion channels and using the CINEB method (59). The 3D visualization models were constructed using VESTA 3 software (60).
