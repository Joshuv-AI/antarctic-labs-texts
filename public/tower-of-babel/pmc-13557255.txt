Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis of the Vulva: A Pediatric Case
Authors: Marina Romero Navarrete, María Elisa Vega Memije, Sabrina Escandón Pérez, Carla Itzel Figueroa Basurto, Roberto Arenas, Aureliano Delfino Castillo Solana
Published: 2026

Case Presentation

A 7-year-old girl presented with a 9-month history of a persistent vulvar plaque that was red, firm, and weeping (erythematous, indurated, exudative). The lesion caused itching and dysuria and was refractory to multiple treatments (Figure 1A).

A. Plaque in the vulvar region. B. Clinical improvement after two treatment cycles. C. H&E, 60x: Epithelial ulceration with a dense infiltrate of small monomorphic cells with a lymphoid appearance, alongside larger histiocytic-like cells with grooved nuclei and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. D. CD1a antibodies showing intense positivity, 40x. E. Langerin antibodies showing intense positivity, 20x.

Biopsy revealed the definitive diagnosis of Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis (LCH) of the vulva. Histopathological examination showed epithelial ulceration and a dense immune cell infiltrate. This infiltrate consisted of small lymphoid-like cells alongside characteristic histiocytic cells featuring grooved nuclei and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. The diagnosis was confirmed by strongly positive immunohistochemical staining for CD1a and Langerin (Figure 1 C, D, E).

The patient was referred to the “Institute of Cancer” in Acapulco. Initial staging studies, including laboratory tests, chest X-ray, scintigraphy, and bone marrow biopsy, were reported as normal, indicating localized disease at that time.

The initial treatment regimen was prednisolone and vinblastine. Since the vulvar lesions persisted, a second cycle was administered, which included the addition of 6-mercaptopurine for one year. This intensified treatment achieved total remission of the lesions (Figure 1B).

Teaching point

LCH of the vulva in pediatric population presents a significant diagnostic difficulty because its clinical features can mimic a wide array of more common conditions. The differential diagnosis includes inflammatory processes, Infections, sexually transmitted diseases, Metabolic disorders, Tumor-related conditions, Traumatic injuries and Conditions related to sexual abuse [1].

The confirmation relies on histopathological examination of the affected vulvar tissue and the immunohistochemistry positive for CD1a, CD207 (Langerin), S-100 [2]. Currently no consensus exists regarding treatment in pediatric population.

The presentation LCH in the vulvar is a diagnostic challenge that requires a high index of suspicion among specialists, including Gynecologists, Pediatricians, Dermatologists, Pediatric Dermatologists and Pathologists.
