A complex-analytic characterization of Lagrangian immersions in ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} with transverse double points
Purvi Gupta, Rudranil Sahu

Abstract
. Given a compact smooth totally real immersed n n -submanifold M ⊂ ℂ n M\subset\mathbb{C}^{n} with only finitely many transverse double points, it is known that if M M is Lagrangian with respect to some Kähler form on ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} , then it is rationally convex in ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} (Gayet, 2000), but the converse is not true (Mitrea, 2020). We show that M M is Lagrangian with respect to some Kähler form on ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} if and only if M M is rationally convex and at each double point, the pair of transverse tangent planes to M M satisfies the following diagonalizability condition: there is a complex linear transformation on ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} that maps the pair to ( ℝ n , ( D + i ) ​ ℝ n ) \left(\mathbb{R}^{n},(D+i)\mathbb{R}^{n}\right) for some real diagonal n × n n\times n matrix D D .



1.
Introduction

A compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is said to be rationally convex if its complement in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a union of complex hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Rational convexity endows a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
with the approximation-theoretic feature that any function holomorphic on some neighborhood of
K
is uniformly approximable on
K
by restrictions of rational functions on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with poles off of
K
. A remarkable symplectic characterization of rational convexity was established by Duval (
n=2
) and Duval–Sibony (
n\geq 2
) for compact totally real submanifolds of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, see
[
1
,
2
,
4
]
. They proved that the image of a totally real smooth embedding
\iota:M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
of a compact smooth manifold
M
is rationally convex in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
if and only if the image is isotropic, or Lagrangian, if
\text{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}M=n
, with respect to some Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, i.e., there is a strictly plurisubharmonic function
\rho:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\iota^{*}dd^{c}\rho=0
. In
[
5
]
, Gayet considered the case where
M
is
n
-dimensional and
\iota:M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a totally real immersion that self intersects in only finitely many transverse double points. He showed that if
\iota(M)
is Lagrangian with respect to some Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then it is rationally convex. This result was extended to immersions with finitely many quadratic self-tangencies by Duval–Gayet in
[
3
]
. In
[
6
]
, Mitrea gave examples to show that the converse may not hold in the presence of self-intersections. In particular, there exist pairs
(R,S)
of
n
-dimensional totally real planes in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
intersecting only at the origin such that
R\cup S
is rationally convex, but
R\cup S
is not Lagrangian with respect to
any
Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Nevertheless, Mitrea showed that if
\iota:M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a totally real immersion with finitely many finite self-intersection points and
\iota(M)
is rationally convex, then there is a nonnegative
(1,1)
-form
\omega
, positive outside an arbitrarily small neighborhood of the self-intersection set of
\iota(M)
, so that
\iota^{*}\omega=0
, see
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]
.

In this article, we consider the case of immersed
n
-dimensional submanifolds of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
that admit only isolated self-intersection points, each of which is a transverse double point. This was the case considered by Gayet in
[
5
]
. We give the precise additional hypothesis required at the double points to obtain a converse of Gayet’s theorem. For this, we need a definition. Let
\iota:M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
be an immersion of a smooth real
n
-dimensional manifold
M
into
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Assume that
\iota
is totally real, i.e., for every
z\in M
, the real subspace
\iota_{*}(T_{z}M)
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
contains no complex lines. Let
p\in\iota(M)
be an isolated transverse double point of
\iota(M)
, and
z,w\in\iota^{-1}(p)
be distinct pre-images of
p
. Since
\iota_{*}(T_{z}M)
is a totally real plane, there is a nonsingular complex linear transformation
G:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
that maps
\iota_{*}(T_{z}M)
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\operatorname{Im}z=0\}
. Due to transversality, there is a real
n\times n
matrix
A
(depending on
G
) such that
G
maps
\iota_{*}\left(T_{w}M\right)
to
(A+i)\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\operatorname{Re}z=Ax\text{ and }\operatorname{Im}z=x\text{ for some }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
; see
[
7
, §2]
. We say that
p
is a
diagonalizable
double point of
\iota(M)
if
A
is diagonalizable over the reals. This condition is independent of the choice of
G
. Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
M
be a compact smooth manifold of real dimension
n
. Let
\iota:M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a totally real immersion such that
\iota(M)
is a smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, except at finitely many points
p_{1},...,p_{m}\in\iota(M)
, which are transverse double points of
\iota(M)
. Then,
\iota(M)
is Lagrangian with respect to some Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
if and only if
\iota(M)
is rationally convex and
p_{1},...,p_{m}
are diagonalizable double points of
\iota(M)
.

The forward implication follows from Gayet’s aforementioned result in
[
5
]
, and an inspection of the linear model of a transverse double point, i.e., the union of two
n
-dimensional totally real planes
R
and
S
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
that intersect only at the origin. In
[
7
]
, Weinstock showed that
R\cup S
is locally polynomially (and rationally) convex at the origin if and only if, after a complex linear transformation,
R=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
S=(A+i)\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some real
n\times n
matrix
A
that has no purely imaginary eigenvalues of modulus greater than one. In Section
3
, we show that
R\cup S
is Lagrangian with respect to some Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
if and only if
A
is diagonalizable over the reals, see Proposition
3.2
. For the converse, we construct a local Kähler form near each diagonalizable double point, with respect to which the immersed manifold is locally Lagrangian, see Section
4
, and patch these local Kähler forms with the global “degenerate” Kähler form granted by Mitrea’s result in
[
6
]
to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
, see Section
5
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect the notation that will be used throughout this paper.

A point

is denoted in complex coordinates by
(z_{1},...,z_{n})
, where
z_{j}=x_{j}+iy_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
1\leq j\leq n
. The origin in any Euclidean space is denoted by
0
.

Fréchet derivatives.
Vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are treated, by default, as column matrices. For a smooth
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued map
f
on an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Fréchet derivative of

at
z\in\Omega
is viewed as an
m\times n
matrix, and is denoted by
(Df)(z)
. Identifying real
m\times n
matrices with
\mathbb{R}^{mn}
in the natural way, the Fréchet derivative of
Df:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{mn}
at
z\in\Omega
is denoted by
(D^{2}f)(z)
, and is viewed as an
(mn)\times n
matrix. In the special case where
m=1
, the
1\times n
row matrix
(Df)(z)
is denoted by
(\nabla f)(z)
and the
n\times n
matrix
(D^{2}f)(z)
is denoted by
(\operatorname{Hess}f)(z).
If

is defined on an open set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Hess}_{xx}f)(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Hess}f_{y})(x),\quad\text{where }f_{y}:x\mapsto f(x,y),

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Hess}_{yy}f)(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Hess}f_{x})(y),\quad\text{where }f_{x}:y\mapsto f(x,y),

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Hess}_{xy}f)(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(DF_{x})(y),\quad\text{where }F_{x}:y\mapsto(\nabla_{x}f)(x,y),

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Hess}_{yx}f)(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(DF_{y})(x),\quad\text{where }F_{y}:x\mapsto(\nabla_{y}f)(x,y).

Note that
(\operatorname{Hess}_{yx}f)=(\operatorname{Hess}_{xy}f)^{T}
.

Matrices and matrix-valued functions.
Let
M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
denote the set of all real
m\times n
matrices. The
n\times n
identity matrix is denoted by
I_{n}
. The zero matrix of any size is denoted by
0
. The transpose of a matrix
A
is denoted by
A^{T}
. Matrix multiplication is denoted by
\cdot
, i.e., for compatible matrices
A
and
B
,
A\cdot B
denotes their matrix product. Given a smooth
(m\times n)
-matrix valued function
A
and a smooth
(n\times p)
-matrix valued function
B
on an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we use the following product rule for differentiation:

(1)

D(A\cdot B)=\left(B^{T}\otimes I_{m}\right)\cdot(DA)+\left(I_{p}\otimes A\right)\cdot(DB),

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product between matrices given by

(a_{jk})_{m\times n}\otimes B_{r\times s}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}B&\cdots&a_{1n}B\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}B&\cdots&a_{mn}B\end{pmatrix}_{mr\times ns}.

Vector fields.
Given a row matrix
V=(V_{1},...,V_{m})
of real vector fields on an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and an
(m\times p)
matrix of functions
A=(a_{jk})
on
\Omega
,
V\cdot A
denotes the row matrix of real vector fields given by

(2)

V\cdot A=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}{a_{1k}V_{k}},...,\sum_{k=1}^{m}{a_{pk}V_{k}}\right).

If
G
is a smooth map on
\Omega
, then

(3)

G_{*}V=(G_{*}V_{1},...,G_{*}V_{n}).

The
\bm{dd^{c}}
operator.
The Dolbeault operator and its complex conjugate on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
are given by

\partial f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{z_{j}}}dz_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\partial}f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{\overline{z}_{j}}}d\overline{z}_{j}.

Then, the usual exterior derivative (acting on
0
-forms) may be expressed as
d=\partial+\overline{\partial}
. Consider the differential operator
d^{c}=i(\overline{\partial}-\partial)
. Then, for a smooth
\mathbb{R}
-valued function
f
on some open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

d^{c}f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\dfrac{\partial{f}}{\partial{x_{j}}}dy_{j}-\dfrac{\partial{f}}{\partial{y_{j}}}dx_{j}\right),

in terms of real coordinates.
We use the notation
dd^{c}f>0
on
\Omega
to denote the fact that the alternating
(1,1)
-form
(dd^{c}f)(z)
is (strictly) positive definite for each
z\in\Omega
. The following chain rule for the operator
dd^{c}
will be useful. Let
G=(G_{1},...,G_{n})
be a smooth
\mathbb{C}^{n}
-valued map on open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
G(\Omega)
is an open set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. For a smooth function
f:G(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

(4)

\displaystyle dd^{c}(f\circ G)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 2i\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial z_{j}\partial\overline{z}_{k}}\circ G\right)\left(\partial G_{j}\wedge\overline{\partial}\,\overline{G}_{k}-\overline{\partial}G_{j}\wedge\partial\overline{G}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2i\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial z_{j}\partial z_{k}}\circ G\right)\partial G_{j}\wedge\overline{\partial}G_{k}+2i\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\overline{z}_{j}\partial\overline{z}_{k}}\circ G\right)\partial\,\overline{G}_{j}\wedge\overline{\partial}\,\overline{G}_{k}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+2i\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_{k}}\circ G\right)\partial\overline{\partial}G_{k}+2i\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{z}_{k}}\circ G\right)\partial\overline{\partial}\,\overline{G}_{k}.

3.
The Lagrangian property for a union of planes

In this section, we give a necessary and sufficient condition for a pair of real
n
-dimensional totally real planes intersecting only at the origin to have the property that their union is Lagrangian with respect to a Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. As observed in
[
7
, § 2]
, any nonsingular complex linear transformation that maps one of the two planes to
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\operatorname{Im}z=0\}
, maps the other plane to
S(A)=(A+i)\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
. We note an elementary result that will be used throughout the paper.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\iota:M\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smooth embedding of a smooth real
n
-dimensional manifold
M
. Let
\omega
be a Kähler form on some neighborhood of
\iota(M)
such that
\iota(M)
is Lagrangian with respect to
\omega
, i.e.
i^{*}\omega=0
. Let
G
be a biholomorphism on a neighborhood of
\iota(M)
. Then,
(G\circ\iota)(M)
is Lagrangian with respect to the Kähler form
{G^{-1}}^{*}\omega
.

By the above lemma, it suffices to assume that the given pair of totally real planes is of the form
(\mathbb{R}^{n},S(A))
for some
A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
. We then have the following result.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
and
S(A)=(A+i)\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,
\mathbb{R}^{n}\cup S(A)
is Lagrangian with repsect to some Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
if and only if
A
is diagonalizable over the reals.

Before we proceed with the proof of this result, we observe that it suffices to work with a real Jordan form of
A
. This is owing to Lemma
3.1
and the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
7
, Lemma 2]
)
.

Let
P\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
be nonsingular. Then, the complex linear transformation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
given by
P
maps
\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
S(A)
onto
S(PAP^{-1})
.

Proof.

That
P
maps
\mathbb{R}^{n}
bijectively onto
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is clear. Let
z\in S(PAP^{-1})
. Then, there is an
x=(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

z=\left(PAP^{-1}\right)x+ix=\left(PAP^{-1}\right)x+i(PP^{-1})x=P\left((A+i)(P^{-1}x)\right)\in P\left(S(A)\right)

since
P^{-1}x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus,
S(PAP^{-1})\subset P(S(A))
. Applying this to
P^{-1}
and
PAP^{-1}
instead of
P
and
A
, respectively, we obtain that
S(A)\subset P^{-1}S(PAP^{-1})
, which gives the other inclusion.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.
We first record a general formula that will be used throughout this proof. Let

\iota:(t_{1},...,t_{n})\mapsto(t_{1},...,t_{n}),\quad(t_{1},...,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

be a parametrization of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For a fixed
A=(a_{jk})\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
, let

\iota_{A}:(t_{1},...,t_{n})\mapsto\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{1k}t_{k}+it_{1},...,\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{nk}t_{k}+it_{n}\right),\quad(t_{1},...,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

be a parametrization of
S(A)
. Given a Kähler form (with constant coefficients)

\omega=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}h_{jk}dz_{j}\wedge d\bar{z}_{k}

on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
h=(h_{jk})\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})
is a positive definite Hermitian matrix, we have that

\iota^{*}\omega=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(h_{jk}-h_{kj})dt_{j}\wedge dt_{k}=2i\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\left(\operatorname{Im}h_{jk}\right)dt_{j}\wedge dt_{k},

and

\displaystyle\iota_{A}^{*}\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}h_{jk}\left(dt_{j}\wedge dt_{k}+\sum_{t,m=1}^{n}(a_{jm}a_{k\ell})dt_{m}\wedge dt_{\ell}+i\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}a_{k\ell}dt_{j}\wedge dt_{\ell}-i\sum_{m=1}^{n}a_{jm}dt_{m}\wedge dt_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\left(h_{jk}+\sum_{r,s=1}^{n}h_{rs}a_{rj}a_{sk}+i\sum_{r=1}^{n}h_{jr}a_{rk}-i\sum_{s=1}^{n}h_{sk}a_{sj}\right)dt_{j}\wedge dt_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2i\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\left(h_{jk}-h_{kj}+\sum_{r,s=1}^{n}\left(h_{rs}-h_{sr}\right)a_{rj}a_{sk}+\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left(h_{jr}+h_{rj}\right)a_{rk}-\sum_{s=1}^{n}(h_{sk}+h_{ks})a_{sj}\right)dt_{j}\wedge dt_{k}.

Thus,
\mathbb{R}^{n}\cup S(A)
is Lagrangian with respect to
\omega
if and only if

(5)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}h_{jk}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad 1\leq j<k\leq n,

(6)

\displaystyle c_{jk}(A)=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left((\operatorname{Re}h_{jr})a_{rk}-(\operatorname{Re}h_{kr})a_{rj}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad 1\leq j<k\leq n.

Let us proceed to prove the claim. First, assume that
A
is diagonalizable over the reals. By Lemmas
3.1
and
3.3
, it suffices to consider a real Jordan form
J(A)
of
A
, which by assumption is a diagonal matrix with real diagonal entries. From (
5
) and (
6
), it follows that
\mathbb{R}^{n}\cup S(J(A))
is Lagrangian with respect to the standard Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Conversely, suppose
A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
is such that
\mathbb{R}^{n}\cup S(A)
is Lagrangian with respect to some Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then, by Lemmas
3.1
and
3.3
, so is
\mathbb{R}^{n}\cup S(J(A))
, where
J(A)
is a real Jordan form of
A
. Suppose

is not diagonalizable. Then,

can be chosen to be of the form

J(A)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&A_{m}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(7)

(8)

. We consider the two cases separately.

Case 1.
Assume that

is Lagrangian with respect to

. Since both

Case 2.
Assume that

). Let

be a Kähler form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}

must be diagonalizable over the reals.
∎

4.
Construction of the Kähler form at diagonalizable transverse points

In this section, we inspect a maximal totally real immersed submanifold of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
near a single diagonalizable transverse double point, and show that the submanifold is locally Lagrangian with respect to a Kähler form on a neighborhood of the double point. In order to patch such local Kähler forms together, as will be done later, we need something stronger: we produce a local Kähler form
dd^{c}f
such that pull-back of
d^{c}f
vanishes on the submanifold near the double point.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\iota:M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smooth totally real immersion of a smooth
n
-dimensional manifold
M
. Let
p\in\iota(M)
be an isolated diagonalizable double point of
\iota(M)
. Then, there exist a neighborhood
U
of
p
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and a smooth function
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

(i)

\iota^{*}(d^{c}f)=0
on
\iota^{-1}(U)
, and

(ii)

dd^{c}f>0
on
U
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}
denote the two local branches of
\iota(M)
at the double point
p
. Due to the hypothesis on
p
, after a complex affine change in coordinates, we may assume that
p
is the origin
0
,
T_{0}\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
T_{0}\mathcal{S}=(A+i)\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where

A=(a_{jk})_{n\times n}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{n}),\quad\text{for some }\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}.

By the implicit function theorem, there exist open balls
\mathcal{B}_{1},...,\mathcal{B}_{4}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
centered at the origin, and smooth functions
\phi=(\phi_{1},...,\phi_{n}):\mathcal{B}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{3}
and
\psi=(\psi_{1},...,\psi_{n}):\mathcal{B}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{4}
, with

(9)

\phi(0)=\psi(0)=D\phi(0)=D\psi(0)=0,

such that
\mathcal{R}=\left\{z\in\mathcal{B}_{1}+i\mathcal{B}_{3}:z=t+i\phi(t)\right\}
and
\mathcal{S}=\left\{z\in\mathcal{B}_{2}+i\mathcal{B}_{4}:z=(A+i)t+\psi(t)\right\}
. Parametrize
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}
locally via the maps

\displaystyle\imath:(t_{1},...,t_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle(t_{1}+i\phi_{1}(t),...,t_{n}+i\phi_{n}(t)),\quad t=(t_{1},...,t_{n})\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
