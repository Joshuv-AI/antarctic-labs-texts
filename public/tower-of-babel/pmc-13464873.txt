Comparison of erector spinae plane, paravertebral, and pectoral nerve blocks on postoperative analgesia and Quality of Recovery-15 in patients undergoing mastectomy: a prospective randomized trial
Authors: Bilal Atilla Bezen, Merve Ay, Ali Kaynak, Murat Akici, Remziye Sivaci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundModified radical mastectomy (MRM) is frequently associated with moderate to severe postoperative pain, which may negatively affect recovery and quality of life. Regional anesthesia techniques such as the erector spinae plane block (ESPB), thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB), and pectoral nerve block (PECS) have been increasingly used as part of multimodal analgesia strategies for breast surgery. However, their comparative effects on analgesic efficacy and quality of recovery remain unclear.MethodsThe study included 90 female patients undergoing unilateral elective MRM. Patients were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 30 each) to receive ESPB, TPVB, or PECS block preoperatively with 0.25% bupivacaine. All patients subsequently underwent standardized general anesthesia. Postoperative pain was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. The primary outcome was total morphine consumption at 24 h. Secondary outcomes included time to first morphine request, incidence of rescue fentanyl use, postoperative nausea and vomiting, patient satisfaction, and scores.ResultsDemographic characteristics and operative durations were comparable among the groups (p > 0.05). Total 24-hour morphine consumption was significantly higher in the ESPB group (median 10 mg) compared with TPVB (9 mg, p = 0.009) and PECS (9 mg, p = 0.018) groups. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that the time to first morphine request was significantly shorter in the ESPB group (p < 0.001), while TPVB provided the longest analgesic duration. No significant differences were found in postoperative pain scores, rescue opioid requirements, or nausea-vomiting incidence (p > 0.05). QoR-15 scores were significantly lower in the ESPB group compared with TPVB and PECS groups (p < 0.001).ConclusionBoth PECS and TPVB provided superior postoperative analgesia and better quality of recovery scores compared to ESPB in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy. These findings support the use of PECS or TPVB as effective components of multimodal analgesia strategies for breast cancer surgery.Trial RegistrationISRCTN Registry, ISRCTN17247698.Keywords: Modified radical mastectomy, Erector spinae plane block, Thoracic paravertebral block, Pectoral nerve block, Postoperative analgesia, QoR-15, Regional anesthesia

Background

Breast cancer persists as the leading malignancy affecting women globally, with modified radical mastectomy (MRM) constituting a fundamental component of its treatment (Cassai et al. 2019). However, MRM is frequently accompanied by moderate to severe postoperative pain because of the intricate innervation of the breast and anterior chest wall (Schnabel et al. 2010, Altıparmak et al. 2019). Achieving adequate postoperative analgesia consequently presents a challenge, typically necessitating a multimodal strategy to reduce opioid requirements and support recovery (Gürkan et al. 2018, Maniker et al. 2020). Pain that is poorly controlled can compromise patient comfort, extend hospital stays, and elevate the risk of chronic postsurgical pain development (Schnabel et al. 2010).

Thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) has historically been regarded as an effective regional technique for breast surgery analgesia (Schnabel et al. 2010, Zhang et al. 2025). Concerns regarding potential risks, including pneumothorax, have prompted the investigation of safer options. Consequently, ultrasound-guided fascial plane blocks such as the Pectoral Nerve Blocks (PECS), introduced by Blanco et al., and the Erector Spinae Plane Block (ESPB), introduced by Forero et al., are increasingly adopted as safer and technically simpler alternatives (Blanco et al. 2012, Forero et al. 2016). While PECS block specifically targets the lateral and medial pectoral nerves, ESPB produces a broader, though less predictable, sensory blockade via diffusion of local anesthetic toward the paravertebral compartment (Altıparmak et al. 2019, Zhao et al. 2022).

While many studies have evaluated these blocks in pairwise comparisons, the existing literature shows inconsistency concerning their comparative effectiveness. Some reports indicate superior analgesia with PECS or TPVB over ESPB, whereas others describe similar outcomes (Eskandr et al. 2022, Hong et al. 2021, Kulhari et al. 2016, Elewa et al. 2022, Hetta and Rezk 2016). Moreover, the majority of these comparative investigations concentrate mainly on pain intensity metrics. Postoperative recovery, in contrast, is a multidimensional outcome. The Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) scale allows for a more holistic evaluation of the patient experience than isolated pain assessments; however, comparative data examining the influence of these three specific blocks on QoR-15 scores remain limited (Kleif and Gögenur 2018, Myles et al. 2022).

We hypothesized that both TPVB and PECS would lead to better analgesia and recovery quality relative to ESPB in patients scheduled for unilateral modified radical mastectomy. Accordingly, evaluating and comparing the analgesic effectiveness of ultrasound-guided ESPB, TPVB, and PECS block in this surgical population was the primary goal of this study. A secondary objective was to examine their differential impacts on postoperative recovery quality using the QoR-15 scale.

Methods

This assessor-blinded, randomized, controlled, single-center trial was performed at the Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University, Department of Anesthesiology and Reanimation, following approval (30.04.2021/335) by the University’s Clinical Research Ethics Committee. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for reporting randomized trials. The Declaration of Helsinki’s ethical standards were followed for all procedures. The study was registered with the ISRCTN registry (ISRCTN17247698).

The study enrolled 90 female patients, aged 18–70 years, classified as ASA physical status I-II, who were scheduled for elective unilateral modified radical mastectomy with axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer. Exclusion criteria comprised obesity, prior breast surgery, coagulopathy, known allergy to the study medications, chronic analgesic or anticoagulant use, local or systemic infection, significant cardiac arrhythmia, and cardiac, hepatic, or renal insufficiency. During the preoperative visit, patients were fully informed about the study, and both verbal and written consent were obtained.

Patient allocation

Participants were assigned randomly in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three groups (ESPB, TPVB, PECS-2) using a computer-generated random sequence with permuted blocks (block size = 6). Allocation concealment was maintained via sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by a research coordinator (RS) not involved in clinical care. The anesthesiologist administering the blocks (BAB) knew the group assignment, but the assessor responsible for outcome assessment and postoperative follow up (MAy) remained unaware of group allocation throughout the postoperative period. Complete blinding of patients was not possible due to differing block anatomical region; however, all postoperative evaluations, pain assessments, data recording, and statistical analyses were carried out by personnel fully blinded to group assignment. The surgical team was not blinded; however, they were not involved in postoperative assessments or data collection, thereby minimizing potential bias.

Block and anesthesia application

After applying standard anesthesia monitoring (ECG, non-invasive blood pressure, SpO₂) in the block room, all patients received premedication with 1 mg intravenous midazolam and 50 µg fentanyl. All blocks were carried out following skin infiltration with 3 mL of 2% lidocaine at the needle entry site.

All blocks were performed preoperatively in a dedicated block area prior to the induction of general anesthesia. A high-frequency linear ultrasound probe (6–13 MHz) and an in-plane needle technique were used in all cases, with a standard 22-gauge, 80 mm echogenic needle. Probe orientation and needle trajectory were adjusted according to the target anatomy, with a longitudinal orientation for ESPB and TPVB, and transverse or sagittal orientations for the PECS II block as appropriate.

Group I (ESPB): Patients were seated with the neck slightly flexed. The injection site was marked 2.5–3 cm lateral to the T4 spinous process. Under ultrasound visualization, the transverse process and overlying erector spinae muscle were identified. Using an in-plane longitudinal approach, the needle was advanced into the fascial plane superficial to the transverse process and deep to the erector spinae muscle. Following confirmation of negative aspiration, 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was administered.

Group II (TPVB): Patients were positioned similarly. The paravertebral space at T4 was identified sonographically. An in-plane longitudinal technique was used to advance the needle through the superior costotransverse ligament into the thoracic paravertebral space. Following negative aspiration, 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was administered.

Group III (PECS-2): The block was performed supine with the ipsilateral arm abducted to 90°. After skin preparation, the ultrasound probe was placed sagittally in the infraclavicular area to identify the axillary vessels. The probe was subsequently moved caudally to identify the thoracoacromial artery before being rotated to a transverse orientation. Under ultrasound guidance, the needle was introduced in-plane from the lateral probe edge into the interfascial plane between the pectoralis major and minor muscles, where 10 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was administered. The probe was then slid toward the axilla over the 2nd to 4th ribs to visualize the serratus anterior muscle. An additional 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was injected into the plane between the pectoralis minor and serratus anterior muscles.

Patients in Groups 1 and 2 were repositioned supine immediately post-block to avoid affecting local anesthetic distribution. Sensory blockade was assessed in all groups, 20 min after block performance using cold and pinprick tests along the midaxillary line across the T2–T6 dermatomes. Confirmation of sensory loss in this region was required before transferring the patient to the operating room. A separate anesthesiologist (AK) induced general anesthesia for all patients using IV propofol (2–3 mg/kg), fentanyl (2 µg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) for tracheal intubation. Anesthesia was maintained with 2% sevoflurane in a 50% oxygen/air mixture, with ventilation adjusted to keep an end-tidal CO2 of 30–35 mmHg. Approximately ten minutes before surgery concluded, patients received IV paracetamol 1 g (analgesia), metoclopramide 10 mg, and dexamethasone 8 mg (antiemesis). Upon wound closure, anesthetic gases were discontinued, neuromuscular blockade was reversed with sugammadex (2 mg/kg), and the trachea was extubated.

Data collection and study outcomes

The time of arrival in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) was designated as postoperative hour 0. From this point, postoperative monitoring and management were conducted by the blinded assessor (MAy). Each patient received patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) with morphine (0.5 mg/mL), set to administer a 1 mg bolus, with a 10-minute lockout interval and a maximum dose of 6 mg per hour. Scheduled IV paracetamol (1 g) was repeated at 8 and 16 h postoperatively. Measurement of postoperative pain intensity was carried out at rest and during movement (active ipsilateral arm abduction) at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS; 0 = no pain, 10 = worst imaginable pain). Patients were asked to indicate their pain intensity by marking a point on a 10-cm horizontal line corresponding to their perceived pain level. Rescue analgesia with IV fentanyl (1 µg/kg) was administered if the VAS score was ≥ 4. Analgesic consumption and opioid-related side effects over the initial 24 h were recorded.

For postoperative nausea or vomiting (PONV) lasting > 5 min or occurring even once, IV ondansetron 4 mg was given. The quality of postoperative recovery was determined at 24 h using the 15-item Quality of Recovery questionnaire (QoR-15). We employed the Turkish adaptation of the QoR-15, which has undergone prior translation and validation (Selvi et al. 2022). Based on established categories, total scores were classified as poor (0–89), moderate (90–121), good (122–135), or excellent (136–150) (Kleif and Gögenur 2018). Additional recorded parameters included patient satisfaction with analgesia (5-point Likert scale: 1 = poor to 5 = very good) and time to hospital discharge.

The primary study endpoint was cumulative 24-hour morphine consumption. Secondary endpoints encompassed the time to first PCA morphine demand, incidence of rescue fentanyl use, PONV incidence, VAS pain scores, and postoperative QoR-15 scores.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0). Data distribution normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and skewness/kurtosis statistics. Normally distributed demographic data (e.g., Age) were analyzed with one-way ANOVA and are expressed as Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). Kruskal-Wallis test was used to analyze the data without a normal distribution (e.g., morphine use, VAS scores), reported as Median (Minimum-Maximum). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons utilized the Bonferroni-adjusted Mann-Whitney U test or Tukey’s HSD test as appropriate. The time to first analgesic request was analyzed with Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the log-rank test. No patient data were censored in this analysis, as all participants required morphine within the 24-hour observation window.

Sample size

Sample size calculation was based on the study by Çiftçi et al., with a targeted effect size of 0.47 (Ciftci et al. 2021). Considering an alpha of 0.05 and 95% statistical power, a minimum of 25 patients per group was needed. Thirty patients per group were enrolled to account for dropouts.

Comparisons of VAS scores at rest and during movement at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h revealed no statistically significant differences among the groups (p > 0.05, Table 2; Figs. 2 and 3).

The median duration until the first morphine request was significantly shorter in the ESPB group (197.5 min, 95% CI: 150–270) compared to the TPVB group (250 min, 95% CI: 180–350) and the PECS group (215 min, 95% CI: 150–340) (p < 0.001). This interval was significantly shorter for ESPB versus both TPVB (p < 0.001) and PECS (p = 0.029), while no significant difference existed between TPVB and PECS (p > 0.05). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed a significant difference among techniques for time to first analgesic demand (Fig. 4). The cumulative proportion of patients requiring analgesia rose most rapidly in the ESPB group, indicating a quicker decline in block effect compared to the TPVB and PECS groups.

No statistically significant differences were detected between the groups regarding the need for rescue fentanyl or the incidence of PONV (p > 0.05). Patient satisfaction scores differed significantly across groups (p = 0.036). While TPVB and PECS groups reported similarly high satisfaction (mean ranks 50.95 and 49.22), the ESPB group reported lower satisfaction (mean rank 36.33). The difference between ESPB and PECS groups approached significance (p = 0.055), while that between ESPB and TPVB did not (p = 0.114) (Table 3).

Postoperative QoR-15 scores were significantly lower in the ESPB group than in the TPVB and PECS groups. Furthermore, the distribution of QoR-15 classification categories differed significantly (p < 0.001). A higher proportion of patients in the TPVB and PECS groups achieved “Excellent” or “Good” recovery ratings, whereas a “Moderate” rating was significantly more frequent in the ESPB group (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study of patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy, the ESPB group was associated with higher postoperative morphine consumption and a shorter time to first analgesic request. Although a statistically significant difference in 24-hour morphine consumption was observed, the absolute difference between groups was small (approximately 1 mg), suggesting limited clinical relevance when interpreted in isolation. The absence of differences in pain scores, rescue opioid use, and opioid-related side effects further supports that this finding does not reflect a meaningful difference in analgesic efficacy. Therefore, opioid consumption should be interpreted within the broader clinical context. Notably, recovery quality, as assessed by QoR-15, emerged as the more clinically discriminative outcome, exceeding the minimal clinically important difference threshold.

A noteworthy finding in our study is that VAS pain scores were comparable across groups despite higher opioid consumption in the ESPB group. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by the use of patient-controlled analgesia, which enables patients to titrate opioid intake according to individual needs, potentially equalizing pain scores across groups despite differences in baseline block efficacy. In addition, VAS scores may lack sensitivity in detecting subtle differences, particularly in the context of generally low postoperative pain levels. The shorter time to first analgesic request in the ESPB group further suggests a shorter block duration, leading to earlier reliance on systemic opioids. Together, these findings indicate that similar pain scores do not necessarily imply equivalent analgesic efficacy, but may instead reflect compensatory opioid use.

An important aspect of this study is the concurrent evaluation of both analgesic potency and recovery quality across three distinct regional techniques in this cohort. The suboptimal performance of ESPB may stem from its variable anatomical spread. Previous cadaveric and radiological studies confirm inconsistent diffusion into the paravertebral space, particularly with single-injection techniques (Aponte et al. 2019, Greenhalgh et al. 2019). Inadequate blockade of key nerves like the intercostobrachial and those supplying the axilla might also result in insufficient coverage for mastectomy. In contrast, the PECS-2 block reliably covers the long thoracic, thoracodorsal, and intercostobrachial nerves, which are critical pain generators after mastectomy, accounting for its favorable results.

The breast and chest wall receive innervation from multiple sources, including the anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of thoracic spinal nerves (T2–T6), the intercostobrachial nerve, long thoracic nerve, thoracodorsal nerve, and supraclavicular nerves (Maniker et al. 2020). The extensive surgical dissection in MRM necessitates consideration of this broad neural anatomy when planning regional analgesia (Maniker et al. 2020). While PECS blocks the lateral (C5–7) and medial (C8–T1) pectoral nerves, its inhibition of the long thoracic and thoracodorsal nerves confers a specific advantage for axillary coverage (Gürkan et al. 2018, Butiulca et al. 2024). TPVB acts on the thoracic spinal nerve roots within the paravertebral space, whereas ESPB is thought to provide analgesia over a wider dermatomal area via longitudinal spread within a fascial plane (Zhang et al. 2025).

Although fascial plane blocks like PECS and ESPB are increasingly favored as potentially safer ultrasound-based options to TPVB, worldwide clinical adoption varies. Our findings add to this dialogue by showing that TPVB and PECS offer superior postoperative analgesia and recovery quality compared to ESPB in the mastectomy setting.

Elewa et al. reported lower 24-hour morphine consumption with ESPB versus TPVB in MRM patients (Elewa et al. 2022), a finding contrary to our results, where ESPB consumption was higher than both comparator blocks. Hetta and Rezk compared TPVB with PECS-2 and concluded TPVB was superior for analgesia (Hetta and Rezk 2016), whereas we observed no significant difference between them. Similarly, Santonastaso et al. reported that bi-level TPVB provided more effective postoperative analgesia compared to bi-level ESPB in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy, which is consistent with our findings (Santonastaso et al. 2023).

Eskandr et al., evaluating TPVB, PECS, and ESPB in breast cancer surgery, noted higher morphine requirements in the ESPB group (Eskandr et al. 2022), aligning with our findings. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Hong et al. concluded PECS provides superior analgesia to ESPB (Hong et al. 2021). Kulhari et al. reported that PECS yielded better postoperative analgesia than TPVB in radical mastectomy (Kulhari et al. 2016). In our study, PECS and TPVB demonstrated equivalent analgesic efficacy, with both outperforming ESPB. A recent Society for Ambulatory Anesthesia review on outpatient mastectomy highlighted that the PECS block can confer an opioid-sparing effect comparable to TPVB (Ardon et al. 2022). That review further gives a Level A recommendation for using PECS when TPVB is not feasible (Ardon et al. 2022). Other studies corroborate PECS block efficacy. Cassai et al. found that it reduced intraoperative opioid use, lowered postoperative pain, and limited chronic pain development (Cassai et al. 2019). Altıparmak et al. reported lower early postoperative pain scores with PECS-2 versus ESPB (Altıparmak et al. 2019). Conversely, Cesur et al., comparing PECS-2 and ESPB, did not find a difference in morphine consumption (Cesur et al. 2023), unlike our study. This discrepancy could be explained by the single-injection ESPB technique and potential failure of the local anesthetic to reach the necessary dermatomal levels via fascial spread.

Recovery quality is determined not just by pain control but also by early mobility, patient comfort, and side effects. Higher QoR-15 scores in the first 24 postoperative hours are associated with fewer 30-day complications (Kleif and Gögenur 2018), and regional anesthesia techniques are known to improve these scores (Taflan et al. 2025). Wang et al., comparing various plane blocks for MRM, found the thoracic paravertebral block superior to the serratus anterior plane block alone for enhancing recovery quality (Wang et al. 2024). Similarly, in our study, TPVB was associated with high recovery scores. It is noteworthy that the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the QoR-15 is around 8 points; the intergroup differences we observed exceeded this threshold, highlighting their clinical significance (Kleif et al. 2018).

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, its single-center nature may affect the generalizability of the results. Second, QoR-15 was assessed only at a single postoperative time point (24 h), which may not fully reflect the dynamic nature of recovery. Serial assessments at multiple time points could provide a more comprehensive understanding of recovery trajectories and the temporal effects of different regional anesthesia techniques. Finally, we did not evaluate long-term pain outcomes, restricting our ability to comment on chronic pain development.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that TPVB and PECS-2 are associated with better recovery quality and longer duration of analgesia compared to ESPB in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy. Although all three techniques provided comparable baseline pain control, the earlier analgesic requirement and lower recovery scores observed with ESPB suggest less effective and shorter-lasting analgesia. These findings support the use of TPVB or PECS-2 when the goal is to optimize postoperative recovery and reduce opioid requirements.
