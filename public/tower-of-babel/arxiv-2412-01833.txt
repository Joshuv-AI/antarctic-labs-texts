Pseudo-Bifurcations in Stochastic Non-Normal Systems
Virgile Troude, Sandro Lera, Ke Wu, Didier Sornette

Abstract
We challenge the prevailing emphasis on bifurcations as the primary mechanism behind many abrupt surges and transient extremes in complex systems and propose an alternative more universally applicable explanation based on non-normal dynamics. We demonstrate that linear or approximately linear stochastic systems near a dynamical attractor exhibit transient repulsive dynamics – termed pseudo-bifurcations – when interacting components are sufficiently asymmetric and hierarchically organized, i.e., non-normal. These pseudo-bifurcations produce early-warning signals commonly linked to bifurcations, such as dimension reduction, critical slowing down, and increased variance. Furthermore, we show that, as actual bifurcations approach, non-normal transients also emerge, complicating their distinction and potentially creating a bias that suggests the system is much closer to a critical point than it actually is. We support our analytical derivations by empirically demonstrating that the brain exhibits clear signs of such non-normal transients during epileptic seizures. Many systems suspected of approaching critical bifurcation points should be reconsidered, as non-normal dynamics offer a more generic explanation for the observed phenomena across natural, physical, and social systems.

In social systems, notable examples are financial markets that experience long periods of low volatility followed by abrupt crashes

Health-related examples involve the sudden onset of conditions such as

As a system approaches a critical bifurcation threshold, it is often marked by increased variance and autocorrelation in observable metrics.
These statistical signatures reveal that the system’s recovery from perturbations is slowing as it approaches bifurcation, alongside an increasing sensitivity to fluctuations in the system’s state–two complementary indicators that signal the proximity to a critical transition.
Thus, it has been suggested that these statistical signatures serve as important early-warning signals for the onset of a looming bifurcation point
[

,
6
,
4
,
7
]
.

Despite the insights offered by bifurcation theory, the framework itself encounters at least two major challenges. First, it relies on the existence of a well-defined control parameter and its proximity to a critical threshold. Second, it assumes the presence of a driving force that pushes the system toward or beyond this threshold. These assumptions are not only challenging to verify empirically, but also conceptually restrictive and lack general applicability.
It is therefore not surprising that the question of whether certain systems are undergoing critical bifurcations continues to be a subject of debate.
For example, evidence is mixed on whether the onset of epilepsy is truly driven by critical transitions
[
18
,
17
]
.
Similarly, it remains uncertain whether components of the Earth’s system, such as the Amazon rainforest or the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), are approaching bifurcation points
[
19
]
.

In this work, we present an alternative, more general mechanism that relies solely on a stable equilibrium attractor with linear non-normal stochastic dynamics around it. We demonstrate that small perturbations around the equilibrium produce transient repulsions - we dub them
pseudo-bifurcations
in this context - with similar statistical signatures to those observed near genuine bifurcations.
Much like the effective dimension reduction seen during the onset of a bifurcation, we find that non-normal transients are confined to movements within a two-dimensional subspace.
We further find that these transients are accompanied by statistical markers such as critical slowing down and increased autocorrelation.
We empirically illustrate our findings by analyzing EEG signals that capture brain activity during epileptic seizures, revealing clear evidence of non-normal dynamics.
We further demonstrate that the approach of actual bifurcations is accompanied by non-normal transients, making it difficult to distinguish between the two phenomena and potentially creating a bias that suggests the system is much closer to a critical point than it actually is.
Finally, we show that the onset of non-normal transients can be anticipated by measuring the system’s alignment along its
non-normal mode
, and provide a numerical algorithm for stable identification of these non-normal modes.
This opens a new path towards the anticipation and control of instabilities in complex systems.

The only requirement to observe such transients is that the system exhibits sufficient non-normality - meaning its components interact within asymmetric hierarchical structures, a feature common in many social and natural systems
[
20
,
21
]
.
This characteristic is also found in non-network systems, such as turbulent flows, where non-normal dynamics arise from the interplay of viscous and advective terms, which leads to asymmetric, non-orthogonal interactions among velocity perturbations
[
22
]
.
From a mathematical perspective, our findings are generic.
To observe pseudo-bifurcation, all one needs to assume is that the Jacobian of the linearized system, around its equilibrium point, forms a non-normal matrix.
This is a general condition, as non-normal matrices form a measure-one set in the space of all matrices.
Our findings thus suggest that many systems previously thought to be approaching criticality may need to be reconsidered, as they could instead be stable yet non-normal systems.
More generally, our results offer a different perspective on how to interpret precursory signals in dynamical systems and highlight the role of non-normal dynamics in improving our understanding and management of a wide range of physical, natural, and social systems.

Results

Reduced Non-Normal Dynamics

We consider a general
n
-dimensional system dynamics

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{A}~\mathbf{x}+B~\boldsymbol{\eta}

(1)

corresponding to the linearization of a general dynamics in the neighborhood of a stable attractor. The vector
\mathbf{x}
quantifies
the distance from the stable attractor.
The matrix
\mathbf{A}
, which governs interactions of the system, is an
n\times n
matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
,
all of which are assumed to have negative real parts, ensuring that the equilibrium point
\mathbf{x}=0

The
n

\boldsymbol{\eta}
is assumed to be normally distributed and
B
quantifies the amplitude of the noise term.
For simplicity, we take

We further assume that the matrix

\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\neq\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}

Mathematically, this is equivalent to at least one pair of eigenvectors being non-orthogonal

,
23
]
.
Phenomenologically, this property implies that the dynamics in (
1

where the system may experience significant deviations from the stable equilibrium before eventually returning to it.

This should not be surprising since non-normal matrices - and by extension, non-normal dynamics - are the norm rather than the exception.
In mathematical terms, non-normal matrices constitute a measure-one subset within the space of all matrices, making them overwhelmingly more prevalent. In contrast, normal matrices form a measure-zero subset, making them a special and less typical case.
This prevalence implies that most real-world systems are inherently non-normal, with dynamics that often exhibit characteristic behaviors like transient growth and sensitivity to perturbations.

Denote by
\mathbf{P}
the basis transformation matrix that diagonalizes
\mathbf{A}
, that is,
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\Lambda\mathbf{P}^{-1}
with
\Lambda=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
.
Because
\mathbf{A}
is non-normal,
\mathbf{P}
is non-unitary, that is transforming
\mathbf{A}
into diagonal form requires not just rotating but also scaling.
Denote by
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{U}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}
the matrix
\mathbf{P}
’s singular value decomposition (SVD) and by
\mathbf{u}_{i}
the
i
-th column vector of unitary matrix
\mathbf{U}
.
The matrix
\mathbf{A}
can be decomposed into pairwise projection operations
\mathbf{A}\sim\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{j}}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\dagger}
where
\sigma_{i}
denotes the
i
-th singular value in
\Sigma=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
.
It is useful to consider the condition number
\kappa
of matrix
\mathbf{A}
, defined as the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular values,
\kappa=\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{n}
, where we assume that the values are sorted without loss of generality.
For a normal system,
\kappa=1
, which means that
\Sigma
reduces to the identity matrix and the SVD of
\mathbf{P}
reduces to
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}
, ensuring its unitarity and thus
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
.
This suggests that the system’s degree of non-normality is well captured by the matrix
\mathbf{A}
’s condition number
\kappa
(see Methods).
Other measures of non-normality include the Kreiss constant or Henrici’s departure from normality
[
23
]
.

In the remainder of this article, we assume, for simplicity, that the first
n-1
eigenvectors are all (nearly) orthogonal to each other, with only the last one being non-orthogonal.
With this assumption,
\sigma_{1}\approx\sigma_{2}\approx\ldots\approx\sigma_{n-1}\approx 1\gg\sigma_{n}=\kappa^{-1}
.
This simplifies some of the subsequent analysis, but does not qualitatively change our results.
As shown in the Method section below,
so long as
\sigma_{n-1}\gg\sigma_{n}
- an assumption that is empirically supported across numerous sociotechnical and natural systems
[
38
]
- the solution of (
1
) exhibits non-normal transients.
In that case, the dynamics of (
1
) can effectively be decomposed into two components:
one along the
non-normal mode

\hat{\mathbf{n}}\sim\mathbf{u}_{n}
and one along its orthogonal
reaction mode

\hat{\mathbf{r}}\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{u}_{i}
,
such that equation (
1
) is reduced to an effective two-dimensional system

\displaystyle\dot{n}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{n}n~+\kappa^{-1}r+B~\eta^{n}+\mathcal{O}\left(\kappa^{-1}\right)

(2a)

\displaystyle\dot{r}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{r}r+~~~\kappa n~+B~\eta^{r}+\mathcal{O}\left(\kappa^{-1}\right)

(2b)

where
n
and
r
denote the dynamics projected onto the non-normal and reaction mode
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
, respectively.
The coefficients
\alpha_{r,n}
are constants of order one (Figure
1
, top).
The eigenvalues of system (
2
) are
\lambda_{\pm}=-\delta_{+}\pm\sqrt{1+\delta_{-}^{2}}
with
\delta_{\pm}=(\alpha_{r}\pm\alpha_{n})/2
.
The associated eigenvectors are given by
\mathbf{v}_{\pm}\sim(\mu_{\pm},\kappa)
with
\mu_{\pm}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
.
The larger
\kappa
becomes, the closer the eigenvectors are to being approximately collinear.

If the system’s degree of non-normality is sufficiently large, the dynamics of (
1
) becomes punctuated by transient repulsion.
This can be understood from equations (
2
) by ignoring the stochastic term and higher-order terms.
Starting at
(n,r)=(1,0)
, the variable
n
, and thus
||\mathbf{x}||_{2}
, initially decreases as the non-normal mode relaxes to zero at a rate
\alpha_{n}
via (
2a
).
Simultaneously, the reaction component
r
grows rapidly at a rate
\kappa
via (
2b
).
When
\kappa
is sufficiently large, as specified below, it dominates the relaxation dynamics, leading to the growth of
||\mathbf{x}||_{2}
.
This growth saturates at its peak when
\dot{r}=0
, after which the relaxation dynamics takes over, restoring the system to its stable equilibrium at
||\mathbf{x}||_{2}=0
at rate
\lambda_{+}
(Figure
1
, bottom).
We stress that this two-dimensional decomposition applies to systems of arbitrary dimension

.

A bifurcation in a continuous dynamical system occurs when a small change in a parameter causes a qualitative change in the stability of an equilibrium or fixed point.
Mathematically, this corresponds to the real part of an eigenvalue of the equilibrium passing through zero.
Formally, for a system
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=f(\mathbf{x};\mu)
, a bifurcation occurs at
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mu_{0})
if the Jacobian
Df_{\mathbf{x}_{0},\mu_{0}}
has eigenvalues with zero real part.
If the eigenvalue has an imaginary component, the bifurcation is classified as a Hopf bifurcation, leading to periodic limit cycles.
Otherwise, it is referred to as a steady-state bifurcation, such as a saddle-node bifurcation (where two equilibria collide) or a pitchfork bifurcation (where one fixed point branches into three).
These bifurcations are not just mathematical constructs, but have significant real-world implications, leading to qualitatively altered system behavior.
For instance, desertification in semi-arid regions, such as the Sahel, occurs when vegetation loss passes a critical threshold, leading to a rapid and often irreversible shift to desert landscapes
[
10
]
.
Similarly, lake eutrophication, such as in Lake Erie, involves a bifurcation where nutrient overload causes a sudden transition from a clear-water state to a turbid, algae-dominated system
[
11
]
.

Assume that we do not have perfect observation of
\mathbf{x}
, nor a clear understanding of the underlying dynamics, but that we observe the system’s mean-field state value
\langle\mathbf{x}\rangle
(or similarly, its norm
||\mathbf{x}||
).
We then model the system’s mean-field dynamics as an effective Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

\dot{\langle\mathbf{x}\rangle}=\phi~\langle\mathbf{x}\rangle+B_{\text{eff}}~\eta

(5)

where
\phi\equiv\exp(\lambda_{\text{eff}})-1<0
captures the system’s mean-reversal component with effective negative eigenvalue
\lambda_{\text{eff}}
.
The system in equation (
5
) exhibits fluctuations with a variance equal to
B^{2}/(2|\phi|)
, and the correlation between states separated by a time interval
\Delta t
is given by
\exp(-|\phi|\Delta t)
.
Therefore, near a critical point, autocorrelation slowly decays with a characteristic time scale diverging as
\sim|\phi|^{-1}
,
causing the system to relax slowly to equilibrium, a phenomenon known as critical slowing down.
At the same time, the variance tends to infinity.
These characteristic statistical signatures are collectively known as early-warning signals for bifurcation transitions
[
5
,
6
,
4
,
7
]
.

However, while bifurcations can generate such patterns, similar dynamics can also emerge when a non-normal system experiences transient repulsion, even in the absence of an actual bifurcation.
Figure
3
shows the temporal evolution of
\phi
and
\lambda_{\text{eff}}
obtained by fitting an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process to the mean-field dynamics (
1
).
During large transients, these coefficients approach zero, while the autocorrelation and variance increase in a way similar to the onset of a critical bifurcation.
These observations can be explained by the fact that the transient dynamics temporarily induce a regime of repulsive, quasi-deterministic growth via (
2b
).
At a qualitative level, the stochastic loops (Figure
2
, right, and Figure
3
, (d)) could easily be mistaken for Hopf limit cycles.
This underscores that non-normality, much like bifurcations, can generate the same early-warning signals, complicating the interpretation of these indicators.
Given the similarity between these statistical patterns and those observed near critical transitions, even when the underlying non-normal system is far from or entirely devoid of true critical behavior, we refer to these transient repulsive dynamics as
pseudo-bifurcations
.
This terminology is further justified by the fact that the natural tool to analyze the transient dynamics of non-normal operators is the pseudospectrum
[
23
]
, which extends the concept of eigenvalues by replacing the condition of a vanishing characteristic determinant with the requirement that it be small, thereby capturing transient amplification effects that standard spectral analysis misses.

Distinguishing true bifurcations (if any) from pseudo-bifurcations becomes even more challenging as a system nears an actual critical bifurcation point.
From equation (
4
) we see that as the system’s effective eigenvalue
\lambda_{\text{eff}}
, and thus
\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}
, approaches zero,
the critical threshold
\kappa_{c}
at some point crosses the system’s non-normality level
\kappa
, and hence repulsive transients emerge stochastically.
In other words, before entering a critical regime, the system first passes through a pseudo-critical regime (Figure
2
, middle).
Early warning signals that are usually used to gauge a system’s distance to criticality indicate wrongly that the system is much closer to a critical point than it actually is.

We emphasize that pseudo-bifurcations are observed at a fixed level of non-normality,
\kappa>\kappa_{c}
, within a stationary system (
1
) fluctuating around an equilibrium point.
This provides a highly general first-order approximation of equilibrium dynamics without requiring the system’s control parameter to move toward a critical point.
Given the prevalence of interdependence structures
\mathbf{A}
known to exhibit non-normality
[
21
]
, we propose that pseudo-bifurcations are likely far more common than true bifurcations in empirical systems.

In addition, complex systems are frequently governed by mechanisms such as proportional growth that drive
\mathbf{A}
towards states of increased hierarchical organizations and hence non-normality
[
39
,
21
,
37
]
.
As shown in the Methods Section below, the variance of system (
5
) scales like
B_{\text{eff}}\approx B\kappa^{2}/\left(\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}(\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-})\right)
.
Similarly, the auto-correlation at lag
\Delta t
is given by
\exp(-\Gamma(\tau)~\Delta t)
with
\Gamma(\tau)\approx 1/B_{\text{eff}}
.
As the system’s non-normality, and thus
\kappa
increases, so does the system’s effective variance and auto-correlation time.
This suggests an increased likelihood of false-positive markers of bifurcation in systems that dynamically self-organize into hierarchical structures.

Brain connectomes, which are comprehensive maps of structural and functional neural connections, are essential tools for studying the complex dynamics of the brain.
In the context of epilepsy, brain connectomes have been used to explore the neural underpinnings of seizures, which involve sudden and excessive synchronization of neural activity
[
40
]
.
It has been hypothesized that seizures may be related to the brain connectome operating near a critical state, a threshold where brain dynamics is poised between order and chaos
[
41
]
.
However, whether or not the brain operates at criticality during epileptic seizures remains debated
[
18
,
17
]
.
A mean-field model of the excitation and inhibition dynamics has been proposed to explain these phenomena
[
42
]
.
This model demonstrates how non-normal transients, driven by an interplay between excitation and inhibition, can lead to large bursts of neural activity, reminiscent of seizure dynamics.
Building on these observations, and because it has been shown that the connectome is strongly non-normal
[
21
]
,
we hypothesize that a non-normal transient burst in the brain’s dynamics may be the underlying source of the apparent increase in excitability during seizures, shifting the system into a more excitable, uncontrolled regime and revealing the inherent non-normal characteristics of the connectome.

To test this claim, we utilize an EEG recording of a patient during an epileptic seizure recorded at the Children’s Hospital Boston
[
43
]
.
The measurement includes data across 23 EEG channels sampled at 256 Hz,
before, during and after a seizure event that has been classified as lasting for 40 seconds
(see top-left in Figure
4
for the mean-field dynamics).
We empirically estimate the system’s non-normality
\kappa
, as well as non-normal and reaction mode (see Methods).
The lower left plot of Figure
4
reveals a marked increase in the connectome’s non-normality that occurs precisely at the onset of the epileptic seizure, persisting well beyond its clinical definition.
This quantitatively demonstrates that the dynamics associated with the seizure extend far beyond the observable clinical symptoms.
This is further supported by the prolonged high amplitude and variance in EEG recordings, which remain elevated long after the seizure has clinically ended.
In line with the observation that
\mathbf{A}
becomes highly non-normal after the onset of the epileptic seizure,
we observe in the top right plots of Figure
4
that there are stochastic loops during, but not before the seizure.

By measuring the lead-lag correlation between the system’s projection along
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{n}
and the mean-field state
\langle\mathbf{x}\rangle
, we observe that the former leads the later by about
0.1
seconds (Figure
4
, bottom right).
This suggests that epileptic seizures may not only be driven by non-normal dynamics but also that the onset of transient episodes could be anticipated by measuring the system’s exposure to non-normal components.
This insight offers a promising pathway to improve the early detection and possible control of seizure events, while also paving the way for the management of self-induced instabilities in complex systems more broadly
[
44
]
.

Discussion

Stanislaw Ulam famously quipped that “using a term like nonlinear science is like referring to the bulk of zoology as the study of non-elephant animals,” humorously highlighting that linear systems are rare in nature, while nonlinear dynamics dominate real-world phenomena.
This remark underscores how the emphasis on linearity has often overshadowed the vastly more prevalent and complex behaviors of nonlinear systems.
We propose that a similar oversight has occurred with respect to non-normal dynamics.
In the realm of complex systems, most interaction matrices are inherently non-normal, meaning that transient dynamics driven by non-normality are not just common but should be anticipated as central features.
In fact, the situation for non-normal systems is even more universal, as these transient behaviors arise even within linear non-normal systems.
Just as linear systems have historically received disproportionate attention at the expense of nonlinear ones, bifurcations have similarly overshadowed the far more general and widespread influence of non-normal transients.
We emphasize again that the dynamics of an arbitrarily large, complex system around an equilibrium point is well approximated, to first order, by (
1
),
and that the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{A}
being non-normal is both mathematically and phenomenologically the norm, not the exception.
Ironically, non-normal matrices are much more “normal” (i.e. common) than
normal matrices
.

Bifurcation theory has been instrumental in understanding critical transitions, offering profound insights into how systems behave as they approach or cross critical thresholds.
However, it addresses only one specific scenario.
In contrast, non-normal transients can occur under much broader conditions, even when a system is far from a bifurcation point.
For instance, for (
5
) to exhibit critical behavior, the real part of the system’s largest eigenvalue has to be extremely close to
0
,
whereas non-normal transients can be observed for values well below this threshold.
This suggests that many abrupt surges and transient extremes presumed to be critical might can, in reality, be better explained by non-normal transients rather than proximity to bifurcation points.
Consequently, we believe such cases merit re-examination to uncover the true underlying dynamics.
For instance, recent research has demonstrated that financial bubbles can be interpreted as sub-critical transient excursions driven by hierarchically organized influence networks
[
36
]
and that these networks self-organize towards non-normal configurations
[
37
]
.

Naturally, we must apply our framework with caution.
It is well-established that many systems experience true bifurcations, where crossing a critical threshold leads to a fundamental change in behavior.
Such transitions result in a qualitative shift in dynamics that is both observable and empirically validated.
Beyond the previously mentioned cases of desertification
[
10
]
and lake eutrophication
[
11
]
, coral reefs provide a notable example:
Under stressors such as pollution, overfishing, and climate change, reefs can cross a threshold, transitioning from coral-dominated to algae-dominated states.
This shift is often irreversible, as the algae-dominated state hinders coral regrowth through altered ecological dynamics
[
45
]
.
Similarly, certain fisheries, such as the Newfoundland cod fishery, have experienced abrupt collapses due to overfishing;
once population levels dropped below a critical threshold, recovery became impossible, disrupting both ecological and economic systems
[
46
]
.

However, in many cases, the assumption that a system is nearing a critical point remains largely hypothetical, with empirical evidence still inconclusive.
For example, there is ongoing debate on whether epileptic seizures constitute a critical transition in brain dynamics.
Although some studies suggest that the brain operates near a critical point, others find the evidence to be inconclusive
[
18
,
17
]
.
Our findings align with the latter perspective, reinforcing the view that seizures do not necessarily correspond to critical transitions.

Similarly, the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is suspected of approaching a bifurcation point that could lead to abrupt climate shifts, yet definitive observations remain lacking
[
19
]
.
It is also possible that warmer interglacial periods observed in Earth’s climate history do not result from crossing a critical bifurcation point but instead reflect non-normal transients within an otherwise glacial climate regime.
In these examples, the system’s proximity to a critical point is speculative, and a reevaluation through the lens of non-normal dynamics could offer new insights.

In summary, by focusing on non-normal dynamics, we have proposed an alternative explanation for early-warning signals traditionally attributed to bifurcations in complex systems.
We demonstrated that systems with a high degree of non-normality can exhibit transient repulsive dynamics - called pseudo-bifurcations - which give rise to the same statistical signatures as true bifurcations, such as critical slowing down and increased variance.
Importantly, these patterns do not necessarily indicate proximity to a critical bifurcation point.
Our empirical analysis of EEG recordings during epileptic seizures further supports this hypothesis, showing that the brain’s non-normal dynamics are prominently dominating epileptic seizures.
These findings suggest that many systems previously believed to be approaching criticality may, in fact, be at long-term equilibrium but exhibit strong non-normal transients.
This calls for a reexamination of systems previously assumed to operate near criticality and offers a new perspective on the anticipation and control of instabilities in dynamical complex systems.

Materials and Methods

Dimensional Reduction of Non-Normal Dynamics

Because matrix
\mathbf{A}
that governs Eq. (
1
) is assumed diagonalizable, there exists a matrix
\mathbf{P}
which is the eigenbasis transformation of
\mathbf{A}
such that

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{P}^{-1}

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=-\text{diag}\left(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}\right)
is the diagonal matrix composed of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
.
Here, we assume that
\lambda_{i}>0~\forall i
and we explicitly set the minus sign to indicate that the eigenvalues are negative.
If
\mathbf{A}
is normal, that is, if
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}
, it would be the case that
\mathbf{P}
is unitary, that is
\mathbf{A}
can be diagonalized by means of a rotation.
But in general, we assume here that
\mathbf{A}
is non-normal, and hence
\mathbf{P}
is not unitary.
Quite generally, a matrix
\mathbf{P}
can be represented as a sequence of a rotation, a rescaling, and another rotation, via the singular value decomposition (SVD) given by

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}

where
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
are unitary matrices,
\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}
is the Hermitian conjugate of
\mathbf{V}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{n})
is a diagonal matrix of the singular values
\{\sigma_{i}\}
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\sigma_{1}\geqslant\sigma_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\sigma_{n}
because a permutation of a unitary matrix remains unitary.
Plugging
\mathbf{P}
into the above expression for
\mathbf{A}
yields

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{j}}\lambda_{ij}\mathbf{\hat{u}}_{i}\mathbf{\hat{u}}_{j}^{\dagger}

(6)

with

\lambda_{ij}=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}v_{ik}v_{jk}^{*},

and where

\mathbf{u}_{i}

and

\mathbf{v}_{i}
denote the
i^{\text{th}}
column of the unitary matrix
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
, respectively.
Further,
v_{ij}
is the element of
\mathbf{V}
at the
i^{\text{th}}
column and
j^{\text{th}}
row and
v_{ij}^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
v_{ij}
.
Expression (
6
) shows how
\mathbf{A}
can be decomposed into
n^{2}
rank-1 projections
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{\dagger}
modulated by a factor
\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{j}}\lambda_{ij}
.
We now assume that only the last dimension has a singular value significantly smaller than
1
, while all other dimensions are roughly equal to one, that is

1=\sigma_{1}\gtrsim\sigma_{2}\gtrsim\ldots\gtrsim\sigma_{n-1}\gg\sigma_{n}
.
This assumption essentially encapsulates the non-normal behavior along one component.
We show in the SM Appendix that our results remain qualitatively similar as long as the ratio of the second last to last singular value is large.
The condition number of matrix
\mathbf{A}
is defined as
\kappa=\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{n}
.
For
i=2,\ldots,n-1
, it is convenient to write

\sigma_{i}=(1-\delta_{i})\sigma_{1}

with

\delta_{i}\ll 1

such that
\sigma_{i}
is slightly less than but close to
\sigma_{1}=1
.
To leading order, this yields

\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{j}}=\begin{cases}1+(\delta_{j}-\delta_{i})+\mathcal{O}(\delta_{i}\delta_{j},\delta_{j}^{2})&\text{if }i,j\neq n\\
\kappa(1-\delta_{i})&\text{if }i\neq n,j=n\\
\kappa^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{-1}\delta_{j})&\text{if }i=n,j\neq n\\
1&\text{if }i=j=n.\end{cases}

(7)

Defining further
\delta^{2}=\sum_{i}\delta_{i}^{2}
and plugging these approximations into (
6
) allows us to decompose the matrix
\mathbf{A}
as

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}_{N}+(\kappa-1)a_{nr}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger}+\kappa\delta\mathbf{e}\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger}(\kappa^{-1}-1)a_{nr}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{\dagger}+\delta\mathbf{E}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2},\kappa^{-1}\delta),

(8)

where

\mathbf{A}_{N}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger},

is the “normal component” of
\mathbf{A}
.
Moreover, we have defined the non-normal mode
\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{n}
, and the reaction mode
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
via

a_{nr}\hat{\mathbf{r}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{in}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}

with
a_{nr}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|\lambda_{in}|^{2})^{1/2}
,
both of which are unit vectors.
The matrix
\mathbf{E}
and vector
\mathbf{e}
are given by

\mathbf{E}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}(\delta_{j}-\delta_{i})\lambda_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\dagger}

and

\mathbf{e}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{\delta_{i}}{\delta}\lambda_{in}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}

and encapsulate the comparatively small interactions resulting from the anisotropy of the small deviation
\delta_{i}
from non-normality along the
n-1
dimensions.
We can see from equation (
8
) that
\mathbf{A}
is decomposed into a normal part as well as off-diagonal parts which are dominated by a term of order
\kappa
.
Since these dominating interactions arise along
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
, to a good approximation, we can restrict our study to these two dimensions,
and project the matrix
\mathbf{A}
into
(\hat{\mathbf{n}},\hat{\mathbf{r}})
to obtain system (
2
).
The exact form of
\alpha_{n}
and
\alpha_{r}
, as well as a derivation that these coefficients are of order one, is found in the SM.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
of system (
2
) are

\lambda_{\pm}=-\delta_{+}\mp\sqrt{1+\delta_{-}^{2}}

where

\delta_{\pm}=\frac{\alpha_{r}\pm\alpha_{n}}{2}

and it holds
\lambda_{+}>\lambda_{-}>0
.
The system is stable if and only if
\lambda_{-}<0
, which implies that
\alpha_{n}\alpha_{r}\geqslant 1
.
The associated eigenvectors are given by

\mathbf{v}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{\mp}^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{\mp}\\
\kappa\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{with }\mu_{\pm}=\delta_{-}\pm\sqrt{1+\delta_{-}^{2}}.

(9)

This shows that, as
\kappa
and hence the non-normality increases, the two eigenvectors become closer and closer to being collinear.
We further detail in the SM that, in our set-up, the matrix condition number
\kappa
well captures the degree of non-normality that is otherwise typically described via
Henrici’s departure from normality
.

Non-Normal Statistics

Introducing the matrix

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha_{n}&\kappa^{-1}\\
\kappa&-\alpha_{r}\end{pmatrix}

allows us to write system (
2
) in vectorial form with explicit solution

\begin{pmatrix}n_{t}\\
r_{t}\end{pmatrix}=B\int^{t}_{0}e^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t-s)}\begin{pmatrix}\eta^{n}_{s}\\
\eta^{r}_{s}\end{pmatrix}dt

and its covariance matrix given by

\mathbf{C}_{t}:=\text{Cov}\left[n_{t},r_{t}\right]=B^{2}\int^{t}_{0}e^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}s}e^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\dagger}s}ds.

Diagonalizing
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
, we find

\mathbf{C}_{t}=\frac{B^{2}}{4\Delta^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}F_{-}(t)+\kappa^{-2}F(t)&&\kappa G_{-}(t)+\kappa^{-1}G_{+}(t)\\
\kappa G_{-}(t)+\kappa^{-1}G_{+}(t)&&F_{+}(t)+\kappa^{2}F(t)\end{pmatrix}

(10)

with the functions
F_{\pm}
and
G_{\pm}
given in the SM, which are of order one.
The variance of the system is dominated by its largest eigenvalue, which we call the effective variance of the system
c_{\text{eff}}(t)
.
In the limit of large
\kappa
, it is given by

c_{\text{eff}}(t)=\frac{B^{2}}{4\Delta^{2}}\left[\kappa^{2}F(t)+F_{-}(t)+\frac{G_{-}(t)^{2}}{F(t)}\right]

which grows like
\kappa^{2}
to leading order.
On the other hand, the second eigenvector is found to be asymptotically independent of
\kappa
(see SM).
In other words, in a highly non-normal system where
\kappa
is large, most of the dynamics is driven by a single component for which the asymptotic variance grows as
\kappa^{2}

to leading order.
Similarly, we detail in the SM that the system’s lead-lag autocorrelation

C_{r}(\tau)

asymptotically decays like

\frac{d}{d\tau}\ln C_{r}(\tau)=-\begin{cases}\frac{\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}(\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-})(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})^{2}}{\kappa^{2}(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})^{2}+(\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-})(\lambda_{-}\mu_{-}^{2}+\lambda_{+}\mu_{+}^{2})+4\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}}&\tau\rightarrow 0\\
\lambda_{-}&\tau\rightarrow\infty.\end{cases}

(11)

In the limit of large times, the correlation decays with
\lambda_{-}
as anticipated.
However, the instantaneous rate of decay tends toward zero as
\kappa
increases,
which implies that the lead-lag correlation is near unity for small lags and hence that the system behaves quasi deterministically over short timescales when
\kappa
is large.

Representation as a Mean-Reversion Process

To represent system (
2
) as a mean reversion process (
3
), which has the general solution

r_{t}=\sigma_{\theta}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(\Omega_{t}-\Omega_{s})}dW_{s}

where

\frac{d\Omega_{t}}{dt}\equiv\theta_{t},

we match the moments of the two representations and find (see SM) that

\displaystyle\theta_{t}

\displaystyle\propto 2\lambda_{+}(\kappa^{2}+\mu_{-}^{2})e^{-2\lambda_{+}t}+\lambda_{-}(\kappa^{2}+\mu_{+}^{2})e^{-2\lambda_{-}t}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~-(\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-})(\kappa^{2}-1)e^{-(\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-})t}.

(12)

We can now ask whether there exist intermediate times for which
\theta_{t}<0
, that is times where the mean-reversion rate temporarily becomes repulsive.
To this end, we look for the roots of (
12
), which are given by

\tau_{\pm}=\frac{(\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-})(\kappa^{2}-1)}{2\lambda_{+}(\kappa^{2}+\mu_{-}^{2})}\pm\frac{1}{2\lambda_{+}(\kappa^{2}+\mu_{-}^{2})}\sqrt{L}

(13)

with

L=(\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-})^{2}(\kappa^{2}-1)^{2}-4\lambda_{-}\lambda_{+}(\kappa^{2}+\mu_{+}^{2})(\kappa^{2}+\mu_{-}^{2}).

If the system is close to normality,
0<\kappa-1\ll 1
,
L
becomes negative, implying no real solution, and the system remains locally stable with
\theta_{t}>0~\forall t
.
However, for highly non-normal systems (
\kappa\gg 1
), a real positive solution
\tau_{+}
exists, showing that there exists a time interval where
\theta_{t}\leqslant 0
.

We can also pinpoint the minimal value
\kappa=\kappa_{c}
required for
\theta_{t}
to exhibit transiently negative values.
To this end, we determine the value of
\kappa_{c}
at which
\tau_{+}=\tau_{-}
, that is, the value at which
\theta_{t}
just touches
0
without crossing below (see the middle plot of Figure
2
).
It holds that
\tau_{+}=\tau_{-}
when the square root of (
13
) is zero, which is the case for

\kappa_{c}=\sqrt{1+2\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}+2\sqrt{\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}(1+\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-})}}.

(14)

Empirical Estimation of Non-Normal and Reaction Modes

For our application to the brain connectome,
we estimate the matrix
\mathbf{A}
of (
1
) as
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
obtained by fitting a VAR(1) process to the EEG data over rolling windows of 40 seconds.
In principle, we could then proceed by first determining the transition matrix
\mathbf{P}
that diagonalizes
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
,
and subsequently calculating the SVD
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{U}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}
to identify the non-normal mode
\hat{\mathbf{n}}\sim\mathbf{u}_{n}
and reaction mode
\hat{\mathbf{r}}\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{u}_{i}
.
However, as we detail in the SM, this approach is highly sensitive to noise, especially when
\mathbf{A}
is non-normal.
Therefore, we propose an alternative approach that consists in estimating the non-normal and reaction component directly from
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
as

\hat{\mathbf{r}},\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\argmax_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\langle\mathbf{v},\hat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}\rangle~~\text{s.t.}~~\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle=0.

(15)

where
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is the unit sphere in
n
dimensions.
Here,

\langle\mathbf{v},\hat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}\rangle
represents the inner product between the vector
\mathbf{v}
and the result of applying the matrix
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
to the vector
\mathbf{w}
.
Thus, it measures the degree of alignment or projection of the transformed vector
\hat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}
on
\mathbf{v}
.
Equation (
15
) directly captures the intuition behind the reduced equation (
2
) whereby the non-normal component maximally projects onto the reaction component.
From (
2
), we further see that
\kappa
can be estimated directly from
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
via

\kappa=\sqrt{\left|\langle\hat{\mathbf{r}},\hat{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\rangle\right/\left|\langle\hat{\mathbf{n}},\hat{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\rangle\right|}

thus allowing us to completely bypass the need for estimating the singular values which are numerically unstable.
In the SM, we test our empirical procedure on synthetic data, finding that our procedure faithfully recovers the non-normal and reaction mode,
and estimates
\kappa
more precisely than when calculating it directly via singular value decomposition.
