Risk factors of clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential
Authors: Qiaoxue Liu, Fen Yang, Hans-Olov Adami, Yingsuo Zhao, Tove Wästerlid, Fang Fang, Qianwei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccumulating evidence demonstrates associations between clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) and increased risks of haematological and non-haematological health outcomes. Although adverse health consequences of CHIP have become well-documented, large knowledge gaps exist regarding the aetiology of CHIP.ObjectiveTo investigate risk factors for CHIP using large-scale phenotypic data and Mendelian randomisation analysis.MethodsWe utilised rich phenotypic and genomic data from the UK Biobank (UKB) to perform a cross-sectional study to systematically investigate risk factors of CHIP, including around 460,000 participants recruited from 2006 to 2010. We used Logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) of CHIP in relation to risk factors (sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, history of diseases, blood cell count, blood biochemistry parameters, proteomics biomarkers and metabolomics biomarkers). In addition, we conducted Mendelian Randomisation (MR) analyses to assess causality between the studied risk factors and CHIP, using publicly available summary statistics of genome-wide association studies (GWAS).ResultsWe found that advanced age, smoking, history of several diseases (including breast cancer and leiomyoma of uterus), as well as several serum biomarkers (monocyte count, proteins LY75, SIGLEC6, SIRPB1 and CYB5R2) were associated with CHIP.ConclusionOur findings expanded existing knowledge base by demonstrating novel risk factors of CHIP, especially history of several diseases and serum biomarkers. These findings advance understanding of the aetiology of CHIP.Keywords: Clonal hematopoiesis, Risk factors, Epidemiology, Observational study

Introduction

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) refers to clonal expansion of somatic mutations without evidence of haematological diseases.1, 2 Gene mutations associated with CHIP can confer selective advantages to hematopoietic stem cells, enabling them to proliferate in the hematopoietic system.3 CHIP, a progressive condition that develops with aging, has a prevalence of 10–20% among individuals aged 70 years or older.4 Accumulating evidence demonstrates associations between CHIP and increased risks of haematological and non-haematological health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Despite increasing knowledge on potential consequences of CHIP, relatively little is known about the aetiology of CHIP. Although previous evidence suggested several plausible risk factors (e.g., age, smoking and inflammation), large knowledge gaps remain.10, 11 Apart from improving the understanding of disease aetiology, it is essential to identify individuals who are susceptible to CHIP with the aim of providing surveillance or early intervention to reduce risk.

Therefore, we used data from the UK Biobank (UKB) to systematically investigate risk factors of CHIP, including sociodemographic factors, lifestyle, history of diseases, and serum biomarkers (metabolic biomarkers, proteins, blood cell count, and blood biochemistry parameters). We further conducted Mendelian Randomisation analyses to assess potential causality between the studied risk factors and CHIP, using publicly available summary statistics from genome-wide association studies (GWAS).

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study using data from the UKB.12 Between 2006 and 2010, over 500,000 participants aged 40–69 were recruited from 22 assessment centres in the UK who completed touch-screen questionnaires and underwent blood sample testing. A total of 469,877 individuals with whole-exome sequencing (WES) data were initially identified. We excluded 216 participants who withdrew from the study, 14 participants with unknown time of blood sampling, and 3,295 participants with a history of haematological malignancy, leaving 466,352 individuals in the final analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1). All participants in the UKB signed informed consent before recruitment. Ethical approval for the UK Biobank has been obtained from the NHS National Research Ethics Service (REC reference: 21/NW/0157). This study utilised the UK Biobank Resource, under Application Number 106912. This research has been approved by the Ethical Review Board in Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University in China (reference number: NFEC-2023-559).

Ascertainment of risk factors

Sociodemographic factors, including sex, date of birth, educational level, ethnicity, and Townsend deprivation index (as an area-based score of social deprivation), were obtained at recruitment through questionnaires. Age was calculated based on the date of recruitment and date of birth. Information on lifestyle factors, including physical exercise (International Physical Activity Questionnaire activity group), diet (data on the reported frequency of intake of a range of common food and drink items), sleep (data on duration of sleep, napping, dozing, snoring, sleeplessness and chronotype), smoking status, alcohol drinking, body mass index, electronic device use, and sun exposure, was also obtained from questionnaires (Supplementary Table S1). Blood cell count and blood biochemistry parameters were measured using the blood sample collected at recruitment, including 31 and 30 items, respectively. History of diseases was ascertained through linkage to the inpatient records using the ninth and the tenth revisions of the International Classification of Diseases codes (Supplementary Table S2).

The UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) used the Olink platform to identify 2,923 unique proteins (Supplementary Table S3) in plasma samples, among approximately 54,000 participants.13 Techniques of the proteomics analysis have been described in detail elsewhere.14 Measurements were expressed as normalised protein values and converted to log base 2.14 Over 280,000 plasma samples from the UKB participants were also analysed using the high-throughput nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) platform (Nightingale Health), including 251 original metabolic biomarkers.15 We derived 76 additional metabolic biomarker ratios based on the original markers referring to previous literature16 (Supplementary Table S4).

Ascertainment of CHIP

The process of CHIP identification has been described in our previous studies.7, 8 Briefly, CHIP status was identified based on 74 genes known to be related to myeloid malignancies.17 GRCh38 was utilised as the genome reference for the analysis. Mutect2 (Genome Analysis Toolkit version 4.2.4.0) was run to identify somatic mutations in the WES data, with the exception of U2AF1.18 We used the Rust-HTSLIB binary (https://github.com/weinstockj/pileup_region) to identify U2AF1 mutant.17 Annotation of genes was performed using ANNOVAR.19 Variants were excluded based on the following criteria: a total read depth of less than or equal to 20, a minimum read depth of fewer than five for the alternate allele, a variant allele frequency (VAF) of less than 2%, or an absence of evidence on both forward and reverse sequencing reads.17

Statistical analyses

We used Logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of CHIP in relation to risk factors (sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, history of diseases, blood cell count, blood biochemistry parameters, proteomics biomarkers, and metabolomics biomarkers), with adjustment for sex, ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index, smoking status, educational level as categorical variables and age (as natural cubic spline) and body mass index (as natural cubic spline). Blood cell count and biochemistry parameters were fitted as continuous variables in the Logistic model to assess the association per each standard deviation (SD) increase. We used the ʻpseudolikelihood’ weighting estimator20 in the analysis of proteomics and metabolomics biomarkers, because only a small proportion of the study participants had information on these risk factors (Supplementary Methods). We also performed analyses for common mutations of CHIP (DNMT3A and TET2) and different size of the clones (large-clone CHIP, VAF ≥0.1; small-clone-CHIP, VAF <0.1). Bonferroni correction was implemented to reduce the risk of false positive findings in analyses of serum biomarkers, including blood cell count, biochemistry parameters, as well as proteomic and metabolomic biomarkers.21

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, to mitigate potential risk of reverse causality between disease history and CHIP, we performed a sensitivity analysis by restricting disease history as diagnoses of disease below the age of 50 (because CHIP is rare under 50 years). Second, we conducted additional analyses on risk factors associated with CHIP in the primary analyses, after stratification by age (≥60 years and <60 years), sex, ethnicity (White and others), body mass index (<25 and ≥25), Townsend deprivation index (Q1–Q2 and Q3–Q4), educational level (college and others), smoking status [yes (including previous and current smoking) and no], and alcohol drinking [yes (including previous and current smoking) and no] to examine potential effect modifications. Third, we further conducted Bonferroni correction on the associations of sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, and history of diseases with CHIP.

Mendelian randomisation analysis

Summary statistics of CHIP GWAS were obtained from a prior study of the UKB.11 The GWAS included 10,203 individuals with and 173,918 individuals without CHIP. The prior study also included summary statistics for DNMT3A-CHIP, TET2-CHIP, small clone-CHIP, and large clone-CHIP.11 The sources of the ascertainment of instrumental variables for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, history of diseases,22 blood cell count, blood biochemistry parameters, and metabolomics biomarkers are described in Supplementary Table S5. First, the genetic variants were extracted with association thresholds at P < 5 × 10−8. Second, independent variants were identified using a strict clumping window to prevent linkage disequilibrium (R2 = 0.001, kb = 10,000). Finally, a threshold of F statistic greater than 10 was applied.

Instrumental variables of protein biomarkers were obtained from the deCODE GWAS data.23 We selected variants with cis-protein quantitative trait loci (cis-pQTL) based on P-value (P < 5 × 10−8), location outside the major histocompatibility complex region (chromosome 6, 26–34 Mb), independent association via linkage disequilibrium clustering (R2 < 0.1), F-statistic value >10, and minor allele frequency >0.01.24 A total of 1,796 proteins with cis-pQTL were identified for subsequent two-sample MR analysis, performed using the R package TwoSampleMR. The inverse variance weighting method was used when multiple instrumental variables (i.e., multiple SNPs) were available, whereas the Wald ratio method was applied when only one instrumental variable (e.g., one SNP) was available.25 Two-sided P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. All analyses were performed in RStudio 4.3.1.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 466,352 individuals were included in the study, among whom 14,476 (3.1%) were identified as carrying CHIP (Table 1). The median age of the studied individuals was 58 years (interquartile range [IQR] 51–64). The most frequent mutations were DNMT3A (N = 7,714, 53.3%) and TET2 (N = 3,353, 23.2%). Among 14,476 individuals with CHIP, 10,181 of them had large-clone CHIP, whereas 4,295 had small-clone CHIP.

Associations between phenotype factors and CHIP

In the analysis of sociodemographic factors, advanced age (≥60 years vs. <60 years) was associated with a higher risk of any CHIP (OR 2.61, 95% CI 2.51–2.70, P < 0.001, Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S6). Males had a higher risk of TET2-CHIP than females, while females had a higher risk of DNMT3A-CHIP. Individuals of White ethnicity had a lower risk of most CHIP types than those with other ethnicity. Compared with never smokers, individuals with previous smoking (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.08–1.17, P < 0.001, Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S7) or current smoking (OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.34–1.50, P < 0.001) had a higher risk of CHIP. We observed a modest association of lamb/mutton intake (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.002–1.05, P = 0.04), and increased risk of CHIP. Tea intake (OR 0.995, 95% CI 0.991–0.9997, P = 0.04) and cereal intake were weakly inversely associated with risk of CHIP (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.988–0.996, P < 0.001).

History of breast cancer (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.10–1.41, P < 0.001), iron deficiency anaemia (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.02–1.46, P = 0.03), urinary tract infection (OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.05–1.35, P = 0.01), secondary and unspecified cancer in lymph nodes (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.03–1.60, P = 0.03), secondary cancer in other sites (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.10–2.62, P = 0.02), anxiety disorder (OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.06–1.99, P = 0.02), hyperplasia of prostate (OR 1.17, 95% CI 1.03–1.33, P = 0.02), disorders of mineral metabolism (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.02–2.27, P = 0.04), leiomyoma of uterus (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.02–1.29, P = 0.02), and benign colorectal and anal neoplasm (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.03–1.32, P = 0.02) were associated with an increased risk of any CHIP (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S8). In contrast, there was a negative association between history of hypothyroidism and any CHIP (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.75–0.99, P = 0.04).

Among 31 markers of blood count, 12 were associated with increased risk of CHIP, including monocyte count (OR per SD increase 1.05, 95% CI 1.04–1.06, P < 0.001), whereas four markers were associated with a decreased risk of CHIP (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table S9). In analyses of 30 blood biochemistry parameters, cystatin C was associated with an increased risk of CHIP (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table S10). Moreover, we found 22 proteins to be associated with a higher risk, whereas nine proteins to be associated with a lower risk, of CHIP (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S11). Finally, only two metabolic biomarkers were associated with the risk of CHIP (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S12).

Sensitivity analyses

(1) When limiting the diagnostic age under 50, the association between a history of breast cancer (OR 1.36, 95% CI 1.01–1.85, P = 0.04, Supplementary Table S13) and leiomyoma of uterus (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.07–1.52, P = 0.01) with CHIP remained; (2) The associations for most of the studied risk factors were generally stable across age, sex, body mass index, educational level, Townsend deprivation index, smoking, and alcohol drinking (Supplementary Figs. S2–S8); (3) When adjusting for multiplicity using Bonferroni approach, the association of advanced age, smoking status, cereal intake, and history of breast cancer with CHIP remained significant (Supplementary Table S14).

Mendelian randomisation

Only few compelling associations were found between genetically determined sociodemographic factors of CHIP (Supplementary Table S15). For lifestyle factors, smoking is the strongest risk factor (OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.08–1.40, P = 0.002, Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S16). Additionally, genetic liability to breast cancer (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.11, P = 0.03, Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S17), leiomyoma of uterus (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.15, P = 0.01), dyspepsia (OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.53–0.97, P = 0.03), hyperplasia of prostate (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.02–1.18, P = 0.01), and sleep disorders (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.98, P = 0.03) were associated with the risk of CHIP.

In the analyses of blood count and blood biochemistry parameters, genetically predicted monocyte count (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.01–1.53, P = 0.04, Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table S18), mean platelet (thrombocyte) volume (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.27–0.84, P = 0.01) and apolipoprotein A (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.01–1.17, P = 0.02, Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table S19) were associated with the risk of any CHIP. A total of 232 proteins showed an association with CHIP risk (Supplementary Table S20). Among the 31 proteins associated with CHIP risk as identified in the primary analyses, the genetically determined levels of four proteins (LY75, SIGLEC6, SIRPB1 and CYB5R2) were also significantly associated with the risk of CHIP (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Table S20). Among the studied 327 metabolomics markers, 111 had GWAS summary statistics. In the genetic analysis, leucine demonstrated statistically significant association with any CHIP (OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.67–0.998, P = 0.047, Fig. 3E, Supplementary Table S21), although it was not associated in the phenotype analysis. Supplementary Table S22 presented a sensitivity analysis of the causal effect estimates based on the MR-Egger method. Finally, we summarised the phenotypic and MR analyses and compiled the key findings (Supplementary Table S23).

Discussion

Utilising the rich phenotypic and genomic data of the UK Biobank and external GWAS datasets, we performed a large observational study to comprehensively investigate risk factors of CHIP. We demonstrated that advanced age, smoking, history of several diseases (including breast cancer and leiomyoma of uterus), as well as several blood biomarkers (monocyte count and proteins LY75, SIGLEC6, SIRPB1 and CYB5R2) were likely causally associated with CHIP.

In line with most previous studies,11, 26, 27, 28 but not all,29, 30 we identified a link between smoking status and CHIP. Contrary to prior research that male sex was significantly associated with a higher risk of overall CHIP,31 we observed no association between sex and CHIP in both the phenotypic and MR analyses. The contrasting findings might be due to the differences in study populations of the current study (healthy volunteers) and previous research (patients with atherosclerosis). Nevertheless, we observed a higher risk of DNMT3A-mutant CHIP among females, consistent with previous findings of more common DNMT3A mutation in females than males.32 Additionally, we found novel evidence suggesting a link between breast cancer and risk of CHIP, extending previous findings of a high prevalence of CHIP among patients with breast cancer.33, 34, 35 Moreover, we found a novel association of leiomyoma of uterus with CHIP, expanding the spectrum of diseases associated with CHIP. Furthermore, we discovered the likely causal associations between monocyte count and four proteins (LY75, SIGLEC6, SIRPB1 and CYB5R2) and CHIP, providing candidate biomarkers for CHIP that warrant further validation.

The mechanisms by which risk factors increase CHIP are probably complicated and multifactored. For instance, exposure to smoking could lead to chronic inflammatory microenvironment, promoting pre-existing defective or mutant clones.36, 37, 38 Additionally, smoking can disrupt the balance of Th17 and Treg immune cells in the bone marrow, further intensifying the selective pressure exerted by the local inflammatory niche on mutant clones.36 Furthermore, aging is accompanied by immunological senescence in the hematopoietic system, including functional decline in the bone marrow microenvironment and the persistent presence of systemic low-grade inflammation; these factors collectively confer a competitive survival advantage on mutant hematopoietic stem cells.39 The link between breast cancer and leiomyoma of uterus and CHIP might be due to both shared etiologies between diseases and treatment of these disorders. For example, in breast cancer and leiomyoma of uterus, oestrogens might modulate the proliferation of DNMT3A-mutated bone marrow cells.32 In addition, chemotherapy and radiotherapy in patients with breast cancer, or gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists in patients with leiomyoma of uterus, might contribute to the development of CHIP.40 Moreover, proteins, including LY75, SIGLEC6 and SIRPB1, play a role in immune regulation processes and contribute to clonal proliferation through mediating intracellular signal transduction.41, 42, 43 LY75 expression may be associated with the differentiation status of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), and it may influence the bone marrow microenvironment surrounding HSPCs,44 leading to easier activation of inflammatory signalling pathways that promote clonal expansion, thereby exacerbating the onset and progression of CHIP. SIRPB1 is an inhibitory receptor expressed on myeloid cells that plays a role in regulating immune cell activation and may influence the progression of CHIP.45 In addition, SIGLEC6 has been shown to play a clear tumour-promoting role in acute myeloid leukaemia (AML); its high expression is closely associated with increased proliferation, invasiveness, and metastatic potential in AML cells. It drives disease progression through the SHP2/Src/ERK/IL-3 signalling axis, suggesting that it may serve as a potential biomarker for assessing AML prognosis and a therapeutic target.46 In TET2-mutated diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, the CYB5R2 gene exhibits high methylation and downregulated expression, suggesting that it may serve as a potential biomarker for the diagnosis or prognosis of this disease.47

One main strength of the current study is the large sample size and comprehensive information on various risk factors, including sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, medical history, and blood biomarkers. Another merit is the application of both phenotypic and Mendelian Randomisation analyses. Not only does it reveal associations at the phenotypic level, but it also provides genetic evidence for potential causal relationships. Nonetheless, there are some limitations. First, there is a concern of reverse causality due to the cross-sectional design of the phenotypic analysis, especially for blood biomarkers. However, the Mendelian randomisation analysis as well as the sensitivity analyses of the phenotypic associations should have partly mitigated such concern. Nevertheless, findings from some studies that only show phenotypic associations may still reflect shared biological processes rather than causal drivers. Second, as information on lifestyle factors was mainly identified through questionnaires, the observed association might be subject to potential recall bias. Third, given relatively young age of participants in the UKB, some participants exposed to certain risk factors but initially free of CHIP might subsequently develop the condition in later life, which could lead to underestimation of the investigated associations. Fourth, many of the studied risk factors lack instrumental variables due to non-existing public GWAS data, limiting the exploration of their causal relationships with CHIP. Fifth, the potential for false positive findings represents a concern in this study. Nonetheless, the use of Bonferroni correction in the investigated associations could partly mitigate such concern. In addition, we employed Mendelian randomisation analysis, using genetic variants as instrumental variables to further validate associations. Yet, this stringent adjustment may also increase the probability of type II error, thereby masking true associations. Sixth, given the extremely limited sample sizes of most non‑White ethnic subgroups, we aggregated all non‑White individuals into a category. This could increase statistical power but mask heterogeneity within the group. The association of lifestyle factors and medical history with CHIP, may be influenced by the specific environmental, cultural, and healthcare system contexts in Europe. Future studies using multiple cohorts from different backgrounds are needed to test the generalisability of our findings. Seventh, several associations had modest effect sizes and reached only borderline statistical significance. Given the inherent limitations of the MR method, the possibility of weak instrumental variable bias and residual multivariate effects cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as suggestive evidence of causality rather than definitive conclusions. The small effect sizes and marginal statistical significance further underscore the need for future studies, including randomised controlled trials or triangulation with other evidence, to validate these potential causal relationships.

In conclusion, through an extensive investigation of potential risk factors for CHIP using both phenotypic and Mendelian Randomisation analyses, we identified several risk factors of CHIP. These findings provide novel insights into the causes of CHIP.
