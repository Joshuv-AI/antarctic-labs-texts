Rapidly Learning Soft Robot Control via Implicit Time-Stepping
Andrew Choi, Dezhong Tong

Abstract
With the explosive growth of rigid-body simulators, policy learning in simulation has become the de facto standard for most rigid morphologies. In contrast, soft robotic simulation frameworks remain scarce and are seldom adopted by the soft robotics community. This gap stems partly from the lack of easy-to-use, general-purpose frameworks and partly from the high computational cost of accurately simulating continuum mechanics, which often renders policy learning infeasible. In this work, we demonstrate that rapid soft robot policy learning is indeed achievable via implicit time-stepping. Our simulator of choice, DisMech , is a general-purpose, fully implicit soft-body simulator capable of handling both soft dynamics and frictional contact. We further introduce delta natural curvature control, a method analogous to delta joint position control in rigid manipulators, providing an intuitive and effective means of enacting control for soft robot learning. To highlight the benefits of implicit time-stepping and delta curvature control, we conduct extensive comparisons across four diverse soft manipulator tasks against one of the most widely used soft-body frameworks, Elastica . With implicit time-stepping, parallel stepping of 500 environments achieves up to 6 × 6\times faster speeds for non-contact cases and up to 40 × 40\times faster for contact-rich scenarios. Finally, a comprehensive sim-to-sim gap evaluation—training policies in one simulator and evaluating them in another—demonstrates that implicit time-stepping provides a rare free lunch: dramatic speedups achieved without sacrificing accuracy.

Introduction

Rigid-body simulators have seen tremendous growth in recent years, with impressive robot policy learning results demonstrated across various platforms such as MuJoCo
(
28
)
, IsaacSim
(
22
)
, and Genesis
(
11
)
.
In contrast, soft-body simulators have experienced much slower adoption within the soft robotics community, where research remains predominantly hardware-oriented.
This limited adoption can be attributed to two key challenges: the lack of easy-to-use, general-purpose soft-body simulators, and the question of whether such simulators can operate fast enough to make data-driven policy learning feasible.
The latter, in particular, remains a significant hurdle.

Rigid manipulators typically have around seven degrees of freedom, resulting in compact equations of motion.
In contrast, soft bodies possess effectively infinite degrees of freedom, requiring simulators to discretize the system finely enough to capture continuum mechanics.
This large number of degrees of freedom, combined with the highly nonlinear dynamics introduced by elasticity, makes soft robot control and policy learning difficult both from a simulation and learning perspective.

In this work, we present a learning setup capable of rapidly and robustly training policies across four diverse soft manipulator tasks via reinforcement learning.
We demonstrate substantial speed improvements over state-of-the-art baselines through two key components:
(1) aggressive implicit time-stepping using the fully implicit simulator
DisMech

(
5
)
, and
(2) a sample efficient delta natural curvature control formulation, a soft-body analogue to delta joint control in rigid manipulators.
While earlier work
(
5
)
showed the speed improvements over explicit methods for simulating canonical examples in mechanics, this work focuses on the speed improvements in policy learning brought forth by implicit time-stepping where
DisMech
enables policy learning up to an order of magnitude faster than
Elastica

(
10
)
.

Rather than introducing a completely novel solution, the focus of this work is to demonstrate that practical tools and strategies for rapid soft robot policy learning exist.
Through extensive comparisons across four representative tasks, we show that
DisMech
closely matches
Elastica
’s dynamics while being substantially faster and computationally more efficient.
To ensure a fair comparison, we adopt (with slight alterations) the same soft manipulator control tasks introduced by
25
.
In a landscape where soft simulation frameworks remain limited, this work provides an invaluable baseline for evaluating new simulators and understanding their effects on policy learning performance.
We hope it serves as a foundation for future research on simulation frameworks as well as provide a practical testbed for evaluating learning algorithms in soft robot control.

The contributions of this article are as follows:

1.

We perform an extensive comparison across four soft manipulator tasks, evaluating policy learning performance between the
DisMech
and
Elastica
simulators.
Since
Elastica
uses a similar reduced-order model representation of a discretized centerline, the same state representation and control methods can be applied, making it a natural baseline.

2.

We demonstrate the feasibility of
delta natural curvature control
, a method analogous to delta joint position control in rigid manipulators, for learning soft robot control policies.

3.

We highlight the “
free lunch
” offered by implicit time-stepping, where larger time steps can be taken without loss of accuracy, as validated by sim-to-sim evaluations.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first instance implicit time-stepping has been leveraged to rapidly accelerate soft robot policy learning.

4.

We introduce a benchmark for soft robot policy learning that can be used to compare both algorithms and, as demonstrated in this paper, simulators.
To facilitate this benchmark, we open-source all code used to run the experiments

Related Work

In simulating soft robotics, one of the most commonly used soft-body representations is the canonical 1D deformable object, often referred to as an elastic rod or deformable linear object (DLO).
A variety of simulation approaches have been developed to capture its deformation behavior, differing in physical fidelity, computational cost, and ease of use.

Early approaches attempted to approximate soft elasticity using rigid-body dynamics, connecting chains of rigid links with compliant spring–damper elements
(
12
)
.
While such models can produce plausible bending behavior, they are unable to capture more complex deformations such as twist, shear, or buckling
(
29
;
31
)
, which limits their utility for modeling soft manipulators or highly deformable structures.

The finite element method (FEM) has long been a standard for soft-body simulation, with frameworks such as SOFA
(
9
)
providing accurate representations of continuum mechanics with further focuses in soft robot control
(
7
)
and reinforcement learning
(
27
)
.
However, FEM-based approaches are often computationally expensive, as they rely on high-resolution volumetric meshes to achieve accuracy.
This has motivated efforts in model order reduction
(
33
)
and asynchronous techniques
(
20
)
to make such methods tractable for real-time or learning-based applications.

For slender structures such as soft manipulators, it is often sufficient to adopt a reduced-order model based on a discrete centerline, rather than high-density FEM meshes.
Two classical formulations serve as the foundation for these models:
the Kirchhoff rod model
(
18
)
, which assumes inextensibility and unshearability, and the more general Cosserat rod model
(
8
)
, which accounts for stretching, shearing, bending, and twisting.

Discrete Elastic Rods (DER)
(
3
;
2
)
provide a discrete differential geometry (DDG) formulation of the Kirchhoff rod model, enabling efficient simulation of slender deformable structures.
Being derived from the Kirchhoff model, DER does not model shearing explicitly, although extensions to DDG-based Timoshenko beams have been proposed to include 2D shear effects
(
21
)
.
There is rich literature showcasing the use of DDG-based simulations for modeling soft robot control in jumping
(
31
)
, locomotion
(
16
)
, swimming
(
17
)
, bacterial tumbling
(
15
)
, and much more.
DisMech
(
5
)
builds upon this foundation by combining DER with Implicit Contact Method (IMC)
(
6
;
30
)
, offering a fully implicit time-stepping scheme for improved stability and performance during learning-based tasks.
More recently, a MATLAB implementation, MAT-DisMech
(
19
)
, has extended the framework with Discrete Shell
(
13
)
elements, allowing for plate–rod hybrid simulations.

Another widely used simulator based on the Cosserat rod model is Elastica
(
10
)
.
Elastica represents deformable rods using a reduced-order discrete centerline model capable of capturing all primary deformation modes—stretching, bending, twisting, and shearing.
Due to its balance between accuracy and simplicity, Elastica has become one of the most popular frameworks for soft robot simulation and reinforcement learning
(
25
)
and has showcased the ability to model complex soft structures such as a muscular octopus arm
(
4
)
.

More recently, SoroSim
(
24
)
introduced the Geometric Variable Strain (GVS) formulation, another simulation framework grounded in the Cosserat model.
Unlike DisMech and Elastica’s centerline-based representation, GVS parameterizes each segment’s strain variables directly—such as curvature, twist, and extension—potentially reducing the system’s degrees of freedom and improving computational efficiency.
This approach has been used to simulate soft manipulators
(
26
)
as well as hybrid soft–rigid robots
(
23
)
, demonstrating its flexibility in modeling complex morphologies.

Finally, while most rigid-body simulators historically lacked dedicated soft-physics capabilities, recent efforts such as Genesis
(
11
)
aim to unify both rigid and soft dynamics within a single framework.
Genesis leverages the Material Point Method (MPM) and FEM backends to simulate a broad spectrum of physical materials, from rigid bodies to soft deformables, offering an exciting direction for general-purpose robotics simulation.

Methodology

Figure 2:
Discrete rod schematic. A continuous centerline can be seen discretized into nodes
\mathbf{q}_{i}

\{\mathbf{d}^{i}_{1},\mathbf{d}^{i}_{2},\mathbf{t}^{i}\}
and a material frame
\{\mathbf{m}^{i}_{1},\mathbf{m}^{i}_{2},\mathbf{t}^{i}\}
, which are used to compute bending and twisting deformations at interior nodes.

In this section, we briefly go over
DisMech
’s elastic energy formulation and our control formulation.
For brevity, we will exclude the full equations of motion and the formulation of external forces such as contact.
These can be read about in detail in the original
DisMech
paper
(
5
)
.

3.1
Reduced-Order Model of Discrete Centerline

Before introducing the elastic energies, it is first necessary to formulate the reduced-order model of the discrete centerline.
An elastic rod’s discrete centerline consists of
N
(typically equidistant) vertices, each represented by a Cartesian position
(\mathbf{q}_{i})_{i=0}^{N-1}
.
This in turn defines
N-1
edges
(\mathbf{e}^{i})_{i=0}^{N-2}
, with each edge given by
\mathbf{e}^{i}=\mathbf{q}_{i+1}-\mathbf{q}_{i}
.
Note that for clarity, subscript indices refer to vertex-related quantities, while superscript indices refer to edge-related quantities.

To capture bending and twisting effects, two orthonormal frames are defined for each edge: a reference frame
\{\mathbf{d}^{i}_{1},\mathbf{d}^{i}_{2},\mathbf{t}^{i}\}
and a material frame
\{\mathbf{m}^{i}_{1},\mathbf{m}^{i}_{2},\mathbf{t}^{i}\}
, as illustrated in Fig.
2
.
The shared tangent director is simply
\mathbf{t}^{i}=\mathbf{e}^{i}/\lVert\mathbf{e}^{i}\rVert
.
The material frame of each edge is used to compute the discrete curvatures along the centerline.
It also describes the rotation of the discrete centerline where rotating an edge’s reference frame by a signed angle
\theta^{i}
about
\mathbf{t}^{i}
yields the material frame.
Without the use of reference frames, each material frame would have to be computed directly from the preceding edge’s material frame, resulting in a dense Jacobian.
By introducing reference frames, we obtain a banded elastic energy Jacobian, which is significantly cheaper to solve.
Details on the temporal update of the reference frame can be found in
(
2
)
.
Overall,
N
vertices and
N-1
edges correspond to a total of
4N-1
degrees of freedom (DOF):

\mathbf{q}=[\mathbf{q}_{0},\theta^{0},\mathbf{q}_{1},\ldots,\theta^{N-2},\mathbf{q}_{N-1}]^{T}
.

3.2
Elastic Energies

As mentioned previously, the three forms of elasticity simulated by DER are stretching, bending, and twisting. We now briefly formulate their respective energy terms, denoted by
E_{s},E_{b},
and
E_{t}
.

Stretching Energy

Stretching deformations are computed from the extension or compression of each edge.
Let
\bar{\lambda}
denote an edge’s rest (natural) length.
Hereafter, the
\ \bar{}\
symbol will be used to indicate quantities in their rest configuration.
The stretching strain of edge
i
is defined as

\epsilon^{i}=\lVert\mathbf{e}^{i}\rVert\ /\ \bar{\lambda}-1.

(1)

The total stretching energy is then

E_{s}=\dfrac{1}{2}K_{s}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\left(\epsilon^{i}\right)^{2}\bar{\lambda},

(2)

where
K_{s}
is the stretching stiffness.

Bending Energy

Unlike stretching deformations, bending deformations occur between two adjacent edges and can be quantified by the misalignment of the tangents of adjacent edges.
At each interior node, we define the curvature binormal as

\left(\kappa\mathbf{b}\right)_{i}=\dfrac{2\mathbf{t}^{i-1}\times\mathbf{t}^{i}}{1+\mathbf{t}^{i-1}\cdot\mathbf{t}^{i}}.

(3)

Using this, we can then compute the integrated curvature along each material frame basis vector as

\displaystyle\kappa_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\kappa\mathbf{b}\right)_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{m}_{2}^{i-1}+\mathbf{m}_{2}^{i}\right),

\displaystyle\kappa_{2,i}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\kappa\mathbf{b}\right)_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{m}_{1}^{i-1}+\mathbf{m}_{1}^{i}\right),

resulting in
\bm{\kappa}_{i}=[\kappa_{1,i},\kappa_{2,i}]
.
The bending energy is then given by

E_{b}=\dfrac{1}{2}K_{b}\sum^{N-2}_{i=1}\left(\bm{\kappa}_{i}-\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{i}\right)^{2}\dfrac{1}{V_{i}},

(4)

where
K_{b}
is the bending stiffness and
V_{i}
is the Voronoi length of the
i
-th interior node.

Twisting Energy

The final mode of deformation is twisting, which measures the relative rotation of adjacent material
frames. As with bending, twisting is computed only at interior nodes using
both the material and reference frames of neighboring edges. The integrated twist at the
i
-th interior
node is defined as

\psi_{i}=\theta^{i}-\theta^{i-1}+\beta^{i},

(5)

where
\beta^{i}
represents the constant signed angle difference between the reference frames of consecutive edges.
Finally, the twisting energy is

E_{t}=\dfrac{1}{2}K_{t}\sum^{N-2}_{i=1}\left(\psi^{i}-\bar{\psi}^{i}\right)^{2}\dfrac{1}{V_{i}},

(6)

where
K_{t}
is the twisting stiffness.

Combining all three deformation modes, the total elastic energy of DER is
E_{\textrm{elastic}}=E_{s}+E_{b}+E_{t}
.

3.3
Delta Natural Curvature Control

As first introduced in
(
5
)
, we explicitly control the 3D bending deformations of the elastic rod by manipulating the natural curvature
\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{i}
at different points along the rod.
For a naturally straight rod,
\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{i}=[0,0]
.
Smooth delta joint control can then be enacted by incrementally changing
\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{i}
with a control
\Delta\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{i}
.
With a sufficiently high bending stiffness
K_{b}
, this results in highly responsive control, as shown in Fig.
1
.
If the rod has
C
control points, this corresponds to
C
degrees of freedom (DOFs) in the 2D case and
2C
DOFs in the 3D case.

We can further expand the soft manipulator’s workspace by performing natural twist control, where twisting deformations are generated by modulating the natural twist
\bar{\psi}_{i}
.
For full 3D actuation combining bending and twist, this results in a total of
3C
DOFs.

Under the effects of gravity, both natural curvature and twist control are analogous to PD joint position control in rigid manipulators.
Intuitively, 2D bending behaves like a revolute joint about an axis orthogonal to the link, 3D bending like a spherical joint, and twisting like a revolute joint collinear with the link.

4
Experiments

We now present results for four soft manipulation control tasks originally introduced in
(
25
)
.
Below we list several pragmatic adjustments made to better model sim-to-real capabilities:

1.

As previously discussed,
delta natural curvature and delta twist control
are now used in place of original torque control approach in
(
25
)
. This choice makes the learned policies more amenable to successful sim-to-real transfer, as torque control typically exhibits a higher sim-to-real gap than delta position control
(
1
)
.

2.

Gravity
is included in all tasks, providing both a more realistic simulation environment and introducing additional nonlinearity to the control problem.

3.

The
control frequency
is set to 2 Hz for contact tasks and 10 Hz for non-contact tasks, in contrast to the original 3500 Hz frequency used in
(
25
)
.
Such high frequencies often exacerbate the sim-to-real gap due to compounding errors.
We hypothesize that the high control frequency was originally chosen out of necessity, rather than preference, given the high computational cost of time-stepping in
Elastica
(a finding we further reinforce in the results section).

Training Parameters

Given the relatively high computational cost of soft body simulation, we opt for an off-policy algorithm to maximize sample efficiency and choose the tried and true Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm
(
14
)
.
Across all tasks, we use the same learning parameters listed in Table
A.1
to demonstrate robustness to hyperparameter tuning.
For all tasks, we employ the exact same reward formulations as in
(
25
)
with further details in Sec.
D
.

Simulation Parameters

For all tasks,
DisMech
uses a simulation timestep size of
\Delta t=0.05
s while
Elastica
uses
\Delta t=0.0002
s.
For non-contact tasks, a control frequency of 10 Hz is used, resulting in
DisMech
and
Elastica
performing one control step every 2 and 500 sim steps, respectively.
For contact tasks, the control frequency is reduced to 2 Hz to coarsen the action space and aid exploration.
This corresponds to one control step every 10 and 2500 simulation steps for
DisMech
and
Elastica
, respectively.
A summary of all sim-related parameters are listed in Table
A.2
.

Control Setup

Across the soft manipulator, five approximately equidistance control points are used.
With five control points, this then results in a total of 5, 10, and 15 DOF for 2D bending, 3D bending, and 3D bending + twist, respectively.
To prevent sharp bending or twisting, the delta control signals are spatially smoothed within each control point’s Voronoi region.
To ensure smooth actuation, we temporally smooth delta commands by linearly interpolating the control signal across the simulation steps.
The same geometric and material parameters for the soft manipulator are used across all tasks, as listed in Table
A.3
.

Task 1:

Follow Target

In this non-contact task, the soft manipulator is required to follow a moving target point with its end effector using 3D bending control, as shown in the top row of Fig.
1
.
Between the two simulators, we observe similar convergence behavior in Fig.
3
with respect to the number of environment steps.
In terms of total training wall-clock time,
DisMech
achieves a
2.4\times
improvement over
Elastica
, primarily due to a
6.21\times
speedup in parallel stepping.

Task 2:

4D IK

In this non-contact task, a random 4D end-effector target pose
(x,y,z,\theta_{\textrm{yaw}})
is sampled at the start of each episode, forming an inverse kinematics problem for the soft manipulator.
Given the yaw target
\theta_{\textrm{yaw}}
, delta natural twist control is used along with 3D bending.
Once again, we observe nearly identical convergence with respect to the number of environment steps, with results consistent with those of
Follow Target
: a
2.45\times
improvement in overall training wall-clock time driven by a
5.69\times
speedup in parallel stepping.

Task 3:

2D Tight Obstacles

The first of our contact tasks, Task 3 requires the soft manipulator to reach a static target position located beyond rigid obstacles in 2D.
Both the target position and the obstacle configuration remain fixed across episodes, but the available clearance is extremely tight (0.12,m clearance for a 0.1,m diameter manipulator), posing a challenging exploration problem.
The control type used is 2D bending.

Contact is notoriously difficult to simulate due to its stiff nonlinear nature, and with its inclusion we begin to see the advantages of implicit time-stepping magnified severalfold.

Elastica
handles contact by computing a repulsive force

\mathbf{F}_{\textrm{contact}}^{\textrm{elastica}}=H(\epsilon)(-\mathbf{F}_{\perp}+k\epsilon+\mathbf{d})\hat{\mathbf{u}},

(7)

where
\epsilon
is the penetration distance (negative when penetrating),
H(\cdot)
is the Heaviside function,
k
is the contact stiffness,
\mathbf{d}
is a damping force, and
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
is the contact normal.
Force equilibrium is maintained as
-\mathbf{F}_{\perp}
represents the sum of all forces pushing the rod against the contact surface.

In contrast,
DisMech
employs a fully implicit contact formulation—the Implicit Contact Method (IMC)
(
6
;
30
)
—which allows for large, aggressive timestep sizes despite the stiffness of contact dynamics.
With this formulation, we observe an overall training speedup of
22.45\times
, driven by a
39.78\times
improvement in parallel stepping.

Task 4:

3D Random Obstacles

In this final task, the soft manipulator must reach a static target position located beyond eight rigid obstacles in 3D, as shown in the middle row of Fig.
1
.
Although the target position remains fixed across episodes, the rigid obstacles are randomized.
Their positions and orientations are sampled within a 3D bounding box in a contact-free manner.
Given this sampling scheme, reaching the target is not always feasible.
The control type used is 3D bending.

Similar to
2D Tight Obstacles
, we again observe a substantial wall-clock speedup of
17.24\times
in overall training, resulting from a
24.16\times
improvement in parallel stepping.

In addition to comparing training convergence and wall-clock speed between the simulators, we also conduct an extensive sim-to-sim evaluation to examine any possible dynamics differences between them.
To this end, we evaluate each of the five
Elastica
and
DisMech
policies for each task within both simulators.
The corresponding discounted returns are reported in Table
2
.

Across all tasks, the mean and standard deviation of the discounted return differ only marginally, indicating close agreement between the two simulators.
One major exception, however, arises in the
Elastica

\rightarrow

DisMech
evaluations for the contact-rich tasks—
2D Tight Obstacles
and
3D Random Obstacles
.
For these evaluations, we observed a significant drop in discounted return with
2D Tight Obstacles
failing completely (negative return) and
3D Random Obstacles
exhibiting an over 40
\%
decrease in performance.

We attribute this large decrease in performance to the fact that
Elastica
’s contact formulation (Eq.
7
) often produced spongy contact behavior, where resistance began slightly above the surface and noticeable penetrations occurred even when using the same contact stiffness of
k=1.6\times 10^{5}
as in
(
25
)
.
This behavior is expected given the simple linear spring term
k\epsilon
in Eq.
7
.
In contrast,
DisMech
employs a fully implicit contact formulation, the Implicit Contact Method (IMC)
(
6
;
30
)
, whose force is derived from a squared potential:

\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{contact}}^{\mathrm{dismech}}=-k\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\left(\dfrac{1}{K}\log\left(1+e^{K\epsilon}\right)\right)^{2},

(8)

where
K=15/\delta
and
\delta
is a contact distance tolerance.
Although this is also a form of soft contact, the squared term produces quadratically higher resistance as a function of
\epsilon
, while
\delta
directly controls the stiffness of the contact energy curve.
By tuning
\delta
until the contact response was visually rigid,
DisMech
achieved noticeably more realistic contact compared to
Elastica
.
In the end, the similar return values labeled with
∗
in Table
2
were obtained only after relaxing the
DisMech
contact parameters
k
and
\delta
to approximate the softer contact dynamics of
Elastica
.
In contrast,
DisMech

\rightarrow

Elastica
evaluations maintained comparable performance despite the latter’s softer contact, suggesting that
DisMech
’s tighter contact bounds make control tasks more challenging rather than easier.

Finally, we note that
Elastica
includes shear deformation in its formulation, whereas
DisMech
does not, as incorporating shear within an implicit time-stepping framework is nontrivial.
However, since the modeled manipulator is sufficiently slender, shear effects are negligible—as evidenced by the nearly identical performance across all tasks in Table
2
.
Overall, the comparable evaluation results highlight the “
free lunch
“ provided by implicit time-stepping.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we conducted an extensive comparison of soft robot policy learning using the fully implicit simulator
DisMech
and the explicit simulator
Elastica
across four representative manipulation tasks.
We also introduced
delta natural curvature control
as an intuitive and efficient method for controlling soft, rod-like robots.
Despite both simulators relying on similar reduced-order centerline formulations,
DisMech
consistently achieved comparable control performance while offering up to
22\times
faster training and nearly
40\times
faster parallel stepping.
Sim-to-sim evaluations further confirmed that
DisMech
closely reproduces
Elastica
’s dynamics, with differences arising primarily from contact modeling rather than elastic behavior.

Several exciting avenues remain for future work.
The most immediate direction is to transition
DisMech
from CPU to GPU.
GPU implementation will be key to achieving the large-scale parallelization seen in rigid-body simulators and would make on-policy learning substantially more feasible.
Finally, effective sim-to-real transfer strategies for delta natural curvature policies must be developed and validated through real robot deployment.
To accomplish this, a sim-to-real mapping function
f(\Delta\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{i})
that translate delta natural curvature actions into real, hardware-specific inputs (e.g., voltage for shape memory alloy (SMA) actuators), must be established.
For quasi-static behaviors, a simple data-driven mapping from delta curvature commands to actuator signals may suffice, whereas dynamic control will require actuator-aware modeling of SMA dynamics.
