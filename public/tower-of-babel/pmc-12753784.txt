Gut plasma membrane proteomes of adults and larvae of the European honey bee, Apis mellifera
Authors: Arinder K Arora, Ruchir Mishra, Bryony C Bonning
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe European honey bee, Apis mellifera, is a vital pollinator of agricultural and horticultural crops. The midgut is the site of entry for honey bee pathogens and for bacterial pesticidal proteins (BPP) used for crop protection. We characterized the proteome of brush border membrane vesicles (BBMV), which are enriched in gut surface proteins, prepared from the midguts of honey bee adults and larvae. A total of 2064 and 2337 proteins were identified via timsTOF mass spectrometry in midgut BBMV from adults and larvae, respectively. These proteins included 71 midgut plasma membrane proteins in adult and 77 in larval samples identified by use of bioinformatic tools and manual curation. The most abundant proteins were identified using a new hybrid method that takes both protein length and sample complexity into account. The potential roles of plasma membrane proteins in interaction with and defense against pathogens were assessed by use of Gene Ontology terms. Seven proteins from three of the protein families (aminopeptidase, cadherin, ATP-binding cassette transporter subfamily C) known to include functional BPP receptors in other insects were identified. Introduction

The European honey bee (Apis mellifera; Hymenoptera) is a vital contributor to world food security1,2, whose pollination provides $12 billion in annual value in the United States alone1. Honey bees also provide valuable ecosystem services3,4. The importance of this insect is expected to grow along with the increasing nutritional needs of a burgeoning human population5. Maintaining the health of honey bee colonies is therefore critical for ensuring global food security. However, pests such as the wax moth (Galleria mellonella) and small hive beetle (Aethina tumida) along with exposure to pesticides through pesticide-contaminated pollen pose major threats to honey bee populations6–8. In addition to these threats, fungal, bacterial, and viral pathogens (particularly viruses vectored by the Varroa mite) have been associated with honey bee decline6,9–12.

While the use of chemical insecticides has contributed to increasing crop yields over > 70 years, adverse effects on the environment and on honey bee health have been observed13–20. There is therefore a need for effective alternatives with lower environmental impacts. Bacterial pesticidal proteins (BPPs), most notably those produced by Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) have gained acceptance as safer tools for pest management21,22. Applied as sprays or produced by insect resistant bioengineered plants, BPPs are ingested by the target insect, and bind specific receptors on the insect midgut, typically resulting in protein oligomerization23. These oligomers then insert into the cell membrane to form pores, creating an osmotic imbalance that leads to cell death24,25. BPP are mainly used against the larval stages of coleopteran, lepidopteran and dipteran pests25–27 and most of those tested are safe to honey bees28–31. However, a few BPPs are toxic to honey bees32 with impacts on feeding, foraging and learning33,34. The economic and ecological significance of A. mellifera necessitates assessing the risk of any new BPPs to honey bees, which can be both time-consuming and costly. Information on the presence and relative abundance of BPP receptor proteins on the surface of the honey bee gut may provide insight into the potential toxicity of BPPs to honey bees.

Microvilli on the luminal surface of insect midgut enterocytes or columnar cells create brush-like borders, providing increased surface area for nutrient absorption35,36. Proteins on the microvillar surface are used as receptors by BPP and by pathogens that enter via the insect gut. The preparation of brush border membrane vesicles (BBMV) from insect guts enriches gut surface proteins to ease identification of the most abundant gut surface proteins and thus candidate pathogen and BPP receptors37–39.

The BBMV proteome has been used to identify gut plasma membrane proteins in five insect species: Aedes aegypti, Trichoplusia ni, Bemisia tabaci, Myzus persicae, and Diaphornia citri38–41. Gene ontology (GO) terms are used to determine the function of plasma membrane proteins, including their potential involvement in pathogen binding. GO terms such as GO:0007165 (initiation of signal transduction after receptor binding), GO:0007166 (cell surface receptor signaling pathway), and GO:0006955 (host immune response) signify potential involvement in immunity, pathogen interactions, and interactions with BPP. Similarly, the GO term GO:0051701 (biological process involved in interaction with host) predicts proteins that directly interact with pathogens or BPPs; and GO:0005515 (protein binding), GO:0044419 (interspecies interactions) and GO:0030260 (proteoglycan binding) can identify putative pathogen or BPP binding partners38,42. These GO terms can be used to narrow the list of gut surface proteins that may interact with pathogens or BPPs.

Honey bee proteomes have been determined for whole insects 43, ovaries44, brain45,46, fat body47, midgut48 and for bees under different stress conditions48–52. However, honey bee midgut-derived BBMV proteomes are not well researched.

Here we characterize the honey bee midgut plasma membrane proteome to determine the presence and abundance of proteins that function as BPP receptors in other insects. We prepared midgut-derived BBMV to enrich gut surface proteins for this analysis. We used bioinformatic predictive tools for protein functions, such as immunity and binding, and to identify candidate BPP and pathogen receptors. Knowledge of the composition and relative abundance of proteins on the surface of the honey bee gut epithelium, along with protein structural analyses may inform in silico prediction of the potential for binding of BPP and toxicity to the honey bee.

Results

Protein identification

Comparison of crude with BBMV protein extracts from the midgut of A. mellifera adults and larvae separated by SDS-PAGE shows a pattern of protein band enrichment indicative of successful BBMV preparation (Fig. S1). Details of peptide and protein numbers from each biological replicate are provided in Table S1. A total of 2,064 and 2,337 proteins were identified from mass spectrometry of honey bee adults and larvae, respectively. Before manual curation, DeepLoc2, eggNog5.2, BUSCA, Phobius, and netGPI classified 165 proteins associated with plasma membranes in honey bee adult BBMV and 189 in larval BBMV (Tables S2, S3). After manual curation using UniProt and FlyBase, 71 proteins were found to be associated with the midgut plasma membrane in honey bee adults and 77 in larvae (Tables S4, S5). Of these, 55 proteins were common to honey bee adults and larvae (Table 1, Table S6). The 55 proteins common to honey bee adults and larvae were compared using a hybrid approach that took protein length and protein complexity into account (Table 1). Of these, seven proteins differed in abundance between honey bee adults and larvae: protein mesh isoforms X1 and X2 (Accession No. XP_006568300.1 and XP_392408.3), alpha glucosidase 2 precursor (Accession No. NP_001035326.1), facilitated trehalose transporter tret1 (Accession No. XP_006571359.2), DE-cadherin isoforms X1 and X2 (Accession No. XP_006563612.1 and XP_006563613.1), and uncharacterized protein LOC410793 (Accession No. XP_006568803.2). Among these differentially abundant proteins, all except the uncharacterized protein LOC410793 (Accession No. XP_006568803.2) were more abundant in adults (Table 1).

Classification of gut plasma membrane proteins

We analyzed all GO terms that were associated with 10 or more unique plasma membrane-associated proteins. A term associated with binding (GO:0005488) was the most common in both adult (31 proteins; Fig. 1) and larval BBMV (35 proteins; Fig. 2). This is consistent with the idea that most plasma membrane associated proteins act as binding partners or receptors for ligands and/or play a role in adhering to neighboring cells. There were 13 and 21 GO terms associated with ≥ 10 unique proteins in adults and larvae, respectively (Figs. 1, 2). The GO terms associated with larval BBMV proteins, but not those of adults were inorganic cation transmembrane transporter activity (GO: 0022890), ribonucleoside triphosphate phosphatase activity (GO: 0017111), hydrolase activity, acting on acid anhydrides (GO:0016817), hydrolase activity, acting on acid anhydrides, in phosphorus-containing anhydrides (GO:0016818), monoatomic cation transmembrane transporter activity (GO: 0008324), pyrophosphatase activity (GO: 0016462), inorganic molecular entity transmembrane transporter (GO:0015318), and active transfer transporter activity (GO: 0022804) (Fig. 2).

Immune related proteins

Three terms (GO:0007165, GO:0007166, and GO:0006955) were used to identify plasma membrane proteins with a predicted role in immunity against pathogens. A total of 20 proteins in adults and 22 proteins in larvae were associated with immunity, with 15 proteins common to both life stages (Fig. 3). Four immunity related proteins were present in adults only and six were found in larvae only (Fig. 3). The most frequent immunity related GO term was GO:0007165, associated with 17 out of 20 proteins in adults and 20 out of 22 proteins in larvae. GO:0007165 is associated with signal transduction, a cascade of molecular processes initiated in response to a signal, which could be pathogen related (Fig. 3). GO:0007166, related to cell surface receptor signaling pathways, was associated with ten proteins in both adults and larvae. GO:0006955, related to host immune response was associated with eight proteins in adults and six in larvae (Fig. 3).

Pathogen host interactions

Thirty-two plasma membrane proteins in adult BBMV and 37 in larval BBMV were predicted to interact with pathogens. Proteins involved in pathogen host interactions were identified based on five GO terms- GO:0005515, G0:0007155, GO:0030260, GO:0044419, and GO:0051701. GO:0005515, related to protein binding, was the most frequent, associated with 27 predicted adult plasma membrane proteins and 32 larval proteins (Fig. 4). Fifteen adult and 20 larval plasma membrane proteins were associated with G0:0007155 (cell adhesion) (Fig. 4). Only four adult plasma membrane proteins were associated with GO:0030260 (proteoglycan binding), GO:0044419 (interspecies interactions), and GO:0051701 (entry, replication, immune evasion, and pathogenesis) (Fig. 4). In larvae, five plasma membrane proteins were associated with GO:0044419 and GO:0051701 and four were associated with GO:0030260 (Fig. 4). Ten of the plasma membrane proteins related to pathogen host interactions were detected in larvae but not in adults: division abnormally delayed protein isoform X1, dystroglycan isoform X1, fasciclin-1 isoform X5, integrin alpha PS2 isoform X2, neurexin-4 isoform X1, plexin-A4, sodium/hydrogen exchanger 9B2 isoform X1, sushi von Willebrand factor type A EGF and pentraxin domain-containing protein 1 isoform X1, uncharacterized protein LOC410975 isoform X1 (ZP superfamily), and zinc transporter 1 isoform X1. Only four proteins related to pathogen host interactions (glucose transporter type 1 isoform X11, integrin alpha PS2 isoform X1, probable phospholipid-transporting ATPase IA isoform X1 and tetraspanin-2A) were detected in adults but not in larvae (Fig. 4).

BPP receptors

Seven proteins from three families known to contain functional BPP receptors in other insects24,53 were also found in the honey bee BBMV proteomes. Specifically, aminopeptidase N (APN, Accession No. XP_006565544.2) was one of the most abundant proteins in both adult and larval BBMV (Table 4; Fig. 4). A second APN, putative aminopeptidase-1 (Accession No. XP_016768511.2). was also present in both life stages. Two cadherin isoforms were identified. Both DE-cadherin isoform X1 (Accession No. XP_006563612.1), and DE-cadherin isoform X2 (Accession No. XP_006563613.1) were more abundant in adult than in larval BBMV (Table 4). Three members of the ATP-binding cassette transporter subfamily C4 (ABCC4), which includes low efficiency receptors for various Cry proteins56, were identified in the honey bee BBMV proteome: probable multidrug resistance-associated protein lethal (2) 03659 (Accession No. XP_393750.5), multidrug resistance-associated protein 4 (Accession No. XP_006566764.2) and multidrug resistance-associated protein 4 isoform X1 (Accession No. XP_026299794.1) (Table 4).

Discussion

We characterized the plasma membrane proteomes of the adult and larval honey bee midgut to assess the presence and relative abundance of known BPP receptor proteins. A total of 93 honey bee midgut plasma membrane proteins were identified. Of these, 22 were unique to larvae, 16 were unique to adults, and 55 were common to both larvae and adults. These data will also facilitate the identification of candidate pathogen receptors. Of note among the proteins predicted to interact with pathogens, is aminopeptidase N: This gut surface protein is one of relatively few proteins with demonstrated pathogen receptor function in other insects37.

Optimized bioinformatics protocol

To provide a quantitative analysis of the relative abundance of plasma membrane proteins, we developed a hybrid normalization approach that integrates NSAF with an entropy‐based normalization to address multiple sources of variation. The NSAF component normalizes for protein length bias and differences in total spectral counts, while the entropy‐based adjustment compensates for variations in sample complexity between replicate samples54,55. This approach is especially useful for comparing different insects, which may differ in protein expression profiles, sample complexities, and protein length distributions. Moreover, this hybrid strategy mitigates systematic biases introduced by differences in software and instrument sensitivity, including variations in peptide detection, spectral counting approaches, and instrument specific noise patterns, through sample complexity adjustments, total spectral count normalization, and built-in quality control metrics such as the coefficient of variation. While differences relative to standard NSAF were minor in this comparison of honey bee adult and larval proteins, the hybrid approach will be a valuable tool for comparing data derived from multiple species and proteomics techniques.

Proportion of plasma membrane proteins

A total of 2,064 adult and 2,337 larval BBMV proteins based on unique accession numbers were identified before manual curation. This is more than the 1,571 proteins identified from whole honey bee midgut48, likely due to the use of an hexafluoroisopropanol-based sample preparation protocol and a Q-Exactive Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometer in the prior study. Whole guts would otherwise be expected to contain more proteins than gut-derived BBMV. In addition, although the honey bee NCBI protein database is not complete, its coverage has improved over the years. We identified 1034 and 1085 unique proteins (based on unique peptides for each protein) from honey bee adult and larval midgut BBMV, respectively. Bioinformatics alone predicted 165 and 189 midgut BBMV proteins to be associated with the plasma membrane of honey bee adults and larvae. Following a conservative manual curation step (with reference to FlyBase and Uniprot), 6.9% (n = 71) of unique proteins in adults and 7.1% (n = 77) of unique proteins in larvae were identified as plasma membrane proteins, comparable to other studies38,39,41.

Differences in plasma membrane protein abundance between adults and larvae

Six plasma membrane associated proteins (< 10% of the total) differed in abundance in adult vs. larval BBMV, but were not necessarily the most abundant BBMV proteins. Aminopeptidase N was the most abundant plasma membrane associated protein in both adult and larval BBMV, followed by alpha glucosidase 2 precursor in adults. Not all plasma membrane proteins identified were shared between adult and larval honey bees; 22 proteins were unique to larval samples and 16 were unique to adult samples. In addition to life stage, these differences may be related to the digestion of food: The adults used in this study were dissected < 24 h after emergence and were unfed, while fed larvae were collected from the colony. The expression of certain digestive enzymes required for nutrient uptake may have been affected by this difference in food intake.

Relevance to risk assessment

As important pollinators, honey bees are routinely tested in risk assessment studies for novel biological and chemical pesticides56,57. Some of these agents, such as BPP, act via the insect gut. BPPs bind to protein receptors on the surface of the gut epithelium that mediate protein toxicity25,58. Characterization of proteins on the surface of the honey bee gut epithelium in conjunction with in silico modeling of potential interaction between BPP and putative receptor proteins will inform the potential for toxicity of novel BPPs to the honey bee.

Comprehensive reviews by Duan et al.29 and Ricroch et al.59 highlight the safety of the majority of BPPs to honey bees. Pollen from widely deployed bioengineered corn and cotton crops expressing Cry1Ac or Cry2A60 did not adversely affect honey bee survival61,62, and tissue damage typically associated with BPP toxicity was not observed following consumption of diet containing Cry1C or Cry2A61,63,64. Microbes in the gut that contribute to honey bee nutrition65, were also unaffected by BPP consumption66–68.

In addition to the use of BPP in bioengineered crops, BPP or Bt strains that produce different combinations of BPPs may be used within the hive for pest management7,69. Pests of bee hives include the wax moth, G. mellonella, and the small hive beetle, A. tumida, both of which are susceptible to certain BPPs. EPA risk assessment studies for different Bt strains conclude that B. thuringiensis subspecies aizawai shows high toxicity against the honey bee, but minimal toxicity against honey bee is seen for other Bt subspecies (kurstaki, israelensis, tenebrionis)70. Interestingly, Bt subsp. aizawai strain ABTS 1857 targeting lepidopteran larvae, is being considered by EPA71,72 for control of wax moths within bee hives. In this case, risk of honey bee exposure would be minimized by applying the product to frames prior to winter storage.

BPP receptors in insects

At least four protein families contain known functional BPP receptors that mediate toxicity24,73,74. These include the GPI-anchored proteins aminopeptidase N (APN) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), along with transmembrane proteins cadherin (CAD) and ATP-binding cassette transporter family C (ABCC). These proteins are necessary but not sufficient for the toxicity of BPPs24. A total of seven proteins from three of these protein families (APN, CAD, and ABCC) were found in honeybee BBMV, with variable relative abundance (Table 4). ALP was present in larval and adult honey bee midgut BBMV at extremely low levels with ALP spectra absent from two of the three biological replicates, such that this protein was excluded from analysis.

In contrast to the two aminopeptidase N and two cadherin proteins, the three protein members of the ABCC4 family, which function only as low efficiency receptors for some Cry proteins58,75, were not predicted to function in pathogen interaction (Fig. 4). Five Cry proteins bound the ABCC4 of Bombyx mori or Tribolium castaneum with low efficiency, but unlike other ABCCs, ABCC4 was not a determinant of susceptibility58,75. Though they may facilitate weak binding of Cry proteins to the honey bee midgut, ABCC4 presence may be less important for risk assessment than the presence of the CAD and APN. The number of genes for other members of the ABC transporters (ABC-A and ABC-B), which function as receptor proteins for various BPPs75,76, are reduced in the honey bee. The honey bee has a total of 41 ABC genes compared to 55 in Bombyx mori and 73 in T. castaneum77. Thus, BPPs that use receptor proteins absent from- or with low abundance in- the midgut plasma membrane proteome (such as ALP or certain ABC transporters) may present lower risks to honey bees. However, our incomplete understanding of BPP mode of action, and the fact that binding to a receptor protein is not always sufficient for toxicity mean that empirical testing of that putative lower risk is needed.

Role of BPP receptors in BPP resistance

The presence, abundance and sequences of BPP receptors each have important functions in both the toxicity of and resistance to BPPs. While ALP activity in the gut lumen of Cry1Ac-resistant Helicoverpa armigera was increased relative to the susceptible strain, ALP activity was low in the BBMV preparation78. This outcome was attributed either to increased production and secretion of soluble ALP, or to ALP receptor shedding resulting in less midgut bound protein. Binding of Cry1Ac to ALP in the gut lumen reduced the binding of Cry1Ac to the midgut epithelium resulting in resistance to this BPP. In our honey bee BBMV preparations we saw extremely low levels of ALP (Accession No. XP_026294961.1). Whether high levels of ALP are present in the honey bee gut lumen and if so, their potential to bind BPP remain to be determined.

In Spodoptera exigua, the lack of expression of one out of the four APNs was associated with resistance against Cry1Ca79. In Helicoverpa armigera, reduced expression and a deletion in an APN gene resulted in the inability of Cry1Ac to bind, resulting in resistance80. Decreased expression and mutation of cadherin, which decreased Cry1Ac binding, was associated with resistance development in Pectinophora gossypiella81,82. In Plutella xylostella, MAP4K4 suppressed the expression of ABCC2 and ABCC3, resulting in resistance to Cry1Ac. Silencing of MAP4K4 increased the expression of ABCC2 and ABCC3 and reversed the resistance phenotype83. These studies highlight that (1) not all proteins within a protein family are functional receptors for a given BPP (e.g., APN), (2) receptor abundance is important for toxicity, with reduced abundance leading to resistance in some cases, (3) minor changes in the amino acid sequence of a receptor protein can have dramatic effects on toxicity. Information on the presence (or absence) and abundance of candidate receptor proteins in honey bee midgut BBMV in conjunction with sequence data may therefore enable in silico predictions of BPP risk to the honey bee.

Conclusions

We present the proteome of honey bee adult and larval midgut BBMV with a focus on plasma membrane proteins. We created a hybrid method to calculate protein abundance that can be used to compare gut plasma membrane proteomes among species. Proteins associated with immunity, protein binding, BPP and pathogen gut interactions, along with candidate BPP receptors, were identified using bioinformatics tools. The utility of data on BPP receptor presence and abundance in conjunction with in silico modeling of potential BPP-receptor interactions in prediction of BPP toxicity to the honey bee warrants further investigation. BPPs that bind receptors such as ALP or certain ABC transporters that are of low abundance or absent from the honey bee gut may pose the least risk to honey bees.

Materials and methods

BBMV preparation

Midguts (25 to 35) of 1-day old honey bee adults and 4th instar larvae were dissected in PBS under a stereomicroscope. The guts from each life stage were pooled and stored in MET-A buffer (0.3 M mannitol with 5 mM EGTA and 20 mM Tris/HCl at pH 7.5) containing a protease inhibitor (Pierce™ Protease Inhibitor Tablets, EDTA‐free; ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and PMSF (1X, phenylmethylsulphonyl fluoride; ThermoFisher Scientific) at − 80 °C. Brush border membrane vesicles (BBMV) were prepared using MgCl2 precipitation and differential centrifugation38,39,84. The dissected guts were thawed on ice and pipetted with 6 ml MET buffer into a Dounce homogenizer (Wheaton Science Products, Millville, NJ, USA). The guts were homogenized on ice by 35 pestle strokes of the homogenizer. The suspension was centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then homogenized a second time on ice by 20 strokes. MgCl2 (240 mM, 1/19th volume of the solution) was added and the preparation incubated on ice for 15 min. The solution was centrifuged at 2500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was decanted into clean tubes and centrifuged at 100,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was homogenized in a Dounce homogenizer in 3 ml MET A buffer on ice. The solution was incubated on ice for 15 min following the addition of 158 µl 240 mM MgCl2. Impurities were removed by centrifuging at 2500 g for 10 min at 4 °C and discarding the pellet. The supernatant was poured into tubes and centrifuged at 100,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in MET A buffer containing protease inhibitor and 1X PMSF.

Three biological replicates each of adult and larval BBMV were prepared. The BBMV protein concentration was measured by Bradford assay85. The protein profile of BBMV was compared to that of the crude midgut homogenate by gel electrophoresis. After adding protein loading buffer [2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 10% glycerol, 0.01% bromophenol blue in 60 mmol Tris‐HCl buffer], 2 μg of protein of each of the BBMV and crude gut preparations was solubilized by boiling at 95˚C. The proteins were separated by electrophoresis in a 4–12% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel. The resulting protein bands were stained for visualization (Pierce™ Silver Stain for Mass Spectrometry; ThermoFisher Scientific).

Sample preparation by easy extraction and digestion (SPEED)

Protein samples were prepared for proteomic analysis as previously described86. Honey bee BBMV (from adult and larvae) were solubilized in 4 volumes of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA; v/v) for efficient protein extraction, followed by incubation at 25 °C for 2 min. Ten times the volume of 2 M Tris-Base buffer (v/v) was added to neutralize the solution. Samples were incubated at 95 °C for 5 min following the addition of 1.1 times of 10 × reduction/ alkylation buffer [100 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP, 29 mg/ml)/ 400 mM 2-chloroacetamide (CAA, 37 mg/ml) in water]. The protein concentration was adjusted to 0.25 µg/µl by the addition of sample dilution buffer [10:1 (v/v) mixture of 2 M TrisBase and TFA]. Proteins were digested using Trypsin (2% of sample, w/w; Pierce™ Trypsin Protease, MS Grade) and incubated at 37 °C and 600 rpm for 20 h in a Thermo Mixer. TFA (2% final concentration) was added, followed by desalting using ZipTip with C18 resin (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA).

Desalting of peptides

The ZipTip (2 μg capacity) with C18 resin was equilibrated sequentially with 10 μl of 100% acetonitrile (ACN), 10 μl of 50% ACN/50% of 0.1% TFA solution, and 10 μl of 0.1% TFA. Digested BBMV proteins were desalted using the equilibrated ZipTip mini-reverse phase (Millipore), with the desalting process repeated three times for each sample. The desalted and suspended peptides were pipetted through the ZipTip 10 times, followed by ZipTip washing with 10 μl of 0.1% TFA 10 times. The resultant sample was eluted with 80% ACN/0.1% TFA solution. The samples were then lyophilized in a SpeedVac.

Mass spectrometry

Protein samples were separated on Bruker Fifteen ReproSil (150 mm × 75 μm; 1.9 μm C18) at 400 nL/min with Solvent A [0.1% formic acid in water (v/v)] and Solvent B [80/20/0.1% ACN/water/formic acid (v/v/v)]. The samples were separated on a hybrid trapped ion mobility‐quadrupole time‐of‐flight mass spectrometer (timsTOF fleX; Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA) with a modified nano-electrospray ion source interfaced with an automated Easy nLC 1000 Ultra (Thermo Scientific). A linear gradient was used with Solvent B from 5 to 17% for 60 min, 25% for 30 min, to 35% for 10 min, to 85% for 10 min, followed by a hold at 85% for 10 min to separate peptides. A spectrum library was produced using data dependent mode with Parallel Accumulation Serial Fragmentation (PASEF), which improves ion utilization efficiency and speed of data acquisition. The MS/MS mode scans were recorded from 100 to 1700 m/z, while operating the dual TIMS by the system at a 100% duty cycle. TIMS ion charge control was set at 5e6 to scan ion mobility from 0.6 to 1.6 Vs/cm2. Four selected ions from the Agilent ESI LC/MS tuning mix [m/z, 1/K0: (322.0481, 0.7363 Vs/cm2), (622.0289, 0.9915 Vs/cm2), (922.0097, 1.1996 Vs/cm2), (1221, 9906, 1.3934 Vs/cm2)] in positive mode were used to linearly calibrate the TIMS dimension.

Data refinement

All raw LC–MS/MS data were preprocessed using the Data Refinement module in PEAKS X (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, Ontario, Canada) under Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode. The Merge Scans, Correct Precursor (Min Charge: 1 and Max Charge: 3) and Associated Features with Chimera Scan and Filter features were enabled. The Merge Scan option combines consecutive MS/MS scans of the same precursor to increase spectral quality and reduce redundancy. The Correct Precursor option corrects precursor masses (and optionally charge states) based on high-accuracy precursor information, improving the mass accuracy before database searching. The associate features (detect peptide ions) with chimera scans helps to deconvolute mixed MS/MS spectra that may contain fragment ions from more than one precursor. The filter feature removes low-abundance or irrelevant features outside the specified m/z, retention time, charge state, or abundance ranges, streamlining the dataset prior to database searching. Following data refinement, the processed spectra were submitted for peptide and protein identification using the PEAKS database search engine.

Database search

Peptide and protein identification was performed using the PEAKS X (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.) search engine under the following conditions: Precursor Mass Tolerance: 20.0 ppm (monoisotopic mass), Fragment Ion Tolerance: 0.5 Da, Enzyme: Trypsin (semi-specific), allowing up to 2 missed cleavages, Fixed Modification: Carbamidomethylation (C), Variable Modifications: Deamidation (N/Q), Oxidation (M), and N-terminal Acetylation, Maximum Variable PTMs per Peptide: 3, Database: Apis mellifera (NCBI Reference Sequence Database, RefSeq) sequences downloaded (supplemented with a contaminant database “CRAP”), FDR Estimation: Enabled with the decoy-fusion option, PEAKS posttranslational modification (PTM) Analysis: Enabled for detection of unspecified post-translational modifications, and SPIDER Option: Enabled to refine sequences and identify potential sequence variants.

Spectra were searched against the combined target-decoy database, and identifications were filtered by a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) at the peptide level (unless otherwise specified). The “decoy-fusion” approach was used for FDR estimation to minimize bias and ensure accurate assessment of false positives. The “Find Unspecified PTMs with PEAKS PTM” option was selected to explore additional modifications beyond those specified. Finally, only the proteins with ≥ 2 unique peptides and ≥ 2 peptide spectral matches were retained for analysis.

Data analysis

First, BUSCA87 and DeepLoc288 were used to determine gut surface or subcellular localization of proteins. EggNOG5.2 was used to predict cellular component Gene Ontology (GO) terms89,90. The proteins were further examined for the presence of signal peptides and transmembrane domains using Phobius91, and GPI anchor signals were assessed using NetGPI v1.192. Each protein predicted to be a plasma membrane associated protein was then checked manually against the Universal Protein Resource—UniProt database93,94 and FlyBase95 to confirm gut localization. Proteins that were not confirmed to be present on the cell plasma membrane based on these two resources were removed from the list. Protein function was predicted using eggNOG5.2. The abundance of unique proteins present on the midgut plasma membrane of A. mellifera larvae and adults was compared using hybrid normalization abundance using Student’s t-test. Other data analyses and manipulations were conducted in RStudio (v. 2021.09.1) using Tidyverse96 or Python 3.9.12 and seqinr packages97.
