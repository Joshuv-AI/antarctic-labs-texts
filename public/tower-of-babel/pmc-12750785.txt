Buffers in intravenous solutions: is the source of bicarbonate a source of confusion?
Authors: Arnau Ulsamer, Antoni J Betbesé, Ana Campos-Gómez, Josep Centelles, Eduardo Domínguez, Daniel Iglesias-Serret, Juan V Lorente-Olazábal, Xosé L Pérez-Fernández, Joan Sabater-Riera, Raul Rigo-Bonnin, John A Kellum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is a widespread belief that organic sodium salts included in intravenous solutions serve as bicarbonate precursors, and that this mechanism explains their effects on plasma pH. We aimed to explain why the effect of organic anions, such as citrate, acetate, gluconate, and lactate on the acid-base balance is independent of bicarbonate generation. For this purpose, we mainly focused on regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA). The sodium load provided with these buffers and its contribution to the plasma strong ion difference is a more suitable model for explaining and predicting their alkalinizing effect. Moreover, the bicarbonate generated from the metabolization of these buffers via the Krebs cycle results from CO2 dissolution in water, and thus yields bicarbonate together with a proton (H+). As such, metabolization of these buffers does not cause alkalosis per se.Keywords: Alkalosis, Acid-base, Bicarbonate, Regional citrate anticoagulation, RCA, Citrate, Acetate, Strong ion difference, SID, Stewart

Introduction

The mechanisms of action of buffers and their respective metabolic pathways are widely discussed in the scientific literature. Exploration of the abundant publications on the subject, revealed substantial confusion and even contradictory information. A common misunderstanding was detected in several works, stating that buffers used in Fluid Therapy and Renal Replacement Therapy (hereinafter referred to as IV solutions) would render its effect as they are metabolized into bicarbonate [1–10]. Buffers that are sodium salts of organic anions, such as citrate, acetate, gluconate, and lactate, exert their effect on pH, regardless of their metabolization. In this comprehensive review, we attempted to thoroughly explain this common misconception. For this purpose, we mainly focused on regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA).

The origin of the notion of buffers as bicarbonate precursors

The common belief of buffer metabolization to bicarbonate as a mechanism of action has various sources. First, alkalosis is often straightforwardly defined as bicarbonate accumulation [11–13] rather than a rise in pH. This is a simplification that certainly contributes to the notion of bicarbonate being the cause of alkalosis. As previously stated [14], there is confusion of correlation with causation.

Awareness of hyperchloremic acidosis caused by chloride in common 0.9% saline, led to the use of alternative IV solutions known as balanced solutions, in which the chloride anion is substituted by buffering substances that do not contribute to acidosis. The use of bicarbonate is usually discarded due to high operational cost, as it should be stored in gas-impermeable bags [15]. Therefore, the majority of manufacturers have replaced it by an organic anion, such as lactate, acetate, gluconate or citrate, expecting it to act as a “precursor” or stable substitute for bicarbonate. It is likely that this rationale also contributed to the confusion (reviewed in Muir [16]).

Exploring the origins of this notion, seminal experiments in anesthetized, starved dogs, showed rapid metabolization of acetate that resulted in CO2 production in a manner similar to bicarbonate infusion. Therefore, acetate was considered to be a source of a fixed base [17] and these anions were considered to be base sources. However, it should be taken into account that, upon starvation, circulating CO2 levels would decrease [18], while reactivation of metabolism by addition of any mediator of the Krebs cycle (as acetate or citrate) would restore the CO2 production [13, 19, 20]. This would cause a bicarbonate level rise, but not necessarily alkalosis as will be discussed further. Indeed, when IV solutions containing citrate, acetate, or lactate are administered to patients, a consistent increase in bicarbonate levels is observed, which is sometimes accompanied by alkalosis [1, 21, 22].

In another report in which solutions containing either bicarbonate, acetate or lactate as a base source were compared, bicarbonate corrected acidosis the fastest [23]. The delayed effect of lactate and acetate was attributed to its metabolization time, meaning the time required for conversion into bicarbonate. However, in these experiments, the bicarbonate solution also had the largest Strong Ion Difference (SID; 48 mEq/L for the bicarbonate solution vs. 29 mEq/L for the lactate solution, and 30 mEq/L for the acetate solution).

In another series of experiments performed in anesthetized dogs, Wathen et al. [24] infused 10 mmol/kg of sodium bicarbonate, sodium acetate, or sodium lactate, and achieved similar degrees of alkalemia. This finding helps to understand the misconception that for each mol of sodium salts of organic anions, one mol of bicarbonate would be formed [22], with the exception of citrate, on the premise that its three carboxyl groups would generate three moles of bicarbonate per mol of compound [25]. However, this reasoning is misguided: it is stoichiometrically inconsistent to suggest that one mol of citrate, which contains six carbon atoms, could generate three moles of bicarbonate, as will be discussed. In contrast, the amount of sodium present in these salts correlates better with their alkalinizing effect, according to the SID theory proposed by Stewart, or earlier, the buffer-base theory of Singer and Hastings [26].

More recently, Ergin et al. [10] developed a model in which a 70% partial hepatectomy was induced in rats while exploring the acid-base response upon Ringer’s lactate, Ringer’s acetate, Plasma-Lyte, or normal saline infusion. They linked the observed effects on the pH to the metabolization of the contained organic buffers by considering them “bicarbonate precursors”. However, as in the experiments by Watten et al. [23] and Wathen [24], their results correlate better with the SID of the fluids used: Plasma-Lyte SID = 50 mEq/L (moderately alkalinizing), Ringer’s Acetate SID = 27 and Ringer’s Lactate SID = 29 (slightly alkalinizing), and saline SID = 0 (acidifying by dilution).

Last, as mentioned above, sodium bicarbonate is not commonly used as a component of intravenous solutions. However, its infusion is occasionally used as a therapeutic agent. In this case, if the patient has intact its gas exchange capacity, as bicarbonate is in equilibrium with CO2, it would be respired away in seconds, probably increasing respiratory rate [27, 28]. Notably, the primary mechanism by which bicarbonate modulates pH has been reported to be through an increase in the SID [29].

Citrate and alkalosis

Anticoagulation of the circuit is often necessary to maintain patency during Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT). The addition of citrate chelates calcium and acts as an anticoagulant, thereby increasing the lifespan of membranes. To inhibit coagulation, the target citrate concentration in blood inside the circuit is 3–4 mmol/L, which allows for an iCa2+ concentration between 0.2 and 0.35 mmol/L [30]. Citrate/citric acid is a tricarboxylic acid with pKa values of 3.1, 4.8, and 6.4 [31, 32]. Therefore, in the blood, it exists predominantly as citrate3−, a trivalent anion.

As citrate is a small molecule, most calcium–citrate complexes freely move across the membrane by diffusion or convection and are lost in the ultrafiltrate or dialysate effluent. Therefore, it is necessary to add a systemic calcium infusion system to replace the calcium lost in the effluent. There are two common options to replace lost calcium: calcium chloride and calcium gluconate, which have different effects on chloride levels [33, 34].

Regional Citrate Anticoagulation (RCA) can result in development of alkalosis [21, 35]. Alkalosis is commonly attributed to the conversion of non-dialyzed citrate to bicarbonate, mostly in the liver. Some studies even claim that this conversion is in a ratio of 3:1 mol [13, 33, 36–40], which is not accurate in terms of stoichiometry, as citrate has six carbons and bicarbonate has one (if referring to the CO2 production in the Krebs cycle, as can be seen in Fig. 1, only two CO2 molecules are formed from citrate). Instead, the reason trisodium citrate results in three times the alkalinizing effect of bicarbonate is that there are three sodium ions per molecule while sodium bicarbonate has only one. If disodium citrate is used, the ratio becomes 2:1.

In RCA for CRRT, citrate enters the body in association with calcium and/or sodium, both being strong cations that increase the SID; thus, leading to a higher risk of alkalosis. In some studies, the use of solutions containing high concentrations of sodium citrate have been linked to an increased risk of alkalosis, suggesting that this may be the cause [41, 42]. Of note, in the study by Gabutti et al., [42] in which low citrate concentration was used, both the citrate solution, the dialysis solution, and the replacement solution had a common SID of 40 mEq/L, similar to physiological SID of plasma. Therefore, the preservation of the acid-base balance could be as well explained by the physiological SID of these solutions (specially, the dialysis solution), rather than its citrate concentration.

Sodium, calcium, and SID

Oudemans-van Straaten et al. [43, 44] proposed that when citrate is administered in the form of sodium citrate, the sodium ion that accompanies citrate increases the SID, having an alkalizing effect.

The authors recalled that each citrate molecule is equivalent to three bicarbonate ions in terms of buffering capacity (not converted into three bicarbonate ions). In the field, it is assumed that each citrate molecule has a buffering capacity equivalent to three bicarbonates and, not by chance, this equivalence seems to work in clinical practice. Indeed, Stewart’s theories on SID fit very well with real-world physiology [45].

The same principle applies to balanced solutions containing buffers other than citrate, which are also characterized by a positive SID. Of note, even if the blood physiological SID is approximately 40–42 mEq/L, it has been experimentally proven that the SID of an IV solution that does not produce any acidifying or alkalinizing effects should be approximately 24 mEq/L [46–48]. As stated by Stewart, there are 3 independent variables that contribute to the acid-base balance of plasma: CO2, weak acids, and SID. Since most IV solutions do not contain any weak acids, their SID needs to be lower than the 40–42 mEq/L found in the blood to compensate for this absence of weak acids [49, 50].

Sodium accumulation as a cause of alkalosis has been empirically validated both in vitro and in clinical practice [51, 52], as has chloride accumulation in acidosis [53, 54]. Additionally, use of intravenous 0.9% saline (SID = 0) causes dilutional acidosis [55, 56].

According to Stewart principles [45, 49], any solution must satisfy the principle of electroneutrality. Therefore, the addition of positive sodium ions has to be balanced and, consequently, all the buffer systems in the body will rearrange accordingly. The principal buffer system, namely the carbonic acid/bicarbonate system, will be no exception, displacing part of the carbonic acid to bicarbonate. Yet, this should be considered an effect, not the cause of alkalinization.

In summary, based on the aforementioned studies, increased SID is the principal explanation for the alkalosis effect observed upon the use of IV solutions containing sodium salts of organic anions such as Na-acetate, Na3-citrate, Na-lactate, or Na-gluconate [57].

Metabolization of the organic anions from IV solutions

The main bicarbonate source in the human body is CO2 conversion by carbonic anhydrase and, as has been described, citrate, acetate, lactate, and -maybe less clearly- gluconate can be metabolized in the Krebs cycle, producing CO2 [58–61] (Fig. 1). Thus, although increased CO2 production and decreased CO2 elimination will result in increased bicarbonate levels, this does not imply an alkalinizing effect. If all substances metabolized in the Krebs cycle would cause alkalosis, then glucose or fatty acids would have this effect as well. Of course, this is not the case (Fig. 1).

Organic anions such as acetate, citrate or lactate are largely metabolized in the liver [10, 43, 64, 65] where they may enter to some metabolic pathways such as Cori cycle and gluconeogenesis. These metabolic routes have been suggested to reduce acid load [66, 67], although there is no evidence supporting the idea of a direct bicarbonate production. Moreover, it should be noted that even if the metabolic fate of organic anions was gluconeogenesis for use in peripheral tissues, the resultant glucose would ultimately be oxidized through the Krebs cycle.

Regarding citrate, to better understand how it is metabolized, it might help to recall a very common but seldom reported clinical situation in which hepatocytes quickly metabolize citrate: It is in response to surgery. This surprisingly unknown phenomenon was first described by Costello et al. [68, 69] and subsequently explored [70, 71]. According to their observations, nearly all patients who underwent surgery developed hypocitricemia that lasted for several days due to rapid citrate metabolization by the Krebs cycle in the liver. However, none of the patients developed hypocitricemia if they only underwent anesthesia. Notably, alkalosis events were not commonly observed under these conditions.

In experiments in both humans and rats, it was shown that the liver does not metabolize citrate under normal conditions but only under stress situations, regardless of circulating citrate levels, also when citrate plasma concentrations are relatively high [70]. Similarly, in critically ill patients (with the partial exception of those with liver disease), citrate is metabolized in the liver [72], as has been described in surgery patients.

This topic was recently addressed by Kumar et al. [73], who showed that the primary citrate source for most cells is mitochondrial production, whereas import of citrate into the cell is dependent on the SLC13A5 gene, encoding for NaCT (sodium-dependent citrate transporter), which is activated independently of the presence of extracellular citrate particularly in the liver and brain.

In a set of experiments, citrate was found to be imported into cells only upon hypoxia, a situation in which pyruvate dehydrogenase flux was reduced. Under these conditions, citrate is mainly catabolized in the cytosol to support acetyl-CoA generation, and is metabolized mainly into fatty acids and Krebs cycle intermediates [73]. These findings indicate that circulating citrate is not a primary biosynthetic substrate but rather serves as a resource upon distinct types of stress such as ischemia, hypoxia, metal toxicity, surgery, or other invasive interventions such as CRRT, which probably in turn involves the aforementioned ischemia and hypoxia processes.

Discussion

The notion of sodium salts of organic anions as bicarbonate sources to explain their effect in IV solutions has been broadly extended in the medical literature. We found several examples of works in which the effect of SID was overlooked when it could be a more suitable explanation for the observed acid-base effects [10, 23, 24, 42].

As the idea of liver metabolism as a source of bicarbonate that supposedly contributes to alkalinization has been so extended, it is understandable the assumption that an unknown metabolic pathway by which bicarbonate could be produced de novo in the absence of a proton in the liver may exist [33, 36]. To the best of our knowledge, such a pathway has not been described; therefore, net bicarbonate production in the liver should not be considered an alkalinizing factor.

A critical review of the available literature highlights the centrality of Stewart’s theories on the balance between strong cations (such as sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium) and strong anions (mainly chloride) as drivers of the acid-base balance. Understanding this balance is relevant for clinical practice, and we believe that its knowledge should be potentiated in the content of health sciences degrees at universities. A good example of a largely unknown topic is the acid-base neutrality of IV solutions with an SID of approximately 24 mEq/L, experimentally proved by Morgan [46, 47] and confirmed by Omron and Omron [48].

An important contribution from Stewart’s work, is the provision of a theoretical framework for what has already been described in classic biochemistry, in which acids were synonymous with strong anions, and bases were synonymous with strong cations [74, 75]. Christensen [75] discussed the possible contribution of sodium that accompanies bicarbonate in the acid-base balance and stated that the alkalinizing effect of sodium bicarbonate could be due to either sodium load or bicarbonate ions. He regretted that the students were poorly trained in chemistry and, therefore, could hardly understand how sodium ions could be alkalinizing. In our opinion, much progress has not yet been made in this regard.

As we aimed to prove in this article, the effect of IV solutions in SID is likely the main cause of the acid-balance disturbance episodes. A recent randomized clinical trial by Dell’Anna et al. [76] reinforces this hypothesis, demonstrating in real patients the fundamental role of SID. Therefore, more attention should be paid to the composition of these solutions regarding strong anions and cations and personalizing treatments should be considered. For instance, hypoperfusion and hyperchloremia are often observed in sepsis, both of which leading to acidosis [77, 78]. In this context, IV solutions with a slightly increased SID might be considered.

In the case of patients undergoing dialysis, the composition of the dialysis solution determines the acid-base equilibrium, as it equilibrates with blood ions and therefore influences its SID. However, when convection is used, the clearance of some ions depends on the ultrafiltration rate. At a high ultrafiltration rate, more ions are filtered, and few ions enter the systemic circulation [40].

Another important consideration is that most patients who receive IV solutions are either sedated or ventilated for long periods of time; therefore, a high bicarbonate concentration can be misinterpreted as a metabolic alkalosis when it is produced by subacute hypercapnia. This misinterpretation has been reported previously [79, 80]. This is an important topic to be addressed: is it accurate to consider only the bicarbonate levels to define alkalemia?

This is not the first text to raise the flag reminding that Krebs cycle produces CO2 and not bicarbonate. In a letter to the Blood Purification Editor, Anstey [81] claimed that it is a common misconception to state that citrate is metabolized directly to bicarbonate when, in the Krebs Cycle, citrate is metabolized to carbon dioxide with the co-production of NADH. There are probably other commendable examples of this, but we believe that they are vastly outnumbered by the articles that oversimplify by stating the product of the Krebs cycle would be straight bicarbonate.

One limitation of our work is that we have not addressed the multicompartmental distribution of ions and water between plasma, interstitial fluid, intracellular fluid, and erythrocytes, an aspect that has been explored using mathematical models based on electroneutrality developed by Wolf and colleagues. These multicompartmental models, validated using in vitro, in vivo, and clinical data, consistently demonstrate that acid-base changes following the addition or removal of strong ions are due to changes in the SID within each compartment. Their simulations show that Na+ or other strong cations quantitatively increase pH, while Cl– and other strong anions exert the opposite acidifying effect. Taken together, these multicompartmental models provide a more comprehensive physiological context that complements the plasma-based approach presented here [82–85].

Conclusion

The effect of sodium salts of organic anions, such as citrate, acetate, gluconate, and lactate, on acid-base balance is mainly independent of their metabolization. The sodium load provided together with these buffers and its contribution to SID is a more suitable model for explaining and predicting their alkalinizing effects. The bicarbonate resulting from the metabolization of these buffers originates from the Krebs cycle, in which bicarbonate is produced together with a proton (H+); thus, it’s effect cannot be alkalizing.

A better understanding of the nature of buffers and their metabolism can contribute to a more personalized clinical practice and to an advanced rationale for improved IV solution design.
