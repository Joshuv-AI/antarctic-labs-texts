Variability of the prognostic nutritional index within 3 months after curative resection for non-small cell lung cancer and 2-year recurrence-free survival
Authors: Guojun Yue, Lingyun Yue, Yi Wang, Wendong Qu, Ming Gong, Hui Wang, Longfeng Gu, Wenbi Huang, Yaqian Zhao, Xi Chen, Gang Shen, Hu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly recurrence after curative resection remains common in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). We evaluated whether early postoperative trajectories of the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) improve risk stratification.MethodsIn a single-center prospective cohort of patients undergoing R0 resection for stage I–III NSCLC (2021–2025), 119 were enrolled; 94 who were alive and recurrence-free at 3 months with complete PNI data formed the landmark cohort. PNI was measured preoperatively and during the first 3 postoperative months and calculated as 10 × serum albumin (g/dL) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm3). The primary exposure was 3-month PNI recovery, and the primary endpoint was 2-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) from the 3-month landmark, estimated with Cox models adjusted for stage and adjuvant therapy.ResultsAmong 94 patients, 51 (54.3%) showed recovered PNI and 43 (45.7%) non-recovered PNI. During follow-up, 27 RFS events occurred, with higher event rates in the non-recovered group (41.9% vs. 17.6%; 2-year RFS 59.2% vs. 82.3%). Non-recovered PNI was independently associated with worse RFS (adjusted hazard ratio 2.3) and improved a clinical model C-index from 0.66 to 0.73. Sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoffs, continuous PNI change, complication adjustment, treatment-timing exclusions, and conservative Cox models were directionally consistent.ConclusionsFailure to recover PNI within 3 months may identify patients at higher risk of recurrence after curative NSCLC resection. Multicenter validation is required before clinical implementation.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Prognostic nutritional index, Postoperative recovery, Recurrence-free survival, Dynamic biomarker, Prospective cohort

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases and remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths globally [1]. Although surgical resection is potentially curative treatment for early-stage NSCLC, a substantial proportion of patients, ranging from 30% to 70%, experience recurrence within five years post-surgery [2, 3]. Early recurrence has a major impact on survival and quality of life. Studies have shown that up to 80% of recurrences occur within the first two years after surgery [4, 5]. Risk of recurrence is influenced by tumor and treatment factors, including disease stage at diagnosis, histology, and the extent of resection, and recurrence patterns differ between intrathoracic and distant sites [4, 5]. Current surveillance strategies frequently rely on regular computed tomography (CT), but there is ongoing debate about the optimal intensity and individualization of post‑resection follow-up [6–8].

The PNI, calculated from serum albumin and total lymphocyte count using the Onodera formula, is an integrated marker of nutritional and immunological status [9]. Hypoalbuminemia reflects a combination of malnutrition and systemic inflammation, while lymphopenia indicates impaired cell‑mediated immunity; both have been associated with inferior outcomes across multiple cancers [10–12]. In NSCLC, lower preoperative PNI has been linked to higher rates of postoperative complications and worse overall survival (OS) and recurrence‑free survival (RFS) [10, 11]. Meta‑analyses suggest that low PNI is associated with increased risks of poor OS and postoperative complications in various malignancies, supporting its role as a robust prognostic biomarker [12].

However, most NSCLC studies have treated PNI as a static measure, often assessed at a single preoperative or early postoperative timepoint. Emerging work has begun to explore perioperative changes in PNI. Hayasaka and colleagues, for example, showed that the ratio of postoperative to preoperative PNI predicts long‑term survival after NSCLC surgery more accurately than either value alone [11, 13]. However, these studies still predominantly utilize only two timepoints, which may not fully capture the dynamic trajectory of immunonutritional recovery during the critical early postoperative period. Other inflammatory indices such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have also been explored, but they are also often assessed statically rather than longitudinally [14, 15].

In this single‑center, prospective study, we aimed to evaluate whether variability in PNI during the first 3 months after curative resection for pathologic stage I–III NSCLC is associated with 2‑year recurrence‑free survival from a 3‑month landmark. Specifically, we sought to determine whether early postoperative PNI trajectories, capturing both the magnitude of the initial decline and the extent of subsequent recovery, provide prognostic information beyond conventional clinicopathologic factors and preoperative PNI. We hypothesized that patients with persistently low or non‑recovering PNI patterns would have a higher risk of recurrence or death within 2 years, independent of stage and adjuvant therapy, and that incorporating 3‑month PNI variability into multivariable time‑to‑event models would improve discrimination for 2‑year RFS compared with models based solely on standard clinical variables.

Methods

Study design and setting

We performed a single-center, prospective, observational cohort study conducted in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University. We identified all adult patients who underwent curative-intent resection for pathologically confirmed NSCLC between June 2021 and June 2025. The protocol conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University. All participants provided written informed consent prior to any study procedures.

Patients and eligibility criteria

From the institutional surgical database, 141 patients who underwent anatomical lung resection for NSCLC during the study period were screened. We excluded patients with pathologic stage IV disease or palliative resections, those with R1/R2 margins or incomplete margin documentation, and those with missing baseline histology or laboratory data required to calculate PNI, leaving 119 patients in the overall analytic cohort. For the 3-month landmark analysis, we further excluded patients who experienced recurrence, died, or were lost to follow-up before 3 months after surgery, as well as those lacking 3-month (T3) PNI or with incomplete serial PNI measurements. Pathologic staging was performed according to the 8th edition TNM classification, and all included patients had R0 resections confirmed by pathology reports and review at a multidisciplinary tumor board. Patients who received neoadjuvant therapy were not excluded. Neoadjuvant treatment was recorded and examined in sensitivity analyses because it could influence the preoperative T0 PNI value.

Laboratory measurements and calculation of PNI

Preoperative and postoperative laboratory data were obtained from the electronic medical record. Serum albumin (g/dL) and complete blood counts, including total lymphocyte counts (/mm3), were measured using standard automated analyzers in the hospital’s central laboratory, which participates in routine internal and external quality control programs. To characterize early postoperative immunonutritional status, four time windows were defined a priori: T0 (preoperative), the closest measurement within 14 days before surgery; T1, 5–10 days after surgery or at discharge if earlier; T2, 3–6 weeks after surgery; and T3, 10–14 weeks after surgery. If multiple laboratory measurements were available within a window, we selected the value closest to the center of the window to reduce timing variability across patients. At each timepoint, the PNI was calculated using the conventional Onodera formula, PNI = 10 × serum albumin (g/dL) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm3) [9]. Patients were included in the landmark analysis only if albumin and lymphocyte data were available at all four timepoints, ensuring complete longitudinal PNI profiles from T0 through T3.

Definition of PNI dynamics and exposure groups

Early PNI dynamics were quantified in several complementary ways. For each patient, PNI was calculated at T0, T1, T2, and T3. To capture overall change in immunonutritional status across the early recovery period, we derived the percentage change from baseline to 3 months, ΔPNI% (T0-T3) = [(PNIT3 − PNIT0)/PNIT0] × 100. We also estimated an individual PNI slope by fitting a simple linear regression of PNI on time (in months) using all four timepoints and reported it as PNI units per month, and computed a within-patient PNI coefficient of variation (CV = standard deviation/mean PNI across T0–T3) as a measure of temporal variability.

For the primary analysis, we defined a pragmatic binary exposure, “PNI recovery status” at 3 months, based on prior NSCLC literature that commonly uses PNI values around 50 as indicative of adequate nutritional and immune status [11, 13, 15] and on clinical reasoning that modest declines from baseline may be acceptable during postoperative recovery. The threshold was prespecified before outcome modeling but was not externally validated. Patients were classified as having recovered PNI if their T3 PNI was ≥ 50 or if ΔPNI% between T0 and T3 was better than a 5% decline (ΔPNI% ≥ −5%), reflecting restoration or maintenance of baseline immunonutritional status. Patients who did not meet either criterion were classified as having non-recovered PNI. This binary PNI recovery variable (recovered versus non-recovered) was used as the main exposure in all primary survival analyses, while continuous measures such as ΔPNI%, PNI slope, and PNI CV were used in exploratory analyses. Alternative cutoffs and continuous ΔPNI% and slope models were evaluated as sensitivity analyses.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary endpoint was 2-year recurrence-free survival measured from the 3-month landmark. Recurrence was defined as radiologic or histologic evidence of new local, regional, or distant NSCLC after R0 resection, as documented in multidisciplinary meeting notes and imaging reports (including contrast-enhanced chest CT with or without positron emission tomography, brain imaging, and biopsy when clinically indicated). Death from any cause was also considered an event for the primary RFS endpoint. For the landmark cohort, time zero was defined as exactly 3 months after the date of surgery. Patients were followed from this landmark until the first occurrence of recurrence or death, or censoring at last clinical or imaging follow-up or at 24 months after the landmark, whichever came first. Clinical follow-up was performed according to institutional protocols aligned with contemporary international guidelines, typically including physical examination and chest CT at regular intervals during the first 2–3 years after resection. Secondary endpoints, analyzed descriptively, included overall survival from surgery, recurrence-only outcomes treating non-cancer deaths as competing events, and associations between PNI dynamics and major postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade II or higher within 30 days of surgery).

Covariates and clinical data

Baseline demographic, clinical, and pathological data were abstracted from electronic records and operative notes by two investigators using a standardized case report form, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Variables included age, sex, smoking history, body mass index (BMI), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, major comorbidities where available (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, or chronic kidney disease), histologic subtype, pathologic T and N classification and composite stage, and the type of surgical procedure and preoperative forced expiratory volume in 1 s as percent predicted (FEV1% predicted). Information on adjuvant therapy was collected, and adjuvant treatment was coded as a binary variable indicating receipt of any adjuvant chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or radiotherapy initiated within 4 months after surgery. The data of adjuvant therapy initiation was also recorded, including whether systemic therapy started before the T3 blood draw. Because this window slightly extends beyond the 3-month landmark, the adjuvant therapy variable should be interpreted as a general marker of postoperative systemic therapy rather than a pure baseline covariate. Major postoperative complications were recorded and graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification, and were dichotomized as grade II or higher versus none or grade I. Given the limited number of events, we prespecified three covariates for the primary multivariable Cox model (PNI recovery status at 3 months, pathologic stage (II–III versus I), and adjuvant therapy (yes versus no)), to maintain an acceptable events-per-parameter, based on anticipated 20–30% 2-year event rate. Major complications and additional clinical covariates were therefore evaluated in sensitivity models.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using complete-case data in R (version 4.4; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with two-sided p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Continuous variables were examined for distributional characteristics and are presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics of patients with recovered versus non-recovered PNI at 3 months in the landmark cohort were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. To account for multiple comparisons in descriptive and PNI-dynamic analyses, false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted q-values were computed using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure within each table.

The cumulative 2-year landmark RFS was estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between PNI recovery groups using the log-rank test. Associations between predictors and RFS were quantified using Cox proportional hazards regression. Univariable models were first fitted for each candidate predictor. The prespecified multivariable model included PNI recovery status, pathologic stage (II–III versus I), and adjuvant therapy (yes versus no). Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. For the multivariable model, FDR q-values were also calculated across the three covariates.

Model discrimination for predicting 2-year RFS from the 3-month landmark was evaluated using Harrell’s C-index with 95% CIs derived from 1,000 bootstrap resamples. We compared a clinical model including only stage and adjuvant therapy with an extended model adding PNI recovery status, reporting C-indices and the change in C-index (ΔC) with a bootstrap-based p-value. Optimism-corrected C-indices were obtained by internal validation with bootstrap resampling. Analyses involving continuous PNI metrics (ΔPNI%, slope, CV) were treated as exploratory. No automated stepwise variable selection procedures were applied in the primary modelling. Given the modest number of events, multivariable estimates were interpreted conservatively and additional sensitivity analyses were performed [16, 17].

Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate robustness of the primary association: (1) alternative PNI recovery definitions (T3 PNI ≥ 50 only, ΔPNI% ≥−5% only, and a less stringent definition of T3 PNI ≥ 48 or ΔPNI% ≥−10%); (2) continuous ΔPNI% and PNI slope; (3) addition of major postoperative complications to the primary model; (4) one-at-a-time adjustment for ECOG performance status, major comorbidity, FEV1% predicted, surgical extent, and neoadjuvant therapy; (5) exclusion of patients who received neoadjuvant therapy; (6) exclusion of patients who initiated adjuvant systemic therapy before the T3 measurement; (7) a simplified Cox model including PNI status and stage only; and (8) ridge-penalized Cox regression [18]. Stage-stratified exploratory analyses estimated the association between PNI recovery and RFS within stage I and stage II–III strata, and interaction was assessed by adding a PNI-by-stage term to the Cox model.

Results

Patient cohort and baseline characteristics

During the study period, 141 patients underwent curative-intent resection for pathologically confirmed NSCLC, of whom 119 met eligibility criteria after exclusion of patients with pathologic stage IV or palliative resections, incomplete margin documentation, or missing baseline histology or laboratory data (Fig. 1). Among these 119 patients, those who died, recurred, or were lost to follow-up before 3 months, as well as those lacking 3-month PNI measurements or complete serial PNI data, were excluded from the landmark analysis, leaving 94 patients who were alive, recurrence-free, and had complete PNI measurements at T0–T3 (Fig. 1).

Study flow diagram. Study flow diagram of patient selection and formation of the 3-month landmark cohort. Of 141 patients who underwent curative-intent resection for stage I–III NSCLC, 119 were eligible for outcome follow-up after exclusions. After removing patients with early events or incomplete 3-month PNI data, 94 patients formed the landmark cohort for analyses of PNI dynamics and 2-year recurrence-free survival

In the landmark cohort, the median age was 65 years (IQR 58–71) and 55 patients (58.5%) were male. Baseline clinical characteristics were broadly similar between patients with recovered and non-recovered PNI at 3 months, including age, sex, smoking history, BMI, ECOG performance status, histology, adjuvant therapy, and major postoperative complications, as well as major comorbidity, pulmonary function, surgical extent, and neoadjuvant therapy (Table 1). Pathologic stage I, II, and III disease accounted for 50 (53.2%), 26 (27.7%), and 18 (19.1%) cases, respectively, with no significant imbalance in stage distribution between PNI groups (global p = 0.63). Major comorbidities were present in 24 patients (25.5%), median FEV1% predicted was 82% (IQR 73–90), and 7 patients (7,4%) received neoadjuvant therapy. None differed significantly by PNI recovery status. Baseline PNI at T0 was moderately high overall (median 51.6, IQR 47.1–56.4) and did not differ significantly between recovered and non-recovered groups (median 52.5 vs. 50.3, p = 0.16, FDR-adjusted q = 0.26), indicating broadly comparable preoperative immunonutritional status (Table 1).

Early postoperative PNI dynamics and PNI recovery

Early postoperative PNI trajectories showed the expected pattern of an immediate postoperative decline followed by partial recovery, with clear differences according to 3-month PNI recovery status. In the landmark cohort as a whole, median PNI fell from 51.6 (IQR 47.1–56.4) at T0 to 45.0 (41.2–49.3) at T1, then increased to 47.2 (43.0–51.6) at T2 and 48.3 (43.9–52.7) at T3 (Table 2 and Fig. 2).

Among patients with recovered PNI (n = 51), median PNI decreased from 52.5 at T0 to 46.1 at T1 but subsequently rose to 49.5 at T2 and 52.8 at T3, exceeding the conventional threshold of 50 by 3 months. In contrast, patients with non-recovered PNI (n = 43) had a decline from 50.3 at T0 to 43.8 at T1, only modest improvement to 44.9 at T2, and persistently low PNI of 44.1 at T3. Between-group differences in PNI were negligible at T0 (p = 0.16) and modest at T1 (p = 0.08), but became statistically significant at T2 (median 49.5 vs. 44.9, p = 0.012, q = 0.021) and were most pronounced at T3 (median 52.8 vs. 44.1, p < 0.001, q < 0.001) (Table 2). The overall percentage change in PNI from baseline to 3 months was − 5.8% (IQR − 13.4 to 2.1), masking a small median decline of − 1.3% (− 6.7 to 4.9) in recovered patients versus a larger median decline of − 12.7% (− 19.5 to − 6.1) in non-recovered patients (p < 0.001, q < 0.001). Similarly, the median PNI slope over the four timepoints was − 0.4 units per month (IQR − 1.6 to 0.8) in the recovered group and − 1.9 units per month (− 3.2 to − 0.8) in the non-recovered group (p = 0.004, q = 0.009), and the within-patient PNI CV was higher among non-recovered patients (0.10 vs. 0.07, p = 0.018, q = 0.027), indicating that failure of PNI recovery reflects both lower absolute PNI values and greater temporal instability during the early postoperative period (Table 2 and Fig. 2).

Landmark 2-year recurrence-free survival by PNI status

Over a median follow-up of 38 months (IQR 31–46) from surgery, 27 RFS events (recurrence or death) occurred between the 3-month landmark and 24 months in the 94-patient landmark cohort, corresponding to a crude event rate of 28.7% (Table 1). The proportion of patients experiencing recurrence or death within 2 years of the landmark was more than twice as high in the non-recovered PNI group compared with the recovered group, with events in 18 of 43 patients (41.9%) versus 9 of 51 (17.6%), respectively (p = 0.011, FDR-adjusted q = 0.019) (Table 1). Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated clear separation in landmark 2-year RFS between the two groups (Fig. 3): the estimated 2-year RFS was approximately 82.3% in patients with recovered PNI and 59.2% in those with non-recovered PNI, with a statistically significant difference by log-rank testing (p = 0.011). Visual inspection of the survival curves suggested that divergence emerged within the first year after the landmark, indicating that non-recovery of PNI in the first 3 months after surgery is associated with both earlier and more frequent failure events.

Multivariable analysis and model performance

In univariable Cox regression, non-recovered PNI at 3 months was associated with a significantly increased risk of landmark 2-year RFS events compared with recovered PNI (HR 2.68, 95% CI 1.33–5.39, p = 0.006), and higher pathologic stage (II–III vs. I) was also associated with worse RFS (HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.06–4.21, p = 0.034), whereas receipt of adjuvant therapy was not significantly predictive (HR 1.42, 95% CI 0.73–2.77, p = 0.30) (Table 3). In the prespecified multivariable model adjusting simultaneously for PNI recovery status, pathologic stage, and adjuvant therapy, non-recovered PNI remained an independent predictor of poorer landmark 2-year RFS, with an adjusted HR of 2.34 (95% CI 1.12–4.90, p = 0.023; FDR-adjusted q = 0.034), while the association for stage II–III versus I became borderline (HR 1.88, 95% CI 0.93–3.81, p = 0.078; q = 0.093) and adjuvant therapy remained non-significant (HR 1.12, 95% CI 0.56–2.24, p = 0.75; q = 0.75) (Table 3). Proportional hazards assumptions were not violated for any covariate based on Schoenfeld residuals. The clinical Cox model including only stage and adjuvant therapy achieved a Harrell’s C-index of 0.66 (95% CI 0.58–0.74) with an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.65, whereas adding PNI recovery status improved the C-index to 0.73 (95% CI 0.65–0.80) with an optimism-corrected value of 0.71. The increase in discrimination (ΔC-index 0.07) was statistically significant (p = 0.037) (Table 4 and Fig. 4). These findings indicate that early failure to recover PNI within 3 months after curative resection provides additional prognostic information after adjustment for the prespecified clinical covariates for 2-year recurrence-free survival beyond standard clinicopathologic factors in this -cohort.

Sensitivity, treatment-timing, and stage-stratified analyses supported the direction of the primary association while underscoring the uncertainty expected with 27 events (Table 5 and Fig. 5). The association of non-recovered PNI with worse RFS was directionally consistent using alternative recovery definitions, including T3 PNI < 50 only (HR 2.08, 95% CI 1.01–4.31; p = 0.047) and ΔPNI% <−5% only (HR 2.41, 95% CI 1.13–5.13; p = 0.022), and when ΔPNI% was modeled continuously (per 10% higher ΔPNI%, HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.55–0.96; p = 0.026). After adding major postoperative complications to the primary Cox model, non-recovered PNI remained associated with worse RFS (adjusted HR 2.27, 95% CI 1.08–4.78; p = 0.030), whereas major complications were not significantly associated with RFS in this model (HR 1.26, 95% CI 0.56–2.86; p = 0.58).

Seven patients (7.4%) received neoadjuvant therapy, all with stage III disease. Adjustment for neoadjuvant therapy did not materially change the association between non-recovered PNI and RFS (HR 2.32, 95% CI 1.10–4.88; p = 0.027), and the estimate remained consistent after excluding these patients (HR 2.38, 95% CI 1.10–5.17; p = 0.028). Among 40 patients who received adjuvant therapy, 18 initiated systemic therapy before the T3 measurement. Time from surgery to adjuvant therapy initiation was not correlated with ΔPNI% (Spearman ρ = 0.12, p = 0.47), and excluding patients who started adjuvant systemic therapy before T3 yielded a consistent association for non-recovered PNI (HR 2.46, 95% CI 1.05–5.75; p = 0.038). Stage-stratified analyses showed a similar direction of association in stage I (HR 2.66, 95% CI 0.70–10.10) and stage II–III disease (HR 2.05, 95% CI 0.75–5.62), with no evidence of interaction by stage (p-interaction = 0.78). The simplified Cox model including only PNI status and stage (HR 2.43, 95% CI 1.17–5.06; p = 0.018) and ridge-penalized Cox model (HR 2.12, 95% CI 1.07–4.19; p = 0.032) were also directionally consistent.

Discussion

In this - single-center cohort of 94 patients with stage I–III non-small cell lung cancer who were alive and recurrence-free 3 months after curative resection, early postoperative PNI trajectories over the first 3 months clearly clustered into distinct patterns, which we operationalized as “recovered” and “non-recovered” PNI status at 3 months. Although baseline PNI was broadly similar, patients in the non-recovered group showed a deeper initial postoperative decline, slower or absent recovery, and greater within-patient variability of PNI than those whose PNI recovered. These dynamic differences translated into clinically meaningful separation in landmark 2-year recurrence-free survival. In multivariable Cox analysis adjusting for pathologic stage and adjuvant therapy, non-recovered PNI at 3 months remained associated after adjustment with approximately a 2.3-fold higher risk of recurrence or death, while stage and adjuvant treatment showed weaker or non-significant effects. Incorporating 3-month PNI recovery status into a clinical model based on stage and adjuvant therapy improved discrimination for 2-year RFS, indicating that early PNI variability provides additional prognostic information beyond conventional clinicopathologic factors.

Our findings extend and refine the existing literature on PNI in NSCLC. Several retrospective studies and meta-analyses have shown that low pre-treatment PNI is associated with higher postoperative complications and poorer survival in lung cancer [19–21]. In our resected stage I–III NSCLC patients, we similarly found that patients with non-recovered PNI had substantially worse 2-year RFS than those with recovered PNI (59.2% vs. 82.3%) and an adjusted HR of 2.34 for recurrence or death, even after controlling for pathologic stage and adjuvant therapy. This aligns closely with prior report demonstrating that a lower perioperative PNI ratio (post-/pre-operative) independently predicted worse long-term survival after NSCLC surgery [13]. Beyond the surgical setting, meta-analyses in advanced lung cancer treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors demonstrate that low baseline PNI or failure to maintain/improve PNI during therapy predicts shorter progression-free and overall survival [22, 23]. Longitudinal work in nasopharyngeal carcinoma similarly shows that multi-visit PNI trajectories, modelled jointly with survival, carry independent prognostic information [24]. What is new in our study is the use of four early peri- and postoperative PNI measurements over the first 3 months, rather than a single pre/post ratio, to capture both the depth and speed of immunonutritional recovery. The focus on 2-year RFS as a clinically tangible, early outcome that matches the peak recurrence window after resection, and the demonstration, in a - single-center cohort, that dynamic risk stratification can be achieved using only routine albumin and lymphocyte values.

The biological plausibility of our results is grounded in the dual components of the PNI and their links to inflammation, nutrition, and anti-tumor immunity. Onodera originally conceptualized PNI as a composite of serum albumin and lymphocyte count to reflect both nutritional reserve and cell-mediated immunity in gastrointestinal cancer surgery, and subsequent observational studies have confirmed that low PNI is associated with worse outcomes across multiple cancer types, including lung cancer [9, 20, 21]. Albumin is now understood to be strongly influenced by systemic inflammation as well as caloric/protein intake. A large prospective cohort showed that hypoalbuminemia, elevated inflammatory markers, and high nutritional risk independently predicted short-term mortality [25]. On the immunologic side, lymphopenia has repeatedly been linked to inferior outcomes in advanced NSCLC patients receiving ICIs and chemotherapy, consistent with impaired adaptive immunity and reduced tumor immune surveillance [22, 23]. Longitudinal work in head and neck cancer has further shown that patients with persistently low or deteriorating PNI are at higher risk of severe radiation-induced oral mucositis, suggesting that dynamic PNI patterns reflect the ability to withstand treatment-related stress and maintain tissue repair [26]. In resected NSCLC, most recurrences cluster in the first 2–3 years after surgery, implying that micro-metastatic disease is often present at resection and subsequently constrained or permitted to grow by host factors. Our finding that non-recovered PNI is characterized by persistently depressed PNI values, steeper negative PNI slopes, and greater intra-patient variability is therefore compatible with a phenotype of sustained inflammation, ongoing catabolism, and impaired immune competence, whereas early PNI recovery likely reflects restoration of nutritional reserves and effective immune surveillance that can restrain micro-metastatic outgrowth.

Clinically, our data suggest that early PNI trajectories may eventually complement into existing guideline-based follow-up strategies for resected NSCLC. ESMO and NCCN guidelines for early and locally advanced NSCLC recommend curative-intent surgery when feasible, followed by regular CT-based surveillance—typically every 6–12 months for the first 2–3 years—because recurrence risk is highest in this interval [27, 28]. Observational work on surveillance imaging suggests that “one-size-fits-all” follow-up may not be optimal, with some patients being over-imaged and others potentially under-monitored [29, 30]. Our results indicate that patients with non-recovered PNI at 3 months form a clearly identifiable high-risk subgroup—nearly doubling the 2-year RFS event rate—who might justifiably receive intensified surveillance (shorter CT intervals or lower thresholds for PET/CT) within the same overall time window, while PNI-recovered patients could remain on standard schedules. Because PNI is potentially modifiable, dynamic PNI could also serve as a trigger for targeted nutritional, rehabilitative, or immunonutritional interventions. Randomized trials and systematic reviews in lung and gastrointestinal cancer surgery show that perioperative immunonutrition and structured nutrition support can improve inflammatory and immune parameters [31–33]. In the future, 3-month PNI trajectories might therefore be combined with pathologic stage, molecular risk markers and patient preferences to inform shared decision-making about the intensity of adjuvant systemic therapy, the need for closer toxicity monitoring, and the allocation of limited supportive-care resources toward those resected NSCLC patients whose early postoperative course signals a failure to recover immunonutritional fitness.

Nonetheless, important limitations must be acknowledged. This was a single-center study with a modest overall sample, which limits statistical power and external generalizability and constrained the number of covariates that could be included in multivariable models without violating events-per-parameter principles. The 3-month landmark design also excluded patients who recurred, died, or were lost to follow-up before 3 months; therefore, the findings apply to patients alive and recurrence-free at the landmark and should not be extrapolated to early postoperative failures. Although the primary model was prespecified, the cohort had only 27 RFS events (approximately 9 events per primary covariate), creating a meaningful risk of model overfitting despite bootstrap optimism correction and simplified and ridge-penalized sensitivity analyses [16–18]. Residual confounding by comorbidities, lung function, performance status, surgical extent, complications, neoadjuvant therapy, and adjuvant therapy timing cannot be fully excluded because these factors could not all be included simultaneously in a stable multivariable model. Finally, our PNI trajectory definitions and cut-offs, as well as the prognostic performance of the models, have not yet been externally validated and should be confirmed and refined in larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts before being used to guide clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, this study indicates that early variability in the prognostic nutritional index during the first 3 months after curative resection for stage I–III non-small cell lung cancer is a promising, low-cost biomarker of 2-year recurrence risk. Given that PNI relies on routine serum albumin and lymphocyte counts, it is readily implementable in daily practice and may provide a pragmatic first step toward dynamic, host-based risk stratification after surgery. Because this was a single-center landmark cohort with limited events and unvalidated cutoffs, PNI recovery should currently be viewed as a candidate risk marker. However, future work should focus on prospective, multi-center validation of PNI trajectories, standardization of cut-offs and timepoints, and integration of dynamic PNI with emerging biomarkers such as radiomic features, circulating tumor DNA and genomic or immunologic signatures to build more refined, multi-dimensional prognostic models that can guide personalized surveillance and adjuvant treatment strategies in resected NSCLC.
