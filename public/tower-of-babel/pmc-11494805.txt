Global burden of ischemic stroke attributable to high body mass index in 204 countries and territories, 1990–2021
Authors: Xiucai Guo, Ziping Zhang, Xueyan Yin, QirongXu, Feng Li, Feng Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA high body mass index (hBMI) is one of the major risk factors for ischemic stroke (IS), although independent estimates of the global burden and trend of IS attributable to hBMI are unclear.MethodsStudy data were extracted from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study and analysed by age, sex, year, and geographical location. The sociodemographic index (SDI) and the human development index (HDI) were used as indicators of national socioeconomic status. Geographic distributions and trends were assessed by calculating the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC). Associations between the age-standardized death rate (ASMR) or disability-adjusted life year rate (DALY) and socioeconomic status were analysed.ResultsDeaths and DALYs increased worldwide from 1990–2021, with increases of 95.74% for the former and 133.00% for the latter; the ASMR and ASDR decreased, with an ASMR and ASDR-related EAPCs of -1.10 (95% CI: -1.24–-0.96) and 0.04 (95% CI:-0.47–0.55), respectively; women had greater numbers of deaths and DALYs, and the majority of deaths and DALYs were shared by those aged ≥ 70. The highest burden rates were shared by Eastern Europe, North Africa, the Middle East, and Central Asia. The ASMR-related EAPCs were associated with the ASMR in 1990 (R = -0.35, P < 0.001) and the SDI in 2021 in different countries (R = -0.66, P < 0.001); these patterns were similar to those of the ASDR; the HDI in 2021 was associated with the ASMR-related EAPC (R = -0.71, P < 0.001) and the ASDR-related EAPC in different countries (R = -0.71, P < 0.001).ConclusionsThe number of deaths and DALYs from ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI worldwide increased substantially from 1990–2021. Successful population-wide initiatives targeting hBMI may mitigate a wide range of burdens on this disease. Taking into account variations in the SDI burden, future prevention and control strategies must be developed and implemented according to country-specific development status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04259-2.

Background

Stroke is one of the most common debilitating diseases in adults [1, 2], the third leading cause of death and disability in populations of all ages (following the mitigation of the impact attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic), and the second leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in populations aged ≥ 50 years according to the global burden of disease (GBD) 2021 [3]. Ischemic stroke (IS) is the most common subtype [4] that occurs due to brain ischemia as a result of cerebral blood vessel thrombosis[5]; its incidence (7.80 million [6.72–8.94]) accounts for 65.33% of all incident strokes, and ischemic stroke-related deaths (3.59 million [3.21–3.89]) accounted for 49.52% of stroke deaths in 2021[6].

A high body mass index (hBMI) was responsible for 128.52 million (95% UI 55.99—202.39) DALY and 3.71 million (1.85–5.66) deaths, with the sixth leading level 3 risk factor for DALY in 2021 [7]. HBMI, a modifiable determinant of vascular health, is implicated in the etiology of vascular diseases and potentially has a bidirectional relationship with physical inactivity, as delineated in Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) database research [8]; moreover, it has been shown to increase morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, including ischemic stroke [9–12], where hBMI was the fifth specific risk factor contributing to ischemic stroke-related DALYs in 2019 and is associated with increased recurrence [13]. Additionally, it indirectly increases the risk of ischemic stroke by inducing hyperlipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, and other diseases [14].

HBMI is typically used to define overweight and obesity in epidemiological studies and has been labelled a pandemic because of its continued growth worldwide for more than two decades. The overweight or obese population has doubled since 1980, reaching approximately one-third in recent years, with an estimated three billion individuals affected in 2019 [15], and more than five million deaths due to various causes of obesity among adults aged ≥ 20 years.

Previous studies reported that hBMI was associated with a variety of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes, although no studies have focused on deaths and DALYs, especially their estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs), of ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI. Therefore, it is critical to elucidate the burden of ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI and to provide timely health surveillance and disease control for high-risk populations in individuals and society around the world. Here, we use GBD 2019 to systematically assess the deaths and DALYs of ischemic stroke attributable to the hBMI within different regions, ages, and genders, as well as the SDI of 204 countries and territories from 1990–2019, which should contribute to the management of human health, as well as the prevention and control of ischemic stroke attributable to the hBMI.

Methods

Data source and study sample

GBD 2021 provides a systematic scientific estimate of publicly available, published, and contributed data with improved method performance and standardization of the prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALY of 371 injuries and diseases for 204 countries and territories from 1990–2021 by age, sex, and country [3]. We focused on ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI and extracted data on deaths and DALYs from the GBD 2021 (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results) [16].

Disease identification

BMI, a commonly used tool to assess the weight-to-height ratio, has gained wide acceptance and application. Its calculation formula and classification standards are simple and straightforward to apply in large-scale population studies. BMI has been shown to be moderately correlated with more direct measurements of body fat obtained through methods such as skin fold thickness measurements, bioimpedance, and densitometry (underwater weighing), and it is also associated with various metabolic diseases and health conditions. On the basis of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD), GBD 2021, and the Cause List Mapped to ICD codes, ischemic stroke was identified as code 8B11 (ICD10: I63). IS occurs as a result of brain ischemia as a result of thrombosis of a cerebral blood vessel, which can be described as acute focal neurological dysfunction caused by focal infarction at a single or multiple sites in the brain. Evidence of acute infarction may come either from a) a duration of symptoms lasting more than 24 h or b) neuroimaging or other techniques in the clinically relevant area of the brain.

High body mass index

In the GBD 2021, criteria for hBMI were defined by mixed-effects modelling and data adjustment according to country, year, age, and sex. In general, an hBMI for adults (aged 20 +) was defined as a BMI greater than 25 kg/m2 using standard cut-off points for BMI to define overweight (BMI 25–29 kg/m2) and obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) among adults or the International Obesity Task Force standard to define overweight and obesity among children [17–19].

Disease burden measurement

Disease burdens were assessed with a range of indicators, including deaths, DALYs, and their age-standardized rates (ASRs; age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR)). The ASR was calculated with nonweighted means of the age-specific proportional distributions of the GBD year for national locations with populations greater than five million in the GBD year to update the age standard of the world population. In brief, several parameters were used, including a standardized rate (ai, where i is the ith age class), a number (or weight) of people (wi) in the same age subgroup i (a reference of the standard population), and a dividend of adding the standard population weight [20]: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ASR\:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^Aa_iw_i}{\sum_{i=1}^Aa_i}\times100,000$$\end{document}ASR=∑i=1Aaiwi∑i=1Aai×100,000. The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), which is widely used to describe the trends of ASRs, was calculated as follows: y = α + βx + ε; EAPC = 100 × [exp (β) − 1]. where y is equal to the natural logarithm of ASR, x corresponds to the calendar year, β represents the annual change in ln (age-standardized rate), and a 95% confidence interval (CI) is also derived from the model. The EAPC and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated via a linear regression model. ASRs would be upwards, downwards, or stable if the EAPCs with 95% CIs were > 0, < 0, or = 0, respectively. The positive EAPC here represents an increasing trend, and the negative EAPC represents a decreasing trend.

The sociodemographic index (SDI) is composed of average educational attainment in the population aged ≥ 15 years, a total fertility rate of less than 25 years, and income distributed lag per capita [18, 21–23]. The SDI is a composite indicator indicating that development status is strongly related to health outcomes. All regions and countries were divided into five SDIs: high, high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low. The Human Development Index (HDI) is a summary measurement of average achievements in key dimensions of human development, including a long and healthy life, knowledge, and a decent standard of living from the United Nations Development Program (http://hdr.undp.org/en/data).

Statistical analysis

Deaths, DALY, ASMR, and ASDR were used to quantify disease burdens at the global, regional, and national levels. The regions and territories were also divided into 21 groups by region and 16 groups by age. All values for each metric were represented as uncertainty intervals (UIs) via the 25th− and 975th-order 1,000 draw values of the posterior distribution. The relationships between the SDI and ASR from 1990–2021 were evaluated. Given the social development and health outcomes of the SDI in 2021 and the baseline level of disease burden of the ASR in 1990, the HDI in 2021 could reflect a national condition in all sources of health. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to estimate the associations of the EAPC with the SDI in 2021, the ASR in 1990, and the HDI in 2021. All analyses in this study were performed via R software (R core team, version 4.2.1; Vienna, Austria), and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Discussion

On the basis of GBD 2021, to estimate spatiotemporal trends in deaths and DALYs of ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI worldwide from 1990–2021, we systematically and comprehensively present the global burden and its fraction due to inadequate control of hBMI by different SDIs and geographic regions, sex, and age. We found increases of 1.96 times for deaths and 2.33 times for DALYs but decreases in the ASMR and ASDR worldwide; additionally, global burdens varied considerably, with higher rates in Eastern Europe, North Africa and the Middle East, Central Asia, Central Europe, and southern sub-Saharan Africa. Our results also revealed an M-shaped association of the SDI with ischemic stroke burden attributable to hBMI in 2021 (especially with a decreasing trend in high-SDI), a decline and a significant association of the ASMR-related EAPC with the HDI in 2021 in different countries, and a significant negative association of the ASDR-related EAPC with HDIs in 2021 in different countries; the ASMR and ASDR in 1990–2021 decreased in most countries and territories, such as Luxembourg, Estonia, Portugal, Slovenia, and Czechia, in addition to increasing in Equatorial Guinea, Viet Nam, Mozambique, Indonesia, and Bangladesh.

A high body mass index is well known as one of the leading risk factors for stroke. Previous studies have described the overall burden of stroke over 32 years [3]. Excess body fat and hBMI have been implicated as important causes of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease [24]. Multiple studies have reported an association between increased BMI and the risk of stroke [25, 26] and a stronger association between BMI and the risk of ischemic stroke [12, 27–29]. BMI was a risk factor for both hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke in a Danish cohort study [30]. This long-term trend reflects a close relationship between BMI and hypertension and between BMI and the occurrence of stroke. Mechanistically, obesity, such as smoking and a history of atrial fibrillation, is one of the important factors affecting metabolic abnormalities; the most intuitive factors are increased blood pressure and diabetes, two recognized causal factors for arteriosclerosis of large and small blood vessels [31]. Along with metabolic derangements, obesity is associated with a significant and prolonged increase in inflammatory oxidative stress, which is damaging to the endothelium, shifting it towards a prothrombotic state. Platelet reactivity, improved coagulation, and impaired fibrinolysis are other recognized mechanisms [32].

Most studies indicate [33, 34] that the fatality rate is greater in women with stroke than in men with stroke and that the functional outcome is better in men. We observed similar conditions in terms of the global burdens of ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI, with more deaths, DALYs, and age-standardized death rates in women from 1990–2021. Men contributed more to deaths and DALYs in those aged < 70 years, whereas women contributed significantly more to those aged ≥ 70 years owing to a greater life expectancy among women. The other reason may be that women pay more attention to body management and experience more dissatisfaction with their body weight and shape than men do. The pressures to thin are present in early adolescence, as is noted in the dieting behavior of very young girls. They manifest greater trust in healthy nutrition and greater involvement in the control of body weight [35], which may be one of the reasons why women are, on average, several years older than men when they suffer their first ischaemic strokes[36].

Sociocultural and psychological factors can be important in the etiology of both eating disorders and hBMI [37], which are much more prevalent in women than in men. Men in particular should be informed and educated about healthy diets [35]. As data reveal sex-related differences in ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI, health policies need to be adapted accordingly to improve prevention and prestroke health in men and women.

A comprehensive evaluation revealed that the global ischemic stroke battlefield situation attributable to the hBMI remains severe. The burdens of ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI from 1990–2021 varied throughout the world, and the absolute numbers of deaths and DALYs increased, with increasing trends in high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low SDIs and slight decreases in high SDIs. The aging population and population growth may be the main reasons. Furthermore, age-standardized rates of death and DALYs related to ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI have decreased significantly in countries with high SDI and high–middle SDI but are increasing in countries with low SDI, low–middle SDI, and middle SDI. High-income countries have advanced technology for ischemic stroke treatment and focus earlier on weight and health management [38]. In middle-income and low-income countries, the lifestyle of people needs to be improved, and compliance with healthy eating and physical exercise is quite poor, which can increase their hBMI. It is well known that the hBMI is related primarily to the lifestyle and dietary habits of people [39]; more changes in recreational and cultural values are accompanied by economic development among those with lower SDIs[40]. Junk food began infiltrating their lives, prompting increased expenditures on items such as sugary drinks, fast food burgers, and fried snacks, as well as episodes of binge eating on pizza and desserts. These behaviors ultimately contribute to the widespread occurrence of obesity among individuals. Even worse, many patients cannot afford basic primary or secondary preventive drugs for ischemic stroke, and the burden of ischemic stroke is increasing in these countries. To address this situation, we need to develop targeted prevention and treatment strategies to reduce the prevalence of risk factors for hBMI and improve the accessibility and affordability of medical services for the prevention and treatment of ischemic stroke [41].

However, the study also has several limitations that should be considered when the results are interpreted. First, the GBD approach hinges on diverse assumptions and modelling strategies, yet discrepancies in the accessibility of fundamental epidemiological information persist as a constraint and a factor contributing to variability in GBD assessments, potentially introducing uncertainty into the projections. Despite the application of stringent statistical techniques to mitigate these uncertainties in GBD studies, the outcomes should be viewed as the most reliable estimates derived from current evidence. Second, the precision of these estimates can be influenced by the quality and accessibility of data across different countries and regions. Notably, in certain low- and middle-income countries, the scarcity of dependable epidemiological data and inadequate reporting of ischemic stroke cases may result in an underestimation of the actual disease burden. Third, this study exclusively delineated and examined the disease burden of ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI, neglecting the analysis of other stroke subtypes. Our endeavors will subsequently encompass the assessment of the global burden of additional stroke subtypes linked to hBMI, thereby capturing the holistic impact of hBMI on stroke. Furthermore, the current analysis relies on historical data. To gain insights into this topic in the future, we will employ suitable disease prediction models to forecast the global burden of ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI, aiming to provide a more extensive foundation for preventive strategies.

We observed the burdens of ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI in different ethnic and racial groups, geographic regions, and countries with different income levels. Advancing knowledge of ethnic and regional variation in causes and outcomes will be beneficial in the implementation of regional healthcare services.

Conclusions

Ischaemic stroke attributable to the hBMI remains one of the main public health problems worldwide. Age-standardized deaths and DALYs have decreased significantly in countries in the high-SDI quintile, although their increasing rates were still observed in countries with relatively low SDIs. Given the significant geographic variation in the burden and underlying incidence of ischemic stroke attributable to hBMI across regions and countries, more specific strategies aimed at preventing hBMI and improving medical care for patients with ischemic stroke are warranted to reduce the future burden of this condition.
