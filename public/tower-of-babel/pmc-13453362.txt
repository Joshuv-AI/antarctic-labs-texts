Exploring Psychosocial Stressors, Exam Performance, and Grit Among Preclinical Medical Students
Authors: Richard J Iwuagwu, Madison F Clark, Ryan R Landoll, Christopher Jonas, Kade Thornton, Dario Torre, Steven J Durning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMedical students encounter high levels of stress linked to poor academic performance. Institutional resources, such as faculty mentors and academic advisors play a critical role in enhancing students’ performance and well-being. Despite this, there is a dearth of research incorporating academic advising notes to explore the interplay between medical students academic and psychosocial functioning. Guided by Cognitive Appraisal Theory, this study examined the impact of psychosocial stressors on exam performance and coping potential among medical students.Materials and MethodsUtilizing an embedded mixed-methods design, researchers analyzed National Board of Medical Examiners (NBME) scores, grit scores, and advising notes of 60 pre-clerkship students at an allopathic medical school. Advising notes were thematically coded to identify psychosocial stressors. Independent sample t-tests were used to compare average exam performance (NBME scores) and coping potential (Grit scores) between students who did and did not report psychosocial stressors in academic advising.ResultsThematic analysis identified four primary stressor domains: physical health, mental health, relational, and environmental. There was an absence of evidence for differences in NBME scores (t (58) = −1.06, p =0.293, 95% CI [−5.29, 1.63]) or Grit scores (t (58) = −0.942, p =0.35, 95% CI [−0.45, 0.16]) between students who did or did not report a psychosocial stressor.ConclusionThese consistent findings call into question the assumption that personal stressors universally diminish academic achievement, suggesting that institutional support and high individual grit may act possibly provide a buffering role. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to further examine these potential effects. This could include specific components of institutional support systems that may help students mitigate the effects of personal challenges on their academic trajectories.

Introduction

Medical students are often reported to have high rates of stress1–3 and burnout,3–5 with medical students reporting higher rates of stress found to be higher in medical students than the general population2 and graduate students in other disciplines.2,5 While stress manifests as a physical and psychological reaction to life demands, or stressors, unchecked stress among medical students can culminate in burnout: a state of emotional exhaustion and cynicism resulting from chronic, unmanaged academic or workplace stressors.3

The high degree of stress and burnout in medical students is reported to be due to a variety of psychosocial stressors in the academic and personal domains.3 In the academic domain, Medical students report psychosocial stressors such as workload or volume of information,2,5,6 performance pressure,6 work–life balance,6,7 peer relationships,5,6 and concerns with career planning and/or choices.5,6 When assessing stressors that may originate from their personal lives, medical students report stressors such as financial issues,2,5,6 intimate relationships,2,6 and major life events (eg, personal or family illness or injury, death of a family member, marriage, birth of children).2 Many of these professional and personal psychosocial stressors have also been reported as risk factors for depression in medical students.7

While some types or levels of stress can be motivating for medical students,1,3 unfavorable stress has been associated with poor academic performance1,8 and may impact professional development.2 Specifically, stress can lead to problems that can affect professional or academic performance, such as dishonesty, substance and/or alcohol abuse, a decrease in empathy and altruism,2 and may influence medical school attrition.2 In a student’s personal life, stress may additionally contribute to issues in relationships, poor physical health and self-care, substance/alcohol abuse, and suicide.2 The same effects have been reported for burnout in addition to a decrease in knowledge, cognitive performance, and personal accountability.4

Cognitive Appraisal Theory posits that the experience of stress derives from a primary appraisal of a threat followed by a secondary appraisal of poor coping potential.9 In academic medicine, while academic advising is a mechanism that can inherently record the primary threats (stressors) facing a student and provide novel tools for externalized coping, it does not fully account for the student’s native internal buffering mechanisms. Conversely, individuals can mitigate their experience of stress when they possess a high coping potential. This theory can explain why grit—a dedication and passion for long-term goals—has been found to act as a buffer to stress and burnout.10–13 Past research in medical student populations has examined the relationship between grit and academic performance,14,15 an intervention to increase grit,16 and grit’s role as a protective factor against burnout.17 However, an evaluation of academic performance and grit when psychosocial stressors are reported has not been conducted in medical student population.

In addition to cultivating grit, institutional support structures offer critical support for medical students. For medical students in their formative academic years, academic advising represents an essential resource to help navigate the demands of the preclinical curriculum.18 Because students facing difficulties often require multi-faceted intervention, academic advisors play a pivotal role in guiding them through overlapping personal and professional challenges.19

Although recent mixed-methods studies examined academic and psychosocial data for medical students,20–22 several important gaps remain in the literature. First, these studies were conducted with medical student samples outside of the United States, did not incorporate a measure for grit, and did not focus exclusively on the preclinical phase of medical education.20–22 Additionally, none of these studies utilized advising notes as a source of qualitative data. This omission represents a missed opportunity, as advisors and mentors can provide valuable insight into factors affecting students’ academic performance and well-being.23,24

The present study addressed these research gaps by exploring medical students’ psychosocial stressors using advising notes, measuring grit, and focusing on a preclinical student population. Specifically, this study sought to utilize academic advising documentation to identify psychosocial stressors experienced by medical students and their impact on exam performance and coping potential (grit). Research has identified negative associations between stress and academic performance1,8 along with the mitigating effects of grit on the stress and performance relationship.10–13 Therefore, it was hypothesized that there would be significant differences in both average exam scores and grit scores; specifically, that students without reported psychosocial stressors would outperform their peers academically, while demonstrating higher overall grit scores as an index of greater coping.

Materials and Methods

Design, Setting and Sample

This study was a part of a larger study on understanding both cognitive and non-cognitive factors influencing medical student performance25 that used data from the Long-term Career Outcome Study conducted at the F. Edward Hébert School of Medicine of the Uniformed Services University of the Health Sciences (USUHS). The current study focused specifically on understanding the role of grit and psychosocial life events along with support on the performance of students. Consistent with embedded mixed methods design, quantitative and qualitative data was collected simultaneously and integrated during data analysis to comprehensively answer research questions.26 This research was determined to be exempt from Institutional Review Board review and was reviewed and approved by the Human Research Protections Program Office (USUHS.2020–041).

USUHS is a Liaison Committee on Medical Education accredited allopathic medical school with structure and curricular governance similar to other medical schools.27 USUHS is a unique medical school as all medical students are commissioned officers of the United States military. However, most students have no prior military experience and have similar demographics and standardized exam (ie, GPA, MCAT) scores to other students at allopathic medical schools.28 Approximately 170 medical students matriculate at USUHS annually.

Between 2020–2022, participants were recruited during their first and second years of medical school at USUHS. Given the annual initial matriculation rate above, this resulted in approximately 500 eligible participants. Recruitment was conducted through Email advertisements to listservs, posted flyers in student common areas, and informal informational briefings given following orientation activities by student volunteers. Sixty students (approximately 12%) expressed interest, met inclusion criteria as enrolled students and ultimately enrolled in the study, which is generally consistent with passive recruitment strategies in behavioral research such as this. Advising and performance data for participants was analyzed for the class of 2024 and 2025. All participants were engaged in an informed consent process with a member of the research team and gave consent prior to being administered study procedures.

Data Collection and Measures

Study Measures

Grit

The Grit Scale assesses perseverance and passion for long-term goals and captures two key domains: Consistency of Interest (ie, whether there is focused area of interest and if it is sustained over time) and Perseverance of Effort (ie, whether effort will be sustained even if it is difficult or taxing).29,30 The 12-item (most commonly used) Grit Scale rates each item on a 5-point Likert scale. It has strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α >0.80) for both subscales29 and high test–retest reliability, with coefficients from .68 to .85.29 It has correlations with self-control, conscientiousness, and academic performance.29,30 Moreover, it has been shown to predict success in challenging domains that include education and military training.29,30

Pre-Clerkship Performance Measures

Pre-clerkship academic performance on written exams was assessed using midterm and final exams scores. Exams were administered on seven integrated organ-based systems modules—each approximately eight to nine weeks in duration—across a 16-month pre-clerkship curriculum. Each module included a midterm and final exam for a total of 14 exams. Exams used the NBME customized assessment services (CAS),31 with each exam consisting of 80 to 100 questions selected by the faculty from the CAS’s question pool. An individual student’s performance across these exams was averaged to measure their overall performance for each module in the pre-clerkship curriculum.

Psychosocial Stressors

De-identified advising notes were analyzed for use of advising resources as well as an understanding of life stressors. These notes are kept as contemporaneous working files for the Office for Student Affairs Advising Deans, along with the Director of Academic Success regarding interactions with students. These notes are generally created following key advising interactions with students and are used to guide advising recommendations over time. Psychosocial stressors identified in the notes were operationally defined as challenging circumstances triggering behavioral and physiological readjustment.32,33

Data Analysis

To identify psychosocial stressors experienced by medical students who seek support, qualitative data from advising notes documentation were analyzed following Braun and Clark’s thematic analysis framework.34 A coding structure was developed by the authors and used to code advising notes. Two authors double‑coded one third of the data until satisfactory reliability was reached before single coding the remainder of the data, as recommended by Campbell et al.35 Coders were blinded to other study data and met iteratively to resolve discrepancies through consensus with 100% agreement. For participants that endorsed psychosocial stress, each specific stressor was listed within the study’s codebook along with the date of advising notes. Coded data was grouped into higher-order themes, revised, and then defined into the final study themes. Participant endorsements (or lack) of experiencing psychosocial stressors were also coded into a binary variable for use in quantitative analyses.

NBME and Grit scores were analyzed to determine the impact of psychosocial stress on performance and coping potential. Using SPSS, independent sample t-tests were conducted to compare mean scores between students who did and did not report psychosocial stressors. Specifically, average NBME scores were compared to assess objective exam performance and grit scores were compared to evaluate differences in student resilience.

Physical Health

Several participants noted various conditions affecting their own health. A few participants mentioned dealing with a coronavirus diagnosis during pre-clerkship. One academic advisor described how a participant (male, 35–39) “developed [gastrointestinal issue] the week of MSK [Musculoskeletal] midterms and unable to prepare [and] perform optimally”. Other participants reported concerns pertaining to sleep problems, gaining weight, or having a sinus infection.

Mental Health

In addition to physical health concerns, behavioral health conditions were also reported. For example, one advising meeting journal entry described a participant’s (Female, 20–24) enrollment in a behavioral health program and stated that she “felt her previous [academic] challenges were heavily influenced by her behavioral health care”. Other participants reported symptoms related to depression and post-traumatic stress disorder. Two participants described experiences of anxiety over exam performance.

Relational

In addition to intrapersonal stressors, participants experienced various stressors pertaining to family members or relationship concerns. Family stressors ranged from issues such as childcare, a spouse’s c-section, and a death of a spouse’s family member. Three participants discussed ongoing health concerns within their family. With respect to relationships, participants described hardships related to being in a long-distance relationship, marital separation, a recent divorce, a “break up”, and general relationship difficulties.

Environmental

Two participants endorsed psychosocial stressors that originated from their environment rather than a specific physical, mental or relational concern. For example, one advisor noted about a participant’s experience with a racial incident. The other participant’s stressor pertained to military life, which was the only stressor identified related to military service. Specifically, the advisor commented that the participant was concerned about passing the Army physical fitness test.

Exam Performance

On average, participants who endorsed psychosocial stressors achieved a lower average NBME score (M = 79.66, SD = 7.47) compared to participants who did not endorse psychosocial stressors (M = 81.49, SD = 5.73). Levene’s test indicated equal variances, F(1,58) = 2.90, p =0.094. Consequently, a standard independent samples t-test was utilized, revealing an absence of statistical evidence for a difference, t(58) = −1.06, p =0.293, 95% CI [−5.29, 1.63]. Discussion

This study provides the first comparative evaluation of academic performance and grit by psychosocial stressor groups in an undergraduate medical school context.

Thematic analysis revealed that the psychosocial stressors experienced by this sample of medical students were generally characterized by four domains: physical health, mental health, relational, and environmental. The psychosocial stressors reported in this study align well within the existing literature medical student stressors including performance pressure, family illness, relationship problems, and depression.2–5 While these psychosocial stressor domains are common, it is important to note that the participants pre-clerkship studies took place during the height of the coronavirus outbreak. The reports of relationship difficulties in this sample are consistent with research findings that the relationships experienced declines in relationship satisfaction,36,37 more conflict,37 and declines in romantic functioning38 during pandemic periods. Similarly, the mental health challenges reported by participants may also be exacerbated by the pandemic as shown in the literature.39–41

Despite these stressors, the average NBME scores of students who reported experiencing psychosocial stressors were statistically on par with students who did not report stressors. This contrasts with our hypothesis and prior research indicating a relationship between stress and poor academic performance.1,8 A possible explanation for this is the nature of the participants being students who have actively sought help from the university’s academic advising services. Research has shown that academic help-seeking behaviors promote academic growth and tend to benefit academic performance.42–44 Furthermore, the act of help-seeking facilitates early academic intervention, which has been shown to benefit academic trajectories in struggling medical student learners.45

Resilience may be an additional buffer mitigating the effects of psychosocial stressors on academic performance.10–13 Contrary to our hypothesis, there was no significant difference in grit scores between psychosocial stressor groups. Both groups had a mean grit score greater than 3.6 out of 5, suggesting that the participants in this sample have moderate to high levels of grit, which is similar to another study on graduating medical students.14 Research on medical students in China and Japan has identified correlations between resilience and academic performance.46,47 Through the lens of Cognitive Appraisal Theory, students may have a primary appraisal of a threat when faced with academic or psychosocial stressors, but their secondary appraisal demonstrates an effective coping potential, which reduces the debilitating effects of stress.9 In other words, for participants in this sample, psychosocial stressors do not undermine academic performance due to the mitigating effects of grit as shown in research.10–13

This study has several limitations. First, the qualitative data was limited by the self-report data collection methodology which may also be subject to misclassification bias based on how the academic advisor chose to code the description of the stressor. It is possible that participants may have underreported the extent of psychosocial stress occurring in their lives, the advisor may have limited disclosure in documentation, as well as other both student and advisor behaviors may have resulted in the absence of documentation in the advising notes. Furthermore, not all psychosocial stressors are created equal. The study did not measure the perceived stress of participants with a way to link that to psychosocial stressors. Moreover, the study was limited in its ability to account for confounding variables (eg number of advising meetings) by solely using independent samples t-tests for quantitative analysis. It is possible that students who have more frequent contact with advisors would be more likely to have advising notes documenting their psychosocial stressors. Future research should examine the perceived stress of medical students in addition to academic performance, psychosocial stressors, and grit using analytical techniques that can better account for potential confounders. Additionally, the study is limited by the cohort sample of medical students who have sought academic advising and overall participation rate may be subject to selection bias. However, as the larger study was focused on academic difficulty, it is likely if a selection bias occurred, it included students who may be more distressed and higher utilization of academic advising. Thus, while this may allow for generalizability to medical students who seek academic advising at other institutions, the generalizability to students who may have lessor help-seeking behavior is limited. Future research should evaluate differences in academic performance, psychosocial stressors, and resilience between students who seek academic advising and those that do not. Furthermore, this sample is one of convenience. However, while there may be some inherent selection bias in the use of a convenience sample, the performance metrics of this sample were similar to the graduating class, as measured by NBME exam performance. Moreover, the relatively small sample size for this study may have reduced the statistical power to detect a significant effect in our analysis.

Another limitation is the use of average NBME scores for comparison. This does not allow for an accurate evaluation of the relationship between study variables across time as it relates to the occurrence of psychosocial stressors. Future studies should consider using multiple NBME assessments across time or a score trajectory measure to account for these differences. Critically, the study only examines grit as a potential buffer in the relationship between stress and academic performance. Future research should evaluate specific institutional supports systems that may aid in mitigate the effects of psychosocial stressors on academic performance. Despite these limitations, this study offers preliminary insight into the nature and relationship between student life stressors and academic help-seeking through its innovative design. Furthermore, by aggregating performance trajectories over time, we can describe the long-term and broad-based impact of psychosocial stressors on performance, which may positively impact advising and help-seeking behavior.

The strengths of this study include its embedded mixed methods design. This allowed for a more comprehensive approach to answering the study’s research questions than simply analyzing quantitative data alone. Additionally, the longitudinal nature of the advising notes allowed for the contextualization of psychosocial stressors to specific time periods.

Conclusion

In this single institution, convenience sample study, authors found psychosocial stressors documented in advising notes were common but not significantly associated with average pre-clerkship NBME performance or Grit scores. These findings potentially challenge the notion that personal stressors universally diminish academic performance. The authors acknowledge limitations of student self-disclosure and the help-seeking cohort group. Findings in this study further suggest the need for larger longitudinal studies, incorporating standardized measures of stress, time-linked academic outcomes, as well as direct assessment of institutional support and coping mechanisms. Future research could also evaluate specific components of institutional support systems that help students mitigate the effects of personal challenge.
