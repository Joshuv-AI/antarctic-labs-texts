Linear varieties and matroids with applications to the Cullis’ determinant
Alexander Guterman, Andrey Yurkov

Abstract
Let V V be a vector space of rectangular n × k n\times k matrices annihilating the Cullis’ determinant. We show that dim ( V ) ≤ ( n − 1 ) ​ k \dim(V)\leq(n-1)k , extending Dieudonné’s result on the dimension of vector spaces of square matrices annihilating the ordinary determinant. Furthermore, for certain values of n n and k k , we explicitly describe such vector spaces of maximal dimension. Namely, we establish that if k k is odd, n ≥ k + 2 n\geq k+2 and dim ( V ) = ( n − 1 ) ​ k \dim(V)=(n-1)k , then V V is equal to the space of all n × k n\times k matrices X X such that alternating row sum of X X is equal to zero. Our proofs rely on the following observations from the matroid theory that have an independent interest. First, we provide a notion of matroid corresponding to a given linear variety. Second, we prove that if the linear variety is transformed by projections and restrictions, then the behaviour of the corresponding matroid is expressed in the terms of matroid contraction and restriction. Third, we establish that if M M is a matroid, I ∗ I^{*} its coindependent set M | S M|S and its restriction on a set S S , then the union of I ∗ ∖ S I^{*}\setminus S with every cobase of M | S M|S is coindependent set of M M .

linear varieties, matroids, rectangular matrices, Cullis’ determinant

1
Introduction

The determinant of a matrix is used in many fields of mathematics and its applications. Its study has a long and rich history, with contributions from mathematicians across the world since ancient times.

One of the subjects in the investigations of the determinant is describing the structure of spaces of matrices annihilating it,
i.e., vector spaces
V\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
such that
\det(X)=0
for all
X\in V
. Dieudonné in
[
4
]
obtained the sharp bound for the dimension of matrix space having this property and established that every such space of maximal possible dimension should be a left or right maximal ideal in the ring of all matrices.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
4
, Théorème 1]
)
.

Assume that
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{F}}
is a field such that
(|{\mathbb{F}}|,n)\neq(2,2)
. Let
V\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
be a vector space,
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}(\mathbb{F})
and
{\mathsf{K}}=\{A\}+V
. Then the following statements hold:

1.

If
\det(X)=0
for all
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
, then
\dim(V)\leq n^{2}-n
.

2.

If
\det(X)=0
for all
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
and
\dim(V)=n^{2}-n
, then either the nullspaces of the matrices from
{\mathsf{K}}
have a common nonzero vector, or the same is true for
{\mathsf{K}}^{t}
.

This allowed Dieudonné to provide a description of linear maps preserving the matrix determinant without any restrictions on ground field
[
4
, Théorème 3]
.

Since the ordinary determinant is defined only for the square matrices, there were made several attempts to extend this notion to the rectangular matrices. One of such extension is due to Cullis who introduced the concept of determinant (he called it
determinoid
) of a rectangular matrix in his monograph
[
2
]
which can be expressed as an alternating sum of its maximal minors (see Corollary
3.6
) and is denoted by
\det_{n\,k}
. Several properties known for the classical determinant were studied and shown to be valid for the Cullis’ determinant in
[
2
, §5, §27, §32]
.

In 1966 Radić
[
17
]
independently proposed an equivalent definition of the Cullis determinant. He also provided in
[
18
,
19
]
its geometrical applications to the calculating areas of polygons on the plane and volumes of certain polyhedrons in space. According to that, it is also sometimes called
Radić’s determinant

[
1
]
or
Cullis-Radić determinant

[
11
,
12
]
in some papers.

Nakagami and Yanai in
[
14
]
formulate the definition of the Cullis’ determinant in the terms of vectors in a Grassmann algebra and provide a definition of the Cullis’ determinant through several characteristic properties. For this reason they consider
n\times k
matrices only with
n\geq k
, whereas the original definition covers rectangular matrices of any size. But according to it, the Cullis determinant of
n\times k
matrix with
n<k
is equal to the Cullis determinant of its transpose and consequently it is sufficient to consider only the case
n\geq k
.

Theorems
1.1
suggest a natural direction for studying the Cullis determinant: describing the maximal vector spaces annihilating the Cullis determinant.

In this paper the authors make the first steps in the second direction of research consisting in studying vector spaces of matrices annihilating the Cullis’ determinant. In particular, we obtain a generalization of the Dieudonné’s theorem for the Cullis’ determinant to the case where
n\geq k+2
and
k
is odd. The main result of this paper is formulated as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(Cf. Theorem
7.11
)
.

Let
n\geq k+2
and
V\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}(\mathbb{F})
be a vector space
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}(\mathbb{F})
and
{\mathsf{K}}=\{A\}+V
. Then
\det_{n\,k}(X)=0
for all
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
and
\codim({\mathsf{K}})=k
if and only if
k
is odd and alternating row sum of every
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
is equal to zero.

Thus, this theorem implies that if
n\geq k+2
and
k
is odd, there is a
unique
vector subspace of
{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
of maximal dimension annihilating
\det_{n\,k}
.

The argumentation presented in this paper is similar to that used by Dieudonné to prove Theorem
1.1
. The main difference is that the authors rely on the following considerations from the matroid theory. First, we provide a notion of matroid corresponding to a given linear variety (Definition
6.3
). Second, we prove that if the linear variety is transformed by projections and restrictions, then the behaviour of the corresponding matroid is expressed in the terms of matroid contraction and restriction (Lemma
6.7
and Lemma
6.16
). Third, we establish that if
M
is a matroid,
I^{*}
its coindependent set
M|S
and its restriction on a set
S
, then the union of
I^{*}\setminus S
with every cobase of
M|S
is coindependent set of
M
(Lemma
5.14
).

Note that these observations may be useful for solving other similar problems. It is also worth to mention that the considerations from the matroid theory were already used to solve the problems of such kind. For example, Meshulam in
[
6
]
applies the Lovász Matroid Parity Theorem to find the maximal dimension of vector space
V
consisting of skew-symmetric matrices such that every element of
V
is a singular matrix.

Similarly to Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
finds its applications in studying linear maps that preserve the Cullis’ determinant. Using this theorem, it is possible to obtain a description of linear maps preserving the Cullis’ determinant of
n\times k
rectangular matrices for the case where
k=3
, see
[
10
]
.

In addition to finding a precise description of vector spaces of matrices of size
n\times k
annihilating the Cullis’ determinant for
k
odd we also provide the following upper bound of the dimension of such spaces for arbitrary
k
.

Theorem 1.3

(Cf. Theorem
7.6
)
.

Let
V\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
be a vector space,
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}(\mathbb{F})
and
{\mathsf{K}}=\{A\}+V
be such that
\det_{n\,k}(X)=0
for every
X\in{\mathsf{K}}.
Then
\codim(V)\geq k.

Here
\codim(V)
denotes a
codimension
of vector space
V\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
, which is defined by
\codim(V)=\dim\left({\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\right)-\dim(V)=n\cdot k-\dim(V).

Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
imply in particular that if
k
is even and
n\geq k+2
, then the codimension of any vector space of matrices of size
n\times k
annihilating the Cullis’ determinant is strictly greater than
k
. This is the sharpest bound known by authors at present, and the question of its exact value remains open.

In accordance with the text above, the two cases (
n=k
and
n=k+1
) remain undiscussed. The case if
n=k
is completely covered by Theorem
1.1
because the Cullis’ determinant of square matrix is equal to the ordinary determinant. The case
n=k+1
, in turn, will be considered in the separate paper because it is carried out using different methods.

It is worth mentioning that Theorem
1.1
admits other generalizations. For example, Pazzis in
[
3
]
provides a description of vectors spaces of matrices of size
n\times k
(where
n\geq k
) of maximal dimension which do not contain a matrix of rank
k
. Unfortunately, it is not possible to apply his results directly to the spaces of matrices annihilating the Cullis’ determinant because there exist matrices of rank
k
belonging to
{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
such that their Cullis’ determinant is zero. For example, this is true for the matrix
E_{1\,1}+\ldots+E_{k\,k}+E_{(k+1)\,k}
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we provide the necessary notations; Sections
3
,
5
contain the preliminary facts from the theory of the Cullis’ determinant and the matroid theory, correspondingly; in Section
4
we provide an explanation of all necessary facts from the theory of linear varieties; in Section
6
we introduce the matroid corresponding to a given linear variety, study the behaviour of this matroid while the corresponding linear variety is transformed using different operations and establish the relationship between matroids corresponding to linear varieties and vector matroids corresponding to matrices; Section
7
is devoted to investigations of linear varieties of matrices annihilating the Cullis’ determinant and contains the proofs of main results of this paper; in Section
8
we discuss the possible further work in this direction.

Theorem 3.1

(
[

)

1.

For

\det_{n\,n}(X)=\det(X).

5.

\mathbf{z^{\mathrm{odd}}}=(\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}-1})^{t}.
Since
n+k
is odd, then

\mathbf{z^{\mathrm{odd}}}=(\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}-1})^{t}\\
=(\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n},(-1)^{n+k+1}\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,(-1)^{n+k+1}\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}-1})^{t}=\mathbf{z}^{\circ},

where
\mathbf{z}^{\circ}
be as defined in (
3.3
).

Property
(S2)
.

Let
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
be such that
\det_{n\,k}(X)
= 0. Then by Lemma
3.9
we have

\det_{n\,k}({\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X))=(-1)^{(i^{\circ}+1)k}\det_{n\,k}(X)=0.

Property
(S3)
.

Assume that
k
is odd. Let
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}
be such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X^{\circ}[i|)=0,

(3.5)

where
X^{\circ}={\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X)
. Rewrite the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)
as follows

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=\sum_{i=1}^{i^{\circ}-1}(-1)^{i}X[i|)+\sum_{i=i^{\circ}}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|).

Since
n
is even in this case,

\sum_{i=1}^{i^{\circ}-1}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=\sum_{i=n-i^{\circ}+2}^{n}(-1)^{i-n+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{i=i^{\circ}}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-i^{\circ}+1}(-1)^{i+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|),

then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=\sum_{i=1}^{i^{\circ}-1}(-1)^{i}X[i|)+\sum_{i=i^{\circ}}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)\\
=\sum_{i=n-i^{\circ}+2}^{n}(-1)^{i-n+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-i^{\circ}+1}(-1)^{i+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)\phantom{XXXXXXXXXXX}\\
=\sum_{i=n-i^{\circ}+2}^{n}(-1)^{i+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-i^{\circ}+1}(-1)^{i+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)=(-1)^{i^{\circ}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X^{\circ}[i|).

Thus, using the equality (
3.5
) we conclude that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=(-1)^{i^{\circ}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X^{\circ}[i|)=0.

The case if
n+k
is even.

Let us define
{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
by

{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X)=\begin{pmatrix}x_{i^{\circ}\,1}&\ldots&x_{i^{\circ}\,k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{n\,1}&\ldots&x_{n\,k}\\
-x_{1\,1}&\ldots&-x_{1\,k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
-x_{(i^{\circ}-1)\,1}&\ldots&-x_{(i^{\circ}-1)\,k}\\
\end{pmatrix}\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}}).

This map is clearly linear and invertible. Now we verify that
{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}
indeed satisfies the properties
(S1)
–
(S3)
.

Property
(S1)
.

Let
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
and
\mathbf{z}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
be such that
\mathbf{z}^{t}X=0
. It follows from (
3.4
) that
\mathbf{z^{\mathrm{odd}}}{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X)=0
, where
\mathbf{z^{\mathrm{even}}}
is defined by

\mathbf{z^{\mathrm{even}}}=(\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n},-\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,-\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}-1})^{t}.
Since
n+k
is even, then

\mathbf{z^{\mathrm{even}}}=(\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n},-\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,-\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}-1})^{t}\\
=(\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n},(-1)^{n+k+1}\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,(-1)^{n+k+1}\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}-1})^{t}=\mathbf{z}^{\circ},

where
\mathbf{z}^{\circ}
be as defined in (
3.3
).

Property
(S2)
.

Let
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
be such that
\det_{n\,k}(X)
= 0. Then by Lemma
3.10
we have

\det_{n\,k}({\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X))=(-1)^{(n-i^{\circ})k}\det_{n\,k}(X)=0.

Property
(S3)
.

Assume that
k
is odd. Let
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}
be such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X^{\circ}[i|)=0,

(3.6)

where
X^{\circ}={\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X)
. Rewrite the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)
as follows

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=\sum_{i=1}^{i^{\circ}-1}(-1)^{i}X[i|)+\sum_{i=i^{\circ}}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|).

Since
n
is odd in this case,

\sum_{i=1}^{i^{\circ}-1}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=-\left(\sum_{i=n-i^{\circ}+2}^{n}(-1)^{i-n+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)\right)

and

\sum_{i=i^{\circ}}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-i^{\circ}+1}(-1)^{i+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|),

then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=\sum_{i=1}^{i^{\circ}-1}(-1)^{i}X[i|)+\sum_{i=i^{\circ}}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)\\
=-\left(\sum_{i=n-i^{\circ}+2}^{n}(-1)^{i-n+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-i^{\circ}+1}(-1)^{i+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)\phantom{XXXXXXXXX}\\
=\sum_{i=n-i^{\circ}+2}^{n}(-1)^{i+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-i^{\circ}+1}(-1)^{i+i^{\circ}-1}X^{\circ}[i|)=(-1)^{i^{\circ}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X^{\circ}[i|).

Thus, using the equality (
3.6
) we conclude that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=(-1)^{i^{\circ}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X^{\circ}[i|)=0.

∎

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
(A,\mathbf{b})
be an equational representation of a linear variety
{\mathsf{K}}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}^{n}

\rk(A)=\codim({\mathsf{K}})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{s}\in{\mathsf{K}}
. Lemma
4.12
implies that
{\mathsf{K}}=\mathbf{s}+\AS(A,\mathbf{0})

\rk(A)=n-\dim(\AS(A,\mathbf{0}))=\codim({\mathsf{K}})

by Lemma

is a vector space which also follows from Lemma

.
∎

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\,n}({\mathbb{F}}),\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{m}
,
{\mathsf{K}}=\AS(A,\mathbf{b})\subsetneq{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
be a linear variety. Then there exists
R\subseteq[m]
such that
{\mathsf{K}}=\AS\Bigl(A[R|),\mathbf{b}[R|)\Bigr)
and
A[R|)
has full rank.

Proof.

Let
{\mathsf{K}}_{H}
be the solution set of the corresponding homogeneous system
A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
. Lemma
4.12
implies that
{\mathsf{K}}_{H}\neq{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
. According to Lemma
4.11
, the linear variety
{\mathsf{K}}_{H}
is a vector subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
and
\rk(A)=r=n-\dim({\mathsf{K}}_{H})
. Since
{\mathsf{K}}_{H}\neq{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
, then
\dim({\mathsf{K}}_{H})<n
and consequently
r>0
.

Let
R=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}
be any set of indices of linearly independent rows in
A
. Since
\rk(A)=r
, then every equation of system
Ax=\mathbf{b}
is a linear combination of equations with indices belonging to
r
. Therefore,
{\mathsf{K}}=\AS(A[R|),\mathbf{b}[R|))
for this
R
.
∎

Lemma 4.21
.

Suppose that
\varnothing\neq\AS(A,\mathbf{b})=\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
. Then

(a)

\AS(A,\mathbf{0})=\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{0})
;

(b)

\rk(A)=\rk(A^{\prime})
;

(c)

if
A
and
A^{\prime}
have full rank, then there exists an invertible matrix
C\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\rk(A)\,\rk(A)}({\mathbb{F}})
such that
CA=A^{\prime}
and
C\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

(a)
Let
\mathbf{s}\in\AS(A,\mathbf{b})
. Since
\AS(A,\mathbf{b})=\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
, then
\mathbf{s}\in\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
. It follows from Lemma
4.12
that

\{\mathbf{s}\}+\AS(A,\mathbf{0})=\AS(A,\mathbf{b})\;\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\;\{\mathbf{s}\}+\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{0})=\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}).

Then the assumption of the lemma implies that

\{\mathbf{s}\}+\AS(A,\mathbf{0})=\{\mathbf{s}\}+\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{0}).

Therefore,

\AS(A,\mathbf{0})=\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{0})

(4.1)

by Lemma
4.3
.

(b)

Lemma
4.11
implies that
\AS(A,\mathbf{0})
and
\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{0})
are both vector spaces. Then the equality (
4.1
) implies that
\dim(\AS(A,\mathbf{0}))=\dim(\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{0})).
Therefore,

\rk(A)=n-\dim(\AS(A,\mathbf{0}))=n-\dim(\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{0}))=\rk(A^{\prime})

by Lemma
4.11
.

(c)

Let
A^{\prime\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
A^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\mathbf{b}^{\prime\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{b}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Since
\AS(A,\mathbf{b})=\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
, then

\AS(A^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime\prime})=\AS(A,\mathbf{b})\cap\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})=\AS(A,\mathbf{b}).

Hence,
\rk(A^{\prime\prime})=\rk(A)
by the part
(b)
of the lemma. Therefore, every row of
A^{\prime\prime}
corresponding to
A^{\prime}
is a linear combination of rows corresponding to
A
, which means that there exists a matrix
C\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\rk(A)\,\rk(A)}({\mathbb{F}})
such that

CA=A^{\prime}.

(4.2)

Since
\rk(A)=\rk(A^{\prime})
, this matrix is invertible.

Let
\mathbf{s}
be any solution of the system of linear equations
Ax=\mathbf{b}
. It exists because we suppose that
\AS(A,\mathbf{b})\neq\varnothing
. Thus,
A\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{b}
. This equality together with the equality (
4.2
) imply that

C\mathbf{b}=CA\mathbf{s}=A^{\prime}\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{b}^{\prime}

because
\AS(A,\mathbf{b})=\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
and consequently
\mathbf{s}\in\AS(A^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})
as well.
∎

Corollary 4.22
.

Let
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\,n}({\mathbb{F}}),\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}

Lemma 4.23

be a vector space belonging to

Indeed, if

Lemma 4.24
.

Let

,

\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{m}

(b)

\codim({\mathsf{K}}_{C})=\codim({\mathsf{K}})

(a)
Indeed, the conditions

(b)

Assume that
{\mathsf{K}}=\mathbf{s}+V
for some

It is clear that

.

If

on

.

Let

. Since

is injective, then

is surjective.

Definition 4.28
.

If

is a finite set,

its element, then by

we denote

Note that the set

Let us recall the necessary facts from the matroid theory following
[

]
.

Definition 5.1

(
[
15
, Definition on p.7]
)
.

A
matroid

M
is an ordered pair
(E,\mathcal{I})
consisting of a finite set E and a
collection I of subsets of E having the following three properties:

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a matroid on
E
,
S\subseteq E
be a set and
I^{*}\subseteq E
be a coindependent set of
\mathbf{M}
. Then

(a)

If
{B^{\prime}}^{*}
is a cobasis of
\mathbf{M}|S
, then
\left(I^{*}\setminus S\right)\cup{B^{\prime}}^{*}
is a coindependent set of
\mathbf{M}
.

(b)

r^{*}(\mathbf{M}|S)\leq r^{*}(\mathbf{M})-\left|I^{*}\setminus S\right|.

Proof.

(a)
Since
{B^{\prime}}^{*}
is a cobasis of
\mathbf{M}|S
, then
B^{\prime}=\left(S\setminus{B^{\prime}}^{*}\right)\subseteq S
is an independent set of
\mathbf{M}|S
. Hence,
B^{\prime}
is an independent set of
\mathbf{M}
by the definition of
\mathbf{M}|S
.

I^{*}
is a coindependent set of
\mathbf{M}
. Therefore,
I^{*}_{S}=I^{*}\setminus S
is a coindependent set of
\mathbf{M}
. In addition,
I^{*}_{S}\cap S=\varnothing
.

Thus,
I^{*}_{S}
and
B^{\prime}
are correspondingly coindependent and independent subsets of
\mathbf{M}
such that
I^{*}_{S}\cap B^{\prime}=\varnothing
. Hence, from Lemma
5.10
we conclude that there exist a basis
B_{0}
and a cobasis
B_{0}^{*}
of
\mathbf{M}
such that
B_{0}\supseteq B^{\prime}
,
B_{0}^{*}\supseteq I^{*}_{S}
and
B_{0}\cap B_{0}^{*}=\varnothing
.

The inclusion
B_{0}\supseteq B^{\prime}
implies the inclusion

B_{0}\cap S\supseteq B^{\prime}\cap S=B^{\prime}.

(5.1)

The definition of
\mathbf{M}|S
implies that
B_{0}\cap S
is an independent set of
\mathbf{M}|S
. Thus, having the inclusion (
5.1
) and the fact that
B^{\prime}
is base of
\mathbf{M}|S
, we obtain that

B_{0}\cap S=B^{\prime}.

(5.2)

Since
E=B_{0}\sqcup B_{0}^{*}
and
S\subseteq E
, then it follows from (
5.2
) that
B_{0}^{*}\cap S={B^{\prime}}^{*}
. Thus,
B_{0}^{*}\supseteq{B^{\prime}}^{*}
and consequently

B_{0}^{*}\supseteq I^{*}_{S}\cup{B^{\prime}}^{*}=\left(I^{*}\setminus S\right)\cup{B^{\prime}}^{*}.

Therefore,
\left(I^{*}\setminus S\right)\cup{B^{\prime}}^{*}
is a coindependent set of
\mathbf{M}
.

(b)

Let
{B^{\prime}}^{*}\subseteq S
be any cobasis of
\mathbf{M}|S
. Then
r^{*}\left(\mathbf{M}|S\right)=\left|{B^{\prime}}^{*}\right|
. From the part
(a)
of the lemma we conclude that

r^{*}(\mathbf{M})\geq\left|\left(I^{*}\setminus S\right)\cup{B^{\prime}}^{*}\right|.

Let us consider the right hand side of this inequality. Since
\left(I^{*}\setminus S\right)\cap{B^{\prime}}^{*}=\varnothing
, then

\left|\left(I^{*}\setminus S\right)\cup{B^{\prime}}^{*}\right|=\left|I^{*}\setminus S\right|+\left|{B^{\prime}}^{*}\right|=\left|I^{*}\setminus S\right|+r^{*}\left(\mathbf{M}|S\right).

Thus,

r^{*}(\mathbf{M})\geq\left|\left(I^{*}\right)\setminus S\right|+r^{*}(\mathbf{M}|S).

which is equivalent to the required inequality.
∎

The following corollary is a direct consequence of Lemma
5.14
applied to the contraction matroid
\mathbf{M}/T
instead of
\mathbf{M}
and the definition of contraction matroid. It will be used in sequel.

Corollary 5.15
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a matroid on
E
,
T\subseteq E
,
S\subseteq E\setminus T
be two sets and
I^{*}\subseteq E
be a coindependent set of
\mathbf{M}
. Then

(a)

If
{B^{\prime}}^{*}
is a cobasis of
\mathbf{M}/T|S
, then
\left(\left(I^{*}\setminus T\right)\setminus S\right)\cup{B^{\prime}}^{*}
is a coindependent set of
\mathbf{M}
.

(b)

r^{*}\left(\mathbf{M}/T|S\right)\leq r^{*}\left(\mathbf{M}\right)-\left|\left(I^{*}\setminus T\right)\setminus S\right|.

Lemma 6.5

Let
V\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}^{E}
be a vector space,
X\subseteq E
. Then a linear map
\pi_{E}^{X}|_{V}
is a surjection if and only if

is an independent set of
\mathbf{M}(V)
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\colon\left({\mathbb{F}}^{X}\right)^{*}\to V^{*}
be defined by
\phi(f)=\sum_{i\in X}f(\mathbf{e}_{i}){\mathsf{x}}_{i}|_{V}
. Note that

\phi=\left(\pi_{E}^{X}|_{V}\right)^{*}.

(6.1)

Indeed, since
{\mathsf{x}}_{i}^{X}\circ\pi_{E}^{X}|_{V}={\mathsf{x}}_{i}^{E}|_{V}
for all
i\in X
, then
\left(\pi_{E}^{X}|_{V}\right)^{*}\left({\mathsf{x}}_{i}^{X}\right)=\phi({\mathsf{x}}_{i}^{X})
for all
i\in X
. Therefore, the equality
6.1
holds since it holds on the basis
\{{\mathsf{x}}_{i}^{X}\;\mid\;i\in X\}
of
\left({\mathbb{F}}^{X}\right)^{*}
.

The basic properties of dual vector spaces (see
[
5
, Sec. 2.7, Exercise 20 on p. 127]
) imply that in this case
\pi_{E}^{X}|_{V}
is a surjection if and only if
\phi
is an injection. The last condition is equivalent to the condition that
X
is an independent set of
\mathbf{M}(V)
. Indeed, the definition of
\mathbf{M}(V)
implies that
X\in\mathbf{M}(V)
if and only if
\dim\left(\Span\left({\mathsf{x}}_{i}|_{V}\mid i\in X\right)\right)=|X|
which means that the set
\{{\mathsf{x}}_{i}|_{V}\mid i\in X\}
is linearly independent. Thus, the required equivalence is established.

∎

(6.3)

(b)

Lemma 6.15

First we define the projection map which formalizes the concept of operation of striking out one row and one column of matrix. For example, this operation is performed when we use the Laplace expansion formula.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
n\geq k>1
and
1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq k
. We denote by
\ins_{n\,k}^{(i,j)}\colon[n-1]\times[k-1]\to[n]\times[k]\setminus\left(\{i\}\times[k]\cup[n]\times\{j\}\right)

\ins_{n\,k}^{(i,j)}((a,b))=\begin{cases}(a+1,b+1),&a\geq i,b\geq j\\
(a+1,b),&a\geq i,b<j\\
(a,b+1),&a<i,b\geq j\\
(a,b),&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

for all
(a,b)\in[n-1]\times[k-1]

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose that
(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\subseteq[n-1]\times[k-1]
belong to the same row (column).Then
\ins_{n\,k}^{(i,j)}((a,b)),\ins_{n\,k}^{(i,j)}((a^{\prime},b^{\prime}))\subseteq[n]\times[k]

It follows from the definition of
\ins_{n\,k}^{(i,j)}
that if

then the first elements of tuples
\ins_{n\,k}^{(i,j)}((a,b))
and
\ins_{n\,k}^{(i,j)}((a^{\prime},b^{\prime}))

Similarly, if

and

{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}=\{\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{1,1}&\cdots&x_{1\,n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&x_{n,1}&\cdots&x_{n\,n-1}\end{pmatrix}\mid x_{1\,1},\ldots,x_{n\,n-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}\}.

If
n=k+1
, then
\det_{n\,k}(X)=\det_{k}(X)=0
for all
X\in{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime\prime}
, where

{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime\prime}=\{\begin{pmatrix}x&x_{1,1}&\cdots&x_{1\,n-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x&x_{n,1}&\cdots&x_{n\,n-2}\end{pmatrix}\mid x,x_{1\,1},\ldots,x_{n\,n-2}\in{\mathbb{F}}\}

which follows from multilinearity of
\det_{n\,k}
with respect to columns andLemma
3.7
because
n+k-1
is even in this case.

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
\mathbf{z}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
be such that
\mathbf{z}_{1}=-1
and
{\mathsf{K}}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}(\mathbb{F})
be a linear variety defined by
{\mathsf{K}}=\left\{X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\mid\mathbf{z}^{t}X=0\right\}
. Then
\det_{n\,k}(X)=0
for all
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
if and only if

1+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\mathbf{z}_{c(\alpha)}(-1)^{\alpha-c(\alpha)}=0\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;c\in\binom{[n]}{k}\;\;\mbox{such that}\;\;1\not\in c.

(7.22)

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{z}_{1}=-1,
then the condition
\mathbf{z}^{t}X=0
for all
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
means that the first row of every
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
could be expressed as a linear combination of other rows as follows

X[1|)=\mathbf{z}_{2}X[2|)+\ldots+\mathbf{z}_{n}X[n|).

(7.23)

Let
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
. By Corollary
3.6

\det_{n\,k}(X)=\sum_{c\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\sgn_{[n]}(c)\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr).

The right-hand side of this equality is divided into two summands as follows.

\sum_{c\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\sgn_{[n]}(c)\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr)\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\binom{[n]}{k}\\
1\in c\end{subarray}}\sgn_{[n]}(c)\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\binom{[n]}{k}\\
1\not\in c\end{subarray}}\sgn_{[n]}(c)\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr).

(7.24)

Consider the first summand of the right-hand side of (
7.24
). Let
c^{\prime}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
be such that
1\in c^{\prime}
. It follows from (
7.22
) that

\det_{k}(X[c^{\prime}|))=\begin{vmatrix}X[1|)\\
X[c^{\prime}(2)|)\\
\vdots\\
X[c^{\prime}(k)|)\\
\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{z}_{2}X[2|)+\ldots+\mathbf{z}_{n}X[n|)\\
X[c^{\prime}(2)|)\\
\vdots\\
X[c^{\prime}(k)|)\\
\end{vmatrix}\\
=\sum_{2\leq i\leq n}\mathbf{z}_{i}\begin{vmatrix}X[i|)\\
X[c^{\prime}(2)|)\\
\vdots\\
X[c^{\prime}(k)|)\\
\end{vmatrix}

(7.25)

Note that

\begin{vmatrix}X[i|)\\
X[c^{\prime}(2)|)\\
\vdots\\
X[c^{\prime}(k)|)\\
\end{vmatrix}=0\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;i\in c^{\prime},\,2\leq i\leq n

(7.26)

because it becomes a determinant of a matrix with two rows equal to each other.

Since the ordinary determinant is an antisymmetric function of the rows of matrix, then for
i\not\in c^{\prime}
,
2\leq i\leq n
we have

\begin{vmatrix}X[i|)\\
X[c^{\prime}(2)|)\\
\vdots\\
X[c^{\prime}(k)|)\\
\end{vmatrix}=\sgn_{n\,k}(\sigma_{i\,c^{\prime}})\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c^{\prime}\triangle\{1,i\}|)\Bigr),

(7.27)

where
\sigma_{i\,c^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{C}_{[n]}^{k}
is defined by

\sigma_{i\,c^{\prime}}(k)=\begin{cases}i,&k=1\\
c^{\prime}(k),&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

By substituting (
7.26
) and (
7.27
) into (
7.25
) we obtain that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c^{\prime}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\\
1\in c^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sgn_{[n]}(c^{\prime})\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c^{\prime}|)\Bigr)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c^{\prime}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\\
1\in c^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sgn_{[n]}(c^{\prime})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq n\\
i\not\in c^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{z}_{i}\sgn_{n\,k}(\sigma_{i\,c^{\prime}})\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c^{\prime}\triangle\{1,i\}|)\Bigr).

Let us rearrange the sum in the right-hand side of this equality. Note that if
2\leq i\leq n
and
c^{\prime}\triangle\{1,i\}=c
, then
i=c(\alpha)
for some unique
1\leq\alpha\leq k
. Hence,
\sgn_{[n]}(c^{\prime})=(-1)^{1-i}\sgn_{[n]}(c)=(-1)^{1-c(\alpha)}\sgn_{[n]}(c)
by the definition of
\sgn_{[n]}
. In addition,
\sgn_{n\,k}(\sigma_{i\,c^{\prime}})=(-1)^{\alpha-1}
because it is equal to the sign of permutation

\begin{pmatrix}c(1)&c(2)&\cdots&c(\alpha)&c(\alpha+1)&\cdots&c(k)\\
c(\alpha)&c(1)&\cdots&c(\alpha-1)&c(\alpha+1)&\cdots&c(k)\end{pmatrix}

by the definition.

Observe also that for every pair
(c,\alpha)
such that
c\in\binom{[n]}{k},1\not\in c
and
1\leq\alpha\leq k
there exist a unique pair
(c^{\prime},i)
, where
c^{\prime}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
and
2\leq i\leq n
such that
i=c(\alpha)
,
c^{\prime}=c\triangle\{1,i\}
and consequently
1\in c^{\prime}
and
i\not\in c^{\prime}
.

Thus,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c^{\prime}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\\
1\in c^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sgn_{[n]}(c^{\prime})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq n\\
i\not\in c^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{z}_{i}\sgn_{n\,k}(\sigma_{i\,c^{\prime}})\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c^{\prime}\triangle\{1,i\}|)\Bigr)\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c^{\prime}\in\binom{[n]}{k},2\leq i\leq n\\
1\in c^{\prime},i\not\in c^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{z}_{i}\sgn_{[n]}(c^{\prime})\sgn_{n\,k}(\sigma_{i\,c^{\prime}})\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c^{\prime}\triangle\{1,i\}|)\Bigr)\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\binom{[n]}{k},1\leq\alpha\leq k\\
1\not\in c\end{subarray}}\mathbf{z}_{c(\alpha)}(-1)^{1-c(\alpha)}\sgn_{[n]}(c)(-1)^{\alpha-1}\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr)\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\binom{[n]}{k}\\
1\not\in c\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\mathbf{z}_{c(\alpha)}(-1)^{\alpha-c(\alpha)}\right)\sgn_{[n]}(c)\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr)

By substituting this instead of the first summand into (
7.24
) we obtain that

\det_{n\,k}(X)=\sum_{c\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\sgn_{[n]}(c)\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr)\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\binom{[n]}{k}\\
1\not\in c\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\mathbf{z}_{c(\alpha)}(-1)^{\alpha-c(\alpha)}\right)\sgn_{[n]}(c)\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\binom{[n]}{k}\\
1\not\in c\end{subarray}}\sgn_{[n]}(c)\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr)\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\binom{[n]}{k}\\
1\not\in c\end{subarray}}\left(1+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\mathbf{z}_{c(\alpha)}(-1)^{\alpha-c(\alpha)}\right)\sgn_{[n]}(c)\det_{k}\Bigl(X[c|)\Bigr)

(7.28)

for all
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
.

Now let
c_{0}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
be such that
1\not\in c_{0}
. Consider
X_{c_{0}}
defined by

X_{c_{0}}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\left(E_{c_{0}(\alpha)\,\alpha}+\mathbf{z}_{c_{0}(\alpha)}E_{1\,\alpha}\right).

X_{c_{0}}
clearly satisfies the equation
\mathbf{z}^{t}X_{c_{0}}=0
and consequently
X_{c_{0}}\in{\mathsf{K}}
. In addition, for every
c\in\binom{[n]}{k}
such that
1\not\in c

\det_{k}(X_{c_{0}}[c|))=\begin{cases}1,&c=c_{0}\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

On the one hand, the equality (
7.28
) implies that

\det_{n\,k}(X_{c_{0}})=\left(1+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\mathbf{z}_{c_{0}(\alpha)}(-1)^{\alpha-c_{0}(\alpha)}\right)\sgn_{[n]}(c_{0}).

On the other hand, since
X_{c_{0}}\in{\mathsf{K}}
, then
\det_{n\,k}(X_{c_{0}})=0
. Since
\sgn_{[n]}(c_{0})\neq 0
, then we obtain the equality

1+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\mathbf{z}_{c_{0}(\alpha)}(-1)^{\alpha-c_{0}(\alpha)}=0,

which holds for every
c_{0}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
such that
1\not\in c_{0}
.
∎

Theorem 7.11
.

Let
n\geq k+2
and
{\mathsf{K}}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}(\mathbb{F})
be a linear variety. Then
\det_{n\,k}(X)=0
for all
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
and
\codim({\mathsf{K}})=k
if and only if
k
is odd and alternating row sum of every
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
is equal to zero.

Proof.

First we prove the necessity and second we prove the sufficiency.

Necessity.

Let
{\mathsf{K}}
be a linear variety satisfying the conditions of the lemma. Lemma
7.8
implies that there exists
\mathbf{z}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
such that

\mathbf{z}^{t}X=0\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;X\in{\mathsf{K}}.

(7.29)

The proof is built up from the following three claims proven independently for all
n,k
and
{\mathsf{K}}
satisfying the conditions of the lemma:

(i)

If
\mathbf{z}_{1}=-1
, then

\mathbf{z}_{i}=-\mathbf{z}_{i+1}\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;1<i<n.

(7.30)

(ii)

The equality (
7.30
) implies the statement of this part the lemma.

(iii)

If the claims
(i)
and
(ii)
hold, then this part of the lemma holds (we mean the
Necessity
part).

Thus, this part of the lemma follows from the claims
(i)
–
(iii)
.

Claim
(i)
.

To establish the equality (
7.30
), consider any subsequence
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k+1}
of the sequence
2,\ldots,n
containing both
i
and
i+1
(such a sequence exists because
n\geq k+2
). Suppose that
i=m_{l}.
Then consider
c_{1}=\{m_{1},\ldots,\widehat{m_{l+1}},\ldots,m_{k+1}\}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
and
c_{2}=\{m_{1},\ldots,\widehat{m_{l}},\ldots,m_{k+1}\}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
. Since
1\not\in c_{1},c_{2}
, then the equalities

1+\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq k}(-1)^{\alpha-c_{1}(\alpha)}{\mathbf{z}}_{c_{1}(\alpha)}=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad 1+\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq k}(-1)^{\alpha-c_{2}(\alpha)}{\mathbf{z}}_{c_{2}(\alpha)}=0

(7.31)

hold by Lemma
7.10
. Since
c_{1}(\alpha)=c_{2}(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\neq l
, then the difference of the left-hand sides of equalities in (
7.31
) is equal to

(-1)^{l-c_{1}(l)}{\mathbf{z}}_{c_{1}(l)}-(-1)^{l-c_{2}(l)}{\mathbf{z}}_{c_{2}(l)}=(-1)^{l}\left((-1)^{i}{\mathbf{z}}_{i}-(-1)^{i+1}{\mathbf{z}}_{i+1}\right).

Hence,

{\mathbf{z}}_{i}+{\mathbf{z}}_{i+1}=0.

This implies the equality (
7.30
).

Claim
(ii)
.

From
(i)
we conclude that there exists
z\in{\mathbb{F}}
such that

\mathbf{z}_{i}=(-1)^{i}z\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;i\geq 2.

(7.32)

Lemma
7.10
applied to
c=\{2,\ldots,k+1\}
implies that

1+\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq k}(-1)^{\alpha+c(\alpha)}\mathbf{z}_{c(\alpha)}=0

and consequently

\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq k}(-1)^{\alpha+c(\alpha)}\mathbf{z}_{c(\alpha)}=-1.

(7.33)

Consider the left-hand side of (
7.33
). By the equality (
7.32
) we obtain that

\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq k}(-1)^{\alpha+c(\alpha)}\mathbf{z}_{c(\alpha)}=\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq k}(-1)^{\alpha-\alpha-1}(-1)^{i+1}z=\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq k}(-1)^{i}z.

(7.34)

Substituting the right-hand side of (
7.34
) into (
7.33
) yields

\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq k}(-1)^{i}z=-1.

(7.35)

If
k
is even, we obtain a contradiction because the left part of (
7.35
) is zero while the right part is nonzero.

If
k
is odd, then the left part of (
7.35
) is equal to
-z
, which implies that
z=1
. Thus,
\mathbf{z}_{i}=(-1)^{i}
.

Claim
(iii)
.

Since
\mathbf{z}\neq 0
, then there exists
1\leq i^{\circ}\leq n
such that
\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}}\neq 0
. The equality (
7.29
) implies that

{{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}}^{t}X=0\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;X\in{\mathsf{K}},

(7.36)

where
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
is defined by

\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{i}=-\frac{{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}}{{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i^{\circ}}}\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;1\leq i\leq n.

Additionally,

\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{i^{\circ}}=-1

(7.37)

by the definition of
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
.

By Lemma
3.11
, there exists an invertible linear map
{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
having the following properties:

(S1)

if
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
and
\mathbf{z}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
are such that
\mathbf{z}^{t}X=0
, then
\mathbf{z^{\circ}}^{t}{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X)=0
, where

\mathbf{z}^{\circ}=(\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n},(-1)^{n+k+1}\mathbf{z}_{1}\ldots,\mathbf{(}-1)^{n+k+1}\mathbf{z}_{i^{\circ}-1})^{t};

(S2)

\det_{n\,k}(X)=0\Rightarrow\det_{n\,k}({\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X))=0
;

(S3)

If
k
is odd, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}\left({\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X)\right)[i|)=0\Rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=0
for all
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}
.

Let
K^{\circ}
be defined by
{\mathsf{K}}^{\circ}={\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}({\mathsf{K}})
. Since
{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}
is an invertible linear map, then
{\mathsf{K}}^{\circ}
is a linear variety with
\codim({\mathsf{K}}^{\circ})=\codim({\mathsf{K}})=k
. By the property
(S2)
of
{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}
we have
\det_{n\,k}(X^{\circ})=0
for all
X^{\circ}\in{\mathsf{K}}^{\circ}.

It follows from the property
(S1)
of
{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}
and (
7.36
) that
\mathbf{z}^{\circ}X^{\circ}=0
for all
X^{\circ}\in{\mathsf{K}}^{\circ}
, where
\mathbf{z}^{\circ}
is defined by

\mathbf{z}^{\circ}=(\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{i^{\circ}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{n},(-1)^{n+k+1}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{1}\ldots,\mathbf{(}-1)^{n+k+1}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{i^{\circ}-1})^{t}.

(7.38)

The equalities (
7.37
) and (
7.38
) imply that
\mathbf{z}^{\circ}_{1}=-1.
Hence, we can apply the claims
(i)
and
(ii)
to
{\mathsf{K}}^{\circ}
and conclude that
k
is odd and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X^{\circ}[i|)=0\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;X^{\circ}\in{\mathsf{K}}^{\circ}.

(7.39)

Let
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}\left({\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}(X)\right)[i|)=0

by (
7.39
). Hence,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}X[i|)=0
by the property
(S3)
of
{\mathbf{S}}_{i^{\circ}}
. That is, the alternating row sum of every element in
{\mathsf{K}}
is equal to zero. This establishes the claim
(iii)
.

Sufficiency.

Assume that
k
is odd and
{\mathsf{K}}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
is a vector space consisting of all the matrices such that alternating sum of their rows is zero. It is clear that
\codim({\mathsf{K}})=k
.

Assume that
X\in{\mathsf{K}}
. Then
\mathbf{z}^{t}X=0
for
\mathbf{z}=(-1,1,\ldots,(-1)^{n})^{t}
by the definition of
{\mathsf{K}}
. Lemma
7.10
implies that in order to show that
\det_{n\,k}(X)=0
it is sufficient to check the condition (
7.22
) for every
c\in\binom{[n]}{k}
such that
1\not\in c
. For every such
c
we have

1+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\mathbf{z}_{c(\alpha)}(-1)^{\alpha-c(\alpha)}=1+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}(-1)^{c(\alpha)}(-1)^{\alpha-c(\alpha)}=1+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}(-1)^{\alpha}=1-1=0.

Therefore, the condition (
7.22
) is satisfied for every
c\in\binom{[n]}{k}
such that
1\not\in c
. This implies that
\det_{n\,k}(X)=0
for all
X\in{\mathsf{K}}.

∎

Further work

First, a natural continuation of this work is to complete the investigation by determining the vector spaces of
n\times k
matrices of maximal dimension that annihilate
\det_{n,k}
when
k
is even. As it is mentioned in the introduction, the only available information is that the codimension of such spaces is strictly larger than
k
. There is not only no hypothesis about the description of such spaces for even
k
of maximal dimension, but also no strict lower bound of codimension of such spaces. Any progress in this direction will be interesting.

Second, the found complete solution to the problem of maximal spaces annihilating the Cullis’ determinant could help to solve linear preserver problem for the Cullis’ determinant in the full generality, without any restriction on the ground field, in contrast to how it is done in
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
.
