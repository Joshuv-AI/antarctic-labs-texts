Defining Alzheimer’s disease: stipulations and the ethics of diagnostic change
Authors: Robin Michalon, Vincent Planche, Maël Lemoine, Lara Keuck, Nicolas Villain
Published: 2026

Abstract

Recent revisions of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) definitions by two leading research groups—the Alzheimer’s Association and the International Working Group—reflect divergent approaches: the former promotes a strictly biological definition, while the latter promotes a clinicalbiological construct. We contend that this emerging controversy is not merely semantic, but scientifically, clinically, and politically significant. Drawing on philosophical tools and situating the current debate within a broader historical context from the reconceptualization of AD in the 1970s onwards, we explore how definitions can serve as transformative instruments, acting as strategic bets that reshape scientific fields and clinical practices. Ultimately, we draw from the AD case study to argue for a critical reflection on the risks and promises of such definitional acts. We also propose a renewed attention to the 'ethics of stipulating' in the field of contemporary biomedical sciences.

Introduction

In response to advances in diagnostics and therapeutics, two major research groups specialising in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) have recently revised their definition and diagnostic criteria for the condition. While they concur on certain aspects—most notably, the centrality of amyloid and tau pathologies—the two groups have proposed different types of definition. The Alzheimer’s Association (AA) group asserts the following fundamental principle: “AD is defined by its unique neuropathologic findings; therefore, detection of AD neuropathologic change by biomarkers is equivalent to diagnosing the disease” 1(p.5145). This definition regards specific biological changes as the unique defining feature rather than a joint characteristic, together with specific symptoms, of a disease. In this framework, asymptomatic individuals can be diagnosed with ‘preclinical AD’. Conversely, the International Working Group (IWG) advocates a ‘clinical–biological construct’,2 wherein AD is diagnosed only in individuals exhibiting characteristic pathophysiological features and corresponding phenotypic expression (or a very high risk thereof). The IWG framework distinguishes two categories of asymptomatic individuals: those at low lifetime risk of developing clinical–biological AD, labelled ‘asymptomatic at risk for AD’, and those with a very high probability of clinical–biological AD, classified as ‘presymptomatic AD’.

What could be considered as an incipient controversy is emerging, as the IWG believes that the AA is proposing a new definition of AD, which is not aligned with its historical clinical–pathological concept. Some commentators have dismissed the divergence between the AA and IWG frameworks as ‘semantic differences’, framing the debate as a matter of effective ‘communication with patients and families’.3 However, while the controversy might be scrutinised as a matter of semantics, its grounds as well as its implications extend far beyond health professionals to patient communication. This article first establishes key conceptual tools for understanding definitional propositions and their place in AD’s scientific history before concluding with an analysis of the strategic choices regarding the current controversy on the redefinition of AD. It ends with a few propositions regarding the ethics of disease definitions drawn from the case study at hand.

Conceptual analysis and scientific change: a potted philosophical approach

Understanding the conceptual dimensions of this debate requires engaging with two central topics of philosophy of science: one concerned with conceptual analysis, that is, the analysis of the relation between words and their referents, and the other with scientific change, namely, how scientific knowledge unfolds.

Descriptive versus stipulative definitions

A definition is a statement intended to regulate the use of a term. In scientific and medical contexts, the rationale guiding the construction of such statements typically follows two distinct strategies.4 The first is retrospective and present oriented: it seeks clarification by aligning a term’s definition with its actual usage, usually as informed by empirical findings about the entity in question. In its ideal form, this approach yields what is known as a descriptive definition.

The second strategy is prospective and future oriented. It mobilises the transformative power of definitions to foster new forms of knowledge, to enhance practical engagement and to improve both individual and population health outcomes. Rather than describing how a term is used, it prescribes how it ought to be used. In its ideal form, this approach results in a stipulative definition. The rationality underlying stipulative definitions brings them close to certain forms of betting—such as wagering on stocks—where uncertainty is acknowledged as partly irreducible but nevertheless minimised, calculated and rationalised. They can also be portrayed as hypotheses guiding action. Stipulations are invariably animated by a positive vision of the future. Yet they also carry risks: of misdirecting research communities, squandering time and resources, delaying beneficial interventions or encouraging premature or inappropriate practices. Stipulations are thus inherently speculative, amounting to calculated risks that their proponents judge worth taking. A conceptual toolbox for understanding definitions in epistemological terms

What renders many scientific concepts complex is their frequent blending of descriptive and stipulative elements within a single definition. Over time, and across diverse scientific and medical traditions, the stipulative nature of certain definitional components may be overlooked or mistaken for descriptive fact. This phenomenon has been examined in the evolving definitions of malignancy, particularly in the case of thyroid cancer and AD itself.5 6

Moreover, assessing the trade-offs between potential benefits and harms is rarely straightforward. Prospectively, competing visions of the future may diverge with respect to what is deemed scientifically feasible or practically desirable. Retrospectively, judgments about whether a stipulation has yielded beneficial or detrimental outcomes may likewise vary according to the values brought to bear on its evaluation. This dual openness—both prospective and retrospective—of stipulative definitions underpins what we refer to as the ethics of stipulation (see Reflections on the ‘ethics of stipulation’). Underlying questions are: who defines diseases and who evaluates the outcomes of such definitions?

Scientific change

Descriptive and stipulative definitions can also be contrasted by examining the roles they play in the production of scientific knowledge. Notably, the emergence of a new theory is often enabled by an explicitly stipulative move—a deliberate, future-oriented proposal to use a term in a new way—which a scientific community may adopt or resist, depending on the theory’s perceived explanatory and heuristic payoff. If adopted, the stipulation functions as a working hypothesis: it sets an epistemic aim and delineates what counts, provisionally, as the phenomena to be tracked.

Here, ‘stipulation’ refers to the initial definitional wager, while ‘epistemic iteration’—following Chang7 (see table 2)—designates the downstream process of operationalisation and correction. Epistemic iterations typically follow a repeated cycle of application, testing and revision, in which the operative content of the definition is progressively recalibrated to better accommodate cases. During this phase, revisions largely take the form of descriptive definitions: adjustments aimed at improving case fit while preserving the original stipulative aim. Definitions are refined when their presuppositions are empirically supported and revised when new findings expose mismatches, heterogeneity or untenable assumptions. Throughout, epistemic iteration is constrained both by empirical results and by the future-oriented epistemic aim introduced by the initial stipulation. When this process stabilises at a formulation that satisfies that aim, one may speak of a ‘local optimum’ (table 2). The iterative process can also lead to a ‘local minimum’, a state where refinement and self-correction stabilise prematurely, failing to achieve broader epistemic goals such as improved prediction, treatment or prevention7 (see table 2). When a community judges that the current iterative dynamics will not allow reaching the initial epistemic objective, a new stipulation may be proposed and, if accepted, set a new iterative dynamic in movement.

A conceptual toolbox for understanding epistemic dynamics

The aforementioned trade-off between possible benefits and harms associated with a given stipulation can also be put in terms of such scientific dynamics. Stipulative definitions can have two positive epistemic effects. First, stipulations can redirect the iterative process, providing an escape from a local minimum, a state where refinement and self-correction stabilise prematurely, failing to achieve broader epistemic goals such as improved prediction, treatment or prevention. Second, stipulations can accelerate epistemic iterations by removing definitional constraints that slow progress. These constraints may be conceptual (such as an overly broad or narrow definition that hinders the cumulative development of knowledge) or material, when prior definitions demand prohibitively rare or expensive resources. In medicine, where disease definitions shape research, clinical practice and public health policy, this accelerative effect can expedite the implementation of new technologies.

However, the redirectional and accelerative properties of stipulations come with epistemic risks. Poorly chosen stipulations can lead epistemic iterations astray or hasten processes that result in harmful, wasteful or otherwise regrettable outcomes. Historical precedents can be quoted. The harmful redefinition of early stages of prostate cancer as the main cause of a rise in the levels of prostate-specific antigen8 may have led to considering some physiological transformation as pathogenic unduly and could have caused over-treatment with no real clinical benefit. Similarly, examples could be drawn from the history of papillary thyroid carcinoma,9 ductal carcinoma in situ and its current suggested downgrading to ‘idiopathic lesions of epithelial origin’.10 This concern is particularly pressing because stipulations are hard to retract unless they are immediately dismissed as delusional. The risk is that they are used as a bypass of a lack of specificity, while a conservative approach to a previous definition may hinder progress.

A short conceptual contemporary history of AD

The 1970s AD stipulation: AD is more than presenile dementia

The history of AD has been marked by two major stipulative moments. The first was catalysed by Katzman’s 1976 editorial,11 which equated what was then termed ‘senile dementia’ with ‘Alzheimer disease’, previously considered a rare form of presenile dementia—referred to in figure 2 as the early 20th century senile dementia stipulation. This shift, gaining traction in neurology and psychiatry, was operationalised through the 1984 McKhann criteria. At its core, this stipulation extended AD as a clinical–pathological construct to the late-onset cases: clinically, a ‘progressive deterioration of specific cognitive functions’—‘that occurs in middle or late life’. Pathologically, a ‘degeneration of specific nerve cells, presence of neuritic plaques and neurofibrillary tangles’ and the ‘alterations in transmitter-specific markers include forebrain cholinergic systems, and, in some cases, noradrenergic and somatostatinergic systems that innervate the telencephalon’.12 It is interesting to note that this last point is now much minimised via the aforementioned process of self-correction in epistemic iterations. Though meant to be restricted to research, these criteria proved to be widely used in clinical practice in the following decades.

Here, the stipulated wager was framed as follows: elderly people with cognitive—and sometimes behavioural—issues would be better understood and better cared for, in the present or the future, if their condition was no longer considered as a mere physiological and multidimensional ageing process or via a psycho-social conception of ‘mental health’.13 This draws mainly on the accelerative properties of stipulations. It is not that senile dementia research had come to a halt, but rather that, for a large segment of the 1970s American neurologists and psychiatrists, it was now considered too fuzzy a term to be efficiently studied and addressed effectively.

This initial stipulation proved highly successful, as it fostered dynamic and multifaceted lines of epistemic inquiry, catalysed by innovations in neuropsychology, clinical neurology, neuropathology, imaging, cellular and molecular biology and diagnostic assays. In clinical practice, for instance, the semiological description of AD has become more refined. It has been gradually demonstrated through clinical–anatomical correlations that AD is not uniformly and indistinctly associated with all cognitive and behavioural disorders but manifests as the common cause of specific syndromic entities, such as the hippocampal amnesic syndrome, logopenic primary progressive aphasia or posterior cortical atrophy.14 The clinical history of the disease and the description of symptom progression are now defined in a more fine-granular way, with the emergence, for example, of the concept of mild cognitive impairment allowing for the recognition and the study of a prodromal stage of the disease, before dementia (ie, when cognitive impairment is severe enough to impact activities of daily living). Other researchers concentrated on improving diagnostic methodologies, elucidating the disease’s epidemiology and identifying risk factors. This included identifying more precisely other causes of dementia and their prevalence in the general population. Meanwhile, an increasingly prominent avenue of investigation explored the neurobiology and pathophysiology of AD. Across all these domains, whether examining symptoms or lesions, defining AD as a distinct entity required integrating both its clinical presentation and pathological underpinnings.

Notably, from the 1990s onward, AD neurobiology became increasingly centred around the amyloid hypothesis, drawn from studies on autosomal dominant AD, then rapidly supported by consistent findings in animal models and the first evidence of an amyloid-targeting treatment that proved efficient in AD mouse models.15 This shift had multiple impacts, encompassing behavioural, ecological and pharmaceutical strategies for secondary prevention, particularly through early amyloid and/or tau clearance.

The progressive split leading to the amyloid stipulation

The formal recognition of a long-lasting disease’s pathophysiological progression triggered a scientific movement aiming for a reliable early diagnosis. This movement drew on the prognostic potential of biomarkers—using MRI, positron emission tomography (PET), cerebrospinal fluid analyses and others—and spurred successive refinements of AD diagnostic criteria by the IWG.14 16 These revisions did not challenge the clinical–pathological foundation established by the 1970s stipulation. The following epistemic iterations, however, led them to consider a new pathophysiological framework, the ‘probabilistic’ form of the amyloid cascade hypothesis,17 where:

‘The risk of progression to cognitive impairment in the asymptomatic at-risk population can be estimated by considering both markers of Alzheimer pathology (amyloid and tau) and other pathologies, including transactive response DNA binding protein 43 kDa (TDP-43), vascular abnormalities and Lewy bodies; resilience; lifetime and environmental factors; genetics; and other biomarker risk factors.”2

The probabilistic amyloid cascade model suggests a nuanced classification of AD, distinguishing three variants: autosomal dominant AD, Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4-related sporadic AD and APOE ε4-unrelated sporadic AD. Each variant exhibits a progressive decline in penetrance and in the influence of the amyloid pathophysiological cascade, while the impact of stochastic factors such as environmental exposures and lower-risk genes correspondingly increases. According to this model, amyloid pathology is necessary but not sufficient to define AD. In the same conceptual vein, it has been proposed that asymptomatic cerebral proteinopathies encompass multiple discrete nosological entities with heterogeneous clinical outcomes rather than forming a disease continuum with stages unrelated to individual prognoses.18 It is suggested that these new nosological entities would allow for a better operational management of asymptomatic individuals exhibiting abnormal cerebral proteinopathies without altering the historical definition of neurodegenerative diseases as clinical–pathological entities, and their associated public health policies. This constitutes an additional iteration leading to an increased local optimum aligned with the 1970 clinical–pathological concept.

The 2011 National Institute on Aging (NIA)–AA criteria mark a significant departure, redefining AD solely based on amyloid pathology, independent of clinical manifestation.19 At first, this constitutes a form of stipulation which, however, was cautious and aimed to apply to research and not clinical practice. In 2024, this proposition was extended to inform clinical practice, while providing the operational disclaimer that this should not be used in cognitively unimpaired people. The disease concept seems to have evolved a little bit from being amyloid anchored ‘to (being) a biological process that begins with the appearance of AD neuropathologic change’.1 This is purely a semantic subtlety since the first AD neuropathologic changes correspond to a Thal amyloid plaque score of 1, including neuritic and non-neuritic β-amyloid plaques, with or without neurofibrillary tangles.20 According to this approach, amyloid pathology is necessary and sufficient to define AD. The operationalisation of these criteria, ie, the practical criteria informed by available biomarkers, is anchored at amyloid PET that detects only higher levels of amyloid pathology, more frequently associated with neurofibrillary tangles.

It is essential to recognise the commonalities between the 1970s and the contemporary moments of stipulation. Neither of those was prompted by an immediate and fundamental breakthrough in knowledge associated with a new definition of AD. The similarity between the lesions found in the brains of individuals with early-onset AD and those with late-onset dementia was well established as early as the beginning of the 20th century.21 Likewise, since the late 1990s, the central role of brain β-amyloidosis in the disease’s pathophysiology has been widely acknowledged.

Both stipulations emerged within contexts shaped less by a singular scientific breakthrough than by the gradual accumulation of concurrent findings and, more decisively, by the articulation of novel possibilities for intervention. In the 1980s, this was primarily driven by the new possibility of clinical trials, reinforced by the development of the cholinergic hypothesis, which followed in the footsteps of major therapeutic innovations such as dopaminergic interventions in Parkinson’s disease. In the 2020s, it is the anticipated availability of potent therapeutics capable of amyloid clearance and a demonstrable preventive effect on cognitive decline, alongside the potential clinical integration of blood-based biomarkers, following in the footsteps of early interventions in other fields of medicine, such as cancer or diabetes, that offer novel possibilities for intervention.

Challenges of stipulating new (Alzheimer’s) disease definitions

As explained above, stipulating a new definition for a disease always carries risks and challenges. While the 1970s stipulation was highly productive for biomedical research into AD, it was not without critics—particularly within social and biogerontology22 and epidemiology.23 As one does with stipulations, these critiques did not fundamentally challenge the definition of AD itself but rather its broader implications. Moreira, for instance, showed how biogerontologists argued that the conceptual framework of AD diverted attention and funding away from ageing as a whole.22 At the same time, Brayne et al highlight how it overshadowed other causes of dementia.24

It is also conceivable that the stipulation formulated in the 1970s delayed the development of alternative lines of research, particularly those focused on late-life neurodegenerative dementias. The identification of non-amyloid amnestic neurodegenerative conditions, such as the limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy or the primary age-related tauopathy, is a relatively recent development. The growing attention to AD copathologies25 reflects the same evolution, especially in relation to its increasing importance in late-onset forms of AD.26 These critical perspectives raise the question of whether the historical subsumption of senile dementia under the AD label has distorted our understanding of both normal and pathological brain ageing, ultimately shaping approaches to intervention. Taken further, such reflections underscore a fundamental tension surrounding ageing, AD, copathologies and comorbidities. On the one hand, investigating each proteinopathic pathway in isolation may hinder our ability to grasp the interactive dynamics characteristic of copathologies, particularly since mixed forms are far more frequent than ‘pure’ monopathological cases. On the other hand, reverting to a broad conceptual model akin to the Kraepelinian ‘senile dementia’, encompassing a heterogeneous array of pathological mechanisms and clinical manifestations, risks generating overly diffuse research goals and clinically impractical categories.

Besides, some of the epistemic iterations of the 1970s’ stipulation of AD as a bioclinical construct also led to local minima, which have come to appear as dead ends of research. Lines of inquiry on the cholinergic theory of AD27 (typical of the 1980s and 1990s), or along the prototyping of early AD around the hippocampal syndrome in the early 2000s, proved insufficient to operationalise the use of emerging protein-targeting therapies.

Such examples, drawn from the recent history of AD research, underscore a specific understanding of the concepts of stipulations and iterations. Both concepts, as they are used in this article, entail the theoretical impossibility of determining a priori how rapidly a given stipulation will lead either to the realisation of favourable outcomes or to entrapment within a local minimum. Likewise, researchers can hardly ever ascertain with certainty that their line of inquiry has reached such a local minimum until a new cycle of iteration is set in motion by a subsequent stipulation. This impossibility of prospectively assessing the epistemic endpoints of stipulations lies at the heart of the controversy discussed here.

Yet anticipatory reasoning within the act of stipulating disease definitions remains possible on two distinct levels. One can both (1) evaluate the visions of the future that a given stipulation implicitly carries and (2) cultivate a form of reflexive thought regarding the very practice of stipulation, mindful of the high epistemic and practical stakes such definitional gestures entail. Drawing on our collective experience with the 2011 NIA–AA stipulation and its 2024 clinical prolongation, the remainder of this article explores how such prospective reflexivity might be effectively implemented.

Assessing visions of the future

There are risks and difficulties entailed with working with the ‘probabilistic amyloid cascade hypothesis’. One of them lies, specifically, in that it is not clear what kind of framework for intervention and evaluation it entails. Working with yet another subdivision of AD variants (encompassing a whole range of stochastic factors and thus equating, on a similar conceptual level, air pollution, head traumas or brain amyloidosis) may risk dispersion of research efforts, thereby potentially limiting successful research, treatment or prevention.

In general, the clinical–pathological approach entails working with a greater level of complexity, making it more challenging to envision the future. Still, from a medical intervention perspective, the IWG advocates for the development of Brain Health Services for Dementia Prevention—an approach that aligns both with the clinical–pathological framework of AD by recognising pathology as a modifiable risk factor. This strategy lays the foundation for personalised, disease-modifying prevention therapies tailored to individuals both cognitively unimpaired and with incipient cognitive impairment while addressing the multifaceted factors that contribute to risk. This approach is comparable to the WHO 2019 guidelines for risk reduction of cognitive decline and dementia. Though initial implementations of such programmes have been described,28 they need refinement. Such a level of complexity also makes it hard to fathom the scalability of these specialised clinics for brain health, which may require state-of-the-art diagnostics and highly educated physicians, and thus costly infrastructures.

This is strikingly in contrast with the NIA–AA amyloid stipulation proposition which, as it draws on the simplifying function of stipulation, makes the core of its encompassed vision of the future very clear. Its premise is that a world without amyloid aggregation in the brain is preferable. It favours all forms of transformations that may lead, as quickly as possible, to the greatest amyloid clearance possible or the prevention of its accumulation. This intuition rests on solid epistemic foundations, given the well-established role of brain β-amyloidosis in the disease’s pathogenesis. Thus, it seems plausible that some form of general amyloid clearance will benefit a considerable share of AD-afflicted individuals clinically. However, it also presents several costs, risks and challenges that must be carefully considered. In this case, a focus on amyloid could obscure other critical biological factors involved in Alzheimer’s pathogenesis and pathophysiology, as suggested by GWAS studies.29 The therapeutic potential of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists, blarcamesine and anti-varicella zoster virus vaccine in AD also illustrates the importance of an approach that extends beyond the amyloid cascade hypothesis.30 This raises questions about future drug development: will a lack of efficacy in the amyloid pathway during early phases be sufficient to abandon a drug development programme, and should this lead to a reassessment of the stipulation?

Another challenge lies in the validation of diagnostic, prognostic and therapeutic tools, as well as in assessing treatment efficacy. This issue is exemplified by the recent proposition of key opinion leaders in the AD field that suggests anti-amyloid antibodies should be approved on the basis of ‘robust and significant amyloid removal’.31 Prioritising amyloid-focused technologies may expedite regulatory approval while failing to capture the full complexity of disease trajectories. However, this fails to consider GRADUATE 1 and 2 phase III trials of gantenerumab in early AD, where significant amyloid PET clearance (~57–48 centiloids) did not translate into clinical efficacy.32 33

Beyond scientific and clinical considerations, amyloid-based diagnosis entails significant public health and ethical implications. Early-stage screening requires the development of a sophisticated infrastructure—one that ensures accurate and well-informed clinical interpretation, ethically sound risk-disclosure practices and appropriate follow-up procedures. As for other precision medicine innovation implementations, it entails the risk of privileging well-educated people with sufficient resources, potentially fostering structural injustice.34 Furthermore, as emphasised by the IWG, as long as the concept of AD continues to evoke the anxious representations currently associated with it, very early diagnosis may provoke psychological distress and could impact an individual’s employability and insurability in profound ways.

A deeper challenge, however, lies in the fact that a substantial proportion of amyloid-positive individuals never develop dementia symptoms in their lifetime and may therefore not require treatment. Whereas taking into account the risk associated with imposing treatment on individuals who will not develop any disease during their lifetime is common to all preventative approaches in medicine, this is all the more problematic if available therapies entail significant risks and costs, such as amyloid-targeting monoclonal antibodies. At the population level, the amyloid stipulation demands a fundamental shift in the understanding of AD. The AA framework posits that any presence of AD lesions corresponds to an AD diagnosis. If taken in absolute terms, irrespective of the technological methods used to determine amyloid positivity, this would imply that over 80% of individuals aged 80 and above qualify for an AD diagnosis.35 Among those 80%, the vast majority have low levels of pathology and will never develop symptoms in their lifetime.36 Given this expansive classification, how sustainable is it to continue portraying AD as one of the greatest contemporary public health challenges, when most of those diagnosed may never develop symptoms? The absence of discussion regarding the ‘dilution of gravity’ in the AA framework may indicate an implicit acknowledgement of the ‘health politics of anguish’, a term coined to qualify the early 1980s’ Alzheimer advocacy movement.13 This approach seems to highlight the urgency surrounding AD’s prevalence while perhaps not fully exploring its more complex implications.

Reflections on the ‘ethics of stipulation’

It may still be too early to fully weigh the pros and cons of either extending the 1970s disease stipulation (although transforming it via epistemic iterations) or adopting the amyloid-based stipulation. Regarding the former option, as previously noted, the relevance and efficacy of integrating diverse diagnostic and preventive approaches within specialised clinics for brain health remains to be demonstrated. As for the latter, the implications of the definitional gamble it entails have yet to be fully revealed. In this light, the coexistence of the two trajectories within the controversy may in fact prove epistemically productive.

However, approaching the issue through the lens of stipulation may offer a productive framework for articulating the forms of reflexive thinking that could underpin an ‘ethics of stipulating’. Stipulations are potent epistemic operations capable of redirecting research agendas, redefining clinical practices or public health policies. An ethical framing foregrounds the responsibility borne by expert groups empowered to introduce new disease definitions and to see those definitions taken seriously across scientific, clinical and public domains.

This orientation is consistent with a growing body of literature that critiques not the substance of the IWG and the NIA–AA/AA AD definitions per se, but rather how they have been introduced and implemented over the past decades. Concerns include the lack of systematic justification for introducing new disease definitions, and the absence of clear answers to critiques of the previous criteria; the impact of conceptual confusion on the comparability across studies and the mutual understanding among the various classification users; and the composition of criteria-setting committees, which are often marked by the dominance of neurobiologists or by close ties to pharmaceutical companies.37 38

A conceptual distinction between descriptive definitions and stipulations allows for a finer form of reflexivity regarding processes of disease definition. Three such operations of reflexive thinking deserve emphasis.

The first involves explicitly recognising the stipulative dimension in any definitional proposal, that is, the ratio of stipulation to description. This can be articulated through a guiding question: To what extent does the proposed definition function as a transformative wager, not grounded in the consolidation of recent empirical findings, but rather aimed at reshaping clinical and research practices in line with a specific vision of the future? Is one strictly describing, or actually stipulating? When a definition is highly stipulative, its proponents should transparently articulate the entailed visions of the future, both its anticipated benefits and inherent risks, presenting these considerations as part of the stipulation itself. One factor to consider when anticipating its inherent risks and benefits is the ‘Tower of Babel’ effect. Some may be connected to the discrepancy between the stipulated definition and representations currently associated with the disease.39 Others again relate to how the stipulation impacts already existing research programmes from associated disciplines. What are the costs and what are the benefits of a definitional hegemony regarding amyloid positivity?

Second, reflecting on stipulations also invites a reconsideration of the justifications for a new stipulative definition. In the field of Alzheimer’s research, such definitions are often introduced in tandem with technological and pharmaceutical implementation processes, giving the impression that they are imposed on the scientific community. Yet, it is essential to reflect on the conditions under which new stipulations are formulated—ideally as early as possible in the translational research process.

The third reflexive operation concerns the evaluation of a stipulation’s success a posteriori. Given their inherent inertia, stipulations are difficult to reverse. This resistance is partly due to the way stipulated entities tend to be naturalised by the field, gradually treated as if they were uncontroversial natural kinds. One way to address this challenge is to operationalise the vision of the future embedded in the stipulation: to define success criteria in advance or, at minimum, to anticipate the need for structured moments of assessment and recalibration.

This brings us back to a central question: who judges the success of a stipulation, and on what procedural grounds is that judgement legitimate? We are aware that we are not completely disinterested parties in the unfolding controversy. We aim mainly to prompt a debate about the norms and principles that should guide organisations involved in selecting and promoting stipulative disease definitions. In AD, neither the IWG nor the AA can act as a standing, structurally independent, membership-governed body with a formal mandate to steward nosology and diagnostic criteria over time. Instead, criteria are typically produced by ad hoc expert workgroups, whose composition is justified by expertise and stakeholder balance, including academic–industry representation. This arrangement generates a governance tension when the convening organisation also assumes an explicit advocacy role in accelerating market approval for treatments—often through affiliated policy and lobbying infrastructures—thereby risking the perception that the same actors serve simultaneously as authors and arbiters of the definitional wagers that underpin particular regulatory pathways.

By contrast, learnt societies ground their authority in professional membership and elected leadership, while delegating criteria development to time-limited task forces appointed through that governance chain. This architecture more clearly distinguishes between member mandate and expert drafting, even when such societies also act as advisers to regulators and governments. A modern form of such a ‘learnt society’ should include heterogeneous forms of expertise, including ones about care and disease experience. We therefore argue that future AD stipulations would benefit from an institutional ‘constitutional’ design that (1) is structurally independent of commercial and lobbying dependencies, (2) incorporates transparent appointment procedures alongside formal mechanisms for internal dissent and external critique and (3) ensures substantive interdisciplinarity and deliberative inclusion of patients and caregivers—so that epistemic iteration remains not only scientifically cumulative, but also procedurally legitimate.
