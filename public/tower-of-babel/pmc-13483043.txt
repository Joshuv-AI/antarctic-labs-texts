Interferon-gamma release assay screening before solid organ transplantation in a low-incidence country: a prospective single-center cohort study
Authors: Thomas Helbo, Sebastian Rask Hamm, Anna Katrine Haslund Roed, Finn Gustafsson, Søren Schwartz Sørensen, Michael Perch, Kristian Schaumburg Kiim, Åse Bengård Andersen, Moises Alberto Suarez-Zdunek, Susanne Dam Nielsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The risk of tuberculosis is increased after solid organ transplantation (SOT) and associated with morbidity and mortality. Pre-transplantation screening and treatment for tuberculosis infection (TBI) is recommended. Aims of this prospective cohort study were determining the prevalence of positive pre-transplantation interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), risk factors for a positive result, and describe TBI management. We included SOT candidates from 2020 to 2023 at Rigshospitalet, Denmark with a pre-transplantation IGRA. Data were collected from records and the Danish Microbiology Database. Of 586 patients, 564 (96%) had a pre-transplantation IGRA. Twenty-two (4%) had positive, 537 (95%) negative, and 5 (1%) indeterminate results. Risk factors for a positive IGRA were diabetes (14/480 (3%) without diabetes, 8/84 (10%) with diabetes, aOR 3.07, 95% CI 1.14–7.78), birth in other low-incidence countries (4/24 (17%) vs. 8/463 (2%), aOR 10.14, CI 2.48–35.95) or medium-/high-incidence countries (10/77 (13%), aOR 8.22, CI 3.11–22.43), and alcohol use (aOR 2.75, CI 1.04–7.15). Seventeen patients had TBI; 16 (94%) treated with isoniazid for 6 months, one (6%) with rifampicin for 4 months; treatment discontinued in one case. In conclusion, IGRA screening coverage was high. Positive IGRAs were associated with birth in other low- or medium-/high-incidence countries, diabetes, alcohol use. Introduction

Worldwide, 7.5 million people were diagnosed with active tuberculosis (TB) in 2022, and 1.3 million died, making TB the second leading cause of death among infectious agents1. Denmark is a low-incidence country with a population of 5.9 million people and a TB rate of 3.9 cases per 100,000 inhabitants per year, with 75% of cases in first- or second-generation immigrants2,3. Solid organ transplantation (SOT) and subsequent immunosuppression increases the risk of TB4.

SOT appears to increase the risk of active TB by reactivation of tuberculosis infection (TBI), previously termed latent tuberculosis infection, at least in low-incidence settings5,6. A recent nationwide study found a TBI prevalence in Denmark of approximately 3% in patients screened before commencing biological anti-inflammatory treatment7. SOT recipients at our centre, Copenhagen University Hospital, Rigshospitalet, a national and regional transplantation centre, have previously been found to have an incidence rate of active TB about 8.5 times higher than that in the general Danish population8. TB among SOT recipients may have an atypical presentation, with a higher rate of disseminated disease at the time of diagnosis and a higher mortality5,9. Furthermore, TB treatment is a challenge in a transplantation setting due to extensive drug-drug interactions especially of rifampicin, and there has been concerns about the hepatotoxicity of anti-tuberculous drugs, especially of isoniazid10,11. Thus, TBI screening and treatment before SOT is recommended in international12,13 and Danish14 guidelines. At Rigshospitalet, systematic IGRA screening of SOT candidates was introduced in 2020 with the establishment of a vaccination clinic for SOT candidates at the Department of Infectious Diseases.

While SOT increases the risk of active TB, end-stage organ failure and potential immunosuppressive therapy negatively affects IGRA reliability. Therefore, other studies have advised against relying too heavily on pre-transplantation screening without considering a patient’s individualised risk profile with attention to the TB rate in the country of birth, symptoms, and chest imaging15–18.

Despite extensive literature on TB risk after SOT, contemporary data describing pre-transplantation IGRA screening in low-incidence European settings remain limited. Recent work has highlighted gaps in understanding of TB epidemiology among SOT candidates in low-burden countries. In an American study of participants with a positive IGRA, only a minority of patients had documented TB epidemiologic risk factors19, emphasizing the need for improved characterization of TB epidemiology among SOT candidates in low-incidence settings. This study did not report on IGRA-negative patients, and therefore did now allow assessment of factors associated with IGRA positivity. Which clinical and demographic factors are associated with IGRA positivity in contemporary SOT candidates in low-incidence settings, especially in Europe, remains insufficiently studied19.

The overall purpose of this prospective cohort study was to evaluate systematic pre-transplantation IGRA screening of SOT recipients at Rigshospitalet after implementation of a vaccination clinic for SOT candidates. We aimed to investigate the coverage of IGRA screening, to determine the prevalence of a positive IGRA result as well as factors associated with a positive result, and to describe the management of TBI among SOT candidates at our centre.

Materials and methods

Setting

Copenhagen University Hospital, Rigshospitalet in the Capital Region of Denmark is a regional (kidney, heart) and national (liver, lung, pancreas, multiorgan) transplantation centre, which also serves Greenland and the Faroe Islands. As of March 1, 2020, candidates for liver, lung, and heart transplantation are referred to a consultation with an infectious disease (ID) physician at the vaccination clinic at the Department of Infectious Diseases before transplantation listing20. Since January 2023, kidney transplant recipients are also routinely referred to ID consultations. The primary purpose of the ID consultations is to plan and administer vaccinations per guidelines, but patients are also screened for TBI with an IGRA. The IGRA (QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus) is collected and analysed pre-consultation at Rigshospitalet or at a referring hospital, or it is sent to the national tuberculosis reference laboratory for analysis21.

Study design

This study is a single-centre prospective cohort study, conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines22.

We included liver, heart, lung, and kidney (including simultaneous pancreas; SPK) transplantation candidates ≥ 18 years old who underwent a pre-transplantation work-up program including an ID consultation, irrespective of whether the patient was transplanted subsequently. We included patients with an ID consultation date between January 1, 2020, and August 1, 2023. If the patient was transplanted more than once during this period, we included only the latest transplantation.

Analysing IGRA screening coverage, we included all patients fulfilling the above criteria. Analysing the distribution of IGRA results and risk factors for a positive pre-transplantation IGRA, we included all screened patients.

Transplanted patients were followed from the date of transplantation until date of data collection (February 14, 2024 – June 22, 2024), or death, to observe for active TB post-transplantation.

TBI treatment

In accordance with Danish14 and international guidelines and consensus statements13,23, TBI treatment was initiated following a positive IGRA result and/or based on an individual risk assessment by a specialist in infectious diseases. Prior to TBI treatment, active TB was ruled out by a medical examination, interview, and chest imaging. The preferred treatment choice for TBI was monotherapy with isoniazid 300 mg + pyridoxin 20 mg once daily for 6 months. However, isoniazid 300 mg + pyridoxin 20 mg + rifampicin 600 mg once daily for 3 months and rifampicin 600 mg once daily for 4 months were considered equivalent treatment regimens. Preferably, at least one full month of treatment was administered before transplantation and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. During treatment, patients were followed monthly to evaluate compliance, side effects and medication changes. Follow-up included assessment of liver biochemistry.

Data collection

A REDCap database hosted at the Capital Region of Denmark was used for handling of study data. From electronic patient record systems covering all hospitals in Denmark, we collected data on demographic and transplantation-related variables, pre-transplantation comorbidities, alcohol and smoking history, use of immunosuppressive therapy at the time of IGRA collection, and any treatment of TB or TBI. This included the treatment regimen, date of treatment initiation, side effects, and treatment pausing or discontinuation. IGRA and all microbiology results were collected from the Danish Microbiology Database (MiBa), a nationwide database containing all Danish microbiology results from hospitals and general practice since 201124.

Definitions

If the patient had more than one IGRA result, we used the IGRA result closest to the ID consultation, unless the patient had both a positive and negative test result, in which case we used the first determinate result. If the IGRA result was indeterminate, we used the next determinate result, if any. We considered patients to be unscreened if they had no pre-transplantation IGRA, or if ≥ 2 years passed between IGRA collection and ID consultation.

Country of birth was categorised as ‘Denmark’; ‘Other low-incidence countries’ including other countries with TB incidence rates < 10.0 per 100.100 per year, and ‘Medium-/high-incidence countries’ including countries with TB incidence rates ≥ 10.0 per 100.000 per year (2022)25.

Country of birth was presumed to be Denmark unless otherwise recorded in the medical record.

Side effects were noted as recorded by the treating physician in the medical record. We recorded drug-induced hepatotoxicity if it led to pausing or discontinuation of treatment.

Alcohol use was considered as habitual consumption of ≥ 1 unit of alcohol per week as noted in the medical record. Alcohol misuse included any history of excessive alcohol consumption.

Statistics

Baseline characteristics, IGRA results, and TBI treatment were reported using descriptive statistics. Continuous data were reported with medians and inter-quartile ranges (IQRs), and comparisons done using Mann-Whitney U-test. Categorical data were reported with proportions, and comparisons done with χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Pre-, and post-transplantation follow-up time (from transplantation until date of data collection or death) were reported with medians and inter-quartile ranges.

The primary outcome of interest was the pre-transplantation IGRA result, positive vs. non-positive. To determine whether any variables were associated with higher odds of a positive result, we performed unadjusted logistic regression for each of the variables. The variables were age, sex, country of birth, SOT type, immunosuppressive therapy at the time of IGRA collection, smoking status, alcohol use, previous alcohol misuse, pre-transplantation diabetes, and pre-transplantation dialysis. In adjusted analyses, we analysed each variable one at a time while adjusting for variables showing significant associations with the IGRA result in the unadjusted analyses. In a sensitivity analysis, we omitted the indeterminate IGRA results instead of combining them with negative results in a non-positive group Two-sided p-values of ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for data analysis.

Ethical statement

This study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent for data retrieval was waived by the institutional review board at the Centre for Regional Development on behalf of the Danish Data Protection Agency (R-20051155) in accordance with Danish legislation. The study was approved by the institutional review board at the Centre for Regional Development.

Results

IGRA coverage

Between January 1, 2020, and August 1, 2023, a total of 586 patients had ID consultations as part of their pre-transplantation vaccination program for liver, heart, lung, or kidney transplantation including SPK (Fig. 1). Twenty-two patients were not screened with an IGRA; the screening coverage was 564/586 (96%).

Of 22 unscreened patients, 18 (82%) had no IGRA at all. The remaining 4 (18%) patients either had an IGRA post-transplantation, or ≥ 2 years passed between the IGRA and the ID consultation.

Baseline characteristics

Of 564 study participants who had a pre-transplantation IGRA (Fig. 1), 369 (65%) had been transplanted at the time of follow-up data collection with a median time from IGRA to transplantation of 150 days (IQR 71–296). Of these SOT recipients, 346 (94%) had their ID consultation pre-transplantation, while 23 (6%) had their ID consultation post-transplantation. Patients who had a post-transplantation ID consultation were either kidney transplant recipients who were only included in the vaccination clinic setup in 2023, or patients on the waiting list for a short time mainly because of emergency transplantation. The remaining 195/564 (35%) patients were SOT candidates who had not been transplanted at the time of follow-up data collection (Supplementary Table 1).

Baseline characteristics of all patients with a pre-transplantation IGRA result are shown in Table 1. Overall, 346/564 (61%) were male with a median age of 53 years (IQR 45–61). In total, 229/564 (41%) were kidney (including SPK) transplant candidates or recipients, followed by 199 (35%) liver, 97 (17%) lung, and 39 (7%) heart transplant candidates or recipients. A total of 61/564 (11%) were re-transplantations. Overall, 463/564 (82%) of patients were born in Denmark, 24/564 (4%) in other TB low-incidence countries, and 77/564 (14%) in TB medium-/high-incidence countries.

IGRA results

Of 564 patients, 537 (95%) had a negative pre-transplantation IGRA result, 22 (4%) had a positive result, and 5 (1%) only had an indeterminate result. The five patients with indeterminate IGRAs were either following two indeterminate IGRAs deemed at low risk of tuberculosis infection, or the test was not repeated because of the treating clinician’s decision.

Among patients with a positive pre-transplantation IGRA compared to patients with a negative IGRA, a higher proportion were born in other TB low-incidence countries than Denmark (18% vs. 4%) or in medium-/high-incidence countries (45% vs. 12%), p < 0.001. Among IGRA positive patients from other low-incidence countries, 3/4 (75%) had other traditional risk factors or previous TB. A higher proportion of patients with a positive IGRA had pre-transplantation diabetes mellitus (36% vs. 14%), p = 0.013. A higher proportion of patients with indeterminate results were heart transplant recipients, although numbers were very low (n = 3). In contrast, we found no significant differences between the proportions of patients with positive, negative or indeterminate pre-transplantation IGRAs with regards to sex, age, pre-transplantation dialysis, alcohol and smoking history, or whether the patient received immunosuppressive therapy at the time of IGRA collection (Table 1).

The proportion with a positive IGRA was 14/480 (3%) among patients without diabetes, 8/84 (10%) among patients with diabetes, 8/463 (2%) among patients born in Denmark, 4/24 (17%) for patients from other low-incidence countries, and 10/77 (13%) for patients from medium-/high-incidence countries.

Risk factor analyses

In unadjusted logistic regression analyses, we found higher odds of a positive pre-transplantation IGRA in patients born in other TB low-incidence countries than Denmark (OR 11.37, 95% CI 2.85–39.47, p < 0.001) or in medium-/high-incidence countries (OR 8.49, 95% CI 3.24–22.97, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Likewise, we found higher odds of a positive IGRA in patients with pre-transplantation diabetes (OR 3.50, 95% CI 1.36–8.47, p = 0.006). We found no significant association between the IGRA result and age, sex, SOT type, whether the candidate was listed for a re-transplantation, immunosuppressive therapy at time of IGRA collection, smoking or alcohol use or previous misuse, or pre-transplantation dialysis.

In adjusted analyses, risk factors of a positive IGRA were being born in other TB low-incidence countries (aOR 10.14, 95% CI 2.48–35.95, p < 0.001) or in TB medium-/high-incidence countries (aOR 8.22, 95% CI 3.11–22.43, p < 0.001), as well as pre-transplantation diabetes (aOR 3.07, CI 1.14–7.78, p = 0.020) and pre-transplantation alcohol use (aOR 2.75, 95% CI 1.04–7.15) (Fig. 2). Country of birth and pre-transplantation diabetes were only adjusted for each other, while the remaining variables were adjusted for these two variables.

Sensitivity analysis

In a sensitivity analysis we omitted patients with an indeterminate IGRA result instead of categorising these with negative results in a non-positive group. This did not change the results (Supplementary Table 2).

Characteristics of patients with positive IGRAs, and treatment for tuberculosis infection

Fourteen out of 22 patients with positive pre-transplantation IGRAs were born outside of Denmark (64%). Of the patients born in Denmark, only two (25%) had a suspected TB exposure (Table 2). Seventeen patients (77%) were considered to have TBI and were treated, while 5 (23%) were considered not to have TBI either because of previously adequately treated active TB, previous infections with nontuberculous mycobacteria, or borderline positive IGRAs that were followed by a negative IGRA upon retesting.

Of the 17 patients treated for TBI, 16 (94%) received isoniazid for 6 months, and one (6%) was treated with rifampicin for 4 months. Treatment was completed in 16 cases (88%), with only one case of treatment discontinuation due to pancytopenia attributed to isoniazid myelosuppression. Treatment was paused for one patient (6%) due to side effects including increased thirst and headache. Including these two cases, possible side effects were reported among 6/17 (35%) treated patients. The other reported side effects included nausea, diarrhoea, dizziness, and drug rash. There were no reports of hepatotoxicity.

The median follow-up time from transplantation was 425 days (IQR 261–887 days). Most (71%) patients completed their treatment before transplantation. None of the patients developed active TB during their post-transplantation follow-up within the observed follow-up period.

Discussion

In a setting of near-universal (96%) pre-transplantation IGRA screening, we found 4% of patients to have a positive, 95% a negative, and 1% an indeterminate IGRA result. A positive IGRA result was associated both with being born in other TB low-incidence countries than Denmark or in TB medium-/high-incidence countries as well as pre-transplantation diabetes and alcohol use in risk factor analyses. When adjusting for country of birth and diabetes, there was a significant association between alcohol use and a positive IGRA. We found no association between IGRA result and the remaining demographic, clinical, or transplantation-related variables. Seventeen of 22 patients with positive IGRAs (77%) were treated for TBI. There were no reports of hepatotoxicity, and only one case of treatment discontinuation. There were no cases of post-transplantation active TB.

The prevalence of positive IGRAs in this study population is in alignment with a recent nationwide Danish study including patients with inflammatory bowel and rheumatic disease. This study calculated a weighted prevalence estimate of a positive IGRA in the general Danish population to be 3.8%7. These results indicate that the SOT candidates in our study were not at greater risk of having a positive IGRA than patients with inflammatory diseases or the general Danish population.

The proportion of indeterminate IGRAs at 1% was low compared to other studies26,27. This may partly be due to repeat testing. In case of an indeterminate result, the standard was to repeat the QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus. Of the 5 indeterminate results, n = 3 had one indeterminate result, and n = 2 had two indeterminate results. Also, autoimmune disease as the reason for liver transplantation is high in this cohort at about 45%28, further lowering the chance of indeterminate results. Immune dysfunction in SOT candidates might also lead to false-negative results due to reduced mitogen responsiveness. At our centre it is now standard practice to do chest imaging in case of an indeterminate IGRA result. Several authors have highlighted the importance of radiographic findings suggestive of prior tuberculosis infection, both for TBI diagnosis and treatment evaluation, particularly in immunosuppressed patients23,29. However, in low-incidence settings, such findings are often unspecific.

We found a strong association between being born in TB medium-/high-incidence countries and a positive IGRA result, which was fully expected and corroborate previous findings7,8. We found an equally strong association between being born in other low-incidence countries than Denmark and a positive IGRA result. This might be explained by travel patterns or socio-economic factors of these patients, but this finding may also be vulnerable to bias, as being born abroad might be more likely to be registered in the medical record, or noticed when data were collected if the IGRA was positive.

We also found a significant association between pre-transplantation diabetes and a positive IGRA. Evidence of an association between TBI and diabetes has previously been presented in systematic reviews and meta-analyses. A newer systematic review including only adjusted estimates reported a pooled adjusted risk ratio of 1.26 (95% CI 0.71–2.23) and a pooled adjusted odds ratio of 1.21 (95% CI 1.14–1.29)30. The association has also been replicated in TB high-incidence settings31. Diabetes has been proposed to increase susceptibility to TBI through various mechanisms, including innate and adaptive immunological deficiencies32. Although the association could be confounded by country of birth, we found both variables to be independently associated with a positive IGRA in the adjusted analysis.

In the adjusted analysis, we found an association between pre-transplantation alcohol use and a positive IGRA. An association between high-level alcohol use and TBI has been reported in different settings33. However, we did not find an association between IGRA result and previous alcohol misuse. These findings should be interpreted with caution as transplant candidates are advised against alcohol consumption, and alcohol use may be under-reported.

We found no significant association between SOT type and the IGRA result. A higher prevalence of TBI has been reported among kidney than among liver transplant candidates in a systematic review and meta-analysis34. However, these results are not comparable with our findings due to considerably higher reported TBI prevalence rates of 26% overall. Furthermore, we found no association with sex and age. Male sex and higher age have been shown to be associated with TBI7,35. Our findings were likely due to a lack of power, as well as the age range of our study population being somewhat narrow.

In our study, 8/22 (36%) patients with positive pre-transplantation IGRAs were born in Denmark, and only 2 (25%) of these had a suspected TB exposure. This finding seems to warrant IGRA screening for all patients, regardless of country of birth or suspected TB exposure. A large American study from 2024 also found TB epidemiologic risk factors in only 125/629 (20%) of SOT recipients with positive IGRAs, including being born or having lived in TB high burden countries19. A recent European TBnet study did, however, find a low risk of progression to active TB in low-incidence settings without clear exposure risk factors even among immunocompromised individuals, though people with HIV appear to be at increased risk27.

Before systematic pre-transplantation IGRA screening, our centre found only 4 cases of post-transplantation active TB among 1,989 SOT recipients. Three out of these four cases were among patients born in TB medium-/high-endemic countries8. We found no cases of active TB post-transplantation in this cohort, and while systematic screening and treatment of TBI has been shown to be effective in reducing the risk of post-transplantation TB36,37, a 2018 review of post-transplantation TB found a median time to presentation of approximately 20 months5.Our median post-transplantation follow-up time was only 14 months. The absence of active TB cases could signal effective screening and TBI treatment; however, the number of SOT recipients and follow-up time might have been insufficient to observe any TB cases, particularly as the number of patients from TB high-incidence countries (n = 77) was lower than in the previous cohort from our centre (n = 151 SOT recipients)8.

A systematic review from 1998 found a median time to TB onset of only 9 months after transplantation38. This could reflect an impact of TBI screening and preventive treatment on early post-transplant TB, but other contextual factors are also likely to play a role.

We observed no cases of hepatotoxicity among SOT candidates who were treated for TBI, and there was only one case of treatment discontinuation due to side effects. An American study from 2024 also reported high tolerability of TBI treatment; among 434 patients, 418 (96%) completed isoniazid pre- or post-transplantation, and adverse events were reported for a total of 29 (7%) patients, of which 13 (45%) were abnormal liver biochemistry tests19. A review reported TBI treatment-related hepatitis among 6% of all SOT recipients and candidates, and 10.9% among liver transplant candidates or recipients37. Given this relative infrequency of hepatotoxicity, it is not surprising we did not observe any cases.

This study had several strengths. Study participants were included from the vaccination clinic at the Department of Infectious Diseases, which is a well-defined clinical program. Another strength of this study is the very high pre-transplantation IGRA screening coverage (96%). Equally, with a national microbiological database and access to medical records from all hospitals, there was almost no loss to follow-up apart from the few patients who moved abroad. Overall data quality was good with few missing data despite retrospective data collection. We included different SOT types, and the number of study participants was relatively high. Lastly, this study provides new insight to inform current clinical practice.

This study also had limitations. As Denmark is a low-incidence country, the limited number of patients with positive pre-transplantation IGRAs resulted in limited power and precluded more extensively adjusted analyses. Also, we chose not to include the interferon-gamma response levels for the positive samples, as they were not available for each antigen for all positive samples.

Conclusion

Adherence to IGRA screening was high (96%), TBI treatment was initiated when indicated with few side effects and no reports of hepatotoxicity. There were no cases of post-transplantation active TB. The prevalence of a positive IGRA was 4%. A positive pre-transplantation IGRA was associated both with being born in either other TB low-incidence or medium-/high-incidence countries, as well as pre-transplantation diabetes and alcohol use. However, a considerable proportion of patients with positive IGRAs were from Denmark or other low-incidence countries, and it appears rational to screen all patients regardless of country of birth. Overall, our study provides new insight into predictors of IGRA positivity in a European low-incidence transplant population, highlighting that reliance solely on traditional TB risk factors would miss a proportion of patients with tuberculosis infection. These findings support continued systematic IGRA screening for all SOT candidates while also informing risk stratification.
