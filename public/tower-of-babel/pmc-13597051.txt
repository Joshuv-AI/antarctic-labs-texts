Comparative Dosimetric Analysis of Jaw Tracking Versus Non-Jaw Tracking Techniques in Head and Neck and Brain Cancers: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Javad Garshad, Baranoosh Rahmani, Mahsa Mansourian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancers affecting the head, neck, and brain are highly prevalent and pose a significant threat to human society. Radiation therapy is the primary treatment modality for these types of tumors, including intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT) being the prominent methods. However, concerns regarding the protection of organs at risk (OARs) persist when using these techniques. To address this, the use of the jaw tracking technique in conjunction with IMRT and VMAT, along with minimizing radiation leakage between leaves, is proposed as a protective measure. Our study aimed to comprehensively examine the quantitative and qualitative impact of jaw tracking on dose reduction in the normal tissues of patients with head, neck, and brain cancers. A systematic literature search was conducted via PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science up to May 2023, resulting in the identification of 674 relevant studies. After applying our criteria, only 5 studies met the inclusion criteria. Our findings indicate that the jaw tracking technique can effectively determine the dose reaching the OAR. However, further research with a larger sample size is necessary to validate and explore further base on these results.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer remains a significant global health concern, with projections indicating a staggering increase in cancer-related deaths. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) estimates that by 2023, cancer deaths will reach a staggering 13 million annually.[1] Among the various types of cancer, head and neck cancers include malignancies affecting the oral cavity, nasopharynx, pharynx, larynx, and salivary glands. Alarmingly, this category accounts for approximately 650,000 new cases and 330,000 deaths each year.[2] Although relatively rare, brain cancers have a devastating impact on patients’ lives. They constitute only 1-2% of all cancers,[3] yet their mortality rate is exceptionally high. In fact, less than one-third of patients with brain cancer survive beyond five years.[4] To combat these cancers, intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT) have emerged as standard treatment approaches in the radiotherapy of head and neck cancers. Basically, in IMRT treatment, the goal is to reduce the dose to OAR and at the same time increase the dose to the target area (tumor). In this method, a heterogeneous spatial dose is generated through a movable multileaf to achieve the treatment goal.[56] In the VMAT treatment method, besides changing and optimizing the spatial dose distribution with the dynamic multileaf, the rotation of the gantry also leads to the optimization of the dose distribution to the target area and OAR during irradiation.[78] These techniques offer the advantage of delivering increased radiation doses to tumors while minimizing radiation exposure to nearby critical structures.[9] Similarly, in the treatment of brain tumors, IMRT and more recently rotating gantry IMRT techniques such as VMAT have demonstrated the ability to conform radiation doses precisely to the target volume, thereby reducing doses to critical organs.[10] However, despite these advancements, IMRT and VMAT still result in low-dose exposure to normal tissues due to interleaf leakage of multileaf collimators (MLCs).[111213141516] The presence of low-dose volume regions in modulated radiotherapy is a major concern as it can potentially lead to the development of secondary malignancies. Indeed, the prevalence of secondary malignancies following radiation therapy in young adult populations is a well-established long-term consequence.[17] To address this issue, the movement of the collimator jaw in addition to MLCs during treatment has been explored as a means of reducing interleaf leakage to the patient.[18] The jaw tracking technique, used in linear accelerators (Linak), In fact, the jaw of the device tries to follow the movement and changes of the MLCs and reduce their leakage as much as possible so that the dose outside the lock reaches its minimum level.[19] Although studies have investigated the use of jaw tracking in radiation therapy with IMRT and VMAT techniques, its potential benefits to OAR in head and neck and brain cancers remain uncertain. Thus, the aim of this study is to comprehensively examine the quantitative and qualitative impact of jaw tracking on dose reduction to normal tissues in patients with head and neck and brain cancers. By evaluating the efficacy of this method, we aim to contribute to the ongoing efforts to optimize radiation therapy techniques and improve patient outcomes in these challenging cancer types.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This systematic review was based on Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[20]

Data sources and search strategy

Two investigators independently performed a comprehensive systematic literature search via PubMed (58), Scopus (537), and Web of Science (79) using terms (“jaw tracking” OR “No jaw tracking” OR “static jaw tracking” OR “with jaw tracking” OR “fixed jaw” OR “dynamic jaw” OR “without jaw tracking”) AND (“intensity-modulated radiotherapies” OR “volumetric-modulated arc therapy” OR “intensity-modulated arc therapy” OR “helical tomotherapy” OR “helical tomotherapies” OR imrt) on May 2023.

After eliminating duplicate studies and those unrelated to the subject of the systematic review, the potential full texts underwent evaluation based on predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The following inclusion criteria were adopted:

Quality analysis of included literature

The methodological quality of all studies included in the analysis was assessed using the risk bias assessment tool outlined as per the Newcastle–Ottawa scale guidelines.[21]

Outcomes statistics

Meta-analysis was conducted using the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software, version 3. A P value of <0.05 was considered a significant statistical difference for all tests. To assess the heterogeneity within the selected data for meta-analysis, the I-square (I2) statistic was used. If the I2 value is 0, indicating no heterogeneity, a fixed-effect model would be used for meta-analysis. In cases where the I2 value is ≥50% or the P value is ≤0.05, it is considered as minor heterogeneity, and a fixed-effect model would be used. However, if the I2 value >50% or the P value is <0.05, a significant heterogeneity is observed, and a random-effects model would be used.

The study findings were presented using statistical measures such as the mean difference (MD) along with a 95% confidence interval (CI).

Dosimetric comparison of jaw tracking and no-jaw tracking methods for OARs

The dosimetric parameters demonstrated a substantial reduction in dose to nearly all OARs when using the jaw tracking method, as indicated in Table 3. However, in the study conducted by Karthick Raj Mani,[22] differences between the two methods were not significant in the patients who were treated with the VMAT method. Moreover, findings by Karthick Raj Mani[22] revealed that the dose received by the thyroid gland did not exhibit a significant variance between both methods. It is worth noting that due to limited data availability in the selected studies, conducting a meta-analysis for most OARs was not feasible. However, right parotid, left parotid, and brain stem were subjected to a thorough meta-analysis for evaluation. As the heterogeneity test showed no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.5136), the fixed effects model was used.

A total of 3 studies[182225] reported Dmean for the left parotid, right parotid, and brain stem. The statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the two groups involving the left parotid (effect size [ES] =0.3, 95% CI = 0.249 to 0.849, P = 0.285) as depicted in Figure 2, the right parotid (ES = 0.267, 95% CI = 0.281 to 0.814, P = 0.34) as shown in Figure 3, and the brain stem (ES = 0.227, 95% CI = 0.265 to 0.719, P = 0.366) in Figure 4. Similar results were observed for Dmax in the brain stem (ES = 0.137(-0.354,0.627) P = 0.585) as depicted in Figure 5. These findings suggest that there is no significant difference in the use of jaw tracking compared with non-use in terms of dose distribution. However, it is important to note that due to the limited number of included studies, additional literature may be necessary in the future to further validate the reliability of these results.

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review, we examined the efficiency of using jaw tracking technique for the VMAT and IMRT treatments in the form of dosimetric analysis. In 2023, Chen D et al. compared dosimetric factors between VMAT and IMRT treatments[25] in a systematic study and meta-analysis. In 2022, Kazi T et al.[26] compared IMRT, VMAT, and 3D Conformal methods in the treatment of lung cancer[26] and head and neck[27] cancers in a review study. In 2023, Feng WG et al.[28] compared dosimetry between IMRT and VMAT methods in the treatment of nasal tumors in a systematic review and meta-analysis. Protecting OAR from receiving an excessive dose is one of the main goals of treatment in radiotherapy. On the contrary, a review study by 2020 Tomaz Vrtovec, analyzed the use of deep learning methods to segment OAR when the tumor tissue receives sufficient dose.[29] In addition, there have been various review studies on OAR dose and sensitivity in radiation therapy.[29303132] In this systematic review, the final articles have evaluated and analyzed the dose that reached lens, eye, optic nerve, parotid, brainstem, spinal cord, optical chiasm, larynx, thyroid and inferior constrictor of these three organs (parotid, brainstem, and spinal cord) among the OARs in head and neck cancer and eye, brainstem, and optical chiasm for brain cancer will be examined more closely.

The results of the meta-analysis conducted for the left parotid, right parotid, and brainstem should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of samples and studies available pertaining to these subjects. Nevertheless, it is important to consider the implications of these findings. To compare the dosimetric data in the articles, common dosimetry factors have been used, which include Dmean and Dmax or both and are different depending on the available information. In head and neck cancer radiotherapy, the brainstem is a critical OAR. Exceeding its tolerance limit can lead to complications such as cranial nerve dysfunction, including hearing impairment.[333435] The parotid glands are also important OARs, and radiation therapy can initially cause xerosalivation followed by hyposalivation, which can disrupt swallowing, speech, and taste.[363738] In addition, the spinal cord is an OAR, and exceeding its tolerance limit can result in pain, paralysis, numbness, and even xerostomia-related problems.[3940] For brain cancers, the three organs – eye, optical chiasm, and brainstem – are among the OARs because of radiation therapy complications, which can cause blurred vision, blindness, cataracts, and hearing problems.[32414243] It appears that the main goal of using the jaw tracking technique is to prevent the reduction of the dose reaching the OAR in radiotherapy, and this technique aims to achieve this goal by reducing the amount of leaf leakage in the two new IMRT and VMAT methods. The results of Dmean in studies involving the parotid show that jaw tracking for the parotid is better in IMRT than VMAT. Although the dose difference in using the jaw tracking technique is almost obvious compared with non-jaw tracking in both methods of IMRT and VMAT; however, this difference is not more than 1Gy in general. For brainstem, the amount of reduction in Dmean and Dmax in IMRT method is more than VMAT in the case of using jaw tracking technique. For spinal cord, like the previous two organs, the amount of reduction of Dmean and Dmax in method IMRT is more than VMAT if jaw tracking technique is used. It appears that the IMRT method can be more effective with jaw tracking technique due to more stability in gantry movement, although more studies and analyzes with a higher number of samples are needed. For brain cancer, only articles that used the jaw tracking technique together with IMRT method are available. Although the amount of Dmean has decreased by 2Gy when using jaw tracking for the eyes that does not seem impressive, it confirms the efficiency and effectiveness of the method. Similarly, reductions in Dmax for the brainstem and Dmean for the optical chiasm have been observed. Although these reductions may not be apparent, it is important to note that the treatment method remains consistent, and thus, achieving specific outcomes may be challenging. It appears that the use of this newly emerging technique in radiotherapy can reduce the leakage dose to the OAR even in new treatment methods such as IMRT and VMAT, which will eventually lead to the spread of this technique.

CONCLUSION

A systematic analysis of five studies indicates that the newly developed jaw tracking technique for radiotherapy in the treatment of head and neck and brain cancers holds significant promise in minimizing damage to critical organs, aligning with the primary objectives of this treatment. However, it is important to note that the number of studies conducted on this technique remains limited, highlighting the necessity for further clinical and research investigations in the future.
