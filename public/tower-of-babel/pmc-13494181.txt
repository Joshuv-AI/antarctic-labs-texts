Revisiting MAC as a static measure of anaesthesia: evidence for dynamic responses under isoflurane in male mice
Authors: Aden Mandel, Jackson Soohoo, Chloe Deitelzweig, Isha Patkar, Dennis Han, Madona Farag, Andrew McKinstry-Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe presence of a righting reflex is known to vary over time in an individual at the population half-maximal effective anaesthetic concentration (EC50). However, it is unclear whether similar variability occurs for other anaesthetic endpoints at comparable anaesthetic concentrations. We sought to test whether individual responses to a pedal pinch, a strong aversive stimulus, vary over time in individuals at the population EC50 for stimulus response and to explore the dynamics of individual responses.MethodsMale mice were exposed to fixed concentrations of isoflurane, and aversive pedal stimulation was repeatedly assessed to determine a population EC50. In a second cohort of male mice, either righting reflex or pedal pinch was repeatedly assessed at empirically determined population EC50 values for those stimuli (0.6 vol% and 0.95 vol%, respectively) over multiple anaesthetic exposures. Stochasticity of responses was determined by evaluating if response dwell times correspond to exponential distributions. The presence of resistance to state transitions (RST) in responses was tested by simulation of response data using a process lacking any RST, comparing real data with the simulated data.ResultsLike a righting reflex, at a population EC50 for response to an aversive stimulus, individual mice switch between responsive and unresponsive states. These responses are stochastic and display resistance to state transitions. Neither individual sensitivity nor RST for righting reflex at population EC50 (0.6 vol% isoflurane) was predictive of sensitivity or RST for aversive stimulus response at its population EC50 (0.95 vol% isoflurane).ConclusionsSimilar to the righting reflex, responses to an aversive stimulus change stochastically in individuals at population EC50 but differ from the dynamics seen with a righting reflex such that sensitivity and resistance to state transition are dependent on the test and vary independently between test modalities within an individual.Keywords: anaesthetic sensitivity, behavioural dynamics, behavioural state transition, isoflurane, MAC, righting reflex

For the past 60 yrs, the field of anaesthesia has used the minimum (though actually a median) alveolar concentration necessary to prevent movement in response to a surgical stimulus in 50% of subjects (Minimum Alveolar Concentration, or MAC) as a tool to compare inhaled anaesthetic potencies and predict the effects of combining different drugs.1 Because general anaesthesia is a combination of rather than a unitary neurophysiologic state, other anaesthetic endpoints have been adapted to the MAC paradigm, including blockade of autonomic response to noxious stimuli (MAC-BAR) and awakening from anaesthetic hypnosis (MAC-awake.)2,3 These response measures have been commonly conceived as varying across the population while being static within individuals.4 Contrary to this conception, recovery of righting reflex (RR) in rodents, a measure correlating to MAC-awake,5 has been shown to stochastically change over time in individuals while at the population EC50 (i.e. the anaesthetic concentration of MAC-awake).6

This stochastically varying response seen with hypnosis corresponds to changes seen over time in electrophysiologic recordings under anaesthesia,7 but it remains unknown whether other anaesthesia-related measures, including MAC, similarly vary over time in individuals. Different factors likely govern MAC than do hypnosis, evidenced by different anaesthetic agents having divergent concentration ratios for MAC:MAC-awake.8,9 Thus, there is good reason to suspect that other measures could behave in a different manner than hypnosis. Apart from the potentially different relevant molecular targets that could govern the various anaesthetic endpoints, the state of the brain might be qualitatively different near the point of hypnotic transition, in a state more similar to sleep than that seen during most of general anaesthesia.10

We tested whether MAC responses vary over time at a population’s half-maximal effective concentration (EC50), as had been shown for RR (Fig. 1a). If they do vary over time, we also sought to determine if those responses exhibit other parallels to the RR response: do they have stochastic transitions, and if so, do MAC responses display resistance to state transition (RST) (Fig. 1b). Finally, we sought to test if sensitivity and RST are consistent in an individual across different stimulus responses with the same anaesthetic agent. To test MAC in a mouse model, we adapted a commonly used technique to determine a surgical level of anaesthesia, movement in response to an aversive stimulus, and pedal pinch,11,12 allowing for rapid and repeat testing with a controlled force level to ensure consistent stimuli.

Methods

Mice

All studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Pennsylvania and were conducted in accordance with National Institutes of Health guidelines. Adult male wild-type C57BL/6 mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) 10–35 weeks of age were used for all assays (32 total).

Righting reflex

Mice were placed in 200 ml cylindrical chambers that were sealed and controlled for temperature as described.13 Briefly, isoflurane was administered in 100% oxygen at a flow rate of 200 ml min−1, ensuring rapid equilibration.6 Isoflurane was initially administered at 1.2 vol% for at least 5 min before being changed to the target concentration (0.60 vol% or 0.95 vol%, empiric population EC50 values for RR and MAC, respectively)14 where it was maintained for a total of 5 h. Chamber isoflurane concentrations in all assays were confirmed using a Riken FI-21 refractometer (AM Bickford, New York, NY, USA). After 2 h of exposure to guarantee pharmacokinetic equilibration,15 the presence or absence of an RR was evaluated every 3 min thereafter, with the final 2 h used for analyses.

Righting reflex was evaluated as described.13 In brief, a mouse was deemed to have an intact RR if it could return to an upright position within 5 s after being placed on its back by rotating the anaesthetic chamber without interrupting anaesthetic delivery. If it failed to right itself, the reflex was considered absent. Each animal underwent three RR exposures at 0.6 vol% isoflurane (n=16), and a subset of animals (n=12) underwent an additional exposure at 0.95 vol%, with each exposure separated by at least 36 h. Water bath warming maintained mean temperatures within 1°C of baseline (Supplementary Fig. S1b).

Movement response to aversive stimulus (MAC)

Mice were placed in an 80 L gas-tight glove box. Temperature was controlled using an external heating pad beneath the box set at 38°C, with mean temperatures maintained within 1°C of baseline (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Mice were exposed to isoflurane in a balance of oxygen with an initial flow rate of 10 L min−1. Chamber isoflurane concentrations in all assays were confirmed using a Riken FI-21 refractometer (AM Bickford) using side samples from both the inflow to and outflow from the glove box. Initially, isoflurane was set at 1.2 vol% and flow was maintained at 10 L min−1 until inflow and outflow isoflurane measurements corresponded (∼15 min) indicating glovebox equilibration. Subsequently, the flow rate was reduced to 5 L min−1. At least 5 min after initial glovebox equilibration, isoflurane concentration was lowered to the target dose and held for at least 2 h before starting stimulation. Responsiveness to paw pinch was then tested every 3 min, rotating across all four extremities, with the final 2 h used for analyses. Paw pinches were performed using an algometer with calibrated forceps (Bioseb In Vivo Research Instruments, Vitrolles, France) set to 0.04 N. Awake mice have a response threshold of up to 0.017 N.16 Forceps were closed with increasing force over 5 s and released once any readily visible spontaneous movement was exhibited or upon reaching 0.04 N of force. A positive response was defined as withdrawal of the stimulated limb or any spontaneous motion of the mouse not associated with the stimulated limb during stimulation. Animals (n=16) underwent three exposures at 0.95 vol%, and a subset of animals (n=12) received one exposure at 0.6 vol%. Each exposure was separated by at least 36 h.

MAC population dose-response

Groups of 6–10 animals were tested using the MAC paradigm (above) at various steady-state concentrations of isoflurane with reflexes repeatedly tested for a duration of at least 1 h. The mean response rate over the testing time was calculated for each animal. A four-parameter Gompertz sigmoid was fit to the mean response rates by isoflurane concentration using the Matlab Curve Fitter App, and the resultant fit function was used to evaluate the population EC50 value using custom code in Matlab (v2023a, Mathworks, Natick, MA, USA).

Within-and-between sensitivity comparisons

Defining sensitivity as the mean rate of response over the 40 trials in a single exposure, we examined differences in sensitivity in the same individuals across three exposures. This was compared with the differences in sensitivities across individuals across three exposures, comparing the variances of those two distributions, with the null hypothesis of no difference in variation of differences in sensitivity within vs between individuals across exposures.

Dwell time distributions

If responses are stochastic and thus equivalent to a Poisson point process, the distance between two consecutive points will be an exponential random variable with parameter λ.17 For the data presented here, this would equate to dwell time distributions for each state (both responsive and unresponsive) following an exponential distribution. To determine whether dwell times were exponentially distributed, we used the testing data at population EC50 (0.6 vol% isoflurane for RR and 0.95 vol% isoflurane for MAC) to plot duration of dwells in terms of number of trials (with a 3-min inter-trial interval) against the total number of dwells of that duration seen over all individuals over the three testing periods. The Matlab Curve Fitter App was used to fit a single negative exponential function to each state and testing modality.

State-independent stochastic response simulation

Using custom code in Matlab, we performed a simulation of responses, randomised from a table of individual responses at population EC50 (thus maintaining the same probability of each response), but with the response probability state-independent (consistent with a Bernoulli process) with 2000 repeats. Using these simulated data, we determined the number of transitions in responsiveness state for each simulated session to generate a distribution of counts of transitions in responsiveness. Experimentally determined number of transitions for each individual was Z-score normalised using that individual’s simulated distribution. To determine whether the actual number of transitions deviated from a Bernoulli process for each individual, a one-sample Student's t-test was used to assess whether the Z-score-normalised values significantly differed from zero.

Transition probability matrix estimation and resistance to state transition

Markov processes relate the current state of a system to the previous state using a transition probability matrix. Four element transition probability matrices were estimated for each individual for each response modality (RR and MAC) based on three exposures of 40 trials at population EC50 (120 trials total) as described.14 In brief, a matrix of the form M=[P(R|R)P(U|R)P(R|U)P(U|U)] was derived for MAC or RR from the empirical data. Resistance to state transition was calculated from the estimated transition probability matrices using the definition RST=P(R|R)+P(U|U)2.

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes were chosen based on the described phenomenon of stochastic changes in the RR in mice.6 For analyses, all time points prior to empirically determined behavioural steady-state were excluded. Analyses were performed using either custom code written in Matlab using the Statistics and Machine Learning toolboxes or with Prism (10.0, GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). Comparisons of means used two-sample Student's t-tests for two-group comparisons or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for three or more, with Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons. Variances of differences in sensitivity were compared using a two-way F-test. Deviation from zero for Z-score-normalised data was determined using one-sample Student's t-tests. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to compare associations between MAC and RR responses.

Results

Responsiveness near steady-state population EC50 for the aversive stimulus response

Serial RR testing at population EC50 showed switching between responsive and unresponsive states (example in Fig. 1a); however, it is unclear whether similar switching occurs with an aversive stimulus (example in Fig. 1b, middle and bottom) or if individuals remain in an unchanging state (Fig. 1b, top). After empirically testing response probabilities to withdrawal from an aversive stimulus (MAC) at a range of steady-state isoflurane concentrations, we determined the population EC50 to be near 0.95 vol% isoflurane (0.93%, rounded to the nearest 0.05 vol%, Fig. 1c). Consistent with reported findings, for mice held at steady-state population EC50 for loss of RR (0.6 vol% isoflurane), every individual switched back and forth between unresponsiveness and responsiveness over the testing period (Fig. 1d). When mice were held at steady state at the empirically derived population EC50 MAC for isoflurane, individuals spontaneously switched between responsive and unresponsive states, similarly to the response to the RR (Fig. 1d and e). State switching for RR and MAC occur at significantly different concentrations, with population EC50 for each modality occurring where there is a paucity of state switching for the other modality (EC<5 or EC95+, Fig. 2a and b, P<0.0001). Despite multiple individual shifts between responsive and unresponsive, population EC does not differ significantly from EC50 over the testing period, nor do mice become sensitised or desensitised to MAC testing over multiple exposures (Fig. 2c and d).

MAC sensitivity is a stable individual trait

To test whether MAC sensitivity is a stable individual trait, we examined the variance in MAC sensitivity, defined by the fraction of trials where a subject was unresponsive at steady state, within subjects, across exposures, comparing it with the variance between subjects, across exposures. MAC sensitivity displayed significantly less variance within subjects than between subjects (Fig. 3a, P=0.0082, two-sample F-test), suggesting that it is a stable individual trait. In contrast, we found that RR sensitivity variance did not significantly differ from within to between subjects (Fig. 3a, P=0.6953, two-sample F-test).

(b) Both MAC (left) and RR (right) responsive (top) and unresponsive (bottom) dwell times show exponential distributions, suggesting that both can be modelled as memoryless processes solely dependent on their current state. As such, both can be represented as two-state Markov processes, as described for the RR (dotted lines represent 95% fit confidence). (c) Using each individual’s sensitivity, a simulation was run for each mouse assuming no resistance to state transition (approximating a Bernoulli process). Each individual’s actual number of transitions was Z-score normalised by the simulated data to test whether the real data could be described by a process lacking resistance to state transitions. Both the RR and MAC have fewer transitions than would be predicted by a process without resistance to state transitions, suggesting both processes exhibit a tendency to remain in the current state over time, that is, they both show resistance to state transitions (∗∗P<0.01, ∗∗∗∗P<0.0001, one-sample Student t-test, n=16). MAC responses are stochastic

We previously described that RR responses at unchanging anaesthetic population EC50 are stochastic, a memoryless process where the subsequent state can be completely described by probabilities based on the current state (in this case, either responsive or unresponsive). We sought to determine if responses to aversive stimuli (MAC) are similarly stochastic. A stochastic process would produce dwell times in an exponential distribution for each state. For both the RR and MAC, the dwell time distributions for both the responsive and unresponsive states are similarly well-fit by single exponentials (Fig. 3b) with less than 3% of the variance unexplained by a single exponential. This suggests MAC responses, like RRs, are stochastic.

MAC responses exhibit resistance to state transitions

We have shown that the RR is not only stochastic but exhibits RST, a tendency towards remaining in the current state of responsiveness more than would be predicted by a process like a simple (or weighted) coin flip. As MAC responses stochastically vary over time at anaesthetic steady-state like the RR, we asked whether MAC responses also show a similar inertial tendency towards remaining in the current state (Fig. 1b, middle). We tested the hypothesis that the real responses could be described by a process completely lacking any RSTs (e.g. a Bernoulli process, Fig. 1b, bottom). We simulated test responses, sampled at the same frequency as the real testing for each individual mouse, using that individual’s shown sensitivity. The simulation assumed a Bernoulli process with no RST, where the probability of a subsequent response was constant and independent of the current state of response. The number of state transitions from the simulated data was then used to Z-score normalise the real responses. The real RR responses at EC50 had fewer transitions than would occur from a process without RST (Fig. 3c, P<0.0001). Similarly, the number of transitions during EC50 MAC testing was fewer than would be expected for a process lacking RST (Fig. 3c, P=0.0045), though the degree of the tendency to stay in the current state was not as strong as with RR testing (two-tailed Student's t-test, P=0.0001).

Resistance to state transition is lower for MAC than for righting reflex

We hypothesised that the increased relative number of normalised transitions seen in MAC testing compared with the RR is a result of differences in RST. We therefore estimated a four-parameter transition probability matrix for each subject for RR responses and separately for MAC responses to calculate and compare RST. At equivalent population ECs for RR or MAC (Fig. 4a), population RST was lower for MAC than for RR in the same population (Fig. 4b, P<0.0001), consistent with the observed increased number of relative transitions seen during MAC testing compared with RR.

Individual sensitivity and resistance to state transition for MAC and righting reflex responses

To test whether anaesthetic sensitivity for the RR predicts MAC sensitivity at the equivalent population EC50, we assessed correlations between individual sensitivities at the population EC50 for the RR and MAC. Sensitivity did not correlate across testing modalities within individuals (Fig. 4c, P=0.5209). RST also did not correlate in individuals across RR and MAC (Fig. 4d, P=0.4894). This suggests that knowing an individual’s sensitivity (or RST) to an anaesthetic for RR will not necessarily be predictive for their MAC sensitivity (or RST) and vice versa. A corollary is that even at the population EC50 for MAC, there will be a substantial portion of individuals with periods of intact RR (Fig. 4e).

Discussion

We found that a classic test for surgical depth of anaesthesia, movement in reaction to an aversive stimulus, fluctuates over time in individual mice at an unchanging concentration of inhaled isoflurane at the population EC50, similar to RR.6 Also similar to RR, the sensitivity to aversive stimuli is a stable individual trait and exhibits a tendency towards remaining in its current state. Yet we find these two processes are not identical; not only does population EC50 for isoflurane significantly differ between RR and MAC, but the two processes exhibit different RSTs. Further pointing to possible underlying differences between the processes governing these two anaesthetic endpoints, there was no correlation between an individual’s relative sensitivity at EC50 of isoflurane for RR and for MAC, despite both of these having been shown to be stable individual traits.6

We found that individual sensitivity to RR, a measure that has been argued to be a proxy endpoint for hypnosis in humans, and MAC, a measure that is associated with a surgical plane of anaesthesia, are not correlated. The two endpoints of MAC and MAC-awake (a parallel of RR) have been known to be pharmacologically dissociable at the population level, with different anaesthetic agents producing different ratios of MAC-awake to MAC.8,9 We describe within individual dissociation of those measures with the same anaesthetic agent: half of the individuals tested for RR at the concentration for MAC EC50 had periods of intact RR during a 3 h testing period. The interindividual variability in sensitivity for the RR at isoflurane MAC EC50 suggests that a subset of individuals in a population are at greater risk of awakening under anaesthesia. In our relatively small sample of 12 mice, where we evaluated RR at 0.95 vol% isoflurane, the population EC50 for MAC, six animals had a period of time where they maintained their RR (with individual response rates of 2%–17%). The human equivalent would be intermittently experiencing connected consciousness (though not necessarily recall) despite appearing to be at a surgical plane of anaesthesia. Whereas reported awareness with recall has an order of magnitude lower incidence (0.13%),18 the reported rate of connected consciousness after tracheal intubation in humans is significantly higher (6%–12%) than that when explicitly tested.19,20 We can extrapolate from this high rate of connected consciousness at a single time point following intubation that if connected consciousness were continuously monitored throughout an extended procedure, the incidence of any single incident of awareness would possibly be closer to the even higher rate of 50% that we observed in mice.

We chose a stimulus interval of 3 min based on the previous testing interval used for the RR as well as for practicality. The choice of stimulus and testing interval can itself influence some of our measures. Stimulation at a higher frequency could provide sustained arousal-promoting input and shift sensitivity for either the RR or MAC downward. Conversely, it could desensitise the individual to the stimulus, resulting in an apparent increased sensitivity to anaesthetic. If stimuli were additive in their effect but had a decay period to their effect duration, altering the testing interval could affect sensitivity by changing the functional amplitude of the stimulus. In contrast to sensitivity, it is not clear whether or how testing interval might affect RST. We previously modelled RST as system noise driving state transitions, but how and if the testing interval could affect this systemic noise is not clear. Future work explicitly varying the frequency of stimulation could reveal how the interval affects conditional response probabilities, and thus its effect on sensitivity and RST.

The change between the RST observed during RR testing and the RST during MAC is substantial and could have concrete implications for known anaesthetic phenomena. The magnitude of RST change between testing modalities with the same anaesthetic (MAC 0.51 vs RR 0.65) is as large as that found between different anaesthetics (e.g. sevoflurane has an RST of 0.6 and halothane an RST of 0.72).14 Modelling stochastic anaesthetic transitions shows that differences in RST are sufficient to recreate the phenomenon of anaesthetic hysteresis,21 which suggests that the differences between RST in MAC and RR for isoflurane are of the same magnitude, sufficient to create the large difference in neural inertia from sevoflurane to halothane. This would suggest that this finding is not a mere distinction without a difference.

There are numerous possible biological bases for the difference in RST between RR and MAC, making it an avenue worthy of further investigation. Although RST differences between agents could be attributable to distinct pharmacologic mechanisms, other explanations might apply for the same agent with different stimuli. The difference in RST between the RR and MAC under isoflurane could be because of both divergent effects of isoflurane at the two concentrations used as well as effects at different (or multiple) organisational levels of the central nervous system, from molecular target to network. Given the promiscuity of the volatile anaesthetics, this variability could arise from a number of sources. One possibility is that a different set of channels and receptors could have altered function at 0.6 vol% isoflurane (RR EC50) than at 0.95 vol% (MAC EC50). If this were the basis for differences in RST, other anaesthetic agents would not necessarily see the same degree or direction of change in RST between RR and MAC, a testable hypothesis. Separately, channel-level changes could also cascade to network-level effects, such as differential system noise. RST is equivalent to the inverse of the noise in a system,14 and at 0.95 vol% isoflurane the brain could potentially experience more noise than at 0.60 vol%. In order to measure this, it would be necessary to devise a measure of RST that could be evaluated at the two differing concentrations tested here (which is not possible with the current behavioural paradigm). EEG correlates of RST could serve this purpose but have yet to be established. Volatile anaesthetics have also been shown to affect cell metabolism and energetics,22, 23, 24, 25, 26 which could provide an alternative basis for differences in RST seen at RR EC50 vs MAC EC50. Higher anaesthetic concentrations would result in greater inhibition of cellular respiration, which in turn could disrupt synaptic transmission. This disruption could lead to an increase in system informational noise, paralleling the increase in system noise (seen as a decrease in RST) experimentally shown between evaluation of the RR at isoflurane 0.6 vol% and evaluation of MAC at isoflurane 0.95 vol%.

Despite showing a novel stochastic behavioural response to a stimulus at a constant higher drug concentration than previously explored, the present study is not without its limitations. We defined responsiveness to the foot pinch stimulus as extremity movement. Whether the defined response was the result of a brain- and brainstem-based reaction, a spinal reflex, or some combination of the two, is not clear. Although a given observed response sometimes involved simple affected limb withdrawal consistent with a spinal reflex, other responses, even in the same individual, involved multiple extremities. Future studies employing EEG with behavioural testing could ask whether specific activity patterns correlate to responsiveness, which suggests responses are not solely spinally mediated. Regardless of whether the MAC response is primarily brain-derived, spinal, or a combination of the two, the fact that two independent behavioural responses, evaluated at different concentrations of isoflurane, have been shown to stochastically switch suggests that such switching patterns are a more general property of the CNS under anaesthesia. Electrophysiologic recordings under anaesthesia suggest the same thing, as do emergence times from anaesthesia.7,21,27

We set out to test individual response variation over time at steady-state population EC50. Although there was no significant variation from population EC50 over the course of testing, there might be a trend towards an increased EC over the course of the 3 h of testing. Any trend present could be attributable to a small shift in temperature of the animals over the same course of the multi-hour experiment. Though we did maintain mean temperatures within 1°C of baseline for the RR and MAC assays (Supplementary Fig. 1), there was a small statistically significant decrease in temperature from baseline. The mean change of 0.87°C for RR and 0.32°C for RR might be reflected in potential though insignificant drift in population EC over the same period.

The current study is limited to male mice, a choice made to simplify the initial characterisation of a novel phenomenon. Given known sex differences in hypnotic potency for anaesthetics28 (and isoflurane specifically)29 and the unknown relationship between the RR and MAC isoflurane potency as affected by sex, we sought to define and explore the parameters of the MAC response in a single sex, as previously done for RR. We expect future studies to explore not only if similar stochastic MAC responses occur in females (which seems likely given other parallels with RR) but also the myriad possible sex differences: population EC50 for MAC, differences in the ratio of population EC50 for the RR for MAC, and differences in RST. An additional limitation in applying the current study to a wild-type population is the genetic homogeneity of the mice used. Sensitivity and RST interindividual differences would likely be exacerbated in a genetically diverse, true wild-type population. A single anaesthetic concentration where a majority of individuals fluctuate between responsiveness might not exist in such a population, as many more individuals would lie far from the median. A different experimental design would have to be developed to test these phenomena in a heterogeneous wild-type population. This could include determining individualised dose-response curves and testing each individual at a concentration where responses could be elicited 50% of the time, thus maintaining a functionally equipotent dose across a population despite interindividual sensitivity variation.

The findings presented here offer an opportunity for further investigations of interindividual variation in anaesthetic response across a population, providing a possible window into mechanisms behind variable anaesthetic responses. Though both RR and MAC show significant interindividual variation at the population EC50, neither the frequency of transitions nor sensitivity of an individual correlates between the two tests. These observations could variously suggest the following: 1. Distinct pharmacologic-physiologic interactions govern the two responses (for which there is existing evidence in the different population ratios of RR: MAC); 2. measures like sensitivity and RST are dependent on the available brain states, which are distinct at different anaesthetic concentrations; or 3. both (and possibly other) factors contribute. These hypotheses could be tested by combining EEG recordings with both behavioural assays across anaesthetic agents in the same subjects. EEG offers a common framework for evaluating brain states across a range of drug doses, whereas behavioural testing provides a consistent functional endpoint, even though EEG signatures can vary between agents. Investigations building upon the findings described here could provide a window into both how anaesthetics interact with the brain and into causes of individual anaesthetic response variation with potential clinical consequences.
