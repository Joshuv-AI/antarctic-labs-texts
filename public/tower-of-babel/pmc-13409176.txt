Phylogeny of Gelatinodiscaceae (Leotiomycetes) with emphasis on the taxonomy of Ascocoryne in North America
Authors: JK Stallman, L Quijada, MC Aime, CA Quandt, DB Raudabaugh, T Melie, I Rimer, S McAdam, D Haelewaters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe family Gelatinodiscaceae and genus Ascocoryne represent diverse lineages in Helotiales (Ascomycota, Leotiomycetes) that lack well-sampled phylogenies and taxonomic study. Our research objectives were to 1) determine whether certain genera that lacked DNA sequence data or had uncertain phylogenetic placement — Clathrosporium, Phaeangellina, Skyathea, and Xerombrophila — belonged to Gelatinodiscaceae, 2) estimate species richness and describe new species in Ascocoryne, 3) determine if morphological characteristics delineate intrageneric clades in Ascocoryne, and 4) test the mitochondrial tricarboxylate transport protein (CTP) as an alternative DNA barcode in Ascocoryne. We made 83 Gelatinodiscaceae collections primarily from the midwestern United States, examined 61 collections previously deposited in herbaria, and generated DNA sequence data for seven loci (18S, ITS, 28S, EF1, RPB1, RPB2, CTP) from these collections. We found that Skyathea, Xerombrophila, and up to four other unnamed, putative genera belong to Gelatinodiscaceae, while Clathrosporium and Phaeangellina are excluded. Up to 37 putative undescribed species-level clades or single isolates are present in Ascocoryne, in addition to the nine previously described species and four newly described ones here: Ascocoryne diminutiva, A. fibrosa, A. neoinflata, and A. wilsoniae. Additionally, descriptions for two unnamed species similar to A. cylichnium are given, a preliminary description of the sexual morph of A. laurisilvae is provided, and A. trichophora is synonymized with A. vinosa. Four main clades are found in the genus Ascocoryne. The size of lipid bodies in living ascospores, the arrangement of conidia borne on ascospores, and the presence of calcium oxalate crystals are the most important morphological characteristics for distinguishing among clades. The CTP and ITS loci successfully identified six of six Ascocoryne species tested. INTRODUCTION

Recent reviews and phylogenetic analyses have greatly improved our understanding of Leotiomycetes, but they have also highlighted gaps in our knowledge of the systematic relationships and species richness in this group. For example, 489 Leotiomycetes genera lack multilocus sequence data, 155 of which also have no ITS sequence data available (Johnston & Park 2025). In the most speciose order, Helotiales, as many as 151 genera are considered incertae sedis (Johnston et al. 2019), and only ~5000 of the estimated 70000 species in the order have been described (Hawksworth 2001, Quandt & Haelewaters 2021). Several recent studies focused at the family rank to find stable taxonomic positions for genera and describe new species. For example, the families Arachnopezizaceae, Hyaloscyphaceae, Hyphodiscaceae, and Mollisiaceae have recently been studied (Tanney & Seifert 2020, Kosonen et al. 2021, Quijada et al. 2022), and the family Tricladiaceae was created to separate a group of polyphyletic genera in Solenopeziaceae (Johnston & Baschien 2020). In contrast, little molecular data are available for Gelatinodiscaceae, and therefore the family has received limited modern systematic study.

Gelatinodiscaceae

Gelatinodiscaceae was established for Gelatinodiscus flavidus (Carpenter 1976) and remained monotypic for over 40 years. More recently, Gelatinodiscus was synonymized with Chloroscypha (Baral et al. 2013a) and a broader Gelatinodiscaceae including nine genera was proposed based on phylogenetic and morphological evidence (Jaklitsch et al. 2016). Now the family contains at least ten genera: Ascocoryne, Ascotremella, Chloroscypha (= Gelatinodiscus), Dimorphospora, Helicodendron, Neobulgaria, Ombrophila, Phaeangellina, Skyathea, and Xerombrophila (Baral et al. 2013a, b, Sri-indrasutdhi et al. 2015, Jaklitsch et al. 2016, Ekanayaka et al. 2019, Johnston et al. 2019). Didymocoryne has been considered a distinct genus in the family (Wijayawardene et al. 2018, Ekanayaka et al. 2019) or a synonym of Ascocoryne (Jaklitsch et al. 2016, Hyde et al. 2024). Clathrosporium grouped with the Gelatinodiscaceae p.p. in ITS phylogenies and was placed either in the family (Johnston et al. 2019) or in Helotiales incertae sedis (Johnston & Park 2025), whereas Ekanayaka et al. (2019) placed it in the newly established Hydrocinaceae based on a five-locus phylogeny. Similarly, Xerombrophila was placed in Gelatinodiscaceae based on a 15-locus phylogenetic hypothesis by Johnston et al. (2019), while Ekanayaka et al. (2019) placed it in Hydrocinaceae. Finally, some older lists have included Neocudoniella in Gelatinodiscaceae (e.g., Jaklitsch et al. 2016, Wijayawardene et al. 2018), but in more recent phylogenetic reconstructions it is not confamilial (e.g., Ekanayaka et al. 2019, Johnston & Park 2025).

Species in Gelatinodiscaceae have diverse life histories as endophytes and both terrestrial and aquatic saprotrophs. Many genera and species form apothecia on wood (e.g., Chloroscypha flavida), while others also produce macroscopic asexual structures (e.g., Ascocoryne sarcoides), or are aquatic (e.g., Dimorphospora) or aero-aquatic (e.g., Helicodendron) fungi. Sexual morphs of Gelatinodiscaceae taxa can be differentiated from Helotiaceae taxa (where many current Gelatinodiscaceae genera used to be placed) by the combination of often containing gel and calcium oxalate crystals in excipular tissues with having a different amyloid ascus ring (Jaklitsch et al. 2016), but determining shared morphological characteristics at the family rank remains a challenge in Helotiales (Baral et al. 2015, Quandt & Haelewaters 2021).

Despite recent large-scale phylogenies of Leotiomycetes taxa (Haelewaters et al. 2021, Johnston & Park 2025), sampling in Gelatinodiscaceae has been low, not inclusive of all putative genera, and studies including multilocus sequence data or protein-coding genes are lacking. For example, there are no DNA sequence data available for the genera Phaeangellina and Skyathea, and only two published phylogenies include more than 15 Gelatinodiscaceae isolates: an ITS phylogeny of 27 Ascocoryne isolates (Gao et al. 2017) and an ITS+28S phylogeny of 27 Gelatinodiscaceae isolates from five genera (Ekanayaka et al. 2019). Both phylogenies showed Ascocoryne isolates that were identified as a single species were either not monophyletic or did not have high bootstrap support. An earlier phylogenetic analysis of Ascocoryne showed limited variability in the ITS region in A. sarcoides isolates but found that the mitochondrial tricarboxylate transport protein locus (CTP) successfully resolved species and subspecies relationships in Ascocoryne (Griffin et al. 2010).

Ascocoryne

Species in the genus Ascocoryne make gasoline-like, volatile organic compounds studied as potential biofuels (Gao et al. 2017). Studies focusing on this secondary metabolite production usually generate DNA sequence data but include little apothecial morphological data as these are typically studied from isolates in pure culture (Griffin et al. 2010, Gao et al. 2017). Other studies of Ascocoryne include detailed descriptions of apothecia, but do not include DNA sequence data (Quijada et al. 2017). Therefore, a link between morphology and DNA sequence data is often not available. Adding to the taxonomic challenge of this group, the genus Ascocoryne was established to accommodate members of the genus Coryne with an apothecial sexual morph (Groves & Wilson 1967) and Index Fungorum (2025) lists 86 taxa in the genus Coryne, many of which have not been reevaluated since their original description (Johnston et al. 2014). Some of these taxa may be species of Ascocoryne that should be investigated before describing new species in the genus. Therefore, incorporating both morphology and DNA sequence data while considering older Coryne names is needed when describing new species of Ascocoryne.

Ascocoryne includes nine species of endophytic–saprotrophic taxa found worldwide (Index Fungorum 2025). Most species in the genus have purplish, gelatinous apothecia that grow in association with dead wood, and some also have macroscopic asexual morphs that may grow independently or co-occur with the sexual morph. Many Ascocoryne are xylophiles, living in the bark or wounds of trees (Arhipova et al. 2015, Meyn et al. 2019, Molina et al. 2020). These endophytes may have antagonistic effects on other wood-decaying fungi and are not reported to cause substantial decay to the wood itself (Roll-Hansen & Roll-Hansen 1979a, Vasiliauskas & Stenlid 1998). One species, A. turficola, usually grows from moss in peat bogs instead of wood and is considered rare and of conservation concern throughout much of its range in the Northern Hemisphere (Bunyard et al. 2008, Filippova et al. 2013). A key to Ascocoryne species in Europe suggests the genus can be split into two groups: 1) the A. sarcoides group of which members lack calcium oxalate crystals, have one to a few large lipid bodies (LBs) with smaller and medium LBs in living ascospores, and produce conidia in chains from mature ascospores; and 2) the A. cylichnium group with members that contain calcium oxalate crystals, have living ascospores without large LBs (with only small and medium-sized LBs), and have conidia on mature ascospores that are not produced in chains (Baral 2000).

The most comprehensive taxonomic treatment of Ascocoryne in the Americas (inclusive of North, South, and Central America, including the Caribbean) was a PhD dissertation by Wilson (1962) focusing on species (at the time, in the genus Coryne) in the northeastern USA and southeastern Canada. Wilson listed four known species — C. cylichnium, C. sarcoides, C. striata, and C. urnalis (now considered a synonym of A. cylichnium) — and proposed four new species — C. angustata, C. constrictospora, C. inflata, and C. minima. Descriptions were based on features of ascomata, conidiomata, and cultures, but the descriptions contained no Latin diagnoses and were never validly published. Despite lack of valid publication, Wilson’s Coryne ‘inflata’ (nom. inval., Art. 36) has been used informally and in scientific literature (now as ‘Ascocoryne inflata’) to describe species with capitate paraphyses that occur in several regions, such as Europe (Moyne 2012) and North America (Valenzuela et al. 2021). Species lists of Ascocoryne in North America include A. sarcoides, A. cylichnium, and A. turficola (e.g., Seaver 1951, Roll-Hansen & Roll-Hansen 1979b, Bunyard et al. 2008, Beug et al. 2014). These three species, along with A. albida, A. lilacina, and A. laurisilvae, were originally described from Europe. The only species described from outside America or Europe is A. javanica from Indonesia (Penzig & Saccardo 1902).

Although studied in ecology, forestry, conservation, and biotechnology contexts for decades, there is no well-sampled phylogeny of Ascocoryne and the broader Gelatinodiscaceae with associated morphological, geographical, and host data. MATERIALS AND METHODS

Collections

We made 79 new collections of Ascocoryne species and four of Neobulgaria species. Two collections were made in the Upper Potaro River Basin of the Pakaraima Mountains in Guyana in 2002 and 2016. Three collections were made in the Merendón Mountains of the Department of Cortés in Honduras in 2022. All remaining collections were made in Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Washington, and West Virginia, USA, from 2021 to 2024. We also examined 61 other Gelatinodiscaceae collections obtained from fungaria. We generated 234 DNA sequences via Sanger sequencing, Illumina sequencing, and genome mining (Table S1).

Collections from Guyana and Honduras were dried in desiccant while specimens collected in the USA were dried at ~25 °C with a fan. Newly collected specimens were deposited at the Kriebel Herbarium (PUL) and University of Guyana (BRG). Fungarium collections and cultures were requested from the University of Arizona (ARIZ), Canada’s National Mycological Herbarium (DAOM), culture collection of the Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute (CBS), University of Copenhagen (C), Cornell University (CUP), Harvard University (FH), University of Helsinki (H), Kew Royal Botanic Garden (K), New York Botanic Garden (NY), Royal Ontario Museum Fungarium (TRTC), and Washington State University (WSP).

Morphology

Field photographs were captured using a Tough TG-6 camera (Olympus, Pennsylvania, USA) or a cell phone camera. Colours are from Kornerup & Wanscher (1978). Stereo microscope photos were captured using a SC30 camera mounted on an Olympus SZ61 stereo microscope, using Olympus cellSens v. 1.18 software to take photographs. Microscopic fungal structures were observed using an Eclipse E600 microscope (Nikon, New York, USA) and photographed using the same camera and cellSens software. Biometric features were measured in Piximètre v. 5.10 (http://ach.log.free.fr/Piximetre/). Micrographs were edited and plates were assembled using Inkscape v. 0.92 (https://inkscape.org/) or Adobe Illustrator. To identify some decorticated tree substrates, sections of wood were made with a Microm HM 430 freezing microtome (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), stained with Toluidine Blue O, observed using an Axio Imager 2 Upright Microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany), and compared with the Wood Database (https://www.wood-database.com/).

Microscopic characters of specimens were studied considering the distinction between living and dead cells (vital taxonomy, Baral 1992). Apothecia were hand-sectioned and mounted in various reagents: Lugol’s solution (IKI), ~ 5 % potassium hydroxide (KOH), 1 % Congo Red (CR), and Melzer’s reagent (MLZ). All morpho-biometric characters observed after treatment with lethal reagents (e.g., KOH, MLZ) correspond to dead cell traits (indicated with the symbol †). Specimens were initially observed in tap water, allowing the observation of living-cell features (indicated with the symbol *) in fresh collections and some herbarium collections. Living features include the pattern of LBs inside ascospores, the arrangement of ascospores within asci, and the presence of vacuolar bodies (VBs) in paraphyses and excipular cells. The same specimens were then treated with lethal reagents to kill the living cells and enable comparisons with dead herbarium material. A minimum of 50 ascospores and 20 of each of the other microscopic structures (e.g., asci, paraphyses, and excipular cells) were measured to complete the descriptions. Measurements are presented as (a–)b–c(–d), where b and c represent the mean minus/plus standard deviation, and a and d indicate extreme values. For the excipulum, we noted the number of distinguishable layers, texture, gelatinization, cell morphology, and any blue reaction with and without KOH pretreatment in IKI. Ascospores were analyzed thoroughly, considering the lipid content index (LCI), which differentiates guttulate content by percentage and type of guttules (oligoguttulate vs. multiguttulate). Mature ascospores may develop septa and conidia. When present, conidial formation and morphology were studied in detail.

Mature ascomata were placed beneath cover slips to observe the ejected living ascospores. The same technique was applied using inverted Petri dishes with potato dextrose agar (PDA) or Rose Bengal (RB) to initiate cultures following the methods of Karakehian et al. (2021).

Sanger sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from desiccated apothecia using the QIAamp DNA Micro, QIAamp Mini Kit (Qiagen, Stanford, California), or Promega Wizard Genomic Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin) following the manufacturer’s instructions. During sample collection in Honduras and Guyana, fresh apothecia were placed in lysis buffers in the field and the remaining steps of DNA extraction were completed later (3 mo to 1.5 yr) in the laboratory per manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA from cultures < 30 d in age was amplified via colony PCR (sensu Albu et al. 2015) in which a sterile toothpick was used to gather and suspend hyphae from the colony in 50 μL sterile H2O, microwaved (at 1100 watt power) for 2 min, then centrifuged for 2 min at 18 400 rcf. Approximately 40 μL of the supernatant was retained and 5 μL was diluted in 45 μL sterilized millipore H2O for subsequent use in PCR.

Amplification reactions were completed in 25-μL reactions with 12.5 μL of 2× MyTaq Mix (Bioline, Swedesboro, New Jersey), 1 μL of DNA extract, 9.5 μL of H2O, and 1 μL of each 10 μM primer for rDNA loci, or 8.5 μL of H2O and 2 μL of each 10 μM primer for protein-coding loci. All primers and thermocycler conditions are provided in Table 1. Multiple primers are listed for the same locus because in cases where PCR was unsuccessful, we attempted an alternate primer pair or primers that targeted a shorter portion of the same region.

High-throughput sequencing and genome mining

Metagenomic sequencing of the ITS and/or beginning of the 28S region was completed for 37 fungarium specimens when Sanger sequencing failed, an approach that has been successful for older samples (Forin et al. 2018, Miller et al. 2022, Olds et al. 2023, Stallman et al. 2024) in which DNA fragmentation and environmental contaminations likely occurred. We followed the protocol from Miller et al. (2022), except that for some isolates, the beginning of the 28S region was also sequenced. Briefly, initial amplification reactions were completed as above and in Table 1. The PCR products were cleaned with exonuclease and shrimp alkaline phosphatase, then sample-specific Illumina indices were ligated onto PCR products in a reaction with 1 μL of PCR product, 1 uL of each forward and reverse index, 9.5 uL of H2O, and 12.5 μL of Master Mix. Paired samples from the initial PCR product and post-ligation were run in adjacent wells on a 1 % agarose gel at 60 V for 60 min to confirm ligation. Ligated samples were again cleaned with exonuclease and shrimp alkaline phosphatase and pooled for Illumina sequencing on a MiSeq v2 platform using 2 × 250 nucleotide paired-end reads. Raw Illumina reads are deposited in NCBI’s Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under BioProject PRJNA1018967 and additional metadata are provided in Supplementary File S1 for these isolates.

Reads were processed with DADA2 v. 1.29.0 (Callahan et al. 2016) with forward and reverse amplicons in R v. 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing 2023) using the methodology from Miller et al. (2022). The “filterAndTrim” function was used with the options maxN = 0, maxEE = c(2,2), truncQ = 1, and rm.phix = True, and the “learnErrors” function was used on forward and reverse reads. Forward and reverse reads were then merged with the “mergePairs” function. The “removeBimeraDenovo” function with the method set to consensus was used to remove chimeric sequences. Resulting amplicon sequence variants were compared against the UNITE general FASTA release for Fungi (Abarenkov et al. 2024) using NCBI BLASTn (Altschul et al. 1990). Amplicon sequence variants (ASV) and resulting taxonomic assignments based on the highest BLASTn match were exported to a spreadsheet. For each isolate, the ASV with the most reads that matched Gelatinodiscaceae was used to assign the correct sequence to the collection. In some cases the percentage of matching reads was low (< 1 %) or multiple Gelatinodiscaceae ASVs were present. In these cases, we used additional evidence such as morphology and checked whether reads were unique to a single isolate or present across other samples and controls to determine the correct sequence or discard it due to ambiguous results. Successfully generated sequences were deposited to NCBI (Table S1).

For two collections, CBS 275.75 (DAOM 127045) and CBS 738.84, sequences were derived in full or partly from draft genomes (C.A. Quandt & D. Haelewaters, unpubl. data) assembled using the workflow in Melie (2023). Full collection information for isolates not listed in the Taxonomy section but for which DNA sequence or other data were generated is provided in Supplementary File S2.

Construction of phylogenetic trees

We used T-BAS v. 2.4 (Carbone et al. 2019) to investigate the phylogenetic positions of Phaeangellina empetri and Skyathea hederae, which previously lacked DNA sequence data, and Clathorosporium intricatum and Xerombrophila crystallifera, which had conflicting familial placements between Ekanayaka et al. (2019), and Johnston et al. (2019) and Johnston & Park (2025). We selected “Place Unknowns” for Skyathea [ITS–28S–18S of K (M)1435354], Phaeangellina (ITS–28S–18S of C157023 and 28S–18S of C125384), Clathrosporium (28S of CBS 115285), Filosporella fistucella (18S–ITS–28S–EF1–RPB1 of CCMF-13091), and Hydrocina chaetocladia (18S–ITS–28S of HME4375) to infer their phylogenetic history onto the Leotiomycetes v. 1 reference tree from Haelewaters et al. (2021), which already includes ‘V.’ delicatum (CCMF-19494) and Xerombrophila crystallifera (TAAM 122762). We used the “de novo” option for the IQ-TREE placement, under partitioned models, with GTRGAMMA as substitution model for each partition. Support for each branch was estimated by 1000 ultrafast bootstrap (UFboot2) replicates (Hoang et al. 2018) and the Shimodaira-Hasegawa-like approximate likelihood ratio test (SH-aLRT) (Guindon et al. 2010). Neurospora crassa and Xylaria hypoxylon were used as outgroups.

To investigate the phylogeny of Ascocoryne and Gelatinodiscaceae, newly generated sequences were combined with sequences obtained from GenBank, UNITE, and iNaturalist.org. We focused on isolates with multilocus data and representatives from different named and unnamed species- and genus-level clades. For the genus Ascocoryne, we also focused on isolates for which the CTP locus was sequenced or could be extracted from a genome assembly. Metadata for all sequences used are provided in Table S1.

We constructed maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian trees from five different sets of sequences. First, we included all seven loci (18S–ITS–28S–EF1–RPB1–RPB2–CTP) and the entire Gelatinodiscaceae dataset with Arachnopeziza araneosa (Arachnopezizaceae), Blumeria graminis and Golovinomyces cichoracearum (Erysiphaceae), and Lemonniera aquatica and Articulospora tetracladia (Discenellaceae) as outgroup taxa. Second, these same seven loci for only species in the genus Ascocoryne, using A. turficola as the outgroup (Gelatinodiscaceae phylogenies recovered A. turficola basal to the rest of Ascocoryne). Third and fourth, the same datasets above but limited to isolates for which at least two loci were present. Fifth, only species in the genus Ascocoryne using only ITS sequences, with A. turficola as outgroup. In the text, we refer to the first two groups as ‘all-taxa’ trees and the third and fourth groups as ‘2-minimum’ trees.

For the Ascocoryne and Gelatinodiscaceae trees, sequences from each locus were aligned using MAFFT in Geneious (Katoh et al. 2002, Katoh & Standley 2013). The ITS alignment was trimmed to begin after the 5’ starting motif 5’–ATCATTA–3’ and to end at the 5’ start of the 28S at 5’–TGACCT–3’. Similarly, 28S sequences were trimmed to begin at the 5’–TGACCT–3’ motif. After manual trimming, all alignments were further trimmed in the command-line version of TrimAl v. 1.3 (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009) using a gap threshold (−gt) of .60 and minimum coverage (−cons) of .50, with other settings on default. Concatenation of loci was completed in SequenceMatrix (Vaidya et al. 2011). Aligned, trimmed, and concatenated (if relevant) alignments are provided in Supplementary Files S3–S7. Because we primarily applied a phylogenetic species concept (with additional morphological and geographical data) to delimit taxa and estimate species richness in Ascocoryne, we also built single-locus trees to check for discordance between loci, following Genealogical Concordance Phylogenetic Species Recognition (Taylor et al. 2000). We found one instance where the 28S, EF1, and CTP loci placed PULF29998 in the group of Ascocoryne species with inflated paraphyses (where it was inferred to belong based on morphology), while the ITS locus placed it distant to these taxa. After confirming the ITS sequence was correct with reextraction and resequencing, all sequences were included in our concatenated analysis.

Maximum likelihood trees were inferred in IQ-TREE (Nguyen et al. 2015) with partitioned models (Chernomor et al. 2015) and 1000 bootstrap (BS) replicates. Within IQ-TREE, ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) was used to determine the best model at each partition using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Bayesian trees were inferred using BEAST2 v. 2.7.7 (Bouckaert et al. 2019) and associated applications (BEAUTi, LogCombiner, TreeAnnotater) and Tracer v. 1.7.2 (Rambaut et al. 2018). In all cases, BEAST2 was run with unlinked site and clock models, and a linked tree model. Ambiguities were considered (box checked), bModelTest (Bouckaert & Drummond 2017) was used for the site model from the model set ‘transitionTransversionsplit’ and mutation rates were estimated. An optimized relaxed clock (Douglas et al. 2021) and the birth death model were used. Two initial runs were completed with a chain length of 10 M, after which the operators were tuned based on suggested outputs. With operator-tuned input files, five independent runs with a chain length of 100 million generations were completed for each tree set. Burn-in was adjusted to optimize effective sample sizes by viewing and editing both individual and combined traces of the log files in Tracer. Trees were combined in LogCombiner and TreeAnnotator was used to generate the maximum clade credibility tree using the mean for node heights. Tree topologies of ML and Bayesian trees were visualized in FigTree v. 1.4.3 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/) and edited in Inkscape v. 1.2.2. Information on alignments and models selected for each methodology is provided in Table S2.

Barcode analysis

We used three different DNA barcoding methodologies to compare the ITS locus to the CTP locus in identifying a priori-identified Ascocoryne species: distance-based (e.g., barcode gap), tree-based (e.g., phylogenetic), and character-based (e.g., diagnostic nucleotide positions in alignment). We examined six species for which we had at least two sequences of both ITS and CTP loci: A. albida (2 sequences), A. aff. cylichnium 1 (9), A. aff. cylichnium 2 (3), A. fibrosa (3), A. sarcoides (7), and A. wilsoniae (5). We included all other Ascocoryne CTP sequences available and their corresponding ITS sequences, resulting in 37 total sequences of 14 different species at each locus. For each method, we first aligned all the sequences at each locus using MAFFT in Geneious (Katoh et al. 2002, Katoh & Standley 2013). We trimmed any regions outside the two flanking primers for the CTP locus and trimmed the ITS as described above for the phylogenetic analysis. Finally, we removed any sequences less than one half the length of the total alignment, which resulted in the removal of two A. aff. cylichnium 1 sequences (PULF29777 and PULF29778) from the ITS dataset; no sequences were removed from the CTP dataset. The CTP alignment (Supplementary File S8) was 698 nucleotides in length and the ITS alignment (Supplementary File S9) was 531 nucleotides in length.

For the distance-based method, we used the MEGA v. 11 (Tamura et al. 2021) distance estimation tool, selecting ‘none’ for variance estimation method, ‘p-distance’ for the model/method, “d: Transitions + Transversions” for the substitutions to include, ‘Uniform Rates’ for rates among sites, and “Pairwise Deletion for ‘Gaps/Missing Data Treatment.” If species had a ‘barcode gap’ (Meyer & Paulay 2005), or a minimum interspecific distance greater than the maximum intraspecific distance, they were considered successfully identified via this method (Hebert et al. 2004).

For the tree-based method, we used IQ-TREE v. 1.6.12 (Nguyen et al. 2015) and the built-in ModelFinder Plus (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) to construct ML phylogenies with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (Hoang et al. 2018). If species were recovered in supported (> 90 BS), monophyletic clades, they were considered successfully identified via this method. For the character-based method, we manually examined alignments and counted the number of diagnostic positions, or sites where a single nucleotide or gap was shared by all members of the same species but not by any other species in the alignment. Species were considered successfully identified via this method if they had at least one diagnostic position (Jaén-Molina et al. 2015).

RESULTS

High-throughput sequencing

We obtained correct ITS1, ITS2, and 28S sequences in 32/115 (28 %) attempts from 37 isolates via Illumina sequencing. In some cases, a second elution from the same extraction, or separate extractions from sporocarps and cultures were both used, leading to more than one attempt per isolate. For individual loci, the success rate of ITS1 was 11/41 (27 %), ITS2 16/44 (36 %), and 28S 5/28 (18 %). For different collection types, we obtained correct sequences from extractions of desiccated cultures in 2/15 (13.3 %) attempts and from extractions of apothecia in 30/100 (30 %) attempts. In the 83 out of 115 (72 %) unsuccessful attempts, 64 (77 %) attempts did not contain Gelatinodiscaceae sequences, and 19 (23 %) attempts contained Gelatinodiscaceae sequences, but they were not used because there were multiple or conflicting ASVs, the ASV did not match the morphology of the specimen, or the ASV was present among multiple samples of putatively different species (i.e., potentially contaminants).

Phylogenetic placement of Clathrosporium, Phaeangellina, Skyathea, and Xerombrophila

In our Leotiomycetes-wide phylogeny (Fig. S1), the two Phaeangellina isolates were not in Gelatinodiscaceae and formed a supported (SH-aLRT 100 % / UFboot2 100 %) clade with Cenangium acuum, Piceomphale bulgaroides, and Moellerodiscus pinicola, sister to the Sclerotiniaceae and Rutstroemiaceae, consistent with Johnston & Park (2025). The Skyathea and Xerombrophila isolates were supported (SH-aLRT 100 % / UFboot2 100 %) in the family Gelatinodiscaceae, with isolates of Ascocoryne, Chloroscypha, and Neobulgaria. The Clathrosporium and Hydrocina isolates formed a supported (SH-aLRT 98 % / UFboot2 99 %) clade with Filosporella fistucella and Varicosporium delicatum outside Gelatinodiscaceae. Therefore, in our phylogenetic analyses of Gelatinodiscaceae, Skyathea and Xerombrophila isolates were included, while Clathrosporium and Phaeangellina isolates were excluded.

Gelatinodiscaceae phylogenies

The topologies for the all-taxa tree and 2-minimum tree for Gelatinodiscaceae were nearly identical using both ML and Bayesian methodologies (ML topology of all-taxa tree Fig. 1; 2-minimum tree Fig. S2; unedited trees Supplementary Files S10–S13). All four analyses supported a monophyletic Gelatinodiscaceae and the following clades: 1) the genera Dimorphospora, Helicodendron, Ombrophila, Skyathea, and Xerombrophila, including four isolates — CBS 301.50 2, NY03416357, 203_OA_2013, and ZML09_3 — that occurred in three clades with uncertain generic placement; 2) the genera Ascotremella, Neobulgaria, and AFTOL_ID1259 with an uncertain generic placement; 3) the genera Ascocoryne and Chloroscypha. In all four analyses, isolates of Ascocoryne turficola formed a separate clade between all other Ascocoryne isolates and Chloroscypha.

Ascocoryne phylogenies — intrageneric clades and morphology

The four multilocus analyses retrieved a monophyletic, supported Ascocoryne, excluding A. turficola, which was used as an outgroup after Gelatinodiscaceae phylogenies recovered it as basal to the rest of Ascocoryne (ML topology of all-taxa tree Fig. 2; 2-minimum tree Fig. S3; unedited trees Supplementary Files S14–S17). The ITS-only phylogeny (Fig. S4) generally lacked high support values at deeper nodes. It will not be used to discuss clades above the species rank and the individual ML and Bayesian tree files can be found in Supplementary Files S18 and S19.

Ascocoryne lilacina was recovered in an unsupported sister position to the rest of the taxa with soil isolates from Estonia and Greenland in our all-taxa trees. All four analyses recovered a clade of A. albida, A. ‘inflata’, A. neoinflata sp. nov., and A. sarcoides, along with several other isolates (clade 1), but this clade only had support in the Bayesian analyses. Morphologically, all these species have conidia that form in chains (or conidia type is unobserved) from ascospores that contain at least one large LB, do not have an amyloid reaction of the ectal excipulum, and do not contain calcium oxalate crystals. Isolates with consistently inflated paraphyses were recovered in a single subclade within clade 1, but this clade was also only supported in Bayesian analyses.

Apart from A. lilacina and clade 1, all remaining taxa in the genus occurred in three clades of a supported (2-minimum tree) or unsupported (all-taxa tree) larger clade, except AJMH-2010, which was retrieved in an unsupported position alone. Clade 2 was supported in all phylogenies and consisted of A. laurisilvae, A. wilsoniae sp. nov., and several other isolates. Shared morphological characteristics of species in this clade are conidia that occur in chains from mature ascospores, the presence of small calcium oxalate crystals, an inamyloid ectal excipulum, and paraphyses without inflated apices.

Clades 3 and 4 formed a larger, supported group in the 2-minimum tree and Bayesian analysis of the all-taxa tree. All apothecial morphs in this group have ascospores with small to medium LBs only and conidia that form directly on the ascospore wall, not in chains. Clade 3 was unsupported in all analyses and isolates recovered in this clade varied in different analyses. In the 2-minimum tree, clade 3 included A. diminutiva sp. nov. and isolates from Guadeloupe, Indonesia, New Zealand, and USA (Hawaiʻi) that have an amyloid ectal excipulum and variable presence of calcium oxalate crystals. In the all-taxa tree, A. trichophora also was recovered in clade 3 with more New Zealand isolates and a collection from Guyana that does not have an amyloid ectal excipulum. In this tree, the Guadeloupe, Indonesia, and USA isolates were placed in clade 4.

Clade 4 was supported in the 2-minimum tree and consisted of A. fibrosa, A. cf. cylichnium, A. aff. cylichnium 1, A. aff. cylichnium 2, along with several other, single isolates. Ascocoryne fibrosa, which does not have crystals and has a variable amyloid reaction of the ectal excipulum (among other divergent characters), was placed sister to the rest of the taxa that all contain crystals and do not have an amyloid ectal excipulum. In the all-taxa tree, clade 4 was unsupported and included the addition of the Guadeloupe, Indonesia, and USA isolates mentioned above. The subclade of A. cf. cylichnium, A. aff. cylichnium 1 and A. aff cylichnium 2 (excluding A. fibrosa and isolates with varying topologies) was supported in the Bayesian analysis.

Ascocoryne — support for described species and species richness

Our four Ascocoryne-wide phylogenies (including all-taxa trees and 2-minimum trees using both ML and Bayesian methodologies) had largely congruent topologies, particularly at supported branches and terminal clades. All described species of Ascocoryne, including those described here and those without formal recognition (A. ‘inflata’, A. aff. cylichnium 1 and 2, and A. cf. cylichnium) were supported in most trees, except for A. albida and A. sarcoides, which were supported in one of four and two of four trees, respectively (Table S3). Depending on the number of isolates used in the phylogeny, anywhere from ~33 (2-minimum tree) to ~50 (all-taxa tree) species-level clades or single isolates were present in Ascocoryne (Figs 1, 2, Supplementary Files S11 and S16)

All six Ascocoryne species could be identified by at least one method at the CTP and ITS loci using our barcoding methods (Table 2). Four of six species at the CTP locus and six of six species at the ITS locus were identified by the distance-based method. The average p-distance at the CTP locus was higher (0.046) than at the ITS locus (0.014). Tree files for the CTP and ITS phylogenies are in Supplementary Files S22 and S23. On average, the CTP locus had 1.65 % of positions in the alignment containing diagnostic characters and the ITS locus had 1.13 %. Overall, the ITS locus worked in 14 of 18 barcoding attempts and the CTP locus in 15 of 18.

Notes: Ascocoryne diminutiva morphologically fits into the broad concept of A. cylichnium with ascospores containing smaller LBs only and conidia budding directly from the ascospore wall, not in chains, but differs in several other characteristics. Ascocoryne diminutiva has small calcium oxalate crystals that do not aggregate into large clusters, smaller ascospores that are 0–3-septate, and a blue reaction of the inner layer of the ectal excipulum in IKI which is composed of yellowish thick-walled cells. Descriptions of A. cylichnium have larger crystals, overmature ascospores with five or more septa, and do not note an amyloid ectal excipulum (e.g., Quijada et al. 2017). Ascocoryne diminutiva differs from A. javanica, which also has an amyloid ectal excipulum (per notes from Richard P. Korf associated with syntype collection CUP-K-4733, T. Iturriaga pers. comm.), by lacking large crystals in general and any crystals in the subhymenium and upper medullary excipulum (vs abundant crystals in A. javanica, Penzig & Saccardo 1902). Additionally, A. diminutiva has smaller ascospores [†13.2–15.3(–16.5) µm vs †19–21 µm] and ascomata (apothecia up to *5.1 mm diam. vs up to 20 mm diam.). Ascocoryne lilacina has an amyloid ectal excipulum (L. Quijada, pers. obs.), but the ascospores are shorter [†6–8(–9) µm] and do not become septate when mature (Dennis 1971).

Bulgaria similis was described from Cuba (Berkeley & Curtis 1868) and later synonymized with Ascocoryne sarcoides (Dennis 1954). Notes from Kent Parsons Dumont associated with slides and preserved sections of the ex-K holotype of B. similis (Wright 189), NY03417477, state the spores are 12–13 × 4–5 µm (vs. 10 × 5 µm long in Berkeley & Curtis 1868 and 13–17 µm long in Dennis 1954), and 1-septate. Examination of prepared slides of this collection revealed degraded tissue that did not yield useful morphological characteristics. The spores of A. diminutiva differ in being up to 3-septate and having conidia of the cylichnium-type, budding from the ascospore walls, not in chains. In synonymizing B. similis with A. sarcoides, Dennis (1954) stated he was, ‘unable to separate this [B. similis] from European material of C. sarcoides.’ Because Dennis (1956) considered conidia budding from the ascospore wall to be a key feature of A. cyclichnium, it is unlikely he would make this statement if B. similis had spore characteristics that more resembled A. cyclichnium than A. sarcoides. Therefore, we do not believe B. similis is an earlier synonym of A. diminutiva. Destructive sampling was not pursued of any material at NY, FH, or K because the holotype collection at K has already been sampled for genome sequencing (L. Davies pers. comm) that, if successful, will help future work on this species.

The inner ectal excipulum of A. wilsoniae maintains a brownish colouration in KOH compared with other species examined here where pigments fully dissolve. Three of the ten collections examined (PULF29771, PULF29775, PULF29776) had crystals present in the medullary and ectal excipulum but we did not observe crystals in others, or there were so few (< 5 total) it was not possible to determine if these were inherent to the ascomata, or exogeneous in origin. Ascocoryne striata has crystals and an ectal excipulum with differentiated cortical cells, but these cells differ by branching and A. striata has ascospores with small LBs (Kučera & Lizon 2005).

DISCUSSION

Based on 234 new sequences generated from 144 collections from recent fieldwork and fungaria studies, we clarify the genera belonging to the family Gelatinodiscaceae and propose four new species and one new combination in Ascocoryne.

Metagenomic sequencing of fungaria specimens recovered ITS1, ITS2, or 28S sequences from the target organism 32 out of 115 times (27.8 %) for the 37 collections attempted. Results were variable, with several successes in collections made during the 1800s (e.g., H6036559 from 1860, K-M121465 and K-M121466 from (≤ 1867) while some collections made as recently as 1989 (NY3416360) did not yield a sequence from the target organism. Our 27.8 % success rate compares to 64 % from 36 specimens collected in the late 1800s identified as Peziza (Forin et al. 2018), 25 % for 20 newer and older specimens across Ascomycota (Miller et al. 2022), and 50 % for four Chlorosplenium specimens (Stallman et al. 2024).

Genera in Gelatinodiscaceae

Gelatinodiscaceae is recovered as a monophyletic, supported family including the 9 genera Ascocoryne, Ascotremella, Chloroscypha, Dimorphospora, Helicodendron, Neobulgaria, Ombrophila, Skyathea, and Xerombrophila (Figs 1, S1, S2). Phaeangellina is excluded from the family and is recovered in a supported clade with Piceomphale bulgarioides and ‘Cenangium’ acuum (Fig. S1) that has previously been recovered in the polyphyletic Rutstroemiaceae and Sclerotineaceae families (e.g., Pärtel et al. 2017). We were unable to sequence both the type of Cenangium empetri nom. illegit. (K32261), later named Phaeangella empetri and then combined in a new genus as Phaeangellina empetri (Dennis 1955), and other collections close to the type locality in the UK (IMI269374 and IMI262167). We did obtain DNA sequence data from two collections from Denmark (C125384 and C157023) and another from Canada (WSP56939) identified as P. empetri that matched its morphological features and were found on its host, Empetrum nigrum (Ericaceae). ITS sequences between the collection from Canada and one collection from Denmark shared 99.8 % pairwise identity.

The 28S isolate of Clathrosporium intricatum is recovered in a supported clade with Filosporella fistucella, Varicosporium delicatum, and Hydrocina chaetocladia outside Gelatinodiscaceae (Fig. S1). ITS-only phylogenies group these species in Gelatinodiscaceae p.p. (Johnston et al. 2019, Johnston & Park 2025), but multilocus sequence data from these same studies, from Ekanayaka et al. (2019), and our analysis confidently show that none of these species belong in Gelatinodiscaceae. Ekanayaka et al. (2019) placed all these taxa, including Xerombrophila, in Hydrocinaceae, but our results, along with Johnston et al. (2019) and Johnston & Park (2025) show Xerombrophila belongs in Gelatinodiscaceae.

Our analysis recovered four potentially unnamed genera based on combinations of phylogenetic position (Figs 1, S2), life histories, and/or morphological characteristics. The first is represented by ZML09-3, from a wetland in China (Wu et al. 2013), and 203 OA-2013, from peatland bog sediment in Russia (Grum-Grzhimaylo et al. 2016). These isolates are sister to an isolate identified as Ombrophila and are recovered in a broader clade with Dimorphospora and Helicodendron, genera with aquatic life histories. Neobulgaria species have also been found in bog sediment (Grum-Grzhimaylo et al. 2016) and on submerged detritus in peatlands (Raudabaugh et al. 2021). We note that sequences identified as Ombrophila in GenBank generally lack multilocus data (including those identified as the type, O. janthina) and often do not share high pairwise identity. Therefore, we are uncertain if our single isolate represents the true Ombrophila, which some have suggested is a synonym of Neobulgaria (Baral 1999). Similarly, sequences identified as Helicodendron are found scattered throughout Helotiales. We selected sequences from the type species in the genus, which if correctly identified, places Helicodendron in Gelatinodiscaceae (see Johnston & Park 2025 for further discussion).

The second potentially new genus is isolate CBS 301.50, identified as Helicodendron triglitziense from a submerged leaf in England, which is recovered as sister to Xerombrophila. Xerombrophila is only known from dead wood, so this isolate indicates either a new life history strategy in Xerombrophila, or an additional aquatic genus. The third clade is represented by a culture originating from a mossy log in New York, USA (AFTOL-ID1259). It is identified as Neobulgaria pura, but is supported as sister to Ascotremella, separate from other Neobulgaria species. Neobulgaria pura is the type species of the genus, but there is not enough information to determine if this sequence from America represents the true N. pura, which was described from Europe. Finally, a collection on dead wood from Guyana (NY3416357) is recovered in an unsupported position near the Dimorphospora/Helicodendron clade. Morphologically, the collection is apothecial and has large, 3-septate, thick-walled, hyaline ascospores (42.1–)44.2–50.3(–52.6) × (10.5–)11.2–13.1(–14.0) µm with a wrinkled appearance. Globose conidia are 1.6–4.0 µm in diam. and abundant, although their interaction with the ascospores, if any, is not apparent. Calcium oxalate crystals are present in the excipular tissues and the ectal excipulum has an amyloid reaction. Despite its unique ascospores, we chose not to describe a new genus in the absence of additional collections, vital taxonomic data, and culture characteristics.

The species A. turficola is recovered in an unsupported position between all other species of Ascocoryne and the genus Chloroscypha (Fig. 1). Ascocoryne turficola is divergent compared with other Ascocoryne species due to its long-stipitate, green ascomata, ascospores that may have a gelatinous sheath (Filippova et al. 2013), and growth on Sphagnum species, although in some cases it may be growing from herbaceous material under moss (Filippova & Bulyonkova 2013). Although its morphology and substrate are unique, its phylogenetic position is unsupported and does not conflict with its current generic placement. Therefore, we do not propose a new genus for this taxon (Vellinga et al. 2015).

We have examined the largest number of isolates in a phylogeny of Gelatinodiscaceae to date, but our sampling is not without bias. Only 33 isolates of the nine genera and four unnamed potentially genus-level clades or single isolates come from outside the genus Ascocoryne, and 12 of these are in the genus Neobulgaria. Therefore, incorporating more DNA sequence data outside of the genera Ascocoryne and Neobulgaria will be key in resolving deeper nodes and better understanding unnamed clades in the future.

Intrageneric clades and morphological characters in Ascocoryne

All but five Ascocoryne isolates are recovered within four clades that are generally supported and identifiable by morphological features (Figs 2, S3). But, depending on the clade and method of analysis (2-minimum tree, all-taxa tree, ML or Bayesian tree), there may be variability in support and morphological information is unknown for many isolates.

Sexual morphs of taxa in clade 1 have ascospores with large LBs, conidia that form in chains from mature ascospores, highly gelatinized medullary excipula, and do not contain calcium oxalate crystals. Paraphyses may be consistently inflated or not. This clade, and the subclade with inflated paraphyses are only supported in Bayesian analyses. Sexual morphs of taxa in clade 2, which is supported in all multilocus analyses, are similar to those in clade 1. Differences include that crystals are present, although this is variable in A. wilsoniae, and that the medullary excipulum is generally less gelatinized. Clade 3 is not supported in any analyses and contains variable taxa between the 2-minimum and all-taxa trees. In the 2-minimum tree, species have amyloid ectal excipula and crystals are variable. Ascocoryne vinosa is recovered in this group in the all-taxa tree. Clade 4 contains the divergent A. fibrosa sp. nov. with an amyloid and gelatinized ectal excipulum and the ‘cylichnium group’ of several species that all have sexual morphs with abundant crystals, inamyloid ectal excipula, and ascospores with small LBs and conidia that form on the ascospore wall, not in chains.

Summarizing, a synapomorphic morphological characteristic of the larger clades 3 and 4 (supported in both 2-minimum trees and the Bayesian all-taxa tree) are the ascospores with small LBs only and conidia that form on the ascospore wall, not in chains. Clades 1 and 2 form ascospores with at least 1 large LB and the conidia form in chains. Apically inflated paraphyses may be a synapomorphic characteristic within clade 1, but this is only supported in the multilocus Bayesian analyses. Other characteristics, including the presence of crystals and an amyloid ectal excipulum are useful in determining clade, subclade, or species, but are not unique to monophyletic groups. We note that care should be taken in determining whether the ectal excipulum is amyloid or only the hyphae connecting the ectal excipulum to the substrate (if present). In many cases, these connecting hyphae are amyloid, but the ectal excipulum is not. For example, a collection of A. albida (K-M1434233, as A. solitaria) has blue connecting hyphae in IKI, which does not appear to have been reported previously, but an inamyloid ectal excipulum.

Species richness of Ascocoryne with notes on individual taxa

Ascocoryne contains 13 described species (including those described here) and our phylogenetic analyses suggest there may be as many as 50 species (Fig. 2). Although we tried to improve sampling from tropical locations, there is still a bias towards temperate America and Europe in our dataset, as is true for most fungi (Haelewaters et al. 2024, Stallman et al. 2024). Of the 143 Ascocoryne isolates, 97 are from temperate Europe or North America. While we have 19 isolates from tropical America, we have no representatives from Africa, only two from tropical Asia, and isolates from tropical localities suggest high species richness at these latitudes. For example, the three isolates we examined from the Hawaiian Islands (CUP063507, CUP063506, ARIZ-M-AN00673) and three from Guadeloupe (FH00374802, FH00374803, FH374851) all represent unique species, indicating potentially high species richness over small geographic areas.

Not enough data are available to draw in-depth conclusions about biogeography or host preference in Ascocoryne species except to say that both may be broad. For example, A. albida is found in Europe, North America, and China on both angiosperm and gymnosperm substrates. Ascocoryne sarcoides is also found in Europe, North America, and Asia, but also in South America and Australasia on a variety of hosts including gymnosperms and angiosperms. Ascocoryne aff. cylichnium 1 grows on trees in Bignoniaceae, Fagaceae, Pinaceae, Platanaceae, and Sapindaceae and occurs in India in addition to the USA. Some species may be localized — for example A. fibrosa in the midwestern USA — but this understanding may be due to a lack of sampling or a skewed specialist effect (Haelewaters et al. 2024) in the few localities where those studying Ascocoryne have lived or traveled for research. While most isolates included in our phylogenetic analysis are known from macroscopic sexual or asexual sporocarps, others were isolated from unique substrates. Isolates from soil (e.g., UDB01334927), orchid roots (Herrera et al. 2010), mines (Held et al. 2020), and caves (Burow et al. 2019) are all found in clade 2 of our analysis (Fig. 2), indicating that life histories beyond lignicolous saprotrophism or endophytism in Ascocoryne are possible.

Ascocoryne javanica

Coryne javanica was described from Indonesia (Penzig & Saccardo 1902) and later transferred to Ascocoryne (Thind & Singh 1972). The type was not available for sampling due to lack of material, but notes written by Dr. Richard P. Korf from microscopic examination of the syntype (CUP-K-4733) state, ‘ectal excipulum blue–green in iodine!’ (T. Iturriaga pers. comm.).

The only Ascocoryne specimen we obtained DNA sequence data for from Indonesia (NY3416361) had an amyloid ectal excipulum, but lacked crystals and had smaller ascospores compared to A. javanica (14.4–16.5 vs 19–21 µm). A collection reported by Thind & Singh (1972) as A. javanica (number 3785) from India was not located at K upon a loan request. The authors stated that this collection resembled the concept of A. javanica in all respects, but the ascospores are reportedly much shorter (12–17 µm) than the original description of A. javanica and the paraphyses are stated to be abruptly enlarged into pyriform heads, while the original description states the paraphyses are filiform (Penzig & Saccardo 1902). Coryne sarcoides var. javanica, described from Indonesia (Hennings 1900), has ascospores closer in size (16–18 µm) to NY3416361, but the asci are (erroneously?) reported as 50–65 µm, much smaller than NY3416361 (X̄ = 110–123 µm). More collections from Indonesia and southeastern Asia will improve our understanding of the species richness of Ascocoryne in this region and may lead to modern collections of A. javanica that can provide additional morphological details and DNA sequence data for this taxon.

Ascocoryne cylichnium

Based on our phylogenetic trees, there are eight different lineages within the A. cylichnium group (Figs 2, S3), four of which are found in Europe, from where the species was described (Tulasne 1853). It is unclear which of these isolates, if any, constitutes the ‘true’ Ascocoryne cylichnium. The holotype of A. cylichnium could not be found at P or PC upon request (B. Buyck, pers. comm.) and we are not aware of modern collections with complete descriptions and associated DNA sequence data from Europe. A small portion of an apothecium of the type of Bulgaria urnalis (H6036559), collected in Finland in 1860 and later synonymized with A. cylichnium, was permitted for DNA sequencing and morphological analysis. Although not enough material was available for a comprehensive description, the ascospores measured †(17.5–)18.8–23.0(–24.7) × (4.2–)4.6–5.4(–5.8) µm, the asci lacked croziers, and the ITS sequence shows > 99.4 % percent similarity with isolates from the USA, Canada, and Korea. Croziers are present in all other species recovered in the cylichnium group in our analyses: A. aff. cylichnium 1 and A. aff. cylichnium 2 from the midwestern USA, PULF29797 from the pacific northwestern USA, and at least one or more taxa from Europe and the Canary Islands (Baral 2000; Quijada et al. 2017). Until the holotype of A. cylichnium is found and yields useful information, or other material is epitypified, it would be premature to describe species in the A. cylichnium group that lack unique features.

Ascocoryne striata

The description of A. striata states that it contains calcium oxalate crystals, conidia in chains, and many small LBs (Kučera & Lizon 2005), meaning it does not morphologically fit into any of the four clades in our phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 2). In no other Ascocoryne species described, or specimen examined in this study, do conidia occur in chains from ascospores without also containing at least one large LB. Photos of the holotype (NY 7596) on Mycoportal.org show a small piece of wood, 1.5 × 10 cm, containing one potentially mature apothecium. Annotations show the collection has been examined at least four times (by J.B. Ellis & B.M. Everhart, F. Petrak, D.E. Wilson, and V. Kučera & P. Lizon), likely depleting apothecia available for additional destructive sampling. Petrak annotated the specimen as conspecific with Coryne sarcoides, while Wilson’s annotation states that she considered the two species distinct, noting that Persoon’s specimen of A. sarcoides at L had larger ascospores (15–26 × 3.8–6.0 μm vs. 13–21 × 3.2–4.6 μm) and more septa [5(–6) vs. 3(–5)], and that differences also occur in the cultural morph. The DNA sequence data show at least two collections identified as A. striata by Wilson in her thesis (Wilson 1962) are actually A. sarcoides (see below), but the only published, full description of the holotype of A. striata (Kučera & Lizon 2005) does not report features consistent with A. sarcoides. Kučera & Lizon (2005) stated that they were unable to examine collections from Wilson (1962) and they only found one other collection of A. striata, W6596, after ‘all major world herbaria were searched.’ The specimen at W, now stored under herbarium number W0383792, was collected in Austria in 1898 and contains four small pieces of wood. The largest piece, approximately 2 × 4cm, appears to host apothecia of approximately 1mm in diam, but both the number of apothecia and whether they are mature is uncertain based on the photos available.

Ascocoryne (Coryne) species reported in Wilson’s thesis and the utility of cultural data

Doreen E. Wilson (née Wells), who passed away in 2021, studied the genus Coryne (Ascocoryne) for her PhD dissertation at the University of Toronto (Wilson 1962), examining ~106 Gelatinodiscaceae collections during at least a six-year period from 1956 to 1962. She reported eight species, including four new species that were never formally published. We were able to loan specimens, view permanent slides, and in some cases destructively sample material for morphological examination and DNA extraction, generating sequence data for at least one isolate identified as each of her eight reported species.

The DNA sequence data for Coryne ‘minima’ (TRTC32202) shows it is a Neobulgaria sp. (Fig. 1), and it will not be further discussed. Collections identified as C. striata (TRTC32215, DAOM57653) and C. constricta (DAOM35421) are recovered in a clade with A. sarcoides (Fig. 2). Wilson proposed TRTC32215 as the neotype of A. sarcoides five years following the publication of her thesis (Groves & Wilson 1967), indicating her species concept, at least in this case, changed after finishing her dissertation. Wilson considered C. ‘constricta’ to be similar to C. striata with minor differences in the shapes of the ascospores and different sizes of conidia and conidiophores in culture. Collections identified as C. ‘angustata’ (TRTC32198, TRTC32257 and TRTC33484), C. urnalis (DAOM40674, DAOM40678), and C. cylichnium (DAOM43039) all are recovered in a clade with the holotype of Bulgaria urnalis (Fig. 2; labelled as A. cf. cylichnium). Wilson’s descriptions of these three species are similar, primarily differing in culture characteristics: C. ‘angustata’ was considered unique for rarely having ovoid conidia, and having narrower, more curved allantoid/rod-shaped conidia. Coryne cylichnium was considered unique for having a slimy/metallic appearance of the culture colony. Measurements of ascospores and asci overlap. A collection of C. ‘inflata’ (TRTC32178) is recovered with other collections from northeastern North America with inflated paraphyses (Fig. 2). Finally, C. sarcoides (DAOM33818) is recovered with collections of A. aff. cylichnium 1 (Fig. 2). Collections identified as C. sarcoides were not available for destructive sampling beyond cultures, but the original description is consistent with a species in the cylichnium group with conidia not occurring in chains from ascospores.

In summary, the eight Coryne species in Wilson (1962) likely represent four Ascocoryne and one Neobulgaria species: A. aff. cylichnium 1, A. cf. cylichnium, A. ‘inflata,’ A. sarcoides, and Neobulgaria sp. (Table S4). These results suggest that features of Ascocoryne cultures are polymorphic and this led Wilson to separate taxa that otherwise had very similar apothecial morphology. In addition to frequently producing two types of conidia, both sizes and shapes of conidia, sizes of conidiophores, and the macroscopic appearance of the colony can vary within the culture of a single species. We note that permission to destructively sample Wilson’s collections was limited, and some collections consisted only of cultures. Therefore, we could not examine several replicates of each of Wilson’s eight putative species. Doing so may have uncovered more species than the five we report here.

Names described in Coryne

The genus Coryne accommodated a wide assortment of taxa described from both asexual and sexual collections since its inception by Nees (1816). Later, Groves & Wilson (1967) introduced the genus Ascocoryne to accommodate Coryne species known from the ‘perfect’ (sexual) morph. In reviewing the 86 Coryne taxa listed on Index Fungorum (2025), we found 31 with a description of a sexual morph that had not been transferred out of Coryne or Ascocoryne (Table S5). Most of these taxa can quickly be excluded as synonyms for the new Ascocoryne species described here based on colour, substrate, ascospore sizes, or other characteristics. The type localities of most of these taxa are from Europe (23) and Brazil (4).

Evaluating the ITS and CTP loci as DNA barcodes of Ascocoryne species

Both ITS and CTP loci were adequate in identifying all six Ascocoryne species tested. The CTP locus performed marginally better, successfully barcoding the target species in 15/18 attempts compared with 14/18 for ITS. Additionally, the CTP locus identified more isolates with the tree-based method (five to three), had more diagnostic characters on average, and had a larger average p-distance of the barcode gap. Higher variability in distances led to fewer identifications at the CTP locus than the ITS locus using the barcode gap methodology, despite the higher average. While both the CTP and ITS loci were successful, we were only able to include data for eight other putative species in our analysis. Because the genus Ascocoryne is species rich, the ability of these single loci to effectively barcode all taxa in the genus when more species are included is unclear. We note that two of the species that failed to be identified in at least two methods were both the best-sampled: Ascocoryne sarcoides (seven sequences) and A. aff. cylichnium (nine sequences). This indicates that in addition to the inclusion of other species, adding more sequences from the same species may decrease identification likelihood if intraspecific phylogenetic diversity is high.

CONCLUSIONS

Gelatinodiscaceae and Ascocoryne are more diverse than previously understood. Clathrosporium and Phaeangellina are excluded from Gelatinodiscaceae, but Skyathea, Xerombrophila, and multiple other unnamed, genus-level clades are included. We describe four new Ascocoryne species and our phylogenies suggest the genus contains more than three times the 13 currently described species. The CTP and ITS loci provide similar discriminatory power as DNA barcodes in the six Ascocoryne species we analysed. Species with ascospores that form conidia in chains and have at least one large LB are found in two distinct clades, while all species with only small LBs and conidia that do not occur in chains form a separate group. Further investigation in Gelatinodiscaceae will benefit from more collections and multilocus sequence data outside the genera Ascocoryne and Neobulgaria. Further investigation in Ascocoryne will benefit from more collections from tropical localities and adding data for species lacking DNA sequence data (e.g., A. striata) or modern descriptions (e.g., A. javanica), only known from a single morph (e.g., A. vinosa), or where it is unclear which species name applies in the modern context (e.g., A. cylichnium).

Neurospora crassa and Xylaria hypoxylon were used as outgroups and 1000 SH-aLRT and ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot2) supports were used. Clades including the taxa of interest are highlighted in pink.
