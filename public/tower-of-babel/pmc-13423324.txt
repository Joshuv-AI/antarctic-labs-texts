The role of probiotics in modulating complications and intestinal barrier integrity after colorectal cancer surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical trials
Authors: Armaghan Kiarsi, Mohammadreza Salehi, Melika Jameie, Amirali Zareie Shab Khaneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim: This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the impact of probiotics on postoperative complications and intestinal barrier integrity markers in patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) undergoing surgery.Background: Postoperative complications pose a substantial challenge in CRC. Probiotics have been shown to alleviate these complications through various mechanisms, including improving intestinal barrier integrity. Methods: Following PRISMA guidelines, a systematic search was conducted in MEDLINE, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Scopus up to February 1, 2026, to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the effects of probiotics on post-surgical complications, recovery parameters, and functional intestinal barrier biomarkers (lactulose/mannitol [L/M] ratio and transepithelial electrical resistance [TER]). Statistical analysis was performed using random-effects models in R. Effect sizes were expressed as risk differences (RD) for binary outcomes and standardized mean differences (SMD) for continuous outcomes. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q and I² statistics (PROSPERO: CRD420251172153).Results: Eleven RCTs involving 1,234 CRC patients were included. Probiotic supplementation significantly reduced the risk of septicemia (RD=-0.158, p=0.029), surgical site infection (RD=-0.060, p=0.024), respiratory tract infection (RD=-0.079, p<0.001), central line infection (RD=-0.096, p=0.007), diarrhea (RD=-0.137, p=0.005), and abdominal distension (RD=-0.172, p=0.005). Functional intestinal barrier markers were significantly improved, evidenced by a decreased L/M ratio (SMD=-1.78, p=0.018) and increased TER (SMD=1.10, p<0.001). Furthermore, the duration of postoperative antibiotic use was significantly shortened (SMD=-0.45, p=0.001). No significant differences were observed for bacteremia, abscess, urinary infection, ileus, anastomotic leak, pyrexia, or hospital stay (p>0.05). Sensitivity analyses showed outcome variability, and substantial heterogeneity existed for pyrexia, septicemia, ileus, and the L/M ratio.Conclusion: Probiotics reduced the risk of specific infectious and gastrointestinal complications and enhanced intestinal barrier function after CRC surgery, highlighting their potential as a safe and effective adjunct to improve postoperative outcomes.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer mortality, causing approximately 930,000 deaths annually

(1, 2). The high mortality rate of this disease is due to its diagnosis at advanced stages, as well as its aggressive nature (3). It is estimated that the global incidence of CRC will reach 2.2 million new cases by 2030 (1). Lifestyle factors, including a high-fat diet, physical inactivity, and smoking, are among the major contributors for the increasing incidence of CRC, especially among the young population and in developing countries (4). Surgery is the most effective treatment modality for CRC; however, it is not without its complications, including various postoperative infections, intestinal hyperpermeability, anastomotic leak, ileus, diarrhea, etc. (5-7). Postoperative complications, especially infections, can worsen patients' general condition, prolong hospitalization, delay recovery, impede adjuvant treatments, increase readmission rates, decrease survival rate, and increase healthcare costs (5, 8-11).

In recent years, researchers have increasingly focused on probiotic supplementation to balance the intestinal microbiota for both the treatment of CRC (12-15) and the management of surgical complications in CRC (16, 17). Probiotics, when administered in adequate amounts, are suggested to confer health benefits to the host through various mechanisms, including the inhibition of harmful bacteria, production of antimicrobial compounds, and modulation of immune responses (18, 19). The gut microbiota consists of trillions of microorganisms residing in the gastrointestinal tract, playing important roles in health, digestion, immunity, and the integrity of the gut barrier. Gut microbiota also plays an important role in CRC pathogenesis and postoperative recovery (11). Probiotics, especially Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, have been shown to improve surgical outcomes and reduce post-surgical complications after CRC surgery (20, 21).

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting probiotic use in colorectal surgery (17, 22, 23), significant gaps remain in our understanding of their impact on the functional restoration of the intestinal barrier. Disruption of intestinal barrier integrity plays a central role in both the pathophysiology of CRC and its postoperative course. In patients with CRC, increased intestinal permeability facilitates the translocation of luminal antigens, toxins, and microbes into the lamina propria, triggering inflammatory cascades that contribute to tumor progression and metastasis (24-27). Beyond tumor biology, impaired barrier function has direct clinical implications in the perioperative setting, as it predisposes patients to infectious complications, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), and anastomotic leakage. Markers such as transepithelial electrical resistance (TER) and the lactulose/mannitol (L/M) ratio provide objective measures of this “physiological seal”. These markers are indicators of intestinal permeability and gut barrier integrity, and are linked to the pathophysiology of postoperative recovery, with reduced TER or elevated L/M ratio reflecting barrier dysfunction and being associated with worse postoperative outcomes (12).

Although previous reviews have examined the impact of probiotics on broad clinical outcomes and inflammatory markers following CRC surgery (13, 28, 29), there is a paucity of studies synthesizing data specifically related to the physiological integrity of the intestinal barrier. Addressing this substantial gap, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), we evaluated the effects of perioperative probiotics in patients with stage I–III CRC across a broad range of postoperative outcomes, including infectious, gastrointestinal, and surgical complications, as well as recovery parameters. Importantly, we assessed key markers of barrier integrity, including TER and L/M ratio, providing a more integrated and mechanistic understanding of probiotic-assisted recovery.

Additionally, two markers of intestinal barrier integrity, including lactulose/mannitol (L/M) ratio and transepithelial electrical resistance (TER), which have been associated with postoperative complications following colorectal surgery (12), were assessed. Study design, information sources, and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis is registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD420251172153) and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (31) (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). We conducted a systematic search in MEDLINE (via PubMed) (Supplementary Table S3), Scopus (Supplementary Table S4), Web of Science (Supplementary Table S5), and Cochrane Library (Table S6) from inception to February 1, 2026. No search filters were applied. The reference lists of all included studies were also manually screened to identify any additional relevant studies.

The search strategy was developed by translating our research question into key PICO components, combined with the study design. Four concept blocks were defined Patient terms (MeSH and freetext): "colorectal neoplasms", "colorectal cancers", "colorectal tumors", "colorectal carcinomas."

Eligibility criteria

All peer-reviewed clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of probiotic supplementation administered during the preoperative and/or postoperative periods on postoperative outcomes of interest in patients with CRC were considered eligible for inclusion in this systematic review. No restrictions were applied regarding patients’ age, type of surgery (open or laparoscopic), or timing of probiotic administration (pre-operative, post-operative, or both).

Studies were excluded for the following reasons: (1) non-eligible publication types (conference abstracts, review articles, trial registries, and study protocols without published results), (2) population mismatch (e.g., patients with metastatic or stage IV colorectal cancer, or patients receiving neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy/radiotherapy, or those undergoing ileostomy reversal instead of primary colorectal surgery), (3) intervention mismatch (e.g., synbiotics, prebiotics, or autoprobiotics), (4) inappropriate comparators (e.g., studies comparing probiotics with other active interventions (e.g., prebiotics) rather than placebo), (5) outcomes not relevant to postoperative complications or recovery or intestinal barrier integrity, and (6) non-English publications.

Notably, while studies were excluded when “all” participants received neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy, studies with mixed populations were retained if subgroup-specific data for eligible patients were not separately reported.

Selection process

Records retrieved from the databases were first imported into EndNote (Version X9, Clarivate PLC), and duplicates were removed. Two independent reviewers then screened all records based on titles and abstracts. Full texts of studies deemed potentially eligible were subsequently evaluated in detail. Articles that did not meet the predefined inclusion criteria were excluded. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through consensus.

Data collection

The following data were extracted by two independent reviewers: study-related variables (author, country, publication year, clinical trial registration number, and sample size), participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics (age, sex, receiving adjuvant or neoadjuvant therapies), probiotic-related variables (type and dosage of probiotics, administration time, duration of therapy), and evaluated postoperative complications or recovery parameters or intestinal integrity markers. Notably, all results that were compatible with each outcome domain in each study were sought.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the meta and metafor packages in R (version 4.5.0). The predefined outcomes included infectious complications (bacteremia, septicemia, IAA, SSI, RTI, UTI, and CLI), non-infectious complications (ileus, diarrhea, abdominal distension, and anastomotic leak), systemic inflammatory response (pyrexia), recovery-related outcomes (LOS and antibiotic duration), and functional biomarkers of intestinal barrier integrity (L/M ratio and TER).

For binary outcomes, effect sizes were expressed as risk differences (RDs) and calculated using both the metabin function from the meta package and the rma function from the metafor package. For continuous outcomes, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated using the rma function. A random-effects model based on the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator was applied in all analyses to account for between-study heterogeneity.

Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I² statistic, with values exceeding 50% were considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity of substantial heterogeneity.

Meta-analysis

For binary outcomes, RD was calculated as the difference in event proportions between probiotic and control groups using the metabin function. For continuous outcomes, SMD was calculated using the following formula:

SMD=MeanIG-MeanCGSDpooled

The pooled standard deviation (SDpooled) was calculated as:

SDpooled=(nIG-1)·SDIG2+(nCG-1)·SDCG2nIG+nCG-22

where 𝑛𝐼𝐺 and 𝑛𝐶𝐺 represent the sample sizes of the intervention and control groups, and 𝑆𝐷𝐼𝐺 and 𝑆𝐷𝐶𝐺 are their respective standard deviations. For each study, the SMD and its variance were calculated, and a random-effects model was fitted using the rma function from the metafor package. This approach provided pooled SMD estimates, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and heterogeneity measures, including the Q-test and I² statistic.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding each study from the meta-analysis. For each exclusion, the pooled RD or SMD was recalculated using the rma function in R. The influence of each study on the pooled estimates and heterogeneity statistics was evaluated. Forest plots were generated to visually represent the impact of each study's exclusion on the overall meta-analysis results.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed for outcomes with at least three studies. Egger's regression test and Begg's rank correlation test were employed to statistically evaluate potential publication bias. Funnel plots were generated using the rma function in R to visually inspect for asymmetry, which can be indicative of publication bias.

Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I² statistic and the Q-test. Galbraith plots were generated using the rma function in R to further investigate heterogeneity and identify potential outliers or influential studies.

Results

Literature search and study selection

An overview of the inclusion process is depicted in the PRISMA Flowchart (Figure 1). Our search resulted in 204 records (Medline: 204, Scopus: 27, WoS: 34, Cochrane Library: 89). After removing 83 duplicates, 121 articles were screened by title and abstract, of which 61 articles did not meet the eligibility criteria. Consequently, 60 articles remained for full‑text screening, of which 49 were excluded. Supplementary Table S7 presents a numbered list of these 49 studies along with the reason(s) for exclusion for each excluded study.

Notably, although the full text of one study was unavailable, it was included in this review based on the sample size and outcomes reported in the abstract (32). In contrast, a Chinese-language study with a research question consistent with our review was excluded; despite having an English abstract, it did not report detailed results on postoperative complications in the abstract (providing only p-values), making it unsuitable for inclusion (33).

Study characteristics

This review evaluated 11 RCTs (Table 1), including 1,234 patients with stage I–III CRC who underwent surgery. Patients were predominantly in their 60s. Study sample sizes ranged from 40 (34) to 400 (35), with most studies enrolling ≤100 patients. Notably, the study with the largest sample size (N=400) lacked a placebo control, and participants were not strictly masked (35). Another study, which recruited 78 patients with CRC, also lacked a placebo control group (36). The majority of studies (7/11) were conducted in China (32, 35, 37-41).

In two studies, a proportion of patients received neoadjuvant (49/400) (35) or adjuvant therapy (14/52) (42) in addition to surgery. Regarding the timing of probiotic administration, 2/11, 3/11, and 6/11 studies administered probiotics preoperatively, postoperatively, and both pre- and postoperatively (perioperatively), respectively. The duration of probiotic use varied considerably, ranging from 3 days (40) to one year (36). Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium were the most commonly used genera. Other microorganisms included Clostridium butyricum (35), Streptococcus thermophilus (36), as well as Enterococcus spp. (38, 40), and Saccharomyces spp. (43).

Notably, 10 of the 11 included trials were conducted before 2019, with only one study published in 2025 (35). Indeed, nearly a decade passed without new clinical trials evaluating the effects of probiotics on postoperative outcomes in CRC. Meta-analysis of postoperative infectious and non-infectious outcomes

The pooled effects of probiotics on postoperative outcomes are summarized in Table 3 and illustrated in the forest plots (Figure 2 for binary outcomes and Figure 3 for continuous outcomes).

Infectious complications

Probiotic administration was associated with a significant reduction in several infectious outcomes. Septicemia/sepsis was significantly reduced (RD = -0.158; 95% CI: -0.30, -0.016; p = 0.029), although with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 85.71%). In contrast, bacteremia did not achieve statistical significance (RD = -0.131; 95% CI: -0.272, 0.01; p = 0.068), despite a trend toward reduction and moderate heterogeneity (I² = 63.01%). Additionally, probiotics significantly reduced SSI (RD = -0.060; 95% CI: -0.111, -0.008; p = 0.024; I² = 0%), while IAA did not achieve statistical significance (RD = -0.050; 95% CI: -0.109, 0.009; p = 0.095; I² = 5.84%).

Among extra‑abdominal infections, significant reductions were observed for RTI (RD = -0.079; 95% CI: -0.124, -0.035; p < 0.001; I² = 0%) and CLI (RD = -0.096; 95% CI: -0.165, -0.027; p = 0.007; I² = 23.53%). However, UTI was not significantly reduced (RD = -0.037; 95% CI: -0.081, 0.008; p = 0.106; I² = 46.44%).

Non-infectious complications

Considering gastrointestinal complications, probiotics significantly reduced diarrhea (RD = -0.137; 95% CI: -0.233, -0.041; p = 0.005; I² = 63.31%) and abdominal distension (RD = -0.172; 95% CI: -0.292, -0.051; p = 0.005; I² = 0%). In contrast, ileus was not significantly affected (RD = -0.109; 95% CI: -0.269, 0.05; p = 0.180), with high heterogeneity (I² = 77.97%).

Considering surgical complications, anastomotic leak showed a non-significant trend toward reduction (RD = -0.034; 95% CI: -0.071, 0.004; p = 0.078; I² = 5.86%).

As shown in Figure 3, probiotics significantly improved intestinal barrier function. The L/M ratio was significantly reduced (SMD = -1.78; 95% CI: -3.25, -0.31; p = 0.018), although with very high heterogeneity (I² = 96.58%). Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses (Figure 4 and Supplementary Table S8) showed that several outcomes changed after exclusion of individual studies. For bacteremia, removal of Kotzampassi et al. (2015) resulted in a statistically significant estimate (RD = -0.22, p = 0.002). For septicemia, exclusion of Liu et al. (2013) or Zhang et al. (2012) resulted in non-significant estimates. For IAA, exclusion of Zhang et al. (2012) resulted in a statistically significant estimate (RD = -0.08, p = 0.006). For SSI, removal of Kotzampassi et al. (2015) resulted in a non-significant estimate (RD = -0.04, p = 0.172). For CLI, exclusion of Liu et al. (2013) or Liu et al. (2011) resulted in non-significant estimates. For ileus, exclusion of Yang et al. (2025) resulted in a statistically significant estimate (RD = -0.21, p = 0.004). For abdominal distension, removal of Chen et al. (2014) resulted in a borderline non-significant estimate (RD = -0.14, p = 0.058). For anastomotic leak, exclusion of Yang et al. (2025) (RD = -0.06, p = 0.012) or Yang et al. (2016) (RD = -0.04, p = 0.037) resulted in statistically significant estimates. Among continuous outcomes, exclusion of Liu et al. (2011) resulted in a non-significant estimate for LM (SMD = -2.14, p = 0.073), while removal of Xu et al. (2019) (SMD = -0.40, p = 0.030) or Yang et al. (2016) (SMD = -1.08, p = 0.046) resulted in statistically significant estimates for pyrexia duration. No changes in statistical significance were observed for RTI, UTI, diarrhea, TER, LOS, or antibiotic duration after exclusion of any single study.

Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity results are summarized in Table 3 and visually assessed using Galbraith plots (Figure 5). Most outcomes demonstrated low to moderate heterogeneity, particularly SSI, RTI, CLI, abdominal distension, and TER (I² ≈ 0–25%). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed for septicemia (I² = 85.71%), diarrhea (I² = 63.31%), UTI (I² = 46.44%), and especially L/M ratio and pyrexia (I² > 90%), indicating variability in study populations or interventions. Despite these variations, the Galbraith plots showed that the majority of studies fell within the confidence boundaries, suggesting no extreme outliers or excessive heterogeneity beyond expected limits.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test (Table 3) and visually examined using funnel plots (Figure 6). Potential publication bias was observed for diarrhea (p = 0.002), suggesting asymmetry and possible small-study effects. This finding was also supported by visual inspection of the funnel plots, where slight asymmetry was noted. No significant publication bias was detected for others. For outcomes with fewer than three studies (e.g., ileus), publication bias could not be reliably assessed.

Discussion

Patients with CRC undergoing surgical resection frequently experience compromised mucosal integrity, increased intestinal permeability, and dysbiosis (11, 44). Recent research has demonstrated the efficacy of probiotics in reducing postoperative infectious and non-infectious complications in patients undergoing abdominal surgery (17, 45). Various mechanisms have been suggested through which probiotics exert these beneficial effects, including the restoration of intestinal homeostasis, modulation of the gut microbiota, reduction of bacterial translocation, enhancement of mucosal integrity and intestinal barrier function, and regulation of the immune response (23, 26, 46).

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 RCTs, including 1,234 patients with stage I–III CRC, we found that probiotic supplementation was associated with a significant reduction in several postoperative complications, including septicemia, SSI, RTI, CLI, diarrhea, and abdominal distention. Additionally, we observed that probiotics significantly shortened the duration of postoperative antibiotic use and enhanced intestinal barrier integrity after CRC surgery, as evidenced by a reduced L/M ratio and increased TER. These findings support the hypothesis that modulation of gut microbiota may translate into clinically meaningful improvements in postoperative recovery, potentially through preservation of mucosal integrity.

However, we did not find significant differences between the probiotic and control groups in terms of postoperative bacteremia (despite a trend toward reduction), IAA, UTI, ileus, anastomotic leak (despite a trend toward reduction), pyrexia duration, or length of hospital stay. The absence of significant effects on key surgical outcomes -particularly anastomotic leakage and hospitalization duration, which are among the most critical determinants of postoperative prognosis and healthcare burden (47)- underscores the uncertainty surrounding the magnitude and consistency of the clinical benefit of probiotics in real-world perioperative settings, suggesting that their effects may be context-dependent and not uniformly generalizable across outcomes, hence the necessity of cautiously interpreting the findings.

Importantly, these findings should be interpreted in light of the substantial heterogeneity observed across several outcomes, including septicemia, ileus, L/M ratio, and pyrexia duration, which may reflect variations in study populations, probiotic formulations, intervention protocols (e.g., dosage and duration of probiotic usage), and the timing of outcome assessment. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses demonstrated that several pooled estimates were not robust, as the exclusion of individual studies altered the statistical significance of multiple outcomes. For instance, depending on the outcome, the exclusion of individual studies rendered certain results statistically significant (e.g., bacteremia, IAA, ileus, anastomotic leak, and pyrexia duration), whereas for others, the removal of individual studies led to a loss of statistical significance (e.g., septicemia, SSI, CLI, abdominal distension, and L/M ratio). Notably, the results for RTI, UTI, diarrhea, TER, length of hospital stay, and antibiotic duration remained stable and were not affected by the exclusion of individual studies. Therefore, there is a substantial need for further well-designed, adequately powered trials to precisely evaluate the impact of probiotics on clinically important endpoints.

Consistent with our findings, a 2022 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs involving 1,566 patients reported that probiotic or synbiotic administration reduced the risk of postoperative infectious complications by 37% (48). Similarly, a 2024 umbrella meta-analysis of 11 meta-analyses demonstrated that probiotic use was associated with significant reductions in the incidence of total infections, SSI, pneumonia, UTI, bacteremia, and sepsis, with moderate to high certainty of evidence (49). However, no significant effects were observed for intra-abdominal, central line, or peritoneal infections. Although these findings are broadly in line with our results, some discrepancies should be acknowledged. Notably, the umbrella review included studies evaluating both probiotics and synbiotics (49), whereas our analysis exclusively focused on probiotics to isolate their independent effect. This may partly explain the differences observed across specific outcomes such as UTI, bacteremia, or CLI.

Mechanistic insights into probiotic-mediated recovery

Probiotics exert their protective effects through an interplay of mechanical, biochemical, and immunological pathways that fortify the "physiological seal" of the gut. By upregulating tight junction proteins, such as occludin and zonula occludens-1 (50), and stimulating mucin secretion from goblet cells, these beneficial microorganisms directly reduce intestinal permeability (17, 26, 48). This stabilization of the mucosal barrier is objectively reflected in the significant improvements we observed in TER and the L/M ratio. From a pathophysiological perspective, a reduced TER and an elevated L/M ratio serve as early indicators of barrier failure, where the breakdown of apical junctional complexes allows the translocation of lipopolysaccharides and other pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) into the portal circulation (51).

This translocation typically triggers Toll-like receptor 4 signaling pathways, fueling a systemic inflammatory response that manifests clinically as pyrexia, increased antibiotic requirements, or SIRS. By stabilizing these functional biomarkers, probiotics likely mitigate the "first hit" of surgical trauma on the gut barrier. Furthermore, specific strains—notably from the genera Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium—produce short‑chain fatty acids, particularly butyrate, which serve as the primary energy source for colonocytes, promoting mucosal healing and maintaining an acidic luminal pH that inhibits the colonization of opportunistic pathogens (17, 26, 48, 52-54).

Beyond physical barrier enhancement, probiotics modulate the perioperative inflammatory milieu by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine cascades and enhancing local immune surveillance. Soluble proteins derived from certain Lactobacillus strains have been shown to inhibit cytokine-induced apoptosis of the intestinal epithelium, thereby preventing barrier breakdown during the metabolic stress of surgery (55). Collectively, these actions, ranging from competitive exclusion and biofilm formation at the anastomotic site to the production of antimicrobial peptides like defensins, provide a mechanistic framework for the reduction in postoperative complications observed in patients with CRC.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several strengths and limitations. We restricted our analysis to RCTs, enhancing internal validity, and evaluated the independent effect of probiotics without confounding from synbiotics or other interventions. Moreover, by incorporating functional biomarkers of intestinal barrier integrity (TER and L/M ratio), we provided mechanistic insight alongside clinical outcomes.

However, several limitations should be considered. Most included studies were conducted before 2019 and had relatively small sample sizes, limiting the contemporaneity and statistical power of the evidence. Substantial heterogeneity in probiotic strains, dosages, timing, and outcome assessment, as well as variable follow-up durations, may have influenced the pooled estimates. Notably, follow-up durations were generally short, restricting assessment of long-term outcomes such as progression-free survival and overall survival. In addition, key confounding factors in RCT settings, such as dietary habits, baseline gut microbiota composition, and variations in adjuvant therapies, were not consistently controlled, potentially influencing outcomes. The lack of strain-specific analyses precludes identification of the most effective probiotic formulations. Methodological limitations in several included trials, including the absence of placebo control and inadequate blinding, may introduce bias. Furthermore, high heterogeneity and sensitivity of several outcomes to individual studies raise concerns about the robustness of certain findings. Finally, the limited geographic representation of the included studies may restrict generalizability, underscoring the need for larger, well-designed, and geographically diverse trials.

Conclusion

Perioperative probiotic supplementation significantly improved specific clinical and physiological outcomes in stage I–III CRC surgery. Probiotics were associated with a reduced risk of septicemia, SSI, RTI, and CLI, as well as a shortened duration of postoperative antibiotic therapy. Mechanistically, these benefits appear to be driven by a significant enhancement of the intestinal barrier, evidenced by improved L/M ratios and TER. However, the lack of significant impact on length of hospital stays and anastomotic leakage—alongside substantial heterogeneity in outcomes like pyrexia and L/M ratio—suggests that the clinical benefits may be influenced by specific patient and intervention characteristics.

While probiotics represent a promising, low-risk strategy for enhancing postoperative recovery, their incorporation into routine clinical protocols requires further refinement. Future research should prioritize large-scale, multi-center trials to establish standardized guidelines regarding optimal strain selection, dosage, and duration of therapy. Special attention should be given to identifying patient subgroups that derive the greatest benefit and determining the ideal timing of perioperative probiotic administration. Furthermore, investigating the long-term oncological implications of probiotic use will be essential to fully defining the role of microbiota modulation in the comprehensive care of CRC.
