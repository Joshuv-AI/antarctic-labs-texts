A Mathematical Evaluation of the Effects of the Head and Neck Diameter on the Arc of Motion and the Implications in Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Stanley E Asnis, Jamie C Heimroth, Todd Goldstein
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInstability following total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a leading cause of revisions. Our objective was to evaluate the options that the surgeon has, to gain inherent stability with the use of conventional large femoral and dual mobility systems, and how the arc of motion (AOM) and jump distances (JDs) vary between them.MethodsThe head sizes examined spanned from 22 mm-54 mm, and neck sizes spanned from 10 mm-14 mm. Autodesk Fusion 360 is full-scale computer-aided designsoftware that can run simulations to validate a design. It was employed to calculate the AOM for each model. The JD was calculated with varying head sizes and 10- and 20-degree highwall liners.ResultsIncreasing head sizes from 22, 28, 32-36 mm showed a considerable increase in the AOM for every neck size; however, there was substantially less of an increase with head sizes larger than 36 mm. As neck sizes increased from 10-14 mm, the AOM decreased. The JD increased substantially with the addition of 10- and 20-degree highwall liners.ConclusionsBoth classical THA and the dual mobility systems achieve greater AOM than an anatomical normal hip. As the head diameter increases, the AOM and the JD increases. Increases in head size improve range of motion; however, head sizes over 36-40 mm gain little regarding component stability. The more durable and thinner modern polyethylene liners allow for larger conventional femoral heads. When considering between a classical THA or a dual mobility system, the minimal gains with increasing the head size in using a dual mobility system may be far outweighed by the increase in directional stability offered by the large head THA with highwall liners.Keywords: Stability THA, Dislocation THA, Dual mobility, Large head THA

Introduction

Instability is the leading complication following treatment of displaced femoral neck fractures with total hip arthroplasty (THA) [1]. Dual mobility systems were created to reduce instability following THA and increase arc of motion (AOM) [2]. The range of motion of a native hip has been measured in healthy male and female college athletes. The males had an average of 115.8 ± 11.2 degrees of flexion and at 90 degrees of flexion gave 26.9 degrees of internal and 44.7 degrees external rotation [3]. The females had an average of 122 ± 10.5 degrees of flexion and at 90 degrees of flexion gave 34.7 degrees of internal and 49.7 degrees external rotation [3]. The anatomical hip joint has constraints such as the ligament of teres, joint capsule, and tendons to keep the hip reduced whereas the THAs do not have as much support so increased AOM becomes advantageous and often necessary [4]. While there may be some advantages of dual mobility systems in the smaller patient to increase the AOM, there are also disadvantages inherent in the system [5,6]. In the average sized patient, the large head THA systems may have even more stability [7].

Multiple physiologic and biomechanical physical factors influence the risk of dislocation of THA. The physiologic factors include integrity of the capsule and pseudo-capsule, muscle and soft tissue tensioning, and enfolding of soft tissue between the neck and liner. Surgeons can control these physiologic factors during the surgery by repairing the capsule and short external rotators in the case of a posterior approach, establishing the proper tension with the neck length and offset, placing the acetabular shell in the best position for that individual, and debriding any soft tissue that may impinge [8]. The surgeon can reduce some of the risks by maximizing the AOM and jump distance (JD).

Hip instability following THA is a complex problem. The purpose of this study was to compare the AOM among dual mobility and conventional large head THA systems and investigate the impact of femoral head diameter, femoral neck diameter, and high wall liner on motion and JD.

Material and methods

There are multiple variations between different manufactures in their acetabular systems (hemisphere or low profile) and their polyethylene liners (flat face, highwall, and offset). The femoral components vary in different femoral neck sizes and shapes. In the conventional total joint prostheses and the dual mobility systems, these variations are usually small as per specific shell and head and neck size (Fig. 1) [9]. It was the goal of this study to establish mathematically how these differences affect the AOM and JDs, thus the potential stability of the construct. The AOM for this study can be defined as the angle formed by the femoral neck contacting the acetabular liner rim then rotated in an equatorial plane until the opposite side of the femoral neck contacts the opposite side of the liner rim [Fig. 2]. The JD in this article can be defined as the distance that the femoral head center must move before dislocation of the femoral head from the liner occurs. In the situation of a high wall liner this is dependent of the direction the femur is moved being increased in the direction of the highwall. The Fusion 360 Autodesk Software (Autodesk, San Francisco, CA) was used. The model that was created assumed that there was a hemispherical liner around the femoral head with the instant center of rotation of that head on the plane of the opening of the hemisphere. It also considered that neck diameters of 10 mm, 12 mm, and 14 mm would span most femoral neck sizes on the market (Fig. 2). This did not consider the shape of the neck. Some manufacturers do not have a circular cylindrical neck but are flattened in 1 plane. With these the neck cross-sectional dimensions are also covered between 10 and 14 mm. Most prosthetic hip stems do not show major differences. The orthopedic clinician can determine how different his specific system varies from this model. The findings demonstrate principles that apply to most systems. The variables included: head diameter and neck diameter. It should be noted that both the dual mobility and conventional large head flat faced systems will have intraprosthetic impingement at the same angle when the head size and neck size are the same. Whether there are 1 or 2 articulating components makes no difference. The inner head bearing size has no effect on the total AOM. The presence of a 22 mm inner bearing or a 28 mm inner bearing is the same for the same size outer head. The total AOM is determined only by the ratio of the outer head diameter and the diameter of the femoral neck. The head sizes spanned from 22 mm-54 mm. The neck sizes spanned from 10 mm-14 mm (Fig. 3). The average size head and neck dimensions quoted in the literature for the human male and female that are clinically measured as quoted in Jamali et al were entered into the software program and the AOM was calculated to determine where the neck would hit the acetabulum without ligaments or soft tissue (Fig. 4) [10]. The reason for the difference in the physically measured AOMs and those calculated is because the ligamentum teres, the labrum, and the soft tissues usually stop the joint before impingement.

The size of the dual mobility or largest conventional head that can be used with different shell sizes. Note that dual mobility often offers a larger head but the difference from a conventional large head option is small. Multiple clinical articles state that after a head size of 36 mm or 40 mm, dislocation is not caused by intraprosthetic impingement [6].

Results

Measurements of the human males showed that they physiologically had a measured AOM of 71.6 degrees and females 84.4 degrees [3]. The Fusion 360 model with the head and neck dimensions showed impingement for men at 95.2 degrees and impingement for women at 100.8 degrees. There was a steady increase in the AOM from the 22-mm heads to the 54-mm heads (Figure 3, Figure 5). The 22-mm head with a 14-mm neck gave the same motion as the average women’s calculated motion of 100.8 degrees. A 22-mm head with a 10-mm neck gave 125.8 degrees. Increasing head sizes from 22, 28, 32-36 mm showed a substantial increase in the AOM for every neck size. With head sizes ranging from 36 mm-54 mm, there was a continued increase in motion but at a much lower magnitude (Fig. 5). A 36-mm head showed an AOM of 147.6 degrees with a 10-mm neck, 141 degrees with the 12-mm neck and 136.6 degrees with the 14-mm neck (Fig. 5). In a practical sense, one must wonder if the small increases in motion beyond the 36-mm head are clinically significant?

A graphic representation of the arc of motion data calculated from the average male and females anatomic femoral head and neck dimensions and the prosthetic head and neck dimensions [7].

The study thus far calculates the motion of our theoretical head sizes with a flat faced liner. Stability also depends on the JDs. Assuming a proper orientation of the acetabular shell and liner (inclination and anteversion) is obtained in the acetabular bone, we can estimate that going from a 32-mm to a 36-mm head increases the diameter 4 mm but the radius only 2 mm. The radius is better correlated with the JD. Most different manufacturers offer highwall liners. They often are available in 10- and/or 20-degrees configurations (Fig. 6, specifications from Enovis FMP System). The dual mobility systems are essentially only flat faced systems without the option of a highwall liner. The head and neck sizes alone determine any changes in JD. A high wall liner, however, does additionally increase JDs in a specific direction. If oriented properly they can significantly add increased stability in the needed direction. It is also true that this added height in the highwall can cause earlier impingement in the specific motion that brings the neck against the highwall liner. This is almost always in a position that the patient rarely gets into and that the patient is inherent stable in due to the soft tissues and intact pseudo-capsule support.

Note that the jump distance gained by the dual mobility vs a standard “flat face” liner is very small. 2) The jump distance increased by using a 10-degree or 20-degree highwall oriented in the proper position with the conventional arthroplasty system is substantial (highwall specifications used from the Enovis Empowr System).

Discussion

Instability following a THA is a leading cause for revision THA that can increase health-care expenditure and length of stay [11]. Previous studies have shown that dual mobility systems increase stability by increasing range of motion [6,12]. Terrier et al. showed that dual mobility systems had greater range of motion until impingement compared to the conventional implants but with lower resistive torque at subluxation compared to constrained implants [12]. However, Terrier et al. only looked at 32 mm femoral head sizes [12]. While there are advantages of dual mobility systems, there are also disadvantages. The disadvantages of dual mobility systems include dislocation, intraprosthetic dissociation, and polyethylene wear. Addona et al. showed a dislocation rate of 4.5% among dual mobility systems with an intraprosthetic dissociation rate of 71% [13]. Reduction with dissociation is a unique failure for dual mobility systems. D’Apuzzo et al. found that an average force of 261 Newtons was needed to cause an intraprosthetic dislocation [14]. They demonstrated that the inner and outer polyethylene both had signs of wear; however, inner polyethylene had higher damage scores [14]. Dual mobility systems for larger head sizes could have increased overall polyethylene wear. Other disadvantages of a metal liner in a metal shell component include: 1) improper mating of the inner metal liner in the outer metal shell, 2) a source of metal ion production, and, 3) years later if the inner liner becomes difficult to remove from the outer shell the acetabular screw head(s) are trapped and the removal of the acetabular component without losing excessive host bone is a problem.

Previous studies have shown that larger head sizes increase the hip range of motion [7,15]. Howie et al showed that head sizes of 36 mm had significantly lower incidence of dislocation within 1 year following primary THA and revision THA when compared to 28 mm femoral head sizes [7]. In these cases, the JD becomes the major factor. JD of a total hip is defined as the degree of lateral translation of the femoral head center required before dislocation occurs. The smaller the distance, the higher the theoretical risk of dislocation. The JD increased as the head diameter increased (0.4 mm each mm diameter for a 45-degree abduction angle) [16].

While larger femoral heads decrease the dislocation rate due to increased JD and increased range of motion, our study shows that there may be a ceiling to the increased stability once the femoral head size reaches 36 mm or 40 mm. The AOM in head sizes above 40 mm is so large that impingement is more dependent on soft tissues and the femur impinging on the pelvis. The newer highly cross-linked polyethylene materials have allowed thinner acetabular inserts with improved wear characteristics. With a thinner polyethylene thickness requirement, the large head options with conventional systems allow a head size very close to that obtained with the dual mobility system. There are tradeoffs to using larger femoral heads, however, such as increased polyethylene volumetric wear and increased trunnionosis [[17], [18], [19], [20]].

Another way to increase the stability of a THA is by increasing the JD. Large femoral heads increase the JD because there is a larger femoral head to displace [21]. JD is increased by increasing the femoral head size but proportional to the increase in radius. Another way to increase the JD is by adding a high wall liner. Alberton et al has shown that using elevated rim liner decreased the dislocation rate [22]. There was a 3.8% dislocation rate with the elevated rim liner compared to 8.4% dislocation rate when no elevated rim liner was used [22].

The data charts given can help the surgeon in making decisions in the planning of their surgery. They can use the specification of the manufacturer’s system they are using and get an estimate of the theoretical motion they can achieve. In the case of a revision, the shell's size and alignment in the acetabulum are known. The use of the charts presented in this paper can help determine if the shell should be changed to a different shell, either larger or in a different orientation. If the surgeon knows the approximate size of the acetabular shell he will use, he can go to the manufacture specifications of the system and see his options. We suggest the clinician individualize our manufacture specification chart to the specifications available from the manufacture they use (Fig. 1). If stability is an issue, then the maximum head size used can be determined and the merits of the different liner options. If stability is not an issue, then in the younger patient the surgeon may use a smaller head to get increased polyethylene thickness.

Dealing with hip instability and dislocation is a complex problem. When faced with this situation the surgeon should evaluate the cause. This can often be done in the operating room with the hip open to determine whether it is impingement of the total joint components or anatomic reasons including joint tension, bony and soft tissue impingement or entrapment. Merely changing the position of the acetabular shell or femoral version may not accomplish the goals of stability. A head size of 36 mm gives excellent intraprosthetic motion. A head size of over 40 mm does not give a significant increase in motion or stability. Dual mobility loses the ability to increase JDs in the desired position which a large head conventional highwall liner can give in the direction needed for stability. The surgeon can then determine whether a constrained prothesis is needed. It must be mentioned that constrained prosthetic designs usually decrease the intraprosthetic AOM and can also fail.

Some limitations of this study include that the simulation cannot consider the patient’s anatomy and the surgical approach used for the THA. Prior to determining whether to use a classical THA system vs a dual mobility system, appropriate position of the acetabular and the femoral components need to be considered. Even with proper positioning of the components, dislocations can occur. This study gives the reader better insight into how to increase the AOM and JD. The figures do not give the dimensions used by every specific manufacturer. This study does not explore the advantages and disadvantages between highwall, lateralized or face changing plastic liners. These liner systems can add directional stability and improve directional JD. The data given should help surgeons to better plan and make logical decisions for their patients.

Conclusions

The AOM in both the classical THA and the dual mobility systems achieve greater AOM than an anatomical normal hip. The limitation in the native human hip range of motion is due to the ligamentum teres which acts as an end-range stabilizer of the hip [4]. This study shows that as the head diameter increases, the AOM and the JD increases. Increases in head size improve range of motion; however, head sizes over 36 mm exceed the range of motion necessary for component stability. When considering between a classical large head THA or a dual mobility system, a properly positioned high wall liner with its increased JD may well offer more stability in the desired direction. In primary and revision THA, the principles discussed will help the orthopedic surgeon evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of dual mobility systems and classical large head THAs.
