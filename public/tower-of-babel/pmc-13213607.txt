Dissuasive measures on tobacco products: a rapid review of global evidence
Authors: Sinan Brown, Emily Banks, Angelina Inthavong, Amy Villarosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNew and innovative measures are needed to combat tobacco industry marketing strategies and reduce the harm caused by tobacco products. This rapid review aimed to identify and synthesize global evidence relating to dissuasive tobacco product measures, defined as unappealing colours and/or messages directly applied to individual tobacco products to discourage uptake and motivate cessation. Scopus, PubMed, Ovid, and Google were systematically searched from January 2000 to September 2024 to capture all published evidence relating to dissuasive measures, regardless of study design and tobacco product investigated. ‘Forward’ and ‘backward’ citation searching was also conducted. Studies were assessed for quality using JBI critical appraisal tools. Forty-seven eligible publications from 6011 titles/abstracts were included, all relating to conventional cigarettes: 37 peer-reviewed journal articles, including 2 reviews, 29 empirical studies (19 quantitative, 9 qualitative, 1 mixed-methods) and 6 commentaries, 8 government publications, 1 market research report and 1 doctoral thesis. Evidence largely related to perceptions of dissuasive products and their likely impacts. No empirical evidence regarding the relation of dissuasive measures to tobacco control outcomes was located. When compared with standard cigarettes, darker coloured cigarettes—often brown and green—were perceived to be less appealing, whilst cigarettes with messages related to the impact of smoking on finances, others, mortality, and life expectancy were viewed as novel and effective, and capable of inciting behavioural change. The available evidence is generally supportive of dissuasive cigarettes as a tobacco control measure, although a lack of empirical evidence on tobacco control outcomes limits the findings.Keywords: tobacco, tobacco control, public health, health warning, dissuasive, policy, health promotion

Introduction

Tobacco use is the leading readily preventable cause of death and disability globally, responsible for over seven million deaths each year (WHO 2025a). Since the adoption of the World Health Organization (WHO) Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) in 2003 (WHO 2003), many countries have strengthened their tobacco control policies, contributing to declines in smoking prevalence (WHO 2025b). However, the tobacco industry continues to innovate, meaning that new measures are needed to maintain and accelerate tobacco control.

Health warnings on tobacco product packaging have been shown to decrease product appeal, increase knowledge about health effects and contribute to cessation knowledge and intentions (Essence Communications 2018, van Mourik et al. 2020, Pang et al. 2021, Gantiva et al. 2022, Kaai et al. 2023). However, people who smoke may conceal or cover the packaging, use a case, or purchase individual cigarettes, meaning that health warnings are avoided. The tobacco industry has facilitated this avoidance behaviour through the sale of purpose-built tins, stickers and split-packs (Swanson 2006, Chapman 2007, Davies 2017). People who smoke, and those around them, see the individual tobacco product more frequently and intimately than the packaging, with an estimate of up to 73 000 views of a cigarette per year for a person who smokes one pack per day (Smith et al. 2017). In certain jurisdictions, cigarettes may be adorned with a brand name/logo or decorative feature, or vary in length, diameter, colour, flavour, or aroma, facilitating product promotion and appeal to carefully selected target markets (Boyd et al. 2003, Cook et al. 2003, Borland and Savvas 2013, Smith et al. 2017). Furthermore, cigarettes often remain visible long after their use and are the most littered item globally (WHO 2022).

These factors highlight the potential of dissuasive measures on tobacco products: the application of unappealing design elements, such as repellent colours and/or messages, to individual tobacco products to prevent uptake and encourage cessation (Fig. 1a). These measures could preclude the tobacco industry from using marketing and promotional features on tobacco products and complement existing measures such as graphic health warnings and plain packaging. They may be particularly useful in settings where individual tobacco products are sold frequently.

A guiding principle of the WHO FCTC (WHO 2003) and a fundamental consumer right (Chapman and Liberman 2005) is accurate information about the harms of tobacco use. Dissuasive measures support this right and are also relevant to Article 11 (WHO 2013a) (guidelines: ‘health warnings and messages to be printed on the filter overwrap portion of cigarettes’) and Article 13 (WHO 2013b) (guidelines: ‘packaging, individual cigarettes or other tobacco products should carry no advertising or promotion, including design features that make products attractive’) of the WHO FCTC.

Despite being included in WHO FCTC guidelines, Canada (Health Canada 2023) and Australia (Australian Government 2024) are the only two nations to mandate dissuasive on-product messages (Fig. 1b). Canadian legislation, as of 1 August 2023, requires messages (in both English and French) be applied to standard- and king-size cigarettes and little cigars with tipping paper and tubes (Health Canada 2023, Campaign for Tobacco-Free Kids 2025). In Australia, as of 1 April 2025, cigarettes with filters must display one of eight messages, such as ‘Damages DNA’ and ‘Causes 16 cancers’. Dissuasive cigarettes have been a topic of debate in UK Parliament (most recently in 2022) (UK Parliament 2022) and were mentioned as a potential regulatory action in Scotland’s 2018 tobacco control action plan (Scottish Government 2018).

There is limited synthesized evidence on dissuasive tobacco measures. A systematic review published in 2018 (Drovandi et al. 2018b), and a scoping review published in 2023 (Al-Zalabani et al. 2023) are, to the best of our knowledge, the only synthesized evidence available globally. There is a need to review the contemporary evidence regarding the likely effectiveness of these measures. The Australian Government Department of Health, Disability and Ageing commissioned this rapid review regarding the likely effectiveness of dissuasive measures as novel tobacco control measures in 2023 to inform Australian policy development, with an update in late 2024.

Methods

Search strategy

This rapid review was conducted according to guidance and methodological considerations from King et al. 2022. In May 2023, Scopus, PubMed, and Ovid (Journals@Ovid, MEDLINE, APA PsycInfo) databases were searched for English-language (due to time constraints) research on dissuasive measures on tobacco products published between January 2000 and May 2023 (see Supplementary File S1 for search strategy). These databases were selected due to their comprehensive collections of public health literature.

In addition, ‘forward’ (identifying relevant research citing the included publication) and ‘backward’ (identifying relevant research that the included publication cited) citation searches were conducted in May 2023. In June 2023, Google was used for the grey literature search, with no date restrictions on inclusion (see Supplementary File S2 for search strategy). Updated searches were conducted in September 2024 following the same procedures.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Due to the paucity of evidence, the Department requested all published evidence on dissuasive on-product tobacco measures—regardless of design and outcome—be included in this review. Therefore, primary and secondary research and commentary papers were all eligible for inclusion. Research on all conventional tobacco products (e.g. cigarettes, cigarillos) was eligible for inclusion.

Data screening and extraction

Database search results were imported into EndNote, where duplicates were identified and removed, before being imported into Covidence where remaining duplicates were removed. In May 2023, one review author (S.B.) screened all titles, abstracts, and full texts in Covidence and extracted relevant data into a prespecified Microsoft Excel template. In September 2024, two review authors (A.I., S.B.) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts in Covidence with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Two review authors (A.I., A.V.) extracted data. For the grey literature and ‘forward’ and ‘backward’ citation searches, search results were manually screened and extracted by one review author (S.B.).

Quality assessment

Two review authors (A.I., A.V.) conducted quality assessment—using JBI critical appraisal tools—for eligible studies. For the purposes of quality assessment, we considered quantitative studies involving an intervention without randomized controls, and studies investigating reactions to dissuasive cigarettes e.g. choice experiments, as quasi-experimental studies (see Supplementary File S4 for quality assessment methods and findings).

Data synthesis

Pooled analysis, such as meta-analysis, was not conducted due to heterogeneity in interventions, outcomes, and designs. Key findings are grouped and narratively summarized by type of dissuasive measure (colour, message, or both) or policy/government considerations. Within each section, quantitative results are described first, followed by qualitative findings.

Results

Overview of included publications

After removal of duplicates, 6011 publications had their titles and abstracts screened. From these, 47 eligible publications were identified (Table 1, Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S1). More than three-quarters (77%) were published in 2016 or later. Publications were from Australia (n = 7), Scotland (n = 7), UK (n = 7), Canada (n = 3), France (n = 2), New Zealand (n = 2), Singapore (n = 2), Colombia (n = 1), India (n = 1), The Netherlands (n = 1), Norway (n = 1), Saudi Arabia (n = 1), US (n = 1), or multiple nations (n = 2) (n = 9 had a global focus). Thirty-seven were peer-reviewed journal articles, including 2 reviews, 29 empirical studies (19 quantitative, 9 qualitative, 1 mixed-methods), and 6 commentaries, 8 were government publications, 1 was a market research report, and 1 was a doctoral thesis. All publications related to conventional cigarettes: 6 focused on colour, 16 on messages, 10 on both, and 15 were related to policy/government.

Selection of publications for inclusion in the review.

No studies of the direct empirical relation of policies and regulations to smoking prevalence, uptake, and quitting were identified. Studies generally provided participants with examples of potentially dissuasive cigarettes—typically photographs/images of cigarettes in the online cross-sectional studies and physical cigarettes in the qualitative studies—and asked them to rate factors such as attractiveness, appeal, quality, taste, strength, and harm. Other outcomes included perceived impact on smoking behaviour, intent or interest in purchasing/trialling/quitting, and level of support. Sample sizes were generally small, ranging from 9 to 1766 participants. Studies were conducted in adolescent, youth, and adult populations, in people who do and do not smoke.

Thirty-two publications were eligible for quality assessment. In two publications, two cross-sectional studies were conducted, and each had their quality assessed. No evidence was excluded from the review on the basis of quality and no publications were rated high quality. The systematic and scoping reviews were both rated medium quality as neither assessed the likelihood of publication bias and the criteria for appraising studies was not appropriate (Supplementary Table S2). Of the 22 quasi-experimental studies, 10 were rated medium quality and 12 were rated low quality, many of which did not use control groups or provide sufficient information to determine whether outcomes were measured in a reliable way (Supplementary Table S3). Of the 10 qualitative studies, 9 were rated medium quality and 1 low quality, largely due to lack of exploration of the cultural and theoretical positions of the researchers and their potential influence on the research (Supplementary Table S4).

Perceptions of dissuasive colours on cigarettes

Three quantitative and three qualitative studies investigated dissuasively coloured cigarettes.

In an Australian cross-sectional study, a cork cigarette with gold band was rated most attractive, highest quality, strongest tasting and most preferred compared to a plain cork cigarette, white-tip cigarette, blue-tip cigarette, and cigarette with fancy embossed tip. The blue-tip cigarette was rated least attractive, lowest quality, and least preferred (Borland and Savvas 2013). In a French cross-sectional study, grey cigarettes were perceived to have a greater impact on smoking behaviour (preventing initiation, reducing consumption, and motivating cessation) than standard, slim, or pink cigarettes. Standard, slim, and pink cigarettes were also viewed as more expensive, tastier, and higher quality than grey cigarettes, which were considered the most dangerous. Females were significantly more likely than males to select grey cigarettes as the most effective for preventing initiation, reducing consumption, and motivating cessation (Gallopel-Morvan et al. 2019b). A US cross-sectional study compared two cigarettes with filters (one cork and one white, both 85 mm in length) and an unfiltered 70 mm cigarette. The cork-tip cigarette was most frequently selected for attractiveness and taste, whilst the white-tip cigarette was selected as least dangerous. The unfiltered cigarette was selected as most dangerous (O’Connor et al. 2015).

In Scottish focus groups, brown cigarettes were consistently viewed as the least appealing, most unpleasant tasting, and most harmful, described as ‘old fashioned’ and ‘disgusting’ [compared with cork, pink, and black cigarettes in one study (Moodie et al. 2015a), and cigarettes of various lengths, diameters, and colours in another (Ford et al. 2014)]. Pink cigarettes—viewed as highly appealing, pleasant tasting, and least harmful—were seen as ‘fun’ and ‘pretty’. Black cigarettes received mixed responses for appeal and taste, whilst the two cork cigarettes generally had low appeal (Moodie et al. 2015a).

Cigarettes of dark shades—particularly brown and green—were regarded as ‘disgusting’, ‘gross’, and ‘weird’ and a reminder of the risks of smoking, whilst white cigarettes were described as ‘clean’, ‘healthy’, and ‘pure’ in New Zealand qualitative research (Hoek and Robertson 2015). Brightly coloured and pastel cigarettes were seen as novel and attractive. Some participants thought changes in cigarette appearance could confound their desired social identity, challenge their reasons for smoking, and trigger quit attempts. Others believed a dependence on nicotine would override the measure (Hoek and Robertson 2015).

Perceptions of dissuasive messages on cigarettes

Ten quantitative studies, five qualitative studies, and one mixed-methods study compared perceptions of a range of dissuasive messages—generally relating to health and mortality impacts, social and financial consequences, and support for quitting.

In Australian cross-sectional research, participants perceived that cigarettes displaying mortality statistics and the social and financial consequences of smoking would be more effective than current packaging warnings in discouraging smoking uptake and encouraging quitting (Drovandi et al. 2018a, 2019a, 2020, 2023). Supportive messages to quit were rated less effective, whilst health risk messages were rated equally effective in two studies and more effective in one. The ‘Minutes of life lost’ cigarette was rated particularly effective across the studies. In general, there were no differences in perceived effectiveness by age, gender, or smoking status. Support for inclusion on cigarettes ranged from 78.7% to 91.4%, with support greater among people who do not smoke and people who formerly smoked relative to people who currently smoke (Drovandi et al. 2018a, 2019a, 2020, 2023).

In a cross-sectional study of people who smoke from Australia, Canada, the UK, and the US, five on-product cigarette messages were perceived to be more effective than current packaging warnings in prompting them to quit (in descending order): financial cost of smoking; effect of smoking on others; risk of mortality from smoking; minutes of life lost; and planning to quit (Drovandi et al. 2019c). ‘Dealing with cravings’ and ‘social issues with smoking’ cigarettes were rated less effective and ‘risk of addiction from smoking’ was equally effective. Males, those from the UK (relative to Australia), and those perceiving smoking as quite/very harmful (relative to some harm) were significantly more likely to perceive the messages as being effective, whilst those smoking ≥21 cigarettes per day (relative to occasional smoking) were significantly less likely. Around half (50.7%) of participants agreed or strongly agreed with the measure (Drovandi et al. 2019c).

In a Saudi Arabian cross-sectional study, cigarettes from all four themes (messages adapted from Drovandi et al. 2023) were perceived to be more effective than packaging warnings in preventing smoking uptake and prompting quitting (Al-Ahmadi et al. 2024). Those with secondary education and lower (relative to university education and higher) were significantly more likely to perceive the messages as effective. There were no differences by age, gender, or employment status. Most participants (71.6%) either agreed or strongly agreed with the measure, with people who do not smoke three times as likely as people who do smoke to support (Al-Ahmadi et al. 2024).

In a Scottish cross-sectional study, the ‘minute’ (‘Each puff reduces your life expectancy’ and 1–11-minute markings) and ‘toxic’ (‘arsenic, benzene, formaldehyde, pesticides, tar’) cigarettes were rated less attractive than a standard cigarette (Hassan and Shiu 2015). Quitting intentions increased after exposure to both cigarettes, although to a greater extent for the ‘minute’ cigarette. In a second cross-sectional study in Greece, quitting intentions increased after participants held a ‘minute’ cigarette (Hassan and Shiu 2015).

Seventy-one per cent and 22% of participants in UK and Indian cross-sectional studies respectively thought ‘Smoking kills’ cigarettes would put people off starting to smoke (Moodie et al. 2017, Mullapudi et al. 2023). Fifty-three per cent thought it would make people want to give up smoking (32% in Indian study) and 85% thought all cigarettes should have a message on them (36% in Indian study). In general, there were no differences in perceptions by age or gender. The UK study found that people who have never smoked and people who formerly smoked or experimented were significantly more likely than people currently smoking to support the measure and perceive that it would put people off starting to smoke and make people want to give up smoking (Moodie et al. 2017, Mullapudi et al. 2023).

Cigarettes displaying ‘Smoking causes cancer’ were included as an attribute in two discrete choice experiments (DCEs) (Guindon et al. 2024, Rodríguez-Lesmes et al. 2024). In a Canadian DCE, plain packaging and the dissuasive cigarette decreased the probability of a purchasing preference for around 20% of participants. Risk perceptions were not affected by any product or attribute (Guindon et al. 2024). A Colombian DCE found that the dissuasive cigarette did not affect perceptions of risk and had a limited role in terms of choice (Rodríguez-Lesmes et al. 2024).

In Australian qualitative research, cigarettes displaying the financial burden of smoking, impact of smoking on physical appearance and others, and reduced life expectancy (‘Minutes of life lost’) were perceived to be particularly novel and effective when shown to individuals of any smoking status, but to a greater extent among people who do not smoke (Drovandi et al. 2019a, 2019b). Messages describing more proximal and tangible consequences of smoking were perceived to be the most likely to influence smoking behaviour, with some participants not identifying with the distal threats of chronic disease (Drovandi et al. 2019a, 2019b).

In two focus group studies in Scotland, participants indicated they would feel embarrassed to display a ‘Smoking kills’ cigarette in public (Moodie et al. 2015b, Moodie et al. 2020). Some participants thought they may be questioned, judged, perceived negatively, or appear foolish (Moodie et al. 2020). Several participants indicated they would question their smoking behaviour, reduce consumption, or stub cigarettes out early, whilst others stated they would not alter their smoking behaviour as they have already made the purchase, are addicted, aware of the health risks, or lack the willpower to quit (Moodie et al. 2020). Some participants thought the cigarette was ‘desperate’ or claimed those who smoke are already cognisant of the risks (Moodie et al. 2015b).

UK packaging and marketing experts viewed ‘Smoking kills’ cigarettes as ‘powerful’ and ‘brilliant’, more difficult to avoid than packaging warnings, and a constant reminder of smoking’s risks (Moodie 2016). There was a general view that they could have a powerful psychological impact, affecting the image of smoking, particularly for young people. Combining messages with unpleasant black, brown, or dark green colours was suggested, as colours elicit meaning and association and can influence taste. Inexpensive, non-toxic vegetable-based inks were mentioned (Moodie 2016).

In Canadian market research, ‘Tobacco kills’ cigarettes were rated most effective in gaining attention and prompting thoughts about the impact of smoking, followed by ‘Every cigarette causes you damage’, ‘Quit smoking—Save money’, and ‘Canada Duty Paid’ (Informa Market Research 2006). Shock was a common initial reaction—likened to the introduction of packaging warnings—and some participants wondered if they could remove the message or if the dye is toxic. Messages centring on the adverse effects of smoking on children and people who do not smoke were also considered viable (Informa Market Research 2006).

Perceptions of dissuasive colours and messages on cigarettes

Two reviews (one systematic, one scoping), six quantitative studies, and two qualitative studies either compared perceptions of cigarettes with colours and cigarettes with messages, or investigated perceptions of cigarettes that featured both.

The 2018 systematic review (Drovandi et al. 2018b), and 2023 scoping review (Al-Zalabani et al. 2023) both concluded that dissuasive cigarettes represent a potentially effective tobacco control measure.

Two UK cross-sectional studies compared perceptions of ‘Smoking kills’, green, and standard cigarettes (Moodie et al. 2018, Moodie et al. 2019). In one study, green cigarettes had the lowest likelihood of trial (Moodie et al. 2019). People who do not smoke rated the green cigarette most unfavourable, whilst people who smoke rated the green and ‘Smoking kills’ cigarettes equally unfavourable (Moodie et al. 2019). In the other study—involving people who smoke—the green cigarette was rated least appealing and least likely to trial (Moodie et al. 2018). The green cigarette was rated most harmful to health and the ‘Smoking kills’ cigarette made more participants think about the dangers of smoking (Moodie et al. 2018). Both dissuasive cigarettes were rated less desirable (more harmful, less appealing, and less likely to be tried) than the standard cigarette. Females and those of low socioeconomic status were significantly more likely to view all three cigarettes as undesirable, compared with males and those not of low socioeconomic status, respectively (Moodie et al. 2018).

A Scottish cross-sectional study found that more participants had an overall negative reaction to the ‘toxic’ cigarette (‘TOXIC’ and a skull and crossbones) (96%), compared with the ‘Smoking kills’ (94%), dark green (93%), and standard (85%) cigarettes (Mitchell et al. 2021). Most participants indicated they would not trial the ‘toxic’ (91%), dark green (90%), ‘Smoking kills’ (89%), or standard (84%) cigarettes. Younger adolescents (versus older adolescents) and people who have never smoked (versus people who have ever smoked) were significantly more likely to have negative reactions to and no trial intentions for all four cigarettes (Mitchell et al. 2021).

In a New Zealand choice experiment, participants were most likely to purchase a standard cigarette (cork filter), followed by ‘feminine’ (white filter), ‘Smoking kills’, faecal yellow-brown, slimy green, and ‘Minutes of life lost’ cigarettes (Hoek et al. 2016). Cigarettes from most to least appealing, were: standard; ‘feminine’; faecal yellow-brown; slimy green; ‘Smoking kills’; and ‘Minutes of life lost’. Females rated the faecal yellow-brown and slimy green cigarettes significantly less appealing than males did. Older people (relative to younger) rated the ‘Smoking kills’ and slimy green cigarettes significantly less appealing (Hoek et al. 2016).

In a Norwegian cross-sectional study, appeal was the highest for a white-tipped cigarette, followed by cork-tip, ‘Smoking kills’, ‘Minutes life lost’, yellow, and green cigarettes (Lund and Scheffels 2018). There were no age or gender differences for appeal; however, people who do not smoke found three of the dissuasive cigarettes significantly less appealing than people who smoke did. White-tip (33.6%) was considered best tasting [‘Smoking kills’ (0.4%) had the lowest proportion], green (43.3%) was considered most harmful [white-tip (0.4%) had the lowest proportion] and the greatest proportion wanted to try white-tip (43.0%) [‘Smoking kills’ (0.8%) had the lowest proportion]. There were no gender differences for taste or harm; however, females were significantly less likely to want to try the dissuasive cigarettes. People who do not smoke were significantly less likely than people who do smoke to nominate the dissuasive cigarettes as best tasting (Lund and Scheffels 2018).

The ‘Cancer, heart disease, stroke’ (‘cancer’) cigarette (compared to ‘Smoking is addictive’, ‘Smoking kills’, and ‘Impotence, yellow teeth, bad breath’ cigarettes) and the drab dark brown cigarette (compared to green, grey, and blue cigarettes) were rated least appealing and most harmful and had the lowest interest in product trial in a Dutch cross-sectional study (van Mourik et al. 2022). Overall, coloured cigarettes were rated more appealing and less harmful than cigarettes with messages. In a second study, the ‘cancer’ cigarette was rated least appealing and most harmful when compared to a standard cigarette, drab dark brown cigarette, and drab dark brown cigarette with the cancer message. There were no significant differences between standard and dissuasive cigarettes for appeal, harm, or trial intentions (van Mourik et al. 2022).

On-cigarette messages (‘Smoking kills’ and ‘TOXIC’ with a skull and crossbones) reduced the appeal of smoking and were viewed as off-putting and embarrassing in Scottish focus groups (Mitchell et al. 2020). The ‘TOXIC’ cigarette was viewed negatively by most participants and was described as a constant reminder of the harms of smoking. Some thought ‘Smoking kills’ would be less effective than ‘TOXIC’ as the message is commonly seen on tobacco packaging. Dark and lighter green cigarettes were seen as horrible, embarrassing, and unattractive. All four dissuasive cigarettes were rated more harmful and less attractive than a standard cigarette; however, the cigarettes with messages were considered more off-putting than the green cigarettes (Mitchell et al. 2020).

In a French qualitative study, dark brown cigarettes generated mostly negative reactions, reminding participants of tar and petrol (Gallopel-Morvan et al. 2019a). Dark green cigarettes elicited both positive (plants and leaves) and negative (algae and drugs) associations. Around half of participants indicated that they would consider quitting, reduce consumption, or not start if dissuasively coloured cigarettes were the only ones available. Dark green and dark brown cigarettes that also displayed messages (‘Smoking kills’ and skull and crossbones) generated more negative reactions than coloured cigarettes alone. The skull and crossbones evoked fear and thoughts of death, poison, and toxicity, whilst ‘Smoking kills’ was less associated with danger and received fewer reactions. Two-thirds of participants indicated they would feel embarrassed to smoke cigarettes with such messages in public. One-third of participants thought upper case messages increased both message visibility and the perceived danger of tobacco. Marlboro and Vogue cigarettes were viewed as tastier, more attractive, less dangerous, and better quality than the dissuasive cigarettes (Gallopel-Morvan et al. 2019a).

The potential of dissuasive cigarettes in tobacco control policy

Six commentaries and one doctoral thesis discussed the potential of dissuasive cigarettes in tobacco control policy. Commentary pieces argued that dissuasive cigarettes are a logical extension of existing policy (Moodie 2018). As a constant reminder of the health harms of smoking, they are visible ‘every puff of every cigarette’ (Cunningham 2022). Dissuasive measures could reduce the psychological distance between a cigarette’s appearance and its effects (Hoek and Gendall 2017). Given that tobacco companies already place their branding on cigarettes, the measure is evidently feasible, and potentially low-cost if borne by industry (Moodie 2018). Physical and psychological avoidance behaviour—as can occur with packaging warnings—would be more difficult (Moodie 2018). They could increase warning exposure for those with less exposure to tobacco packaging, such as people experimenting with single cigarettes, often youth (Moodie 2018), and those in locations such as Southeast Asia where the sale of individual cigarettes is prevalent (Sarode et al. 2024). Messages could be tailored to high-use populations as a pro-equity measure (Houghton et al. 2019). One commentary noted the importance of implementing a comprehensive policy to limit industry’s ability to exploit regulatory loopholes (van der Eijk 2024). A doctoral thesis from The Netherlands evaluated health warnings and dissuasive cigarettes in the European Union and included a cross-sectional study (identified in the database search as a peer-reviewed journal article) assessing perceptions of dissuasive cigarettes (van Mourik 2019).

Government consideration of dissuasive cigarettes

Eight government-related publications were identified in the search.

In a 2018 Health Canada public consultation, most respondents, other than those from the tobacco industry, supported dissuasive cigarettes (Health Canada 2019). Key suggestions included: focusing on concise messages related to health impacts and smoking cessation; using large, impactful font and different colours for different types of messages; tailoring messages to high-use populations; and ensuring cultural appropriateness. Two respondents not affiliated with the tobacco industry thought the measure was disrespectful to people who smoke, whilst industry expressed concerns regarding ink toxicity, a lack of evidence, and the cost of changing printing machinery and inks (Health Canada 2019).

In an impact analysis by Australia’s Office of Impact Analysis, dissuasive cigarettes were a proposed policy option to drive continued reductions in smoking prevalence (Office of Impact Analysis 2023). Potential costs to government included establishing a new regulatory framework, ongoing monitoring and enforcement, and other costs. Potential costs to manufacturers and importers included re-engineering products to ensure compliance with regulations. The total estimated compliance costs for business, which included manufacturers, retailers, and wholesalers, averaged $2.1 million annually (Office of Impact Analysis 2023).

The ‘Cigarette Stick Health Warnings Bill [HL]’, a Private Member’s Bill brought from the UK House of Lords, proposed eight messages for individual cigarettes and cigarette rolling papers (UK Parliament 2022). Mentions of dissuasive cigarettes appear in the House of Lords Hansard (UK Parliament 2020) and House of Commons Hansard (UK Parliament 2013). Scotland’s 2018 tobacco control action plan listed ‘making cigarettes less attractive’—through changes to colour, composition, and/or warning messages—as a potential regulatory action to restrict demand (Scottish Government 2018). Since March 2013, all cigarettes sold in Singapore have been required to display the letters ‘SDPC’ (Singapore Duty-Paid Cigarette) and a series of vertical bars (Singapore Customs 2008, 2012). Designed to curb the sale, purchase, and possession of duty-unpaid (contraband) cigarettes, the markings reveal an added benefit of dissuasive cigarettes in helping distinguish licit and illicit products.

Discussion

This review was conducted to identify evidence of the likely effectiveness of dissuasive measures on tobacco products, with the purpose of rapidly informing Australian policy. Reflecting the lack of global implementation sufficient to allow policy evaluation, evidence to date is largely limited to perceptions/ratings of various qualities of dissuasive cigarettes, such as harm, strength, appeal, and taste and perceived behavioural impact, including purchase, trial/initiation, consumption, and cessation.

The available evidence is largely supportive of dissuasive cigarettes as a strategy to reduce the desirability of cigarettes. In general, darker coloured cigarettes, particularly green and brown, were least preferred, whilst standard (white and cork), pink, and other brightly coloured cigarettes were viewed as most appealing. Cigarettes bearing certain messages, especially the financial consequences of smoking and impact of smoking on others, were perceived to be the most novel and effective, as were messages that centred on mortality and reduced life expectancy, such as the ‘Minutes of life lost’ cigarette. ‘Smoking kills’ was a commonly used message that was generally perceived to be effective, however some participants [from Scottish (Mitchell et al. 2020) and French qualitative studies (Gallopel-Morvan et al. 2019a)] thought it may not produce the optimal response as it is commonly included on tobacco packaging. Participants indicated dissuasive cigarettes could challenge their smoking behaviour, reduce the appeal of smoking, and reduce consumption, with embarrassment and fear of judgement or ridicule cited. Furthermore, the available evidence suggests the impact of dissuasive cigarettes may be more pronounced in people who do not smoke or who formerly smoked, relative to people who do smoke. Commentaries frequently highlighted that dissuasive measures could act as a constant reminder of the harms of smoking, in addition to making avoidant behaviour more difficult, including for those who consume/purchase individual cigarettes. A majority (21/34) of studies were rated medium in quality assessment, and no studies were rated high quality.

Various theories and models were put forward by the authors of the included publications as putative mechanisms. Borland and Savvas (2013) cite the ‘sensation transfer technique’, a testing methodology often used by the tobacco industry which has shown that perceptions of cigarette attributes, such as taste, vary depending on elements of cigarette design and branding. The health belief model describes how an individual’s perceptions can influence their health-related decisions and behaviours (Janz and Becker 1984). Cue consistency theory relates to the impact of inconsistent/disruptive cues (Slovic 1966). When a green cigarette is presented instead of a white one for instance, a consumer’s expectations and consumption experience are potentially subverted. Cognitive dissonance theory may also be relevant: dissuasive measures could generate cognitive dissonance, a state of tension where inconsistent beliefs or attitudes are held (Festinger 1957). Construal level theory describes how thought and behaviour are shaped by ‘psychological distance’ (either time, space, social distance, or hypothetically), whereby individuals use concrete/detailed low-level construals for proximal events and abstract high-level construals for distant events (Trope et al. 2007).

This review has also highlighted significant gaps in the research literature. No empirical evidence on the relation of dissuasive measures to tobacco control outcomes was located as only Canada and Australia have very recently implemented policies. As such, studies measured behavioural intentions or perceptions as opposed to actual behaviour. All tobacco products were eligible for inclusion; however, no data relating to non-cigarette products were identified. Studies were almost exclusively conducted in high-income Western settings. Studies were generally small and in nonrepresentative populations, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Exposure to the cigarettes was brief, failing to replicate a real-world situation of repeated exposures over time. Similarly, cigarettes were not presented alongside the packaging they are typically contained in. Many studies used photographs/images of cigarettes, preventing tactile interaction. Participant responses may have been influenced by the novelty of the cigarettes, the order in which they were presented, and socially desirable responding. Visibility of known brand names may also have influenced responses. Given the policy/advocacy-driven nature of the topic, there is the possibility of publication bias. Key limitations of this review itself were the rapid nature of the review (meaning that not all papers were screened and appraised by two reviewers), the heterogeneity of identified studies (meaning that pooled analyses were not feasible), and restriction to English language research (meaning that relevant non-English evidence may have been missed). A key strength of the review was its use of grey literature, enabling more extensive insight into the topic.

Future research should be conducted in a wider range of settings, focusing on low- and middle-income countries and those where the sale of individual cigarettes is commonplace. This is particularly important in light of evidence that colour perceptions are culture-specific (Aslam 2006). Large longitudinal studies that assess the impact of repeated exposure to dissuasive cigarettes over time are needed. Research should expose participants to packaging and dissuasive cigarettes simultaneously, more accurately replicating real-world exposure. There is scope to investigate other outcomes, such as attention and comprehension and the impact of typographical variations; an included study found that upper case messages increased message visibility and perceived danger (Gallopel-Morvan et al. 2019a), with marketing research not identified in this review suggesting perceptions of brand friendliness are heightened by lower case text (Xu et al. 2017).

Tobacco companies are motivated by the generation of profit and the transfer of this wealth to shareholders and investors (Wood et al. 2023). As the most consumed nicotine-delivery product globally (WHO 2025a), conventional cigarettes are a major driver of company profits (Branston 2021, Tobacco Tactics 2025). It is therefore in the interests of tobacco companies to make cigarettes as appealing and addictive as possible in order to continue maximizing sales and company profits. Visual elements of cigarette design, including branding, decorative features, and colour contribute to their appeal (Borland and Savvas 2013, Smith et al. 2017). The potential of dissuasive cigarettes as a tobacco control measure lies in their ability to make cigarettes less appealing in two ways: first by applying unappealing colours and/or messages, and second by obstructing the appealing elements mentioned above.

Dissuasive cigarettes, and any other measure that reduces the appeal and desirability of cigarettes—a major contributor to tobacco-related morbidity and mortality—should be considered in public health policy. The tobacco industry has a long history of litigating, or threatening to litigate, governments in order to obstruct the implementation of tobacco control measures (George et al. 2018). Policymakers and governments intending to legislate dissuasive on-product measures should be aware of and prepare for industry interference, a major barrier to WHO FCTC implementation (WHO 2021).

Conclusion

This review has identified dissuasive cigarettes to be a promising strategy in tobacco control policies. However, direct empirical evidence investigating the relation of dissuasive cigarettes to tobacco control outcomes is lacking as only Canada and Australia have implemented policies. Additionally, included evidence was rated low/medium in quality assessment. When used in parallel with strategies such as plain packaging and health promotion inserts, dissuasive cigarettes could stymie promotional product features and create a negative consumption experience from when the pack is purchased through to the last puff. This review was commissioned by the Australian Government, with the findings informing subsequent policy development. Findings of the review can inform tobacco control policy in other jurisdictions.
