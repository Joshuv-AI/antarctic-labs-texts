Scaling Limits of a Weakly Perturbed Random Interface Model
Patrícia Gonçalves 1 , Martin Hairer 2 , Maria Chiara Ricciuti 3

Abstract
We consider a random interface model on the discrete torus with 2 ​ n 2n sites, obtained from the classical corner flip dynamics but with a weak global perturbation, namely an asymmetry of order n − γ n^{-\gamma} of the direction of growth that switches direction based on the sign of the total area under the interface. The slopes of this model can be viewed as a non-simple exclusion process at half filling with globally dependent rates. We show that, for γ = 1 \gamma=1 , the hydrodynamic equation of the empirical density is given by a time concatenation of the viscous Burgers equation and the heat equation. Moreover, for n n prime and γ > 6 7 \gamma>\frac{6}{7} , we establish convergence in law of the equilibrium fluctuations to an infinite-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.



1
Introduction

Interacting particle systems are a class of stochastic models describing the collective evolution of many simple components under random dynamics. They are a central tool in probability theory and statistical mechanics, both for their rich mathematical structure and for the insight they provide into universal scaling laws. Among the most studied examples are
exclusion processes
, where particles perform random walks on a lattice subject to the exclusion rule that forbids more than one particle per site. This simple rule already gives rise to a remarkably broad spectrum of macroscopic phenomena, and variants of the exclusion process underpin much of the modern theory of interacting particle systems.

A cornerstone of this field is the study of
hydrodynamic limits
. At the macroscopic level, the empirical particle density of exclusion processes typically converges, after rescaling, to the solution of a deterministic partial differential equation (PDE). In the nearest-neighbour symmetric case (SSEP) and under diffusive scaling, the limit is governed by the linear heat equation, whereas in the weakly asymmetric case (WASEP)  – when the asymmetry scales like
N^{-1}
– the density profile evolves according to the viscous Burgers equation. In contrast, when the asymmetry is of order one as in the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP), the microscopic dynamics exhibit a privileged direction and the characteristic time scale is much shorter: diffusive scaling no longer applies and the natural hydrodynamic time scale becomes hyperbolic. In this regime, the macroscopic density satisfies the inviscid Burgers equation, a nonlinear conservation law that typically develops shocks from smooth initial data. These results form one of the foundational successes of the subject; see the monograph of Kipnis and Landim
[
22
]
for a detailed account.

Beyond the hydrodynamic law of large numbers, one can study the
fluctuations
around the deterministic behaviour. In the diffusive setting, the stationary fluctuations of SSEP are described by an infinite-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
[
32
]
, whereas for WASEP  – this time for an asymmetry scaling as
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
– they converge to a suitably defined solution of the
stochastic Burgers equation

[
16
,
15
]
. For TASEP, in the hyperbolic time scale, fluctuations are trivial and get linearly transported in time: in order to get non-trivial fluctuations, one needs to scale time as
N^{\frac{3}{2}}
, yielding a non-Gaussian limiting field described by the
KPZ fixed point

[
31
]
constructed in
[
26
]
.

In a broader setting, considerable attention has been devoted to
multi-species exclusion processes
, in which particles of different types interact and there is more than one conservation law. A paradigmatic example is the ABC model, originally introduced in
[
12
]
as a minimal model for phase separation in one-dimensional driven systems. The hydrodynamic behaviour of a variation of this model has been extensively investigated, showing convergence to coupled systems of nonlinear PDEs with rich phase diagrams; see
[
9
,
6
,
17
]
. At the level of fluctuations, multi-component exclusion systems fall within the framework of
nonlinear fluctuating hydrodynamics
, which predict long-time correlations induced by the interaction of characteristic modes. Some rigorous results supporting these predictions in related classes of one-dimensional systems have been derived; see, for example,
[
3
,
8
,
19
]
.

More generally, nonlinear fluctuating hydrodynamics and mode-coupling theory predict that one-dimensional systems with several conserved quantities give rise to an infinite discrete family of dynamical universality classes, with dynamical exponents given by ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers. Besides the diffusive and KPZ classes, this includes, in particular, the
Lévy
\frac{3}{2}
, the
Lévy
\frac{5}{3}
, and the
golden mean
universality classes. In these cases, dynamical structure functions are predicted to be governed by asymmetric Lévy-stable scaling profiles; see
[
34
,
29
,
28
,
33
]
and references therein. While numerical evidence has been reported in a variety of driven diffusive systems, the rigorous understanding of these universality classes remains largely open.

It is well known that the exclusion process can be represented in terms of an evolving interface. In one dimension, the occupation variables of the process correspond to the discrete slopes of a height function, and the associated dynamics are given by the so-called
corner flips
. In this dual picture, each particle corresponds to an upward slope and each hole to a downward slope: a rightward particle jump translates into a local maximum flipping downwards, while a leftward jump corresponds to a local minimum flipping upwards. This coupling between exclusion and interface dynamics provides an alternative geometric viewpoint and has played an important role in connecting interacting particle systems to random surfaces and growth phenomena. In particular, the seminal paper
[
21
]
introduced the
KPZ equation
as a universal model for one-dimensional interface growth, whose microscopic representatives include corner-flip evolutions. In
[
5
]
, the authors proved that the weakly asymmetric exclusion process, when written in its height-function formulation and under the characteristic
N^{\frac{1}{2}}
scaling, converges in distribution to the KPZ equation, providing one of the first rigorous derivations of this stochastic PDE from an interacting particle system. Weakly asymmetric corner flips have also been extensively studied when evolving on the finite one-dimensional lattice; see
[
10
,
24
]
and references therein.

In the present work, we introduce and study a variant of the corner-flip dynamics, which we call the
weakly perturbed interface model
. Equivalently, the slopes of the height function define a particle configuration on a discrete torus of size
2n
at half filling. The distinctive feature of our model is that the weak asymmetry of the dynamics is not fixed in time, but instead depends on a global observable, namely the signed area under the height function. When the area is positive, the dynamics exhibit a weak downward bias, while when it is negative, the bias points upward. In this sense, the model interpolates between SSEP and WASEP, but with a global feedback mechanism that drives the area back towards zero.

From a modelling perspective, such a mechanism can represent situations where global balance constraints affect local dynamics. A natural instance is the interface between two metals forming an alloy. Below the miscibility gap, namely their “mixing temperature”, the two components do not mix uniformly throughout, but at the boundary atoms of one metal can still exchange with atoms of the other. This interfacial region can be modelled via corner flips and evolves randomly, yet the total mass of each component is conserved. If the boundary drifts too far into one material, the conservation laws produce a weak restoring effect, so that flips are then biased back towards a balanced position. At the continuum level, the macroscopic evolution of the corresponding phase-field model is classically described by the Cahn-Hilliard equation, introduced in
[
7
]
. Our weakly perturbed interface model then provides a tractable instance of this type of interaction, where local randomness is tempered by a global conservation law.

1.1
Main Results

Our first main result (Theorem
2.4
) is the identification of the
hydrodynamic limit
at the critical perturbation scale. We assume that the system starts from a measure which has a relative entropy of order at most
N
with respect to the stationary measure, and that it converges to an interface profile which lives in the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{1}
. When the asymmetry is of order
N^{-1}
(
\gamma=1
), we prove that the rescaled height function converges to the solution of a nonlinear PDE which evolves according to a viscous Burgers-type equation up to the time at which the total signed area of the interface vanishes, and subsequently follows the linear heat equation. At the particle level, this means that initially the density evolves according to the viscous Burgers equation, reflecting the behaviour of WASEP. Once the area of the interface vanishes, however, the nonlinear term cancels out and the limiting equation crosses over to the heat equation, so that the macroscopic dynamics are described by a time concatenation of Burgers and heat flow. This phenomenon has no parallel in the classical exclusion setting and highlights the qualitative effect of the global feedback: even though the microscopic asymmetry is always present, its macroscopic signature disappears after the global observable changes sign.

At the level of
equilibrium fluctuations
, starting from the invariant measure of the dynamics, and provided that
\gamma>\frac{6}{7}
as well as the number of sites satisfies
N=2p
with
p
prime, we establish convergence of the fluctuation field to an infinite-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (Theorem
2.8
). This extends the classical fluctuation results for SSEP and WASEP to a setting with a non-product invariant measure and globally dependent dynamics. The threshold
\frac{6}{7}
is purely technical and we do not expect it to be optimal, and in particular we conjecture that convergence should hold at least for
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
, paralleling the corresponding result for the classical WASEP. Since our dynamics are in a sense even “more symmetric”, this further suggests that the cancellation of the nonlinear terms should persist for at least the same range of
\gamma
. On the other hand, the prime-number condition arises in our proof of correlation bounds. We obtain these estimates via a combinatorial analysis which involves exploiting algebraic properties of primitive roots of unity; although we expect the result to hold for all even
N
, this remains an open technical restriction.

Our approach follows the standard program developed for interacting particle systems, but with substantial adaptations. At both the hydrodynamic and fluctuation levels, we represent the empirical fields through Dynkin-type martingales, establish tightness of the associated processes, and characterise all possible limit points as solutions to the corresponding deterministic or stochastic PDEs. Uniqueness of solutions then allows us to deduce convergence of the process of interest. While this overall strategy is standard in the literature, the presence of a global, state-dependent asymmetry introduces several substantial technical difficulties compared to classical exclusion models.

For the hydrodynamic limit, we study both the empirical density of the underlying particle system and the appropriately-scaled area of the interface, and show that these converge to the solution of a system of coupled PDEs. Because of the nonlinearity in the martingale equation, one needs to show a
replacement lemma
for the occupation variables in order to close the equation in terms of the empirical density. However, in our setting, the entropy method of
[
18
]
cannot be directly applied, and must be modified to incorporate the presence of the sign of the area in front of the nonlinearity. We successfully achieve this in Lemma
3.4
.

Unlike the classical simple exclusion processes, the stationary measure of our dynamics, given in (
2.3
), is not of product form when projected onto particle configurations. At the level of equilibrium fluctuations, this means that its correlation functions must be estimated in order to control the degree-two term appearing in the martingale decomposition. As previously mentioned, we obtain these correlation bounds via a combinatorial analysis; see Appendix
B
. Moreover, the classical block-type estimates  – needed to study the degree-two term in the martingale equation  – require the stationary measure to be invariant under particle jumps, a property that fails in our model. To get around this, we introduce an appropriate correction term which allows us to apply the result of
[
15
]
(Lemma
4.7
). This produces additional error terms, and controlling these extra contributions imposes restrictions on the admissible scaling exponent
\gamma
: after optimising the resulting bounds, this yields the technical threshold
\gamma>\frac{6}{7}
appearing in our fluctuation theorem.

\nu_{\alpha}\left\{\xi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{T}_{N}}:\xi_{x}=1\right\}=\alpha.

The result above means the following. At first  – assuming that the integral of the initial condition
h_{0}
is not zero  – the integral of the height profile moves towards zero with a drift proportional to the average compressibility of the system, while the density profile evolves according to the viscous Burgers equation. This aligns with the fact that, until the integral changes sign, the particle system
coincides with a WASEP. Once the integral hits zero, it remains there and the density profile evolves according to the heat equation. Roughly speaking, this means that, after the discrete interface changes sign for the first time, it keeps switching sign on a faster time scale than the diffusive one, so that the nonlinearity in the Burgers equation flips sign very quickly and thus “cancels out” in the hydrodynamic equation.

A sequence of real-valued processes
\{X_{t}^{N},

t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
is tight with respect to the Skorokhod topology of
D([0,T],\mathbb{R})
if:

1.

the sequence of real-valued random variables
\{X_{t}^{N}\}_{N}
is tight for every
t\in[0,T]
, and

2.

for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mylim_{\delta\to 0}\mylimsup_{N\to\infty}\mysup_{\gamma\leq\delta}\mysup_{\tau\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|X^{N}_{(\tau+\gamma)\wedge T}-X^{N}_{\tau}\right|>\varepsilon\right\}=0,

where
\mathcal{I}_{T}
is the set of stopping times almost surely bounded from above by
T
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

i) By a classical argument (see for example
[
22
, Section 4.1]
), in order to show tightness of
\{X^{N}\}_{N}
it suffices to show that, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mylim_{\gamma\to 0}\mylimsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{\mysup_{|t-r|\leq\gamma}\left|X_{t}^{N}-X_{r}^{N}\right|>\varepsilon\right\}=0,

which follows immediately from the fact that
|X_{t}^{N}-X_{r}^{N}|\leq C|t-r|
.

ii) Given a stopping time
\tau
almost surely bounded by
T
and
\varepsilon,\gamma>0
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|X^{N}_{\tau+\gamma}-X^{N}_{\tau}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X^{N}_{\tau+\gamma}-X_{\tau}^{N}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\langle X\rangle^{N}_{\tau+\gamma}-\langle X\rangle^{N}_{\tau}\right|\right]\leq\frac{C\gamma}{\varepsilon^{2}}

for some constant
C
independent of
N
. Tightness then follows from Aldous’s criterion.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

By
[
22
, Proposition 1.7]
, it suffices to show that, for each function
\phi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, the sequence of measures induced by the sequence of real-valued processes
\{\langle\pi_{t}^{N},\phi\rangle\}_{N}
is tight. Hence, it suffices to show that each term on the RHS of (
3.10
) is tight, where
\phi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is chosen to be constant in time.

From (
3.4
), we have that

\left\langle\pi_{0}^{N},\phi\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\theta_{0}^{N}\left(\nabla_{N}\phi\right).

It is easy to see that
\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)
converges to
\frac{1}{2}\langle 1,\phi\rangle
. Also, by the hypothesis (
2.7
) on the initial profile, it is easy to see that
\frac{1}{2}\theta_{0}^{N}\left(\nabla_{N}\phi\right)
converges to
\langle h_{0},\nabla\phi\rangle
in probability. This implies that the sequence
\{\langle\pi_{0}^{N},\phi\rangle\}_{N}
is convergent, and hence tight. In particular, since
h_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
, an integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mu_{N}\left\{\left|\left\langle\pi_{0}^{N},\phi\right\rangle-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\nabla h_{0}(u)\right)\phi(u)\de u\right|>\delta\right\}=0

for each
\delta>0
.

By the mean value theorem, we have the bounds

\left|K_{t}^{N}(\phi)-K_{r}^{N}(\phi)\right|=\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{r}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\Delta_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\xi^{N}_{s}(x)\de s\right|\leq|t-r|\|\Delta\phi\|_{\infty}

and

\displaystyle\left|B_{t}^{N}(\phi)-B_{r}^{N}(\phi)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{r}^{t}\text{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\sgn(Y^{N}_{s})\left[\xi_{s}^{N}(x)-\xi_{s}^{N}(x+1)\right]^{2}\de s\right|

\displaystyle\leq|t-r|\|\nabla\phi\|_{\infty},

which shows tightness of
\{K^{N}_{t}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
and
\{B_{t}^{N}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
by Lemma
3.2
.

Finally, recalling (
3.6
), an easy computation shows that (
3.3
) can be rewritten as

\langle M^{N}(\phi)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}\eta_{s}^{N}(x)\de s,

where

\eta_{s}^{N}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\xi_{s}^{N}(x)-\xi_{s}^{N}(x+1)\right]^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,\text{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\sgn(Y_{s}^{N})\left[\xi_{s}^{N}(x)-\xi_{s}^{N}(x+1)\right].

Since
|\eta_{s}^{N}(x)|
is uniformly bounded in
N
, this again yields tightness of
\{M_{t}^{N}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}
by Lemma
3.2
.

The fact that all limit trajectories have a density
\{\rho_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
with
0\leq\rho_{t}\leq 1
follows from standard arguments.
∎

3.3
Replacement Lemma

In this section we will show the following lemma, which is a key result in the proof of Theorem
2.4
. Given an integer

\vec{\xi}^{\ell}(x):=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\ell}\xi(y),\qquad\cev{\xi}^{\ell}(x):=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{y=x-\ell}^{x-1}\xi(y),

where the sites in the sums are taken modulo
N
. Throughout, if
\ell
is not an integer,
\vec{\xi}^{\ell}
should be interpreted as
\vec{\xi}^{\lfloor\ell\rfloor}
, and similarly for
\cev{\xi}^{\ell}
. Moreover, recall the definition of
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}
as the probability measure that the process
\{h^{N}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
induces on the space of càdlàg functions
D([0,T],\Omega_{N})
when starting from a measure
\mu
: throughout, we will denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\,\cdot\,]
expectations with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}
. The following holds.

Lemma 3.4

(Replacement Lemma)
.

Let
G:[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded function, and let
\{\psi_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}
be a uniformly bounded family of real-valued functions on
\Omega_{N}
such that, for each
z\in\{x+1,\ldots,x+\varepsilon N\}
,
\psi_{x}
is invariant under the change of variable
h\mapsto h^{z,z+1}
. If
\mu_{N}
satisfies (
2.8
), then for any
t\in[0,T]
we have that

\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\sgn(Y_{s}^{N})\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G_{s}\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\psi_{x}(h_{s}^{N})\left[\xi_{s}^{N}(x)-\vec{\xi}_{s}^{\varepsilon N}(x)\right]\de s\right|=0.

(3.11)

The same result holds with the right average replaced with the left average, as long as
\psi_{x}
is invariant under the change of variable
h\mapsto h^{z,z-1}
for
z\in\{x-\varepsilon N,\ldots,x-1\}
instead.

The result above is almost identical to the well-known replacement lemma introduced in
[
18
]
, except for the presence of the term
\sgn(Y_{s}^{N})
in the integral in (
3.11
). The first part of our proof follows the argument given, for example, in the proof of
[
17
, Lemma 6.6]
. As we shall see, the presence of the sign term will present an additional difficulty, and in order to treat the resulting correction we will need the following elementary bound:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\{x_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of positive real numbers such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}x_{j}\leq M<\infty
. Then

M\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{x_{j}}-\sqrt{x_{j+1}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{(x_{1})^{2}}{4}.

Proof.

By Titu’s Lemma, we have that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{x_{j}}-\sqrt{x_{j+1}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{x_{j}-x_{j+1}}{\sqrt{x_{j}}+\sqrt{x_{j+1}}}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(x_{j}-x_{j+1}\right)\right)^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{x_{j}}+\sqrt{x_{j+1}}\right)^{2}}.

(3.12)

But now, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}x_{j}\leq M
, we have the upper bound
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{x_{j}}+\sqrt{x_{j+1}}\right)^{2}\leq 4M
. Also,
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(x_{j}-x_{j+1}\right)=x_{1}
, and thus

\eqref{eq:titu}\geq\frac{(x_{1})^{2}}{4M},

which concludes the proof.
∎

We will also use the following fact: given
h\in\Omega_{N}
and

\Theta_{N}^{k}(f):=\int_{\Omega_{N}^{k}}f(h)|U(h)|\de\mu_{N}^{*}
for each
k
odd positive integer. Since the measure
\mu_{N}^{*}(h)
only depends on the integral of
h
, there is a map
k\mapsto\nu_{N}^{*}(k)
such that
\mu_{N}^{*}(h)=\nu_{N}^{*}(k)
for any
k
and any
h\in\Omega_{N}^{k}
. Note that
q^{N}_{x,x+1}(h)\geq\frac{1}{3}
for any
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
and
h\in\Omega_{N}
, so by the inequality
(\sqrt{\sum_{i}a_{i}}-\sqrt{\sum_{i}b_{i}})^{2}\leq\sum_{i}(\sqrt{a_{i}}-\sqrt{b_{i}})^{2}
we have that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N}\left(\sqrt{f},\mu_{N}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{3}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\left[\sqrt{f(h)}-\sqrt{f(h^{x,x+1})}\right]^{2}\de\mu_{N}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\nu_{N}^{*}(k)\sum_{h\in\Omega_{N}^{k}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\left[\sqrt{f(h)}-\sqrt{f(h^{x,x+1})}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{N-1}\nu_{N}^{*}(k)\sum_{h\in\Omega_{N}^{k}}\sum_{x\in U(h)}\left[\sqrt{f(h)}-\sqrt{f(h^{x,x+1})}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{h\in\Omega_{N}^{k}}f(h)|U(h)|\nu_{N}^{*}(k)}-\sqrt{\sum_{h\in\Omega_{N}^{k}}\sum_{x\in U(h)}f(h^{x,x+1})\nu_{N}^{*}(k)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{\int_{\Omega_{N}^{k}}f(h)|U(h)|\de\mu_{N}^{*}}-\sqrt{\int_{\Omega_{N}^{k+2}}f(h)|D(h)|\frac{\nu_{N}^{*}(k)}{\nu_{N}^{*}(k+2)}\de\mu_{N}^{*}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{\Theta_{N}^{k}(f)}-\sqrt{\Theta_{N}^{k+2}(f)}\cdot e^{\frac{1}{2N}}\right)^{2}.

The equality in the penultimate line comes from Remark
3.6
and from the following argument: if
h\in\Omega_{N}^{k}
and
z\in U(h)
, then
t=h^{z,z+1}
is in
\Omega_{N}^{k+2}
, and in particular any configuration
t\in\Omega_{N}^{k+2}
has exactly
|D(t)|
preimages in
\Omega_{N}^{k}
via the maps
h\mapsto h^{z,z+1}
. Thus, by (
3.26
) and Remark
3.6
we get that

\eqref{eq:remaining_zero}\leq t\varepsilon\|G\|_{\infty}\|\psi\|_{\infty}\Theta_{N}^{1}(f)-\frac{tN}{6M}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{\Theta_{N}^{k}(f)}-\sqrt{\Theta_{N}^{k+2}(f)}\cdot e^{\frac{2}{N}}\right)^{2}.

(3.27)

It remains to show that the RHS of (
3.27
) is bounded from above by zero when
N\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since
|U(h)|\leq N
for any
h\in\Omega_{N}
and
f
is a density, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\Theta_{N}^{k}(f)\leq N\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega_{N}^{k}}f(h)\de\mu_{N}^{*}=N\;,

and we can apply Lemma
3.5
to the sequence
\{\Theta_{N}^{k}(f)\}_{k\geq 1}
. Together with the Taylor expansion of

, this yields
\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mylim_{N\to\infty}\eqref{eq:remaining}\leq 0
. Sending
M\to\infty
in (

) finally concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

It is worth noting that the Feynman–Kac formula is valid even when the measure is not invariant for the process of interest (see for example
[
2
, Lemma A.1]
). But then, consider a measure
\tilde{\mu}_{N}
on
\Omega_{N}
which is almost invariant for nearest neighbour exchanges in the sense that (
3.24
) holds: by slightly adapting the proof of
[
4
, Corollary 5.3]
, one can show that for any density
f
we have the bound

-\mathscr{D}_{N}\left(\sqrt{f},\tilde{\mu}_{N}\right)\leq-\frac{1}{4}\Gamma_{N}\left(\sqrt{f},\tilde{\mu}_{N}\right)+\frac{C}{N}

where
C
is a constant independent of
f
and
N
. Now take a measure
\tilde{\mu}_{N}
as above and such that the initial measure
\mu_{N}
satisfies the entropy bound (
2.8
) with respect to it. Then, it is not hard to see that if we change the measure to
\tilde{\mu}_{N}
instead of
\mu_{N}^{*}
in (
3.16
), the proof of the replacement lemma goes through in exactly the same way, except for an additional summand
\frac{C}{M}
in the supremum (
3.17
), which vanishes as
M\to\infty
. But the proof of the replacement lemma is the only instance where we need the relative entropy bound of the initial measure: this means that we can relax the hypothesis (
2.8
) in Theorem

, as long as
\mu_{N}
satisfies the same entropy bound with respect to
some
measure on

which is almost invariant for nearest neighbour exchanges in the sense of (

).

.

ii) This follows immediately from the fact that the set of measurable functions

iii) For

, and by Lemma

is continuous in the weak topology, then the map

and

\displaystyle\nu\left\{\left|Y_{0}\varphi_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}Y_{t}\partial_{t}\varphi_{t}\de t-\int_{0}^{T}2Z_{t}\left\langle\pi_{t},1-\pi_{t}\ast\cev{i}_{\varepsilon}\right\rangle\varphi_{t}\de t\right|>\delta\right\}

(3.31)

&\phantom{+\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg\{\Bigg|}-\text{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\xi_{t}^{N}(x)\xi_{t}^{N}(x+1)\nabla_{N}\phi_{t}\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\Bigg]\de t\Bigg|>\frac{\delta}{6}\Bigg\}\end{split}

(3.40)

To treat (
3.38
) and (
3.39
), it is enough to use the Taylor expansion of
\phi_{t}
to conclude that they both vanish as
N\to\infty
. As for (
3.40
), calling
\delta^{\prime}=\frac{\delta}{6}
, we further bound it from above by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg\{\Bigg|\int_{0}^{T}Z_{t}^{N}\text{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\xi_{t}^{N}(x+1)\nabla\phi_{t}\left(\frac{x+1}{N}\right)\Bigg[\pi_{t}^{N}\ast\cev{i}_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{x+1}{N}\right)\\
&\phantom{\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg\{\Bigg|}-\cev{\xi}_{t}^{\varepsilon N}(x)\Bigg]\de t\Bigg|>\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{3}\Bigg\}\end{split}

(3.41)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&+\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg\{\Bigg|\int_{0}^{T}Z_{t}^{N}\text{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla\phi_{t}\left(\frac{x+1}{N}\right)\xi_{t}^{N}(x+1)\times\\
&\phantom{+\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg\{\Bigg|}\times\left[\xi_{t}^{N}(x)-\cev{\xi}_{t}^{\varepsilon N}(x)\right]\de t\Bigg|>\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{3}\Bigg\}\end{split}

(3.42)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&+\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg\{\Bigg|\int_{0}^{T}Z_{t}^{N}\text{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\xi_{t}^{N}(x)\xi_{t}^{N}(x+1)\times\\
&\phantom{+\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg\{\Bigg|}\times\left[\nabla\phi_{t}\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)-\nabla_{N}\phi_{t}\left(\frac{x+1}{N}\right)\right]\de t\Bigg|>\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{3}\Bigg\}.\end{split}

(3.43)

Now, by the same argument used in the proof of Proposition
3.8
, (
3.41
) vanishes as
N\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
. By the replacement lemma (Lemma
3.4
) with
G_{t}=\nabla\phi_{t}
and
\psi_{x}(h)=\xi(x+1)
, and a Taylor expansion of
\text{tanh}(\frac{1}{N})
, we get that
\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\eqref{eq:FIVE}=0
. Finally, by using the Taylor expansion of
\phi_{t}
, we have that (
3.43
) also vanishes as
N\to\infty
.

It only remains to show (
3.36
), but this follows immediately from the continuity of
\sgn(\cdot)
on
(-\infty,0)
and
(0,+\infty)
.
∎

We now proceed to show that the result above  – together with the fact that
Y\in C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
and
\rho\in L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
– implies that any limit point
\nu
of
\{\nu^{N}\}_{N}
is concentrated on paths satisfying
Z_{t}=\sgn(Y_{t})
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
f:[0,T]\to(0,1]
be a continuous map and let
Y:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
solve the differential equation

\partial_{t}Y(t)=-Z(t)f(t)

started at
Y_{0}
, where
Z:[0,T]\to[-1,1]
satisfies
Z(t)\in\sigma(Y(t))
for all
t\in[0,T]
, with
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{P}([-1,1])
defined in (
3.1
). Then,
Z(t)=\sgn(Y(t))
for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Since
Y
is necessarily Lipschitz continuous,
we only need to show that, once
Y
hits zero, it remains there. Assume that
Y_{0}=0
and, by contradiction, that there exists
0<t\leq T
such that
Y(t)=\delta
for some arbitrary
\delta>0
. Let
t_{1}=\myinf\{s\in[0,T]:Y_{s}=\delta\}
and
t_{0}=\mysup\{s\in[0,t_{1}]:Y_{s}=0\}
. By continuity of
Y
, there exists
t_{0}<\tilde{t}<t_{1}
such that
Y_{\tilde{t}}=\frac{\delta}{2}
. But then,
Y_{r}=Y_{\tilde{t}}-\int_{0}^{r}f(s)ds\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
for
r>\tilde{t}
until
Y
hits zero again, which contradicts either the definition of
t_{0}
, or the fact that
t_{1}<\infty
. The argument for
\delta<0
is identical.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

The only thing left to show is that
\rho
belongs to
L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
and that the weak solutions of (
2.2
) are unique. Indeed, note that
\rho\in L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
implies, in particular, that
\rho_{t}
is continuous on the torus for almost every
t\in[0,T]
, and thus
\langle\rho_{t},1-\rho_{t}\rangle>0
for almost every
t\in[0,T]
, which allows us to apply Lemma
3.11
. The proof is postponed to Appendix
A
.
∎

4
Equilibrium Fluctuations

4.1
Dynkin’s Martingales

Given
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T})
, Dynkin’s formula shows that the process
\{\mathscr{M}^{N}_{t}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}
defined by

\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N}(\phi):=\mathscr{U}^{N}_{t}(\phi)-\mathscr{U}^{N}_{0}(\phi)-\int_{0}^{t}N^{2}\mathcal{L}^{N}\mathscr{U}_{s}^{N}(\phi)\de s

(4.1)

is a martingale with quadratic variation

\langle\mathscr{M}^{N}(\phi)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\left\{N^{2}\mathcal{L}^{N}\mathscr{U}_{s}^{N}(\phi)^{2}-2N^{2}\mathscr{U}_{s}^{N}(\phi)\mathcal{L}^{N}\mathscr{U}_{s}^{N}(\phi)\right\}\de s.

(4.2)

One can check that (
4.1
) can be written as

\begin{split}&\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N}(\phi)=\mathscr{U}^{N}_{t}(\phi)-\mathscr{U}_{0}^{N}(\phi)+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\Delta_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x)\de s\\
&+\sqrt{N}\text{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\gamma}}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\sgn(Y_{s}^{N})\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x+1)\de s,\end{split}

(4.3)

where
\Delta_{N}
and
\nabla_{N}
are defined in (
3.9
) and (
3.8
), respectively. For
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T})
, define the processes
\{\mathscr{K}^{N}_{t}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}
and
\{\mathscr{B}^{N}_{t}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}
as

\mathscr{K}^{N}_{t}(\phi):=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\Delta_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x)\de s

(4.4)

and

\mathscr{B}^{N}_{t}(\phi):=\sqrt{N}\text{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\gamma}}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\sgn(Y_{s}^{N})\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x+1)\de s,

(4.5)

so that (
4.3
) becomes

\mathscr{U}^{N}_{t}(\phi)=\mathscr{U}_{0}^{N}(\phi)+\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N}(\phi)+\mathscr{K}_{t}^{N}(\phi)+\mathscr{B}_{t}^{N}(\phi).

(4.6)

Similarly to the hydrodynamic case, equation (
4.6
) will be the building block of our argument, and in the following sections we will study each of its terms separately.

4.2
Correlations and Tightness

From now on we let
N=2p
with
p
prime, and we proceed to show tightness of the sequence of measures
\{\mathcal{Q}^{N}\}_{N}
with respect to the Skorokhod topology of
D([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
. We will actually show tightness of each of the terms on the RHS of (
4.6
), as well as some properties of the limit points:

Proposition 4.1
.

For
\gamma>\frac{6}{7}
and starting from
\mu_{N}^{*}
, the sequences of processes
\{\mathscr{U}_{t}^{N},t\in[0,T]\}_{N},\{\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N},t\in[0,T]\}_{N},\{\mathscr{K}_{t}^{N},t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
and
\{\mathscr{B}_{t}^{N},t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
are tight with respect to the Skorokhod topology of
D([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
.

Remark 4.2
.

In fact, one should show tightness of the “original” terms appearing in (
4.1
). However, it is easy to check that, once we have tightness of (
4.6
), the same argument yields tightness of the remainder terms.

As well as Aldous’s Criterion (Proposition
3.3
), the following results will be useful.

Proposition 4.3

(Mitoma’s Criterion,
[
27
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a nuclear Fréchet space. A sequence of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
-valued stochastic processes
\{\mathscr{Y}^{N}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
taking values in
D([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
is tight with respect to the Skorokhod topology if and only if, for any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
, the sequence of real-valued processes
\{\mathscr{Y}^{N}_{t}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
is tight with respect to the Skorokhod topology of
D([0,T],\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 4.4

(Kolmogorov’s Criterion)
.

A sequence of real-valued stochastic processes
\{X^{N}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
with continuous trajectories is tight with respect to the uniform topology of
C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
if the sequence of real-valued random variables
\{X_{0}^{N}\}_{N}
is tight and if there exist constants
K,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{t}^{N}-X_{r}^{N}\right|^{\gamma_{1}}\right]\leq K\left|t-r\right|^{1+\gamma_{2}}

for any
r,t\in[0,T]
and any
N
. Moreover, for any
\alpha<\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{1}}
, any limit point is concentrated on
\alpha
-Hölder continuous paths.

A fundamental ingredient will be given by the following asymptotic result about correlations with respect to the invariant measure, whose proof is postponed to Appendix
B
:

Theorem 4.5

(Bound for the
2m
-point Correlations)
.

Let
N=2p
with
p
prime. For each
\gamma>0
and
m
positive integer, there exists a constant
C
such that, for
N
sufficiently big and for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2m}
in
\mathbb{T}_{N}
pairwise distinct,

\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{2m}\bar{\xi}^{N}(x_{i})\right]\right|\leq\frac{C}{N^{m}}.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Since
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T})
is a nuclear Fréchet space, by Mitoma’s Criterion it suffices to show tightness of the sequences of fields evaluated at
\phi
for any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T})
. Note that, by Theorem
4.5
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\mathscr{U}_{0}^{N}(\phi)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\bar{\xi}_{0}^{N}(x)\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\bar{\xi}_{0}^{N}(x)^{2}\right]\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{N}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\bar{\xi}_{0}^{N}(x)\bar{\xi}_{0}^{N}(y)\right]\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\phi\left(\frac{y}{N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi\|_{\infty}^{2}\left(\sigma^{2}+C\right),

where
\sigma^{2}:=\Var(\bar{\xi}^{N}(x))=\frac{1}{4}
. Thus,
\{\mathscr{U}_{0}^{N}(\phi)\}_{N}
is tight.

Consider the martingale term: it is not hard to check that the quadratic variation (
4.2
) can be rewritten as

\langle\mathscr{M}^{N}(\phi)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}\eta_{s}^{N}(x)\de s,

(4.7)

where

\eta_{s}^{N}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\left[\bar{\xi}_{s}^{N}(x)-\bar{\xi}_{s}^{N}(x+1)\right]^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,\text{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\gamma}}\right)\sgn(Y_{s}^{N})\left[\xi_{s}^{N}(x)-\xi_{s}^{N}(x+1)\right].

(4.8)

Since
|\eta_{s}^{N}(x)|
is uniformly bounded in
N
, given
\varepsilon>0
and a stopping time

\tau
almost surely bounded by
T
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left(\left|\mathscr{M}^{N}_{\tau+\gamma}(\phi)-\mathscr{M}^{N}_{\tau}(\phi)\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\left(\mathscr{M}^{N}_{\tau+\gamma}(\phi)-\mathscr{M}_{\tau}^{N}(\phi)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\gamma}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}\eta_{s}^{N}(x)\de s\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\gamma\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}_{\infty}}{\varepsilon^{2}}

for some constant
C
independent of
N
. Also, from (
4.7
) we get that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}[\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N}(\phi)^{2}]\leq t\|\nabla\phi\|_{\infty}^{2}
. Tightness of
\left\{\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\right\}_{N}
then follows from Aldous’s Criterion.

Now consider (
4.4
): by the stationarity of
\mu_{N}^{*}
, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Theorem
4.5
, for any
0\leq r<t\leq T
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\left(\mathscr{K}_{t}^{N}(\phi)-\mathscr{K}_{r}^{N}(\phi)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\int_{r}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\Delta_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x)\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(t-r)\int_{r}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\Delta_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x)\right)^{2}\right]\de s

\displaystyle=\frac{t-r}{4N}\int_{r}^{t}\Bigg\{\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\Delta_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{N}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\Delta_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\Delta_{N}\phi\left(\frac{y}{N}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(x)\bar{\xi}^{N}_{s}(y)\right]\Bigg\}\de s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(t-r)^{2}}{4}\|\Delta\phi\|^{2}_{\infty}(\sigma^{2}+C).

Hence, by Kolmogorov’s Criterion, the sequence of processes
\{\mathscr{K}^{N}_{t}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
is tight with respect to the uniform topology of
C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
and any limit point has
\alpha
-Hölder continuous trajectories for any
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
.

Finally, tightness of (
4.5
) follows directly from Proposition
4.6
in the next section.
∎

4.3
One-Block Estimate and the Nonlinear Term

The goal of this section is to show that the variance of the non-linear term (
4.5
) vanishes in the limit as
N\to\infty
:

Proposition 4.6
.

For
\gamma>\frac{6}{7}
and any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T})
, we have that
\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}[\mathscr{B}^{N}_{t}(\phi)^{2}]=0.

In order to prove the result above, we will need a version of the classical one-block scheme introduced in
[
18
]
. Given a function
G:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
, let

\|G\|_{2,N}^{2}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2},

and given
\varphi:\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, let

\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{N}^{*})}^{2}:=\int_{\Omega_{N}}\varphi(h)^{2}\de\mu_{N}^{*}.

Given an integer
1\leq L\leq N
, define

\vec{\Sigma}^{L}(x):=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+L}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\xi}(z)-\bar{\xi}(z+1)\right]\left[\frac{1+\sigma(z)}{2}\right]

and

\cev{\Sigma}^{L}(x):=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{y=x-L}^{x-2}\sum_{z=y+1}^{x-1}\left[\bar{\xi}(z)-\bar{\xi}(z+1)\right]\left[\frac{1+\sigma(z)}{2}\right],

where

\sigma(z)=\sigma_{z}(h):=\frac{\sgn(Y(h^{z,z+1}))\mu_{N}^{*}(h^{z,z+1})}{\sgn(Y(h))\mu_{N}^{*}(h)}.

(4.9)

Throughout, if
L
is not an integer, we will keep the notation
L
to denote
\lfloor L\rfloor
. Also, for each
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
, let
\tau_{x}
denote the translation operator by
x
, namely
\tau_{x}h(\cdot):=h(x+\cdot)
. Then, the following holds:

Lemma 4.7

(One-Block Estimate)
.

Let
\ell_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\varphi:\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a mean-zero function with respect to
\mu_{N}^{*}
, and such that its support does not intersect the set of points
\{0,\ldots,\ell_{0}\}
. There exists a constant
C
independent of
N
such that, for any
t>0
and any bounded
G:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\sgn(Y_{s}^{N})\varphi(\tau_{x}h_{s})\vec{\Sigma}_{s}^{\ell_{0}}(x)\de s\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{Ct\ell_{0}^{2}}{N}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{N}^{*})}^{2}\|G\|_{2,N}^{2}.

The same result holds with
\vec{\Sigma}^{\ell_{0}}
replaced with
\cev{\Sigma}^{\ell_{0}}
, as long as the support of
\varphi
does not intersect the set of points
\{-\ell_{0},\ldots,0\}
instead.

This lemma is almost identical to
[
15
, Proposition 5]
, except for the presence of
\frac{1+\sigma(z)}{2}
: indeed, calling

\vec{\xi}^{L}(x):=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+L}\bar{\xi}(y)

(4.10)

the right average of centred particles, we have that

\bar{\xi}(x+1)-\vec{\xi}^{\ell_{0}}(x)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+L}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\xi}(z)-\bar{\xi}(z+1)\right].

Compared to the statement and hypotheses of that result, note that not only do we have the additional factor
\sgn(Y_{s}^{N})
, but also  – and more importantly  – our measure is
not
invariant under nearest-neighbour exchanges. By following an argument similar to the one given in
[
15
]
, we will see how the introduction of
\frac{1+\sigma(z)}{2}
precisely makes up for the resulting correction.

Proof of Lemma
4.7
.

By the Kipnis–Varadhan inequality
[
23
, Lemma 2.4]
and (
3.15
), the expectation in the statement is bounded from above by

\displaystyle Ct\left\|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\sgn(Y(h))\varphi(\tau_{x}h)\vec{\Sigma}^{\ell_{0}}(x)\right\|_{-1}^{2}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=Ct\cdot\mysup_{f\in L^{2}(\mu_{N}^{*})}\bigg\{2\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\sgn(Y(h))\varphi(\tau_{x}h)\vec{\Sigma}^{\ell_{0}}(x)f(h)\de\mu_{N}^{*}\\
&\phantom{=}-N^{2}\mathscr{D}_{N}\left(f,\mu_{N}^{*}\right)\bigg\},\end{split}

(4.11)

where the Dirichlet form
\mathscr{D}_{N}(f,\mu_{N}^{*})
was defined in (
3.14
). To ease the notation, we drop the superscript
N
and we call
Y=Y(h)
and
Y^{z,z+1}=Y(h^{z,z+1})
. Now we write
\vec{\Sigma}^{\ell_{0}}(x)
as

\displaystyle\vec{\Sigma}^{\ell_{0}}(x)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\xi}(z)-\bar{\xi}(z+1)\right]

(4.12)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\xi}(z)-\bar{\xi}(z+1)\right]\left[\frac{\sigma(z)-1}{2}\right]

(4.13)

in (
4.11
): then, the integral term in (
4.11
) corresponding to (
4.12
) can be written as

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\sgn(Y)\varphi(\tau_{x}h)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\xi}(z)-\bar{\xi}(z+1)\right]\right)f(h)\de\mu_{N}^{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\sgn(Y)\varphi(\tau_{x}h)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\xi}(z)-\bar{\xi}(z+1)\right]\right)\times

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\left[f(h)-f(h^{z,z+1})\right]\de\mu_{N}^{*}

(4.14)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\sgn(Y)\varphi(\tau_{x}h)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\xi}(z)-\bar{\xi}(z+1)\right]\right)\times

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\left[f(h)+f(h^{z,z+1})\right]\de\mu_{N}^{*}.

(4.15)

By Young’s inequality, for any set of positive real numbers
\{A_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}
we have that (
4.14
) is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\frac{A_{x}}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}q^{N}_{z,z+1}(h)\left[f(h)-f(h^{z,z+1})\right]^{2}\de\mu_{N}^{*}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\frac{1}{2A_{x}}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\varphi(\tau_{x}h)^{2}\frac{\left[\bar{\xi}(z)-\bar{\xi}(z+1)\right]^{2}}{q_{z,z+1}^{N}(h)}\de\mu_{N}^{*}.

By (
3.15
), choosing
A_{x}=\frac{N^{2}}{4\ell_{0}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)}
the first integral term is bounded from above by

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}[\bar{\xi}^{N}_{t}(x)^{2}]=\frac{1}{4}
, the first term clearly converges to
\frac{1}{4}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}
. As for the second term, by Theorem
4.15
we have that

\displaystyle\mylim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{N}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\bar{\xi}_{t}^{N}(x)\bar{\xi}_{t}^{N}(y)\right]\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\phi\left(\frac{y}{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\mylim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{4N^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{N}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\phi\left(\frac{y}{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\mylim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\right)^{2},

and thus

\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[\mathscr{U}_{t}^{N}(\phi)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{4}\left(\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}-\left\langle 1,\phi\right\rangle^{2}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Energy Estimate and Uniqueness of Weak Solutions

In this appendix we complete the proof of the characterisation of the hydrodynamic limit by showing that any limit point
Q
of
\{Q^{N}\}_{N}
must be concentrated on trajectories whose density
\rho
belongs to the Sobolev space
L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
, and that the weak solutions of (

) are unique.

A.1
Energy Estimate

In order to show that
\rho
belongs to
L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
, it suffices to prove the following proposition, which is usually called an
energy estimate
. The result then follows from Riesz’s Representation Theorem; for a full argument, see for example
[
13
, Section 5.2]
or
[
14
, Section 4.3]
.

Let
C_{k}^{m,n}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})
be the set of continuous functions on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}
with compact support which are
m
times differentiable in the first variable and
n
in the second, with continuous derivatives, where
m
and
n
are positive integers.

Proposition A.1
.

Given any limit point
Q
of
\{Q^{N}\}_{N}
, there exists a positive constant
\kappa>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\mysup_{H}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{u}H(s,u)\rho_{s}(u)\de u\de s-\kappa\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}}H(s,u)^{2}\de u\de s\right\}\right]<\infty,

where the supremum is along all functions
H\in C_{k}^{0,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})
.

Proof.

To ease the notation, we drop the superscript
N
, and we denote by
C
a generic finite positive constant changing from time to time or even within the same line. Following the same argument given in the proofs of the replacement lemma (Lemma
3.4
) and
[
17
, Lemma 6.10]
, it suffices to show that there exists a positive constant
\kappa>0
such that

\mylimsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mylog\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}\left[e^{\int_{0}^{T}\left\{\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\xi_{s}^{N}(x)\partial_{u}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)-\kappa N\|H(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\right\}\de s}\right]<CT.

(A.1)

By the Feynman-Kac formula, the expression in the limit in (
A.1
) is bounded from above by

\mysup_{f}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\partial_{u}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)\left\langle\xi(x),f\right\rangle_{\mu_{N}^{*}}-\kappa\|H(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-N\mathscr{D}_{N}\big(\sqrt{f},\mu_{N}^{*}\big)\right]\de s\right\},

(A.2)

where the supremum runs along all probability densities
f
with respect to
\mu_{N}^{*}
. Note that
\partial_{u}H\big(s,\frac{x}{N}\big)=N\left[H\big(s,\frac{x+1}{N}\big)-H\big(s,\frac{x}{N}\big)\right]+O\big(\frac{1}{N}\big)
, so we can perform a summation by parts and rewrite

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\partial_{u}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)\left\langle\xi(x),f\right\rangle_{\mu_{N}^{*}}=\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)\left[\xi(x)-\xi(x+1)\right]f(h)\de\mu_{N}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)\left[\xi(x)-\xi(x+1)\right]\left[f(h)+f\left(h^{x,x+1}\right)\right]\de\mu_{N}^{*}

(A.3)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)\left[\xi(x)-\xi(x+1)\right]\left[f(h)-f\left(h^{x,x+1}\right)\right]\de\mu_{N}^{*}

(A.4)

up to an error of order at most
\frac{1}{N}
. If we multiply and divide by
q^{N}_{x,x+1}(h)
, defined in (
3.13
), then by Young’s inequality, for any
A>0
we get the bound

(
A.4
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{4}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}q^{N}_{x,x+1}(h)\left[\sqrt{f(h)}-\sqrt{f\left(h^{x,x+1}\right)}\right]^{2}\de\mu_{N}^{*}

(A.5)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\frac{1}{4A}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}\frac{\left[\sqrt{f(h)}-\sqrt{f\left(h^{x,x+1}\right)}\right]^{2}}{q^{N}_{x,x+1}(h)}\de\mu_{N}^{*}.

(A.6)

Choosing
A=2N
and using (
3.15
), we obtain that

\eqref{eq:H5}\leq N\mathscr{D}_{N}\big(\sqrt{f},\mu_{N}^{*}\big)

and

\eqref{eq:H6}\leq\frac{C}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}.

As for (
A.3
), by performing the change of variable
h\mapsto h^{x,x+1}
, we see that

\eqref{eq:H1}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)\left[\xi(x)-\xi(x+1)\right]f\left(h^{x,x+1}\right)\left[1-\frac{\mu_{N}^{*}\left(h^{x,x+1}\right)}{\mu_{N}^{*}(h)}\right]\de\mu_{N}^{*}.

Then, recalling that
\mu_{N}^{*}
is invariant for nearest neighbour exchanges up to a correction of order
\frac{1}{N}
(see (
3.24
)), by Young’s inequality, for any
B>0
we get the bound

(
A.3
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{B}{4}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}\de\mu_{N}^{*}

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\frac{1}{4B}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}f\left(h^{x,x+1}\right)^{2}\left[1-\frac{\mu_{N}^{*}\left(h^{x,x+1}\right)}{\mu_{N}^{*}(h)}\right]^{2}\de\mu_{N}^{*}.

Choosing
B=\frac{1}{4N}
then yields

\eqref{eq:H1}\leq\frac{C}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}+C.

Combining everything together, we finally get

\eqref{eq:feynman_term_2}\leq\int_{0}^{T}\left\{\frac{C}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T_{N}}}H\left(s,\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}+C+\frac{C}{N}-\kappa\int_{\mathbb{T}}H(s,u)^{2}\de u\right\}\de s.

The conclusion follows from noting that
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}H(s,\frac{x}{N})^{2}
converges to
\int_{\mathbb{T}}H(s,u)^{2}\de u
.
∎

A.2
Uniqueness of Weak Solutions

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

First, let
Z:[0,T]\to[-1,1]
and assume that
\rho:[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}\to[0,1]
is fixed and belongs to
L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
. It is then immediate that the equation

\partial_{t}Y_{t}=-2Z_{t}\langle\rho_{t},1-\rho_{t}\rangle

admits a unique weak solution with initial condition
Y_{0}
in the sense of Definition
2.2
. We now show that, if
Z:[0,T]\to[-1,1]
is fixed, the PDE

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\rho_{t}-Z_{t}\nabla[\rho_{t}(1-\rho_{t})],\\
\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0},\end{cases}

(A.7)

also has a unique weak solution in the sense of Definition
2.2
(in particular, satisfying
\rho\in L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
). Let
\rho,\tilde{\rho}
be two weak solutions of (
A.7
) and let
\bar{\rho}:=\rho-\tilde{\rho}
. Note that we can write

Z_{t}\left[\rho_{t}(1-\rho_{t})-\tilde{\rho}_{t}(1-\tilde{\rho}_{t})\right]=Z_{t}\bar{\rho}_{t}(1-\rho_{t}-\tilde{\rho}_{t}),

and thus, since
\bar{\rho}(0,\cdot)\equiv 0
, calling
b_{t}:=Z_{t}(1-\rho_{t}-\tilde{\rho}_{t})
, we have that
\bar{\rho}
satisfies

\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\bar{\rho}_{t},\partial_{t}\phi_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\phi_{t}+b_{t}\nabla\phi_{t}\right\rangle\de t=0

(A.8)

for each test function
\phi\in C_{0}^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})
. Since
\rho
and
\tilde{\rho}
are in
L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
and
Z
is in
L^{\infty}[0,T]
, we see that
b
belongs to
L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
, so we can find smooth functions
\{b^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
such that
b^{\varepsilon}\to b
in
L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
; see, for example,
[
11
, Theorem 5.3.2.2]
. Hence, we have that

\|b^{\varepsilon}-b\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})}+\|\nabla b^{\varepsilon}-\nabla b\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})}\to 0\ \text{as}\ \varepsilon\to 0.

(A.9)

For each
\varepsilon>0
, given a smooth function
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})
, consider the PDE

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\phi_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\phi_{t}+b^{\varepsilon}_{t}\nabla\phi_{t}=\psi_{t}\ \text{on}\ [0,T)\times\mathbb{T},\\
\phi(T,\cdot)=0.\end{cases}

(A.10)

Then, its (unique) solution
\phi^{\varepsilon}
is in
C_{0}^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})
, so we can use it as a test function in (
A.8
) and get that, for each
\varepsilon>0
,

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bar{\rho}_{t},\psi_{t}\rangle\de t+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bar{\rho}_{t},(b_{t}-b_{t}^{\varepsilon})\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}_{t}\rangle\de t=0.

(A.11)

Now, from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bar{\rho}_{t},(b_{t}-b_{t}^{\varepsilon})\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}_{t}\rangle\de t\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})}\int_{0}^{T}\|b_{t}-b_{t}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\|\nabla\phi_{t}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\de t

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|b-b^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})}\|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})}.

(A.12)

From (
A.9
), we have that both
\|b^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})}
and
\|\nabla b^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})}
are uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
, and hence, since
\phi^{\varepsilon}
solves (
A.10
), by a standard argument we can also get a uniform bound in
\varepsilon
for
\|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})}
, namely there exists a constant
C<\infty
independent of
\varepsilon
such that

\|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})}\leq C.

(A.13)

From (
A.11
), (
A.12
), (
A.9
), (
A.13
) and the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we get that for each
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T})
we have that
\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bar{\rho}_{t},\psi_{t}\rangle\de t=0
, which yields
\bar{\rho}=0
almost everywhere on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}.

Finally, assume that
(\rho,Y)
and
(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{Y})
are both weak solutions of (
2.2
), and call
Z:=\sgn(Y)
,
\tilde{Z}:=\sgn(\tilde{Y})
,
\tau:=\myinf\{t\in[0,T]:Z_{t}=0\}
and
\tilde{\tau}:=\myinf\{t\in[0,T]:\tilde{Z}_{t}=0\}
. Note that, until
\tau_{\mymin}:=\tau\wedge\tilde{\tau}
, we have that
Z=\tilde{Z}
, so the solutions
(\rho,Y)
and
(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{Y})
must coincide by our above arguments. But then,
\tau
and
\tilde{\tau}
must also coincide, since they are both equal to
\myinf\{t\in[0,T]:\int_{0}^{t}Z_{s}\langle\rho_{s}(1-\rho_{s}),1\rangle\de s=Y_{0}\}
. Now, since both
Y
and
\tilde{Y}
solve an equation of the form
\partial_{t}Y_{t}=-\sgn(Y_{t})f(t)
for some positive
f
, it is easy to see that after they hit zero they remain equal to zero indefinitely; in particular, after
\tau_{\mymin}
,
Z=\tilde{Z}=0
, so that
\rho
and
\tilde{\rho}
must again coincide by the above argument. This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Asymptotics of the Correlation Functions

In this appendix we show Theorems
4.15
,
4.5
,
4.8
and
4.9
. For the rest of the section we let
N=2p
with
p
odd prime and we denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}^{*}}[\,\cdot\,]
the expected value with respect to the measure
\mu_{N}^{*}=\mu_{N,\gamma}^{*}
on
\Omega_{N}
.

We start by showing an asymptotic result for the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{N,\gamma}
, defined in Lemma
2.1
. In order to do so, we will use the following preliminary result, whose proof contains the main ideas necessary to show all the results proved in this section. Let
A=A(N)=\{\xi\in\{-1,1\}^{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}=0\}
. For
k=1,3,\ldots,N-1
, let

A_{k}=A_{k}(N):=\left\{\xi\in A:\sum_{i=1}^{N}i\xi_{i}\equiv k\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0muN})\right\}

and let
\alpha_{k}=\text{card}(A_{k})
.

Theorem B.1
.

For any
k=1,3,\ldots,2p-1
,

\left|\alpha_{k}-\frac{1}{p}\binom{2p}{p}\right|\leq 2.

Proof.

Given a configuration
\xi
in
A
, let
j_{1},\ldots,j_{p}
be the indices of the
-1
’s, namely
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{p}\}
is the subset of
\{1,\ldots,2p\}
such that
\xi_{j_{1}}=\ldots=\xi_{j_{p}}=-1
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2p}i\xi_{i}\equiv k\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0mu2p})

\displaystyle\iff\sum_{i=1}^{2p}i-2\sum_{i=1}^{p}j_{i}\equiv k\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0mu2p})

\displaystyle\iff 2\sum_{i=1}^{p}j_{i}\equiv p-k\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0mu2p})

\displaystyle\iff\sum_{i=1}^{p}j_{i}\equiv\frac{p-k}{2}\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0mup}).

Hence, computing the
\alpha_{k}
’s is equivalent to calculating how many
p
-element subsets of
\{1,2,\ldots,2p\}
have a sum congruent to
m\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0mup})
for each
m=0,1,\ldots,p-1
. Let
\omega
be a primitive
p
-th root of unity: then note that we can write

(x^{p}-1)^{2}=(x-\omega)(x-\omega^{2})\cdots(x-\omega^{2p}),

but on the other hand we can also write

(x^{p}-1)^{2}=x^{2p}-2x^{p}+1.

Hence, the coefficient of
x^{p}
on the RHS of the two expressions above must be the same, so we get

-\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{p}\leq 2p}\omega^{i_{1}}\cdots\omega^{i_{p}}=-2.

Since
\omega^{p}=1
, we can read the powers of
\omega
modulo
p
on the LHS of the equality above, and write it as

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}a_{k}\omega^{k}=2,

(B.1)

where
a_{k}
is the number of
p
-element subsets of
\{1,\ldots,2p\}
whose elements have a sum congruent to
k\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0mup})
; in particular, the
a_{k}
’s are a permutation of the
\alpha_{k}
’s. But now, a linear combination of
1,\omega,\ldots,\omega^{p-1}
can only be zero if all the coefficients are the same, so (
B.1
) implies that

a_{0}-2=a_{1}=\ldots=a_{p-1}.

Since
\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\alpha_{k}=\binom{2p}{p}
, the conclusion easily follows.
∎

Corollary B.2
.

We have that

\mylim_{N\to\infty}N^{1-\gamma}\binom{N}{\frac{N}{2}}^{-1}\mathcal{Z}_{N,\gamma}=1.

In particular, for

The following remark will be used repeatedly. For

odd, let
\tilde{A}_{k}=\{h\in\Omega_{N}:Y(h)=k\}
and let
\tilde{\alpha_{k}}=\text{card}(\tilde{A}_{k})
. By shifting a height configuration upwards by one, its integral increases by
N
, and hence, if
k\equiv j\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0muN})

\tilde{A}_{j}

On the other hand, for

[formula]

But then, by Theorem

B.2
Bound for the
\boldsymbol{2m}
-Point Correlations

We now show Theorem
4.5
. The argument is, again, very similar to that used in the proof of Theorem
4.15
, but it gets a bit more involved. To make the presentation clearer, we first give a full argument for the case
m=2
, and then briefly explain the steps necessary to generalise it to any
m
.

Fix four pairwise distinct points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{4}
in
\mathbb{T}_{N}
and, for
j=0,1,\ldots,4
, let

\displaystyle C^{j}

\displaystyle=C^{j}(N;x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})

\displaystyle:=\{\xi\in A:\xi(x_{1})=\ldots=\xi(x_{j})=1,\xi(x_{j+1})=\ldots=\xi(x_{4})=-1\}.

Again, the index in the
C

1
. Also, as before, for
k=1,3,\ldots,N-1
and
j=0,1,\ldots,4
let

C_{k}^{j}:=\left\{\xi\in C^{j}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}i\xi_{i}\equiv k\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0muN})\right\}

and
\gamma_{k}^{j}=\text{card}(C_{k}^{j})
.

Theorem B.4
.

For each
k=1,3,\ldots,2p-1
and
j=0,1,\ldots,4
, we have that

\left|\gamma_{k}^{j}-\frac{1}{p}\binom{2p-4}{p-j}\right|\leq p^{3}.

Proof.

We assume
x_{i}=i
for each
i=1,\ldots,4
, but again the proof can be adapted to any set of four pairwise distinct sites. By a symmetry argument, we only need to show the result for
j=2,3,4
. First, let
j=2
: then, by the same argument used in the proof of Theorem
B.3
, computing the
\gamma_{k}^{2}
’s is equivalent to calculating how many
(p-2)
-element subsets of
\{5,6,\ldots,2p\}
have a sum congruent to
m\mkern 4.0mu({\text{mod}\mkern 3.0mup})
for each
m=0,1,\ldots,p-1
. Now, if
\omega
is a primitive
p
-th root of unity, we can write

\frac{(x^{p}-1)^{2}}{\prod_{i=1}^{4}(x-\omega^{i})}=(x-\omega^{5})(x-\omega^{6})\cdots(x-\omega)^{2p}.

(B.7)

On the other hand, we can also write

\displaystyle\frac{(x^{p}-1)^{2}}{\prod_{i=1}^{4}(x-\omega^{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{p}-1}{(x-\omega)(x-\omega^{2})}\cdot\frac{x^{p}-1}{(x-\omega^{3})(x-\omega^{4})}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{l=0}^{p-2}x^{p-2-l}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{l}}\omega^{\theta}\right)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{p-2}x^{p-2-l}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{l}}\omega^{\lambda}\right)

(B.8)

where
\Theta_{0},\ldots,\Theta_{p-2}
and
\Lambda_{0},\ldots,\Lambda_{p-2}
are some subsets of
\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
such that, for each
l=0,\ldots,p-2
,
\text{card}(\Theta_{l})=\text{card}(\Lambda_{l})=l
.
This implies that the coefficient of
x^{p-2}
in (
B.7
) and (
B.8
) must be the same, so we get that

-\sum_{5\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{p-2}\leq 2p}\omega^{i_{1}}\cdots\omega^{i_{p-2}}=\sum_{l=0}^{p-2}\left(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{l}}\omega^{\theta}\cdot\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{p-2-l}}\omega^{\lambda}\right).

By reading the powers of
\omega
modulo
p
, the equality above can be written as

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}a_{k}\omega^{k}=-\sum_{l=0}^{p-2}\left(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{l}}\omega^{\theta}\cdot\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{p-2-l}}\omega^{\lambda}\right),

where the
a_{k}
’s are a permutation of the
\gamma^{2}_{k}
’s. Now, the total number of powers of
\omega
that get added in the RHS of the equation above is
\sum_{l=0}^{p-2}l(p-2-l)\leq p^{3}
. This implies that, for each
k,j=0,\ldots,p-1
, we have that
|a_{k}-a_{j}|\leq p^{3}
. Now let
a_{\mymin}=\mymin_{k}a_{k}
and
a_{\mymax}=\mymax_{k}a_{k}
. Since
\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}a_{k}=\binom{2p-4}{p-2}
, then

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(a_{\mymin}+p^{3})\geq\binom{2p-4}{p-2}

which yields
a_{\mymin}\geq\frac{1}{p}\binom{2p-4}{p-2}-p^{3}
; similarly we get
a_{\mymax}\leq\frac{1}{p}\binom{2p-4}{p-2}+p^{3}
. The proof for
j=3
and
j=4
follows from the exact same argument, except that one needs to look at the coefficient of
x^{p-3}
and
x^{p-4}
, respectively, in (
B.7
) and (
B.8
).
∎

The key to generalising the result to any positive integer
m
lies in the following identity.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
N,m
be positive integers with
m\leq N
. Then,

\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2m}\binom{2m}{j}\binom{2N-2m}{N-j}(-1)^{j}}{\binom{2N}{N}}=\frac{(-1)^{m}\binom{N}{m}}{\binom{2N}{2m}}.

(B.11)

Proof.

Consider a set
X
of
2N
elements, fix a subset
Y\subset X
of cardinality
2m
, and sample a subset
Z
of
X
uniformly from the subsets of
X
of cardinality
N
. Then, the LHS of (
B.11
) can be read as the probability that the cardinality of
Z\cap Y
is even minus the probability that it is odd. But, since we are sampling uniformly at random, this is equivalent to the same difference of probabilities when first fixing
Z\subset X
of cardinality
N
and then sampling
Y
uniformly at random from the subsets of
X
of cardinality
2m
. Hence, we have the identity

\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2m}\binom{2m}{j}\binom{2N-2m}{N-j}(-1)^{j}}{\binom{2N}{N}}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2m}\binom{N}{j}\binom{N}{2m-j}(-1)^{j}}{\binom{2N}{2m}}.

(B.12)

Now consider the polynomial
(x^{2}-1)^{N}
: the coefficient of
x^{2m}
is

\binom{N}{m}(-1)^{N-m}.

On the other hand, by writing
(x^{2}-1)^{N}=(x+1)^{N}(x-1)^{N}
, we see that the coefficient of
x^{2m}
is also equal to

\sum_{j=0}^{2m}\binom{N}{j}\binom{N}{2m-j}(-1)^{N-j}.

This implies that the RHS of (
B.12
) is equal to the RHS of (
B.11
), which concludes the proof.
∎
