β-cell adaptation to increased insulin demand is a swift and dynamic process orchestrated by unfolded protein response
Authors: Laura Maria Daian, Andrei Mircea Vacaru, Gabriela Tanko, Elena Mirela Lamba, Oana-Ana-Maria Mardare, Luiza Ghila, Simona Chera, Ana-Maria Vacaru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic β-cells adapt to increased insulin demand through coordinated functional and molecular responses. However, in diabetes, these adaptive pathways eventually fail, driving β-cell dysfunction and demise. One of these pathways is unfolded protein response (UPR), a complex network of sensors and effectors that cooperate to restore endoplasmic reticulum (ER) function under stress. To define the early UPR programs engaged during β-cell adaptation, we employed a murine model of partial β-cell ablation that preserves normoglycemia while imposing an increased secretory workload on the remaining β-cells. Integrating bulk islet transcriptomics with imaging, ultrastructural and physiological analyses, we identified dynamic molecular and functional adaptations during the compensatory phase. The initial transcriptional response was characterised by induction of apoptosis genes and a stress response. This was later replaced by activation of adaptive UPR pathways accompanied by increased insulin processing. Nuclear Atf4 and Xbp1 increased transiently, consistent with engagement of multiple UPR branches through different mechanisms to restore homeostasis. Functionally, partially ablated islets displayed enhanced secretory competence during an acute secondary ER stress challenge, although this adaptive advantage was lost during prolonged stress. Together, these findings define a transient adaptive UPR program that sustains β-cell function during the early response to β-cell loss and provide insight into mechanisms that may be leveraged to enhance β-cell resilience in diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03110-9.

Background

Diabetes is a global health concern associated with impaired insulin secretion, coupled with increased pressure on the β-cells to compensate for the deficit by producing more insulin spiralling into stress [1]. β-cells are responsible for maintaining physiological glucose concentrations by sensing glucose levels via their glucose metabolism, which leads to ATP generation. This increase in ATP triggers insulin synthesis and secretion in response to the glucose increase [2]. Chronic demand and various metabolic stresses can overwhelm β-cells, impairing their function and contributing to diabetes development [3, 4].

Previous data had pointed towards the contribution of a fundamental cellular process, the unfolded protein response (UPR), into both β-cell survival and in β-cell demise [5–7]. UPR, a mechanism conserved from yeasts to mammals, helps the secretory performance of a cell by improving its capacity to cope with a burden in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [8]. Physiologically, the ER of β-cells, “professional secretory cells”, has to cyclically manage tremendous amounts of insulin, which puts a strong pressure on its intrinsic folding capacity [5, 7]. Thus, the constant demand for insulin production results in misfolded proinsulin, triggering the upregulation of UPR to restore homeostasis, which is successfully resolved in healthy individuals [9, 10]. However, in diabetes, this sustained activity is not optimally supported due to malfunctioning adaptive mechanisms, leading to stress and ultimately to β-cell death via apoptosis [11, 12].

The UPR consists of three main signalling pathways mediated by ER stress sensors: PERK (protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase), IRE1 (inositol-requiring enzyme 1), and ATF6 (activating transcription factor 6). Stimulation of these pathways activates downstream effectors that provide adaptation support, by reducing global protein synthesis, enhancing ER-associated degradation (ERAD) of misfolded proteins, and upregulating genes involved in ER function and stress response [13]. Deletion of any of the UPR sensors in mice impairs glucose metabolism, depending on the cellular stress milieu, decreases β-cell mass, and induces diabetes [10, 14–16]. On the other hand, persistent stress on the ER driven by chronic hyperglycaemia, free fatty acids or pro-inflammatory cytokines triggers sustained activation of pro-apoptotic factors that eventually leads to β-cell death [17–20]. Thus, the UPR is a mechanism that can easily steer from protective and supportive into pathogenic and apoptotic [21]. What characterises the UPR responses that drive the pro-survival or pro-apoptosis cell fate and how these are implicated in the function of the β-cell is still less understood. In this context, it is critical to design suitable models that allow us to follow in vivo the involvement of UPR components in the transition between the physiologic-to-pathologic processes for the β-cells, or for maintaining homeostasis.

Several in vivo models have been developed to study β-cell loss, including cell-targeted chemical ablation (streptozotocin or alloxan) [22, 23], and toxin-receptor-mediated approaches (RIP-DTA, RIP-DTR, ins-TREK-Tg) [24–27]. Many of these models either expose surviving β-cells to additional stress, or trigger systemic complications, limiting their utility for dissecting intrinsic β-cell adaptation [28–30].

To address this gap, we employed the hemizygous NSG RIP-DTR (Rat Insulin Promoter - Diphtheria Toxin Receptor) female mice, which allow selective and swift ablation by apoptosis of approximately 50% of the β-cells through DT administration due to random X-chromosome inactivation [27, 31, 32]. This mosaic reduction produces a rapid increase in insulin demand while preserving a population of β-cells for study, without major inflammatory confounders [33, 34]. Using this system, we aimed to characterize how DT-insensitive β-cells engage UPR-mediated adaptation mechanisms under acute ER stress.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were from Carl Roth GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany, unless otherwise specified.

Animal model

Moderate β-cell ablation was achieved using heterozygous female NSG RIP-DTR mice at 8–12 weeks of age. Diphtheria toxin (DT) (Sigma-Aldrich, #D0564) was administered via multiple injections following previously established protocols [27, 32]. Briefly, 126 ng DT diluted in 0.9% NaCl solution were administered intraperitoneally on days 0, 3 and 4. Mice were kept under controlled temperature (23 °C), humidity (55–60%) and light conditions (12 h light:12 h darkness cycle) and with ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the European Union Directive 2010/63/EU and approved by the national competent authority (Authorization No. 590/13.01.2021 and 27/17.11.2025).

Physiological parameters

Blood glucose was evaluated bi-weekly by puncture of the lateral tail vein and using either an Accu-Chek Performa glucometer (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) or a Contour Plus Elite Glucometer (Ascensia Diabetes Care Holdings, Basel, Switzerland) with the corresponding strips. Bodyweight was measured with a tabletop scale (Compass CX221, Ohaus). Glucose tolerance was determined upon intraperitoneal injection of a dose of 2.0 g/kg of D-glucose, following 6 h of fasting. The dynamics of glycaemic levels were followed by repetitive glycaemic measurements spread over two hours after injection.

Insulin secretion and content

Pancreatic insulin content was quantified by harvesting tissue in acid ethanol and weighing using a precision balance (Adventurer Pro Precision, Ohaus). After homogenization, the tissue was incubated overnight at -20 °C. For islet insulin and C-peptide content, the islets were harvested in acid ethanol, sonicated and incubated overnight at -20 °C. The samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm 15 min at 4 °C and the aqueous solution was neutralised 1:1 with 1 M Tris pH 7.5. Insulin and C-peptide from total islet or from serum were measured using ELISA kits (Mouse insulin, Mercodia, #10-1247-01; Ultra-sensitive Mouse Insulin, Crystal Chem, #90080; Mouse C-peptide, Crystal Chem, #90050). Insulin and C-peptide content and secreted insulin into the culture media were normalized to total protein determined using RotiQuant Universal (CarlRoth, Karlsruhe, Germany, #0120.1), following the manufacturers’ instructions.

Pancreas collection and immunostaining

Pancreata were collected from female NSG RIP-DTR mice, fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 2 h, and dehydrated through 3 successive sucrose gradients for 1.5 h. Tissues were embedded in optimum cutting tissue medium (CellPath), frozen, and stored at -80 °C. Five-micron cryosections were rehydrated in PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 1% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies (listed in Table 1) were diluted in 1% BSA and applied for 1 h at room temperature. Visualisation of the primary stains was performed with the following secondary antibodies from Invitrogen: Streptavidin AF568 (#S11226), donkey anti-rabbit AF488 (#A21206), goat anti-rat AF 594 (#A11007), donkey anti-rabbit AF568 (#A10042), goat anti-mouse AF568 (#A11004), donkey anti-mouse AF647 (#A31571), donkey anti-rabbit AF647 (#A31573). All the secondary antibodies were used at a concentration of 1:200. They were incubated for 45 min at room temperature, followed by DAPI counterstaining of cell nuclei and mounting with antifade mounting media (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA, #H-1000).

Fluorescence image acquisition and analysis

Pancreatic islet images were captured using a Leica DMi8 fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and processed with LasX software (v3.7.4.23463), maintaining consistent parameters across channels and experiments. Analysis was performed using QuPath software (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-17204-5, v0.3.2), which enables simultaneous quantification of three fluorescence channels (blue, green, red). DAPI nuclear staining enabled cell identification through intensity threshold optimization within manually defined islet boundaries. Individual cell cytoplasmic regions were established using QuPath’s expansion tool, maintaining consistent parameters throughout analysis. Protein expression was quantified by measuring fluorescence intensity in either cytoplasmic (proinsulin, insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, Chop) or nuclear compartments (Atf4, sXbp1, Atf6), with channel-specific thresholds applied to each marker. The positive cells for nuclear staining were presented as a percentage of total insulin positive cells and normalised to control (0 daDT).

Islet isolation and RNA extraction

For transcriptomic analysis, islets were isolated from 3 mice per condition. Pancreata were perfused via the ampulla of Vater with 2.5 mL Collagenase XI solution (Sigma-Aldrich #C7657 in HBSS with Ca2+/Mg2+ and 0.08% BSA) after bile duct clamping. Tissues were digested at 37 °C for 15 min, stopped with cold RPMI 1640 (Corning, #10-040-CV)/10% FBS (PanBiotech, #P30-3306), filtered through a metallic strainer, and washed twice. Islets were separated using a Histopaque gradient (1.119, 1.077, RPMI1640), collected, washed thrice in RPMI, and hand-picked. Isolated islets were preserved in the RLT buffer (Qiagen) with β-Mercaptoethanol (Sigma Aldrich). RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kits (Qiagen) and quality-checked using NanoDrop 2000c (ThermoFisher Scientific), before shipment to Novogene facilities for sequencing.

Reverse transcription and qPCR

Reverse-transcription reaction was performed using 300–500 ng RNA from transcriptomic samples, to obtain cDNA with 9902 Veriti PCR (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) by using iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). Gene expression was analysed via qPCR using a Roche LightCycler 480 System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with PerfeCTa SYBR Green SuperMix (Quantabio, Beverly, MA, USA), using 12.5 ng cDNA equivalent per reaction. RNA sequencing, data and pathway analysis

Total RNA samples were shipped to Novogene (Cambridge, UK), where a second quality control and library preparation (polyA enrichment) were performed. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq PE150 platform – 30 million reads each end for a total of 9G raw data output.

Sequencing data was processed in the CLC Genomics Workbench 23.0 (Qiagen, Aarhus, Denmark). Adapter and quality score-base trimming was performed using the default settings provided by the trimming tool in the CLC Workbench. The DEG lists were obtained by comparing the groups using the “Empirical Analysis of DGE” algorithm from the CLC software. The thresholds set for selection of DEGs for downstream analysis were FC > 1.5 and p < 0.05.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the Biological Process (BP) ontology was performed using the enrichGO function from the clusterProfiler R package (v4.10.1), with gene symbols converted to Entrez IDs via the org.Mm.eg.db annotation database. Enrichment was tested against the full mouse genome as background, with a minimum and maximum gene set size of 10 and 500, respectively, and significance defined at an adjusted p-value below 0.05 and a q-value below 0.2. Redundant GO terms were removed using the simplify function (semantic similarity cutoff = 0.7), retaining the most significant representative term per cluster.

Transmission electron microscopy

For each time point (0, 1, 3, 5 daDT), pancreatic tissue samples were processed for transmission electron microscopy. Immediately after excision, tissues were immersion-fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1.5% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2) containing 2.5 mM CaCl₂. Tissues were cut into ~ 1 mm³ pieces, fixed for an additional 90 min, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 90 min at 4 °C, and stained en bloc with uranyl acetate. Samples were dehydrated through graded ethanol series, transferred to propylene oxide, and embedded in Epon 812 resin. Semi-thin sections (~ 1 μm) were cut using an ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems, Germany), stained with toluidine blue, and examined by light microscopy to identify islets. Ultrathin sections (~ 70 nm) from islet-containing regions were cut with a diamond knife, placed on formvar-coated 200-mesh copper grids, double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using a Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTwin transmission electron microscope (ThermoFisher Scientific, Eindhoven, The Netherlands) operated at 100 kV. Images were acquired using an Eagle 4 K slow-scan bottom-mounted CCD camera. β-cells were identified based on the presence of insulin granules, defined by electron-dense cores of crystallized insulin surrounded by a halo and a limiting membrane.

Due to the limited presence of islets, only a small number of β-cells (typically 1–3 per grid) were identifiable, resulting in analysable images at consistent magnifications (x4800) from one mouse at day 0, one mouse at day 1, two mice at day 3 (one contributing few images), and two mice at day 5. Granules were morphologically categorized as filled mature (electron-dense core), immature (lighter grey core), rod-shaped (elongated dense core, atypical), or empty (lacking visible insulin).

Automated granule quantification

Automated morphometric analysis was performed using a custom workflow integrating Fiji [39], Ilastik (v1.4.0.post1), an interactive machine learning tool for image segmentation [40], and Python scrip. Ilastik (v1.4.0.post1) pixel classification was trained using manual annotations feature-based learning incorporating texture, edges, and intensity features. Image processing involved several steps. First, the original electron microscopy images were split into quadrants to improve computational efficiency during downstream processing, and were processed in Fiji to enhance granule visibility and facilitate feature extraction. The pre-processed images were converted into Ilastik-compatible HDF5 files (.h5, a hierarchical data format) using Python and loaded into Ilastik where a feature-based classifier was trained and subsequently applied in batch mode to generate probability maps for the entire data set. During pixel classification training, four-pixel classes were defined: (1) insulin granules (electron-dense cores), (2) proinsulin granules (large, lighter grey), (3) halo, and (4) background. The resulting probability maps, exported as .h5 prediction files, were converted using Python scripts into individual .tif images (one per class), each assigned to its corresponding granule type and organized into structured folders for batch analysis in Fiji. After loading the prediction channel .tif images into Fiji, they were converted into binary masks via auto-thresholding and refined using morphological operations. Finally, Fiji’s particle analysis tool was used in batch mode to automatically count the identified granules and measure their morphological properties, including area and maximum and minimum Feret diameters. Mean grey values were extracted by overlaying the region-of-interest (ROI) outlines generated during particle analysis onto the corresponding electron microscopy raw images. As these were 8-bit images with intensity values ranging from 0 (black) to 255 (white), lower grey values indicated highly condensed insulin cores, while higher values reflected loosely packed insulin. Thus, granules with lower mean grey value were considered more mature, and those with higher values, less mature. All images used for training and prediction had a calibrated pixel size of 4.525 × 4.525 nm² (equivalent to 0.221 pixels per nm).

Image analysis and distribution assessment

Morphometric measurements obtained at the individual granule level were pooled and analyzed per time point to characterize temporal changes in granule morphology across conditions. To minimize the influence of extreme values potentially arising from segmentation artifacts, individual granule measurements were filtered using an interquartile range (IQR)-based criterion (Q1 − 1.5×IQR to Q3 + 1.5×IQR), applied per feature and per time point. Density-normalized histograms with overlaid kernel density estimation (KDE) curves and corresponding violin plots with embedded boxplots were then constructed in R (RStudio) from the filtered datasets to visualize distributional structure, including skewness and spread. In the histograms, bin heights were scaled to represent probability density rather than raw counts, allowing comparison of distribution shapes independent of sample size. Violin plots were derived from the same KDE framework to depict the full distributional profile of each feature, while the embedded boxplots summarized median values and interquartile ranges.

Glucose stimulated insulin secretion

Islets were isolated and pooled from four NSG RIP-DTR hemizygous female mice at both 0 and 2 daDT and left overnight in RPMI 1640 medium containing vehicle, 32 µM 4µ8C (Millipore), or 1 µM GSK2606414 (Biovision). The following day, islets were washed and starved in KRBH buffer (138 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 1.2 mM MgCl₂, 1.2 mM CaCl₂, 0.5 mM KH₂PO₄, 10 mM HEPES, 5 mM Na₂HCO₃, and 0.1% BSA) containing 2.8 mM glucose and vehicle or inhibitors, respectively, for 1 h. Afterwards, for each condition, 10 islet equivalents were either maintained at 2.8 mM or stimulated with 16.7 mM glucose in KRBH buffer supplemented with vehicle or inhibitors for 1 h. Secreted insulin was normalized to total insulin content obtained by acid ethanol extraction. Insulin was determined by ELISA (Mouse insulin, Mercodia, #10-1247-01), based on manufacturers’ protocol.

Insulin secretion under thapsigargin-induced ER stress

Islets were isolated and pooled from NSG RIP-DTR hemizygous female mice at both 0 and 5 daDT, respectively. For each condition, islets were cultured with either 1 µM Tg (Tocris) or vehicle (0.1% DMSO) in RPMI 1640 medium. Insulin was determined from medium samples collected at 0.5 and 2 h (from the same islets), and at 16 h from another set of islets. At 2 and 16 h, islets were lysed in RIPA buffer for protein analysis and normalization.

Statistical analysis

Graphs and statistics were done in GraphPad Prism, version 9.4.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.001 (∗∗), p < 0.0005 (∗∗∗), and p < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗). In addition, for image analysis (Fig. 3), statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R (RStudio). Analyses summarized granule-level distributional differences across time points. Distributional asymmetry was quantified using skewness coefficients calculated separately for each feature and time point (e1071 package in R). Global differences were evaluated using nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis tests followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons. Because individual granules do not constitute independent biological replicates, p-values reflect shifts in granule-level feature distributions and are interpreted descriptively rather than as tests of independent biological replication.

Results

DT-mediated ablation in hemizygous NSG RIP-DTR females resulted in reduction of nearly 50% insulin content from the islets, without affecting glucose control.

In the NSG RIP-DTR hemizygous females, upon DT injection, normoglycemia was maintained, despite a ~ 50% reduction in total pancreatic insulin content (~ 19.38 ng/mg of pancreas), by two weeks post-initial DT administration, as compared to vehicle NaCl-injected littermates (~ 40.13 ng/mg of pancreas) (Fig. 1A, B). Injection with DT in age-matched NSG female mice did not show any significant difference as compared to NaCl-injected mice (Fig. 1C). Based on these observations, we conducted experiments to investigate the acute alterations that occurred in the islet’s microenvironment after DT-mediated mosaic ablation. Efficient ablation was achieved through administration of three DT doses on days 0, 3, and 4, as previously established [27, 32]. Samples were collected at 0 (control, uninjected), 1, 3 (before the 2nd dose), and 5 days after the first DT administration (daDT), as illustrated in Fig. 1D. Blood glucose levels and body mass of the mice remained unchanged throughout this time frame (Fig. 1E, F).

Individual islets were differentially affected by the loss of β-cell mass, as evidenced in Fig. 1G. At 0 daDT (Fig. 1G, top), the islets showed a typical distribution of β-cells (green), with dense, uniform presence throughout the islet. In contrast, at 5 daDT, there was a noticeable lower number of β-cells, (Fig. 1G, bottom). This reduction was quantified (Fig. 1H), and significant drops in the average number of insulin-positive cells per islet section were observed over time, from ~ 99.76% (0 daDT), to ~ 82.71% (1 daDT), ~ 67.97% (3 daDT) and ~ 63.33% (5 daDT), respectively.

Despite the reduced number of β-cells, the functional capacity to respond to glucose stimulation remained similar in partially ablated, as compared to the non-ablated female mice, as demonstrated by intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (ipGTTs, Fig. 1I). The maintenance of normoglycemia in females with mosaic β-cell depletion indicated a robust compensatory mechanism, enabling these mice to preserve metabolic balance (Fig. 1J).

β-cells swiftly increased insulin processing and secretion upon DT-mediated ablation

We asked how the remaining β-cells compensated for the DT-mediated loss of the other β-cells. For that, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of their function focusing on insulin production, processing and secretion. Levels of insulin (green) and proinsulin (red) proteins from islets of mice that were injected with DT were evaluated by immunofluorescence microscopy at various time points.

Upon DT injection, proinsulin expression did not change significantly over time, from 0 to 5 daDT, suggesting a constant rate of synthesis and processing, without accumulation (Fig. 2A, B). On the other hand, insulin fluorescence intensity decreased significantly at 3 daDT, however, by 5 daDT the cellular insulin recovered to the initial 0 daDT levels (Fig. 2A, C). This decrease in cellular insulin content at 3 daDT was confirmed in protein extracts from whole islets, where total insulin levels, determined by ELISA, decreased significantly at 3 and 5 daDT (Fig. 2D). Moreover, the corresponding C-peptide content from isolated islets had a considerable decrease by 3 and 5 daDT, as compared to 0 daDT (Fig. 2E). To assess if the reduction was caused by increased secretion, the partial ablation of the β-cells, or both, we determined the levels of secreted insulin in the serum. We found that insulin levels in serum showed a significant increase from 3 to 5 daDT (Fig. 2F). Moreover, as previous studies showed that the clearance rate of insulin in the blood is ~ 5-fold higher than that of C-peptide [41], we assessed the levels of C-peptide at the corresponding time points. Our results showed that serum C-peptide concentration was increased at 3 and 5 daDT (Fig. 2G). These data indicated that the remaining cells, being under pressure to compensate for the loss of their counterparts, were pushed to swiftly ramp up the insulin supply.

Residual β-cells showed hallmarks of early secretory pathway stress and dynamic granule remodelling

We next addressed what was the impact of depleting a significant number of β-cells on the secretory pathway of the residual β-cells. For that, we performed ultrastructural analysis coupled with morphometry of the β-cells at the different time points.

At 0 daDT, the appearances of the rough endoplasmic reticulum (rER) and the Golgi complex (Gc) were normal (Fig. 3A, 0 daDT). In addition, β-cells exhibited abundant insulin secretory granules, with a broad normalized distribution that indicated a higher variability in granule size, with an area of 31721.02 ± 218.96 nm2 and a corresponding Feret diameter of 223.44 ± 0.75 nm (Fig. 3B-E, blue). The normalized distribution of the mean grey value was relatively tight and peaked around lower grey values (at 121.89 ± 0.30), suggesting dense and mature granules (Fig. 3F-G, blue). At 1 daDT, we found a clear shift towards smaller granule size, displaying a decrease to 25390.39 ± 243.97 nm2 and 200.59 ± 0.96 nm of granule area and Feret diameter, respectively (Fig. 3B-E, purple). In addition, the mean grey value showed an increase in granule brightness to 127.86 ± 0.35 (Fig. 3F-G, purple), that was coupled with the rER and Gc displaying cisternal distension, most likely due to increased secretory rate (Fig. 3A, 1 daDT). By 3 daDT, ER stress became evident, with substantial enlargement of the ER cisternae (Fig. 3A, 3 daDT). We registered an expansion of granule size, with an area of 37460.86 ± 445.45 nm2 and a Feret diameter of 239.81 ± 1.37 nm (Fig. 3B-E, magenta). Moreover, the distribution of the mean grey value was shifted even further, peaking at 136.56 ± 0.38, suggesting less packed and immature insulin granules (Fig. 3D-E, magenta). However, at 5 daDT, we found that although the hallmarks of stress persisted (Figs. 3A, 5 daDT), the granules’ area and diameter decreased towards baseline, indicating values of 30717.63 ± 260.15 nm2, and 219.06 ± 0.88 nm, respectively (Fig. 3B-E, salmon). Additionally, the mean grey value distribution curve shifted back leftward, with the peak at 119.64 ± 0.39, and displayed no difference as compared to 0 daDT, correlating with granule replenishment and maturation (Fig. 3F-G, salmon). In sum, the remaining β-cells initially increased the secretion of readily available insulin to keep up with the demand. This caused a stress surge, as these cells might not have had the sufficient secretory capacity to manage packaging and releasing insulin efficiently. Nevertheless, they employed additional resources that needed to be further characterized.

Mosaic ablation of β-cells resulted in transcriptional modifications that characterised distinct stress responses in the islets.

As the β-cells showed hallmarks of early secretory pathway stress, we aimed to characterise the molecular response that occurred in the islets during the acute intervention. For that, we performed bulk RNA sequencing analysis of isolated islets collected at different time points during the mild ablation, at 0, 1, 3 and 5 daDT, respectively.

Based on these analyses, we found at 1 daDT, a substantial number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 1548 genes downregulated (38%) and 2554 genes upregulated (62%), as compared to 0 daDT. This suggested a robust transcriptional response to the modifications determined by the DT-mediated ablation, likely involving stress response pathways. By 3 daDT, the number of DEGs compared to 0 daDT decreased significantly, with 115 genes downregulated (10%), and 1099 genes upregulated (90%). This indicated that, while the immediate stress response had lessened, the cells were still actively engaged in gene expression changes that might have been related to ongoing adaptation or recovery processes. At 5 daDT the number of downregulated genes decreased even further to 78 (5%), whereas the upregulated genes were increased to 1463 (95%) (Fig. 4A). This behaviour suggested that the remaining cells were actively responding to stress, potentially undertaking recovery or compensatory mechanisms more robustly.

GO BP enrichment analysis revealed that DT-induced partial β-cell ablation modulates multiple biological processes in islets, including extracellular matrix organization, RNA processing, nuclear division, chemotaxis, and intracellular transport. Notably, a number of UPR-related processes were consistently represented across comparisons, suggesting a sustained activation of this pathway following DT administration (Fig. 4B).

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the genes contained in the endoplasmic reticulum unfolded protein response GO term (GO:0034976, response to endoplasmic reticulum stress), showed that the two principal components (PC1 and PC2) together explained a significant portion of the variance of the gene expression data (45.9% for PC1 and 31.3% for PC2) (Fig. 4C). We found that the 0 daDT samples clustered together, indicating similar gene expression profiles. The 1 daDT samples were significantly separated from the 0 daDT samples along both PC1 and PC2, indicating a marked change in gene expression immediately after DT injection. Samples from 3 to 5 daDT displayed a trend back towards the 0 daDT cluster, however they remained distinct, especially the 5 daDT group, suggesting a partial recovery, or adaptation with long-standing effects.

Unsupervised clustering of the gene expression data belonging to the same GO term (response to endoplasmic reticulum stress) shown in the heatmap in Fig. 4D revealed two major groups (i.e. subclasses) of gene expression patterns. The first group (fuchsia) that showed increased expression at early time points (1 daDT) contained members that indicated activation of stress pathways, designated as stressed response (Fig. 4D, Supplemental Table 1). The second group (orange) showed varying levels of expression changes, with some being upregulated and others downregulated over time. The latter group contained genes involved in adaptive response (Fig. 4D, Supplemental Table 1). Of note, the latter was clearly better represented, as it comprised 127 genes, as compared to 84, respectively.

For example, in the stress response group we identified Ddx3x, which has been involved in β-cell redox homeostasis and apoptosis, through the Erk/hnRnpk/Ddx3x signaling activated in islets during metabolic stress [42]. We also detected Fbxo6, a component of a ubiquitin ligase complex, involved in the DNA damage response and ER stress-induced apoptosis [43]. In addition, Ptpn1, which encodes a tyrosine protein phosphatase that regulates UPR by dephosphorylating EIF2AK3/PERK [44, 45], and Usp19 encoding a deubiquitinating enzyme implicated in protein degradation and ER stress signaling [46], were initially upregulated. Finally, Eif2b5, a component of the eIF2B complex involved in translation initiation and regulation of global protein synthesis [47], was also identified (Fig. 4E).

Moreover, we found initially upregulated genes directly linked to apoptosis, like Bbc3 [48] and Trp53 [49, 50], expected as a response to the DT treatment in the targeted β-cells, however, the global islet apoptosis response declined in time, suggesting the clearance of the source-cells (Supplemental Fig. 1).

In the other set of genes, the ‘adaptive’ response, we observed increased expression of multiple UPR genes chaperones (Erp44, Pdia4, Pdia6, Sdf2l1) and proteins involved in ER-associated degradation (ERAD) (Derl1, Dnajc10, Dnajc3, Edem3, Erlin2), consistent with an increased ER protein folding burden (Fig. 4F, Supplemental Table 1). Moreover, we detected induction of additional UPR-associated genes, such as Bhlha15, which has been implicated in several cellular processes, including cellular response to glucose [51, 52], and Creb3l1, a transcription factor that regulates genes involved in extracellular matrix production, consistent with increased insulin demand and tissue remodelling after partial β-cells ablation [53] (Supplemental Table 1).

Activation of ATF4 and its targets occurred during β-cell adaptation

We set to further characterize and validate the UPR responses that occurred in the islets during the adaptive process. We focused on one of the major downstream transcription factors, Atf4, a key player in the integrated stress response (ISR), that is primarily activated through the PERK (PKR-like ER kinase) branch of UPR. Previous studies have shown induction of Atf4 in β-cells, as a response to increased insulin demand [54].

We found by RNAseq robust activation of Atf4 at 1 daDT, followed by a rapid decrease at 3 daDT, while at 5 daDT, mRNA expression returned to initial values. This data was confirmed by RT-qPCR, when we found an initial, significant ~ 2.6-fold increase in Atf4 mRNA levels at 1 daDT that was succeeded by a decrease towards baseline (0 daDT), at 3 and 5 daDT (Fig. 5A).

We aimed to characterize Chop expression, a direct target of Atf4, as it is an important exponent of the stress response determined by various stressors [55–57]. By transcriptomics, we found strong activation of the gene that encodes for Chop, Ddit3, in the islets of 1 daDT experimental group as compared to 0 daDT. At 3 daDT, mRNA expression exhibited a sharp decrease, returning to 0 daDT values by 5 daDT. This dynamic was confirmed by RT-qPCR, which highlighted a significant ~ 3.9-fold increase in Ddit3 mRNA levels at 1 daDT that was followed by a decrease to baseline (0 daDT), at 3 and 5 daDT (Fig. 5B). However, we found by immunofluorescence that the expression levels of Chop in the β-cells were not significantly induced within the 5 daDT time frame (Supplemental Fig. 2A-C).

To further elucidate the mechanism by which β-cells adapt to increased insulin demand, we analysed other targets of Atf4 that were significantly modulated in the islets during the partial ablation. We found that expression of Ppp1R15A, which encodes Gadd34, a phosphatase that regulates eiF2α phosphorylation and helps restore normal protein rates after the initial stress response was significantly increased at 1 daDT. Expression then progressively decreased at 3 and 5 daDT, returning towards baseline levels observed at 0 daDT (Fig. 5C) [58]. Additionally, mRNA expression of Ppargc1a (Pgc-1α), a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, presented a peak at 1 daDT, followed by a sharp decrease in expression, at 3 and 5 daDT. Mitochondria function is critical for the remaining β-cells, as they require immediate high levels of ATP to synthesise and process insulin (Fig. 5D) [59]. There were also genes that showed a later induction, peaking at 3 daDT. Among these, Hmox1 (Ho-1), an antioxidant gene that was previously shown to protect β-cells from oxidative damage (Fig. 5E) [60], or Ptgs2 (Cox2), which appeared to improve insulin secretion [61–63] (Fig. 5F).

In addition, a significantly increased number of β-cells expressing nuclear Atf4 was detected at 3 daDT, as compared to 0 and 1 daDT. Importantly, that increase was transient, as at 5 daDT, the number of β-cells expressing Atf4 was significantly reduced (Fig. 5G, H). Of note, increased nuclear ATf4 levels were also found across all insulin-negative cell populations, including non-β endocrine, exocrine and ductal cells (Fig. 5G, Supplemental Fig. 3). However, the frequency of Atf4-positive nuclei was significantly lower than in insulin-positive β-cells, indicating that Atf4 activation was enriched in the surviving β-cell population (Supplemental Fig. 3).

These results suggested that Atf4 and its targets may play an important role in the adaptive mechanisms through stress resolution, restoration of protein synthesis, and activation of mitochondrial and oxidative defence mechanisms. However, the responses to ER stress are vast and require implication from additional pathways of the UPR.

Adaptive UPR components were upregulated during later phases upon DT administration

The IRE1/Xbp1 axis is a critical mediator of ER homeostasis and can play a complementary role to the PERK/Atf4 pathway. In addition, Atf6 and spliced Xbp1 (Xbp1s) can crosstalk to activate UPR components, facilitating compensatory responses of the β-cells to increased insulin demand. To further elucidate these adaptive components, we analysed the modulation of Xbp1, Atf6 and their targets during the experimental time-course covering the mild β-cell ablation.

Analysis of the RNAseq data showed that at 1 daDT, expression of Xbp1 mRNA was significantly decreased, however, at 3 and 5 daDT returned to the 0 daDT levels (Fig. 6A). In addition, RT-qPCR results showed reduced expression levels for spliced Xbp1 (sXbp1), as compared to 0 daDT, that were maintained at all the studied time-points (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, we found a significantly increased number of β-cells that expressed nuclear sXbp1 at 3 daDT, as compared to 0 and 1 daDT. Importantly, at 5 daDT, the expression of sXbp1 in the β-cells was reduced, and comparable to the levels from 0 daDT (Fig. 6C, D). On the other hand, from RNAseq data we found that the expression of Atf6 was marginally reduced at 1 daDT. Plus, at 3 and 5 daDT, expression of Atf6 in the islets was not significantly modified as compared to the control (Supplemental Fig. 4A). However, the Atf6 pathway gets activated post-translationally, by sequential cleavage of the nuclear fragment. To assess the induction of this pathway, we determined the percentage of β-cells expressing nuclear Atf6 in the islets of mice at different time points. Our analysis showed that nuclear Atf6 was expressed in less than 5% of the β-cells, regardless of the time point (Supplemental Fig. 4B, C).

We next asked what was the impact of sXbp1 3 daDT overexpression peak on the downstream targets that would control the compensatory response present in the islets. With a few exceptions, we found that the majority of the targets had a significant dip at 1 daDT, followed by a recovery towards initial values within the next couple of days upon ablation. Within these targets, we found members of ERAD, such as Edem1 and Sel1L, with established roles in proinsulin processing and ER stress defence [64] (Fig. 6E). Moreover, our analysis identified the master chaperone Hspa5 (Bip) (Fig. 6F) along with other chaperone genes such as Dnajb9, Pdia4, Hsp90b1 (Grp94), that were initially downregulated (1 daDT), but at the later time points, their expression was increased (Fig. 6G). In addition, we found Dgat2 significantly induced, with an approximately 8 fold increase at 3 daDT as compared to 0 daDT, that later, at 5 daDT, was reduced to ~ 2 fold (Fig. 6H), pointing towards a protective effect due to decreased lipotoxicity [65–67]. Moreover, our analysis identified chaperone genes, Besides, members of the secretory pathway such as Sec61b (part of the ER translocon complex), Sec23a and Sec24d (components of the COPII vesicles) were at first downregulated, and at later time points were induced, suggesting an increase of the secretory capacity (Fig. 6I) [68, 69].

Adaptive UPR dynamics transiently preserve β-cell function during a secondary ER stress challenge

We questioned if induction of these adaptive mechanisms allowed remaining β-cells to withstand additional ER stress. To characterize stressful conditions for β-cells, we used Tg, a potent ER stressor that drives UPR activation, in complementary experiments in MIN6 cells.

Tg treatment induced a robust UPR, evidenced by an increased Xbp1s/Xbp1u ratio (Supplemental Fig. 5A) and elevated expression of Atf4 and Ddit3 (Chop) (Supplemental Fig. 5B), while insulin mRNA levels remained unchanged, indicating that insulin gene expression was not affected under these conditions (Supplemental Fig. 5C). Consistent with the known effect of Tg on ER Ca²⁺ homeostasis, a transient increase in insulin secretion was observed at 0.5 h, likely reflecting acute Ca²⁺ mobilization (Supplemental Fig. 5D). However, at 2 h, insulin secretion was significantly reduced in Tg-treated cells, whereas tunicamycin (Tm)-treated cells maintained elevated secretion, indicating that Tg more effectively suppresses insulin release once ER stress signaling predominates (Supplemental Fig. 5D). Importantly, these timeframes are shorter than those typically associated with Tg-induced cytotoxicity [70, 71], and insulin release remained detectable and reproducible across conditions, arguing against non-specific insulin leakage due to widespread cell death. Together, these data indicate that Tg elicits dynamic, time-dependent effects on β-cell secretory function, while robustly activating the UPR, supporting the use of Tg to probe β-cell responses to supplemental ER stress in our model.

Using this approach, we next examined how primary islets isolated from partially ablated mice responded to an acute secondary ER stress challenge (Fig. 7A). Under vehicle (DMSO) conditions, islets from 5 daDT mice secreted less insulin than 0 daDT islets, consistent with their reduced β-cell number (Supplementary Table 2). Surprisingly, acute exposure to Tg elicited opposite responses in the two groups: insulin secretion decreased in 0 daDT islets but increased in 5 daDT islets, resulting in higher insulin secretion despite their lower β-cell number (Supplementary Table 2). This difference persisted after 2 h. In contrast, following prolonged exposure (16 h), this functional advantage was lost, and Tg-treated partially ablated islets secreted less insulin than control islets, indicating that the adaptive response was transient and became exhausted during sustained ER stress (Supplementary Table 2). These differences were reflected by the Tg/DMSO secretion ratios (Fig. 7B-D), which were significantly higher in 5 daDT compared to 0 daDT islets during acute exposure (0.5 and 2 h), but lower after extended (16 h) Tg treatment. Of note, Tg/DMSO ratios explain the behaviour of the β-cells exposed to ER stress, while minimizing the modifications in baseline insulin secretion resulting from islets with variable β-cell numbers.

The improved functional performance of 5 daDT islets under acute Tg-induced ER stress suggested that surviving β-cells undergo a transient functional adaptive state. Given that this period coincides with dynamic activation of UPR effectors such as ATF4 and spliced XBP1, we next examined whether these pathways functionally contribute to the regulation of insulin secretion during early adaptation. Pharmacological perturbation of the PERK and IRE1 branches using specific inhibitors, like 4µ8C and GSK2606414 (GSK), respectively, increased glucose-stimulated insulin secretion in both control (Fig. 7E, 0 daDT, darker blue bars), and in partially ablated islets (Fig. 7E, 2 daDT, darker magenta bars), although the relative effect was more pronounced in control islets. More explicitly, ~ 1.89% to ~ 5,26% and ~ 2.79%, respectively, obtained for 0 daDT and compared to ~ 0.39% to ~ 1.55% and ~ 0.93%, respectively, obtained for 2 daDT (Fig. 7E). These findings suggest that basal UPR activity may impose a regulatory constraint on insulin secretion, thereby helping to balance insulin output with ER folding capacity during the adaptive response to increased functional demand.

Thus, our results indicate that partial β-cell ablation elicits a transient adaptive program in surviving β-cells, characterized by activation of UPR-associated pathways and enhanced functional competence during an acute secondary ER stress challenge while longer-term glucose homeostasis is preserved in vivo, despite the mild β-cells ablation [32].

Discussion

We and others have previously shown that partial reduction of the β-cells number did not result in glycemia perturbations, as it was way above the dangerous threshold of residual 10–20% of the β-cells mass [24, 72–74]. However, it was sufficient to produce a regenerative response in the islets [32, 75]. Beside this response, other mechanisms of adaptation were expected to take place within the short period during the ablation process. This is due to the remaining healthy β-cells being under increased pressure to compensate for the loss of their counterparts. In consequence, these cells had to increase their workload to sustain normoglycemia. We aimed to identify how the dynamic compensation is supported by the surviving β-cells, as this is essential from multiple points of view, both at basic research and clinical level.

Although we expected bottlenecks in several insulin processing steps in the remaining β-cells [11], proinsulin levels remained stable over 5 days post ablation. This unchanged response was likely due to low impact of the mild ablation on proinsulin synthesis at transcriptional and translational level, efficient posttranslational processing, a functional ERAD, or a damaging stress threshold that probably was not reached [11, 64, 76]. In contrast, intracellular insulin and islet insulin content significantly decreased 3 daDT and remained low, suggesting rapid depletion of reserves to meet increased demands. This behaviour resembled β-cells subtypes found in obesity and hyperglycaemia, characterized by increased secretion, but low insulin content [77, 78]. In parallel with these changes, a qualitative assessment of mitochondrial ultrastructure suggested a shift toward a more fragmented mitochondrial network at the intermediate time point (Fig. 3A). Such remodelling has been associated with metabolic adaptation and may facilitate localized ATP production to support increased biosynthetic and secretory demands in β-cells under stress [79]. Moreover, by ultrastructural analysis, granule area and diameter showed an initial shrinkage, previously associated with rapid insulin release [80]. However, by 3 daDT, we found enlarged granules, coupled with increase of mean grey value, suggesting a lighter and less dense core, consistent with an immature granule phenotype [81, 82]. Importantly, by 5 daDT, the majority of the parameters recovered towards the baseline, pointing to a replenishment and maturation of the granules. Although the EM analyses revealed consistent temporal changes in insulin granule morphology, the limited number of biological replicates means that these observations should be interpreted primarily as descriptive and supportive of the accompanying biochemical, transcriptional and functional data instead as a standalone quantitative analysis. Additionally, serum C-peptide – more stable than insulin – rose at 3 and 5 daDT, supporting the increased secretory response. Reduced levels of islet C-peptide point to the “β-cell overdrive” phenomenon, where remaining cells compensate for the loss of their counterparts by hyper-secreting insulin [83, 84]. Such cells were shown to be vulnerable during diabetes progression. Thus, our study concentrated on stressed β-cells’ capacity for functional recovery, though in a simplified set-up, unlike in diabetes, where they are lost due to apoptosis or dedifferentiation [85–88]. The acute reduction in β-cell mass achieved in this model differs from the progressive β-cell decline characteristic of type 1 and type 2 diabetes, where metabolic and inflammatory stressors accumulate over prolonged periods. Such chronic conditions may engage UPR pathway differently and may limit the emergence of transient adaptive responses. In contrast, the rapid, but partial β-cell ablation used here reveals early compensatory mechanisms in surviving β-cell, before long-term maladaptive processes predominate. This distinction should be considered when extrapolating these findings to human diabetes, in which β-cell stress is typically sustained and multifactorial.

During the ablation, the remaining healthy β-cells adapted their secretory pathway to accommodate increased insulin load and processing in a short period of time. We aimed to characterize the molecular dynamics that govern this adaptation. At the early stage (1 daDT), bulk RNA-seq revealed a strong stress response, marked by upregulation of apoptosis and stress-related genes, consistent with the acute elimination of DT-targeted β-cells. This transcriptional response diminished by 3 daDT, suggesting partial stress resolution. However, at 5 daDT, the overall molecular response slightly increased, pointing to ongoing cellular recovery. In particular, UPR-associated genes initially downregulated at 1 daDT, became upregulated at 3 daDT, suggesting a compensatory mechanism to restore ER function [7, 76]. Importantly, by 5 daDT, UPR activity stabilized, indicating adaptation to sustained insulin demand. This pattern is in line with previous observations from healthy human β-cells, where UPR levels are inversely associated with insulin production, while correlating with increased proliferation, consistent with a previously evidenced response [89, 90]. Moreover, induction of β-cells proliferation was shown upon partial ablation [32, 75]. In sum, these findings support a coordinated transition from the increased workload stress toward adaptive functional regeneration capacity of the β-cells.

An important limitation of this study is that the early transcriptomic analyses were performed while DT-mediated β-cell ablation was ongoing. Consequently, bulk RNA-seq captured transcriptional programs originating from both DT-targeted apoptotic β-cells and the DT-insensitive β-cells adapting to increased secretory demand. We therefore interpreted the pronounced apoptotic signature observed at 1 daDT — including transient induction of Tp53, Bbc3 and other stress-associated genes [47, 48] — as predominantly reflecting the direct effects of DT-mediated EF-2 inhibition in DTR-expressing β-cells [91, 92]. However, several observations argue against persistent DT intoxication as the explanation for the adaptive UPR signatures described here. Notably, although mice received an additional DT injection at 4 daDT, no second wave of apoptotic or integrated stress response gene activation was detected at 5 daDT, despite this time point corresponding to approximately 24 h after toxin administration, analogous to 1 daDT. This finding is consistent with the rapid elimination of DTR-expressing β-cells during the initial phase of ablation, leaving predominantly DTR-negative β-cells that are no longer susceptible to intracellular DT intoxication. Moreover, the later activation of ATF4- and XBP1- associated pathways, together with their localization in surviving insulin-positive β-cells, is reflecting the adaptive response to increased functional demand placed on the remaining β-cell population rather than a direct consequence of DT exposure.

Unlike streptozotocin-induced injury or chronic obesogenic models, where β-cells are exposed to persistent cytotoxic or metabolic stress, our system imposes a primarily functional burden on otherwise healthy surviving β-cells. This distinction enables clearer identification of intrinsic adaptive programs activated in response to increased insulin demand, rather than secondary responses to systemic inflammation or lipotoxicity. Such framing highlights the value of this model for dissecting early adaptive UPR signalling.

Persistent hyperglycaemia was shown to drive chronic and stressed UPR, incapable of restoring β-cell homeostasis, resulting in activation of the apoptotic machinery via the phosphorylated eiF2α/ATF4/CHOP signalling pathway [21, 93, 94]. In addition, there are studies pointing that the transcription factor Atf4 plays a crucial role in pancreatic β-cell function and identity, suggesting a context-dependent role [54, 95, 96]. For example, by applying different stressors, such as acute high glucose or free fatty acids, β-cells elicited variable Atf4-driven responses that helped maintain β-cells function in the first case, while the latter resulted in detrimental effects [54]. However, how and if Atf4 was regulated in the remaining, healthy β-cells forced to maintain insulin homeostasis during the partial β-cell ablation was not described yet. Our analysis showed that expression of activated Atf4 in the β-cells was dynamic, displaying a strong peak at 3 daDT and recovering to near the baseline by 5 daDT. This dynamic was essential for the β-cell survival and function, as was shown that sustained Atf4 overexpression resulted in compromised β-cell homeostasis [97]. Although nuclear Atf4 was detected in other pancreatic cell populations, its frequency was significantly enriched in the remaining insulin-positive β-cells, indicating preferential activation in the cells experiencing the greatest secretory burden following partial β-cell ablation. In the context of the temporal transcriptomic analyses discussed above, these observations support the interpretation that the later ATF4 response reflects an adaptive program engaged by the surviving DT-insensitive β-cells rather than persistent DT-induced toxicity.

Furthermore, we aimed to explore mechanisms by which Atf4 controls β-cell homeostasis, playing roles in the adaptive responses. Our transcriptomic analysis suggested several possibilities related to documented targets of Atf4. Specifically, we detected variable expression of multiple genes involved in acute stress response and mitochondrial function, which presented an initial induction that later was attenuated. In contrast, those linked to antioxidant defence, or correlated with protective effects for β-cell were upregulated at later time points upon the ablation. However, the responses to ER stress are vast and possibly require implication from additional pathways of the UPR.

Interestingly, although Ddit3 transcripts were significantly induced, Chop protein was not detectable in β-cells by immunofluorescence, suggesting that post-transcriptional regulation or rapid protein turnover may limit Chop accumulation, thereby preventing activation of the pro-apoptotic arm of the UPR.

We and others have previously documented that there was increased β-cell proliferation following mild β-cell ablation [32, 75]. This regenerative response has been previously linked to activation of ATF6 arm of the UPR, particularly in contexts of increased insulin demand [89, 98]. However, despite the proliferative activity observed in our model, we did not find significant induction of ATF6, neither at transcriptional level in whole islets, nor at the protein level within the remaining β-cells as assessed by immunofluorescence, throughout the analysed time course (Supplemental Fig. 4). In addition, the mild and adaptive nature of the stress imposed may not have reached the activation threshold required for robust ATF6 signalling, or ATF6 activation may have occurred transiently outside the temporal windows that we analysed. While active suppression of ATF6 cannot be excluded, direct evidence for such a mechanism in β-cells under mild stress remains limited. Dissecting the temporal and quantitative thresholds governing ATF6 activation will require future studies with finer temporal resolution and branch-specific reporters.

Of note, the present model relies on female mice to exploit X-linked mosaic expression of the diphtheria toxin receptor. Although core UPR pathways are conserved, sex-dependent differences in β-cell responses and proliferative capacity have been reported [99, 100], and whether identical adaptive mechanisms operate in β-cells from male individuals following comparable loss of β-cell mass remains to be determined.

Given the known functional overlap between ATF6 and the IRE1-XBP1 axis, specifically in regulating ER chaperons and components of the secretory pathway, we inferred that activation of XBP1 may compensate for the absence of detectable ATF6 induction in this context [101–103]. Moreover, it was demonstrated that activation of XBP1 in β-cells through splicing of Xbp1 mRNA by the UPR transducer IRE1 had been potentiated by glucose [15, 104]. Further on, XBP1 directly enhanced the expression of genes involved in ER-associated degradation (ERAD), chaperone production, and lipid biosynthesis [105, 106]. In addition, Xbp1 was largely associated with maintenance of β-cells identity, repressed β-to-α transdifferentiation, increased antioxidant response under metabolic stress, and enhanced folding capacity [15, 107]. Thus, we aimed to characterize if the response from the islets upon the mild ablation included XBP1 and the corresponding downstream targets. Interestingly, though we found reduced mRNA levels of spliced Xbp1 at later time points, this concurred with a transient peak in spliced XBP1 (XBP1s) protein expression. This discrepancy could be explained by the distinctive regulation of XBP1, where its activation is primarily controlled at the level of IRE1-dependent mRNA splicing, rather than transcriptional induction [35, 108].

Importantly, the 3 daDT Xbp1s peak followed by the decrease of expression at 5 daDT likely reflected a temporary adaptive response to ER stress. This was an important result as it underlines the existence of a tight control for the expression of XBP1s protein under mild stress conditions, keeping it below a certain threshold. A further increase would otherwise have a negative impact on glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, being associated with β-cells apoptosis [109]. To further elucidate the adaptive mechanisms governed by Xbp1 in our β-cells mild ablation model, we analysed the induction of its targets. With a few exceptions, the majority of documented XBP1 target genes that we identified had an expression pattern that mostly overlapped with the expression of XBP1s, strongly supporting this association. They displayed an initial significant decrease, followed by recovery to the baseline levels that were maintained at later time points. This indicated an early disruption of the adaptive stress responses, accompanied by a prompt restoration of the protective mechanisms as part of the β-cell adaptation process. For example, we identified key genes involved in proinsulin processing and protection against ER stress-related dysfunction, such as Edem1 and Sel1L, both of which are critical for maintaining ER-associated degradation (ERAD) and proteostasis under metabolic overload [64, 110, 111]. Another perspective of the adaptive response was suggested by the temporary induction of Dgat2 mRNA that overlapped with the activation of XBP1s [66, 112]. Considering the role of DGAT2 in triacylglycerol synthesis, this might reflect a way of mitigation of lipotoxic stress during the regenerative process. Through promoting lipid storage and reducing the load of free fatty acids and diacylglycerol, DGAT2 could help recover ER function and β-cell health [65]. It was important that this induction was short lived, as it was shown that sustained DGAT2 activation could become maladaptive, leading to cell dysfunction [113].

Another group of XBP1 targets found deregulated in our data set was represented by chaperons. These molecules were previously shown to control proper folding of insulin and of other secretory proteins to maintain β-cell functionality during stress (Bip) [114, 115], to reduce oxidative stress (Pdia4) [116, 117], to manage proinsulin processing and degradation (Bip co-chaperons, Erdj3 (Dnajb9) and Erdj4 (Dnajb11) [118–120], and to confer β-cell survival (Grp94) [121, 122]. Additionally, during the mild ablation of the β-cells, genes representing components of the secretory pathway presented a dynamic that showed a significant activation after the initial downregulation, further supporting adaptive mechanisms that were operating under Xbp1 control [123]. For example, we identified elements of the early stages of insulin synthesis, represented here by Sect. 61, member of the ER-translocon machinery and a constituent of the TRAP complex, essential for pre-proinsulin translocation in the ER [124, 125]. Further on the pathway, we identified constituents of the ER-to-Golgi trafficking, such as Sec23a and Sec24d [123].

Our in-depth analysis of islets following mild β-cell ablation showed a concerted and complex response that involved, to varying degrees, all the UPR branches. However, an important limitation of our study is that the bulk RNA-seq does not resolve cell type-specific transcriptional responses, and therefore cannot fully distinguish between β-cell-intrinsic changes from alterations in islet cellular composition. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) or spatial transcriptomic approaches would provide higher-resolution characterization of the adaptive transcriptional programs engaged by the DT-insensitive β-cells. Nevertheless, our bulk RNA-seq data served as a hypothesis-generating tool to identify candidate stress and adaptation pathways. Thus, we identified key markers that were validated at the protein level in remaining insulin-positive β-cells, supporting the interpretation that these pathways contribute to the adaptive response following partial β-cell ablation. Based on these observations, we proposed that the adaptive response is similar to a hormetic-like phenomenon, where prior exposure to sublethal stress transiently enhances subsequent adaptability [126, 127]. This concept is consistent with recent findings demonstrating priming-dependent stress resilience in β-cells [128].

Functional assays further support the existence of a transient adaptive state in the DT-insensitive β-cells. Isolated islets from partially ablated RIP-DTR mice displayed an altered response to a secondary ER stress challenge with thapsigargin (Tg), maintaining greater insulin secretory competence during the early phase of stress than control islets, with normal β-cell mass. Notably, despite containing fewer β-cells, partially ablated islets secreted more insulin than control islets following acute Tg exposure, whereas control islets exhibited reduced secretion. Because the same pharmacological stimulus elicited opposite functional responses in the two groups, these findings are difficult to explain solely by Tg-induced ER Ca²⁺ mobilization. Instead, suggest that the prior adaptive state of the surviving β-cells modifies their response to a secondary ER stress challenge. This functional advantage was not sustained during prolonged Tg exposure, indicating that prior adaptation delays, but does not prevent the detrimental effects of persistent ER stress. Together with the effects of pharmacological modulation of the PERK-ATF4 and IRE1-XBP1 pathways on insulin secretion, these findings suggest that adaptive UPR signaling contributes to the regulation of the β-cell secretory function during the compensatory phase following β-cell loss.

Moreover, this response proved sufficient in vivo, to sustain insulin requirements, even for mice challenged with a high fat diet for 10 weeks [32]. Importantly, this should not be interpreted as evidence for an indefinitely durable UPR-mediated adaptation in the β-cells. Rather, we propose that UPR engagement represents an early, transient stress-adaptive response that supports β-cell survival and function during the immediate period following acute loss of β-cell mass. As homeostasis is progressively restored through longer-term compensatory mechanisms [32], such as β-cell proliferation and mass recovery, which have been reported to occur in this model, the selective pressure for sustained UPR activation is likely reduced. Thus, whether adaptive UPR involvement requires ongoing mild stress or can establish a longer-lasting “memory” remains an open question. While epigenetic mechanisms have been proposed to underlie stress memory in other cellular contexts [129], direct evidence for durable epigenetic imprinting of UPR programs in β-cells is currently limited. Future studies combining temporal profiling with chromatin-based analyses will be required to determine whether transient stress exposure confers lasting changes in β-cell stress resilience.

Moreover, these findings also raise the question of whether specific UPR branches could be therapeutically targeted to enhance β-cell resilience. Our data suggest that transient engagement of the ATF4 and IRE1–XBP1 axes contributes to the adaptive regulation of β-cell function under conditions of increased demand, with XBP1 likely playing a central role in maintaining ER folding and secretory capacity. Modulation of this pathway may therefore represent a tractable strategy to reinforce β-cell fitness. However, sustained or excessive activation of UPR signaling carries potential risks, as chronic PERK–ATF4 or IRE1 signaling can promote translational repression, inflammation, or apoptosis. Therapeutic approaches would thus need to carefully balance enhancement of adaptive UPR outputs while avoiding prolonged activation that could compromise β-cell survival.

By integrating transcriptomic, ultrastructural, protein localization and functional analyses, this study defines the dynamic molecular and cellular adaptations that occur in surviving β-cells following partial β-cell loss (Fig. 7F). These findings support a model in which engagement of adaptive UPR pathways helps preserve β-cell function during the early compensatory phase after β-cell ablation. From a translational perspective, understanding how these adaptive programs are initiated and regulated may inform strategies aimed at enhancing β-cell resilience and delaying β-cell failure in diabetes.
