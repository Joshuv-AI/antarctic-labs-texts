Nonparametric Least squares estimators for interval censoring
Piet Groeneboom

Abstract
The limit distribution of the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator for interval censored data with more than one observation time per unobservable observation, is still unknown in general. For the so-called separated case, where one has observation times which are at a distance larger than a fixed ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 , the limit distribution was derived in [ 5 ] . For the non-separated case there is a conjectured limit distribution, given in [ 4 ] , Section 5.2 of Part 2. But the findings of the present paper suggest that this conjecture may not hold. We prove consistency of a closely related nonparametric isotonic least squares estimator and give a sketch of the proof for a result on its limit distribution. We also provide simulation results to show how the nonparametric MLE and least squares estimator behave in comparison. Moreover, we discuss a simpler least squares estimator that can be computed in one step, but is inferior to the other least squares estimator, since it does not use all information. For the simplest model of interval censoring, the current status model, the nonparametric maximum likelihood and least squares estimators are the same. This equivalence breaks down if there are more observation times per unobservable observation. The computations for the simulation of the more complicated interval censoring model were performed by using the iterative convex minorant algorithm. They are provided in the GitHub repository [ 8 ] .

Introduction

The simplest and most studied interval censoring model is the so-called interval censoring, case 1, or current status model.
This model can be defined in the following way (see, e.g., Section 2.3 of
[
6
]
).

Consider a sample
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
, drawn from a distribution with distribution function
F_{0}
. Instead of observing the
X_{i}
’s, one only observes for each
i
whether or not
X_{i}\leq T_{i}
for some random
T_{i}
(independent of the other
T_{j}
’s and all
X_{j}
’s). More formally, instead of observing the
X_{i}
’s, one observes

(T_{i},\Delta_{i})=(T_{i},1_{\{X_{i}\leq T_{i}\}}).

(1.1)

One could say that the
i
-th observation represents the current status of item
i
at time
T_{i}
.

We want to estimate the unknown distribution function
F_{0}
based on the data given in (
1.1
). For this problem the log likelihood function for distribution functions
F
(conditional on the
T_{i}
’s) is given by

\ell(F)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\Delta_{i}\log F(T_{i})+(1-\Delta_{i})\log(1-F(T_{i}))\right).

(1.2)

The (nonparametric) maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{F}_{n}
maximizes
\ell
over the class of
all
distribution functions.

Since distribution functions are by definition nondecreasing, computing the maximum likelihood estimator poses a shape constrained optimization problem. As can be seen from (
1.2
), the value of
\ell
only depends on the values that
F
takes at the observed time points
T_{i}
. Hence one can choose to consider only distribution functions that are constant between successive observed time points
T_{i}
. The lemma below shows that this estimator can be characterized in terms of a greatest convex minorant of a certain diagram of points. The following result is Lemma 2.7 in
[
6
]
(and Proposition 1.2 in
[
4
]
).

Lemma 1
.

[Characterization of the nonparametric ML estimator in the current status model]
Consider the cumulative sum diagram consisting of the points
P_{0}=(0,0)
and

P_{i}=\left(i,\sum_{j=1}^{i}\Delta_{j}\right),\,\,\,1\leq i\leq n,

where the
\Delta_{i}
’s correspond to the
T_{i}
’s, which are supposed to be ordered
0<T_{1}\dots<T_{n}
(one can also allow ties, but we disregard this further complication here).
Then the nonparametric MLE
\hat{F}_{n}(T_{i})
is given by the left derivative of the convex minorant of this diagram of points, evaluated at the point
i
. This maximizer is unique.

Remark 1
.

The
left
derivative of the convex minorant at
P_{i}
determines the value of
\hat{F}_{n}
at
T_{i}
and hence (by right continuity of the distribution function
\hat{F}_{n}
) on
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
, a region to the
right
of
T_{i}
.

Lemma
1
shows that
\hat{F}_{n}
is in fact the
isotonic regression
on the indicators
\Delta_{i}=1_{\{X_{i}T_{i}\}}
, that is, it minimizes

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{F(T_{i})-\Delta_{i}\}^{2}

(1.3)

over all monotone nondecreasing (not necessarily bounded by
0
and
1
) functions
F
. so we have the two characterizations of the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator, the characterization as a maximizer of (
1.2
) and the characterization as a minimizer of (
1.3
) (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
). Alternatively, we can derive the equivalence of these formulations from Theorem 1.5.1 in
[
9
]
.

If we turn to the (common) situation where there are more observation times per unobservable
X_{i}
, the situation is considerably more complicated, and the limit distribution of the nonparametric MLE is still unknown generally. We consider here the simplest extension, where one has two observation times per unobservable
X_{i}
(
interval censoring, case 2
). Instead instead of observing the
X_{i}
’s, one observes

(U_{i},V_{i},\Delta_{i0},\Delta_{i1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(U_{i},V_{i},1_{\{X_{i}\leq U_{i}\}},1_{\{U_{i}<X_{i}\leq V_{i}\}}),

(1.4)

so instead of only observing that
X_{i}
is larger than or smaller than an observation
T_{i}
, we now have an observation interval
(U_{i},V_{i})
,
U_{i}<V_{i}
, and we know whether our unobservable
X_{i}
is inside the interval or to the left or right of it. The log likelihood (
1.2

\displaystyle\ell(F)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\Delta_{i0}\log F(U_{i})+\Delta_{i1}\log(F(V_{i})-F(U_{i}))+\Delta_{i2}\log(1-F(V_{i}))\right\},

(1.5)

where
\Delta_{i2}=1-\Delta_{i0}-\Delta_{i1}

It is not clear that there is an equivalent to the minimization of (

[
9

and

in the terms
\log(F(V_{i})-F(U_{i}))

or

The perhaps most natural least squares approach is to consider minimization of

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\left\{F(U_{i})-\Delta_{i0}\right\}^{2}+\left\{F(V_{i})-F(U_{i})-\Delta_{i1}\right\}^{2}+\left\{1-F(V_{i})-\Delta_{i2}\right\}^{2}\right\}

(1.6)

over all distribution functions
F
, so an isotonic regression on the indicators
\Delta_{i0},\Delta_{i1},\Delta_{i2}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.

The least squares estimators

In the characterization of the nonparametric MLE one can use the fact that the logarithms provide a natural logarithmic boundary, preventing values of the solution to leave the interval
[0,1]
. This is no longer true for the least squares estimate minimizing (
1.6
) and for this reason we use Lagrange multipliers in its characterization.

Let, for a distribution function
F
, the process
W_{n,F}
be defined by

\displaystyle W_{n,F}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{u\leq t}\{\delta_{0}-F(u)\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1})-\int_{u\leq t}\{\delta_{1}-\{F(v)-F(u)\}\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1})

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{v\leq t}\{\delta_{1}-\{F(v)-F(u)\}\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1})+\int_{v\leq t}\{\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}-F(v)\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1}),

(2.1)

where
{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}
is the empirical probability measre of the
(U_{i},V_{i},\Delta_{i0},\Delta_{i1})
.

The least squares estimate of
F_{0}
has the following characterization.

Lemma 2
.

Let the process
W_{n,F}
be defined by (
2
) and let the Lagrange multipliers
\lambda_{1,F}
and
\lambda_{2,F}
be defined by

\displaystyle\lambda_{1,F}=-\int_{(u,v):F(u)=0\,\,\text{\rm or }F(v)=0}\,dW_{n,F}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1}),

(2.2)

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{2,F}=\int_{(u,v):F(u)=1\,\,\text{\rm or }F(v)=1}\,dW_{n,F}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1}).

(2.3)

Then the distribution function
\hat{F}_{n}
minimizes (
2.8
) over all distribution functions
F
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
if and only if the following conditions are satisfied

(i)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1,\hat{F}_{n}}+W_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(t)\geq 0,\qquad t\geq 0,

(ii)

\displaystyle\int\hat{F}_{n}(t)\,dW_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(t)-\lambda_{2,\hat{F}_{n}}=0.

The process
W_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}
as a function of the
2n
ordered observations
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
for Example
1
and
n=100

\lambda_{1,\hat{F}_{n}}=0.003148
and
\lambda_{2,\hat{F}_{n}}=0.014758

The proof is based on the so-called Fenchel duality condition, as also used in
[
6
]
in the characterization of the nonparametric MLE for interval censoring (apart from the Lagrange multipliers in the present case) and is therefore omitted. A picture of the process
W_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}
for
n=100
is given in Figure
5
for Example

above.
W_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}
touches zero at points just to the left of points where the least squares estimator
\hat{F}_{n}
has a jump. The iterative convex minorant algorithm for computing
\hat{F}_{n}
is based on this characterization.

The algorithm proceeds in the following way. At each step we solve the isotonic regression problem without the Lagrange multipliers, under the restriction
y_{1}\leq\dots\leq y_{2n}
, where
y_{i}=F(T_{i})
represents the value of the distribution function at the
i
th order statistic of set of
U_{i}
’s and
V_{i}
’s (ties can also be handled, but we disregard this further complication here). Typically, this will give values
y_{i}<0
and
y_{i}>1
. If
y_{i}<0
we put
y_{i}=0
and if
y_{i}>1
we put
y_{i}=1
. For this new value of the
y_{i}
we compute the (preliminary) Lagrange multipliers
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, using (
2.2
) and (
2.3
). We repeat this procedure until the conditions (i) and (ii) of Lemma
2
are satisfied up to an accuracy of say
10^{-8}
. Convergence of this algorithm is very fast.

One can also compute the estimate by the interior point method, using a logarithmic barrier function. This algorithm is a kind of opposite of the iterative convex minnorant algorihm, since it converges to the solution from the interior of the parameter space, whereas the iterative convex minorant algorithm immediately hits the boundary in the first iteration step. Both algorithms were programmed for the present problem and give exactly the same result, though.

The least squares estimator minimizing (
1.6
) is consistent, as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\hat{F}_{n}
be the isotonic least squares estimator, minimizing (
1.6
) over distribution functions
F
under the conditions of Theorem
1
below. Then
\hat{F}_{n}
converges almost surely to
F_{0}
in the supremum metric.

Let the function
\psi
be defined by

\displaystyle\psi(F)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int\left\{\{F(u)-\delta_{0}\}^{2}+\{F(v)-F(u)-\delta_{1}\}^{2}+\{F(v)-\delta_{0}-\delta_{1}\}^{2}\right\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1}).

(2.4)

Since
\hat{F}_{n}
minimizes (
2
) over
F
, we must have

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{-1}\left[\psi\bigl((1-\epsilon)\hat{F}_{n}+\epsilon F_{0}\bigr)-\psi(\hat{F}_{n})\right]\geq 0.

Note that the limit exists by the convexity of the function
\psi
.
This means

\displaystyle\int\{F_{0}(u)-\hat{F}_{n}(u)\}\{\hat{F}_{n}(u)-\delta_{0}\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\{F_{0}(v)-F_{0}(u)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)+\hat{F}_{n}(u)\}\{\hat{F}_{n}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(u)-\delta_{1}\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\{F_{0}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(u)\}\{\hat{F}_{n}(v)-\delta_{0}-\delta_{1}\}\,d{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1})

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Proceeding as in Section 4 of Part 2 of
[
4
]
, using the Helly compactness theorem, we get from this, for a limit point
F
of a subsequence of
\hat{F}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\int\{F_{0}(u)-F(u)\}\{F(u)-F_{0}(u)\}\,h(u,v)\,du\,dv

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\{F_{0}(v)-F_{0}(u)-F_{(}v)-F(v)+F(u)\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\{F(v)-F(u)-F_{0}(v)+F_{0}(u)\}\,h(u,v)\,du\,dv

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\{F_{0}(v)-F(v)\}\{F(v)-F_{0}(v)\}\,h(u,v)\,du\,dv

\displaystyle=-\int\{F(u)-F_{0}(u)\}^{2}\,h(u,v)\,du\,dv-\int\{F(v)-F(u)-F_{0}(v)+F_{0}(u)\}^{2}\,h(u,v)\,du\,dv

\displaystyle\qquad-\int\{F(v)-F_{0}(v)\}^{2}\,h(u,v)\,du\,dv

\displaystyle\geq 0.

By the assumptions on
F_{0}
and

this means that

. This implies that
\hat{F}_{n}
converges uniformly to

.

The smooth functional theory for the nonparametric MLE in the separated case is discussed in

The score operator for the model is given by

\displaystyle[La](u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1})=E\left[a(X)|(U,V,\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})=(u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{0}\int_{x\in[0,u]}a(x)\,dF(x)}{F(u)}+\frac{\delta_{1}\int_{x\in(u,v]}a(x)\,dF(x)}{F(v)-F(u)}+\frac{\delta_{2}\int_{x\in(v,M]}a(x)\,dF(x)}{1-F(v)}\,,

(3.1)

where
0/0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}0
and
\delta_{2}=1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}

\displaystyle\int a(x)\,dF(x)=0.

As in

]

\displaystyle\phi_{F}(x)=\int_{y\in(x,M]}a(y)\,dF(y).

(3.2)

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{y\in[0,x]}a(y)\,dF(y)=-\int_{y\in(x,M]}a(y)\,dF(y)=-\phi_{F}(x).

(3.3)

Using this notation, we have

\displaystyle[La](u,v,\delta_{0},\delta_{1})=-\frac{\delta_{0}\phi_{F}(u)}{F(u)}-\frac{\delta_{1}\{\phi_{F}(v)-\phi_{F}(u)\}}{F(v)-F(u)}+\frac{\{1-\delta_{0}-\delta_{1}\}\phi_{F}(v)}{1-F(v)}.

The adjoint of

can be written

\displaystyle[L^{*}b](x)

\displaystyle=E_{F}[b(U,V,\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})|X=x)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\leq u\leq v}b(u,v,1,0)\,dH(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{u<x\leq v}b(u,v,0,1)\,dH(u,v)+\int_{u\leq v<x}b(u,v,0,0)\,dH(u,v).

(3.4)

\displaystyle\kappa_{F}(x)=\kappa(x)-\int\kappa(y)\,dF(y)

in the “hidden space”, we get the equation

\displaystyle[L^{*}La](x)=\kappa_{F}(x).

4
Conclusion

We stated a limit result for the least squares estimator minimizing (
1.6
) for interval censoring, case 2, in the “non-separated case” (“observation intervals can be arbitrariy small”), in which situation the limit behavior of the nonparametric MLE is still unknown. This is Theorem
1
in section
2
. For the separated case the limit distribution of the nonparametric MLE was derived in
[
5
]
. One could say (as has been done) that the separated case is the more important case (“there will always be a positive time interval between examination times of the doctor”), but the situation where one does not have the limit for the non-separated case is rather unsatisfactory. After all, allowing arbitrarily small intervals is mostly a matter of type of scaling one allows.

There is a conjecture that the nonparametric MLE has a faster rate of convergence in section 5.2 of part 2 of
[
4
]
and there also has been the construction of a histogram-type estimator with a faster local rate in
[
1
]
, but the result for the least squares estimator and simulations suggest that this conjecture may not hold for the MLE. See, in particular, Figure
3
for sample size
n=10,000
in the present paper, which suggests that the limit behavior of the nonparametric MLE might be the same as that of the least squares estimator, minimizing (
1.6
). For the least squares estimator minimizing (
1.6
) one can extend the result to cover the whole range of situations from separated to non-separated. The least squares estimator minimizing (
1.6
) seems generally to have a smaller (pointwise) variance than the nonparametric MLE for moderate sample sizes (like
n=1000
).

We also discussed a simpler least squares estimatior, minimizing (
1.7
) which does not have to be calculated by an iterative procedure and also does not need Lagrange multipliers to keep the values of the estimator inside the interval
[0,1]
. But this estimator is clearly inferior to that of the least squares estimator minimizing (
1.6
) in the pointwise estimation problem. In estimating smooth functionals, like moments, the estimators seem to have a rather similar behavior, as shown in Section
3

Algorithms for computing the least squares estimator were discussed and in Section
3
we provide smooth functional theory needed to show that the so-called “off-diagonal terms” in the derivation of the asymptotic distribution are negligeable. Here some new theory is introduced, replacing the integral equations in
[
2
]
and
[
6
]
by finite matrix equations w.r.t. the empirical measure.

For the well-known case of current status data (the simplest case of interval censoring) the least squares approach coincides with the aproach directly based on analyzing the likelihood, since in that case the least square estimate and the nonparametric MLE are the same.

C++
routines with makefiles to check the algorithms and simulations are given in
[
8
]
. They use the iterative convex minorant algorithm.

(5.4)

We have:

\displaystyle n^{4/3}\text{var}\bigl(X_{n}(t)\bigr)

\displaystyle\sim tF_{0}(t_{0})\{1-F_{0}(t_{0})\}\bigl\{h_{1}(t_{0})+h_{2}(t_{0})\bigr\}

\displaystyle\qquad+t\int_{v=t_{0}}^{M}\{F_{0}(v)-F_{0}(t_{0})\}\left[1-\{F_{0}(v)-F_{0}(t_{0})\}\right]\,h(t_{0},v)\,dv

\displaystyle\qquad+t\int_{u=0}^{t_{0}}\{F_{0}(t_{0})-F_{0}(u)\}\left[1-\{F_{0}(t_{0})-F_{0}(u)\}\right]\,h(u,t_{0})\,du

\displaystyle\qquad+2tF_{0}(t_{0})\int_{v=t_{0}}^{M}\{F_{0}(v)-F_{0}(t_{0})\}\,h(t_{0},v)\,dv

\displaystyle\qquad+2t\{1-F_{0}(t_{0})\}\int_{u=0}^{t_{0}}\{F_{0}(t_{0})-F_{0}(u)\}\,h(u,t_{0})\,du,

(5.5)

where
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are the marginal densities of
h
.

This is seen as follows. Conditionally on
(U_{j},V_{j})
, the triple
(\Delta_{j0},\Delta_{j1},\Delta_{j2})
has a trinomial distribution, with parameters

\displaystyle p=F_{0}(U_{j}),\qquad q=F(V_{j})-F_{0}(U_{j}),\qquad 1-p-q=1-F(V_{j}).

This yields the first three terms on the right of (
5.1
), since the conditional variances are of the form
p(1-p)
,
q(1-q)
and
(p+q)(1-p-q)
. The last two terms on the right of (
5.1
) are due to the covariance of
\Delta_{j0}
and
-\Delta_{j1}
in the first two terms and
\Delta_{j1}
and
-\Delta_{j2}
in the last two term on the right-hand side of (
5.1
), respectively. Other covariances are of lower order, since these involve values of
U_{j}
and
V_{j}
in the shrinking interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]
.
So we get

\displaystyle n^{2/3}X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\cal D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma_{t_{0}}W,\qquad n\to\infty.

from tightness and the central limit theorem.
∎

We can now write

\displaystyle W_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t)-W_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(t_{0})=X_{n}(t)+Y_{n}(t),

where
Y_{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle Y_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\left\{F_{0}(u)-\hat{F}_{n}(u)\right\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\left\{F_{0}(v)-F_{0}(u)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)+\hat{F}_{n}(u)\right\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{v\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\left\{F_{0}(v)-F_{0}(u)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)+\hat{F}_{n}(u)\right\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{v\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\left\{F_{0}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)\right\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v),

(5.6)

and where
{\mathbb{H}}_{n}
is the empirical distribution function of the
(U_{i},V_{i})
.

Let the process
G_{n}
be defined by

\displaystyle G_{n}(t)=\int_{u\in[0,t]}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)+\int_{v\in[0,t]}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v),\qquad t\geq 0.

Then
\hat{F}_{n}
is the left-continuous slope of the (“self-induced”) cusum diagram, consisting of the points
(0,0)

and

\displaystyle\left(G_{n}(t),\int_{w\in[0,t]}\hat{F}_{n}(w)\,dG_{n}(w)+W_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(t)\right),\qquad t\geq 0,

truncated at
0
and
1
(if the slope at
t
is
<0
, we put
\hat{F}_{n}(t)=0
and similarly, if the slope at
t
is
>1
, we put
\hat{F}_{n}(t)=1
).

We have:

\displaystyle\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\hat{F}_{n}(w)\,dG_{n}(w)+Y_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=2\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}F_{0}(u)\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)+2\int_{v\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}F_{0}(u)\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\left\{F_{0}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)\right\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{v\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\left\{F_{0}(u)-\hat{F}_{n}(u)\right\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v).

(5.7)

The last two terms are smooth functionals of the model and can be shown to be of order
O_{p}(n^{-5/6})
. We shall discuss this matter below in sections
5.2
and Section
3
.

The conclusion is that

\displaystyle\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\hat{F}_{n}(w)\,dG_{n}(w)+W_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t)-W_{n,\hat{F}_{n}}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=2\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}F_{0}(u)\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)+2\int_{v\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}F_{0}(v)\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad+X_{n}(t)+O_{p}(n^{-5/6}).

This means that
n^{1/3}\{\hat{F}_{n}(t_{0})-F_{0}(t_{0})\}
behaves asymptotically as the slope of the greatest convex minorant of the process

\displaystyle 2n^{2/3}\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\{F_{0}(u)-F_{0}(t_{0})\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad+2n^{2/3}\int_{v\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\{F_{0}(u)-F_{0}(t_{0})\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)+n^{2/3}X_{n}(t)+O_{p}(n^{-1/6}).

in the time scale

\displaystyle n^{1/3}\{G_{n}(t_{0}+n^{-1/3})-G_{n}(t_{0})\}\sim t\{h_{1}(t)+h_{2}(t)\}.

Since

\displaystyle 2n^{2/3}\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\{F_{0}(u)-F_{0}(t_{0})\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad+2n^{2/3}\int_{v\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\{F_{0}(u)-F_{0}(t_{0})\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle\sim f_{0}(t_{0})\{h_{1}(t_{0})+h_{2}(t_{0})\}t^{2},

the result now follows from Brownian scaling. using a similar computation on the interval
[t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t,t_{0}]
for
t<0
.

5.2
Negligibility of the “off-diagonal terms”

In (
5.1
) we met two terms which we call “off-diagonal terms”, because, for example in the interior point method for solving the minimization problem, they correspond to non-zero off-diagonal terms of the Hessian. This is a difficulty we do not have to deal with in the current status model. They are the terms

\displaystyle\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\bigl\{F_{0}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)\bigr\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

(5.8)

and

\displaystyle\int_{v\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\bigl\{F_{0}(u)-\hat{F}_{n}(u)\bigr\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v).

(5.9)

In (
5.8
) the variable
v
roughly varies between
t_{0}
and
M
, since we have
v\geq u
. Likewise, in in (
5.9
) the variable
u
roughly varies between
0
and
t_{0}
.

We now take a closer look at (
5.8
). First of all, we can write

\displaystyle\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\bigl\{F_{0}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)\bigr\}\,d{\mathbb{H}}_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\bigl\{F_{0}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)\bigr\}\,d\bigl({\mathbb{H}}_{n}-H\bigr)(u,v)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\bigl\{F_{0}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)\bigr\}\,dH(u,v).

For the first term on the right we have:

\displaystyle\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\bigl\{F_{0}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)\bigr\}\,d\bigl({\mathbb{H}}_{n}-H\bigr)(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}F_{0}(v)\,d\bigl({\mathbb{H}}_{n}-H\bigr)(u,v)-\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\hat{F}_{n}(v)\,d\bigl({\mathbb{H}}_{n}-H\bigr)(u,v),

where both terms are of order
O_{p}(n^{-5/6})
. This is obvious for the first term, and for the second term if follows from the entropy with bracketing for bounded monotone functions.

So we turn to the term

\displaystyle\int_{u\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{-1/3}t]}\bigl\{F_{0}(v)-\hat{F}_{n}(v)\bigr\}\,dH(u,v).

This term is also expected to be of order
O_{p}(n^{-5/6})
.
The key to this is the fact that integrals of the form

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\bigl\{F_{0}(x)-\hat{F}_{n}(x)\bigr\}\,dx

for
b>a
are of order
O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
. This follows from smooth functional theory, discussed in Section
3
. We cannot work with simple upper bounds for the distance between
\hat{F}_{n}
and
F_{0}
, since these will only give us order
O_{p}(n^{-2/3})
instead of order
O_{p}(n^{-5/6})
.
