Tristan van der Vlugt

Abstract
. Every conditionally convergent series of real numbers has a subseries that diverges. The subseries numbers, previously studied in [ BBH19 ] , answer the question how many subsets of the natural numbers are necessary, such that every conditionally convergent series has a subseries that diverges, with the index set being one of our chosen sets. By restricting our attention to subseries generated by an index set that is both infinite and coinfinite, we may ask the question where the subseries have to be convergent. The answer to this question is a cardinal characteristic of the continuum. We consider several closely related variations to this question, and show that our cardinal characteristics are related to several well-known cardinal characteristics of the continuum. In our investigation, we simultaneously will produce dual results, answering the question how many conditionally convergent series one needs such that no single infinite coinfinite set of indices makes all of the series converge.

1.
Introduction

Given a conditionally convergent series
\sum_{i\in\omega}a_{i}
, we can always find some infinite set
X\subseteq\omega
of indices such that the subseries
\sum_{i\in X}a_{i}
no longer converges. Sparked by a question from Mohammadpour on MathOverflow,
1
1

1

See:
https://mathoverflow.net/questions/221106/
Brendle, Brian and Hamkins
[
BBH19
]
wrote an article answering the question how large a family
\mathcal{X}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}
of infinite sets of natural numbers should be such that for every conditionally convergent series
\sum_{i\in\omega}a_{i}
there exists some
X\in\mathcal{X}
for which
\sum_{i\in X}a_{i}
is no longer convergent. The minimal cardinality of such a family
\mathcal{X}
is called the
subseries number
. It turns out that the subseries number is a cardinal characteristic of the continuum, that is, a cardinality between
\aleph_{1}
and
2^{\aleph_{0}}
whose size is consistently different from either bound, and hence independent from
\mathsf{ZFC}
.
In their article, Brendle, Brian and Hamkins consider some variations of the subseries number depending on the convergence behaviour. For instance, they consider he number of subsets needed such that every conditionally convergent series can be made to diverge by tending to infinity.

A closely related family of cardinal characteristics is the family of
rearrangement numbers
, studied by Blass, Brendle, Brian, Hamkins, Hardy and Larson in
[
BBB+19
]
. The rearrangement number answers a similar question: how many permutations
\pi
of
\omega
are necessary such that for each conditionally convergent series
\sum_{i\in\omega}a_{i}
the permutation of the indices results in a series
\sum_{i\in\omega}a_{\pi(i)}
that no longer converges to the same limit. Note that Riemann’s rearrangement theorem
[
Rie76
]
states that any conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to a different limit or to diverge, which makes this notion well-defined.

One particular variation on this theme, is the rearrangement number
\mathfrak{rr}_{f}
, being the least cardinality of a set
P
of permutations on the natural numbers, such that for every conditionally convergent series
\sum_{i\in\omega}a_{i}
there is a permutation
\pi\in P
for which
\sum_{i\in\omega}a_{\pi(i)}
is still convergent, but has a different limit than the original series. It is noted in the paper on the subseries number that a similar subseries number is not very interesting, as it is easily observed that for every conditionally convergent series
\sum_{i\in\omega}a_{i}
there is some
n\in\omega
such that
\sum_{i\in\omega\setminus\mathopen{}\left\{n\right\}\mathclose{}}a_{i}
converges to a different limit (namely, by letting
n
be any index such that
a_{n}\neq 0
), and thus the countable set
\mathcal{X}=\mathopen{}\left\{\omega\setminus\mathopen{}\left\{n\right\}\mathclose{}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ n\in\omega\right\}\mathclose{}
is a witness for this potential variation on the subseries number.

In our article we will show that there are ways to define a subseries number such that questions related to convergent subseries produce interesting cardinal characteristics. Our deviation from the original article, is that we will not consider all infinite subsets of
\omega
, but only those subsets that are also coinfinite, that is, whose complement is infinite as well.

We will also investigate dual cardinals to the subseries numbers. These dual subseries numbers answer questions such as: how many conditionally convergent series are needed such that no single infinite subset of
\omega
makes all of them divergent? As with the subseries numbers, the dual subseries numbers are usually cardinal characteristics of the continuum. Relational systems and Tukey connections between them allow us to show relations between cardinal characteristics and their duals simultaneously, so we will prove our relations in this framework where possible.

2.1.
Infinite Series

For sequences of real numbers, we use boldface to denote the entire sequence, and lightface with subscript indices to denote individual terms. For example, we write
\boldsymbol{a}=\mathopen{}\left\langle a_{i}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ i\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
for a sequence in
{}^{\omega}\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathfrak{S}\subseteq{}^{\omega}\mathbb{Q}
be the set of all sequences
\boldsymbol{a}\in{}^{\omega}\mathbb{Q}
such that
\boldsymbol{a}
vanishes at infinity, i.e. for each
\varepsilon>0
there is
n\in\omega
such that
|a_{i}|<\varepsilon
for all
i\geq n
.

Given an increasing enumeration
\mathopen{}\left\langle i_{n}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ n\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
of some
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}
, we will define the restriction
\boldsymbol{a}\restriction X=\mathopen{}\left\langle a_{i_{n}}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ n\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
. It is clear that if
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}
, then
\boldsymbol{a}\restriction X\in\mathfrak{S}
as well.
For
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}
,
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}
and
k\in\omega
, we will write the partial sum of all elements in
X
smaller than
k
as:

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{X\cap k}\boldsymbol{a}=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in X\\
i<k\end{subarray}}{\sum}a_{i}.

In order to discuss the convergence of sequences
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}
, we study the accumulation points of the sequence of partial sums
\boldsymbol{x}=\mathopen{}\left\langle\sum_{k}\boldsymbol{a}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ k\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
, where we note that
\boldsymbol{x}\in{}^{\omega}\mathbb{R}
. A real number
c\in\mathbb{R}
is an
accumulation point
of a sequence
\boldsymbol{x}\in{}^{\omega}\mathbb{R}
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there are infinitely many
n\in\omega
with
|x_{n}-c|<\varepsilon
. Moreover, we say
\infty
(respectively
-\infty
) is an
accumulation point
of
\boldsymbol{x}
if for every
k\in\omega
there is
n\in\omega
such that
x_{n}>k
(respectively
-x_{n}>k
). Let
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}\cup\mathopen{}\left\{\infty,-\infty\right\}\mathclose{}
. Given
\boldsymbol{x}\in{}^{\omega}\mathbb{R}
, we define
\mathrm{acc}(\boldsymbol{x})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
to be the set of all accumulation points of
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Given
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}
, if
\mathrm{acc}(\mathopen{}\left\langle\sum_{k}\boldsymbol{a}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ k\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{})=\mathopen{}\left\{c\right\}\mathclose{}
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
, then we say that
c
is the
limit of the series

\sum\boldsymbol{a}
; in other words,
c
is the unique accumulation point of the sequence of partial sums of
\boldsymbol{a}
. In this case, we will write
\sum\boldsymbol{a}=c
for notational convenience and say that
\sum\boldsymbol{a}

tends to a limit
. If, on the other hand,
\mathrm{acc}(\mathopen{}\left\langle\sum_{k}\boldsymbol{a}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ k\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{})
is not a singleton, we say that
the series

\sum\boldsymbol{a}

has no limit
or that
\sum\boldsymbol{a}

diverges by oscillation
, and for notational convenience we write
\sum\boldsymbol{a}=\mathsf{osc}
. Here
\mathsf{osc}
is merely a notational convenience and does not indicate a specific value of the infinite series
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
.

If
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}
and
X\subseteq\omega
, we define the following notation:

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum a\restriction X,

\displaystyle P_{\boldsymbol{a}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{i\in\omega\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ a_{i}>0\right\}\mathclose{},

\displaystyle N_{\boldsymbol{a}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{i\in\omega\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ a_{i}<0\right\}\mathclose{},

\displaystyle Z_{\boldsymbol{a}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{i\in\omega\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ a_{i}=0\right\}\mathclose{}.

We will define several types of convergence and divergence. Let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}
, then
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
…

…

is
convergent
if it tends to a limit in
\mathbb{R}
, and
divergent
otherwise.

…

is
conditional
if
\sum_{P_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\boldsymbol{a}=\infty
and
\sum_{N_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\boldsymbol{a}=-\infty
.

…

is
conditionally convergent
if it is both convergent and conditional.

…

is
absolutely
(or
unconditionally
)
convergent
if it is convergent and not conditional.

…

tends to infinity
if it tends to a limit in
\mathopen{}\left\{\infty,-\infty\right\}\mathclose{}
, that is,
\sum\boldsymbol{a}=\pm\infty
.

…

is
divergent by oscillation
if has no limit, that is,
\sum\boldsymbol{a}=\mathsf{osc}
.

Under our notation, we recall the well-known facts that a series
\boldsymbol{a}
is divergent if and only if
\sum\boldsymbol{a}=\pm\infty
or
\sum\boldsymbol{a}=\mathsf{osc}
, and that
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
is absolutely convergent if and only if
\sum|\boldsymbol{a}|
is convergent, where
|\boldsymbol{a}|=\mathopen{}\left\langle|a_{i}|\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ i\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
denotes the absolute values of
\boldsymbol{a}
.

Each of these forms of convergence or divergence, provides us with a subset of
\mathfrak{S}
.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{c}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \textstyle\sum\boldsymbol{a}\text{ is convergent}\right\}\mathclose{},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{cc}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \textstyle\sum\boldsymbol{a}\text{ is conditionally convergent}\right\}\mathclose{},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{ac}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \textstyle\sum\boldsymbol{a}\text{ is absolutely convergent}\right\}\mathclose{},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \textstyle\sum\boldsymbol{a}\text{ tends to infinity}\right\}\mathclose{},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{o}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \textstyle\sum\boldsymbol{a}\text{ diverges by oscillation}\right\}\mathclose{}.

We will also fix the convention for the above classes that multiple subscripts separated by commas are the union of the individual classes. For example,
\mathfrak{S}_{o,i}=\mathfrak{S}_{o}\cup\mathfrak{S}_{i}
is the set of all
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}
that are divergent.
The following proposition is trivial to check.

Proposition 2.1
.

We have
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}_{c,i,o}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{c}=\mathfrak{S}_{cc,ac}
, and note that all unions are in fact disjoint unions.

2.2.
Relational Systems

In this section we will introduce a very powerful tool that will aid us in providing an order (of cardinality) between certain cardinal characteristics. Most of the cardinal characteristics discussed in this article can be described as the norm of a relational system, and the existence of a morphism between relational systems implies an order between the norms. We refer to
[
Bla10
, § 4]
for a detailed exposition on relational systems.

A
relational system
is a triple
\mathscr{X}=\mathopen{}\left\langle X,Y,R\right\rangle\mathclose{}
consisting of sets
X,Y
and a relation
R\subseteq X\times Y
. We call
X
the set of
challenges
and
Y
the set of
responses
, and we say that a response
y\in Y

meets
a challenge
x\in X
if
(x,y)\in R
.
The
norm

\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}\right\rVert\mathclose{}
of
\mathscr{X}
is defined as the least cardinality of a set
B\subseteq Y
of responses such that any challenge is met by at least one response from
B
. The
dual

\mathscr{X}^{\bot}
of
\mathscr{X}
is defined as the triple
\mathopen{}\left\langle Y,X,R^{\bot}\right\rangle\mathclose{}
, where

\displaystyle R^{\bot}=\mathopen{}\left\{(y,x)\in Y\times X\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ (x,y)\notin R\right\}\mathclose{}.

Note that, with respect to
\mathscr{X}
, we can describe the norm
\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}^{\bot}\right\rVert\mathclose{}
as the least cardinality of a set of challenges
A\subseteq X
such that no single response will meet all of the challenges from
A
.

A
Tukey connection
is a pair of maps between relational systems
\mathscr{X}=\mathopen{}\left\langle X,Y,R\right\rangle\mathclose{}
and
\mathscr{X}^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\left\langle X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},R^{\prime}\right\rangle\mathclose{}
, specifically

\displaystyle\rho_{-}:X\to X^{\prime},

\displaystyle\rho_{+}:Y^{\prime}\to Y,

such that if
(\rho_{-}(x),y^{\prime})\in R^{\prime}
for
x\in X
and
y^{\prime}\in Y^{\prime}
, then
(x,\rho_{+}(y^{\prime}))\in R
. The existence of a Tukey connection is written as
\mathscr{X}\preceq\mathscr{X}^{\prime}
. Two systems
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{X}^{\prime}
are
Tukey equivalent
if both
\mathscr{X}\preceq\mathscr{X}^{\prime}
and
\mathscr{X}^{\prime}\preceq\mathscr{X}
, which we will abbreviate as
\mathscr{X}\equiv\mathscr{X}^{\prime}
. The existence of a Tukey connection provides us with an ordering between the norms of the relational systems.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
\mathscr{X}\preceq\mathscr{X}^{\prime}
, then
\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\leq\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}^{\prime}\right\rVert\mathclose{}
and
\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}^{\prime\bot}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\leq\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}^{\bot}\right\rVert\mathclose{}
.

For our subseries numbers, we define the relation
S_{(\cdot)}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}\times[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
where
(\boldsymbol{a},X)\in S_{(\cdot)}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{a}\restriction X\in\mathfrak{S}_{(\cdot)}
. For example,
(\boldsymbol{a},X)\in S_{i}
if and only if
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
tends to infinity. We define the relational systems

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{(*)}_{(\cdot)}=\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{(*)},[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega},S_{(\cdot)}\right\rangle\mathclose{}\text{ with norm }\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}_{(\cdot)}^{(*)}\right\rVert\mathclose{}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}^{(*)}_{(\cdot)}.

The symbol
s
\mathfrak{z}
used for the cardinality of the norm is the German letter

, used in most of the German dialects as a ligature for a

. Since we will usually have the set
\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
as our set of challenges and never use
\mathfrak{S}
as our set of challenges, we will write

instead of
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}^{cc}_{(\cdot)}

\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}
,

, and
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}
were written as
\text{\ss}_{i}
,
\text{\ss}_{o}
, and

, respectively. Our deviation from previous notation is to use a fraktur font for the cardinal characteristics (as is common), and to separate the subscripts with commas (to avoid ambiguity).

3.
Why the Old Cardinals are Unchanged

Before we investigate the new subseries numbers related to convergence, we will shortly discuss the original subseries numbers from
[
BBH19
]
, and the minor changes that we have made. We argue that these changes are harmless, and do not lose any generality with regard to the results from the original paper.

In our paper we have defined
\mathfrak{S}
to be the set of sequences of rational numbers (i.e.
{}^{\omega}\mathbb{Q}
), instead of the more general sequences of real numbers (i.e.
{}^{\omega}\mathbb{R}
).
We remark that if
\boldsymbol{a}\in{}^{\omega}\mathbb{R}
then there exists
\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}\in{}^{\omega}\mathbb{Q}
and
\boldsymbol{c}\in{}^{\omega}\mathbb{R}
such that
\sum\boldsymbol{c}
is absolutely convergent and
a_{i}=a^{\prime}_{i}+c_{i}
for each
i\in\omega
. Since the relevant convergence behaviours (conditional convergence, tending to infinity and divergence by oscillation) are all invariant under termwise addition with an absolutely convergent series, it follows that
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}
have equivalent convergence behaviour. Therefore, we lose no generality by restricting our attention to sequences of rational terms.

Now let us discuss another difference between our subseries numbers and those from the original paper. The original subseries numbers can be defined as the norms of the following three relational systems:

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}\!\mathscr{S}_{i}^{cc}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{cc},[\omega]^{\omega},S_{i}\right\rangle\mathclose{}

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}\!\mathscr{S}_{o}^{cc}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{cc},[\omega]^{\omega},S_{o}\right\rangle\mathclose{}

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}\!\mathscr{S}_{i,o}^{cc}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{cc},[\omega]^{\omega},S_{i,o}\right\rangle\mathclose{}

That is, the sets of responses consists of infinite sets,
[\omega]^{\omega}
, whereas this paper uses infinite
coinfinite
sets,
[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
, as the sets of responses. It is easy to see that these relational systems are Tukey equivalent to the systems
\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}_{i}^{cc}
,
\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}_{o}^{cc}
and
\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}_{i,o}^{cc}
, respectively.
To see this, note that if
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
is convergent and
X
is a cofinite subsets of
\omega
, then
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
is still convergent. Therefore, the only responses
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}
for which a challenge
\boldsymbol{a}
can be met (that is,
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=\pm\infty
or
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=\mathsf{osc}
, depending which relational system we work with), are those responses that were coinfinite to begin with.

It is exactly this point that makes relational systems of the original type with respect to convergent series, such as
\mathscr{O}\!\mathscr{S}_{c}^{cc}=\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{cc},[\omega]^{\omega},S_{c}\right\rangle\mathclose{}
, rather uninteresting as well: the singleton
\mathopen{}\left\{\omega\right\}\mathclose{}
clearly witnesses for trivial reasons that every conditionally convergent series
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
has a response
X\in\mathopen{}\left\{\omega\right\}\mathclose{}
such that

4.
Convergent Subseries

In this section we will take a look at the norm

such that
n^{\prime}\geq n
and
I_{n^{\prime}}\subseteq J_{k^{\prime}}
. Let
k\in\omega
be such that
x\in J_{k}
. If
I_{n}\subseteq J_{k}
, then we are done, so let us assume that either
i_{n}<j_{k}
or
j_{k+1}<i_{n+1}
.

If
i_{n}<j_{k}
, we show that either
I_{n+1}\subseteq J_{k}
or
I_{n+2}\subseteq J_{k+1}
. Assume that
I_{n+1}\nsubseteq J_{k}
, then

\displaystyle i_{n}<j_{k}\leq x<j_{k+1}\leq i_{n+2}<i_{n+3}=f(x)\leq g(x),

and because
x<j_{k+1}
implies
g(x)<j_{k+2}
, we see that
I_{n+2}\subseteq J_{k+1}
.

Otherwise, if
j_{k+1}<i_{n+1}
, we have

\displaystyle x<j_{k+1}<i_{n+1}<i_{n+3}=f(x)\leq g(x)<j_{k+2},

and thus
I_{n+1}\subseteq J_{k+1}
.
∎

Theorem 4.6

\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}\leq\mathfrak{d}

4.3.
Unconditionally Convergent Subseries

The final of our four subseries numbers related to convergence is the cardinal
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{ac}
. It turns out that this cardinal is equal to the dominating number.

Theorem 4.7
.

\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{ac}=\mathfrak{d}
and dually
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{ac}^{\bot}=\mathfrak{b}
.

Proof.

First, we give a Tukey connection witnessing
\mathscr{S}_{ac}^{cc}\preceq\mathscr{D}
, consisting of
\rho_{-}:\mathfrak{S}_{cc}\to{}^{\omega}\omega
and
\rho_{+}:{}^{\omega}\omega\to[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
.
Given
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
, we let
\rho_{-}(\boldsymbol{a})=g
be any function such that
\mathrm{ran}(g)
is coinfinite and
|a_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}
for all
i\geq g(n)
. For
f\in{}^{\omega}\omega
, we let
\rho_{+}(f)=\mathrm{ran}(f)
if
\mathrm{ran}(f)
is coinfinite, and arbitrary otherwise. Let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
and
f\in{}^{\omega}\omega
, and let
g=\rho_{-}(\boldsymbol{a})
and
X=\rho_{+}(f)
. If
g\leq^{*}f
holds, then since
\mathrm{ran}(g)
is coinfinite, so is
\mathrm{ran}(f)
. Now note that
|a_{f(n)}|\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}
for almost all
n\in\omega
, and thus
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
is unconditionally convergent.

For the other direction we give a Tukey connection witnessing
\mathscr{D}\preceq\mathscr{S}_{ac}^{cc}
, consisting of
\rho_{-}:{}^{\omega}\omega\to\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
and
\rho_{+}:[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}\to{}^{\omega}\omega
.
Given
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
, let
\rho_{+}(X)=g:\omega\to X
be the unique increasing bijection. Given
f\in{}^{\omega}\omega
, we will assume without loss of generality that
f
is strictly increasing and
f(0)=0
. We define
\rho_{-}(f)=\boldsymbol{a}
as follows. For every
i\in\omega
there exists
n\in\omega
such that
i\in[f(n),f(n+1))
, and we define
a_{i}=\frac{(-1)^{i}}{n}
.

Let
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
and
f\in{}^{\omega}\omega
and let
\rho_{+}(X)=g
and
\rho_{-}(f)=\boldsymbol{a}
. To show this satisfies the conditions of a Tukey connection, we argue by contrapositive. Suppose that
f\mathrel{\cancel{\leq^{*}}}g
, then
g\leq^{\infty}f
and thus we may find a sequence of integers
\mathopen{}\left\langle k_{n}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ n\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
such that
g(k_{n})\leq f(k_{n})
and such that
k_{n+1}>2k_{n}
for all
n\in\omega
. Now note that
|a_{g(i)}|>\frac{1}{k_{n+1}}
for all
i\in[k_{n},k_{n+1})
, hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[k_{n},k_{n+1})}|a_{g(i)}|\geq(k_{n+1}-k_{n})\cdot\frac{1}{k_{n+1}}=1-\frac{k_{n}}{k_{n+1}}\geq 1-\frac{k_{n}}{2k_{n}}=\frac{1}{2}

and therefore
\sum_{i\in\omega}|a_{g(i)}|=\infty
. Hence if
\boldsymbol{a}\restriction X
is convergent, then it is conditionally so.
∎

5.
Subseries with Specific Limits

Let us define some additional subsets of
\mathfrak{S}
. Given
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{l}^{A}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \text{there is }r\in A\text{ such that }\textstyle\sum\boldsymbol{a}\text{ tends to }r\right\}\mathclose{}.

Let us additionally define the following relations
S_{l}^{A}
and
S_{e}
on
\mathfrak{S}\times[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
:

\bullet

(\boldsymbol{a},X)\in S_{l}^{A}
if and only if
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
tends to a limit
r\in A
.

\bullet

(\boldsymbol{a},X)\in S_{e}
if and only if
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
and
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
both tend to the same limit.

We may now define the subseries number
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}
as the least size of a family
\mathcal{X}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
such that each conditionally convergent series
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
has a subseries
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
, with
X\in\mathcal{X}
, that converges to a limit in
A
. We define
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}
in a similar way, except that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
must converges to the same limit as
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
does. In terms of relational systems,
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}
and
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}
are the norms of the following systems.

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{cc,A}_{l}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{cc},[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega},S_{l}^{A}\right\rangle\mathclose{},

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{cc}_{e}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{cc},[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega},S_{e}\right\rangle\mathclose{}.

In this section we will first show that the cardinals
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}
and
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}
are related, and that for
|A|<\mathfrak{c}
both cardinals are equal to
\mathfrak{c}=2^{\aleph_{0}}
and the dual cardinals are finite. By introducing an arbitrarily small margin for error to the definition of
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}
and by considering
A
that contain an open interval, we can create more interesting cardinals that turn out to be equal to
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}
, although the dual cardinals remain small and are equal to
\aleph_{0}
.

5.1.
Convergence to Specific Limits

Let us first consider the case of singleton
A=\mathopen{}\left\{r\right\}\mathclose{}
for some
r\in\mathbb{R}
. We will need the following observation to relate
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}
to
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{\mathopen{}\left\{r\right\}\mathclose{}}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
and
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
be such that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
is convergent, and let and
r,r_{X}\in\mathbb{R}
be the limits
\sum\boldsymbol{a}=r
and
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=r_{X}
. Then
\sum_{\omega\setminus X}\boldsymbol{a}=r-r_{X}
.

Proof.

This is easy by considering the decomposition
\sum_{k}\boldsymbol{a}=\sum_{k\cap X}\boldsymbol{a}+\sum_{k\cap(\omega\setminus X)}\boldsymbol{a}
of the partial sums for all
k\in\omega
.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{\mathopen{}\left\{0\right\}\mathclose{}}
and
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\bot}={\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{\mathopen{}\left\{0\right\}\mathclose{}\bot}}
.

Proof.

We will give a Tukey connection
\mathscr{S}_{e}^{cc}\preceq\mathscr{S}_{l}^{cc,\mathopen{}\left\{0\right\}\mathclose{}}
consisting of
\rho_{-}:\mathfrak{S}_{cc}\to\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
being the identity function and
\rho_{+}:[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}\to[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
mapping
X\mapsto\omega\setminus X
.
Lemma

5.1
shows us that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=\sum\boldsymbol{a}
if and only if
\sum_{\omega\setminus X}\boldsymbol{a}=0
.

Note that the same pair
(\rho_{-},\rho_{+})
is also a Tukey connection for
\mathscr{S}_{l}^{cc,\mathopen{}\left\{0\right\}\mathclose{}}\preceq\mathscr{S}_{e}^{cc}
.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}=\mathfrak{c}
for all
A\in[\mathbb{R}]^{<\mathfrak{c}}
and
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\bot}={\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{B\bot}}=2
for all
B\in[\mathbb{R}]^{<\omega}
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
be arbitrary and for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
let
\boldsymbol{a}^{t}
be given by
a^{t}_{i}=a_{i}+\frac{t}{i^{2}}
It is clear that
\boldsymbol{a}^{t}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
, since
\sum_{i\in\omega}\frac{t}{i^{2}}
is absolutely convergent, and the termwise sum of a conditionally convergent series with an absolutely convergent series is conditionally convergent.

Now suppose that
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
is such that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{t}=r
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
t\neq s
. Then
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{s}\neq\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{t}
. Therefore
\mathopen{}\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \exists X\in\mathcal{X}\mathopen{}\left(\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{t}\in A\right)\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}
has cardinality
|\mathcal{X}|\cdot|A|
, and thus it follows that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}=\mathfrak{c}
when
|A|<\mathfrak{c}
. By
Corollary

5.2
we also have
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{\mathopen{}\left\{0\right\}\mathclose{}}=\mathfrak{c}
.

For the dual cardinal
{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{B\bot}}
, let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
|x-y|\geq\frac{\varepsilon\cdot\pi^{2}}{6}
for all
x,y\in B
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
and let
\boldsymbol{b}
have
b_{i}=a_{i}+\frac{\varepsilon}{i^{2}}
, then
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{b}-\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}<\frac{\varepsilon\cdot\pi^{2}}{6}
for any
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
. Hence,
{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{B\bot}}=2
for every finite
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and, by
Corollary

5.2
,
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\bot}={\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{\mathopen{}\left\{0\right\}\mathclose{}\bot}}
.
∎

We note that the above proof shows that
{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{B\bot}}=2
even for countable
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
for which
\inf\mathopen{}\left\{|x-y|\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ x,y\in B\land x\neq y\right\}\mathclose{}>0
holds.

5.2.
Introducing Error Margins

Let
\varepsilon>0
, then we define
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\varepsilon}
as the norm of the system
\mathscr{S}_{e}^{cc,\varepsilon}=\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{cc},[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega},S_{e}^{\varepsilon}\right\rangle\mathclose{}
where
(\boldsymbol{a},X)\in S_{e}^{\varepsilon}
if and only if
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
is convergent with a limit in the open interval of length
2\varepsilon
centred around the limit of
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, then
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\varepsilon}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}
and
{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\varepsilon\bot}}={\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\bot}}
.

Proof.

We will show that
\mathscr{S}_{e}^{cc,\varepsilon}\equiv\mathscr{S}_{l}^{cc,(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}
. Consider the Tukey connection consisting of
\rho_{-}:\mathfrak{S}_{cc}\to\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
being the identity function and
\rho_{+}:[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}\to[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
mapping
X\mapsto\omega\setminus X
.
Suppose
(\boldsymbol{a},X)\in S_{e}^{\varepsilon}
, then there is
\delta\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
such that
\delta+\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=\sum\boldsymbol{a}
. By
Lemma

5.1
we have then that
\sum_{\omega\setminus X}\boldsymbol{a}=-\delta
, and thus
(\boldsymbol{a},\omega\setminus X)\in S_{l}^{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}
. This shows
\mathscr{S}_{e}^{cc,\varepsilon}\preceq\mathscr{S}_{l}^{cc,(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}
, but note that the same pair
(\rho_{-},\rho_{+})
is also a Tukey connection for
\mathscr{S}_{l}^{cc,(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\preceq\mathscr{S}_{e}^{cc,\varepsilon}
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be bounded open intervals, then
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{B}
.

Proof.

We will show that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{B}
holds in the following two cases:

\bullet

(Scaling) If
A=(-1,1)
and
B=(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon>0
.

\bullet

(Translation) If
A=(c,c^{\prime})
and
B=(b+c,b+c^{\prime})
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
.

This suffices to prove the lemma, as scaling and translation suffice to transform any bounded open interval into any other bounded open interval.

For scaling, we prove
\mathscr{S}_{l}^{cc,(-1,1)}\equiv\mathscr{S}_{l}^{cc,(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}
. Let
\rho_{-}:\mathfrak{S}_{cc}\to\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
map
\boldsymbol{a}=\mathopen{}\left\langle a_{i}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ i\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
to
\boldsymbol{a}^{\varepsilon}=\mathopen{}\left\langle\varepsilon\cdot a_{i}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ i\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
and let
\rho_{+}:[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}\to[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
be the identity. It is clear that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\cdot\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
, and thus
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{\varepsilon}\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
if and only if
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}\in(-1,1)
.

For translation, let
\mathcal{X}
be a witness for
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{(c,c^{\prime})}
and assume without loss of generality that
x\mathbin{\triangle}X\in\mathcal{X}
for every
x\in[\omega]^{<\omega}
and
X\in\mathcal{X}
. Let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
and find
X\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}\in(c,c^{\prime})
. Say that
r=\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
is the limit value, then we may find some
\varepsilon>0
such that
(r-\varepsilon,r+\varepsilon)\subseteq(c,c^{\prime})
.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}
be defined by
a_{i}^{\prime}=a_{i}
if
i\notin X
and
a_{i}^{\prime}=-a_{i}
otherwise. Since we only change the signs of terms in
X
and
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
is convergent, it follows that
\sum_{P_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}}\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}=\infty
and
\sum_{N_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}}\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}=-\infty
. Therefore we can find some finite
x\in[\omega]^{<\omega}
such that
\sum_{x}\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}\in(b-\varepsilon,b+\varepsilon)
. Note that
\sum_{x\triangle X}\boldsymbol{a}=(\sum_{x}\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime})+(\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a})
, because for all
i\in x\triangle X
we have
a^{\prime}_{i}=a_{i}
and for all
i\in x\cap X
we have
a^{\prime}_{i}=-a_{i}
, and thus the latter terms cancel out by
x
being finite. It follows that

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{x\triangle X}\boldsymbol{a}\in(b+r-\varepsilon,b+r+\varepsilon)\subseteq(b+c,b+c^{\prime}).

Therefore, since by assumption
x\mathbin{\triangle}X\in\mathcal{X}
, we see that
\mathcal{X}
is also a witness for
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{B}
.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\varepsilon}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}
for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
A
contains a nonempty open interval.

Proof.

Firstly,
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\varepsilon}
is obvious by
Fact

2.3
.

Let first take
A=(-1,1)
and show that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}
. Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
witness
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}
, and assume that
X\setminus k\in\mathcal{X}
for all
k\in\omega
. Let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
and
X\in\mathcal{X}
be such that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=r
is convergent. Then there is some
k\in\omega
such that
\sum_{X\cap k}\boldsymbol{a}\in(r-1,r+1)
and such that the partial sum
|\sum_{n\cap X\setminus k}\boldsymbol{a}|<1
for all
n\geq k
. It then follows that
\sum_{X\setminus k}\boldsymbol{a}\in(-1,1)
. Hence
\mathcal{X}
is a witness for
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{(-1,1)}
as well.

Unfixing
A
, the previous two Lemmas imply that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\varepsilon}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}
for all
\varepsilon>0
and bounded open intervals
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, it is obvious that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{\mathbb{R}}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}
by definition. If
B\subseteq A
, then we easily see that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{B}
. Hence, if
B
is a (bounded) nonempty open interval, then
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{\mathbb{R}}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{B}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}
.
∎

Interestingly we have no duality:

Theorem 5.7
.

{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\varepsilon\bot}}=\aleph_{0}
for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

For convenience we will take
\varepsilon=1
. We already know that
{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\varepsilon\bot}}={\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{(-1,1)\bot}}
from
Lemma

5.4
. We show that the latter is equal to
\aleph_{0}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}=\mathopen{}\left\langle a_{i}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ i\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
and define
\boldsymbol{a}^{n}
by
a_{i}^{n}=a_{i}+\frac{n}{i^{2}}
. Suppose that
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
is such that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=r\in(-1,1)
and let
s=\sum_{X}\frac{1}{i^{2}}
, which as a subseries of an absolutely convergent series is itself absolutely convergent and clearly nonzero. Hence
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{n}=r+n\cdot s
. Therefore we can choose
n
large enough such that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{n}\notin(-1,1)
. This shows that
{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{(-1,1)\bot}}\leq\aleph_{0}
.

In the other direction, suppose
\boldsymbol{a}^{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{a}^{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
, then we find some
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
such that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{i}
converges for every
0\leq i\leq k
(using that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}^{\bot}
is infinite). There must be some
n_{i}
such that
\sum_{X\setminus n}\boldsymbol{a}^{i}\in(-1,1)
for all
n\geq n_{i}
. Let
N=\max\mathopen{}\left\{n_{i}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ 0\leq i\leq k\right\}\mathclose{}
, then
\sum_{X\setminus N}\boldsymbol{a}^{i}\in(-1,1)
for all
0\leq i\leq k
.
∎

We remark that the first part of the above proof also shows that
{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{A\bot}}\leq\aleph_{0}
for any
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
that is not dense: for each
q\in\mathbb{Q}
define
\boldsymbol{a}^{q}
by
a_{i}^{q}=a_{i}+\frac{q}{i^{2}}
, then
\mathopen{}\left\{\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}^{q}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ q\in\mathbb{Q}\right\}\mathclose{}
is dense for any
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
.

6.
Conditional and Unconditional Series

Remember that we call
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}

conditional
if both
\sum_{P_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\boldsymbol{a}
and
\sum_{N_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\boldsymbol{a}
tend to infinity. Every series that diverges by oscillation is easily seen to be conditional, but not every convergent series is, as convergent series could be absolutely convergent. A similar thing can be said for series that tend to infinity. Let us say that
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
tends conditionally to infinity if
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
tends to infinity and
\boldsymbol{a}
is conditional, and otherwise we will say that
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
tends unconditionally to infinity. We introduce the following subsets
\mathfrak{S}
.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \textstyle\sum\boldsymbol{a}\text{ is conditional}\right\}\mathclose{},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{ci}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \textstyle\sum\boldsymbol{a}\text{ tends conditionally to infinity}\right\}\mathclose{},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{ui}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \textstyle\sum\boldsymbol{a}\text{ tends unconditionally to infinity}\right\}\mathclose{}.

Under our naming convention this provides us with new cardinal characteristics, that we will discuss in the next subsections.

6.1.
Subseries that are Conditional

Let us consider
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{n}
, which is the least cardinality of a set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
such that for each conditionally convergent
\boldsymbol{a}
there is some
X\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
is still a conditional series. In other words,
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{n}
is the norm of
\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{cc}_{n}
.

For a rather pathological reason we can see that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{n}
is finite.

Lemma 6.1
.

\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{n}=3

Proof.

Firstly
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{n}>2
. Namely, if we have
\mathcal{X}=\mathopen{}\left\{X_{0},X_{1}\right\}\mathclose{}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
, then either:

\bullet

Z=\omega\setminus(X_{0}\cup X_{1})
is infinite, in which case we define
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
such that
P_{\boldsymbol{a}}\subseteq Z
, then clearly
\boldsymbol{a}\restriction X_{i}
contains no positive terms for either
i
, or

\bullet

Z_{0}=X_{0}\setminus X_{1}
and
Z_{1}=X_{1}\setminus X_{0}
are both infinite, in which case we define
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
such that
P_{\boldsymbol{a}}\subseteq Z_{0}
and
N_{\boldsymbol{a}}\subseteq Z_{1}
, then clearly
\boldsymbol{a}\restriction X_{0}
contains no negative terms and
\boldsymbol{a}\restriction X_{1}
contains no positive terms.

Next we show that the following three sets suffice: let
X_{i}=\omega\setminus\mathopen{}\left\{3k+i\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ k\in\omega\right\}\mathclose{}
for each
i\in 3
. Let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, then we consider for each
i\in 3
:

\displaystyle P_{i}=P_{\boldsymbol{a}}\cap\mathopen{}\left\{3k+i\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ k\in\omega\right\}\mathclose{},

\displaystyle N_{i}=N_{\boldsymbol{a}}\cap\mathopen{}\left\{3k+i\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ k\in\omega\right\}\mathclose{}.

Now we can choose
i_{P},i_{N}\in 3
such that
\sum_{P_{i_{P}}}\boldsymbol{a}=\infty
and
\sum_{N_{i_{N}}}\boldsymbol{a}=-\infty
. But note that if
j\in 3\setminus\mathopen{}\left\{i_{P},i_{N}\right\}\mathclose{}
, then
P_{i_{P}},N_{i_{N}}\subseteq X_{j}
, hence
\sum_{X_{j}}\boldsymbol{a}
is conditional.
∎

6.2.
Unconditional Series as the Set of Challenges

So far, the subseries numbers have been defined as norms of relational systems
\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{cc},[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega},R\right\rangle\mathclose{}
for some relation
R
. In particular, our set of challenges has always been
\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
. In this section we briefly consider what happens if we allow for unconditional challenges, specifically the sets
\mathfrak{S}_{ac}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{ui}
.

First, let us remark that if
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{ac,ui}
, that is, if
\sum\boldsymbol{a}
is not a conditional series, then
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
is not conditional for any
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
either. Moreover, subseries of absolutely convergent series are absolutely convergent, thus if
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{ac}
, then
\boldsymbol{a}\restriction X\in\mathfrak{S}_{ac}
as well. This makes
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{ac}^{ac}=1
and
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{ac}^{ac\bot}
undefined.

It is, however possible for a divergent unconditional series (which necessarily tends to infinity) to have a convergent subseries. The subseries number
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}^{ui}
is the least cardinality of a subset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
such that every series that tends unconditionally to an infinite value has a subseries given by some
X\in\mathcal{X}
that is convergent. Note that the previous paragraph implies
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}^{ui}_{c}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}^{ui}_{ac}
. It turns out that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}^{ui}
is another characterisation of the dominating number. We omit the proof, as it is almost entirely similar to the proof of
Theorem

4.7
.

Theorem 6.2
.

\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}^{ui}=\mathfrak{d}
and
{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}^{ui\bot}}=\mathfrak{b}

7.
Bonus: Dualising the Original Subseries Numbers

In the original subseries paper
[
BBH19
]
, several relations between the cardinal characteristics
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}
,
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}
,
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}
and various other cardinal characteristics are shown. Although the article does not use the structure of relational systems that we have used in our article, it is not hard to see that almost every proof from the original paper can be written in the form of a Tukey connection. Specifically, the proofs that
\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{N})\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}
, that
\mathfrak{s}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}
, that
\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{M})\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}
and that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}\leq\mathrm{non}(\mathcal{M})
all can be written using a Tukey connection. Even the theorems connecting the subseries numbers with the rearrangement numbers by
\mathfrak{rr}\leq\max\mathopen{}\left\{\mathfrak{b},\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}\right\}\mathclose{}
and
\mathfrak{rr}_{i}\leq\max\mathopen{}\left\{\mathfrak{d},\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}\right\}\mathclose{}
, can be readily translated into a Tukey connection using a sequential composition (see below).

We therefore observe the following facts about the dual subseries numbers:

\displaystyle\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}^{\bot}\leq\mathrm{non}(\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}^{\bot}\leq\mathfrak{r}

\displaystyle\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}^{\bot}\leq\mathrm{non}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{M})\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}^{\bot}

\displaystyle\min\mathopen{}\left\{\mathfrak{d},\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}^{\bot}\right\}\mathclose{}\leq\mathfrak{rr}

\displaystyle\min\mathopen{}\left\{\mathfrak{b},\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}^{\bot}\right\}\mathclose{}\leq\mathfrak{rr}_{i}^{\bot}

Some of these statements are made trivial by the fact that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}^{\bot}=4
, as was shown in
[
Bri18
]
. Although it is not known whether
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}<\mathfrak{c}
is consistent, the fact that the dual cardinal
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}^{\bot}
is finite is a strong indicator that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i}=\mathfrak{c}
might be provable in
\mathsf{ZFC}
.

It is unknown whether
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}
is provable, nor do we know this about their duals. Nevertheless,
[
BBH19
, § 8]
shows a weaker claim, that
\mathfrak{b}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}
implies
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}
, or succinctly that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}\leq\max\mathopen{}\left\{\mathfrak{b},\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}\right\}\mathclose{}
. The original proof does not yield a Tukey connection, because the cases of divergence by oscillation and tending to infinity are treated separately.

In this section, we will give a variation of the proof that allows us to state it in terms of Tukey connections, and thus provides a dual result. In order to do this, we must also consider the splitting number
\mathfrak{s}
, but this will have no effect on the result. We need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
and
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
. If
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
diverges, there is a positive real number
c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for which there are infinitely many disjoint intervals
I_{i}\subseteq\omega
such that one of the following is true:

\bullet

\sum_{n\in I_{i}\setminus X}a_{n}<-c
and
c<\sum_{n\in I_{i}\cap X}a_{n}
for all
i\in\omega
, or

\bullet

\sum_{n\in I_{i}\cap X}a_{n}<-c
and
c<\sum_{n\in I_{i}\setminus X}a_{n}
for all
i\in\omega
.

Proof.

We know that
\sum_{n\in\omega}a_{n}
converges, thus for any
\varepsilon>0
we could find some
m^{\prime}\in\omega
for which
\sum_{m^{\prime}\leq n\leq m}a_{n}\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
for every
m>m^{\prime}
.

If
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
diverges by oscillation, find
d\in\mathbb{R}
and
c\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
such that
\sum_{X\cap m}\boldsymbol{a}<d-c
for infinitely many
m\in\omega
and
\sum_{X\cap m}\boldsymbol{a}>d+c
for infinitely many
m\in\omega
. Fix
\varepsilon<c
and
m^{\prime}\in\omega
as above, and also fix
m_{i},k_{i}\in\omega
for each
i\in\omega
such that for all
i\in\omega
:

\bullet

m^{\prime}<m_{0}
,

\bullet

m_{i}<k_{i}<m_{i+1}
,

\bullet

\sum_{X\cap m_{i}}\boldsymbol{a}<d-c
, and

\bullet

\sum_{X\cap k_{i}}\boldsymbol{a}>d+c
.

It follows from this that
\sum_{X\cap[m_{i},k_{i})}\boldsymbol{a}>2c
and
\sum_{[m_{i},k_{i})}\boldsymbol{a}<\varepsilon<c
(since
m_{i}>m^{\prime}
), and thus
\sum_{[m_{i},k_{i})\setminus X}\boldsymbol{a}<-c
. We can therefore take
I_{i}=[m_{i},k_{i})
for each
i\in\omega
to see that the first bullet point from the lemma holds.

If
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=\infty
, then we could take any
c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and fix some
\varepsilon<c
and
m^{\prime}
as above. We could fix
m_{i}
for all
i\in\omega
such that:

\bullet

m^{\prime}<m_{0}
,

\bullet

m_{i}<m_{i+1}
,

\bullet

\sum_{X\cap[m_{i},m_{i+1})}\boldsymbol{a}>2c
.

For the same reasons as before, we see that
\sum_{[m_{i},m_{i+1})\setminus X}\boldsymbol{a}<c
. Therefore we take
I_{i}=[m_{i},m_{i+1})
for each
i\in\omega
to see that the first bullet point from the lemma holds.

Finally if
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=-\infty
, we have the same argument as above (with
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}=\infty
), except we now let
\sum_{n\in X\cap[m_{i},m_{i+1})}a_{n}<-2c
and get
\sum_{n\in[m_{i},m_{i+1})\setminus X}a_{n}>c
, meaning that the second bullet point from the lemma holds instead.
∎

To show the theorem, we will use a Tukey connection between the relational system
\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{cc}_{i,o}
and a composition of the three systems
\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{cc}_{o}
,
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{S}
. For this we define the
sequential composition
of two relational systems
\mathscr{X}=\mathopen{}\left\langle X,Y,R\right\rangle\mathclose{}
and
\mathscr{X}^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\left\langle X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},R^{\prime}\right\rangle\mathclose{}
as the relational system
\mathscr{X}^{\frown}\mathscr{X}^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\left\langle X\times{}^{Y}X^{\prime},Y\times Y^{\prime},K\right\rangle\mathclose{}
where
((x,f),(y,y^{\prime}))\in K
if and only if
(x,y)\in R
and
(f(y),y^{\prime})\in R^{\prime}
. If
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{X}^{\prime}
have infinite norms, then
\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}{}^{\frown}\mathscr{X}^{\prime}\right\rVert\mathclose{}=\max\mathopen{}\left\{\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}\right\rVert\mathclose{},\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}^{\prime}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}
and
\mathopen{}\left\lVert(\mathscr{X}{}^{\frown}\mathscr{X}^{\prime})^{\bot}\right\rVert\mathclose{}=\min\mathopen{}\left\{\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}^{\bot}\right\rVert\mathclose{},\mathopen{}\left\lVert\mathscr{X}^{\prime}{}^{\bot}\right\rVert\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}
, see also
[
Bla10
, Thm 4.11]
.

Theorem 7.2
.

\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}\leq\mathrm{max}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathfrak{b},\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}\right\}\mathclose{}
and
\mathrm{min}\mathopen{}\left\{\mathfrak{d},\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}^{\bot}\right\}\mathclose{}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}^{\bot}
.

Proof.

Remember the systems
\mathscr{S}=\mathopen{}\left\langle[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega},[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega},\text{``}{\text{is split by}}\text{''}\right\rangle\mathclose{}
and
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathrm{IP},\mathrm{IP},\sqsubseteq^{\infty}\right\rangle\mathclose{}
, that we introduced in
Section

4.2
. We will give a Tukey connection
\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{cc}_{o}\preceq(\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{cc}_{i,o}{}^{\frown}\mathscr{B}^{\prime}){}^{\frown}\mathscr{S}

(\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{cc}_{io}{}^{\frown}\mathscr{B}^{\prime}){}^{\frown}\mathscr{S}

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\left\langle\mathfrak{S}_{cc}\times{}^{[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}}\mathrm{IP}\times{}^{[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}\times\mathrm{IP}}[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega},\quad[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}\times\mathrm{IP}\times[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega},\quad Z\right\rangle\mathclose{}

Here
Z
is the defined for
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathfrak{S}_{cc}
, functions
f:{[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}}\to{\mathrm{IP}}
and
g:{[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}}\times{\mathrm{IP}}\to{[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}}
, interval partition
\mathcal{I}=\mathopen{}\left\langle I_{k}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ k\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
and sets
X,S\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
, by
((\boldsymbol{a},f,g),(X,\mathcal{I},S))\in Z
if and only if all of the following hold:

\bullet

\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
diverges,

\bullet

for interval partition
f(X)=\mathopen{}\left\langle J_{n}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ n\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
, there are infinitely many
k\in\omega
such that
J_{n}\subseteq I_{k}
for some
n\in\omega
, and

\bullet

g(X,\mathcal{I})
is split by
S
.

The Tukey connection is witnessed by functions
\rho_{-}:\mathfrak{S}_{cc}\to\mathfrak{S}_{cc}\times{}^{[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}}\mathrm{IP}\times{}^{[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}\times\mathrm{IP}}[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}

Of these two functions
\rho_{+}

and

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(X,\mathcal{I},S)=\mathopen{}\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{k\in S}I_{k}\cap X\right)\mathclose{}\cup\mathopen{}\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{k\notin S}I_{k}\setminus X\right)\mathclose{}.

To define
\rho_{-}
, we let
\rho_{-}(\boldsymbol{a})=(\boldsymbol{a},f,g)
, where
f
and
g
are defined as below.

Let
X\in[\omega]^{\omega}_{\omega}
and
\mathcal{I}=\mathopen{}\left\langle I_{k}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ k\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}\in\mathrm{IP}
. If
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
converges, we take any arbitrary value for
f(X)
and
g(X,\mathcal{I})
, as this case will be irrelevant. Therefore, assume that
\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
diverges. By
Lemma

7.1
we can find a family of disjoint intervals
\mathopen{}\left\{K_{n}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ n\in\omega\right\}\mathclose{}
and a positive real number
c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
\sum_{K_{n}\setminus X}\boldsymbol{a}<-c
and
c<\sum_{K_{n}\cap X}\boldsymbol{a}
for all
n\in\omega
or such that
\sum_{K_{n}\cap X}\boldsymbol{a}<-c
and
c<\sum_{K_{n}\setminus X}\boldsymbol{a}
for all
n\in\omega
. We will assume without loss of generality that the first is the case and we will also assume that
\mathrm{min}(K_{n})<\mathrm{min}(K_{n+1})
for all
n\in\omega

f(X)=\mathopen{}\left\langle J_{n}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ n\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}
where
J_{n}=[\min(K_{n}),\min(K_{n+1}))
.

In case there are only finitely many
k\in\omega
such that
J_{n}\subseteq I_{k}
for some
n\in\omega

g(A,B)
be arbitrary, as this case will once again be irrelevant. Otherwise we will define

Now we show that this is indeed a Tukey connection.
Let
C=\rho_{+}(X,\mathcal{I},S)
. Suppose that
((\boldsymbol{a},f,g),(X,\mathcal{I},S))\in Z

\sum_{X}\boldsymbol{a}
diverges, thus
f(X)=\mathopen{}\left\langle J_{n}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ n\in\omega\right\rangle\mathclose{}

such that

for some
n\in\omega

\sum_{K_{n}\cap C}\boldsymbol{a}>c

\sum_{C}\boldsymbol{a}
diverges by oscillation. Therefore, our Tukey connection has shown that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}\leq\mathopen{}\left\lVert(\mathscr{S}\!\!\mathscr{S}^{cc}_{i,o}{}^{\frown}{\mathscr{B}}^{\prime}){}^{\frown}\mathscr{S}\right\rVert\mathclose{}=\mathrm{max}\mathopen{}\left\{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o},\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{s}\right\}\mathclose{}=\mathrm{max}\mathopen{}\left\{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o},\mathfrak{b}\right\}\mathclose{}

. Dually we get
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}^{\bot}\geq\mathrm{min}\mathopen{}\left\{\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}^{\bot},\mathfrak{d}\right\}\mathclose{}
.
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

If
\mathfrak{b}\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}
, then
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}
. Dually, if
\mathfrak{d}\geq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}^{\bot}
, then
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{i,o}^{\bot}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.02228pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{o}^{\bot}
.

We note that because both
\mathfrak{s}
and
\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{M})
are lower bounds for
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}
, that it is consistent that
\mathfrak{s}<\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}
or that
\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{M})<\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}
. The first holds in the Cohen model, whereas the second holds in the Mathias model (see e.g.
[
Bla10
, § 11]
for definitions of these models). Several open questions with regard to consistency remain.

Firstly, many of our subseries numbers were equal to
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}
, specifically
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{f}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{e}^{\varepsilon}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{l}^{(0,1)}
. Simultaneously, many other subseries numbers were shown to be equal to
\mathfrak{d}
, specifically
\mathfrak{d}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{ac}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}^{ui}=\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{f}^{ac}
.

Since
\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{M})\leq\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}\leq\mathfrak{d}
and consistently
\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{M})<\mathfrak{d}
(e.g. in the Miller model), a natural question is:

Question 8.1
.

Which of
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}<\mathfrak{d}
and
\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{M})<\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}
is consistent?

Natural forcing notions to consider, are the Miller, Mathias and Laver forcing, all of which increase
\mathfrak{d}
without increasing
\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{M})
. Another candidate is random forcing, which can be used to force
\mathrm{non}(\mathcal{N})<\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{N})
without affecting the cardinality of
\mathfrak{d}
from the ground model.

The situation for
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}
is similar. If
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}<\mathfrak{d}
is consistent, then the Miller model would be an excellent candidate to check, since this model also has
\mathfrak{s}=\aleph_{1}
. On the other hand, if
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}=\mathfrak{d}
holds in the Miller model, this could indicate that
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}=\mathfrak{d}
is provable in
\mathsf{ZFC}
.

Question 8.2
.

Is
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}<\mathfrak{d}
consistent? Is
\max\mathopen{}\left\{\mathfrak{s},\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{M})\right\}\mathclose{}<\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}
consistent?

Naturally the questions above could be asked for the dual cardinal characteristics
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{cc}^{\bot}
,
\text{{\swabfamily s}\kern-1.00006pt$\mathfrak{z}$}_{c}^{\bot}
and their relationship to
\mathrm{non}(\mathcal{M})
and
\mathfrak{b}
.
