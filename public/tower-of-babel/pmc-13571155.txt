Single-cell multi-omic profiling of heterogeneous PROCR+ cells in mouse mammary gland and its implication for breast cancer
Authors: Koon-Kiu Yan, Erin Nekritz, Zhen Xie, Bensheng Ju, Xinran Dong, Rachel Werner, Dayanira Alsina-Beauchamp, Celeste Rosencrance, Partha Mukhopadhyay, Qingfei Pan, Andrej Gorbatenko, Honglei Zhou, Lei Yan, Liang Ding, Chenxi Qian, Sivaraman Natarajan, Hongbo Chi, John Easton, Jose Silva, Jiyang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryProtein C receptor (PROCR) marks a rare population of mammary epithelial cells with stem-like properties, but their low abundance has hindered characterization of their functional diversity and regulatory programs. Here, we integrate targeted enrichment with single-cell transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility profiling to construct a multimodal atlas of PROCR+ cells across development. We identify heterogeneous PROCR+ subpopulations, including a Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ population with features consistent with a mammary stem cell (MaSC) state, such as high developmental potency, an EMT-associated program, and an early differentiation position. Integrative analyses reveal dynamic transcriptional regulatory circuits underlying stemness and lineage commitment. Cross-species comparisons uncover analogous PROCR+ populations in human mammary epithelium, and this stem cell-like signature is enriched in triple-negative and claudin-low tumors. Together, these findings refine the mammary epithelial hierarchy, define regulatory programs underlying stemness and lineage bifurcation, and establish a molecular link between a distinct MaSC state and tumor aggressiveness.

Introduction

The mammary gland is a dynamic organ that undergoes extensive tissue remodeling during the female lifetime. The small, rudimentary epithelial placode formed during embryogenesis expands during puberty to generate a branched ductal network. In adulthood, the mammary epithelium undergoes cyclic expansion and regression during the ovarian cycle, while pregnancy triggers a massive epithelial proliferation and differentiation program that produces specialized secretory alveolar cells responsible for milk production.1,2,3 In all these stages, epithelial enlargement is supported by a population of adult mammary epithelial stem cells (MaSCs) residing at the apex of the hierarchy.4,5 MaSC homeostasis needs to be tightly regulated in time and space to ensure proper organ function and avoid pathological consequences. Thus, dysregulation of stem cell activity has been linked to tumor initiation, and aggressive breast cancers are frequently enriched in cells exhibiting stem cell-like properties.6,7

Although MaSCs are fundamental to mammary gland development, their precise identity and defining characteristics remain poorly understood. During embryogenesis, MaSCs exhibit multipotency, giving rise to both luminal and basal epithelial lineages.8 In contrast, whether adult MaSCs retain bipotency or become lineage restricted (unipotent) remains controversial.9,10,11,12 Furthermore, increasing evidence suggests that multiple MaSC populations may exist, each activated in a context-dependent manner in response to distinct physiological cues and regenerative demands.4,13,14,15 A major challenge has been the absence of reliable markers that uniquely and reproducibly distinguish MaSCs from more differentiated progeny. Notably, a purified population of mammary epithelial cells (MECs) expressing protein C receptor (PROCR+) has been shown to regenerate the mammary gland in cleared fat pads with unprecedented efficiency.9 Lineage-tracing analyses further demonstrated that PROCR+ mammary epithelial cells are enriched for bipotent MaSC activity.9

While fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)- and lineage-tracing-based approaches have been instrumental in defining MaSC populations, they inherently rely on predetermined markers and promoters, introducing potential bias. In contrast, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides an unbiased, genome-wide approach that does not depend on predefined surface markers or lineage assumptions. Consistent with marker-based studies, PROCR+ cells have been identified in scRNA-seq analyses of the normal mouse mammary gland.16,17

Although previous scRNA-seq studies greatly advanced our understanding of the heterogeneity within the mammary epithelium,16,17,18 they remain limited in their ability to resolve mammary stem cell populations. Because MaSCs are rare compared to more differentiated epithelial populations, these datasets typically included only a few Procr+ cells, precluding in-depth molecular characterization.16,17,18 The restricted cluster size and depth of analysis have therefore limited our capacity to define the molecular fingerprint and regulatory networks underlying PROCR+ MaSC identity and function.

To overcome these limitations and obtain a comprehensive molecular portrait of the PROCR+ MaSC population, we FACS-purified PROCR+ mammary epithelial cells from mice at distinct developmental stages and performed both scRNA-seq and single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (scATAC-seq). This strategy enabled the capture of a large number of PROCR+ cells, revealing transcriptional and epigenetic heterogeneity within the population. We identified distinct PROCR+ subpopulations characterized by unique stem cell signatures, lineage biases, and regulatory networks. Additionally, by integrating scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data, we reconstructed the transcriptional regulatory network (TRN) of mammary development and identified candidate master regulators (MRs) controlling lineage specification within the PROCR+ compartment. Finally, motivated by the connection of the roles of PROCR in normal mammary gland development and breast cancer,19 we further investigated PROCR+ subpopulations in normal human breast tissue and breast cancer patients’ cohort. These analyses revealed analogous PROCR+ cell populations in human tissues and confirmed that PROCR+ transcriptional signatures are enriched in aggressive triple-negative breast cancers (TNBCs).

Collectively, our study provides a comprehensive molecular and regulatory landscape of mammary epithelial stem cells, uncovering previously unrecognized heterogeneity within the PROCR+ compartment and identifying transcriptional regulators that guide the transition from multipotency to lineage commitment.

Results

Single-cell profiling of PROCR+ cells reveals high heterogeneity

scRNA-seq is a powerful method to identify cell subtypes and track the trajectories of cell lineages.20,21 We performed scRNA-seq on mammary epithelial cells isolated at different stages of mouse development using inbred BALB/cJ female mice: puberty (young, 5–6 weeks), nulliparous adult (adult, 3–4 months), and multiparous old (old, >22 months) (Figure 1A). Because of the well-known impact of the ovarian hormones,2,22,23 we also separated adult females into estrus (adult estrus) and diestrus (adult diestrus) groups. A wide range of terms has been used to describe mammary epithelial subpopulations. Although recent efforts have aimed to standardize this nomenclature, it has not been widely adopted yet.27 Therefore, we use the simpler, commonly used classification of basal and luminal cells, with luminal cells further divided into hormone receptor-positive (HR+) and hormone receptor-negative (HR–). PROCR+ MaSCs are located in the basal compartment in the mammary epithelium.9 Basal and luminal epithelial cell types could be efficiently separated by their differential expression of EpCAM and CD49f.18,28 Thus, to investigate PROCR+ at a high resolution, we purified by FACS the EpCAM+/CD49fhigh/PROCR+ cells (Figure S1A). For each time point, the total epithelial cells and the enriched PROCR+ cells were integrated (Figure S1B). Procr+ cells were detected across all developmental stages analyzed, with the highest relative abundance observed in pubertal tissues (Figure S1B). Principal component analysis (PCA) comparison of sorted and unsorted Procr+ cells revealed that the sorted population faithfully recapitulated the transcriptional profile of the unsorted cells (Figure S1C). As expected, the sorted PROCR+ cells have higher Procr expression and express genes that are previously identified as the Procr+ signature9 (Figure S1D and Table S1).

We next performed clustering analysis and annotation of cell populations using established lineage markers (Figures 1B and S2–S6). Basal cells (Krt5+, Krt14+, Krt18–, and Krt8–), luminal estrogen receptor-positive cells (Lum:HR+; Krt5–, Krt14–, Krt18+, Krt8+, and Esr1+), and luminal estrogen receptor-negative cells (Lum:HR–; Krt5–, Krt14–, Krt18+, Krt8+, and Esr–) were identified at all age time points. Lum:HR+ cells expressed high levels of Areg, Prlr, and Pgr, consistent with hormone-sensing luminal ductal cells, whereas Lum:HR− cells expressed higher levels of Cd14 and Aldh1a3, consistent with luminal progenitor cells. Notably, small fractions of proliferating (Mki67+) cells were observed in both basal and luminal compartments. A distinct cluster corresponding to alveolar differentiated cells, characterized by expression of the whey acidic protein (Wap) gene, was detected exclusively in old parous females. This cluster represents the long-term cellular imprint on the mammary epithelium that persists after pregnancy and involution, reflecting the long-term remodeling and memory of the parous mammary gland.

Interestingly, Procr+ cells displayed substantial heterogeneity. We identified five distinct Procr+ subpopulations (Figure 1B): (1) Procr+Cdh5+Cd36+; (2) Procr+Cdh5+Cd36–; (3) Procr+Cdh5–Des+; (4) Procr+Cdh5–Col1a1+; and (5) Procr+Pdpn+. Previous studies have reported that Procr+ cells express mesenchymal-associated genes such as Vim, Cdh5, Zeb2, and Fabp4.9 We found that these genes were not equally co-expressed across the identified subpopulations but instead exhibited distinct expression patterns, underscoring the molecular heterogeneity within the Procr+ compartment (Figure 1C). The relative abundance of these subpopulations varied by developmental stage, with all five represented in most stages, except in aged mammary epithelium, which retained only three (Figure 1B).

Next, we performed scATAC-seq analysis on isolated Procr+ cells, along with the major differentiated mammary epithelial populations (basal, Lum:HR+, and Lum:HR−). scATAC-seq enabled us to assess chromatin accessibility at a genome-wide level in individual cells. Analyzed cells were derived from the mammary epithelia of virgin adult females in the estrus phase. In total, more than 12,800 nuclei were profiled (Figure 1D). For each cell, we calculated a gene accessibility score for Procr, defined as the ATAC-seq signal at the Procr locus. As expected, PROCR+ cells displayed the highest Procr accessibility scores (Figure 1D, inset, and Figure S7A).

To further validate cell-type identity, we computed accessibility scores at canonical marker loci that define basal, Lum:HR+, and Lum:HR− populations. The resulting accessibility profiles closely mirrored the gene expression patterns observed in our scRNA-seq dataset (Figures 1D and S7A). Similar to transcriptional profiles, Procr+ cells exhibited pronounced heterogeneity in chromatin accessibility (Figure S7B).

Finally, integration of scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq datasets revealed a striking concordance between transcriptional and chromatin accessibility-based substructures within the Procr+ compartment, indicating that the heterogeneity identified at the RNA level is underpinned by corresponding differences in chromatin state (Figure 1E).

PROCR+ subpopulation shows high potency in putative developmental trajectory

To investigate the heterogeneity observed among Procr+ cells and estimate their differentiation potential, we first applied the CytoTRACE algorithm29 to the scRNA-seq dataset. This analysis revealed distinct potency levels among Procr+ subpopulations (Figure 2A). Notably, the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ cluster exhibited the highest predicted potency, whereas both Cdh5+ subpopulations displayed low potency. Given the previously described enrichment of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-associated genes in Procr+ cells,9 we also examined the EMT signature30 (Table S2) across Procr+ subpopulations. We found substantial variation in EMT gene expression among them. To infer potential developmental hierarchies among mammary epithelial cells, we next applied trajectory inference analyses to the scRNA-seq data. Using Slingshot,31 we found that the high-potency Procr+Cdh5–Col1a1+ subpopulation is an undifferentiated state that gives rise to the more committed Procr+Pdpn+ cluster, which subsequently diverges into the basal and luminal lineages (Figure 2C). Consistent with this analysis, diffusion maps32 produced similar results, supporting the Slingshot and CytoTRACE model (Figures 2D and 2E). Thus, in both analyses, the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ cluster corresponded to the earliest pseudotime state, while luminal cells transitioned from HR− to HR+, and basal cells progressed through Basal:Procr+, Pdpn+ → Basal:Procr–, Pdpn+ → Basal:Procr–, Pdpn– states. Hereafter, we will refer to the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ subpopulation as the putative MaSC cluster. These analyses also revealed that some Procr+ clusters were positioned far from the main epithelial cell clusters (data not shown). Because PROCR+ cells have been reported in other cell types,33,34 we excluded these clusters from further analysis.

Single-cell multimodal data reveal MRs in putative MaSCs

Given the potential developmental hierarchy placing the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ subpopulation at the top, we investigated the molecular mechanisms that define lineage relationships. To this end, we analyzed the expression data from the six clusters in the hierarchical model and inferred a TRN using SJARACNe35 facilitated by metacell conversion.36 Here, a TRN consists of a web of connections between transcription factors (TFs) and their targets. A TF-gene connection can show a positive (activator) or a negative (repressor) correlation.

In the context of a TRN, of particular interest are the potential hidden drivers,37 also referred to as MRs, which are TFs that tend to control a large number of target genes, being also critical regulators of the cell fate.38,39 The collective expression of the targets of an MR can be summarized as the activity of the corresponding MR. Thus, next, we applied NetBID37 to identify potential MRs of each cell subpopulation, defined as TFs whose inferred activities differ significantly among cell populations (Figure 3A). To provide more mechanistic insights, we performed cross-modality integration and arrived at a pseudo-scRNA-seq profile for each scATAC-seq cell. Next, for each TF, we examined the accessibility of its binding motif at the single-cell level based on scATAC-seq profiles. TFs whose motif accessibility correlated positively with their inferred activity (increased target expression for activators or decreased target expression for repressors) were considered likely direct regulators (Figure 3B and Table S3). Finally, we computed activity profiles for all direct regulators using scRNA-seq data, and correlation analysis of these profiles revealed that the direct regulators form distinct co-activating modules (Figure S8A).

To further dissect the regulators driving basal and luminal lineage specification, we performed differential activity analysis between putative MaSCs and basal or luminal cells. As expected, well-known MRs associated with lineage commitment (e.g., Bcl11b, Sox4, or Notch1) as well as MRs active in MaSCs populations (e.g., Zeb1, Zeb2, Twist1, Twist2, and Prxx1) emerged from our analysis (Figure 3C and Tables S4 and S5). Similar but weaker trends were observed when the differential expression, instead of activity, of these TFs was examined (Figure S8B). Interestingly, with a few exceptions, most TFs that showed highly significant differential activity across cell types were not classified as direct regulators. This likely reflects indirect regulatory effects involving broad downstream signaling cascades, which can amplify their overall impact (Figures 3D and S8C and Tables S6, S7, and S8).

To extend these findings, we examined TF dynamics across developmental and physiological states (Figures S8D and S8E). Aging is associated with a shift in the transcriptional state of mammary epithelial cells that reflects reduced lineage stability and increased engagement of stress-associated pathways.41 As described previously,41 we observe the strongest changes in the transcriptome of aging basal cells. These included a clear decrease in expression of Cebpa and Tcf4, consistent with studies showing that these TFs supports epithelial identity and tissue homeostasis and that a reduction in their expression is linked to a decrease in epithelial cells under chronic inflammatory conditions, which represent a key feature of aging tissues.42,43 In contrast, aged basal mammary epithelial cells showed increased Nfkb1 expression, consistent with acquisition of pro-inflammatory programs and elevated NF-κB signaling during aging (Figure S8E). We also detected upregulation of Klf4 and the ETS family member Elf2 (Figure S8E), suggesting activation of transcriptional programs associated with epithelial plasticity and increased responsiveness to injury-associated epithelial states.41,44,45

Next, we leveraged scATAC-seq data to investigate how direct regulators with cell-type-specific activity patterns evolve along the differentiation trajectory. We calculated motif accessibility scores for each direct regulator at the single-cell level and ordered the cells along pseudo-time (Figure S9A). This analysis provided an unprecedentedly detailed temporal activation map of these MRs, revealing, for example, the rapid and sustained activation of Trp63 and Tcf7l1 within the basal lineage and Foxp4 and Foxk1 within the luminal lineage. In contrast, regulators such as Cebpa, Cebpb, Nr2f6, Klf6, and Rarg exhibited their highest activity at the early stem cell stage, followed by a rapid decline during the lineage commitment. By correlating the activity profiles of these regulators along the basal and luminal trajectories separately, we found that they form distinct co-activating modules (Figures 3E and S9B). Finally, beyond motif accessibility, lineage-specific binding of these direct regulators was further supported by TF footprinting analysis (Figure 3F). Regulators like Foxp4 and Trp63 that are known to be playing critical roles in regulating epithelial cell fate were recapitulated.46

Analogous PROCR+ cells in human mammary glands underline specific breast cancer subtype

Next, we transition our studies to human mammary epithelium. We obtained normal healthy tissue from mastectomies and performed scRNA-seq on the dissociated human MECs and a similar FACS strategy as the one used in mice (Figure S10A). We found that, similar to the mouse, a small population of cells expressing Procr was identified in human MECs (Figures 4A and S10B). In particular, the PROCR+ cells in humans are also heterogeneous with the presence of a Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ cluster (Figure 4A). Importantly, when we used the signature of the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ mice cells (Table S9), we observed that, on average, this signature is highly expressed in this subpopulation in human cells (Figures 4B and S10C). Furthermore, we examined this signature in a large, publicly available dataset of human breast scRNA-seq data (The Human Breast Cell Atlas, HBCA47). Given the close link between stem cell states and tumorigenesis, we next examined whether the newly identified Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ population is represented in human breast cancers. To do so, we analyzed the METABRIC48 cohort and quantified expression of the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ signature across molecular subtypes. We found that this signature is markedly enriched in TNBCs, particularly within the claudin-low subtype (Figure 4C), a molecular subtype characterized by pronounced stem-like features.49

To complement these findings, we compared the claudin-low breast cancer signature7 (Table S10) with the transcriptional profiles of all mammary epithelial subpopulations identified in the normal mouse mammary gland. Notably, the claudin-low signature exhibited its strongest association with the putative MaSC cluster (Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+, Figure 4D), with no significant enrichment observed for signatures corresponding to other breast cancer subtypes (Figure S11A). We further assessed the relationship between claudin-low tumors and each of the Procr+ subpopulations. Although all PROCR+ clusters showed a clear association, the putative MaSC population (Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+) demonstrated the highest correlation with claudin-low signatures (Figure S11B and Tables S11, S12, S13, and S14).

Next, we leveraged published scRNA-seq datasets from human breast cancers50 to evaluate the presence of the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ transcriptional program at single-cell resolution. A small fraction of tumor cells expressing this signature was detectable in tumors across all clinical subtypes; however, the highest representation was again observed in TNBCs, with the most pronounced enrichment in claudin-low tumors (Figures 4E–4G). Notably, the strongest enrichment was observed in a tumor sample (CID4513), classified as a metaplastic spindle-cell carcinoma with sarcomatous features. This is a rare, highly undifferentiated, fast-growing, and metastatic form of breast cancer.

Finally, we aimed to directly compare the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ populations between non-tumor and tumor tissue. Analyses based on publicly available datasets are inherently limited by inter-donor variability and differences in analytical platforms, which can introduce substantial bias. To overcome these limitations, we obtained matched tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue from a TNBC patient and performed scRNA-seq analysis. As expected, expression of the estrogen receptor was detected only in the non-tumor epithelial compartment (Figure 5A). When comparing all epithelial tumor cells to epithelial cells from adjacent non-tumor tissue, we observed robust activation of canonical cancer-associated programs, including proliferative signaling (E2F and MYC), EMT, increased glycolysis, and mTOR signaling. (D) Pathway analysis of Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ cells comparing tumor versus non-tumor compartments reveals selective enrichment of EMT, glycolysis, and altered TP53 signaling in tumor-derived cells, while proliferation-associated programs are not significantly enriched, indicating functional heterogeneity in the bulk tumor epithelium.

As expected, the proportion of cells expressing the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ signature was increased within the epithelial tumor compartment (Figure 5C). Notably, when we specifically compared Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ cells from tumor versus adjacent non-tumor tissue, we found that tumor-derived cells recapitulated a subset, but not all, of the hallmarks observed at the bulk epithelial level. These included enrichment of EMT, glycolysis, and altered TP53 signaling, whereas hallmarks associated with enhanced proliferation were not observed (Figure 5D). These findings suggest that highly proliferative signaling programs may be acquired by a subset of tumor cells that constitute the bulk of the tumor and that are at least partially transcriptionally divergent from the Procr+ population. This distinction likely reflects part of the well-known functional heterogeneity within the tumor epithelium, where Procr+ cells may contribute to tumor progression through mechanisms that are complementary to, but distinct from, those of highly proliferative cells.

Discussion

Understanding the identity, molecular regulation, and developmental potential of putative mammary epithelial stem cells (MaSCs) has been a long-standing challenge in the field.27 Although PROCR+ cells were previously implicated as highly regenerative MaSCs, their rarity and the lack of unbiased molecular characterization have hindered mechanistic insight. Consequently, despite substantial efforts to characterize the mammary epithelium using scRNA-seq to delineate its differentiation dynamics, PROCR+ cells have typically appeared as a rare population of merely dozens of cells, precluding meaningful analysis.16,18,51,52 By combining targeted enrichment, single-cell transcriptomics, and single-cell chromatin accessibility profiling, our study resolves this long-standing gap and provides a comprehensive molecular framework for the PROCR+ compartment.

A central finding of our study is that PROCR+ cells are not a uniform population but instead consist of multiple transcriptionally and epigenetically distinct subpopulations. This heterogeneity provides a straightforward explanation for transplantation studies in which not all PROCR+ cells exhibit reconstitution capacity.9 Among these, a previously unrecognized Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ population emerged as the most primitive and potent MaSC state. This population exhibits the highest CytoTRACE scores, the strongest EMT-associated signature, and the greatest developmental plasticity in trajectory analyses. Our findings suggest that this population represents a bona fide MaSC state, whereas other PROCR+ subpopulations may represent transitional, more lineage-committed intermediates.

The integration of scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq provided unique mechanistic insights into how TFs coordinate MaSC identity and lineage specification. Regulatory network inference revealed a set of direct and indirect MRs whose activities are linked to the transcriptional and epigenetic landscapes of each subpopulation. Known regulators of MaSC or lineage commitment were recovered, validating the analytical framework. This multimodal approach also pinpointed a comprehensive list of regulators that exhibit peak activity at different states of lineage commitment. The ability to follow TF motif accessibility across pseudotime further delineated lineage-restricted activation programs, such as the early, sustained engagement of Trp63 and Tcf7l1 in basal cells and Foxp4 and Foxk1 in luminal cells. These results provide a hierarchical and temporal map of chromatin-encoded regulatory logic guiding mammary epithelial fate decisions.

Our identification of the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ population also has important implications for breast cancer. The connection between stemness programs and tumor aggressiveness is well established, and PROCR has been implicated in the biology of tumor-initiating cells. By analyzing both bulk and single-cell datasets from human breast cancers, we demonstrate that the transcriptional signature of this putative MaSC population is enriched in TNBCs, with the strongest representation in claudin-low tumors. This molecular subtype is characterized by high plasticity, EMT features, and stem-like behavior, providing a direct link between the transcriptional and epigenetic programs governing normal MaSC plasticity and aggressive breast cancer. While enrichment of stem-like signatures in TNBC has been previously reported, this is not the primary focus of our study. Rather, our findings refine this concept by resolving heterogeneity within the PROCR+ compartment. Specifically, we identify a distinct PROCR+ subpopulation (Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+) whose transcriptional program shows the strongest and most selective enrichment in claudin-low tumors. This suggests that not all stem-like programs are equivalent and provides a more precise cellular and molecular framework linking normal mammary epithelial states to tumor subtypes.

Previous studies have shown that PROCR+ cells in breast tumors are enriched for cancer stem cell (CSC) activity and have suggested PROCR as a potential therapeutic target.19 However, despite the higher CSC frequency in PROCR+ cells compared with PROCR− cells, the estimated frequency of CSCs within the PROCR+ compartment is only 1 in 100, indicating that not all PROCR+ tumor cells possess CSCs.19 Interestingly, even though the frequency of CSCs in PROCR+ cells is much higher than in PROCR− cells, the estimate of CSC frequency in PROCR+ cells is only about 1/100, suggesting that not all PROCR+ cells in tumors are CSCs. Our identification of multiple transcriptionally distinct PROCR+ subpopulations provides an explanation for this observation and suggests that only a subset, potentially those resembling the Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ state, have CSC potential. These findings also underscore the importance of developing therapeutic strategies that selectively target this stem-like subset rather than the broader PROCR+ population.

Our work has limitations that warrant further investigation. The identity and differentiation potential of the identified populations require additional functional validation. The small proportion of PROCR+ mammary epithelial cells, along with their low expression of epithelial markers and high mesenchymal gene expression, complicates their identification and lineage assignment.9 Analysis of our scRNA-seq data shows that ∼70% of Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ cells express low-moderate levels of epithelial keratins (e.g., Krt5, Krt8, Krt14, and Krt18), while this is not seen in fibroblasts (Figure S12A). Additionally, integration of our dataset with previously published mouse mammary gland scRNA-seq data16 revealed that our Procr+, Pdpn+ population aligns with their basal population. The majority of our Procr+, Cdh5–, Des+ cluster is consistent with their Procr+ population, and a smaller fraction of these cells also aligns with their basal population. Our Procr+, Cdh5–, Col1a1+ cells are consistent with their uncharacterized 10.1 cluster, with a smaller fraction also aligning with their Procr+ and basal populations (Figure S12B). Overall, these results suggest that most PROCR+ cells identified here were previously captured as epithelial-associated populations but are now resolved at higher resolution.

While transplantation assays have historically been used to assess regenerative potential, it is now well recognized that transplanted cells do not reflect in vivo behavior accurately due to transdifferentiation and facultative stem cell behavior.27 Lineage-tracing strategies are now preferred, though they have given conflicting results, depending on the promoter used.27 Therefore, final validation will require multiple orthogonal approaches that are beyond the scope of the current study, as the main goal of our work is to provide a comprehensive multimodal molecular atlas of the PROCR+ compartment, serving as a resource and framework for the field to guide future studies.

In sum, our work defines the molecular, epigenetic, and functional architecture of the putative PROCR+ MaSC compartment and establishes its relevance to the biology of aggressive human breast cancer. These findings lay the groundwork for further investigating models of MaSC dynamics, provide candidate regulators of early lineage specification, and point toward refined cellular and molecular targets for the treatment of TNBC, particularly claudin-low disease.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the identity and developmental potential of the identified PROCR+ subpopulations remain to be functionally validated. Although integrative analyses support their annotation and lineage relationships, these conclusions are based primarily on computational inference rather than direct in vivo evidence. Finally, while transplantation and lineage-tracing approaches provide complementary insights, both have inherent limitations and may not fully reflect physiological behavior. Future studies using orthogonal experimental strategies will be required to establish lineage relationships and functional capacity of these populations in vivo.

Experimental model and study participant details

Wild-type BALB/cJ female mice (Strain #:000651) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Mice were euthanized at the indicated ages using CO2 inhalation. All animal husbandry procedures and experimental protocols were conducted in accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines and approved institutional regulations. Human mammary tissue samples were obtained from three female patients undergoing mastectomy through the Mount Sinai Biorepository Core under an approved institutional review board (IRB) protocol. As this study was designed to investigate mammary gland biology, a female-specific tissue, only female mice and human participants were included. Consequently, the influence of sex on the study outcomes could not be evaluated.

Method details

Determination of estrous cycle stage

Vaginal smears were taken by flushing with 15 μL of sterile PBS which were then spread on a slide and allowed to dry. The cells were visualized after staining with crystal violet and washing twice with deionized water. The stages were then identified as described.57

Mammary gland dissociation and epithelial enrichment (mouse)

The 3rd and 4th mammary glands were dissected from female mice, pooled, and minced followed by enzymatic digestion in DMEM/F12 media containing 2 mg/mL Collagenase A (Roche #11088793001) and 100 U/mL Hyaluronidase (Sigma #H3506) for 2 h at 37°C. Single-cell suspensions were obtained via sequential incubations in pre-warmed 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA followed by 5 mg/mL Dispase II (Life Technologies #17105-041) in PBS with 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Stemcell #07900), pipetting up and down at each step. Finally, the cells were incubated in Red Blood Cell Lysing Buffer (Sigma #R7757) and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer. The samples were enriched for Lin-epithelial cells using the EasySep Mouse Epithelial Cell Enrichment Kit II (Stemcell #19758) according to the protocol.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

The epithelial cell-enriched cell suspensions were incubated with the following antibodies: EpCAM-PerCP/Cy5.5, CD49f-APC, PROCR-PE, and Sca-1-BV421 (BioLegend #118220, #313616, #141504, and BD #562729). The LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain (ThermoFisher #L34957) was used to determine cell viability. Antibody incubations were performed for 15 min on ice. The cells were sorted using a FACSAria II (BD) cell sorter using the following method: following size and doublet discrimination and viability screening, EpCAM+ cells were sorted for the Total Epithelium samples. To enrich for MaSCs, EpCAM+, CD49fhi PROCR+ cells were sorted.

Mammary gland dissociation and epithelial enrichment (human)

Fresh tissue was obtained from 3 patient-unidentified mastectomies surgeries (matched tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue) via the Biorepository Core at Mount Sinai according to an approved protocol. Samples were dissociated according to a published method (Shehata and Stingl, 2017). Briefly, the tissue was minced and enzymatically digested in DMEM/F12 media containing 2 mg/mL Collagenase A (Roche #11088793001), 100 U/mL Hyaluronidase (Sigma #H3506), 2% BSA, 5 μg/mL insulin, 50 μg/mL gentamycin, and 10 mM HEPES overnight at 37°C. The samples were enriched for epithelial cells via centrifugation at 200xg for 5 min. Single-cell suspensions were obtained via sequential incubations in pre-warmed 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA followed by 5 mg/mL Dispase II (Life Technologies #17105-041) in PBS with 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Stemcell #07900). The dissociated cells were subjected to FACS using the same method as above with the following antibodies: EpCAM-PerCP/Cy5.5, CD49f-APC, and PROCR-PE (BioLegend #324213, #313615, and #351903, respectively).

Bulk RNA library preparation and sequencing

For samples with limited amount of RNA, we used NEBnext Single Cell/Low RNA Library prep Kit for Illumina to generate sequencing libraries (New England BioLabs). Libraries were sequenced on Illumina NextSeq 500 system (Illumina). For samples with abundant RNA, stranded RNA libraries were prepared with 100 ng total RNA using the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit with RiboErase (HMR) (Roche). RNA sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina). The reference genome mm10 was used.

Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) sample processing

Flow-sorted single cells were captured with the 10×Genomics Chromium Controller (10×Genomics). Libraries were prepared with the Chromium Single Cell Gene Expression 3′ v2 or v3 kits and sequenced on either Illumina Hiseq 2500 or NextSeq 500 systems (Illumina). Sequencing data were run on Cellranger to generate feature barcode matrices and to perform clustering and gene expression analysis. The reference genome mm10 was used for mouse samples and hg38 for human samples.

Single cell ATAC sequencing (scATAC-seq) sample processing

Sample preparation and flow-sorting were performed using the same procedures as scRNA-seq. Libraries were prepared with Epi ATAC v1.1 by 10× Genomics and sequenced on NextSeq 500 systems (Illumina). Sequencing data were run on Cell Ranger to generate feature barcode matrices and to perform clustering and gene expression analysis. The reference genome mm10 was used.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Processing and analysis in scRNA-seq data

Count matrices of individual samples were independently imported and processed by Seurat.53 Cells with very high or low gene/UMI counts, as well as cells with high percentage of mitochondrial reads were filtered. For each time point, the MEC sample and the sorted PROCR+ sample were merged. Clustering was performed for each time point, and the resultant clusters were annotated using well-known markers. Additional clustering analysis using the tool scMINER54 was performed to refine the clusters. Next, cells from the 4 time points were further merged, a normalization bƒetween total MECs and sorted PROCR+ cells were performed to correct the transcriptome size.55 To identify differentially expressed genes in each of the 5 Procr+ subpopulations, the FindMarkers() function in Seurat was employed. The Procr+, Cdh5-, Col1a1+ signature used in Figure 4 was defined by identifying genes differentially expressed between the cluster Procr+,Cdh5-,Col1a1+ and the other cells (criteria: pct.1 > 0.5, avg_log2FC > 2 and p_val_adj<10−10). To further refine the signature, genes that are also highly expressed in the other four Procr+ populations were excluded.

For dimensionality reduction and visualization, distinct batch-correction strategies were applied depending on the comparison being performed. For analyses shown in Figure 1B, batch correction was optimized to minimize technical differences between sorted and unsorted samples, enabling accurate comparison of cell populations across isolation conditions. In contrast, for integrated analyses across developmental stages and physiological conditions (Figure S6), batch correction parameters were adjusted to prioritize alignment of shared biological states across samples.

Processing and analysis in scATAC-seq data

Fragments files of 4 scATAC-seq samples (Basal, PROCR+, ER+, ER-) outputted by cell ranger were imported using ArchR.40 Basic quality controls like doublet filtering were performed. The geneScore for each gene, essentially the number of reads mapped to the entire gene body, were calculated using the function addGeneScoreMatrix(). scRNA-seq data and scATAC-seq data were further integrated using ArchR. Specifically, cells from scRNA-seq data were aligned with the cells from scATAC-seq data based on their gene expression and gene scores (accessibility), using the function addGeneIntegrationMatrix(). To show the consistency of the 5 Procr+ subpopulations, cell types were jointly defined by scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data using rliger.56 Finally, footprinting analysis of binding motifs was performed using ArchR.

Inference of potency and developmental trajectory

The potency of individual cells was calculated using the tool Cytotrace.29 The relative scores that scale between 0 and 1 were employed. The EMT score reported in Figure 2B was the average expression of a set of signature genes curated in Gutierrez et al.30 Slingshot was employed to infer the developmental trajectory starting from the putative MaSCs to terminally differentiated cells.31 Given the high potency scores by Cytotrace, the putative MaSCs was inputted as the root of the process. To get the tree, 3 principal components were used. An independent tool based on diffusion map, DPT,32 was also employed for the inference of trajectory. Pseudotime was defined using the leading diffusion components, and the cells were ordered by the pseudo-time.

Inference of transcriptional regulatory networks and identification of master regulators

Transcriptional regulatory networks were inferred from single-cell expression data using SJARACNe.35 Specifically, we used the tool SuperCell36 to coarse-grain the single cells into so-called metacells. The expression profiles of these metacells were used as input for SJARACNe. By estimating the mutual information between the expression of a transcription factor and a potential target gene, the resulting network is a collection of regulons, where the target genes of transcription factors are identified. There are two sets of targets: positive and negative, indicating that the expression of the transcription factor is positively or negatively correlated with the expression of the target genes, respectively.

NetBID237 was then employed to identify potential drivers or master regulators. Specifically, NetBID2 quantified the activity of a transcription factor by averaging the expression of its targets. Positive and negative targets were weighted in opposite directions so that high activity of a transcription factor corresponds to high expression of its positive targets and low expression of its negative targets. Similar to standard differential expression analysis between cell types, differential activity analysis was performed to identify transcription factors whose activities are specific to a particular cell type, or whose activities vary the most from putative MaSCs to luminal/basal populations. Statistically significant transcription factors are estimated with multiple testing by Benjamini-Hochberg. Since single-cell data are sparse, analyses such as network inference, differential activity, and co-expression were performed at the metacell level.

To determine whether a transcription factor with differential activity is a direct regulator, scATAC-seq data were employed. First, the motif accessibility score of a transcription factor was calculated at the single-cell level using its binding motif and scATAC-seq data via the ArchR function addMotifAnnotation(). Next, as described above, scRNA-seq data and scATAC-seq data were integrated using ArchR with the function addGeneIntegrationMatrix(), resulting in a pseudo-scRNA-seq profile for each cell in the scATAC-seq matrix. Based on the pseudo-gene-expression profile, the two activity profiles (positive and negative targets) of the transcription factor were calculated for each cell in the scATAC-seq matrix. Finally, the activity profiles were correlated with the motif score profile across cells. Transcription factors whose motif accessibility score is positively correlated with their activity were determined to be direct regulators, i.e., an increase (decrease) in the expression values of its targets for an activator (repressor).

Estimation of pseudotime in scATAC-seq data was performed using ArchR. Basal and luminal trajectories obtained from scRNA-seq data were used as input, and cell ordering was calculated using the ArchR function addTrajectory(). The activity of individual basal/luminal transcription factors was analyzed as functions of pseudotime, and the correlation of their activities across time was calculated.

Annotation using existing mouse mammary gland data

The referencing scRNA-seq data of mouse mammary gland were obtained from publicly available databases.58 Cluster annotation used in Figure S12 was curated by authors of the reference, including the uncharacterized clusters not shown in their manuscript. Procr+ cells in this study was then mapped to the reference with mapping scores using the tool SingleR.

scRNA-seq of matched tumor and adjacent tissue in human patient

Count matrices of samples generated by Cellranger were independently processed using the scRNA-seq pipeline described above. Tumor sample and matched normal samples were aligned and integrated using the FindIntegrationAnchors() function in Seurat. Clusters were manually annotation using well-known markers of epithelial cells, fibroblast, endothelial cells, and various immune cells. Infercnv package was used to compare the tumor and normal samples. Clusters of epithelial cells were extracted based on markers’ expression, as well as the deviation between tumor and normal cells. Epithelial cells from tumor and matched normal were further analyzed, including the Procr+, Cdh5-, Col1A1+ signatures, and differential expression.

Publicly available human data sources

Human Breast Cell Atlas data47 was downloaded from CZ CELLxGENE. Epithelial cells annotated as basal-myoepithelial cells, luminal adaptive secretory precursor cells and luminal hormone-sensing cells were kept. The gene signature of the claudin-low subtype was obtained from METABRIC.48 METEBRIC data was downloaded from the cBioPortal, in which there were 1904 tumors with both expression data and clinical data. The METABRIC data included the classification of tumors into the 6 molecular subtypes (Basal, claudin-low, Her2, LumA, LumB and Normal). The claudin-low subtype displays the highest expression of putative MaSC signature. Statistical significance was tested using the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test adjusted for multiple testing by Benjamini-Hochberg. Single-cell breast cancer data were obtained from publicly available data resources.50 Based on the authors’ curation, immune cells and stromal cells were filtered, only tumor cells were used in our analysis.
