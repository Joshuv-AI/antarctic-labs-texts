Tamoxifen-pretreated MSC exosomes promote endocrine resistance in ER-positive breast cancer cells through the miR-137/Serpina3 axis: a mechanistic study
Authors: Xiaolan Huang, Tingting Li, Shiqian Ye, Yanan Lan, Lin Zeng, Yan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTamoxifen (TAM) resistance remains a challenge in estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer treatment. Current research suggested that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) derived exosomes may mediate chemoresistance, but the underlying mechanisms are unclear. This study investigates how exosomes from tamoxifen-pretreated MSCs (Tt-MSC-exos) promote tamoxifen resistance through the miR-137/SERPINA3 axis.MethodsWe isolated exosomes using ultracentrifugation. To investigate the effect of Tt-MSC-exos on TAM resistance, we co-cultured Tt-MSC-exos with ER-positive breast cancer cells and evaluated the cellular functional changes and apoptosis levels. Second-generation transcriptome sequencing was used to analyze the key genes for TAM resistance, and a dual luciferase reporter assay was used to detect the upstream factors of the key genes. The results were further validated by cellular function assays, xenograft modeling studies, and database analysis.ResultsWe demonstrated that Tt-MSC-exos promote TAM resistance in ER-positive breast cancer cells. Next-generation sequencing revealed that the key gene for Tt-MSC-exos promoting resistance was SERPINA3. Downregulation of SERPINA3 expression enhances TAM resistance in breast cancer cells. In addition, we found that miR-137, which was highly expressed in Tt-MSC-exos, could bind to the 3’ UTR region of SERPINA3 in breast cancer cells and thus inhibit the expression of SERPINA3.ConclusionsIn this study, we clarified that Tt-MSC-exos inhibit the expression level of SERPINA3 in cancer cells by delivering miR-137 to ER-positive breast cancer cells, which in turn leads to TAM resistance in breast cancer. This study provides a new idea to overcome endocrine therapy resistance in ER-positive breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13058-026-02306-8.

Introduction

Estrogen receptor (ER)-positive breast cancer is the most common molecular subtype of breast cancer [1], and its first-line treatment is mainly endocrine therapy [2]. Tamoxifen (TAM) was one of the first drugs used in endocrine therapy for breast cancer [3]. It has been shown in many clinical trials to be effective in reducing recurrence and mortality in patients, but 30–40% of patients still develop drug resistance after treatment [4].

Currently, the main mechanisms of endocrine resistance can be summarized into three aspects: aberrant alterations of key genes related to drug therapy [5, 6], aberrant alterations of key signalling pathways [7, 8], and the phenomenon of metabolic reprogramming of tumors [9]. Despite the heterogeneity of resistance mechanisms at the protein level, different resistance pathways may share common features at upstream regulatory nodes [10, 11]. Therefore, tamoxifen, a widely used endocrine therapeutic drug in the clinic, was selected as a research subject, and resolving the molecular origin of its resistance mechanism can provide clues to understand the commonality of resistance to multiple endocrine drugs.

The Tumor Microenvironment (TME) is a complex ecosystem composed of mesenchymal cells, extracellular matrix, and secreted factors, whose dynamic interactions not only drive tumor progression but also weaken drug efficacy through multiple mechanisms, making it one of the core reasons for breast cancer treatment failure [12–14]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a class of adult stem cells with self-renewal and multidirectional differentiation potential, which are widely distributed in tissues such as bone marrow and adipose [15, 16] and have the ability to home to tumors and sites of injury [17]. MSCs, as core members of the TME, have been demonstrated to influence tumor progression, metastasis, and drug resistance through various mechanisms, such as modulation of the immune response and delivery of endogenous or exogenous signalling molecules to the target cells [18]. Fan An et al. observed that mesenchymal stem cells promote breast cancer lung metastasis in a mouse model of breast cancer, identifying a metastasis-promoting role for mesenchymal stem cells in the breast cancer tumor stroma [19]. In an in vivo experiment in SCID/beige mice in the presence or absence of estrogen, co-injection of the ER-positive breast cancer cell line MCF7 with bone marrow-derived MSC enhanced the primary tumor growth of MCF7 [20]. These findings suggest that MSC is closely associated with breast cancer progression and is likely to be a new therapeutic strategy to reduce metastasis, recurrence, and drug resistance in breast cancer patients in future clinical treatment.

Exosome, a double-layered lipid-membrane vesicle secreted by cells and widely found in various body fluids, can mediate intercellular information transfer and regulate different biological processes [21]. Exosomes secreted by MSCs (MSC-exos) can regulate the tumor microenvironment and play an essential role in tumor development, which is closely related to resistance to cancer chemotherapeutic agents, targeted therapies, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy [22]. In breast cancer, cancer cells initiate MSCs release of exosomes that contain varying miRNA content, such as miR-222/223, which promotes quiescence and confers drug resistance in cancer cell subpopulations [23]. Additional studies have shown that miR-1236 carried by MSC-exos enhances the sensitivity of breast cancer cells to cisplatin by inhibiting SLC9A1 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling [24]. These findings suggest that there may be bidirectional regulation of MSC-exos in regulating cancer progression, and the specific regulatory network has not been clarified.

This study delves into the impact of MSC-exos on the proliferation, migration, and stemness of ER-positive breast cancer cells, exploring the molecular mechanisms of their resistance to tamoxifen in the ex vivo and in vivo environments. In addition, we analyzed the relationship between the expression of the key drug resistance gene SERPINA3 and breast cancer progression and patient survival outcomes. Our study shows that MSC-exos alter the proliferation, migration, and stemness and affect the sensitivity of ER-positive breast cancer cells to tamoxifen. In addition, upon tamoxifen stimulation, MSC-exos delivered miR-137 to breast cancer cells, which inhibited the expression of SERPINA3 and thus promoted tamoxifen resistance in breast cancer cells.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

The human breast cancer cell lines MCF7, T47D, and the human embryonic kidney cells 293T were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences cell bank. Human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were a gift from the Oriental Hepatobiliary Hospital of the Second Military Medical University. MCF7 and 293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), while MSCs were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium α (MEMα, Gibco). All cells were grown in 10 cm cell culture dishes with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The incubation was conducted in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at a constant temperature of 37 °C.

Extraction and characterisation of MSC-secreted exosomes

MSCs growing in logarithmic phase were divided into two groups: the control group, fresh medium culture; the experimental group, tamoxifen-containing medium culture, with a final concentration of tamoxifen of 10 µM. After 24 h of culture, the culture was switched to serum-free and penicillin-streptomycin-free MEMα medium to continue the culture for 48 h. The cell supernatant was collected, and the suspended cells were removed by centrifugation at room temperature, 300 x g. 10,000 x g at 4 °C twice to remove dead cells and cell debris. The supernatant was collected and filtered, and centrifuged twice at 4 °C, 100000 x g for 70 min each time. Resuspend the collected exosome precipitate with PBS. Transmission electron microscopy and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) were used to determine the characteristic structure and size of exosomes.

Uptake of exosomes

To validate exosome uptake, lipophilic fluorescent dye DiO was employed for exosome labelling. Quantified exosomes (1 × 10¹⁰ particles/ml) were incubated with 50 µl DiO dye at room temperature for 20 min, followed by centrifugation at 120,000×g for 70 min to remove unbound dye. The labelled exosome pellet was resuspended in 1 ml DMEM medium and added to a transwell chamber system for co-culture with MCF-7 cells for 12 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and nuclei stained with DAPI. The exosome uptake process was observed and photographed using fluorescence microscopy.

Cell co-culture system

We used the transwell chamber system for co-culture experiments. MSCs in a stable state were divided into two groups: Tt-MSCs group, MEM-α medium containing 20 µM tamoxifen; MSCs group, MEM-α medium containing the same volume of PBS as the drug. MCF7 and T47D cells cultured with MEM-α medium were inoculated into lower chambers. The treated MSCs were inoculated into upper chambers. The MCF7 and T47D cells were collected after 48 h of culture. GW4869 (HY-19363, MedChemExpress, USA) is an exosome inhibitor that suppresses exosome synthesis and release. In the intervention experiment, 10 µM GW4869 was added to the upper chamber to inhibit exosome release from MSCs. The Transwell chamber system was placed in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, and after incubation for 24 h, MCF7 and T47D cells were collected for subsequent analysis.

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was explored using the Annexin-V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences). Cells (1 × 105–2 × 105 cells/well) were inoculated into 6-well plates overnight. The cells were washed with pre-cooled 1×PBS, collected, and resuspended in 300 µL of 1×Binding Buffer (Invitrogen). Annexin V-FITC and PI dye solutions were added, respectively. After that, it was incubated in the dark for 15 min. Before loading, 200 uL of 1xBinding Buffer was supplemented. Apoptotic cells were finally detected using FACS Calibur (BD Biosciences). The total apoptotic rate was calculated as the sum of the percentage of cells in the Q2 (late apoptosis) and Q4 (early apoptosis) quadrants.

RNA sequencing and data processing

Total RNA was then extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop one spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, USA), and RNA integrity was assessed with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100(Agilent technologies, Santa Clara, CA, US). Global gene expression profiling was performed by mRNA sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Biomarker Technologies, Beijing, China). To ensure statistical significance and reliability, three biological replicates were included for each group, including the control group, MSC-exos group, and Tt-MSC-exos group. Each biological replicate represented an independent sample collected under the same experimental conditions, and all samples were processed identically.

Transfection of miR-137 mimics and inhibitors

To modulate the expression levels of miR-137, miR-137 mimics and inhibitors were synthesized by Genepharma (Shanghai, China). The sequences used in this study were as follows: miR-137 mimics : UUAUUGCUUAAGAAUACGCGUAG; miR-137 inhibitors: CUACGCGUAUUCUUAAGCAAUAA. Cells were inoculated into 6-well plates. Cells were transfected with the 50nM miR-137 mimics or inhibitor and 5ul Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). After 24 h, collect the cells for subsequent experiments.

Western blotting

The cell lysate was added to the breast cancer cells, and the cells were vortexed on ice every 10 min three times. The lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. 20 µg of protein were subjected to gel electrophoresis after the BCA kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) determined the protein concentration. Protein samples were aliquoted on SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1.5 h, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies and their source were as follows: GAPDH (G8795, SIGMA), SERPINA3 (ab205198, ABCAM). The secondary antibodies were incubated for two hours. ECL Western Blotting Detection Kit (Solarbio, China) was used for chemiluminescent detection of PVDF membrane.

For exosome Western blotting, exosome pellets were lysed in 5× RIPA lysis buffer and vortexed on ice every 10 min three times. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. 15 µg of exosomal protein were loaded per lane. The exosome samples included MSC-exos, Tt-MSC-exos, and 293T cell lysate as positive control. Primary antibodies against exosomal markers were: CD63 (AP5333B, ABGENT), CD9 (AP1482D, ABGENT), and TSG101 (AM8662b, ABGENT).

Cellular viability assay and proliferation assay

Cell viability was determined using a cell counting kit (CK04-500, Dojindo, Japan). Cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were incubated in 96-well plates. Add tamoxifen (20 µM) and incubate for 24 h. Then, the medium was replaced(90 µL serum-free medium and 10 µl CCK-8 per well)and incubated in the dark atmosphere for two hours. Finally, the Microplate Tester (BioTek, USA) was used to measure absorbance at 450 nm. Cell proliferation assay was similarly conducted using a cell counting kit. Cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were incubated in 96-well plates. Following treatment with tamoxifen (20 µM) for 12, 24, 36, and 48 h, respectively, cell proliferation capacity was assessed.

Colony formation assay

1000 cells were plated in 6-well dishes and incubated for 2 weeks. Subsequently, 6-well plates were taken out of the incubator and stained with crystal violet. After 10 min, cell colonies were counted and photographed.

Transwell assay

A cell suspension containing 10,000 cells was added to the chambers. The chambers were placed in 24-well plates with 600 µL of 10% FBS medium. Treat with TAM the next day. After 24 h, the chambers were fixed with paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet for 10 min, and imaged with an inverted microscope.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) SERPINA3 3’-UTR fragments containing miR-137 binding sites were amplified and subsequently cloned into the pmirGLO luciferase reporter vector (LMAI Bio). HEK293T cells were seeded in 96-well plates. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the SERPINA3 3’-UTR plasmids (100ng/well) and miR-137 mimics (50nM/well) using 1ul Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). After 48 h, the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System was used to measure luciferase activity.

Xenograft model studies

Animal experiments were ethically approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital, adhering to Animal Protection Society guidelines. Nude mice (Shanghai Academy of Chinese Academy of Sciences) of 4–5 weeks were selected and divided into five groups of 3 mice each as follows: (1) MCF7 group; (2) MCF7 + TAM group; (3) MCF7 + MSC-exos + TAM group; (4) MCF7 + Tt-MSC-exos + TAM group; (5) MCF7 + Tt-MSC-exos_miR-137 + TAM group. Mices received a subcutaneous injection of Estradiol valerate (1.5 mg/kg; Selleck, USA) 24 h prior to cell inoculation. MCF7 (6 × 106 cells/cell) were mixed with matrix gel and implanted in the axillary subcutis of mice. On the day of tumor implantation, exosomes were injected into the tail vein according to the above grouping. Exosomes’ in vivo intervention concentration was 108 particles/100ul per mouse every three days for three weeks. After tumor formation, drug intervention (50 mg/ml) was added and injected subcutaneously twice a week. The mice’s tumor size and body weight were observed and recorded every 5 days. The experiment was terminated after one month, and the tumor tissues were photographed.

Statistical analysis

The measured data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The two groups were compared by t test, and the multiple groups were compared by one-way ANOVA. For different time points and treatment groups, data were compared by two-way ANOVA. Each experiment was carried out in triplicate. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ****P<0.0001;***P; **P; *P<0.05, and ns represents not significant.

Result

Extraction and identification of MSC-exos

Exosomes secreted by tamoxifen-pretreated MSCs (Tt-MSC-exos) and untreated MSCs (MSC-exos) were extracted by the ultrafast centrifugal method. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that the extracted exosomes exhibited a bilayer disc-like structure (Fig. 1A, B). NTA analysis revealed median particle diameters of 188.6 ± 3.3 nm for MSC-exo and 133.5 ± 5.2 nm for Tt-MSC-exo(Fig. 1C). Western blotting revealed that Tt-MSC-exos and MSC-exos expressed exosome marker proteins CD9, CD63 and intramembrane protein TSG101 (Fig. 1D), indicating successful extraction of Tt-MSC-exos and MSC-exos. After staining exosome membranes with DIO dye, the exosomes were cocultured with ER-positive breast cancer cells, and the uptake of exosomes in the cells was observed under a fluorescence microscope. The results showed that both Tt-MSC-exos and MSC-exos were co-localized with breast cancer cells (Fig. 1E), indicating that exosomes had been taken up by ER-positive breast cancer cells.

Tt-MSC-exos reversed the inhibition caused by Tam treatment

We determined by migration assay (Fig S2A, B, C) and cell viability assay (Fig S2D, E) that Tt-MSC, but not MSCs, significantly enhanced TAM resistance in ER-positive breast cancer cells. GW4869 is an indirect inhibitor of exosome release by inhibiting neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase). This inhibitor was used to inhibit the release of Tt-MSC-exos and MSC-exos to verify whether Tt-MSC-exos could mediate this effect. The results showed that the proliferation ability of the Tt-MSC group with GW4869 was lower than that of the Tt-MSC group without GW4869 (Fig S2F), indicating that the Tt-MSC-exos might mediate TAM resistance in breast cancer.

To further investigate the potential impact of Tt-MSC-exos on TAM treatment in ER-positive breast cancer cells, we conducted co-culture experiments using two distinct ER-positive breast cancer cell lines (MCF7 and T47D) with Tt-MSC-exos, and the effects on cell viability, clone formation, and cell migration ability were evaluated. The results demonstrated that co-culture with Tt-MSC-exos significantly reversed the Tamoxifen-induced inhibition of cell migration (Fig. 2A, B), cell viability (Fig. 2C), and colony formation (Fig. 2D). In contrast, MSC-exos co-culture did not alleviate these suppressive effects in either cell line (Fig. 2A, B, C, D). Furthermore, flow cytometry was used to detect apoptosis of the cells in the above treatment groups, and the results showed that after co-culture with Tt-MSC-exos and treatment with TAM, the apoptosis rate of breast cancer cells was lower than that in the MSC-exos group (Fig. 2E).

Downregulation of SERPINA3 in breast cancer cells co-cultured with Tt-MSC- exos

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying Tt-MSC-exos induced TAM resistance in ER-positive breast cancer, RNA-seq was performed on MCF7 cells co-cultured with Tt-MSC-exos, MSC-exos, or no exosomes for 24 h, and 1533 differentially expressed genes were identified. To identify potential Tamoxifen-resistance-related genes, we cross-analysed these differentially expressed genes with transcriptome data from parental MCF7 cells and tamoxifen-resistant MCF7 cells in the GEO database (GSE241654). A final selection of 68 differentially expressed genes with consistent expression trends was identified, comprising 24 downregulated genes and 44 upregulated genes. The expression profiles of these candidate genes in MCF7 cells co-cultured with Tt-MSC-exos and MSC-exos were visualised as heatmaps, with biological replicates shown for each group (Fig. 3A).

Survival analysis using the GEPIA 2.0 database revealed that low expression of SERPINA3, CREB3L2, and SYTL4 was associated with shorter disease-free survival, whereas low expression of SMAD6 was associated with longer disease-free survival (Fig. 3B). RT-qPCR validation showed that SERPINA3 was significantly down-regulated in the Tt-MSC-exos group, while CREB3L2, SMAD6, and SYTL4 were up-regulated. Notaboly, the difference in SERPINA3 expression was the most pronounced (Fig. 3C). Additionally, we compared the expression levels of SERPINA3 in tissues from patients with different breast cancer subtypes and found that SERPINA3 was predominantly elevated in Luminal A and Luminal B types (Fig. 3D). GSCA online analysis indicated that SERPINA3 expression level gradually decreased with disease progression (Fig. 3E). Western Blotting further confirmed that the expression of SERPINA3 protein was significantly reduced in the Tt-MSC-exos treatment group (Fig. 3F).

Downregulation of SERPINA3 promotes TAM resistance in breast cancer cells

To investigate the impact of SERPINA3 on TAM resistance in ER-positive breast cancer cells, SERPINA3 knockdown was performed in MCF7 and T47D cells. The effects on SERPINA3 expression, cell viability, and cell migration ability were evaluated. The results indicated a significant downregulation of SERPINA3 expression in both cell lines after SERPINA3 knockdown (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, under TAM treatment, SERPINA3 knockdown reversed the inhibition caused by TAM, repromoted cell viability and migratory ability (Fig. 4B, C).

miR137 inhibits SERPINA3 expression in breast cancer cells by binding to the SERPINA3 3′UTR region

To investigate the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling SERPINA3 expression, TargetScan was utilised to predict the miRNAs that bind to SERPINA3. We discovered that only miR-137 has a binding site within the 3′UTR of SERPINA3. (Fig. 5A). The dual luciferase reporter assay was performed to verify that miR-137 binds to the 3′UTR of SERPINA3. As shown, overexpression of miR-137 inhibited the luciferase activity of SERPINA3-WT, while it was unaffected in the mutant (Fig. 5B).

Next, miR-137 mimics and inhibitors were transfected into MCF-7 and T47D cells. RT-qPCR confirmed the successful knockdown and overexpression of miR-137 in cells. Levels of miR-137 were elevated in the mimic group, whereas they were suppressed in the inhibitor group (Fig. 5C). The expression level of SERPINA3 was detected by RT-qPCR. We observed that the expression level of SERPINA3 increased when miR-137 was knocked down and decreased when it was overexpressed (Fig. 5D). We performed a rescue experiment using a 3′UTR‑deficient SERPINA3 that resists miR‑137 binding. Restoration of SERPINA3 expression significantly reversed exosomal miR‑137‑induced tamoxifen resistance and reinstated cellular sensitivity (Fig. S1).

miR-137 derived from Tt-MSC-exos affects TAM resistance in ER-positive breast cancer

To investigate whether miR-137 functions downstream of SERPINA3, we treated SERPINA3-knockdown MCF7 and T47D cells with a miR-137 inhibitor. We observed no statistically significant difference in cell proliferation between the SERPINA3-knockdown (KD) group and the SERPINA3-KD + miR-137 inhibitor group (Fig. 6A). However, compared with cells treated with tamoxifen alone, both groups exhibited enhanced proliferative capacity. In the absence of SERPINA3, the miR-137 inhibitor failed to affect cell proliferation, indicating that miR-137 regulates tamoxifen resistance in a SERPINA3-dependent manner. Furthermore, comparing the expression levels of miR-137 in ER-positive breast cancer tissue samples and paired standard breast tissue samples via the TCGA database, miR-137 was found to be slightly lower in ER-positive breast cancer tissue samples compared to normal breast tissue (Fig. 6B). To trace the origin of miR-137, RT-qPCR analysis revealed significant enrichment of miR-137 in Tt-MSC-exos compared to MSC-exos (Fig. 6C). Notably, co-culture of MCF7 and T47D cells with Tt-MSC-exos markedly elevated intracellular miR-137 levels (Fig. 6D). The above results indicated that miR-137 is transferred to breast cancer cells through Tt-MSC-exos.

To verify the effect of miR-137 from Tt-MSC-exos on TAM resistance, we used miR-137 inhibitors to knockdown miR-137 in Tt-MSCs-exos, and the exosomes were extracted to treat breast cancer cells. We examined the protein levels of SERPINA3 in the cells. Western blot revealed that Tt-MSC-exos effectively downregulated SERPINA3 protein expression, but this suppression was reversed in the Tt-MSC-exos_miR-137 KD group (Fig. S4). The effects on the TAM-resistant phenotype of breast cancer cells were evaluated. The results showed that following the knockout of miR-137, the cell viability and migration capability of MCF7 and T47D cells continued to be inhibited by TAM (Fig. 6E, F). The ER-positive breast cancer xenograft models were established in nude mice, and the mice were treated with exosomes without miR-137 knockout or with miR-137 knockout. The results showed that Tt-MSC-exos without miR-137 knockout promoted tumor growth in the mice. In contrast, the growth of tumors in mice treated with miR-137-knockout Tt-MSC-exos was partially inhibited by TAM (Fig. 6G), which was consistent with the results of the in vitro cell experiments.

Discussion

In this study, we observed that co-culture with Tt-MSCs significantly reversed the tamoxifen-induced inhibition of ER-positive breast cancer cells. This observation aligns with previous reports suggesting that anti-tumor drugs can trigger a protective response in MSCs, thereby fostering a resistant tumor microenvironment [25]. MSC-secreted exosomes, which act as crucial mediators of intercellular communication with cancer cells, have been shown to influence the progression of multiple solid tumors. To validate whether tamoxifen resistance is mediated by Tt-MSC-exos, we suppressed Tt-MSC-exos release and observed a restoration of cancer cell sensitivity to tamoxifen. This indicates that Tt-MSC-exos are the primary mediators of TAM resistance. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, sequencing identified SERPINA3 as a key resistance gene.

SERPINA3 is one of the inhibitors of serine proteases and is mainly produced by the liver [26]. In the past, it was thought to be mainly involved in inflammatory processes. However, recent studies have found that the expression level of this gene may be associated with cancer and can act as a tumor promoter or repressor in different tumors [27]. In breast cancer, the role of SERPINA3 is subtype-dependent. In ER‑positive disease, it is an estrogen‑responsive gene linked to favorable hormone therapy response. In triple‑negative breast cancer (TNBC), SERPINA3 is upregulated and promotes tumor aggressiveness and chemotherapy resistance [28–30]. Conversely, in ER‑positive breast cancer, SERPINA3 was down‑regulated following aromatase inhibitor treatment, which contributed to resistance [31]. This is consistent with our results, with the difference that the drug used in that study was an aromatase inhibitor. The two complement each other and strengthen the evidence that SERPINA3 influences the efficacy of endocrine therapy.

In order to profoundly investigate the upstream regulatory mechanisms that modulate SERPINA3 expression, we predicted miR-137, which has a binding site to the 3′UTR of SERPINA3, online via the TargetScan website and verified this in dual luciferase reporter experiments. In previous studies, miR-137 expression is dysregulated in various malignancies [32–36] and regulates tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis. Notably, the function of miR-137 is highly dependent on breast cancer subtype. In TNBC, miR‑137 acts as a tumor suppressor by impairing stemness and attenuating chemoresistance [37–38]. In contrast, in our ER‑positive breast cancer model, we found that miR‑137 plays a different role. To determine whether miR‑137 regulates SERPINA3 in ER‑positive breast cancer cells, we examined SERPINA3 expression after knockdown or overexpression of miR‑137 in MCF7 and T47D cells by RT‑qPCR and observed that miR‑137 negatively regulates SERPINA3. Based on this, we further demonstrated that miR‑137 promotes tamoxifen resistance by targeting SERPINA3. These opposite functions of miR‑137 in different breast cancer subtypes highlight that its role is context‑dependent, determined by hormone receptor status and the specific gene targets available.

Next, it was found that co-culture of Tt-MSC-exos with ER-positive breast cancer cells dramatically increased the level of miR-137 in breast cancer cells, suggesting that Tt-MSC-exos may be one of the primary sources of miR-137 in breast cancer cells. In conclusion, the present study indicates that Tt-MSC-exos are likely to affect the expression level of SERPINA3 in breast cancer cells by delivering miR-137 to ER-positive breast cancer cells. Finally, it was verified that Tt-MSC-exos ultimately affected TAM resistance in ER-positive breast cancer through miR-137 by cell phenotyping experiments and in vivo experiments in nude mice.

The study still has some limitations. The specific molecular mechanism of TAM resistance induced by SERPINA3 down-regulation has not yet been elucidated.The signalling pathways that SERPINA3 may be involved in include the inflammatory response pathway [39], tumor suppressor signalling [40], NF-κB signalling [41], IL-1 and TNF-α signalling pathways [42, 43], and so on. In the process of secondary resistance to endocrine therapy in breast cancer, ER signalling pathway [44] and growth factor receptor pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR [45] are now more widely accepted to play a role. Our preliminary Western blot data showed that SERPINA3 knockdown under TAM treatment activated NF-κB and PI3K/Akt signaling and shifted the Bax/Bcl-2 balance toward anti-apoptosis (Figure S5). Our findings are consistent with previous reports. A study in HR-positive breast cancer indicated that high SERPINA3 mRNA levels predict a good prognosis and a favorable response to adjuvant hormone therapy [46]. This supports our conclusion that SERPINA3 is crucial for tamoxifen sensitivity. Additionally, SERPINA3 has been reported to act as a tumor suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma by activating PTEN, thereby inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway [47]. This aligns with our Western blot data, as downregulation of SERPINA3 led to activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway.These findings provide initial evidence that SERPINA3 loss may promote tamoxifen resistance through multiple pro-survival pathways. Further detailed mechanistic dissection is needed.

Despite evidence from our in vitro and xenograft models indicating that tamoxifen induces resistance in ER+ breast cancers via the Tt-MSC-exos miR-137 axis, clinical validation within the complex tumor microenvironment remains imperative. We selected human umbilical cord MSCs for this mechanistic study to ensure reproducibility, as patient‑derived MSCs exhibit significant heterogeneity and are challenging to obtain. The technical challenges in isolating and purifying tumor-associated MSCs from patients undergoing endocrine therapy have constrained clinical research. Nevertheless, our findings indicate the potential role of mesenchymal stem cells in endocrine therapy resistance. Future studies using patient‑derived MSCs or analyzing circulating exosomes from tamoxifen‑treated patients are needed to validate our proof‑of‑concept findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that Tt-MSC-exos promote TAM resistance through the miR-137/SERPINA3 axis in ER-positive breast cancer. Specifically, Tt-MSC-exos efficiently deliver functional miR-137 to ER-positive breast cancer cells, where this exosomal miRNA inhibits SERPINA3 expression. The downregulation of SERPINA3 enhanced TAM resistance in breast cancer cells. These findings demonstrated that SERPINA3 could be a novel predictive biomarker for response to endocrine therapy and also suggested a potential mechanism of cellular communication between stromal and malignant cells within the tumor microenvironment, which could serve as a therapeutic target. The mechanisms of breast cancer cell resistance mediated by various components of the tumor microenvironment need to be supplemented and refined by a large number of experiments in the future.
