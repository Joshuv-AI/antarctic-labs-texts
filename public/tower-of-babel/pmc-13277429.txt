Minimal adipose infiltration and good functionality of subscapularis after Latarjet using an inverted L-shaped tenotomy: results at minimum 10 years of follow-up
Authors: Chiara Fossati, Carlo Stoppani, Riccardo Parolini, Rossella Ravaglia, Mauro Gallazzi, Alessandra Menon, Pietro Simone Randelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Latarjet procedure is a well-established surgical technique for treating anterior shoulder instability. However, concerns remain regarding its long-term effects on subscapularis muscle integrity and glenohumeral joint health, particularly when using an inverted L-shaped tenotomy. This study aimed to evaluate subscapularis fatty infiltration and function, glenohumeral osteoarthritis, and bone graft osteointegration after a minimum 10-year follow-up.MethodsThis prospective study included 32 patients who underwent the Latarjet procedure with an inverted L-shaped subscapularis tenotomy and single-screw coracoid fixation before January 2014. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Rowe and Constant Murley scores (CMS), subscapularis-specific strength testing, and internal rotation strength measured with a dynamometer. Magnetic resonance imaging was used to assess subscapularis fatty infiltration according to the Goutallier classification, while glenohumeral osteoarthritis was graded on X-ray using the Samilson-Prieto classification.ResultsAt final follow-up, 78.3% of patients achieved Rowe scores of good to excellent, and the mean Constant Murley scores was 94 ± 5.9. The mean subscapularis strength was 5 of 5 on the Medical Research Council scale in more than 90% of the patients, with an average internal rotation strength of 7.4 kg. Fatty infiltration of the subscapularis was classified as Goutallier grade 2 or 3 in 21.9% of cases. Glenohumeral osteoarthritis was identified in two-thirds of patients; however, 50% of these cases were classified as mild (grade 1). Complete graft osteointegration was observed in all cases.ConclusionThe Latarjet procedure with an inverted L-shaped subscapularis tenotomy provides satisfactory long-term clinical and radiological outcomes, with preservation of subscapularis function and limited fatty infiltration. These findings suggest that this approach represents a viable surgical option for anterior shoulder instability; however, no direct comparison was performed, and results should be interpreted in light of the study limitations.

The Latarjet procedure is a widely performed surgical technique for treating recurrent anterior shoulder dislocation, aimed at enhancing anterior glenohumeral stability. Despite its effectiveness, the procedure is associated with potential complications, including recurrent instability, nonunion of the coracoid graft, restricted external rotation, and subscapularis tendon damage.7,11 The subscapularis, the most powerful muscle of the rotator cuff, plays a crucial role in the active dynamic stabilization of the anterior glenohumeral joint.12 Therefore, preserving its function during the Latarjet procedure remains a key concern.

The procedure is commonly performed via a deltopectoral approach, with ongoing debate regarding subscapularis management. Some surgeons favor a subscapularis-splitting technique, allowing preservation of muscle integrity, while others use an L-shaped tenotomy to optimize glenoid exposure.3 Advocates of the latter technique argue that it provides superior visualization, particularly during the early stages of the learning curve, facilitating accurate graft placement.

However, literature suggests that an inverted L-shaped tenotomy may predispose the subscapularis muscle to post-operative atrophy and fatty infiltration, potentially leading to muscular insufficiency and inferior clinical outcomes.18 Despite these concerns, few studies have investigated subscapularis functionality following this approach, and none have reported long-term outcomes.3 In addition, the development of glenohumeral osteoarthritis and coracoid nonunion remains a subject of concern in the post-operative period.1

The primary outcome of this study was to evaluate the degree of fatty infiltration and functionality of the subscapularis muscle at a minimum of 10 years after the Latarjet procedure using an L-shaped tenotomy approach. Based on previous literature reporting a prevalence of approximately 15.8% of Goutallier grade ≥2 fatty infiltration after subscapularis-splitting Latarjet at short-term follow-up,5 we aimed to evaluate whether the prevalence remains low even with an L-shaped tenotomy at long-term follow-up.

All patients provided written informed consent for imaging examinations and data management.

Attempts were made to contact all eligible patients by telephone; however, a substantial proportion could not be reached. Telephone follow-up was not considered adequate, as the study required standardized in-person clinical and radiological assessments.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under an interscalene brachial plexus block combined with general anesthesia, with the patient positioned in the beach chair position. A 7-8 cm incision was made using the deltopectoral approach, with the cephalic vein retracted laterally. The coracoacromial ligament was excised, and the pectoralis minor tendon was detached. The coracoid process was osteotomized to create a 2 cm bone graft, which was then decorticated and drilled to allow fixation.

An inverted L-shaped tenotomy of the subscapularis muscle was performed, preserving its inferior insertion. The anterior capsule was released to allow adequate glenoid exposure, and the labrum was not repaired, in accordance with the standard Latarjet technique. The anterior glenoid was prepared using a burr, and the coracoid graft was aligned flush with the glenoid surface and fixed using a single malleolar screw with washer.

The subscapularis was repaired using high-strength, nonabsorbable sutures. A suction drain was left in place for 24 hours. All patients were immobilized for four weeks, followed by a structured rehabilitation protocol.

Clinical and radiological assessment

All enrolled patients underwent clinical evaluation, including the Rowe score,16 the Constant Murley score (CMS),2 and subscapularis strength testing. Muscle strength was assessed using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, a 6-point grading system ranging from 0 (no muscle contraction) to 5 (normal muscle strength),8 and applied to the lift-off, bear-hug, and belly-press tests. Strength of the contralateral limb was used as a reference.

Internal rotation strength of the operated limb was further quantified using a handheld dynamometer, with the patient seated, the arm at the side of the body (0° abduction), the elbow flexed at 90°, and the forearm in neutral rotation.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed to assess fatty infiltration of the subscapularis muscle according to the Goutallier classification,19 and to evaluate coracoid graft osteointegration. Standard radiographs were obtained for all patients to assess glenohumeral osteoarthritis based on the Samilson-Prieto classification.17

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v6.0. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used for normality assessment. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and median with interquartile range [Q1-Q3]. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, with a significance threshold of P < .05.

Among the evaluated patients, 7 (21.9%) showed moderate to severe fatty infiltration (Goutallier grade ≥2), while 25 (78.1%) had absent or mild infiltration (grades 0 and 1). To further assess the condition of the subscapularis muscle, the degree of fatty infiltration in the remaining rotator cuff muscles was also evaluated using Goutallier's classification. All other muscles showed absent or mild fatty infiltration (grades 0 or 1). Data analysis showed that glenohumeral osteoarthritis was present in approximately two-thirds of the examined patients. Based on the Samilson-Prieto classification, 10 patients (32.3%) were classified as grade 0, 11 (35.5%) as grade 1, 6 (19.3%) as grade 2, and 4 (12.9%) as grade 3.

No cases of recurrent instability were reported. Complete osteointegration of the coracoid graft was observed in all patients, with no cases of nonunion.

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that the Latarjet procedure, performed using an inverted L-shaped tenotomy of the subscapularis, is associated with satisfactory long-term clinical outcomes with limited functional impact on the subscapularis muscle. The prevalence of fatty infiltration graded ≥ 2 according to the Goutallier classification (21.9%) is in line with values reported in the literature for the subscapularis-splitting technique, although only indirect comparisons can be made.5

However, the high attrition rate represents a major limitation and may introduce selection bias, as patients available for follow-up could represent a subset of more satisfied or asymptomatic individuals. Therefore, the reported clinical outcomes may be overestimated, and the rate of complications potentially underestimated.

Subscapularis function appeared to be preserved, as demonstrated by a mean internal rotation strength of 7.4 kg measured with a handheld dynamometer. This observation is consistent with Valencia et al,20 who observed recovery of subscapularis strength over time following a splitting approach.

Contrary to previous studies,3,5 specific subscapularis strength tests (lift-off, bear-hug, and belly-press) did not show significant differences between patients with and without fatty infiltration. Dos Santos et al5 observed a significant reduction in subscapularis strength proportional to the degree of fatty infiltration, suggesting a potential functional impact. In contrast, our results suggest that any impairment may be subtle and not detectable using standard clinical assessments.

Although no significant differences in subscapularis strength were observed between groups, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Given the small sample size (n = 32; n = 7 with fatty infiltration), the study is underpowered to detect meaningful differences in functional outcomes. Moreover, manual muscle testing using the MRC scale may lack sensitivity in detecting subtle deficits, particularly in high-functioning patients. In addition, dynamometric testing was performed only on the operated side, preventing objective side-to-side comparison.

However, comparing the strength of the dominant limb with that of the non-dominant limb may also be misleading.

The mean CMS in our cohort exceeded values reported in previous studies involving the L-shaped tenotomy approach,3,13 further supporting the effectiveness of this technique. Interestingly, the CMS was slightly higher in patients with fatty infiltration (94.14 vs. 93.44); however, this difference did not reach the statistical significance. Similarly, the mean Rowe score in our cohort (87.66) was consistent with values reported in the literature using the subscapularis-splitting technique.3,9,15 No significant differences were observed between groups, suggesting that subscapularis fatty infiltration may not substantially affect overall shoulder function or stability, although subtle deficits cannot be excluded.

While reported recurrence rates after the Latarjet procedure range between 2.5% and 5.9% in the literature,14,15 no cases of recurrent instability were observed in this series. However, this result should be interpreted with caution given the high attrition rate and the limited sample size, which may underestimate the true recurrence rate.

Glenohumeral osteoarthritis was observed in a substantial proportion of patients, although most cases were mild. Furthermore, these osteoarthritic changes cannot be directly attributed to the surgical technique alone and may instead reflect the natural history of shoulder instability, patient age, or other degenerative factors.

Another relevant outcome is the complete osteointegration of the coracoid graft in all patients, with no cases of pseudoarthrosis or graft dislocation. This is noteworthy given the use of single-screw fixation, which has been debated in the literature. Our findings support the feasibility of this fixation method in achieving stable graft incorporation.

Finally, no significant correlation was found between subscapularis fatty infiltration and degeneration of other rotator cuff muscles, suggesting that these changes may not reflect a generalized degenerative pattern.

Our data show functional outcomes, rates of fatty infiltration, and osteoarthritis progression that are in line with studies utilizing the subscapularis-splitting technique.3,5,18 Importantly, no significant strength deficits were identified through clinical testing, indicating that despite the presence of fatty infiltration, shoulder function remains well preserved. These results suggest that the L-shaped tenotomy approach may represent a viable option in the Latarjet procedure, although findings should be interpreted as descriptive and not indicative of superiority over alternative techniques.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have evaluated 10-year outcomes of the Latarjet procedure using MRI. This makes our series a unique contribution to the current literature and strengthens the value of our long-term findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. A primary limitation of this study is the high attrition rate, with only 21.1% of the originally eligible patients completing follow-up, primarily due to the inability to reach or recall patients for clinical reassessment. Although high attrition rates have been reported in long-term studies,4,6,10 the magnitude observed in the present study remains a significant limitation.

Second, while subscapularis strength was assessed bilaterally using the MRC scale, which enabled side-to-side comparison, this method is subjective and may lack the sensitivity to detect subtle strength deficits. Conversely, dynamometric testing was performed only on the operated side, thus preventing objective comparison with the contralateral limb.

Third, no pre-operative or contralateral shoulder MRI was available. This limits the ability to determine whether the observed subscapularis fatty degeneration is specifically related to the surgical procedure or reflects pre-existing or bilateral degenerative changes.

Finally, the procedures were performed by 3 different surgeons, potentially introducing variability in surgical technique and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

The Latarjet procedure using an inverted L-shaped subscapularis tenotomy is associated with good long-term preservation of subscapularis function in the available cohort, with limited fatty infiltration. This technique provides adequate glenoid exposure and allows accurate graft positioning, with satisfactory clinical and radiological outcomes observed at long-term follow-up.

These findings suggest that this approach represents a viable surgical option for managing anterior shoulder instability; however, no direct comparison with other techniques was performed, and the results should be interpreted in light of the study limitations.
