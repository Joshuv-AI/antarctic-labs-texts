Chagas disease across pregnancy and early infancy: Leukoencephalomalacia and achondroplasia in two unusual cases

Introduction

Chagas disease, caused by Trypanosoma cruzi, remains an important public health problem in Latin America and increasingly affects non-endemic regions through population mobility [1,2]. Although vector-borne transmission predominates, congenital transmission is increasingly relevant and may occur during any stage of maternal infection [3]. Most congenitally infected neonates are asymptomatic, contributing to underdiagnosis, whereas symptomatic disease may include prematurity, hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, respiratory distress, myocarditis, meningoencephalitis, and multiorgan failure [4,5]. Early diagnosis is critical because antiparasitic treatment during infancy is highly effective [5].

Neurologic involvement and neurologic sequelae after severe Chagas disease in early infancy remain incompletely characterized [6]. In critically ill infants, distinguishing direct central nervous system (CNS) involvement from secondary hypoxic–ischemic injury is particularly important. Moreover, the interaction between maternal T. cruzi infection, fetal development, and coexisting genetic disorders is poorly understood [7]. Achondroplasia, the most common skeletal dysplasia, is associated with respiratory complications and increased early-life mortality [8].

We report two unusual clinical presentations: acute Chagas disease in early infancy, with the route of transmission remaining uncertain, followed by extensive cystic leukoencephalomalacia; and maternal T. cruzi infection during pregnancy coexisting with fetal achondroplasia and fatal neonatal respiratory failure. These cases illustrate uncommon clinical scenarios and highlight unresolved questions concerning neurologic injury and developmental vulnerability in the context of T. cruzi infection.

Cases

Case 1

A 2-month-old female infant from El Salvador presented with diarrhea, vomiting, respiratory distress, hypotonia, abnormal sucking movements, gaze deviation, cyanosis, and poor peripheral perfusion. Oxygen saturation was 63% on room air, requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation for 7 days (Figure 1). On admission, she was hypothermic (35°C), hypotensive (53/31 mmHg), and bradycardic (72 beats/min). She developed refractory shock requiring dobutamine, acute kidney injury requiring peritoneal dialysis, severe metabolic abnormalities, and coagulopathy and had an 8-day intensive care stay (Supplementary Table S1).

The infant had been born at term after a pregnancy with limited prenatal care. Maternal screening for human immunodeficiency virus and toxoplasmosis, other agents, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus infections was negative; however, the mother was not tested for T. cruzi infection during pregnancy, and maternal Chagas disease was, therefore, not diagnosed at that time. The initial diagnosis was acute gastroenteritis with severe dehydration, metabolic acidosis, and septic shock. Examination showed pallor, hepatomegaly, and impaired peripheral perfusion.

Peripheral blood smear showed T. cruzi trypomastigotes, confirming acute infection (Figure 1), although the initial Strout test was negative. We initiated nifurtimox (20 mg/kg/day for 60 days). During hospitalization, necrotic lesions compatible with thrombotic complications developed in the extremities, together with diffuse petechiae, and enoxaparin was administered. Given the absence of maternal T. cruzi testing and entomological investigation, the route of transmission could not be definitively established, and congenital and early postnatal vector-borne transmission remained possible.

Clinical and parasitological improvement occurred during the first week of treatment. At 6 months, the Strout test remained negative. Cardiac evaluation showed no structural or functional abnormalities. However, brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated bilateral frontal cystic leukoencephalomalacia and symmetric ischemic lesions involving the parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes.

Case 2

A 23-year-old pregnant woman from a rural Chagas-endemic area of Sucre Department, Colombia presented with pelvic pain, headache with visual aura, bilateral eyelid edema, and palpitations. She lived in housing favorable to triatomine infestation and reported frequent exposure to wild animals. Tests for dengue, Zika, chikungunya, and syphilis, toxoplasmosis, other agents, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus infections were negative. There was no known family history of achondroplasia or other skeletal dysplasias.

At 32 weeks gestation, ultrasonography demonstrated findings consistent with achondrodysplasia (Figure 1). At 39 weeks and 2 days, examination showed bipalpebral edema, mild jaundice, cervical lymphadenopathy, rash, and hepatomegaly. Electrocardiography demonstrated ventricular extrasystoles and left anterior fascicular block (Figure 1). T. cruzi immunoglobulin G was positive by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and indirect immunofluorescence. Blood smears were negative, whereas polymerase chain reaction was positive, confirming maternal infection. She had not received specific treatment for Chagas disease before pregnancy, and no trypanocidal treatment was administered during pregnancy.

The neonate had achondroplasia, developed severe respiratory distress, and died 10 days after birth despite supportive care. We identified no alternative infectious or metabolic explanation for the fatal respiratory course. Neonatal testing for T. cruzi infection and genetic confirmation of an FGFR3 mutation were unavailable. Consequently, we could not confirm congenital T. cruzi transmission.

Discussion

These cases illustrate two uncommon clinical scenarios involving T. cruzi: severe acute infection in early infancy with an undetermined route of transmission, followed by extensive ischemic neurological sequelae, and maternal infection during pregnancy coexisting with fetal achondroplasia and fatal neonatal respiratory failure (Figure 1). Together, they highlight the clinical heterogeneity of T. cruzi infection during pregnancy and early life and the challenges of interpreting complex outcomes when infection coexists with developmental abnormalities [3,5,7]. Despite the absence of detectable cardiac abnormalities, the infant developed profound shock and hypoperfusion, respiratory failure, renal failure, metabolic abnormalities, and severe coagulopathy. In this context, the subsequent leukoencephalomalacia and ischemic lesions are most consistent with secondary hypoxic–ischemic brain injury resulting from systemic critical illness rather than primary T. cruzi-associated encephalitis or direct CNS parasitic invasion. This interpretation is further supported by the relatively unremarkable cerebrospinal fluid findings on day 14 and the absence of direct parasitological or molecular evidence of CNS infection. Accordingly, although acute T. cruzi infection contributed to the severe systemic illness, a direct neurologic effect of the parasite cannot be established. Nevertheless, the case highlights severe neurologic sequelae occurring after acute Chagas disease in early infancy [5,9].

Case 2 raises a different clinical question. Achondroplasia is primarily caused by activating FGFR3 variants that impair endochondral ossification and can be complicated by respiratory insufficiency and early mortality [8]. Because neonatal T. cruzi infection was not confirmed and molecular testing for FGFR3 was unavailable, no causal relationship between maternal infection, skeletal dysplasia, and neonatal death can be inferred. The coexistence is nevertheless noteworthy because pregnancy-associated immunologic changes can influence maternal parasitemia and congenital transmission, whereas T. cruzi infection can alter host cell signaling and inflammatory pathways [7,10].

Experimental observations indicate that T. cruzi can modulate growth-factor and tyrosine-kinase signaling. Whether gestational infection could interact with pathways relevant to fetal growth or modify the severity of an underlying skeletal dysplasia remains unknown. The present observation should, therefore, be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence that T. cruzi causes or modifies achondroplasia.

These cases reinforce two practical messages. First, acute Chagas disease, including possible congenital transmission, should be considered in critically ill infants from epidemiologically relevant settings, particularly those presenting with extracardiac manifestations. Second, screening pregnant women at risk and ensuring neonatal parasitological follow-up remain essential because maternal infection alone does not establish congenital transmission [5,11]. Rapid, accessible, and reliable diagnostic tools for congenital T. cruzi infection are also needed, particularly in resource-limited settings where conventional parasitological or molecular testing may not be readily available [12], [13], [14], [15]. Recent advances in point-of-care molecular approaches, including loop-mediated isothermal amplification, are promising but require further validation and implementation studies [16].

Limitations

The principal limitations are the descriptive nature of two cases and incomplete diagnostic characterization. Parasite genotyping, maternal parasite load quantification, and placental studies were unavailable. In case 1, maternal T. cruzi testing and entomological investigation were unavailable; therefore, although the infant had parasitologically confirmed acute Chagas disease, congenital transmission could not be demonstrated, and early postnatal vector-borne transmission could not be excluded. Moreover, neuroimaging was performed 6 months after the acute illness, preventing determination of the timing and mechanism of cerebral injury. In case 2, neonatal T. cruzi testing and FGFR3 molecular confirmation were unavailable; therefore, congenital transmission and any mechanistic relationship between maternal infection and achondroplasia cannot be established. Limited long-term follow-up further restricts interpretation.

Conclusions

Severe T. cruzi infection during early infancy may present with multisystem critical illness followed by neurologic sequelae secondary to hypoxic–ischemic injury, even without evident cardiac involvement. Maternal T. cruzi infection may also coexist with complex fetal disorders whose relationship to infection remains uncertain. These cases support systematic screening of at-risk pregnant women, timely parasitological evaluation and follow-up of exposed neonates, and cautious investigation of atypical developmental outcomes associated with maternal infection.
