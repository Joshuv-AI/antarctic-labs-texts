Abstract
. Air quality prediction is a challenging forecasting task due to its spatio-temporal complexity and the inherent dynamics as well as uncertainty. Most of the current models handle these two challenges by applying Graph Neural Networks or known physics principles, and quantifying stochasticity through probabilistic networks like Diffusion models. Nevertheless, finding the right balancing point between the certainties and uncertainties remains an open question. Therefore, we propose Double-Diffusion, a novel diffusion probabilistic model that harnesses the power of known physics to guide air quality forecasting with stochasticity. To the best of our knowledge, while precedents have been made of using conditional diffusion models to predict air pollution, this is the first attempt to use physics as a conditional generative approach for air quality prediction. Along with a sampling strategy adopted from image restoration and a new denoiser architecture, Double-Diffusion ranks first in most evaluation scenarios across two real-life datasets compared with other probabilistic models, it also cuts inference time by 50% to 30% while enjoying an increase between 3-12% in Continuous Ranked Probabilistic Score (CRPS).



1.
Introduction

Air pollution is a notorious environmental problem due to its lethal threats toward human health
(
Burns et al. 2020
;
Sivarethinamohan et al. 2021
)
as well as the future climate of the planet (e.g. carbon emissions)
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, being able to produce accurate forecasts is paramount towards managing these chronic issues.

Figure 1.
Current air quality DDPM vs Double-Diffusion: A rough forecast is calculated directly from physics diffusion ODE hence no learnable parameters are required. The result is fed in both forward and reverse processes for sampling acceleration and denoising.

In the field of deep learning, air pollution forecasting has gone through several stages of evolution, from the early experiments with Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) like
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Tao et al. 2019
;
Jin et al. 2021
)
to the recent endeavour of spatial-temporal graph neural networks (STGNN)
(
Qi et al. 2019
;
Xiao et al. 2022
)
. Moreover, with the popularity of Phyiscs-informed Neural Network (PINN)
(
Raissi et al. 2017a
;
Raissi et al. 2017b
)
and Ordinary/Partial Differential Equation(ODE/PDE) based neural networks
(
Chen et al. 2019
;
Rubanova et al. 2019
)
, attempts also have been made to combine known physics with neural networks
(
Zhou et al. 2022
;
Tian et al. 2024
)
in the bid of improved model robustness. However, due to the deterministic nature of these models, they are less proficient in capturing the embedded uncertainties in realistic air quality settings. Recently, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM)
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
has become the focal point in terms of time series and spatio-temporal prediction due to its stochastic sampling procedure by approximating the distribution of sample data
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
. While some early efforts have achieved promising results, they suffer from prolonged inference time due to the step-by-step noise sampling strategy
(
Tashiro et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Song et al. 2022
)
.

To this end, this paper presents Double-Diffusion, a denoising diffusion probabilistic model for air quality forecasting. As shown in Figure 1. The main difference that separates Double-Diffusion from existing DDPM paradigms is the utilization of known physics in both directions of the noise sampling process. Here, this physics-generated forecast serves two purposes: It allows the physics knowledge to be passed on into Double-Diffusion as a customized noise called residual noise during the forward process and as conditions for the denoiser for the reversal process. Secondly, it also functions as the foundation for an accelerated sampling scheduler. We summarize our contribution as follows:

(1)

We propose a novel strategy of fusing known physics into diffusion models without extra learnable parameters.

(2)

We reduce inference latency by 30% to 50% compared with other diffusion models for air quality forecasting.

(3)

We construct a new denoiser architecture to handle spatio-temporal sequences.

To better illustrate Double-Diffusion, we first outline the landscape of air quality forecasting in Section 2, and then elaborate on the foundations of DDPMs and how Double-Diffusion distinguishes itself from existing works in Section 3. Empirical results from Double-Diffusion and benchmarks are presented in Sections 4 and 5, with some model limitations and future work scope discussed at the end.

2.
Related Works

Air quality forecasting as a separate spatial-temporal downstream tasks has decades of research history, from the classic theory based models such as the Community Multiscale Air Quality(CMAQ) model
(
Wyat Appel et al. 2008
)
to the recent endeavors on diffusion probabilistic models like
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
, it would deviate the purpose of this paper if trying to illustrate all works done, instead, we focus on most recently developed methods using these deep learning techniques: spatio-temporal graph (STGNN) and attention based models, physics related neural networks, as well as denoising diffusion probabilistic models.

2.1.
Spatio-temporal graph and attention-based architecture

This class refers to the conventional data-driven deep learning based models,
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
developed a message-passing graph network called PM25-GNN through the enhancement of domain-specific knowledge like wind directions, temperature, humidity as well as pressure etc. This GNN resembles the process of a Gated Recurrent Unit(GRU), at each time steps, current time step graph representation of the aforementioned meteorological data along with previous time step hidden layer is fed into the GNN.

Another great demonstration of GNN is MasterGNN+
(
Han et al. 2023
)
, which leverages heterogeneous graphs along with recurrent neural network to capture the geological features for simultaneous air quality and weather forecast.
On the other hand, AirFormer
(
Liang et al. 2023
)
combines causal attention mechanism with Variational autoencoders (VAE) to capture both the spatial-temporal pattern as well as the uncertainty, they achieved state-of-the-art performance in terms of predicting short to medium future (1-72 hours) as well as in the scenario of sudden change.

Neverthelesss, both of these architectures inherit the underlying drawbacks from GNNs and attention. For example,
(
Alon and Yahav 2021
)
illustrates the over-squashing problem of GNNs for handling long range signals, which makes GNNs over-focus on the local connections instead of the global ones. On the other side, transformer based model is notorious for its quadratic complexity for the application multi-head attention
(
Vaswani et al. 2023
)
, which in the case of air quality prediction, would require tremendous computing power when covering a graph network with a large scale
(
Liang et al. 2023
)
.

2.2.
Physics-related neural networks

Due to the popularity of physics-informed deep learning networks
(
Raissi et al. 2017b
)
, air fluid dynamics are being applied in air quality prediction more frequently. Here, we focus on two types of physics-related air quality forecasting networks. The first paradigm utilizes the known physics differential equations to guide network architecture. For example,
(
Hettige et al. 2024
)
combined diffusion and advection air dynamics with Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODE) to predict PM2.5 concentration levels. This was further polished by
(
Tian et al. 2024
)
with an additional neural ODE to account for data-driven dynamics. Both achieved great results as they surpassed the previously mentioned graph-based networks (AirFormer & PM25-GNN) in terms of mean absolute error
(
Hettige et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2024
)
.

On the other hand, the second type of networks follows
(
Raissi et al. 2017b
)
and applies physics towards the learning steps of the networks.
(
Zhou et al. 2022
)
applied the same diffusion-advection differential equation as
(
Hettige et al. 2024
)
and
(
Tian et al. 2024
)
, but rather as extra regularization of the training loss, this way it can enhance the ability to make prediction that abides by physics laws.

However, due to the deterministic nature of these physics-related networks, most of them do not possess the ability to capture naturally embedded uncertainties in air pollution level fluctuation. On the other hand, while
(
Hettige et al. 2024
)
did incorporate a stochastic process via the reparameterization trick
(
Kingma and Welling 2022
)
into the encoder, it undermines the interpretability of physics ODE as the initial condition for the NODE may not be consistent with historical air quality readings.

2.3.
Diffusion probabilistic models

Denoising diffusion or commonly referred to as a diffusion probabilistic model is a generative network that aims to learn the distribution of sample data. While there are many types of diffusion models
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Cachay et al. 2023
;
Yuan et al. 2023
)
, they generally contain two parts – A forward noise addition or diffusion process and a backward noise removal process
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
, which are often called denoisers as well. As the diffusion model utilizes the input data alone to generate supervised signals, their generation process is usually unconditional
(
Song et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
. Nevertheless, several diffusion works are also done by feeding conditions, such as additional features of inputs to the network during the noise reversal process
(
Gao et al. 2024
;
Cachay et al. 2023
)
. These conditioned generative models can be more robust when dealing with domain-specific tasks
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
.

Recently there is an uprising trend of diffusion probabilistic models in time series and spatio-temporal analysis, including air quality forecasting. For example,
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
created DiffSTG, which is an U-Net based denoiser architecture with temporal and graph convolution layers that uses historical temporal reading as conditions to generate forecasts for multiple spatio-temporal tasks such as air quality and traffic. Another example comes from
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
, which applies an attention mechanism to the denoiser and uses embeddings generated from a pretrained graph neural network as conditions to tackle both forecasting and kriging tasks in air pollution and traffic sensing.

As great as it is, diffusion probabilistic models like DDPM suffer from heavy inference time complexity due to the necessity of an enormous number of sampling steps
(
Yue et al. 2023
)
, making it quite time comsuming for diffusion probabilistic models to handle spatio-temporal forecasting tasks with longer sequences.

Recently, a solution to handle long sampling time complexity was risen in the field of image restoration, where degraded images are being taken into consideration for faster and better performed diffusion models
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Yue et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024
)
. Nevertheless, the prerequisite of having a version of the original image makes this technique out of reach for spatio-temporal forecasting.
Inspired by both the residual learning of diffusion probabilistic models and physics ODE applications, this paper proposes a new air quality denoising diffusion model Double-Diffusion, that utilizes pollutant concentration diffusion ODE output as an incomplete version of the forecast for sampling expedition and robust performance.

3.
Methodology

3.1.
Problem Formulation

Consider a set of air quality sensors that assembles as an undirected graph
\mathbf{G}=(N,E)
, here
N
represents the nodes while E represents the edges among them. An adjacency matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is derived from the geological distance among air quality sensors. The goal of the air quality forecasting network
NET
is to use
H
timesteps of
C
features of historical air pollution
x_{t-H:t}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times N\times C}
along with the graph
\mathbf{G}
to predict
H^{\prime}
time steps of future air pollution level
{x}^{Pred}_{t+1:t+H^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{H^{\prime}\times N\times C}
.

As mentioned in section 2, Double-Diffusion aims for a faster sampling process by applying a residual learning technique from image restoration. The structure of Double-Diffusion is shown in Figure 1, it involves using an ODEsolver to produce a preliminary prediction based on historical data through air pollutant concentration diffusion ODE, before feeding the preliminary results as a ’degraded image’ into the diffusion probabilistic foward and reverse sampling scheme, which is also why the name of Double-Diffusion. We begin by discussing the general practices of transitioning from unconditional DDPM to conditional DDPM for air quality, and then moving on to the specific components of Double-Diffusion.

Figure 2.
Double-Diffusion – We use pollutant concentration diffusion ODE to generate a preliminary prediction for the future time steps before feeding it as conditions to the diffusion model, hence the name Double-Diffusion, blue arrow indicates training only steps, denoised
x_{0}
is the final output

3.2.
Unconditional DDPM

According to
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
, the original DDPM is unconditioned, and the forward diffusion process is a Markov chain which can be expressed as:

(1)

D(x_{1:S}|x_{0})={\prod_{k=1}^{S}}D(x_{s}|x_{s-1})

(2)

D(x_{s}|x_{s-1})={\mathcal{N}(x_{s};\sqrt{1-\beta_{s}}x_{s-1},\beta_{s}\mathbf{I})}

Here,
\beta
stands for the variance schedule that will increase the Gaussian noise portion within
x_{s}
as the sample step
s
becomes bigger. It can either be learned through the parameterization trick
(
Kingma and Welling 2022
)
or maintained as constant.
x_{0:S}
then becomes the target of the reverse process, where a network (also called a denoiser) is trained to output the noise portion of
x_{s-1}
using
x_{s}
as prior.
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
represents the reverse process as below:

(3)

y_{\theta}(x_{0:S})={\mathcal{N}(x_{S};0,\mathbf{I})}\prod_{s=S}^{1}y_{\theta}(x_{s-1}|x_{s}).

(4)

y_{\theta}(x_{s-1}|x_{s})=\mathcal{N}(x_{s-1};{\mu_{\theta}(x_{s},s),\Sigma_{\theta}(x_{s},s)})

The idea is to approximate the true distribution of
x_{0}
given
x_{s}
, which should be pure Gaussian noise, while
\mu_{\theta}
and
\Sigma_{\theta}
are parameters of the denoiser that can be learnt
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
.

3.3.
Conditional DDPM

Here we follow
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
by feeding both historical readings and the air quality sensor graph network into the denoiser and change DDPM from unconditional to conditional, as a result, equation (3) and equation (4) can be transformed into:

(5)

y_{\theta}(x^{A}_{0:S}|x^{a},\mathbf{G})={\mathcal{N}(x^{A}_{S};0,\mathbf{I})}\prod_{s=S}^{1}y_{\theta}(x^{A}_{s-1}|x^{A}_{s},x^{a},\mathbf{G}).

(6)

\begin{array}[]{l}y_{\theta}(x^{A}_{s-1}|x^{A}_{s},x^{a},\mathbf{G})=\mathcal{N}(x^{A}_{s-1};{\mu_{\theta}(x^{A}_{s},s|x^{a},\mathbf{G}),\Sigma_{\theta}(x^{A}_{s},s|x^{a},\mathbf{G})}).\end{array}

Here,
\mathbf{G}
represents the sensor network graph,
x^{A}
represents the full air quality sensor records from
t-H
to
t+H^{\prime}
(i.e.
x^{A}=concat(x_{t-H:t},x_{t:t+H^{\prime}})
), and
x^{a}
equals to
x^{A}
with
t:t+H^{\prime}
masked (i.e.
x^{a}=concat(x_{t-H:t},x^{mask}_{t:t+H^{\prime}}
). This way, both the history and future readings can be utilized to train the model.
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
choose to use zero to mask the future, whereas we use the preliminary prediction
\hat{x}
calculated by solving diffusion partial equation as the placeholder for future, hence in the case of Double-Diffusion,
x^{a}=concat(x_{t-H:t},\hat{x}_{t:t+H^{\prime}})
.

3.4.
Double-Diffusion

However, directly feeding the physics-based preliminary results into the denoiser does not improve the performance of the probabilistic diffusion process, this is because the preliminary results is not involved into the forward noise addition process during existing DDPM setup, hence the denoiser will only treat the preliminary results no more than a feature along side the historical input data
x_{t-H}
, which limits the utilization of such known physics knowledge. Double-Diffusion fills this gap by absorbing physics knowledge as the baseline to indicate how much the current air quality level deviates from the physics assumptions. This deviation is then fed into the sampling scheme along with normal probabilistic diffusion noises. We elaborate on Double-Diffusion by breaking it down into three components: Physics-based prediction generation, Noise sampling scheme with residual learning, and Zigzag Denoiser.

3.4.1.
Physics-based prediction generation

As mentioned in section 2, while there are several attempts to integrate physics with neural networks
(
Hettige et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2024
)
, these methods apply physics ODE in the latent space through encoding-decoding neural networks. Nevertheless,as physics in Double-Diffusion serves the purpose of providing a preliminary prediction instead of the final one, no parameterization is required. Therefore, here we first utilize the pure physics diffusion ordinary differential equation to produce a rough forecast towards future pollution level, which according to
(
Hettige et al. 2024
)
and
(
Choi et al. 2023
)
can be expressed with graph networks as follows:

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x(n)}{\partial n}=-k\,\Delta x(n)=-\,k\,L\,x(n)

As shown above,
x(n)
represents the pollution level at time
n
for all air quality sensors,
L
equals the Laplacian matrix, and
k
is the coefficient of the diffusion process.
(
Hettige et al. 2024
)
proved that this equation describes the diffusion of pollutants from high to low concentration areas through Fick’s Law
(
Paul et al. 2014
)
and the continuity equation. Nevertheless, an initial condition,
x(0)
is required to compute the continuous dynamics using such ODE. Unlike past works have tried to use hidden dimension produced by an neural network encoder as initial conditions
(
Hettige et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2024
)
, Double-Diffusion directly uses the last historical reading
x(t)
as the initial conditions, we follow
(
Hettige et al. 2024
)
and set
k=0.1
, this way
\displaystyle\hat{x}_{(t+1:t+H^{\prime})}
can be produced via any ODE solver as below:

(8)

\hat{x}_{(t+1:t+H^{\prime})}=ODESolver\,(x(t),\frac{\partial x(n)}{\partial n},[{n_{1}},...,{n_{H^{\prime}}}])

3.4.2.
Noise sampling scheme with residual learning

As previously mentioned, having physics-based diffusion conditions alone will not be enough, as the relationship between preliminary prediction and the actual future pollutant levels has not yet been fed into the model. As a result, it won’t be taken into consideration by the noise addition process
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
.

Therefore, extra measurements are required to ensure the denoiser learns from both Gaussian noise as well as the residual difference between the preliminary prediction and the truth. Having seen the recent uprising of diffusion probabilistic model for image restoration (IR)
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Yue et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2024
)
, it strikes us that the nature of preliminary prediction in air quality resembles a degraded image for image restoration, as both of them provide a sketch of the ground truth. Moreover,
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
further highlights that model performance benefits greatly by learning from Gaussian noise and residuals between the degraded image and the true image simultaneously.

Figure 3.
Denoiser ZigZag

Inspired by these residual learning techniques in IR, we adopt Resfusion from
(
Shi et al. 2024
)
for air quality forecasting. Resfusion blends the residuals derived from the degraded image and the target image into the noise addition and removal process, which forces the denoiser to learn from both the noise level and residual level at different probabilistic diffusion steps
(
Shi et al. 2024
)
. In our case, the residuals represent the gap between the preliminary prediction
\hat{x}_{(t+1:t+H^{\prime})}
and the target
x_{(t+1:t+H^{\prime})}
, we denote residuals
Res
as below:

(9)

Res=\hat{x}_{t+1:t+H^{\prime}}-x_{(t+1:t+H^{\prime})}

According to
(
Shi et al. 2024
)
,
Res
can then be fused into Gaussian noise and added gradually into
x^{A}
, hence, we can rewrite equation (1) as below:

(10)

D(x_{s}|x_{s-1},Res)={\mathcal{N}(x_{s};\sqrt{1-\beta_{s}}x_{s-1}+(1-\sqrt{1-\beta_{s}})Res,\beta_{s}I)}

(11)

x_{s}={\sqrt{1-\beta_{s}}x_{s-1}+(1-\sqrt{1-\beta_{s}})Res+\sqrt{\beta_{s}}\epsilon,\ \ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}

As the ground truth
x^{A}
is not available during inference stage,
(
Shi et al. 2024
)
also proved that there exists a time step
S^{\prime}
when
x^{A}_{S^{\prime}}
can be approximated using
X^{a}_{0}
as below:

(12)

x_{S^{\prime}}{\displaystyle\approx}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{S^{\prime}}}x^{a}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{S^{\prime}}}\epsilon)

(13)

\bar{\alpha}_{s}={\prod_{k=1}^{s}}(1-\beta_{k})

(14)

S^{\prime}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{i=1}^{S}\left|\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}-\frac{1}{2}\right|

Moreover, this also means that both training and inference stage of the DDPM can start from
S^{\prime}
, which skips all the steps before
S^{\prime}
and hence expedite the sampling process
(
Shi et al. 2024
)
.

3.4.3.
Denoiser Zigzag

The final step is to orchestrate a denoiser to learn from the guided noise illustrated above. We build our denoiser based on the foundation of DiffSTG’s UGnet
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
. The essence of DiffSTG comes from its delicate design of harmonizing temporal and spatial features with different granularities. According to
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
, this is done with UNet-based and Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) structures, respectively. However, as the original U-Net relies heavily on convolutional layers
(
Ronneberger et al. 2015
)
, so does DiffSTG. Nevertheless, convolutional networks prefer focusing on adjacent pair dependencies over distant ones when dealing with longer sequences
(
Vaswani et al. 2023
)
. As a result, we propose a denoiser called Zigzag, which follows DiffSTG’s strategy of capturing spatio-temporal relationships yet making improvements by integrating gmlp from
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
to handle longer sequences of air pollution forecasts.

As shown in Figure 3, unlike the original UNet that lowers the feature scales and back them back up, we first try doubling the temporal resolution, followed by two consecutive downscaling, and then finally restore the granularity back to normal. This zigzag shape architecture can capture temporal features both upward and downward.
At each level of temporal granularity, a Res-Block consists of multiple gmlps and a single GCN layer will extract features from both the spatial and temporal dimensions.

gMLP has been proven to produce the same level of accuracy compared with the transformer in vision and text tasks
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
. gMLP achieves inductive biases via Spatial Gating Units (SGU) that filter the information between different positions of the sequences. It splits the input along the channel dimension before performing spatial projection along the sequence dimension on one half and then dot product with the other half. This can be considered as an alternative to multi-head attentions in certain circumstances without having to increase the training time tremendously. We further improve the spatial-feature extraction by adding an SGU for GCN as well.

5.2.
Benchmarks

We compare Double-Diffusion against 3 probabilistic models, which include both spatio-temporal and time series forecasting networks.

•

DiffSTG
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
. It is a spatio-temporal diffusion networks that generate future predictions using history and graph adjacency matrix as conditions. DiffSTG utilizes a U-Net-shaped denoiser to capture temporal granularity at different levels
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
. This denoiser can fit diffusion schedules for both DDPM and Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM), we use DIFFSTG with DDPM in this setup.

•

AirPhyNet
(
Hettige et al. 2024
)
. AirPhyNet is a physics-guided neural ODE that makes forecasts based on pollutant concentration diffusion and advection ODEs. It is considered a probabilistic model as they sample their initial conditions of their Neural ODE from a distribution approximated by a Recurrent Neural Network, we use only the physics diffusion part of AirPhyNet as the benchmark to ensure the same input feature.

•

CSDI
(
Tashiro et al. 2021
)
. CSDI is a denoising diffusion probabilistic model improved base on DiffWave
(
Kong et al. 2021
)
that also takes historical data as conditions to generate forecasts. While it was originally designed for imputation of missing values, CSDI can also handle forecasting tasks by using both history and future as input and then masking all future time steps.

5.3.
Computing Setup

Double Diffusion is coded in PyTorch 3.11 and computed with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. We follow
(
Hettige et al. 2024
)
and adopt dopri5 to solve the diffusion ODE. As for the training schedule, to ensure fair comparison, we apply the setup from
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
. Specifically, the batch number for training is set at 32 for training and 64 for validation and test, with train, validation and test split ratio of 6:2:2 for both datasets. For all diffusion models applied in this setup, we set the sample number at 8, the denoiser hidden states at 64 and the diffusion steps at 100. In terms of evaluation metrics, we follow
(
Wen et al. 2023
;
Tashiro et al. 2021
)
and choose the evaluation metric of inference time Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS). The CRPS, according to
(
Matheson and Winkler 1976
)
, is used to measure how reasonable an estimated distribution is given the observed value, while MAE and RMSE are well-known deterministic evaluation metrics. According to past literature
(
Wen et al. 2023
;

7.
Limitations and future works

We see Double-Diffusion as the first step of tackling air quality forecasting tasks by integrating both the certainties and uncertainties within the surrounding environment, and there is still much work to be done.

First, we believe more baselines and datasets are required to further test model performance, both datasets used in this paper are from China, as cities within the same country may share certain patterns, adding tests on other countries’ air quality datasets may provide a better view on the model.

What is more, the current setup allows Double-Diffusion to use only past PM2.5 readings, as there are many auxiliary features such as traffic volume, geological landscapes, and other air pollutants which may be helpful to improve model performance. future works should also consider multi-pollutant air quality prediction for a more comprehensive forecast.

Lastly, as shown both in the ablation study and inference time comparison, preliminary forecasts based on pollutant concentration physics ODEs can be powerful tools to boost diffusion models, and there are still many known physics to explore, such as advection ODEs to simulate pollutant movements caused by wind
(
Hettige et al. 2024
)
. We plan on exploring these directions in the future to further enhance the predictability of diffusion probabilistic models.

8.
Conclusion

This paper presents Double-Diffusion, a novel diffusion probabilistic model that applies physics diffusion ODE to generate a PM2.5 concentration level forecast. The preliminary prediction calculated using pure physics provides a veiled version of the future target, and we apply resfusion
(
Shi et al. 2024
)
to infuse the information into the forward and reverse diffusion process of the model for faster sampling. Combined with a denoiser powered by gMLP and graph convolution layers, Double-Diffusion has greatly shortened the inference time by 30%-50% while still being able to maintain top performance when compared against other diffusion probabilistic models. What is more, it significantly reduced the inference time required when rising diffusion steps by hundreds. We believe this is a first step towards a physics-conditioned diffusion model for forecasting generation, and we will continue to build upon it.
