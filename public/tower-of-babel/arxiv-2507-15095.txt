Criticality in 1-dimensional field theories with mesoscopic, infinite range interactions
ab Kurt Langfeld, b Amanda Turner

Abstract
The study of critical phenomena in one-dimensional field theories with long-range interactions has garnered significant attention following the implications of the Hohenberg-Mermin-Wagner theorem, which precludes phase transitions in most low-dimensional theories with local interactions. This research investigates a novel class of one-dimensional theories characterised by a distinctly defined infinite interaction range. We propose that such theories emerge naturally through a mesoscopic feedback mechanism. In this proof-of-concept study, we examine Ising-type models and demonstrate that the natural emergence of phase transitions, criticality, and previously unidentified universality classes is evident.



I
Introduction

The well-known Hohenberg-Mermin-Wagner theorem

Mermin and Wagner 1966
;
Hohenberg 1967
addresses the question whether
or not long-range order can exist in a superfluid or superconductor at
non-zero temperature, in either one or two dimensions. For the
specific cases of a Heisenberg ferromagnet or
antiferromagnet, with SU(2) symmetry, or a magnetic system with U(1)
symmetry and an order parameter perpendicular to the symmetry
axis, rigorous proofs exist that translational long-range order
cannot exist. Mermin and Wagner addressed spins on a lattice while Hohenberg
considered bosons or fermions in the continuum. Already Hohenberg, in
his 1967 paper, pointed out that the absence of long-range order does
not rule out phase transition at finite temperatures. In fact, we now
understand, due to the seminal work by
Berezinskii
Berezinsky 1971
, Kosterlitz and
Thouless
Kosterlitz and Thouless 1973
;
Kosterlitz 1974
and of Nelson
and
Kosterlitz
Nelson and Kosterlitz 1977
, that there is a sharp transition
temperature in the 2-dimensional XY model, above which the behaviour
of the correlation function changes qualitatively, but never shows long
range order: above the critical temperature, the correlation function
falls off exponentially with distance, while below the correlation
function has power law fall off - a phenomenon that is known as
quasi-long-range order
.

Shortly after the Hohenberg-Mermin-Wagner studies of the ferromagnet,
Dyson
Dyson 1969
challenged the conventional
understanding of 1D systems by demonstrating that 1D Ising models can exhibit phase transitions if the interactions between the constituents decay as a power law, i.e.,

S=\sum_{i,k}J(i-k)\;z_{i}\,z_{k}\;,\hbox to28.45274pt{\hfill}J(r)\propto r^{-\alpha}\;,1<\alpha<2\;,

where
S
is the action with constituents
z_{i}=\pm 1
. The existence of phase transitions for
the one-dimensional ferromagnetic Ising model with long-range two-body
interaction has subsequently been an object of much
debate
Cassandro et al. 2009
;
Cassandro et al. 2014
;
Turban 2016
;
Halperin 2019
;
Suzuki 2025
.

Suzuki
Suzuki 2025
recently studied Ising models with
multiple interacting
spins on one- and two-dimensional lattices. The paper shows that in one
dimension, increasing the number of interacting spins enhances spin
correlations—especially at low temperatures for larger spin
values—while in two dimensions, these higher-order interactions lead
to stronger first-order phase transitions, with clear changes in
order, energy, and heat capacity as interaction complexity grows.

Apart from the question of the mere existence of phase transitions, the
concept of
universality
is a key concept with exceptional
phenomenological impact: systems with vastly different microscopic
details exhibit the same mesoscopic behaviour near phase
transitions. So-called universality classes are thereby defined by the
features dimensionality of the system, symmetries of the order
parameter and range of interactions, and systems within the same class
share identical critical exponents and scaling functions. Since
universality ensures that microscopic details are irrelevant at
criticality, experimental data from one system can validate
theoretical models developed from others, making it a powerful tool in
both verifying theories and guiding new experimental
investigations. In solid state physics, universality has helped to
understand phase transitions in magnetic systems
Ma 1976
,
phenomena such as
superfluidity and superconductivity
Zinn-Justin 2021
or quantum
critical behaviour in systems like heavy fermion compounds and
low-dimensional magnets
Sachdev 2011
. Although biological
systems are more complex and less “ideal” than physical systems,
universality still emerges in contexts where collective behaviour
dominates. Networks of neurons in the brain display critical-like
dynamics, and the observation of critical avalanches of activity in networks of cultured
neurons suggests they operate near a critical
point
Mora and Bialek 2011
. Models like the Vicsek model show that groups
of animals (e.g., birds, fish) exhibit phase transitions between
ordered and disordered motion. These transitions belong to specific
universality classes
Cavagna and Giardina 2014
. Protein folding is
often described in terms of an energy landscape, with valleys
representing stable folded states and hills representing unstable or
partially folded conformations. The transition from unfolded to folded
state can, in many proteins, resemble a thermodynamic phase
transition, particularly for small, fast-folding proteins, where
folding occurs cooperatively
Bryngelson and Wolynes 1987
;
Nelson Onuchic et al. 2000
.

The paper by Fröhlich and Spencer
Fröhlich and Spencer 1982
is a
landmark result in mathematical physics and statistical mechanics
since it not only establishes a phase transition in a
one-dimensional (1D) Ising model with
1/r^{2}
interaction but it also
suggests a new universality class distinct from both the standard 1D
short-range Ising class (no phase transition) and 2D/3D Ising models
(with well-known exponents).

In this paper, we study 1-dimensional field theories for which the
interaction between elementary degrees of freedom depends on
mesoscopic observables such as the energy density or magnetisation of
the systems. We
illustrate this mesoscopic feedback mechanism for a densely packed
polymer system. Despite the infinite range and multi-spin
interactions, those theories are well defined by the nature of their
origin. We illustrate this further in the context of 1-dimensional
field theories that naturally emerge on a subspace of a higher
dimensional field theory by “integrating out” degrees of freedom. We
then study in detail Ising spin systems with such mesoscopic induced
interactions and explore a novel type of universality emerging.
These models turn out to be amenable to analytic approaches. We are
able to rigorously establish the presence of new 1-dimensional
universality classes which correspond to a break in symmetry (Section

II
), and obtain the values of critical exponents (Section

III
).

I.1
Rise of 1D field theory with mesoscopic long-range interactions

In this paper we introduce a novel class of one-dimensional non-local field
theories in which the fundamental constituents interact through
conventional local interactions, yet the parameters governing these
interactions are dynamically dependent on global field
configurations. This mesoscopic feedback mechanism allows the system’s
parameters—such as coupling strengths or temperature-like
quantities—to be modulated by the state of the
entire field. As a result, these systems naturally exhibit complex
collective behaviour, including self-organised criticality, emergent
phase transitions, and rich dynamical phenomena not present in purely
local theories. The interplay between local dynamics and global
feedback encapsulates a new paradigm for modelling statistical
systems, with potential applications ranging from condensed matter
physics to biological and social systems.

In the standard Ising model on the line with open boundary conditions, the degrees of freedom are spins
z_{i}\in\{-1,+1\},i=1\dots L
, where
L
is the total number of spins. The
local
standard action is given by the
standard nearest neighbour interaction:

S\;\propto\;\sum_{i=1..L-1}z_{i}z_{i+1}\;.

In this paper, we will explore versions of Ising models, the actions of which are
(simple) functions of
S
, such as

S^{2}\;\propto\left(\sum_{i=1..L-1}z_{i}z_{i+1}\right)^{2}=\;\sum_{i,j=1..L-1}z_{i}z_{i+1}z_{j}z_{j+1}\;.

This induces a type of non-locality where all
spins interact with each other.
We motivate the study of models with actions of this form below by showing that this type of interaction can occur naturally in many physical systems.

Denote the action of a field theory by

S(\theta;\kappa)
, where

\theta(x)
encapsulates the degrees of freedom in 1-dimension
spanned by the variable
x
, and
\kappa
encapsulates the model parameters. If
S
is chosen so that

S(\theta;\kappa)
, has only short range interactions for the field

\theta
for given parameters
\kappa
, non-locality can be induced
by introducing a dependence of the parameters
\kappa
on observables
(expectation values) of the field theory such as action density or
magnetisation. Examples include models for social dynamics or opinion
models, where individuals modify their openness to influence (noise
level) based on the perceived consensus
Sîrbu et al. 2017
.

To illustrate how non-locality arises, we will explore in more detail the example of feedback-driven effective temperatures in protein
thermodynamics. We adopt a rigid polymer model where polymers are
described by a set of angles
\theta_{1}\ldots\theta_{L}
specifying
the position of rigid links along the polymer chain (see
figure
1
). A typical local action is given by

S\;=\;\sum_{a,i=1..L-1}\beta\;\cos\Bigl(\theta^{(a)}_{i+1}-\theta^{(a)}_{i}\Bigr)\;+\;V(\theta),

where
i
labels the position and
a
the polymer in a multi-polymer
ensemble. The parameter
\beta
describes stiffness. For low
\beta
, the angles are (almost randomly) oriented leading to a low action
“crumpled-up” state of the polymers, whereas for large
\beta
,
the state with
\theta_{i}=\,
constant is preferred leading to
“stretched-out” polymer ensembles. The potential
V
ensures that
the polymers are confined to the encasing (shown as the boxes in
figure
1
(middle)). More interaction terms could be added that ensure
self-avoidance.

Let us now introduce mesoscopic feedback by
considering polymers in a dense environment. If the polymers are in a low
action state and if each of them is mainly confined to a sphere then, with
self-avoidance and densely packed spheres, it is more difficult for the
polymers to move to the higher action state of a linear
configuration. Consequently, the effective stiffness parameter should be
lower. Conversely, if the polymers are in a high action, linear state, it
would be more difficult in a dense environment to reach a crumpled
state and consequently the effective stiffness parameter should be higher.

Rather than working with a microscopic theory of many polymers, if we are only
interested in their geometry we can
switch to an effective description of a 1-polymer state where the stiffness parameter is now taken as an increasing function of the
action density

s\;:=\;\frac{1}{L-1}\sum_{i=1..L-1}\;\cos\Bigl(\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}\Bigr)\;,

for example
\beta(s)=\kappa s
. Note that this is an intensive quantity as needed for the stiffness.
A simple effective model for the angles

\theta_{1}\ldots\theta_{L}
of a single polymer in a dense environment is then given by

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1..L-1}\beta(s)\;\cos\Bigl(\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}\Bigr),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{L-1}\left(\sum_{i=1..L-1}\;\cos\Bigl(\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}\Bigr)\right)^{2}\;.

We next discuss a second motivation for a non-local 1D field
theory, which belongs to the large class of models that emerge from
higher dimensional field theory. These emerging theories (QFTs) are
central to understanding holography, dimensional reduction, and
effective field theories. Examples are rooted in both
string theory
Maldacena 1999
;
Witten 1998

and condensed matter physics
Hartnoll et al. 2008
, where
low-dimensional QFTs emerge through compactification, boundary
effects, or dualities.

To illustrate how a mesoscopic type of interaction with infinite range
emerges from a local 3D field theory, we consider Ising spins
z_{i}=\pm 1
,
i=1\ldots 3
that exist in the 1-dimensional subdomain at the

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in(x,y)}z_{x}J_{\ell}z_{y}\;-\;\frac{1}{4\kappa}\sum_{\ell}J_{\ell}^{2}\;-\;\kappa_{2}\,\sum_{x,(\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3})\in x}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{(J_{\ell_{1}}-J_{\ell_{2}})^{2}}{J_{\ell_{1}}^{2}+J_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}+\frac{(J_{\ell_{1}}-J_{\ell_{3}})^{2}}{J_{\ell_{1}}^{2}+J_{\ell_{3}}^{2}}+\frac{(J_{\ell_{2}}-J_{\ell_{3}})^{2}}{J_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+J_{\ell_{3}}^{2}}\right]\;.

The first sum extends over all links

\ell

The 1D effective theory emerges by “integrating out” the interaction

\kappa_{2}=0

Integrating over the scale

Standard local Ising model:
Here, we need to solve

\bar{a}(e)=\beta
. The function

is monotonic leaving us

provides the expectation value

Non-local Ising model with

The crucial

\bar{a}(e)=\kappa e
. Possible intersection

. If we start increasing

. The transition is second order.

Non-local Ising model with

We now encounter

\bar{a}(e)=\omega e^{2}

. The question is whether there are

III.1
Internal energy and specific heat

The internal energy density is defined by

\langle A_{\ell}\rangle/(L-1)\;,\hbox to28.45274pt{\hfill}\ell=2,3\;.

(26)

For the
S^{2}
theory, we observe typical 2nd order behaviour: the
internal energy density is small in the symmetric phase and then rises
smoothly for couplings
\kappa
greater than the critical value. For
large volumes
L
, this behaviour is more pronounced. The energy
density develops a cusp in the infinite volume limit but remains
smooth (see figure
5
). By contrast, the
S^{3}
theory shows a sharp rise at the
critical coupling
\omega_{c}
, the steepness of which increases with
increasing volume. Increasing
L
from a moderate
101
to
1001
lets
us anticipate the 1st order jump of the energy density in the infinite
volume limit (see figure
5
).

In the following, we will focus on the interacting case of the second
order transition of the non-local 1D Ising model with
S^{2}
action.

The specific heat
C
is a further thermodynamical observable with high
phenomenological relevance:

C_{L}(\kappa)\;=\;\left(\langle A^{2}\rangle\;-\;\langle A\rangle^{2}\right)\;/\;(L-1)\;.

(27)

It can be viewed as an integrated correlation function and is therefore
proportional to a power of the correlation length
\xi
.
Close to the second order transition, the correlation length
\xi

diverges with a scaling law of the type

\xi\propto\left|1-\frac{\kappa}{\kappa_{c}}\right|^{-\nu}\;,\hbox to28.45274pt{\hfill}\nu>0\;,

(28)

where
\nu
is the so-called critical exponent.
Close to the transition, the singularity is usually described by a

power law
or
logarithmic
behaviour:

\displaystyle C_{L}(\kappa)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle|t|^{-\alpha^{\prime}},\;\;\hbox{for}\;\kappa>\kappa_{c}\;,\;\;t:=\frac{\kappa_{c}-\kappa}{\kappa_{c}}\;;

(29)

\displaystyle C_{L}(\kappa)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle-\ln\,|t|\;.

(30)

We can relate the divergence of
C
to that of the correlation length
by noting
|t|\propto\xi^{-1/\nu}
. At finite volume

L
and for
\kappa=\kappa_{c}
, the (in the infinite volume
divergent) correlation length
\xi
is limited by
L
. We then observe

\displaystyle C_{L}(\kappa_{c})

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\left(|t|^{-\nu}\right)^{\alpha/\nu}\;\propto\;\xi^{\alpha/\nu}\;=\;L^{\alpha/\nu}\;,

(31)

\displaystyle C_{L}(\kappa_{c})

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\ln\xi\;=\;\ln L\;.

(32)

The later equations are ideal to study finite size scaling: We
calculate the specific heat for several
values
L
as a function of
\kappa
. At the (pseudo-) critical
coupling
\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}\approx\kappa_{c}
, the specific heat
is maximal. From the function

L\to C_{L}(\kappa_{\mathrm{max}})\;,

we then can infer the scaling via (
31
) or (
32
).

Figure

, left panel, shows the specific heat as a function
of
\kappa

increases with increasing

moves closer to the infinite volume limit

\kappa_{c}=1

We find that the first three terms of the expansion excellently fit

Let us now investigate the scaling with the system size

A convenient way to
extract the critical coupling and the exponent

is by means of the
Binder cumulant:

U_{L}\;=\;1\;-\;\frac{\langle O^{4}\rangle}{3\,\langle O^{2}\rangle^{2}}\;,

(33)

where
O
is the order parameter of the statistical system. In the
broken phase, order parameter expectation values are dominated by a
non-vanishing scale
O_{s}
:

\langle O\rangle=0\;\;\;\hbox{(finite L)},\hbox to28.45274pt{\hfill}\langle O^{4}\rangle\approx O_{s}^{4},\hbox to28.45274pt{\hfill}\langle O^{2}\rangle\approx O_{s}^{2},

which suggests that the Binder cumulant approaches
2/3
for large

\kappa\gg\kappa_{c}
. In the disordered phase, the
probability distribution of the order parameter

Gaussian: the moments fulfil the relation

\langle O^{4}\rangle\;\approx\;3\,\langle O^{2}\rangle^{2}

and the cumulant vanishes for
\kappa\ll\kappa_{c}

At the critical coupling
\kappa=\kappa_{c}

\kappa

therefore can be used to categorise statistical systems in

Close to the phase transition, the only relevant ratio of scales is the

\displaystyle\frac{2\langle O^{4}\rangle[\langle O^{2}A_{2}\rangle-\langle O^{2}\rangle\langle A_{2}\rangle]}{3\,\langle O^{2}\rangle^{3}}\;.

IV
Conclusions

We have constructed a novel family of one‐dimensional field theories
in which coupling strengths depend explicitly on mesoscopic
observables (e.g. magnetisation or energy density), thereby generating
effective infinite‐range interactions that evade the constraints of
the Mermin–Wagner theorem. As a consequence, these models sustain
bona fide critical phenomena—including spontaneous symmetry breaking
and emergent long‐range order—despite their intrinsically low
dimensionality.

By augmenting the conventional nearest‐neighbour Ising action with
non‐local feedback terms proportional to powers of the local action
(denoted
S^{2}
and
S^{3}
), we identify two new 1D universality
classes, each characterized by critical behaviour irreducible to any
known class in one, two, or three dimensions. In particular, we
observe:

•

The
S^{2}
‐model undergoes a continuous, second‐order phase
transition at
\kappa_{c}=1
. The specific heat diverges logarithmically
with system size and the correlation‐length exponent is anomalously
large (
\nu\approx 2
), signalling non‐trivial scaling beyond traditional Ising
limits.

•

The
S^{3}
‐model exhibits a discontinuous, first‐order
transition at
\omega\approx 2
, marked by an abrupt jump in internal energy
and the emergence of a dominant ferromagnetic phase. This
dichotomy underscores the decisive role of the feedback functional
form in setting the transition order.

We have further demonstrated that these non‐local interactions admit
natural physical realisations—ranging from the stiffness‐dependent
conformations of dense polymer chains to one‐dimensional effective
theories obtained via dimensional reduction of higher‐dimensional
field models. Such derivations confirm that mesoscopic feedback and
attendant long‐range couplings can arise organically in both physical
and biological contexts, thereby enhancing the models’ empirical
relevance.

Our analysis is supported by exact analytic calculations of partition
functions, density of states, and Binder cumulants, complemented by
high‐precision numerical studies employing finite‐size scaling to
extract critical exponents. All results cohere to validate the
presence of genuine phase transitions in one dimension and to
substantiate the classification of these systems into hitherto
unexplored universality classes.

In summary, one‐dimensional field theories endowed with mesoscopic
feedback–induced infinite‐range interactions not only contravene the
traditional prohibition of 1D criticality but also inaugurate new
avenues for theoretical and applied research. These findings invite
further exploration of mesoscopically regulated interactions in
complex systems, with potential implications spanning statistical
field theory, soft‐matter physics, and beyond.
