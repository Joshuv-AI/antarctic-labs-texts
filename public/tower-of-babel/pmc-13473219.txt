A bibliometric analysis of research on developmental dysplasia of the hip (2000–2025): Trends, leading contributors, impact, and collaboration patterns
Authors: Murat Koçak, Feza Korkusuz, Mehmet İsmail Safa Kapıcıoğlu, Hakan Ömeroğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIt was aimed to evaluate publication trends, scientific impact, and collaboration patterns in developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) research.MethodsDDH-related publications indexed in the Web of Science database between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2025, were analyzed. Publication output, citation metrics, author and institutional collaboration networks, leading journals, and major research themes were assessed.ResultsA total of 3462 articles were identified. Annual publication output increased substantially over the study period. The most cited article, by Steppacher et al., reported long-term outcomes of periacetabular osteotomy. Sankar WN (USA) was the most prolific author, while Clohisy JC (USA) had the highest citation impact and h-index. Shanghai Jiao Tong University (China) produced the greatest number of publications, whereas Western institutions achieved higher citation impact despite lower publication volumes. The USA led in publication output, while Switzerland demonstrated the highest citation impact. North American authors and institutions showed the strongest international collaboration rates, with the USA occupying the central position in the global collaboration network. The Journal of Pediatric Orthopaedics published the largest number of DDH-related articles. Research themes exhibited notable geographical variation.ConclusionDDH research has grown rapidly over the past 25 years. North American and Western European researchers have played a leading role through strong international collaborations and publication in high-impact journals. Publication quality and global partnerships appear to be key determinants of scientific influence in the field.Significance of the studyThis study provides novel bibliometric insights into the current landscape of DDH research for trainees, researchers, clinicians, and policymakers.

Introduction

Incidence 1 and overall pooled prevalence 2 of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) ranges from 0.06 to 76.1 per 1000 live births and approximately 14 cases per 1,000 infants, respectively, presenting considerable variations across racial groups and geographic regions. 3 It is a major cause of functional disability due to the early development of degenerative hip osteoarthritis. The global disability weight for DDH has been classified as nearly equivalent to a severe musculoskeletal condition of the lower limb. Utility weights for DDH have also been estimated across several countries, ranging from 0.26 to 0.89 (where 0 represents death and 1 indicates full health). 4 Among patients aged 40 years or younger, DDH represents the underlying etiology in one-quarter of total hip arthroplasties. 5

Bibliometric analysis is a systematic approach used to identify and evaluate relevant research within a specific field of interest. It provides a quantitative overview of publication trends, author contributions, academic productivity, and journal quality. This method focuses on measuring and summarizing key bibliometric indicators, while also examining research components, authorship patterns, collaboration networks, and the intellectual structure of a given research domain. 6 Bibliometric indicators quantify the volume and characteristics of scholarly output rather than its clinical significance, rendering them more suitable for academic evaluation and historical documentation than for assessing impact on clinical practice.

DDH has been a longstanding area of interest among researchers. From the bibliometric perspective, DDH ranked second among the most frequently studied topics in pediatric orthopaedics, with a considerable clinical impact and enduring research significance within the field by number of publications. 7 Studies however specifically addressing the global bibliometric analysis on DDH remain limited in number. A limited number of publications have previously analyzed author, institutional, and country/region contributions, as well as funding sources, journal types, and citation patterns within the field.8–11 We assumed that publications on DDH have increased steadily over years and demonstrated measurable trends in authorship patterns, institutional and geographic distribution, citation impact (CI), researcher and institutional collaborations, journal preferences, and research themes.

This bibliometric analysis evaluated the global research trends in DDH using publications indexed in the Clarivate Web of Science (WoS) database between the years 2000 and 2025. The aim of this study was to identify the leading researchers, institutions, countries and journals contributing to DDH research. Total number of articles, citation counts, h-index, category normalized citation impact (CNCI), impact relative to world (IREW) and collaboration patterns were assessed. Author and institutional collaboration networks were additionally analyzed. Articles were grouped by journal impact factor quartiles (Q1-Q4). Influential researchers, institutions and countries involved in DDH publication including prevention, early diagnosis, and conservative and surgical treatments were covered.

Methods

A cross-sectional bibliometric study was conducted, including data retrieved from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2025. The guide of the Global Research Council (https://globalresearchcouncil.org/) on Responsible Research Assessment (RRA) launched by Science Europe (https://www.scienceeurope.org/news/practical-guide-for-rfos/) was followed.

A specific search query for the topic (TS) = “developmental dysplasia of the hip” OR “congenital dislocation of the hip” was performed in the WoS database (accessed January 15, 2026) to identify relevant publications. The TS field tag was used to screen titles, abstracts, and keywords.

The retrieved publications were evaluated using a set of well-established bibliometric measures grouped under four main categories:

Publication Output: Total number of documents, annual growth rate, and mean document age were recorded.

Citation Performance: Total citation counts, average citations per document, and the H-index were calculated to assess the overall scholarly impact of the literature.

Normalized Impact Measures: The CNCI was used to evaluate each article’s citation performance relative to the global average for documents with the same subject category, document type, and publication year. The IREW was also calculated to compare the citation impact of country-specific publications.

Journal Quality: All journals were classified into quartile groups (Q1 to Q4) based on Journal Citation Reports (JCR) classifications within the WoS platform.

Collaborative relationships among authors and affiliated institutions were modeled as bibliometric networks and rendered through dedicated network visualization software. Node centrality indices, clustering coefficients, and overall network density are the key structural parameters derived to pinpoint dominant research hubs, characterize prevailing patterns of scientific cooperation and delineate discrete thematic clusters. Node size was scaled proportionally to publication volume, whereas edge thickness encoded the frequency and strength of pairwise collaborative ties, thereby enabling an intuitive visual appraisal of the field’s structural architecture within each network map.

The data extraction process consisted of six steps, with bibliometric analysis as the final stage (Figure 1). Articles indexed in the Scopus and PubMed databases were not included. Only English-language articles were considered, and conference abstracts and proceedings, editorials, news items, and retractions were excluded (Figure 1). While analyzing the leading contributors across different search fields in DDH, management subgroups were defined, including “newborn hip screening and hip ultrasonography”, “brace treatment”, “closed/open reduction”, “pelvic osteotomy in younger children”, “pelvic osteotomy in adolescents and young adults”, “hip arthroscopy”, and “total hip arthroplasty”. Relevant keywords, including those representing the most commonly performed procedures, were then generated for each group.Figure 1.Data extraction process.

Top 5 leading authors in different research areas

Analysis of the leading contributors across different DDH research areas showed that the top five authors in newborn hip screening and hip ultrasonography were all from Turkey (Table 6). Among these authors, Dogruel H demonstrated a notable CI with his three publications. Authors investigating abduction brace treatment were primarily affiliated with institutions in North America (Table 7), whereas research on surgical treatment at earlier ages was led mainly by authors from North America and China (Table 8). In studies addressing closed or open reduction of DDH, Weinstein SL achieved a high CI. Research on DDH-related single pelvic osteotomies in children was predominantly conducted by authors from Taiwan and Turkey (Table 9). In contrast, studies on pelvic osteotomies in adolescents, young adults and adults were led mainly by authors from the USA and Germany (Table 10). Notably, the Boston group demonstrated substantially higher CIs in publications concerning pelvic osteotomies in young adults and adults. Discussion

In the present study, a holistic view of research contributions and impact quality over quantity was the main focus. Therefore, number of articles and citations were monitored, evaluated and reviewed. We analyzed 3462 DDH-related articles published between 2000 and 2025. Over the past 25 years, the sevenfold increase in DDH publications reflects the field’s successful integration into mainstream academic discourse and its emergence as a recognized area of sustained scholarly inquiry. This substantial growth is particularly notable, with annual publication counts exceeding 220 and approaching 300 between 2020 and 2025. These findings are consistent with those reported in previous bibliometric studies on DDH.10,11

Higher number of citations indicate greater academic impact of the publications. The geographic and institutional distribution of the ten most influential papers shows a strong concentration in the USA, which accounts for six of the ten articles. Seven of the ten articles primarily focus on various aspects of diagnosing and treating DDH sequelae in adults. The journals publishing these influential studies are predominantly general orthopaedic or subspecialty orthopaedic journals. A study analyzing the 100 most-cited articles on DDH published between 1950 and 2009 found that Ganz R authored the highest number of influential articles, while the USA led in total citations. Approximately three-quarters of these articles focused on therapeutic or diagnostic topics, and the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery–American Volume published nearly one-third of these articles. 9

The geographic distribution of the top ten leading authors highlights the predominance of North America, while the remaining five are based in Europe and Asia. Some leading authors demonstrate sustained, high-impact scholarly output over extended periods, whereas others exhibit lower citation impact despite substantial publication volume, likely due to the temporal lag in citation accumulation. The 2015–2025 period demonstrates particularly strong productivity across multiple authors, suggesting a recent surge in high-impact DDH research. These findings suggest that leadership in DDH research is shaped by both quantitative productivity and qualitative impact, with major pediatric orthopaedic centers serving as key drivers of high-output research programs.

A strong degree of internal collaboration, accompanied by moderate external connectivity, is evident among the authors. Globally, DDH research appears to be organized within geographically defined communities, with varying levels of international integration. Researchers in North America and China demonstrate the highest levels of collaboration, whereas specialized European groups continue to exert substantial influence through high-impact publications despite maintaining more selective collaborative networks.

The institutional analysis highlights notable geographic and strategic differences in DDH research approaches. North American institutions dominate the list and collectively exhibit high CI along with a balanced distribution across journal quartiles, particularly strong in Q1 and Q2 journals. Institutions in England demonstrate exceptional research quality, achieving the highest citation impact, strong placement in Q1 journals, and robust levels of international collaboration.

Improving research quality and reliability is equally important for accelerating real-world impact. Centrality of collaboration is therefore extremely important. Collaboration can improve responsible research culture, equity, open science and societal impact. Collaboration between institutions has increased over the years.10,11 In the present study, institutional research and international integration strategies vary significantly across regions. Western institutions operate within a densely interconnected network—particularly through intercontinental partnerships characterized by high CI and strong rates of international collaboration. Despite high research productivity, lower levels of international collaboration of the Chinese institutions are associated with reduced CI and more peripheral positions within the global research network.

Geographical contributions to DDH research demonstrate significant disparities, similar to those observed in the institutional analysis. Among the top 10 contributing countries, substantial differences are identified in publication volume, CI, and journal quartile distribution. These findings suggest persistent challenges in international research visibility and integration. North American and European countries, which show higher rates of international collaboration and a greater proportion of publications in Q1 and Q2 journals, also demonstrate markedly higher CIs. The core of a dense, productive network is primarily centered in North America, Western Europe and East Asia. Eastern Europe, Africa and Latin America contribute with smaller volumes of research and exhibit weaker link strengths. The greater international connectivity of North American and then Western European authors and institutions may be partly explained by their established research and clinical fellowship programs, which host physicians from diverse countries. The participation of these fellows in research activities likely contributes to the development of extensive international collaborations and the production of multiple, high-quality publications.

In the present study, Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research and the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery-American Volume achieve the highest total citation counts. This diversity reveals that while the highest volume of DDH research is published in specialized pediatric journals, the most highly cited and impactful “landmark” studies have been published in general orthopaedic journals. These findings suggest that authors seeking broader visibility and greater citation impact may preferentially submit their work to high-impact Q1 journals.

The most frequent topics among the 100 most-cited DDH articles were osteotomies and hip ultrasonography. 9 Publications between 1994 and 2013 primarily focused on imaging methods and diagnosis, whereas studies published between 2014 and 2024 showed a greater emphasis on treatment methods. 10 In the present study, research areas in DDH demonstrate notable geographical variation. Turkish researchers have taken a leading role in studies on newborn hip screening and hip ultrasonography. A probable explanation for this prominence is the implementation of a nationwide ultrasonographic newborn hip screening program in Turkey since 2013, in a country with nearly one million live births annually. 12 Conservative treatment of DDH with abduction bracing represents a major research focus among North American researchers. Studies on closed and open reduction of DDH are predominantly conducted by Chinese and North American researchers, with the Iowa team appearing to make particularly significant contributions in this field. These trends suggest that researchers in these regions are especially focused on the management of late diagnosed or neglected DDH cases through conservative or minimally invasive surgical approaches at younger ages, combined with structured long-term follow-up. Single pelvic osteotomy in DDH particularly in children younger than school age appears to be a particular area of interest among researchers from Taiwan and, subsequently, from a certain region of Turkey, possibly reflecting a relatively high prevalence of late-presenting cases in these regions due to various healthcare and socioeconomic factors. Research on reconstructive pelvic osteotomies in adolescents and young adults is predominantly led by contributors from the USA, who demonstrate substantially higher publication and citation counts. This trend reflects the leading contribution of authors from the USA to the field of periacetabular osteotomy, a complex surgical procedure that is preferably undertaken by well-trained and experienced surgeons in specialized centers. The largest number of publications on the management of DDH sequelae through hip arthroscopy or total hip arthroplasty originates from East Asia, particularly China, where the prevalence of such complex cases is likely high. However, despite the substantial publication volume, the CI of all but one of these studies remains relatively limited.

This study has several limitations. First, only the WoS database was used. The exclusive use of WoS was a methodological choice rather than a matter of convenience. WoS provides fully standardized citation metadata through its integrated InCites analytics platform, which is the only environment in which CNCI and IREW can be computed in a directly comparable and reproducible manner; equivalent normalized metrics are neither natively available nor methodologically interchangeable within Scopus or PubMed. Furthermore, the JCR quartile stratification system used for journal quality assessment is intrinsic to the WoS infrastructure, ensuring internal consistency across all impact-related analyses conducted in this study. Second, the analysis was restricted to English-language publications. The exclusion of non-English articles from WoS may have resulted in an underestimation of the scientific contributions and impacts of authors and institutions in many countries. Third, citation counts of recent articles may be lower because they have not had enough time to receive citations. Fourth, the restriction of search terms may have resulted in the incomplete retrieval of relevant studies. Last but not least, the present analysis did not include the publications published before 2000, despite the fact that numerous influential and classic articles on DDH appeared during that period. Consequently, the historical development of the field may not be fully represented.

In conclusion, publication volume alone does not define leadership in DDH research worldwide. The results of this bibliometric analysis demonstrate that the DDH field is largely driven by researchers and institutions in North America and Western Europe, particularly those in the USA, whose influence is reinforced by extensive international collaborations and selective publication in high-impact journals. These observations emphasize the importance of publication quality and global partnerships as major determinants of scientific influence. Furthermore, preferred research themes related to patient age groups and management strategies demonstrate notable geographical variation. We believe that this study provides valuable and novel bibliometric insights for trainees, researchers, clinicians, and policymakers regarding the current landscape of DDH research.
