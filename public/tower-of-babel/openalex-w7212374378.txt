Abstract
Magnesium is proposed as an insulin sensitizer. Prior meta-analyses pooled diabetic and non-diabetic populations and
suggested benefit, but none isolated the effect on insulin resistance in non-diabetic adults or examined whether it is
reproduced across independent research groups. The objective was to estimate that effect and to test its
reproducibility. PubMed was searched for randomized controlled trials of oral magnesium, as the sole differing
component, versus control in non-diabetic adults reporting the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance.
The primary outcome was the mean difference in that measure. A random-effects model was fitted by restricted
maximum likelihood, with pre-specified subgroup, leave-one-out and risk-of-bias analyses. Seven trials comprising
559 participants were meta-analysed. Overall, magnesium reduced insulin resistance by a mean difference of −0.82
(95% confidence interval −1.57 to −0.08), but heterogeneity was very high and the prediction interval spanned harm
to benefit. Heterogeneity was almost entirely explained by study origin: three trials from a single group in Durango,
Mexico, using a magnesium chloride oral solution, showed a large effect, whereas four independent replications were
null and homogeneous. The between-subgroup difference was highly significant. The only trial at low risk of bias was
independent and null, and the pooled effect lost significance on removing a single trial. In conclusion, the apparent
benefit of magnesium is confined to trials from one research group, and current evidence does not support magnesium
supplementation for insulin resistance outside deficiency states.
Keywords: Magnesium; Insulin resistance; Prediabetic state; Dietary supplements; Reproducibility of results.

1. Introduction
Magnesium is a cofactor for enzymes of glucose metabolism and insulin signalling, and observational data link low
magnesium status to insulin resistance and to incident type 2 diabetes (de Baaij et al., 2015). On this basis, oral magnesium
supplementation has been proposed as an insulin sensitizer, and prior meta-analyses reported improvements in insulinsensitivity parameters (Simental-Mendía et al., 2016; Veronese et al., 2021).
Those syntheses, however, pooled participants with established diabetes together with non-diabetic and at-risk
individuals, and did not isolate the effect on the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (Matthews et al., 1985) in
a strictly non-diabetic population. A distinct and clinically relevant question is whether magnesium improves insulin resistance
before diabetes develops (Tabák et al., 2012) and, equally important, whether any such effect is reproducible across
independent investigators. Reproducibility across research groups is now recognised as a core criterion for judging a body of
evidence rather than an optional refinement (Ioannidis, 2005; Munafò et al., 2017).
The objective was to estimate that effect and to test its reproducibility. PubMed was searched for randomized
controlled trials of oral magnesium, as the sole differing component, versus control in non-diabetic adults reporting the
homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance.

2. Methodology
This is a bibliographic study (Snyder, 2019), specifically classified as a systematic literature review with quantitative
synthesis. Following the classification of research approaches proposed by Gil (2026) and by Risemberg et al. (2026), the
study adopts a quantitative approach: the corpus was assembled through the systematic selection of randomized controlled
trials, of which eight met the eligibility criteria and seven provided extractable data, and the synthesis of their results was
carried out by statistical meta-analysis, without a qualitative component in the treatment of the data.

2.1 Eligibility criteria
Following the PRISMA 2020 guideline (Page et al., 2021), randomized controlled trials of parallel, crossover or
factorial design were included when they enrolled non-diabetic adults aged 18 years or over with insulin resistance or
metabolic risk, namely prediabetes, impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance, metabolic syndrome, overweight
or obesity, polycystic ovary syndrome, or non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Trials had to compare oral magnesium, of any salt
or dose, with a non-magnesium control such as placebo or usual care, where magnesium was the only component differing
between the compared arms, and had to report the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance.
Trials in established type 1, type 2 or gestational diabetes were excluded (Rodríguez-Morán & Guerrero-Romero,
2003), as were trials with fixed co-supplementation, for example magnesium combined with vitamin E, vitamin D or multimineral formulas, in which magnesium could not be isolated; special critically ill or organ-failure populations; non-randomized
designs; and studies without an extractable insulin-resistance outcome.
2.2 Search and selection
PubMed was searched from inception to 2024, combining terms for magnesium, insulin resistance and metabolic risk
with a randomized-trial filter, restricted to humans and excluding overtly diabetic or gestational populations. Titles and
abstracts were screened with the assistance of a large language model; potentially eligible reports underwent independent fulltext assessment. For a factorial trial with a magnesium-alone arm, only that arm versus placebo was used.
2.3 Data extraction and risk of bias
Two reviewers independently extracted the per-arm sample size and the homeostatic model assessment of insulin
resistance, at baseline and at the end of the intervention or as change, together with the population, the magnesium salt and
dose, and the duration. Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane RoB 2 tool across five domains with an overall judgement
(Sterne et al., 2019). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.
2.4 Statistical analysis
The primary outcome was the mean difference in the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance, calculated
as magnesium minus control, so that negative values favour magnesium. Estimates were pooled with a random-effects model
by restricted maximum likelihood using the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010). Heterogeneity was quantified with the
inconsistency statistic and tau squared, a 95% prediction interval was computed (IntHout et al., 2016), and a subgroup analysis
by study origin was pre-specified, contrasting trials from the Durango group using a magnesium chloride solution with
independent trials, together with leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. Formal testing for small-study effects was not undertaken,
because funnel-plot and regression-based methods are unreliable with fewer than ten studies (Egger et al., 1997; Sterne et al.,
2011).
2.5 Role of artificial intelligence
The literature search and the initial title and abstract screening were assisted by a large language model. All
subsequent steps, namely eligibility, data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment, were conducted independently by the two
authors, who reconciled differences by consensus and are responsible for the data reported.

3. Results
3.1 Study selection
The search returned 111 records. At title and abstract screening, 77 records were excluded and 34 reports underwent
full-text assessment, of which 26 were excluded for established diabetes as the target condition, fixed co-supplementation
preventing the isolation of magnesium, critically ill or organ-failure populations, non-randomized design, or absence of an
extractable insulin-resistance outcome. Eight trials met the eligibility criteria. Seven reported an extractable outcome and were
meta-analysed. One crossover trial (Chacko et al., 2011) reported fasting insulin but not the calculated homeostatic model
assessment and was retained in the narrative synthesis only. The flow of records is shown in Figure 1.

3.3 Overall effect on insulin resistance
Pooled across all seven trials, magnesium reduced the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance versus
control, with a mean difference of −0.82 (95% confidence interval −1.57 to −0.08; p = 0.031). Heterogeneity, however, was
very high, with an inconsistency of 85% and tau squared of 0.82, and the 95% prediction interval, from −3.34 to +1.70, ranged
from substantial benefit to harm, indicating that the pooled point estimate is not a reliable summary of a consistent effect.
3.4 The effect is explained by study origin
A pre-specified subgroup analysis resolved the heterogeneity almost entirely. The three trials from the Durango group,
using a magnesium chloride solution, showed a large reduction, with a mean difference of −1.68 (95% confidence interval
−2.39 to −0.97), whereas the four independent trials were null and homogeneous, with a mean difference of +0.04 (95%
confidence interval −0.37 to +0.44; inconsistency 0%). The between-subgroup difference was highly significant (p <
0.000001) and residual heterogeneity fell to 18%. In other words, essentially all of the apparent benefit, and all of the
heterogeneity, is attributable to which group conducted the trial.

3.6 Study not pooled
Chacko et al. (2011), a crossover trial in overweight adults, reported fasting insulin, glucose and inflammatory
markers but not the calculated homeostatic model assessment, and so could not contribute to the primary analysis. Its
metabolic findings were broadly neutral, consistent with the independent-trial subgroup.

4. Discussion
4.1 Main finding
In this updated meta-analysis restricted to non-diabetic adults, the apparent benefit of magnesium on insulin resistance
was confined to three trials from a single research group using a specific magnesium chloride oral solution, while four
independent replications were null and mutually consistent, with an inconsistency of 0%. The naive pooled estimate of −0.82 is
therefore misleading: it is a weighted average of a large single-group signal and a null independent signal, not evidence of a
reproducible effect.
4.2 Interpretation
Two non-exclusive explanations deserve consideration. First, the large effects arose predominantly in populations
selected for hypomagnesemia or marked metabolic abnormality, so a genuine benefit may be restricted to magnesium-deficient
individuals rather than to the general non-diabetic population (de Baaij et al., 2015). Second, the clustering of all positive
results within one group and one formulation, together with the higher risk of bias of those trials and the loss of significance on
leave-one-out analysis, raises the possibility of group-specific or formulation-specific effects that have not replicated, a pattern

evidence does not support a general recommendation of magnesium supplementation to improve insulin resistance in nondiabetic adults.
4.3 Relation to previous reviews
Earlier meta-analyses reported that magnesium improves insulin-sensitivity parameters, but they pooled diabetic and
non-diabetic participants and did not test consistency by study origin; notably, at least one of those syntheses shares authorship
with the Durango trials (Simental-Mendía et al., 2016; Veronese et al., 2021). By restricting the population to non-diabetic
adults, focusing on a single outcome measure and pre-specifying an origin subgroup, the present review reframes the evidence:
the signal is real in the data but not independently reproduced.
4.4 Limitations
Only seven small-to-moderate trials contributed, populations and magnesium formulations differed, and endpoint
values were pooled. One trial reports dispersion explicitly as the standard error of the mean, which was treated here as the
standard deviation (Zhao et al., 2024); this understates the variance of that trial and therefore over-weights it, although the trial
sits in the null independent subgroup, so the effect of the approximation is to strengthen rather than weaken the main finding.
One trial showed a numerical baseline difference in the outcome, of 4.6 versus 5.2 units, which the trial itself reported as nonsignificant (Guerrero-Romero et al., 2004). The search was limited to PubMed, and publication bias could not be formally
assessed with seven studies (Sterne et al., 2011). The origin subgroup, though pre-specified and both mechanistically and
methodologically motivated, remains observational and should be interpreted as hypothesis-strengthening rather than
definitive.

5. Conclusion
Among non-diabetic adults, the reduction in insulin resistance attributed to magnesium is driven entirely by trials
from a single research group, while independent replications show no effect and the overall result is fragile. Magnesium
supplementation should not be recommended to improve insulin resistance in non-diabetic adults on current evidence, except
possibly in documented deficiency. Independent, adequately powered trials at low risk of bias are needed to settle the question.
