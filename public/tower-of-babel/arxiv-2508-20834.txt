High-Resolution Atomic Magnetometer-Based Imaging of Integrated Circuits and Batteries
Dominic Hunter, Marcin S. Mrozowski, Stuart J. Ingleby, Timothy S. Read, Allan P. McWilliam, James P. McGilligan, Ralf Bauer, Peter D. Schwindt, Paul F. Griffin, Erling Riis

Abstract
We present a high-resolution magnetic imaging system based on a free-induction-decay (FID) optically pumped magnetometer (OPM), integrated with a two-axis scanning micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) micromirror for automated beam steering. A double-pass optical geometry enables the millimeter-scale device under test (DUT) to be placed directly behind the vapor cell, minimizing the standoff distance and thereby improving spatial resolution, which is fundamentally limited by atomic spin diffusion and the optical beam waist. The functionality of the imaging system was tested on a diverse set of magnetic sources, including a custom printed-circuit board (PCB) with alternating copper tracks, whose measured results agreed well with theoretical predictions based on the Biot–Savart law. The system successfully resolves spatially varying current distributions, identifies polarity-dependent asymmetries in an integrated bridge rectifier chip, and tracks the dynamic current evolution of a ceramic battery in situ. These capabilities highlight its potential for noninvasive diagnostics of electronic circuits and batteries.



I
Introduction

Magnetic field imaging is a powerful diagnostic tool with applications spanning biomedical science
[
1
]
, integrated circuit (IC) analysis
[
2
]
, industrial monitoring
[
3
]
, and security
[
4
]
. While these diverse use cases impose varying demands on sensor design, all require the ability to resolve both the spatial and temporal characteristics of magnetic signatures with high precision. Among the most promising technologies for meeting these requirements are optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs), which can be fabricated to satisfy low size, weight, and power constraints while routinely achieving sub-picotesla sensitivities under ambient conditions
[
5
,
6
]
.

A variety of sensing modalities have emerged to address specific magnetic imaging challenges. For example, nanoscale nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond have been used to map activity in ICs
[
7
]
. While the spatial resolution of NV-based sensors exceeds that of OPMs by several orders of magnitude
[
8
]
, their achievable magnetic precision is typically much lower, often by a similar factor, due to limitations such as photon shot noise and material imperfections. Consequently, considerable attention has recently been directed toward bridging the gap between spatial resolution and sensitivity by targeting the sub-millimeter regime, where OPMs can operate effectively while preserving high sensitivity.

For example, radio-frequency (RF) alkali vapor magnetometers have been used for non-destructive detection of concealed defects
[
9
]
, where a conductive sample was mechanically actuated using a translation stage. This technique is primarily used to image conductive materials by measuring the eddy currents induced in the target, rather than detecting magnetic fields from ferromagnetic materials, or the direct current sources considered in this work. RF OPMs have also been employed for electromagnetic induction imaging by raster scanning light beams across the vapor cell in an orthogonal pump–probe configuration
[
10
]
. While the RF sensing mode offers impressive sensitivity, it exhibits a narrow—albeit tunable—dynamic range, and therefore typically requires active or passive magnetic field compensation to maintain optimal operating conditions.

In biomedical applications, most efforts have focused on developing sensor modules that can be integrated into large-scale sensor arrays
[
11
]
. This approach is partly motivated by the high technology readiness level of these devices and their commercial availability. However, the spatial resolution of such systems generally remains on the order of centimeters, limited by the physical dimensions of the sensors and their self-contained module designs
[
12
]
. Notably, there have been demonstrations of multi-channel arrays based on a single vapor cell for biomedical imaging
[
13
]
. Such configurations can enhance spatial resolution, and improve common-mode noise rejection when operated in a gradiometric arrangement.

OPM-based magnetic imaging within a single vapor cell presents an exciting pathway toward achieving high-sensitivity performance at the sub-millimeter scale. Recently, a spatial light modulator based on a digital micromirror device (DMD) was implemented with an OPM in the spin-exchange relaxation-free (SERF) configuration, achieving a single-channel sensitivity of
\mathrm{25\,fT/\sqrt{Hz}}
with a spatial resolution of
216\,\mathrm{\mu{m}}

[
14
]
. DMDs have also been demonstrated using the free-induction-decay (FID) modality
[
15
]
; however, the reported sensitivity was
\mathrm{10\,pT/\sqrt{Hz}}
, and the dynamic range was limited to less than
5\,\mathrm{\mu{T}}
. In contrast, the system presented in this work demonstrates sub-
\mathrm{pT/\sqrt{Hz}}
performance using a FID-based imaging system that can be operated at high bias field’s (e.g.,
\sim 50\,\mathrm{\mu{T}}
)
[
16
]
.

FID sensors offer broad and tunable bandwidths extending into the kilohertz regime, made possible by flexible digital signal processing (DSP) techniques
[
17
]
. In this work, a Hilbert transform-based DSP method
[
18
]
is employed for rapid frequency extraction, offering over an order-of-magnitude enhancement in processing efficiency over nonlinear fitting algorithms while preserving accuracy and precision. Notably, because the optical pumping beam is extinguished during the detection phase of the FID cycle, systematic effects such as light shifts are strongly suppressed, improving measurement accuracy. Furthermore, the FID method allows free-running operation without the need for field modulation or feedback, making it ideal for robust, high-dynamic-range applications.

The primary goal of this work is to develop an automated magnetic imaging system capable of robust and repeatable mapping of diverse magnetic sources. Here, we present an approach using an FID-based OPM incorporating a microfabricated micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) vapor cell and a two-axis electro-static gimbal-less scanning MEMS micromirror. The system features a double-pass optical geometry, which minimizes the standoff distance between the vapor cell and the device under test (DUT). This enhances spatial resolution by capturing stronger near-field magnetic signals before significant field decay occurs, a common constraint in high-resolution magnetic imaging. This limitation can be further mitigated through microfabrication of thinner alkali vapor cells and employing thin reflectors, thereby reducing the standoff distance further. The imaging system is fully automated and performs raster scanning to acquire spatially resolved magnetic field measurements with high sensitivity and contrast.

We showcase the system’s capabilities by imaging magnetic fields from various sources, including a custom printed-circuit board (PCB) with alternating current paths, an integrated bridge rectifier chip under different bias current conditions, and a ceramic battery during both charging and discharging cycles. The system resolves polarity-dependent asymmetries, spatial current distributions, and time-varying field behavior with high contrast and repeatability. Its performance in finite-field conditions makes it a compelling candidate for scalable, noninvasive diagnostics in electronics and battery monitoring in industrial environments.

In this FID system, the pump beam is pulsed while the probe light is continuous-wave (cw), thereby temporally separating the optical pumping and readout phases of the magnetic measurement. The alkali vapor is optically pumped and probed using separate, co-propagating laser beams at wavelengths of approximately 852.3 nm and 894.6 nm, corresponding to the Cs D

<10~\mu

1/\Delta{t}=2
MHz

.

To improve optical pumping efficiency and facilitate compact integration, the vapor cell is embedded in a custom flexible Kapton PCB with a thickness of
100\,\mu

\sim 4~
mT) parallel to the propagation axis of the optical beam is applied during the pumping phase, synchronized with the optical pulse

\gamma_{2}

[
29
]
. Secondly, the coils provide thermal control to heat the vapor cell and ensure adequate atomic density. Magnetic noise from the heating circuit is effectively eliminated by switching off the field during the detection phase
[
17

[
17
]

The sensor head is enclosed in a four-layer mu-metal magnetic shield (MS-1L, Twinleaf), which passively suppresses ambient magnetic interference. The shield includes internal coils that enable precise control of the static magnetic field vector, as well as auxiliary gradient coils used to cancel spatial inhomogeneities. All coils are powered by a highly stable programmable current driver
[
30
]
, whose noise is negligible compared to the intrinsic noise floor of the magnetometer.

II-B

Frequency Estimation

During the detection phase, the atomic spins undergo Larmor precession at a frequency determined by the magnitude of the local magnetic field

\omega_{L}=\gamma B_{0},

(1)

where
\gamma\approx 3.5
Hz/nT is the gyromagnetic ratio of Cs. The atomic polarization decays due to several relaxation mechanisms, including collisions with the cell walls, interactions with buffer gas atoms, and spin-exchange collisions among Cs atoms
[
23
,
31
]
. A typical signal resulting from this precession, taking the form of a damped sinusoid, is depicted in Fig.
2
(a).

Multiple techniques are available for extracting the Larmor precession frequency, each offering different trade-offs in sensitivity, temporal resolution, and computational efficiency
[
32
]
. Fitting the data to a nonlinear damped sinusoidal model has been adopted in previous works
[
20
,
21
,
16
,
17
]
, and enables frequency estimation with a precision approaching the Cramér–Rao lower bound (CRLB), which represents the theoretical limit set by the signal-to-noise ratio and damping rate
[
33
]
. Here, we demonstrate an alternative method based on a Hilbert transform.

173\,
kHz. Inset: zoomed-in view, denoted by the black dashed lines, showing the in-phase (blue) and quadrature (orange) components extracted after applying finite impulse response (FIR) filters. (b) Frequency response of the bandpass (solid blue) and Hilbert transformer (dashed orange) FIR filters. The gain ratio between the two filters is overlaid in gray on the secondary axis, showing variation within the
\mathrm{70–500\,kHz}
passband. (c) Top: Instantaneous phase
\phi(t)
extracted from the argument of the analytic signal (see Eq.
3
). Bottom: residuals from a linear fit (blue) and from a fit including an additional exponential term (purple).

The Hilbert transform approach
[
18
]
offers significantly greater computational efficiency, which makes it particularly advantageous for the magnetic imaging strategy employed here by enabling faster scanning rates. Two finite impulse response (FIR) filters are applied independently to the raw FID signals. The first is a Hilbert transformer FIR filter, which imparts a
90\,^{\circ}
phase shift to all frequency components within a specified passband. The second is a matched bandpass filter with a gain response identical to that of the Hilbert transformer, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(b). The outputs of these filters yield the quadrature
Q(t)
and in-phase
I(t)
components, respectively, from which the complex analytic signal is constructed as

z(t)=Q(t)+i\,I(t).

(2)

The accuracy of this method depends on the gain ratio, defined as the ratio of the gain responses of the two FIR filters. Within the flat passband, the gain ratio varies by around
0.0005\,\%
, representing negligible systematic uncertainty introduced by filter mismatch. Although the passband can be extended to lower frequencies with comparable gain ratio variation, doing so requires additional filter coefficients, resulting in a longer impulse response. Consequently, more of the initial portion of the FID signal must be truncated, ultimately degrading the achievable sensitivity.

The FID signal is linearized by computing the instantaneous phase, defined as

\phi(t)=\arg\{z(t)\}=\mathrm{tan^{-1}}\bigg(\frac{Q(t)}{I(t)}\bigg),

(3)

as shown in Fig.
2
(c). This can be expressed in terms of the angular Larmor frequency,
\omega_{L}
, and an initial phase offset,
\phi_{0}
, as

\phi(t)=\omega_{L}t+\phi_{0},

(4)

assuming that
\omega_{L}
is constant. A linear fit applied to the unwrapped instantaneous phase data yields the Larmor frequency from the fitted slope. This approach offers high computational efficiency and can reach the CRLB after accounting for the filter impulse response
[
5
]
.

The variation in the linear-fit residuals observed in Fig.
2
(c) arises from the decaying spin polarization
[
34
]
, which perturbs the Larmor frequency during a single FID cycle
[
17
]
. To account for this effect, Eq.
4
can be extended with an exponential correction term giving

\phi(t)=\omega_{0}t+\phi_{0}+A_{p}e^{-\gamma_{p}t},

(5)

where
\omega_{0}
is the DC component of the time dependent Larmor frequency,
A_{p}
is the amplitude of the correction, and
\gamma_{p}
the associated decay constant. By evaluating the time derivative at
t=0
, the corrected Larmor frequency can be calculated as

\omega_{L}=\left.\frac{d\phi}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=\omega_{0}-\gamma_{p}A_{p}.

(6)

In this way, we can obtain the measured Larmor frequency at the instant of highest ground-state polarization
[
34
]
. It can be seen from Fig.
2
(c) that fitting this extended model considerably reduces residual trends.

II-C

Two-Axis MEMS Micromirror Implementation

The core of this magnetic imaging strategy is a
2~\mathrm{mm}
diameter, gimbal-less, two-axis scanning MEMS micromirror (Mirrorcle Technologies, A7M20.2-2000AL) which facilitates beam scanning in the
x
–
y
plane. The
250\,\mathrm{\mu{m}}
diameter co-propagating pump and probe beams are directed onto the mirror and subsequently reflected onto the vapor cell. A
0.5\,\mathrm{mm}
thick reflector is affixed to the rear surface of the cell using an optical adhesive, enabling a double-pass geometry. Compared to the single-pass configuration used in Ref.
[
16
]
, this approach offers two key advantages: (i) the optical rotation signal is effectively doubled due to the increased interaction length, and (ii) the DUT can be positioned directly behind the reflector without obstructing the beam path, thereby minimizing and better defining the standoff distance. After transmission through the vapor cell, the light is directed onto a lens that re-images the beam onto a balanced photodetector, which helps prevent beam clipping during scanning.

The micromirror exhibits a mechanical resonance at approximately
1.3\,\mathrm{kHz}
in both movement axes. As a result, its step-response performance is governed by an external low-pass filter, typically set to half the resonant frequency to suppress mechanical ringing. Although the magnetometer bandwidth is Nyquist limited to
500\,\mathrm{Hz}
, residual mechanical ringing from the micromirror introduces aliased noise centered near
330\,\mathrm{Hz}
. Figure
3
compares the magnetometer’s sensitivity when using the micromirror versus a standard static mirror. The prominent noise peak at
330\,\mathrm{Hz}
results from coupling between spatial magnetic field gradients—e.g., due to bias field inhomogeneity—and the mirror’s mechanical resonance, leading to spurious modulation of the magnetometer signal. This was confirmed by increasing the strength of the magnetic field gradient, which in turn elevated the noise peak amplitude. The noise floor is also slightly elevated due to the spectral wings of the mechanical resonance, limiting the sensitivity to approximately
1\,\mathrm{pT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}
in this case, compared to the optimal
500\,\mathrm{fT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}
achieved with a static mirror.

For this measurement, the micromirror was kept stationary. Welch’s method was used with 20 non-overlapping segments to reduce spectral fluctuations
[

]
. The static mirror configuration achieves an optimal sensitivity of approximately
500\,\mathrm{fT/Hz}
, while the MEMS mirror exhibits elevated noise due to mechanical resonance near
1.3\,\mathrm{kHz}
, which is folded into the magnetometer bandwidth via aliasing at around

. This is caused by coupling between spatial magnetic gradients and mirror motion, elevating the noise floor to around

.

In attempt to mitigate this ringing, a steeper low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of approximately
100\,\mathrm{Hz}
was implemented. While this reduced the mirror-induced noise, complete suppression was not achieved. Nonetheless, this filtering did not pose a limitation for the measurements presented here, as the DUTs investigated exhibited dynamics well below the imposed bandwidth limit.

The magnetic field distributions presented in this work were acquired using a raster scanning procedure to systematically map a grid of the spatially varying magnetic field. At each grid point, the Larmor frequencies were extracted from a train of FID signals recorded over a user-defined acquisition period, typically set to
100\,\mathrm{ms}
, and averaged to yield a single magnetic field value
[
16
]
. The precision at each grid point is governed by the acquisition time and can be adjusted to meet the contrast requirements necessary to resolve the magnetic features of interest. Increasing the acquisition time per point improves precision; however, also extends the total scan duration.

Another factor influencing the overall imaging speed is the signal processing time required to extract the Larmor frequency from each FID trace. When using a nonlinear least-squares fitting approach
[
20
]
, the computation time varies depending on convergence conditions and constitutes the dominant contribution to the total scan time. In contrast, the Hilbert transform method provides over an order-of-magnitude improvement in processing efficiency and offers more consistent timing performance.

Consequently, the minimum time to complete a full scan is determined by three factors: the micromirror step response time, the total number of grid points required to build the image, and the acquisition and processing times of the magnetometer. Importantly, the total number of grid points should also be selected with consideration of the system’s spatial resolution limits, which are constrained by atomic spin diffusion within the vapor cell, beam waist, and the standoff distance between the DUT and the sensing volume
[
16
]
. These physical factors define the smallest magnetic features that can be resolved and thus guide the appropriate grid density. By optimizing the mirror control, signal processing, and spatial sampling parameters, scan durations can be minimized while maintaining high-fidelity magnetic imaging.

634~\mathrm{nT/mA}
. A custom-designed coil configuration enabled prediction of the expected field profile, which was then compared with the output of the magnetic imaging system.

Theoretical predictions were generated using the Magpylib software package for magnetic field computation
[
36
]
, which applies the Biot–Savart law to compute the magnetic field produced by current-carrying conductors of arbitrary geometry at user-defined spatial locations. In this case, the field is evaluated at the laser beam position relative to the center of the vapor cell over a 2D grid.

The measured data exhibit a slight angular dependence, likely resulting from mechanical tolerances in the mounting of the vapor cell within the coil former. To account for this, a tilt angle of
1.5^{\circ}
was introduced into the simulation. The measured and predicted magnetic field distributions show strong agreement, validating both the modeling approach and the experimental alignment.

The magnetic gradient produced by the custom-made coils exhibits higher-order components beyond the first spatial derivative, which complicates the generation of a uniform bias field through simple gradient cancellation. To address this, a second coil configuration was employed using the integrated coil system provided with the MS-1L magnetic shield. These coils support full three-axis magnetic field control and include dedicated windings for first-order gradient cancellation. The coil along the shield’s axial
x
-axis is a solenoid, while the orthogonal directions use saddle coil geometries. The system is specifically designed to provide high axial field uniformity, with the solenoid coil exhibiting a gain of
130~\mathrm{nT/mA}
. As a result, a higher current is required to achieve the same bias field compared to the custom-made coils; however, the integrated coils produce a first-order spatial field gradient that is easier to condition.

Figure
4
(c) shows the measured magnetic field when the solenoid coil is active. The first-order field gradient is effectively nulled using the integrated gradient cancellation coils, as shown in Fig.
4
(d). The residual sensor background arises primarily from condensed Cs on the cell windows, which partially blocks the optical path. This introduces two systematic effects: (1) altered optical pumping dynamics, and (2) localized variations in the perceived fictitious field generated by light shifts. These systematics can be subtracted by adopting interleaved measurements of the sensor background
[
16
]

When the current polarity is reversed, as shown in Fig.

IV
Conclusion

We have demonstrated a fully automated magnetic imaging system based on an FID OPM integrated with a two-axis MEMS scanning micromirror. The system employs a double-pass optical geometry that minimizes the standoff distance between the vapor cell and the DUT, thereby enhancing spatial resolution by capturing stronger near-field signals before significant magnetic field decay. The use of thinner vapor cells with reduced glass substrate and reflector thickness would further alleviate physical constraints, allowing spatial resolution to approach fundamental limits set by atomic spin diffusion and optical beam waist. Additionally, increasing the buffer gas pressure could reduce the spin diffusion length and further improve spatial resolution. For thinner vapor cells, this increase in pressure may also help maintain coherence time by compensating for elevated wall-collision rates.

A computationally efficient Hilbert transform-based signal processing technique was implemented to enable rapid frequency estimation across spatial grids, offering a substantial speed advantage over traditional nonlinear fitting methods. An optimal magnetometer sensitivity of
500\,\mathrm{fT/\sqrt{Hz}}
was achieved using a static mirror whilst the MEMS micromirror sensitivity was slightly reduced to approximately
1\,\mathrm{pT/\sqrt{Hz}}
, primarily due to mechanical resonances elevating the sensor’s noise floor.

This imaging system was used to perform automated 2D magnetic field mapping of both custom-made sources and functional devices. Additionally, this work represents a significant improvement over previous methods relying on manual translation, which required hours to accumulate a full dataset
[
16
]
. In contrast, the technique presented here produces high-precision images within minutes with real-time processing, representing an improvement in speed approaching two orders of magnitude. This capability, in turn, allowed investigation of various magnetic sources including a PCB with alternating current paths, an integrated bridge rectifier, and a ceramic battery under both charging and discharging conditions. The system successfully resolved polarity-dependent asymmetries, captured dynamic current evolution, and imaged spatially varying magnetic field distributions with high contrast and precision.

This work has shown the versatility of this approach for noninvasive diagnostics and quality assurance of integrated electronics and batteries. Future work will aim to reduce acquisition time, enhance spatial resolution, and extend the system’s capability to operate in more dynamic environments in real time. One promising direction involves replacing the MEMS mirror with a DMD
[
38
]
, which offers faster switching speeds and the potential to explore compressed sensing strategies for further accelerated image acquisition.
