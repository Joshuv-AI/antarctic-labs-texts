Sylvester’s problem.

One cannot write a paper about random points in convex domains without mentioning Sylvester and his
four-points problem

[
30

. This problem, which initially was ill-posed, was focusing on the convex domain
K
optimizing the probability that 4 i.i.d. uniform points drawn in
K
formed the vertices of a convex quadrilateral.
The answer was given by Blaschke in 1917
[
7

who showed that for any
K

{\bf Q}^{\triangle}_{4,0}\leq{\bf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{4,0}\leq{\bf Q}^{\bigcirc}_{4,0},

where
\triangle
and
\bigcirc
symbolize a triangle and a disk.
Recently, Marckert and Rahmani
[
22
]
proved that we have also

{\bf Q}^{\triangle}_{5,0}\leq{\bf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{5,0}\leq{\bf Q}^{\bigcirc}_{5,0}.

Expected number of vertices.

In 1963, Rényi and Sulanke
[
25
]
gave the expected number
\textbf{n}({n})
of vertices of the boundary of the convex hull of
n
points
\mathsf{U}^{(n)}_{\textbf{{K}}}
-distributed when
K
is either a polygon or domain whose frontier is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
(a disk for example). In the first case we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\textbf{n}({n})\right)=\frac{2\kappa}{3}\log(n)+{\sf Cste}+o(1),

where
\kappa
is the number of sides of
K
, and in the second one,

\mathbb{E}\left(\textbf{n}({n})\right)\sim{\sf Cste}({\textbf{{K}}})n^{1/3}.

For the first case, Groeneboom
[
13
]
later gave a central limit theorem for
\textbf{n}({n})
:

\frac{\textbf{n}({n})-\frac{2\kappa}{3}\log(n)}{\sqrt{\frac{10\kappa}{27}\log(n)}}\xrightarrow[n]{(d)}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The interlaced quantities
{\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,m}
and
{\bf Q}^{\textbf{{K}}}_{n,m}
.

The particular cases
{\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,0}
and
{\sf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,0}

) of

of Bárány
et al.
,
[
4
]
also includes results about the asymptotic behavior of

under the conditional law

. We already mentioned the fact that under this conditional law,
\partial{\sf CH}(U[n])
converges for the Hausdorff distance towards the parabola
\mathcal{P}
, but in
[
4
]
is also given a functional central limit theorem for the fluctuation around the limit, at the scale

.

These theorems are almost the only tools used to evaluate

for a general

(see
Section

5.2

[

31

, who gave in 1995 when

\mathbf{Q}^{\Box}_{n,0}=\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}{2n-2\choose n-1}^{2},

and in 1996 when

\mathbf{Q}^{\triangle}_{n,0}=\frac{2^{n}(3n-3)!}{(2n)!((n-1)!)^{3}},

though Valtr’s proof relies on some slightly different arguments.

Since the late 90s, a substantial body of work has followed. Essential work tackles limit theorems established around convex polygons formed on lattices. In this model, for a given integer
n
, a convex polygon on a lattice is a convex polygon contained in the square
[-n,n]^{2}
and whose vertices have integer coordinates.
Vershik then questioned the possibility of identifying the number and typical shape of such a convex polygon. Three coherent solutions were put forward by Bárány
[
1
]
, Vershik
[
33
]
and Sinai
[
28
]
in 1994, and are described below:
In the square, the convex polygon can be naturally decomposed into 4 pieces (delimited by its extremities in the North/East/South/West directions), which delimit 4 “polygonal convex lines” between them. By studying these objects, which can initially be considered as convex chains joining
(0,0)
to
(n,n)
in the square
[-n,n]^{2}
(apart from rotations and translations), it was shown that when
n\to+\infty,

1.

the number of such polygonal lines is
\exp(3(\zeta(3)/\zeta(2))^{1/3}n^{2/3}+o(n^{2/3}))
where
\zeta
is Riemann’s zeta function,

2.

the random number of vertices in such a chain is concentrated around
\left(\zeta(3)^{2}/\zeta(2)\right)^{-1/3}n^{2/3}
,

3.

the limit form of such a chain, normalized in both directions by
n
, is an arc of parabola.

In 1997, Bárány
[
2
]
generalized most of these results to characterize the asymptotics under
{\sf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,0}
: He showed that the convex hull of a tuple

Presentation of the family of distributions

Lemma 2.7
.

[Upper bound on
{\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,\lfloor n{\lambda}\rfloor}
] For any

\limsup_{n}n^{-1}\left(\log\left({\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,\lfloor n{\lambda}\rfloor}\right)+2n\log(n)\right)\leq\beta_{\lambda}.

Remark 2.8
.

For two positive sequences
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
if there exists a sub-exponential sequence
(c_{n})
(that is, such that
\log(c_{n})/n\to 0
), such that
a_{n}=b_{n}c_{n}
then
\limsup_{n}n^{-1}\log(a_{n})=\limsup_{n}n^{-1}\log(b_{n})
. In particular,
\limsup_{n}n^{-1}\log(a_{n})=\limsup_{n}n^{-1}\log(a_{n}n^{K})
. Hence, if in an inequality we “lose” a polynomial factor in
n
while handling
{\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,\lfloor n{\lambda}\rfloor}
, it does not jeopardize our search for the best possible bound for
\limsup_{n}n^{-1}\left(\log\left({\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,\lfloor n{\lambda}\rfloor}\right)+2n\log(n)\right)
.

Proof.

The proof of this lemma is quite complex. It is divided in 9 sections.

1. Slicing
{\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,m}
into sub-sums.

For any
n\geq 1,m\geq 0,s\geq 0
, define

{\bf Q}_{n,m,s}:=2^{n}\sum_{k[n]\in{\sf Comp}(n,m)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(1+k_{i})}{(s+i+S_{i})(s+1+i+S_{i})},

so that, in particular
{\bf Q}_{n,m,0}={\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,m}
.

The new array
({\bf Q}_{n,m,s})
appears when one decomposes
{\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,m}
as in the following example:

{\bf Q}^{\triangle\bullet\bullet}_{n,m}=\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{m}{\bf Q}_{n_{1},m_{1},0}\;{\bf Q}_{n-n_{1},m-m_{1},n_{1}+m_{1}}

For each

6. Reduction to convex sequences

Notice that, though

To prove the claim it suffices to observe the effect on

If one is given two coinciding lists of positive increments

\displaystyle C(\ell[K])-C(\ell^{\prime}[K])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{K}\Bigl((s+a-1)\log(s+a-1)-(s+a)\log(s+a)

9. Final optimization argument.

Gathering now the terms of
A+C
in a functional
\theta
, it suffices to prove that any convex function
M^{\star}
such that
M^{\star}\geq 0
and
M^{\star}(1)=\lambda
satisfies
\theta(M^{\star})\leq\beta_{\lambda}
, where
\theta
is defined as

\displaystyle\theta(M):=-\log(2)+\int_{0}^{1}(M^{\prime}(t)+2)\log(2+M^{\prime}(t))-M^{\prime}(t)\log(M^{\prime}(t))-2\log(t+M(t))\textrm{d}t.

(2.48)

We will use an alternative to the Euler-Lagrange optimization method, and prove a little bit more, namely, in addition to
\theta(M^{\star})\leq\beta_{\lambda}
, the uniqueness of
\argmax\theta
, in the set of functions we study.We proceed to the change of variable
\ell(t)=M(t)+t
, so that
\ell(1)=1+\lambda
,
\ell(0)=1
. We need to maximize

\ell\mapsto{\sf Op}(\ell):=\int_{0}^{1}\overline{L}(t,\ell(t),\ell^{\prime}(t))\textrm{d}t

with

\overline{L}(t,\ell(t),\ell^{\prime}(t))=(\ell^{\prime}(t)+1)\log(1+\ell^{\prime}(t))-(\ell^{\prime}(t)-1)\log(\ell^{\prime}(t)-1)-2\log(\ell(t))

(or more formally,
\overline{L}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(\gamma+1)\log(\gamma+1)-(\gamma-1)\log(\gamma-1)-2\log(\beta)
).
By Beltrami identity (the Euler-Lagrange special case
\partial\overline{L}/(\partial\alpha)=0
), the functions
\ell
that maximize
\ell\mapsto{\sf Op}(\ell)
satisfy, for a constant
C
,

\displaystyle\overline{L}-\ell^{\prime}\frac{\partial\overline{L}}{\partial\ell^{\prime}}=C

(2.49)

which is equivalent to

C+2\log(\ell)=\log((\ell^{\prime}+1)(\ell^{\prime}-1)).

Solving this standard type of ODE leads to

\ell(x)=s\sinh(e^{C/2}(x-c))\exp(-C/2)

for a second constant
c
, and a “sign”
s\in\{-1,1\}
.
Since
\ell(0)=0
,
\ell(x)\geq 0
,
\ell(1)=1+\lambda
, we must have
c=0
,
s=1
. The only solution is
\ell^{\star}(x)=\sinh(\alpha x)/\alpha
with
\alpha
such that
\sinh(\alpha)/\alpha=1+\lambda
(so that
\exp(C/2)=2r_{\lambda}
, i.e.
C=2\log(2r_{\lambda})
), and we recover
\alpha=2r_{\lambda}
, so that

M^{\star}(t)=\ell^{\star}(t)-t=-t+\frac{\sinh(2r_{\lambda}t)}{2r_{\lambda}}

(2.50)

is the maximizing function, and (
2.49
) holds with this function.

There remains to give an argument to prove that the function
\ell^{\star}
we found is indeed a maximum for
{\sf Op}
(it could be a singular point in which we have cancellation of the derivative (

5.2
About the evaluation of
{\bf Q}_{n,m}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}
and a potential limit shape theorem

Take a convex compact set of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with area 1.
Denote by
{\sf C}_{n,0}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}
the subset of
{\textbf{{K}}}^{n}
formed by the
z[n]
that are in convex position.
For
n\geq 1
,
m\geq 0
denote by
{\sf C}_{n,m}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}
the subset of
{\textbf{{K}}}^{n}\times{\textbf{{K}}}^{m}
of pairs
(z[n],w[m])
such that
z[n]\in{\sf C}_{n,0}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}
, and
w_{1},\cdots,w_{m}\in{\sf CH}(\{z_{1},\cdots,z_{n}\})
. Set

c_{n,m}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}=\Leb({\sf C}_{n,m}^{{\textbf{{K}}}})=\int_{{\textbf{{K}}}^{n}}\Leb({{\sf CH}\{z_{1},\cdots,z_{n}\}})^{m}dz_{1}\cdots dz_{n}

so that
{\bf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,m}
, the probability that the number of vertices of the convex hull of
n+m
iid uniform points in
K
is
n
, satisfies
{\bf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,m}=\binom{n+m}{n}c_{n,m}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}
. Computing
{\sf C}_{n,0}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}
or
{\bf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,0}
are then equivalent problems.

In this section, we provide some elements about
1.20
and explain the obstructions that prevent us from completing the proof; we tried to be specific enough, so that an interested reader could engage with this problem from this section, without too much effort. To explain a bit this conjecture, we will need to discuss the concluding approach of Bárány
[
3
]
in the case
{\sf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,0}
, to make apparent the differences.

We will also need to discuss the link between his limit shape theorem under
{\sf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,0}
and the computation of
{\bf Q}_{n,0}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}
which are deeply interconnected. Even if most of the ideas of the four next pages are due to Bárány, we discute them in a new light.

Let us start by a remark:

Lemma 5.3
.

For all
\lambda\geq 0
, the sequence
({\sf CH}(U[n+\lfloor n{\lambda}\rfloor])_{n\geq 0}
is tight in
CS({\textbf{{K}}})
, equipped with the Hausdorff topology.

Indeed, the set
{\sf CCS}_{\textbf{{K}}}
(of compact subsets of
K
) equipped with the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}
is compact, and seen as a topological space
({\sf CCS}_{\textbf{{K}}},d_{H})
is complete and separable (it is a Polish space). Hence, as a consequence of Prokhorov theorem, see e.g. Billingsley
[
6
, Theo.5.1]
, any sequence of probability measures
(\mu_{n},n\geq 0)
on
({\sf CCS}_{\textbf{{K}}},d_{H})
is tight, and, further the sequence
(\mu_{n},n\geq 0)
is relatively compact, meaning that it contains a sub-sequence converging weakly: in our case, it implies the following general statement:

Lemma 5.4
.

For any sequence of non-negative integers
(a_{n})
, and
{\sf CH}(U[n+a(n)])
taken under
{\sf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,a(n)}
, there exists a sub-sequence
(n_{k})
such that
(U[n_{k}+a(n_{k})])
converges in distribution for the Hausdorff topology.

This result is “trivial” in the sense that it is a consequence of compactness and it is then valid for all sequences
(a(n))
. It allows us to concentrate on the difficult point: to prove a limit shape theorem, it is necessary and sufficient to prove the uniqueness of the accumulation point. An accumulation point is a measure on
{\sf CCS}_{\textbf{{K}}}
that can be a Dirac mass on a single convex domain but could also be a more general probability distribution on
{\sf CCS}_{\textbf{{K}}}
.
Under
{\sf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,0}
, Bárány states the convergence in distribution toward a deterministic shape, which is the only element
C^{\star}
of
\argmax\Phi_{0}^{\textbf{{K}}}
. As usual in probability theory, when the limit law is a Dirac mass, there are additional ways to prove convergence, in particular concentration arguments. In this case, it amounts to proving that,

\textrm{ for all }{\varepsilon}>0,~~{\sf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,\lfloor n{\lambda}\rfloor}\left(d_{H}({\sf CH}(U[n]),C^{\star})\geq{\varepsilon}\right)\to 0.

However, it is hard to precisely compute
{\sf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,\lfloor n{\lambda}\rfloor}\left(d_{H}({\sf CH}(U[n]),C^{\star})\geq{\varepsilon}\right)
since even the global volume of points in convex position, that is, the normalizing constant
c_{n,0}^{\textbf{{K}}}
, can not be computed exactly. Bárány shows that the exact value is not important because, somehow, all volumes and probability that we need to evaluate in order to conclude, have the form either
\exp(nf(g))
or
\exp(-2n\log(n)+nf(g))
when
n\to+\infty
, where in this formula
g
represents a geometric property, and
f
a function of this geometry. These formulas holds up to a factor
\exp(o(n))
and in general. Because all quantities have different exponential order (the coefficients of the linear terms in
n
in the exponentials are different), at the end, we can only care about the geometry
g
that maximizes
f(g)
, and don’t care about sub-linear terms in
n
in the exponential.

The main ideas in Bárány’s proof are the following:

First, the bi-pointed triangular model

is the source of everything, going from the limit shape theorem, passing through the asymptotic behavior of
{\sf Q}^{{\textbf{{K}}}}_{n,0}

.
