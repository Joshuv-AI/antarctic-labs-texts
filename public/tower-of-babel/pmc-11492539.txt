Incidence of serious respiratory tract infections and associated characteristics in a population exposed to immunosuppressive therapies: a register-based population study
Authors: Cindy Etienne, Ana-Maria Vilcu, Flora Finet, Sylvain Chawki, Thierry Blanchon, Olivier Steichen, Thomas Hanslik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmunosuppressive therapies are associated with a risk of infections. Nevertheless, their incidence in this population remains unclear. This study aims to determine the incidence of serious respiratory tract infections (SRI) in a population exposed to immunosuppressive therapies.MethodsData from a representative sample of the French healthcare claims from 01/01/2014 to 12/31/2019 were analyzed. Exposure to immunosuppressive therapy was defined by the dispensation of drugs through community pharmacies or in hospitals. SRI diagnosis was based on ICD-10 codes from hospitalization records. A cohort analysis was performed to estimate standardized SRI incidence rates. A nested case-control analysis within this cohort was used to study the characteristics associated with SRI.ResultsWe identified 24,122 individuals exposed to immunosuppressive therapies, among which 1,559 developed SRI, resulting in a standardized incidence rate of 1,398 per 100,000 person-years. In this population, the risk of SRI was associated with a history of cancer (OR 2.68, 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) 2.24–3.21; p < 0.001), chronic respiratory disease (2.62, 95%CI 2.17–3.16; p < 0.001), end-stage renal failure (2.38, 95%CI 1.37–4.13; p = 0.003), neurodegenerative diseases (1.52, 95%CI 1.07–2.17; p = 0.026), diabetes (1.44, 95%CI 1.14–1.82; p < 0.001), psychiatric diseases (1.27, 95%CI 1.06–1.52; p < 0.001), and cardiovascular diseases (1.26, 95%CI 1.04–1.52; p = 0.002). Compared to corticosteroids alone, the risk of SRI was lower in individuals treated with conventional synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (csDMARD) only (0.44, 95%CI 0.25–0.78; p < 0.001).ConclusionIn the population exposed to immunosuppressive therapies, a history of chronic disease is associated with an increased risk of SRI. This risk is lower in those receiving csDMARD alone than corticosteroids alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10039-2.

Background

Immunosuppressive therapies are currently indicated for a wide range of diseases. Indeed, diseases in the fields of allergy, immunology, and rheumatology are often caused by immune dysfunction, and the drugs used to treat them target different components of the immune system. Immunosuppressive therapies are also indicated to inhibit host immune function to improve graft survival in solid organ transplantation (SOT) or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). As new agents emerge and indications evolve, the landscape becomes increasingly complex, as shown by the European recommendations for treating various immunological diseases [1–7]. In this context, the management of individuals exposed to immunosuppressive therapies, such as prolonged high-dose corticosteroids, immunosuppressive drugs, and conventional synthetic or biological disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARD), poses a challenge for clinicians. Due to their action on the immune system, these therapies increase susceptibility to infections [8–13].

There is a strong association between autoimmune and inflammatory diseases and the occurrence of infections, due to several factors, including the effects of treatments. In this population, infectious complications induced by immunosuppressive therapies are responsible for high morbidity and mortality [14, 15]. Respiratory tract infections represent one of the main infectious complications reported in this immunocompromised population. A U.S. study using the Medicaid database assessed morbidity and mortality associated with infections in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [16]. The incidence rate of all serious infections among patients with SLE was 10.8 per 100 person-years; most of these infections were of bacterial origin (96%), with pneumonia being the primary diagnosis (37%). The study observed a higher incidence of infections in the oldest age group (51–64 years) compared to the youngest (18–34 years) (incidence rate ratio 1.16, 95% CI 1.10–1.23). Immunosuppressive medication users had a significantly increased risk of infection compared to non-users (hazard ratio (HR) 1.11, 95% CI 1.03–1.20), and this was also true for corticosteroids users (HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.43–1.61).

In another study focused on the risk of infection in ANCA-associated vasculitis during induction or maintenance treatment, more than half of the serious infections (53%) involved the respiratory tract, especially pneumonia [17]. Finally, a recent Spanish study investigating the risk of infection at different times during the follow-up of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) showed an increased prevalence of infection during the period of immunosuppressive therapy (5% vs. 3%) [18]. Although prophylactic measures are available for some of these respiratory infections, including vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis, the incidence of serious respiratory tract infections (SRI) in individuals receiving immunosuppressive therapies remains uncertain. Available estimates come from small sample studies that included selected patients, with exposure to study treatment often limited.

Identifying risk factors for respiratory tract infections is crucial in the management of patients treated with immunosuppressive therapies. Underlying medical comorbidities, such as diabetes, play a significant role, although their contribution to infectious risk is difficult to quantify [19]. Cardiac insufficiency, chronic lung diseases, severe asthma, history of cancer, renal failure, and chronic liver diseases present an increased risk of invasive pneumococcal pneumonia [20]. Nevertheless, in two French registry-based studies, comorbidities did not appear as risk factors for serious infections in rheumatoid arthritis patients treated with either abatacept [21] or tocilizumab [22].

We aimed to estimate the incidence of SRI in a population exposed to immunosuppressive therapies in a representative sample of the French population, and to describe risk factors for SRI in exposed individuals, using a medico-administrative database of the French health insurance system.

Methods

This report complies with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data (RECORD) guideline [23].

Study design and setting

An observational retrospective cohort study using healthcare data from a large administrative French database was conducted to estimate the incidence of SRI, defined as those requiring in-patient care, in the adult population receiving immunosuppressive therapies between 2014 and 2019 in metropolitan France. Follow-up began at inclusion in the study, defined as the first dispensation or administration of an immunosuppressive therapy observed in the database during the study period. The follow-up lasted until one of the following events occurred: (1) the date of death, (2) the end of affiliation with the general health insurance scheme, (3) the end of exposure to immunosuppressive therapies during the study period, or (4) the end of the study period, whichever came first (Fig. 1A).

A nested case-control study within this cohort was then carried out to investigate the characteristics associated with the occurrence of SRI (Fig. 1B).

The nested case-control approach aimed to evaluate the association between SRI and the combinations of immunosuppressive therapy regimens to which individuals had been exposed over a similar duration, or with the chronic conditions they presented at treatment initiation.

This study was restricted to individuals with no history of immunosuppressive therapy exposure within the past five years, ensuring that only their first episode of exposure to immunosuppressive therapy was considered. This new-user design helps minimize biases related to prior treatments and allows for a clearer evaluation of the impact of SRI. “Cases” were subjects who developed SRI during their first exposure to immunosuppressive therapies, while “controls” were subjects who did not develop SRI during their first exposure to immunosuppressive therapies. The index date for the cases was the date of the first hospitalization for SRI. The duration of exposure to immunosuppressive therapies for the cases was calculated as the time between immunosuppressive therapy initiation and the index date.

To assess whether SRI is associated with the combinations of immunosuppressive therapy regimens, we ensured that the immunosuppressive exposure of the controls had the same length as that of the cases. Each case was matched to four controls of the same age (+/- 1 year) and sex, with at least an equal duration of exposure to immunosuppressive therapies, using random sampling without replacement. The exposure time of the controls was then truncated to equal that of their matched cases. To this end, the index date for controls was set as the date when their immunosuppressive exposure time matched that of their corresponding case. When no matching controls were available, the respective case was excluded from the analysis. When 4:1 matched controls were not available, all available matches for the case were included.

We conducted this study using the Echantillon Généraliste de Bénéficiaires (EGB), a 1/97th sample of the Système National des Données de Santé (SNDS), the French nationwide claims database which covers around 99% of the French population. The EGB database provides anonymous sociodemographic and medical information on more than 700,000 individuals representative of the French population in terms of age, gender, and medical expenses. It allows for longitudinal follow-up. The EGB database contains information about medications and the number of units dispensed by community pharmacies but does not include diagnosis details or precise dosage prescribed. The EGB is linked to the Programme de Médicalisation des Systèmes d’Information (PMSI) database, which includes all hospital discharge data in France, both in the public and private sectors. It contains information on the date of admission, length of stay, and medical diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Edition (ICD-10). Finally, the EGB database provides information on the administration of innovative drugs included in a derogatory financial list, as well as long-term diseases (LTD), that entitle patients to 100% health insurance coverage [24–26].

Study population

The population eligible for the study included all individuals identified in the EGB, affiliated with the general health insurance scheme, aged 18 years or older, residing in metropolitan France, and having been exposed to at least one immunosuppressive therapy during the study period, which lasted from January 1st, 2014, to December 31st, 2019.

HIV-infected individuals were excluded from the analysis because HIV infection is a known independent risk factor for SRI, which could introduce confounding bias. In current practice, individuals considered for immunosuppressive therapy should be screened for HIV infection, and all HIV-infected subjects indicate antiretroviral treatment. The probability of a known HIV-infected person not being treated is consequently minimal. We used two different methods to identify HIV-infected individuals: medical diagnosis during hospitalization identified with ICD-10 codes in the hospitalizations database and dispensation of HIV treatment drugs in community pharmacies. HIV infections were determined based on the presence of at least one hospitalization with an HIV-specific ICD-10 code (B20, B21, B22, B23, B24) as the main, related, or associated diagnosis and/or at least one dispensation of an HIV-specific drug class (list of ATC codes is provided in Supplemental Table 1).

The ATC drug codes used to identify immunosuppressive therapies are presented in Supplemental Tables 2 and 3.

To account for residual immunosuppression after stopping treatment, we assumed six months of immunosuppression for each immunosuppressant dispensation or biological drug administration (seven months for rituximab) as well as for the last dispensation of each corticosteroid treatment sequence. The type of immunosuppressive therapy during the follow-up was categorized into four classes: csDMARD, biological DMARD, immunosuppressive drugs, and corticosteroids.

Outcome: definition of serious respiratory tract infections

SRI was defined as a hospitalization with specific ICD-10 codes as the main, related, or associated diagnosis. The selected ICD-10 codes covered pneumococcal infections, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP), and other pulmonary infections such as influenza, as well as viral or bacterial pneumonia (Supplemental Table 4). To avoid potential misclassification of SRI episodes, SRI hospitalizations within 30 days of each other were deemed connected to the same SRI episode, starting at the date of the first hospitalization in the sequence. Additionally, individuals with more than five SRI episodes during the study period were excluded as outliers. SRI was considered related to immunosuppressive therapies if it occurred during a period of exposure to immunosuppressive therapy, and at least two weeks after its initiation. Therefore, individuals with less than two weeks of follow-up were also excluded from the study.

The SRI episodes were described in terms of severity, specifically based on the need for intensive care. More precisely, SRI requiring hospitalization in intensive care units were identified when either the Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS II) [27], or the number of days reported in an intensive care unit, critical care unit, or continuous care unit was greater than zero. This information is reported in the EGB database through the PMSI.

Collection of associated characteristics

Comorbidities were evaluated at the time of inclusion in the cohort based on algorithms developed by the National Health Insurance System [28]. These algorithms require up to five years of historical data. Therefore, individuals with less than five years of historical presence in the EGB database at the time of inclusion were excluded. Diseases potentially associated with the prescription of immunosuppressive therapy were identified when specific ICD-10 codes were recorded as diagnoses during hospital stays, or when they motivated the declaration of specific LTDs, either during a patient’s follow-up period or in the five years before cohort entry. We classified these diseases into 11 categories: vasculitis, connective tissue diseases, polyarthritis, ankylosing spondylitis and spondylarthritis, inflammatory bowel diseases, sarcoidosis, multiple sclerosis, psoriasis, asthma, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, organ transplantation, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

Statistical analyses

The individuals included in the cohort were described based on age, sex, comorbidities at inclusion, immunosuppressive therapy usage during follow-up, and diseases potentially associated with the prescription of immunosuppressive therapy. Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers and percentages, while quantitative variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges.

We calculated the incidence rate (episodes per 100,000 person-years) of SRI in the population exposed to immunosuppressive therapy. The incidence rate was standardized according to the age structure of the world standard population [29].

The factors evaluated in the case-control study included classes of immunosuppressive therapy used throughout the exposure period and chronic conditions present at cohort inclusion. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted using conditional logistic regression, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Factors significantly associated with SRI in the univariate analyses (p-value < 0.2) were included in the multivariate models. In the multivariate analyses, only variables with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Population’s characteristics

A total of 417,712 individuals aged over 18, with at least five years of data in the EGB and affiliated with the general health insurance scheme, were eligible for inclusion in the study. Of these, 24,283 had at least one period of exposure to immunosuppressive therapy between January 1st, 2014, and December 31st, 2019.

After excluding individuals with HIV infection, those with less than two weeks of follow-up, and those with more than five episodes of SRI, 24,122 individuals exposed to immunosuppressive therapy were included in the study. Among them, 1,559 experienced at least one episode of SRI, resulting in 1,915 SRI episodes (Fig. 2).

In this cohort, the most represented age group was 45–64 years (n = 8,917, 37.0%). Women accounted for 60% of the population exposed to immunosuppressive therapy. The majority of patients exposed to immunosuppressive therapy (n = 18,233, 75.6%) had at least one chronic condition at inclusion. The main comorbidities were cardiovascular (n = 9,173, 38.0%), psychiatric (n = 7,007, 29.0%), and a history of cancer (n = 5,114, 21.2%). Corticosteroids were the most commonly used, either alone (n = 17,275, 71.6%) or in combination (n = 2,940, 12.1%). csDMARD were used by 3,364 (13.9%) patients, biological DMARD by 2,855 (11.8%) patients, mostly anti-TNF alpha antibodies (n = 1,632, 6.8%), and immunosuppressive drugs by 2,004 (8.3%) patients. A single class of immunosuppressive therapy was used by 20,473 (84.9%) individuals, while 3,649 (15.1%) were exposed to multiple classes. These characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Risk factors of SRI in a population exposed to immunosuppressive therapies

The characteristics of the population included in the nested case-control cohort, as well as the univariate and multivariate analyses, are reported in Table 5. We identified 775 cases matched to 3,080 controls, with comparable age and sex, as expected. In the multivariate analysis, a history of cancer (OR 2.68, 95% CI 2.24–3.21; p < 0.001), chronic respiratory disease (OR 2.62, 95% CI 2.17–3.16; p < 0.001), end-stage renal failure (OR 2.38, 95% CI 1.37–4.13; p = 0.003), neurodegenerative diseases (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.07–2.17; p = 0.026), diabetes (OR 1.44, 95% CI 1.14–1.82; p < 0.001), psychiatric diseases (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.06–1.52; p < 0.001), and cardiovascular diseases (OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.04–1.52; p = 0.002) were associated with an increased risk of SRI. Compared to treatment with corticosteroids only, treatment with DMARD only was associated with a lower risk of SRI (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.25–0.78; p < 0.001). No other significant differences were found between the other treatments.

Discussion

This study describes the population exposed to immunosuppressive therapy in France, as well as the incidence and risk factors of SRI in this population, based on data from the EGB. This work presents new data on exposure to immunosuppressive therapy in France. Our study allowed the assessment of unselected individuals from daily practice, including those with comorbidities who are usually ineligible for randomized controlled trials. This makes the results applicable to real-life practice. In addition, the extended follow-up period (2014 to 2019) allowed for the study of rare adverse events such as SRI, which are difficult to identify in therapeutic trials typically conducted over shorter durations.

In this immunocompromised cohort, we estimated the standardized incidence rate of SRI at 1,398 episodes per 100,000 person-years. To our knowledge, the incidence rate of SRI in such a large sample of immunocompromised subjects had never been reported. In a review of the epidemiology of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) among adults in Europe, the prevalence and incidence of CAP in hospitals were estimated at 0.9 to 1.6 per 1,000 population and 7.0 to 15.1 per 1,000 person-years, respectively. The risk increased with age [30]. Studies referenced in this review were conducted in the general population, and no information was provided regarding immune status. Nevertheless, the use of oral corticosteroids was associated with increased mortality. Another study conducted in the French general population between September 2011 and July 2012 estimated the yearly incidence of CAP at 4.7 per 1,000 inhabitants aged 18 years or over and 6.7 per 1,000 inhabitants aged 65 years or over, with a hospitalization rate of 7% [31]. In a U.S. study, using a methodology comparable to ours for identifying pneumonia in the general population, the average annual age-adjusted pneumonia-associated hospitalization rate was estimated at 464.8 per 100,000 population (95% CI, 462.5–467.1). The proportion of pneumonia-associated hospitalizations colisting an immunocompromising condition increased from 18.7% in 2001 to 29.9% in 2014 [32]. All those studies confirmed that bacteria were responsible for the majority of hospitalized cases of CAP, the most frequently isolated pathogen being Streptococcus pneumoniae. In our study, we identified very few invasive pneumococcal diseases (n = 67). We potentially underestimated the rate of pneumococcal infection, as bacteriological documentation was limited in our population. Indeed, as is usual in clinical practice, where treatment of SRI is often initiated empirically, the majority of SRI episodes were reported as pneumonia or bronchitis “without precision” of the microorganism involved.

Individuals exposed to immunosuppressive therapy also have a higher risk of opportunistic respiratory tract infections, such as PCP [33]. The number of PCP in our population was 41 episodes (2.9%). In 2014 a French study on the incidence of PCP was carried out between 1990 and 2010 and 293 cases of PCP were detected, 154 (52%) of which were in HIV-uninfected patients, but we have no information on the treatments they received [34].

We were able to identify only a small number of diseases potentially associated with immunosuppressive therapy prescriptions, and we could not determine whether the treatment was actually prescribed for those conditions. The algorithms we used to identify the causes for immunosuppressive therapy prescription, based on ICD-10 codes or LTD declarations, did not permit exhaustiveness.

Comorbidities are currently considered to play a role in the risk of infections. A case-control study showed that factors associated with SRI occurrence in individuals exposed to immunosuppressive therapy included the presence of chronic conditions such as psychiatric disorders, cancer, cardiovascular diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, and chronic end-stage renal disease. The role of comorbidities in the risk of respiratory tract infections has already been highlighted. Ewig and colleagues observed that the incidence of CAP increased with advancing age and that the overall in-hospital mortality rate for CAP patients was 14%, with the highest mortality among those with comorbid conditions. Among individuals with malignancies, dementia, and pulmonary diseases (other than chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), mortality was more than twice higher compared to those with no known underlying conditions [35].

Compared to individuals treated with corticosteroids alone, the risk of SRI was lower in individuals treated with csDMARD alone, suggesting relative safety from respiratory infectious complications. Some studies in patients with rheumatoid arthritis also suggest the relative safety of csDMARD compared to corticosteroids [36–38]. A study in Canada on rheumatoid arthritis found that the use of csDMARD without corticosteroids was not associated with an increased risk of serious infections (adjusted RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.85–1.0) [36]. In contrast, the use of corticosteroids increased the risk of both mild and serious infections. In the U.S., a large cohort of rheumatoid arthritis patients from the National Data Bank for Rheumatic Diseases showed that prednisone use increased the risk of hospitalization for pneumonia (hazard ratio [HR] 1.7, 95% CI 1.5–2.0), as did the use of leflunomide (HR 1.2, 95% CI 1.0–1.5), while no evidence of increased pneumonia rates was associated with methotrexate or sulfasalazine [37].

Our study has some limitations. First, the use of an administrative database is subject to information bias. Indeed, the EGB does not include the diagnosis prompting the drug prescription, nor does it provide precise information on the prescribed dosage or actual drug consumption. Additionally, our analysis of clinical outcomes lacked data from chart reviews. Our approach has already been used and validated by other research teams. A French study investigated pneumococcal pneumonia by identifying cases from the PMSI database [39]. The authors analyzed the concordance of data from the PMSI with clinical, radiological, and microbiological data from medical records, showing a diagnostic sensitivity of 82.3% using the PMSI database. We adopted the same approach as Blanc et al. in their study on the impact of comorbidities and their accumulation on the short- and long-term prognosis of patients over 50 with community-acquired pneumonia, by combining ICD codes for improved accuracy [40]. Several published studies have used PMSI data to estimate the incidence of respiratory infections [41–43]. However, this is also a limitation of our study, as ICD coding in hospital discharge data may be incomplete and subject to misclassification. Despite the risk of information bias inherent in using administrative databases, this risk appears acceptable given that the PMSI has already demonstrated its effectiveness.

In the nested case-control study, we excluded cases due to a lack of available matched controls. Although excluding a substantial number of cases could increase selection bias and confounding, we believe the impact on our study is minimal. The proportion of exclusions was below 5%, and it likely involved individuals with extreme characteristics, such as those with very old or very young ages, or long durations of immunosuppressive exposure before developing an SRI.

Our study was limited to SRI, defined as those requiring in-patient care. We had no data on respiratory infections treated in outpatient settings. As a result, this study is not designed to evaluate the impact of immunosuppressants on the severity of respiratory infections, which may differ across agents [44].

In our cohort, patients are highly diverse in terms of factors that can impact the risk of respiratory infections, such as underlying disease, age, and comorbidities. While we made efforts to account for these factors, the EGB database does not allow for a high degree of precision. Our goal was to estimate the overall incidence of respiratory infections among all individuals exposed to immunosuppressive therapies, without restricting the analysis to specific pathologies as done in other published studies.

The causal association between increased SRI and immunosuppressive therapy exposure is limited by the fact that information on potential confounders was not always available or comprehensive. For example, the baseline risk of SRI differs depending on the disease for which immunosuppressants are prescribed, but this information was available for 31.6% of patients only. We also lacked data on smoking habits or alcohol consumption. However, the aim of the study was not to prove causality, but rather to explore potential associations and generate hypotheses for future research.

Conclusion

This study highlighted, in a real-life setting, the incidence rate of SRI in a population exposed to immunosuppressive therapy. Evaluating the risk of SRI at the individual level remains very challenging due to the diversity of situations. Our results suggest that the risk of SRI primarily affects individuals with comorbidities when treated with immunosuppressive therapy and is lower in those receiving csDMARD only compared to those prescribed corticosteroids. This information can help inform prescribers and patients when making decisions about initiating immunosuppressive therapy.
