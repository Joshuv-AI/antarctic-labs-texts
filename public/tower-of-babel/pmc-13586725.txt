Clinical, parasitological, and hematologic profile of pregnant women with malaria in Colombia: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Catalina Tovar Acero, Paola Aparicio, Roger Tovar-Falon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Plasmodium vivax predominated among pregnant women with malaria in Colombia.•Anemia and thrombocytopenia were frequent hematologic findings.•P. falciparum was associated with referral and adverse fetal indicators.•Thrombocytopenia was linked to intensive care unit admission, transfusion, and intrauterine growth restriction.•Hematologic markers may support risk assessment in malaria in pregnancy.

Introduction

Malaria in pregnancy remains a condition of substantial clinical and epidemiologic importance in endemic regions because of its effects on maternal, fetal, and neonatal health. Pregnancy is accompanied by immunologic and hematologic changes that may increase susceptibility to infection and contribute to adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes. Malaria in pregnancy has been associated with maternal anemia, low birth weight, preterm birth, fetal death, and neonatal mortality, with particularly relevant effects in populations exposed to social vulnerability and limited access to health care services [1].

The maternal and fetal consequences of malaria are determined not solely by the presence of parasites in peripheral blood but also by inflammatory, hematologic, and placental mechanisms. In Plasmodium falciparum infection, parasitized erythrocytes may sequester in the placenta, a process linked to placental inflammation, impaired maternal–fetal exchange, fetal growth restriction, and other adverse perinatal outcomes [2,3]. Hematologic alterations, particularly anemia and thrombocytopenia, may also reflect disease severity and help to guide clinical assessment. Although P. falciparum has historically received greater attention because of its association with severe and placental malaria, P. vivax can also cause clinically relevant morbidity during pregnancy [4,5].

Infection with P. vivax during pregnancy has been associated with maternal anemia, recurrent clinical episodes, and an increased need for medical care [6]. In fetuses and newborns, P. vivax infection has been linked to lower birth weight, preterm birth, and intrauterine growth restriction. Although the mechanisms underlying these associations are not fully understood, they likely involve a combination of maternal anemia, systemic inflammation, recurrent malaria episodes, and placental alterations that may compromise oxygen and nutrient exchange within the maternal–fetal unit [7].

In 2020, an estimated 121.9 million pregnancies occurred in areas of malaria transmission, of which approximately 70.9 million (58.1%) resulted in live births. Although sub-Saharan Africa accounts for the highest burden of adverse outcomes associated with malaria in pregnancy, a substantial proportion of pregnancies at risk also occur in Latin America [8], where P. vivax predominates and transmission foci persist in several territories, including the Colombian Amazon, northwestern Colombia, and the Pacific coast. A meta-analysis of studies conducted in malaria-endemic areas of Latin America and the Caribbean reported prevalence estimates of 14.20% during antenatal care and 5.28% at delivery, highlighting the persistent burden of the disease and the need to strengthen regional clinical evidence [1].

In Colombia, evidence on malaria in pregnancy remains limited. A national meta-analysis of studies conducted between 2000 and 2020 found prevalence estimates of 5.8% by thick blood smear and 16.7% by molecular methods, suggesting that microscopy-based surveillance may underestimate the burden of malaria in pregnancy [9].

Hospital-based clinical data from endemic regions may provide useful information for maternal and perinatal surveillance. However, evidence from Colombian endemic settings remains limited regarding the combined clinical, parasitological, and hematologic profile of pregnant women with malaria, particularly in hospital-based series with detailed clinical and perinatal information. We hypothesized that the infecting Plasmodium species and hematologic alterations, particularly, anemia and thrombocytopenia, could reflect clinically relevant patterns among pregnant women with malaria and help identify those requiring closer monitoring. Therefore, this study aimed to describe the clinical, parasitological, and hematologic profile of pregnant women with malaria in Colombia and explore maternal, obstetric, and neonatal outcomes according to Plasmodium species.

Methods

Study design

A hospital-based retrospective observational study was conducted through a review of the medical records of pregnant women with a documented positive thick blood smear for Plasmodium.

Study area and population

The study was conducted in the department of Córdoba, located in the Caribbean region of Colombia, where ecologic and social conditions favor malaria transmission. The study population comprised 166 medical records of pregnant women with confirmed malaria who received care at health care institutions in the municipality of Tierralta, Córdoba or were referred to higher-level health care centers between 2018 and 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Records of pregnant women with microscopically confirmed malaria during pregnancy and available information on maternal, clinical, parasitological, hematologic, obstetric, and/or fetal variables were included. Confirmation was based on the recorded Plasmodium species and quantitative parasite density. Clinical symptoms were not required to define a confirmed case. Duplicate records, medical records without diagnostic confirmation of malaria, records with incomplete information, and records of pregnant women with concomitant dengue or leptospirosis were excluded.

Variables and data collection

Data were obtained through a retrospective review of medical records from health care institutions in the municipality of Tierralta, Córdoba, corresponding to cases of confirmed malaria during pregnancy. An anonymized database was constructed that included sociodemographic, clinical, parasitological, hematologic, obstetric, and neonatal variables.

The main variables of interest were the infecting Plasmodium species, parasite density, anemia, and thrombocytopenia. Maternal clinical indicators included the length of hospital stay, referral to higher-level care, blood transfusion, and intensive care unit (ICU) admission. Obstetric and neonatal variables included intrauterine growth restriction, preterm birth, low birth weight, and birth length. The number of malaria episodes was defined as the total number of episodes documented in the medical record during the current pregnancy.

Anemia was defined as hemoglobin <11 g/dL and classified as mild (10.0-10.9 g/dL), moderate (7.0-9.9 g/dL), or severe (<7.0 g/dL), according to the World Health Organization criteria. Thrombocytopenia was defined as a platelet count <150,000 platelets/µL, low birth weight as birth weight <2500 g, and preterm birth as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation. Intrauterine growth restriction and obstetric complications were defined according to diagnoses recorded in the medical records.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using measures of central tendency and dispersion according to their distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as absolute and relative frequencies. Species-specific comparisons were performed between women with P. vivax and those with P. falciparum monoinfection. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test according to distributional assumptions.

Multivariable regression models were fitted according to the nature of each outcome. Binary logistic regression was used for dichotomous outcomes, with results reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Negative binomial regression was used for length of hospital stay because this variable was treated as an overdispersed count outcome, with results reported as adjusted incidence rate ratios (aIRRs) and 95% CIs. Multiple linear regression was used for continuous outcomes when applicable. The number of documented malaria episodes was evaluated as a continuous candidate predictor in the outcome-specific models.

Variable selection was based on clinical relevance and findings from bivariate analyses. Given the sample size and the limited number of events for some outcomes, models were kept parsimonious to reduce the risk of overfitting. All covariates retained in each outcome-specific model were entered simultaneously; therefore, each adjusted estimate accounted for the other covariates included in that analysis. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike information criterion, adjusted R², and residual analysis, as appropriate for each model type. Analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with a two-sided significance level of 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted using secondary data obtained from a retrospective review of medical records, with no direct contact with participants and no intervention in their medical care. The database was anonymized before analysis to protect confidentiality. Given the retrospective design and the use of anonymized data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived in accordance with Resolution 8430 of 1993 for risk-free research in Colombia. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Universidad del Sinú through Act No. 005 of December 1, 2023.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of patients with gestational malaria

A total of 166 medical records of pregnant women with confirmed malaria were included. The median maternal age was 20 years, and all women lived in rural or dispersed rural areas. Prenatal care attendance was limited, with a median of two visits. Malaria was diagnosed mainly after the first trimester, with 45.2% of cases diagnosed during the second trimester and 42.8% during the third trimester. P. vivax was the predominant species, accounting for 80.7% of cases. The median number of documented malaria episodes during pregnancy was three (interquartile range [IQR]: two to six) overall and was higher among women with P. vivax than among those with P. falciparum infection (three [IQR: two to six] vs one [IQR: one to two]; P < 0.001). Overall, hematologic parameters showed a comparable pattern between species. Anemia was frequent in both groups, occurring in 88.1% of women with P. vivax infection and 96.7% of those with P. falciparum infection, with no statistically significant difference. When anemia severity was examined, moderate anemia was the most frequent category, observed in 90.0% of women with P. falciparum infection and 69.4% of those with P. vivax infection. Severe anemia was documented in two women, both of whom had P. vivax infection. Median hemoglobin, hematocrit, and platelet counts did not differ significantly between the groups (Table 2).

Regarding maternal clinical outcomes, hospitalization was frequent in both species groups. The median length of hospital stay was slightly longer among women with P. falciparum infection than among those with P. vivax infection, but this difference was not statistically significant. ICU admission and hemorrhage were numerically more frequent in the P. falciparum group, whereas blood transfusion occurred in approximately 30% of women in both groups. None of these maternal clinical outcomes differed significantly between the species groups, except for referral to a higher level of care.

Regarding obstetric and neonatal outcomes, the gestational age at delivery was similar between species groups, with a median of 39 weeks in both groups. Intrauterine growth restriction was numerically more frequent among women with P. falciparum infection than among those with P. vivax infection (76.7% vs 57.5%); birth weight was lower in the P. falciparum group (3028.5 g vs 3137 g), although neither difference reached statistical significance. Birth length was significantly lower in newborns of mothers with P. falciparum infection, with median values of 49 cm compared with 50 cm in the P. vivax group (P = 0.012). No maternal deaths, eclampsia, or perinatal deaths were recorded in either species group.

Multivariable analysis of factors associated with maternal, obstetric, and neonatal outcomes in malaria in pregnancy

P. falciparum infection and anemia were associated with more hospitalization days (aIRR = 1.63 and 1.70, respectively). In addition, a significant interaction was identified between P. falciparum infection and institutional referral (aIRR = 0.34), indicating that the association between referral and length of hospital stay differed according to the infecting species, compared with women infected with P. vivax.

Thrombocytopenia was associated with two clinically relevant outcomes: ICU admission (aOR = 8.07) and intrauterine growth restriction (aOR = 6.81). P. falciparum infection was also associated with intrauterine growth restriction, compared with P. vivax infection (aOR = 2.80).

The need for blood transfusion was associated with a profile of greater maternal and obstetric complexity. Preeclampsia showed the strongest association with this outcome (aOR = 7.79), followed by preterm birth (aOR = 6.06), thrombocytopenia (aOR = 5.25), and intrauterine growth restriction (aOR = 2.90).

Referral to another health care institution was associated with P. falciparum infection, thrombocytopenia, and parasite density. Compared with P. vivax, P. falciparum infection showed a 3.09-fold higher association with referral to another institution, whereas thrombocytopenia showed an association of a similar magnitude (aOR = 3.25). Likewise, for each increase of 1000 parasites/µl, the estimated aOR for referral to another institution was approximately 1.22.

Regarding the neonatal outcome, preterm birth was associated with an average reduction of 3.35 cm in birth length, whereas maternal hemorrhage was associated with an average reduction of 0.77 cm. Discussion

Malaria in pregnancy occurred mainly among young women from rural and dispersed rural areas, with limited prenatal care attendance and diagnosis predominantly during the second and third trimesters. This pattern may reflect barriers to health care access and prenatal follow-up previously described in malaria-endemic areas of Colombia [10]. These findings underscore the need to strengthen malaria screening during prenatal care in these populations.

The predominance of P. vivax was consistent with the regional epidemiology of malaria in pregnancy in northwestern Colombia. The higher number of documented episodes among women with P. vivax may partly reflect relapses during pregnancy, when primaquine is contraindicated; although Colombian guidelines conditionally recommend weekly chloroquine chemoprophylaxis, its prescription and adherence were not documented in the medical records [11]. In addition to the high frequency of anemia, which is a well-recognized feature of malaria in pregnancy, the finding that 80.1% of women had moderate anemia indicates a substantial hematologic burden in this population [12,13].

The higher peripheral parasite density observed in women with P. vivax infection contrasted with the higher frequency of referral among those with P. falciparum infection. Although unusual, similar or higher P. vivax parasitemia has been reported in previous studies of pregnant women from northwestern Colombia. Rather than indicating greater intrinsic replication by P. vivax, this pattern may reflect the local epidemiologic context, where this species predominates. Whether repeated infections, including relapses arising from hypnozoite reactivation, contribute to this finding warrants further investigation [14,15]. The higher frequency of referral among women with P. falciparum, despite their lower peripheral parasite density, suggests that referral was influenced not only by parasite density but also by clinical and institutional criteria and is consistent with the clinical relevance attributed to P. falciparum infection [16,17].

Hematologic abnormalities and hospital care were frequent in both species groups. The high frequency of anemia, hospitalization in more than 90% of cases, and similar proportions of blood transfusions suggest that P. vivax infection was also associated with substantial maternal morbidity during pregnancy. This finding is consistent with previous evidence linking P. vivax malaria in pregnancy to maternal anemia, even in settings with low parasite density [18]. Although ICU admission and hemorrhage were more frequent among women with P. falciparum infection, maternal morbidity in this cohort was not restricted to a single Plasmodium species.

Birth length was lower among newborns of mothers with P. falciparum infection, despite a similar gestational age at delivery; the lower birth weight observed in this group, although not statistically significant, may partly account for this difference. Although this result should be interpreted in light of the study design and available clinical information, it aligns with previous evidence linking malaria in pregnancy to reduced neonatal anthropometric measurements, including birth length [19]. This finding is also biologically plausible in P. falciparum infection, in which placental sequestration and local inflammation may contribute to impaired fetal growth [3,20]. These findings support assessing neonatal growth beyond birth weight in pregnancies complicated by malaria.

Adjusted models showed that P. falciparum infection and anemia of any severity were associated with a longer hospital stay, and P. falciparum infection was associated with referral to another health care facility. Together, these findings suggest that the clinical burden of malaria in pregnancy may reflect the infecting species and hematologic status rather than parasite density alone. The interaction between species and referral further indicates that the relationship between referral and hospital stay differs between P. vivax and P. falciparum infections. This pattern is consistent with evidence that recognizes malaria in pregnancy as a condition requiring timely clinical care to reduce maternal morbidity and adverse perinatal outcomes [21,22].

Thrombocytopenia was the hematologic abnormality most consistently associated with clinically relevant outcomes, including ICU admission, intrauterine growth restriction, blood transfusion, and referral to another health care facility. As a frequent finding in malaria in pregnancy, a low platelet count may serve as a useful marker of greater maternal or obstetric complexity, supporting its assessment during clinical evaluation and follow-up [23,24]. Rather than a direct causal effect, the association between thrombocytopenia and intrauterine growth restriction may reflect shared malaria-related inflammatory and vascular processes that affect platelet homeostasis and placental function. This hypothesis warrants prospective investigation. Blood transfusion was also linked to preeclampsia, preterm birth, intrauterine growth restriction, and thrombocytopenia, suggesting that transfusion support occurred in the context of broader maternal and obstetric morbidity [25].

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Its retrospective medical record-based design limited the availability of some maternal, nutritional, placental, and socioeconomic variables and did not allow differentiation between relapse and new infection. The smaller number of P. falciparum infections is consistent with the local epidemiology, where P. vivax predominates but may have reduced the precision of species-specific estimates for infrequent outcomes. Patients with documented coinfections with dengue or leptospirosis were excluded to reduce clinical heterogeneity. Urinary tract infections and sepsis were frequently documented in the medical records. Some urinary diagnoses were supported by urinalysis findings suggestive of bacteriuria, but urine cultures were unavailable, and sepsis could not be independently confirmed. Physiologic changes associated with pregnancy may increase susceptibility to urinary tract infections [26]. Referral to another health care facility should also be interpreted as an outcome influenced by clinical status and institutional criteria. Associations related to bleeding should not be interpreted as causal because a temporal relationship with the evaluated outcomes could not be established. Despite these limitations, this study provides a detailed hospital-based characterization of malaria in pregnancy in a Colombian endemic region and identifies clinically relevant patterns, particularly, the high burden of anemia and thrombocytopenia, that may support risk assessment and closer monitoring.

Conclusions

In this population of pregnant women with malaria from rural and dispersed rural areas, malaria in pregnancy was characterized by the predominance of P. vivax, multiple malaria episodes during gestation, and a high frequency of moderate anemia. Although P. vivax infection was associated with a higher parasite density, P. falciparum infection was more frequently associated with referral to another health care facility and showed associations with intrauterine growth restriction and lower birth length. Thrombocytopenia was consistently associated with ICU admission, blood transfusion, intrauterine growth restriction, and referral, suggesting its clinical relevance as a marker of maternal and obstetric complexity. Overall, these findings indicate that the clinical burden of malaria in pregnancy was not defined solely by the predominant species or parasite density but also by hematologic alterations and clinically relevant maternal, obstetric, and neonatal outcomes.
