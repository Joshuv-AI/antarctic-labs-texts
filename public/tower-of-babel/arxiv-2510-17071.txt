Differentiating Through Power Flow Solutions for Admittance and Topology Control
Samuel Talkington, Daniel Turizo, Sergio A. Dorado-Rojas, Rahul K. Gupta, Daniel K. Molzahn

Abstract
The power flow equations relate bus voltage phasors to power injections via the network admittance matrix. These equations are central to the key operational and protection functions of power systems (e.g., optimal power flow scheduling and control, state estimation, protection, and fault location, among others). As control, optimization, and estimation of network admittance parameters are central to multiple avenues of research in electric power systems, we propose a linearization of power flow solutions obtained by implicitly differentiating them with respect to the network admittance parameters. This is achieved by utilizing the implicit function theorem, in which we show that such a differentiation is guaranteed to exist under mild conditions and is applicable to generic power systems (radial or meshed). The proposed theory is applied to derive sensitivities of complex voltages, line currents, and power flows. The developed theory of linearizing the power flow equations around changes in the complex network admittance parameters has numerous applications. We demonstrate several of these applications, such as predicting the nodal voltages when the network topology changes without solving the power flow equations. We showcase the application for continuous admittance control, which is used to increase the hosting capacity of a given distribution network.

Contributions:

This work presents a generalized technique to analytically differentiate power flow solutions with respect to network admittance parameters. Earlier investigations of this idea, such as the work of
[
25
,
30
,
31
]
, show that this technique enables several admittance control applications to be made more efficient.
Our work contributes an array of new generalizations and extensions of this earlier work, spanning theory, modeling, and applications. In terms of modeling, we consider a broad range of electrical quantities of interest that derive from power flow solutions, generalizing the sensitivity computations to line currents and to arbitrary bus types; in addition, the proposed method is capable of handling shunt admittances, and connections that do not yet exist—these capabilities are new, to the knowledge of the authors.

Moreover, these advances open the door to a wide range of applications in improving the scalability of both continuous and discrete topology control and optimization problems. We demonstrate just a few of these applications—topological impact prediction, voltage control, and congestion management—in Section

Further, we provide additional theoretical contributions that extend the existing literature. Compared to
[
25
,
33

This mild assumption ensures our method’s practical relevance.

We also present several applications of the proposed method. The method
provides a continuous approximation of power flow solutions as a function of admittance parameters. This allows for the outcome of the iterative procedure of solving the AC power flow equations with changed admittance to be predicted by taking linearizations about arbitrary admittance parameters of the user’s choice. This is particularly useful when performing fast analyses of topology control.

III
Differentiating power flow solutions with respect to admittance parameters

This section presents the proposed method to derive the sensitivity coefficients of nodal voltage and current phasors with respect to the admittance parameters. We show how they can be derived by implicit differentiation. The derivations for the voltages and the currents are presented as follows.

III-A

Nodal voltages

Assumption 1

\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
is the standard power flow Jacobian matrix in polar coordinates. If Assumption
1
holds, then
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{P}},\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\bullet})
is non-singular, so
\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa}
is non-singular as well. By the Implicit Function Theorem, there exists unique, continuously differentiable functions
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{b})
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{b})
defined in a neighborhood of
(\boldsymbol{g}^{\bullet},\boldsymbol{b}^{\bullet})
such that
\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}(\boldsymbol{g}^{\bullet},\boldsymbol{b}^{\bullet})=\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\bullet}
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{g}^{\bullet},\boldsymbol{b}^{\bullet})=\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\bullet}
, and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{b});\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{0}_{2n-g}
for all
(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{b})
in the neighborhood. Moreover, the derivatives of these functions satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}&\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{b}}\\
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}&\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{b}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=-(\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa}}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}&\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa}}{\partial\boldsymbol{b}}\end{bmatrix}

(13a)

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{P}},\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\bullet})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa}}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}&\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\kappa}}{\partial\boldsymbol{b}}\end{bmatrix},

(13b)

so by construction the claim holds.
∎

In the expression (
13b
), the Jacobian matrices taken with respect to the admittance parameters can be analytically computed from (
9
); see Appendix
-A
. Furthermore, from (
1
) we can compute the Wirtinger derivatives of the power flow solution with respect to the network parameters
\boldsymbol{w}
as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}-\mathrm{j}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{b}}\right),

(14a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}+\mathrm{j}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{b}}\right),

(14b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}-\mathrm{j}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{b}}\right),

(14c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}+\mathrm{j}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{b}}\right).

(14d)

Theorem

shows that the expressions (

III-B

Line current flows

Now, we establish how to differentiate the line currents with respect to the admittance parameters.

III-B
1
Shuntless networks

When there are no shunts in the network, by Ohm’s law, the line current phasors are

u_{ij}=(g_{ij}+\mathrm{j}b_{ij})(x_{i}-x_{j}),\quad\forall\;(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}.

(15)

Now, define the voltage phasor difference
\Delta_{ij}:=x_{i}-x_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
across a line
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
. Recall that for any continuously differentiable function
\boldsymbol{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
, we have that
\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\operatorname{Re}\{\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\}+\mathrm{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\operatorname{Im}\{\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\}
. Consequently, by the above identities and the chain and product rules, the derivatives of the line currents with respect to the network conductance parameters are

\frac{\partial u_{ij}}{\partial g_{kl}}=\begin{cases}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial g_{ij}}g_{ij}+\Delta_{ij}+\mathrm{j}\,\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial g_{ij}}b_{ij}&(i,j)=(k,l)\\
\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial g_{kl}}g_{ij}+\mathrm{j}\,\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial g_{kl}}b_{ij}&\operatorname{\sf otherwise}\end{cases}

(16)

and similarly, the derivatives with respect to the susceptance parameters are

\frac{\partial u_{ij}}{\partial b_{kl}}=\begin{cases}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial b_{ij}}g_{ij}+\mathrm{j}\left(\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial b_{ij}}b_{ij}+\Delta_{ij}\right)&(i,j)=(k,l)\\
\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial b_{kl}}g_{ij}+\mathrm{j}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial b_{kl}}b_{ij}&\operatorname{\sf otherwise}\end{cases}

(17)

for all
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{K},i\neq j
and
(k,l)\in\mathcal{E}_{K},k\neq l
. Notice that
u_{ij}=-u_{ji}
, so we only need to compute sensitivities for currents in one direction.

III-B
2
Generalization to networks with shunts

In the case where there are shunts in the network, let
\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf br}\in\mathbb{C}^{n(n-1)/2}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf sh}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
denote the branch and shunt current phasors in rectangular coordinates, respectively. Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{n(n-1)/2}
be the concatenation of these two vectors. The branch and shunt currents can be computed from (
9

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf br}\\
\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf sh}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{w})\mathsf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{x}

(18a)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{w})\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}(n)\\
\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}

(18b)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}(n)\\
\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\boldsymbol{w},

(18c)

where
\boldsymbol{A}(n)
is the generalized incidence matrix of a complete graph with
n
nodes and phase shifts matching that of the system. Thus, differentiating, we obtain the block matrix

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf br}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}&\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf sh}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

(19)

where the blocks

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf br}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{A}(n)\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\sf br})\boldsymbol{A}(n)\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}},

(20a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf sh}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\sf sh})\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}},

(20b)

where
\boldsymbol{w}^{\sf br}
is the vector formed by the first
n(n-1)/2
entries of
\boldsymbol{w}
(the branch weights) and
\boldsymbol{w}^{\sf sh}
is the vector formed by the last
n
entries of
\boldsymbol{w}
(the shunt weights). Similarly, differentiating with respect to the complex conjugate of the admittance vector, we arrive at

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf br}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}&\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf sh}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

(21)

where the blocks are computed as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf br}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\sf br})\boldsymbol{A}(n)\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},

(22a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}^{\sf sh}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\sf sh})\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}.

(22b)

Lastly, we derive the sensitivities of the current flow magnitudes
\boldsymbol{\ell}=|\boldsymbol{u}|
. To this end, observe that

\boldsymbol{\ell}=\left(\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{u})\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{u}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\right)^{1/2},

(23)

so the sensitivities are computed as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\ell})^{-1}\left(\operatorname{diag}(\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{u}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}+\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{u})\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\right)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\right),

(24a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\ell})^{-1}\left(\operatorname{diag}(\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{u}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\boldsymbol{w}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}+\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{u})\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\right)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\right).

(24b)

IV
Application of Admittance Sensitivities

In this section, we illustrate two distinct applications of the proposed power flow linearization technique. The first is on estimating the power flow solutions with change in topology without the need of solving the power flow equations directly. The second consists of controlling the admittance parameters continuously such as line impedances for voltage regulation.

Predicting power flow solution with a change in topology

The voltage-admittance sensitivity matrices allow us to linearize a power flow solution around nominal admittance parameters
\boldsymbol{g}^{\bullet}
and
\boldsymbol{b}^{\bullet}
. For example, we can form functions that approximate the voltage magnitude component of a power flow solution
\boldsymbol{\hat{v}}
and the corresponding branch current magnitudes
\boldsymbol{\hat{\ell}}
as a function of the topology parameters, where

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\hat{v}}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{b})\\
\boldsymbol{\hat{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{b})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{P}}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\bullet}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{K}^{v}_{g}&\boldsymbol{K}^{v}_{b}\\
\boldsymbol{K}^{\ell}_{g}&\boldsymbol{K}^{\ell}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{g}-\boldsymbol{g}^{\bullet}\\
\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{b}^{\bullet}\end{bmatrix}.

(30)

where
\boldsymbol{K}^{v}_{g}=\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}/\partial\boldsymbol{g}
,
\boldsymbol{K}^{v}_{b}=\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathcal{P}}/\partial\boldsymbol{b}
,
\boldsymbol{K}^{\ell}_{g}=\partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{g}
, and
\boldsymbol{K}^{\ell}_{b}=\partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{b}
. For the line flows, we may consider only the flows of lines in
\mathcal{E}
instead of
\mathcal{E}_{K}
. These coefficients are computed using (
13b
) and (
20
) for a pre-defined operating point around which power flow equations are linearized. Later in the numerical experiments in Section
V
, we compare the estimation performance with different operating points.

Admittance control

Continuous admittance control has been proposed in the existing literature such as
smart wires
, which allow for induced changes in line admittances
[
45
]
and shunt reactance control
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. Below, we show a formulation using the proposed power flow linearization for carrying out admittance control for voltage regulation.

Voltage regulation with variable admittance

A simple application of the proposed framework is in allowing admittance parameters to directly become a decision variable in linearized voltage regulation problems. Traditionally, this is solved via heuristic approaches; in FACTS problems, the controllable admittance is modeled as the reactive power injection at a PQ bus, i.e.,
Q_{i}=v_{i}^{2}Y_{ij}^{\sf sh}
.

We demonstrate the practical application of these coefficients via a simple voltage control problem. In particular, applying the linearization approach from Section
IV
, we can construct a linear approximation of the voltage magnitudes and currents around both active and reactive power injections via the matrix

\boldsymbol{K}:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{K}^{v}_{p}&\boldsymbol{K}^{v}_{q}&\boldsymbol{K}^{v}_{g}&\boldsymbol{K}^{v}_{b}\\
\boldsymbol{K}^{\ell}_{p}&\boldsymbol{K}^{\ell}_{q}&\boldsymbol{K}^{\ell}_{g}&\boldsymbol{K}^{\ell}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(31)

Define the overall state of the network as

\boldsymbol{z}:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{q}^{\top}_{\mathcal{P}}&\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{b}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

(32)

and let
\boldsymbol{z}^{\bullet}
be the nominal values of these variables. Define their lower and upper bounds as

\boldsymbol{z}^{\sf min}:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}^{\sf min}\\
\boldsymbol{q}^{\sf min}_{\mathcal{P}}\\
\boldsymbol{g}^{\sf min}\\
\boldsymbol{b}^{\sf min}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{z}^{\sf max}:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}^{\sf max}\\
\boldsymbol{q}^{\sf max}_{\mathcal{P}}\\
\boldsymbol{g}^{\sf max}\\
\boldsymbol{b}^{\sf max}\end{bmatrix}.

(33)

Let
\boldsymbol{y}:=[\boldsymbol{v}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be the approximated solutions, where
\boldsymbol{y}:=\boldsymbol{y}^{\bullet}+\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\bullet})
. The lower and upper bounds of the output are

\boldsymbol{y}^{\sf min}:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sf min}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\sf min}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{y}^{\sf max}:=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sf max}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\sf max}\end{bmatrix}.

(34)

Finally, set
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in[\gamma^{\sf min},\gamma^{\sf max}]^{m}
, where
\gamma^{\sf min},\gamma^{\sf max}\in[0,1]
, with
\gamma^{\sf min}<\gamma^{\sf max}
. We can then form an approximated voltage regulation program where the goal is to minimize active power curtailment with controllable reactive power injections and network admittances. This program can be written as

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}

(35a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\sf s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{\sf min}\leqslant\boldsymbol{y}^{\bullet}+\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\bullet})\leqslant\boldsymbol{y}^{\sf max}

(35b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}^{\sf min}\leqslant\boldsymbol{z}\leqslant\boldsymbol{z}^{\sf max}

(35c)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\sf min}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}\leqslant\boldsymbol{\gamma}\leqslant\gamma^{\sf max}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}

(35d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\boldsymbol{g}^{\bullet}

(35e)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\boldsymbol{b}^{\bullet},

(35f)

which is a linear program. Section
V-B
shows how continuous admittance control can be used for voltage regulation with the formulation in (
35
). We also demonstrate its use for increasing the hosting capacity of a sample distribution network.

Discrete topology control for congestion management

Admittance sensitivities also enable fast discrete line switching algorithms, where we optimize over a vector of binary
switching variables

\boldsymbol{z}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{m}
, where
z_{ij}=1
(resp.
z_{ij}=0
) indicates that the line
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}

is closed (resp. switched open). In this section, we illustrate an efficient way to optimize topologies using the framework of the present paper (Alg.
1
), which we present below.

In many applications involving congestion management, the objective of a grid planning or topology control problem can be written as a parameterized function of the line admittances, or modifications thereof, i.e., switching statuses. As a simple example, consider the problem of minimizing the sum of the flow-to-capacity ratios of each line
c_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{z}):=|u_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{x}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)|^{2}/(u_{ij}^{\sf\max})^{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}(\boldsymbol{z})
is a power flow solution which depends on
\boldsymbol{z}
, see
[
15
]
for more details on this metric. Seeking to minimize this congestion metric yields a program of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{m},\,\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}z_{ij}\,c_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{z})=:C(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{z})

(36a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\sf s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z},

(36b)

\displaystyle\mathsf{power\ flow\ equations},

(36c)

\displaystyle\mathsf{engineering\ constraints,}

(36d)

where constraint (
36b
) represents switching constraints, which are not included in (
36d
). For example, one simple choice is the set
\mathcal{Z}=\left\{\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{1}\;\leq\;K\right\}
, which represents a budget of at most
K
switches being switched closed. The program (
36
) is a challenging mixed-integer non-linear program due to the binary variables and the AC equations embedded in
c_{ij}(\cdot)
through the constraint (
36c
).

Continuous relaxation and rounding

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in[0,1]^{m}
be the relaxed switch vector. Using the current sensitivity results from Section
III-B
, the gradient of the squared current magnitude through each line
l
w.r.t. the relaxed switching variables is

\mathbf{\mathfrak{g}}:=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left|u_{l}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right|^{2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left|u_{l}\right|^{2}=2\cdot\left|u_{l}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right|\boldsymbol{k}_{l},

(37)

where
(\boldsymbol{k}_{l})_{e}=\partial\left|u_{l}\right|/\partial\gamma_{e}
are the sensitivities of the current magnitude through line
l
w.r.t. the admittance parameter
\gamma_{e}
.

In particular, in the simple case where
\mathcal{Z}=\left\{\boldsymbol{z}:\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{1}\leq K\right\}
, we can use the gradients (
37
) to approximate the objective of the program (

V-C

Implications and Analysis of Results

The contributions of our work have several engineering implications, from both practical and theoretical perspectives. One of the most immediate theoretical consequences of the technique outlined in Section
IV
is centrally showcased in the numerical results in Section
V-A
, showing the
prediction of power flow solutions
as a function of network admittance parameters.

Conventionally, determining power flow solutions across a range of network admittances would require repeatedly executing a power flow solver across
k
intervals, as shown in the dotted lines in Fig.
3
. If a conventional Newton-Raphson algorithm was executed for
m
iterations, sweeping across the admittance space would require
km
Jacobian factorizations. In contrast, applying the proposed linearization technique allows us to
estimate the outcome of this iterative procedure
with a precomputed Jacobian with respect to admittance. This prediction would amount to
k
matrix multiplications.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we developed a rigorous framework to compute the sensitivities of power flow solutions with respect to network admittance parameters. Our method efficiently derives these sensitivities by differentiating the bus injection model of the power flow equations and leverages the implicit function theorem to derive admittance sensitivities. We established that these coefficients can be obtained by solving a linear system of equations, which remains uniquely solvable as long as the power flow Jacobian is invertible. The scheme was applied to compute sensitivity of complex voltages, line currents and power flows.

We demonstrated the application of these sensitivities in obtaining a linearized formulation for continuous admittance control, enabling computationally efficient network control. We evaluated the proposed method on multiple benchmark networks demonstrating the tractability of admittance control in power networks. The proposed framework paves the way for tractable linear formulation for adaptive network reconfiguration, optimal line switching, and continuous admittance control operational flexibility.
