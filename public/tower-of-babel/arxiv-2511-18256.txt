First-Passage Times for the Space Fractional Fokker-Planck Equation
Christopher N. Angstmann, Daniel S. Han, Bruce I. Henry, Boris Z. Huang

Abstract
We extend the random walk framework to include compounded steps, providing first-passage time (FPT) properties for a new class of superdiffusive processes, which are governed by the space-fractional spectral Fokker-Planck equation. This first-passage process leads to novel FPT properties, different from Lévy flights and walks, that account for space dependent forces and hitting boundaries throughout the path of a jump. The FPT distribution can be derived for different types of barriers and potentials, for which we also provide specific examples. For the one-sided absorbing boundary on the semi-infinite line, we find that the FPT density scales as t − 1 / ( 2 α ) − 1 t^{-1/(2\alpha)-1} , in agreement with the method of images but in violation of the Sparre-Andersen scaling. In this case, there exists an optimal space-fractional exponent α \alpha to minimize the mean FPT.

These models, referred to as Lévy flights and walks, include jumps with divergent variance, causing the paths to be almost surely discontinuous.
This leads to the neglect of path-dependent effects such as barriers, boundaries and potential fields.
A recent study has shown that superdiffusion and the space fractional Fokker-Planck equation can arise from compounded random walks
1

These compounded random walks share the same governing equation as the Lévy flight on
\mathbb{R}
:

\dfrac{\partial\rho(x,t)}{\partial t}=-D_{\alpha}(-\nabla^{2})^{\alpha}\rho(x,t),

(1)

where
\rho(x,t)
is the probability density of finding the random walker at
x

\alpha\in(0,1]
is the fractional exponent,
D_{\alpha}
is the anomalous diffusion coefficient and
(-\nabla^{2})^{\alpha}
is the spectral fractional Laplacian, which can be defined on bounded or unbounded domains

In this Letter, we explore the FPT of the compounded random walk
1
and show that the FPT properties differ from Lévy flights and walks.
Physically, the differences arise due to the compounded random walk having connected paths in the continuum limit.
Unlike previous formulations, this compounding allows the superdiffusive particle to interact with boundaries and sample local forces across its entire path.
The link between the compounded random walk and the spectral fractional Laplacian
1
allows us to formulate FPT problems in the spectral sense.
This is possible due to the homogeneous boundary conditions which arise from the formulation, as opposed to the exterior conditions necessary in Lévy flight models.
In addition, the compounded random walk can be easily extended to include space-dependent biases, which leads to the space-fractional spectral Fokker-Planck equation

;

;
1

\dfrac{\partial\rho(x,t)}{\partial t}=-D_{\alpha}(-\mathcal{L})^{\alpha}\rho(x,t),

This operator is spectrally defined by

In what follows, we derive the FPT distribution from the compounded random walk, provide examples of FPT problems for different boundary conditions and outline a Monte Carlo scheme for stochastic simulations.
The examples on a finite interval and the semi-infintie line highlight how the FPT distribution, density and mean governed by (

Model Framework.

Consider a discrete-time random walk on the set
\Omega=\{x_{i}=i\Delta x|0\leq i\leq M,\Delta x=L/M\}
, where a particle makes a step after every time increment. We denote the random variable
Y_{n}\in\Omega
as the location of the particle after the
n
time increments, given some initial position
Y_{0}\in\Omega
. Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\{0,L\}\subset\Omega
be a set of points such that the particle is absorbed once it enters
\Omega^{\prime}
. We are only concerned with the FPT into
\Omega^{\prime}
where it is absorbed, so the particle is considered to have stopped once it enters
\Omega^{\prime}
. We will denote the probability of stepping from
x_{i}
to
x_{j}
via the probability transition function

\Lambda_{1}(x_{i},x_{j})=P(Y_{n}=x_{j}|Y_{n-1}=x_{i}),

(5)

for all
n

x_{i}
to
x_{j}
in exactly
n
steps, without entering
\Omega^{\prime}
during the intermittent steps, is defined recursively by

\Lambda_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})=\sum_{x_{k}\notin\Omega^{\prime}}\Lambda_{n-1}(x_{i},x_{k})\Lambda_{1}(x_{k},x_{j}).

(6)

Note that the sum does not include the probability of stepping into the absorbing set so the sum over all
x_{j}
may not be equal to
1
, which is to say

\sum_{x_{j}\notin\Omega^{\prime}}\Lambda_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\sum_{x_{j}\in\Omega}\Lambda_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq 1.

(7)

The FPT into
\Omega^{\prime}
is the first time step for which
Y_{n}\in\Omega^{\prime}
and is written as

n^{*}=\inf\{n|Y_{n}\in\Omega^{\prime}\}.

(8)

For a random walk initially at
x_{i}\notin\Omega^{\prime}
, the probability of making
n
steps without being absorbed is called the survival function given by

P(n^{*}>n)=\sum_{x_{j}\notin\Omega^{\prime}}\Lambda_{n}(x_{i},x_{j}).

(9)

To obtain the FPT for the compounded random walk, we embed the steps from the discrete-time random walk above onto real values of time
t>0
.
Suppose that for some
\Delta t>0
, the particle undergoes a compounded stepping process, where in the time interval
((m-1)\Delta t,m\Delta t]

K_{m}

is an i.i.d. random variable, or it has been absorbed into
\Omega^{\prime}

The probability of making

.
For convenience, we will assume that each of these

steps are uniformly spaced in time over the interval with the last step occurring at time
m\Delta t

The total number of steps taken up to time

may be expressed as

N_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}K_{i}.

(10)

We define
T_{n}\in[0,\infty)
as the time when the
n
th
step occurs.
Then, the FPT of the compounded random walk is defined as the time when the
n^{*}
th

T_{n^{*}}=\inf\{T_{n}|Y_{n}\in\Omega^{\prime}\}
and can be written as

T_{n^{*}}=(m-1)\Delta t+\dfrac{n^{*}-N_{m-1}}{K_{m}}\Delta t,

(11)

where
m=\sup\{i|n^{*}>N_{i-1}\}
,
and

(m-1)\Delta t<T_{n^{*}}\leq m\Delta t.

(12)

Stochastic simulation method.

The simulation of each step of the compounded random walk would have a divergent expected simulation time due to the divergent mean in each Sibuya random variable. Instead, we consider the location of the particle after each time step and find the conditional probability that the particle was absorbed during the intermittent steps. The simulation method for the Monte Carlo scheme is performed as follows: (1) Choose the value of
\Delta t
and calculate the value of
\Delta x
via the relation (
18
); (2) Initialize particle at some position
X=x_{0}
and set initial time as
t=0
; (3) Draw a Sibuya random variable
K

8
; (4) Draw a binomial random variable
Y
for the number of steps to the right so that the number of steps taken to the left is
K-Y
; (5) Calculate the probability
P
, that the particle will be stopped in the next interval of
\Delta t
time with the following rules: (5a) If
X\leq 0
, then
P=1
; (5b) If
X>0
and
X+Y\Delta x\leq K\Delta x
, then we have

P=\frac{\Gamma(Y+1)\Gamma(K-Y+1)}{\Gamma(\lceil X/\Delta x\rceil+Y+1)\Gamma(K-\lceil X/\Delta x\rceil-Y+1)};

(5c) Otherwise,
P=0
; (6) Update
t\leftarrow t+\delta t
; (7) Draw a Bernoulli random variable with parameter
P
to stop the process; (8) If not stopped, then given
X=x_{j}

Summary.

We conclude that the space-fractional spectral Fokker-Planck equation (
2
) arising from a compounded random walk is amenable to first-passage time problems.
Here we have embedded a discrete-time random walk in real time and taken a diffusion limit to obtain the governing equation (
2
), the same equation can be derived using a CTRW framework
1
.
The underlying compounded process directly corresponds to the physical path which a particle traverses, thus permitting local effects from a potential to properly influence its trajectory.
This property also makes the first hitting or arrival time equivalent to the FPT, unlike the Lévy flight.
Furthermore, the spectral nature of the governing equation allows us to obtain analytical solutions for the FPT distribution and its properties given different constraints.
These analytical solutions have a natural connection with their diffusion FPT counterparts making the superdiffusive results physically interpretable.

We examined the difference between the two models in the semi-infinite domain and found the compounded random walk has a finite mean FPT for
0<\alpha<1/2
, as opposed to the divergent mean FPT for Lévy flights
25
.
Surprisingly, we find that there is a choice of
\alpha
dependent on the initial position
x_{0}
and
D_{\alpha}
that minimizes the mean FPT for the compounded random walk.
Thus, the compounded random walk model introduced in this paper provides an alternative underlying microscopic model that shares the governing equation and anomalous MSD properties with Lévy flights but also retaining the spectral and asymptotic properties with the fractional Fokker-Planck equation.
Finally, we detail an efficient Monte Carlo method for stochastic simulation of these compounded random walks.
This makes the spectral fractional Fokker-Planck equation and its underlying compounded random walk an ideal tool for modeling across a range of physical systems.
