An iterative approach to a fluid-rigid body interaction problem
Charles M. Elliott ⋆, Thomas Sales †

Abstract
. We study a novel approach for the existence of solutions to an incompressible fluid-rigid body interaction problem in three dimensions. Our approach introduces an iteration based on a sequence of related problems posed on domains with prescribed evolution. In particular we prove the short-time existence of strong solutions to a system coupling the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations to the ordinary differential equations governing the motion of a rigid body, with no slip boundary conditions on the boundary of the rigid body, provided that the relative density, ρ ρ B \frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}} , is sufficiently small. We also discuss the use of our iterative approach in numerical methods for the moving boundary problem, and complement this with some numerical experiments in two dimensions which demonstrate the necessity of the smallness assumption on ρ ρ B \frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}} .

Introduction

This paper considers a novel iterative approach to construct solutions to a
moving boundary problem
where the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations are coupled to the equations of motion for a rigid body.
The movement of the boundary comes from the motion of the rigid body, and so one must simultaneously solve for both the solutions to the system of differential equations and the (time-dependent) fluid domain.
In particular we study the short time existence of functions
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathbf{u},p)
which solve, in a suitable weak sense,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right)=-\nabla p+\mu\Delta\mathbf{u},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0,

\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Omega(t)\times\{t\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]

Here

At each time the fluid domain is given by

Our approach to the existence of solutions is based on an iteration of solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations posed on a sequence of evolving domains with prescribed evolution.
The crux of this approach is quite intuitive: if we make a guess for the motion of the rigid body, we obtain a guess for the motion of the fluid, which in turn gives a new guess for the motion of the rigid body.
By iterating accordingly we hope to obtain a fixed point which, by construction, will solve the moving boundary problem.
For the sake of clarity we only consider a single rigid body, although our approach extends to multiple rigid bodies in a straightforward way, and likewise we do not consider a body force acting on the fluid.

2.1.
Function spaces

As our approach is based on a sequence of solutions to the Navier–Stokes equations posed on a sequence of evolving domains it is necessary for us to discuss the appropriate function spaces in which solutions shall live.
As such we shall use the theory of evolving function spaces, initiated by Alphonse, Elliott & Stinner in
[
6
,
7
]
, which has been useful in the analysis of PDEs on evolving domains (see for instance
[
5
]
).
To this end we now briefly recall some of the theory of
[
5
,
6
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(X(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a family of real Banach spaces indexed over
[0,T]
, such that for each
t\in[0,T]
there exists a bounded, invertible, linear map

\Phi_{t}:X(0)\rightarrow X(t),

with inverse

\Phi_{-t}:X(t)\rightarrow X(0).

We say that the indexed pair
(X(t),\Phi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is compatible if:

(1)

\Phi_{0}
is the identity map on
X(0)
.

(2)

There exists a constant,
C
, independent of
t\in[0,T]
such that

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{t}u\|_{X(t)}\leq C\|u\|_{X(0)},

\displaystyle\forall u\in X(0),

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{-t}v\|_{X(0)}\leq C\|v\|_{X(t)},

\displaystyle\forall v\in X(t).

(3)

For all
u\in X(0)
the map
t\mapsto\|\Phi_{t}u\|_{X(t)}
is measurable.

For a compatible family of time-dependent Banach spaces,
(X(t),\Phi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, one defines time-dependent Bochner spaces as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(X(t),\Phi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a compatible family of time-dependent Banach spaces.
The space
L^{p}_{X}
consists of (equivalence classes of) functions

\displaystyle u:[0,T]\rightarrow\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}X(t)\times\{t\},

\displaystyle t\mapsto(\overline{u}(t),t),

such that
\Phi_{-(\cdot)}\overline{u}(\cdot)\in L^{p}(0,T;X(0))
.
We identify
u
with
\overline{u}
throughout.
This space is equipped with norm

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{X}}:=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|^{p}_{X(t)}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&p\in[1,\infty),\\
\underset{t\in[0,T]}{\operatorname{ess\ sup\ }}\|u(t)\|_{X(t)},&p=\infty.\end{cases}

It is known (see
[
5
,
6
]
) that these are Banach spaces, and for
p=2
and
X(t)
a family of Hilbert spaces these are Hilbert spaces with inner product

(u,v)_{L^{2}_{X}}=\int_{0}^{T}(u(t),v(t))_{X(t)},

for
u,v\in L^{2}_{X}
.
In this case, as justified in
[
6
]
, we make the identification of dual spaces
(L^{2}_{X})^{\prime}\cong L^{2}_{X^{\prime}}
.
To distinguish between two function spaces parametrised over the same initial space we shall include the parametrisation as an argument, i.e.
L^{p}_{X}(\Phi)
, which will help distinguish which evolving domain (and parametrisation) we are considering.

2.2.
Some useful results

We now recall some results to be used throughout.
We begin with a crucial observation — since the geometry of the boundary does not change (as we consider rigid body motion) one may obtain constants from elliptic regularity theory/Sobolev embeddings which are independent of
t\in[0,T]
.
This follows by observing that estimates obtained by “straightening the boundary”, as in
[
45
, §6.2]
, are unchanged by translations/rotations of the rigid body.
Likewise the constants appearing in Sobolev embeddings are unchanged (see for instance
[
45
, §7.7]
).
This observation will be hidden throughout our analysis, and indeed any extension of our method to moving boundary problems with boundary deformation would require more careful usage of tools such as elliptic regularity theory.

Firstly we define the following divergence-free subspace of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))
,

\mathbf{H}_{\sigma}(\Omega(t)):=\left\{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbf{C}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))\mid\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}=0\right\}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}}.

Here the superscript denotes the closure in the
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))
norm.
We now state some well known results.

Lemma 2.3

(Helmholtz decomposition)
.

Let
\Omega(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded, evolving domain with Lipschitz boundary.
Then for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))
there exist unique
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}_{\sigma}(\Omega(t)),\phi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega(t))
such that

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}+\nabla\phi.

Proof.

See
[
67
, Theorem 2.16]
.
∎

Definition 2.4
.

For
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))
we define the Leray projection of

to be
P_{\sigma}\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{H}_{\sigma}(\Omega(t))

P_{\sigma}\mathbf{u}:=\mathbf{v},

for

Lemma 2.5
.

For
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))
one has that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|P_{\sigma}\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))},

and if one has further regularity,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|P_{\sigma}\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}\leq C\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))},

Proof.

The first result follows from the fact that

\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{u}\cdot P_{\sigma}\mathbf{u}=\int_{\Omega(t)}|P_{\sigma}\mathbf{u}|^{2}.

The second result follows from standard elliptic regularity theory, and our above observation that the constants obtained depend only on the geometry of
\Omega(0)
due to the rigid body motion.
∎

Lemma 2.10

(Reynolds Transport Theorem)
.

Let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}
be two sufficiently smooth functions defined on an evolving domain
\Omega(t)
moving with velocity
\mathbf{V}
.
Then

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}&=\int_{\Omega(t)}\partial^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\mathbf{w}+\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{u}\cdot\partial^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{w}}+\int_{\Omega(t)}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w})(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V})\\
&=\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}\cdot\mathbf{w}+\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{u}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}}{\partial t}+\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w})(\mathbf{V}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}),\\
\end{split}

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{w}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\partial^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{u}}:\nabla\mathbf{w}+\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\partial^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{w}+\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{V})\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{w},

where we have used
\partial^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{V}}
as shorthand notation for the material derivative following the flow of
\mathbf{V}
,

\partial^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{u}:=\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+\mathbf{V}\cdot(\nabla\mathbf{u}),

where
\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{V})
denotes the deformation tensor

\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{V}):=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V})\mathbb{I}-(\nabla\mathbf{V}+(\nabla\mathbf{V})^{T}).

Proof.

We only prove the second equality as the first is variation of the usual Reynolds transport theorem (see for instance
[
48
]
), and indeed this proof is essentially the same as that of
[
30
, Lemma 5.2]

For the second equality we note that by pulling back onto
\Omega(0)

\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{w}=\int_{\Omega(0)}J(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{u}}:(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{w}},

where
\Phi(t):\Omega(0)\rightarrow\Omega(t)
is the flow generated by
\mathbf{V}
, and

, and hats denote the pullback by

\widehat{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{u}\circ\Phi(t)
.
This is discussed later in Lemma

Differentiating the above in time yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{u}}:(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{w}}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{u}}:(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega(0)}J(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}:(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{w}}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{u}}:\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega(0)}J(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{u}}:(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}{\partial t}.

We then recall that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}=J\widehat{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}}

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}=-(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}(\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{V}})^{T}(\nabla\Phi)^{-T},

from which one finds that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\partial^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{u}}:\nabla\mathbf{w}+\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\partial^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{w}+\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{w}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V})

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega(t)}(\nabla\mathbf{V})^{T}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{w}-\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\mathbf{u}:(\nabla\mathbf{V})^{T}\nabla\mathbf{w},

which one can readily rewrite as in (
2.8
).
∎

3.
Navier–Stokes equations on a domain with prescribed evolution

3.1.
Statement of the problem

In this section we recall some results on the Navier–Stokes equations posed on a bounded evolving domain,
\Omega(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with prescribed evolution.
Motivated by the moving boundary problem we consider spatial domains of the form
\Omega(t)=(\Omega\setminus B(t))^{\circ}
, where
B(t)
is a rigid body moving with a prescribed velocity (i.e.
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
are known functions).
We are interested in
strong solutions
of the problem

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right)&=-\nabla p+\mu\Delta\mathbf{u},\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}&=0,\end{split}

on the non-cylindrical space-time domain
\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Omega(t)\times\{t\}
, with inhomogeneous boundary conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=0,\text{ on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t)),\text{ on }\partial B(t),

and such that
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}
.
Here we crucially assume that the functions
\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega}
are
known a priori
, sufficiently regular (for our purposes
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])
), and define the motion of
B(t)
.
In particular, this means that the space-time domain
\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Omega(t)\times\{t\}
is prescribed, and evolves with a velocity field
\mathbf{V}
(which is an appropriate extension of the above boundary data for
\mathbf{u}
).
This problem, and similar problems, have been studied previous in
[
13
,
29
,
36
,
39
,
56
,
69
,
70
]
and so our presentation is largely formal.
We note that
[
29
]
uses the same evolving function space framework as us, although it considers the Oseen equations rather than the Navier–Stokes equations.
We assume compatibility, in the sense of Definition
2.1
, of the pairs
(\mathbf{W}^{k,q}(\Omega(t)),\Phi_{t})
for
k=0,1,2
and
q\in[1,\infty]
throughout this section.

We note that the boundary conditions are compatible with the divergence-free condition.
This follows by noting that

\nabla\left(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega_{3}&\omega_{2}\\
\omega_{3}&0&-\omega_{1}\\
-\omega_{2}&\omega_{1}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\omega_{i}
are the components of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, and so
\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right)=0
.

3.1.1.
Transformation to homogeneous boundary conditions

As this problem has inhomogeneous boundary data is it natural to study a transformed problem with homogeneous boundary data.
For each
t\in[0,T]
we define
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)
to be the unique solution to the steady state Stokes problem on
\Omega(t)
:

(3.2)

\begin{aligned} -\mu\Delta\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}+\nabla\widetilde{p}&=0\\
\nabla\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}&=0\end{aligned}\quad\text{on }\Omega(t),

such that

\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}=0\text{ on }\Gamma,\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\text{ on }\partial B(t).

Existence of a weak solution follows from standard results on the Stokes equation, and we have a regularity result (
B.2
).
We will also require that the time derivative of
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}
is sufficiently smooth, namely an element of
L^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(\Phi)
.
Further discussion of the Stokes equation on an evolving domain is discussed in Appendix
B
.

Given these
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}
and
\widetilde{p}
we now define
\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{u}-\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}
and
\overline{p}=p-\widetilde{p}
which formally solve

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\rho\left(\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right)=-\nabla\overline{p}+\mu\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}}-\rho\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}+(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla)\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}+(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla)\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\right),\\
\nabla\cdot\overline{\mathbf{u}}=0,\end{gathered}

such that
\overline{\mathbf{u}}=0
on
\partial\Omega(t)
.
Motivated by this, we study the weak formulation of the Navier–Stokes equation: find
\overline{\mathbf{u}}\in L^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{2}}(\Phi)\cap H^{1}_{\mathbf{H}_{0}^{1}}(\Phi)
and
\overline{p}\in L^{2}_{H^{1}}(\Phi)
solving

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}+\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}+\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left((\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}})+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}})\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}+\mu\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\\
&-\int_{\Omega(t)}\overline{p}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}=-\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}+(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}})\boldsymbol{\eta}\right),\end{aligned}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega(t)}q\nabla\cdot\overline{\mathbf{u}}=0,

for all
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbf{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega(t)),q\in L^{2}(\Omega(t))
, and almost all
t\in[0,T]
, such that
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}
.
We note that here we do not concern ourselves with Leray–Hopf weak type solutions, since our approach will require strong solutions.

In the interest of brevity we introduce shorthand notation,

(3.7)

\mathbf{B}:=\rho\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}+(\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}})^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\right),

which can be interpreted as a body force due to the motion of the rigid body — indeed if the rigid body were stationary then term vanishes.
Notice that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}

\displaystyle\leq\rho\left\|\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}+\rho\|(\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}})^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{2,4}(\Omega(t))}\right),

and hence using the results from Appendix
B
we see that
\mathbf{B}\in L^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(\Phi)
.

3.2.
Energy estimates

We present some formal calculations establishing energy estimates for a solution of (
3.5
), (
3.6
).
Although these calculations are formal, they can be rigorously justified by means of Faedo–Galerkin approximation.

Lemma 3.1
.

A weak solution of (
3.5
) satisfies

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(s))}^{2}\leq e^{\frac{T}{\rho\kappa}}\left(\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right),

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.
Moreover, for
t\in[0,\widetilde{T})
for some
\widetilde{T}=\widetilde{T}(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{u}_{0})
, the solution satisfies

(3.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}\sup_{t\in[0,\widetilde{T}]}\int_{\Omega(t)}|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\int_{\Omega(t)}|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}\leq\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{T}),

for

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(T):=\left(\frac{\left(\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}+\left(\kappa\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}(T)}{4-\left(\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}+\left(\kappa\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}\right)(\mathcal{E}(T)-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(T):=\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\|\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}+\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}^{8}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)\right).

Here
\kappa\in(0,1)
denotes an arbitrary constant to be fixed later, and
C
denotes a constant depending on
\Omega(0)
,
\rho
and
\mu
, and
A_{\sigma}:=-P_{\sigma}\Delta
.

Proof.

To show (
3.9
) we test (
3.5
) with
\overline{\mathbf{u}}
and (
3.6
) with
\overline{p}
so that

\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{u}}+\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right)\overline{\mathbf{u}}+\mu\int_{\Omega(t)}|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}+\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left((\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}}+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla)\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\right)\overline{\mathbf{u}}\\
+\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{B}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{u}}=0.

From Lemma
2.10
one finds that

\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\frac{\rho}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}|\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}-\frac{\rho}{2}\int_{\partial B(t)}|\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}(\mathbf{V}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})=\frac{\rho}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}|\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2},

where we have used the fact that
\overline{\mathbf{u}}
vanishes at the boundary.
Similarly using the divergence theorem one finds, in a standard calculation for the Navier–Stokes equations,

\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right)\overline{\mathbf{u}}=0,

where we have used the homogeneous boundary condition on
\partial B(t)
.
Hence one finds that

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}|\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}+\mu\int_{\Omega(t)}|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}=-\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left((\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}})+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}})\right)\overline{\mathbf{u}}-\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{B}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{u}}.

For this final term we use Young’s inequality for

\left|\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{B}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\kappa}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2},

where
\kappa>0
is an arbitrary constant that we will fix later.
We then calculate that, as
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},\overline{\mathbf{u}}
are divergence-free

\int_{\Omega(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}})\overline{\mathbf{u}}=-\int_{\Omega(t)}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}})\overline{\mathbf{u}}

and so

\frac{\rho}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}|\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}+\mu\int_{\Omega(t)}|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\kappa}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2},

whence the Grönwall inequality now yields (
3.9
).

To show (
3.10
) the argument is more involved.
Our presentation is similar to that of
[
70
]
.
One tests (
3.5
) with
A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}:=-P_{\sigma}\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\cdot A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}+\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right)A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}+\mu\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}:\nabla A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{B}\cdot A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle-\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left((\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}})+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}})\right)A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}.

The difficulty now is that since the domain is evolving one finds that, in contrast to the stationary domain setting,
\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\neq P_{\sigma}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}
, and likewise the spatial and temporal derivatives do not commute — this is in contrast to a stationary domain (see for instance the proof of
[
67
, Theorem 6.8]
).
However, we notice that from the Helmholtz decomposition

\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}-P_{\sigma}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\right)\cdot\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\psi\cdot\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}},

for some
\psi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega(t))
.
Hence we find that, for sufficiently smooth
\bar{\mathbf{u}}
,

\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\psi\cdot\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\psi\nabla\cdot\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\psi\Delta\nabla\cdot\overline{\mathbf{u}}=0,

and this can be rigorously justified by an approximation argument.
We also refer the reader to
[
70
, p.166]
for a related calculation.
One thus obtains

\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\cdot A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}=-\int_{\Omega(t)}P_{\sigma}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\cdot\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}}=-\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}\cdot\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}:\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}.

Now by using Lemma
2.10
we see that

\displaystyle\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\frac{\partial\overline{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial t}:\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\frac{\rho}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}-\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla(\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}):\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}-\frac{\rho}{2}\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{V})\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}:\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}.

Likewise from integration by parts, and the definition of the Leray projection we find

\mu\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}:\nabla A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}=-\mu\int_{\Omega(t)}\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\mu\int_{\Omega(t)}|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}.

Hence we find that

(3.11)

\frac{\rho}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}+\mu\int_{\Omega(t)}|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}=\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla(\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}):\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}+\frac{\rho}{2}\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{V})\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}:\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\\
-\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right)A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}-\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{B}\cdot A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}-\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left((\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}})+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}})\right)A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}},

where it remains to estimate the terms on the right-hand side.
Firstly one finds that

\left|\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla(\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}):\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,\infty}(\Omega(t))}+\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))}\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))},

and so by recalling (
2.5
) and using Young’s inequality one finds

\left|\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla(\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}):\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq C\left(\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,\infty}(\Omega(t))}+\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{10}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}.

We similarly have that

\left|\frac{\rho}{2}\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{V})\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}:\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq C\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,\infty}(\Omega(t))}.

Next we use Hölder’s inequality to see that

\left|\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right)A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq\rho\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))},

and then by Agmon’s inequality (
2.3
), Poincaré’s inequality, (
2.5
), and Young’s inequality one has

\displaystyle\left|\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right)A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{3}{2}}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{10}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+C\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{6}.

We estimate the “force term” using Young’s inequality again, but here the treatment is more delicate than that of the previous estimate.
By integrating by parts one finds that

\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{B}\cdot A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}=-\int_{\Omega(t)}P_{\sigma}\mathbf{B}\cdot\Delta\overline{\mathbf{u}}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla P_{\sigma}\mathbf{B}:\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}-\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}((P_{\sigma}\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu},

where this boundary integral is non-zero.
To deal with this boundary integral we use the trace theorem (see
[
2
, Chapter 7]
) to see that

\left|\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}((P_{\sigma}\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{u}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\leq C\|P_{\sigma}\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega(t))},

where
\mathbf{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega(t))
is a Sobolev–Slobodeckij space.
Now by using standard interpolation results (again we refer to
[
2
, Chapter 7]
) we see that

\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega(t))}\leq C\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{1}{2}},

where the constant can be shown the be independent of
t
.
Combining this with (
2.2
) one finds

\left|\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{B}\cdot A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq C\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}+C\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Now by using (
2.5
) and Young’s inequality suitably we obtain

\left|\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{B}\cdot A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\frac{C}{\kappa^{2}}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{10}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2},

where we have used the fact that
\kappa\in(0,1)
.
For the final term we notice that

(3.12)

\left|\int_{\Omega(t)}\left((\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}})+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}})\right)A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right|\\
\leq\left(\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}+\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t)))}\right)\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}.

We now use the
\mathbf{H}^{2}
analogue of (
2.4
), i.e.

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}\leq C\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{3}{4}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{1}{4}},

as well as (
2.5
), and Young’s inequality for

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{\frac{7}{4}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}^{8}\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{10}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}.

Similarly, by using the Sobolev embedding
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))
and Young’s inequality one finds

\displaystyle\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{10}\|A_{\sigma}\overline{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}.

Hence we find that

3.3.
Construction of the velocity field

In this subsection we now give a concrete construction of the velocity field,
\mathbf{V}
, to be used in our iterative argument along with the estimates established in §
3.3
.
We assume that we are given rigid body quantities,
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])
, which are compatible with the initial data (
1.4
) in the following sense.

Definition 3.5
.

We say that functions
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])
are compatible with the initial data if:

(1)

\mathbf{q}(0)=\mathbf{q}_{0},\quad\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{v}_{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}(0)=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}.

(2)

The centre of mass
\mathbf{q}
and angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\omega}
define the motion of a rigid body,
B(t)
, by evolving the points in
\mathbf{x}(t)\in B(t)
via

(3.15)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t)),\quad\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}\quad\forall\mathbf{x}_{0}\in B(0).

Moreover, this rigid body is such that, for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\mathrm{dist}(B(t),\Gamma)>\frac{\delta}{2},

where
\delta>0
is some constant such that
\mathrm{dist}(B(0),\Gamma)>\delta
.

We note that
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
are sufficiently smooth so that the point evaluations in this first condition are well defined.
This second condition is required so that there is no collision of the rigid body with the fixed portion of boundary
\Gamma
.
An example of functions
\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega}
which are compatible with the initial data in the above sense are the functions

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(t):=\mathbf{q}_{0}+t\mathbf{v}_{0}\qquad\boldsymbol{\omega}(t):=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0},

on some sufficiently small time interval which is determined solely by the initial data.

Given admissible functions in the sense of Definition
3.5
this determines
B(t)
by evolving points via (
3.15
).
This in turn defines the evolving domain,
\Omega(t):=(\Omega\setminus B(t))^{\circ}
, in which we solve the fluid equations using the evolving function space framework discussed in §
2.1
.
To use these evolving function spaces we require a parametrisation of the domain
\Omega(t)
, which we now construct via an appropriately defined velocity field.

For a given point,
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\partial B(0)
we know that this point evolves by (
3.15
), and similarly for a point
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\Gamma
we find that it remains fixed under the evolution of the domain.
We want to extend this flow to all points in
\overline{\Omega(0)}
to define a parametrisation of
\overline{\Omega(t)}
.
One such way to do this to do this is to consider the harmonic extension of the boundary data on
\Omega(t)
.
That is to say, for each
t\in[0,T]
, we define
\mathbf{V}(t):\Omega(t)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
to be the unique solution of

(3.16)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta\mathbf{V}(t)=0,&\text{on }\Omega(t),\\
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t)),&\text{on }\partial B(t),\\
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x},t)=0,&\text{on }\Gamma,\end{cases}

where
-\Delta
is understood componentwise.
By standard results for the Laplace equation (see for instance
[
45
]
) one finds that

(3.17)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))}\leq C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))},

where the constant
C
depends only on the geometry of
\Gamma
and
\partial B(0)
.
This choice of extension is essentially arbitrary, provided one has a bound like (
3.17
).
Perhaps a more suitable approach would be to consider an extension given by solving the Stokes problem (see Appendix
B
), but we do not consider other choices of velocity field here.
From the identification of
\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))=\mathbf{W}^{3,\infty}(\Omega(t))
we note that the third order spatial derivatives of
\mathbf{V}
exist almost everywhere and are essentially bounded.

Continuity in the time argument is harder to establish, as it is not clear what continuity means in this case since the functions
\mathbf{V}(t),\mathbf{V}(s)
are defined on different domains for
t\neq s
.
To understand continuity here, we consider an arbitrary family of diffeomorphisms
(\Phi(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
such that
\Phi(t):\Omega(0)\rightarrow\Omega(t)
is a
C^{3}
diffeomorphism, and
\Phi(0)=\mathrm{id}
.
We then understand continuity of
t\mapsto\mathbf{V}(t)
to mean that the pullback
t\mapsto\Phi_{-t}\mathbf{V}(t)
is continuous in the usual sense for all such families
(\Phi(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
.
With this in mind, one can then establish continuity of
t\mapsto\Phi_{-t}\mathbf{V}(t)
by considering the weak formulation of (
3.16
) pulled back onto
\Omega(0)
.
We omit the details of this calculation as it is tedious and yields no valuable insight — we refer to
[
34
, Appendix A]
for details on a similar calculation.
In particular, from this one may deduce the measurability of
t\mapsto\mathbf{V}(t)
, and thus one finds that
\mathbf{V}
is a Carathéodory function.

3.3.1.
The pushforward map

Since
\mathbf{V}
is a Carathéodory function we may use the Carathéodory existence theorem (see for instance
[
49
]
) for ordinary differential equations to see that

(3.18)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbf{p},t)&=\mathbf{V}(\Phi(\mathbf{p},t),t),\\
\Phi(\mathbf{p},0)&=\mathbf{p},\end{aligned}

admits a solution on
[0,T]
, for all
\mathbf{p}\in\overline{\Omega(0)}
.
Moreover the Lipschitz continuity of
\mathbf{V}(\cdot,t)
implies the solution to this ODE is in fact unique.
By construction one finds that
\Phi(\overline{\Omega(0)},t)=\overline{\Omega(t)}
, and moreover one finds that
\Phi
is such that
\Phi(t):\Omega(0)\rightarrow\Omega(t)
is invertible for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Owing to the regularity of
\mathbf{V}
in space, one can show further spatial regularity of
\Phi
(see for instance
[
8
, §32]
).
By differentiating the above ODE in space one finds that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}\nabla\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbf{p},t)&=\nabla\mathbf{V}(\Phi(\mathbf{p},t),t)\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},t),\\
\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},0)&=\mathbb{I},\end{aligned}

and a straightforward application of Grönwall’s inequality yields

\|\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},t)\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}\leq\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\nabla\mathbf{V}(\Phi(\mathbf{p},s),s)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\right),

and using (
3.17
) one finds then that

(3.19)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},t)\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}\leq\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(s))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

Similarly, noting that the Jacobian determinant satisfies the ODE

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\det(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},t))=\det(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},t))(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V})(\Phi(\mathbf{p},t),t),\quad\det(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},0))=1,

one can show that

(3.20)

\displaystyle|\det(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},t))|\geq\exp\left(-C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(s))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)>0,

and since
\det(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},0))=1
it follows that
\det(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p},t))>0
, and
\nabla\Phi
is invertible.

3.3.2.
Compatibility of function spaces

To use the evolving function space framework discussed in §
2.1
we must first show compatibility of the evolving function space pairs, as in Definition
2.1
.
For this, we begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Phi:\Omega(0)\rightarrow\Omega(t)
and
f:\Omega(0)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be sufficiently smooth, we define
\widehat{f}:=f\circ\Phi:\Omega(t)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Then the gradient and Hessian of
f
transform as

(3.21)

\displaystyle(\nabla f)\circ\Phi=(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{f},

(3.22)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}f)\circ\Phi=(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-T}\nabla\big((\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{f}\big),

and the Laplacian transforms as

(3.23)

\displaystyle(\Delta f)\circ\Phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{g}G^{-1}\nabla\widehat{f}),

where
G=(\nabla\Phi)^{T}\nabla\Phi,g=\det(G)=\det(\nabla\Phi)^{2}
.
Similarly if
f:\Omega(0)\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is sufficiently smooth then one has

(3.24)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right)\circ\Phi=\frac{\partial\widehat{f}}{\partial t}-(\nabla\Phi)^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\nabla\widehat{f}=\frac{\partial\widehat{f}}{\partial t}-(\nabla\Phi)^{-1}\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\widehat{f}.

Proof.

(
3.21
) and (
3.23
) follow almost identically to the proofs of
[
22
, Lemma 3.3]
and
[
22
, Lemma 3.4]
respectively.
The proof of (
3.22
) follows a straightforward calculation using (
3.21
) with
f
replaced by
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}
for
i=1,2,3
.

The proof for (
3.24
) follows by using the chain rule, (
3.21
), and the definition of
\Phi
so that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f(\Phi(x,t),t)=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right)\circ\Phi+\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot(\nabla{f})\circ\Phi=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right)\circ\Phi+\mathbf{V}\cdot(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{f}.

∎

This result extends to vector-valued functions in the obvious way.
With this preliminary lemma one can show the following compatibility result.

Lemma 3.7
.

For
\Phi,f,\widehat{f}
as in the previous lemma, one has

\|f\|_{\mathbf{W}^{k,p}(\Omega(0))}\leq C(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\|\widehat{f}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{k,p}(\Omega(t))},

for
k=0,1,2
and
p\in[1,\infty]
.
Here
C(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
is a constant depending only on
\mathbf{q}
,
\mathbf{\omega}
, and
\Omega(0)
.

Proof.

We show the result for
k=2
, and
p\neq\infty
.
By a straightforward change of variables and using (
3.21
), (
3.22
) one has

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathbf{W}^{2,p}(\Omega(0))}^{p}=\int_{\Omega(t)}J\left(|\widehat{f}|^{p}+|(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{f}|^{p}+|(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\big((\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{f}\big)|^{p}\right),

for
J=\det(\nabla\Phi)
.
From (
3.19
) it is clear that

\|J\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}\leq\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\right),

and from (
3.20
) it is a straightforward calculation to show that

\|(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}\leq\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

Lastly one uses the fact that
\nabla(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}=(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla(\nabla\Phi)^{T}(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}
to see that

\displaystyle|(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\big((\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\nabla\widehat{f}\big)|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}^{3p}\|\nabla(\nabla\Phi)^{T}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}^{p}|\nabla\widehat{f}|^{p}

\displaystyle+\|(\nabla\Phi)^{-T}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}^{2p}|\nabla^{2}\widehat{f}|^{p},

from which the result readily follows.
The case of
p=\infty
uses similar arguments and is omitted.
∎

From the regularity of
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
one finds that
C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))}
can be bounded independent of
t
, and so this translates into a uniform in time bound for
C(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
.
One can similarly show an inequality of the form

\|\widehat{f}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{k,p}(\Omega(t))}\leq\widetilde{C}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\|{f}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{k,p}(\Omega(0))},

for some constant
\widetilde{C}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
independent of
t
but possibly depending on
\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
From this it is straightforward to show compatibility (as in Definition
2.1
) of the pairs
(\mathbf{W}^{k,p}(\Omega(t)),\Phi(t))
for
k=0,1,2
and
p\in[1,\infty]
— we omit further details.
With this preliminary set up, we may now use the evolving function spaces detailed in
[
5
,

]
.

4.
The iterative argument

We now expound upon our iterative process.
Given functions
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])
which are compatible with the initial data in the sense of Definition
3.5
one can define an evolving domain
\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Omega(t)\times\{t\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]
, which is parametrised by the flow map
\Phi(\cdot,t)
constructed in §
3.3
.
With this we use the evolving function space framework discussed in §
2.1
to solve the evolving Navier–Stokes equations on
\Omega(t)
for
strong solutions

\mathbf{u}\in H^{1}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}(\Phi)\cap L^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{2}}(\Phi),\qquad p\in L^{2}_{H^{1}}(\Phi),

as in Section
3
.
With these strong solutions we define functions
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{W})\in\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,\widetilde{T}])\times\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,\widetilde{T}])
, for
\widetilde{T}
as in Lemma
3.1
, to be the unique solutions to

(4.1a)

\displaystyle m\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}=-\int_{\partial B(t)}\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu},

(4.1b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\mathbb{J}\mathbf{W}\right)=-\int_{\partial B(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\times\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu},

such that

\mathbf{Q}(0)=\mathbf{q}_{0},\quad\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}(0)=\mathbf{v}_{0},\quad\mathbf{W}(0)=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0},

and

\mathbb{J}:=\int_{B(t)}\rho_{B}\left(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t)|^{2}\mathbb{I}-(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\otimes(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right).

We recall that

Note that in contrast to (

We summarise this construction as a map

\mathscr{F}:(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\mapsto(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{W}).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])
be compatible with the initial data in the sense of Definition

for some given

, determined by the initial data, which is sufficiently large.
Then there exists some sufficiently small
T
, independent of
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, such that
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{W}):=\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
exists on
[0,T]
.

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
3.1
that
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{W}
are defined on
[0,\widetilde{T}]
, where there are two situations which may cause this time interval to be a strict subset of
[0,T]
: a collision of the rigid body with
\Gamma
, and the interval on which strong solutions to (
3.1
) shrinking due to the condition (
3.13
).
We consider this second case first, as it will help our treatment of the first case.
To this end, for our given functions
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
, we show that
\widetilde{T}
as in Lemma
3.1
is independent of
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
Recall that
\widetilde{T}
was chosen so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}+C\left(\kappa\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}}{4+\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}+C\left(\kappa\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}C\left(\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\|\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}+\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}^{8}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)<0.

This is equivalent to

(4.2)

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{T})+C(\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{T})-1)\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{T})<4+\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4},

where we have defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{T}):=\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\|\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}+\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}^{8}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{T}):=\left(\kappa\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}.

We recall the body force,
\mathbf{B}
, as defined in (
3.7
), is determined by
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
From here on we fix
\kappa=\widetilde{T}^{\frac{1}{3}}
.
From (
3.17
) and (
B.2
) we observe that one has

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))},

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))},

respectively.
Using these we find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{T})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(C\widetilde{T}^{\frac{1}{3}}+C\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))}^{8}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(C\widetilde{T}^{\frac{1}{3}}+C\widetilde{T}R^{8}\right)=:F_{1}(\widetilde{T},R),

where we have used the Sobolev embeddings
4
4

4

An important observation here is that in one dimension these Sobolev embeddings hold with a constant independent of
T
.

\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{1,\frac{1}{2}}([0,T])
and
\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{0,\frac{1}{2}}([0,T])
, and the fact that
\kappa=\widetilde{T}^{\frac{1}{3}}

We need to obtain a similar bound for

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{T})=\widetilde{T}^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\widetilde{T}^{\frac{2}{3}}\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))}^{8}\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle+C\widetilde{T}^{\frac{2}{3}}\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\mathrm{d}t}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})-\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{4}\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

Using the boundedness of
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
we find that

\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{T})\leq C\widetilde{T}^{\frac{8}{3}}R^{8}\exp(C\widetilde{T}R)+C\widetilde{T}^{\frac{2}{3}}R^{4}\exp(C\widetilde{T}R)=:F_{2}(\widetilde{T},R).

Hence one finds that

\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{T})+C(\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{T})-1)\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{T})\\
\begin{aligned} \leq\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}F_{1}(\widetilde{T},R)+C\left(F_{1}(\widetilde{T},R)-1\right)F_{2}(\widetilde{T},R),\end{aligned}

where one observes that for a fixed
R
,

\lim_{\widetilde{T}\rightarrow 0^{+}}F_{1}(\widetilde{T},R)=1,\qquad\lim_{\widetilde{T}\rightarrow 0^{+}}F_{2}(\widetilde{T},R)=0.

Thus one can now take
\widetilde{T}
sufficiently small in terms of
R
and the initial data so that (
4.2
) holds independent of
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
Hence one has
L^{2}
maximal regularity of (
3.1
) on a time interval independent of
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.

We now outline how we avoid collisions with the boundary.
From (
4.1a
) we see that

\mathbf{Q}(t)=\mathbf{q}_{0}+t\mathbf{v}_{0}-\frac{1}{m}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\partial B(r)}\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
m
is the mass of the rigid body.
As such we find that

|\mathbf{Q}(t)-\mathbf{q}_{0}|\leq Ct\left(1+\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\|p\|_{H^{1}(\Omega(t))}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))}\right)\leq Ct,

where we have used (
3.10
), (
3.14
), and the previously established bounds on
\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{T})
and
\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{T})
for the last inequality.
Recall that when we pose the problem (see the discussion in Section
1
) we suppose the existence of some
\delta>0
such that

\mathrm{dist}(B(0),\Gamma)>\delta>0,

and hence the bound on
|\mathbf{Q}(t)-\mathbf{q}_{0}|
implies that there exists
\widetilde{T}
depending only on
R
and the initial data such that

\mathrm{dist}(\widetilde{B}(t),\Gamma)>\frac{\delta}{2}>0,

where
\widetilde{B}
is the rigid body governed by
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{W}
, for all
t\in[0,\widetilde{T}]
.
Note that this argument is only an outline of the proof, as one also requires a similar argument applied to the angular velocity,
\mathbf{W}
, but we omit further details as it is more or less the same calculation.
∎

The preceding lemma lets us now unambiguously consider
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
and
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{W})
on the same time interval.
We now show that this the new centre of mass,
\mathbf{Q}
, and angular momentum,
\mathbf{W}
, inherit the same bounds as
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])
be compatible with the initial data in the sense of Definition
3.5
, and such that

\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\leq R,

for some given
R>0
, determined by the initial data, which is sufficiently large.
If the relative density
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
is sufficiently small then
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{W}
are such that

\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\leq R.

Here the smallness of
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
is determined by
\Omega(0)
and
\mathrm{diam}(B(0))
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.3
we find that if
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
, defined on a sufficiently small time interval
[0,T]
, is compatible with the initial data in the sense of Definition
3.5
then
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{W}):=\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
is defined on
[0,T]
and is compatible with the initial data.
We wish to show that
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{W})
inherits the same bound as
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
.
To this end we observe that

\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\partial B(t)}\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{C_{B}}{m^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\|p\|_{H^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2},

where
C_{B}
denotes a constant, depending on
\Omega(0)
, appearing due to use of the trace theorem.
By using (
3.10
), (
3.14
), and the above bounds for
\mathcal{E}(T)
and
\mathcal{B}(T)
one finds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{B}}{m^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+C\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle+C\left(1+\int_{0}^{T}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)\mathcal{D}(T)^{2}.

The aim is to show this right-hand side is appropriately bounded so that we may conclude that
\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}\leq\frac{R}{2}
.
One would hope that we can fix
R
to be sufficiently large (in terms of the initial data) and
T
to be sufficiently small (in terms of
R
and the initial data) so that this is true, however the first term,
\frac{C_{B}}{m^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}
, makes this problematic.
This is because the estimates in Appendix
B
yield

\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2}R^{2}),

where we cannot make the constant vanish as
R\rightarrow\infty
or
T\rightarrow 0
.
Instead, we notice that the definition of
\mathbf{B}
contains a factor of
\rho
, and we obtain a factor of
\frac{1}{\rho_{B}}
from
\frac{1}{m}
so that we may control this term by only considering a relative density,
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
, which is sufficiently small.
The desired smallness of
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
depends only on
\Omega(0)
and
\mathrm{diam}(B(0))
.
In this case we may choose
T
to be sufficiently small so that

\frac{C_{B}}{m^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\leq\widetilde{C_{B}}\frac{\rho^{2}R^{2}}{\rho_{B}^{2}}<\frac{R^{2}}{8},

where
\widetilde{C_{B}}
is a constant depending only on the geometry of
\Omega(0)
.
One can then take
R
sufficiently large, and
T
sufficiently small, so that the other terms are also bounded by
\frac{R^{2}}{8}
as well — we omit further details on these calculations as they are more or less the same as those used above.
This outlines how one controls the highest order term in
\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}
, and the control on the lower order terms follows similarly, so that one concludes that

\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}\leq\frac{R}{2},

for some fixed sufficiently large
R>0
, and small
T
depending on
R
and the initial data.

We now consider similar estimates for
\mathbf{W}
, which we recall is such that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\mathbb{J}\mathbf{W}\right)=-\int_{\partial B(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\times\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}.

Clearly one has that

\mathbb{J}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{J}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{W}-\int_{\partial B(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\times\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu},

where one can use the Reynolds transport theorem to calculate that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{J}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{B(t)}\rho_{B}\left(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}|^{2}\mathbb{I}-(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})\otimes(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{B(t)}\rho_{B}\left(2\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{x}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)\mathbb{I}-\left(\mathbf{x}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)\otimes(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})-\left(\mathbf{x}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)\otimes\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial B(t)}\rho_{B}\left(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}|^{2}\mathbb{I}-(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})\otimes(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})\right)\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu},

where this final term uses the fact that
\mathbf{V}|_{\partial B(t)}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}).

From this calculation we see that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{J}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq CR,

where we have used that

\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{\mathbf{C}^{0}([0,T])}\leq C(\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])})\leq CR,

for a constant independent of
T
and
R
.

Now we also recall from classical mechanics that the inertial tensor,
\mathbb{J}
, is positive definite and hence invertible.
Thus we may write

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathbb{J}^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{J}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{W}-\mathbb{J}^{-1}\int_{\partial B(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\times\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu},

and since integrating the ODE for
\mathbf{W}
yields

\mathbb{J}(t)\mathbf{W}(t)=\mathbb{J}(0)\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial B(s)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(s))\times\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\mathrm{d}s

we find that

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathbb{J}^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{J}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{J}^{-1}\left(\mathbb{J}(0)\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial B(s)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(s))\times\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)-\mathbb{J}^{-1}\int_{\partial B(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\times\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}.

It is now clear we need to see that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\mathbb{J}^{-1}|
is bounded appropriately.
For this we observe that the rigid body motion of
B(t)
implies that each
\mathbf{x}(t)\in B(t)
can be written as

\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{q}(t)+\mathbb{O}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0}),

for some
\mathbf{y}\in B(0)
, and
\mathbb{O}(t)
is the orthogonal matrix such that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{O}(t)\mathbf{z}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times\mathbb{O}(t)\mathbf{z}_{0},

for all
\mathbf{z}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\mathbb{O}(0)=\mathbb{I}
.
A change of coordinates now yields that

\mathbb{J}(t)=\int_{B(0)}\rho_{B}\left(|\mathbb{O}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})|^{2}\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{O}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})\otimes\mathbb{O}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})\right),

from which one finds that

\mathbb{J}(t)=\mathbb{O}(t)\mathbb{J}(0)\mathbb{O}(t)^{T}.

Thus one finds that for any eigenvector,
\mathbf{z}
, of
\mathbb{J}(0)
we have that
\mathbb{O}(t)\mathbf{z}
is an eigenvector
\mathbb{J}(t)
with the same eigenvalue.
Hence the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbb{J}(t)^{-1}
is bounded independent of
t
and
R
, and hence norm equivalence readily translates this into a bound for
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\mathbb{J}^{-1}(t)|
.

As we saw for the centre of mass,
\mathbf{Q}
, we cannot immediately show the bound for
\mathbf{W}
by taking
R
large and
T
small.
In this case the problematic term is now
\mathbb{J}^{-1}\int_{\partial B(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\times\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}
, which we control by only allowing sufficiently small relative densities — as we did in showing the bound for
\mathbf{Q}
.
In this case, we observe that the entries of
\mathbb{J}
depend linearly on
\rho_{B}
so that the entries of
\mathbb{J}^{-1}
depend linearly on
\frac{1}{\rho_{B}}
.
Hence we only allow a relative density,
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
, which is sufficiently small so that one finds

\int_{0}^{T}\left|\mathbb{J}^{-1}\int_{\partial B(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\times\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|^{2}\leq C_{B}^{2}\frac{\rho^{2}R^{2}}{\rho_{B}^{2}}\leq\frac{R^{2}}{8},

as we saw above, where again
C_{B}
is a constant arising from the use of the trace theorem.
We omit further details on this calculation.

Thus by arguing along the same lines as we did for the bound on
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}
we find that
\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{R^{2}}{4},

and the bound for the lower order term follows, from which we conclude that by taking
T
sufficiently small one has

\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\leq\frac{R}{2},

for some sufficiently small
R>0
.
Thus we have shown that

\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\leq R\Rightarrow\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\leq R,

provided that
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
is sufficiently small.
∎

These lemmas show that
\mathscr{F}
maps some small ball in
\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])
onto itself, and hence we may consider the use of fixed point arguments.

4.1.
Some preliminary calculations

Owing to Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
one finds that if the relative density,
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
, is sufficiently small we may uncontroversially consider
\mathscr{F}
as a map from some suitably defined subset of
\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])
onto itself.
As such we define the domain of
\mathscr{F}
to be given by

\displaystyle D(\mathscr{F}):=\left\{(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in B_{R}(\mathbf{H}^{2}\times\mathbf{H}^{1})\mid(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\text{ are compatible in the sense of Definition}~\ref{defn: compatible with IC}\right\},

\displaystyle B_{R}(\mathbf{H}^{2}\times\mathbf{H}^{1}):=\left\{(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])\times\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])\mid\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\leq R\right\}.

The plan for proving Theorem
1.2
is to show that
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction when
T
is sufficiently small.
It is straightforward to verify that the domain
D(\mathscr{F})
is a complete metric space, and so the use of the Banach fixed point theorem is indeed justified.
In order to show that
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction we firstly require some preliminary results.
From here on we shall consider two pairs,
(\mathbf{q}^{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i})\in D(\mathscr{F})
, such that
\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{q}^{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i})=:(\mathbf{Q}^{i},\mathbf{W}^{i})
for
i=1,2
.
We denote the associated velocity field as
\mathbf{V}^{i}
, the associated parametrisation as
\Phi^{i}
, and the associated strong solution of the Navier–Stokes equations on
\Omega^{i}(t)
as
(\mathbf{u}^{i},p^{i})\in H^{1}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}(\Phi^{i})\cap{L}^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{2}}(\Phi^{i})\times L^{2}_{{H}^{1}}(\Phi^{i})
.
It is important to note that
\Omega^{1}(t)\neq\Omega^{2}(t)
for
t\neq 0
— that is to say
(\mathbf{u}^{1},p^{1})
and
(\mathbf{u}^{2},p^{2})
are defined on different evolving domains.
This will be the main difficulty for our subsequent analysis.

We begin with the following lemma relating to control of
\nabla\Phi^{i}
to the difference of the pairs
(\mathbf{q}^{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i})
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For
(\mathbf{q}^{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i})\in D(\mathscr{F})
, for
i=1,2
, one has that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\Phi^{1}(t)-\nabla\Phi^{2}(t)\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}\times(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})-(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{q}^{2}\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))},

for a constant
C
independent of
\mathbf{q}^{1}
,
\mathbf{q}^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}
.

Proof.

To begin, we observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\nabla\Phi^{1}(\mathbf{x},t)-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},t))=\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1}(\mathbf{x},t),t)\nabla\Phi^{1}(\mathbf{x},t)-\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},t),t)\nabla\Phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},t),

with initial condition
\nabla\Phi^{1}(\mathbf{x},0)-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},0)=0
.
One rewrites this ODE as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\nabla\Phi^{1}(\mathbf{x},t)-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},t))

\displaystyle=(\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1}(\mathbf{x},t),t)-\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},t),t))\nabla\Phi^{1}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle+\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},t),t)(\nabla\Phi^{1}(\mathbf{x},t)-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},t)),

from which one can readily observe that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Phi^{1}(t)-\nabla\Phi^{2}(t)\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2}(s),s)\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}\|\nabla\Phi^{1}(s)-\nabla\Phi^{2}(s)\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\|(\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1}(s),s)-\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2}(s),s))\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}\|\nabla\Phi^{1}(s)\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}\,\mathrm{d}s.

It is now clear that using Grönwall’s inequality yields the result, provided that one has an appropriate bound on
\|(\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1}(s),s)-\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2}(s),s))\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}
.
To this end we recall (
3.16
) defining
\mathbf{V}^{i}
.
By pulling back to
\Omega(0)
and using Lemma
3.7
we find that the functions
\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{i}}(t):=\mathbf{V}^{i}(t)\circ\Phi^{i}(t)
solve

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\nabla\cdot\left(\det(\nabla\Phi^{i}(t))\nabla\Phi^{i}(t)(\nabla\Phi^{i}(t))^{T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{i}}(t)\right)=0,&\text{ on }\ \Omega(0),\\
\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}^{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}^{i}),&\text{ on }\partial B(0),\\
\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma,\end{cases}

respectively in a weak sense, for
i=1,2
.
In order to study the difference of these functions we now introduce an intermediate function
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{1,2}}
which is the unique solution to the PDE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\nabla\cdot\left(\det(\nabla\Phi^{2}(t))\nabla\Phi^{2}(t)(\nabla\Phi^{2}(t))^{T}\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{1,2}}(t)\right)=0,&\text{ on }\ \Omega(0),\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{1,2}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}^{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}^{1}),&\text{ on }\partial B(0),\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{1,2}}(\mathbf{x},t)=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma.\end{cases}

Now one has

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{1}}(t)-\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{2}}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))}\leq\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{1,2}}(t)-\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{2}}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))}+\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{1}}(t)-\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{1,2}}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))},

and it is straightforward to verify, using standard elliptic regularity results
[
45
]
, that

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{1,2}}(t)-\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{2}}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}\times(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})-(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{q}^{2}\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))},

and

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{1}}(t)-\widetilde{\mathbf{V}^{1,2}}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\det(\nabla\Phi^{2}(t))\nabla\Phi^{2}(t)(\nabla\Phi^{2}(t))^{T}-\det(\nabla\Phi^{1}(t))\nabla\Phi^{1}(t)(\nabla\Phi^{1}(t))^{T}\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\nabla\Phi^{2}(t)-\nabla\Phi^{1}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))},

where the final inequality is a consequence of the fact that
(\mathbf{q}^{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i})\in B_{R}(\mathbf{H}^{2}\times\mathbf{H}^{1})
for
i=1,2
.
Now by noting that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1}(s),s)-\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2}(s),s)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))}\leq C\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{1}}(s)-\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{2}}(s)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}\times(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})-(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{q}^{2}\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle+C\left\|\nabla\Phi^{2}(t)-\nabla\Phi^{1}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{1,1}(\Omega(0))},

it is clear that the result follows by using Grönwall’s inequality.
∎

One may notice that our use of the Grönwall inequality means that the constant appearing in the previous lemma depends on
T
.
However, since we will be interested in taking
T
to be sufficiently small we may ignore this issue and assume without loss of generality that the constant is independent of
T
.
Next we show a lemma regarding the control on the time derivative of
\Phi^{1}-\Phi^{2}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For
(\mathbf{q}^{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i})\in D(\mathscr{F})
for
i=1,2
one has that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\Phi^{1}-\Phi^{2})\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}\times(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})-(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{q}^{2}\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}\times(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})-(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{q}^{2}\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(0))},

for a constant
C
independent of
\mathbf{q}^{1}
,
\mathbf{q}^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}
.

Proof.

By definition of
\Phi^{i}
one immediately finds that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\Phi^{1}-\Phi^{2})=\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1}(t),t)-\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2}(t),t),

and the result thus follows as a consequence of the calculations in the proof of Lemma
4.5
.
∎

4.1.1.
Pullbacks and the Piola transform

Before using Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
to show that
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction we need a way to preserve the divergence-free property of
\mathbf{u}^{i}
under some coordinate transformation.
This will help us to control the pressure terms which arise in the calculations to show that
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction.
To do this we use the Piola transform, which is often used in finite element approximations of equations of continuum mechanics (see
[
10
,
35
,
68
]
), and more recently in the analysis of fluid equations on evolving domains (see
[
29
,
34
,
63
]
).
We begin by defining maps
\Phi^{2,1}(t):\Omega^{2}(t)\rightarrow\Omega^{1}(t)
given by

\Phi^{2,1}(t):=\Phi^{1}(t)\circ\Phi^{2}(t)^{-1}.

We shall also denote the inverse of
\Phi^{2,1}(t)
as

\Phi^{1,2}(t)=\Phi^{2}(t)\circ\Phi^{1}(t)^{-1}:\Omega^{1}(t)\rightarrow\Omega^{2}(t).

We consider the associated Piola transform to be defined as

\mathcal{P}_{t}^{1,2}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{x})={\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2}(t)(\mathbf{x}))}(\nabla\Phi^{2,1}(t)\boldsymbol{\psi})\circ\Phi^{1,2}(t)

for a function
\boldsymbol{\psi}:\Omega^{2}(t)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
A useful relation that we state for later use is that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}\circ\Phi^{1,2}(t)=\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\mathcal{P}_{t}^{1,2}\boldsymbol{\psi}.

The Piola transform defined above maps functions in
\mathbf{W}^{k,p}(\Omega^{2}(t))
onto functions in
\mathbf{W}^{k,p}(\Omega^{1}(t))
, for a result in this direction we refer the reader to
[
29
, Appendix A]
.
One can define an inverse Piola transform in the same manner, but we do not consider this here.
For further details on the Piola transform, as well as its applications in mathematical elasticity, we refer the reader to
[
23
]
.
We now recall the following property of the Piola transform which will be useful in our fixed point argument.

Lemma 4.7

(
[
29
, Lemma 2.8]
)
.

For
t\in[0,T]
, let
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in C^{1}(\Omega^{2}(t);\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
Then we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathcal{P}^{1,2}_{t}\boldsymbol{\psi}=\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2}(t))(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi})\circ\Phi^{1,2}(t).

In particular the Piola transform preserves divergence-free functions.

Using the established bounds on
\Phi^{i}
one can readily show the compatibility of the evolving function spaces on
\Omega^{2}(t)
with the Piola transform in the sense of
[
5
,
6
]
by adapting the arguments in Lemma
3.7
.
With these preliminaries we now prove
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction.

4.2.
The fixed point argument

As we have done throughout we assume that
(\mathbf{q}^{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i})\in D(\mathscr{F})
, for
i=1,2
, and we write
\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{q}^{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i})=(\mathbf{Q}^{i},\mathbf{W}^{i})
.
Our aim is to show that

\|\mathbf{Q}^{1}-\mathbf{Q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbf{W}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\leq L\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\right),

for a constant
0<L<1
.
To begin we observe that
\mathbf{Q}^{1}-\mathbf{Q}^{2}
solves the ODE

m\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}(\mathbf{Q}^{1}-\mathbf{Q}^{2})=\int_{\partial B^{2}(t)}\mathbb{T}^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{2}-\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\mathbb{T}^{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1},

pointwise for almost all
t\in[0,T]
and such that

(\mathbf{Q}^{1}-\mathbf{Q}^{2})(0)=0,\qquad(\mathbf{Q}^{1}-\mathbf{Q}^{2})^{\prime}(0)=0.

By pulling the first integral back onto
\partial B^{1}(t)
one finds that

(4.6)

\displaystyle m\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}(\mathbf{Q}^{1}-\mathbf{Q}^{2})=\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\left(\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\widehat{\mathbb{T}^{2}}-{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\right)\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1},

where
\widehat{\mathbb{T}^{2}}=\mathbb{T}^{2}\circ\Phi^{1,2}(t)
.
Using Lemma
3.6
and (
4.4
) one finds that

\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\widehat{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}=-\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\widehat{p^{2}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}\\
\begin{aligned} &+\mu\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}+(\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}})^{T}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{T}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}\\
&+\mu\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}\\
&+\mu\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1},\end{aligned}

where

\widehat{p^{2}}:=p^{2}\circ\Phi^{1,2},\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}:=\mathcal{P}_{t}^{1,2}\mathbf{u}^{2}.

We now use this expression in (
4.6
) to see that

\displaystyle m\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}(\mathbf{Q}^{1}-\mathbf{Q}^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}(p^{1}-\widehat{p^{2}})\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}+\mu\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\nabla(\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}-\mathbf{u}^{1})+(\nabla(\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}-\mathbf{u}^{1}))^{T}

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}(1-\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2}))\widehat{p^{2}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}

\displaystyle+\mu\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I}\right)\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}

(4.7)

\displaystyle+\mu\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}(\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}})^{T}\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{T}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}-\mathbb{I}\right)\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}

\displaystyle+\mu\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}

\displaystyle+\mu\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1}.

The plan now is to bound the first two terms in (
4.7
) by establishing bounds analogous to those in Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
, and bounding the other terms by using the uniform bounds on
\widehat{p^{2}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}
in combination with Lemma
4.5
.
The issue now is that we must emulate the proof of Lemma
3.1
to establish appropriate bounds for
p^{1}-\widehat{p^{2}}
and
\mathbf{u}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}
in
L^{2}_{H^{1}}(\Phi^{1})
and
L^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{2}}(\Phi^{1})
respectively.
For this we firstly must find the equations which
\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}},\widehat{p^{2}}
solve, which we do by using Lemma
3.6
and (
4.4
) again.

As before, we write
\mathbf{u}^{i}=\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{i}+\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{i}
and
p^{i}=\overline{p}^{i}+\widetilde{p}^{i}
, where
(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{i},\overline{p}^{i})
solves a system like (
3.5
), (
3.6
) with homogeneous boundary conditions, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{i}
solves a Stokes problem with inhomogeneous boundary conditions like in Appendix
B
.
The difficulty now comes from bounding the
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{i},\overline{p}^{i}
as in Lemma
3.1
, and this shall be the majority of the proof.

Remark 4.8
.

Our use of the Piola transform is due to the fact that recreate the proof of Lemma
3.1
we wish to use (
2.5
) to control the second order terms and this is only valid for divergence-free functions.
We note that this is also where our higher regularity assumption on the boundary of
\partial\Omega(0)
comes into play as
\Phi^{1,2}
must be sufficiently smooth so that

\mathcal{P}_{t}^{1,2}:\mathbf{W}^{2,q}(\Omega^{2}(t))\rightarrow\mathbf{W}^{2,q}(\Omega^{1}(t)),\quad q\in[1,\infty].

If one can remove the use of the Piola transform then we imagine our approach would apply to domains with a
C^{1,1}
boundary.

It is a routine calculation to verify that the functions
(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2})
defined by

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}:=\mathcal{P}_{t}^{1,2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{2},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}:=\overline{p}^{2}\circ\Phi^{1,2}(t),

solve

\displaystyle\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}{\partial t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle-\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}\frac{\partial\Phi^{1,2}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle-\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}\frac{\partial\Phi^{1,2}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}

(4.8)

\displaystyle+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle+\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle+\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{T}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}=0,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}q\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}=0,

for all
q\in L^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t)),\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbf{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))
and almost all
t\in[0,T]
.
Here we are also using notation

\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}\circ\Phi^{1,2}(t),\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}}:=\mathbf{B}^{2}\circ\Phi^{1,2}(t).

Thus we may combine (
3.5
), (
3.6
) for
(\mathbf{u}^{1},p^{1})
with (
4.8
), (
4.9
) to see that

\displaystyle\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\frac{\partial(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})}{\partial t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\boldsymbol{\eta}-\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}

(4.10)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\nabla(\overline{p}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}+\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\nabla(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}):\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left((\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1})+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{1})\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}-\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left((\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})+(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}})\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}(\mathbf{B}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}=\sum_{k=1}^{12}I_{k}[\boldsymbol{\eta}],

(4.11)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}q\nabla\cdot(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})=0,

for all
q\in L^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t)),\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbf{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))
and almost all
t\in[0,T]
.
Here the
I_{k}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]
are some linear functionals to be bounded, given by:

\displaystyle I_{1}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I})\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}{\partial t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta},

\displaystyle I_{2}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta},

\displaystyle I_{3}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=-\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}\frac{\partial\Phi^{1,2}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\eta},

\displaystyle I_{4}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=-\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}\frac{\partial\Phi^{1,2}}{\partial t}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta},

\displaystyle I_{7}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left(\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}-\mathbb{I}\right)\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta},

\displaystyle I_{8}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I}\right)\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta},

\displaystyle I_{9}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta},

\displaystyle I_{10}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{T}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}-\mathbb{I})\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta},

\displaystyle I_{11}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\eta},

\displaystyle I_{12}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}(\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})-1)\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}.

One can think of these terms as “consistency errors” arising from the fact that
(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2})
are not the solution of (

) on

) with

), provided the functions

.

\displaystyle\left|I_{5}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right|

\displaystyle\left|I_{7}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right|

\displaystyle\left|I_{8}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq CT\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\right)\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))},

\displaystyle\left|I_{9}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq CT\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\right)\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))},

\displaystyle\left|I_{10}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq CT\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\right)\|\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))},

\displaystyle\left|I_{11}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq CT\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\right)\|\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))},

\displaystyle\left|I_{12}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq CT\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\right)\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))},

where
C
denotes a constant independent of
\mathbf{q}^{1}
,
\mathbf{q}^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}
and
T
.

Proof.

For
I_{1}[\cdot]
one immediately finds that

\left|I_{1}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right|\leq\rho\left\|\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\left\|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))},

where one can calculate that

\nabla\Phi^{1,2}(t)=\nabla(\Phi^{2}(t)\circ\Phi^{1}(t)^{-1})=\left(\nabla\Phi^{2}(t)(\nabla\Phi^{1}(t))^{-1}\right)\circ\Phi^{1}(t)^{-1}.

Then clearly one has that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega^{1}(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}-\mathbb{I}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\Phi^{2}-\nabla\Phi^{1}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))},

whence the bound follows from using Lemma
4.5
and the uniform bounds on
\|\nabla\Phi^{i}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}
.

For
I_{2}[\cdot]
one immediately finds that

\displaystyle\left|I_{2}[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right|\leq C\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))},

where we have used the uniform bound for
\nabla\Phi^{1,2}
.
Clearly the issue is bounding the
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}
term, and to control this term we firstly find an expression for
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}
.
Firstly we observe that since

\nabla\Phi^{1,2}(t)=\left(\nabla\Phi^{2}(t)(\nabla\Phi^{1}(t))^{-1}\right)\circ\Phi^{1}(t)^{-1},

one has that

\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}=(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\circ\Phi^{1}.

Differentiating this equation in time yields

\nabla\frac{\partial\Phi^{2}}{\partial t}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}+\nabla\Phi^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\Phi^{1}\right)^{-1}=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\circ\Phi^{1}+((\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1,2})\circ\Phi^{1})\frac{\partial\Phi^{1}}{\partial t},

which simplifies to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\circ\Phi^{1}=\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2})\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1})-((\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1,2})\circ\Phi^{1})\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1}),

where we understand
\mathbf{V}^{i}(\Phi^{i})
to mean
\mathbf{V}^{i}(\Phi^{i}(\cdot,t),t)
.
Hence one finds that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega^{1}(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2})\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1})-((\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1,2})\circ\Phi^{1})\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1})\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}.

To bound this term we see that

\displaystyle\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2})\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1})

\displaystyle=\nabla\Phi^{2}\left((\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2})\nabla\Phi^{2}-(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1})\nabla\Phi^{1}\right)(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1},

and hence by using Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
one finds that

\left\|\nabla\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2})\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1})\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\\
\leq C(1+T)\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\right).

For the higher order term we firstly calculate

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1,2}

\displaystyle=\nabla\left((\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1})\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1},

and similarly to the above one writes

\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}\right)\\
=\nabla\Phi^{2}\left((\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}-(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}\right)(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}.

Thus by using our uniform bounds and Lemma
4.5
one finds that

\left\|((\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1,2})\circ\Phi^{1})\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1})\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\leq CT\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\right).

The bound for
I_{2}[\cdot]
follows after noting the time derivative of the determinant term may be bounded similarly since

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\right)=-\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\operatorname{tr}\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right),

from Jacobi’s formula.

To bound
I_{3}[\cdot]
one can calculate that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi^{1,2}=\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{1,2})-\left(\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{1},

and hence

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi^{1,2}\right)\circ\Phi^{1}=\nabla\Phi^{2}\left((\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{2}(\Phi^{2})-(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{1}(\Phi^{1})\right),

so that the result follows in the obvious way.

One can show the bound for
I_{4}[\cdot]
after using the above expressions for
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi^{1,2}
and
\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1,2}
and arguing as we have above.
For
I_{5}[\cdot]
we have that

\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{T}=\left(\frac{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1})}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{2})}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-T}(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{T}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1},

and hence by pulling back onto
\Omega(0)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{T}-\mathbb{I}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega^{1}(0))}\leq C\left\|\frac{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1})}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{2})}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-T}(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{T}-\mathbb{I}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\det(\nabla\Phi^{1})(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-T}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\left\|\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{2})}(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{T}-\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1})}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{T}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\nabla\Phi^{1}-\nabla\Phi^{2}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))},

so that the result will readily follow from Lemma
4.5
after noting that

\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}.

The bounds for
I_{6}[\cdot],I_{7}[\cdot]
are straightforward after using the above expressions for
\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1,2}
and
\nabla\Phi^{1,2}
respectively.

Since we will want to choose
\boldsymbol{\eta}=A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})
we cannot bound
\boldsymbol{\eta}
in the
\mathbf{H}^{1}
norm, and so to bound
I_{8}[\cdot]
we firstly integrate by parts to see that

\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I}\right)\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\\
=-\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\nabla\cdot\left(\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I}\right)\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}.

We then observe that

\displaystyle(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I}

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-T}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{T}\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}-\mathbb{I}

\displaystyle=\nabla\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-T}\left((\nabla\Phi^{1})^{T}-(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{T}\right)\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1},

which we can bound in the usual way.
One may also repeat a similar argument for the higher order term
\nabla\cdot\left((\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I}\right)
by using the expression for
\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1,2}
.
In both cases one concludes by using Lemma
4.5
once again.
The bound for
I_{9}[\cdot]
is similar, where one first integrates by parts for

\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\\
=-\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\nabla\cdot\left(\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-1}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{-T}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta},

where we note that this will involve a third order derivative of
\Phi^{1,2}
, which is defined almost everywhere since the
\Phi^{i}
are
\mathbf{C}^{2,1}
in space.
To control this we first calculate
\nabla^{3}\Phi^{1,2}
as

\nabla^{3}\Phi^{1,2}=\nabla\left(\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}\right),

which one can expand to find

\displaystyle\nabla\left(\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}-\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla^{3}\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-3}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-3}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-3}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle+\left(\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-3}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{3}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-3}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle+\left(\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-3}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle+\left(\nabla\Phi^{2}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}\right)\circ(\Phi^{1})^{-1}.

By appropriately grouping these terms together one finds

\displaystyle(\nabla^{3}\Phi^{1,2})\circ\Phi^{1}

\displaystyle=\nabla\Phi^{2}\left[(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-1}\nabla^{3}\Phi^{2}-(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{3}\Phi^{1}\right](\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-3}

\displaystyle+2\nabla\Phi^{2}\left[(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}-(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}\right](\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-3}

\displaystyle+\nabla\Phi^{2}\left[(\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}-(\nabla\Phi^{2})^{-1}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{2}\right](\nabla\Phi^{1})^{-2}\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1}(\nabla^{2}\Phi^{1})^{-2},

where the terms in the square brackets can be suitably bounded by using Lemma
4.5
, and the terms outside the brackets are uniformly bounded.
It is then straightforward, albeit tedious, to show that this gives an appropriate bound on the divergence term, as well as the determinant term (by similar logic to the bound for
I_{2}[\cdot]
) we omit further details.
Likewise the remaining bounds for
I_{10}[\cdot],I_{11}[\cdot],I_{12}[\cdot]
are dealt with by similar arguments to those above and so we omit these calculations too.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

It may seem that the bounds for
I_{2}[\cdot],I_{3}[\cdot],I_{4}[\cdot]
are problematic as they do not gain a power of
T
, however this is compensated for by the
L^{\infty}
in time bounds for the corresponding fluid terms appearing in inequalities, which allows one to gain a power of
T
after integrating in time.

With this lemma we now show that
\mathscr{F}:D(\mathscr{F})\rightarrow D(\mathscr{F})
is a contraction, under suitable smallness assumptions as in Lemma
4.4
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Given some sufficiently large
R>0
, and a sufficiently small relative density,
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
, as in Lemma
4.4
, there exists some small
T>0
(determined by
R
and the initial data) such that
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction.

Proof.

To streamline this proof we omit many of the calculations and instead refer back to earlier proofs which provide details on related calculations — namely Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
.
By taking
\boldsymbol{\eta}=A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})
in (
4.10
) one obtains

(4.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\frac{\partial(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})}{\partial t}\cdot A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})+\mu\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}|A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})|^{2}\\
&+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})-\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\\
&+\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left((\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1})+(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{1})\right)A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\\
&-\rho\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left((\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})+(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}})\right)A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\\
&+\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}(\mathbf{B}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}})\cdot A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{12}I_{k}[A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})].\end{aligned}

While
A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})
is not an element of
\mathbf{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))
, the above equality can be justified by an approximation argument choosing
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{1}(t))
such that
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k}\rightarrow A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})
in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

To control the advective terms we observe that

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}),

and so by using Young’s inequality one finds

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})-\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{16}\|A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle+C\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle+C\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}\|\nabla(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2},

where we use the Sobolev embedding
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))
and Poincaré’s inequality for

\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}\|\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}\leq\|\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}\|\nabla(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}

Notice that, in contrast to Lemma
3.1
, we have not gained a term like
\|\nabla(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{6}
, and so we will be able to apply Grönwall’s inequality, rather than Lemma
2.9
, for our estimate.
The same argument applies to the terms involving
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{1},\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}
, for example

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{1}\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})-\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}})\cdot\nabla\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})

\displaystyle+\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}\cdot\nabla(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}),

which is bounded similarly to the above.

From here on one may now argue as in Lemma
3.1
to obtain a bound of the form

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}|\nabla(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}|A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}T^{\frac{1}{3}}\|\mathbf{B}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle+K(t)\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{12}|I_{k}[A_{\sigma}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2})]|,

where
K(t)
is some integrable function, and we have again taken
\kappa=T^{\frac{1}{3}}
.
Clearly one obtains a suitable bound on
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}
in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{2}}(\Phi^{1})
by using the bounds from Lemma
4.9
, an appropriate bound on
\|\mathbf{B}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}
, Young’s inequality, and concludes by using Grönwall’s inequality.
The only ingredient here we have not discussed is the required bound on
\mathbf{B}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}}
in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(\Phi^{1})
.
This term is bounded by arguing along the same lines as we have in this proof, we refer the reader to §
B.3
for some of the details on this calculation.
After a dense and tedious calculation one finds that

(4.13)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\leq C\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{q}^{1}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{q}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\right|+C\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|+\text{ lower order terms},

which is sufficient for our purposes, and lets one conclude an appropriate bound for
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}
in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{2}}(\Phi^{1})
.

The bound for
\overline{p}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}
in
L^{2}_{H^{1}}(\Phi^{1})
is more involved, for which one emulates the proof of Lemma
3.3
.
In particular one finds that

\int_{0}^{T}\|\overline{p}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))}^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{B}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}^{2}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}+CT^{s}\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\right),

for some
s>0
.
We now face the same difficulty as in the proof of Lemma
4.4
, namely that there is no power of
T
involved in the first term on the right-hand side, and hence (
4.13
) may not be sufficient to prove that
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction.
We resolve this in the same way as in the proof of Lemma
4.4
, where we observe that one gains a factor of
\rho
from the definition of
\mathbf{B}^{i}
, and one will gain a factor of
\frac{1}{\rho_{B}}
from the
m
on the left-hand side of (
4.7
).
In particular, we will be able to show that
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction if the relative density
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
is sufficiently small.
We omit further details on the bound for
\overline{p}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}
.

Once one obtains the bounds for
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}
and
\overline{p}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{p}}^{2}
in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{2}}(\Phi^{1})
and
L^{2}_{H^{1}}(\Phi^{1})
respectively it is then straightforward to show bounds for the functions
{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}^{2}}
and
{p}^{1}-\widehat{p^{2}}
by arguing along the lines of Appendix
B
to show that
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{1}-\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{2}}
and
\widetilde{p}^{1}-\widehat{\widetilde{p}^{2}}
are bounded appropriately (see (
B.13
) in particular).
Finally, we use our bounds in (
4.7
) to control the first two integrals on the right-hand side, and one controls the other terms by essentially the same arguments those in the proof of Lemma
4.9
.
All in all, this gives us a bound of the form

(4.14)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{Q}^{1}-\mathbf{Q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}^{2}&\leq C_{1}\frac{\rho^{2}}{\rho_{B}^{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}^{2}\right)\\
&+C_{2}T^{2s}\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}^{2}\right),\end{aligned}

for some
s>0
.
Here the constant
C_{1}
depends only on
\Omega(0)
and
\mathrm{diam}(B(0))
, but
C_{2}
depends on
R
(which defines
D(\mathscr{F})
) and the initial data.
From here it is almost apparent that if the relative density,
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
, is sufficiently small one may choose
T
to be sufficiently small (determined by the initial data) so that
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction.
Of course for this to be the case we also require an analogous bound on the difference
\|\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbf{W}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}
.

We now outline how one shows such a bound.
Firstly, it is clear that

(4.15)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\mathbb{J}^{1}\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2}\mathbf{W}^{2}\right)=\int_{\partial B^{2}(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}^{2}(t))\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{2}-\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}^{1}(t))\times\mathbb{T}^{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1},

with initial data

(\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbf{W}^{2})(0)=0.

The idea here is that one can write the integrals on the right-hand side in a similar way to (
4.7
), but we now have the added complication of dealing with two distinct inertial tensors on the left-hand side.
For this we observe that

(4.16)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\mathbb{J}^{1}\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2}\mathbf{W}^{2}\right)&=\mathbb{J}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbf{W}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}\\
&+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{J}^{1}(\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbf{W}^{2})+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})\mathbf{W}^{2},\end{aligned}

and hence it is clear we require bounds on
(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})
.
We recall from the proof of Lemma
4.4
that we may write

\mathbb{J}^{i}(t)=\int_{B(0)}\rho_{B}\left(|\mathbb{O}^{i}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})|^{2}\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{O}^{i}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})\otimes\mathbb{O}^{i}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})\right)

where
\mathbb{O}^{i}(t)
is the orthogonal matrix such that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{O}^{i}(t)\mathbf{z}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i}(t)\times\mathbb{O}^{i}(t)\mathbf{z}_{0},

for all
\mathbf{z}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\mathbb{O}^{i}(0)=\mathbb{I}
.
Thus one obtains that

\mathbb{J}^{1}(t)-\mathbb{J}^{2}(t)=\int_{B(0)}\rho_{B}\left(\mathbb{O}^{2}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})\otimes\mathbb{O}^{2}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})-\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})\otimes\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{q}_{0})\right).

It is now clear that we must bound
\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)-\mathbb{O}^{2}(t)
which we observe is such that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)-\mathbb{O}^{2}(t))\mathbf{z}_{0}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}(t)-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}(t))\times\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)\mathbf{z}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}(t)\times(\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)-\mathbb{O}^{2}(t))\mathbf{z}_{0}.

Thus applying the Grönwall inequality one obtains

\left|(\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)-\mathbb{O}^{2}(t))\mathbf{z}_{0}\right|\leq|\mathbf{z}_{0}|\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}(s)-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s\right),

and we may use the embedding
\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{0}([0,T])
so that

\left|(\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)-\mathbb{O}^{2}(t))\mathbf{z}_{0}\right|\leq Ct|\mathbf{z}_{0}|\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s\right),

It is an immediate consequence to observe that

\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)-\mathbb{O}^{2}(t))\mathbf{z}_{0}\right|\leq|(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}(t)-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}(t))||\mathbf{z}_{0}|+Ct|\mathbf{z}_{0}|\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

These readily translate into bounds for
(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})
.

We now use (
4.15
) and (
4.16
) to see that

(4.17)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbf{W}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-(\mathbb{J}^{1})^{-1}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}-(\mathbb{J}^{1})^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{J}^{1}(\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbf{W}^{2})

\displaystyle-(\mathbb{J}^{1})^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})\mathbf{W}^{2}+(\mathbb{J}^{1})^{-1}\int_{\partial B^{2}(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}^{2}(t))\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{2}

\displaystyle-(\mathbb{J}^{1})^{-1}\int_{\partial B^{1}(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}^{1}(t))\times\mathbb{T}^{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{1},

where we recall from the proof of Lemma
4.4
that
(\mathbb{J}^{1})^{-1}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}([0,T])
.
It is clear how most of these terms are controlled, but we briefly explain how one controls the term involving
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})
.
Firstly we use the
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}([0,T])
bound on
(\mathbb{J}^{1})^{-1}
to see that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left|(\mathbb{J}^{1})^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})\mathbf{W}^{2}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})\mathbf{W}^{2}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})\right|^{2},

where we have used the fact that
\mathbf{W}^{2}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])
(due to Lemma
4.4
) and the embedding
\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{0}([0,T])
for the second inequality.
One then uses the above bound for
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{O}^{1}(t)-\mathbb{O}^{2}(t))
to deduce a bound for
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}(t)-\mathbb{J}^{2}(t))
so that one obtains a bound of the form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}^{1}-\mathbb{J}^{2})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\left(|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}|^{2}+t\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq CT\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}^{2},

where we have once again used the embedding
\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{0}([0,T])
.
One proceeds in this manner for the other terms, and controls the integral terms in (
4.17
) in the same manner as we did for the bounds on
\mathbf{Q}^{1}-\mathbf{Q}^{2}
.
After repeating these arguments one ultimately obtains a bound of the form

(4.18)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{W}^{1}-\mathbf{W}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}^{2}&\leq C_{3}\frac{\rho^{2}}{\rho_{B}^{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}^{2}\right)\\
&+C_{4}T^{2s}\left(\|\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}([0,T])}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}([0,T])}^{2}\right),\end{aligned}

for some
s>0
, where
C_{3}
depends only on
\Omega(0)
and
\mathrm{diam}(B(0))
, and
C_{3}
depends on
R
and the initial data.
By combining (
4.14
) and (
4.18
) is clear that if the relative density,
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
, is sufficiently small in terms of the geometry of the problem, and
T
is sufficiently small in terms of the initial data, then the map
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

It is straightforward to show that
D(\mathscr{F})
is a complete metric space, and hence using the Banach fixed point theorem, one finds that
\mathscr{F}
admits a unique fixed point,
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in D(\mathscr{F})
.
Defining
(\mathbf{u},p)
to be the unique strong solution to (
3.1
) defined by the fixed point,
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega})
, we find that the quadruple
(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathbf{u},p)
is a strong solution to (
1.1
), (
1.2
) in the sense of Definition
1.1
.
Since any strong solution (in the sense of Definition
1.1
) to (
1.1
), (
1.2
) yields a fixed point of
\mathscr{F}
, and
\mathscr{F}
has a unique fixed point, it is clear that such a strong solution is unique.
All in all we have shown Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

We have shown that strong solutions, in the sense of (
1.1
), are unique under suitable assumptions on
T
and
\frac{\rho}{\rho_{B}}
.
In the literature there are stronger uniqueness results for (
1.1
), (
1.2
).
For example in
[
60
]
a weak-strong uniqueness result is proven for strong solutions satisfying a Prodi–Serrin condition.
Similar weak-strong uniqueness results have also been established for related problems in
[
21
,
28
,
46
,
55
,
58
]
.
Note however that the choice of
\Phi
defining the function spaces for the fluid quantities in Theorem
1.2
is clearly not unique, and depends on the choice of extension of the boundary data.

4.3.
An alternate iteration

We now briefly discuss an alternate approach to our iterative argument which may be useful in the analysis of numerical methods, as evaluating the boundary integrals in (
4.1a
) and (
4.1b
) may be problematic for the numerical analysis.
As such one may want to reformulate this iteration using only integral in the bulk domain,
\Omega(t)
.
For this we choose
\zeta(t):\Omega(t)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to be a sufficiently smooth function such that
\zeta(t)=1
on
\partial B(t)
and
\zeta(t)=0
on
\Gamma
.
One such example of
\zeta(t)
is given by the harmonic extension of this boundary data.
For this one realizes that the boundary integral in (
4.1a
) is

\int_{\partial B(t)}\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}=\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}\zeta\mathbb{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\cdot\left(\zeta\mathbb{T}\right)=\int_{\Omega(t)}(\nabla\zeta)^{T}\mathbb{T}+\int_{\Omega(t)}\zeta\nabla\cdot\mathbb{T}.

Recalling that
\nabla\cdot\mathbb{T}=\rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right)
, one can now rewrite this final integral by observing, from Lemma
2.10
and the fact that
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{u}
on
\partial\Omega(t)
,

\displaystyle\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}\zeta\mathbf{u}=\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\zeta\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right)+\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial t}\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\zeta)\mathbf{u}.

Hence one can alternatively write (
4.1a
) as

m\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{d}t}=m\mathbf{v}_{0}-\rho\int_{\Omega(t)}\zeta\mathbf{u}+\rho\int_{\Omega(0)}\zeta(0)\mathbf{u}_{0}+\rho\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega(s)}\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial t}\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\zeta)\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega(s)}(\nabla\zeta)^{T}\mathbb{T}\,\mathrm{d}s.

One may perform similar calculations to rewrite (
4.1b
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathbb{J}\mathbf{W})=-\int_{\Omega(t)}(\nabla\zeta)^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q})\times\mathbb{T}+\int_{\Omega(t)}\zeta(\nabla\times\mathbb{T})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}).

5.
A numerical implementation

5.1.
An evolving finite element method

The novelty of our proof of Theorem
1.2
is in its constructive nature.
As such it is natural to use this construction to influence the design of numerical methods solving (
1.1
), (
1.2
).
We now consider such a numerical method, by discretising in space and time, and using evolving finite element methods, cf.
[
33
]
, for which we shall denote our timestep size as
\tau
and our spatial mesh size as
h
.
We will discretise in space using
\underline{\mathbb{P}^{2}}
–
\mathbb{P}^{1}
finite elements, cf.
[
14
, Section 8.8]
), for the velocity-pressure pairs where the underline denotes vector-valued elements.
Similar problems have been studied by using a semi-Lagrangian approach as in

[
24
]
, an Eulerian approach as in
[
15
,
16
,
61
,
76
]
, or an arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian approach as in
[
42
,
57
,
65
]
— we shall take this latter approach.

For the following numerical method we assume that one begins with data

(\mathbf{q}^{k}_{n,h})_{n=0,\ldots,N_{T}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad(\mathbf{v}^{k}_{n,h})_{n=0,\ldots,N_{T}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k}_{n,h})_{n=0,\ldots,N_{T}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\mathbf{u}_{0,h}\in\underline{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\Omega_{0,h}),

which are approximations of the centre of mass, velocity of the rigid body, angular momentum, and initial fluid velocity.
Here
\Omega_{0,h}
is some triangulated domain approximating the known domain
\Omega(0)
, which will be used as the initial domain in each iteration, i.e.
\Omega_{0,h}^{k}=\Omega_{0,h}^{k-1}=\ldots=\Omega_{0,h}
, and we shall denote the nodes of
\Omega_{h}(0)
as
(\mathbf{x}^{0}_{0,i})_{i=1,\ldots,N_{h}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Likewise we denote the number of timesteps at
N_{T}:=T/\tau
and the number of degrees of freedom as
N_{h}
.
We note that the superscript,
(\cdot)^{k}
, refers to the iteration we are at, and the subscripts
(\cdot)_{n,h}
refer to the timestep
n
, and the dependence on the mesh size
h
.

Given this data the algorithm is as follows:

(1)

Given some known spatial domain,
\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k}
, one must compute the next spatial domain,
\Omega_{n,h}^{k}
, which in turn requires computation of a discrete harmonic extension,
\mathbf{V}_{n-1,h}^{k}\in\underline{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k})
.
This is computed as the
\underline{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k})
finite element solution of the vector-valued Laplace problem:

(5.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta\mathbf{V}_{n-1,h}^{k}=0,&\text{ on }\ \Omega_{n-1,h}^{k},\\
\mathbf{V}_{n-1,h}^{k}=\mathbf{v}^{k}_{n-1}+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k}_{n-1}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}^{k}_{n-1}),&\text{ on }\partial B_{n-1,h}^{k},\\
\mathbf{V}_{n-1,h}^{k}=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma_{h},\end{cases}

where here spatial domain has disjoint boundary
\partial\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k}=\Gamma_{h}\cup\partial B_{n-1,h}^{k}
, where
\Gamma_{h}
denotes the fixed boundary and
\partial B_{n-1,h}^{k}
the moving boundary.
One computes a finite element approximation of the pushforward map,
\Phi^{k}_{n,h}\in\underline{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k})
, by approximating (
3.18
) as

(5.2)

\frac{\Phi^{k}_{n,h}-\mathbf{x}}{\tau}=\mathbf{V}_{n-1,h}^{k},

where
\mathbf{x}
is the identity on
\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k}
.
One now updates spatial domain via
\Omega_{n,h}^{k}:=\Phi_{n,h}^{k}(\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k})
, i.e. we define new nodes
\mathbf{x}^{k}_{n,i}:=\Phi_{n,h}^{k}(\mathbf{x}^{k}_{n-1,i})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N_{h}\}
.

(2)

We now solve the Navier–Stokes equations on
\Omega_{n,h}^{k}
.
That is to say, we look for functions
(\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k},p_{n,h}^{k})\in\underline{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\Omega_{n,h}^{k})\times\mathbb{P}^{1}(\Omega_{n,h}^{k})
solving the saddle-point problem

(5.3a)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\rho}{\tau}\left(\int_{\Omega_{n,h}^{k}}\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n,h}^{k}-\int_{\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k}}\mathbf{u}_{n-1,h}^{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n-1,h}^{k}\right)-\rho\int_{\Omega_{n,h}^{k}}(\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n,h}^{k})\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}\\
+\rho\int_{\Omega_{n,h}^{k}}(\mathbf{V}_{n,h}^{k}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n,h}^{k})\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}+\mu\int_{\Omega_{h}}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n,h}^{k}=0,\end{aligned}

(5.3b)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{n,h}^{k}}\psi_{n,h}^{k}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}=0,

for all
(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n,h}^{k},\psi_{n,h}^{k})\in\underline{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\Omega_{n,h}^{k})\times\mathbb{P}^{1}(\Omega_{n,h}^{k})
and such that

(5.4)

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}=\mathbf{v}^{k}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k}_{n}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}^{k}_{n}),\quad\text{on }\partial B_{n,h}^{k},\\
\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}=0,\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{h}.\\
\end{gathered}

Here
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n-1,h}^{k}\in\underline{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k})
denotes the finite element function with the same nodal values as the test function
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n,h}^{k}\in\underline{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\Omega_{n,h}^{k})
but defined on the previous spatial domain,
\Omega_{n-1,h}^{k}
.

(3)

Finally we create new guesses for the centre of mass, rigid body velocity, and angular momentum

(\mathbf{q}^{k+1}_{n,h})_{n=0,\ldots,N_{T}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad(\mathbf{v}^{k}_{n,h})_{n=0,\ldots,N_{T}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k+1}_{n,h})_{n=0,\ldots,N_{T}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

by approximating (
4.1a
) and (
4.1b
) by

(5.5a)

\displaystyle m\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_{n,h}^{k+1}-\mathbf{q}_{n-1,h}^{k+1}}{\tau}\right)=\mathbf{v}_{n,h}^{k+1},

(5.5b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{v}_{n,h}^{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{n-1,h}^{k+1}}{\tau}=-\int_{\partial B_{n,h}^{k}}\mathbb{T}_{n,h}^{k}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{n,h}^{k},

(5.5c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{J}_{n,h}^{k}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{n,h}^{k+1}-\mathbb{J}_{n-1,h}^{k}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{n-1,h}^{k+1}}{\tau}=-\int_{\partial B_{n,h}^{k}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}_{n,h}^{k})\times\mathbb{T}_{n,h}^{k}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{n,h}^{k},

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{n,h}^{k}:=-p_{n,h}^{k}\mathbb{I}+\mu\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}+(\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k})^{T}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}_{n,h}^{k}:=\int_{B_{n,h}^{k}}\rho_{B}\left(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}_{n,h}^{k}|^{2}\mathbb{I}-(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}_{n,h}^{k})\otimes(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}_{n,h}^{k})\right).

Note that here the initial conditions known since

\mathbf{q}^{k+1}_{0,h}=\mathbf{q}^{k}_{0,h},\quad\mathbf{v}^{k+1}_{0,h}=\mathbf{v}^{k}_{0,h},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k+1}_{0,h}=\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k}_{0,h}.

(4)

Repeat this iteration until some desired stopping criteria.

Remark 5.1
.

The advective terms appearing in (
5.3a
) appear from the use of Reynold’s transport theorem since, at the continuous level,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}+\int_{\Omega(t)}{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\partial t}+\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{V}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}+\int_{\Omega(t)}{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\partial t}+\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}+\int_{\Omega(t)}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u})\boldsymbol{\phi}+\int_{\Omega(t)}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi})\mathbf{u}-\int_{\Omega(t)}(\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi})\mathbf{u},

for all functions
\boldsymbol{\phi}
of the form
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\circ\Phi(t)^{-1}
for functions
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
defined on
\Omega(0)
where we have used that
\partial^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{V}}\boldsymbol{\phi}=0
and hence
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\partial t}=-\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}
.
This is precisely the form of the finite element basis functions we consider.

Remark 5.2
.

For practical numerical methods one may alternatively write the quasilinear term,
\int_{\Omega_{n,h}^{k}}(\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n,h}^{k})\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}
, as
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{n,h}^{k}}(\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n,h}^{k})\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}-\int_{\Omega_{n,h}^{k}}(\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n,h}^{k})\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n,h}^{k}
to preserve the anti-symmetric property of the associated trilinear form.
We refer the reader to
[
53
]
for related discussion.

Generalisations

Our method readily extends to multiple rigid bodies with some minor, but tedious, modifications to our arguments.
Some more interesting topics would be whether this extends to the case of Navier-slip boundary conditions (
1.5
), and to compressible fluids as in
[
37
]
.
Indeed the latter of these topics relies heavily on the short time existence of strong solutions to the compressible Navier–Stokes equations (see
[
38
]
or the recent monograph
[
54
]
) which would impact the proof of Lemma
4.3
.
Lastly, and the most challenging extension of our method would be to problems with a boundary that changes shape, as we have repeatedly exploited the fact that the boundary does not change shape.
Examples of such problems are fluid-elastic body interactions (such as those discussed in
[
11
,
17
]
), free boundary problems (for example the problems discussed in
[
32
]
), and problems coupling the geometric evolution of a surface to the solution of a PDE posed on the surface (see for example
[
1
]
).

Appendix A
A calculation for Lemma
3.1

In this appendix we perform the calculation which was omitted at the end of the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
That is, we verify that (
3.10
) holds given that (
3.13
) holds.
We recall from the proof of Lemma
3.1
that we want to use Lemma
2.9
with
\lambda(s)=s+s^{3}
, where for a small, fixed
y_{0}>0
one finds that

\displaystyle\Lambda(y)=\int_{y_{0}}^{y}\frac{1}{s+s^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{y^{2}}{1+y^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+y_{0}^{2}}{y_{0}^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\Lambda^{-1}(z)=\left(\frac{y_{0}^{2}}{1+y_{0}^{2}-y_{0}^{2}\exp(2z))}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp(z),

\displaystyle\text{dom}(\Lambda^{-1})=\left(-\infty,\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+y_{0}^{2}}{y_{0}^{2}}\right)\right).

It is clear to see that the condition (
3.13
) is equivalent to

\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}+C\left(\kappa\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}}{4+\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}+C\left(\kappa\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+y_{0}^{2}}{y_{0}^{2}}\right)\\
+\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}C\left(\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\|\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}+\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}^{8}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)\\
\in\left(-\infty,\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+y_{0}^{2}}{y_{0}^{2}}\right)\right),

and hence we may apply
\Lambda^{-1}
to this term.
For ease of notation we now define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}:=\frac{\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}+C\left(\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}}{4+\rho^{2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}^{4}+C\left(\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right)^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}:=\int_{0}^{\widetilde{T}}C\left(\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\|\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}+\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}^{2}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega(t))}^{8}+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,4}(\Omega(t))}^{2}\right).

Now using the above expression for
\Lambda^{-1}
one finds that

\Lambda^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(\mathcal{A}_{1})+\mathcal{A}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+y_{0}^{2}}{y_{0}^{2}}\right)\right)\\
=\left(\frac{y_{0}^{2}}{1+y_{0}^{2}-y_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{1+y_{0}^{2}}{y_{0}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{A}_{1}\exp(2\mathcal{A}_{2})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{1+y_{0}^{2}}{y_{0}^{2}}}\exp(\mathcal{A}_{2})\\
=\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\exp(\mathcal{A}_{2})}{\sqrt{1-\mathcal{A}_{1}\exp(2\mathcal{A}_{2})}}.

Unpacking the expression for
\mathcal{A}_{1}
, and recalling
\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{T})=\exp(2\mathcal{A}_{2})
we see

\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\exp(\mathcal{A}_{2})}{\sqrt{1-\mathcal{A}_{1}\exp(2\mathcal{A}_{2})}}=\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{T}),

for
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
as defined in Lemma
3.1
, from which one finds that (
3.10
) holds.

Appendix B
The Stokes equations on an evolving domain

Let
\mathbf{v}(t)
to be the unique solution to the following problem

5

Here we have set the viscosity to be
\mu=1
for notational simplicity.
on
\Omega(t)

(B.1)

\displaystyle\left.\begin{aligned} -\Delta\mathbf{v}+\nabla p&=0\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}&=0\end{aligned}\right\}\text{ on }\Omega(t),

such that

\mathbf{v}=0\text{ on }\Gamma,\quad\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\text{ on }\partial B(t).

Here we are considering
\Omega(t)

\mathbf{q}

Existence of a weak solution follows from standard results on the Stokes equation, and moreover one finds that, for

(B.2)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{2,r}(\Omega(t))}+\|{p}\|_{W^{1,r}(\Omega(t))}\leq C\|\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\|_{\mathbf{W}^{2,r}(\Omega(t))},

for a constant

\mathbf{v}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{W}^{2,r}}(\Phi),\quad p\in L^{\infty}_{W^{1,r}}(\Phi)\quad\forall r\in[1,\infty).

\mathbf{v}

We verify this by pulling this equation back to
\Omega(0)

B.1.
Some estimates

We introduce shorthand notation
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{v}\circ\Phi(t)
and
\widehat{p}=p\circ\Phi(t)
.
Then these solve the system

(B.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega(0)}J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}=\int_{\Omega(0)}J\widehat{p}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{D}^{T}\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\right),

(B.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega(0)}Jq\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{D}^{T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}}\right)=0,

for all
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega(0)),q\in L^{2}(\Omega(0))
, where
\mathbb{D}=\nabla\Phi,J=\det(\mathbb{D})
.
We observe that

\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{D}^{T}\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\right)=\mathbb{D}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta},

and will use this expression throughout.
It is useful to note that the bilinear form on the left of (
B.3
) is coercive, and the bilinear form in (
B.4
) satisfies an inf-sup condition.
This first statement is trivially true, but we briefly prove this latter statement.

Lemma B.1
.

Let

L_{t}^{2}(\Omega(0)):=\left\{q\in L^{2}(\Omega(0))\mid\int_{\Omega(t)}q\circ\Phi(t)^{-1}=0\right\}.

This is a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega(0))
, and moreover there exists a constant
C(\Phi(t))
such that

(B.5)

\displaystyle\inf_{q\in L_{t}^{2}(\Omega(0))}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(0))}\frac{\int_{\Omega(0)}Jq\mathbb{D}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(0))}\|q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega(0))}}\geq C(\Phi(t))>0.

Proof.

Firstly we notice that by pulling back the integral which defines
L_{t}^{2}(\Omega(0))
we find

\int_{\Omega(t)}q\circ\Phi(t)^{-1}=\int_{\Omega(0)}J(t)q,

and so we can view
L_{t}^{2}(\Omega(0))
as the preimage of
0
under the map
q\mapsto\int_{\Omega(0)}J(t)q
, and is hence a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega(0))
.

By considering the pushforward of this bilinear form we find that an equivalent quantity to consider is

\inf_{\widetilde{q}\in L_{0}^{2}(\Omega(t))}\sup_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}\frac{\int_{\Omega(t)}\widetilde{q}\nabla\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{C_{L^{2}}(t)C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}\|\widetilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega(t))}},

where
C_{L^{2}}(t),C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)
are norm equivalence constants which appear in the compatibility of the pairs
(L^{2}(\Omega(t)),\Phi(t))
,
(\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t)),\Phi(t))
respectively, and

L^{2}_{0}(\Omega(t)):=\left\{\widetilde{q}\in L^{2}(\Omega(t))\mid\int_{\Omega(t))}\widetilde{q}=0\right\}.

It is well known that there is a constant
\widetilde{C}>0
, which depends only on the geometry of
\Omega(0)
, such that

\inf_{\widetilde{q}\in L_{0}^{2}(\Omega(t))}\sup_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(0))}\frac{\int_{\Omega(t)}\widetilde{q}\nabla\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}\|\widetilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega(t))}}\geq\widetilde{C}>0.

Thus our result holds for
C(\Phi(t)):=\widetilde{C}C_{L^{2}}(t)C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)
.
∎

With this inf-sup condition we can now show a bound for
\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}
.

Lemma B.2
.

\mathbf{v}
solving (
3.2
) for almost all
t\in[0,T]
is such that
\mathbf{v}\in H^{1}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}\cap L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{W}^{2,r}}
for all
r\in[1,\infty)
.

Proof.

We have already provided the
\mathbf{W}^{2,r}
estimate above, and so our focus is on the time derivative of
\mathbf{v}
.
One can show the existence of strong time derivatives (see
[
34
, Appendix A]
for a similar calculation), but we provide only a formal argument which yields bounds for this function.
By differentiating (
B.3
) in time, for a sufficiently smooth test function, one finds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T})\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}\nabla\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}}:\nabla\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\widehat{p}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{D}^{T}\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\right)+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\frac{\partial\widehat{p}}{\partial t}\mathbb{D}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\widehat{p}\frac{\partial\mathbb{D}}{\partial t}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\widehat{p}\mathbb{D}:\nabla\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\partial t},

and we can use (
B.3
) to see that this simplifies to

(B.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T})\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}\nabla\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\widehat{p}\mathbb{D}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\frac{\partial\widehat{p}}{\partial t}\mathbb{D}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\widehat{p}\frac{\partial\mathbb{D}}{\partial t}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}.

Similarly we find that differentiating (
B.4
) yields

(B.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}q\mathbb{D}:\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}}+\int_{\Omega(0)}Jq\frac{\partial\mathbb{D}}{\partial t}:\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}}+\int_{\Omega(0)}Jq\mathbb{D}:\nabla\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}=0.

Thus we find that the pair
\left(\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t},\frac{\partial\widehat{p}}{\partial t}\right)
solve the saddle point problem

(B.8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\Omega(0)}J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}\nabla\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}-\int_{\Omega(0)}J\frac{\partial\widehat{p}}{\partial t}\mathbb{D}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}&=-\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T})\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\\
&+\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\widehat{p}\mathbb{D}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega(0)}J\widehat{p}\frac{\partial\mathbb{D}}{\partial t}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta},\end{aligned}

(B.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega(0)}Jq\mathbb{D}:\nabla\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}=-\int_{\Omega(0)}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}q\mathbb{D}:\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}}-\int_{\Omega(0)}Jq\frac{\partial\mathbb{D}}{\partial t}:\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}},

for all
q\in L^{2}(\Omega(0)),\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbf{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega(0))
.
We have written the system in this way so that the terms on the left-hand side involve the time derivatives, and the terms on the right-hand side can be seen as some “forcing” term in the system.
This system is subject to boundary conditions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}=0\text{ on }\Gamma,\quad\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}=\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))-\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)\text{ on }\partial B(0).

Now owing to the fact that these bilinear forms have coercivity and inf-sup properties respectively we can apply standard results for saddle point problems in Hilbert spaces, see for instance
[
14
, Chapter 4]
, to obtain a bound of the form

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T})\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|\widehat{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle+\frac{C}{\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|\mathbb{D}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|\widehat{p}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle+\frac{C}{\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|J\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbb{D}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|\widehat{p}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega(0))}

(B.10)

\displaystyle+\frac{C\sup_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}{C(\Phi(t))\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|\mathbb{D}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|\widehat{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle+\frac{C\sup_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}{C(\Phi(t))\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|J\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbb{D}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|\widehat{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle+\frac{C}{\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))-\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(0))}

\displaystyle+\frac{C\sup_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}{C(\Phi(t))\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))-\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(0))}.

We then pushforward onto
\Omega(t)
to transform this into a bound for
\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}
, using the compatibility of the function space pairs.
Firstly we notice that, due to the evolving domain, taking the pushforward of
\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}
does not yield
\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}
but rather the material derivative,
\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}
.
All in all we obtain a bound of the form,

(B.11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}&\leq\|\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}\\
&+\frac{C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)^{2}}{\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T})\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}N_{1}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)\\
&+\frac{C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)^{2}}{\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|\mathbb{D}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}N_{1}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)\\
&+\frac{C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)^{2}}{\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|J\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbb{D}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}N_{1}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)\\
&+\frac{C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)^{2}\sup_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}{C(\Phi(t))\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\|\mathbb{D}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}N_{1}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)\\
&+\frac{C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)^{2}\sup_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}{C(\Phi(t))\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\left\|J\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbb{D}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}N_{1}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)\\
&+\left(\frac{C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)^{2}}{\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}+\frac{C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)^{2}\sup_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}{C(\Phi(t))\inf_{\Omega(0)}|J\mathbb{D}\mathbb{D}^{T}|}\right)N_{2}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t),\end{aligned}

where we have introduced shorthand notation

\displaystyle N_{1}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t):=\|\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))},

\displaystyle N_{2}(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t):=\left\|\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))-\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))},

for better legibility.
∎

B.2.
Estimates using the construction in §
3.3

Lastly we end this appendix by turning (
B.11
) into something more conducive to our iterative argument by expressing this bound in terms of
\mathbf{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
We assume that the map
\Phi(t):\Omega(0)\rightarrow\Omega(t)
is given by the construction in §
3.3
.
This means that one has bounds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)}\leq|\nabla\Phi|\leq E(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t),

where we have introduced
E(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)
given by

E(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t):=\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\right),

for more concise notation.
Similarly one finds that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial\nabla\Phi}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(0))}\leq C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))}E(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t).

This lets one control all of the terms involving
J,\mathbb{D}
and their time derivatives, but it remains to deal with the term
C(\Phi(t))
.
For this we require Lemma
3.7
, where by inspecting the proof one can verify that

C(\Phi(t))=C\|J\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))}(1+\|\mathbb{D}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega(t))})\geq\frac{C}{E(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)},

and likewise

C_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}(t)\leq CE(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t).

It is now straightforward to use these bounds in (
B.11
) to find that now

(B.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}&\leq C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))}E(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)\\
&+C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega(t))}^{2}E(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t)\\
&+C\left\|\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}(t)\times(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(t))-\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\times\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega(t))}E(\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega};t),\end{aligned}

for constants independent of
\mathbf{q},\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
This result will be used in the proof of Lemma
4.4
.

B.3.
A perturbation result

We now consider the problem of bounding the difference of two solutions of (
B.1
) defined over two different domains — this complements some of the missing calculations from Lemma
4.11
.
Given
(\mathbf{q}^{i},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{i})
defining a domain
\Omega^{i}(t)
, as above, we denote the solution of (
B.1
) by
\mathbf{v}^{i}:\Omega^{i}(t)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
p^{i}:\Omega^{i}(t)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, for
i=1,2
.
As in Section
4
we define maps
\Phi^{2,1}(t):\Omega^{2}(t)\rightarrow\Omega^{1}(t)
given by

\Phi^{2,1}(t):=\Phi^{1}(t)\circ\Phi^{2}(t)^{-1},

and the inverse of
\Phi^{2,1}(t)
as

\Phi^{1,2}(t):=\Phi^{2}(t)\circ\Phi^{1}(t)^{-1}:\Omega^{1}(t)\rightarrow\Omega^{2}(t).

We consider the associated Piola transform to be defined as

\mathcal{P}_{t}^{1,2}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{x})={\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2}(t)(\mathbf{x}))}(\nabla\Phi^{2,1}(t)\boldsymbol{\psi})\circ\Phi^{1,2}(t),

for a function
\boldsymbol{\psi}:\Omega^{2}(t)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Our plan now is to pull
\mathbf{v}^{2},p^{2}
back onto
\Omega^{1}(t)
and bound the transformed functions

\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}:=\mathcal{P}^{1,2}_{t}\mathbf{v}^{2},\quad\widehat{p^{2}}:=p^{2}\circ\Phi^{1,2}(t).

We find that
\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}},\widehat{p^{2}}
solve

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\nabla\Phi^{1,2}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{T}\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\widehat{p^{2}}\nabla\Phi^{1,2}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}=0,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}q\nabla\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}=0,

for all
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{1}(t)),q\in L^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))
and all
t\in[0,T]
, along with the corresponding boundary conditions.
We then observe that the differences
\mathbf{v}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}
and
p^{1}-\widehat{p^{2}}
then solve

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\nabla(\mathbf{v}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}})\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}(p^{1}-\widehat{p^{2}})\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}&=\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\left(\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\nabla\Phi^{1,2}(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})^{T}-\mathbb{I}\right)\nabla\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}\\
&+\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}\widehat{p^{2}}\left(\det(\nabla\Phi^{1,2})\nabla\Phi^{1,2}-\mathbb{I}\right):\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{1}(t)}q\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}})=0,

with boundary conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}=0,\text{ on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}\times(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})-(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{q}^{2},\text{ on }\partial B^{1}(t).

The idea is now to use this PDE to obtain bounds for the differences
\mathbf{v}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}
and
p^{1}-\widehat{p^{2}}
, which follows from elliptic regularity theory for the Stokes problem (see for instance
[
40
]
) provided we can bound the terms on the right-hand side.
This is straightforward, and follows by using Lemma
4.5
as we did in the proof of Lemma
4.9
.
This allows one to obtain a bound of the form

(B.13)

\|\mathbf{v}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}+\|p^{1}-\widehat{p^{2}}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\\
\leq C\left(1+\|\widehat{p^{2}}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega^{1}(t))}+\|\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega^{1}(t))}\right)N_{3}(\mathbf{q}^{1},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1},\mathbf{q}^{2},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2};t),

where

N_{3}(\mathbf{q}^{1},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1},\mathbf{q}^{2},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2};t):=\\
\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}\times(\mathbf{q}^{1}-\mathbf{q}^{2})-(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2})\times\mathbf{q}^{2}\right\|_{\mathbf{C}^{2,1}(\Omega^{1}(t))}

the constant
C
depends only on the geometry of
\Omega(0)
.
One may similarly obtain a bound for the difference of the time derivatives,
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\mathbf{v}^{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{v}^{2}})
, by combining the methods used in this appendix.
We omit details on this calculation as it is incredibly long, and adds nothing of value to the presentation of this paper.
