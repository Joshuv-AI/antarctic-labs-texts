Research on prediction of high energy microseismic events in rock burst mines based on BP neural network
Authors: Hongwei Wang, Lianman Xu, Huating Yu, Jizhi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In response to the frequent occurrence of high-energy microseismic events in coal mines in China, a back propagation neural network (BPNN) prediction model based on surface subsidence data has been proposed to provide a basis for safely and efficiently predicting coal mine disasters. Theoretical research on the relationship between surface displacement, mining disturbance, and high-energy microseismic event levels has demonstrated a significant correlation among these factors. When there is a sudden increase or decrease in surface displacement or mining disturbance, the advancing working face typically exhibits dynamic characteristics. Therefore, feature parameters relevant to predicting high-energy microseismic event levels were selected as input variables for the BPNN model. Raw data from 88 sets of microseismic events in the 301 working face of the third mining area of a certain coal mine in Inner Mongolia were extracted. First, outlier preprocessing was conducted to obtain a complete dataset, which is then divided into a training set and a testing set. The BPNN model was trained with the training set and subsequently tested to evaluate its predictive performance. Finally, by comparing several model evaluation metrics, it was found that the BPNN model outperforms other common models in predicting high-energy events. The overall prediction accuracy is 86.4%, and the root mean square error (RMSE) is 0.45, indicating that the BPNN-based prediction model for high-energy microseismic events in coal mines is feasible.

Introduction

Microseismic events are detected through micro seismic monitoring systems that capture seismic data generated by mining activities1. These events arise from the disruption of the original stress equilibrium caused by human mining operations. The frequent occurrence of high-energy microseismic events in coal mines restricts the safe and efficient extraction of coal resources2. High-energy vibrations not only induce stress imbalances in rock masses, leading to subsidence of goaf areas and pillar collapses, but also trigger severe dynamic hazards such as gas outbursts and rock bursts3,4. The high-stress field generated by coal seam extraction can lead to varying degrees of fracturing and collapse of the working face roof. When the roof of the coal seam and the overlying rock layers move towards the surface, it results in surface subsidence5. Practical experience demonstrates that before and after the occurrence of high-energy microseismic events, the surface above the mining area undergoes varying degrees of displacement and movement. Li Yongjing et al.6 proposed analyzing rock layer displacement and deformation to predict the magnitude and distribution of microseismic events. Tang Jupeng et al.7 proposed a new method for predicting dynamic disasters by utilizing the surface subsidence rate as an indicator. Wang Li et al.8 performed a correlation analysis between surface deformation parameters and dynamic disasters, using these parameters as auxiliary indicators for predicting related disasters, thereby enhancing the accuracy of mine disaster predictions. Malinovska et al.9 proposed a two-stage theory for surface subsidence caused by high-energy events that includes a rapid dynamic displacement stage and a stabilization phase. These studies have established a connection between microseismic events and surface subsidence. However, there are few successful examples of using surface subsidence data to predict high-energy microseismic events and to forecast dynamic disasters in mines. Two primary reasons exist for this. First, the mechanisms leading to high-energy events are quite complex, and the mechanisms behind dynamic disasters in mines have not yet been fully elucidated. Second, a significant amount of information contained in surface subsidence data has not yet been fully developed or utilized. Therefore, analyzing and researching surface subsidence data to predict high-energy microseismic events represents an urgent area of study.

With the continuous development of machine learning, deep learning models have gradually replaced traditional prediction models, and have been introduced by many experts into relevant fields10–14. Duan Yi et al.15 achieved an accuracy rate of over 90% with four different machine learning methods used to classify clustered microseismic events in coal mines. Lan Tianwei et al.16 introduced the MEA-BP neural network to predict microseismic energy, which resulted in a 46.6% reduction in relative error. Zhang Peng et al.17 employed the spatiotemporal Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise algorithm (DBSCAN) to identify microseismic clusters and subsequently utilized Support Vector Regression (SVR) to forecast the daily frequency and locations of high-energy events. Although these microseismic event prediction methods have yielded some application results, most are based on the spatiotemporal sequences of microseismic events to predict high-energy events in rock burst mines.This lack of interpretability and concrete phenomena as precursor evidence makes it difficult for the models to be trusted.

In summary, this study proposes a prediction model for microseismic event energy levels utilizing spatial data of surface subsidence in ultra-longwall mining faces. A correlation analysis was conducted between surface movement variables, mining disturbance variables, and microseismic event energy levels. The key parameters selected were advancing position, subsidence rate, mining speed, and horizontal displacement. These parameters are combined with a Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN) to establish a predictive model for microseismic event levels. The aim is to utilize more readily measurable surface subsidence data to predict the occurrence of abrupt, high-energy microseismic events, thereby offering guidance for on-site safe mining practices.

Working face overview

Overview of the 301 retrieval working face

The 301 working face in the third mining area of a coal mine in Inner Mongolia has a strike length of 3549 m and an inclined length of 300 m, making it a typical ultra-long strike, characterized by extremely thick mudstone. The coal seam is located at an elevation of 664–685 m, with an average elevation of 674 m. The average seam thickness is 6.85 m, and the dip angle ranges from 1° to 5°. From August 2019 to December 2020, the average mining advancement rate was 180 m per month. During this period, the 301 working face revealed 5 faults in the auxiliary transportation entry, 1 fault in the cutting entry, and 2 faults in the coal transportation entry, with fault drop ranges between 1.0 and 4.8 m.

Dynamic manifestations of the working face

The 301 working face in the third mining area is situated in a low-seismicity zone, with rare occurrences of geological hazards such as debris flows, landslides, and subsidence. The structural units are considered to be relatively complete and stable. However, due to the presence of numerous faults within the working face, a high frequency of microseismic events occurs during mining, with notable clustering effects in localized areas. Records show that during the mining period, localized dynamic manifestations are occasionally observed in the 301 working face, including coal wall rib spalling, roof spalling, and floor bursts.

Layout of surface subsidence observation lines

As shown in Fig. 1, the observation stations for the 301 working face are arranged in two strike lines (AB and CD) and two dip observation lines (EF and GH). Since the working face in the 3−1 mining area has nearly horizontal coal seams, the strike observation line CD is positioned at the center of the goaf. To study surface displacement under conditions of insufficient mining at a single working face, an additional strike observation line AB was established at the 301 working face. The dip observation lines are 224.7 m long and are positioned 522.55 m from the cutting face or stoppage line, with a separation of more than 50 m between lines GH and EF. The strike observation lines AB and CD are situated along the main strike profile, extending a length of 4793 m. They include 6 control points and 142 working measurement points, with a spacing of 32 m between each point.Fig. 1301 working face rock shift observatory design plan.

Factors associated with microseismic events

Since the beginning of mining operations, the mine has intermittently experienced high-energy events. Although these events have not caused damage to the underground excavation spaces, they continue to pose a potential threat to mining activities. Therefore, there is a necessity to monitor and analyze microseismic event data. Currently, the coal mine is equipped with an SOS microseismic monitoring system capable of detecting vibrations within the frequency range of 0.1–150 Hz. The system is designed to monitor vibrations with energy levels exceeding 102 J and is capable of locating these events and calculating their energy. To explore the linkage effects between surface subsidence and microseismic events, statistical analysis of the microseismic monitoring data from the 301 working face was performed.

Sinking speed

To date, many scholars in China have achieved significant research outcomes in the study of surface subsidence patterns. Typically, the surface movement during coal seam extraction is classified into three stages based on the amount of subsidence and the subsidence rate: the initial period, the active period, and the decline period18. Figure 4 shows the relationship between microseismic events and subsidence velocity. This study focuses on the subsidence activity at point A51. During the initial movement period (Aug 2019 to Oct 2019), a total of 80 microseismic events were recorded during this period, averaging 40 events per month, predominantly comprising low-energy events with energy levels below 103 J. The active movement period (Nov 2019 to Jun 2020) marks a concentrated phase of surface deformation, with 887 microseismic events occurring, averaging 110 events per month. In this phase, as the surface subsidence and subsidence rate increased, the frequency of microseismic events rose, tripling the monthly average relative to the initial movement period, with a notable increase in mid- to high-energy events. During the decline movement period (Jul 2020 to Oct 2020), a total of 100 microseismic events were recorded, averaging 20 events per month. As the influence of mining disturbances diminished, both the subsidence amount and rate decreased, leading to a significant reduction in the frequency of microseismic events, all of which were low-energy events. This demonstrates that faster surface subsidence rates correlate with an increased frequency and greater energy of microseismic events19–23.Fig. 4Plot of microseismic events versus subsidence velocity.

Plot of microseismic events versus subsidence velocity.

From Fig. 5, the 301 working face experienced three significant increases in the surface subsidence rate. The first occurrence was in October 2019, with the subsidence rate spiking to 5.47 m/d and a subsidence amount of 86 mm. Due to the formation of the initial square position in the goaf of the 301 working face, a limited number of microseismic events with energy levels exceeding 103 J were recorded. The second occurrence was in December 2019, with the subsidence rate increasing from 2.8 to 5.4 m/d and a subsidence amount of 353 mm. Due to the formation of a secondary square position in the goaf of the working face, a small number of microseismic events with energy levels exceeding 104 J were recorded. In January 2020, when large-energy microseismic events occurred in the 301 working face, the working face was approximately 132 m away from the formation of the tertiary square position in the 301 goaf. During this period, the subsidence rate at the measurement points increased from 1.3 to 3.1 m/d, with a subsidence amount of 465 mm.Fig. 5Subsidence of the surface point of the 301 working face.

Subsidence of the surface point of the 301 working face.

When high-energy microseismic events occurred at the 301 working face, both the surface subsidence rate and subsidence amount exhibited a significant increase. Analysis of surface subsidence characteristics and the location of microseismic events indicates that a sudden increase in the sinking speed correlates with notable dynamic manifestations at the working face. Based on this observation, surface subsidence data can be used as a basis for predicting high-energy microseismic events. The microseismic events in the 301 working face are primarily attributed to the extensive excavation length, which results in the roof losing support from the underlying coal and rock mass during the mining process. This creates a suspended roof that, along with the overlying rock layers, stores a significant amount of elastic potential energy, leading to frequent microseismic events during excavation.

Mining speed

From August 2019 to December 2020, both the 301 working face and the new auxiliary transportation entry at Coal Mine experienced varying degrees of dynamic manifestations. Studies show that the goaf behind a mining face creates a support pressure zone in front of the working face. The stress concentration factor can reach 2–5 times the original rock stress, making the roof prone to collapse and subsidence. Figure 6 illustrates the relationship between microseismic frequency and mining progress across different energy levels. The data indicates that from September 2019 to March 2020, the frequency of microseismic events at all energy levels remained fewer than 20, suggesting relative stability within the mine. Starting in March, there was a significant increase in the frequency of low-energy microseismic events, specifically those below 102 J and those between 102 and 103 J. Over the course of three months, a total of 1232 low-energy microseismic events occurred, including 12 high-energy microseismic events with energy levels between 102 and 103 J. The events primarily consisted of low-energy microseismic events below 102 J and those between 102 and 103 J, with a small number of microseismic events in the energy ranges of 103–104 J and 104–105 J. The distribution of the seismic sources shifted forward with the advance of the working face. It is noteworthy that during November 2019, February 2020, and November 2020, when the mining speed reached regional peaks, these periods coincided with the peaks in microseismic frequency. In April 2020, when the mining speed rapidly increased, there was a lag in the peak of microseismic frequency. This delay is hypothesized to result from the presence of a hard roof located at a depth of 1,468 m in the working face, which caused an initial accumulation of energy before being released later.Fig. 6Plot of mining rate versus frequency of microseismic events.

Between September 2019 and December 2020, there were four instances of increased mining speed, occurring in the following periods: October 2019 to November 2019, January 2020 to February 2020, June 2020 to July 2020, and September 2020 to November 2020. During three of these instances of increased mining speed, the total energy of microseismic events significantly increased. In February 2020, after increasing the mining speed to 8.1 m/d, the total energy of microseismic events also reached a quarterly high of 8.66 × 105 J. At advancing distances of 300 m, 600 m, 900 m, and 1200 m along the working face, the energy of the monitored microseismic events is near its maximum value at each of these points. This is due to the occurrence of the “square position” in the working face when it advances to integer multiples of the dip length of the working face in the strike direction. Deep mining can cause damage, fracturing, or even breaking of the surrounding rock and overlying strata. This damage often occurs when the working face exhibits a “square position” effect, leading to stress concentration in roadways, coal pillars, and the working face itself, which is a primary cause of microseismic events. Field measurements show that when the working face approaches the “square position”, the movement of the overlying strata intensifies, leading to a significant increase in both the frequency and energy of microseismic events.

Methods and materials

BP neural network

Deep learning has been implemented for early warning of high-energy microseismic events, with extensive research conducted in the fields of tunneling and mining, which has led to high efficiency and accuracy in event prediction. Comparing the common network architectures in deep learning is crucial for selecting an appropriate network model for predicting high-energy microseismic events. Considering the advantages and applicability of neural networks, recurrent neural networks (RNN) are suitable for processing sequential and time series data, convolutional neural networks (CNN) are effective for handling image and visual data, and deep neural networks (DNN) can manage large datasets and highly complex tasks, such as natural language processing and computer vision24–26. Compared to the aforementioned models, BPNN offers high scalability and good generalization ability, demonstrating strong performance in tasks such as classification, regression, and prediction with small sample sizes. This study focuses on the prediction of high-energy microseismic events based on feature parameters, where RNN and CNN cannot leverage their advantages, and DNN faces issues related to insufficient samples. In summary, BPNN can be used to construct a small sample network classification model to predict high-energy microseismic events while avoiding the risk of overfitting.

Artificial neural networks are intelligent information processing systems that emulate the behavior and characteristics of biological neural networks. They process and transmit information to handle tasks in a manner similar to that of the human brain. They possess self-learning, self-adaptation, and self-organization capabilities, with their information processing functions facilitated by the advanced data processing capabilities of computers. Artificial neural networks consist of numerous interconnected information processing units (neurons), and the way in which these neurons are connected is known as the network’s topology. These units can process and transmit information, and they learn based on specific criteria. These units can process and transmit information, learning according to specific criteria. Once they reach equilibrium, the distribution state of the entire network is determined by the weights of the individual units, which represent the final results obtained. The learning process in a neural network system is essentially the process of adjusting the neurons. The backpropagation (BP) algorithm is a commonly used optimization method in neural network training. It primarily involves training artificial neural networks using a large set of samples to adjust the weights and minimize errors. The fundamental idea of the backpropagation (BP) algorithm is to compute the gradients of the loss function with respect to all weights and biases in the neural network. These gradient values are then used to adjust the weights and biases, thereby establishing the most suitable nonlinear relationship between the input and output variables. Figure 10 is a schematic diagram of the topological structure of the BPNN.Fig. 10Schematic diagram of topology.

Schematic diagram of topology.

A backpropagation neural network consists of three main components: the input layer, hidden layers, and output layer. The data to be processed is received by the input layer, handled and transformed by the hidden layers, and the output layer outputs the final result. The amount of hidden layers can be adjusted according to the requirements. Each layer in the network is fully connected through neurons, with each neuron having an activation function. The activation function maps the neuron’s input to a new range, allowing the neural network’s output to be more diverse and flexible. The neuron’s activation values propagate from the input layer through the hidden layers to the output layer. The error between the desired and actual outputs is calculated. This error is then used to adjust the connection weights layer by layer from the output layer back through the hidden layers, eventually reaching the input layer. By continuously adjusting the weights and biases between neurons, the network improves its ability to accurately reflect the relationship between input data and the target.

Selection of characteristic parameters

The feature parameters are selected based on factors analyzed in relation to the predicted values (microseismic events)27–29. The chosen features include advancing position, mining speed, sinking speed, and horizontal displacement (ΔU)30. “Single event energy” serves as a widely used indicator for microseismic warnings in mining. By classifying microseismic energy levels based on the impact of seismic hazards at the site, more targeted predictive research can be conducted.

Based on the microseismic warning indicators, microseismic events are categorized as follows: Events with energy less than 103 J are classified as low-energy events (Label 001). Events with energy between 103 and 104 J are classified as medium-energy events (Label 010). Events with energy between 104 and 105 J are classified as high-energy events (Label 100). These categories are used as output targets for the BPNN model.

Model establishment

Data segmentation and training

First, the microseismic event levels are designated as the output target (i.e., classification attribute), while horizontal displacement (ΔU), sinking speed, mining speed, and advancing position are utilized as input variables. We construct a BPNN model based on this indicator system to identify the characteristics and probabilities of microseismic events under different conditions. To train the BPNN model, the extracted sample data needs to be split and “shuffled”. One part is used as the training set, while the other part is reserved as the test set, with a 7:3 ratio between the training and test sets. From the specific data on microseismic events in Table 1, 62 sets of microseismic event data were randomly selected as the training set to build the BPNN classification and prediction model, while the remaining data were used as the test set. Each cell contains a positive integer value representing the number of samples for that particular classification prediction combination, while the percentage indicates the proportion of these samples relative to the total number of samples. The green cells on the diagonal represent correct predictions for the microseismic event levels, while the pink cells indicate incorrect predictions. However, it incorrectly classified 1 events as low-energy when they were actually medium-energy events. Limitation

Although the BPNN prediction model for high-energy microseismic event levels based on ground subsidence data can produce reliable prediction results, the methods outlined possess certain limitations that require addressing in future work to achieve more effective outcomes.Differences in the feature parameter dataset. The predictive performance of various machine learning algorithms is greatly influenced by the quantity and quality of the input dataset. When the dataset is relatively limited, the model’s generalization and reliability often decrease. In most cases, when using datasets with different sizes or different feature parameters, the model’s training effectiveness and predictive ability are affected differently. Although BPNN demonstrates effective performance when applied to smaller datasets, the predictive results can still be enhanced by increasing the number of feature parameters in the dataset. Generally, comprehensive and large databases contain more valid data, thereby improving the model’s generalization ability. Therefore, it is crucial to construct a rich multi-feature parameter database of ground subsidence and microseismic events.The results of the predictive model may be influenced by external factors. Although this study utilizes four variables that can predict high-energy microseismic events to a certain extent, this does not imply that other factors do not affect the prediction of high-energy microseismic events. It is essential to consider all possible external factors that may influence microseismic events, such as geological conditions, mining depth, and mining methods. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate the impact of other significant features on the prediction results.

A correlation exists between surface subsidence in mining areas and energy events. As the working face advances, abrupt changes in maximum subsidence values are frequently associated with high-energy events. The results from microseismic monitoring closely align with subsidence observations, reflecting similar patterns in rock layer movement38. This correlation arises from the accumulation of energy in the overlying strata as the working face progresses, causing minor movements and deformation damage in the rock layers. When the accumulated energy reaches a critical threshold, it is released suddenly. This release amplifies the movement and deformation damage of the rock layers. As this process extends to the surface, it results in a marked increase in surface subsidence. Conclusions

The frequent occurrence of high-energy microseismic events in coal mines restricts the safe and efficient extraction of coal resources, making the accurate prediction of these events essential. This paper constructs a Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN) prediction model, selecting advancing position, sinking speed, horizontal displacement, and mining velocity as input feature parameters, with the energy level of microseismic events as the output. A total of 88 microseismic events were collected, with 62 used for model construction and the remaining dataset for testing. Finally, various model evaluation metrics were employed to demonstrate the model’s predictive capability for high-energy microseismic events. Based on the research findings, the three key points can be summarized:A correlation analysis was conducted between surface subsidence spatial data and microseismic events at a mine in Inner Mongolia. The analysis summarized that as surface horizontal displacement, sinking speed, and mining speed increase,both the total energy and frequency of microseismic events also increase.Four influencing factors—surface horizontal displacement (ΔU), sinking speed, mining speed, and advancing position—were selected as feature parameters to construct a BPNN model for predicting microseismic event levels. A total of 88 microseismic data instances were extracted for statistical analysis. The sample data were normalized and outliers were removed to create a sample database. The model was trained using the training samples, resulting in an overall prediction accuracy of 86.4% and anroot mean square error (RMSE) of 0.45.The prediction model can forecast the level of microseismic events based on input variables, allowing for preemptive measures to prevent high-energy events from triggering hazards such as rock bursts. In the future, the model should further increase the number of feature parameters and the volume of field data for predictions, while conducting a comprehensive analysis based on additional influencing factors from field cases. When undertaking other projects, the model proposed in this study should serve as a foundation, providing opportunities for retraining, analysis, evaluation, and revision. It is also recommended to utilize a sufficiently large dataset to address the aforementioned limitations.
