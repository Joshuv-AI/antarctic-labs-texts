CdmR, a MarR family protein from Clostridioides difficile, binds with positive cooperativity in the predicted promoter region of a MATE efflux transporter
Authors: Jacob Canepa, Ruben Garcia-Ortiz, Kara Thompson, Julia Glidden, Bhumika Panchal, Rei Takahashi, Da Eun Kim, Steven P. Wilkinson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clostridioides difficile is one of the most common causes of nosocomial infections worldwide and is responsible for a spectrum of conditions ranging from colitis to severe cases of sepsis, and even death. Of significant concern is the evolving antibiotic resistance of this pathogen, notably the recent emergence of strains with resistance to vancomycin and fidaxomicin, the first-line antibiotics currently used to treat C. difficile infections. Numerous members of the multiple antibiotic resistance regulator (MarR) protein family have been shown to play important roles in mediating drug-resistance in bacterial species, however very little is understood about their functions in C. difficile. Of the 14 MarR proteins predicted to be encoded in the C. difficile genome, only one has been functionally characterized to date, so the roles these proteins play in C. difficile pathogenicity are largely unknown. Here, we report the first biochemical study of the MarR homolog, CdmR, from C. difficile. CdmR binds with high sequence-specificity and high affinity at two DNA sites in the promoter region of an operon that encodes a predicted multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) family efflux transporter. The location of these sites strongly suggests that CdmR binding represses its own expression and that of the MATE gene. CdmR exhibits positive binding cooperativity at the two DNA sites, suggesting a highly sensitive and tightly controlled regulatory mechanism that may play an important role in the antibiotic resistance profile of C. difficile.

Introduction

Clostridioides difficile is an anaerobic, endospore-forming pathogen that inhabits the digestive tracts of humans and other animals. C. difficile infections (CDIs) are characterized by clinical symptoms of diarrhea and colitis that range in severity, with some cases resulting in death [1]. CDIs are one of the most common nosocomial infections worldwide, with estimates of approximately half a million infections and 15,000–29,000 deaths each year in the United States, alone [2]. Of particular concern is the evolving resistance of this pathogen to various antibiotics, notably the recent identification of strains resistant to metronidazole, rifaximin, vancomycin, and fidaxomicin (currently, the most used antibiotics to treat CDI in humans) [3].

C. difficile has evolved diverse mechanisms of antibiotic resistance, including drug alteration, modification of the antibiotic target, and extrusion of drugs via efflux pumps [3]. Implicit in these mechanisms is the ability of the organism to detect and respond to antimicrobial compounds. Members of the multiple antibiotic resistance regulator (MarR) family of proteins are especially well-suited to function as environmental sensors to regulate gene expression in response to drugs or other agents [4]. MarR family proteins are ubiquitous in bacterial genomes where they regulate a wide variety of processes including virulence factor expression, oxidative stress response mechanisms, and the degradation or export of toxic compounds [4]. The majority of characterized MarR proteins function as transcriptional repressors by binding at DNA sites that overlap promoter elements, thus competing with RNA polymerase for promoter occupancy. Proteins in this family commonly bind to specific, small molecule ligands or monatomic ions, which modulate DNA-binding affinity and therefore alter gene expression [e.g 5]. A significant number of MarR proteins regulate the transcription of genes important for resistance to antimicrobials, including the expression of several different families of efflux pumps [5].

The C. difficile 630 genome encodes 14 predicted MarR proteins, of which only one (HsmR, which senses host-derived heme) has been functionally characterized [6]. In addition, the structures of two other C. difficile MarR proteins have been published, corresponding to PDB code 5ERI [7] for which no functional data has been reported, and PDB code 8XTA (MarR_CD0473) [8]. MarR_CD0473 was shown to bind at two locations in an intergenic region of the C. difficile genome that suggests a possible role in regulating a β-lactam sensor and signal transducer, hinting at a possible role in mediating resistance to β-lactam antibiotics

In this study, we provide the first biochemical analysis of another C. difficile MarR family protein which we have named CdmR (Clostridioides difficile MarR; gene locus CD630-RS17080; NCBI reference sequence WP_021403412.1). We demonstrate that CdmR binds with high sequence-specificity and high affinity at two sites in the promoter region of an operon that encodes a predicted multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) family efflux transporter. CdmR dimers bind with positive cooperativity at these sites, suggesting a highly responsive and tightly controlled regulatory system. These findings point towards a possible role of CdmR in the antibiotic resistance profile of C. difficile, and advocate for additional research to better understand CdmR's regulatory functions in this pathogen.

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

The codon-optimized cdmR gene from C. difficile (gene locus CD630-RS17080; NCBI reference sequence WP_021403412.1) was chemically synthesized (GenScript) and cloned into the pET28a(+) vector which introduced an amino-terminal 6xHis-tag. The vector was transformed into competent E. coli BL21(DE3) by heat-shock. Cells were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) media with 50 μg/mL kanamycin at 37 °C. Protein expression was induced at OD600 0.7 with 1 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside and cells were grown for an additional 2 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and pellets resuspended in Buffer A (50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, 5 % v/v glycerol, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME), and 0.15 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF)). BugBuster and Benzonase (Millipore-Sigma) were added to 1x and 37.5 U/mL final concentrations, respectively, and the suspension was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, followed by brief sonication. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation, and the supernatant was incubated with HIS-Select Nickel Affinity Gel resin (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C for batch binding. The resin was subsequently packed into a column. Proteins were separated by stepwise elution in Buffer A with imidazole concentrations from 10 to 250 mM. The purest fractions were pooled, concentrated using an Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter (Millipore-Sigma), and dialyzed into protein storage solution (50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, 10 % v/v glycerol, 0.1 mM Na2EDTA, 12.5 mM BME, and 0.1 mM PMSF). Purified protein samples were flash frozen in an ethanol-dry ice bath and stored at −80 °C. CdmR concentration was determined by absorbance measurements at 280 nm using the calculated extinction coefficient (21,430 M−1 cm−1 for the monomer).

Differential scanning fluorimetry

Thermal denaturation studies were carried out in a StepOne Real-Time PCR System equipped with a 48-well block (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each sample contained 3 μg protein in DSF buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM BME, and a 1x concentration of Protein Thermal Shift Dye (Applied Biosystems, USA)) at a final volume of 20 μL. Data were fit to a Boltzmann function assuming a two-state protein unfolding transition according to the procedure described by Ref. [9] to determine protein Tm, ΔGu°, ΔHu°, and ΔSu°. Values are reported as the mean ± standard deviation for 3 experimental replicates.

Analytical size exclusion chromatography

Analytical size exclusion chromatography was performed using an ENrich SEC 70 10x300 column (Bio-Rad) in SEC Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT, 5 % v/v glycerol, 0.2 % v/v Tween-20). The average distribution coefficient (Kav) was calculated for CdmR and the protein standards using the following equation: Kav = (Ve – Vo)/(Vc – Vo) where Ve is the elution volume, Vc is the column geometric bed volume, and Vo is the void volume. Vo was experimentally measured as the Ve of blue dextran. The plot of protein standard Kav vs. Log10(molecular mass) was fit by linear regression to generate a standard curve, which was used to determine the molecular mass of CdmR.

Glutaraldehyde crosslinking

The assay was performed by incubating 1.5 μg of CdmR in crosslinking buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, 1 % v/v glycerol) at 25 °C for 20 min in the absence or presence of glutaraldehyde (GA) at concentrations ranging from 0.001 to 0.1 % v/v. Each 5 μL reaction was terminated by adding 2.5 μL of 4X Laemmli buffer without BME. Samples were heated at 80 °C for 10 min and subsequently run on a 17 % w/v SDS-PAGE gel. Protein bands were visualized by staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250.

DNA binding assays

DNA binding was measured by electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) using 6.5 % w/v native polyacrylamide gels (39:1 w/w acrylamide:bisacrylamide) in 0.5x Tris/borate/EDTA buffer (45 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.3), 45 mM borate, and 1.25 mM Na2EDTA). Gels were prerun for 30 min at 10 V/cm at room temperature before loading samples. DNA probes ≤ 60 bp were generated by annealing a 5′-Cy5-labeled strand with its unlabeled complement (Integrated DNA Technologies). Binding reactions between CdmR and labeled dsDNA probes were conducted in binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 50 μg/mL BSA, 5 % v/v glycerol, 0.075 % v/v Tween-20, and 0.75 mM BME). Reactions were incubated at room temperature for 30 min and then loaded onto the gel with the power on. Gels were run for 35 min at room temperature and then imaged on a ChemiDoc MP (Bio-Rad). Free DNA and protein-DNA complexes were quantified using ImageLab software (Bio-Rad).

Competition assays to determine binding specificity were performed with variable concentrations of unlabeled pUC57 or cdmO-40-site1 incubated with 0.5 nM Cy5-labeled cdmO-40-site1 and 1.3 nM CdmR dimer. Fractional saturation was measured as the fraction of bound labeled cdmO-40-site1. In single site binding assays to determine Kd, 0.5 nM Cy5-labeled DNA was titrated with CdmR. Because the measured Kd values were similar to the DNA concentration used in these assays, data were fit to the quadratic single site binding equation [10]. Experiments were conducted in triplicate or quadruplicate and Kd values reported as the mean ± standard deviation. EMSAs to determine binding stoichiometry used 150 nM Cy5-labeled cdmO-40-site1. Fractional complex formation (mean ± standard deviation of three data sets) was plotted against [CdmR]/[cdmO-40-site1]. Stoichiometry was extrapolated algebraically from the intersection of the tangents generated from the data points in the upward slope and plateau of the plot. The multisite binding experiments were conducted with a 259 bp Cy5-labeled dsDNA probe (cdmO-259), generated by PCR, that spans positions +84 to −175 relative to the cdmR start codon. These data were fit to the Hill equation: θ=[P]n/(K+[P]n). Here, θ represents the fraction of DNA in complex, [P] is the total protein concentration, n is the Hill coefficient, and K is a constant that reflects half-maximal saturation. Experiments were conducted in triplicate with n reported as the mean ± standard deviation.

Results and discussion

Genomic locus of cdmR

Gene locus CD630-RS17080 (NCBI reference sequence WP_021403412.1) from Clostridioides (formerly Clostridium) difficile strain 630 encodes a predicted MarR family transcriptional regulator which we have named CdmR (C. difficile MarR). The cdmR gene is separated by 16 bp from (and expected to be co-transcribed with) the non-divergent gene CD630-RS17075 which is annotated as a multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) family efflux transporter (Fig. 1A). Biophysical properties of CdmR

The cdmR gene from C. difficile was overexpressed in E. coli and purified to greater than 95 % homogeneity (Fig. S1). Thermal unfolding analysis of CdmR by differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) indicated a melting temperature (Tm) of 59.3 ± 0.1 °C (Fig. 2A). Thermodynamic parameters of CdmR unfolding were determined by van't Hoff analysis of the DSF data, yielding a ΔGu° (at 298.15 K) of 71.3 ± 3.3 kJ/mol, a van't Hoff enthalpy (ΔHu°) of 690 ± kJ/mol, and a van't Hoff entropy (ΔSu°) of 2.07 ± 0.09 kJ/(mol.K). Analytical size exclusion chromatography (SEC) of CdmR under reducing conditions resulted in a single observable species corresponding to a molecular mass of 43.6 kDa (Fig. 2B and C). Given the predicted molecular mass of 20.0 kDa for the CdmR monomer, these data indicate that native CdmR exists predominantly as a homodimer under these solution conditions. The oligomeric state of CdmR in the absence of a reducing agent could not be determined by analytical SEC due to apparent protein aggregation at the high protein concentration required for the assay (data not shown). Chemical crosslinking of CdmR with glutaraldehyde under non-reducing conditions at a relatively low protein concentration revealed a predominant species of approximately 40 kDa, suggesting that CdmR also forms a homodimer in an oxidizing environment in vitro (Fig. 2D).

CdmR binds as a homodimer in its promoter/operator region

Given that many MarR family proteins regulate their own transcription [12], we searched the cdmR promoter/operator region for potential DNA binding sites. A single CdmR-DNA complex was observed in an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) with a 60 bp DNA probe (cdmO-60, Fig. S2) spanning positions +10 to −50 relative to the cdmR start codon, suggesting the presence of a single binding site in this sequence Fig. S3). The measured dissociation constant (Kd) for the CdmR-cdmO-60 interaction was 1.55 ± 0.28 nM.

Inspection of cdmO-60 revealed a 12 bp palindromic sequence located near the upstream end of the probe (Fig. S2). As many MarR DNA binding sites are characterized by palindromic or pseudo-palindromic sequences [4], we suspected that this site may function as a CdmR binding locus. EMSAs with a 40 bp probe (cdmO-40-site1, Fig. S2) centered on the palindrome revealed a single binding complex (Fig. 3A), suggesting a single CdmR binding location in this DNA sequence. The measured binding affinity for the CdmR-cdmO-40-site1 interaction was very high, with a Kd = 0.17 ± 0.04 nM (Fig. 3B). The sequence specificity of the CdmR-DNA binding interaction was measured by EMSAs in which variable concentrations of unlabeled competitor DNA were added to reactions with constant concentrations of CdmR and labeled crmO-40-site1 DNA (Fig. 3C and Fig. S4). Unlabeled cdmO-40-site1 was a very effective competitor for CdmR binding to the labeled probe, with 5 nM unlabeled cdmO-40-site1 decreasing complex formation by approximately 50 %. In contrast, 5 nM unlabeled pUC57 DNA, which has a 68-fold greater number of base pairs relative to cdmO-40-site1 but does not contain the Site 1 sequence, reduced complex formation by only approximately 8 %. These data confirmed that the CdmR-DNA binding interaction is highly sequence specific. We next addressed the question of whether the oligomeric state of CdmR when bound to Site 1 differs from the homodimeric state that was observed in the absence of DNA. EMSAs performed under stoichiometric conditions revealed that CdmR binds cdmO-40-site1 at a measured ratio of 2.2:1 indicating that CdmR binds as a homodimer to this DNA site (Fig. 3D). These data indicate that CdmR fits the prevailing view that most MarR proteins exists as homodimers in their DNA-bound and unbound states; however, recent reports on the MarR homolog, ScoC, have demonstrated that this property is not universally true, as this protein binds DNA as a tetramer [13], and other MarR homologs have been shown to exist as tetramers under oxidizing conditions.

To further investigate the DNA sequence requirements for CdmR binding, we conducted EMSAs to measure CdmR binding to DNA probes with mutations in, or adjacent to, the 12 bp palindromic sequence in Site 1. Base pair substitutions in three positions of each palindromic half-site (probe cdmO-40-site1-mut1, Fig. S2) abolished all observable CdmR-DNA binding (Fig. 3B, Fig. S5) confirming the requirement of the palindromic DNA sequence in Site 1 for the binding interaction. The DNA binding sites of many MarR proteins, including CdmR Site 1 (Fig. S2), are characterized by AT-rich regions immediately flanking their cognate palindromic sequences [e.g. 13]. Mutation of three A:T base pairs to G:C base pairs on either side of the palindrome (cdmO-40-site1-mut2, Fig. S2) reduced CdmR binding affinity approximately 5-fold relative to binding to the unmutated Site 1 (Kd = 0.81 ± 0.07 nM versus 0.17 ± 0.04 nM, respectively) (Fig. 3B, Fig. S5).Taken together, these data indicate that CdmR binding Site 1 is comprised of a palindromic sequence that is essential for CdmR binding, and AT-rich regions on either side of the palindrome that contribute to DNA binding affinity to a lesser extent. These biochemical data are consistent with structural data for other MarR proteins in complex with their DNA sites which demonstrate base-specific contacts between the MarR DNA recognition helices (Fig. 1B) and bases in the DNA major groove in each (pseudo)palindromic half-site, and base-specific contacts between amino acids in each MarR “wing” (two antiparallel β-strands connected by a loop) with DNA sequences on either side of the palindrome [14].

CdmR binds with positive cooperativity at multiple sites in its promoter/operator region

To determine if additional CdmR binding sites are in the cdmR promoter/operator region, we PCR amplified overlapping segments of the intergenic region between cdmR and padR for use in EMSAs. EMSAs with a 259 bp probe (cdmO-259) spanning positions +84 to −175 relative to the cdmR start codon revealed two discrete CdmR-DNA complexes suggesting the presence of two CdmR binding sites within this DNA region (Fig. 4A). Inspection of the 259 bp sequence did not reveal a second palindromic sequence identical to that in Site 1. However, an imperfect palindrome was identified immediately upstream of Site 1, comprised of half sites identical to those of Site 1 in four of the five base pair positions. We generated a 32 bp DNA probe (cdmO-32-site2, Fig. S2) centered on this pseudo-palindromic sequence. It is worth noting that this 32 bp probe contained only a portion of one Site 1 half site, which would not be sufficient for CdmR binding. EMSAs revealed a single CdmR binding complex with cdmO-32-site2 (Fig. 4C) leading us to identify the pseudo-palindromic sequence as a second CdmR binding site (Site 2). The measured binding affinity of CdmR for Site 2 (Fig. 4D) was slightly lower than that for Site 1, with a Kd = 0.41 ± 0.05 nM (compared to 0.17 ± 0.04 nM for Site 1).

Interestingly, the locations of CdmR binding Sites 1 and 2 are very close, with only 20 bp separating their dyadic centers, corresponding to approximately 65 Å (assuming linear, B-form DNA). Therefore, two CdmR homodimers bound at DNA Sites 1 and 2 would be expected to be in very close proximity to one another. Based on a CdmR homology model (Fig. 1B), the distance from the center of a homodimer to the edge of one of its wings is approximately 30 Å. Therefore, the distance separating the centers of two CdmR dimers placed immediately adjacent to one another on the same face of a DNA double helix would be approximately 60 Å, a distance that is very close to the 65 Å distance between the dyadic centers of DNA Sites 1 and 2. This proximity suggests possible mechanisms for the positive DNA binding cooperativity demonstrated in this work (Fig. 4A and B). One possibility is that cooperativity is mediated by direct protein-protein contacts between adjacent homodimers. A second possibility (not mutually exclusive with the first) is that CdmR binding at one DNA site distorts the DNA in a manner that increases the binding affinity at the second DNA site, as has been suggested for the MarR homolog, MepR [14].

Computational search for additional DNA binding sites

The bioinformatics tool, fuzznuc (EMBOSS), was used to search for other potential DNA binding sites within the C. difficile strain 630 genome. The search pattern, WWWTTGCNNNNGCAAWWW (where TTGC and GCAA represent the nucleotides conserved between the experimentally determined Sites 1 and 2, N = any nucleotide, and W = A or T), identified 9 locations (excluding Sites 1 and 2) within the genome with zero nucleotide mismatches. Eight of these sequences are located near the middle of gene coding regions, and one sequence (corresponding to positions 187,326 through 187,343 in the genome) is located approximately 23 nucleotides upstream of the start codon of a gene encoding a predicted copper homeostasis protein (CutC). Using a search pattern with one fewer A/T position at each end resulted in 31 additional hits; however, all of these were located near the middle of gene coding regions, and therefore less likely to be involved in regulating gene expression. Whether CdmR binds at the sequence upstream of the gene for CutC, and the significance of such an interaction, remains to be determined. However, it is worth noting that other MarR homologs, most notably E. coli MarR [15], have been shown to be copper-sensors involved in stress-response mechanisms, thus making this a particularly interesting line of future inquiry. It is also worth noting that the degree of degeneracy in the DNA binding sequence of CdmR is unknown. It is therefore possible that additional binding sites not identified in our search exist within the genome; future studies will be aimed at determining if any such sites exist.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that the MarR protein, CdmR, from C. difficile binds at two DNA sites within its promoter/operator region, suggesting that this protein regulates its own transcription, and likely that of an adjacent gene encoding a MATE efflux transporter. The positive cooperativity of the CdmR-DNA binding interactions at these DNA sites suggests that transcription of this operon is tightly controlled in C. difficile, with expression being highly sensitive to CdmR concentrations and/or CdmR functionality. The mechanism underlying the binding cooperativity at Sites 1 and 2 is an unresolved question and an area warranting further investigation. Several other MarR homologs have been shown to bind with positive cooperativity at closely spaced DNA sites in promoter/operator regions, notably Vibrio vulnificus PecS [16], Streptomyces coelicolor TamR [17], Pectobacterium atrosepticum PecS [18], and Agrobacterium tumefaciens PecS [19]. These MarR proteins have been shown to regulate the expression of virulence or stress response genes, and it is likely that their cooperative binding in promoter/operator regions enables the organism to respond rapidly to changing environmental conditions. As the DNA binding affinities of many MarR proteins, including the cooperatively-binding homologs mentioned above, are altered by the binding of specific effector molecules, we plan to follow this report with studies to identify the natural ligand(s) of CdmR.

The likely regulation by CdmR of the expression of a MATE transporter suggests an intriguing possibility that CdmR plays a role in the antibiotic resistance profile of C. difficile. MATE proteins are secondary active transporters that function to excrete xenobiotics, including diverse drugs, in numerous microbial pathogens, thereby conferring antibiotic-resistance [20]. The C. difficile 630 genome is predicted to encode 15 MATE proteins [21], only one of which has been functionally characterized to our knowledge (CdeA, an efflux transporter of ethidium bromide and acriflavine, [22]). The MATE transporter gene (CD630-RS17075, formerly CD630-32070) adjacent to cdmR is predicted to encode a MepA-like efflux pump belonging to the DNA damage-inducible protein F (DinF) subfamily. Importantly, the expression level CD630-RS17075 was shown to be upregulated in response to eight different antibiotics, most notably fidaxomicin and vancomycin which are the primary antibiotics used for CDI treatment [21]. Taken together, this report on the DNA binding properties of CdmR suggests that it tightly controls an operon containing a MATE efflux transporter and argues for the importance of additional work to elucidate its functionality and its possible role in the antibiotic resistance and pathogenicity of C. difficile.
