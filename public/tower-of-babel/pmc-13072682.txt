The registration status of maternal, newborn, and child health medical products: evidence from 9 countries
Authors: Jane Briggs, Kate Kikule, Helena Walkowiak, Javier Guzman, Martha Embrey, Helen Petach, Jeremie Fikiri, John Patrick Mwesigye, Abul Kalam Azad, Juliet Sanyu Namugambe, Raphael Shedafa, Birna Trap, Abdoul Karim Goita, Eliangiringa Kaale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRegistration challenges in resource-limited countries can compromise the availability of life-saving maternal, newborn, and child health (MNCH) medical products – quality-assured generic products may not be registered or renewed.MethodsTo identify barriers, we assessed the registration status of MNCH tracer medicines in 9 countries through document review and interviewed country regulators and 11 pharmaceutical manufacturers using structured questionnaires.ResultsOn average, 72% of the tracer medicines had at least one registered product across the 9 countries (ranging from 28% to 100%). Some countries only had one or two registered products for some MNCH tracer medicines. Five countries had not registered any of the four World Health Organization-prequalified products. The average registration timeline ranged from six months to four years due to national regulatory authority backlogs and complicated procedures. Only two countries officially recognised regulatory decisions from other countries’ Good Manufacturing Practices inspections. No country had formal processes embedded in legislative provisions to prioritise and expedite registration of essential MNCH medical products.DiscussionSolutions to registration impediments involve legal, organisational, and procedural changes. MNCH medicines have specific challenges including low profit margins due to the ease of production and variety of manufacturers; additionally, an inconvenient registration process is a disincentive for manufacturers. Likewise, procurers who are more price than quality conscious may not procure costlier prequalified medicines. Registration catalysts include streamlining the process with electronic management information systems providing market information, maintaining qualified staffing, and emphasising reliance on and recognition of other regulatory authority decisions.ConclusionSome life-saving MNCH products are not available because national registration processes are flawed. Consequently, governments and donors must invest in changes to streamline procedures to register vital medicines to protect the health of women and children. To maximise health outcomes, countries should prioritise the registration of products of public health importance, including MNCH medicines.KEYWORDS: Maternal, newborn and child health; pharmaceutical registration; prequalification; regulatory reliance; national regulatory authority; low- and middle-income countries

Background

National regulatory authorities (NRAs) perform the fundamental role of ensuring the efficacy, safety, and quality of medical products that circulate in national markets (World Health Organization, 2014). A key function of the NRA is to provide marketing authorisation, or registration, for a medical product after evaluating data from the manufacturer.1 This quality assurance process is specific to the manufacturer, product specifications, and the manufacturing location.

In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), challenges with the registration process can limit the availability of life-saving, quality-assured maternal, newborn, and child health (MNCH) medical products in several different ways (Briggs et al., 2018; IQVIA, 2025). For example, the market entry of innovative products may be slow (Access to Medicine Foundation, 2024), and quality-assured generic products, including those prequalified by the World Health Organization (WHO), may not be registered or have their registration renewed, potentially leaving a void that may be filled by substandard or falsified products, especially in countries with weak regulatory enforcement. As an example, an analysis of antimicrobial registration in 102 LMICs showed that 39% of the generic medicines supplied by multinational pharmaceutical companies were registered in more than 10 countries with that number dropping to 29% of products from generic medicine producers; in addition, manufacturers rarely registered Reserve antimicrobials on WHO’s AWaRe (Access, Watch, Reserve) list (World Health Organization, 2025) due to the lower potential sales (Access to Medicine Foundation, 2021).

Addressing barriers to access, including registration, is required to create healthier, more resilient communities (IQVIA, 2025), and findings from this multi-country study lead to important conclusions on how to increase the availability and accessibility of life-saving MNCH products. Although the findings are not generalisable to all LMICs, they do illustrate the importance of addressing registration as a part of overall regulatory system strengthening efforts.

Methods

This paper’s objective is to present empirical evidence on the registration status of 18 essential MNCH tracer medicines (Table 1) and to identify factors affecting registration. Our 18 tracer medicines are available as generic essential medicines on the global market, and a WHO-prequalified generic product exists for four of them (oxytocin 10IU/ml injection, misoprostol 200mcg tablets, magnesium sulphate 500 mg/ml injection, and zinc sulphate 20 mg dispersible tablets). The list was adapted from the United Nations Commission on Life-Saving Commodities for Women and Children’s list of 13 commodities (UN, 2012). We chose to focus on MNCH essential medicines because children and women in LMICs have higher mortality related to infectious disease and reproductive health (Patwardhan et al., 2024); in addition, a landmark study by the Bellagio Child Survival Study Group showed that 63% of child deaths were avoidable through available interventions including treatment with essential medicines (Jones et al., 2003).

As a result of our findings and analysis, we present options for governments to consider and to help orient donor investment and partner support to improve access to MNCH medical products.

We analyzed data from nine countries: Bangladesh, Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Mali, Mozambique, Nepal, Rwanda, Senegal, Tanzania, and Uganda. The countries were purposively selected from the countries where the US Agency for International Development Medicines, Technologies, and Pharmaceutical Services Program provided pharmaceutical systems strengthening support. To increase the sample’s heterogeneity, Asian and African countries with varied market size were included. All the countries had a growing local pharmaceutical manufacturing industry and depended on donor support to strengthen the pharmaceutical sector and regulatory body. The countries’ regulatory maturity as measured by the WHO Global Benchmarking Tool (GBT) varied from low (1) to acceptable (3) (World Health Organization, 2024), and this range in maturity also promoted higher applicability of the findings to other LMICs.

Desk reviews included each country’s policies, laws, regulations, guidelines, procedures, and reports related to the registration of medicines and lists of registered products. The study relied on documentation including registration status; however, information accuracy and availability relied on the NRAs’ structure and capacity, so therefore varied by country. In addition, we conducted key informant interviews with regulators from each country and with 11 pharmaceutical manufacturers of MNCH medicines based in Africa, Asia, and Europe using a structured questionnaire to ascertain the registration process and fee structure, determine the registration status of the 18 essential tracer medicines, and identify important challenges that regulators and manufacturers face in the registration process.2 We promoted consistency by using the same piloted questionnaire and the same lead researcher in all nine countries. The researcher trained all national interviewers and analysed all responses. The key informants interviewed at the NRAs from the LMICs under study included the directors, heads of registration, and medicines assessors.

Registration status was confirmed according to the list the NRA maintained, as this is what stakeholders, including the public and manufacturers, can access – as noted, accuracy relied on each NRA’s recordkeeping practices. We triangulated the interview and desk review findings and generated summary statistics for key quantitative measures. The tool developed and used to conduct key informant interviews on the registration process aligns with international requirements as specified in the WHO GBT. The study was intended to be a qualitative mapping to orient interventions to improve the registration process rather than a rigorous comparative study.

Results

Number of registered MNCH products

The percentage of tracer medicines with at least one registered product averaged 72% across the nine countries, ranging from 28% in Nepal and Senegal to 100% in Rwanda and Uganda (Figure 1). In Bangladesh, a large proportion of the tracer medicines registered were manufactured locally (78%). In the African countries, most medicines were imported, and a much smaller proportion of tracer medicines were manufactured locally – from 22% in Uganda to 0 in Rwanda, Mozambique, and Senegal (Figure 1). There was no association between high percentages and the number of tracer medicines with locally manufactured registered products given that countries with more registered products did not have high levels of local manufacturing.

The number of registered products varied by both type of medicine and country. More antibiotics are registered than other products (Table 2). Bangladesh has the highest number of registered products at 376, while Nepal has only 6 registered products. Some countries only have one or two registered products for several of the MNCH tracer medicines.

Only ceftriaxone 1 g injection was registered everywhere. Six countries had no registered injectable hydralazine 20 mg, four did not have magnesium sulphate 500 mg/ml injection registered, and two did not have oxytocin 10IU/ml injection registered. Notably, for newborn health, seven countries did not have a registered paediatric injectable formulation of gentamicin, and two lacked a paediatric ceftriaxone product. Three countries lacked a registered benzylpenicillin 600 mg injectable product, and five lacked a registered procaine benzylpenicillin 1 g injectable product (Nepal and Senegal lacked both). Amoxicillin 125 and 250 mg dispersible tablets – formulations that have been more recently recommended for LMIC markets – were each registered in six countries, and Nepal was the only country that had not registered amoxicillin 250 mg/5 ml suspension. Nepal, Mali, and Senegal did not have a registered product for zinc 20 mg tablets despite the existence of a WHO-prequalified alternative.

Medicines registration processes

The length of time to register a MNCH medicine, particularly in Africa, was a major concern for the manufacturers we interviewed, who indicated that registration times averaged about six months in Asian countries and up to four years in African countries. They indicated that delays occurred for many reasons, including backlogs of dossiers at the NRA, complicated or bureaucratic procedures, and NRA requirements for packaging or importing registration samples. From the information gathered from the NRAs, we determined that DRC, Rwanda, Senegal, Tanzania, and Uganda used standard procedures for assessing dossiers that are in line with WHO Good Review Practice guidelines (WHO, 2015). Procedures in Bangladesh, Nepal, Mali, and Mozambique did not align with the WHO guidance. NRAs also required that manufacturers work through a local agent, but the manufacturers interviewed spoke of challenges in finding a qualified, efficient local agent to help complete the registration and provide timely feedback. Additionally, a related study showed that manufacturers also contribute to delays by submitting insufficient data and being slow to respond to regulatory queries on their dossiers (Ahonkhai et al., 2016).

According to documents and NRA interviews, we determined that none of the countries had formal processes in place to prioritise and expedite the registration of essential MNCH medical products, which means that they processed the applications in the order that they were received, without considering the country’s epidemiologic profile or existing product availability. Although Mozambique, Rwanda, Senegal, and Uganda did have formal prioritisation procedures in place, they did not include MNCH products.

Fees charged for registration

All countries in our study collected fees for registration and other regulatory services, but in all except Uganda and Tanzania, the NRAs’ function still greatly relied on funding allocated by the state budget. The WHO GBT for evaluating national regulatory systems recommends that ‘the NRA has authority to manage the funds allocated and/or generated internally,' (World Health Organization, 2024) and the trend has generally been toward more financial autonomy (Ndomondo-Sigonda et al. 2020). Apart from Mozambique, Tanzania, and Uganda, all revenue generated from fees reverted to the general treasury; in Mozambique, the NRA retained 60% of the fees, and the rest reverted to the treasury. From the interview with the Senegalese NRA, we heard that although fees were earmarked for the NRA’s use, delays in reallocating the fee revenue back to the NRA have led to the deferral of meetings of its registration advisory and decision-making bodies because the NRA was unable to pay committee members their allowances. All NRAs also received technical and financial support from donors and/or development partners, and in some countries, most regulatory activities relied on donor funding.

Fees varied across countries and product origin, ranging from USD 27 in Nepal to USD 2,000 in Tanzania for imported medicines and from USD 3 in Nepal to USD 675 in DRC for locally manufactured products. Only Nepal halved its already low fee to import essential, life-saving, and emergency medicines, which presumably included MNCH medicines. The data indicated that the fees charged in Bangladesh, Mali, Mozambique, Nepal, and Senegal may be low relative to the income level of the country as indicated by gross domestic product (GDP) per capita (Figure 2).

Reliance on and recognition of other regulatory decisions

No country had a WHO-prequalified product registered for all four tracer medicines for which prequalified products are available. Five countries (Bangladesh, DRC, Nepal, Rwanda, and Senegal) had not registered any prequalified tracer products (Table 3).

In its prequalification process, WHO conducts manufacturer-specific assessments, which include dossier review, performance evaluation, and site inspections. Countries can use this assessment information to expedite the registration of these products. To facilitate the approval of prequalified medicines, WHO established a collaborative registration procedure (CRP) that enables NRAs to use the results of WHO assessments and inspections as part of the country’s registration process. Six countries who are CRP members used the WHO assessment results to expedite the registration of WHO-prequalified products (Figure 3), however, according to their NRAs, these countries did not have legal provisions to fully rely on the WHO decisions. Mali participated in the WHO CRP, but only for vaccines. Bangladesh and Nepal were not members of the WHO CRP, and Nepal had no formal reliance mechanism in place.

Status of reliance or recognition on regulatory decisions by country.

The document review showed that all countries required certification for Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) prior to product registration. Most of the nine countries conducted GMP inspections themselves, which may not be a cost-effective or efficient use of resources, and a few relied on GMP certification and inspections by other NRAs or on the WHO-prequalification program. Only Mali and Senegal officially recognised and relied fully on the GMP certificate from the country of manufacture. DRC, Mozambique, Nepal, and Tanzania considered but did not fully recognise WHO inspection reports or GMP inspection certificates issued by reference members within regional bodies (e.g. South African Development Community, East African Community), meaning that the NRAs themselves reviewed the manufacturing facility’s GMP status to determine compliance (Figure 3).

Manufacturers interviewed identified benefits of WHO-prequalification in some countries as expedited registration, exemption of GMP inspection, and perhaps a competitive advantage in procurements where quality assurance requirements are enforced. Their major challenge was that often buyers prioritised price rather than quality, and therefore, procurement agencies did not always recognise the value of WHO-prequalification (particularly since non-prequalified products can be two to three times less expensive). Also, as mentioned above, some regulators did not expedite the registration of prequalified products.

NRA structure

Although the nine NRAs differed in terms of their organisation, autonomy, staffing levels, and the number of marketing authorisation applications processed each year (Table 4), the document review illustrated a few common issues: most were dependent on their health ministries for funding and oversight, and all were inadequately staffed and funded. The Ugandan NRA had full administrative, financial, and technical autonomy, while the Rwanda and Tanzania NRAs were semi-autonomous. The other six entities (circled in Table 4) depended on their health ministries and relied on funding from the state government; however, many LMICs, including DRC, Mozambique, and Nepal were moving toward establishing an autonomous medical products regulatory body. Although the nine countries mandated that medicines be registered before they enter the market, and their NRAs had the legal provisions to do so, all NRAs reported that they lacked enough competent staff and funding, even as their regulatory responsibilities expand to include a more complex range of medical products. The number of staff members assigned to registration activities varied widely, as did the number of applications received per year. While not all assigned personnel represented skilled assessors, the ratio of application to staff suggested that Bangladesh and Mali (circled in Table 4) may be particularly under-resourced.

Discussion

Registration of essential medical products

Quality MNCH products have a major impact on the morbidity and mortality of women and children (UN, 2012). In our study, most of the MNCH tracer medicines were registered in the nine countries, but there were important disparities – Senegal, Mali, and Nepal had lower levels of registered medicines, and some tracer medicines were registered in fewer countries. Nepal’s low figures were primarily because 90% of their registrations had expired due to the country’s short (two-year) registration validity and their system’s lack of updated information. Compared with high numbers of antibiotics that likely had a higher profit margin, other products, for instance, magnesium sulphate 500 mg/ml, which is the most effective medicine for preventing and treating deadly seizures caused by pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, were either not registered or only had one or two products registered. Registration should clearly follow national treatment guidelines and countries that did not include some of these medicines in their standard treatment guidelines may have been using alternatives, for example, injectable ampicillin instead of penicillin or injectable labetalol instead of hydralazine. However, no suitable alternatives for magnesium sulphate and oxytocin exist for certain indications, and every country should register them. While paediatric formulations can be replaced by adult formulations, it makes dosing and administration more problematic.

Some experts recommend a minimum of one or two registered products to provide a sufficient profit margin for all participating manufacturers, particularly for medicines with limited indications. However, a minimum of at least three registered products is generally recommended to reduce vulnerability to shortages or stock outs if the manufacturer experiences production difficulties or removes the product from the market (World Health Organization, 2016). None of our survey countries prioritised registration applications for essential MNCH products, sometimes because they had no legal provisions to do so. With this omission, countries are missing the opportunity to ensure sufficient numbers of quality essential MNCH products on the market.

Complications in registering MNCH products

While other factors influence manufacturers’ decisions to market a product in a country, bottlenecks in the registration process such as those detailed here influence a manufacturer’s willingness to navigate the regulatory system (IQVIA, 2025); moreover, MNCH commodities have unique challenges that affect their registration status. Most notably, they often have a low profit margin due to the ease of production and the wide variety of generics manufacturers, and so unnecessary costs or inconveniences in the registration process are a disincentive for manufacturers to enter or stay in the market. Examples include burdensome or expensive processes, including for renewals and variations; the requirement to identify a qualified local agent; and specifically in Nepal, the short registration validity. Furthermore, the low prices negotiated by multinational organisations and donors may also erode profit margins. As such, if the costs to register MNCH commodities are the same as the costs to register products with higher profit margins, manufacturers may not be motivated to register MNCH commodities. Similarly, this environment affects costlier WHO-prequalified medicines when procurement officers are more sensitive to product price than quality. Manufacturers also reported the inability to gain sufficient information on the local market and competitor products to determine whether they had a business case for entering or keeping their products registered, in part because they cannot easily access updated lists of registered products. There can also be strong competition from local suppliers and, in some countries, a preference for purchasing locally manufactured products. Finally, market size is limited to only the mothers and children who need these treatments, and new treatments may not be fully implemented or scaled up. Market efficiency might improve if MNCH commodities had a more straightforward, lower-cost registration process.

Approaches to streamline registration of MNCH commodities

Many opportunities exist for NRAs to remove barriers and streamline the process to motivate suppliers of quality-assured MNCH medicines to enter the market. For instance, optimising registration may include increasing adherence to appropriate timeframes through consistent, predictable procedures that align with international standards that promote assessment rigor and quality. Also, the NRAs’ default position on most application renewals and variations is to treat them as new registrations, which is unnecessarily onerous and contributes to backlogs and product shortages (Roth et al., 2018). In addition to streamlining NRAs’ dossier management practices, the use of electronic management information systems would allow manufacturers to submit registration dossiers for their products online and would also give manufacturers reliable information on the number and source of registered products in the market, which would allow them to better evaluate the market. More transparency would also enable manufacturers to track application status more easily. Few LMICs have fully implemented a risk-based approach to renewals, such as an abridged review for products where nothing has changed since they were last approved. NRAs are increasingly relying on the regulatory decisions made by WHO, regional bodies, and other reference NRAs, (Doerr et al., 2021) which enables LMICs to allocate their limited resources to improving the quality of regulatory decisions and expedite registrations. Countries need clear legal provisions to enable good reliance practices that allow them to recognise and use other countries’ regulatory decisions (Deloitte, 2023). Full use of regional harmonisation efforts to ensure quality, safety, and efficacy, such as the East African Community’s medicines regulatory harmonisation project, can also increase efficiency (Access to Medicine Foundation, 2023; Ncube et al., 2021; Ndomondo-Sigonda et al., 2021), with member countries having decreased median registration times from one or two years to seven months (Schofield, 2017). Other recommendations include harmonising a country’s health technology assessments with its registration assessments to achieve economies of scale (Ofori-Asenso et al., 2020).

Registration of locally produced products

Registration processes can support domestic pharmaceutical manufacturing, particularly for simple, generic medicines with lower-risk profiles that include many MNCH commodities. The 74th World Health Assembly passed a resolution to strengthen local manufacturing – in large part to correct imbalances highlighted during the COVID-19 crisis (Ravelo, 2021). If a country has adequate availability and pricing of domestically produced, quality MNCH commodities, accelerating the registration process for locally manufactured products is one potential opportunity for countries to increase access to quality-assured MNCH medicines. In this study, tracer product registration did not seem to be associated with local manufacturing capacity. Developing the industry for pharmaceutical manufacturing, including for export, could be a strong argument for governments to invest more heavily in strengthening the regulatory system and NRA (Deloitte, 2023; IQVIA, 2025).

Although the registration process mostly focuses on maintaining a quality supply of medicines in the market, it also affects the market considerably; thus, assessing and improving the implementation of registration policies is critical to mitigate factors that affect product availability and quality.

Trends that could increase MNCH product registration

Several of the countries that do not have autonomous NRAs are moving toward creating standalone government agencies. In addition to increasing regulatory effectiveness (Dube-Mwedzi et al., 2020), this can provide an opportunity to upgrade registration procedures, restructure the NRA organisation, and modernise legislation, for example, by putting in place a regulatory framework to formally recognise the WHO-prequalification scheme or regulatory decisions on medicines registration from reference NRAs. A positive development is that NRAs have been enhancing their operations, including those for registration or marketing authorisation, in an effort to meet GBT targets. The GBT facilitates coordination, puts countries in the driver’s seat, and improves the effectiveness of regulatory strengthening efforts. The WHO is also developing a complementary curriculum framework that defines different competencies needed by regulatory staff (Gwaza et al., 2017). The systematic GBT assessment will continue to be a driving force for countries to act to raise their maturity levels (Guzman et al., 2020; Khadem Broojerdi et al., 2020).

To address the process challenges that we describe here, we developed a set of recommendations that governments in general, and NRAs in particular, can take to improve registration processes that affect the availability of critical products to safeguard maternal, newborn, and child health (Box 1).

Box 1. Important recommendations to improve product registration processes

Streamlining Policy and Legal Framework

• Formally incorporate recognition, reliance, and risk-based regulation in legislative frameworks

Streamlining NRA Organization and Resources

• Encourage and support regulators to improve their positions in the health system hierarchy and advocate for NRA autonomy

• Diversify funding to reduce dependence on fees and foster sustainability

• Carefully assess staffing levels and competency to reduce backlogs and improve quality of evaluations

Streamlining the Registration Process: Registration Requirements, Process, and Decision-Making

• Prioritize processes to expedite the registration of products of public health benefit, including MNCH medicines

• Issue five-year registrations and maintain a strong regulatory information system in line with the WHO best registration practices

• Implement standardized registration approaches including good review practices and procedures to assess medicines electronically and strengthen regulatory practices to align with maturity level 3, per the WHO GBT

Study limitations

There were two study limitations in specific countries. In Nepal, the electronic system was not updated with the current product registration status, which is what we used to determine registration status. In addition, the newly established Rwanda Food and Drug Authority had not started formally registering medicines and products currently authorised for use in the country; therefore, ‘listed' products in Rwanda were considered as a proxy for ‘registered’ in this study. Listing is a less rigorous and less costly process for manufacturers, so products may be listed now that will not be registered in the future.

Our selection included countries from sub-Saharan Africa and Asia, but the results do not represent the full array of health product registration processes in LMICs. In addition, although our MNCH tracer products were adapted from a globally recognised source, product characteristics vary, and different products could have produced different results (UN, 2012). However, we feel that the study settings and targeted products represent a good example of the challenges NRAs face.

Conclusions

Although the medical product registration process mostly focuses on maintaining a consistent supply of quality medicines in the market, the process also affects the market itself, making it essential to assess the impact of registration policies and fine-tune them. Our findings in nine countries indicate that short-, mid-, and long-term structural solutions will involve legal, organisational, and procedural changes.

Though not generalisable to all LMICs, the findings from this multi-country study highlight the need to address registration bottlenecks as an important way to increase availability and accessibility of life-saving MNCH products. Actions to increase registration of MNCH products include authorizing regulators to formally incorporate recognition, reliance, and risk-based regulation in their legislative frameworks and prioritising and expediting the registration of products of public health benefit, including MNCH medicines. Local manufacturing, often touted as a solution for access, was not associated with high registration rates in our study, but a government strategy to promote local manufacturing could use MNCH medicines as an entry point. Likewise, the path for manufacturers can be smoothed by making market information more accessible and reducing inefficiencies or marginal costs in the registration process such as by implementing standardised good review practices and electronic medicine assessment procedures; however, such solutions will require more and diversified funding, including both government budget and user fees, that stays within the agency and provides adequate and sustainable resources for effective operation.
