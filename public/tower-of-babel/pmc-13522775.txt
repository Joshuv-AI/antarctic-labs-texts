Phase 1 study of intracranial T cell therapy with pemetrexed for refractory brain metastases and glioblastomas
Authors: Congcong Li, Shuang Wang, Xiumei Rong, Chuxiong Zeng, Feifei Huo, Haiyang Zhao, Dazhao Xu, Lina Hu, Linyao Lu, Bo Sun, Yanhua Yuan, Xiaoli Wang, Xinna Zhou, Amy Hobeika, Michael A. Morse, Guanghui Ma, Li Ren, Jiyu Li, Herbert Kim Lyerly, Zhijun Bao, Jun Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTherapeutic options remain limited for advanced intracranial malignancies, including brain metastases and glioblastomas, particularly in patients ineligible for surgery, radiotherapy, or systemic therapies. This phase 1 study evaluates the safety and feasibility of intracranial administration of low-dose pemetrexed followed by RetroNectin-activated killer cells (RAKs) and interleukin 2 via an Ommaya reservoir (collectively referred to as SCOPE-T) in 25 patients with refractory intracranial malignancies. Nineteen patients completed at least one treatment cycle, and 17 received the maximum tested RAK dose (5 × 108 cells). Treatment is well tolerated, with no treatment-related adverse events greater than grade 2; transient fever and headache are the most common toxicities. Exploratory radiographic and clinical assessments suggest disease stabilization in evaluable patients. These findings support the feasibility and safety of intracranial SCOPE-T and indicate preliminary signals of therapeutic activity. Given the small sample size and non-randomized design, the results should be interpreted as exploratory (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05459441).

Introduction

Refractory intracranial malignancies, including brain metastases and glioblastomas, remain associated with poor clinical outcomes, particularly in patients who progress after surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapies.1 For several decades, supportive palliative care has often been the only standard option because of the lack of effective treatments. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the complex brain microenvironment as well as neurologic toxicity risks present challenges for the use of systemically therapeutic administrations available and even immune therapies to treat intracranial malignancies.2,3 Utilizing an Ommaya reservoir for direct infusion into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or tumor bed offers a means to bypass the BBB. For tumors with immunosuppressive microenvironments, autologous T cell- and NK cell-based therapeutics have emerged as an alternative to immune checkpoint blockade and cytokines. Cellular therapies are versatile as they can be administered both systemically and locally4,5,6,7,8; however, there has been limited study of intrathecal or intracavitary administration of immune cell therapies for refractory intracranial malignancies. In a study of patients with malignant gliomas, intracavitary administration of lymphokine-activated killer (LAK) cells and interleukin 2 (IL-2) was well tolerated and led to tumor regression and partial improvement in clinical symptoms.9 Some studies have reported the use of intraventricular injection of CART cells for the treatment of glioma. In a recent study, three participants with recurrent glioblastoma were treated with CARv3-TEAM-E T cells via an Ommaya reservoir, and no adverse events (AEs) exceeding grade 3 or dose-limiting toxic effects were observed.10 More recently, 21 patients with diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma were treated with repeated doses of ICV B7-H3 CAR T cells, and only one dose-limiting toxicity (intratumoral hemorrhage) was observed.11

We previously developed autologous RetroNectin-activated killer (RAK) cells, which demonstrate anti-cancer activity when administered intravenously.12,13,14,15 Our previous clinical studies revealed that intracavitary RAK cell infusion effectively treated refractory malignant pleural effusions and ascites.16 However, the effectiveness of RAK cells for brain malignancies, especially when administered intracranially, remains unexplored.

The efficacy of immunotherapy can be modulated by certain chemotherapeutic agents. Among them, pemetrexed has been shown to enhance immunotherapeutic activity through multiple innate and adaptive immune mechanisms17 and can be safely administered intrathecally.18,19 This was the rationale for us to initiate a first-in-human, phase 1 clinical trial to evaluate the safety, feasibility, and preliminary clinical activity of a combined intracranial infusion of pemetrexed, RAK cells, and IL-2 (collectively referred to as SCOPE-T therapy) in patients with heavily pre-treated recurrent or refractory brain metastases originating from primary tumors of the breast, lung, pancreas, colon, and primary glioblastomas (ClinicalTrials.gov number: NCT05459441).

Given the substantial clinical challenges faced by patients with refractory intracranial malignancies who are no longer candidates for surgical resection, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy, this trial was designed to evaluate the feasibility and safety of SCOPE-T therapy. The primary objective was to assess safety and tolerability, with comprehensive monitoring of both systemic and intracranial AEs. Key considerations included ethical and enrollment criteria (e.g., inclusion of selected patients with poor ECOG performance status (PS) when clinically appropriate), the selection of appropriate response evaluation systems (RECIST 1.1, RANO-BM, and RANO-LM), and comparative assessment of their interpretive consistency. Clinical outcomes, including radiographic response, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS), were assessed as secondary or exploratory endpoints to characterize potential signals of therapeutic activity. This autologous, non-chimeric T cell intracerebral infusion trial represents a continuation of our previous work on intracavitary RAK cell therapy,16 aiming to define the feasibility and potential clinical role of SCOPE-T as an investigational intracranial immunotherapeutic strategy.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 25 patients from 2 participating hospitals were enrolled between January 1, 2022 and March 30, 2024 (Table S1). The demographic and baseline clinical characteristics of all patients enrolled are summarized in Table 1. The majority of patients were diagnosed with brain metastases or glioblastoma and had received prior treatment for brain lesions. All patients with brain metastases had received prior treatment (such as surgery, radiotherapy, bevacizumab, and targeted therapy) for the primary tumor. The patient disposition flow diagram and detailed therapy procedure of each patient are shown in Figures 1A–1C. Patients were first implanted with an Ommaya reservoir and subsequently received one treatment cycle consisting of two intracranial infusions of pemetrexed (10 mg) plus dexamethasone (5 mg) on days 0 and 4, followed 10 days later by one cycle of RAK cell treatment. A complete cycle of RAK cell treatment consisted of three intracranial infusions of RAK cells at the same dose level, administered weekly. Each RAK cell infusion was followed by two intracranial infusions of IL-2 administered every other day (5 × 105 units) together with dexamethasone (2.5 mg). In addition, folic acid supplementation was administered once daily starting from day −7, and vitamin B12 was administered intramuscularly once on day −7, in accordance with standard pemetrexed supportive care protocols. All patients who received at least one dose of the study treatment were included in the full analysis set (FAS, n = 25) and safety set (SS, n = 25). Six of the 25 patients could not complete one cycle of RAK cell infusion therapy because of consent withdrawal (n = 1; Pt10) and complication/disease progression-related death (n = 5; Pt12, Pt14, Pt 15, Pt17, and Pt 24). The remaining 19 patients who received at least 1 cycle of therapy (per-protocol set [PPS]) participated in the dose-escalation and/or dose-expansion phase(s). Seventeen patients who completed at least one treatment cycle at the maximum tested dose and had at least one post-baseline tumor assessment were included in the efficacy-evaluable set (EES).

Manufacturing feasibility and treatment delivery

To further evaluate the feasibility of intracranial SCOPE-T administration, we assessed the manufacturing success, product characteristics, and treatment-delivery feasibility of RAK cell therapy across all enrolled patients (Table S2). RAK cell manufacturing was successfully completed for all 25 enrolled patients, using approximately 50 mL peripheral blood collected from each patient, corresponding to a manufacturing success rate of 100%. The median RAK cell yield was approximately 5 × 109 cells per 50 mL peripheral blood, which was sufficient to support the planned intracranial treatment cycles, including administration at the RP2D dose level (5 × 108 cells per infusion). Cell viability remained consistently high across manufactured products (96.0%–98.8%). Phenotypic characterization demonstrated that the expanded products were predominantly composed of CD8+ T cells, with variable proportions of CD4+ T cells and NK cells across patients. To ensure product safety before infusion, aliquots of manufactured RAK cells were subjected to microbiological quality control testing 5 days prior to administration, including aerobic bacteria, anaerobic bacteria, mycoplasma, and chlamydia detection. All manufactured products passed sterility testing without detectable microbial contamination.

For treatment delivery, Ommaya implantation and treatment were initiated in all 25 patients; 19 completed at least one full cycle. Overall, these data support the practical feasibility of repeated intracranial SCOPE-T administration in heavily pre-treated patients with refractory intracranial malignancies.

Safety of SCOPE-T treatment

Overall AEs of all 25 enrolled patients are also summarized in Table 2. There were no treatment-related deaths. Among these patients, 5 (Pt1–Pt5) participated in the dose-escalation phase. In this phase, 3 patients (Pt1, Pt2, and Pt3) were first treated with 5 × 106 RAK cells (n = 3) in the first cycle therapy, and two of these patients (Pt1 and Pt2) continued to receive the escalated dose (5 × 107 RAK cells), while one patient (Pt3) died of a lung infection caused by prolonged bed rest associated with aspiration pneumonia. Two additional patients (Pt4 and Pt5) subsequently participated in the trial of 5 × 107 RAK cell infusion (n = 4) for safety evaluation. Furthermore, three of these four patients (Pt1, Pt2, and Pt5) continued to receive the 5 × 108 RAK cell infusion (n = 3), while Pt4 died of systemic tumor progression. There were no dose-limiting toxicities observed during dose escalation; therefore, the highest dose tested (5 × 108 RAK cells) was selected for dose expansion and designated as the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D). The overview of AEs related to intracranial treatment in the PPS is presented in Table S3. Overall, SCOPE-T was generally well tolerated, and the most common AEs were headache (26.3%) and pyrexia (36.8%), with the majority of these being grade 1 or 2, and no AEs above grade 2 were observed. Headache and pyrexia were considered to be induced by the intracranial infusion of IL-2. Intracranial infection was reported in two (10.5%) patients (one grade 1 and one grade 2), which was attributed to complications of epifolliculitis rather than the RAK cell-related AE.

Exploratory radiographic and clinical assessments

Clinical responses are shown in Figure 1C and summarized in Table 3. Two patients demonstrated partial radiographic responses according to RECIST 1.1, while most evaluable patients showed stable disease (SD). In addition, 14 patients in the EES achieved SD. For RECIST-based evaluation, 16 of 17 patients in the EES had evaluable post-baseline imaging and were included in radiographic response analyses. One patient did not have post-baseline imaging and was, therefore, not evaluable under RECIST 1.1 criteria. Beyond imaging evaluation, patient benefits—such as improvements in brain edema, headache, nausea, vomiting, dysphasia, muscle loss, hemiparesis, response to questions, state of consciousness, psychiatric symptoms, decreased appetite, asthenia, levels of carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and tumor cells in CSF—were documented (Figure S1). To further characterize treatment response in intracranial disease, we applied RANO-BM and RANO-LM criteria alongside RECIST 1.120 (Table 4). Unlike RECIST 1.1, the RANO frameworks incorporate clinical status, corticosteroid use, and CSF-based assessments, enabling response evaluation in all patients in the EES, including those without complete imaging data. Using RANO-BM and RANO-LM criteria, all 17 patients in the EES demonstrated clinical benefit. Concordance analysis across RECIST 1.1, RANO-BM, and RANO-LM showed generally consistent response patterns, with RANO-based criteria providing additional clinical interpretive resolution in selected cases. RANO (response assessment in neuro-oncology): Encompasses specific criteria for brain metastases (RANO-BM) and leptomeningeal metastases (RANO-LM).

RANO-BM: Intracranial disease status is evaluated independently from systemic disease, taking into account corticosteroid use, tumor-related neurological decline, and neurological symptoms.

RANO-LM: Recommends that all patients with leptomeningeal disease (LMD) enrolled in clinical trials undergo standardized neurological examination, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis (including cytology), and comprehensive brain and spinal MRI.

PFS definition: PFS is determined according to the RECIST criteria.

NA (not applicable): Indicates tumors not metastatic to the specified anatomical region and, therefore, not evaluable.

UA (unable to assess): Indicates patients who did not meet the criteria for evaluation.

These observations illustrate the heterogeneity of clinical outcomes within this small cohort.

Exploratory proteomic analysis of CSF samples

As an exploratory correlative analysis within this phase 1 trial, a total of 13 CSF samples were successfully collected for proteomic analysis (92 proteins), using the Olink immune-oncology proximity extension assay (PEA) assay. These samples included 3 pre-treatment and 10 post-treatment samples, with patient details and sampling times shown in Figure 1C. Given the limited number of patients with available paired samples and the non-random nature of CSF sample availability, these analyses were conducted as descriptive exploratory assessments.

Compared with the pre-treatment CSF samples, multiple proteins exhibited differential expression following treatment. After controlling for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, three proteins (CCL17, GZMB, and GZMA) were found to be significantly upregulated after intracranial therapy based on an FDR-adjusted threshold of q < 0.10 (Figure S7A). Among these, GZMA showed the largest increase during treatment. Among patients with paired CSF samples, proteomic profiling was associated with changes in immune-related protein expression patterns following SCOPE-T treatment, characterized by increased expression of cytotoxic and chemokine-related proteins and decreased expression of immunosuppressive factors (Figures S7B and S7C). Comparative analyses across patients with different response patterns further suggested that a subset of these proteins may be associated with treatment response dynamics (Figure S7D). Given the very limited number of patients with longitudinal samples, these observations should be considered hypothesis generating, and larger prospective studies are required to validate these candidate biomarkers.

Exploratory tumor DNA analysis of CSF samples

Previous studies have shown that tumor DNA from CSF better reflects brain tumor genomic alterations than plasma, as it can detect brain tumor-specific mutations and track tumor progression.21 In an exploratory genomic analysis embedded within this trial, 21 CSF samples, including 12 pre-treatment and 9 post-treatment samples, were successfully collected for tumor DNA detection (patient information and sampling time points are shown in Figure 1C). Across these samples, we identified a total of 128 somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and 14 insertions and deletions (indels). The top 18 mutated genes are shown in Figure 3A. At the population level, the overall fraction of tumor-derived mutations, measured by variant allele frequency (VAF), was significantly reduced in post-treatment samples compared with baseline (Figures 3B and 3C), consistent with potential changes in tumor-derived genomic signals following treatment. Neoantigens derived from tumor-specific mutations are considered important targets for immune-mediated tumor elimination.22,23 In this study, 44 predicted neoantigens were identified across 12 patients (Data S1). Discussion

Refractory intracranial malignancies remain difficult to treat after standard local and systemic options have been exhausted. In this setting, the BBB, the immunologically specialized central nervous system, and concerns about neurologic toxicity have historically limited the development of intracranial immunotherapies. Building on prior safety data for intracranial pemetrexed administration and our more than two decades of experience with autologous T cell administration via intravenous, intrapleural, and intrapericardial routes, our study addresses this gap by testing repeated intracranial administration of pemetrexed followed by autologous ex-vivo-expanded RAK cells and IL-2. To our knowledge, this is the first clinical study to evaluate this non-chimeric autologous T cell-based strategy in patients with refractory brain metastases and glioblastomas. The major objective was safety, with particular attention to central nervous system-related toxicity and systemic reactions after repeated intraventricular injections. SCOPE-T showed a favorable safety profile and was technically feasible in this heavily pre-treated population, while exploratory radiographic, clinical, and molecular findings suggested potential biological activity in a subset of patients.

Patient selection was a critical issue in this trial. We included selected patients with ECOG PS scores of 3 or 4 because, in the context of intracranial disease, poor PS may reflect neurologic deficits or limited mobility rather than irreversible systemic decline. Many patients with brain metastases were bedridden but still had preserved cardiac, pulmonary, and renal functions. Thus, ECOG PS alone was not used as an exclusionary criterion; instead, enrollment decisions focused on whether the patient was approaching the terminal phase of disease or had an immediately life-limiting extracranial condition. A second practical lesson relates to the treatment timing. Because outcomes in brain metastases are strongly influenced by control of the primary malignancy and prior systemic therapies, intracranial SCOPE-T should be considered in the context of patients’ broader disease trajectory. Future studies may benefit from better defining the window in which intracranial disease is the dominant clinical problem, thereby improving patient selection and interpretation of outcomes.

Local CSF changes also deserve consideration. As knowledge of T cell trafficking and immune surveillance in the central nervous system has expanded, CSF has become an informative compartment for monitoring local immune responses.24 In this study, routine CSF testing after SCOPE-T revealed flocculent material or particulates in approximately half of the samples, together with transient increases in white blood cell counts and the proportion of polymorphonuclear cells. These findings occurred without microbiologic evidence of infection and without corresponding fever or headache, and they subsequently returned to the normal range. We, therefore, interpret them as possible markers of transient local immune activation or aseptic inflammation rather than infection. Nevertheless, new or persistent neurologic symptoms after intracranial therapy should prompt evaluation for tumor progression, infection, obstructive CSF circulation, or adhesive arachnoiditis.25,26

The exploratory CSF proteomic and tumor DNA analyses provided complementary evidence of biological effects but should be interpreted cautiously. Proteomic profiling identified changes in immune-related proteins, including candidate markers that differed between patients with SD and PR. These results suggest that CSF proteomics may help characterize the treatment response and support future biomarker development, but the small number of paired samples precludes definitive conclusions. Similarly, the reduction in CSF tumor-derived mutational burden and neoantigen-associated VAF after treatment is consistent with a potential antitumor effect, but it requires validation in larger, longitudinal cohorts with standardized sampling.

In conclusion, SCOPE-T therapy represents a feasible investigational intracranial immunotherapy strategy for patients with refractory brain metastases or glioblastomas who have limited therapeutic options. The favorable safety profile and the feasibility of repeated intracranial administration support further clinical investigation of SCOPE-T therapy. Encouraged by the exploratory clinical observations in this trial, we treated seven additional patients under compassionate or humanitarian use; six showed preliminary clinical stabilization or symptomatic improvement, whereas one patient with advanced glioblastoma did not. These anecdotal observations are supportive of feasibility but should not be interpreted as evidence of efficacy. Future studies should refine dosing strategies, manage immune-related CSF reactions, define the optimal therapeutic window, and evaluate long-term outcomes in more homogeneous patient populations.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, as expected for a first-in-human phase 1 trial designed primarily to assess safety and feasibility rather than efficacy. The small cohort also limited subgroup analyses and biomarker interpretation. Second, the population was clinically heterogeneous with different primary tumors, prior therapies, and intracranial disease patterns, including both brain metastases and glioblastomas. This heterogeneity may confound comparisons of treatment response and survival across subgroups. Third, the single-arm, non-randomized design and lack of a concurrent control group limit causal attribution of clinical outcomes to SCOPE-T and introduce the possibility of selection bias. Because there was no pemetrexed-only control arm, the independent contributions of intrathecal pemetrexed, RAK cells, and IL-2 cannot be determined. Although Pt13 and Pt22 had previously received intrathecal pemetrexed without clear benefit and later experienced disease stabilization after SCOPE-T, these observations are descriptive only and require controlled evaluation. Finally, CSF proteomic and tumor DNA analyses were performed in a limited subset of samples, and sampling was not uniform across patients or time points. These biomarker findings should, therefore, be considered hypothesis generating and require validation in larger, prospective studies with standardized sampling and controlled designs.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and patients

This open-label, single-arm phase 1 study was conducted at Fudan University Pudong Medical Center in Shanghai and Beijing Zhongguancun Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Beijing. A total of 25 patients from two participating hospitals were enrolled between 1 January 2022 and 30 March 2024.

As an early-phase, first-in-human study, the planned sample size was not based on formal hypothesis testing but was guided by safety evaluation requirements, estimation precision, prior studies of intracranial cellular immunotherapy (NCT02208362, NCT04185038, NCT04196413), and feasibility considerations. The selected sample size (n = 25) is consistent with typical phase 1/1b trials designed to evaluate safety and tolerability.

Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years and had relapsed or refractory intracranial malignancies, including brain metastases or primary glioblastoma, and were deemed unsuitable for further surgery, radiotherapy, or systemic therapy. Patients with brain metastases were required to have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–3, whereas patients with glioblastoma were eligible regardless of ECOG status (0–4). All patients were required to have an anticipated survival of at least 1 month and be candidates for Ommaya reservoir implantation. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient prior to Ommaya reservoir implantation and the initiation of treatment. Details of prior therapies of each patient are provided in Table S1.

Study oversight

The study was approved by the institutional review boards of both study sites. It was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonisation Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. No participants were lost to follow-up during the study period, resulting in 100% retention of the baseline cohort for final analysis.

Method details

Objectives and endpoints

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the safety and tolerability of intracranial administration of non-chimeric autologous expanded T cells combined with pemetrexed. The primary endpoint was the incidence of dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), assessed during a prespecified DLT evaluation window defined as inside the intracranial treatment cycle (once weekly for 5 weeks). DLTs were evaluated in all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment (safety set, SS). During the dose-escalation phase, DLTs were used to guide dose-escalation decisions, whereas in the dose-expansion phase, DLTs contributed to the overall safety characterization of the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D).

Secondary or exploratory endpoints included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and disease control rate (DCR). OS was defined as the time from study enrollment to death from any cause. PFS was defined as the time from study enrollment to the first documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. DCR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete response (CR), partial response (PR), or stable disease (SD) based on best overall response during the study evaluation period. Efficacy endpoints were evaluated in the efficacy-evaluable set (EES), defined as patients who completed at least one treatment cycle at the maximum tested dose and had at least one post-baseline tumor assessment. Efficacy assessments were performed at predefined time points, including the end of each treatment cycle and subsequent follow-up evaluations every 7–8 weeks, using RECIST and RANO criteria. Tumor response was determined based on the best overall response from treatment initiation until disease progression or last adequate tumor assessment.

Stopping rules were triggered by ≥ 1 treatment-related death or ≥1 unanticipated adverse reaction above grade III.

Treatment regimen

In brief, patients underwent Ommaya reservoir implantation before treatment. Each treatment included two intracranial administrations of pemetrexed (10 mg), followed by RAK cell infusion and interleukin-2 (IL-2) support. RAK cells were administered weekly for three doses per cycle. Each RAK infusion was followed by IL-2 administration every other day. Details are provided in the following method details.

Dose-escalation design

The dose-escalation strategy was designed to evaluate the safety and tolerability of intracranial administration of RAK cells in a first-in-human setting. The starting dose and escalation scheme were determined based on (i) prior clinical experience with intravenous and intracavitary administration of RAK cells, (ii) the achievable yield of RAK cells from peripheral blood (typically 108-109 cells per 50 mL), and (iii) published intracranial CAR-T cell studies (e.g., NCT02208362). Based on these considerations, three dose levels were defined: 5 × 106, 5 × 107, and 5 × 108 RAK cells.

A cohort-based escalation design was implemented, with 3–4 patients enrolled at each dose level. Dose escalation proceeded sequentially following safety evaluation within each cohort. DLT was defined as the occurrence of ≥1 treatment-related death or ≥3 unanticipated grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events within the predefined DLT evaluation period. The prespecified escalation rules were as follows: (i) If no DLT was observed in a cohort, escalation to the next dose level proceeded; (ii) If one DLT occurred, up to three additional patients were enrolled at the same dose level; (iii) If ≥ 2 DLTs occurred, dose escalation was terminated and the previous dose was considered the maximum tolerated dose (MTD). In addition to the cohort-based design, a step-up dosing strategy within individual patients was permitted. Patients who completed treatment at a lower dose level without experiencing DLT were allowed to proceed to the next higher dose level in subsequent cycles, provided they had stable clinical status and willingness to continue treatment. This approach is consistent with early-phase cell therapy trials.

Because no DLTs were observed during dose escalation, the highest tested dose (5 × 108 RAK cells) was selected for dose expansion and designated as the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D).

Manufacture of RAK cells and SCOPE-T treatment

Autologous RAK cells were generated following previously reported protocols.12,13,14 Because of the generally poor clinical condition of many of the patients, instead of leukapheresis, though, we drew a peripheral blood volume of 50 mL for RAK cell induction. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated and cultured in T175 flasks pre-coated with RetroNectin and anti-human CD3 monoclonal antibody. The culture medium (T503) was supplemented with 2% (v/v) patient plasma and 1000 IU/mL of recombinant human IL-2. After 96 h, the cells were harvested and transferred to fresh flasks for continued culture, with media replenishment every two days. After a further 10 days of culture, the RAK cells were harvested, washed, and prepared for infusion.

For the SCOPE-T combination treatment (Figure 1A), patients were first implanted with an Ommaya reservoir and subsequently received one treatment cycle consisting of two intracranial infusions of pemetrexed (10 mg, Alimta, Lilly Company) plus dexamethasone (5 mg) on days 0 and 4, followed 10 days later by one cycle of RAK-cell treatment. A complete cycle of RAK-cell treatment consisted of three intracranial infusions of RAK cells at the same dose level, administered weekly. Each RAK-cell infusion was followed by two intracranial infusions of IL-2 administered every other day (5 × 105 units, Sihuan Pharm) together with dexamethasone (2.5 mg). In addition, folic acid supplementation was administered once daily starting from day −7, and vitamin B12 was administered intramuscularly once on day −7, in accordance with standard pemetrexed supportive care protocols. Following completion of one treatment cycle, subsequent cycles were administered at 3-week intervals when clinically feasible. All the above-mentioned drug administration procedures were carried out by professional medical personnel in the aforementioned hospitals. For patients with brain metastases, previously prescribed systemic therapies for the primary malignancy were generally continued during intracranial SCOPE-T treatment whenever clinically appropriate. For those from whom CSF samples could be collected, Olink proteomics, CSF tumor DNA, CSF cytology and chemistries were routinely performed. All patients were followed until disease progression, and long-term follow up continued or until withdrawal of consent or until death (with some cases still under follow up).

Toxicity analysis

Participants were monitored for adverse events throughout the study. Safety assessments primarily consisted of prospectively collected clinical adverse events and laboratory evaluations, including hematologic and biochemical parameters, which were obtained at regular intervals (once a week) during each treatment cycle. These data formed the core safety dataset and were complete for all patients in the Safety Set. Radiological imaging was performed as a supportive assessment to evaluate intracranial safety at the end timepoint of each treatment cycle, including treatment-related changes such as edema, hemorrhage, and structural alterations, when clinically indicated. Because imaging was not uniformly required at all time points for all patients, it was not included as part of the primary safety dataset. Adverse events (AEs) were graded according to the Common Toxicity Criteria/National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. Immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) was graded according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy consensus guidelines. Two independent investigators served as arbiters for determining the relatedness to the investigatory product.

Disease assessment

MRI or computed tomography imaging was evaluated at predefined time points, including the end of each treatment cycle and subsequent follow-up evaluations every 7–8 weeks, in a blinded fashion by dedicated radiologists. Both RECIST and RANO criteria were used to evaluate response in above evaluation window. All efficacy analyses were conducted at the patient level, and lesion-level measurements were used only to determine patient-level response outcomes according to established response criteria. For RECIST-based evaluation, only patients with at least one post-baseline imaging assessment were considered evaluable. For RANO-based evaluation, additional clinical and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) parameters were incorporated, allowing response assessment in cases where imaging data were incomplete. Tumor response (CR, PR, SD, or PD) was defined based on the best overall response from treatment initiation until disease progression or last adequate assessment. Additional assessments included improvement of brain edema, headache, nausea, vomiting, dysphasia, muscle loss, hemiparesis, response to questions, state of consciousness, psychiatric symptoms, decreased appetite, asthenia, carcinoembryonic antigen and tumor cells in CSF.

Proteomic analysis of CSF samples

CSF samples were collected and analyzed using the Olink immune oncology panel (Olink Proteomics AB, Uppsala, Sweden) to measure the levels of 92 proteins according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This technology is based on the PEA. Briefly, pairs of oligonucleotide-labeled antibodies bind to each target protein. If the two antibodies are in close proximity, the oligonucleotides will hybridize in a pairwise manner. The addition of DNA polymerase generates a unique PCR target sequence, which is formed through proximity-dependent DNA polymerization. The generated DNA sequence was detected and quantified with a microfluidic real-time PCR instrument (Biomark HD, Fluidigm). Then a set of internal and external controls were used for quality control and to normalize the resulting Ct-data. The final results were presented as normalized protein expression (NPX) values.

The internal controls were designed to mimic and monitor the different steps of PEA, and these controls were introduced to all samples. They consisted of two incubation/immuno controls, an extension control and a detection control. The internal controls and the external controls are used for quality control and normalization of the data. The external controls consisted of a negative control that was used to calculate the limit of detection (LOD), as well as triplicate interplate controls (IPCs) that were used for normalization of data. Quality control of the data was performed in two steps. First, the run was quality controlled by calculating the standard deviation for the detection control and the in-cubation/immuno controls. The standard deviation should be below 0.2 for a run to pass the quality control. Second, each sample was quality controlled by comparing the results for the detection control and one of the incubation controls against the run median. Samples that fall more than 0.3 NPX from the run median with regard to these two internal controls are considered to have failed quality control. The median value of protein level was used as cut-off value for dividing high and low.

Tumor DNA analysis of CSF samples

CSF and whole blood samples were collected in cell-free DNA BCT tubes (Streck Inc., La Vista, NE, USA). Within 24 h of CSF and blood collection, the cellular fraction was removed by two-step centrifugation at 4°C (1900 g for 10 min within 2 h of collection and 16,000 g for 10 min). The white blood cells were used for genomic DNA extraction (DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit, Qiagen) as the germline controls. Samples were stored at −80°C until further processing. ctDNA was extracted using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen), and analyzed using comprehensive genomic profiling of 127 cancer-related genes in a central testing laboratory (YuceBio Technology, Shenzhen, China). For patients with extracranial tumor tissues, genomic DNA was also extracted from tis-sue biopsy samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen) and subjected to next-generation sequencing with the same panel described above. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq4000 platform and data analysis was performed as previously described. In brief, sequencing data were analyzed by Trimmomatic to remove low-quality (quality <15) or N bases, and then mapped to the human reference genome hg19 using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (https://github.com/lh3/bwa/tree/master/bwakit). PCR duplicates were removed by Picard (available at: https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). The Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) (https://software.broadinstitute.org/gatk/) was used to perform local realignments around indels and base quality reassurance. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and indels were analyzed by VarScan2 and Haplotype Caller/UniedGenotyper in GATK, with the mutant allele frequency (MAF) cutoff of 0.2% for cfDNA samples, and a minimum of three unique mutant reads. Common SNPs were excluded if they were present in >1% population frequency in the 1000 Genomes Project or the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC) 65,000 exomes database. The resulting mutation list was further filtered by an in-house list of recurrent artifacts based on a normal pool of whole blood samples. The median depth of coverage after the removal of PCR duplicates was >2000× and >500× for liquid biopsies and tissue samples, respectively.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Analysis populations

The enrolled population included all patients who provided written informed consent. The full analysis set (FAS) and safety set (SS) included all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment. The efficacy-evaluable set (EES) included patients who completed at least one treatment cycle at the maximum tested dose and had at least one post-baseline tumor assessment. The per-protocol set (PPS) included patients who completed at least one treatment cycle without major protocol deviations. Baseline characteristics and time-to-event endpoints (OS and PFS) were analyzed in the FAS, safety endpoints in the SS, and efficacy endpoints in the EES, with PPS analyses performed as supportive analyses.

Missing data handling

Missing data were handled according to endpoint type. No imputation was performed for safety analyses, as primary safety data (clinical adverse events and laboratory assessments) were complete for all treated patients. For efficacy analyses, limited missing imaging variables were addressed using multiple imputation as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of response evaluation. Time-to-event endpoints (OS and PFS) were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with administrative censoring applied at the date of last follow-up for patients without documented events. Exploratory biomarker analyses were conducted in available samples only without imputation, and results were interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were primarily descriptive given the exploratory nature of this phase 1 study. No formal hypothesis testing or power calculation was prespecified. Time-to-event endpoints (OS and PFS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method.

Exploratory biomarker analyses (including CSF proteomics and tumor DNA VAF) were conducted in available subsets of patients and interpreted as hypothesis-generating. For CSF proteomic analysis using the Olink platform, differential protein expression between pre- and post-treatment samples was assessed using paired or unpaired tests as appropriate. To account for multiple comparisons across proteins, false discovery rate (FDR) control was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and statistical significance was defined based on FDR-adjusted q-values. For tumor DNA variant allele frequency (VAF) analyses, non-parametric methods were used due to small sample size and distributional considerations. Specifically, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, and the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparisons test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Given the limited sample size and exploratory nature of these analyses, no adjustment for multiplicity was applied to non-proteomic comparisons, and all findings should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0).
