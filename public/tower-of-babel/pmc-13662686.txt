Feeding study of GM Rice bran (Oryza Sativa L. cv Rojolele) overexpressing Bacillus thuringiensis cry1B::cry1Aa fusion genes under the maize ubiquitin promoter in broiler chicken strain cobb
Authors: Andriyanto, Aulia A. Mustika, Amaq Fadholly, Amy Estiati, Dwi Widyajayantie, Enny Rimita Sembiring, Satya Nugroho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genetically modified (GM) rice (Oryza sativa L) cv Rojolele overexpressing cry1B::cry1Aa fusion genes, driven by the maize Ubiquitin promoter, has been developed to incorporate resistance trait against and to reduce loss due to the rice yellow stem borer (rice YSB, Scirpophaga incertulas Walker). Since rice bran is one of the most commonly used major components in chicken feed, a feeding study of the rice bran of the GM Rojolele rice to the commercial broiler day-old-chick (DOC) strain Cobb was performed. Rice feed compositions were prepared with 6, 8 and 10% GM Rojolele rice bran. Control feed was prepared with non-GM Rojolele rice bran. Nutritional analyses showed no significant differences among the feed compositions. Fifty DOCs were used in each feeding study, treatment and control, and were performed for 30 days. The results indicated that all parameters in the duration evaluated, including total body weight, mortality, feed conversion ratio, hematological responses, liver and kidney functions, and energy digestibility coefficient, exhibited no statistically significant differences between DOCs receiving GM rice bran and those in the control group. The results support the absence of detectable adverse effects within the evaluated endpoints and provide additional information relevant to the feed safety aspects in the regulatory assessment of GM rice-derived feed ingredients.

Introduction

The rice yellow stem borer (rice YSB, Scirpophaga incertulas Walker) may cause substantial yield loss in rice production.1, 2 To manage rice YSB, the application of pesticides is ineffective and environmentally damaging.3 Even though rice possess resistant traits against the rice YSB through a combination of morphological, biochemical and molecular mechanisms that reduce infestation and damage, no genotype shows complete immunity.4 Therefore, the development of resistant rice cultivar via genetic engineering approach is plausible. The development of a resistant rice variety through genetic engineering can be achieved by introducing Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) crystal proteins with toxicity to rice YSB, which is a promising solution.2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Genetic engineering technology allows the overexpression of Bt crystalline (Cry) protein genes in major crops, including maize,12, 13, 14 cotton,15, 16, 17, 18 soybean,19, 20, 21 eggplant,22, 23, 24, 25 rice,2, 25, 26 and other crops,23, 27 to introduce a resistant trait for crop protection against insect pests. Currently, several Bt crops have been commercially cultivated, greatly contributing to the control of numerous insect pests.28

To assess the potential unintended effects following the overexpression of the exogenous gene in crops, rigorous safety examinations are required and applied to the introduced proteins in the genetically modified (GM) crops prior to the commercial release to ensure their safety for consumption, including the absence of allergenic and toxic substances.29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36 Such studies have been conducted previously in various GM crops, such as GM maize, which include compositional and agronomic performance comparisons to determine whether a particular GM crop resulted in unintended effects, showing that the insect pest-resistant and herbicide-tolerant GM maize's forage and grain were compositionally equivalent and their agronomic traits observed were comparable to those of the non-GM maize.37, 38 Subchronic rodent feeding studies conducted in support of the safety assessment process for several maize and rice GM crops also indicated no adverse effects on test animals following the dietary exposures to GM crops.13, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45

An array of studies on the impact of GM feed, expressing insect resistance and herbicide tolerance, on monogastric animal such as chicken have been performed, including GM cotton expressing cry-X (cry1Ac and cry2Ab),46 GM maize expressing ZMM28 and PAT,47 GM corn harboring cry1Ab,48 and GM soybean expressing herbicide-tolerant genes AAD-12, PAT, GM maize expressing the IPD079Ea protein49 and 2mEPSPS (double-mutant 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase, glyphosate tolerance),48 which showed no adverse effects on the growth and development of chicken consuming diets formulated with those GM crops as compared with the control.50 Feeding studies were also conducted on quails fed with GM maize expressing Cry1Ab/EPSPS51 and GM soybean expressing CP4-EPSPS,52 which showed no adverse effect on the test animals. Other feeding studies include, among others, cp4-epsps, Vip3Aa, and pat genes expressing soybean in channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus), which also showed no adverse effect.53

Animal feeding studies have consistently confirmed the safety of GM crops, which are compositionally equivalent to their non-GM counterparts. However, some regulatory agencies, such as those in Indonesia, still require feeding studies to support the safety assessment of new GM crop events (Head of the Agricultural Research and Development Agency-Ministry of Agriculture Decision No. 466.2/Kpts/OT.210/H/11/2016 on Technical Guidelines for the Assessment of Feed Safety from Genetically Engineered Products). Therefore, it is necessary to conduct and publish animal feeding study results for new GM events to provide more evidence to regulatory bodies to make scientific judgements on feed safety. The results will also serve as references for future modification of the regulations based on scientific evidence.

GM Bt rice overexpressing the Cry1B-Cry1Aa fusion protein driven by the maize ubiquitin promoter to improve resistance against rice YSB,2, 10, 54, 55 has been developed using the cultivar Rojolele, a popular aromatic tropical japonica rice in Indonesia, as the background. Efficacy studies, both at the contained facilities in the glasshouse and confined field trials, showed increased tolerance against rice YSB.2, 56 They have also undergone field trials to assess their environmental safety to biodiversity, including insects, Collembola57 and soil microbes. Since rice bran is commonly used as sources of dietary fiber and protein in animal feeds, to assess their impact on animals, feeding studies of the GM rice bran on broiler chicken strain Cobbs were conducted.

This study reports the results of a broiler chicken feeding study, fed with chicken feed formulation according to commercial practice, containing rice bran obtained from GM Rojolele rice expressing cry1B::cry1Aa fusion genes. Parameters observed include body weight gain, mortality, feed conversion ratio (FCR), hematology profiles, liver and kidney function, and feed digestibility.

Materials and methods

The GM Rojolele rice plants used were T8 homozygote generation expressing cry1B::cry1Aa fusion genes, driven by the maize ubiquitin promoter, conferring resistance to the rice YSB, with non-GM Rojolele as the control. The selectable marker gene hygromycin phosphotransferase (hpt) under 35S promoter, conferring resistance to the antibiotic hygromycin B, is also presence in the GM rice plants. The GM Rojojolele rice was generated using the fusion gene construct obtained from CIRAD, France. The GM rice plants were grown in a contained facility located at the Cibinong Science Center-Indonesian Institute of Sciences (LIPI), currently Soekarno Science and Technology Area-National Research and Innovation Agency (BRIN) in Cibinong, West Java, Indonesia. Day-old-chick (DOC) broiler chickens, strain Cobb, were used in this experiment. Feeding studies were conducted at the Laboratory of Animal Management Unit, Faculty of Veterinary, IPB University, Bogor, West Java, Indonesia (IPB University). The studies obtained clearance and followed the guidelines from the Ethical Committee of IPB University number 099/KEH/SKE/VII/2019.

Preparation of chicken feed

Before rice harvesting, DNA from leaf samples was extracted, and PCR analyses using primers specific for the fusion genes were performed for Bt rice confirmation (F 5′- GCT GTC AAC GCT CTC TTC AC-3′ and R 5’-CAA TGC GTT CTC CAC CAA GT-3′). Rice grains were harvested and milled under identical conditions for both GM and non-GM rice. Rice bran was collected immediately after the milling process and used directly for chicken feed preparation without additional grinding or sieving. The particle size of the rice bran was not measured. The same milling procedure was applied to all rice samples to ensure consistency during feed preparation. The chicken feed compositions were prepared following the commercial broiler chicken farm formulation, without antibiotic growth promoter, in accordance with the Indonesian National Standard for Chicken Feed (SNI 01–3931-2006) (Table 1). Three different concentrations of GM rice bran (6%, 8%, and 10%) was included in the formulation to replace the commercial rice bran. Rice bran from WT Rojolele was used as control. The nutrient composition analyses were performed on the final feed formulations containing GM or non-GM rice bran. Preparation of chicken and experimental design

The experiment was conducted at the Laboratory Animal Management Unit, School of Veterinary Medicine and Biomedical sciences, IPB University with a 12 h light/dark cycle. The experiment was conducted with 200 Cobb broiler DOCs (n = 50 per treatment group). Sex of DOCs was not determined before random assignment to treatment groups. The selection of 50 DOCs per treatment group was based on the commonly used group sizes in broiler feeding studies and the practicality of assessing growth performance and physiological responses under production conditions. DOCs were randomly distributed into four treatment groups (50 DOCs/group) and maintained in cages of 10 DOCs each and under the same environmental and management conditions throughout the study. Feed and tap water were available ad libitum throughout the study. To minimize transportation and environmental stresses, the DOCs were acclimatized for 4 days before the dietary treatments started. Therefore, dietary treatments were initiated at 4 days of age. The feeding trial was conducted for 30 days as this period is generally used for evaluating the growth performance, feed utilization, hematological responses and physiological parameters during the rapid commercial growth phase of Cobb broilers. During the experimental period, daily husbandry including health monitoring, litter management, feeding, watering and routine vaccination were carried out following standard broiler management practices.

The treatments were DOCs fed with feed compositions containing 6%, 8%, and 10% rice bran from the GM rice plants and were assigned as 6%, 8%, and 10% GM, respectively. The 6%, 8%, and 10% GM rice bran were included in this experiment to evaluate feed performance across a range of practical dietary inclusion levels. The 10% GM treatment served as the most direct comparator to the 10% non-GM Rojolele rice bran in the control diet (Table 1). Tap water was provided ad libitum. The DOC's daily maintenance and vaccination were performed following the commonly used practice. During the experimental period, all chickens were observed clinically daily for mortality, signs of morbidity, food consumption, and behavior.

Parameters observed

Growth performance indicators were assessed, including body weight (g), feed conversion ratio (FCR), and mortality. Hematological parameters—such as red blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, total white blood cell count, and differential counts of lymphocytes, monocytes, heterophils, basophils, and eosinophils—were measured using an automated hematology analyzer (Mindray BC-5000Vet, China), following previously described procedures.58, 59, 60 Liver function tests were performed by measuring serum glutamic-pyruvic transaminase (SGPT/ALT) and serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT/AST) using commercial assay kits (Glory Diagnostic) and a Rayto Biochemical Analyzer (Rayto, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.61 Renal function was assessed by measuring serum urea and creatinine levels using the same assay kits and analyzer according to the manufacturer's instructions.61 Total collection method was used to determine feed digestibility.62 Excreta were quantitatively collected and digestibility of crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, nitrogen-free extract (NFE), and Energy digestibility coefficient were calculated as described before.62 Measurements were done on day 0 before treatment and on day 30 post treatment

Statistical analysis

The experiment was performed in a completely randomized design (CRD). The DOCs were randomly distributed to the four treatment groups and no blocking factor was used in the experimental design. Data normality and homogeneity of variance were tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and followed by Duncan's Multiple Range Test using Minitab 18 Statistical Software (Minitab, Inc., USA). Statistical significance was considered at p-value of <0.05 for all analyses.

In Indonesia, the nutritional requirements of the broiler feeds are regulated by the Indonesian National Standard (SNI) 01–3931-2006. The average FCR, which is commonly used as an indicator of success in poultry farming in Indonesia, was also not significantly different between treatments and control (P > 0.05), indicating that there was no difference in nutrition absorption between treated chicken and control. Feed composition plays a significant role in FCR. An ideal feed composition will result in a lower FCR.

Similarly, the mortality rate among all treatments and the controls was also not significantly different, in the range of 4% − 6% throughout the experiment. The mortality rates observed in this study were lower than those in commercial production systems62 and a study in conventional and slow-growing broiler chicken conducted at Purdue University, US (8.6%).64, 65 During the experimental period, no clinical signs of abnormality, changes in behavior or health problems related to treatment were observed in any of the treatment groups. The few mortalities recorded were sporadic and were not related to any particular clinical abnormality or dietary treatments.

Hematology

Hematology profiles can be used to indicate chicken health status, such as the presence of infectious diseases, signs of injuries and infections, or decreases in vital organ functions due to stress. Unusual changes in hematology profiles may indicate chickens' responses to feed. Our observations showed that there was no significant difference (p > 0.05) observed in the hematology response variables among any of the treatment groups after consuming corresponding diet formulations for 30 days (Table 4). The red blood cell counts ranged from 2.93 to 2.96 × 106/mm3 before treatment and from 2.63 to 2.68 × 106/mm3 after treatment. These values were within the reported reference range for healthy broiler chickens (2.3–3.5 × 106/mm3).66 The results indicated that the application of the GM rice bran did not cause red blood cell lysis nor erythropoiesis. The applications of the GM rice bran did not show significant differences in hematocrit and hemoglobin contents between treatments and the control (P > 0.05). Hematocrit and hemoglobin contents, before and after treatments, were within reference ranges of 22–35%67 and 7–13%68 for hematocrit and hemoglobin, respectively. Before treatments, hematocrit contents were between 27.30 and 27.80%, and hemoglobin contents were between 12.09 and 12.20%. After treatments, the ranges were between 29.15 and 29.30%, and 11.64 and 11.96%, for hematocrit and hemoglobin, respectively. The use of GM rice bran in chicken feed also did not show significant differences in the white blood cell, lymphocyte, monocyte, heterophil, eosinophil and basophil count (P > 0.05). The white blood cell counts were within the reported reference range for healthy chickens (7–32 × 103/μL). The lymphocyte (20–65%), monocyte (2–5%), heterophil (20–75%), eosinophil (1–4%) and basophil (0–6%) percentages were within their respective reference ranges.77,78 The results revealed that GM rice bran did not alter the leucocytes profiles indicating the absence of infection or inflammatory responses.

The heterophil and lymphocyte ratio (H/L) can be used as an indicator of stress in fowl. The higher the H/L ratio, the more stressful the fowl. Fowl with decreased heterophil and increased lymphocyte contents tend to be in cozy states.69 The feeding studies showed that H/L ratios among treatments and controls were not significantly different, indicating no observable stress among them.

Liver and kidney functions

Liver plays roles in many processes, such as detoxification, carbohydrate metabolism, chemical synthesis of nutrients, blood filtration, biosynthesis of amylase, and thermoregulation.70, 71 Liver can regenerate to maintain its function when exposed to a moderate concentration of toxin. However, it can undergo necrosis when exposed to an intolerable concentration of toxin. The damage affects the liver functions and increases the blood serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase (SGPT) and serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT) levels.

Kidneys are vital organs that filter blood, remove waste, and regulate fluid balance in the body. They clean toxins and waste (urea, creatinine, and acids) out of the blood. Kidneys are the most vulnerable organ against toxins since they filter roughly 25–30% of blood in circulation. The decline in kidney function can be detected by the increase presence of urea and creatinine in the blood.

There was no significant difference (p > 0.05) observed in the liver functions between different feed treatments and the control, as shown by the observed blood SGPT and SGOP concentrations (p > 0.005) after 30 days of treatments (Table 5). Likewise, no significant difference was observed (p > 0.05) in kidney functions by observing the urea and creatinine contents. Discussion

Broiler chicken is one of the most cultivated chicken races due to its high productivity. In addition, broiler chickens are also widely used in feeding studies.29, 30, 46, 47, 48, 50, 74, 75 Although genetically superior, they still require good feed composition to grow to their potential. In Indonesia, the chicken feed composition has been standardized by the Indonesian National Standard (SNI 01–3931-2006). Rice bran, as a source of amino acids, is among the highest components in chicken feed in Indonesia, in which allowable contents range from 5 to 15%.63 The feed compositions used in this experiment had no significant difference in terms of their nutritional contents (Table 2) and were in agreement with the Indonesian National Standard. The 10% rice bran content included was in accordance with SNI 01–3931-2006, while the inclusion levels of 6% and 8% GM rice bran were used to evaluate feed performance across a range of practical dietary inclusion levels. The 10% GM rice bran treatment served as the most direct comparator to the 10% non-GM control diet (Table 1). The quality of the nutritional contents among the feed compositions can be determined by the body weight gain, final weight after 30 days of treatment, mortality, and FCR (Table 3). The average body weight gain of all treatments was approximately 49 g/day, which was consistent with the previous report for broiler chicken (48.65 g/day).76 The mortality rates, which were between 4% and 6%, were also not significantly different among treatments (Table 3), indicating that the GM rice bran did not cause an increase in mortality rates. FCR was calculated to estimate the efficiency of nutrition conversion to chicken productivity (body weight). Table 3 shows that there were no significant changes in FCR among different treatments, indicating that the use of GM rice bran in the feed composition did not change the ability of nutrition to be absorbed by chickens for body development. The results indicated that the nutritional composition of the feed formulations with GM rice bran was comparable to that of the control formulation. However, direct compositional comparison between GM and non-GM rice bran needs to be evaluated in the future study.

Hematology responses are important to diagnose changes in physiological condition, such as due to injury, infections, and changes in physiological function, such as due to stress.77, 78 The results from the hematology observations in all parameters tested indicated no significant difference in the hematology responses among treatments (Table 4). All parameters observed 30 days after treatment were also still within the reference ranges.79, 80 Their contents in all treatments and controls were also within the reference ranges. The normal contents of white blood cells in chickens are in the range of 7–32 × 103/μL, which are composed of 20–65% lymphocyte, 2–5% monocyte, 20–75% heterophil, 1–4% eosinophil, and 0–6% basophil. The results indicated no defect in the red blood cell production and no changes in the ability to bind oxygen. The normal amount of white blood cell produced indicated that there was no infection or inflammation.

The ratios of heterophil (H) to lymphocyte (L) can be used as a stress indicator in fowl. The higher the H/L ratio, the higher the stress experienced by the fowl. On the other hand, fowl in a relaxed state tends to have a lower H/L ratio, indicated by a decrease in heterophil and an increase in lymphocyte.69 The observation on the chickens treated with and without the GM rice bran showed no significant difference in the H/L ratios, indicating that the levels of stress among treatments are the same. No significant differences were observed in the hematological responses of chickens fed GM and non-GM diets. The absence of significant hematological alterations suggested that the inclusion of GM rice bran in the diet did not negatively impact the blood parameters evaluated during the study period.

Liver function was not different among all feed treatments as evidenced by serum glutamate-pyruvate transaminase (SGPT) and serum glutamate-oxaloacetate transaminase (SGOT) (Table 5). Blood SGPT and SGOT levels are commonly used to assess liver damage.81 In general, elevated enzyme levels are associated with impaired liver functions. Elevated SGPT and SGOT may be indicative of hepatotoxicity. In the GM rice bran treatments and the non-GM control, no significant differences were observed. The standard deviations for SGPT and SGOT were relatively high but the individual values were all within the reported physiological reference ranges for broiler chickens. No significant differences were detected among treatment groups (P > 0.05). The variability seen is more likely to be a result of normal biological variation between individual birds rather than being related to the treatment. These results indicate that there were no toxic compounds in GM rice bran that could increase blood SGPT or SGOT levels. The blood SGPT and SGOT levels were within the reported reference ranges.82

There was no significant difference in the blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels among the treatment groups (Table 5). Kidney normal function can be estimated by the levels of blood urea nitrogen and creatinine. The increased levels of blood urea nitrogen and creatinine may indicate kidney failure. The blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels in this study were not significantly different indicating that the inclusion of GM rice bran in the diet did not have an adverse effect on the markers of kidney function measured in this study. The levels of blood urea nitrogen and creatinine in all treatments were still within the reference range.83

There was no significant difference among the parameters observed to evaluate feed digestibility (Table 6). The digestibility of crude protein, crude fat, and crude fiber of all feed compositions was similar. Likewise, the energy digestibility coefficient and NFE were not significantly different among all treatments. The results indicated that the GM rice bran did not alter feed digestibility. All parameters observed were consistent with those previously performed similar experiments on broiler chickens exposed to different cry toxins, such as GM cotton containing the cry-X gene (cry1Ac and cry2Ab genes), GM corn with the mcry1Ac gene, and GM rice expressing Cry1ab/1Ac protein.

Statistical analysis showed no significant differences (P > 0.05) in the average content of crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, NFE, and digestible energy between the digested GM feed and the non-GM control feed from before to after treatments. The results suggest that the digested GM feed exhibited no difference in percentages of ingested and absorbed nutrients compared with the control non-GM feed. Feed digestibility reflects the ease and efficiency with which an animal can absorb and utilize nutrients. Feed digestibility is influenced by the feed composition and processing, in addition to the individual animal's physiology.84 Based on the results, it may be inferred that the broiler chickens absorbed and utilized nutrients from the GM feed at the same rate and efficiency as those from the control non-GM feed. The results indicated that there were no detectable adverse effects of dietary GM rice bran on the nutrient digestibility parameters evaluated in broiler chickens during the study period. Although the present study was limited to a 30-day feeding period, this duration represents the main commercial production phase of broiler chickens and is generally accepted for the evaluation of feed performance, physiological responses and feed safety. However, longer term or multigenerational studies would add further information on the possible chronic or cumulative effects.

There was no significant difference in growth performance, mortality, feed conversion ratio, hematological responses, liver and kidney function indicators, or nutrient digestibility of broiler chickens fed with diets containing Cry1B-Cry1Aa GM rice bran when compared with the control diet. These results are consistent with previous feeding studies using GM crops expressing a range of Bt toxins.29, 30, 31, 46, 47, 48, 50, 74, 75, 85, 86, 87, 88, 89 However, the present study was limited to growth performance, some selected clinical pathology parameters and nutrient digestibility. No direct compositional comparison of GM and non-GM rice bran, quantification of Cry1B-Cry1Aa proteins, assessment of exposure, organ pathology, histopathological examination, gut microbiota analysis and detection of transgene DNA in animal tissues were included. Therefore, the results should be interpreted in relation to the parameters assessed.

The objective of the present study was to evaluate the nutritional performance and safety of broiler chickens fed with diets containing GM rice bran under practical feeding conditions. This feeding study on broiler chickens using GM rice bran has several limitations. The trial was conducted on a single strain (Cobb), which may limit the applicability of results to other commercial or indigenous breeds. The study duration was restricted to the standard broiler growth cycle, preventing assessment of long-term health or reproductive effects. The experimental design relied on controlled farm conditions, which may not fully reflect diverse field environments where feed quality and management vary. Biochemical analyses focused on selected hematological and organ function parameters, leaving other physiological responses unexplored.

Critical assessments such as direct compositional analysis of GM versus non-GM rice bran, quantification of Cry1B–Cry1Aa protein levels, dietary exposure estimation, gross necropsy, organ weight measurement, histopathological examination, gut microbiota profiling, and detection of transgene DNA in animal tissues were not included. These methodological constraints should be carefully considered when interpreting the results and drawing broader conclusions from the study. Furthermore, these limitations suggest that future research should include extended monitoring periods with broader physiological, biochemical and molecular assessments under varied production systems to strengthen the generalizability and robustness of the findings.

Conclusions

The feed composition analyses showed no significant difference in nutritional composition between GM-containing feeds and the non-GM control. The inclusion of GM rice bran expressing cry1B::cry1A in the DOC's diet for 30 days did not significantly affect growth performance, hematological parameters, liver and kidney functions parameters, and nutrient digestibility. The physiological and biochemical responses of the broiler chickens fed GM rice bran diets were similar to those of chickens fed with the control diet. These results suggest that the addition of rice bran, from GM Cry1B-Cry1Aa rice, did not show adverse effects within the parameters and duration evaluated.
