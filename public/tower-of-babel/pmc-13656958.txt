Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor 1 Regulates Colonic Mesenchymal Cell Diversity and the Epithelial Stem Cell Niche
Authors: Safina Gadeock, Nandini Girish, Cambrian Y. Liu, Ying Huang, Barun Das, Tracy C. Grikscheit, D. Brent Polk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsTumor necrosis factor receptor 1 regulates intestinal epithelial survival and repair, yet its role in the colonic mesenchyme remains unclear. We investigated how mesenchymal tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 signaling controls stromal cell identity, stem cell niche function, and epithelial homeostasis.MethodsColonic mesenchymal populations from germline tumor necrosis factor receptor 1–deficient (TNFR1−/−) and mesenchymal-specific Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl mice were analyzed using immunostaining, Western blotting, bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing, and organoid–mesenchymal coculture systems. Functional studies included wound healing assays, recombinant R-spondin 3 rescue, and integrin A6 blockade.ResultsTumor necrosis factor receptor 1 deletion altered mesenchymal composition, reducing platelet derived growth factor receptor α expression while increasing α smooth muscle actin expression in vivo and in vitro. Single-cell RNA sequencing demonstrated loss of mesenchymal heterogeneity following tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 deletion, including depletion of a distinct tumor necrosis factor– and interferon-responsive mesenchymal subpopulation enriched for Mmp9, Cd200, Traf1, and Tnfrsf9. Transcriptomic profiling revealed downregulation of stem cell niche factors including Rspo3, Wnt2b, and Fgf2, alongside increased extracellular matrix and proliferative signaling pathways, particularly integrin A6, phosphoinositide 3-kinase, mitogen-activated protein kinase, and Ras-associated protein 1 signaling. Mesenchymal tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 deletion impaired epithelial proliferation and reduced Lgr5+ and Ephb3+ intestinal stem cell expression in vivo. In organoid coculture systems, tumor necrosis factor receptor 1–deficient mesenchymal cells suppressed epithelial stem cell marker expression and altered colonoid morphology, effects which were rescued by recombinant R-spondin 3. Integrin A6 blockade restored platelet derived growth factor receptor α and R-spondin 3 expression and normalized proliferative signaling and migration in tumor necrosis factor receptor 1–deficient myofibroblasts. Similarly, tumor necrosis factor receptor 1–dependent regulation of integrin A6, platelet derived growth factor receptor α, and R-spondin 3 was observed in human colonic myofibroblasts.ConclusionsMesenchymal tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 preserves colonic mesenchymal diversity, sustains R-spondin 3–dependent stem cell niche signaling, and maintains intestinal stem cell homeostasis.

SummaryTumor necrosis factor–tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 signaling by pericryptal platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha+ mesenchymal cells maintain colonic leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 5+ epithelial stem cell numbers, overall colonic mesenchymal cell diversity, and a distinct sub-population of pericryptal mesenchymal cells enriched for tumor necrosis factor and interferon signaling genes. What You Need to Know

Background

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling has dual roles in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), driving both epithelial damage and wound repair. This study examined how tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1) on mesenchymal cells shapes the epithelial stem cell niche, using TNFR1-deficient mice, single-cell RNA-sequencing, and organoid-mesenchymal cocultures.

Impact

This work identifies mesenchymal TNFR1 as a key regulator of the stem cell niche, revealing that TNF signaling supports epithelial regeneration not only through direct effects on the epithelium but also indirectly through the surrounding mesenchyme. By demonstrating that this niche-support function is reversible, the study reframes how TNF-targeted therapies may influence mucosal healing in IBD, highlighting the mesenchymal compartment as a previously underappreciated determinant of treatment response and a potential avenue for improving outcomes in patients who fail to heal on current anti-TNF therapies.

Future Directions

Future studies should test whether this axis is disrupted in human IBD (especially anti-TNF nonresponders), explore niche factor–based adjuncts to anti-TNF therapy, and map which fibroblast subpopulations drive this support.

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs) are associated with elevated circulating and mucosal levels of cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), a central mediator of disease pathogenesis.1 TNF’s pathogenic role is underscored by the initial success of anti-TNF monoclonal antibodies, which reduce symptoms and promote mucosal healing in patients with ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s disease.1 However, despite these early clinical benefits, the majority of patients lose response, reflecting limitations in our understanding of how anti-TNF therapies work and why they fail.1,2

TNF exerts its effects through 2 receptors, tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR)1 (p55, Tnfrsf1a) and TNFR2 (p75, Tnfrsf1b). TNFR1 is widely expressed throughout the intestinal mucosa, including in epithelial and stromal compartments, whereas TNFR2 is primarily expressed on immune cell subsets and is upregulated in epithelial cells in human IBD and in mouse models of colitis.2, 3, 4 TNF signaling is highly pleiotropic: it drives proinflammatory pathways that promote autoimmune tissue damage and apoptosis,5, 6, 7 yet paradoxically it can also activate programs supporting cell survival,8 epithelial regeneration,9,10 and immunologic tolerance that limit colonic inflammation.11 These dual functions, together with evidence that colitis is exacerbated in multiple models of TNF pathway ablation12,13 and the clinical observation that anti-TNF therapy fails in most patients,4 illustrate the complexity of TNF biology in IBD. Despite its central role, the tissue- and cell-specific contributions of TNFR1 and TNFR2 to intestinal homeostasis and disease remain poorly understood, limiting our ability to predict or improve therapeutic outcomes. A more precise understanding of how TNF signaling operates in distinct intestinal compartments is therefore critical to decipher both its pathogenic and protective functions and to guide strategies for more effective, targeted interventions.

Mesenchymal stromal cells have been implicated in several processes central to mucosal healing14 and IBD-associated inflammation.15 The colonic myofibroblast population, which harbors a specialized pericryptal mesenchymal subpopulation, provides critical molecular cues (eg, Wnt ligands, transforming growth factors, insulin growth factors, Notch ligands, epidermal growth factors, and Bmp-type molecules) that maintain the self-renewal of crypt stem cells and guide the proper differentiation of epithelial cells along the crypt vertical axis.14,16 During epithelial wound healing, mesenchymal cells secrete noncanonical Wnt ligands that drive crypt morphogenesis and cell proliferation.17,18 Thus, the function of mesenchymal cells is intimately associated with epithelial regenerative capacity in homeostasis and during states of challenge. Whether cytokines associated with IBD might regulate mesenchymal cell behaviors, and consequently the epithelial stem cell pool, remains to be determined, as it is primarily considered that cytokines act cell-autonomously on epithelium. In fact, recent single-cell profiling has demonstrated that the colonic mesenchyme undergoes significant remodeling in IBD,15,19, 20, 21 which would suggest that the inflammatory milieu has a profound ability to regulate the specification and function of colonic myofibroblasts.

Here, we hypothesized that TNF signaling through TNFR1 regulates the function and niche-supporting capacity of colonic myofibroblasts. Using a PDGFRαCre-mediated TNFR1 deletion model, which predominantly targets mesenchymal populations, together with single-cell transcriptional profiling and mesenchymal–epithelial coculture assays, we found that TNFR1 signaling contributes to the maintenance of colonic myofibroblast populations and supports epithelial stem cell maintenance in the colon. These findings suggest that stromal TNFR1 signaling is an important regulator of epithelial homeostasis and raise the possibility that similar pathways may be modulated during anti-TNF therapy.

Results

Mesenchymal Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor 1 Deletion Alters Colonic Mesenchymal Cell Marker Expression

TNFR1 promotes intestinal epithelial cell survival,8 stem cell proliferation,10 colonic barrier integrity, mucosal immune cell function, and repair pathways to restrict IBD-associated inflammation.11 Its role in the intestinal mesenchyme, however, remains unknown. To investigate the role of TNFR1 in mesenchymal cell specification and function, we compared mesenchymal cell populations in the colonic submucosa of TNFR1−/− and control (TNFR1+/−) mice. The colonic submucosa is composed of a heterogeneous mixture of cell types, including endothelial cells, immune/inflammatory cells, and a diverse population of mesenchymal cells. Mesenchymal subtypes can be classified by the expression of markers such as α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA), vimentin (VIM), platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRα), GLI family zinc finger 1 (GLI1), and/or forkhead box L1 (FOXL1), which collectively identify pericryptal fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and other stromal populations. We characterized mesenchymal cell populations using immunostaining and quantitative analysis, supported by Western blotting to confirm marker expression at the protein level. In TNFR1−/− mouse submucosa compared with littermate controls, we found a shift in mesenchymal cell markers—a 40% reduction in VIM along with a 58% increase in αSMA expression (Figure 1AI, AII, BI, and BII). Pericryptal mesenchymal cells, previously shown to serve as a stem cell niche,22 here identified a 30% reduction in expression of PDGFRα in TNFR1−/− mice (Figure 1AIII and BIII) compared with littermate controls. To determine if the specific expression of TNFR1 on the subepithelial mesenchyme alters specification of mesenchymal populations, we generated Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl animals (Figure 1C) to ablate TNFR1 in subepithelial PDGFRα+ cells. We show that, although αSMA+ cells increased by 40%, pericryptal PDGFRα+ cells were downregulated by 25% in Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl animals relative to TNFR1fl/fl and Pdgfra-Cre controls (Figure 1D).

We next asked whether key mesenchymal subpopulations could be recovered in vitro. Colonic mesenchymal cells from TNFR1−/− and control (TNFR1+/−) mice were cultured for 5 days in 3-dimensional conditions. The resulting cultures were immunostained for mesenchymal markers, including VIM, αSMA, PDGFRα, GLI1, and FOXL1 (Figure 1E) with cultures demonstrating partially overlapping colonic myofibroblasts populations expressing all markers. Importantly, consistent with observations in TNFR1−/− mice in vivo, the proportion of PDGFRα+ mesenchymal cells within colonic myofibroblasts was reduced by 21% in TNFR1−/−-derived mesenchymal cells compared with control (Figure 1E). Western blot analysis of mesenchymal cell homogenates confirmed increased αSMA and decreased VIM expression in TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts (Figure 1E). These findings indicate that the in vitro culture system recapitulates key features of TNFR1-dependent regulation of colonic myofibroblast composition, supporting its use to study mesenchymal cell identity and function.

Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor 1 Is Required to Preserve Colonic Mesenchymal Cell Diversity, Including a Novel Subpopulation Defined by Tumor Necrosis Factor and Interferon Signaling Genes

Because we determined that both germline and PDGFRα-specific deletion of TNFR1 results in significant alterations of mesenchymal cell expression profiles in vivo and in vitro, we hypothesized that TNFR1 regulates colonic myofibroblast populations. To test this prediction, we isolated and expanded myofibroblasts from 3 independent littermate TNFR1+/− (control) and TNFR1−/− mice, followed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). All cultures were prepared and sequenced concurrently as a single batch, generating >34,000 cells across 6 samples (Figure 2A). Clustering identified 6 distinct cell populations (Figure 2AI). TNFR1 deficiency substantially reduced mesenchymal heterogeneity, with 4 control clusters collapsing into 2 TNFR1-deficient clusters (Figure 2AII). Control-specific clusters (2, 3, 5, and 6) were absent, whereas TNFR1-deficient–specific clusters (1 and 4) emerged consistently across all cultures, indicating that TNFR1 loss reshapes colonic myofibroblast cell type specification and promotes novel mesenchymal populations. We next mapped mesenchymal markers across clusters. Krt8+ epithelial cells were nearly absent, confirming minimal epithelial contamination (Figure 2B). Clusters 2, 3, 5, and 6 expressed primary TNF receptors, whereas Tnfrsf1a expression was reduced in TNFR1-deficient cluster 1 (Figure 2B). TNFR1-deficient cluster 1 showed elevated extracellular matrix remodeling genes, including Fbln5, Ccl7, Col12a1, Col5a1, Col4a1, and Col5a2, whereas TNF signaling–associated genes enriched in control cluster 6, including Jun, NfkBia, Nfkbb2, Mmp9, Tnfrsf9, Pdgfra, and Traf1, were largely depleted (Figure 2B). Clusters 2 (control) and 4 (TNFR1-deficient) lacked distinct molecular signatures and primarily expressed general mesenchymal markers, including Actb and Vim; despite passing quality control, they were excluded from further analysis. Clusters 1, 3, 5, and 6 expressed colonic myofibroblast markers, including Pdgfra, Gata6, Col1a1, Fn1, Wnt5a, Gli1, and Foxl1, supporting the presence of distinct myofibroblast subpopulations. Notably, control cluster 6, defined by unique markers absent in TNFR1-deficient cultures, indicated a TNFR1-dependent mesenchymal population (Figure 2B and C). To further define cluster relationships, we identified cluster-specific markers and the associated signaling pathways. TNFR1-deficient cluster 1 contained 190 markers, including Meg3, Crlf1, and Fbln5, linked to epithelial mesenchymal transition, myogenesis, UV response downregulation, and cGMP signaling (Figure 2B and C). Control cluster 3 contained only 18 markers, including shared expressions of Col1a1 and Fbln2 with clusters 1 and 6 (Figure 2B and D), preventing meaningful pathway enrichment analysis. Control cluster 5 was characterized by 293 markers, including Smim1 and Ctla2a, but showed no significantly enriched pathways (q < 0.05). In contrast, cluster 6 expressed 190 unique genes, including Mmp9, Cd200, and Traf1, associated with transcriptional responses to TNF, interferon (IFN), and other cytokines (Figure 2B–D).

The loss of distinct cluster 6 markers in TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts, identified by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and gene clustering in Figure 2B and D, was confirmed in TNFR1−/− animals. These markers were selected based on their high magnitude of upregulation in cluster 6 of control colonic myofibroblasts, and their near-complete depletion in TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts. By immunostaining, there was a 70% reduction in MMP9+; 89% in CD200+; 37% in TRAF1+; and 53% in TNFRSF9+ cells in TNFR1−/− animals relative to controls (Figure 2E). Consistently a reduction in MMP9+ subepithelial mesenchymal cells was seen in Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl mice relative to TNFR1fl/fl and Pdgfra-Cre controls (Figure 2F), demonstrating a specific role of pericryptal mesenchymal TNFR1 expression in the regulation of the cluster 6 cells. Thus, cluster 6 represents a specialized population of mesenchymal cells distinguished by enrichment of TNF signaling pathways. In the absence of TNFR1, this cluster fails to emerge, and the overall colonic myofibroblasts population is correspondingly less diverse. Thus, TNFR1 regulates the colonic homeostatic mesenchymal cell census and its diversification into specialized subpopulations. Importantly, TNFR1 on PDGFRα+ mesenchymal cells seem to determine the presence of subepithelial mesenchymal cells that are a hub for TNF, IFN, and other cytokine signaling.

Transcriptome Profiling of Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor 1–Deficient Colonic Myofibroblasts Identifies Upregulation of Integrin A6 but Loss of the Epithelial Stem Cell Niche Factor R-Spondin 3

Although single-cell experiments enable profiling of mesenchymal cell diversity, they can miss crucial gene products expressed at low levels. To distinguish TNFR1−/− from control colonic myofibroblasts, we therefore investigated expression via bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis using cultured primary myofibroblasts. Bulk and single-cell transcriptome analyses of primary mouse TNFR1-depleted colonic myofibroblasts identified disruptions in cytokine signaling pathways across subepithelial mesenchymal clusters, consistent with patterns observed in human ulcerative colitis mesenchyme.19 A Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes showed that the loss of TNFR1 alters many biologically significant processes including anchoring and cell substrate junctions, focal adhesions, extracellular matrix, and actin binding processes (Figure 3A). One gene relevant to enhanced matrix signaling, Itga6, was significantly upregulated in TNFR1−/− compared with control colonic myofibroblasts (Figure 3B, CI and II). Furthermore, we found a specific reduction in key stem cell niche factors such as Rspo3, Wnt2b, and Fgf2 in TNFR1-deficient colonic myofibroblasts (Figure 3B). Notably, Rspo3, crucial for leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 5 (Lgr5)+ stem cell maintenance,22,23 was downregulated 5.1-fold in TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts (Figure 3B). In vivo, we found that, in germline TNFR1-deficient mice (Figure 3B), similar to specific targeted deletion of TNFR1 in PDGFRA+ mouse cells (Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl animals), R-spondin 3 (Rspo3) levels were reduced in the pericryptal mesenchyme (Figure 1C III). Interestingly, in contrast to the shifts in basal crypt stem cell niche factors, no major differences were observed in the transcript levels of “upper crypt” (ie, differentiated) niche factors24 such as Wnt5a, Bmp2, and Bmp4 (Figure 3D). Several transcripts or pathways associated with wound healing, including Tgfb2, Hbegf, Ctgf, Ptgs2, Itga6, and Il6, and with proliferation and differentiation (eg, phosphoinositide 3-kinase [PI3K], mitogen-activated protein kinase [MAPK], Ras-associated protein 1 [RAP1], transforming growth factor β, and RAS) were upregulated in TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts, whereas TNF signaling genes associated with immunity to microbial infections were downregulated (Mmp3, Cebpb, Icam1, Socs3)13 (Figure 3E–G). Moreover, we observed upregulation of Jag1 and several other genes (eg, Ccl2, Csf1r, Irf1 and Irf7) involved in mucosal repair and in adaptive immune response (Figure 3F). Thus, deletion of TNFR1 from colonic myofibroblasts alters mesenchymal cell–matrix interactions while downregulating stem cell–associated niche transcript expression and mucosal immunity.

Loss of Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor 1 in Platelet-Derived Growth Factor Receptor α+ Mesenchymal Cells Impairs Intestinal Stem Cell Expression and Epithelial Proliferation

We have shown previously that targeted TNFR1 deletion in the intestinal mucosa results in an altered colonic mucosal phenotype with increased neutrophil accumulation, decreased epithelial proliferation, increased DNA damage, and crypt abscess.11 Here, we show that the TNFR1-deletion phenotype relative to TNFR1+/− controls is also characterized by alterations in mesenchymal cell expression patterns. Selective deletion of TNFR1 in the PDGFRα+ mesenchymal cell lineage recapitulates increased neutrophil accumulation, decreased epithelial proliferation, and increased DNA damage in Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl animals relative to TNFR1fl/fl and Pdgfra-Cre controls (Figure 3A), Thus, cell-specific TNFR1 deletion in PDGFRα+ cells in vivo selectively alters the colonic mesenchymal cell census.

Because epithelial proliferation is maintained by stromal support of intestinal stem cells, we next investigated whether the proliferation defect in Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl mice was associated with impaired stem cell maintenance. Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl animals demonstrated a significant decrease in Lgr5+ (Figure 4BI) and Ephb3+ (Figure 4BII) stem cells. Similarly, we compared the colons of TNFR1−/− mice with their heterozygote littermate controls (TNFR1+/−). Consistently, there was a 44% reduction in Lgr5+ stem numbers and a ∼2-fold decrease in Lgr5 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression (Figure 4CI) in TNFR1−/− animals. Similar results were obtained from an analysis of Ephb3 expression (Figure 4CII). Together, these findings demonstrate that TNFR1 signaling in PDGFRα+ mesenchymal cells is required to maintain intestinal stem cell expression. We next asked whether RSPO3 was sufficient to restore functional support of Lgr5+ epithelial cells in the presence of TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts. Colonoids from Lgr5::DTR-EGFP mice were cocultured with TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts supplemented with increasing concentrations of recombinant RSPO3 (r-RSPO3) for 5 days. Colonoids treated with 5 ng r-RSPO3 showed visibly more crypt-like budding structures compared with untreated controls (Figure 5C) and restored expression to Lgr5 and Ephb3 to control levels (Figure 5D). However, treatment with higher RSPO3 concentrations (50 ng) reduced the number of crypt-like budding structures, with Lgr5–green fluorescent protein expression becoming diffusely distributed throughout the organoid rather than concentrated in budding regions. Concurrently, the levels of Lgr5 and Ephb3 were also reduced in these colonoids. To confirm the specificity of r-RSPO3 in restoring stem cell marker expression, control colonic myofibroblasts were treated with an anti-RSPO3 antibody, and stem cell expression in organoid cocultures was measured. Anti-RSPO3 inhibited Lgr5 and Ephb3 expression in a dose-dependent manner, such that at the 0.05-μg dose, the levels of Lgr5 and Ephb3 expression are comparable to those found in cocultures with TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts (Figure 5E and F). Therefore, RSPO3 rescues the loss of mesenchymal TNFR1 signaling by maintaining epithelial stem cells, preserving their marker expression and functional activity.

Integrin α6 (ITGA6) is a laminin-binding adhesion receptor expressed on colonic mesenchymal and stem-like cells, where it regulates adhesion, migration, and proliferation27; given these functions, we next investigated the role of ITGA6 in controlling proliferation and migration of TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts. TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts were treated with an anti-ITGA6 blocking antibody to determine if it rescued its altered proliferation-associated pathways and migratory capacity. Blocking ITGA6 restored ERK and RAP1 signaling (Figure 6C), and normalized wound closure rates (Figure 6D), demonstrating that ITGA6 mediates the enhanced proliferative and migratory phenotype of TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts. Because the crosstalk between integrins and PDGFRs is essential to effective growth factor signaling,27 we next assessed ITGA6’s role in stem cell niche function. Anti-ITGA6 antibody treatment of TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts restored the numbers of PDGFRα+ cells, expression of PDGFRα protein, and expression of RSPO3 to near control levels (Figure 6E). These results indicate that elevated ITGA6 in TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts contributes to their enhanced proliferation, migration, and maintenance of PDGFRα+ cells, highlighting ITGA6 as a key regulator of mesenchymal function in the absence of TNFR1.

To determine whether these findings translate to the human intestinal mesenchyme, we treated human primary 18Co myofibroblasts with 0.5 μg of anti-TNFR1 antibody to block receptor signaling. Treated cells exhibited significant increase in the transcript levels of ITGA6 and a downregulation of PDGFRA and RSPO3 (Figure 6F I), similar to mouse TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts. Furthermore, we showed that ITGA6 and RSPO3 colocalize in human primary 18Co myofibroblasts and that treatment of these cells with the anti-TNFR1 antibody reduced the balance of RSPO3/ITGA6 expression (Figure 6F II). Therefore, TNFR1 regulates ITGA6 and the stem cell niche factors, PDGFRα and RSPO3, in both mouse and human colonic mesenchymal cells. Hence, in summary, we show that mesenchymal TNFR1 is required for preserving colonic mesenchymal diversity, regulating RSPO3 dependent pericryptal niche signaling, and maintaining the intestinal stem cell compartment. Loss of TNFR1 disrupts mesenchymal heterogeneity, compromises stem cell support, and is partially mediated through ITGA6-associated dysregulation.

Discussion

The profusion of therapies that inhibit TNF signaling in the context of autoimmunity necessitates a fuller understanding of their potential physiological impact. This is a critical need due to the high rates of clinical nonresponse and loss of response in IBD.4 Studies have demonstrated protective and physiological roles of TNF in IBD8, 9, 10, 11 and reported new-onset IBD with the use of anti-TNF agents,28 consistent with possible anti-inflammatory and/or reparative roles for TNF in the intestine. Although previous studies have supported a role for TNF signaling in the maintenance of the epithelium and healing responses in murine intestinal inflammation models, the mechanisms have remained unknown and have been thought to be epithelial cell–autonomous.8, 9, 10, 11 Our study shows that TNFR1-mediated signaling is central to the proper specification of colonic pericryptal mesenchymal cells and to the fundamental mesenchymal–epithelial paracrine axis that maintains the crypt stem cell pool. This cytokine signal is essential in shaping mesenchymal cellular diversity of the colon. Combined with recent work showing TNFR1’s role in mediating mucosal tolerance to the microbiome in preclinical IBD models,11 our study outlines a multimodal mechanism, with mesenchymal cells regulating the epithelial response to cytokines, through which TNFR1 signaling supports mucosal homeostasis. In this study, the genetic or antibody-mediated depletion of TNFR1 signaling had profound effects on mesenchymal cell function and epithelial stem cell census, key aspects of mucosal health. Future studies should explore how these intercellular networks are altered in patients treated with anti-TNF agents.

We report that mesenchymal TNFR1 is required to preserve the overall diversity of colonic mesenchymal populations at homeostasis, including a subpopulation of pericryptal cells that are uniquely enriched for TNF and IFN signaling genes. TNFR1 ablation in vivo alters the proportions of mesenchymal cell subpopulations. As there are several known markers of mesenchymal subpopulations with potential overlap, we performed single-cell RNA transcriptomics to precisely enumerate the diversity of colonic mesenchymal cell types. Moreover, as the “cytokine storm” of IBD is associated with broad changes in mesenchymal cell differentiation,15,19, 20, 21 we had anticipated that synergy between many cytokines could drive these changes. However, in single-cell experiments, we found that the deletion of a single cytokine receptor results in fundamental changes such that TNFR1−/− and TNFR1+/− cell clusters were mostly mutually exclusive. Although cluster exclusion can sometimes arise from batch effects, these analyses were performed as a single batch with independent cultures from cohoused littermate mice. Thus, we believe the results are faithful to the composition of the cultures; however, we cannot rule out elimination or modification, due to culturing, of certain cell types initially present in vivo. However, our analysis, on several occasions, demonstrates that in vitro cultures faithfully recapitulated the alterations in the major mesenchymal phenotypes observed in vivo, and this was further validated by both scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data. Multiple approaches confirmed that a mesenchymal cell type appears to be downregulated in TNFR1−/− mice. This cell type is located near the base of the crypt, expresses Mmp9 and Cd200 markers, and has a transcriptional profile with hallmarks of TNF signaling. The cell type appears to be a subset of PDGFRα+ cells and shares some similarity with an ulcerative colitis–associated mesenchymal cell type.15 Future studies will need to characterize how this cell type integrates with other colonic myofibroblast subpopulations in terms of hierarchy and niche function, as well as determine whether it has specific functions that distinguish it from other PDGFRα+ cells. Taken together, these results demonstrate that TNF is an essential driver of colonic myofibroblast specification and function.

Our study defines a novel pathway of TNFR1-driven repression of integrins (ITGA6), the result of which is differential alteration of cell proliferation and wound healing-associated pathways within the mesenchymal subpopulations. Moreover, we found that the TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts upregulating ITGA6 fail to perform 1 of their key functions: the secretion of sufficient niche-specific growth factors to sustain the epithelial stem cell pool.27 The effects of TNFR1 signaling on the stem cell niche converge on RSPO3 expression. Supplementation of TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts with exogenous RSPO3 in vitro restored the number of Lgr5+ stem cells to control levels. RSPO3, the most highly expressed intestinal Wnt ligand, is crucial for sustaining the Lgr5+ stem cell pool and is thought to be entirely produced by pericryptal niche cells.22,23,29 Consistent with our findings, PDGFRα+ pericryptal mesenchymal cells have been identified as a key source of RSPO323 and support epithelial architecture and the renewal of stem cells during both homeostasis and the injury response.30,31 Hence, the development of approaches to restore RSPO3-mediated signaling or inhibit ITGA6 may partially compensate for the effects of TNFR1 ablation or anti-TNF on mesenchymal–epithelial interactions and the stem cell niche.

The deletion of mesenchymal TNFR1 has profound effects on epithelial stem cells in vivo and in vitro. We show in Figure 4 that animals with global and mesenchymal-specific TNFR1 depletion exhibit a loss of Lgr5+ stem cells. That this occurs without attendant changes in crypt height suggests the presence of other homeostatic mechanisms to maintain the overall crypt surface area. The coordinated mechanism through which mesenchymal TNFR1 regulates the epithelium through the niche factor expression reinforces the need for cell culture systems that partially reconstitute these heterotypic cellular interactions. We believe the epithelium and mesenchyme coculture model demonstrated here is a good example of this. Coculture systems will become increasingly important for investigation of cytokine signaling pathways, as these components are expressed by multiple tissue types in the mucosa, and for resolution of the mechanistic basis of inflammatory diseases. Our findings also provide insight into the limitations of current anti-TNF therapies by showing that TNFR1 signaling in colonic mesenchymal cells is not only involved in inflammatory signaling but is also required to maintain mesenchymal diversity, RSPO3-dependent stem cell niche function, and epithelial homeostasis. Therefore, the broad inhibition of TNF/TNFR1 pathways may unintentionally disrupt protective mesenchymal-dependent repair pathways that are important for maintaining intestinal regeneration and mucosal healing.

Methods and Materials

All authors had access to the study data and reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Experimental Animals

The animals were cared for as per Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee rules and regulations at Children’s Hospital Los Angeles under internal protocol number 288 and University of California San Diego, S21028. Preceding dissection, the animals were humanely anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation. C57BL/6J-WT (cat# 000664), TNFR1−/− (cat# 002818), and Pdgfra-Cre (cat# 013148) mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. Lgr5::EGFP-IRES-CreERT2 (Fred de Sauvage, Genentech), and TNFR1fl/fl (George Kollias, Alexander Fleming Biomedical Sciences Research Center) mice were kindly gifted. Mesenchymal Pdgfra-Cre mice were crossed to TNFR1fl/fl mice to generate Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl animals.32 TNFR1 deletion in mesenchymal PDGFRα+ cells were confirmed by genotyping from 3 independent Pdgfra-Cre;TNFR1fl/fl, TNFR1fl/fl, and Pdgfra-Cre animals. To maintain proper experimental controls, littermate controls of transgenic mice were cohoused for >4 weeks prior to experimental collection and analysis. Adult male and female mice (6 at 12–16 weeks) were involved in the study.

Mice Tissue Collection

The colon and cecum were dissected from euthanized mice and cleared of fecal contents. For histology, the colon was Swiss-rolled and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Five-micrometer slices of the tissue were sectioned. The distal end of the colon (2 cm from the anus) was longitudinally split into half and processed for RNA and protein isolation and in the same manner for generation of organoids and primary mesenchymal cells.

Murine Three-Dimensional Organoid Culture

The distal colonic tissues isolated from control (TNFR1+/−), TNFR1−/−, or Lgr5::EGFP-IRES-CreERT2 mice were processed for digestion and crypt isolation as per previously established protocols.11,33 For passage, the organoids were broken mechanically by syringe disruption using a 27 G × 1/2 needle (Becton Dickinson, cat# 305109), then transferred to fresh Matrigel. The passage was performed every 5–8 days with a 1:4 split ratio, and the medium was changed after every 2 to 3 days. Each experiment was repeated at least 3 times. Each condition was examined in triplicate with multiple (>30–50) organoids in each sample.

Coculture Experiments

Isolation of primary colonic myofibroblasts

Primary mouse colonic myofibroblasts were isolated and maintained as described previously.16 Briefly, the primary colonic myofibroblasts isolated from the distal end of 16-week-old control (TNFR1+/−) and TNFR1−/− mice were repeatedly stirred at 250 rpm in 5 mM EDTA–Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution medium (without Ca++ and Mg++) at 37 °C for 15 minutes for a total of 5 washes. Following 2 washes of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the tissue was digested in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), low glucose, GlutaMAX Supplement, pyruvate (Gibco, cat# 10567014) plus penicillin–streptomycin (10,000 U/mL) with 0.31 mg/mL Dispase (Gibco, cat# 17105041) and 0.25 mg/mL collagenase Type XI (Gibco, cat# 17104019) for up to 60 minutes at 250 rpm at 37 °C, until the tissue appeared stringy. The digested tissue was treated with ACK lysis buffer (4.15 g NH4Cl, 0.5 g KHCO3, 18.6 mg Na2EDTA, 400 ml H2O, pH 7.2-7.4) for 5 minutes and then passed through a 100-μm cell strainer into 6-well dishes of colonic myofibroblasts growth medium. The primary cultures were maintained in a colonic myofibroblasts growth medium comprised of DMEM, low glucose, GlutaMAX Supplement, pyruvate (Gibco, cat# 10567014) plus 10,000 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (cat# 15140163) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Corning, cat# 35-010-CV) and 1X recombinant human insulin, human transferrin, and sodium selenite solution (Sigma, cat# I3146), and 20 ng/mL recombinant murine epidermal growth factor (Peprotech, cat# 315-09) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2/air mix. After a 24-hour incubation, the nonadherent cells were washed away. The colonic myofibroblasts were grown in a 2-dimensional culture for amplification of the cells for the main experimental setup. The control or TNFR1−/− primary colonic myofibroblasts were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells/mL in parallel in 6-well plates and were grown to 70% confluence. At day 5, the colonic myofibroblasts in 2-dimensional culture recovered similar numbers of mesenchymal populations (VIM+, αSMA+, PDGFRα+, GLI1+, or FOXL1+) as the colonic myofibroblasts in 3-dimensional culture.

Tumor necrosis factor treatment of primary colonic myofibroblasts

Control or TNFR1−/− primary colonic myofibroblasts were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells/mL in parallel in 6-well plates and were grown to 70% confluence. The cells were starved (devoid of FBS) for 24 hours in DMEM, low glucose, GlutaMAX Supplement, pyruvate medium plus 10,000 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin, before treatment with 1 ng, 10 ng, or 100 ng/mL TNF (R&D Systems, cat# 410-MT) for 20 minutes. The primary colonic myofibroblasts were collected for RNA or protein isolation thereafter.

Organoid and myofibroblasts coculture

Briefly, the passaged organoids were broken into single cells mechanically by passing through a syringe and needle and were mixed with the colonic myofibroblasts at a ratio of 10:1 into Matrigel in a 24-well plate. The cocultures were grown and maintained in 60% IntestiCult (STEMCELL, cat# 06005) and 40% colonic myofibroblasts growth medium at 37 °C. The identity and purity of the isolated primary colonic myofibroblasts was determined by immunofluorescence for different myofibroblast cell populations.

Genotypic recombination in coculture experiments

The effects of the TNFR1−/− mesenchymal genotype on the epithelium were determined by a series of recombination coculture experiments in which control or TNFR1−/− organoids were cocultured with control or TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts for 5 days as described above.

Coculture experiments with recombinant R-spondin 3 and anti–R-spondin 3 treatments

The Lgr5::EGFP-IRES-CreERT2 organoids cocultured with TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts were supplemented with increasing doses (5–50 ng/mL) of r-Rspo3 (R&D Systems, cat# 4120-RS) at beginning of the passage for 5 days to determine its effect on stem cells, relative to Lgr5::EGFP-IRES-CreERT2 organoids cocultured with control colonic myofibroblasts. Similarly, the Lgr5::EGFP-IRES-CreERT2 organoids cocultured with control colonic myofibroblasts were treated with an anti-RSPO3 antibody (R&D Systems, cat# AF35001) or its IgG isotype control (R&D Systems, cat# 5-001-A) in increasing doses of 0.005 to 5 μg at passage for 5 days to investigate its inhibition of stem cells relative to Lgr5::EGFP-IRES-CreERT2 organoids cocultured TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts. The cocultures were incubated for 10 minutes with 2 mg/mL FM4-64X (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# F34653), were diluted in PBS, and were subsequently imaged using a Zeiss LSM 710 Inverted Confocal Microscope (Zeiss). Subsequently, the cocultures were processed for RNA isolation for transcript quantification of stem cells.

Immunostaining

The immunostaining of paraffin-embedded native tissue and organoids as well as whole mounts of cocultures was carried out as per established protocols.4,25 The antibodies used in this study are listed in Tables 1 and 2. Pericryptal cells were defined as mesenchymal cells immediately adjacent to the crypt base. Only fully longitudinal, clearly separated crypts were analyzed. Wound healing assay

Wounds ∼1 mm in width were created by scratching confluent monolayers of control or TNFR1−/− primary colonic myofibroblasts with pipette tips obliquely cut (45° angle) with a sterile razor blade. Cells in suspension were removed by aspiration with PBS. Phase contrast images were taken at 0, 2, 4, 16, and 24 hours, after scratching, using a Leica DM IL LED inverted microscope (Leica) equipped with a 5×/0.12 NA objective and a digital camera. The wound closure areas were measured with ImageJ by subtracting the total amount (A) of grayscale pixel counted in the cell-free area remaining after 24 hours from the initial wound area; hence: “Relative wound area” [pixel] = Ainitial-AT hrs. For antibody blocking experiments, control or TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells/mL in 24-well plates and treated with an anti-ITGA6 antibody and its IgG isotype control at a concentration of 0.2 μg/mL for 0 to 16 hours at passage.

Human primary myofibroblast culture and anti–tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 experiments

The normal human primary colonic myofibroblast cell line, CCD-18Co (ATCC, cat# CRL-1459) was cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (ATCC, 30-2003), supplemented with nonessential amino acids, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 1500 mg/L sodium bicarbonate and 10% FBS with 1% penicillin–streptomycin (10,000 U/mL) and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2/air mix. The cultures were passaged every 5 days at a ratio of 1:3. To determine the optimal concentration for blocking TNFR1-mediated signaling in human mesenchymal cells, preliminary experiments using human control organoids determined that, in response to TNF, epithelial Iκβα is degraded at the highest concentration of 100 ng TNF at 1 hour (not shown). H-organoids subsequently exposed to increasing doses of H-anti–TNFR1 demonstrated that the levels of Iκβα degradation were restricted in a dose-dependent manner, with little to no effect on the total levels of TNFR1, relative to the IgG isotype control (not shown). Therefore, anti-TNFR1 at a concentration of 0.5 μg/mL effectively blocks TNFR1-mediated signaling. Thus, 18Co cells were treated with an anti-TNFR1 antibody and its IgG isotype control at a concentration of 0.5 μg/mL for 5 days at passage. The cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells/mL in 24-well plates in parallel for immunostaining and RNA collection.

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine assay for proliferation

Whole mounts of organoids or cocultures were stained with the Click-iT Plus EdU Alexa Fluor488 Imaging Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, cat# C10632) as per the manufacturer’s recommendations. For antibody blocking experiments, control or TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells/mL in 24-well plates and treated with an anti-ITGA6 antibody and its IgG isotype control at a concentration of 0.2 μg/mL for 72 hours at passage.

RNA sequencing

The control (TNFR1+/−) and TNFR1−/− primary colonic myofibroblasts (passage 6–8) were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells/50 μL of Matrigel into 24-well plates and were grown to 70% confluency. At day 5, the colonic myofibroblasts were extracted from Matrigel after a brief incubation at 4 °C via centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 5 minutes. The total RNA from primary colonic myofibroblasts was extracted using the Ambion PureLink RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# 12183025) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. mRNA transcripts from control and TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts were prepared for 2 × 75 bp sequencing on a HiSeq 4000 (Illumina). The library preparation and sequencing were performed by Children's Hospital Los Angeles Single Cell, Sequencing, and CyTOF Core Laboratory. The reads were first mapped to the latest UCSC transcript set using Bowtie2 version 2.1.0, and the gene expression level was estimated using RSEM v1.2.15. The trimmed mean of M-values method was used for the normalization of the raw count. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the edgeR program. Genes showing altered expression with P < .05 and more than 1.5-fold changes were considered differentially expressed. Cluster profiler was used for the Gene Ontology and pathway enrichment analysis.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Control (TNFR1+/−) and TNFR1−/− primary colonic myofibroblasts were prepared in a similar manner to RNA-seq. Following centrifugation, cells were reconstituted in 10 mL of HEPES-buffered saline and 1% bovine serum albumin solution and were passed through a 40-μm filter to suspend into single cells. To remove dead cells for the final flow, single-cell suspensions of control or TNFR1−/− colonic myofibroblasts were stained with the EasySep Dead Cell Removal (Annexin V) Kit (STEMCELL, cat# 17899) according to the manufacturer's guidelines. The live cells were quantified and reconstituted in 100 to 200 μL of HEPES-buffered saline and 1% bovine serum albumin solution. The samples with live cell counts of 95% and above only were processed for scRNA-seq. The transcript abundance measurements were normalized by the per-cell read total, log-transformed, and collapsed to principal components. The top 50 principal components were used for UMAP. The UMAP was used for clustering. Clustering was performed with the Leiden algorithm with a resolution parameter of 1e-5. The markers of each cluster were identified using the Jensen-Shannon specificity score. The significance of each marker was computed via linear regression, which provided an r2 value that could be used to screen the initial gene lists.

For pathway enrichment analysis, the transcript abundances of each cluster were collapsed as a per-cluster, per-gene mean. Genes with an expression level <0.2 were filtered out. Markers of each cluster were defined as a set of genes whose cluster expression was >2 times higher than their next highest expression level among the cell clusters. The resulting matrix file was used as input into enrichr. Because clusters 2 and 4 did not exhibit markers of enriched gene expression, these clusters were excluded from enrichr. The search for enriched pathways was run against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes and MSigDB HALLMARK target databases. Pathways with an enrichment-adjusted P value (q value) < .01 were considered significant and displayed.

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism software was used for statistical analyses. Data are presented as means with standard error of the mean. Analysis was carried out by either an unpaired Student t test or a 1-way analysis of variance, with a Šídáks post-hoc correction, as indicated in figure legends, where ∗P < .05; ∗∗P < .01; ∗∗∗P < .001; ∗∗∗∗ < .0001; and #P = .05 to .09.
