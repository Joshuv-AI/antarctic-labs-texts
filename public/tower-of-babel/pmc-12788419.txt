Knowledge, Practice, and Awareness of Self-Medication among Dental Outpatients: A Cross-Sectional Institutional Study
Authors: Jagath Kaushal Singaraju, Kannan Natarajan, Aditya Darsi, Sai Snigdha Mandava, Gowri Sankar Singaraju, Kamakshi Bhavagna Singaraju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Self-medication for dental issues is a prevalent public health concern, especially in resource-limited regions. Though it may provide temporary relief, unsupervised drug use risks misdiagnosis, antibiotic resistance, and organ damage.Objectives:To evaluate the Knowledge, Practice, and Awareness (KPA) of self-medication among dental outpatients at a teaching dental hospital in South India and identify key influencing factors.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional, interviewer-administered survey was conducted among 437 adult dental outpatients using a pre-validated structured questionnaire. Data collected included sociodemographic details, history of self-medication, drug types, sources, and related beliefs. Binary logistic regression was applied to identify predictors of self-medication, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.Results:Self-medication was reported by 75.5% of participants, with 45.8% using medication for dental issues in the past year. Toothache (68.6%) was the main symptom, and paracetamol was the most used drug. Pharmacies (43.2%) and informal sources (26.5%) were common providers. Only 28.2% were aware of drug names or side effects, and 35% recognized risks like antibiotic resistance. Primary education level emerged as a significant predictor (AOR = 3.52; 95% CI: 1.08–11.49; P = 0.037).Conclusion:Self-medication is highly prevalent among dental patients, particularly those with lower education levels. Public awareness is inadequate. Targeted education and tighter control on over-the-counter drug sales are needed to mitigate risks.

INTRODUCTION

Self-medication, as defined by the World Health Organization, is the use of medicines by individuals to treat self-recognized symptoms without professional guidance.[1] Though it may offer short-term relief, it carries risks such as misdiagnosis, treatment delays, adverse reactions, and contributes to rising antibiotic resistance.

Globally, dental self-medication is on the rise. A study from Pakistan reported a 76.4% prevalence, with paracetamol and amoxicillin commonly used.[2] Sohal et al.[3] found that toothache due to caries was a primary reason, with patients bypassing dental care for easily available OTC drugs. Similar trends were reported in Saudi Arabia (69%)[4] and Nigeria (over 60%),[5] driven by convenience, cost, and accessibility.

In India, the situation is similarly concerning. Kalyan et al. (2013)[6] reported widespread self-medication among dental students in South India. A North Indian study noted a 70.2% prevalence linked to fear, cost, and delays in accessing care.[7] Even among informed populations like students, self-medication remains normalized.[8] Ramesh et al. (2021)[9] recorded 64.7% prevalence in Tamil Nadu, with paracetamol being the most used. Surenthar et al. (2021) highlighted low awareness of drug risks and poor professional consultation rates.[10]

Although self-medication has been widely studied at national and regional levels, there is a lack of focused research specifically addressing dental self-medication behaviours in the South Indian population. The unique cultural, linguistic, and healthcare access characteristics in this region warrant localized investigations. Most existing studies either generalize findings across India or focus on medical self-care, with limited emphasis on dental-specific practices. Furthermore, there is insufficient data exploring how factors such as education level, urban–rural residence, and personal beliefs influence this behaviour. Given the potential risks of unregulated drug use, there is a pressing need to evaluate the knowledge, practice, and awareness of self-medication among dental outpatients in this region. This study, conducted in a tertiary care dental hospital serving a semi-urban population, aims to identify key predictors that contribute to this practice.

METHODOLOGY

Study design and setting

This was a descriptive, cross-sectional survey conducted among adult dental outpatients at a tertiary care dental hospital in South India. The study was carried out over a two-month period in 2024, targeting individuals attending the outpatient department (OPD) for dental complaints. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee (IEC) of Narayana Dental College and Hospital, Nellore (Ref No: IEC/NDCH/2024/MAY–AUG/P-66). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Confidentiality of all collected information was strictly maintained.

Sample size determination

A convenient sampling method was adopted wherein every consenting adult patient visiting the outpatient department during the study period and meeting the eligibility criteria was invited to participate.

Assuming a prevalence of self-medication among dental outpatients from a previous study[10] at 64.7% with a 95% confidence interval (Z = 1.96) and a 5% absolute margin of error (d = 0.05), the calculated sample size was 355. After accounting for a 20% non-response rate, the final target sample size was increased to 425 participants.

Study duration and population

The study was carried out for 2 months (September 2024 to October 2024). All adult patients (≥18 years) visiting the outpatient department during this period were considered eligible, provided they gave informed consent and were able to respond to the questionnaire in English or Telugu.

Study population and participant selection

Adult patients aged 18 years and above who visited the outpatient department (OPD) of the dental hospital for treatment were considered eligible for inclusion in the study. Participants were included if they were willing to participate voluntarily and were capable of understanding and providing informed consent. Individuals were excluded if they had any cognitive impairments that could affect their ability to respond accurately or if they were unable to comprehend the questionnaire language, which was available in English and Telugu.

A consecutive sampling technique was employed to recruit participants. Eligible individuals were approached systematically as they attended the OPD during routine clinic hours over the study period. This method ensured a pragmatic and unbiased recruitment strategy, capturing a representative sample of the outpatient dental population until the required sample size was achieved.

Data collection tool

The questionnaire was adapted from previously validated instruments used in Indian studies[8911] and modified to suit the local linguistic and cultural context of the target population. Content validity was established through expert review by three subject specialists in oral medicine, public health dentistry, and pharmacology, who assessed each item for relevance, clarity, and coverage.

The questionnaire was initially developed in English and then translated into Telugu using a forward–backward translation protocol to ensure semantic equivalence and cultural appropriateness. This method followed internationally accepted standards for cross-cultural adaptation[12] and has been successfully used in Indian settings, such as in the validation of pharmacy service satisfaction tools in regional languages.[13] The tool underwent forward–backward translation from English to Telugu and back into English by bilingual experts, following World Health Organization translation guidelines.[1] Discrepancies were resolved by consensus to maintain conceptual and semantic equivalence. The questionnaire’s internal consistency was evaluated using the Cronbach’s alpha method in a pilot sample (n = 30) to assess clarity, linguistic acceptability, and relevance.

Data analysis

Data were entered in Microsoft Excel and analysed using an online statistics calculator, DATAtab (DATAtab e.U. Graz, Austria). Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) summarized participant characteristics. Associations between demographic variables were tested using the Chi-square test. The strength of associations was assessed by Odds Ratios (ORs) with appropriate reference categories. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Validation and reliability of the questionnaire

The Item-Level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) values ranged from 0.83 to 1.0, and the Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) was calculated at 0.92, indicating excellent agreement among experts. The internal consistency of the questionnaire, particularly the knowledge and awareness items, was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.76, demonstrating acceptable reliability.

Demographic characteristics

A total of 437 dental outpatients participated in the study, with a nearly equal gender distribution: 224 males (51.26%) and 213 females (48.74%). Most participants were aged between 18 and 30 years (32.95%), followed by 31 and 45 years (30.89%), 46 and 60 years (24.94%), and over 60 years (11.21%). Educational backgrounds varied, with 30.43% being graduates, 24.26% having secondary education, 15.56% with intermediate education, 6.86% with primary, and 19.91% reporting no formal education. About 57.44% of respondents were from rural areas, and 42.56% were urban residents. The prevalence of self-medication for dental issues in the last preceding year was 37.07%, while 75.51% reported having self-medicated at some point in their lifetime. [Table 1]

Chi-square tests revealed significant associations between self-medication (in the last one year) and several demographic variables. These included age (χ² =13.64, P = 0.03), gender (χ² =6.17, P = 0.013), education (χ² =12.34, P = 0.03), and residence (χ² =4.68, P = 0.028), suggesting these factors influence self-medication behaviour [Table 1].

The odds of self-medication among males were 0.6 times that of females (OR = 0.6), with the difference being statistically significant. The 31–45 years age group had the highest proportion of self-medication (15.1%), with an odds ratio of 2.49 compared to the reference group (18–30 years). Respondents with no formal education had the highest odds of self-medication (OR = 1.0, reference group). Urban residents were less likely to self-medicate (OR = 0.64). The difference was statistically significant for all the variables [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

This study explored the prevalence and predictors of self-medication among dental patients and revealed a considerable tendency toward self-medication, especially over the lifetime. The lifetime prevalence of self-medication was 75.5%, and 45.8% of participants reported self-medicating for dental complaints in the past year. These figures are comparable to previous studies by Surenthar et al.,[10] who reported a lifetime prevalence of 75.7% and a past-year rate of 45.76% in a South Indian population. Dhaimade et al.[14] similarly reported a high prevalence (81%) among dental outpatients in Mumbai. In contrast, Simon et al.[15] observed a lower dental-specific self-medication rate of 30% in a coastal Karnataka outreach population, possibly due to rural demography and restricted access to formal dental care.

Toothache (68.6%) emerged as the most common reason for self-medication in the present study, mirroring findings of earlier studies.[215] Paracetamol was the most commonly used medication, as also reported by earlier studies.[1617] Alarmingly, 62.35% of respondents were unaware of the specific drug they consumed, reinforcing concerns raised by prior authors about poor awareness of drug safety. This reliance on analgesics raises concerns of unintentional overdose. Educational level was a statistically significant predictor, with primary education associated with significantly higher odds of self-medication (AOR = 3.52; 95% CI: 1.08–11.49; P = 0.037). Similar educational gradients were documented by Dhaimade et al.,[14] reinforcing the importance of health literacy in mitigating self-medication practices. This association has been confirmed by the study of Kalra,[16] which demonstrated that lower levels of health literacy and education were strongly linked with unsafe self-medication practices.

Gender-wise, although females had a marginally higher prevalence, males showed significantly lower odds of self-medication. This finding contrasts with earlier studies where males were more likely to self-medicate. For instance, a Bangalore-based study found higher self-medication rates among men.[18] Simon et al.[15] also reported lower odds among females in a rural Karnataka cohort. Kalra et al.[16] acknowledged that male-dominated self-medication was more evident in general health or non-dental populations, often due to greater autonomy and delayed care-seeking. Our contrasting findings may reflect gender-specific barriers such as fear of dental procedures, time constraints, or sociocultural factors encouraging self-care among women.

Pharmacies were the most frequent source of medications (98.8%), aligning with a previous study,[15] which also identified retail pharmacists as the principal source in over 60% of cases. Together, these findings emphasize the urgent need for public health initiatives that regulate over-the-counter sales and promote rational drug use through educational outreach. Moreover, the likelihood of future self-medication was significant among our participants, echoing concerns raised by a previous study[15] that habit-forming patterns and lack of deterrent policies may worsen outcomes. The low proportion of respondents who knew the name or class of the drug (only 28%) and the even lower awareness of risks (7.55%) mirror findings from the previous literature,[10] emphasizing poor public knowledge and lack of dental health literacy [Table 2]. The widespread use of antibiotics without prescription, even if secondary to analgesics, contributes to the global crisis of antimicrobial resistance.

Reasons and Causes of Self-Medication

Chi-square tests were performed to evaluate the association between participants’ attitudes, beliefs, and awareness related to dental health and their history of self-medication [Table 3]. The comparison of attitudes reveals stark differences between patients who self-medicated and those who did not. A statistically significant association was observed between participants’ intention to self-medicate in the future and their past self-medication behavior (χ² = value, P = 0.008), with 30.66% of self-medicating individuals stating they were not likely to self-medicate again, in contrast to 57.67% among non-self-medicators. Similarly, awareness of the oral-systemic health link was significantly associated with lower self-medication (χ² = value, P = 0.001), as 35.7% of non-self-medicators acknowledged the link compared to only 14.87% of self-medicators. Notably, most participants cited time-saving and cost concerns as reasons for self-medication, also identified as key motivators. A significantly higher proportion of self-medicating individuals believed that it was safe to take medications without dental consultation and were more likely to repeat such behaviour. This is concerning, particularly when coupled with their relatively low intent to consult professionals in the future. While self-medicators had better awareness of drug names (possibly due to experience), both groups showed poor understanding of drug side effects and long-term consequences. Moreover, the perception that operative dental procedures weaken teeth was more prevalent among non-self-medicators and showed a strong association with self-medication status (P < 0.001). These findings are consistent with the results of earlier studies,[10] which reported that habitual self-medication reduces professional reliance and reinforces risky drug behaviour. No significant differences were found regarding financial reasons, cost comparison, or preference for traditional methods. These findings underscore the need for differentiated health education strategies targeting behaviour change in both groups. One limitation of this study was the lack of data on how patients chose medications without professional advice. Understanding these sources, such as past prescriptions, pharmacist input, family, internet, or advertisements, is key to addressing self-medication behaviours. Future surveys should include a closed-ended, multiple-response item to capture this information systematically.

Contrary to common assumptions, our study found no significant association between perceived accessibility to a dentist and self-medication behavior [Table 2, Table 3]. Although over 90% of participants in both groups agreed that dental consultation is necessary even for minor issues, this did not reduce self-medication prevalence.[911]

CONCLUSION

Our findings suggest that behavioural and perceptual factors, such as cost-saving habits, low risk perception, and convenience, may outweigh physical accessibility in influencing self-medication. Awareness, beliefs about dental treatment, and perceived need for professional care play a crucial role in shaping such behaviour. Call for stricter drug regulation, targeted educational interventions, improved patient counselling, and integration of oral health promotion into primary healthcare systems.
