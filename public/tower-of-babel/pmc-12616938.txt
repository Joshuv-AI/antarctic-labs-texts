Subnational life expectancy disparities in low and middle-income countries: measurement and determinants
Authors: Andreas Kyriacou, Ronald Miranda-Lescano, Leonel Muinelo-Gallo, Oriol Roca-Sagales
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRelatively little is known about subnational life expectancy disparities in low and middle-income countries (LMICs). We construct indicators of subnational disparities in life expectancy, offering critical insights into health inequalities within countries. Moreover, we investigate the factors that account for cross-country and over-time variations in subnational life expectancy in less developed countries.MethodsWe measure subnational disparities in life expectancy within a country by way of a population-weighted coefficient of variation indicator. Our sample covers 101 LMICs during the period 2000–2021 and we consider overall, male and female health disparities. We employ regression analysis to identify important drivers of subnational disparities in life expectancy and focus on the role of subnational disparities in income and education.ResultsThe findings reveal that subnational disparities in life expectancy are markedly higher in Sub-Saharan Africa compared to other LMIC world regions. Moreover, while subnational disparities in life expectancy have decreased in most regions, Sub-Saharan Africa stands out as an exception, with persistently high disparities alongside rising average life expectancy. A gender-specific analysis highlights that, while women generally live longer than men, subnational disparities are greater for women. Regression analysis identifies a range of factors influencing life expectancy disparities. Variables such as good governance and public health spending help reduce subnational disparities, while decentralization, country size, geographic diversity and ethnic fractionalization tend to increase them. Subnational disparities in income and education emerge as the most significant drivers, with disparities in female education playing a particularly critical role.ConclusionsSubnational life expectancy disparities in some LMICs, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa, are high and persistent. Reducing subnational disparities in female education emerges as a key strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13561-025-00676-x.Keywords: Subnational disparities, Life expectancy, Inequality measurement, Inequality determinants

Background

Life expectancy is an important dimension of human development [32]. Based on evidence on the evolution of life expectancy at the country level, life expectancy in the twenty-first century has been increasing around the world, only to fall somewhat in the period 2020–21 due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic [37]. Less is known about how life expectancy, or health outcomes more generally, have evolved at subnational levels in the last two decades or so. Mapping and explaining disparities in life expectancy among a country's regions is crucial if we are to reduce health inequalities within countries. Those studies that have taken a subnational perspective, have mostly focused on the evolution of subnational differences in health outcomes – including life expectancy – in high-income countries namely, Australia, Canada, Ireland, the Netherlands, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the United States [8, 9, 12, 17, 23, 38, 40].

There are three exceptions. Timonin et al. [30] track trends in life expectancy across Russian regions between 2003 and 2014 and report that life expectancy at birth has improved markedly across regions, while regional disparities in life expectancy, as measured by way of a population weighted coefficient of variation, have fallen only slightly. Gutiérrez et al. [16] calculate a Gini coefficient of life expectancy at birth across Mexican states and find that inter-state differences in life expectancy fell over the period 1980 to 2019. The third exception is Permanyer and Smits [26] who exploit data for 1625 subnational regions within 161 high, medium and low-income countries from the Subnational Human Development Index (SHDI) database, to explore the evolution of subnational inequality of the Human Development Index (HDI) and its three subcomponents – mean years of schooling, income per capita and life expectancy at birth – over the period 2000 to 2015. To measure inequality across subnational regions (labeled global inequality), they turn to a Gini index and a mean log deviation indicator. Based on the Gini index, they find that global inequality in life expectancy has declined across these regions, except for a small increase in 2005 which they suggest may be due to the HIV/AIDS epidemic in Sub-Saharan Africa. The mean log deviation indicator allows them to break down global inequality in the SHDI and its components into between and within-country inequality. They find that in 2015, within-country inequality in life expectancy contributed a bit over 20% to total inequality in low-income countries, around 45% in middle-income countries and less than 10% in high-income countries.

In this article, we combine the current version of the SHDI from the Global Data Lab (GDL) with subnational population data from the Area Database (also from the GDL), to track subnational inequalities in life expectancy at birth within less developed countries over the period 2000 to 2021. Our approach is similar to that taken by Timonin et al. [30], since we measure the subnational disparity in life expectancy within a country by way of a population weighted coefficient of variation, which is commonly used to track within-country spatial disparities across various dimensions – most notably income per capita (for example, [21, 39]). Our sample consists of 101 low and middle-income countries, and we focus on them given the relative scarcity of evidence about the size and evolution of subnational health disparities in less developed countries. Our approach allows us to track subnational disparities in life expectancy in each country and thus provide potentially valuable information on a country-by-country basis.

By way of illustration, consider the evolution of life expectancy at birth in three countries – Colombia, Bangladesh and Nigeria – based on the available data. As can be seen in Fig. 1 (Maps A, B, C, respectively), a comparison of life expectancy between the early 2000s and 2018, indicates that in all three countries life expectancy has improved across most regions. The figure also shows subnational differences in life expectancy between the worst performing and best performing region in each country ranging from 4.4 years in Colombia in the year to 2002, to 20.5 years in Nigeria in 2003. Almost two decades later, in 2018, the gap between the worst and best performers had increased in Colombia (going from 4.4 to 8.9 years) and fallen in both Bangladesh (from 11 to 5.2) and to a lesser extent in Nigeria (from 20.5 to 18.7). As will be shown more fully in Section "Data and methods" below, a similar picture emerges from the vantage point offered by the population weighted coefficient of variation: spatial disparities in life expectancy have increased in Colombia, fallen in Bangladesh and fallen slightly in Nigeria. Several questions follow. How large are spatial disparities in LMICs and how have they evolved in the first two decades of the twenty-first century? What explains cross-country and over-time differences in subnational life expectancy in less developed countries? We will take a first step towards answering these questions in this article.

Subnational life expectancy in selected countries. Source: Prepared by the authors based on the SHDI from the GDL

We find that subnational disparities in life expectancy at birth have generally been decreasing in all LMIC world regions except Sub-Saharan Africa where they are and have remained notably higher. Furthermore, we find that in all the world regions considered, subnational disparities in life expectancy within countries are larger for women than for men and that, moreover, over the whole period, the gap between women and men is greatest in Sub-Saharan Africa. Our exploration of the drivers of life expectancy disparities highlights the importance of income and especially education disparities, and in the case of the latter, particularly, subnational education disparities among women.

The article is structured as follows. In Section "Data and methods", we present the data we employ, as well as the corresponding spatial disparity indicators we generate, before mapping subnational life expectancy disparities across countries and over time. We also explain the empirical method that we use to identify the key determinants of these inequalities. In Section "Regression results", we present the corresponding empirical results. In Section "Discussion" we discuss our main findings before concluding the article with Section "Conclusion".

Data and methods

Subnational life expectancy data

Our data is drawn from the Global Data Lab (v7.0).1 Specifically, we employ subnational data on life expectancy at birth provided by the SHDI database of the GDL. As explained by Smits and Permanyer [29], because LMIC household surveys and censuses generally do not contain information on life expectancy, subnational values of this indicator are estimated by the GDL using subnational data on under-five-year mortality (U5M) as provided by the Area Database (AD) of the GDL. According to these authors, this requires the use of interpolation and extrapolation techniques to address missing values of U5M, the fitting of a regression model that predicts life expectancy based on U5M, and the scaling of the subnational values so that their population weighted mean for a given year aligns with the known national United Nations Development Program (UNDP) value for that year, as drawn from the UNDP-HDI database.2 As detailed in sub-Section "Subnational life expectancy disparity indicators", the construction of our chosen indicator for spatial disparities requires subnational population data, which we obtain from the GDL-AD. The resultant sample of 101 LMICs emerges when countries are dropped because they either do not have subnational population data or when the GDL-SHDI assigns the national life expectancy value to all the subnational regions of a country due to data limitations.

Whenever possible, the GDL uses first-level administrative units based on the official administrative subdivisions used in each country. For example, in the case of Colombia, the existing administrative division of the country is adopted, consisting of 33 regions corresponding to the 32 "departments" plus the capital. For Nigeria, the GDL similarly follows the existing administrative division of the country, considering 37 regions corresponding to the 36 "states" plus the federal capital. However, the GDL occasionally diverges from this norm. In Bangladesh for instance, the existing administrative division divides the country into 8 regions (divisions), which are further subdivided into a total of 64 "districts". The GDL however, defines 23 regions. Finally, if administrative subdivisions change over the period, the GDL adjusts for this to allow for comparability over time.

The GDL-SHDI also provides country level data on life expectancy at birth taken from the UNDP. We plot this data in Fig. 2 that illustrates life expectancy across politico-geographic LMIC regions over the period 2000–2021, taking average values from the 101 countries considered in our analysis (see Table 5 in the Appendix for the countries and years included in each world region). Like WHO [37], it shows that in the last two decades, all LMIC world regions, and especially Sub-Saharan Africa, have experienced progress in life expectancy at birth. Figure 2 also suggests some convergence in life expectancy at birth across world regions, something that has been previously documented (see, [1]). Nonetheless, significant differences in life expectancy can be appreciated across regions, mostly between Sub-Saharan Africa, with relatively lower life expectancy, and the rest. For most of the period, life expectancy is highest in Middle Eastern and North African countries. The data also highlight a common negative impact on life expectancy during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and 2021. Although not shown, average life expectancy in the whole sample increased from 61.63 in 2000 to 66.32 in 2021.

Life expectancy across LMIC world regions 2000–21. Source: Prepared by the authors based on the SHDI from the GDL

In Fig. 3, we again show the evolution of life expectancy at birth but now differentiating between men and women. Again, consistent with WHO [37], while life expectancy has been rising for both sexes up to the incidence of COVID-19, it is higher for women than for men in all the LMIC regions, and in each one of the countries considered (see Tables OA.1 and OA.2 in the online appendix). The largest gaps between men and women occur in Eastern Europe and Central Asia, and in Latin America and the Caribbean. The figure also indicates that the gap between males and females has generally been maintained over time in each world region, with the exception of Sub-Saharan Africa, where it seems to have increased somewhat since the second decade of the century.

Subnational life expectancy disparity indicators

To measure subnational disparities in life expectancy, we turn to the population weighted coefficient of variation (PW-CV-LEXP), a measure widely used in the literature of regional disparities [28]. It is defined as:

where LEXPjt and pjt are the life expectancy and population share, respectively, for region j in country i during year t, with n denoting the total number of regions. LEXP¯ is the country’s average life expectancy, calculated as ∑j=1ipjt∗LEXPjt, with ∑j=1ipj=1.

The PW-CV-LEXP basically depicts disparities between a country's regions, taking into consideration their relative size (population weights), and theoretically can range from 0 (equality) to 100 (maximum disparities). Because it measures variability relative to the mean, this indicator is independent of the level of life expectancy. The PW-CV-LEXP accurately reflects true disparities because more populated regions contribute more to the overall disparity measurement than smaller, less populated ones. Without population weighting, small regions with large variances could exaggerate disparities, while large regions might be underrepresented [22, 39]. From the perspective of regional policy, the population weighted measure is appropriate since regional outcomes are population weighted in the welfare function of the policymaker [33].

Using a population weighted index is particularly suitable for our sample, given the significant differences in the size of regions within countries. By way of illustration, consider the three countries mentioned in the "Background" section above. Based on population data in 2021, Colombia has regions ranging in population size from around 50,000 inhabitants (e.g. Vaupis and Guainja) to the nearly 8 million people of the capital region (Bogota D.C.). In Bangladesh the region of Daka has more than 12 million inhabitants while Khagrachhari-Rangamati has 1.4 million. Similarly, in Nigeria, the Lagos region has 14 million inhabitants compared to the capital region of Abuja with just 1.35 million. Another notable example is India that includes one region with nearly 243 million inhabitants (Uttar Pradesh), and regions with less than 1 million (e.g. Sikkim). In this context, it seems more appropriate to use population-weighted measures of subnational disparities since they account for the fact that what happens in larger regions (such as Uttar Pradesh) affects more people than those in smaller regions (such as Sikkim). Accordingly, the PW-CV-LEXP is largely shaped by trends in larger regions.

Our second measure of subnational disparities is the population weighted standard deviation of the logarithm of subnational life expectancy (PW-LOG-LEXP), which, in its unweighted version, is widely used in the income convergence literature to capture sigma convergence [13]:

where μ¯it=∑j=1ipjtlog(LEXPjt).

Like the PW-CV-LEXP, this logarithmic measure is scale invariant, but it is more sensitive to the bottom part of the distribution [31].

Subnational life expectancy disparities in LMICs

Figure 4 shows the evolution of subnational life expectancy disparities in the twenty-first century across LMIC world regions based on the PW-CV-LEXP. There are important differences between Sub-Saharan Africa and the other regions. Subnational disparities in life expectancy are notably higher in the former during the whole period. Moreover, while subnational disparities in life expectancy have been generally decreasing in all LMIC world regions, they have remained persistently high in Sub-Saharan Africa. The figure also indicates an increase in life expectancy disparities in Sub-Saharan African countries in 2020, something that is suggestive of the unequal subnational incidence of COVID-19. In contrast, the figure does not indicate that COVID-19 may have increased subnational life expectancy disparities in other LMIC world regions, except perhaps for the case of the Middle Eastern and North African countries, where disparities increase somewhat in 2021. Additionally, Fig. 4 indicates several noteworthy trends: disparities in Sub-Saharan Africa started to rise in 2015, and disparities increased in the Asia and Pacific region in the period 2006–09 and in Latin America over the period 2015–2019.

Subnational disparities in life expectancy across LMIC world regions 2000–21. Source: Prepared by the authors following Eq. 1 based on SHDI and AD data from the GDL

World region averages can mask very different country performances. For a more precise view of the evolution of subnational disparities in life expectancy within countries, Fig. 5 shows the full list of countries that we consider and the changes in life expectancy disparities based on initial and final period values (see Table 5 in the Appendix for full details). Now we can see that disparities have increased in 16 out of 43 Sub-Saharan African countries, but they have fallen in 24 of them and are unchanged in the remaining 3. In the other LMIC world regions the distribution is as follows: among 20 Asian and Pacific countries, they increased in 3, fell in 13 and remained unchanged in 4; among 10 East European and Central Asian countries, disparities increased in 3, decreased in 6 and remained unchanged in 1; among 18 Latin American and Caribbean countries, disparities increased in 7, fell in 9 (including Mexico, in line with [16]) and remained unchanged in 2, and finally, in the 10 Middle Eastern and North African countries, disparities increased in 1 and decreased in the rest.

Subnational disparities in life expectancy across LMICs. Evolution over time. Source: Prepared by the authors following Eq. 1 based on SHDI and AD data from the GDL

Overall, in the 101 countries considered, subnational disparities in life expectancy fell in 61, increased in 30 and they remained unchanged in 10. The group of countries with falling subnational disparities includes some of the most populous countries in the world namely India, China, Indonesia, Pakistan, Brazil and Bangladesh. The unequal evolution of subnational disparities across countries contrasts with the increase in average life expectancy observed in almost all the LMICs considered (the exceptions are Tunisia, India and Nepal – countries for which the sample is shorter and includes the COVID-19 pandemic shock).

We next turn, in Fig. 6, to the evolution of subnational disparities across LMIC world regions but now separating males and females. The figure reveals that in all the world regions considered, subnational disparities in life expectancy within countries are larger for women than for men. This result is, perhaps, striking given the fact that we also saw that life expectancy is, on average, higher for women than for men (Fig. 3). The figure also shows that, over the whole period, the gap between women and men is greatest in Sub-Saharan Africa. So, while women in Sub-Saharan Africa may, on average, live longer than men (see Fig. 3), this region also has both the greatest subnational disparity in female life expectancy and the largest gender gap in disparities. This said, we again observe notable differences at the country level. For instance, in Nigeria and Congo Democratic Republic – the two most populous Sub-Saharan African countries – the gap between female and male life expectancy disparities has increased over time while in Kenya and Uganda this gap has fallen (see Tables OA.1 and OA.2 in the online appendix).

Model specification

Having traced subnational disparities in less developed countries since the beginning of the twenty-first century, we will now turn to the potential determinants of cross-country and over-time variation. To do so, we employ an unbalanced panel of up to 1626 observations from 101 LMICs, to estimate the following empirical model:

where i=1,⋯,101 refers to countries and t=2000,⋯,2021 refers to years. Subnational disparities in life expectancy are measured through the PW-CV-LEXP, and for robustness purposes, the PW-LOG-LEXP is also used. X is a vector of explanatory variables, dtt denotes period fixed effects, dri represents politico-geographical region fixed effects, and ε is the error term. We estimate Eq. (3) using Prais-Winsten regression with panel-corrected standard errors (PCSE) which are robust to heteroskedasticity, serial correlation within panels and contemporaneous correlation across panels. We include period fixed effects, to control for the impact of time varying factors common to all countries (e.g. COVID-19 pandemic), and to account for the influence of time constant factors specific to each region, we have included five regional dummies corresponding to the classification of politico-geographical regions provided by the V-Dem Institute [34]. However, we do not include country fixed effects because of the limited within-country variation compared to the cross-section variance in our key variables of interest (see Table 6 in the Appendix).

The choice of explanatory variables is guided by the desire to uncover observable time varying and time constant factors driving subnational disparities in life expectancy. Two potentially important factors are subnational disparities in GDP per capita and education. Higher economic development has been found to increase life expectancy arguably because households with higher incomes can better cover health needs and richer countries can afford more advanced health technologies (see, for example, [18, 27]). Higher education levels imply a greater ability to process information and utilize resources efficiently to promote health (e.g., [6]). Thus, subnational disparities in income and education are potential determinants of subnational disparities in life expectancy. To calculate these indicators, we employ subnational data on income per capita and human capital from the GDL and apply the PW-CV formula shown in sub-Section "Subnational life expectancy disparity indicators" to calculate population weighted measures of regional disparities in income and education for each LMIC in our sample.

We also account for average income per capita in each country since the level of development may be associated with a host of unobservable factors that may impact on life expectancy disparities. Additionally, we include governance quality since it may be that better governed countries are more successful in reducing socio-economic disparities — including life expectancy disparities — among subnational units (see, [21] for the general argument and relevant citations). Finally, we account for public and private spending on health given that previous work has identified both these variables as potentially drivers of better health outcomes in less developed countries (e.g., [5, 25]).

Regression results

In Column 1 of Table 1, we report the estimates of Eq. 3 incorporating the group of variables described in the previous section. Immediately obvious is the positive association between subnational income and educational disparities, and disparities in life expectancy. This is consistent with prior work that has shown that life expectancy increases with income and education levels. Also consistent with prior work is the negative relationship between better governance and subnational disparities in life expectancy. Less robustly, we find some evidence that public health expenditure reduces subnational life expectancy disparities. In contrast, private health expenditure does not have any effect.

The relative importance of each variable can be gauged from the standardized beta coefficients reported for the continuous explanatory variables in Table 1. The most important determinants of life expectancy disparities are income and education disparities. Column 1 shows that a one standard deviation increase in these variables leads to an increase in life expectancy disparities by 19.8% and 21.1% of one standard deviation, respectively. By comparison, the impact of better governance and public health spending is smaller: a one standard deviation increase in each reduces life expectancy disparities by 6.7% and 2.4% of one standard deviation respectively. The regional fixed effects confirm what we observed in Fig. 4 above. Taking Sub-Saharan Africa as the reference category, we see that life expectancy disparities are greatest in that region followed, in decreasing order, by the Asia and Pacific region, Eastern Europe and Central Asia, Middle Eastern and North African countries, and Latin America and the Caribbean.

These findings are maintained in the remaining columns of Table 1, where we aim to uncover additional covariates of subnational life expectancy disparities. In Column 2, we account for ethnic fractionalization since it could be that subnational health disparities are greater in more ethnically heterogeneous countries, partly because agreement over public policy in general, and health policy in particular, may be more difficult [2]. Columns 3 and 4 include population and country size based on the expectation that larger countries may have larger subnational disparities on many dimensions, including life expectancy. In Column 5, we include a quadratic income per capita term to allow for the possibility that, like subnational income disparities [22, 39], life expectancy disparities may initially increase, and then fall with economic development. Because we also account for income disparities in the regressions, this could be due to unobserved factors related to development and that, moreover, are quadratically linked to subnational disparities in life expectancy. In Columns 6 and 7 we consider if the degree of political or fiscal decentralization affects disparities in life expectancy on the strength of the argument that in more decentralized systems the central government may have fewer resources at its disposable to reduce spatial disparities [28], including disparities in life expectancy. Finally, in column 8 we consider the possibility that subnational disparities in life expectancy could be due to different geographic endowments across subnational units (such as elevation, precipitation, temperature, distance to coast and land quality). These different endowments could impact on disparities in life expectancy through their effect on the incidence of the local disease environment.

The results in Table 1 confirm the positive association between ethnic heterogeneity, country size, decentralization and geographic diversity on the one hand, and life expectancy disparities on the other. It also indicates an inverted quadratic relationship between life expectancy disparities and GDP per capita. In terms of the economic impact of each variable, the results reveal the importance, from largest to smallest driver, of fiscal decentralization, country area, geographic diversity, ethnic fractionalization, population size and political decentralization. However, the estimated effect of fiscal decentralization should be qualified because it emerges from a reduced sample of 41 countries. In general, the beta coefficients confirm the relative importance of income and education disparities as primary determinants of life expectancy disparities.

In Table 2, we pursue the robustness of the estimates of Eq. 3. In particular, we allow for the real possibility that subnational disparities in life expectancy can also determine disparities in both income and educational attainment since people who live shorter lives may accumulate less education and earn less income. This is especially relevant given the importance of these variables when explaining life expectancy disparities. Ideally, to deal with such reverse causality, we would adopt an identification strategy based on instrumental variables, difference-in-difference, regression discontinuity or similar methods. In the absence of valid instruments or quasi experimental designs, we re-estimate Eq. 3 taking lagged values of the subnational income and education disparity variables. We use one to five-year lags in the first five columns of the table that employ annual data. In Columns 6 to 8, we turn instead to five year means of all the time variant variables. In Column 6, we estimated Eq. 3 but now using five-year averages. This can help attenuate measurement error derived from the interpolation and extrapolation techniques employed to generate the annual data. This approach also helps focus on the long-term or structural relationships between the dependent and independent variables. To address reverse causality, we employ the lagged value of the five-year average of subnational income and education disparities (Column 7) and also the first-year value of each five-year period (Column 8). Through all this, the substantive empirical results reported in Column 1 of Table 1 are maintained. This said, in the absence of a more rigorous identification strategy to deal with reverse causality, we must allow for the possibility that the estimated effect of income and education disparities is biased upwards.

Discussion

The United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) seek to ensure healthy lives, promote wellbeing (Goal 3) and reduce inequalities (Goal 10), especially in low and middle-income countries. While the United Nations tends to emphasize the importance of reducing interpersonal life expectancy differences [35], we would argue that attention must also be given to life expectancy disparities across a country’s regions. Measuring such disparities is a necessary step towards ensuring that all people, regardless of where they live, have the opportunity to live longer, healthier lives.

Based on our newly constructed indicators of subnational inequalities on life expectancy, we find that while average life expectancy has increased across LMICs, this does not necessarily imply a reduction in subnational life expectancy disparities within these countries. Specifically, we find that subnational disparities in life expectancy have generally been decreasing in all LMIC world regions except Sub-Saharan Africa where they have remained high. When referring to economic well-being, a distinction must be made between aggregate economic growth and income inequality. Our results highlight the need to make a similar distinction between average life expectancy and disparities in life expectancy. The gender-based analysis shows that while on average women live longer than men, subnational disparities in life expectancy within countries are larger for women. This suggests the relative urgency of policies that help reduce subnational life expectancy disparities in women, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa which has both the greatest subnational disparity in female life expectancy and the largest gap in life expectancy disparities between men and women.

The regression analysis identifies a host of variables that can reduce disparities (good governance, public health spending) or potentially increase them (fiscal and political decentralization, country size, geographic diversity and ethnic fractionalization). Importantly, it identifies subnational disparities in income and, especially education, as the most crucial drivers of life expectancy disparities. While both male and female education disparities emerge as relevant, female education disparities tend to play a larger role. This stronger effect in shaping subnational variation in life expectancy could be explained by the fact that educated women are more likely to make informed health decisions, access healthcare services, and adopt healthier lifestyles, which not only enhance their own health but also positively influence the health and longevity of their families and communities. From this, the key policy implication that emerges is that disparities in life expectancy could be reduced through policies that reduce subnational education disparities among women. In this regard, previous work has identified a range of policies whose implementation in lagging regions would be desirable including, eliminating school fees [10], financial support for school attendance for girls [11, 20], facilitating transportation to school [24], more female teachers [7], and child marriage bans [41].

Finally, the analysis has limitations. First, the subnational data on life expectancy at birth that we employ to generate the population weighted regional disparities measures, is sometimes generated by way of interpolation and extrapolation techniques that aim to address missing values in the reference variable (under-five-year-mortality). This said, and as extensively explained by Permanyer and Smits [26], the measurement error that arises from these techniques is limited. Second, our sample of LMICs excludes countries that do not have subnational population data as well as countries for which the GDL-SHDI assigns the national life expectancy value to all the subnational regions. Based on the World Bank’s income classification in 2015, this basically means that we exclude 22 middle-income and 3 low-income countries. With this in mind, the finding by Permanyer and Smits [26]—that within-country variation explains a larger share of life expectancy disparities across regions in middle-income countries than in low-income ones—suggests that our estimated disparities are likely biased downward due to selection bias, especially since their sample includes nearly all of the LMICs we drop. Lastly, the estimated effect of income and education disparities may be biased upwards due to the persisting presence of reverse causality. Future work should try to address this issue using suitable identification designs.

Conclusion

In this study, we have provided empirical evidence on the evolution of subnational life expectancy disparities within LMICs during the last two decades. We have shown that subnational disparities in life expectancy are markedly higher in Sub-Saharan Africa compared to other LMIC world regions throughout the period. Moreover, while subnational disparities in life expectancy have decreased in most regions, Sub-Saharan Africa stands out as an exception, with persistently high disparities alongside rising average life expectancy. We also track the evolution of subnational disparities in life expectancy across LMIC regions and countries by gender. We find that while on average women live longer than men in all the countries considered, subnational disparities in life expectancy within countries are larger for women and, moreover, Sub-Saharan Africa has both the greatest subnational disparity in female life expectancy and the largest gap in life expectancy disparities between men and women. We have also analyzed the potential drivers of cross-country and over-time differences in subnational life expectancy disparities and have determined that subnational disparities in income and, especially, education significantly influence variations in life expectancy within low and middle-income countries.
