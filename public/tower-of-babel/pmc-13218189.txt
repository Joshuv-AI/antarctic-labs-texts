What is the diagnostic accuracy of contrast enema for Hirschsprung disease during COVID-19 service disruption in Vietnam? A retrospective study
Authors: Quynh Anh Tran, Hien Duy Pham, Trang Thu Dang, Linh Vu Thuy Nguyen, Dung Boi Ly, Nhung Thi Nguyen, Hoang Viet Nguyen, Quang Thanh Nguyen

Background

Hirschsprung disease (HSCR) is a congenital disorder characterised by the absence of ganglion cells in the distal bowel, most often in the rectosigmoid region.1 This aganglionosis impairs peristalsis, leading to functional obstruction.1 Clinically, HSCR usually presents in neonates and infants with delayed passage of meconium, constipation, abdominal distension and feeding intolerance.2,4 If untreated, complications such as Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis (HAEC) contribute substantially to morbidity and mortality.5

Definitive management of HSCR is surgical, with resection of the aganglionic segment and pull-through of normally innervated bowel. At our institution, recent studies have shown favourable outcomes across several minimally invasive approaches, including one-stage neonatal surgery and single-incision laparoscopic endorectal pull-through, with low major morbidity and encouraging medium-to long-term bowel function and cosmetic results.6,8 However, these surgical studies primarily addressed operative feasibility, technique selection and postoperative outcomes after patients had already entered a diagnostic pathway. They do not answer a separate but equally important question: how reliable contrast enema is as a preoperative diagnostic test when histological confirmation is not immediately available.

Rectal suction biopsy (RSB) remains the diagnostic gold standard because it enables histological assessment of ganglion cells and acetylcholinesterase activity.2 9 10 Its accuracy approaches 100%,11 but it requires specialised equipment and trained personnel, which can be limited in many healthcare systems.10 Contrast enema (CE) has long been used as an adjunct to diagnosis. Radiographic features such as inverted rectosigmoid index (RSI), transition zone (TZ) and irregular mucosal patterns can suggest HSCR.1 However, reported diagnostic accuracy varies, with sensitivity ranging from 69% to 90% and specificity from 68% to 89%.12 13 For this reason, CE is usually combined with anorectal manometry or RSB rather than relied on as a sole diagnostic test.10

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed the fragility of this diagnostic pathway. Between May 2020 and October 2021, strict lockdowns and global supply chain disruptions rendered RSB completely unavailable at our national paediatric surgery centre. During this period, CE became the only available preoperative imaging test for children with suspected HSCR. This circumstance created a natural experiment that allowed evaluation of CE under conditions in which the usual gold standard was inaccessible.

Unlike prior studies that assessed CE alongside RSB, this setting provided a rare opportunity to examine the diagnostic performance of CE within a large consecutive paediatric cohort managed at a very high-volume referral institution. By analysing 354 consecutive cases, it provides one of the largest single-centre datasets to date and uniquely highlights age-specific diagnostic differences. These findings not only clarify CE’s diagnostic role but also generate practical lessons for low-resource settings and for healthcare systems under crisis, where reliance on CE may once again be necessary.14

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective diagnostic accuracy study conducted at the Department of Paediatric Surgery, Vietnam National Children’s Hospital, Hanoi, Vietnam. The study period extended from 1 May 2020 to 31 October 2021, during which routine preoperative RSB was unavailable because of coronavirus disease 2019-related service disruption and supply limitations. During this period, CE was the principal preoperative imaging test used in children with suspected HSCR.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of VinUniversity, under the oversight of the Ethics Committee in Biomedical Research of Vinmec International General Hospital (approval number 147/2023/CN HĐĐĐ VMEC, dated 17 March 2023). Given the retrospective design and use of anonymised clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. The study was reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies statement.15

Study population

We reviewed all consecutive children aged under 16 years who underwent CE for clinical suspicion of HSCR during the study period. Clinical suspicion was based on routine assessment by the treating team and typically included one or more of the following features: delayed passage of meconium, persistent constipation, abdominal distension, feeding intolerance, recurrent obstructive symptoms or episodes suggestive of HAEC. Referrals for CE were made through the usual clinical pathway at our tertiary paediatric surgical centre, primarily by the paediatric surgery team.

At our institution, all children with suspected HSCR underwent CE during the study period. Patients were eligible if they underwent CE within the study window and had sufficient records to permit final diagnostic classification. Exclusion criteria were technically inadequate or incomplete contrast studies that could not be interpreted, unavailable or incomplete medical records preventing ascertainment of the final diagnosis and inadequate follow-up in children who did not undergo surgery.

Index test

The index test was CE, performed according to the institutional radiology protocol in use during the study period under fluoroscopic guidance using a single contrast technique. No routine bowel preparation or rectal cleansing medications were used. A 16 to 18 French Foley catheter was inserted with the tip just inside the rectum, and no balloon was inflated. The colon was filled under fluoroscopic guidance, and early lateral images were obtained during filling of the rectum and sigmoid colon. For patients aged 1 month or younger, Xenetix 300 (iobitridol 300 mg/mL) diluted with distilled water at a ratio of 1:3 was used. For patients older than 1 month, 200 mL of barium sulphate diluted with 300 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride was used. Delayed films were not part of the routine protocol in this cohort.

The radiographic features assessed were those routinely used in clinical practice for suspected HSCR: RSI, TZ and irregular mucosal pattern. The RSI was defined as the ratio of the maximal rectal diameter to the maximal sigmoid diameter on the contrast study, and an index below 1 was considered abnormal. A CE was classified as suggestive of HSCR when one or more of these characteristic abnormal features were present and the overall interpretation favoured HSCR.

All index test interpretations were based on routine clinical image review rather than rereading for research purposes. The initial interpretation was made by the reporting radiologist. The images were then reviewed by the attending paediatric surgeon as part of the clinical decision-making process. In cases of disagreement, the images were reviewed jointly, and a final consensus interpretation was reached for clinical management. Formal interobserver agreement statistics were not available because the study was retrospective, and duplicate blinded research readings had not been prospectively planned.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the diagnostic accuracy of CE for HSCR, expressed as sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio and negative likelihood ratio, each with 95% CIs. The principal analysis used the final clinical classification described below as the reference standard.

Secondary outcomes were age-specific diagnostic accuracy and the diagnostic performance of individual radiographic signs, including inverted RSI, TZ and irregular mucosal pattern. Age subgroup analyses were prespecified according to age at the time of CE.

Data collection and follow-up

Data were extracted retrospectively from hospital medical records and operative records using a standardised data collection form. The reference standard differed according to whether the patient proceeded to surgery. In operated patients, the final diagnosis of HSCR was based on intraoperative frozen section biopsy and confirmation by postoperative histopathological examination of the resected bowel specimen. Histologically confirmed absence of ganglion cells was required for classification as HSCR.

In children who did not undergo surgery, final classification was based on structured clinical follow-up documented in the medical record. These children were classified as non-HSCR only if they showed sustained clinical improvement without a subsequent operation or histological confirmation of HSCR during follow-up. Clinical improvement was defined by the absence of persistent obstructive symptoms, together with documented improvement in bowel function and overall clinical course, such as weight gain, absence of enterocolitis and reduced abdominal distension. Because tissue confirmation was not available in this group, this pathway carries a risk of partial verification bias, particularly for ultrashort-segment disease or atypical presentations, and this limitation was considered in the interpretation of diagnostic accuracy estimates. As Vietnam National Children’s Hospital is the major tertiary paediatric referral centre for northern Vietnam, we were able to review the institutional records comprehensively for the study period, and no patients were lost to follow-up. A minimum follow-up duration of 12 months was required before classification as non-HSCR.

Potential sources of bias were considered in advance. Consecutive case inclusion was used to reduce selection bias. However, because only a subset of children, primarily those with abnormal imaging or persistent symptoms, underwent histological confirmation, partial verification bias could not be eliminated. Observer bias was also possible because image interpretation occurred within routine clinical care, and formal blinded rereading was not performed. We therefore describe CE as part of a pragmatic diagnostic pathway rather than a standalone replacement for histological diagnosis. We also recognise that CE may be less reliable in defining the proximal extent of aganglionosis, especially in long-segment disease and total colonic aganglionosis. In such cases, disease extent was determined intraoperatively and confirmed by final histopathology rather than inferred from imaging alone.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise baseline characteristics and imaging findings. Continuous variables are presented as median with IQR or mean with SD, as appropriate according to data distribution. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

Diagnostic accuracy was calculated from 2×2 contingency tables comparing CE classification with the final diagnostic classification. True positive (TP), false positive (FP), false negative (FN) and true negative (TN) counts are reported for the overall cohort and for each age subgroup. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were calculated with 95% CIs. CIs for proportions were calculated using the Wilson method. The analysis was descriptive by design, and no formal hypothesis testing was prespecified. Missing data were handled by casewise exclusion from the relevant analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, V.4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or dissemination of this research. The research question was developed by the study team based on prior institutional findings and gaps identified in the literature regarding paediatric imaging. Outcome measures were selected based on their relevance to the objectives of the study and established frameworks in the field of paediatric imaging.

Results

A total of 354 consecutive children underwent CE for suspected HSCR during the study period. The median age at the time of CE was 2.0 months. Overall, 259/354 (73.2%) were male, and 95/354 (26.8%) were female. Of the 354 children, 170 underwent surgery, and 184 were managed without surgery. The operated group comprised 133/170 (78.2%) males and 37/170 (21.8%) females, whereas the non-operated group comprised 126/184 (68.5%) males and 58/184 (31.5%) females. CE was interpreted as positive in 150/354 (42.4%) children and negative in 204/354 (57.6%). A flowchart of the study population is shown in figure 1.

Among the 170 children who underwent surgery, 151 were ultimately diagnosed with HSCR, and 19 were not. Of the 151 children with HSCR, 20 had an initially negative CE but later developed persistent or worsening symptoms, subsequently underwent surgery and were confirmed histologically to have HSCR. The remaining 184 children improved without surgery and were classified as non-HSCR. Mean follow-up in the non-operated group was 44.3±24 months, with a minimum follow-up of 12 months.

The overall 2×2 contingency table comprised 131 TPs, 19 FPs, 20 FNs and 184 TNs. Based on these counts, the overall sensitivity of CE for HSCR was 86.75% (95% CI 80.43 to 91.26), specificity was 90.64% (95% CI 85.85 to 93.93), PPV was 87.33% (95% CI 81.06 to 91.74) and NPV was 90.20% (95% CI 85.34 to 93.56) (table 1).

Discussion

This study assessed the diagnostic accuracy of CE for HSCR in a resource-constrained setting where RSB was unavailable. CE showed good overall diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 86.75%, specificity of 90.64%, PPV of 87.33% and NPV of 90.20%. Although previous studies, including the systematic review by de Lorijn et al,2 have shown CE to be less accurate than RSB and anorectal manometry, our findings indicate that CE remained a practical diagnostic tool within a constrained clinical pathway. However, these results should not be interpreted as supporting CE as a replacement for histological confirmation. Rather, in our setting, CE functioned as part of a pragmatic diagnostic strategy that was combined with clinical assessment, follow-up and intraoperative frozen section biopsy when surgery was undertaken. In contrast, RSB is not without limitations. It carries a risk of rectal and sigmoid perforation, particularly when followed closely by CE, as reported by Harned et al.16 Furthermore, up to 5.6% of RSB samples may be inconclusive for diagnosis, potentially leading to delays because repeat procedures may be required.2 17

Among individual radiographic features, inverted RSI showed the highest sensitivity, at 77.78%, with a specificity of 96.35%. Vlok et al1 also reported sensitivities of 78% and 86% for RSI and TZ, respectively, while Lourenção et al18 demonstrated improved diagnostic accuracy when multiple CE features were interpreted together, with sensitivity reaching 92.3% and NPV exceeding 90%.

TZ and irregular mucosal pattern were both highly specific in our cohort, at 98.44% and 99.48%, respectively, but their sensitivities were limited, at 34.03% and 17.36%. This pattern is broadly consistent with Peyvasteh et al,12 who found similarly high specificity but low sensitivity for mucosal irregularity and contraction-related signs. As in our study, these features were helpful when present but could not independently exclude HSCR when absent. Accordingly, CE findings should be interpreted in conjunction with the overall radiographic pattern and the clinical context rather than as isolated diagnostic markers.9 18

The anatomical location of TZ remains important when interpreting CE. Haikal et al19 reported only 30.8% concordance between rectal TZ and intraoperative pathology, compared with 100% for TZ in the rectosigmoid. Chen et al20 similarly observed a strong correlation between TZ and pathology in rectosigmoid disease but lower accuracy with more proximal aganglionosis. We also support the view that CE may help suggest HSCR but is less reliable for defining the proximal extent of aganglionosis, particularly in long-segment disease and total colonic aganglionosis.

In age-stratified analysis, CE showed higher sensitivity in children aged 1 month or younger, at 96.00%, but higher specificity in those older than 1 month, at 97.67%. These findings suggest age-related differences in diagnostic performance, although they should be interpreted cautiously. Frongia et al10 noted increased false-negative rates in neonates younger than 30 days, while Diamond et al21 found that young age, female sex and absence of TZ increased the likelihood of false-positive interpretation.

The false-negative findings in this study are particularly important. 20 children with an initially negative CE later developed persistent or worsening symptoms, underwent surgery and were confirmed histologically to have HSCR. This again demonstrates that CE cannot serve as a standalone rule-out test, especially when clinical suspicion remains high. Although the NPV was 90.20%, predictive values are influenced by disease prevalence and referral spectrum. Putnam et al22 found that a negative CE reduced the post-test probability of HSCR to 2.5%, while Sharma et al23 reported an NPV of 99.7% for 48-hour delayed contrast retention.

Delayed imaging has been proposed to improve diagnostic clarity. Wong et al24 found that adding a 24-hour delayed film improved sensitivity (85.7%) but reduced specificity (17.6%). In this patient cohort, delayed imaging was not employed as part of the diagnostic protocol. Instead, initial CE films were interpreted by a radiologist, followed by independent evaluation by the attending paediatric surgeon. In cases of diagnostic discrepancy, the final interpretation was determined through collaborative discussion, with the surgeon making the final decision on whether the findings were consistent with HSCR.

Additional signs such as the ‘question-mark-shaped colon’ and ‘ileocaecal valve reflux’, studied by Yan et al,25 increased diagnostic accuracy to 75% when used together. This supports our practice of interpreting multiple features collectively to guide surgical decision-making in the absence of RSB. Advanced diagnostic tools such as machine learning also hold promise. Huang et al26 developed a model combining clinical and radiological data that achieved 86.36% accuracy and an AUC of 0.9132, exceeding standard radiologist performance.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the few to systematically evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of CE for HSCR under real-world, resource-constrained conditions. Conducted during a period when RSB was unavailable because of COVID-19-related service disruption, it provides practical evidence for the role of CE within a constrained diagnostic pathway.

However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces the possibility of information bias, as clinical data and imaging interpretations were derived from existing medical records rather than a prospective standardised protocol. Second, the reference standard was not uniform across the entire cohort. Operated patients underwent intraoperative frozen section and postoperative histopathological confirmation, whereas non-operated patients were classified as non-HSCR on the basis of structured clinical follow-up. Although no patients were lost to follow-up and a minimum follow-up duration of 12 months was required before classification as non-HSCR, the absence of tissue confirmation in this group introduces partial verification bias and leaves open the possibility that ultrashort segment disease or atypical presentations may have been missed. Third, formal blinded rereading of CE images was not performed, and interobserver variability could not be measured. Fourth, the study was conducted at a high-volume tertiary referral centre during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period of service disruption and altered workflow, which may limit generalisability to lower-volume centres or more typical healthcare settings. Finally, CE is less reliable for estimating the proximal extent of aganglionosis, particularly in long-segment disease and total colonic aganglionosis.19 20 25

Conclusion

CE showed moderate to high diagnostic accuracy for HSCR disease and may be useful as part of a pragmatic triage pathway when RSB is unavailable. However, its false-negative rate indicates that it should not be used as a standalone rule-out test when tissue diagnosis is available.
