Risks of free flap reconstruction amongst smoking patients: a NSQIP analysis of 4653 patients from 2010 to 2021
Authors: Taylor Schumann, Victoria Hodkiewicz, Christopher Kujalowicz, Parinaz Jila Siemers, Bianca Di Chiaro, Irena Helenowski, Eleanor Bucholz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Microvascular free flap reconstruction for complex soft tissue defects relies on vascular perfusion for overall flap survival. Smoking has been linked to complications in postoperative wound healing due to vasoconstriction and weakened immune status. We aim to investigate the impact of smoking on postoperative complications in patients undergoing free flap reconstructive surgery.Methods:Using the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP) database, adult patients (≥18 years old) undergoing microvascular free flap surgery (Current Procedural Terminology: 15 756–15 758) from 2010 to 2021 were included. Patients were excluded if they were <18 years old or if they lacked postoperative follow-up data or documented smoking status. Smokers were compared to non-smokers across demographics and postoperative complications.Results:Among 4653 free flap patients, 1202 (25.8%) were smokers. Smokers were more likely to be male, younger, non-diabetic, and have lower body mass index. After multivariable adjustment, smoking status was associated with higher odds of organ/space SSI (OR = 1.653, 95% CI: 1.068–2.558, P = 0.024) and wound disruption (OR = 1.597, 95% CI: 1.092–2.336, P = 0.016). There were no significant differences in rates of superficial or deep incisional SSIs, graft/flap failure, sepsis/septic shock, or venous thromboembolism.Conclusion:Smoking was associated with increased rates of organ/space surgical site infections and wound dehiscence, but not with flap failure in patients undergoing microvascular free flap reconstruction. These findings support the role of preoperative smoking cessation counseling and more nuanced risk stratification focused on wound-related complications rather than flap survival.

Introduction

Microvascular free flap reconstruction provides soft tissue coverage for complex defects resulting from trauma, tumor resection, or congenital abnormalities[1]. Previous studies have shown that smoking delays wound healing across various plastic surgery procedures[2]. This is likely due to the harmful effects of nicotine, such as vasoconstriction and tissue ischemia, which pose a significant risk to microvascular free flap success[3–5]. Although smoking cessation is recommended prior to surgery, there are select clinical scenarios, such as limb salvage, where a delay in the operation is not feasible. Therefore, it is important to understand the implications of smoking for postoperative outcomes when counseling patients prior to microvascular free flap surgery.

Despite these known risks, the impact of smoking on postoperative outcomes following microvascular free-flap surgery is less clearly defined. A retrospective study of 102 patients undergoing head and neck microvascular free-flap reconstruction found that tobacco use was associated with anastomotic failure among patients who experienced free-flap loss[6]. Similarly, a systematic review found that among 2246 patients undergoing free-flap reconstruction, smokers experienced significantly higher rates of flap necrosis, hematoma, and fat necrosis compared to non-smokers[7]. While several studies have examined the general effects of smoking on free-flap surgery outcomes, large national database reviews evaluating outcomes for smokers undergoing microvascular free-flap surgery – particularly regarding the overall rates of flap failure, surgical complications, and prolonged hospital stay – are currently lacking.

To study these effects on a larger scale, this study uses the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) database to compare postoperative outcomes between smokers and non-smokers undergoing myocutaneous or fasciocutaneous microvascular free-flap surgery. By comparing smokers and non-smokers, this study aims to evaluate the occurrence of postoperative complications, hospital length of stay (LOS), medical complications, and overall demographic trends. By understanding the risks associated with smoking in patients undergoing microvascular free-flap surgery, physicians may better tailor perioperative management to improve patient outcomes and strengthen preoperative counseling.

Methods and materials

Database

Following Institutional Review Board (IRB) exemption from Loyola University Medical Center, a Data Use Agreement (DUA) was obtained from the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP) to perform a retrospective database analysis from 2010 to 2021. NSQIP databases were accessed on 13 February 2024. Included patients were selected using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes for microvascular free flap surgery (CPT 15 756–15 758). Eligible patients were 18 years of age or older who underwent microvascular free flap surgery at any anatomic location and had available smoking status and 30-day postoperative follow-up data. Patients were excluded if they lacked smoking status or postoperative complication data, were lost to follow-up, or were younger than 18 years of age. Patients were categorized based on smoking status (smoker vs non-smoker), with smokers defined as smoking within 12 months prior to the time of the procedure and non-smokers defined as never smokers or former smokers who quit more than 12 months prior to the procedure. Each patient encounter included Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) of 1996–compliant variables as per the NSQIP protocol, including demographics and 30-day postoperative complications. Patient data acquired included demographics (sex and ethnicity), body mass index (BMI), smoking status, length of hospital stay (LOS), and both surgical and medical 30-day postoperative complications (Table 1). Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized by frequencies and percentages, and differences by sex, race, ethnicity, BMI, age, and smoking status were assessed using the Monte Carlo-simulated Fisher’s exact test. Univariate analyses were performed using smoking status as the independent variable, with demographic factors and 30-day postoperative complications as outcomes. Continuous variables, including age, BMI, and LOS, were summarized by means, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and differences by smoking status were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

For multivariable analyses, separate logistic regression models were constructed with postoperative complications (organ/space surgical site infection and wound disruption) as the outcome variables and smoking status as the primary independent variable, adjusting for age, sex, race, ethnicity, BMI, total LOS, flap type (CPT 15 757 vs. 15 756), disseminated cancer, and diabetes. Multivariable models were developed based on complications significantly associated with smoking status and adjusted for relevant demographics and clinical factors. Collinearity was examined via the variance inflation factor (VIF). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals and P-values were reported. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

A total of 4653 patients who underwent microvascular free flap reconstruction met inclusion criteria, of whom 1202 (25.8%) were smokers. The majority of patients were male (n = 3022, 65.0%). Racial composition was predominantly White (68.6%), followed by Black (6.5%) and other races (4.4%). A total of 204 patients (4.4%) were identified as Hispanic. Smokers were significantly younger (mean, 56.3 years) compared to non-smokers (60.1 years; P < 0.001). Additionally, smokers had a significantly lower median BMI (25.3) compared to non-smokers (27.6; P < 0.001), longer hospital LOS, with a median of 9 days compared to 8 days for non-smokers (P < 0.001), and lower rates of diabetes (10.7% vs. 15.2%; P < 0.001). Means, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for all continuous variables are presented in Table 1. Differences in distributions among smoking groups were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Table 1).

This study compared the incidence of 21 postoperative complications between smokers and non-smokers undergoing free flap reconstruction (Table 1). In univariate analyses, smokers were significantly more likely to be on a ventilator for >48 hours postoperatively (4.7% vs. 3.4%, P = 0.042), have organ/space surgical site infection (SSI) (3.4% vs. 2.0%, P = 0.008), and have wound dehiscence (5.2% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.0004). Current smokers also had higher readmission rates (9.0% vs. 7.3%, P = 0.0597) (Table 1). Importantly, smoking was not associated with graft/flap failure. Other post-operative complications that did not reach statistical significance are summarized in Table 1.

After adjusting for age, sex, race, ethnicity, BMI, total LOS, disseminated cancer, and diabetes, multivariable logistic regression analysis indicated smoking status remained a significant predictor of organ/space SSI (OR = 1.653, 95% CI: 1.068–2.558, P = 0.024) and wound disruption (OR = 1.597, 95% CI: 1.092–2.336, P = 0.016). No significant differences were observed in graft/flap failure or other postoperative complications (Table 2). Discussion

Smoking is one of the most prevalent modifiable preoperative risk factors for postoperative wound complications[8,9]. This study was carried out to explore the impact of smoking on postoperative complications in plastic and reconstructive surgery patients undergoing microvascular free flap surgery. After adjusting for relevant demographic and clinical factors, smoking remained independently associated with higher observed rates of organ/space surgical site infections and wound disruption. However, we did not observe an association between smoking and graft/flap failure, suggesting that the impact of smoking may be more specific to impaired wound healing than microvascular flap viability. These results highlight the association between smoking and select postoperative wound-related outcomes for myocutaneous and fasciocutaneous free flap surgeries, thus suggesting the need for smoking cessation.

The detrimental effects of smoking on wound healing have been well established in the literature and are largely due to impaired tissue perfusion, reduced oxygen delivery, and altered inflammatory responses[3,4,10,11]. These mechanisms are especially relevant in microvascular free flap reconstruction, where successful healing depends on maintaining adequate blood flow. In a retrospective study conducted in 2019, Crippen et al found that smoking in patients undergoing free flap reconstruction of the head and neck was associated with wound disruption and unplanned reoperation[12]. Similarly, another retrospective study analyzing postoperative complications in patients undergoing pedicled TRAM flaps reported that smokers had a higher incidence of delayed wound healing compared to their non-smoker counterparts[13]. Consistent with earlier studies, our findings suggest that smoking is independently associated with an increased risk of wound dehiscence in free flap reconstructive surgery. These findings build upon the existing literature linking tobacco exposure to impaired wound healing and demonstrate that this association is maintained within a large national cohort of patients undergoing microvascular free flap reconstruction. Furthermore, the observed increase in postoperative wound disruption among smokers likely reflects the harmful effects of smoking on the integrity of the vasculature required for wound repair, thus reinforcing the importance of tobacco exposure as a key contributor to postoperative wound healing complications.

Previous studies have identified smoking as a risk factor for surgical site infections in patients undergoing free flap reconstruction[14]. In 2015, Chung et al published a retrospective study using the NSQIP database between 2005 and 2012 and found that among 2899 patients undergoing free flap tissue transfer for breast reconstruction, smoking was significantly associated with an increased risk of surgical site infections, including both superficial and deep SSIs [15]. Similarly, a study published in 2023 using the NSQIP database identified smoking as an independent risk factor for both superficial and deep surgical site infections following immediate autologous breast reconstruction[16]. Interestingly, while prior studies have primarily focused on superficial and deep incisional infections, our findings demonstrate an association between smoking and increased odds of organ/space SSIs following microvascular free flap reconstruction, with no significant differences in superficial or deep incisional SSIs. Organ/space infections often require additional interventions, prolonged antibiotic therapy, and increased healthcare utilization, which may pose a greater threat to reconstructive outcomes than those posed by superficial wound complications. This observation suggests that the influence of smoking on postoperative infectious morbidity may extend beyond incisional wound complications, with a need for further investigation into its relationship to deeper postoperative infections in reconstructive surgery.

Maintaining adequate tissue perfusion and microvascular anastomotic patency following free flap reconstruction is essential for flap survival, raising concerns that the ischemic effects of smoking may affect flap viability. In 2015, Wong et al conducted a large, national database analysis of the factors that contribute to microvascular free flap failure and found that smoking was not linked to free flap failure[17]. Another retrospective study by Ooms et al found that smoking was not associated with decreased flap survival among patients undergoing free flap reconstruction in the head and neck region[18]. Consistent with these findings, our analysis demonstrated no association between smoking status and graft/flap failure following microvascular free flap reconstruction. Since flap failure is a major concern for microvascular reconstruction, the absence of an association between smoking and graft/flap failure is reassuring. This suggests that although smoking poses a risk for impaired perfusion, it may not adversely affect flap survival to the same extent as it affects wound healing.

This study has potential limitations that should be acknowledged. First, NSQIP defines smoking status as tobacco use within 12 months of surgery and records it as a binary variable. As a result, important smoking-related characteristics, such as smoking intensity, duration, and timing of cessation, are unavailable, with pack-year data no longer collected after 2014. Furthermore, former smokers who quit more than 12 months prior to surgery are still classified as non-smokers, creating the potential for exposure misclassification and limiting evaluation of dose-dependent associations between smoking and postoperative outcomes. Second, NSQIP lacks procedure-specific clinical granularity that is relevant to microvascular free flap reconstruction, including flap recipient site, surgical indication, prior radiation, vessel quality, ischemia time, and procedure complexity. These factors may introduce biases that could affect the outcomes of reconstructive flap surgery in ways that we were not able to fully consider during our analysis. Third, differences in surgical technique, perioperative management, and postsurgical care across participating institutions could introduce variability that we could not fully control. Finally, within retrospective studies, additional confounding from unmeasured variables is possible despite multivariable adjustment. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. While this study provides important insights, future research should include studies that control relevant variables to further investigate these findings.

The results of this study have significant implications for clinical practice, particularly in the management of smokers undergoing microvascular free flap surgery. Understanding the risks associated with smoking is necessary to help surgeons counsel their patients prior to these procedures and to reconsider offering procedures to certain patient populations until smoking status has changed. These findings suggest that preoperative counseling should emphasize wound-healing risks rather than flap loss, which may better inform patient expectations and surgical decision-making. Although microvascular free flap surgery is often performed in urgent settings, establishing standardized guidelines for preoperative smoking cessation, when feasible, may help minimize these complications. It has been documented in the literature that complete smoking cessation for a period of 4 weeks prior to surgical procedures and continuing until primary healing of the surgical site (about 2 weeks) appears to reduce perioperative risks and improve surgical outcomes[19]. However, there are certain cases in which smoking cessation is not possible, such as trauma cases attempting limb salvage. In these situations, patients should be counseled appropriately. While preoperative smoking cessation remains recommended when clinically feasible, further research is needed to establish the optimal cessation interval specifically for patients undergoing microvascular free flap reconstruction. Additionally, incorporating smoking cessation programs and support systems within a patient’s surgical care plan can help patients achieve and maintain abstinence, ultimately enhancing their overall health and surgical results. From a healthcare systems perspective, understanding the cost implications of prolonged hospital stays due to higher complication rates among smokers is essential for optimizing care delivery. Prolonged recovery periods can lead to increased hospital costs due to the need for additional interventions, increased resource use, and extended monitoring, resulting in a significant financial burden not only on the patient but also on the healthcare system. This highlights the importance of understanding the risks associated with smokers undergoing microvascular free flap surgery, for which the implementation of targeted interventions could help lessen complications and ultimately reduce healthcare expenses associated with extended hospital stays.

Conclusion

This retrospective, multi-institutional, multi-surgeon, national study represents the first large-scale database analysis to identify smoking trends and their association with postoperative complications in plastic and reconstructive surgery patients undergoing microvascular free flap reconstruction. Identification of smoking as a risk factor for selected postoperative complications in flap reconstruction patients may help inform surgical planning, improve patient outcomes, and reduce hospital costs.
