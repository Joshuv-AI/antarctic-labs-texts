Steklov Eigenvalues of Nearly Circular Area-Normalized Domains
Lucas Alland, Robert Viator

Abstract
. We consider Steklov eigenvalues of nearly circular domains in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} of fixed unitary area. In [ VO18 ] , the authors treated such domains as perturbations of the disk, and they computed the first-order term of the asymptotic expansions of the Steklov eigenvalues for reflection-symmetric perturbations; here, we expand these first-order results beyond reflection-symmetry. We also recover the second-order asymptotic expansions, which enable us to prove that no Steklov eigenvalue beyond the first positive one is locally shape-optimized by the disk.

35C20, 35P05, 41A58

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain. The Steklov eigenvalue problem for
(\lambda,u)
on
\Omega
is given by

(1a)

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\ \ \textnormal{ in }\Omega,

(1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\lambda u

\displaystyle\ \ \textnormal{ on }\partial\Omega,

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian acting on
H^{1}(\Omega)
,
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u=\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}
is the unit outward normal derivative on the boundary
\partial\Omega
, and
\lambda
is the spectral parameter. It is well-known that the Steklov spectrum is discrete as long as the trace operator
T\colon H^{1}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
is compact
[
GP17
]
. Moreover, the eigenvalues are real and we enumerate them, counting multiplicity, in increasing order

0<\lambda_{0}(\Omega)<\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{2}(\Omega)\leq\dots\nearrow\infty.

The Steklov eigenvalue problem has received considerable attention in the literature; see the survey papers
[
GP17
,
Col+23
]
and references therein.

The Steklov eigenvalue problem was first introduced by Vladimir Steklov in
[
Ste02
]
to describe the stationary heat distribution in a body
\Omega
whose heat flux through the boundary is proportional to the temperature. For planar domains, the Steklov eigenvalues are the squares of the natural frequencies of a vibrating free membrane with all its mass concentrated along the boundary
[
Ban80
, p. 95]
. Steklov eigenvalues also have applications in optimal material design for both electromagnetism and torsional rigidity
[
Lip98
,
Lip98a
]
. Recently, Cakoni et al.
[
Cak+16
]
used Steklov eigenvalues in nondestructive testing, where they established a crucial relationship between small changes in the (possibly complex valued) refractive index of a scattering object and the corresponding change in the eigenvalue of a modified Steklov problem. For this problem, numerical results in
[
Cak+16
]
revealed that a localised defect of the refractive index in a disc perturbs only a small number of modified Steklov eigenvalues.

The problem of Steklov shape optimization has seen many new developments in the past decade. In
[
BBG17
]
, the authors discovered that the global optimizing domain for the
k
th Steklov eigenvalue (among shapes of fixed area) is a union of at most
k
disjoint Jordan domains. The authors of
[
AKO17
]
explore the shape-optimization of the Steklov problem among simply connected domains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
numerically, developing a conjecture that symmetric “star-shaped” domains are the optimizers among this class of shapes in the plane. In
[
FS19
]
, the authors explore the fixed surface volume Steklov optimization problem for contractible domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n\geq 3
, proving that Weinstock’s inequality
[
Wei54
]
for disks in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
does
not
hold in dimension
n\geq 3
. In
[
FS16
]
, the same authors find a remarkable connection between optimal metrics for the first non-zero Steklov eigenvalue of surfaces with boundary and free-boundary minimal surfaces: this relationship is explored in great numerical detail in
[
EO21
]
and
[
EO21a
]
. In
[
VO18
]
,
[
VO22
]
, and
[
TV24
]
, the authors further explore the area- and volume-constrained problems for domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 3
using perturbative methods, showing for all
d\geq 3
, the ball is never a local maximizer for an infinite list of Steklov eigenvalues; this is done by expanding the Steklov eigenvalues in a power series expansion in a shape-deformation parameter
\varepsilon>0
and calculating the order-
\varepsilon
coefficients for the power series expansions.

1.1.
Main results

We now further explore the method outlined in
[
VO18
]
,
[
VO22
]
, and
[
TV24
]
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; in particular, we seek out the order-
\varepsilon^{2}
terms in the power series expansions of the Steklov eigenvalues of nearly-circular domains. The primary deviations of this paper from
[
VO18
]
are twofold. First, we consider nearly-circular domains of general shape, ignoring the constraint of reflection-symmetry imposed in
[
VO18
]
. Second, we will consider only domains
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
of fixed area by normalizing the domains to have unit area
before
we begin the asymptotic analysis.

In particular, we consider domains of the form

(2)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{(r,\theta)\mid 0\leq\theta<2\pi,0\leq r\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta)}{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}}\}\text{ , }

where
v(\varepsilon)
is an area-normalization factor, and
\rho\in C^{1}(S^{1})
. We expand the Steklov eigenvalues of the domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
as a power series in
\varepsilon
, and we carefully extract the order-
\varepsilon
and order-
\varepsilon^{2}
terms. The order-
\varepsilon^{2}
terms give insight into the case where the order-
\varepsilon
terms are zero, revealing the following theorem about all non-zero Steklov eigenvalues after the first:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\sigma_{k}(\Omega)
be the
k
th non-zero Steklov eigenvalue of the area-normalized domain
\Omega
. If
k\geq 2
, then
\sigma_{k}
is never
locally
maximal when
\Omega
is a disk.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on explicit calculation of the second-order terms in the perturbation expansion of the Steklov eigenvalues in terms of the perturbation parameter
\varepsilon
.

1.3.
Outline

This paper is organized as follows. We begin by reviewing polar coordinates and compute the first-order and second-order asymptotic expansions for geometrical quantities related to
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
in Section
2
, including the area-normalization constraint. We also, in the same section, briefly discuss the analytic dependence of the Steklov eigenvalues on the parameter
\varepsilon
for suitable functions
\rho
. In Section
3
, we use the perturbative ansatz presented in Section
2
to derive the full asymptotic expansion for the Steklov eigenvalue problem on
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. In Section
4
, we derive the first-order asymptotic expansion for the Steklov eigenvalues of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.
In Section
5
, we obtain the second-order asymptotic expansion for Steklov eigenvalues of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, which we leverage to prove Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we first review polar coordinates, including the polar basis vectors
\hat{r}
and
\hat{\theta}
. We then area-normalize the nearly-circular domain
\omega_{\varepsilon}
to obtain a nearly circular domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
of unit area, and we calculate the first- and second-order asymptotic expansions for the unit outward normal vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}
to
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and powers of
R_{\varepsilon}
, the radius of
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

2.1.
Polar coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

We will work in the following moving basis for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{bmatrix}\ \ \hat{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta\\
\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\text{ . }

We record useful properties of this basis:

(1)

\frac{d}{d\theta}\hat{r}=\hat{\theta}
.

(2)

||\hat{r}||=||\hat{\theta}||=1
and
\hat{r}\cdot\hat{\theta}=0
.

(3)

(a\hat{r}+b\hat{\theta})\cdot(c\hat{r}+d\hat{\theta})=ac+bd
.

(4)

For a differentiable
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

(4)

\displaystyle\nabla f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\hat{r}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\hat{\theta}\text{ . }

We also recall the Steklov eigenvalue problem on the unit disk

=D=\{(r,\theta):0\leq r\leq 1,0\leq\theta<2\pi\}.

The problem (
1a
), (
1b
) becomes

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\left(r\partial_{r}u\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial^{2}_{\theta}u

\displaystyle=0\text{ in }D^{\circ}

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}u|_{r=1}

\displaystyle=\lambda u

The eigenvalues for this problem are the non-negative integers. The eigenvalue
\lambda=0
is multiplicity one, with eigenspace given by constant functions defined on
D
. For
\lambda=n>0
, the eigenvalue has multiplicity two: the eigenspace
E_{n}
is given by
E_{n}=\text{span}\{r^{n}\operatorname{cos}(n\theta),r^{n}\operatorname{sin}(n\theta)\}
.

In the case of dilated disk
D_{a}=aD
with
a>0
, the eigenspaces remain the same, but the corresponding eigenvalues
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}
for the eigenspace
E_{n}
satisfy the homothety property:
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}=\frac{1}{a}\lambda_{n}=\frac{1}{a}n
.

2.2.
Area-Normalized Domains

We define the
general nearly circular domain

\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
in polar coordinated by

(7)

\displaystyle\omega_{\varepsilon}=\{(r,\theta)\mid 0\leq\theta<2\pi,0\leq r\leq 1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta)\}

where
\rho\in C^{2}(S^{1})
where
S^{1}
denotes the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
\rho
may be expanded in the Fourier basis by

(8)

\displaystyle\rho(\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}\sin(j\theta)+b_{j}\cos(j\theta)

We recall Cauchy’s product for infinite sums,

(9)

\displaystyle(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j})(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b_{j})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{j}a_{i}b_{j-i}\text{ , }

to calculate a Fourier basis expansion for
\rho^{2}
in terms of the expansion of
\rho
:

(10)

\displaystyle\rho^{2}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Big[-a_{i}a_{j-i}(\cos(j\theta)-\cos((j-2i)\theta))+a_{i}b_{j-i}(\sin(j\theta)-\sin((j-2i)\theta))

\displaystyle+b_{i}a_{j-i}(\sin(j\theta)+\sin((j-2i)\theta))+b_{i}b_{j-i}(\cos(j\theta)+\cos((j-2i)\theta))\Big]\text{ . }

Now we define the function
v(\varepsilon)=|\omega_{\varepsilon}|
and explicitly calculate it:

(11)

\displaystyle v(\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta)}rdrd\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}1+2\varepsilon\rho(\theta)+(\varepsilon\rho(\theta))^{2}d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big[2\pi+4\pi\varepsilon b_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\int_{0}^{2\pi}[-a_{i}a_{j-i}(\cos(j\theta)-\cos((j-2i)\theta))

\displaystyle+a_{i}b_{j-i}(\sin(j\theta)-\sin((j-2i)\theta))+b_{i}a_{j-i}(\sin(j\theta)+\sin((j-2i)\theta))

\displaystyle+b_{i}b_{j-i}(\cos(j\theta)+\cos((j-2i)\theta))]d\theta\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi+2\pi\varepsilon b_{0}+\frac{\pi}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Big[\delta_{j,0}(b_{i}b_{j-i}-a_{i}a_{j-i})+\delta_{j-2i,0}(a_{i}a_{j-i}+b_{i}b_{j-i})\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi+2\pi\varepsilon b_{0}+\frac{\pi}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\Big[b_{0}^{2}-a_{0}^{2}+\sum_{\text{even }j}^{\infty}a_{\frac{j}{2}}^{2}+b_{\frac{j}{2}}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi+2\pi\varepsilon b_{0}+\frac{\pi}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\Big[b_{0}^{2}-a_{0}^{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}+b_{j}^{2}\Big]

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi+2\pi\varepsilon b_{0}+\frac{\pi}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\Big[2b_{0}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}+b_{j}^{2}\Big]\text{ . }

So we may now define the
area-normalized nearly circular domain
,
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
by

(13)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{(r,\theta)\mid 0\leq\theta<2\pi,0\leq r\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta)}{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}}\}\text{ , }

and we see that it is indeed area-normalized:

(14)

\displaystyle|\Omega_{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta)}{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}}}rdrd\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta)}{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}})^{2}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v(\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}(1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta))^{2}d\theta=\frac{v(\varepsilon)}{v(\varepsilon)}=1\text{ . }

Following the approach of
[
VO18
]
,
[
VO22
]
, we make the following perturbation ansatz for the solution
u_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}(r,\theta)
of (
1a
), (
1b
):

(15)

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^{k}(A_{k}(\varepsilon)\sin(k\theta)+B_{k}(\varepsilon)\cos(k\theta)),

where the coefficients
A_{k}(\varepsilon),B_{k}(\varepsilon)
are expanded in a power series in
\varepsilon
:

(16)

\displaystyle A_{k}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\delta_{k,n}\gamma_{n}+\mu_{k}\varepsilon+\eta_{k}\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})

(17)

\displaystyle B_{k}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\delta_{k,n}\alpha_{n}+\beta_{k}\varepsilon+\sigma_{k}\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})\text{.}

Here
n
enumerates the eigenpairs, not counting multiplicity. For simplicity, we will always assume
n>0
since the
0
th eigenpair has trivial eigenvalue, always identically 0.

2.3.
Asymptotic expansions for geometric quantities

We calculate a first- and second-order asympotic expansion for the outward unit normal
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}
to
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

We see we may parameterize
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
in
\theta
by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}}(1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta))\hat{r}
. Thus we see the tangent vector to the boundary is

(18)

\displaystyle\vec{\tau}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{d}{d\theta}\frac{1}{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}}(1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta))\hat{r}=\frac{\varepsilon\rho^{\prime}(\theta)}{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}}\hat{r}+\frac{1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta)}{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}}\hat{\theta}\text{ . }

Clearly
\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}
is normal to
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
when
\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\varepsilon}=0
so we find the normal vector

(19)

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon\rho(\theta)+1)\hat{r}-\varepsilon\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta}\text{ . }

Furthermore, we see that
\tilde{\nu}_{0}=\hat{r}
is the outward unit normal to
\Omega_{0}
. So we just need
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||}
. We find that the outward-facing unit normal is given by

(20)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{(\varepsilon\rho(\theta)+1)\hat{r}-\varepsilon\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}(\theta)+2\varepsilon\rho(\theta)+1+\varepsilon^{2}{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta)}}\text{ . }

By Taylor’s Theorem we can find first- and second-order approximations of
\hat{\boldsymbol{{\nu}}}_{\varepsilon}
by evaluating
\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\hat{\boldsymbol{{\nu}}}_{\varepsilon}\mid_{\varepsilon=0}
and
\frac{d^{2}}{d\varepsilon^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{{\nu}}}_{\varepsilon}\mid_{\varepsilon=0}

By the product rule,
\frac{d\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}}{d\varepsilon}=(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||})\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||}(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon})
. We will calculate these derivatives separately:

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{(2||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}}(\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}}(\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}(\theta)+2\varepsilon\rho(\theta)+1+\varepsilon^{2}{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta)))

(22)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}}(\rho(\theta)+\rho^{2}(\theta)\varepsilon+\rho^{\prime^{2}}(\theta)\varepsilon)

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(\varepsilon\rho(\theta)+1)\hat{r}-\varepsilon\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta}=\rho(\theta)\hat{r}-\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta}\text{ . }

Applying the product rule now yields

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}}{d\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho(\theta)\hat{r}-\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta}}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||}-\frac{(\rho(\theta)+\rho^{2}(\theta)\varepsilon+\rho^{\prime^{2}}(\theta)\varepsilon)((\varepsilon\rho(\theta)+1)\hat{r}-\varepsilon\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta})}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}}

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}}[(||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2}\rho(\theta)-\rho(\theta)-\varepsilon\rho^{\prime^{2}}(\theta)-2\varepsilon\rho^{2}(\theta)-\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{3}(\theta)-\varepsilon^{2}\rho(\theta)\rho^{\prime^{2}}(\theta))\hat{r}

\displaystyle-(||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2}\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-\varepsilon\rho(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{\prime^{3}}(\theta))\hat{\theta}\text{ . }

Using
\frac{d\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}}{d\varepsilon}
, we calculate
\frac{d^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}}{d\varepsilon^{2}}
. Let

(26)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon,\hat{r}}

\displaystyle=||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2}\rho(\theta)-\rho(\theta)-\varepsilon\rho^{\prime^{2}}(\theta)-2\varepsilon\rho^{2}(\theta)-\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{3}(\theta)-\varepsilon^{2}\rho(\theta)\rho^{\prime^{2}}(\theta)

(27)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon,\hat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2}\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-\varepsilon\rho(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{\prime^{3}}(\theta)\text{ . }

Then,

(28)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}}{d\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}[\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}|^{3}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon,\hat{r}}\hat{r}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon,\hat{\theta}}\hat{\theta})]

(29)

\displaystyle=(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon,\hat{r}}\hat{r}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon,\hat{\theta}}\hat{\theta})+\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}}(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon,\hat{r}}\hat{r}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon,\hat{\theta}}\hat{\theta}])\text{ . }

We calculate these derivatives separately:

(30)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}}=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||})^{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3(\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||})^{2}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2}}\frac{-1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}}(\rho(\theta)+\rho^{2}(\theta)\varepsilon+\rho^{\prime^{2}}(\theta)\varepsilon)

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-3}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{5}}(\rho(\theta)+\rho^{2}(\theta)\varepsilon+\rho^{\prime^{2}}(\theta)\varepsilon)

(32)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}[\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon,\hat{r}}\hat{r}-\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon,\hat{\theta}}\hat{\theta}]=

\displaystyle\Big((\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2})\rho(\theta)-{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta)-2\rho^{2}(\theta)-2\varepsilon\rho^{3}(\theta)-2\varepsilon\rho(\theta){\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta)\Big)\hat{r}

\displaystyle-\Big((\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{2})\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-\rho(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-2\varepsilon\rho^{2}(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-2\varepsilon{\rho^{\prime}}^{3}(\theta)\Big)\hat{\theta}

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big((2\rho(\theta)+2\varepsilon\rho^{2}(\theta)+2\varepsilon{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta))\rho(\theta)-{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta)-2\rho^{2}(\theta)-2\varepsilon\rho^{3}(\theta)

\displaystyle-2\varepsilon\rho(\theta){\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta)\Big)\hat{r}-\Big((2\rho(\theta)+2\varepsilon\rho^{2}(\theta)+2\varepsilon{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta))\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-\rho(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle-2\varepsilon\rho^{2}(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-2\varepsilon{\rho^{\prime}}^{3}(\theta)\Big)\hat{\theta}\text{ . }

Substituting into the product rule gives

(34)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}}{d\varepsilon^{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{-3}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{5}}(\rho(\theta)+\rho^{2}(\theta)\varepsilon+\rho^{\prime^{2}}(\theta)\varepsilon)(\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon,\hat{r}}\hat{r}-\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon,\hat{\theta}}\hat{\theta})+\frac{1}{||\tilde{\nu}_{\varepsilon}||^{3}}(((2\rho(\theta)+2\varepsilon\rho^{2}(\theta)

\displaystyle+2\varepsilon{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta))\rho(\theta)-{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta)-2\rho^{2}(\theta)-2\varepsilon\rho^{3}(\theta)-2\varepsilon\rho(\theta){\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta))\hat{r}-((2\rho(\theta)+2\varepsilon\rho^{2}(\theta)

\displaystyle+2\varepsilon{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta))\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-\rho(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-2\varepsilon\rho^{2}(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)-2\varepsilon{\rho^{\prime}}^{3}(\theta))\hat{\theta})\text{ . }

Now, we evaluate (
25
) and (
34
) at
\varepsilon=0
:

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}}{d\varepsilon}(0)

\displaystyle=-\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta}

(36)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}}{d\varepsilon^{2}}(0)

\displaystyle=-{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta)\hat{r}+2\rho(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta}

Thus, the outward-facing unit normal has the expansion

(37)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\varepsilon}=\hat{r}-\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta}\varepsilon+\Big(-{\rho^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta)\hat{r}+2\rho(\theta)\rho^{\prime}(\theta)\hat{\theta}\Big)\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})\text{ . }

We wish to find first- and second- order expansion of
R_{\varepsilon}^{k}
, where
R_{\varepsilon}
is the radius of
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary. From (
13
) we have that

(38)

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta)}{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}}\text{ . }

So,

(39)

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}^{k}

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta))^{k}(v(\varepsilon))^{-\frac{k}{2}}\text{ . }

We will find our expansion for
R^{k}_{\varepsilon}
by expanding each of these factors. By the binomial theorem we have

(40)

\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta))^{k}=1+k\rho(\theta)\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}k(k-1)\rho^{2}(\theta)\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})\text{ , }

and by Taylor’s theorem we have

(41)

\displaystyle(v(\varepsilon))^{-\frac{k}{2}}=v^{-\frac{k}{2}}(0)+\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}[v^{-\frac{k}{2}}(\varepsilon)](0)\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{d\varepsilon^{2}}[v^{-\frac{k}{2}}(\varepsilon)](0)\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})

We thus compute:

2.4.
Analytic Dependence on
\varepsilon>0

In
[
VO20
]
, the authors show that the perturbation of Steklov eigenvalues associated with domains of the form (
7
) is analytic in the parameter
\varepsilon
, or more specifically, that the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator (DNO) is analytic in this parameter. This was accomplished by introducing new coordinates

(50)

\displaystyle(r^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})=((1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta))^{-1}r,\theta)\text{ , }

which transforms
\omega_{\varepsilon}
to the disk and the Laplacian into a more sophisticated
\varepsilon
-dependent operator. We then show that the harmonic extension of functions defined on
\partial\omega_{\varepsilon}
, which are also transformed under the above change of coordinates, depend analytically on
\varepsilon
, and then leverage the analyticity of harmonic extensions and formula for the normal derivative in the new coordinates to show that the DNO is analytic as well.

This process can be repeated for the perturbation problem for area-normalized domains (
13
) presented in this paper. Here, the corresponding change of coordinates would be

(51)

\displaystyle(r^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})=\Big((\frac{\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}}{1+\varepsilon\rho(\theta)}r,\theta\Big)\text{ . }

Proceeding as in
[
VO20
]
section 2.1, we see that the term
\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}
comes out as a non-zero factor in the tranformed Laplacian, which can be divided from both sides of the homogeneous equation which the harmonic extension must satisfy. The analyticity of the harmonic extensions then follows in the same manner as
[
VO20
]
.

To prove the analyticity of the DNO, the authors of
[
VO20
]
again work in the tranformed coordinates (
50
), obtaining the following representation of the DNO
G_{\rho,\varepsilon}
:

\displaystyle G_{\rho,\varepsilon}\varphi

\displaystyle=M_{\rho}(\varepsilon)\left[\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(\rho^{\prime})^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon\rho)^{2}}\right)\partial_{r}u_{\varepsilon}-\frac{\varepsilon\rho^{\prime}}{1+\varepsilon\rho}\partial_{\theta}u_{\varepsilon}\right]

where
M_{\rho}(\varepsilon)
is an analytic real-valued function of
\varepsilon
, and
u_{\varepsilon}
is the harmonic extension of
\varphi
into the perturbed (non-area-normalized) domain. Applying the same method to the domain (
13
) and the cooresponding change of coordinates (
51
), it is easy to see that

\displaystyle G_{\rho,\varepsilon}\varphi

\displaystyle=M_{\rho}(\varepsilon)\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}\left[\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(\rho^{\prime})^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon\rho)^{2}}\right)\partial_{r}u_{\varepsilon}-\frac{\varepsilon\rho^{\prime}}{1+\varepsilon\rho}\partial_{\theta}u_{\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{M}_{\rho}(\varepsilon)\left[\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(\rho^{\prime})^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon\rho)^{2}}\right)\partial_{r}u_{\varepsilon}-\frac{\varepsilon\rho^{\prime}}{1+\varepsilon\rho}\partial_{\theta}u_{\varepsilon}\right]

where
\tilde{M}_{\rho}(\varepsilon)=\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}M_{\rho}(\varepsilon)
. Since
\sqrt{v(\varepsilon)}
is an analytic function of
\varepsilon
in a neighborhood of
\varepsilon=0
, the analyticity of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator associated with domains (
13
) in the parameter
\varepsilon
, and hence the analyticity of the Steklov eigenvalues of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, follows.
