Knowledge and experience of modern contraception use among Maasai communities in Longido district: a qualitative study
Authors: Sweetness Naftal Laizer, William Nkenguye, Mathayo Melubo, Nairisha Olanana, Clotilda S Tarimo, Sanjura Biswaro, Ester Kiwelu, Anita Kelly, Asia Hemed Kipengele, Nateiya Mmeta Yongolo, Blandina T Mmbaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionModern contraceptives play a crucial role in empowering women and couples to manage their fertility, achieve desired family size and promote optimal child spacing. Insufficient contraceptive use contributes to unplanned pregnancies, with adverse health and socioeconomic consequences, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. This study therefore aimed to explore the knowledge and experience of modern contraceptive use within the Maasai communities by learning the facilitators and barriers to its use.MethodsThis was a phenomenological qualitative study conducted among the Maasai community in Longido district, Arusha, to explore the knowledge and experiences of using modern contraceptives. In-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted among women and men in the community. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, translated and analysed using a thematic approach.ResultsA total of 35 participants (23 females, 12 males) took part in 12 IDIs and 3 FGDs, revealing a varied understanding of modern contraceptive methods alongside the continued use of traditional methods. Experiences highlighted diverse motivations for using modern contraceptives, emphasising their importance despite concerns about side effects and misconceptions. Facilitators included healthcare workers, supportive partners, friends and self-initiatives, while barriers included resistance from partners and entrenched traditions.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the need for the development and implementation of inclusive, culturally tailored and community-specific educational programmes that aim to involve all segments of the community, with a particular focus on engaging men and the elderly. The goal is to address existing knowledge gaps, dispel misconceptions and emphasise the benefits of modern contraceptives.Keywords: Qualitative Research, Public Health, Sexual Health

Introduction

Modern contraceptive methods, as defined by the WHO, include technological products or medical procedures used to prevent or delay pregnancy, such as implants, intrauterine devices (IUDs), injectables, oral contraceptive pills, condoms and permanent methods (female or male sterilisation). In contrast, traditional methods include fertility awareness-based approaches (eg, calendar/rhythm method), withdrawal and prolonged postpartum abstinence, which are generally less effective and more dependent on consistent user practice.1

Globally, in 2019 approximately 270 million women of reproductive age lacked access to modern contraceptive methods, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).2 By 2030, demand for modern contraceptives will escalate to 1.19 billion women, the majority of whom will reside in lower-middle-income countries.2 Within SSA, over 14 million pregnancies occur unplanned, with the highest incidence recorded in East and Southern Africa. In these regions, over 25% of women of reproductive age lack access to modern contraceptive use.3

In Tanzania, the modern contraceptives prevalence rate among women aged 15–49 was 31% in 2022, however, progress in reducing the unmet need for modern contraceptives has been slow from 22% in 2010 to 31% in 2022.4 Insufficient use of modern contraceptives can lead to unplanned pregnancy which results in poor health and socioeconomic consequences for the mother and child.5 Poor health outcomes such as increased risk for the mother such as eclampsia, puerperal endometritis and systemic infections due to abortion. For the child, inadequate interpregnancy intervals are associated with higher risk of low birth weight, preterm delivery and severe neonatal conditions.6

Tanzania aims to increase the modern contraceptive prevalence rate for all women to 42%, to do so, rural areas a home to 65.1%7 of the country’s population must be educated and encouraged to use modern contraceptives, as these areas reportedly have higher fertility rates than urban areas. The Maasai are predominantly pastoralist and maintain strong cultural traditions related to marriage, gender roles and fertility. Large family size is often socially valued, and reproductive decision-making frequently occurs within the context of male partner influence and extended family or elder authority. In addition, mobility associated with pastoral livelihoods and geographical distance from health facilities may limit consistent access to reproductive health services, which are typically provided through primary health facilities and periodic outreach programmes.8

Limited studies have been conducted in this population to understand their knowledge and experience in modern contraceptive use. Understanding community beliefs, social norms and access-related challenges is essential for designing culturally appropriate and acceptable family planning interventions. Therefore, this study explored the knowledge and experience of modern contraceptive use within the Maasai communities by identifying the facilitators and barriers to its use.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a phenomenological qualitative study conducted from April to May 2022 in Tinga Tinga village, Longido District, Arusha Region. A phenomenological approach was selected to explore participants’ lived experiences, perceptions and meanings related to modern contraceptive use within the Maasai cultural context. Data were collected using both in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs). IDIs were used to capture individual experiences and sensitive issues related to contraceptive use, while FGDs were conducted to explore shared community norms, beliefs and social influences. The use of both methods enabled triangulation of individual and collective perspectives to enhance the depth and credibility of the findings. Tinga Tinga was selected because it is a predominantly Maasai rural settlement that reflects common sociocultural and livelihood characteristics of Maasai communities in the district, including pastoralism, extended family structures, and strong adherence to traditional norms related to marriage, fertility and gender roles. The village is served by nearby primary health facilities and periodic outreach services that provide maternal and family planning education, making it an appropriate setting to explore community knowledge and experiences related to modern contraceptive use. The Maasai community is known for relatively high fertility preferences, with large family size often culturally valued and some women having multiple births, including cases of up to 10 or more children.9

Research team and reflexivity

The interviews and focus groups were conducted by a team of five trained research assistants (RAs) proficient in qualitative data collection methods, including three females and two males. Each RA had prior experience in qualitative and community-based research. Before each session, informed consent was obtained, and the study’s structure and objectives were explained to establish rapport with participants.

Participants characteristics

The study included women aged 18 years and above. The age restriction was applied primarily for ethical reasons, as participants needed to provide independent informed consent; inclusion of minors would have required additional consent procedures and protections. Adult participants were considered appropriate for discussing reproductive experiences and decision-making. Older adults, including those beyond reproductive age, were also included as they shared experiences related to the transition between traditional and modern contraceptive practices and served as key community influencers. Village leaders, partners and traditional birth attendants (TBAs) were also included, recognising their important roles as stakeholders in influencing and supporting modern contraceptive use within the community.

Data collection

Village leaders and community health workers helped identify eligible participants, including women of reproductive age, their husbands and TBAs. Convenience sampling targeted individuals with the necessary information and availability. Participants were recruited purposively based on their relevance to the study objectives and their availability during the data collection period.

On the day of data collection, the research team conducted interviews and FGDs. After obtaining written or fingerprinted consent using a semistructured interview guide, interviews which were recorded lasting 45 to 60 min were conducted in quiet areas at the village leader’s offices. All interviews and discussions were conducted in Kiswahili or Maasai based on participant preference. Quality control measures were implemented daily, including debriefings and secure handling of recorded interviews.

Data management and analysis

Audio recordings were transferred daily to a password-protected computer and securely stored. Recordings were transcribed verbatim in Kiswahili and/or Maa and then translated into English by a bilingual linguist. Two bilingual researchers independently cross-checked transcripts against the audio recordings and field notes to verify accuracy and resolve discrepancies through discussion, ensuring reliability of transcription and translation.

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis guided by Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework.10 First, the analysis team familiarised themselves with the data by repeatedly reading transcripts and reviewing field notes while documenting initial reflections. Second, initial codes were generated systematically by identifying meaningful segments of text relevant to the study aims. Third, codes were grouped and organised into potential themes by examining patterns across interviews and focus groups. Fourth, candidate themes were reviewed and refined by checking coherence within themes and distinction between themes and by comparing them against the full dataset. Fifth, themes were defined and named, and clear descriptions were developed to capture the core meaning of each theme and related subthemes. Finally, the report was produced by selecting illustrative quotations and linking themes to the study objectives and existing literature.

To enhance credibility, two analysts independently coded the initial transcripts and then met to compare coding interpretations and agree on a shared coding structure. Regular team discussions were held throughout analysis to refine codes and themes and to ensure that interpretations were grounded in the data.

Themes and subthemes

Theme 1: knowledge of contraceptives (modern and traditional)

Knowledge of modern contraceptives

Participant’s knowledge of modern contraceptives varied by gender and age. While younger women demonstrated greater awareness of specific contraceptive methods, older women and men exhibited limited knowledge due to factors such as timing of methods introduction within the community and limited access to health education.

Two young women said:

To my knowledge about modern contraceptives, we received training on family planning that if you want to use contraceptives there are many types of contraceptives, like an implant, an injection method and a pill (Young woman_01)

Yes, modern methods are like using injections, implants, intrauterine devices (IUD), pills, condoms, using calendar and prolonged breastfeeding (Young woman FGD_R1)

Some older women in the community lacked knowledge of modern contraceptives, as these became available after their menopause:

My child, we don’t know because we have never used them, because it has no meaning to us so we don’t ask at all, we gave birth until it was the end for us (Elder woman FGD_R5)

Despite Ministry of Health efforts to promote antenatal clinic attendance for pregnant women and their partners, some men in the community did not attend, missing out on health education and counselling sessions, including those about modern contraceptive use. One male participant pointed out:

For us, we don’t get training on modern contraceptives, women only receive in that way of attending the clinic (Partner_04)

Knowledge of traditional methods of contraceptive

Maasai communities have traditionally used methods like abstinence and the calendar method for contraception, along with local herbs and cold water. Abstinence is commonly practised after childbirth until the child is around 3 years old, contributing to the prevalence of polygamy among Maasai men as they spend time with other wives during this period.

I personally do this, which is why I have three wives. When one wife is pregnant, I stay away from her and go to my other wife who has an older child. I stay there until this other wife delivers and the child is old enough, about three years old, before I return to her. (Partner_08).

Women also expressed their knowledge and practice of this abstinence method by saying:

So if he knows that this woman has a young child, he leaves you for a whole year, even for two years or three, until the child has completely grown up. You stay without having sex with a man until the child has completely grown up (Elder woman FGD_R7).

The use of the calendar method and male withdrawal during sex has also been reported by participants as one of the traditional methods used to avoid pregnancy in Maasai communities.

But I understand that there is a menstrual cycle, that you look at the days of menstruation, four days after menstruation, then you can continue to have sex, it can avoid getting pregnancy. (Young woman_01).

The other method that I use is the withdrawal method. Even when the woman has a small child or when she is pregnant, I can use the withdrawal method. However, I always ensure that the woman is satisfied. (Partner_08).

Some participants mentioned traditional methods to avoid pregnancy, such as drinking a lot of water or ‘Coca-Cola’ after sex or consuming herbs like aloe vera.

I have heard that if you do not want to get pregnant after being with a man, you drink a litre of water… it destroys the man’s sperm and you will not get pregnant, but I’m not really sure as I never used it. (Young woman FGD_R1)

There’s a belief that if you accidentally sleep with someone during your fertile days, you should drink Coca-Cola soda. (Elder woman FGD_R2)

You can drink water that has aloe vera roots from the soil. (Elder woman FGD_R3)

Sources of information for modern contraceptive use

When participants did possess knowledge of modern contraceptives, they most often attributed it to the formal health channels. Health facilities, outreach programmes and school-based education emerged as the primary sources of information, highlighting the critical role of healthcare access in providing contraceptive awareness.

For us, we know at the hospital they give education about the contraception methods (Elder woman FGD_R6)

We were given education at the clinic when we attended the clinic for the delivery of the child, we are often given this training, and the healthcare workers are ready to help anyone who agrees to use it right there (Young woman _01)

I have studied in school, and I have heard it in many of the courses we are taught by various organizations (Young woman FGD_R4)

Theme 2: experience or practice of modern contraceptive use

Importance of modern contraceptives

Participants highlighted the importance of modern contraceptives, noting their role in facilitating child spacing, enabling women to maintain work or daily activities and contributing to healthier growth and education for their children.

One young woman stated:

They are important because they help us women not to get pregnant often and enable us to support the family. If you look at it, you have a baby every year and you can’t even work until you get overwhelmed by responsibilities in the family… but using contraceptives helps in planning and helps the family to send kids to school and feed them well… because if you are giving birth every year the family will deteriorate as kids will be malnourished and vulnerable to different diseases. We are happy for the initiation of contraceptive methods (Young woman FGD_R01)

An elderly woman contributed that:

First of all, when you use this, you gain the strength to carry out your activities without feeling tired. Lack of sufficient food can leave someone feeling worn out or even emaciated. If a woman has many children, it can mentally and sometimes psychologically affect her to a point where she may not be okay. For instance, in our Maasai community in the past, fathers favored a large number of children as they didn’t see the importance of education. But now, people understand that children need to study. If you have ten children and attempt to send them all to school, it may cause them hardship. That’s why we try to influence them. Honestly, I had these six children by mistake, because if it were up to me, I would have three or four children. (Elder woman_05)

Even the husbands acknowledged the importance of using modern contraceptives to improve their family’s living conditions. One of them said:

From how I understand modern contraceptives, they help ensure children receive their basic needs according to their age difference. It also allows you time to prepare for their needs, like education. When there’s a three year age gap before the next child goes to school, it gives you time to prepare a good plan for the other one. This reduces the dependency on the mother always being pregnant and carrying a child. Otherwise, she will be dependent on you, causing conflict within the family. Thus, even the mother will have no time to look for her daily livelihood (Partner FGD_R4).

Usage of modern contraceptives

In this community, many women have been using modern contraceptives for years. Some of these women reported making this decision as a family, with both the woman and her husband agreeing. Others decided to use contraceptives for their health after having multiple pregnancies. Some women reported that their husbands were opposed to the use of modern contraceptives, but they chose to use them secretly.

I started using injections last year. In our traditions and cultures, we are not allowed to use such methods, so it’s done secretly. You can’t tell your partner that you’re using it, and you have to ask the doctor not to write your name to maintain confidentiality. This is an absolute secret and should not be known. People use implants secretly too, but if they do, it can be seen in the hand, because many are now looking at women’s hands. (Young woman_01)

Side effects of modern contraceptives

Modern contraceptive use was often tempered by concerns about its side effects. Women reported a range of physical reactions such as dizziness, weight fluctuations and menstrual changes that sometimes interfered with its use or required them to switch methods. Notably, some women tolerated these side effects rather than risk pregnancy, particularly when facing spousal opposition.

One of the elder women revealed:

After I gave birth, I started using pills but they did not accept me because I was always dizzy, When I just take them, they make me feel dizzy I had to change into injections (Elder woman FGD_R2)

It depends on the person because one injection and it may cause changes in her body as some can be overweight and some can be underweight depending on how somebody’s body reacts (Young woman FGD_R4)

Despite some of these side effects, some women in this community continue using modern contraceptives and tolerate the side effects just to prevent from giving birth to more children, as elaborated below:

Others use injections and they say they get heavy periods, also the periods don’t have the normal flow. Some even say they don’t get to see their periods, so if a person doesn’t see their periods, it’s something bad. Others get pain in their backs and legs, so they say that they are just using it to prevent pregnancy because the husbands don’t understand they only want children (Elder woman FGD_R6)

Furthermore, one of the husbands believed that the side effects of modern contraceptives not only affect the woman using it but can also affect the husband as well. He said:

The pills, even for men is a problem… may lead you to get prostate problems. For a woman, it may affect her and bring cancer to her womb and you might find that she has a lot of headaches, sometimes a stomach-ache, it is a disturbance…and for yourself also you feel there are times when you do the marriage act, from the fluids of the woman, it passes in your reproductive ducts… it affects you… you find that you are also sick, you feel your kidneys hurt (Partner FGD_R4)

Sources of help for modern contraceptive side effects

With the reported side effects of modern contraceptives experienced by women in these communities, they further elaborated on who they go to for help when they face these side effects. The help ranges from advice from a friend, support from husbands and seeking help from healthcare workers in the nearby health facilities.

Friends can help you, persuade you to use another method if the previous one has been difficult for you to use, or health centres help you such as… when you have a headache, they give you pain medication so you can relieve the pain. (Young woman FGD_R4)

My husband, he can help me if now the injection doesn’t suit me, and he can persuade me to use implants to see if that goes well with me (Young woman FGD_R5)

Theme 3: facilitators/barriers/influence of modern contraceptive use

Facilitators of modern contraceptive use

Women’s decision to use modern contraceptives was influenced by multiple sources of support. These facilitators ranged from intrinsic decisions, partner support, friends and healthcare workers’ influence as well.

One of the women revealed that her decision to start using modern contraceptives was her own decision to make:

I haven’t been influenced by anyone I influence myself after seeing my children I thought quickly that now I will reach a point as a mother because, in the beginning, I told you I was young I didn’t have the intelligence so you give birth to a one year-old and you finish a year you get pregnant again the one hasn’t grown well, eh yes you need to be aware on your own and say let me use family planning and stay for a while so that this one grows (Elder woman_05)

Some husbands can be motivators for their wives to use modern contraceptives as relayed by this woman:

When the baby was one year and five months old, we agreed with my husband so he took me to hospital. I told him I needed injections so he agreed and went to the hospital, and now I have an appointment to return there after three months (Young woman FGD_R8)

Friends who are also using modern contraceptives have been identified as one of the facilitators. Their success in family planning and having time to work themselves are big motivators for other women in the communities.

My friend who uses injections, brags when she sees you with a baby while she keeps doing their activities, She is also encouraging, because she gets to work every day and fights poverty (Young woman FGD_R6)

Through the trust and rapport that healthcare workers have built within the communities, they tend to be one of the most common facilitators for modern contraceptive use, not only to women but also to men.

At the clinic, there is the midwife who provides the seminar when we are there and not only once, other women come and ask her to tell me what should I do so I don’t want to give birth again quickly or I am tired of giving birth what should I do (Elder woman_05)

Barriers to modern contraceptive use

Despite several facilitators for modern contraceptive use, significant barriers exist. The main obstacle reported is husbands’ refusal to allow or accept their wives’ using contraceptives. This has led some women to forgo contraception despite their desire or need, while others use contraceptives secretly.

The husbands refuse to allow other wives to use contraceptives because large percent say maybe when you get the injection you will cheat… eh why do you get syringes so that you can cheat or you become a prostitute and you stop getting pregnant… (Elder woman_05)

No women now, they hide because in our society only a few men are aware of these family planning methods now it’s up to you women because in our society it is women who carry the burden of family, if you feel overwhelmed with the responsibilities its when you look for an alternative to raise the family and even when he asks you deny using it “ask the doctor if they have even seen me?” (Young woman FGD_R1)

My friend has used injections for two years now, she has been using them secretly because she fears beating from her husband. She used it secretly without permission from her husband because she conceived consecutively but this injection method is perfect it gives peace in life and time to work. For me, my husband has forbidden to let me use it (Young woman FGD_R6)

The Maasai tradition poses a barrier to modern contraceptive use due to the belief that wealth is measured by having many children, particularly daughters who bring substantial livestock as dowry when they marry.

Tradition in the Maasai community doesn’t want someone to take contraceptives, their enjoyment is having children every day to saying they are enjoying eeh but do you think customs sometimes especially those that are misleading should be outdated because they enjoy, the father enjoys when he has a lot of children you give birth, especially female children so that he gets wealth. But now the mothers are aware nobody lies to them and that thing (contraceptive) in the community is a secret, she goes to use it secretly (Elder woman_05)

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the knowledge and practice of modern contraceptives among members of the Maasai community in the Longido district. Our findings indicate a varied understanding of modern contraceptive methods, with traditional methods still in use. Experiences with modern contraceptives revealed diverse motivations for their use, highlighting their importance alongside associated side effects. Facilitators identified included healthcare workers and supportive partners, while barriers encompassed resistance from spouses.

Our research noted a contrast in modern contraceptive knowledge between younger and older Maasai women, unlike a study in Kenya where knowledge was lacking among young Maasai women.11 This difference may stem from educational efforts in remote Tanzanian regions and the availability of services in local dispensaries. However, older women seemed to miss out on this knowledge transfer, creating gaps in understanding. Similar findings in rural Ethiopia highlight the effectiveness of educational interventions for younger populations, emphasising the need for inclusive programmes across age groups to promote widespread acceptance and understanding of modern contraceptives.12

Traditional contraceptive methods like postpartum abstinence, withdrawal and calendar methods are prevalent in the community, favoured for their perceived lack of side effects compared with modern alternatives. Similar practices are seen in Kenya, West Africa13 and rural Ghana, driven by cultural acceptance and perceived lower side effect profiles.14 These findings emphasise the need for culturally sensitive health promotion that respects traditional values while educating about the benefits and safety of modern contraceptives.

Our study highlights the benefits of modern contraceptives in child spacing, enabling women to work and promoting overall health and child welfare, aligning with previous Tanzanian studies.15–17 However, concerns about side effects remain a common barrier to adoption. A Nigerian study found that fears of menstrual irregularities and weight gain deterred contraceptive use, echoing our findings.18 Addressing these concerns through transparent communication about side effects and management strategies is vital for informed contraceptive choices. Additionally, dispelling misconceptions, especially regarding potential harm to male partners, is crucial for promoting acceptance and understanding of modern contraceptives.

Furthermore, healthcare workers, partners and friends play pivotal roles in motivating modern contraceptive use among women, aligning with findings from Ghana,19 where trust in healthcare providers and partner approval were influential. A study in Kenya demonstrated that male partner involvement in family planning discussions improved contraceptive uptake among couples.20 Conversely, some Maasai women in our study opted for modern contraceptives without partner consent to balance work and childcare responsibilities. A barrier identified is partners’ disapproval, hindering women’s autonomy in contraceptive decision-making. Despite the notion of family involvement, some men restrict discussions, leading to secretive contraceptive use, a phenomenon noted in research from Uganda where women covertly used contraceptives to navigate partner opposition and cultural expectations. This lack of male involvement echoes findings from other regions in Tanzania21,22 and Nigeria.23 Moreover, community norms favouring large families as a symbol of wealth can deter women from adopting modern contraceptives, reflecting similar sentiments in rural Uganda.24 All these however highlight a demand for family planning services that prioritise women’s autonomy and confidentiality

Strengths and limitations

The study benefits from a diverse participant group, including different age groups, genders and community roles such as partners, village leaders and TBAs, providing a comprehensive understanding of perceptions and experiences related to modern contraceptive use within the Maasai community. The focus on a culturally distinct population with strong traditional values offers context-specific insights that are important for designing culturally appropriate family planning interventions in similar pastoralist and rural settings.

However, several limitations should be considered. The qualitative design provides in-depth understanding but limits the generalisability of findings to other populations or settings. Participants were recruited purposely with enrolment based on availability, which may have introduced selection bias. The relatively high proportion of participants with five or more children reflects prevailing fertility norms in the Maasai community and the inclusion of older participants and key community influencers, but this may have influenced the range of perspectives represented.

Given the sensitive nature of reproductive health topics, responses may have been affected by social desirability bias, particularly where community leaders or health workers assisted in recruitment. Although interviews were conducted in local languages and efforts were made to ensure accurate translation, some meaning or cultural nuance may have been lost during transcription and translation into English.

In addition, researcher positionality and participants’ perceptions of the research team as being affiliated with health services may have influenced participants’ responses. Daily team debriefings and reflexive discussions were conducted to minimise this effect.

Conclusions

This study in Maasai communities of Longido District, Tanzania, explores modern contraceptive knowledge and usage complexities. It found varying awareness levels, notably among different age groups like younger versus older women. Key facilitators for modern contraceptive use include healthcare workers’ involvement and partner support, while barriers include male partner opposition and traditional beliefs. Targeted educational efforts are needed to address misconceptions and promote modern contraceptives’ benefits for family planning and overall well-being, especially targeting men and the elderly.

The findings highlight the importance of culturally sensitive and community-tailored family planning interventions that acknowledge local fertility norms, gender dynamics and decision-making structures. Programmes that actively engage men, elders, TBAs and community leaders may enhance acceptability and uptake of modern contraceptive methods. Strengthening community outreach, couple-based counselling and locally appropriate health education could help address persistent myths and fears related to side effects.

Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of culturally adapted interventions in improving contraceptive uptake and explore strategies to enhance women’s autonomy and informed decision-making within pastoralist and other underserved communities.
