A smart fiber-optic towed hydrophone array for open-sea cruising observation
Authors: Shuaichen Li, Quan Chai, Muxin Jia, Heping Shen, Liang Zhang, Wenyang Duan, Jingwei Yin, Jianzhong Zhang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Ocean observation has traditionally relied on surface and spaceborne platforms, including buoys, satellite altimeters, radar, optical imagery, and AIS-based vessel tracking, which provide broad coverage of surface waves and maritime activity.1,2 Beneath the sea surface, interferometric fiber-optic hydrophones provide passive, lightweight, multiplexed, and high-sensitivity acoustic sensing.3,4 Distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) on submarine cables has also enabled long-distance observations of seafloor earthquakes, ocean-solid Earth interactions, storms, ships, and marine bioacoustics.5,6 However, standard fibers and telecom cables have limited responsivity to weak underwater signals. Recent helically wound and sensitized DAS configurations improve pressure-to-strain coupling and support towed hydrophone and marine streamer applications.7,8 Nevertheless, the broader capability of DAS hydrophones in a moving towed cable remains underexploited. Here, we demonstrate a self-aware hybrid fiber-optic towed hydrophone array that integrates a DAS section with a high-signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) interferometric section, enabling depth and array-shape self-sensing, ocean-wave observation, and underwater acoustic detection within one intelligent composite cable.

In the present sea trial, the deployed towed cable had a total length of approximately 770 m, and the sensing array was towed about 500 m behind the vessel. Within the same cable, the interferometric and DAS sections used separate sensing fibers and independent optical interrogation paths. The interferometric section contained 128 hydrophone elements over approximately 104 m, while the DAS section contained 32 hydrophone elements with a 5 m spacing over approximately 160 m (Figure 1A). Both sections employ compact fiber-wound air-backed mandrels with a length of 42 mm and an outer diameter of 11.6 mm as pressure-sensitive transducer structures, but they differ in wound-fiber length and optical readout mechanism. Each interferometric hydrophone element contains 22 m of wound fiber in a push-pull configuration and forms the high-SNR acoustic backbone, whereas each DAS hydrophone element contains 55 m of helically wound sensing fiber and converts pressure- and temperature-induced strain into distributed optical phase responses. Embedded depth sensors and weak fiber Bragg gratings provide depth references, gauge-length marking, and temperature monitoring. This passive and highly multiplexed architecture assigns complementary functions to the two mechanisms: sensitive acoustic detection by the interferometric section and distributed depth, wave, and acoustic sensing by the DAS section.

Figure 1B shows the phase response of a representative DAS element during the sea trials. The subsequent functional demonstrations use frequency-partitioned interpretation: the quasi-static (<0.02 Hz) component for depth sensing and array-shape inversion, the 0.02–0.3 Hz band for ocean-wave observation, and the 5 Hz–2 kHz band for acoustic detection. This frequency-partitioned logic is central to the system: the same DAS cable acts as a distributed physical interface linking the towed array, the ocean environment, and the acoustic field.

We first examined whether DAS can sense depth comparably to a depth sensor (Figure 1C). When a towed element undergoes vertical motion, the hydrostatic pressure acting on its package changes, and this pressure perturbation modulates the DAS phase. In Figure 1C1, the DAS-derived depth, converted using a measured pressure sensitivity of −129 dB re 1 rad/μPa, agrees well with the depth variation measured by the depth sensor, with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.99. This result indicates that the quasi-static DAS phase contains a stable hydrostatic-pressure component and can therefore serve as a measurable observable for distributed depth sensing along the towed cable.

The time-domain DAS-derived depth also follows the depth-sensor record with an average relative deviation of 0.80% (Figure 1C2), demonstrating recovery of slow cable-depth variations. The DAS element has a measured temperature sensitivity of 1.61 × 104 rad °C−1, but the water-column temperature variation during the trial was only on the order of 10−2°C. The thermal phase contribution was therefore much smaller than the pressure-induced phase change, and pressure dominated the depth inversion. Under the uncommon condition of large temperature variations, the embedded weak FBG can provide real-time temperature monitoring to compensate for the temperature-induced phase contribution before depth inversion.

Using all valid DAS elements, we reconstructed the time-varying depth profile of the DAS section (Figure 1C3). The result shows that the actual towed cable was not an ideal horizontal array under towing and hydrodynamic forcing. Unlike conventional towed arrays that rely on separate and sparsely deployed depth-sensing units, each DAS hydrophone element provides local depth information at the acoustic-sensing position, yielding denser depth constraints for array-shape inversion and subsequent depth-aware beamforming correction.

We next examined the ocean-wave band using the EU Copernicus Marine Service Information (CMEMS) Global Ocean Waves Analysis and Forecast product as a reference.9 At 11:00 and 14:00, the measured DAS-phase PSD peaks are 0.102 and 0.098 Hz, whereas the CMEMS peak frequencies are 0.129 and 0.131 Hz (Figure 1D1). After accounting for wave direction, towing heading, and the Doppler shift, the theoretical encounter frequencies sensed by the towed cable become 0.105 and 0.107 Hz. The relative differences between the measured and theoretical frequencies are 2.86% and 8.41%, respectively. This agreement indicates that the DAS cable captured wave-induced dynamic pressure modulation in the moving reference frame of the towed array.

Band-pass filtering within the same wave band reveals the distributed response (Figure 1D2). Continuous stripe-like phase structures show that the wave-induced disturbance is spatially traceable along the array rather than local to one sensing point. Compared with a point sensor, DAS provides both temporal evolution and spatial propagation of low-frequency wave signals, allowing the cable to act as a moving line-array observer of ocean dynamics.

This low-frequency response was further converted into significant wave height using real-time element depth (Figure 1D3) because wave-induced dynamic pressure attenuates with depth. Based on linear wave theory, the DAS-phase spectrum was first converted into the dynamic-pressure spectrum at the element depth and then corrected using the depth-dependent pressure response factor to obtain the surface-elevation spectrum.10 The significant wave height estimated from the zeroth-order spectral moment ranges from 1.41 to 2.66 m. Relative to CMEMS values at 11:00 and 14:00, the differences are 4.31% and 11.03%, respectively, showing that DAS depth self-sensing supports quantitative oceanographic inversion. The minimum detectable significant wave height depends on the sensitivity of the hydrophone elements, the noise floor of the DAS system, and the towing depth of the array. A shallower towing depth increases the received wave-induced pressure and therefore lowers the detection threshold. At a towing depth of 50 m, the system is estimated to have a minimum detectable significant wave height of 0.15 m and a theoretical dynamic range of 180 dB.

Finally, we examined the acoustic detection band. Figure 1E1 compares the PSDs of the DAS hydrophone array under land-quiet and open-sea towing conditions and compares DAS with interferometric hydrophones during towing. Over the 5–2,000 Hz acoustic band, the DAS PSD under open-sea towing shows a median elevation of 11.57 dB relative to the land-quiet condition, indicating that the array responds to the real underwater sound field in the sea-trial environment. The interferometric hydrophone has a lower high-frequency noise level under the same towing condition, especially above approximately 2 kHz, confirming its role as the high-SNR acoustic backbone for weak high-frequency acoustic detection. The two mechanisms are therefore complementary rather than substitutive.

Conventional beamforming (CBF) was applied to DAS data at 120–150 Hz, containing cooperative-vessel mechanical noise, and 900–1,100 Hz, containing 1 kHz pulses from a cooperative source 5.7 km away with a nominal source level of 160 dB re 1 μPa at 1 m (Figure 1E2). In both bands, the beamforming energy follows the GPS-derived bearing trajectory, demonstrating acoustic target detection under open-sea towing. The beamforming results obtained independently from the DAS and interferometric hydrophone sections can also be compared to cross-check the detected target bearing.

Standard CBF was first performed using an uncorrected straight-array model. However, Figure 1C3 shows that the actual towed array had a curved depth profile, causing the assumed array manifold to deviate from the true element positions. We therefore introduced the DAS-derived depth profile into the array manifold and obtained a depth-aware corrected beamforming model. In the 900–1,100 Hz band, the corrected model increased peak beamforming power by up to 4.10 dB relative to the uncorrected model (Figure 1E3), indicating that DAS-derived array-shape information can directly improve coherent focusing. This result demonstrates a closed loop of acoustic target sensing, array-shape self-sensing, and beamforming correction within the DAS section.

In summary, the hybrid cable integrates three functions traditionally treated separately: array-state sensing, ocean observation, and acoustic detection. The interferometric section provides high-SNR acoustic capability, whereas the DAS section converts the towed cable into a distributed self-sensing line array, enabling depth inversion, array-shape reconstruction, wave-band observation, significant-wave-height retrieval, acoustic target detection, and depth-aware beamforming correction. The value of the system is not the simple coexistence of two hydrophone technologies but the scalable integration of distributed DAS and high-sensitivity interferometric hydrophones. By interleaving intrinsically scalable DAS hydrophones with high-sensitivity interferometric hydrophones, this architecture provides a practical route toward shape-aware, high-performance towed arrays for next-generation underwater acoustic detection.
