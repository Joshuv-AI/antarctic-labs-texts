The impact of COVID-19 on pancreaticoduodenectomy outcomes in a hepatopancreatobiliary centre of excellence
Authors: Helia Nabavian, Lev Bubis, Shiva Jayaraman, Melanie Tsang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:At the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, access to “planned” surgical care was restricted as the health care system responded to the coronavirus. We hypothesized that the pandemic resulted in diagnostic and therapeutic delays, leading to stage migration among patients with malignancies treated with a Whipple procedure.Methods:This study is a retrospective review of adults who underwent surgical exploration for a planned pancreaticoduodenectomy for malignancy at St. Joseph’s Health Centre between March 11, 2019, and March 11, 2021.Results:We included 180 patients in the study. Baseline characteristics, pathologic diagnoses, and perioperative outcomes were similar between the 2 cohorts. The post-COVID group had longer median wait times from date of consent (p < 0.001), and from computed tomography (CT) scan (p < 0.001), to surgery. There were increased rates of R1 margin positivity in the post-COVID group (p = 0.01). We saw an association between higher wait times from consent and the last CT scan to the date of operation, and increased rates of R1 margin positivity in the first year of the pandemic.Conclusion:This study demonstrated the importance of prioritizing care during a pandemic and provided evidence for potential long-term consequences when there are delays in surgery for aggressive gastrointestinal malignancies.

The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in substantial restrictions to medical care delivery, including the provision of surgical oncology care.1–3 In Ontario, which has a population of about 14 million residents, a backlog of 148 364 surgeries accumulated, requiring an estimated 1.5 years to clear.3–5 During this period of elective surgery disruption, health care workers and resources across various specialties were diverted to treat those infected with the virus on both a national and local level, limiting the capacity to urgently operate on patients with cancer.5,6 Given these reasons for restriction, treatment protocols that normally entailed urgent surgery for pancreas, gallbladder, and liver cancers underwent temporizing changes.5 The consequences of these disruptions and alterations in care pathways are not, however, well understood. As we may anticipate potential future pandemics or other forms of disruptions of surgical oncology care, elucidating the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on hepato-pancreatobiliary (HPB) surgical oncology care is paramount for enhancing the resilience of cancer care systems.

Around the world, systemic redirection of resources in the first 12 weeks (March–May 2020) of the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in changes to HPB-related care. A European survey was organized among surgeons, demonstrating a 60% reduction in HPB referrals during the COVID-19 outbreak.6 Moreover, the pandemic caused the discontinuation of multidisciplinary tumour boards, induced substantial delays in preoperative consultations and diagnostics, and caused direct reductions in operating capacity and intensive care unit access.7 Emerging studies estimate a 38% global reduction of oncologic surgeries during the height of the coronavirus crisis, with patients temporarily redirected to nonoperative treatments.7–10

The objective of this study was to determine whether restrictions to surgical care resulted in stage migration among patients with gastrointestinal malignancies undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD). We hypothesized that there were higher rates of nontherapeutic laparotomies with findings of occult metastatic disease or locally advanced primary lesions, owing to diagnostic and therapeutic delays during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a retrospective cohort study of patients with periampullary malignancies who underwent operative exploration with intent to perform a PD at a single high-volume HPB centre in the province of Ontario, Canada. We included patients aged 18 years and older undergoing curative-intent surgery between March 11, 2019, and March 11, 2021. We excluded patients without a diagnosis of malignancy on the final pathology report. We categorized patients according to the date at which the World Health Organization declared the COVID-19 pandemic (March 11, 2020), into a pre-COVID group (PD between March 11, 2019, and March 10, 2020) and post-COVID group (March 11, 2020, to March 11, 2021).

We performed electronic chart review to obtain relevant demographic and clinical information and anonymized included patients via computer-generated randomized numerical digits. All data were de-identified and stored in a secured drive.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was stage migration — defined here as a nontherapeutic laparotomy owing to findings of local unresectability or radiographically occult distant metastatic disease. Secondary outcomes included time from consent to surgery, time from most recent staging computed tomography (CT) scan to surgery, margin status (among patients undergoing complete resection), nodal status among patients undergoing complete resection, 90-day perioperative major morbidity (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III), and 90-day perioperative mortality, rate of vascular resection and rate of multivisceral resection (among patients undergoing complete resection).

Covariates

We selected covariates a priori based on clinical significance. Baseline patient demographics included gender, age at operation, and major comorbidities. We recorded receipt of neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy or both, as well as final pathologic diagnosis.

Data analysis

We described data using medians and interquartile range for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. We performed comparisons between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test or χ2 tests, respectively. Statistical significance was accepted at the 0.05 level, and all tests were 2-sided. We made no adjustments for multiple comparisons. We performed a prespecified sensitivity analysis, restricted to patients with a final pathologic diagnosis of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Unity Health Toronto Research Ethics Board.

Results

We included 180 patients in the study, with 87 patients in the pre-COVID cohort and 93 in the post-COVID cohort. There were no significant differences in age, gender, and baseline characteristics between the 2 groups. The median age of our cohort was 68 years, and 43% of patients were female (Table 1). In the entire cohort, 14% received neo-adjuvant therapy, with 13% of the pre-COVID and 16% of the post-COVID cohort undergoing chemotherapy (p = 0.65). Only 1 patient in the study received neoadjuvant radiotherapy, and they were in the post-COVID cohort. Of patients, 51% had a pathologic diagnosis of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), followed by cholangiocarcinoma and ampullary carcinoma, respectively (Table 2).

There were no significant differences between the 2 groups with respect to rates of unresectable disease, vascular resection, and multivisceral resection (Table 4). There was a trend toward a higher rate of nontherapeutic laparotomy in the post-COVID group than in the pre-COVID group (19.4% v. 11.5%), but this was not statistically significant (p = 0.21). In addition, there was a greater portion of R1 margin positivity, defined as less than 1 mm or at the margin, in the post-COVID group (17.2% v. 5.7%, p = 0.01). The rates of node-negative disease, however, were not statistically different between the 2 years. Postoperative morbidity and mortality, including length of stay and Clavien–Dindo complications grade III to V, were similar between the 2 groups.

Discussion

In this study we examine the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on diagnostic and postoperative outcomes for patients with gastrointestinal malignancies. In our cohort of 180 such patients, there was an association between the pandemic and higher rates of nontherapeutic laparotomy due to radiographically occult distance metastasis, albeit not statistically significant. When analyzing for secondary outcomes, there was a statistically significant increase in time from consent or last staging CT scan to operation during the pandemic. Furthermore, there was a significant increase in rates of R1 margin positivity during the pandemic.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine whether there was an association between disruptions in usual care during the COVID-19 pandemic and postoperative outcomes for patients undergoing curative-intent PDs. A few studies, described below, have assessed the impact of COVID-19 on pancreatic cancer outcomes, but their results have been inconsistent. In our study, we found statistically significant delays from the date of consent for surgery and the date of the last CT scan to the date of operation. Although 1-week delays between time of consent and surgery may not result in a clinical difference, it can be argued that 3-week delays since the last imaging for malignancies as aggressive as those treated with PD can falsely increase the number of those potentially eligible for curative surgical resection and subsequently increase the rates of nontherapeutic laparotomy. These data agree with a study that found trends toward increased time to diagnosis and delays in treatment of patients with pancreatic adenocarcinoma, with a statistically significant increase in those presenting with stage III and stage IV disease.10 In contrast, another retrospective study found no difference in the median time from diagnosis to treatment when comparing prepandemic patients with PDAC to those during the pandemic, and their study concluded that COVID-19 had not significantly prevented patients with pancreatic cancer from seeking care.11 Another retrospective study in Spain found a trend toward longer wait times to diagnosis and treatment, although they were not of statistical significance.12

We found a trend toward increased rates of nontherapeutic laparotomy and a statistically significant increase in R1 margin positivity in the post-COVID cohort, and these findings have been validated by other studies. A single-centre study in the United States looking at rates of meta-static disease in patients with PDAC found a significant decrease in the overall survival during the pandemic at 6.4% compared with 8.1% (p < 0.001).13 In our sensitivity analysis, we found comparable statistical significance when comparing patients with PDAC on their own. This was also seen in a British study that found that patients with PDAC had a reduction in median survival from 7.6 months to 3.3 months in the pandemic cohort.14 Although the intraoperative complexity in our cohort did not differ between the years, the statistically significant increase in the rates of R1 margin positivity supported our hypothesis that patients during the pandemic presented with more extensive disease. A retrospective study in Poland found a trend toward patients presenting with more advanced disease, and more palliative procedures for those with pancreatic cancer, but neither were statistically significant.15 However, a Spanish study compared the survival rates of patients with pancreatic cancer before and during the pandemic and found a significant increase in rates of stage III and IV disease (p = 0.006).12 The same study found that palliative treatment was the most common approach among their cohort of 25 patients. On the other hand, a multi-institutional retrospective study found no difference in the rates of resectable, locally advanced, or metastatic disease between patients with PDAC in the prepandemic and pandemic groups.11

A limited number of studies have directly assessed restrictions on surgical care for patients undergoing PD and whether these had effects on outcomes. Our 2 populations were statistically comparable, with similar distributions of age, gender, and common comorbidities. In addition, the total number of patients who underwent PD in the 2 different years was comparable, allowing for an accurate comparison of primary and secondary outcomes.

Limitations

One limitation of note is that our study did not capture patients who may not have received any surgical consult if they presented to the health care system with metastatic disease and sought palliation. There was also a push toward a neoadjuvant approach for pancreatic cancer in Ontario at the time. At St. Joseph’s Health Centre, the community came together to adopt this approach, but the uptake was variable among our medical oncology partners. This may explain why in this study there was no significant difference in those undergoing neoadjuvant therapy between the pre-COVID and post-COVID cohort despite the push toward neoadjuvant therapy. In addition, we were unable to systematically capture the patients who had been referred for neoadjuvant systemic therapy and had not yet undergone surgery, and therefore these patients were not included in the analysis.

At St. Joseph’s Health Centre, timeliness of access to the operating room for each individual service was assessed by the administration. As a result, oncologic surgeries were prioritized, and resource distribution was adjusted accordingly. We decided to focus on the first year of the pandemic when restrictions were at their height, but expanding the study to the current day may show differing results, and one would expect that the delays would have improved. In addition, the lack of information on long-term oncological outcomes in the context of the pandemic limits the results. We also know that each institution’s approach to managing clinical and surgical resources during this time was unique, and hence the findings may not be generalizable to other locations. Another limitation of this study is its retrospective nature, making it difficult to fully control for confounding variables.

Conclusion

Our study found an association between increased wait times from CT scan and consent to the operation, and increased rates of R1 margin positivity during the first year of the pandemic. This adds to the strong literature that exists on the importance of early diagnosis and surgery to achieve curative intent for these aggressive gastrointestinal malignancies. Timely diagnostic work-up contributes to greater chances for cure and ultimately improves outcomes. It will be worthwhile assessing whether expanding the timeline of the study to the present day would show improved outcomes with less restrictions and delays, or would highlight a surge of more advanced disease as patients who did not seek medical attention or were affected by restrictions begin to present to health care. Larger studies that include more surgical centres across Ontario and Canada are needed to truly delineate the effects of restrictions and assess for the multiple avenues that may have led to poorer outcomes for our patients. As we continue to learn more about the unforeseen effects of this ongoing pandemic, we need to have stronger systems in place to continue health care services for our patients and to optimize their outcomes.
