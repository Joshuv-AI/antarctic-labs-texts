Framework for Machine Evaluation of Reasoning Completeness in Large Language Models for Classification Tasks
Avinash Patil

Abstract
The growing adoption of machine learning (ML) in sensitive domains has heightened the demand for transparent and interpretable artificial intelligence. Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly capable of producing natural language explanations, yet it remains unclear whether these rationales faithfully capture the predictive signals that underlie decisions. This paper introduces RACE — Reasoning Alignment for Completeness of Explanations —a systematic framework to evaluate the alignment between LLM-generated explanations and interpretable feature importance scores derived from a logistic regression baseline. We analyze four widely used text classification datasets— Wiki Ontology , AG News , IMDB , and GoEmotions —and compare LLM rationales against top-ranked supporting and contradicting lexical features. To capture alignment at multiple levels of granularity, RACE implements token-aware, exact string, and edit-distance matching techniques. Empirical results reveal a consistent asymmetry: correct predictions exhibit higher coverage of supporting features, while incorrect predictions are associated with elevated coverage of contradicting features. Edit-distance matching further uncovers paraphrastic overlaps, boosting coverage while preserving this asymmetry. These findings demonstrate that LLM rationales combine both surface-level and flexible evidence reuse, yet can also amplify misleading cues in error cases. RACE provides new insights into the faithfulness of LLM explanations and establishes a quantitative basis for evaluating reasoning completeness in neural language models.



1
Introduction

The increasing adoption of machine learning (ML) in high-stakes domains has driven the demand for transparent and interpretable AI systems. Among recent advances, Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT and DeepSeek have demonstrated impressive capabilities in generating natural language explanations for complex decision-making tasks. In parallel, traditional ML models—such as logistic regression, decision trees, and support vector machines—continue to be widely used due to their computational efficiency, well-understood behavior, and interpretable feature importance measures.

Despite the rise of LLMs in explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), a critical question remains: do the explanations generated by LLMs actually reflect the information that drives their decisions? Understanding this alignment is vital for assessing the trustworthiness of AI systems, particularly in text classification tasks where model transparency is essential for stakeholders and domain experts.

While previous research has explored whether LLMs can approximate the predictions of classical models or produce plausible explanations rated favorably by humans, relatively little work has quantitatively examined the semantic alignment between LLM-generated explanations and statistical measures of feature importance. Most explanation benchmarking efforts emphasize self-consistency or subjective quality, without systematically testing whether LLM rationales mention the same predictive cues that traditional models identify as most influential.

In this paper, we address this gap by proposing a systematic framework to compare LLM-generated explanations against the top-weighted lexical features identified by a logistic regression classifier in a text classification setting. Our approach involves prompting an LLM to predict labels and provide free-text rationales, extracting the most predictive supporting and contradicting features from logistic regression, and computing overlap between rationales and features under multiple matching strategies. To capture both surface overlap and more flexible reformulations, we evaluate alignment using three levels of matching: token-aware, exact string, and fuzzy edit-distance similarity.

Our empirical study spans four benchmark datasets—
Wiki Ontology
,
AG News
,
IMDB
, and
GoEmotions
—covering diverse domains and levels of label granularity. Results reveal a consistent asymmetry: correct predictions exhibit higher coverage of supporting features, while incorrect predictions show elevated coverage of contradicting features. Edit-distance matching further uncovers paraphrastic or near-synonymous overlaps, yielding higher alignment scores while preserving the same asymmetry. These findings suggest that LLM rationales are not merely surface justifications but capture semantically relevant evidence, though they can also amplify misleading cues when errors occur.

The main contributions of this paper are:

•

We introduce a framework for quantifying the alignment between LLM-generated explanations and feature importance weights from a logistic regression classifier.

•

We propose three complementary matching techniques—token-aware, exact, and edit-distance—to assess overlap at multiple levels of textual similarity.

•

We present empirical results on four widely used text classification benchmarks, showing consistent patterns of support/contradiction asymmetry across tasks.

•

We provide actionable insights on the faithfulness and limitations of LLM-generated rationales, highlighting when and why they align (or misalign) with interpretable model features.

2
Related Work

2.1
Faithfulness of LLM Explanations

A growing body of research has examined the faithfulness of natural language explanations generated by Large Language Models (LLMs).
Fayyaz et al. 2024
evaluate LLM rationales through both prompting-based and attribution-based methods, measuring their alignment with human-provided explanations and their robustness under misclassification. Similarly,
Admoni et al. 2025b
study self-consistency in LLM explanations and introduce a benchmark for evaluating whether generated rationales align with model behavior.
Li et al. 2025a
propose
Drift
, a framework to improve rationale faithfulness by mitigating omissions of key evidence, while
Maes et al. 2025
highlight the risks of misleading natural explanations and formalize the notion of “broad truthfulness,” combining factuality and faithfulness.

2.2
Feature Importance and Classical Models

Parallel research has focused on interpretable feature importance in classical models.
Lai et al. 2019
compare built-in and post-hoc importance measures for text classification, showing that feature relevance can vary depending on methodology.
Wang et al. 2020
extend Shapley-based attribution methods with
Shapley Flow
, a graph-based technique to capture dependencies among features. These approaches demonstrate the interpretability advantages of classical models such as logistic regression and motivate their use as a transparent reference point for evaluating LLM explanations.

2.3
Explanation Evaluation and Alignment

Several works explore quantitative methods for assessing explanations.
Hase et al. 2021b
analyze the pitfalls of perturbation-based importance methods, particularly out-of-distribution artifacts introduced by feature removal.
Fang et al. 2024
investigate faithfulness in LLM reasoning for abstractive summarization, finding that explanation quality varies with task complexity. More recently,
Li et al. 2025b
propose using LLMs themselves as attribution regularizers for simpler models, highlighting the potential for cross-model alignment as a strategy for improving generalization.

2.4
Positioning of This Work

Our study complements these efforts by introducing
RACE
(
Reasoning Alignment for Completeness of Explanations
), a framework that evaluates whether LLM rationales capture the most predictive lexical features identified by a logistic regression classifier. Unlike prior work that measures human alignment
(
Fayyaz et al. 2024
)
, self-consistency
(
Admoni et al. 2025b
)
, or attribution robustness
(
Hase et al. 2021b
)
, RACE quantifies alignment directly against a transparent, interpretable baseline. Furthermore, while existing methods typically assess faithfulness at a coarse level, we analyze both
supporting
and
contradicting
features and employ multi-level matching (token-aware, exact, and edit-distance) to capture both surface overlap and flexible paraphrastic alignment. This positions RACE as a complementary tool for studying the completeness and faithfulness of LLM-generated explanations in text classification tasks.

3
Methodology

This section introduces the framework for quantifying overlap between LLM-generated rationales and feature-importance rankings from a transparent reference model. We formalize the task, outline the system architecture, describe matching strategies, and summarize implementation and reproducibility details.

3.1
Problem Formulation

Let the dataset be
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
x_{i}
is a text instance and
y_{i}
its ground-truth label. For each
x_{i}
, an LLM produces a predicted label
\hat{y}_{i}
and a rationale
\texttt{llm\_response}_{i}
.
A logistic regression model
M_{\text{LR}}
, trained on the same dataset with TF–IDF features, serves as a transparent reference. For each instance, we extract the top-
k
features most influential to the predicted class:

\texttt{top\_features}_{i}=\{(f_{i1},w_{i1}),\dots,(f_{ik},w_{ik})\},

where
w_{ij}
is the feature weight. Supporting and contradicting subsets are defined as:

\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{f_{ij}:w_{ij}>0\},\quad\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{f_{ij}:w_{ij}<0\}.

3.2
Feature–Rationale Matching

We determine whether a feature
f
appears in a rationale
\texttt{llm\_response}_{i}
using a matching function
m_{\tau}
parameterized by matcher type
\tau\in\{\text{token},\text{exact},\text{edit}\}
:

m_{\tau}(f,\texttt{llm\_response}_{i})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }f\text{ matches any span under criterion }\tau,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The matcher types are:

•

Token-aware:
Lemma-level match after normalization (lowercasing, punctuation removal, lemmatization).

•

Exact:
Strict string equality.

•

Edit-distance:
Fuzzy alignment allowing small character-level deviations (
\text{ED}(f,s)\leq\delta
).

3.3
Coverage Computation

Given subset
\mathcal{T}\in\{\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{C}_{i}\}
and matcher
\tau
, per-instance coverage is

\text{cov}_{\tau}(i,\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{T}}m_{\tau}(f,\texttt{llm\_response}_{i}).

We report

\text{support\_cov}_{i,\tau}=\text{cov}_{\tau}(i,\mathcal{S}_{i}),\quad\text{contradict\_cov}_{i,\tau}=\text{cov}_{\tau}(i,\mathcal{C}_{i}),

along with raw counts and normalized proportions:

\text{raw\_count}_{i,\tau}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{S}_{i}\cup\mathcal{C}_{i}}m_{\tau}(f,\texttt{llm\_response}_{i}),\quad\text{proportion}_{i,\tau}=\tfrac{\text{raw\_count}_{i,\tau}}{k}.

Aggregated coverage is analyzed across all instances and partitioned by correctness (
\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}
vs.
\hat{y}_{i}\neq y_{i}
) to test whether correct rationales emphasize supporting evidence and incorrect ones reference contradicting cues.

3.4
System Architecture

The pipeline proceeds through:

1.

Data Extraction:
Collect
llm_response
and
top_features
.

2.

Normalization:
Tokenize and clean text.

3.

Matching:
Apply token-aware, exact, and edit-distance matching.

4.

Metric Computation:
Compute coverage and overlap statistics.

5.

Aggregation:
Summarize results across instances and correctness partitions.

3.5
Novel Contributions

Our framework quantifies per-instance alignment between LLM rationales and salient lexical evidence from a traditional model. The inclusion of edit-distance matching reveals semantic correspondence beyond surface-form reuse, uncovering cases where rationales paraphrase predictive features rather than replicating them verbatim.

3.6
Implementation and Reproducibility

Tokenization and lemmatization use standard NLP libraries (NLTK, spaCy). LR models are trained with scikit-learn and TF–IDF (1–2 grams, stopword removal). Thresholds for edit distance are empirically tuned. All parameters, dataset splits, and preprocessing details are documented for reproducibility.

Experiments

4.1
Datasets

We evaluate across four benchmarks:

15-class entity descriptions (e.g.,
Album
,
Company
).

•

AG News:
4-class news categorization (

,

Sports

World

•

IMDB:
Binary sentiment classification (
Positive
,
Negative
).

•

GoEmotions:
27 fine-grained emotion labels plus
Neutral

4.2
LLM Predictions and Rationales

For each dataset, the LLM (DeepSeek-R1) is prompted to output both a label prediction and a textual rationale. Prediction accuracy is computed against ground truth, and rationales are compared with LR-derived features using the matching functions defined in Section
3
.

4.3
Reference Feature Model

The logistic regression classifier with TF–IDF vectorization (1–2 grams) yields per-class feature weights. For each instance, we extract top-
k=5
features and separate them into supporting (
w_{ij}>0
) and contradicting (
w_{ij}<0
) subsets. The model is fixed and reused across evaluations to ensure consistent feature references.

4.4
Coverage Metrics and Analysis

Coverage metrics follow the definitions in Section
3
, quantifying textual overlap under three matchers (token, exact, edit). Dataset-level averages and correctness-conditioned comparisons evaluate how faithfully LLM rationales reflect predictive evidence. Visualizations—including confusion matrices, scatter plots of support vs. contradiction coverage, and summary tables—illustrate the relationship between rationale content and model correctness.

6
Discussion

Our findings highlight both the strengths and limitations of LLM-generated rationales when compared against feature-based explanations from a traditional classifier. Several themes emerge.

6.1
Surface lexical overlap vs. flexible alignment

Across datasets, exact and token-aware matching show that LLM rationales frequently reuse the same lexical items that logistic regression identifies as predictive. This surface-level alignment suggests that LLMs rely heavily on salient keywords to justify their predictions. However, edit-distance matching reveals that many rationales also incorporate close variants or paraphrases of top features, substantially boosting measured coverage. This indicates that LLM reasoning is not restricted to verbatim feature reuse but instead reflects a mix of direct lexical alignment and flexible reformulation. Importantly, the support/contradiction asymmetry persists under all three matching schemes, underscoring that the alignment signal is robust rather than an artifact of matching criteria.

6.2
Task sensitivity of rationale alignment

The strength of rationale–feature alignment varies markedly across tasks. Topical classification tasks (
Wiki Ontology
,
AG News
) exhibit the clearest separation between matches and mismatches, reflecting the high lexical grounding of category distinctions. In contrast, sentiment analysis (
IMDB
) and fine-grained emotion recognition (
GoEmotions
) show weaker alignment. For
IMDB
, this may reflect that sentiment expressions are distributed across many words and constructions, diluting the role of any single feature. For
GoEmotions
, the challenge lies in mapping subtle affective cues to discrete emotion categories, a task where both logistic regression and the LLM face substantial ambiguity. These differences highlight that alignment-based evaluation is task-dependent: rationales may appear less faithful in domains where ground-truth features are diffuse or semantically overlapping.

6.3
Implications for faithfulness evaluation

Our results suggest that evaluating LLM explanations against traditional models provides a useful but partial perspective. Logistic regression features serve as an interpretable reference point, but they capture only a subset of the linguistic and semantic cues that LLMs may exploit. The fact that edit-distance matching consistently uncovers additional overlaps suggests that rationales encode semantically related evidence beyond surface features. At the same time, the elevated contradiction coverage in errors warns that rationales can amplify misleading cues, raising questions about whether explanations are genuinely faithful or merely post-hoc justifications. Future work should therefore triangulate faithfulness evaluation across multiple baselines (e.g., attention distributions, feature attribution methods, or human-annotated rationales).

6.4
Limitations and future directions

While our methodology captures systematic patterns of overlap, it has limitations. Logistic regression provides a transparent baseline but does not capture higher-order compositional cues. Edit-distance matching improves flexibility but cannot fully capture semantic equivalence (e.g., antonyms or idioms). Furthermore, fixing
k=5
features per instance imposes a strong bottleneck that may miss diffuse or distributed signals. Future work could extend the framework to richer models of feature importance, incorporate contextual embeddings for semantic alignment, and explore interactive human evaluations of rationale completeness.

Overall, the study demonstrates that LLM rationales show measurable alignment with traditional predictive features, especially under flexible matching. However, the persistence of contradiction signals in errors and the variability across tasks caution against overinterpreting lexical alignment as evidence of faithful reasoning.

7
Conclusions

This study evaluated the completeness of LLM-generated rationales by comparing them against the most predictive lexical features of a logistic regression classifier across four benchmark datasets. Our analysis yielded three key findings. First, rationales systematically differentiate between correct and incorrect predictions: correct cases emphasize supporting features, whereas errors are associated with elevated coverage of contradicting features. Second, the strength of this asymmetry varies by task—being strongest in topical or domain classification, moderate in sentiment analysis, and weakest in fine-grained emotion recognition. Third, while exact and token-aware matching reveal substantial lexical overlap, edit-distance alignment consistently increases coverage, showing that rationales frequently incorporate close variants and paraphrases of classifier features.

These results underscore that LLM rationales capture both direct lexical evidence and more flexible reformulations of predictive features, but they also risk amplifying misleading cues when predictions are incorrect. Taken together, the findings highlight that alignment-based evaluation provides a robust diagnostic of explanation quality while revealing domain-specific limitations. Future research should extend this framework to richer semantic alignment methods and alternative reference models, with the goal of developing more comprehensive measures of explanation faithfulness.
