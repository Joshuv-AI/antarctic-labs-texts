Tracing the global spread of maize (Zea mays L.) through plastome analysis of landraces
Authors: Jin Seong Park, Seongmin Hong, Jiyun Go, Myeong-Geon Seok, Hobin Lee, Gibum Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaize (Zea mays L.) is the most recently domesticated and dispersed worldwide among major cereals. Chloroplast genomes (plastome), with their highly conserved tetrad structure, hold a significant value in phylogenetic research due to their typical maternal inheritance. In this study, a total of 286 maize plastomes, 262 newly assembled and 24 publicly available, were investigated which included worldwide landraces, nested association mapping founders, and teosinte accessions. The maize plastomes were assembled with the filtered reads of the whole genome sequences from our re-seq data and public database by mapping to the reference plastome of B73. The maize plastomes were put into the phylogenetic analyses, with Z. perennis accession as the outgroup. The maize landraces were divided into four clades, showing a closer relationship with the Z. mays ssp. parviglumis plastome than with mexicana, indicating that parviglumis was the direct donor of the plastome to maize. Landrace diversities in Europe were quite similar to those in China and the Korean Peninsula, suggesting that the dispersal of maize following European contact with America was both rapid and accompanied by the translocation of a broad spectrum of maize germplasm. The introduced landraces have less diversity than the old landraces found in South America. This study provides information for understanding the genetic diversity of maize landraces and the process of maize dispersal in the world for exploiting the maize germplasm in breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07829-4.Keywords: Corn, Chloroplast genome, CpGenome, Phylogenetic analysis, Teosinte

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) is one of the most important staple crops globally. It was domesticated from two teosinte species in southern Mexico about 9,000 years ago [1]. Genomic evidence shows gene flow between maize and its wild relatives Z. mays ssp. mexicana and ssp. parviglumis, suggesting adaptation to new environments during dispersal [2]. Population genomic studies have tracked how maize moved southward into South America, where distinct landraces emerged, and northward into the southwestern U.S [3]. Maize was rapidly embraced in various regions around the world after European contact with America [4]. It was spread worldwide due to its adaptability to different climates and its high yield compared to traditional cereals [5–7]. Examples of environmental adaptation in maize include adaptive changes in flowering time in response to temperature and photoperiod, as well as physiological adaptations to high-altitude environments [8, 9]. Maize was dispersed worldwide in a relative short time, couple hundred years, from Europe to China [10] through the Silk Road and from China to Korea in early 15th century [11, 12].

Landraces are traditional varieties of crops that have adapted to local environmental conditions over time. It often preserves genetic diversity and useful alleles for breeding such as disease and pest resistance and resilience to climate change [8, 9]. In the Americas, they exhibit clinal variation associated with altitude and latitude (e.g., flowering-time genes), whereas introduced landraces in Asia and Europe show adaptations to temperate environments [9, 13]. Maize landraces can be divided into two groups: original and introduced [14]. The original landraces - direct descendants of domesticated Z. mays ssp. mays from its wild ancestor- are found in South and North America. In contrast, introduced landraces are those found outside the Americas, such as in Europe, Africa, and Asia, where maize was introduced following its spread from the New World [4]. Both of those landraces, having various genetic diversities, are valuable resources for maize breeding. Landraces also provide important evidence for the dispersal of agricultural species into new environments. Many landraces have been lost in various regions because maize was one of the earliest crops to undergo commercial breeding and widespread adoption of commercial cultivars, especially before the importance of landraces was recognized [15, 16].

Understanding the phylogeographic history of crops can help breeders develop climate-resilient and high-yielding varieties. Conservation of crop diversity relies on tracing the genetic origins of landraces to protect their unique traits. Genomic research informs strategies for maintaining biodiversity in the face of climate change and modern monoculture farming [17–19]. Chloroplast possess an independent genome separated from the nucleus. Typically, chloroplast genomes are a single circular DNA molecule ranging in length from 120 to 160 kb [20]. Structurally, chloroplast DNA is divided into four regions: the large single-copy (LSC) region, the small single-copy (SSC) region, and two complementary inverted repeat (IR) regions [21]. This genome has been widely utilized in eukaryotic species identification and hybridization studies.

Plastome are particularly useful for studying plant lineage and dispersal due to several key characteristics. After the complete plastomes of maize and rice were first assembled, plastomes have been used to resolve phylogenetic relationships in many plant species [21–24]. In most angiosperms, including maize, plastid DNA is typically maternally inherited [25]. It does not undergo recombination like nuclear DNA, preserving lineage-specific mutations [26]. As a result, it provides a clear and stable record of plant ancestry, making it ideal for tracking lineage relationships and migration patterns. The plastome is highly conserved in structure, with relatively few rearrangements across plant species [27]. Its mutation rate is slower and more stable than that of the nuclear genome, allowing for the resolution of evolutionary and phylogeographic relationships with minimal genetic noise.

In cross-pollinating maize, plastome analysis is particularly useful for distinguishing maternal lineages in hybridization events [28]. This approach helps trace the spread of domesticated maize, its interbreeding with wild relatives, and its adaptation to diverse environments across different regions. Plastome analysis has been instrumental in tracing maize’s migration from Mesoamerica to South America, and later to North America and beyond, highlighting the roles of environmental adaptation and human-mediated selection [29]. Moreover, phylogenetic analyses based on plastome sequences have significantly contributed to understanding the genetic relationships among landraces and cultivars, complementing other genetic studies [30].

While previous studies investigated plastome diversity in American and European maize, little is known about Asian landraces, particularly those in the Korean Peninsula, which represents a terminal point of maize’s overland dispersal [3, 4, 29]. This study addresses this gap by further investigating the dispersal history of worldwide maize through plastome analyses of landraces and NCBI-accessed sequences. Identifying genetic patterns that reveal maize’s spread routes, adaptation signatures, and relationships between ancient and modern maize would enhance our understanding of maize genetics and breeding.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and whole genome sequencing data

We collected fresh leaves from 25 Z. mays ssp. mays inbred lines, including 21 Korean, 2 Nepali, 1 Bolivian, and 1 Bulgarian landrace, for DNA extraction (Supplementary Table S1). DNA was extracted from mature leaf tissue using the CTAB method [31]. An Illumina paired-end genomic library with insert size of 151 bp was constructed as following manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced using the ILLUMINA NOVAseq6000.

Collecting sequence data for worldwide maize landraces

To obtain whole-genome resequencing data for maize landraces from diverse global regions, including both the geographic locations where the accessions were originally collected and the regions of subsequent dispersal, we downloaded SRA files from six different BioProjects listed in Supplementary Table S2 [1, 3, 32–36]. The downloaded SRA files were converted into Illumina paired-end fastq files using the fasterq-dump of the SRA Tool kit [37]. Adaptor sequences in the raw reads were identified and removed using FastQC [38].

Plastome sequence assembly

A total of 286 maize plastomes were investigated, consisting of 262 newly assembled plastomes (from 25 re-seq in this study and 237 public re-seq data) and 24 complete plastome sequences including B73 obtained from NCBI (Supplementary Table S1) [28, 39]. A total of 262 raw read datasets (Supplementary Table S3) were trimmed by Trimmomatic v0.36 [40]. Cleaned fastq files were mapped to published plastome sequences, including B73 (NCBI accession: KF241981.1), using BWA [41] with default parameters. Unmapped or non-primarily alignments were filtered out using SAMtools [42]. Duplicated reads were removed with the MarkDuplicates function of Picard [43]. Paired-end plastome reads for each sample were extracted using the SamToFastq function in Picard. Chloroplast genome assembly was performed with GetOrganelle v.1.7.4.1, using a series of k-mer sizes (-k 21, 29, 39, 45, 59, 65, 79, 85, 99, 105, 119) and multiple assembly rounds (-R 20) to obtain optimal circular plastome sequences [44] (Supplementary Table S4). Plastome gene annotation was performed using PGA [45], with the B73 plastome serving as a reference [39]. A circular gene map of plastome was visualized using OrganellarGenomeDRAW v1.3 [46].

Variant calling for plastome

Plastome reads were mapped to the B73 reference plastome using BWA. Sample names were assigned to each mapping results using the AddOrReplaceReadGroups function in GATK v4.1.8.1 [47]. Variants were called for each sample using HaplotypeCaller and combined into a single GenomicDB file using GenomicsDBImport. Joint genotyping was performed with SelectVariants, and variants were filtered using VariantFiltration with the following parameters: QUAL < 30, MQ < 40.0, QD < 2.0, FS >60.0, MQRankSum < −12.5. Bcftools [48] was used to extract biallelic variants that passed these filters.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

All 156 non-redundant plastome sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.511 with the FFT-NS-2 algorithm [49]. The MAFFT-aligned sequences were used to calculate pairwise identity, including gaps, and a heatmap was generated based on the results. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using IQ-TREE 2 [50] with default parameter, with Z. perennis designated as the outgroup and bootstrap support values were obtained based on 1,000 replicates. The structural variations identified from the all assembled plastome sequences in this study were visualized by Blast Ring Image Generator [51].

Population genomic analysis of plastomes

The variant calling VCF file was imputed using the LD KNNi algorithm implemented in TASSEL5 [52]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was also conducted using TASSEL5. Nucleotide diversity (π) and population differentiation (Fst) were calculated with VCFtools [53]. Nucleotide diversity within and between clades was estimated using sliding window analysis (5 kb window length, 1 kb step size). The imputed VCF file was used to evaluate regional variation in nucleotide diversity across the plastome sequences.

Detection of plastome variants in the nuclear genome

Nuclear genomic sequences homologous to 101 bp plastome segments having each variant in the center were identified using MEGABLAST [54] against the 26 nested association mapping (NAM) nuclear genomes (parameters: -evalue 0.01, -dust no, -penalty − 2, -word_size 9). To account for the 14 bp insertions/deletions (InDels) observed among the plastome variants, only alignments longer than 87 bp were retained from the MEGABLAST results. These sequences were further filtered against the B73 plastome using blastn (parameters: -task blastn-short, -max_target_seqs 1, -max_hsps 1) to remove residual plastid-origin matches. Nuclear genomic positions and mapped plastome variants were visualized using the R package ggplot2 for graphical rendering, and FNN for local density-based scaling of variant blocks [55, 56].

Results

The maize plastomes were grouped into four distinct clades

Landrace samples were collected from diverse geographic regions, encompassing South and North America, Europe, Africa, and Asia (Fig. 1A). In total, 262 complete plastome were newly assembled in this study. The representative plastome of Jeongseon Chal-2, a Korean landrace, was 140,458 bp in length and exhibited the typical quadripartite structure consisting of SSC, LSC, and two IR regions (Supplementary Fig. S1). The overall GC content of the Jeongseon Chal-2 plastome was 38.4%, slightly higher than that of B73 reference plastome (37.8%; Supplementary Table S4). Gene annotation revealed that the Jeongseon Chal-2 plastome harbors an identical gene complement to that of B73, comprising a total of 130 genes (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Based on whole plastome sequence analysis, the samples were largely grouped into four distinct clades (Fig. 1B). Z. may ssp. mexicana accessions did not cluster within any of these clades but were positioned on external branches, indicating substantial divergence in their plastome sequences (Supplementary Fig. S2, S3). In contrast, Z. mays ssp. parviglumis accessions were included within clades 1 and 2, but absent from clades 3 and 4. Notably, clade 1 was exclusively composed of American accessions. Furthermore, increasing distance from parviglumis (clades 1, 2, and 4) was associated with a decline in the proportion of American accessions, from 53% in clade 2 (41 of 77) to 44% in clade 4 (79 of 176).

Plastomes of the NAM founders were also assembled, as these lines were specifically selected for their broad nuclear genetic diversity and have been widely utilized in genetic and genomic studies [57]. While the plastomes exhibited overall genetic variation, the NAM founders displayed relatively limited plastome diversity. All NAM lines were confined to clades 2 and 4, indicating a narrow range of plastome genetic diversity. Notably, seven NAM founders (CML247, B97, CML333, M162W, Mo18W, Ms71, NC358, and OH7B) shared an identical plastome sequence.

Particularly, the Korean landraces exhibited substantial plastome diversity despite originating from a relatively small geographic region, consistent with previous observation [12].

Genetic diversities among clades

Among the 156 non-redundant plastomes analyzed, 149 accessions can be genotyped with their raw sequencing reads obtained from the SRA database. Genetic relationships among these 149 accessions were assessed based on genotypes at polymorphic sites, comprising 145 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and 61 InDels. PCA plot where PC1 and PC2 explained 48.3% and 15.6% of the variance, respectively, revealed relatively clear separation of the four clades. Accessions belonging to clades 1, 2, and 3 were primarily located on the left side of the PCA, while those in clade 4 were predominantly positioned on the right (Fig. 2A). Among them, most accessions of clade 4 clustered together, although some South American accessions were dispersed. Notably, the Korean landraces in clade 4 appeared to form an independent subgroup, in contrast to Chinese accessions that were tightly clustered. Analysis of within-clade genetic diversity showed that clade 4 harbored the highest diversity, whereas clade 2 exhibited the lowest (Fig. 2B). After accounting for the number of accessions per clade, clade 1 also displayed considerable diversity, while accessions in clade 2 were highly homogeneous (Fig. 2).

Highly polymorphic regions were variable depending on between and within clades

To investigate whether patterns of sequence variation across the plastome were associated with clade differentiation, nucleotide diversity (π) was calculated within each clade. Analysis of nucleotide variation across all 156 non-redundant plastome sequences revealed a relatively low maximum diversity (π = 0.0007), localized within the LSC region encompassing rpl22, rps19, trnH-GUG, and rpl2 (Fig. 3A). To assess differentiation among clades, pairwise nucleotide divergence was quantified using Fst values (Fig. 3B). The greatest population differentiation was observed between clades 1 and 4, with a maximum Fst of 0.5877 at the IRb-SSC junction. Comparatively, the maximum Fst values between clades 1 and 2, and clades 2 and 4, were 0.3429 and 0.4358, respectively. The former was located in the LSC region, while the latter was found within the rpoC2 gene, also in the LSC region.

Analysis of nucleotide diversity within each clade revealed a consistent pattern, with the highest peaks of variation occurring in the LSC region across all clades. Notably, a distinct peak at the LSC–IR boundary was observed only in the comparison across all clades and specifically within clade 4. In the SSC region, clades 1, 2, and 4 exhibited relatively low levels of nucleotide diversity individually; however, elevated diversity was detected when all clades were analyzed collectively (Fig. 3A). The IR region was consistently the most conserved across all clades. Furthermore, the 0–40 kb region of the LSC displayed stability within clades. In contrast to within-clade patterns, between-clade comparisons revealed distinct variation profiles. Notably, substantial sequence differentiation was observed within the typically conserved initial segment of the LSC region. Consistent with within-clade results (Fig. 3A), the IR region remained stable across all between-clade comparisons. The comparison between clades 1 and 2 revealed high sequence similarity within the 50–90 kb region of the plastome, a pattern not observed in comparisons between clades 1 and 4 or clades 2 and 4.

Clade specific plastome genotypes and its partial conservation in the nuclear genome

A total of 79 loci with a minimum allele frequency greater than 5% were identified across the analyzed plastomes (Fig. 4A). Accessions in clades 1 and 2 exhibited genotypic profiles more similar to each other and distinct from those in clade 4. Notably, clade 2 displayed minimal within-clade genotypic variation (Figs. 2B and 3A). Overall, clade 4 accessions exhibited genotypes largely consistent with those of B73, whereas accessions in clades 1 and 2 predominantly carried alternative alleles. Within clade 4, a subset of accessions—including Coroico and InJe Chal-2—displayed distinct genotypic patterns compared to the rest of the clade and clustered together in the phylogenetic tree (Figs. 1B and 4A). All accessions in clade 3 shared identical genotypes across the 79 loci, matching to the B73 genotype at all positions except for 13 sites (Fig. 4A).

To assess whether these polymorphic sequences were plastome-specific, 101 bp segments containing the polymorphic sites from the B73 plastome were aligned against the nuclear genomes of 26 NAM founder lines using BLAST. This analysis revealed the presence of tens to hundreds of homologous sequences for each polymorphic plastome locus (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. S4). Across all polymorphic positions, the number of homologous nuclear sequences per locus ranged from 67 to 518. Chromosome 1 contained the highest number of plastome-homologous sequences across the NAM panel, with a total of 3,521 hits. This chromosome also harbored the largest number of sequences that were either identical to or divergent from the B73 plastome. Among the NAM founders, Oh43 exhibited the greatest number of homologous sequences (1,640), including 1,376 identical and 264 alternative variants relative to the B73 plastome.

Discussion

We assembled a total of 262 complete plastomes using both our own sequencing data and publicly available whole genome resequencing datasets (Supplementary Table S1). By adding 24 plastome sequences deposited in NCBI database, we could analyze a large and globally representative set of maize landrace plastomes. The occurrence of multiple lines sharing an identical plastome became more prominent, with as many as 52 accessions carrying the same plastome. This may reflect a specific maternal donor profoundly selected during domestication and subsequent adaptation. Phylogenetic analysis conducted with 156 non-redundant plastomes revealed that maize plastomes divided into 4 clades. The phylogenetic tree showed possible dispersal patterns of Z. mays ssp. mays from its origin to worldwide (Fig. 1B). After the divergence of mexicana and parviglumis, parviglumis appears to have been the predominant cytoplasmic donor in the development of modern maize [58]. This pattern is consistent with previous findings identifying parviglumis as the primary progenitor of domesticated maize [1]. Following landraces which can be classified as modern maize seem to have spread to Central and South America [4]. In this process, multiple parviglumis plastomes appears to have been involved as maternal donors (Figs. 1 and 2).

Accessions in three of the identified clades were found among introduced landraces from Europe, China, and Korea. Clade 1 was predominantly detected in old landraces from the western regions of Central and South America, suggesting it was not introduced to the New World. Clade 4, the largest group, included accessions collected across a broad latitudinal range—from Canada to South Africa—indicating it may be day-neutral and adaptable to diverse climates. These patterns are consistent with the rapid dispersal of maize from America to Europe and subsequently to Asia [4]. Notably, all three clades were observed among the 21 Korean maize landraces included in this study, despite the relatively small sample size. As the Korean Peninsula represents the terminus of overland maize dispersal, the presence of diverse clades suggests that multiple accessions spread together. In addition, the Korean landraces in clade 4 formed a distinct subgroup separated from other American, European, and Asian accessions (Fig. 2A). This suggests that the Korean landraces may have maintained their genetic diversity as landraces since the initial introduction of maize, although their spread likely occurred through China [14]. While there is no direct evidence that plastome variation determines the traits associated with each clade, these observations offer supporting insight into the global dispersal history of maize.

Another line of evidence for plastome diversity within maize is provided by the B37-N, -C, -S, and -T lines, which are known to possess substituted cytoplasms and were distributed across three distinct clades (Fig. 1B). Previous analyses have demonstrated that B37-N and B73 share an identical cytotype, whereas B37-C, -S, and -T exhibit distinct plastome sequences [39]. Furthermore, B37-C and B37-S have been identified as harboring more ancient cytoplasmic lineages compared to B37-T, as previously reported [39].

We also assembled plastome sequences for 25 NAM founders with complete circular sequence level [34]. The NAM founders correspond large genetic diversity and are important breeding materials for current maize breeding industry and research [57]. NAM founders tend to cluster within the same clades, and in some cases, even share redundant plastomes (Fig. 1B).

Sequence differences were observed between the two IR regions within a single individual. The overall low nucleotide diversity across the 156 plastome sequences supports the high conservation of the maize plastome (Fig. 3A). Notably, within-clade nucleotide diversity consistently peaked in the LSC region, while the IR region remained highly conserved across and within clades, reflects the structural and functional constraints of the plastome [59]. Interestingly, high diversity in the SSC region was only apparent when comparing across clades, but not within, suggesting that the SSC may play a role in inter-accession plastome divergence, despite remaining stable within accessions [60]. Moreover, the specific pattern of similarity between Clades 1 and 2 in the 50–90 kb region of the plastome (which is absent in comparisons involving Clade 4) may point to a shared maternal ancestry between Clades 1 and 2 and divergence events associated with modern maize development in Clade 4 (Fig. 3A). Collectively, these observations support the hypothesis that plastome differentiation during domestication occurred in a gradual and structured manner, with certain regions of the plastome potentially being more sensitive to lineage-specific selection pressures [61, 62].

While clade-specific variation was clearly observed within the plastome, these variants were not confined to specific clades in the nuclear genome (Fig. 4B). Instead, homologous nuclear regions were frequently found at consistent chromosomal locations across multiple genomes, suggesting long-standing nuclear-plastid DNA integration events [63, 64]. This extensive presence of plastome homologous nuclear regions highlights a challenge in designing plastome-specific markers for PCR applications.

This study provides maize plastome sequences of diverse worldwide accessions and phylogenetic relations, revealing four major plastome types in four clades. Our findings offer new insights into the dispersal history of maize and highlight the potential of these landraces as valuable genetic resources for maize research and breeding.
