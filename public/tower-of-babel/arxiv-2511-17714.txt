Learning the Value of Value Learning
Alex John London & Aydin Mohseni

Abstract
. Standard decision frameworks address uncertainty about facts but assumes fixed values. We extend the Jeffrey–Bolker framework to model refinements in values and prove a value-of-information theorem for axiological refinement. In multi-agent settings, we establish that mutual refinement will characteristically transform zero-sum games into positive-sum interactions and yields Pareto-improving Nash bargains. These results show that a framework of rational choice can be extended to model value refinement and its associated benefits. By unifying epistemic and axiological refinement under a single formalism, we broaden the conceptual foundations of rational choice and illuminate the normative status of ethical deliberation.



1.
Introduction

Agents in the real world, whether human or artificial intelligence, make decisions in the face of uncertainty. One component of such decisions is
epistemic
, involving knowledge of, or beliefs about, states of the world. Another component is
axiological
, involving judgments about the value or worth of various states of affairs and the actions that can produce them. Classic value of information theorems demonstrate the importance of epistemic refinement understood as gathering information in order to change the agent’s beliefs about states of the world. In particular, under certain conditions,
1

1

(

;

.
rational agents should prefer to acquire information about external conditions. In contrast, there is little discussion of the value of axiological refinement, understood as the process of gaining greater clarity about the nature and scope of the values that determine the relative value or worth of different states of affairs. In this paper we demonstrate the importance of axiological refinement—that pausing to refine one’s values, under quite general conditions, has strictly positive expected utility.

To demonstrate that axiological refinement cannot be collapsed into purely epistemic refinement, we provide results that go beyond individual decision-making contexts and show that there is strategic, not merely epistemic, value from consulting one’s values. In particular, we demonstrate that in two-player zero-sum games, value refinement transforms the interactions into ones that are positive-sum with moral certainty. Similarly, in Nash bargaining scenarios, value refinement tends to result in Pareto improvements for all involved parties. In these contexts, realistic agents who refine their preferences systematically outperform naïve expected utility maximizers, both individually and collectively.

Our results are important on several levels. First, the most compelling arguments for the normative recommendation to maximize expected utility rest on the presumption that the agent in question has a stable set of values that can be represented by a unique (up to positive affine transformation) utility function. Yet actual agents–humans, machines, and institutions–typically fall short of idealized expected utility maximization. Agents’ preferences or choice behavior may be intransitive, incomplete, context dependent in ways that violate independence axioms, or otherwise inconsistent. Further, agents frequently face uncertainty not just about external states of the world, but also about their own preferences and values.

This latter challenge raises a significant practical and normative question: when agents should treat their current utility function as authoritative—take direct action to maximize expected utility in light of those utilities—and when they should instead pause to reflect—to refine, revise, or better represent their values.

Second, although there is an intuitive sense in which ethical reflection is relevant to decision and game theory, this connection is often implicit. Our results make this connection explicit in a way that can be represented within the mathematical formalism of decision theory. This reveals an expanded scope for decision theory and also opens the door for more rigorous exploration of the interaction between epistemic and axiological uncertainty.

2.
Background

Good
(
Good 1967
)
demonstrated that free information cannot decrease expected utility, establishing that rational agents should prefer more information when deciding under uncertainty. Skyrms
(
Skyrms 1990
)
expanded this framework, proving the expected utility of acting with additional information equals or exceeds that without it. Further work by Godfrey-Smith
(
Godfrey-Smith 1991
)
, Fallis
(
Fallis 2007
)
, and Horowitz
(
Horowitz 2014
)
clarified boundary conditions and extended these results across decision contexts. The core insight remains: expected utility maximizers benefit from reducing uncertainty about world states before selecting actions.

In contrast to the extensive scholarship on epistemic uncertainty, axiological uncertainty—uncertainty about the nature, content, or scope of one’s own values—remains underexplored, despite its prevalence in real-world decision-making for both humans and artificial agents. The existence of value uncertainty has been recognized by a few authors. For example,
Dewey and Tufts 1936
distinguish cases of weakness of will, where agents struggle to act in conformity with their judgments of right and wrong from cases where the agent is uncertain about how to reconcile their values in order to arrive at a determination about right and wrong. In this second case, they argue that what is required is not strength of will, but reflection and clarification of the values in question. On their view, such reflection is a core component of theorizing in ethics. But their work does not provide a formal model of what such reflection should look like or how agents should approach decisions in the face of such axiological uncertainty.

Levi
(
Levi 1990
)
provides a detailed, formal model of how rational agents should approach decisions when they remain uncertain about how to reconcile all of their values. He argues that this situation can arise from two sources. The first is pragmatic—an agent may be able to reconcile their various values in principle but not know how to do so within the time required for a decision. The second is the possibility of deep incommensurability. In this case, an agent may not know how to reconcile their various values because there is no unique tradeoff function that reconciles the distinct claims or reasons for action associated with each of the values in question. To this we add a third, normative reason. Some theories in normative ethics hold that it might be possible to reconcile or trade-off particular values but that doing so is morally wrong. For example, Kantian moral theories hold that “things” can be assigned a price and so can be given a value in terms of an equivalent amount of some other thing but that rational agents have dignity and that it is a fundamental moral wrong to treat a rational agent as having a price
(
Bjorndahl et al. 2017
)
.

In response to such situations, Levi argues that an agent should first step back, identify the values that are directly relevant to the choice, and then entertain all possible convex combinations of those values. Acts that are on or above the surface created by these convex combinations are admissible for choice, whereas those that fall below this surface are inadmissible. If the resulting set of admissible options contains more than one act, then second-order values can be applied to that set. If there is no conflict among the agent’s values at this second level, then they will induce an ordering of the admissible options. If there is conflict among these values as well, then the agent can iteratively apply the procedure just described. Levi refers to this ordered system of values as a value structure.

A value structure is, in a sense, a weaker notion than a utility function in that an agent who acts on the basis of a utility function can be represented as employing a value structure of a particular form—one where the value functions can be commensurated, for example by the assignment of weights to each value function, and so represented by a single all-told utility function that aggregates all values into univocal evaluations of any given state or action. However, when an agent chooses from a value structure in accordance with Levi’s principles there will be cases in which those decisions cannot be represented as choosing in accordance with any utility function.

Our results differ from the approach taken by Levi. In response to uncertainty about a set of values, Levi understands inquiry into one’s values as a process of identifying their relative weights. Until precise weights can be specified, agents entertain all convex weightings of those values. In contrast, we show that inquiry can result in an alteration to a value such that some intra-personal and some interpersonal conflicts can be resolved. As such, our results are sufficiently general that they are not limited to the stronger assumptions required for expected utility maximization and apply within Levi’s framework as well. This is demonstrated in section 5. This result is sufficient to indicate how our approach might also be relevant to theorists who attempt to accommodate incommensurability in other ways, such as parity or ambiguity.

The work closest in spirit to ours is

These operations follow reverse Bayesianism, preserving the agent’s original likelihood ratios. Bradley’s refinement process parallels ours, but we extend it to decision-theoretic contexts involving utility uncertainty, not just probability uncertainty. This extension requires more general and relaxed Bayesian principles that we provide.

Steele & Stefánsson

propose two principles for awareness growth: awareness reflection and preference awareness reflection. Awareness reflection applies the law of total probability to awareness changes: an agent’s current credence should equal their expected credence conditional on anticipated awareness changes. Preference awareness reflection requires that agents not expect their preference rankings to change. Our model extends awareness reflection to decision-theoretic contexts, satisfying preference awareness reflection while providing a more complete account of value learning in both individual and strategic settings.

Likewise, our results are distinct from problems of coherence across time including the way that certain “transformative” experiences can lead to various changes in agents

Value of information guarantees break down in game-theoretic contexts where act-state independence fails
(
Skyrms 1985
;
Gibbard and Harper 1978
)
. When actions alter the relative probabilities of the realization states, additional information can decrease expected utility. Given this, one might conclude that value refinements offer no advantage in strategic settings. We demonstrate otherwise: learning about one’s values yields substantial benefits in game-theoretic contexts of extreme conflict.

A key question for decision theory asks when limited agents can implement expected utility maximization. Multiple arguments converge on this normative recommendation. Representation theorems
(
von Neumann and Morgenstern 1944
;
Savage 1954
;
Jeffrey 1965
)
show that if an agent’s choices satisfy fundamental axioms, her preferences can be represented by a unique utility function (up to positive affine transformation) coupled with a credence function satisfying probability axioms. Dutch book arguments
(
Ramsey 1931
;
de Finetti 1937
;
Kemeny 1955
)
prove agents violate probability axioms if and only if they accept guaranteed losses in fair bets. Accuracy arguments
(
Joyce 1998
;
Pettigrew 2016
;
Schoenfield 2017
)
establish that credences violating probability axioms are weakly dominated by conforming ones. Consequentialist arguments
(
Hammond 1988
;
McClennen 1990
)
demonstrate that maximizing expected utility in sequential decisions produces outcomes optimal by the agent’s standards.

When bounded agents can implement these recommendations remains unclear. Research on bounded rationality
(
Simon 1955
;
Gigerenzer and Selten 2002
;
Kahneman 2003
)
explores various departures from idealized rationality. We address a more focused question: what if an agent is uncertain about her values and can act to refine that understanding? We explore conditions where she should refine her situation understanding and values—in individual decisions, non-cooperative interactions, and Nash bargaining—before maximizing expected utility. Moreover, such refinements alter the way an agent represents the decision problem to be addressed. This connection between value specification and problem representation offers an important insight into the role that values play in problem formulation.

The Jeffrey-Bolker decision framework
(
Jeffrey 1965
;
Bolker 1966
)
suits this project well. Unlike Savage’s
(
Savage 1954
)
separation of states, acts, and outcomes, Jeffrey-Bolker employs propositions as the fundamental evaluation unit. Its atomless Boolean algebra structure allows preferences to be defined over propositions directly, with probability and desirability assigned to the same entities. This facilitates refinement operations where agents subdivide coarse propositions into finer ones as understanding develops. Because propositions in the algebra can be refined in well-defined ways, the framework’s flexibility makes it ideal for modeling epistemic and axiological refinements. That said, we conjecture that analogous results to those presented here can be proven in the decision frameworks of Savage, von Neumann-Morgenstern, and others.

3.1.
A Fruitful Vignette: The Arborist and the Baker

We begin with a vignette to illustrate value refinement. Consider an Arborist
A
and Baker
B
who jointly discover a rare orange in the wild. Both claim the fruit and find themselves in conflict over the resource.

3

The vignette here is inspired by one used in Fisher, Ury, and Patton’s “Getting to Yes” (2011).

Initially, each sees two coarse-grained possibilities for joint action:

EU_{A}(c)=EU_{A}(\neg c)\quad\text{and}\quad EU_{B}(c)=EU_{B}(\neg c)

This reflects their perception that any division merely redistributes fixed value, offering no advantage over conflict. Either action is rationalizable.

Before proceeding, each agent steps back and refines their representation of the situation. In practice, this can be through a process of reflection and introspection or conversation and third-party counsel. As a result, they discover that proposition
c
contains distinct possible realizations. They recognize that the arborist primarily values the fruit for the seeds because they provide a means of propagating a rare specimen, while the baker values the rind for its zest in specialty pastries. That is,

is decomposable into mutually exclusive propositions
(c\land f_{A}\land r_{B})\lor(c\land f_{B}\land r_{A})
where

\mathbb{A}_{0}=\{c,\neg c\}
to a new, finer partition
\mathbb{A}_{1}=\{c\land f_{A}\land r_{B},c\land f_{B}\land r_{A},\neg c\}
.

After refinement, their utilities over their actions are as follows:

\displaystyle EU_{A}(c\land f_{A}\land r_{B})>EU_{A}(c)=EU_{A}(\neg c)>EU_{A}(c\land r_{A}\land f_{B}),

\displaystyle EU_{B}(c\land f_{A}\land r_{B})>EU_{B}(c)=EU_{B}(\neg c)>EU_{B}(c\land r_{A}\land f_{B}).

Relative to their initial valuation of the choice to cooperate and divide the resource, they discovered that there are both worse and better ways to do so. Indeed, since
A
and
B
are rational,
4

4

In the sense that they are expected utility maximizers who also obey a reflection principle we will discuss shortly.
they realize that, when they can refine their understanding of the decisions available to them, they should expect this sort of improvement to be the typical outcome! Indeed, in this case, the refinement of their shared conceptualization of the interaction transforms a zero-sum situation into a potentially positive-sum arrangement:

EU_{A}(c\land f_{A}\land r_{B})+EU_{B}(c\land f_{A}\land r_{B})>0.

By consulting their values and distinguishing finer-grained realizations of their values, the parties discover a strict (ex ante) Pareto improvement—each can expect a better outcome without leaving the other expectably worse off.

This vignette illustrates one of our central contentions: when agents refine their values it can transform perceived zero-sum interactions into positive-sum arrangements and systematically improve individual and collective welfare. To make this case with greater precision, we begin with value refinement for a single agent, and progress carefully to strategic interactions.

3.2.
The Jeffrey–Bolker Decision Framework

The Jeffrey-Bolker decision framework boasts several features that make it well-suited to modeling value refinement. First, in Jeffrey-Bolker, acts, states, and consequences are all propositions in a single Boolean algebra. This allows the agent to assign probabilities to propositions about her own acts like anything else. This is essential to represent uncertainty about the structure of fine-grained actions that may result from refining her algebra. Collapsing the state/act/outcome distinction streamlines the analysis of value refinement, because every potential more detailed description of an option is already present in the algebra.

In Savage and vNM axioms, a decision problem needs to be setup so that it respects act-state independence. However, this is not required in Jeffrey-Bolker, as it is partition invariant–one can calculate expected values with respect to any partition and get the same answer. This is particularly important when one wants to simultaneously represent the prescription of competing decision theories that require different notions of act-state independence—e.g., causal, evidential, or functional. And it is important when one wishes to model decision situations where act-state independence is violated, as in cases of moral hazard or strategic interactions where agents’ actions are dependent on one another through sequential play or common knowledge.

Importantly, an agent’s representation of a decision situation is encoded by a
\langle\mathcal{A},\mathbb{A},U,P\rangle
where an algebra
\mathcal{A}
encodes the set of relevant possibilities she is entertaining, a partition
\mathbb{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
encodes her action set, and
U
and
P
encodes her utilities and credences over the elements of
\mathcal{A}
. The agents can then have coarser
\mathcal{A}_{0}
or finer-grained representations of the decision situation
\mathcal{A}_{1}
such that
\mathcal{A}_{0}\subset\mathcal{A}_{1}\subset\mathcal{A}
.

Given our algebra

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}\ \setminus\{\bot\}

\bot

is

Atomlessness guarantees our agent’s representation of the decision situation admits arbitrary precision. Every

3.4.4.
Modeling Agent Attitudes Regarding Refinement

An agent contemplating refinement faces a decision under uncertainty—she must estimate refinement’s value without knowing what distinctions she’ll discover. We model this meta-uncertainty through a probability distribution over possible refinement outcomes.

When facing novel, uncertain problems, we expect greater returns from reflection than with familiar ones where prior consideration has identified optimal strategies. Such differential expectations reveal that we operate with tacit models of refinement’s expected value across problem types. To capture these attitudes formally, we equip our agent with a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
where each

We define the refinement outcome mapping

3.4.5.
The Refinement Reflection Principle

Importantly, an agent must decide
whether
to refine their understanding of an act in the first place. To do so, we require some structure to the agent’s expectations regarding the results of reflection. To this end, we proffer two assumptions: a reflection-type principle relating a rational agent’s current valuations of each act to its expected decomposition when refined; and a non-triviality assumption capturing when an agent is genuinely uncertain regarding the outcome of a possible refinement.

First, we establish the general principle for arbitrary finite refinements:

Definition 8

(Refinement Reflection Principle - General Form)
.

For any proposition
A\in\mathbb{A}_{0}
and a
k
-ary refinement
\mathcal{R}_{A}=\{A\land B_{1},\ldots,A\land B_{k}\}
, a rational agent’s credences obey:

U_{0}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A}}[U_{1}(A)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A}}\left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\cdot u_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}}\Bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}>0\right]

where
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})\sim\mu_{A}
with
u_{i}=U_{1}(A\land B_{i})
representing the utility of the
i
-th refined act,
p_{i}
denoting the raw (unnormalized) probability assigned to
A\land B_{i}
after refinement.
15
15

15

Note that we require
\sum_{i}p_{i}>0
almost surely under
\mu_{A}
.

This principle states that the agent’s current valuation of
A
equals what she expects her valuation will be after more careful consideration, accounting for the possibility that refinement may lead her to revise
P(A)
itself from
P_{0}(A)
to
\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}
. The general RRP can be equivalently expressed using conditional probabilities. If we let
q_{i}=p_{i}/(\sum_{j}p_{j})
denote the conditional probability
P_{1}(B_{i}|A)
after refinement, then
U_{1}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}\cdot u_{i}
, and the RRP states that
U_{0}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A}}[U_{1}(A)]
, where the expectation accounts for both the uncertainty in the conditional probabilities and the utilities.

Since any
k
-ary refinement can be achieved through sequential binary refinements (as noted in Section 3.3.3), we focus on the binary case throughout our analysis:

Definition 9

(Refinement Reflection Principle - Binary Case)
.

For any proposition
A\in\mathbb{A}_{0}
and a binary refinement
\mathcal{R}_{A}=\{A\land B,A\land B_{2}\}
, a rational agent’s credences obey:

U_{0}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A}}[U_{1}(A)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A}}\left[\frac{p_{1}\cdot u_{1}+p_{2}\cdot u_{2}}{p_{1}+p_{2}}\bigg|p_{1}+p_{2}>0\right]

where
(u_{1},u_{2},p_{1},p_{2})\sim\mu_{A}
with
u_{1}=U_{1}(A\land B)
and
u_{2}=U_{1}(A\land B_{2})
representing the utilities of the refined acts,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
denoting the raw (unnormalized) probabilities assigned to
A\land B
and
A\land B_{2}
after refinement.
16
16

16

Note that we require
p_{1}+p_{2}>0
almost surely under
\mu_{A}
.

This formulation parallels classical reflection principles where an agent’s current credence equals her expected future credence, yet her actual future credence typically differs from the current one after consideration.

Our second assumption ensures agents have genuine uncertainty about refinement outcomes:
19
19

19

For now, we will also assume

That said, one can imagine conditions under which this assumption will be relaxed.

Definition 10

(Refinement Uncertainty)
.

An agent has refinement uncertainty if her distribution
\mu_{A}
assigns positive probability to any open neighborhood in the outcome space. For the binary case, this means
\mu_{A}
has continuous marginals on
\{(u_{1},u_{2},p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[0,1]^{2}:p_{1}+p_{2}\in(0,1)\}
.

This ensures the agent has genuine uncertainty about how refinement will shape her credences and utilities by ruling out point-mass beliefs about refinement outcomes. The continuity assumption is crucial: it ensures that exact equalities like
u_{1}=u_{2}
occur with probability zero, enabling our strict inequality results. An agent satisfying refinement uncertainty believes that it is possible for refinement to reveal value.

Together with the RRP, these conditions ensure positive probability of discovering strict improvements through refinement. The key intuition for upcoming results is that if an agent has genuine uncertainty about refinement outcomes (refinement uncertainty), and their current valuation reflects the expected outcome of such consideration (RRP), then with positive probability they will identify superior options, and being rational, the agent selects these promising prospects where possible, thereby creating value through refinement of their act partition.

4.
Value Refinement for One Agent

We can now apply our formal framework to analyze value refinement for a single agent. To summarize our setup: An agent with decision problem
D_{0}=\langle\mathcal{A}_{0},A_{0},P_{0},U_{0}\rangle

\{\mu_{A}\}_{A\in\mathbb{A}_{0}}

\mu_{A}
encodes her beliefs about the utilities and probabilities of refined propositions, constrained by the Refinement Reflection Principle:
U_{0}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A}}[p_{1}\cdot u_{1}+p_{2}\cdot u_{2}]
.

Before presenting the formal result, consider why value refinement generates expected value. When an agent chooses a coarse-grained act
A
, she commits to accepting whatever mixture of outcomes that act represents. This is like buying a bundle without knowing exactly what’s inside---she gets the weighted average of the bundle’s contents. Refinement unbundles the act, allowing her to see the components and choose among them. Since she can now select the best component rather than accepting the average, her expected utility improves.

The theorem establishes that value refinement creates strictly positive value in expectation whenever there is genuine uncertainty about how an act decomposes.

Several aspects merit emphasis. Refinement uncertainty ensures that
\mathbb{P}(u_{1}=u_{2})=0
. This is crucial because if
u_{1}=u_{2}
, then refinement would be pointless—the agent would be choosing between identical options. This guarantee that with probability one, the refined options differ, creating the possibility of selecting the better one.

The RRP ensures that the agent expects refinement to constitute a mean-preserving spread of utility. The agent’s current valuation
U_{0}(A_{j})
already reflects her expectation about refined utilities. Together, these make it so the agent assigns positive probability to a refined act being an improvement on the original act. What refinement adds is not a predictable change in average value, but rather finer-grained choices and the consequent potential to avoid below-average realizations while realizing above-average ones.

The framework naturally extends to sequential or simultaneous refinements. If an agent can refine multiple acts or perform iterative refinements, each refinement operation strictly increases expected utility:

Corollary 12

(Monotonicity of Refinement Value)
.

Consider successive refinements

\mathcal{A}_{0}\subset\mathcal{A}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{A}_{k}

with corresponding optimal values
V_{0},V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}
.
If each refinement satisfies Theorem

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[V_{0}]<\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[V_{1}]<\cdots<\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[V_{k}].

An agent need not fully resolve all uncertainty. Even partial refinement—distinguishing some but not all relevant propositions—creates value. Expected value increases whenever the refinement introduces meaningful choice.

The theorem and its corollary assume refinement incurs no cost. In practice, refinement requires time, cognitive effort, and may delay decision-making. We can therefore extend the result to incorporate such costs and characterize the resulting optimal refinement policies.

Having established that refinement can increases an individual’s expected utility, even when costly, we now turn to cases where the agent’s values themselves conflict. Can refinement dissolve dilemmas between competing ends when no common metric exists?

5.
Resolving Dilemmas without Commensuration

Our model of value refinement is also relevant to theories of choice that deviate from expected utility maximization. Recall Levi’s
(
Levi 1990
)
theory discussed earlier, in which agents may be uncertain about how to commensurate or reconcile the various values to which they are committed. An agent with distinct values such as compassion and honesty might confront actions that realize those values to different degrees, requiring judgments regarding commensuration. For instance, how great a gain in harm aversion justifies how great a loss in fairness? While the agent may easily rank actions across each dimension of value, she may struggle to determine how to commensurate between the dimensions themselves.

Standard expected utility theory resolves such cases by commensuration: choose weights for each value and maximize the aggregate. Yet often the correct weights are unknown or normatively contested. We ask whether
value refinement
can sometimes eliminate the need for commensuration by revealing actions that are jointly better across multiple dimensions.

We extend our framework to accommodate agents with multiple distinct values. Let
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}:\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
represent distinct value dimensions. Under standard aggregation, a weight vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})
with
w_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
yields

U(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}V_{i}(A)\quad\text{for }A\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}.

Uncertainty about
\mathbf{w}
or the normative inadmissibility of commensuration complicate choice when actions trade off values.

Consider the simplest case with two values,
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, with initial partition
\mathbb{A}_{0}=\{A,\neg A\}
with opposing realizations:

V_{1}(A)>V_{1}(\neg A)\quad\text{and}\quad V_{2}(A)<V_{2}(\neg A).

The agent faces a dilemma:
A
is favored by
V_{1}
, while
\neg A
is favored by
V_{2}
. Within expected utility theory, resolution requires determining the relative weight
w\in[0,1]
such that her utility becomes
U(A)=(1-w)V_{1}(A)+wV_{2}(A)
. When
w=0
, only
V_{1}
matters; when
w=1
, only
V_{2}
matters; and when
0<w<1
, value
V_{1}
matters
\frac{1-w}{w}
times as much as
V_{2}
. This confronts all the fundamental challenges of commensuration between potentially incommensurable values.

The possibility of value refinement opens a third path. Rather than resolving her uncertainty about the correct weight
w
, the agent can refine her understanding of the available actions themselves. As established in our earlier results, she can often expect to improve her expected utility through such refinement. However, in certain fortunate instances, refinement may eliminate the dilemma entirely by revealing an action that excels along both value dimensions, obviating the need for commensuration.

Following our framework, the agent fine-grains
A
by considering some previously unconsidered proposition
B\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0}
, producing the refined act partition

\mathbb{A}_{1}=(\mathbb{A}_{0}\setminus\{A\})\cup\{A\land B,\;A\land B_{2}\}.

Crucially, this refinement affects both value dimensions simultaneously. For each value function
V_{i}
, the agent maintains uncertainty about the refined values, captured by distributions
\mu_{A}^{(i)}
over the triple
(v_{i,1},v_{i,2},q^{(i)})
where
v_{i,1}=V_{i}(A\land B_{1})
,
v_{i,2}=V_{i}(A\land B_{2})
, and
q^{(i)}=P(B_{1}|A)
represents the conditional probability.

The Refinement Reflection Principle extends naturally to each value dimension. For each
i\in\{1,2\}
, we require
V_{i}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A}^{(i)}}[q^{(i)}\cdot v_{i,1}+(1-q^{(i)})\cdot v_{i,2}]

The resolution occurs because, in expectation, refinement constitutes a multi-dimensional mean-preserving spread. Just as single-valued refinement creates option value by allowing selection of above-average components, multi-valued refinement can reveal components that simultaneously exceed the average across multiple dimensions. When this occurs, the agent discovers an action that remains optimal regardless of how she might weight her values, transforming an apparent dilemma requiring difficult commensuration judgments into a choice with a clear solution.

This possibility is not guaranteed—refinement may simply provide additional options without revealing a dominating action. The result demonstrates that value refinement affects not merely the expected utility of available actions but can alter the fundamental structure of choice problems themselves. When refinement reveals multi-value dominating actions, it allows agents to bypass rather than resolve their uncertainty about value weights, providing a third path between the horns of a dilemma.

Having established the value of refinement for individual agents, including its potential to resolve apparent dilemmas between incommensurable values, we now turn to strategic settings. Classical value of information results break down in game-theoretic contexts where actions influence the probability of states, potentially making additional information harmful. We demonstrate that value refinement maintains its benefits even in these challenging environments, beginning with the case of two-agent zero-sum games.

8.
Implications and Future Directions

Our results give a mathematical twist to the injunction at Delphi to “know thyself.” If you pause to reflect on your values before you act then, under broad conditions, value refinement at a reasonable cost is
ex ante
beneficial for a rational agent (Theorem
11
), can address classical dilemmas about incommensurable values (Theorem
16
), turns zero-sum conflicts into positive-sum opportunities (Theorem
17
), and expands Nash-bargaining frontiers so that each party can secure a strictly higher expected payoff (Theorem
18
). These theorems carry normative, methodological, and practical consequences that reach from individual reflection and ethical deliberation to multi-agent coordination and institutional design.

Decision makers, whether they are individual human agents, AI systems, organizations or institutions must rely on their values and knowledge of the world to make decisions. Unlike the idealized agents of textbooks, these decision makers can be uncertain about whether their current representation and understanding of their values is adequate. There is thus a tension between taking the time to learn and acting on the values and information at hand. One of the lessons of the present work is that when decision makers find themselves in situations of conflict, whether from individual moral dilemmas or coordination problems in group decisions, it can be beneficial to avoid the temptation to move immediately to tradeoffs. Rather, taking conflict as an occasion to reflect on one’s values can open up the possibility that conflicts can be averted or mitigated and that dilemmas can be avoided or resolved. Failure to reflect on one’s values on such occasions can entrench suboptimal values and lock decision makers into perspectives that do not adequately reflect their own ultimate interests.

The work also extends the scope of decision theory. Savage and von Neumann–Morgenstern treat the act–state–outcome partition as fixed; by contrast, the atomless Boolean algebra of the Jeffrey–Bolker framework lets that partition be indefinitely refined in a single model. We provide a natural formalism for how this refinement might proceed. This flexibility allows us to formalize a deliberative process that is ubiquitous in practice yet underappreciated in traditional theory: interrogating and sharpening one’s evaluative map prior to choice. In doing so, we supply a rational-choice foundation for heuristics that urge parties to separate positions from interests and to explore unconsidered options for mutual gain.

Classical coherence arguments—Dutch books, accuracy dominance, sequential optimality—assume from the outset that an agent possesses a settled utility function. We show that taking such settlement for granted is itself a substantive decision: foregoing refinement can be strictly dominated by clarifying one’s values first. Rational choice therefore has a two-stage character: refine, then maximize. By revealing that finer descriptions of available acts can expose alternatives that dominate on every value dimension, our analysis shows that value pluralism need not entail decision paralysis, while monism may sacrifice long-run efficacy for short-term consistency.

The results we present are sufficiently general that they apply to approaches to decision making that are weaker than expected utility in that they do not require commensurability of all objects of choice. Our results alone do not say anything in favor or against any of these approaches.

However, our results give rise to what we call the Paradox of Bounded Optimization, which we regard as the computational analogue of the paradox of hedonism. The optimal response to this paradox likely has important implications for the way that realistic agents should think about their utility function.

For our purposes, the paradox of hedonism can be understood as a reductio on the combination of two seemingly intuitive claims. The first is the axiological claim that pleasure is the only intrinsic good. The second is the prudential claim that an agent who cares about their own wellbeing should directly seek to maximize the amount of pleasure in their life. The paradox derives from the claim that if an agent pursues an activity purely for the goal of deriving pleasure from it, they are unlikely to achieve their own prudential goal. The reason is that in order to derive pleasure from that activity, the agent must value something other than pleasure, contradicting the axiological claim. For example, deriving pleasure from a game of chess requires that the agent value winning, or elegant play, or bold openings or cunning defenses. Valuing something other than pleasure allows the agent to derive pleasure from an activity but contradicts the axiological claim that only pleasure is intrinsically valuable.

In the broadest sense, our results demonstrate that there are cases where a bounded agent who seeks to maximize expected utility cannot do so by the direct application of the utility function they accept at that time. Rather, the agent will do better by pausing to reflect on the underlying values that are relevant to the problem at hand and of which their overall utility is a function.

On a theoretical level, this aligns with pluralistic approaches to value theory, which hold that acknowledging and consulting a diversity of ultimate values can lead to better outcomes in the long run. This contrasts with monistic approaches, where decision-making is governed by a single utility function formulated to encode choice behavior and avoid inconsistencies. While monistic systems may be less vulnerable to the sorts of incoherence indicated by Dutch book and money-pump arguments, for bounded agents they can fail to capture the full richness of agent’s uncertainty regarding their underlying values, or of boundedly rational solutions to decision problems. Refining values not only enriches the state space of possible outcomes, but also transforms the character of strategic interactions.

Moreover, this approach challenges certain reductive interpretations of utility. If utility functions are merely mathematical constructs reflecting underlying values, then what matters are those values themselves. For agents who do not already know all of their values, consulting their values is critical for better realizing those values, as opposed to merely optimizing within a coarse-grained or incomplete framework.

In this sense, the problem of value refinement echoes the paradox of hedonism: just as directly pursuing pleasure may lead to its absence, so too acting on the agent’s current utility function may lead to systematically suboptimal outcomes, even by one’s own light. Such an agent would be better off reflecting on and refining the values from which that utility function is constructed.

Good-style value-of-information theorems guarantee that learning about the world is worth its price. We have provided the parallel guarantee for learning about, and refining, one’s own values. Agents who treat their utility functions as provisional hypotheses, open to disciplined revision, can expect to fare better both individually and collectively. Rationality, then, is not merely the calculation performed with a fixed utility function; it is the iterative work of making that function worthy of calculation.

Our findings thus highlight the importance of investigating ultimate values and refining their representation in decision-making frameworks. By doing so, agents can systematically improve their outcomes and transform adversarial interactions into cooperative ones, advancing both individual and collective well-being.

Important limitations remain. Our results are formulated within the Jeffrey–Bolker decision framework, owing to its congenial treatment of acts as propositions—however, analogous results could be formulated in the decision frameworks of Savage, von Neumann and Morgenstern, Anscombe and Aumann, and others. We assumed negligible cognitive and temporal costs; introducing stochastic refinement costs would permit an optimal-stopping analysis of how much reflection suffices. The strategic results relied on common probabilistic beliefs; relaxing that assumption may attenuate the positive-sum guarantee, and bounding the resulting effect sizes remains an open problem. Furthermore, in iterated interactions, early-round refinement shapes later payoffs and information flows; richer dynamics—including signaling about one’s value-learning intentions—await investigation.

k-1
such refinements, we obtain
\mathbb{A}_{k-1}=(\mathbb{A}_{0}\setminus\{A\})\cup\{A\land B_{1},\ldots,A\land B_{k}\}
, which equals the direct
k
-ary refinement.
∎

Proof of Theorem
11
(Value of Value Refinement).

Let
V_{0}=\max_{A\in\mathbb{A}_{0}}U_{0}(A)
denote the agent’s optimal value before refinement, let
V^{\prime}_{0}=\max_{A\in\mathbb{A}_{0}\setminus\{A^{*}\}}U_{0}(A)
denote the best value excluding the refined act, and let
V_{1}=\max\{V^{\prime}_{0},u_{1},u_{2}\}
denote the optimal value after refinement, where
(u_{1},u_{2},p_{1},p_{2})\sim\mu_{A^{*}}
with
u_{1}=U(A^{*}\land B_{1})
,
u_{2}=U(A^{*}\land B_{2})
, and we define
p=\frac{p_{1}}{p_{1}+p_{2}}
as the conditional probability
P(B|A^{*})
.

We must show that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[V_{1}]>V_{0}
. Since
A^{*}
is rationalizable, we have
U_{0}(A^{*})=V_{0}
. By the Refinement Reflection Principle,

\displaystyle U_{0}(A^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[p\cdot u_{1}+(1-p)\cdot u_{2}].

The expected gain from refinement equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[V_{1}]-V_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[\max\{V^{\prime}_{0},u_{1},u_{2}\}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[p\cdot u_{1}+(1-p)\cdot u_{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[p\cdot u_{1}+(1-p)\cdot u_{2}].

For any realization
(u_{1},u_{2},p)
, we have

\displaystyle\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\geq p\cdot u_{1}+(1-p)\cdot u_{2},

with equality only when
u_{1}=u_{2}
. Since
\mu_{A^{*}}
has continuous marginals, we know that
\mathbb{P}(u_{1}=u_{2})=0
. Hence the inequality is strict almost surely. By dominated convergence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\}]>\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[p\cdot u_{1}+(1-p)\cdot u_{2}].

Therefore
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}}}[V_{1}]>V_{0}
, as desired.
∎

Proof of Corollary
12
(Monotonicity of Refinement Value).

We establish strict monotonicity by applying Theorem
11
at each refinement stage. First, each refinement from
\mathcal{A}_{i-1}
to
\mathcal{A}_{i}
involves refining some rationalizable act
A^{*}_{i-1}
. Second, since each refinement satisfies RRP and refinement uncertainty by hypothesis, Theorem 11 guarantees
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A^{*}_{i-1}}}[V_{i}]>V_{i-1}
, yielding strict improvement at each stage. By transitivity of strict inequality we have that,
V_{0}<V_{1}<\cdots<V_{k}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
14
(Optimal Refinement with Fixed Costs).

The agent faces sequential decisions where at stage
i
, having incurred total cost
i\cdot k
, she chooses whether to refine by paying
k
for expected gain
\Delta_{R_{i}}
or to stop and act.

If
k>\Delta_{R_{0}}
, then by vanishing returns we have
k>\Delta_{R_{i}}
for all
i\geq 0
. Each refinement would yield negative net value, so the optimal policy is immediate action.

If
k\leq\Delta_{R_{0}}
, we define the stopping time

\displaystyle t^{*}=\max\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\Delta_{R_{t}}\geq k\}.

This stopping time is well-defined since
\Delta_{R_{0}}\geq k
and
\lim_{i\to\infty}\Delta_{R_{i}}=0<k
.

By construction, for
i<t^{*}
we have
\Delta_{R_{i}}>k
, yielding positive net gain from refinement. At
i=t^{*}
we have
\Delta_{R_{t^{*}}}\geq k
, yielding non-negative net gain. For
i>t^{*}
we have
\Delta_{R_{i}}<k
, yielding negative net gain. By backward induction, the optimal policy refines exactly
t^{*}
times. The total net gain equals

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{t^{*}-1}\Delta_{R_{i}}-t^{*}\cdot k=\sum_{i=0}^{t^{*}-1}(\Delta_{R_{i}}-k)>0,

since each term satisfies
\Delta_{R_{i}}-k\geq 0
with strict inequality for
i<t^{*}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
16
(Dilemma Resolution).

Let
(v_{1,1},v_{1,2},p_{1})\sim\mu_{A}^{(1)}
and
(v_{2,1},v_{2,2},p_{2})\sim\mu_{A}^{(2)}
represent refinement uncertainties for
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
respectively. An action multi-value dominates when it achieves the maximum along both value dimensions. This occurs when either

(D1):

\displaystyle v_{1,1}\geq\max\{V_{1}(\neg A),v_{1,2}\}\text{ and }v_{2,1}\geq\max\{V_{2}(\neg A),v_{2,2}\}

(D2):

\displaystyle v_{1,2}\geq\max\{V_{1}(\neg A),v_{1,1}\}\text{ and }v_{2,2}\geq\max\{V_{2}(\neg A),v_{2,1}\}

with at least one strict inequality in each case.

By hypothesis, the support of the joint distribution includes realizations satisfying (D1) or (D2). Since the marginal distributions are continuous, there exists an open set
\Omega^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
with
\mathbb{P}((v_{1,1},v_{1,2},v_{2,1},v_{2,2})\in\Omega^{*})>0
where multi-value dominance occurs.

When (D1) holds, action
A\land B_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle U_{w}(A\land B_{1})=(1-w)V_{1}(A\land B_{1})+wV_{2}(A\land B_{1})\geq U_{w}(A^{\prime})

for all
A^{\prime}\in\{\neg A,A\land B_{2}\}
and all weights
w\in[0,1]
. A similar statement holds for (D2) with
A\land B_{2}
. Therefore we have
\mathbb{P}(\text{dilemma resolved})=\mathbb{P}(\text{(D1)}\cup\text{(D2)})>0
, as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
17
(Zero-Sum Escape).

We consider a baseline 2×2 zero-sum game
G_{0}
with actions
\{A^{1},\neg A^{1}\}\times\{A^{2},\neg A^{2}\}
and payoffs satisfying
U_{1}^{0}+U_{2}^{0}\equiv 0
. The unique interior mixed Nash equilibrium is
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})
, where
\alpha^{*}
denotes player 1’s probability on
A^{1}
and
\beta^{*}
denotes player 2’s probability on
A^{2}
. Player 1 refines action
A^{1}
into two variants
\{A^{1}\land B_{1},A^{1}\land B_{2}\}
, transforming the game into a 3×2 game
G_{\varepsilon}
. The refinement introduces perturbations
\eta^{i}_{C}(a^{2})
for
i\in\{1,2\}
,
C\in\{B_{1},B_{2}\}
, and
a^{2}\in\{A^{2},\neg A^{2}\}
, which are independent across players and variants, have continuous distributions, and by RRP satisfy
\mathbb{E}[\eta^{i}_{C}(a^{2})]=0
. Our goal is to show that both players benefit in expectation at the welfare-optimal Nash equilibrium of the perturbed game.

We begin by characterizing the structure of the perturbed game. After player 1’s refinement,
G_{\varepsilon}
becomes a 3×2 game with payoffs:

For
(A^{1}\land C,a^{2})
where
C\in\{B_{1},B_{2}\}
and
a^{2}\in\{A^{2},\neg A^{2}\}
:

\displaystyle U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C,a^{2})

\displaystyle=U_{1}^{0}(A^{1},a^{2})+\varepsilon\eta^{1}_{C}(a^{2}),

\displaystyle U_{2}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C,a^{2})

\displaystyle=U_{2}^{0}(A^{1},a^{2})+\varepsilon\eta^{2}_{C}(a^{2})=-U_{1}^{0}(A^{1},a^{2})+\varepsilon\eta^{2}_{C}(a^{2}).

And for
(\neg A^{1},a^{2})
, we have
U_{i}^{\varepsilon}(\neg A^{1},a^{2})=U_{i}^{0}(\neg A^{1},a^{2}).

Since
\eta^{1}_{C}
and
\eta^{2}_{C}
are independent, the perturbed game is generically non-zero-sum.

To understand the equilibrium structure near
\varepsilon=0
, we examine which refined variant player 1 prefers. Define the payoff difference between refined variants for player 1:

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(\beta,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land B_{1},\beta)-U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land B_{2},\beta)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon[\beta(\eta^{1}_{B_{1}}(A^{2})-\eta^{1}_{B_{2}}(A^{2}))+(1-\beta)(\eta^{1}_{B_{1}}(\neg A^{2})-\eta^{1}_{B_{2}}(\neg A^{2}))].

At the baseline equilibrium mix
\beta^{*}
, player 1 was indifferent between
A^{1}
and
\neg A^{1}
. After refinement, define:

X=\eta^{1}_{B_{1}}(A^{2})\beta^{*}+\eta^{1}_{B_{1}}(\neg A^{2})(1-\beta^{*})-[\eta^{1}_{B_{2}}(A^{2})\beta^{*}+\eta^{1}_{B_{2}}(\neg A^{2})(1-\beta^{*})].

Since the
\eta
terms are continuous and independent,
\mathbb{P}(X=0)=0
. Thus, in expectation, one variant is strictly preferred at
\beta^{*}
. Let
C^{*}=\arg\max_{C\in\{B_{1},B_{2}\}}U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C,\beta^{*})
.

We now characterize equilibria for small
\varepsilon
. For small
\varepsilon>0
, any Nash equilibrium near
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})
has player 1 mixing between at most two actions:
\{A^{1}\land C^{*},\neg A^{1}\}
. This reduces to analyzing a 2×2 subgame.

The indifference conditions for an interior equilibrium are:

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C^{*},\beta)-U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(\neg A^{1},\beta)=0,

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=U_{2}^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,A^{2})-U_{2}^{\varepsilon}(\alpha,\neg A^{2})=0,

where for player 2’s condition,
\alpha
now represents the probability on
A^{1}\land C^{*}
(with remaining mass on
\neg A^{1}
).

By the implicit function theorem, since
\nabla_{(\alpha,\beta)}\phi|_{(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*},0)}
is non-singular (from uniqueness of the baseline equilibrium), there exists a unique continuation
(\alpha(\varepsilon),\beta(\varepsilon))
with:

\displaystyle\alpha(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{*}+\varepsilon\alpha_{1}+o(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle\beta(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\beta^{*}+\varepsilon\beta_{1}+o(\varepsilon).

To analyze player 1’s payoff at first order, note that player 1’s expected payoff at this equilibrium is:

V_{1}(\varepsilon)=\alpha(\varepsilon)U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C^{*},\beta(\varepsilon))+(1-\alpha(\varepsilon))U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(\neg A^{1},\beta(\varepsilon)).

Since player 1 is indifferent at equilibrium:
U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C^{*},\beta(\varepsilon))=U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(\neg A^{1},\beta(\varepsilon))
, we have:

V_{1}(\varepsilon)=U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(\neg A^{1},\beta(\varepsilon))=U_{1}^{0}(\neg A^{1},\beta(\varepsilon)).

We can also evaluate using the fact that
C^{*}
was chosen optimally:

V_{1}(\varepsilon)-V_{1}(0)=\alpha^{*}\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[\max\{\eta^{1}_{B_{1}}\cdot\beta^{*}+\eta^{1}_{B_{1}}\cdot(1-\beta^{*}),\eta^{1}_{B_{2}}\cdot\beta^{*}+\eta^{1}_{B_{2}}\cdot(1-\beta^{*})\}]+o(\varepsilon).

Since
\mathbb{E}[\max\{Y_{1},Y_{2}\}]>0
for independent, continuous, mean-zero
Y_{1},Y_{2}
, player 1 gains at first order.

Turning to player 2’s payoff, the expected payoff is:

V_{2}(\varepsilon)=\alpha(\varepsilon)U_{2}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C^{*},\beta(\varepsilon))+(1-\alpha(\varepsilon))U_{2}^{\varepsilon}(\neg A^{1},\beta(\varepsilon)).

The perturbations affecting player 2’s payoffs are:

U_{2}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C^{*},a^{2})=-U_{1}^{0}(A^{1},a^{2})+\varepsilon\eta^{2}_{C^{*}}(a^{2}).

At first order:

V_{2}(\varepsilon)-V_{2}(0)=\alpha^{*}\varepsilon[\beta^{*}\eta^{2}_{C^{*}}(A^{2})+(1-\beta^{*})\eta^{2}_{C^{*}}(\neg A^{2})]+o(\varepsilon).

Since
\eta^{2}_{C^{*}}
is independent of
\eta^{1}_{C^{*}}
and has mean zero with continuous distribution:

\mathbb{E}[V_{2}(\varepsilon)-V_{2}(0)]=\alpha^{*}\varepsilon\cdot 0+o(\varepsilon)=o(\varepsilon).

The above analysis considered a particular equilibrium continuation. We now demonstrate that welfare optimization creates strict gains for both players in expecation. The total welfare at any equilibrium is:

W(\varepsilon)=V_{1}(\varepsilon)+V_{2}(\varepsilon).

At the baseline,
W(0)=V_{1}(0)+V_{2}(0)=0
(zero-sum game).

For the perturbed game, the sum of payoffs for action profile
(A^{1}\land C,a^{2})
is:

U_{1}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C,a^{2})+U_{2}^{\varepsilon}(A^{1}\land C,a^{2})=\varepsilon[\eta^{1}_{C}(a^{2})+\eta^{2}_{C}(a^{2})].

Since
\eta^{1}_{C}
and
\eta^{2}_{C}
are independent and continuous, with positive probability we have
\eta^{1}_{C}(a^{2})+\eta^{2}_{C}(a^{2})>0
for some
(C,a^{2})
. This creates positive-sum outcomes.

The welfare-optimal equilibrium selects among all Nash equilibria to maximize total surplus. With positive probability, there exists an equilibrium placing positive weight on positive-sum outcomes, yielding:

\mathbb{E}[W(\varepsilon)]>0.

It remains to show that both players expect to benefit at the welfare-optimal equilibrium. At the welfare-optimal equilibrium with
W(\varepsilon)>0
, suppose player 2’s payoff decreased:
W_{2}(\varepsilon)<W_{2}(0)=-V_{1}(0)
. Then:

W_{1}(\varepsilon)=W(\varepsilon)-W_{2}(\varepsilon)>0-(-V_{1}(0))=V_{1}(0).

But player 1 could guarantee at least
V_{1}(0)+\varepsilon\cdot\mathbb{E}[\max\{X_{B_{1}},X_{B_{2}}\}]+o(\varepsilon)
by optimal play. If the welfare-optimal equilibrium gave player 2 less than her baseline payoff, player 1 would have incentive to deviate, contradicting equilibrium.

By continuity of the Nash correspondence and the fact that both players can guarantee approximately their baseline payoffs (through strategies close to
(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*})
), the welfare gains must be shared. Specifically, for small
\varepsilon
:

W_{i}(\varepsilon)\geq W_{i}(0)+c_{i}\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon)

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
and
c_{1}+c_{2}=\mathbb{E}[\max_{C,a^{2}}[\eta^{1}_{C}(a^{2})+\eta^{2}_{C}(a^{2})]]>0
.

Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[W_{i}(\varepsilon)-W_{i}(0)]>0
for both
i\in\{1,2\}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
18
(Nash Bargaining).

We consider the baseline Nash bargaining game
\mathcal{G}_{0}
where agents divide a one-dimensional resource with allocation
x\in[0,1]
giving agent
1
share
x
and agent
2
share
1-x
, with utilities
u^{i}_{0}(x)
that are continuous, increasing, and concave. The Nash solution selects a unique
x^{*}
, with payoffs
u^{i*}_{0}=u^{i}_{0}(x^{*})
.

Refinement reveals two latent dimensions of the resource.
Each agent
i
has weights
\mathbf{w}^{i}(\omega)=(w^{i}_{1}(\omega),w^{i}_{2}(\omega))
and strictly increasing, concave per-dimension utilities
v^{i}_{j}(\cdot,\omega)
, all drawn independently from continuous distributions.
A refined allocation
(x_{1},x_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}
yields

u^{i}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},\omega)=w^{i}_{1}(\omega)\,v^{i}_{1}(x_{1},\omega)+w^{i}_{2}(\omega)\,v^{i}_{2}(x_{2},\omega).

The Refinement Reflection Principle requires

u^{i}_{0}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{i}}\!\left[u^{i}_{1}(x,x,\omega)\right]\quad\text{for all }x\in[0,1].

We assume that the agents’ weight vectors differ with positive probability.

We now show that refinement strictly increases each agent’s expected Nash payoff.
Let
x^{*}
be the Nash solution in the unrefined game
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.
For each refinement state
\omega
, define

b_{i}(\omega):=u^{i}_{1}(x^{*},x^{*},\omega),

the payoff agent
i
would receive if the allocation in the refined game were kept bundled.
By the Refinement Reflection Principle,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[b_{i}(\omega)]=u^{i*}_{0}.

To compare refinement to the baseline, write each agent’s gain in state
\omega
as

\Delta_{i}(\omega):=u^{i*}_{1}(\omega)-b_{i}(\omega).

Because
(x^{*},x^{*})
is feasible for every refined game
\mathcal{G}_{1}(\omega)
, the refined Nash solution can only do weakly better, so
\Delta_{i}(\omega)\geq 0
for all
\omega
.

The next step is to identify when this inequality is strict.
Bundling is Nash-optimal in the refined game only if the two agents’ marginal rates of substitution across the revealed dimensions match exactly at
(x^{*},x^{*})
.
This condition depends smoothly on a finite list of state variables (the agents’ weights and marginal utilities).

Since these variables are independently and continuously distributed, the probability that the required equality holds is zero.
Thus there is a set of states, call it
\Omega^{+}
, with strictly positive probability, on which bundling is not optimal for the refined game.

On any state in
\Omega^{+}
where the agents’ weight vectors differ, their marginal rates of substitution differ at
(x^{*},x^{*})
.
Together with strict monotonicity and concavity of each
v^{i}_{j}
, this ensures the existence of a small, feasible reallocation across dimensions that raises both agents’ utilities relative to bundling.

Since the Nash product is strictly increasing in each argument above the disagreement point, and the feasible set contains a point that strictly Pareto-dominates
(b_{1}(\omega),b_{2}(\omega))
, the Nash maximizer must lie strictly above
(b_{1}(\omega),b_{2}(\omega))
in both coordinates. Hence, for all
\omega\in\Omega^{+}
,

\Delta_{i}(\omega)>0\qquad\text{for each }i.

Finally, because
\Delta_{i}(\omega)\geq 0
everywhere and strictly positive on a set of positive probability, its expectation is strictly positive:

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\Delta_{i}(\omega)]>0.

Using the earlier identity for the expected bundled payoff gives

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[u^{i*}_{1}(\omega)]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[b_{i}(\omega)]+\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\Delta_{i}(\omega)]=u^{i*}_{0}+\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\Delta_{i}(\omega)]>u^{i*}_{0}.

Thus each agent strictly gains in expectation from refinement.
∎

Proof of Proposition
19
(Preference Divergence).

With linear utilities
v^{i}_{j}(x_{j},\omega)=a^{i}_{j}(\omega)x_{j}
, the Nash product under allocation
(x_{1},x_{2})
becomes

\displaystyle N(x_{1},x_{2})=[w^{1}_{1}a^{1}_{1}x_{1}+w^{1}_{2}a^{1}_{2}x_{2}-d_{1}][w^{2}_{1}a^{2}_{1}(1-x_{1})+w^{2}_{2}a^{2}_{2}(1-x_{2})-d_{2}].

Since
N
is linear in each
x_{j}
when the other is fixed, the first-order condition
\partial N/\partial x_{j}=0
cannot hold at interior points. The maximum must occur at a corner of
[0,1]^{2}
.

The allocation rule assigns dimension
j
entirely to agent 1 if
\frac{w^{1}_{j}a^{1}_{j}}{w^{2}_{j}a^{2}_{j}}>\frac{U^{1}}{U^{2}}
and to agent 2 otherwise, where
U^{i}
denotes equilibrium utility minus disagreement.

When
\theta=0
and weight vectors are identical, both ratios
\frac{w^{1}_{j}}{w^{2}_{j}}
are equal, eliminating gains from specialization. As
\theta
increases toward
\pi/2
, the weight ratios diverge, enabling increasingly efficient specialization. At
\theta=\pi/2
with orthogonal preferences, complete specialization occurs where each agent receives only their valued dimension, maximizing the Nash product.

Therefore
N^{*}(\theta)
is weakly increasing in
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
and achieves its maximum at
\theta=\pi/2
, as desired.
∎
