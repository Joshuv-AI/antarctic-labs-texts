Investigating the effects of pine biochar on intake and digestibility in beef steers consuming forage
Authors: Emily C Fowler, Sabrina Fortunatti, Merritt L Drewery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiochar is a charcoal-like material made from burned organic material of agricultural or forestry biomass. Pyrolysis, the primary method of biochar production, is achieved in limited oxygen conditions which allows the thermally altered biomass to retain most of its carbon. Biochar has potential to enhance soil health and sequester carbon in soils, but must be mixed with an organic material, such as urine or manure, to maximize effectiveness. Feeding biochar to cattle allows them to deposit biochar-enriched manure onto pasture without human intervention while simultaneously receiving any potential benefits associated with consumption. The objective of this study was to evaluate diet utilization in beef steers fed one of four treatments in addition to a basal forage diet: no biochar (CON), or a pinewood-based biochar provided at 0.8%, 1.6%, or 2.4% of the previous 3-d forage intake. Four ruminally cannulated steers were utilized in a 4 × 4 Latin square with four 14-d periods, consisting of an 8-d treatment adaptation (d 1 to 8), fecal and diet sampling for intake and digestibility (d 9 to 13) and rumen fluid sampling (d 14). Steers had ad libitum access to hay and water and were offered treatments once daily mixed with cottonseed meal provided at 125 mg N/kg BW. Statistical analyses employed orthogonal contrasts with fixed effects of treatment and period and a random effect of steer. There was a tendency for biochar to linearly affect (P = 0.08) forage organic matter intake (FOMI) where FOMI was 5.23 kg/d for CON, 4.96 kg/d for 0.8% biochar, 4.52 kg/d for 1.6% biochar, and 4.54 kg/d for 2.4% biochar. There was also a linear trend (P = 0.08) for total digestible organic matter intake (TDOMI) where CON steers consumed the most TDOMI (4.29 kg/d) and steers receiving 2.4% biochar consumed the least (3.53 kg/d). There were no linear (P ≥ 0.11) or quadratic effects (P ≥ 0.31) of biochar inclusion on measures of digestibility; organic matter digestibility averaged 64.6% across treatments. Our observations suggest that pinewood derived biochar supplemented from 0.8% to 2.4% of intake did not significantly affect diet utilization, but the tendencies and numerical depressions observed suggest moderate (1.6%) and high (2.4%) doses of biochar may have practical concerns. However, there were methodological limitations that affected our ability to draw definitive conclusions, and further research is necessary to confirm these findings.Keywords: alternative feed, beef cattle, grazing, regenerative agriculture, soil health, sustainability

Introduction

Biochar is a carbon-rich, porous, charcoal-like material produced by pyrolyzing biomass under limited oxygen conditions. During pyrolysis, substrates are typically heated to 400 to 600°C with duration distinguishing the process and affecting characteristics (eg particle size and distribution, porosity, pH) of the resulting biochar. Properties of biochar also vary depending on the feedstock (substrate) used, which can include manure, crop residues, food waste, or wood waste. For example, previous research indicates that wood-derived biochar typically has a higher carbon content (60% to 85%) and greater structural stability compared with those produced from other substrates (Waheed et al. 2025); however, the carbon fraction (eg total, organic, fixed) and mass basis are not consistently reported across the literature (Li et al. 2023), complicating direct comparison across studies. Among wood sources, pine is widely available across the United States and is frequently used as a biochar substrate. Therefore, pine-derived biochar has potential for broad application and deserves research attention.

Biochar has traditionally been utilized as a soil amendment because it improves soil health, water retention, and crop productivity (Bruun et al. 2012). The use and relevance of biochar has increased in recent decades as global soil health continues to decline (Timmis and Ramos 2021). Biochar enhances soil carbon sequestration by storing stable forms of organic carbon within the soil; the highly porous structure facilitates absorption and long-term stabilization of carbon, preventing its release as carbon dioxide and allowing for prolonged retention (Gross et al. 2021). Although soils have historically served as significant carbon reservoirs, decades of intensive agricultural practices have diminished their capacity (Derpsch et al. 2006). In the Midwestern U.S. alone, an estimated 57 billion metric tons of topsoil, including the nutrients and organic matter (OM) necessary for crop growth, have eroded since the 1870s (Thaler et al. 2022; NASEM 2024). This has significant relevance for beef production as forage systems are foundational to the industry.

Widespread adoption of biochar as a soil amendment has been limited by the variable efficacy of biochar, which might be attributed to different processing methods and feedstock substrates. Further, biochar must be “activated” or “conditioned” prior to application to maximize effectiveness; this may be achieved through co-composting, the addition of enzymes, and/or adding a N source (eg urine; Jaiswal et al. 2018; Panwar and Pawar 2022). This conditioning step enhances the properties of biochar and resulting soil health parameters as compared to the application of raw, untreated biochar (Kammann et al. 2015). However, conditioning biochar can be time-, labor-, and cost-intensive for producers.

There is an opportunity to utilize ruminants as carriers to land-spread biochar. When biochar is consumed by grazing cattle, it becomes “conditioned” (or “activated”) as it passes through the gastrointestinal tract, becomes microbially enriched, and is subsequently deposited onto soil via manure. The C-lattice structure of biochar remains intact as it travels through the gastrointestinal tract (Joseph et al. 2015) suggesting that biochar is not itself digested or absorbed by the animal. Therefore, feeding biochar to grazing animals could reduce practical barriers associated with conditioning while also providing a practical approach to integrating biochar into forage-based livestock systems. However, biochar is currently not a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved feed ingredient for livestock in the U.S., although it is more widely approved and utilized in the European Union, where vegetal carbon (biochar) has been authorized as a feed material since 2013 (European Commission 2013). The lack of approval in the U.S. may be due to variability in production, from pyrolysis parameters to substrate, which complicates establishing consistent safety and quality standards.

Feeding biochar to cattle may have benefits beyond soil health. It has been suggested that incorporating biochar in cattle diets could reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by mitigating methane production within the rumen and decreasing release from manure (Romero et al. 2022). Most studies investigating biochar supplementation in beef cattle have been conducted in feedlot settings and have primarily focused on methane or carbon dioxide emissions rather than feed intake or digestibility under grazing conditions (Terry et al. 2020; Sperber et al. 2022).

The limited body of literature investigating the effects of biochar on diet utilization in cattle is further constrained by a lack of graded, intermediate dietary inclusion rates; for example, Winders et al. (2019) evaluated biochar at 0.8 and 3% of dietary dry matter (DM) and Leng et al. (2012) investigated relatively low inclusion rates (0 and 0.6%). Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the dose-response effects of graded levels of biochar (ie 0, 0.8, 1.6, and 2.4% of forage intake) on diet utilization and fermentation in beef steers consuming forage. We hypothesized that cattle would readily consume biochar in conjunction with their main forage diet and that intake and digestibility would not be significantly affected by dietary inclusion of biochar.

Materials and methods

Experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Texas State University (#10140). Four ruminally cannulated Angus beef steers (595 ± 30 kg initial BW) were used in a 4 × 4 Latin square design to evaluate intake and digestibility in response to increasing biochar inclusion rates. Biochar was commercially sourced from Biochar Now (Berthoud, CO, USA) and was pine-based; chemical and physical properties are detailed in Table 1. Briefly, biochar contained 84.8% organic carbon (dry mass basis; determined via dry combustion elemental analysis) and particle size was mainly 1 to 2 mm (52% of particles). Bulk density was 205 kg/m³ and BET surface area was 293 m2/g.

Steers were housed in a partially enclosed barn within individual stalls and provided ad libitum access to water and a trace mineral block. Wheat straw (82% neutral detergent fiber [NDF], 4.8% crude protein [CP]) was offered daily at 0600 h at 130% of the previous 3-d average intake (Table 2) and cottonseed meal (CSM) was supplemented daily at 125 mg N/kg BW to ensure N was not limiting to ruminal microbes (Drewery 2012) and as a carrier for biochar.

The experiment consisted of four 14-d periods, with treatments randomly assigned to individual steers within each period. Biochar was mixed with CSM and provided at one of four inclusion levels as a percentage of the previous 3-d forage intake: 0% (CON), 0.8% (LO), 1.6% (MED), or 2.4% (HI). To ensure complete consumption, any CSM/biochar mixture remaining in the pan after at least 30 mins of animal exposure was placed directly into the rumen via the cannula. Each experimental period included 8 days for treatment adaptation, 5 days for measures of intake and digestibility, and 1 day for measures of rumen fermentation.

Intake and digestion were evaluated through diet samples (ie hay, CSM, biochar) collected on days 9 to 12 and hay refusals (orts) collected on days 10 to 13. Fecal grab samples were obtained daily from days 10 to 13 with sampling time increasing by 2 h each day to achieve collection every 2 h over a 24-h period. Fecal samples were immediately frozen at −20°C.

On day 14, ruminal fermentation was characterized. Samples were collected via suction strainer every 4 h from four locations within the rumen to account for spatial variability. At the time of collection, ruminal pH was measured with a portable pH meter fitted with a combined electrode. Subsamples of fluid were collected and frozen at −20°C for subsequent volatile fatty acid (VFA) and ammonia N analysis. Before freezing, rumen fluid (8 mL) was combined with 2 mL of 25% m-phosphoric acid for VFA analysis and 9 mL of rumen fluid was combined with 1 mL of 1 N HCl for ammonia N determination.

Hay, ort, and fecal samples were dried in a forced air oven at 55°C for 96 h, allowed to air equilibrate for 24 h, and weighed to determine partial DM. Diet samples were composited across day within period and supplement, ort, and fecal samples were composited by steer across day within period. Composited samples were ground with a Wiley Mill to pass a 1-mm screen. Samples were then dried at 105°C for 24 h for DM determination followed by OM which was the loss in dry weight after combustion for 8 h at 450°C. Nitrogen was measured through Dumas combustion and CP was calculated as N × 6.25. Neutral detergent fiber and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were performed non-sequentially using an Ankom Fiber analyzer with sodium sulfite and amylase omitted and without correction for residual ash.

Acid detergent insoluble ash (ADIA), an internal marker, was used to estimate fecal production for digestibility calculations; ADIA was analyzed by combusting ADF residues for 8 h at 450°C. Because biochar is not physically amenable to ADF extraction using the Ankom system (ie particle size is too small after grinding), ADIA was not measured. As biochar represented ≤ 2.2% of dietary OM intake and the ADIA component could not be directly quantified, its contribution to dietary intake was excluded from marker calculations to avoid disproportionate influence of an analytically uncertain component.

Rumen samples were thawed and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 mins and VFA analysis was conducted according to Goestch and Galyean (1983) using an Agilent 7890A gas chromatograph (Agilent, Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) equipped with 30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm (i.d.) column (Alltech, Deerfield, IL) with an initial column temperature of 80°C held for 1 min, ramped to 185°C at 15°C/min, then held for 2 minutes. Injector and detector temperatures were 250°C. The column flow rate was 2 mL/min, and H2 was the carrier gas. To determine ammonia-N, rumen fluid samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 mins and colorimetric procedures were conducted using a UV-vis (Broderick and Kang 1980).

Calculations and statistical analysis

Daily fecal production was estimated as: kg = [DMI × (ADIAd)]/(ADIAf) where DMI = DM intake, ADIAd = dietary ADIA concentration (% DM), and ADIAf = fecal ADIA concentration (%DM). Digestibility was calculated as: [1 - (output of nutrient/intake of nutrient) × 100].

Data for intake and digestion were analyzed using the MIXED procedure of SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). The statistical model included fixed effects of treatment and period, with steer as a random effect. The compound symmetry covariance structure was specified based on the Bayesian Information Criterion. Analysis of rumen fermentation variables was conducted using the MIXED procedure of SAS. The model included fixed effects of treatment, period, hour, and treatment × hour with steer as a random term. Hour was the repeated term, with treatment × steer as the subject. Calculation of treatment means was performed with the LSMEANS option and separated using linear and quadratic polynomial orthogonal contrasts for graded levels of biochar. A planned contrast was also used to compare the control treatment with the average of all biochar treatments.

Results

There was a tendency for forage OM intake (FOMI) to respond linearly (P = 0.08) to increasing biochar dose such that FOMI was highest for CON (5.23 kg/d) then decreased and plateaued for MED (4.52 kg/d) and HI (4.54 kg/d; Table 3). There was not a difference in FOMI when contrasting CON versus averaged biochar treatments (P = 0.12). Per experimental design, supplement OM intake (SOMI), which was CSM, was not linearly or quadratically affected by treatment (P ≥ 0.32) and was not different between CON versus biochar treatments (P = 0.85), averaging 0.93 kg/d. Also per design, biochar OM intake (BOMI) increased linearly (P ≤ 0.01) from CON to HI and was different between CON and biochar treatments (P < 0.01). Steers receiving the LO treatment consumed an average of 0.04 kg (41.0 g), the MED treatment consumed an average of 0.09 kg (86.4 g), and the HI treatment consumed an average of 0.12 kg (124.7 g) of biochar OM per day.

Discussion

This study evaluated an increasing dietary inclusion rate of biochar, a soil amendment currently receiving significant research interest, in beef steers consuming forage. Despite its demonstrated potential, biochar has not been widely adopted in large-scale agricultural operations, perhaps because its “conditioning” and land-spreading can be cost-prohibitive and labor-intensive. Thus, practical application of biochar has been restricted to small-scale, intensively managed systems.

There is potential to utilize beef cattle as both a conditioning and delivery mechanism for biochar, thereby enhancing the ease of use and, perhaps, broader adoption of biochar across agricultural operations. Therefore, cattle-mediated excretion of microbially enriched biochar could represent a practical, scalable pathway for integrating biochar into pasture- and rangeland-based agricultural systems. While biochar-enriched feedlot manure could be applied to soil through conventional manure management practices, confined systems require additional handling and land application processes compared to grazing systems where biochar-enriched manure could be directly deposited onto pasture during grazing. This illustrates the potential for biochar to be incorporated into grazing beef systems to promote circularity and sustainable agricultural production.

While biochar passes through the gastrointestinal tract mostly intact (Joseph et al. 2015), there remains potential that it could impact the animal and diminish performance. Further, biochar is not an FDA-approved feed for cattle and research is needed to ensure its safe integration into feed and food systems. The effects of biochar on beef cattle have been investigated in other studies, albeit at limited inclusion rates. Some of this previous research has focused on growing and finishing diets (Winders et al. 2019) but, in grazing cattle, diet utilization has not been evaluated (Damiran et al. 2024) and/or graded dietary inclusion rates have not been investigated to evaluate the potential for thresholds beyond which performance would be expected to diminish. Therefore, this study aimed to address prevailing research gaps by evaluating the effects of three levels of biochar (0, 0.8, 1.6, and 2.4% of forage intake) on diet utilization in beef steers consuming forage.

In the present study, intake was not statistically affected by dietary biochar, although trends were observed. More specifically, we observed numerical depressions in forage intake for the moderate (1.6%) and high levels (2.4%) of biochar inclusion. As forage was the basal diet–representing approximately 84% of DM intake–these findings may have practical relevance and suggest that biochar exceeding 0.8% of forage intake may negatively affect intake. We also observed a tendency for depressed TDOMI associated with increasing levels of biochar supplementation. Due to our reliance on dietary ADIA and analytical limitations associated with the analysis of biochar ADIA, there is potential we over-estimated digestibility and the depression in TDOMI is more marked than presented here.

Our findings conflict with previous research which evaluated biochar at 0, 0.8 and 3.0% of diet DM in growing beef steers and did not report changes in intake (Winders et al. 2019). Further, Leng et al. (2012) evaluated biochar at 0.62% of diet DM to cattle consuming cassava root chips and foliage and did not observe effects on OM intake. In lactating dairy cows consuming a forage-based ration, DM intake was also not affected by biochar provided at 0.9% of diet DM (234 g/d; (Dittmann et al. 2024)).

The differences between our findings and others with regards to intake patterns may lie in the basal diet as we utilized low-quality forage containing 4.8% CP and 82% NDF. While we provided supplemental protein to ensure dietary N was adequate to support fermentation, physical and chemical characteristics of the forage might have been exacerbated by the inclusion of a low density, highly porous feed additive that is not ruminally degradable (ie biochar). This likely resulted in high bulk density and rumen fill, decreasing voluntary intake which was reflected as numerically depressed forage intake at higher rates of biochar supplementation. At this stage, this is a hypothesis which should be tested through follow-up work that utilizes high-quality forage less likely to compound with characteristics of biochar that may limit intake through rumen fill. More specifically, passage rate should be directly measured utilizing external markers, which could have enhanced the current work and confirmed this hypothesis.

It is also possible that, due to the strong absorptive capacity of biochar (Moreira et al. 2017), including of ammonium and nitrate (Fidel et al. 2018), it could bind dietary components and/or fermentative products that could reduce ruminally available N or energy and ultimately stymie microbial activity or animal performance. This has previously been posited in research with monogastric animals where it was theorized that high dietary levels of charcoal may interfere with the absorption of vitamins, lipids, and enzymes (Plank et al. 1990; Kutlu et al. 2001). Although not significant, we observed total VFA were the same between steers not receiving biochar and those receiving the highest level but increased at the low (0.8%) and moderate (1.6%) rates of dietary biochar inclusion. However, ruminal ammonia N concentrations were numerically increased from control (0% biochar) to steers receiving the highest level of biochar (2.4%). Therefore, it is unlikely that the absorptive capacity of biochar translated to negative effects in terms of the ability of rumen microbes to access dietary components and/or for fermentative products to be created then absorbed across the rumen epithelium. These findings should be considered carefully for future research that evaluates higher dietary inclusion rates of biochar and confirms our digestibility findings with the use of total fecal collections or external markers to estimate fecal production and, thus, digestibility.

In our study, biochar was delivered to steers blended with CSM, a regionally significant protein supplement, as a carrier ingredient provided at 125 mg N/kg BW; in cases of incomplete consumption after 30 mins, the biochar/CSM blends were directly infused into the rumen. While we did not measure biochar/CSM refusals, we also did not anecdotally observe repeated issues with steers refusing the blends, except during the first period with the steer receiving the highest dose of biochar (2.4% of forage intake) which we ruminally infused. Previous research has established that consumption of novel feeds generally increases and stabilizes over the first few days of introduction (Drewery et al. 2021; Tasci et al. 2024). Strategies to deliver supplemental biochar to grazing cattle should be investigated with attention to longitudinal animal acceptance responses.

As discussed above, we did not observe significant effects of dietary inclusion of biochar on total tract digestion, although there were tendencies for decreased DMD and OMD in accordance with biochar supplementation. Notably, this decrease appeared to follow a threshold-type pattern rather than a dose dependent decline, suggesting digestibility may be affected when biochar is included in the diet, regardless of the inclusion rate. In contrast, Winders et al. (2019) reported quadratic trends for total tract digestibility in crossbred steers consuming a growing ration and supplemented graded doses of biochar (0, 0.8, 3.0% of dietary DM) such that the 0.8% dose optimized digestibility; there was not a response for digestibility in finishing steers. The growing ration utilized by Winders et al. (2019) included dietary components of corn silage (30%), wet distillers grains plus solubles (22%), brome hay (21%), and wheat straw (20%) with a corn-based supplement provided at 7% of diet DM; perhaps diet differences drove differences between our findings and theirs or there may be differences between physical and chemical properties (eg surface area, particle size) of the biochar itself that underlie these observations. The organic carbon content of biochar (84.8% of dry mass) used in the present trial was consistent with the carbon content typical of wood-derived biochar, but properties are not uniformly and consistently reported across various research studies, complicating inter-study comparisons.

It must be noted that, as stated in the Methods, the dietary ADIA contribution of biochar was assumed to be 0%. We originally estimated biochar as 100% ADIA which substantially altered digestibility estimates without corresponding changes in ruminal fermentation parameters, suggesting that such assumptions likely overestimated marker contribution and introduced error into our calculations. Therefore, given the low dietary inclusion of biochar and uncertainty regarding its analytical recovery as ADIA, the ADIA component of biochar was excluded from marker calculations, introducing the potential of overestimating digestibility of steers consuming biochar treatments, although any error introduced would be far lower than assuming biochar is 100% ADIA given the relatively small amount of biochar that was consumed by steers in this trial (≤2.2% dietary OM).

This analytical limitation was not realized a priori as the physical composition of biochar is not a powder, although it degrades into a small particle size when physical pressure is applied. This limitation is not anticipated to have affected our forage intake and fermentation outcomes, but might have impacted our ability to detect dose responses in digestibility such that increasing doses of dietary biochar may be associated with over-estimates of digestibility. To address this, we opted for a pooled statistical comparison of biochar versus the control treatment (ie no biochar was supplemented) instead of individual comparisons, to determine the effect of dietary biochar, regardless of dose. Future research should employ the use of external markers and/or total fecal collections to overcome the challenges of analyzing ADIA in biochar, especially when higher dietary inclusion rates are utilized.

There is no direct evidence linking biochar consumption by grazing ruminants to pasture-scale soil restoration via fecal deposition. However, biochar is known to persist in soil for long durations (Kuzyakov et al. 2014), suggesting fecal deposition could contribute to cumulative soil benefits over time. The frequency and duration of feeding needed to achieve measurable pasture-level effects remain unclear and likely depend on factors such as stocking density and grazing patterns, representing an important direction for systems-level research. If future research demonstrates that biochar does not need to be fed for long periods or over multiple seasons, perhaps the numerical depressions we observed in intake and digestibility responses would not have long-term or sustaining relevance for producers.

Conclusion

This study, which characterized dose responses of forage utilization and rumen fermentation parameters in beef steers consuming low-quality forage (wheat straw) and supplemented with graded levels of biochar ranging from 0% to 3% of forage intake, indicates that biochar exceeding 0.8% inclusion rate may negatively affect intake. Biochar did not significantly affect fermentation parameters under the conditions of this study; however, given the limited power of single-day rumen sampling, smaller but biologically relevant shifts may have gone undetected, and further research is needed to assess the effects on supplementation on rumen microbial communities and activities. Other research is suggested throughout the discussion, using the current data as the foundation upon which to continue to develop this repository of literature. Our data may not be generalizable to all grazing systems, and other forages should be assessed utilizing a similar experimental design as employed here.

Study limitations (ie design and analytical) include the inability to directly quantify the ADIA component of biochar. Based on its high carbon content (84.8%), low H:C ratio (0.42), measurable ash fraction (3.1% to 3.8%), and extensive surface area (293 m²/g), the biochar used in this study contains highly recalcitrant, insoluble material that behaves functionally as an indigestible residue. Total fecal collections or external markers are recommended for future biochar feeding trials to avoid this challenge.
