CD28-ICOS costimulation enhances the therapeutic efficacy of L1CAM-targeted CAR-T cells against ovarian and gastric cancers
Authors: Xiumei Che, Un-Jung Yun, Junsub Lee, Hyun-Hee Koh, Ju-Yeon Lee, Jungmin Jeon, Subin So, Youngyoub Kim, Jin-A Chae, Xianglan Zhang, Hyoyoung Kim, Jiwoo Hwang, Eunhyang Park, Eun Ji Nam, Gun Min Kim, Minkyu Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvarian and gastric cancers are associated with poor prognosis and derive limited benefit from current therapies, highlighting the need for novel immunotherapeutic approaches. The L1 cell adhesion molecule (L1CAM) is aberrantly expressed in several types of solid tumors and is associated with aggressive clinical behavior. This study aimed to develop and characterize optimized L1CAM-targeted chimeric antigen receptor-T (CAR-T) cells with improved efficacy and safety, using patient-derived preclinical models of ovarian and gastric cancer.MethodsL1CAM expression was assessed in tumor tissues from patients with ovarian cancer (OC;n=227) and gastric cancer (GC;n=132), followed by an analysis of its clinical impact. A panel of L1CAM-targeted CAR constructs incorporating distinct hinge and co-stimulatory domains was systematically evaluated. Functional activity was assessed by cytotoxicity and cytokine release assays using cancer cell lines and patient-derived tumor cells. Antitumor efficacy, persistence, and safety were evaluated in xenograft and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, including NanoLuc-based in vivo tracking of CAR-T cell distribution.ResultsHigh L1CAM expression correlated with aggressive clinicopathological features and adverse prognosis in patients with OC. Among the constructs tested, CAR-T cells incorporating an IgD/IgG1 hybrid hinge and dual CD28-ICOS co-stimulatory domains demonstrated the most potent antigen-specific activity. These cells exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity, robust interferon-γ production, and sustained functional activity. In vivo, optimized L1CAM CAR-T cells achieved complete tumor regression in OC PDX models and significant tumor suppression in GC models, with durable tumor control and CAR-T persistence. Intraperitoneal delivery further improved therapeutic efficacy and CAR-T cell localization compared with intravenous administration. Importantly, no evidence of neurotoxicity or systemic toxicity was observed, supporting a favorable safety profile.ConclusionsOptimized L1CAM-targeted CAR-T cells demonstrate potent, durable, and antigen-specific antitumor activity with a favorable safety profile in preclinical models of OC and GC. The combination of rational CAR design (CD28-ICOS signaling) and optimized delivery strategy provides a strong rationale for the clinical development of L1CAM-targeted CAR-T therapy, particularly in patients with L1CAM-positive solid tumors.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Chimeric antigen receptor-T (CAR-T) therapy has shown remarkable success in hematological malignancies, but its application to solid tumors remains limited due to challenges such as antigen heterogeneity, tumor microenvironment-associated immunosuppression, and safety concerns. L1 cell adhesion molecule (L1CAM), a cell surface protein overexpressed in aggressive ovarian and gastric cancers, is associated with poor prognosis, yet its therapeutic potential as a CAR-T target has not been systematically validated in clinically relevant models.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study identifies L1CAM as a clinically relevant and actionable target for CAR-T therapy and reports an optimized L1CAM-directed CAR construct incorporating an IgD/IgG1 hybrid hinge and dual CD28-ICOS co-stimulatory domains. This design enhances CAR-T cell activation, persistence, and antitumor efficacy both in vitro and in vivo, particularly in ovarian cancer models. In addition, locoregional delivery significantly improves tumor control and CAR-T cell persistence compared with systemic administration, highlighting the importance of delivery strategy. Importantly, antigen density-dependent CAR activation and the absence of detectable toxicity in vivo suggest a favorable therapeutic window.

Graphical abstract

Background

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has revolutionized the treatment landscape of hematologic malignancies, achieving durable remission in B-cell leukemia and lymphoma through genetic reprogramming of patient-derived T cells to recognize tumor-associated antigens.1–5 However, translating this success to solid tumors remains challenging because of tumor antigen heterogeneity, poor T-cell trafficking, and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).6 7 Overcoming these barriers will require optimized CAR design, improved tumor localization, and rational combination approaches.

Ovarian cancer (OC) remains the most lethal gynecological malignancy and is often diagnosed at advanced stages owing to the absence of early symptoms and reliable screening tools.8 Despite initial responses to cytoreductive surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy, over 80% of patients relapse and develop platinum resistance, leaving limited treatment options.9 Although PARP inhibitors have improved outcomes in BRCA-mutant tumors, treatment options remain limited for BRCA wild-type and platinum-refractory patients, highlighting the need for novel immunotherapies.9 10 Similarly, gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally.8 Although immune checkpoint blockade benefits programmed death-ligand 1-positive and microsatellite instability-high tumors, most patients show limited responses, and recurrence remains common after curative treatment.11 12 These limitations highlight the need for more effective and durable therapeutic strategies.

The L1 cell adhesion molecule (L1CAM, also known as CD171) is a transmembrane glycoprotein of the immunoglobulin superfamily that plays an essential role in neural development. It comprises six immunoglobulin-like domains, five fibronectin repeats, a transmembrane region, and a highly conserved cytoplasmic domain.13 Aberrant L1CAM expression has been reported in several malignancies, including OC and GC, where it is associated with tumor growth, invasion, stemness, and resistance to apoptosis.14–19 Importantly, L1CAM expression in normal adult tissues is relatively restricted, suggesting the potential for a therapeutic window in targeted immunotherapy.20

Here, we report the development and preclinical evaluation of an optimized third-generation L1CAM-targeted CAR-T cell incorporating an IgD/IgG1 hybrid hinge and dual CD28-ICOS costimulatory domains. Using in vitro assays and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, we demonstrate potent antitumor activity against L1CAM-positive ovarian and gastric tumors, supporting its clinical translation.

Methods

Patient samples

Tumor tissues and ascitic fluids were collected from patients with OC (n=227; median age=65) and GC (n=132; median age=55 years; female:male ratio=1:2.2), who underwent treatment at Yonsei Cancer Center (Seoul, Republic of Korea).

Inclusion criteria included availability of fresh tumor or ascitic fluid samples and a histologically confirmed diagnosis of OC or GC. Patients were excluded if they had received chemotherapy or radiotherapy within 2 weeks prior to sample collection or if insufficient tissue volume was obtained. All samples were deidentified and assigned coded identifier before experimental use to protect patient confidentiality.

Cell lines and culture conditions

HEK293, HEK293T, L-Wnt-3A, 293T-HA-Rspo1-Fc, OVCAR3, SKOV3, SNU719, MKN28, SH-SY5Y, SK-Hep2, HeLa, and AGS cell lines were obtained from the ATCC or the Korean Cell Line Bank. SKOV3-FLuc cells were acquired from PerkinElmer and maintained in McCoy’s 5A medium (Gibco; cat. No. 16600082) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco; cat. No. 16000-044) and penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco; cat. No. 15140122). L-Wnt-3A and 293T-HA-Rspo1-Fc cells were cultured to generate conditioned media according to the provider’s instructions. All other cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Welgene; cat. No. LM 011-01) supplemented as described above. Firefly luciferase-F2A-GFP reporters were introduced by lentiviral transduction, and GFP-positive cells were sorted by flow cytometry. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using MycoStrip (InvivoGen; cat. No. REP-MYS-10).

Generation of L1CAM-knockdown cell line

MKN28-FLuc cells were reverse-transfected with L1CAM-targeting siRNA (siL1CAM) or a non-targeting control (Bioneer) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. No. 13778075) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were incubated for 48 hours, and knockdown was confirmed by western blotting. siRNA sequences are listed below.

Human L1CAM siRNA#1:

Sense strand: 5′-CAC ACA ACC UGA CCG AUC U-3′ (dTdT overhang).

Antisense strand: 5′-AGA UCG GUC AGG UUG UGU G-3′ (dTdT overhang).

Human L1CAM siRNA#2:

Sense strand: 5′-CAG AUG ACA UCA GCC UCA A-3′ (dTdT overhang).

Antisense strand: 5′-UUG AGG CUG AUG UCA UCU G-3′ (dTdT overhang).

Patient-derived organoids and cells

Fresh tumor tissues and ascites were mechanically and enzymatically dissociated, filtered through 70 µm strainers, and embedded in Matrigel. Organoids were cultured in custom medium (online supplemental tables S1 and S2), with medium changed every 3 days and passaged every 2–4 weeks. Organoid-derived single cells were isolated using Cultrex Harvesting Solution (R&D Systems; cat. No. 3700-100-01) and TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. No. 12-605-010) and cultured in 2D using diluted organoid medium.

CAR construct design and retrovirus production

Single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) targeting L1CAM were identified by solid-phase panning of a human synthetic phage display library (KBIOHEALTH). Selected clones were validated by ELISA and purified using Strep-Tactin affinity chromatography. CAR constructs with different hinge and costimulatory domains were generated using overlap-extension PCR and cloned into the MT retroviral vector. Retroviruses were produced by transfecting HEK293T cells with plasmids encoding gag-pol, RD114 envelope, and CAR constructs. Supernatants were harvested, filtered (0.45 µm), and quantified by RT-qPCR.

CAR-T cell generation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from healthy donors using Ficoll-Paque gradient centrifugation. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were purified via magnetic bead separation (Miltenyi Biotec) and activated using TransAct or OKT3 in AIM-V medium supplemented with 5% human serum (Sigma-Aldrich; cat. No. 202-IL-500), immune cell serum replacement (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. No. A2596101), and 100 µg/mL recombinant interleukin (IL)-2 (R&D Systems; cat. No. 202-IL-500).

For generation of NanoLuc-expressing CAR-T cells (CAR-T_Nluc), a NanoLuc luciferase reporter was incorporated into the CAR construct to enable in vivo tracking of CAR-T cell distribution and persistence.

Cells were transduced on RetroNectin-coated plates with CAR-expressing retrovirus, including CAR-T_Nluc constructs, and subsequently expanded for 5 days prior to cryopreservation.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were sectioned and stained using the Ventana BenchMark XT system. Anti-L1CAM antibodies were applied at 1:1000 (clone UJ127.11, Sigma; cat. No. L4543) for OC or 1:50 (clone EPR18750, Abcam; cat. No. ab208155) for GC. The staining was scored using the H-score method (maximum=300). Tumors were classified as L1CAM-High (H-score≥10) or L1CAM-Low (H-score<10). Anti-CD3 antibody (Abcam; cat. No. 135372) was diluted 1:50 to detect T cell infiltration in mouse spleens.

Flow cytometry

Cells were stained with primary anti-L1CAM antibody (H8 clone; Helixmith), followed by fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human IgG-PE (20 µg/mL, Southern Biotech, cat. No. 9042–09). For CAR detection and T cell phenotyping, the following antibodies were used: Protein L-PE (1:50; ACROBiosystems; cat. No. RPL-PP2H2), CD3-APC (1:100; BioLegend; cat. No. 300312), CD4-BV421 (1:100; BioLegend; cat. No. 300532), CD8-APC (1:100; BD; cat. No. 560179), CD62L-FITC PE (1:100; BioLegend; cat. No. 304804), and CD45RO-PE (1:100; BioLegend; cat. No. 304206). Data were acquired on a FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Western blotting

Cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (Pierce). A total of 10 µg of protein per lane was separated on 8%–10% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-L1CAM (1:1000; Abcam; cat. No. ab20148) and anti-GAPDH (1:5000; Cell Signaling Technology; cat. No. 2118S). HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:2000; Invitrogen) were used for 1 hour at room temperature. Signals were detected using an ECL substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized on X-ray film (Kodak). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Cytotoxicity assays

Target cells (5×103–5×104) were co-cultured with L1CAM CAR-T cells or untransduced (UT) T cells at varying effector-to-target (E:T) ratios. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release was quantified using the LDH-Glo Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega; cat. No. J2381), and the viability of luciferase-expressing target cells was assessed by measuring bioluminescence after the addition of D-luciferin (Gold Biotechnology; cat. No. LUCK-1G).

Cytokine analysis

Supernatants from in vitro co-cultures were collected after 24 hours and analyzed with the Human Th1/Th2 Cytokine Kit II (BD Biosciences; cat. No. 551809). For in vivo studies, serum levels of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and granzyme B were quantified using Luminex assays (R&D Systems) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Live cell imaging

T cells were labeled with PKH26 dye (Sigma-Aldrich; cat. No. MIDI26) and co-incubated with GFP-expressing target cells at an E:T ratio of 3:1. Fluorescent images were captured at 48 hours using an Olympus IX73 fluorescence microscope.

In vivo tumor models

Female NOD-scid IL2Rγnull (NSG; Jackson Laboratory) or NOD/Shi-scid/IL-2Rγnull (NOG; Saeron Bio) mice aged 6–8 weeks were used in human xenograft experiments unless otherwise specified. All animal experiments were performed under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUCs) of Yonsei University and Helixmith.

For subcutaneous (s.c.) tumor models, SKOV3 cells (3×10⁶) were mixed with Matrigel (1:1, v/v) and injected subcutaneously. Three days later, mice received intravenous (i.v.) injections of CAR-T cells or UT cells (3×10⁷ cells per dose) for three consecutive days.

For intraperitoneal (i.p.) tumor models, SKOV3-FLuc or PDC-FLuc cells (1.5×10⁶–1.5×10⁷) were injected intraperitoneally. CAR-T cells (1.2×10⁶ total T cells; approximately 50.8% CAR-positive) or UT cells (1.2×10⁶) were administered intraperitoneally on day 5.

For route-of-administration studies, SKOV3-FLuc cells (1.5×10⁶) were injected intraperitoneally. CAR-T cells were administered once on day 5 via either the i.p. or i.v. at a low dose (LD; 2×10⁶ total T cells) or a high doses (HD; 5×10⁶ total T cells).

Tumor burden was monitored weekly by caliper measurements (s.c. models) or bioluminescence imaging (BLI; i.p. models) using IVIS SpectrumCT following intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin (150 mg/kg). The primary endpoint was tumor burden assessed by BLI, which was used to determine the sample size for evaluating treatment efficacy. Mice were monitored two times per week for signs of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD). Following tumor inoculation, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups before treatment. All procedures were performed under identical experimental conditions. No additional measures were taken to control potential confounding factors, such as treatment order or cage location. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation during animal experiments or data analysis. Mice were euthanized on body weight loss exceeding 20%, hunched posture, or tumor size exceeding 2000 mm³.

Sample size determination

A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power (V.3.1) to estimate the minimum sample size required per group for both s.c. and i.p. tumor models.

For the s.c. tumor model, pilot data showed mean tumor volumes of 421±53 mm³ for UT and 316±54 mm³ for H8-CAR4 at the study endpoint, corresponding to an effect size (Cohen’s d) of approximately 2.0. Using a two-tailed unpaired t-test (α=0.05, power=0.8), the minimum sample size was four mice per group. To account for potential attrition, five mice per group were included.

For the i.p. tumor model, power analysis was based on pilot BLI data. Mean total photon flux values were 6.0×10⁹ photons/s for UT and 2.5×10⁸ photons/s for CAR-T-treated mice. After log₁₀ transformation (9.78 vs 8.40 log₁₀[photons/s]), the between-group difference was 1.4 log units with a within-group SD of 0.4, corresponding to an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 3.4. Using the same statistical assumptions, the minimum sample size was three mice per group. To ensure adequate statistical power and account for potential attrition, three to five mice were included per group. No animals were excluded from the final analysis based on predefined criteria.

Blood and serum collection

Blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30–60 min, and centrifuged to isolate serum, which was stored at −70°C until analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean±SD for in vitro studies and as mean±SEM for in vivo studies. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s, Tukey’s, or Sidak’s multiple comparisons test, as appropriate. Longitudinal in vivo data were analyzed using a mixed-effects model fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, as appropriate. Bioluminescence imaging data were log10-transformed before statistical analysis when applicable. Continuous clinicopathological variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, whereas categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The assumptions of the statistical tests were assessed based on the distribution and characteristics of the data, and non-parametric tests were applied when appropriate. Graphs and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.0. Significance was defined as *p<0.05; **p<0.01; ***p<0.001; ****p<0.0001.

Results

Patient characteristics according to L1CAM expression in patients with OC and GC

We evaluated L1CAM protein expression in clinical samples from 227 patients with OC and 132 patients with GC using immunohistochemistry (table 1 and online supplemental table S3). L1CAM expression was independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists and categorized as negative, low, or high. Representative images illustrate varying L1CAM staining intensities (figure 2A). To explore the clinicopathological significance of L1CAM in OC patients, we compared the clinical characteristics between L1CAM-low (n=142, 62.6%) and L1CAM-high (n=85, 37.4%) groups (table 1). The median age at diagnosis was similar in the two groups (64 vs 66 years; p=0.07). L1CAM expression was significantly associated with tumor histology (p=0.002). High-grade serous carcinoma was the most common subtype in both groups, with a higher proportion observed in the L1CAM-high group (92.9%). High L1CAM expression was nearly absent among endometrioid and mucinous tumors, whereas it was detected in 41.7% of clear cell carcinomas. No significant differences were observed between the groups in tumor grade (p=0.291), FIGO stage (p=0.102), or type of debulking surgery (p=0.631).

To assess the clinical relevance of L1CAM in GC, we analyzed tumor specimens from 132 patients who were enrolled in the CLASSIC trial and underwent gastrectomy and with/without adjuvant chemotherapy at Yonsei Cancer Center, classifying them into L1CAM-low (H-score<10; n=103; 78.0%) and L1CAM-high (H-score≥10; n=29; 22.0%) groups (online supplemental table S3).21 The median age at diagnosis was similar between the two groups (55 vs 57 years; p=0.25). There was a significant sex-based difference in L1CAM expression: a higher proportion of males were classified as L1CAM-high than females (27.5% vs 9.8%; p=0.024). However, no significant associations were observed between L1CAM expression and the Lauren classification (p=0.427), tumor stage (p=0.134), T stage (p=0.814), or adjuvant chemotherapy (p=0.145). Similarly, the surgical type (subtotal vs total gastrectomy; p=1.0) and recurrence status (p=0.672) did not differ significantly according to L1CAM expression. Notably, L1CAM-high tumors were more frequently associated with nodal involvement (p=0.05).

Prognostic value of L1CAM expression in patients with OC and GC

We performed Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to evaluate the prognostic significance of L1CAM expression in patients with OC and GC. OC patients with high L1CAM expression (n=85) showed significantly shorter overall survival (OS) than those with low L1CAM expression (n=142; p=0.01) (figure 2B). The 5-year survival rate was markedly lower in the L1CAM-high group, and the survival curves continued to diverge over time, suggesting a sustained long-term impact of L1CAM expression on prognosis (figure 2C). These findings indicate that L1CAM may serve as an adverse prognostic biomarker for OC. In contrast, no significant difference in disease-free survival (DFS) was observed between L1CAM-high and L1CAM-low patients with GC (n=29 vs n=103; p=0.70) (figure 2D,E). The survival curves largely overlapped, providing no evidence of an association between L1CAM expression and DFS in this GC cohort.

Development and optimization of L1CAM-specific CAR-T cells with enhanced antitumor activity

To develop an effective L1CAM-targeted CAR-T cell therapy, we first identified a high-affinity anti-L1CAM scFv from a synthetic human phage display library. Four candidate clones were obtained after four rounds of biopanning against recombinant mouse L1CAM-Fc (online supplemental figure S1A). Among these candidates, Clone H8 was selected based on its high binding affinity, with EC50 values of 2 nM (human) and 20 nM (mouse), which was confirmed by ELISA (online supplemental figure S1B). Peptide microarray-based epitope mapping identified a linear H8-binding epitope (LDYSDQG; residues 581–587) within the Ig6 domain of L1CAM (UniProt: P32004), which is conserved between human and murine proteins, consistent with cross-species binding (online supplemental figure S1C).

Since hinge/spacer architecture critically influences CAR performance, including antigen binding, synapse formation, and cell surface expression, we evaluated CAR variants with different hinge lengths: short (IgD hinge), medium (IgD-CH3, IgD-IgG1), and long (IgD-IgG1-CH3) configurations (online supplemental figure S1D and online supplemental table S4). All constructs exhibited antigen-specific cytotoxicity in vitro. However, the medium-length IgD-IgG1 hinge construct (H8-CAR4_4) demonstrated superior tumor control in vivo (online supplemental figure S1E,F). Considering both CAR transduction efficiency and antitumor activity, subsequent optimization of intracellular signaling domains was performed using the H8-CAR4_5 construct as the backbone. The third-generation CAR incorporating both CD28 and ICOS co-stimulation (H8-CAR4_5-28ICOS) consistently achieved superior antitumor efficacy compared with both UT and the second-generation construct (H8-CAR4_5-28) (p<0.01; online supplemental figure S1G–I and online supplemental table S4). Notably, H8-CAR4_5-28ICOS showed the strongest antitumor activity despite not exhibiting the highest CAR transduction efficiency, suggesting that transduction efficiency alone did not account for its enhanced efficacy. The functional potency of the third-generation CAR constructs varied depending on the specific co-stimulatory domain combinations, underscoring the importance of structural optimization. Based on these findings, we established a lead CAR construct consisting of H8 scFv, IgD-IgG1 hinge, and CD28-ICOS co-stimulatory domains (figure 3A). Engineered CAR-T cells exhibited efficient transduction, a balanced CD4/CD8 composition, and a predominantly central memory phenotype (figure 3B).

To benchmark the performance of the H8 CAR design, we performed side-by-side comparisons with a previously reported second-generation CE7-based L1CAM CAR construct. In real-time cytotoxicity assays, H8 CAR-T cells exhibited markedly enhanced tumor cell killing, achieving approximately 95% maximal cytotoxicity, compared with approximately 60% for CE7 CAR-T cells under identical conditions. H8 CAR-T cells also produced substantially higher levels of IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), consistent with stronger CAR-mediated activation. In parallel, systematic optimization of H8-based CAR constructs revealed that CAR designs incorporating CD28 and ICOS co-stimulatory domains exhibited improved functional activity and enhanced tumor suppression compared with second-generation CAR configurations (online supplemental figure S2).

Given the cross-reactivity of H8 with murine L1CAM, functional assays were performed using HEK293T cells expressing human L1CAM (HEK293T-hL1CAM) and HEK293 cells expressing murine L1CAM (HEK293-mL1CAM). CAR-T cells exhibited comparable cytolytic activity and cytokine secretion against both targets, demonstrating that murine L1CAM could activate H8 CAR signaling (online supplemental figure S3).

To evaluate antigen density-dependent activity, we analyzed L1CAM expression across tumor cell lines (online supplemental figure S4A). L1CAM CAR-T cells demonstrated negligible cytotoxicity against HEK293T cells exhibiting low to undetectable L1CAM expression. Conversely, robust cytotoxic activity was observed against L1CAM-expressing SH-SY5Y and HeLa cells, confirming antigen-dependent activation with minimal tonic or antigen-independent cytotoxicity (online supplemental figure S4B).

To assess donor variability and reproducibility, CAR-T cells were generated from nine independent healthy donors, four of whom were selected for functional evaluation using L1CAM-positive SK-Hep2 cells. CAR-T cells from all four evaluated donors exhibited robust cytotoxicity and secreation of IFN-γ and GM-CSF after co-culture with the target cells (online supplemental figure S5A). Cytokine secretion increased with increasing multiplicity of infection (MOI), indicating CAR transduction dependent functional activity (online supplemental figure S5B). Overall, CAR-T cells exhibited comparable activity among the evaluated donors, supporting the reproducibility of the H8 CAR construct across donors (online supplemental figure S5C).

L1CAM-targeted CAR-T cells exhibit potent and antigen-specific cytotoxicity in vitro

We assessed the in vitro efficacy of L1CAM CAR-T cells across a panel of tumor cell lines with varying L1CAM expression. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed L1CAM moderately expressed in SKOV3 (ovarian) as well as MKN28 and AGS (gastric) cells, weakly expressed in OVCAR3, and absent in SNU719 or HEK293T cells, which served as negative controls (figure 3C).

L1CAM CAR-T cells induced robust cytotoxicity against L1CAM-positive cells but not in L1CAM-negative controls, accompanied by increased IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 secretion in a dose-dependent manner (figure 3D–F; online supplemental figure S6A). Similar antigen-dependent activity was observed in GC models (figure 3G–J; online supplemental figure S6B). Knockdown of L1CAM in MKN28 cells markedly reduced CAR-T-mediated cytotoxicity, supporting antigen-specific targeting (figure 3K,L).

These observations are consistent with the concept that CAR-T cell activation typically requires a threshold level of antigen density, allowing discrimination between tumor cells with high antigen expression and normal tissues expressing the target antigen at low physiological levels.

L1CAM CAR-T cells demonstrate potent and antigen-specific cytotoxicity in patient-derived tumor models

To better capture tumor heterogeneity and clinical relevance, we evaluated the efficacy of L1CAM CAR-T cells using patient-derived tumor cells (PDCs), which retain key molecular and phenotypic features of primary tumors. The PDCs used in this study were established from clinical specimens obtained from patients with OC or GC (online supplemental table S5). L1CAM expression profiling identified MK-OC-A03 (moderate) and EJ-OC-119 (negative) as representative OC models (figure 4A,B). L1CAM CAR-T cells induced robust cytotoxicity in L1CAM-positive MK-OC-A03 cells but showed minimal activity against EJ-OC-119, accompanied by increased IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 secretion (figure 4C,D; online supplemental figure S6C). No cytotoxicity or cytokine production was observed in the UT group. Live-cell imaging confirmed selective lysis of L1CAM-positive MK-OC-A03 cells (figure 4E). Cytotoxicity increased in a dose-dependent manner, further supporting antigen-specific activity (figure 4F).

We extended this analysis to GC PDCs, which were categorized as L1CAM-negative (MK-GC-02), low (MK-GC-17), and moderate (MK-GC-10) in expression based on immunoblotting and flow cytometry analysis (figure 5A,B). L1CAM CAR-T cells induce robust tumor regression in an intraperitoneal OC xenograft model

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of L1CAM-targeted CAR-T cells, we established a peritoneal xenograft model by injecting SKOV3-FLuc cells into female NSG mice, thereby modeling peritoneal dissemination, a common metastatic pattern of advanced OC. Five days post-tumor inoculation, mice were randomized to receive a low dose (1.2×10⁶ total T cells) of L1CAM CAR-T cells or UT cells via i.p. injection (figure 6A). Tumor burden, body weight, and clinical signs of GvHD were monitored longitudinally. CAR-T treatment resulted in near-complete tumor clearance, whereas UT and No T groups showed progressive tumor growth (figure 6B,C). CAR-T–treated mice showed no weight loss or GvHD-like clinical signs (figure 6D).

To further evaluate the safety profile of L1CAM CAR-T therapy, we performed serum biochemical and histopathological analysis. CAR-T-treated mice showed stable body weight and no abnormalities in renal function (blood urea nitrogen and creatinine) and hepatic function (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and alkaline phosphatase) (online supplemental figure S7A–E). Mild inflammatory changes were observed in UT-treated mice but not in CAR-T-treated mice (online supplemental table S6). No overt histopathological abnormalities were detected in major organs, including the brain (online supplemental figure S7F). These results demonstrate a favorable safety profile of L1CAM CAR-T cells.

To assess the impact of the delivery route, NanoLuc-labeled CAR-T cells were administered via i.p. or i.v. routes. Intraperitoneal delivery induced dose-dependent tumor suppression, with the high dose showing the strongest effect (online supplemental figure S8A,B). In contrast, intravenous delivery showed minimal tumor control, comparable to control groups (online supplemental figure S8C,D). Total NanoLuc flux was significantly higher in the high-dose i.p. administration group than in the i.v. administration group on day 5. Although the intravenous signal subsequently increased and was highest on day 19, this delayed increase did not result in effective tumor control, suggesting that greater early CAR-T cell exposure may contribute to the sperior efficacy of i.p. delivery (online supplemental figure S8E). Collectively, these results indicate that i.p. delivery enhances both antitumor efficacy and produce a stronger early CAR-T cell signal in this peritoneal tumor model, whereas systemic i.v. administration results in delayed CAR-T accumulation and limited tumor control.

L1CAM CAR-T cells induce durable tumor remission in OC and GC PDX models

We evaluated L1CAM CAR-T therapy in PDX models to better recapitulate tumor heterogeneity and the native TME. In the OC model, NOG mice were intraperitoneally inoculated with L1CAM-positive MK-OC-A03-FLuc cells and treated 5 days later with a single low dose of CAR-T cells or UT cells (1.2×106 per mouse) (figure 7A). CAR-T treatment resulted in complete tumor clearance within 1 week, whereas UT and No T groups showed progressive tumor growth (figure 7B,C). Body weight remained stable in the CAR-T group, whereas UT-treated mice experienced transient weight loss without lasting toxicity (figure 7D). Immunohistochemistry showed robust T cell infiltration in the spleen of CAR-T-treated mice, suggestive of systemic expansion and persistence (figure 7E). No signs of on-target or off-tumor toxicity were observed across the treatment groups (online supplemental figure S9), indicating a favorable safety profile.

We established a GC PDX model using MK-GC-17-FLuc cells to evaluate the broad applicability of this therapy. While tumor progression was observed in the UT and No T groups, treatment with L1CAM CAR-T cells led to tumor suppression in three out of five mice, without inducing weight loss or clinical toxicity (online supplemental figure S10A–E). Systemic IFN-γ levels increased on day 7, consistent with CAR-T cell activation (online supplemental figure S10F). Immunohistochemistry revealed T cell accumulation in the periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths of the spleen in the most responsive mouse (No. 3) on day 85 (online supplemental figure S10G), and CAR-T cells were still detectable in another mouse (No. 2) more than 150 days post-CAR-T administration, indicating long-term persistence. Interestingly, therapeutic efficacy appeared to correlate with tumor L1CAM expression levels, as the relatively low expression in MK-GC-17 tumors may have limited the extent of tumor eradication (online supplemental figure S10H).

Discussion

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of L1CAM as a CAR-T cell target, we first comprehensively analyzed its expression and clinical relevance in OC and GC. In OC, high L1CAM expression was observed in 37.4% of cases and was significantly associated with shorter OS, supporting its role as a negative prognostic biomarker. In contrast, although 22% of GC tumors exhibited high L1CAM expression, no survival correlation was identified. The lack of prognostic significance in the GC cohort may be attributable to the limited sample size and long-term storage of archived tissue specimens, which could have affected antigen preservation and detection.22 These findings support L1CAM as a rational target for immunotherapy in epithelial tumors, especially in OC. Interestingly, a statistical association between sex and L1CAM expression was observed in our cohort. However, this finding has not been consistently reported and may reflect cohort imbalance rather than a biologically meaningful difference. Further studies using larger independent cohorts are warranted to clarify whether sex-related factors influence L1CAM expression in GC.

Several CAR-T strategies targeting L1CAM (CD171) have been explored in solid tumors. Although promising preclinical efficacy has been reported, clinical translation remains challenging due to heterogeneous antigen expression, limited CAR-T cell persistence, and the immunosuppressive TME, as well as the risk of on-target/off-tumor toxicity. To address these limitations, we developed an optimized L1CAM-specific CAR construct (H8-28ICOS). This design enhanced functional activity compared with second-generation CAR-T cells and improved therapeutic efficiency under higher tumor burden and lower dosing conditions. In comparison with previously reported CE7-based CAR-T cells, the H8-28ICOS CAR-T cells exhibited improved functional potency, as demonstrated by enhanced cytotoxicity and cytokine production under matched experimental conditions. Compared with other targets such as B7-H3, MUC16, and TAG72, L1CAM offers broader applicability across multiple epithelial malignancies. CAR-NK therapy represents an alternative strategy but remains limited by reduced in vivo persistence and tumor infiltration capacity.23–25 Collectively, these findings support the clinical translation of L1CAM-targeted CAR-T therapy.

To enhance translational relevance, we employed PDX models, which more faithfully recapitulate tumor heterogeneity.26–28 L1CAM CAR-T cells demonstrated potent cytotoxicity against L1CAM-positive PDCs and achieved complete tumor clearance in OC PDX models following a single low-dose treatment. These results highlight the potency of the optimized CAR design in clinically relevant settings. Importantly, these models represented diverse histologic subtypes of OC and GC. Therapeutic responses in GC models were more heterogeneous, likely reflecting lower antigen density and spatial heterogeneity.29 30 The route of CAR-T cell delivery critically influenced efficacy. Intraperitoneal administration resulted in superior tumor control and sustained CAR-T persistence compared with intravenous delivery, consistent with improved local accumulation and reduced trafficking barriers. These findings support locoregional delivery as a strategy to enhance CAR-T efficacy in peritoneal malignancies.

Safety remains a key consideration. Consistent with the restricted expression of L1CAM in normal tissues, our data demonstrate antigen density-dependent activation, with strong activity in L1CAM-positive tumors and minimal activation in low-expression settings. In vivo studies showed no evidence of systemic toxicity or organ damage, supporting a favorable therapeutic window. Several limitations should be noted. CAR-T activity was not evaluated in immunocompetent models, limiting assessment of long-term immune interactions and toxicity. Although PDX models improve clinical relevance, they do not fully recapitulate host immunity. In addition, although residual bioluminescence signals were observed in a subset of treated mice, the limited tumor burden at the study endpoint precluded further characterization of residual tumor cells. Future studies should include analyses at intermediate stages of treatment, when sufficient residual tumor tissue remains available, to characterize residual tumor cells, including L1CAM expression and cellular phenotype, and to better understand mechanisms of treatment resistance and antigen escape.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that optimization of CAR structure and delivery strategy significantly enhances therapeutic efficacy against L1CAM-positive tumors. The combination of dual CD28-ICOS signaling and locoregional administration may represent a promising strategy for improving CAR-T therapy in solid tumors and supports future clinical translation.
