Interpretable Artificial Intelligence for Topological Photonics
Ali Ghorashi ★, Sachin Vaidya ★, Ziming Liu, Charlotte Loh, Thomas Christensen, Max Tegmark, Marin Soljačić

Abstract
Topological photonic crystals (PhCs) offer robust, disorder-resistant modes engendered by nontrivial band symmetries, but designing PhCs with prescribed topological band properties remains a challenge due to the complexity involved in mapping the continuous real-space design landscape of photonic crystals to the discrete output space of band topology. Here, we introduce a new approach to address this problem, employing Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (KANs) to predict and inversely design the band symmetries of two-dimensional PhCs with two-fold rotational ( C 2 C_{2} ) symmetry. We show that a single-hidden-layer KAN, trained on a dataset of C 2 C_{2} -symmetric unit cells, achieves 99% accuracy in classifying the symmetry eigenvalues of the lowest transverse-magnetic band. For interpretability, we use symbolic regression to extract eight algebraic formulas that characterize the band symmetry classes in terms of the Fourier components of the dielectric function. These formulas not only retain the full predictive power of the network but also enable deterministic inverse design. Applying them, we generate 2,000 photonic crystals with target band symmetries, achieving over 92% accuracy even for high-contrast, experimentally realizable structures beyond the training domain.

Artificial intelligence for photonic topology.
To date, machine learning has achieved significant progress in photonics and the study of topological materials
Peurifoy et al. 2018
;
Claussen et al. 2020
. In this section, we briefly review some of the major advances to contextualize our work.

Most efforts to predict photonic crystal band properties have focused on band dispersions, using images of the periodic dielectric function as inputs. Motivated by the success of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in image recognition
Rawat and Wang 2017
;
Guo et al. 2017
, CNNs have been effectively applied to predict band structures of two-dimensional photonic crystals
Christensen et al. 2020
. In contrast, our approach—detailed in the following sections—uses the Fourier components of the dielectric function as input parameters instead of image representations. This choice is motivated by two key factors. First, it significantly enhances interpretability: a model based on a small number of Fourier components is far easier to analyze than one which uses 1,024 image pixels (e.g., a
32\times 32
input grid). Second, from a physical standpoint, the perturbation theory suggests that the lowest photonic bands are governed primarily by the lowest Fourier components
Kittel and McEuen 2018
;
Kaxiras and Joannopoulos 2019
, indicating that the primary physical information necessary for the analysis of both band dispersion and band symmetry is contained in this representation, despite its dimensional sparsity.

In the context of topological materials, CNNs have also been employed to predict topological invariants, such as winding numbers in one-dimensional models
Zhang et al. 2018
, with high accuracy. Notably, Zhang et al
Zhang et al. 2018
showed
through a subsequent analysis of the trained networks’ weights that they had implicitly learned meaningful physical laws. While instructive, this approach relies on interpreting the network’s weights, which is not always feasible—especially when the underlying physics is unknown. Our work instead prioritizes interpretability through symbolic regression, which offers a more direct and generally applicable path to analytical understanding.

In terms of inverse design, various neural network architectures have been proposed to engineer photonic and topological properties. For photonic crystals, generative adversarial networks (GANs) have been successfully employed to optimize bandgaps
Christensen et al. 2020
, while other photonics applications have included neural network-driven design of metasurface-enabled invisibility cloaks
Zhen et al. 2021
. In the topological domain, inverse design has been used to tailor edge state dispersions, for instance in the Aubry–André model
Pilozzi et al. 2018
. A central challenge in inverse design is the many-to-one problem
Deng et al. 2024
, where multiple input structures yield the same output properties. To address this, tandem networks have been developed to constrain the solution space and improve inversion fidelity
Chen et al. 2022
;
Jiang et al. 2021
;
Gupta et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2025
. In our work, we circumvent the many-to-one problem by employing gradient descent to explore the input space, identifying multiple local minima that correspond to distinct photonic crystals within the desired target class.

Kolmogorov–Arnold networks for photonic topology.
Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs)
Liu et al. 2024
are a class of neural networks inspired by the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem, in contrast to traditional multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) which draw from the universal approximation theorem. KANs fundamentally differ by assigning learnable one-dimensional functions, parametrized as splines, to the edges rather than fixed activation functions to the nodes. This structure eliminates the need for conventional linear weight matrices, replacing them with nonlinear, trainable activation functions. KANs are further regularized through sparsity-promoting training and pruning, resulting in compact models that lend themselves to symbolic regression. This enables the extraction of interpretable, algebraic relationships between inputs and outputs, making KANs particularly suited for discovering underlying physical laws from scientific data.

To create a machine learning model that provides an interpretable physical law governing band symmetries in
C_{2}
symmetric photonic crystals, we use KANs on our PhC dataset. As input parameters, we use the Fourier components of the dielectric function. We find that a one-hidden-layer KAN can predict the symmetry of the lowest transverse-magnetic (TM) polarized band with more than 99% accuracy, even when pruned to only three input parameters and four nodes in the hidden layer (
Fig.

2
). We note that the network discovers that the topology of the lowest band depends only on the lowest three Fourier components—those corresponding to the smallest reciprocal lattice vectors, which is to be expected on perturbation theory arguments (see SI).

Next, we use symbolic regression
Liu et al. 2024
to map the neural network to eight formulas,
f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{8}
, one for each symmetry label, which we report in the SI. Since symbolic regression is performed after the pruning of the network, each function,
f_{n}

C_{1,0}
,
C_{0,1}
and
C_{1,1}

\begin{split}\text{argmax}\{f_{1}(C_{1,0},C_{0,1},C_{1,1}),f_{2}(C_{1,0},C_{0,1},C_{1,1}),...,\\
f_{8}(C_{1,0},C_{0,1},C_{1,1})\}\end{split}

(3)

We find that these formulas can be used to predict the symmetry of the lowest band with 99 percent accuracy.

Discussion

We have demonstrated that a Kolmogorov–Arnold network (KAN) with a single hidden layer can predict the band symmetries of transverse-magnetic (TM) polarized modes below the fundamental gap with over 99% accuracy. Using symbolic regression, we have translated the network’s behavior into a concise set of eight analytical formulas, each corresponding to a target symmetry class. These formulas enable the inverse design of band symmetries below the gap, offering an interpretable and efficient alternative to the network itself.

Our findings open several promising directions for future work. A natural extension would be to apply this framework to more complicated two-dimensional plane groups or three-dimensional space groups, particularly those with higher rotational symmetry or additional mirror and non-symmorphic symmetries. Such generalizations would need to address potential challenges related to band connectivity and degeneracies at high-symmetry points, which could either be enforced by-hand or otherwise by extending the classification scheme to account for these additional considerations.

Beyond photonics, it would also be interesting to explore whether KANs can achieve comparable success in predicting structural or electronic properties in materials science. For crystalline materials, a natural analog for the Fourier components of the dielectric function would be the charge density or Kohn-Sham wavefunctions
Kohn and Sham 1965
, provided in a plane-wave basis. For inverse design of new, experimentally realizable materials, however, ensuring the additional constraint of dynamical and thermodynamic stability
Siriwardane et al. 2022
is important. As a result, a KAN-assisted inverse design approach would likely have to be followed by density functional theory calculations to filter out unstable materials
Lyngby and Thygesen 2022
. Although in this work we also employ post-processing in our inverse design workflow to obtain experimentally realizable two-tone photonic crystals, our approach is somewhat different in that no tools beyond the KAN/formulas themselves are used throughout our workflow.
