Fragmented versus traditional resistance training: a randomized controlled trial on muscular strength, endurance, and enjoyment in recreationally trained young adults
Authors: Rongxuan Zhai, Kunnian Yang, Hanya Dai, Yulu Wang, Yushan Jiang, Xudong Wu, Lejun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional resistance training requires prolonged continuous sessions, which may limit participation among time-constrained individuals. Fragmented resistance training (FRT), derived from ‘exercise snacking,’ may offer a more flexible alternative, but comparative evidence with traditional resistance training (TRT) remains limited. This study examined the effects of 8 weeks of FRT and TRT on muscular fitness and exercise enjoyment in 45 recreationally trained male students (18–25 years, ≥6 months training). Participants were randomised to FRT (three daily short bouts, 1×20 squats each), TRT (one continuous session, 3×20 squats), or a time-matched control group. Training was performed three times/week for 8 weeks using Smith-machine squats, with volume matched and intensity progressing from 40% to 55% of 1RM. Outcomes included 1RM strength, isometric squat force, relative muscular endurance (repetitions at 40% of current 1RM), and PACES enjoyment. Both FRT and TRT significantly outperformed the control in 1RM strength (FRT: +20.59 kg [8.69–32.49], p < 0.001; TRT: +28.93 kg [17.05–40.82], p < 0.001), isometric squat force (FRT: +27.89 kgf [12.15–43.64], p < 0.001; TRT: +35.24 kgf [19.48–51.00], p < 0.001) and relative muscular endurance (FRT: +9.97 repetitions [1.68–18.27], p = 0.020; TRT: +14.11 repetitions [5.84–22.38], p < 0.001), with no significant differences between FRT and TRT group (p = 0.164, 0.353 and 0.320). FRT reported significantly higher enjoyment than TRT (p = 0.034). These findings suggest FRT and TRT produce comparable improvements in strength and endurance under volume-matched conditions, both superior to control. Introduction

Resistance training is a widely recommended exercise modality for improving muscular strength, muscular endurance, and skeletal muscle mass, with additional benefits for neuromuscular function, bone health, and metabolic regulation.1,2 Conventional resistance-training programmes involving multiple exercises or major muscle groups are often completed in continuous sessions lasting approximately 30–60 min, including inter-set recovery.3,4 Although not every session requires this duration, lack of time remains a common barrier to regular exercise participation among university students and young working adults with fragmented academic, occupational, and social schedules.5 Distributing resistance exercise across several shorter bouts throughout the day may therefore provide a more flexible alternative. In experimental settings, matching total training volume between fragmented and traditional formats can help isolate the influence of exercise distribution.

The concept of “exercise snacking” refers to brief bouts of structured physical activity performed several times throughout the day.6,7 Emerging evidence suggests that exercise snacking can improve cardiorespiratory fitness, glycemic regulation, and several markers of cardiometabolic health.8 Some studies have also investigated muscular fitness, although evidence regarding muscular endurance remains limited and is largely based on unsupervised, home-based, or mixed-modality interventions. Most previous research has focused on aerobic activities, such as stair climbing and brisk walking.8 Consequently, the chronic effects of distributing resistance-training sets across the day remain unclear, particularly when exercise selection, load, repetitions, and total training volume are controlled.

Fragmenting resistance exercise may alter the physiological conditions under which successive sets are performed. During traditional continuous training, later sets may begin with residual neuromuscular fatigue and metabolic perturbation, which has been suggested to provide an adaptive stimulus to muscle adaptation and thus can improve muscle strength and endurance training gains9, 10, 11, 12. In contrast, separating sets by several hours may permit more complete recovery and better preservation of force and repetition quality, while reducing within-session fatigue and metabolic stress.13 Research examining different loading structures and resistance modalities has shown that changes in eccentric loading, force application, velocity, and repetition-to-repetition characteristics may modify the internal training stimulus even when the overall external workload is similar.14 Thus, volume-equated fragmented and continuous training may expose participants to different acute stimuli, although both formats may provide sufficient mechanical and neuromuscular stimulation to improve maximal strength and relative muscular endurance.15

The temporal distribution of training may also influence psychological responses to exercise. Completing a large training volume in one continuous session may be perceived as physically demanding, monotonous, or difficult to incorporate into a busy schedule. By dividing the same volume into several brief bouts, exercise snacking may reduce the perceived duration and psychological burden of each exercise episode. Shorter bouts may also increase perceived competence because each task appears easier to initiate and complete. In addition, allowing individuals to perform exercise at several convenient times may enhance perceived autonomy and control over training. Greater autonomy, competence, and compatibility with daily schedules may promote more positive affective responses, exercise enjoyment, and willingness to participate in subsequent exercise sessions.16,17 However, fragmented training may also require repeated preparation and access to exercise facilities, meaning that its psychological and practical advantages may depend on the individual and the training environment.

The back squat is a representative multi-joint exercise commonly used in lower-limb resistance training programs. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial aimed to compare the effects of 8 weeks of fragmented squat-based resistance training and traditional squat-based resistance training on muscular strength, muscular endurance, and perceived exercise experience under volume-equated conditions among resistance-trained individuals. We hypothesized that both fragmented and traditional resistance training would improve maximal strength, isometric force, and relative muscular endurance compared with the control condition. We also hypothesized that fragmented training would produce greater exercise enjoyment because of its shorter individual bouts and lower perceived exercise burden, while traditional training would produce greater improvements in relative muscular strength and endurance because of greater fatigue accumulation.

Methods

Overall study design

This study was a three-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial comparing two volume-equated squat-based resistance training interventions with a non-exercise control condition. Participants were randomly allocated to fragmented resistance training (FRT), traditional resistance training (TRT), or a control group (CON). All groups were time-matched with respect to the study duration and assessment schedule to minimize potential bias associated with differences in time commitment and testing exposure. The intervention lasted 8 weeks.

Outcome assessments were conducted at baseline and within 72 h after the final training session. For each assessment time point, participants attended the laboratory on two separate occasions separated by at least 72 h. Muscular strength assessments were performed during the first visit, whereas muscular endurance assessments were conducted during the second visit. Participants were instructed to refrain from alcohol consumption, strenuous physical activity, and sleep deprivation for at least 24 h prior to each testing session. Moreover, the cross-sectional assessment of training enjoyment of the FRT and TRT group were conducted immediately after the final training session of the final week.

Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation throughout data collection and analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji University (Approval No. tjdxsr2025166). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Participants and randomization

Sample size estimation was based on G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) for an ANCOVA model (α = 0.05, power = 0.80, Cohen's f = 0.55), indicating a minimum of 12 participants per group; accounting for approximately 20% dropout, 15 participants were recruited per group (total n = 45).

All participants were male university students recruited from Tongji University through posters and online advertisements. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 18 and 25 years; (2) at least six months of regular resistance training experience, defined as performing lower-body resistance exercises at least twice per week during the previous six months; and (3) body mass index (BMI) between 18 and 24 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria included any diagnosed cardiovascular, metabolic, or respiratory disease, musculoskeletal injury, or other health condition that could contraindicate high-intensity physical activity.

Eligible participants were randomly assigned using a computer-generated randomization sequence (SPSS version 26.0) prepared by an independent researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment or outcome assessment. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened by the supervising investigator immediately prior to the first intervention session.

During the 8-week intervention period, no participants withdrew from the study, and all participants completed the post-intervention assessments. Attendance rates were 92.8% in the FRT group and 91.5% in the TRT group. Baseline participant characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Training intervention

Both training groups completed supervised lower-limb resistance training three times per week on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday for 8 weeks, resulting in a total of 24 training sessions.18 The intervention primarily consisted of barbell back squat exercise. External training volume was matched between groups, with both groups completing a total of 60 repetitions per training day. Training intensity was prescribed relative to individual one-repetition maximum (1RM) values. The initial training load was set at 40% 1RM and progressively increased by 5% every two weeks, reaching 55% 1RM during the final two weeks of the intervention.19 To align with the definition of an ‘exercise snacking’,6,7 the protocol prescribed 20 repetitions per set to ensure that each bout lasted approximately 1 min. Accordingly, the intensity range of 40–55% 1RM was selected to ensure that participants could complete the prescribed 20 repetitions with consistent technique. Session intensity was additionally monitored using the Borg rating of perceived exertion (RPE) scale, with target exertion maintained between 10 and 14 throughout the intervention. No between-group difference in RPE was observed.

All squat exercises were performed using a Smith machine, which was conveniently located on the ground floor of the student dormitory building to minimize the time and travel burden associated with multiple daily visits.20 Movement cadence was standardized using a digital metronome, with participants instructed to perform a 2 s eccentric phase followed by a 2 s concentric phase for each repetition. Participants were required to squat until the thighs were parallel to the ground, which was verified using a goniometer and visual inspection by an experienced researcher, before returning to full knee extension21, 22, 23.

Participants in FRT completed three separated exercise bouts during each training day, with a minimum interval of 1 h between bouts. To accommodate participants' schedules while maintaining laboratory control, FRT participants were asked to attend the laboratory during three designated time windows: morning (9:00–12:00), afternoon (13:00–17:00), and evening (18:00–21:00), with flexibility to choose specific times within each window. All three daily bouts were directly supervised by at least one trained fitness instructor and one medical staff member to ensure correct exercise technique, consistent effort, and participant safety. Each bout consisted of one set of 20 squat repetitions.7,24 Before each bout, participants performed a brief standardized warm-up including light aerobic activity and dynamic stretching.

Participants in TRT completed all training within a single continuous session. Each session consisted of three sets of 20 squat repetitions, with 3 min inter-set rest intervals.25 A standardized warm-up including light aerobic activity and dynamic stretching was performed before training.

Participants in CON were instructed to maintain their usual daily activities. Participants in the FRT and TRT groups were asked not to engage in any additional lower-body resistance training outside the supervised protocol but were allowed to maintain their usual upper-body training. CON participants were permitted to continue their habitual training activities throughout the 8-week intervention period. Consistent with the concept of a time-matched training group, this design provides an estimate of the changes expected among resistance-trained individuals in the absence of a structured intervention, rather than serving as a traditional sedentary no-exercise control.26

All training sessions were supervised by at least two trained staff members to ensure correct exercise technique and participant safety. Training was immediately discontinued if discomfort or pain occurred, and participants were evaluated by a physiotherapist when necessary. No adverse events were reported during the intervention period.

Outcome measures

Muscular strength measures

1RM measures

Before testing, participants completed a standardized warm-up consisting of 5 min of light cycling and dynamic lower-limb stretching, followed by several submaximal squat attempts with progressively increasing loads. The 1RM testing protocol was conducted according to established resistance training guidelines. Briefly, participants performed 5–10 repetitions at 50% of their estimated 1RM, followed by 3–5 repetitions at 70%, and then single attempts at progressively heavier loads until failure, with 3–5 min rest intervals between attempts.

All squat tests were performed using a Smith machine. Participants were instructed to descend until the thighs were parallel to the ground before returning to full knee extension. Squat depth was verified using a goniometer and visual inspection by an experienced investigator to ensure movement consistency across testing sessions. The highest successfully completed load was recorded as the 1RM value.

The 1RM values obtained at each assessment time point were used to determine the relative load applied during the relative muscular endurance assessment, while baseline 1RM values were additionally used to prescribe individualized training loads during the intervention. All 1RM data were included in the final statistical analysis.

Isometric squat test measures

Force output was measured using a wireless traction dynamometer (K-Pull, Kinvent Physio, Montpellier, France).27 During testing, participants assumed a squat position with the thighs parallel to the ground, corresponding to approximately 90° of knee flexion. The dynamometer was connected to a fixed anchor point, and participants were instructed to gradually exert maximal upward force while maintaining the standardized squat position for at least 5 s.28

Before formal testing, participants completed a standardized warm-up and familiarisation procedure. Three maximal trials were performed, with 1–2 min rest intervals provided between trials to minimize fatigue. Strong verbal encouragement was provided throughout testing to ensure maximal effort. All tests were supervised by the same experienced investigator to maintain consistency across sessions. The highest peak force value obtained from the three trials was recorded for subsequent statistical analysis.

Relative muscular endurance measures

Relative muscular endurance was assessed using a continuous squat-to-fatigue test performed on a Smith machine. Participants completed repeated back squats at an external load corresponding to 40% of their 1RM obtained at the corresponding assessment time point.29

All repetitions were performed under standardized tempo control using a digital metronome set at a 2 s eccentric and 2 s concentric phase. Participants were required to descend until the thighs were parallel to the ground, as verified by a goniometer and visual inspection by an experienced investigator, and then return to full knee extension in a continuous manner.

The test was terminated when participants were unable to maintain the prescribed movement cadence or achieve the required squat depth for three consecutive repetitions despite strong verbal encouragement, indicating volitional exhaustion. The total number of successfully completed repetitions was recorded as the measure of relative muscular endurance performance.30

Training enjoyment measures

Training enjoyment was assessed using the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PACES).31 The PACES questionnaire was administered in the FRT and TRT groups during the final training session.

A 7-point Likert-type version of the full PACES questionnaire was used. The scale consists of validated items assessing enjoyment of physical activity, with higher scores indicating greater enjoyment. In the present study, the mean score across all items was calculated and used as the final PACES score for statistical analysis.32

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

For the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PACES), which was administered only to the two training groups at post-intervention, between-group differences were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test, as the data were ordinal in nature (Likert-scale responses). Results are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR) along with the U statistic and z-score.

For all other outcome variables, a one-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used as the primary analytical strategy. For each outcome, the change score (post-intervention minus pre-intervention) was entered as the dependent variable, the corresponding baseline value was entered as a covariate, and group (FRT, TRT, CON) was entered as the fixed factor. The assumptions of ANCOVA were examined, and the robustness of the results was further validated using bootstrap resampling (5000 iterations). Given the well-documented robustness of ANCOVA to mild violations of normality, particularly when group sizes are balanced and variance assumptions are met, we considered the ANCOVA results reliable. Complete details of the assumption checks and bootstrap results are provided in Supplementary Material 1. Results are reported as adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals and Cohen's d as effect size.

The raw data for 1RM strength, isometric squat force, relative muscular endurance, and PACES scores are presented in Supplementary Material 2. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The principal findings were that both FRT and TRT produced greater improvements in maximal strength, isometric squat force, and relative muscular endurance than the control condition. In addition, participants allocated to FRT reported significantly greater training enjoyment than those performing TRT. These findings are consistent with our original hypotheses. However, our hypothesis that traditional resistance training would produce greater improvements in muscular strength and relative muscular endurance than fragmented resistance training was not supported. Although the TRT group consistently demonstrated larger mean improvements in maximal strength, isometric squat force, and relative muscular endurance than the FRT group, none of these between-group differences reached statistical significance.

Both FRT and TRT produced significant improvements in maximal strength, isometric squat force, and relative muscular endurance compared with the control condition. Although TRT consistently demonstrated greater mean improvements across all performance outcomes, these numerical advantages did not translate into statistically significant differences compared with FRT. Therefore, the present findings do not support the conclusion that traditional continuous resistance training is superior under volume-equated conditions. Instead, they indicate that distributing resistance training across multiple short bouts throughout the day provides neuromuscular adaptations comparable to those achieved through conventional continuous training. These findings are generally consistent with previous resistance training studies demonstrating that manipulating training frequency or distributing training volume across multiple sessions does not substantially alter strength adaptations when overall training volume is matched.33 Although previous investigations primarily manipulated weekly training frequency, the present study extends this evidence by demonstrating that comparable adaptations can also be achieved when identical training volume is distributed across multiple bouts within a single day. Despite these consistent numerical trends, the absence of statistically significant differences between FRT and TRT suggests that any potential advantage of continuous training, if present, is likely small under the present experimental conditions. Importantly, the interpretation of relative muscular endurance performance warrants specific consideration. Because the endurance test load was prescribed as a fixed percentage (40%) of each participant's current 1RM, participants who increased their maximal strength completed the post-intervention test using a heavier absolute load. As a result, improvements in relative muscular endurance reflect both enhanced fatigue resistance and the ability to sustain repeated contractions at a higher absolute workload—a distinction that is often overlooked when endurance is assessed without this qualification.13

An important finding of the present study was that participants performing FRT reported greater exercise enjoyment than those undertaking TRT. This finding is consistent with the broader concept of exercise snacking, in which shorter exercise bouts are generally perceived as less physically and psychologically demanding.34 Dividing the same training volume into several brief sessions may reduce the perceived burden associated with prolonged exercise while allowing individuals greater flexibility to integrate exercise into their daily schedules. Furthermore, shorter bouts may enhance perceptions of autonomy and competence because each exercise episode appears easier to initiate and complete successfully.35 According to self-determination theory, greater perceptions of autonomy and competence are associated with more positive affective responses during exercise, which may partially explain the higher PACES scores observed following FRT.36 In the present study, the Smith machine was located on the ground floor of the student dormitory, and each FRT bout required only a brief warm-up and no change of gym attire, which minimised the logistical burden associated with multiple daily training visits and likely contributed to the higher enjoyment reported in the FRT group. However, such feasibility may depend on contextual factors such as facility accessibility and individual schedules. However, because exercise enjoyment was a cross-sectional assessment, baseline differences in exercise preference cannot be excluded. Consequently, the observed between-group difference should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed by studies incorporating both pre- and post-intervention assessments of exercise enjoyment.

The improvements observed in the control group also warrant consideration. Importantly, the control group should not be interpreted as a sedentary or no-exercise control. Rather, participants continued their habitual daily activities without participating in either supervised resistance-training intervention. Consequently, modest improvements in maximal strength may be expected even in the absence of the experimental training programme. Several factors may have contributed to these changes, including familiarisation with the Smith-machine squat testing procedure,37 improved technical proficiency during repeated 1RM testing,38 and unrecorded lower-body resistance exercise performed outside the study.39 Previous research has demonstrated that repeated exposure to strength testing alone can produce modest improvements through learning effects.37 Nevertheless, the magnitude of improvement observed in the present study suggests that habitual resistance exercise performed outside the experimental protocol may also have contributed. Importantly, despite these modest improvements, both supervised intervention groups still demonstrated significantly greater adjusted post-intervention performance than the control condition. Therefore, the additional gains observed following fragmented and traditional resistance training are unlikely to be explained solely by familiarisation or habitual activity, supporting the effectiveness of the structured resistance-training interventions.

From a practical perspective, the present findings suggest that fragmented resistance training may represent a feasible alternative to conventional resistance training for individuals who are already accustomed to resistance exercise but have limited opportunities to complete longer uninterrupted training sessions. Although TRT consistently demonstrated slightly larger mean improvements across the strength-related outcomes, these differences were small and failed to reach statistical significance. Therefore, the current evidence indicates that distributing resistance training across multiple short bouts can provide training adaptations comparable to those achieved through conventional continuous resistance training. This aligns with previous work showing that modifications in resistance structure can influence performance-related outcomes without necessarily affecting all fitness components equally,14 reinforcing the practical relevance of fragmented training as a viable option for improving selected fitness outcomes while simultaneously enhancing exercise enjoyment. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that fragmented resistance training improves long-term exercise adherence. All participants were recreationally resistance-trained young men, adherence was high in both intervention groups, and the intervention was conducted under supervised conditions. Therefore, whether fragmented resistance training can enhance long-term participation among inactive, untrained, or clinical populations remains unknown and warrants further investigation.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although the sample size met the statistical requirements, a relatively modest sample was included, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Second, all participants were resistance-trained young male university students; therefore, the results may not be directly applicable to females, older adults, untrained individuals, or clinical populations. Third, only one exercise (Smith-machine back squat), one loading range (40–55% 1RM), and one training configuration (three sets of 20 repetitions) were investigated. Consequently, the findings should not be generalised to other exercises, loading zones, or resistance-training programmes. Fourth, exercise enjoyment was assessed only one time; therefore, pre-existing differences in exercise preference cannot be excluded. Finally, the present study primarily focused on functional performance outcomes and did not assess potential physiological mechanisms such as muscle hypertrophy, electromyographic activity, or metabolic responses. Sixth, we acknowledge that the magnitude of strength gains observed in the intervention groups may appear substantial; however, these improvements are consistent with previous studies, and the raw individual data are provided in Supplementary Material 2 for independent verification. Additionally, slight deviations from normality were noted for two outcomes, but ANCOVA is robust to such mild violations, particularly with balanced group sizes and homogeneous variances and bootstrap resampling (Supplementary Material 1) confirmed the stability of the results. Future studies with larger samples may consider robust regression approaches to further validate these findings. Future studies incorporating mechanistic measurements are warranted to further clarify the physiological adaptations associated with fragmented resistance training.

Conclusion

Both fragmented resistance training and traditional resistance training improved maximal strength, isometric squat force, and relative muscular endurance compared with the control condition under volume-equated conditions. Although TRT consistently demonstrated greater mean improvements in strength- and endurance-related outcomes than FRT, these between-group differences did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, participants performing FRT reported greater exercise enjoyment than those undertaking TRT. Further research is required to determine whether the numerical advantage observed for TRT becomes meaningful over longer intervention periods, different training protocols, or in other populations.
