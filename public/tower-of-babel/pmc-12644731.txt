Glucose shields RINm5F beta cells against arsenic-induced apoptosis
Authors: Rosa Isela Ortiz-Huidobro, Pablo Pánico, Ana María Salazar, Marcia Hiriart, Patricia Ostrosky-Wegman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 2 Diabetes (T2D) is characterized by hyperglycemia due to pancreatic beta cell failure and the development of insulin resistance. Although high carbohydrate intake and arsenic exposure increase the risk of developing T2D, their interactions and mechanisms associated with changes in insulin signaling pathways that lead to beta-cell apoptosis remain unknown. We determined the effects of in vitro exposure for 48 and 72 h to arsenic (As, 2 µM), glucose (Gluc, 22 mM), and their interaction (As + Gluc) on the apoptosis in RINm5F insulinoma beta-cells and explored the possible mechanisms dependent on alterations in insulin signaling pathways. We found that arsenic induced early apoptosis, while glucose co-treatment reduced arsenic-induced apoptosis. Mechanistically, co-treatment with As + Gluc deregulates the PI3K/Akt pathway, increasing the activation of Akt and S6K1, whereas in the MAPK pathway decreases arsenic-induced ERK1/2 activation. Furthermore, in these cells we observed an increased calpain proteolytic activity and down-regulation of the pro-apoptotic Bax/Caspase-3 pathway, compared to the effects of arsenic alone. Overall, our results suggest that 48 and 72 h of glucose co-treatment mitigates the pro-apoptotic effect of arsenic in RINm5F cells. Introduction

T2D comprises a group of metabolic disorders characterized by hyperglycemia due to impaired insulin production and secretion by pancreatic beta-cells as well as the development of insulin resistance, which is a defect in the metabolic response of peripheral tissues to insulin1. Risk factors for T2D include high-calorie intake, low physical activity, genetic predisposition, and exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), such as arsenic2,3. Diabetes is one of the most significant public health challenges of the 21 st century. The WHO estimates that the number of adults with diabetes has quadrupled from 200 million in 1990 to 830 million in 20224.

Pancreatic beta cells are located within the pancreatic islets, which constitute the endocrine portion of the pancreas. Mature beta cells synthesize and secrete insulin in response to blood glucose5. In patients with obesity and metabolic syndrome, beta cells are exposed to conditions that promote glucotoxicity, lipotoxicity, and chronic inflammation, which leads to cellular stress, dysfunction, and cell death6. Moreover, exposure to EDC, such as arsenic, impacts beta cell function, contributing to the development of T2D7–10.

In pancreatic beta cells, insulin primarily carries out mitogenic functions. Through the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways, insulin promotes cell proliferation and survival by regulating cell cycle progression proteins and inhibiting apoptotic pathways, such as SMAD and caspases pathways1. The mechanisms by which dysregulation of insulin signaling pathways affects beta-cell survival have not been fully clarified.

Arsenic is an environmental pollutant that naturally occurs in the Earth’s crust and aquifers. This element contaminates surface water, soil, crops, and vegetation due to volcanic emissions, fossil fuel extraction and mining11. Human populations with a high prevalence of obesity and elevated exposure to arsenic through drinking water exhibit a higher incidence of T2D compared to populations with obesity but without arsenic exposure12–15.

Mechanistically, this metalloid impairs insulin synthesis and secretion by downregulating insulin gene expression16, disrupting cytoplasmic calcium homeostasis, and impairing the activity of calpain-10, which is necessary for insulin secretion17,18. Furthermore, arsenic induces mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and endoplasmic reticulum stress10,19. It also decreases cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis7,20.

We previously showed the combined effects of arsenic and sucrose in drinking water in Wistar rats, demonstrating that each factor along with their interaction induces systemic insulin resistance21. Interestingly, arsenic and sucrose altered different molecules of the insulin signaling pathway, rather than acting additively in the development of insulin resistance. This suggests that carbohydrate and arsenic ingestion could have complex interactions in cells important for glucose homeostasis, such as beta-cells. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms underlying beta-cell death induced by high glucose and arsenic exposure remain poorly understood.

This study aimed to investigate the beta-cell apoptosis induced by arsenic and high-glucose concentrations, and the relevance of alterations in the insulin signaling pathways for beta-cell survival.

Results

Cell viability and apoptosis assays

Cell viability was evaluated using the fluorescein diacetate (FDA) assay, which is metabolized into fluorescein by mitochondrial enzymes22, in RINm5F cells treated for 48, 72, or 96 h with glucose (Gluc; 15.6, 22, 30, and 50 mM), sodium arsenite (As; 1, 2, 5, and 10 µM), or 2 µM As combined with 22, 30, or 50 mM glucose (As + Gluc). Glucose treatments induced a dose-dependent increase in viability at 48 h, which persisted at 15.6 and 22 mM after 72 h; however, at 96 h, viability declined to 43% and 50% at 30 and 50 mM, respectively. As exposure caused a concentration- and time-dependent reduction in viability. Remarkably, viability decreased by only ~ 11% in cells treated with 2 µM As + 22, 30, or 50 mM glucose for 48 h. It is worth noting that in the 2 µM As + 22 mM glucose condition, viability remained higher than with 2 µM As alone for up to 96 h. In contrast, 2 µM As combined with 30 or 50 mM glucose reduced viability by > 65% after 72 h (Fig. 1A).

The concentration of As at 2 µM was selected for subsequent assays, as it induces a 50% reduction in cell viability for 72 h, representing a toxic effect without causing lethality. Additionally, we focused on the cellular response to the combined treatment of 2 µM As and 22 mM glucose, since after 96 h, cells exhibited higher viability compared to those treated with 2 µM As alone.

To further investigate the cell death mechanism involved in these effects, we performed a double staining with Annexin V (AV)/7-amino-actinomycin D (7-AAD). Early apoptotic cells are labeled by AV, which binds to exposed phosphatidylserine residues on the plasma membrane. In late apoptosis, 7-AAD penetrates the compromised membrane and binds to DNA, indicating more advanced apoptotic damage23. In this way, this assay distinguishes between viable (AV−/7-AAD−), early apoptotic (AV+/7-AAD−), late apoptotic (AV+/7-AAD+), and necrotic (AV−/7-AAD+) cells.

We observed a 28% reduction of viable cells after 48 h of As and As + Gluc treatments, compared with the control. Gluc did not alter the viable cell percentage. Likewise, As and As + Gluc treatments increased the percentage of early apoptotic cells, while only a small percentage (< 10%) of late apoptotic cells were observed, primarily in the As-treated group. Necrotic cells were less than 0.6% of total cells (Fig. 1B).

In contrast, As treatment for 72 h reduced the percentage of viable cells by 56.5% and just 27.6% in cells exposed to As + Gluc, which was consistent with the FDA assay results. The decrease in viable cells induced by As was accompanied by an increase in early apoptotic cells, while this effect was less pronounced in As + Gluc treated cells. Gluc-treated cells had fewer early apoptotic cells, compared with all the other groups. As treatment also increased the percentage of cells in late apoptosis, while this was prevented in cells treated with As + Gluc. Necrotic cells were fewer than 0.1%, with As and As + Gluc treatments inducing the highest levels of this cell population (Fig. 1C).

Cell proliferation, insulin secretion, and oxidative damage

To characterize the alterations induced by the treatments in RINm5F cells in terms of cell proliferation, function, and oxidative damage, we performed assays for cell cycle progression, insulin secretion, and lipid peroxidation.

All experiments were performed under low-serum conditions (1% fetal bovine serum, FBS) to reduce cell proliferation and avoid confunding effects in the assessment of treatment-induced apoptosis. We validated this condition through cell cycle analysis with propidium iodide staining. In untreated cells grown under standard conditions (10% FBS), 61.4% were at G0/G1, 24.4% at S, and 14.2% at G2/M phases, indicating active proliferation. In contrast, cells kept under our experimental conditions had ~ 60% of cells at G0/G1 and ~ 40% at S phases, while cells at the G2/M phases were not detected. No statistically significant differences were found (Fig. 2A).

Additionally, basal and stimulated insulin secretion were assessed in RINm5F cells treated for 72 h. In control cells, insulin secretion increased by 49% following stimulation with secretagogues. However, cells subjected to As, Gluc, and As + Gluc treatments failed to respond to the stimulus. Basal insulin secretion was reduced by 76% in the As treatment compared to the control, remained unchanged in the glucose treatment, and decreased by 70% in the As + Gluc treatment relative to the control. Notably, stimulated insulin secretion was higher in the As + Gluc treatment than in the As treatment alone (Fig. 2B).

Malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation, was measured in RINm5F cells after 72 h of treatment. We observed that the As treatment produced a higher amount of MDA compared to the control. In the Gluc and As + Gluc treatments, no significant differences were observed with respect to the control (Fig. 2C).

Insulin signaling pathways: PI3K/Akt and MAPK

Since autocrine insulin signaling is important for beta-cell survival, we assessed the alterations of the critical nodes of the insulin signaling pathway: phosphorylation of Akt at S473, S6K1 at T389, and ERK1/2 at T202/Y204, in total lysates of treated cells, these phosphorylations indicate activation of these proteins. We also evaluated phosphorylation of IRS1 at S1101, which inhibits IRS1 function under metabolic stress24 (Fig. 3A and B). IRS1 phosphorylation in S1101 was induced by Gluc and reverted in As + Gluc (Fig. 3C). Contrarily, Akt phosphorylation at S473 was slightly induced by As, but we found a synergistic effect when cells were exposed to As + Gluc, while Gluc treatment did not change this phosphorylation (Fig. 3C). Regarding S6K1, both As and Gluc treatments reduced its phosphorylation, whereas As + Gluc increased it by around two-fold (Fig. 3C). The treatment with As promoted the phosphorylation of ERK1/2, but this was prevented in cells treated with As + Gluc (Fig. 3C).

The Calpain system

Lastly, we evaluated the alterations in the members of the calpain system. Calpain-10 is involved in insulin granule trafficking and exocytosis25, as well as calpain 1 and 2 in pro- and anti-apoptotic pathways in pancreatic beta-cells10,26,27 (Fig. 5A and B). Western blot analysis showed reduced levels of calpain-1, −2, and − 10 in As and As + Gluc treated cells, while Gluc did not affect the abundance of these proteases with respect to control (Fig. 5C). We measured the proteolysis of α-II spectrin, a well-characterized substrate of calpains28. Proteolytic activity was enhanced by As + Gluc. Notably, the 140 kDa fragment generated by calpain activity was more abundant in cells treated whith As and As + Gluc (Fig. 5D). We next evaluated mRNA expression of calpains and observed that, in contrast to protein levels, As and As + Gluc treatments upregulated the gene expression of calpain-1 and − 2 (Fig. 5E).

In parallel, we investigated whether Akt kinase activation was associated with the increased viability observed in the As + Gluc treatment compared with As alone. RINm5F cells were treated for 72 h with the specific Akt activator SC79. Cells treated with As + Akt activator displayed an increase in viability comparable to that observed with As + Gluc compared with As alone (Fig. 6C and D).

Discussion

Glucose attenuates arsenic-induced apoptosis

We conducted an in vitro study using pancreatic beta cells from the rat insulinoma-derived RINm5F cell line, which retains the capacity to produce and secrete insulin29. Sustained exposure to elevated glucose concentrations has been extensively reported to induce cellular stress and, in turn, trigger an adaptive response in pancreatic beta cells, characterized by increased cell mass and enhanced functional capacity6,30,31. Consistent with this, FDA viability assays and AV/7-AAD apoptosis staining revealed that RINm5F cells treated with glucose (22 mM) for up to 72 h exhibited increased viability, which may reflect this adaptive response. However, at higher glucose concentrations (30 and 50 mM), this response was lost, favoring the onset of glucotoxicity.

In contrast, As induced apoptosis in a dose- and time-dependent manner. Treatment with 2 µM As for 72 h reduced cell viability by approximately 50%. This diabetogenic effect of As has been previously reported, involving impairment of beta cell function, proliferation, and survival7,10,17,20. Notably, in cells exposed to As (2 µM) in combination with Gluc (22 mM), the arsenic-induced loss of viability was attenuated for up to 96 h. These findings suggest that glucose may partially counteract the cytotoxic effects of arsenic in RINm5F cells under these experimental conditions. This beta cell behavior is consistent with an adaptive mechanism, in which glucose stimulation under specific conditions may modulate the cellular response to arsenic exposure.

Treatments with As and As + Gluc primarily induced early apoptosis in RINm5F cells at both 48 and 72 h. This finding is particularly relevant, as it allows for the study of molecular mechanisms underlying early beta-cell damage, like that occurring during insulin resistance, before the onset of beta-cell death in T2D1,6.

Arsenic + glucose treatment improves the function of RINm5F cells to respect arsenic treatment

On the other hand, regarding beta-cell function, insulin secretion assays revealed that in cells treated with As, both basal and stimulated secretion were reduced, and these cells lost their responsiveness to stimulation, consistent with previous reports9,10,16. In cells exposed to Gluc, insulin secretion was comparable between basal and stimulated conditions; however, it remained higher than in the As-treated cells. In contrast, in the As + Gluc treatment, the increase in stimulated secretion was not significant compared with basal secretion, yet it was higher than that observed in the As treatment. These findings indicate that the As + Gluc combination exerts a positive effect on RINm5F cell function compared with As alone.

It has been widely reported that As induces oxidative stress in beta-cells, promoting MDA formation10. In agreement with this, we detected elevated MDA levels in As-treated cells. Conversely, Gluc treatment did not increase lipid peroxidation compared to the control. Notably, in the As + Gluc treatment, MDA levels tended to decrease relative to As treatment. These results suggest that in the As + Gluc combination, cells undergo less oxidative damage, which is of relevance, as oxidative stress is a critical initiating factor in beta cell dysfunction and subsequent apoptosis10,32.

The Akt/S6K1 signaling axis is overactivated in arsenic + glucose treated cells

The canonical insulin signaling pathway PI3K/Akt regulates diverse metabolic and mitogenic processes in pancreatic beta-cells and is tightly regulated under physiological conditions1,33. However, this regulation is disrupted under cellular stress or inflammation, compromising beta-cell function and survival34,35. Altered insulin signaling has been observed in models of insulin resistance, obesity, and T2D10,30; however, the molecular mechanisms underlying early beta-cell damage, particularly those involving loss of insulin-mediated repression of apoptotic pathways remain unclear.

We analyzed the PI3K/Akt pathway at the level of the kinase Akt, a key regulatory node in insulin signaling. We also evaluated the phosphorylation of S6K1, a downstream target of mTORC1 that is involved in protein synthesis and autophagy regulation. Additionally, we measured the phosphorylation of IRS1, a key insulin signaling component that can be inhibited via phosphorylation by stress-related kinases such as JNK, IKK, and S6K11.

We observed an increase in Akt phosphorylation relative to the control in cells treated with As, a response that has been associated with cellular stress32. On the other hand, glucose treatment did not enhance Akt activation, nevertheless, S6K1 kinase is downregulated in both treatments. Interestingly, in the As + Gluc treatment, an overactivation of Akt and S6K1 was observed. Several studies have reported that, in contexts of metabolic stress associated with nutrient overload, inflammation, or cancer, this axis becomes constitutively activated, thereby deregulating insulin signaling and promoting insulin resistance1,24. Such alterations impair insulin-regulated processes, including metabolic control, cell proliferation, and survival1,33.

While glucose and arsenic alone elicit different responses in this pathway, their combination enhances the activation of Akt and S6K1. This effect is likely related to the adaptive responses triggered in cells under cellular stress conditions induced by this co-treatment. It is worth noting that further research is needed to better understand the adaptive mechanisms of pancreatic beta-cells when exposed to multiple stimuli simultaneously.

S6K1 is activated by mTORC1, the master regulator of protein synthesis. The Akt/mTORC1/S6K1 axis has been implicated in cell proliferation and survival of beta-cell during early development36–38 as well as in pathophysiological contexts such as obesity and cancer39,40. Constitutive S6K1 activation promotes IRS1 inhibition and degradation, thereby disrupting insulin signaling41. The mechanisms underlying S6K1 overactivation, however, remain poorly understood, although pro-inflammatory cytokine–activated pathways have been proposed as contributors1.

IRS1 protein contains approximately 70 phosphorylation sites that modulate its function. Specifically, phosphorylation at serine 1101 has been observed under conditions of insulin resistance at metabolic tissues, where it is associated with reduced signal transduction through the downregulation of Akt24. In our study, we observed increased phosphorylation at this site in glucose treated cells compared to controls; however, Akt activation was similar between glucose-treated and control cells. Interestingly, As + Gluc treated cells, while there is reduced inhibitory phosphorylation at IRS1 (S1101), Akt activity was higher, as expected; nevertheless, in this context, S6K1 overactivation does not appear to correlate with inhibitory phosphorylation of IRS1 at S1101. This suggests that, under these conditions, this mechanism may not be relevant, probably other IRS1 phosphorylation sites may play a more critical role in regulating its activity. These observations highlight the need for further investigation into this regulatory mechanism.

Additionally, previous studies have reported that As promotes MAPK pathway activation10,42, which is consistent with our observations. However, it is worth noting that As + Gluc treatment maintains ERK1/2 phosphorylation at levels comparable to the control.

Although the overactivation of Akt in As- and As + Gluc-treated cells would be expected to inhibit the pro-apoptotic Bax/caspase-3 pathway and reduce apoptosis34, we observed that apoptosis was not suppressed, indicating a dysregulation of the insulin signaling pathway and suggesting that other mechanisms regulating cell death may be more relevant in this context, for example the calpain system.

The Calpain system may be involved in Bax/caspase-3 pro-apoptotic pathway attenuation under arsenic + glucose-induced cell survival

Calpains are known to modulate the activation of a wide range of proteins, including members of the caspase family10,26. Specifically, calpain-10 has been reported to regulate the transcription of the gene encoding caspase-343. In our study, mRNA levels of calpain-1 and -2 were elevated in As and As + Gluc treated cells. However, at the protein level, the abundance of calpain-1, -2, and -10 was reduced in these same treatment conditions. This may be associated with the autolytic activity of calpains, which is exacerbated under conditions of cellular stress28,44,45. It has been reported that calpain-10 promotes the cleavage of SNAP-25, a protein involved in the exocytosis of insulin granules25. We observed a lower protein abundance of calpain-10 in cells treated with As and As + Gluc, which may be related to the defects in the insulin secretion mechanism that we observed in these cells.

Calpain activity, as assessed by the cleavage of its substrate α-II spectrin28. We observed that calpain activity was elevated in cells treated with As, consistent with previous reports10. Notably, cells exposed to the As + Gluc combination exhibited even higher calpain activity. On the other hand, the effect of As observed here is consistent with prior reports of arsenic-mediated upregulation of caspase-310. It should be noted that the level of active caspase-3 was lower in the As + Gluc treatment compared with As alone, suggesting that calpain activity under these conditions promotes the enhanced cell survival. To confirm the role of calpains in As + Gluc-induced cell survival, cells were treated with calpain inhibitor XII, which is highly specific for calpains 1 and 2. We found that the increase in viability observed in As + Gluc-treated cells was abolished in the presence of calpain inhibitor XII. These findings suggest that the calpain system appear to play a critical role on the cell survival in response to As + Gluc treatment. Previous studies in calpain-1 knockout models indicate that this calpain is involved in survival pathways in response to cell death stimuli27, which may explain why its activity promotes increased survival of cells treated with As + Gluc.

On the other hand, to further explore the role of Akt overactivation in cell survival, a proof-of-concept experiment was performed. Cells were treated with As and a specific Akt activator, SC79. This treatment increased cell viability in a manner comparable to the As + Gluc treatment. These results suggest that Akt activation may contribute in the survival phenotype observed in As + Gluc-treated cells. However, since Akt represents a key regulatory node in cellular signaling, its endogenous activation responds to diverse stimuli that differentially modulates its downstream mechanisms. Based on the results obtained under these experimental conditions, we observed a dysregulation of the PI3K/Akt insulin signaling pathway, and it becomes relevant to further investigate the impact of these signaling alterations on pancreatic beta-cell function and survival.

Conclusion

Arsenic induced apoptosis in RINm5F cells in both a dose- and time-dependent manner, while exposure to glucose (22 mM) for up to 72 h increased cell viability. In cells co-treated with As + Gluc, apoptosis was attenuated compared with arsenic treatment alone. This combined treatment induced alterations in the insulin signaling pathway, specifically an increase in Akt and S6K1 activity, enhanced calpain activity, and a down-regulation of caspase-3 activation relative to arsenic treatment. Overall, our findings indicate that glucose co-treatment for 48 and 72 h mitigates the pro-apoptotic effects of arsenic in RINm5F cells. Notably, calpains appear to play a critical role in this response, while the Akt/S6K1 signaling axis shows significant changes compared with control conditions.

Despite these findings, some limitations must be acknowledged. The function, identity, and survival of pancreatic beta cells in vivo are regulated by multiple mechanisms related to innervation, paracrine communication, and the microenvironment within the pancreatic islets. Our observations in RINm5F cells under these conditions provide important data on the involvement of calpains and the insulin signaling pathway, which have not been addressed before from this perspective. However, we consider it highly relevant to further the study of these mechanisms and to extend these ideas into other models.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and in vitro treatments

The RINm5F rat insulinoma pancreatic beta cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CRL-11605, Virginia, USA) and maintained according to ATCC protocols. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Biowest, cat. L0495-500, Bradenton, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco cat.16000044, NY, USA) and 1% L-glutamine (Sigma, cat. G6392, USA). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ (Thermo Scientific, Series 8000 WJ, USA). For experiments, cells were seeded in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 1% FBS and 1% L-glutamine, at a cell density of 0.15 × 10⁶ cells/cm², which corresponds to 80–90% confluence. These conditions were established to reduce cell proliferation of this cell line to recapitulate the low proliferative capacity of normal pancreatic beta-cell. Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium by 24 h. After cells were treated with sodium arsenite (2 µM), glucose (22 mM), sodium arsenite (2 µM) + glucose (22 mM), calpain inhibitor XII (20 µM) (Santa Cruz #181769-57-3) or the specific Akt activator SC79 (8 µg/mL) (Sigma Aldrich, SML0749) for 48, 72, or 96 h, depending on the experimental conditions.

Cell viability

The culture medium was removed after treatments, and cells were incubated with fluorescein diacetate (FDA, 0.016 mg/mL) in serum-free medium for 5 min at room temperature under gentle agitation. Cells were washed once with PBS, and fluorescence was measured using a Fluoroskan Ascent microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA) with excitation/emission at 488/533 nm. Each experimental condition was performed in triplicate. Puromycin (2 µg/mL) (Sigma, cat. P9620, USA) was used as a positive control for cell death.

Cell cycle assay

Cells were washed three times with PBS and detached with 0.025% trypsin (Biowest, cat MS0180, USA) in PBS. Then, cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 75% (v/v) cold ethanol, and stored at −20 °C for 24 h. Fixed cells were washed with PBS and stained with propidium iodide (PI, 1 mg/mL, (Sigma, P4171, USA), 0.1% Triton X-100, and 10 mg/mL RNasa DNasa Free (Thermo Scientific, #EN0531, USA) in PBS for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Cells were washed with PBS and analyzed with the blue/violet Attune Acoustic Focusing Cytometer (Applied Biosystems, USA) using the BL2 channel. Mitomycin C was used as positive control for cell cycle arrest.10,000 events were recorded for each sample. Single cell events were selected by plotting the high of the BL2 signal against the area of the BL2 signal. Analysis was performed with ModFit LT 4.1 software.

Apoptosis detection by Annexin V/7-AAD staining

After treatments, cells were washed with PBS and detached with PBS-EDTA 0.1%. Staining was performed in 1X Annexin V binding buffer (BD Pharmingen cat. 51-66121E, USA) with phycoerythrin-conjugated annexin V (AV, BD Pharmingen cat. 556421, dilution factor, DF 5:100) and 7-amino-actinomycin D (7-AAD, Biolegend cat 420404, DF 5:100) for 20 min at room temperature. Samples were immediately analyzed with the blue violet Attune Acoustic Focusing Cytometer (Applied Biosystems, USA) using the BL2 and BL3 channels. Compensation controls were performed in each experiment. We evaluated at least 10,000 cells for each sample by duplicate. Data was analyzed with the FlowJo V 10.10.0 software.

Insulin secretion assay and quantification

After 72 h of treatment, cells were incubated in RPMI-1640 medium containing low glucose (5.6 mM), without FBS or glutamine, for 30 min at 37 °C. Insulin secretion was then assessed by incubating cells for 2 h at 37 °C under basal conditions (Krebs–Ringer–Henseleit [KRH] buffer containing 5.6 mM glucose) or stimulatory conditions (KRH buffer supplemented with 15.6 mM glucose, glutamine 10 mM, leucine 10 mM, tolbutamide 1 mM, and phorbol ester 1µM). Supernatants were collected and stored at − 70 °C until analysis. Cells were subsequently lysed with RIPA buffer (Tris-HCl 10 mM, EDTA 1 mM, EGTA 1 mM, Triton x-100 1% (v/v), sodium deoxicolate 0.1% (m/v), SDS 0.1% (m/v), NaCl 140 mM) supplemented with cOmplete™, Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche), centrifuged, and stored at − 70 °C for later protein quantification using the DC™ Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad, #5000113, USA). Insulin concentrations in the supernatants were determined using a commercial ELISA kit, according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Rat INS [Insulin] ELISA Kit, FineTest ER1113, Wuhan, China).

Lipid peroxidation analysis

The formation of malondialdehyde (MDA), a lipid peroxidation byproduct commonly used as an indicator of cellular oxidative stress, was quantified using the TBARS Assay Kit (Cayman Chemical, No. 10009055, MI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 72 h of treatment, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with cOmplete™ Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche) and stored at − 70 °C for 24 h. MDA standards and samples were incubated with the Color Reagent (thiobarbituric acid (5.3 mg/mL) in acetic acid (3.5 M) and sodium hydroxide (3.5 M) solution) for 1 h at 95 °C. The reaction was terminated on ice, and samples were centrifuged at 1600 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Supernatants were transferred to a clear 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 530 nm. MDA concentrations were determined from the standard curve and normalized to the total protein content of the lysates.

Western blot analysis

Total protein extraction and immunoblotting were conducted as previously described21 using RIPA buffer (Tris-HCl 10 mM, EDTA 1 mM, EGTA 1 mM, Triton x-100 1% (v/v), sodium deoxicolate 0.1% (m/v), SDS 0.1% (m/v), NaCl 140 mM) supplemented with Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (ThermoFisher Scientific, #1861281, USA). Samples were sonicated and centrifuged to remove cell debris. Protein concentration was quantified using the DC™ Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad, #5000113, USA). Equal amounts of protein were denatured with 2X-Laemmli buffer at 85 °C for 5 min. Proteins were resolved on 8% SDS-PAGE for p70S6K, p-P70S6K(T389), Akt, p-Akt(S473), IRS-1, p-IRS1(S1101), and α-II spectrin; 12% SDS-PAGE were used for CAPN1, CAPN2, CAPN10, Bax, BcL, ERK1/2, and p-ERK1/2; and 15% SDS-PAGE for cleaved caspase-3 (Asp175). Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes using a semi-dry transfer system (Bio-Rad, USA) at 20 V for 60 min. Membranes were blocked with TBS-tween containing 4% Blotto non-fat dry milk (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-2325, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in TBS-Tween 20 with 4% milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-p-Akt (Ser473) (#9271, DF 1:500, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-Akt (#9272, DF 1:2000, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-p-P70S6K (Thr389) (#9205, DF 1:500, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-P70S6K (#9202, DF 1:1000, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-p-IRS1 (S1101) (#2385, DF 1:1000, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-IRS1 (#2382, DF 1:1000, Cell Signaling), mouse anti-p-ERK 1/2 (T202/Y204.22 A) (SC-136521, DF 1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), mouse anti-ERK 1/2 (SC-514302, DF 1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), rabbit anti-cleaved caspase-3 (N175) (#9661, DF 1:1000, Cell Signaling), mouse anti-BcL-xL (SC-8392, DF 1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), rabbit anti-Bax (SC-493, DF 1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), rabbit anti-calpain-1 (#2556, DF 1:1000, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-calpain-2 (ab126600, DF 1:1000, Abcam), rabbit anti-calpain-10 (ab28226, DF 1:1000, Abcam), rabbit anti-αII spectrin (BML-FG 6090 − 0100, DF 1:1000, ENZO).Secondary antibody: Peroxidase-conjugated mouse anti-rabbit IgG (light chain specific, Jackson ImmunoResearch #211-032-171, DF 1:6000) and peroxidase-conjugated affinipure goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch #115-035-146; DF 1:4000). Protein bands were detected using the ECL Prime kit (Amersham, UK) and visualized with a Li-Cor C-Digit scanner. Band intensities were quantified using Image Studio Lite software (Version 5.2, Li-Cor Biosciences, USA). We used the optical density of the entire lane in the gels stained with Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) as a loading control, as a previously described46. Supplementary images of the membranes corresponding to each blot and their replicates are provided. These membranes were sectioned prior to incubation with the primary antibody to minimize the amount of primary antibody required for each assay.

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from RINm5F cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed from 1300 ng of total RNA using the SuperScript™ III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix Kit (Invitrogen, cat. 18080400, USA) with random hexamers according to the protocol of the manufacturer. Gene expression was quantified by real-time PCR using 15 ng of cDNA, TaqMan universal master mix (Applied Biosystems, USA), and TaqMan probes (Applied Biosystems, USA) targeting CAPN1 (Rn01479699_m1), CAPN2 (Rn00567422_m1), and CAPN10 (Rn00581535_m1). Expression levels were normalized to the mRNA expression of glucuronidase beta (GUSB, Rn00566655_m1). The expression analysis was carried out with the 2-ΔΔCt method47.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. The analysis of the data was performed with the GraphPad Prism 8.0 software using one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Graphs present the mean ± standard error (SE), and each circle denotes the individual data points. The final figures were assembled using Adobe Photoshop 2022.
