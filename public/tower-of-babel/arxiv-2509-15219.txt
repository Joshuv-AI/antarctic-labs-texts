Abstract
Trajectory prediction is a critical task in computer vision and autonomous systems, playing a key role in applications such as autonomous driving, robotics, surveillance, and virtual reality. Existing methods often rely on complete and noise-free observational data, overlooking the challenges associated with out-of-sight objects and the inherent noise in sensor data caused by limited camera coverage, obstructions, and the absence of ground truth for denoised trajectories. These limitations pose significant safety risks and hinder reliable trajectory prediction in real-world scenarios. In this extended work, we present significant advancements in Out-of-Sight Trajectory (OST), a novel task that predicts the noise-free visual trajectories of out-of-sight objects using noisy sensor data. Building on our previous research, we broaden the scope of Out-of-Sight Trajectory Prediction (OOSTraj) to include both pedestrians and vehicles, extending its applicability to autonomous driving, robotics, surveillance, and virtual reality. Our enhanced Vision-Positioning Denoising Module leverages camera calibration to establish a vision-positioning mapping, addressing the lack of visual references, while effectively denoising noisy sensor data in an unsupervised manner. Through extensive evaluations on the Vi-Fi and JRDB datasets, our approach achieves state-of-the-art performance in both trajectory denoising and prediction, significantly surpassing previous baselines. Additionally, we introduce comparisons with traditional denoising methods, such as Kalman filtering, and adapt recent trajectory prediction models to our task, providing a comprehensive benchmark. This work represents the first initiative to integrate vision-positioning projection for denoising noisy sensor trajectories of out-of-sight agents, paving the way for future advancements in this domain. The code and preprocessed datasets are available at https://github.com/Hai-chao-Zhang/OST .

Trajectory prediction plays a crucial role across a wide range of research areas, including computer vision, surveillance, virtual reality, robotics, vehicle safety systems, and autonomous driving, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. This capability is essential not only for understanding human behavior through deep learning models but also for empowering autonomous systems to predict and react to the movements of pedestrians and vehicles. By forecasting future trajectories from multimodal signals—often noisy or incomplete—trajectory prediction significantly enhances safety and helps prevent potential collisions.
However, real-world environments introduce a myriad of complexities, such as occlusions, blind spots, and noisy sensor inputs, which make reliable prediction exceedingly difficult. While prior work has made considerable progress in this domain, existing methods predominantly rely on computer vision-based algorithms under the assumption of ideal conditions, such as noise-free and consistently visible observations.
This reliance neglects two critical challenges often encountered in practical scenarios: the presence of objects that fall outside the camera’s field of view and the pervasive noise in multimodal sensor data. To address these challenges, our research proposes an innovative vision-positioning denoising framework, specifically designed to enhance trajectory prediction by leveraging both visual and sensor data in challenging real-world conditions.

Let us consider a representative case where neglecting out-of-sight objects leads to critical issues in the domain of autonomous driving and robotics. The inherent limitations of camera-based systems pose significant challenges in such scenarios. As illustrated in Fig.
2
, pedestrians or vehicles are often obscured by other vehicles, physical barriers, or fall into blind spots, making them temporarily or entirely invisible to cameras. These blind spots can result in agents suddenly reappearing within the camera’s field of view, leaving insufficient time for trajectory prediction algorithms to process the visual data and respond effectively. The problem is further exacerbated by the high speeds at which autonomous vehicles operate; rapid movement allows pedestrians or other vehicles to emerge from occluded regions faster than the system can register and act.
Traditional camera systems face additional difficulties in situations where the visual history of an object is entirely absent, significantly impairing their ability to predict future trajectories. This limitation compromises the reliability of these systems, as their functionality relies heavily on constant visibility. Consequently, the inability to account for out-of-sight objects introduces severe safety risks, such as delayed reactions and potential collisions. Beyond immediate safety concerns, these challenges also hinder the scalability and practical implementation of autonomous driving technologies in complex and dynamic environments where occlusions are inevitable.
To bridge these gaps, our research introduces a vision-positioning denoising framework specifically designed to address the out-of-sight problem in trajectory prediction. By integrating multimodal data, including both noisy sensor inputs and visual information, our approach enhances the system’s ability to predict the trajectories of occluded agents with greater accuracy. This multimodal fusion not only mitigates safety risks but also offers a scalable and robust solution for autonomous systems, enabling them to operate reliably in unpredictable real-world scenarios.

Addressing the out-of-sight problem in trajectory prediction introduces two primary challenges, the first of which is managing noisy sensor measurements. Without access to visual timestamps, our approach relies on widely used localization sensors, such as GPS and odometers, to provide approximate spatial location data. This reliance arises from the inherent nature of the out-of-sight problem, where traditional visual observations are unavailable. However, as highlighted in prior studies, these sensor modalities are inherently prone to noise. For instance, GPS readings can exhibit errors ranging from 1 to 4 meters
[
11

Adding to this complexity is the lack of ground truth for denoised sensor trajectories, which precludes the use of traditional supervised learning techniques for denoising. To overcome this limitation, our research adopts an unsupervised learning framework to train the denoising model, enabling effective noise reduction without the need for labeled denoised data. This approach not only addresses the issue of noisy inputs but also lays the foundation for a more adaptable and scalable solution in out-of-sight trajectory prediction.

To address this issue, we propose leveraging visual information to aid in denoising sensor data. While our scenario inherently lacks direct visual observations for out-of-sight objects, object tracking algorithms can provide highly accurate and noise-free location data within image coordinates for visible entities. By utilizing these precise visual trajectories, we aim to refine and denoise the trajectory data obtained from noisy sensor inputs. This integration not only enhances the reliability of the sensor-derived trajectories but also provides a robust mechanism for noise mitigation. However, since direct visual observation of out-of-sight subjects is unavailable, establishing a connection between sensor data and visual coordinates becomes imperative. This introduces a second critical challenge—how to effectively map noisy sensor data to the visual domain in the absence of direct visual references. Solving this challenge is key to enabling a seamless fusion of sensor and visual information for out-of-sight trajectory prediction.

However, since the pedestrian is out-of-sight, implementing a denoising method with vision-positioning introduces a significant challenge: the absence of a direct visual reference, even when the localization data is assumed to be accurate. To overcome this limitation, it becomes crucial to establish a localization-to-vision mapping that bridges the gap between sensor data and the visual domain. Fortunately, the camera’s intrinsic matrix, which defines the internal parameters of the camera, remains consistent within a frame or across frames in stationary cameras. This consistency enables us to construct the necessary localization-vision mapping by estimating the intrinsic matrix. Importantly, all agents within the camera’s scene, such as pedestrians and vehicles, share the same intrinsic parameters when captured within the camera’s field of view. Leveraging this shared property, we can develop a model to predict the mapping relationship between sensor-derived localization trajectories and visual trajectories. By analyzing the motion patterns of visible agents (e.g., pedestrians and vehicles) captured in the camera’s view, this model can infer the mapping relationship, which is fundamentally associated with the camera’s intrinsic matrix.

Moreover, this approach is particularly advantageous in scenarios involving stationary cameras, such as video surveillance

For stationary cameras, a single mapping relationship suffices, as the ego-camera system’s camera-to-world mapping remains unchanged unless the camera itself moves. To ensure compatibility with moving ego-camera systems, we propose extending the approach by employing a sequence of mapping matrices. This sequence dynamically represents the changing localization-vision relationship over time, accommodating the shifting perspectives of the ego-camera as it moves. By adopting this flexible framework, our method effectively adapts to both stationary and moving cameras, providing a robust foundation for accurate trajectory prediction in diverse real-world scenarios.

Related Works

II-A

Vision-Wireless Fusion

The concept of vision-wireless fusion represents an emerging interdisciplinary domain that combines the complementary strengths of visual and wireless modalities to overcome their respective limitations. The visual modality, grounded in image processing and computer vision techniques, offers detailed and direct observational capabilities. However, its effectiveness is often hindered by challenges such as occlusions, constrained fields of view, high computational overhead, and its inability to handle out-of-sight scenarios. These challenges are especially pronounced in dynamic and complex environments, such as urban settings or industrial zones, where visual obstructions frequently occur.

On the other hand, the wireless modality provides extensive sensing and communication capabilities, utilizing technologies such as GPS, RFID, and sensor networks. These technologies excel in situations where visual data is unavailable or unreliable. Nonetheless, wireless modalities face their own set of challenges, including susceptibility to sensor noise
[
17
]
and environmental interference
[
5
]
, which can significantly degrade data quality. Furthermore, wireless data lacks the rich contextual information inherently present in visual observations, complicating tasks such as labeling and ground truth acquisition, which often depend on human interpretation aligned with visual inputs.

Despite these inherent limitations, the fusion of visual and wireless modalities has emerged as a promising avenue for addressing complex real-world challenges. Several pioneering works have demonstrated the potential of vision-wireless fusion. For instance, Liu
et al.

[
18
]
advanced the field by integrating wireless trajectories to enhance person reidentification, a task traditionally dominated by visual data. Their work addressed the complexities of human appearance variations, such as changes in clothing and posture, by leveraging the complementary strengths of wireless data. Similarly, Alahi
et al.

[
1

innovatively combined RGB-D cameras and Wi-Fi signal data to improve indoor localization, demonstrating how wireless signals from individuals’ smartphones could augment visual systems in navigation tasks. In another significant contribution, Papaioannou
et al.

[
21

applied vision-wireless fusion in dynamic industrial environments, effectively tracking individuals across modalities to ensure robust performance in high-stakes applications.

While these foundational studies have highlighted the potential of vision-wireless fusion in various tasks, a notable gap remains in leveraging visual data for denoising noisy sensor trajectories. This limitation is particularly critical in scenarios involving out-of-sight agents, such as autonomous driving, surveillance, and robotics, where sensor noise and the absence of visual context can significantly impair system reliability. Our research addresses this gap by focusing on the use of visual modalities to denoise sensor data, enabling more accurate and robust trajectory predictions. By bridging this gap, our work contributes to the advancement of vision-wireless fusion, particularly in enhancing the performance of systems operating in increasingly complex and unpredictable environments.

II-C

Denoising Sensor Signals

Traditional approaches, such as the Kalman filter, have been widely adopted for state estimation in dynamic systems under noisy observation conditions. The Kalman filter is particularly effective at minimizing uncertainty by optimally combining prior state estimates with new measurements, assuming the dynamics are linear and the noise is Gaussian. However, its utility diminishes in environments characterized by complex interactions, nonlinear dynamics, and external disturbances. Scenarios involving multi-agent interactions, intricate environmental structures, and varying road geometries often introduce nonlinearities and emergent behaviors that the Kalman filter’s linear framework cannot adequately model.

To address some of these shortcomings, variants like the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) and Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) have been developed, incorporating nonlinearity into their formulations. Yet, even these enhanced methods struggle to cope with highly dynamic systems where chaotic behaviors, such as multi-agent coordination or abrupt environmental shifts, dominate. Such scenarios frequently lead to the accumulation of noise over time, resulting in significant uncertainty amplification—a phenomenon often referred to as ”Cumulative Noise” or ”Butterfly Effects.” These effects can propagate unpredictably, further exacerbating the difficulty of accurate trajectory estimation. To overcome these challenges, researchers have explored more sophisticated alternatives, such as particle filters, physics-based simulations, and deep learning-based models. These approaches offer greater flexibility and adaptability, allowing for more accurate modeling of complex dynamics and environmental intricacies.

Our work builds on these advancements by incorporating visual modalities into the denoising process, addressing a critical limitation of existing approaches. By leveraging noise-free visual data as a reference for correcting noisy sensor trajectories, our framework bridges the gap between traditional denoising techniques and modern data-driven methods. This integration enables more reliable predictions, particularly in scenarios where visual data is unavailable for direct observation but can be inferred through localization-vision mapping. Our vision-positioning denoising framework represents a significant step forward in addressing the challenges of trajectory prediction in noisy, dynamic environments.

Symbol Annotations

N

A_{n},n=1,2,\ldots,N

A_{n}

III-B

Task Definition

Our primary objective is to tackle two interconnected challenges by leveraging the noisy sensor trajectory data
S_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
for agents that are out-of-sight:

1.

Develop an unsupervised denoising approach to refine the noisy sensor trajectory
S_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
, mitigating the adverse effects of sensor measurement noise on prediction accuracy.

2.

Accurately predict the noise-free future visual trajectories
V_{A_{o}}^{t_{e}:t_{p}}
for out-of-sight agents
A_{o}
in the camera frame coordinates, spanning from the last observation timestamp
t_{e}
to the target prediction timestamp
t_{p}
.

It is crucial to note that out-of-sight agents (
A_{o}
) lack visual trajectory references due to their absence from the camera’s field of view. Nonetheless, through the analysis of available signals from in-view agents (
A_{i}
), a robust mapping relationship between noisy sensor trajectories and visual trajectories can be inferred, as described in Sec.
III-A
. This allows us to address the complexities posed by environmental factors and occlusions.

Our proposed framework adopts a two-step approach: first, denoising and projecting noisy sensor trajectories into the visual modality in an unsupervised manner, leveraging signals from in-view agents as pseudo-supervision; second, employing a dedicated prediction module to generate precise, future visual trajectories for out-of-sight agents. This design ensures comprehensive handling of noisy data while enabling accurate trajectory forecasting in challenging real-world environments.

IV
Methodology

The overall architecture of our proposed method is depicted in Fig.
3
. Our approach is designed to predict noise-free future trajectories for out-of-sight agents by utilizing noisy sensor data without relying on direct denoising supervision or visual trajectory references.

To achieve this goal, we establish an unsupervised denoising pipeline that is integral to our framework. This pipeline aims to mitigate the noise inherent in sensor measurements before it adversely affects the trajectory prediction model. The design addresses the dual challenges of integrating visual references indirectly and constructing effective denoising supervision for out-of-sight agents. We decompose this sophisticated process into multiple distinct components, ensuring a holistic solution to the challenges posed by noisy and incomplete sensor data.

IV-A

Sensor Denoising Encoder (SDE)

At the foundation of our methodology lies the Sensor Denoising Encoder (SDE), previously referred to as the Mobile Denoising Encoder. The SDE is designed to function as an unsupervised denoising module that refines noisy sensor trajectories for out-of-sight agents, even in the absence of direct supervision. Its role is pivotal in mitigating the impact of sensor noise, which can significantly degrade prediction accuracy.

The SDE’s architecture comprises a Transformer model, surrounded by two fully connected layers—one preceding and one succeeding the Transformer layers. This architecture effectively captures the sequential dependencies in sensor trajectories and models the complex relationships inherent in the data.

The input to the SDE consists of noisy sensor trajectories, denoted as
S_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
, where
A_{o}\in\mathbb{A}_{out}
represents an out-of-sight agent sampled from the set of out-of-sight agents. The SDE processes these trajectories and outputs refined, denoised trajectories, referred to as noise-free sensor trajectories
\hat{S}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
. The operation of the SDE can be mathematically expressed as follows:

\hat{S}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}=\mathbf{E}_{denoise}(S_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}),~~~~~~o\sim\mathbb{A}_{out},

(1)

where
\mathbf{E}_{denoise}
represents the Sensor Denoising Encoder,
\mathbb{A}_{out}
denotes the set of out-of-sight agents, and
A_{o}
is a specific agent sampled from this set.

This denoising step plays a crucial role in ensuring that the noisy sensor data is accurately refined before being passed to subsequent modules for projection and prediction. By addressing noise at this foundational level, the SDE sets the stage for more reliable trajectory mapping and forecasting in the later stages of our framework.

However, the practical implementation of the Sensor Denoising Encoder (SDE) encounters a critical challenge: the ideal supervision in the form of noise-free real sensor trajectories,
\hat{S}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
, is inherently unavailable in real-world scenarios. The pervasive nature of sensor measurement noise complicates the possibility of obtaining such ideal data. In theory, one might consider employing high-accuracy sensors to collect data under identical conditions, which could serve as a proxy for noise-free trajectories. However, this approach presents significant practical limitations, as it is neither feasible for most datasets nor scalable across diverse application scenarios. Even under ideal conditions, higher-accuracy sensors would still introduce some level of residual measurement noise, albeit reduced, making the complete elimination of noise unattainable.

Given these constraints, constructing a truly noise-free real sensor trajectory for supervision remains beyond the capabilities of current sensor technologies. As a result, the direct use of such trajectories for supervised denoising, as suggested in Eq.
1
, becomes impractical.

To address this limitation, we shift our focus towards alternative strategies for constructing effective approximations of noise-free supervision. These strategies aim to replicate the conditions of an ideal noise-free trajectory while navigating the inherent limitations of existing sensor technologies. By leveraging innovative methods that rely on indirect supervision, such as vision-based inference and unsupervised denoising approaches, we approximate the idealized conditions necessary for refining noisy sensor data. This enables the SDE to perform effectively even in challenging real-world scenarios where direct noise-free supervision is not available.

IV-B

Visual-Positioning Denoising Module (VPD)

The Visual-Positioning Denoising Module (VPD) serves as the cornerstone of our denoising framework, leveraging visual information to construct noise-free supervision for the Sensor Denoising Encoder (SDE). This module addresses the intrinsic limitations of the sensor modality, which is susceptible to significant measurement noise and presents challenges for refinement. Visual data, in contrast, benefits from advancements in object detection and tracking technologies, providing highly accurate and reliable information. Moreover, the discretization inherent in image rasterization ensures that visual trajectories are naturally precise, straightforward to annotate, and effectively noise-free.

While the precision of visual trajectories makes them an ideal supervisory signal for trajectory prediction, their utility is constrained by the lack of visual data for out-of-sight agents. This limitation necessitates a robust method for translating denoised sensor trajectories into the visual domain. To accomplish this, we divide the translation process into three integral components: the Visual Positioning Projection Module (Sec.
IV-C
), the Mapping Parameters Estimator (Sec.
IV-D
), and the Denoising Loss (Sec.
IV-E
). These components collaboratively ensure the seamless mapping of sensor trajectories into the visual modality, bridging the gap between noisy sensor data and precise visual references. By integrating these elements, the VPD not only enhances the accuracy of trajectory prediction but also facilitates effective supervision in scenarios where visual data is inherently unavailable.

IV-C

Visual Positioning Projection Module (VPP)

The Visual Positioning Projection Module (VPP) is a fundamental component in our framework, responsible for mapping noisy sensor trajectories into the visual domain. Since visual trajectories are inherently recorded in the camera’s coordinate frame, the VPP focuses on transforming 3D world coordinates, represented by denoised sensor data, into 2D camera coordinates. This transformation aligns sensor-derived data with visual trajectories, leveraging geometric principles of camera calibration
[
26
]
.

The core of the VPP is the World-To-Camera Transformation, which computes a 2D point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1}
in the camera’s frame from a corresponding 3D point
P\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}
in world coordinates. Typically, the height dimension of the 3D point remains constant to simplify the projection from 3D to 2D. The mathematical formulation of this transformation is expressed as:

[p,1]^{T}=w\cdot K\cdot R_{t}\cdot[P,1]^{T},

(2)

where
K\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
denotes the camera’s intrinsic matrix, capturing parameters like focal length and optical center. The scale factor
w
adjusts the spatial scale for consistency with the camera’s field of view.
R_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 4}
represents the extrinsic matrix, combining rotation and translation parameters to define the camera’s position and orientation relative to the 3D world.

To streamline the transformation, we combine these parameters into a single matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 4}
, encapsulating all necessary transformations for converting world coordinates into camera coordinates. The simplified transformation equations are:

M=w\cdot K\cdot R_{t},

(3)

[p,1]^{T}=M\cdot[P,1]^{T}.

(4)

However, real-world scenarios necessitate considering the temporal variability of camera parameters. In stationary camera setups, such as surveillance systems,
R_{t}
remains fixed over time, simplifying the projection process. Conversely, in dynamic environments, where cameras are mounted on moving devices,
R_{t}
varies across timestamps. To account for this, we extend
M
into a temporal sequence, termed the Camera Matrix Embedding
M^{t_{s}:t_{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t_{e}-t_{s})\times 3\times 4}
, defined as:

M^{t_{s}:t_{e}}=w\cdot K\cdot R_{t}^{t_{s}:t_{e}},~~~~~~t\in[t_{s},t_{e}],

(5)

where
R_{t}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
and
M^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
represent the extrinsic rotation and translation matrices, and the Camera Matrix Embedding over timestamps from
t_{s}
to
t_{e}
, respectively.

By treating the noise-free real sensor trajectory
\hat{S}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
as a sequence of 3D world coordinates
P\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}
, we combine Eq.
4
and Eq.
5
to project
\hat{S}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
into the visual domain:

V_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}=M^{t_{s}:t_{e}}\cdot\hat{S}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}},~~~~~~t\in[t_{s},t_{e}],o\sim\mathbb{A}_{out},

(6)

where
V_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
represents the visual trajectory for an out-of-sight agent
A_{o}
. This transformation ensures that the denoised sensor trajectory is accurately mapped into the visual modality, enabling the effective use of visual information for trajectory prediction.

IV-D

Mapping Parameters Estimator (MPE)

While the Visual Positioning Projection (VPP) defined in Eq.
6
provides a method to map denoised sensor trajectories into the visual domain, the camera matrix
M^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
remains inaccessible for most datasets. Typically, datasets do not provide explicit camera matrices, and they cannot be directly extracted from the images. Furthermore, in practical applications involving dynamic systems, such as autonomous driving, mobile robots, or smartphones, the camera’s parameters, including its intrinsic and extrinsic properties, may change over time as the camera moves. This temporal variability significantly complicates the acquisition of the camera matrix sequence.

To address this challenge, we propose the Mapping Parameters Estimator (MPE), a module designed to predict the camera matrix embedding
M^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
dynamically by leveraging the correlation between visual and sensor trajectories of in-view agents. In dynamic environments, where multiple agents are simultaneously visible to the camera, both their visual trajectories (captured in the camera frame) and sensor trajectories (measured in the world frame) provide valuable clues about the underlying camera parameters. By analyzing these paired trajectories, the MPE can effectively infer the camera matrix embedding that governs the mapping relationship between the two coordinate systems.

The MPE module employs a transformer-based architecture augmented with fully connected layers positioned before and after the transformer. This design ensures the MPE’s capacity to process temporal and contextual dependencies inherent in trajectory data. The prediction process is formalized as follows:

M^{t_{s}:t_{e}}=\mathbf{E}_{mpe}(V_{A_{0}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}},S_{A_{0}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}},...,V_{A_{i}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}},S_{A_{i}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}),~~~~~~i\in\mathbb{A}_{in},

(7)

where
V_{A_{i}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
and
S_{A_{i}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
are the visual and sensor trajectories of in-view agents
A_{i}
, respectively. The set of in-sight agents is denoted by
\mathbb{A}_{in}
.
\mathbf{E}_{mpe}
represents the Mapping Parameters Estimator, responsible for predicting the camera matrix embedding
M^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
.

The MPE plays a pivotal role in dynamically estimating the camera matrix sequence across varying timestamps. By exploiting the complementary information provided by in-view agents’ visual and sensor trajectories, the MPE enables robust alignment of sensor data with the visual domain, even in scenarios involving moving cameras. This capability is essential for achieving accurate trajectory prediction in dynamic, real-world environments.

IV-E

Denoising Loss

In the preceding subsection, we established the process for obtaining the visual trajectory of out-of-sight agents using Eq.
6
. As we lack ground truth for noisy sensor trajectories, and since the visual modality is inherently noise-free and relatively easy to label or obtain, it serves as an effective supervisory signal for training the Sensor Denoising Encoder and the Mapping Parameters Estimator. By leveraging the noise-free characteristics of visual trajectories, we construct a robust supervision mechanism for the denoising process.

The denoising loss function, denoted as
\mathcal{L}_{Denoise}
, is defined as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{Denoise}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(V_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}},\overline{V}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}),

(8)

where
\mathcal{L}_{2}
represents the L2 loss function
[
2
]
, commonly used to minimize the squared differences between predicted and ground truth values. Here,
V_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
refers to the predicted visual trajectories of the out-of-sight agents, and
\overline{V}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{e}}
represents the ground truth visual trajectories. It is important to note that the ground truth visual trajectories for out-of-sight agents are available only during the training phase, allowing the model to learn the mapping and denoising processes effectively.

IV-F

Out-of-Sight Prediction Decoder (OPD)

To predict the future visual trajectories of out-of-sight agents, we propose an Out-of-Sight Prediction Decoder (OPD). Instead of predicting directly from noisy sensor trajectories, the OPD leverages the denoised visual trajectories obtained from Eq.
6
. By first transforming sensor data into the visual domain, the OPD ensures that predictions are based on noise-free, well-structured data, thereby improving accuracy and robustness.

The OPD employs a transformer-based model, chosen for its superior ability to handle sequential data and capture temporal dependencies. The future trajectory prediction process is defined as follows:

V_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{p}}=\mathbf{D}_{pred}(V_{A_{o}}^{t_{e}:t_{p}}),~~~~~~o\sim\mathbb{A}_{out},

(9)

where
V_{A_{o}}^{t_{e}:t_{p}}
denotes the observed visual trajectory of the out-of-sight agent
A_{o}
within the timestamp range
[t_{e}:t_{p}]
, and
V_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{p}}
represents the predicted future visual trajectory spanning timestamps
[t_{s}:t_{p}]
. The
\mathbf{D}_{pred}
represents the Out-of-Sight Prediction Decoder, while
\mathbb{A}_{out}
is the set of out-of-sight agents.

To train the OPD, we define a prediction loss
\mathcal{L}_{Pred}
, which minimizes the discrepancy between predicted and ground truth trajectories:

\mathcal{L}_{Pred}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(V_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{p}},\overline{V}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{p}}),

(10)

where
\mathcal{L}_{2}
is the L2 loss function,
V_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{p}}
is the predicted future visual trajectory, and
\overline{V}_{A_{o}}^{t_{s}:t_{p}}
is the corresponding ground truth. This loss ensures that the predicted trajectories closely match the actual future movements, enabling accurate and reliable out-of-sight trajectory predictions.

IV-G

Implementation Details

For training, the out-of-sight agents are randomly sampled from the complete set of agents present in a given scenario. All remaining agents that are fully visible in both the visual and noisy sensor modalities are treated as in-sight agents. These in-sight agents are utilized to estimate the camera parameters through the Mapping Parameters Estimator, enabling the model to derive the camera matrix embedding required for trajectory projection.

The wireless data used in this study consists of sensor localization signals, such as noisy GPS data or odometer readings, commonly sourced from mobile devices carried by agents. The ego-camera system (like autonomous vehicle)equipped with both a camera and a wireless receiver, collects sensor-based trajectories and visual trajectories of the agents within its environment. This combined dataset is then fed into our proposed model for processing and prediction. By leveraging both modalities, the system can predict noise-free visual trajectories for out-of-sight agents, even in challenging real-world scenarios.

Multimodal Dataset

The Vi-Fi dataset is a rich multimodal dataset explicitly curated for vision-wireless systems, focusing on linking pedestrian identities across visual and wireless modalities. Wireless data in this dataset are collected from smartphones carried by pedestrians, utilizing technologies such as Fine Time Measurement (FTM), Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), and noisy GPS. Simultaneously, an RGB-D camera surveillance system, accompanied by a wireless receiver, is either wall-mounted or bicycle-mounted to record pedestrian bounding boxes, ensuring synchronized acquisition of visual and wireless data. Notably, the wireless data in this dataset inherently exhibit substantial noise, which must be accounted for during processing.

The dataset includes 90 sequences, each approximately 3 minutes long, covering both indoor and outdoor environments. Indoor data were collected with five legitimate users and without any passersby, while outdoor data featured three active participants and 12 passersby. For our experiments, we used the noisy GPS signals from this dataset as representative noisy mobile trajectories and employed visual points as the visual modality. To simulate out-of-sight scenarios, we artificially obscured one pedestrian in each sequence, effectively creating a controlled environment for testing our methodology.

V-A
2
JackRabbot
[
19

Dataset (JRDB)

The JRDB dataset, collected from the JackRabbot social mobile robot, is specifically designed to support research in autonomous robot navigation and social robotics within human environments. It consists of 60,000 annotated frames, providing 2.4 million 2D human bounding boxes and 2.8 million 3D human bounding box annotations captured by a combination of 360° RGB cameras (five cameras) and LIDAR point clouds. This dataset contains both obstructed and in-sight pedestrians, with individual cameras covering a limited 72-degree field of view, resulting in instances of out-of-sight scenarios among agents.

The 3D and 2D bounding boxes are manually annotated but with low temporal resolution, necessitating upsampling through linear interpolation to achieve frame-level continuity. While this interpolation introduces some noise, it provides an essential basis for trajectory estimation. The obstructed pedestrians in LIDAR data are human-estimated in terms of size and location. For our experiments, we used the center points of the 3D bounding boxes as noisy sensor trajectories and the visual points as visual trajectories. To simulate out-of-sight conditions, we randomly selected a pedestrian to be obscured and utilized the visual trajectories of other visible pedestrians to estimate the camera parameters through our model.

V-B

Evaluation Setup

In both the JRDB and Vi-Fi datasets, we observe the initial 100 timestamps of out-of-sight sensor trajectories and predict the subsequent 100 timestamps in the visual modality. Simultaneously, we utilize paired in-sight visual trajectories and noisy sensor signals over 100 timestamps to estimate the camera matrix embeddings through the Mapping Parameters Estimator. This setup ensures the simultaneous evaluation of both denoising and prediction modules under realistic conditions.

V-E

Ablation Study

An ablation study was conducted to quantify the individual contributions of each module component within our model. By systematically removing one component at a time, we evaluated its impact on the overall performance. The results of this study are detailed in Table
II
, highlighting the critical role each module plays in achieving optimal performance.

The exclusion of the Camera Parameter Estimator (CPE) resulted in a significant drop in performance. Without the CPE, the model is unable to predict the camera matrix sequence necessary for establishing the visual-positioning relationship, effectively reducing the architecture to a simplified encoder-decoder structure. This leads to the loss of a core capability for bridging sensor and visual modalities. Similarly, the removal of the Mobile Denoising Encoder (MDE) caused an even more pronounced degradation in performance. Without the MDE, the model is unable to adequately filter noise from the sensor trajectories, allowing the noise to propagate unchecked into subsequent stages, including the Visual Positioning Projection (VPP) process.

The VPP module itself proved indispensable, as it explicitly incorporates geometric camera calibration to map noisy sensor trajectories into the visual domain. The absence of the VPP significantly impaired the model’s ability to project out-of-sight trajectories accurately, demonstrating its essential role in integrating domain knowledge into the learning process. Additionally, the removal of the Out-of-Sight Prediction Decoder (OPD) introduced challenges in trajectory prediction. Without the OPD, the model struggled to effectively generate future trajectory predictions, as it was forced to handle both denoising and sequence inference simultaneously, which diluted its predictive accuracy.

In summary, this ablation study underscores the necessity of each module in the proposed architecture. The Camera Parameter Estimator, Mobile Denoising Encoder, Visual Positioning Projection Module, and Out-of-Sight Prediction Decoder collectively contribute to achieving the state-of-the-art performance demonstrated by our model in noise-free out-of-sight trajectory prediction. Each module plays a synergistic role, addressing distinct aspects of the problem and reinforcing the robustness of the overall framework.

V-I

Comparison with Traditional Denoising Approaches

V-I
1
Baseline

Denoising sensor signals has long been a focus of research, with traditional methods like the Kalman filter
[
13
]
being widely adopted for this purpose. The Kalman filter’s popularity stems from its ability to estimate the state of a dynamic system under noisy observations by assuming linear dynamics and Gaussian noise. However, the application of deep learning-based methods for sensor signal denoising has gained traction only in recent years. A significant barrier to adopting supervised learning in this context is the lack of ground truth data. Since sensor trajectories are captured in real-world environments, dataset collectors cannot fully eliminate noise to create noise-free signals as ground truth. This limitation renders supervised learning infeasible and reinforces the reliance on traditional methods like the Kalman filter as preprocessing tools in signal processing pipelines. To evaluate the effectiveness of our approach, we select the Kalman filter as a baseline for comparison in this experiment.

V-I
2
Results Analysis

Traditional methods such as the Kalman filter operate under simplifying assumptions, including linear system dynamics and Gaussian noise, which restrict their applicability in capturing the complex interactions and non-linear behaviors inherent in real-world autonomous systems. These methods focus solely on denoising based on state transitions without accounting for dynamic environmental changes or the intricate relationships between agents in autonomous systems.

As shown in Table
V
, our proposed Vision-Positioning Denoising (VPD) model significantly outperforms the combination of the Kalman filter for denoising and a transformer model for trajectory prediction. Specifically, the VPD model demonstrates superior performance across both denoising and prediction metrics. This improvement highlights the VPD model’s ability to transcend the limitations of traditional methods by leveraging advanced geometric calibration and context-aware data fusion, enabling it to model complex environmental dynamics and multi-agent interactions effectively. These results emphasize the transformative potential of our approach in addressing the limitations of traditional denoising techniques for out-of-sight trajectory prediction tasks.

VI
Limitation Discussion

VI-A

Implicit Constraints in Camera Calibration

The maintaining calibration constraints is important. In many practical scenarios, intrinsic matrices are not included in datasets, and real-world camera operations such as zooming and autofocus can modify it. Furthermore, collecting extrinsic matrices is arduous and error-prone, as it involves solving PnP problems with noisy high-precision GPS data
[
16
]
. Considering the dynamic nature of cameras in autonomous vehicles and robotics, our method’s capability to estimate both matrices as a unified embedding allows for robustness in these more complex, realistic scenarios. When intrinsic matrices are available, our approach can seamlessly integrate them by directly multiplying the provided camera matrix embeddings, ensuring compatibility with traditional calibration methods.

VI-B

The Perception Distance of Out-of-Sight

Since the out-of-sight trajectory prediction model can involve agents very far from the objects, We need to consider the situation like what if multimoadl modality receive a very long distance signal. And furthermore, what is the most far distance can our model receive? Like if we receive a mobile signal from a few miles away, the performance of our model will definetly dropped.
Our model does not necessitate that in-sight and out-of-sight objects be at the same distance.
As both share the same camera projection matrix and the trajectories are captured at various distances over different timestamps, a strict distance parity is not essential. However, model generalization is contingent upon the data distribution; objects positioned at extreme distances could lead to out-of-distribution issues, potentially affecting performance. Currently, our model effectively covers distances within our dataset’s scope, and the performance at significantly farther distances, like several miles away, remains to be evaluated.

VII
Conclusion

Our study highlights a vital issue in the trajectory prediction literature and real-world scenarios, which assumes every agent can have a visual observation regardless of the completely out-of-sight agents. We propose a novel task that predicts the out-of-sight agents’ visual trajectories from noisy sensor trajectories. We propose a Visual-Positioning Denoising Module for this task, in which we solve the challenges of no visual reference by utilizing camera calibration to estimate the camera matrix sequences to construct a vision-positioning mapping. Additionally, we tackle another challenge, where noisy sensor trajectories are highly corrupted and lack supervision for denoising, by constructing supervision through the visual positioning projection.

Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate the superior performance of our pipeline and architecture, both in terms of denoising and trajectory prediction for out-of-sight agents. Our results clearly showcase the benefits of integrating visual positioning into the denoising process, which directly enhances the out-of-sight trajectory prediction performance. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to predict the trajectories of completely out-of-sight agents using noisy sensor trajectories. Furthermore, it is also the first to estimate vision-positioning projections to effectively denoise out-of-sight noisy sensor trajectories.

The methodologies and findings presented in this study pave the way for further research in the field of trajectory prediction, particularly in addressing complex real-world scenarios where visual observations of agents are not guaranteed, or the input signals are inherently noisy. By introducing a robust framework that seamlessly combines visual and sensor modalities, our work sets a strong foundation for future advancements aimed at improving the safety, reliability, and accuracy of autonomous systems operating in dynamic and unpredictable environments.
