Bad News for Couples: Tight Lower Bounds for Fair Division of Indivisible Items
Max Dupré la Tour

Abstract
We consider the problem of fairly allocating indivisible goods to couples, where each couple consists of two agents with distinct additive valuations. We show that there exist instances of allocating indivisible items to n n couples for which envy-freeness up to Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(\sqrt{n}) items cannot be guaranteed. This closes the gap by matching the upper bound of Manurangsi and Suksompong [ 13 ] , which applies to arbitrary instances with n n agents in total. This result is somewhat surprising, as that upper bound was conjectured not to be tight for instances consisting only of small groups.



1
Introduction

Fair division is a central problem in economics and theoretical computer science, with applications in resource allocation and task assignment. A prominent fairness notion is
envy-freeness
. When goods are indivisible, exact envy-freeness is often unattainable, which motivates approximate relaxations. A standard relaxation is
envy-freeness up to
c
goods
(EF
c
): for any two agents, any envy can be eliminated by removing at most
c
items from the other agent’s bundle.

Most of the literature studies allocations to individuals. In many practical settings, however, agents form small groups–such as couples or families–whose members receive a
shared
bundle but may have different preferences. In this group-allocation model, allocations are assigned to groups, while fairness is evaluated at the level of individuals, making guarantees substantially more delicate.

Manurangsi and Suksompong
[
13
]
initiated a systematic study of this model with
k
groups and
n
agents, where group sizes satisfy
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\dots\geq n_{k}
and
n=\sum_{i}n_{i}
. Using multicolor discrepancy results of Doerr and Srivastav
[
3
]
, they proved that an allocation always exists that is envy-free up to
O(\sqrt{n})
items, and they constructed instances where no allocation is envy-free up to
\Omega(\sqrt{n_{1}/k^{3}})
items. For a constant number of large groups, these bounds are tight up to constant factors.

The dependence on
k
in the lower bound was subsequently improved. Caragiannis, Larsen, and Shyam
[
2
]
proved a lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{n/(k\log k)})
, and Manurangsi and Meka
[
11
]
strengthened this further to
\Omega(\sqrt{n_{1}})
. A key ingredient in the latter result is a tight
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
multicolor discrepancy lower bound that is independent of the number of colors.

For bounded group sizes, however, an intriguing gap remained: while the general upper bound is
O(\sqrt{n})
, the known lower bounds were only
\Omega(1)
. At the other extreme, when
n_{1}=\dots=n_{k}=1
(i.e., no groups), an EF
1
allocation is always guaranteed
[
10
]
. This contrast led Manurangsi and Meka
[
11
]
to suggest that sharper guarantees might be possible when groups are small.

We show that this hope is unfounded: even the smallest nontrivial group structure already exhibits worst-case behavior.

Theorem 1
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist instances with
n
couples such that if an allocation is EF
c
, then
c\geq\sqrt{n-1}/96
.

Theorem
1
matches the worst-case
O(\sqrt{n})
guarantee for general instances, thereby closing the gap and showing that small groups do not improve worst-case approximate envy-freeness. Our proof is a simple reduction from the multicolor discrepancy lower bound. The hard instances are binary, and within each couple the two agents have complementary preferences: every item is valued by exactly one of the two agents.

1.1
Further related work

There is a rich literature on fair division for groups, spanning a range of settings and fairness notions. Examples include cake cutting
[
15
,
17
,
18
]
, maximin share guarantees
[
14
,
19
]
, asymptotic fairness under random valuations
[
12
]
, and models in which groups are not fixed but can be chosen to facilitate fairness
[
8
,
4
]
. We next summarize results most closely related to our setting; each of the cited papers contains many additional contributions beyond those we mention here.

In the two-group setting,
[
16
]
gave the first general existence guarantee, showing that for
n
agents split into two groups, an allocation satisfying EF
n
is guaranteed to exist. The first nontrivial lower bound,
\Omega(\log n)
, was proved by
[
8
]
, who also showed that EF
1
may not exist for two groups of three agents. Using multicolor discrepancy theory, these bounds were later sharpened in
[
13
,
2
,
11
]
: they currently stand at
O(\sqrt{n})
for the upper bound and
\Omega(\sqrt{n_{1}})
for the lower bound, where
n_{1}
is the size of the largest group. Discrepancy-based techniques have also been partially extended beyond additive valuations
[
9
,
6
]
, yielding an
O(\sqrt{n\log n})
upper bound for two groups of
n
agents.

Bu et al.
[
1
]
give an algorithm producing an EF
1
allocation for a couples model in which one member of each couple is a copy of the same agent. They also prove existence of an EF
1
allocation for two couples, and ask whether EF
1
continues to hold for more couples, or more generally what is the smallest
c
such that EF
c
is always guaranteed.

Gölz and Yaghoubizade
[
5
]
provide another proof of EF
1
existence for two couples, and further show that EF
1
may fail for three couples.

Finally, Kawase et al.
[
7
]
study a variant in which the
same
set of items must be removed to eliminate the envy of all members of a group.

2
Preliminaries

Let
M
be a set of
m
items. We consider
n
couples. Each couple
i\in[n]
consists of two agents with additive valuation functions
v_{i}^{1},v_{i}^{2}:2^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. An allocation is a partition of the items,
M=S_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup S_{n}
.
An allocation is
EF
c
if for all
i,j\in[n]
, there exist subsets
R^{1},R^{2}\subseteq S_{j}
such that
|R^{1}|\leq c
and
|R^{2}|\leq c
, and

v_{i}^{1}(S_{i})\geq v_{i}^{1}(S_{j}\setminus R^{1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{i}^{2}(S_{i})\geq v_{i}^{2}(S_{j}\setminus R^{2}).

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer. A
k
-coloring
of
M
is a function
\chi:M\to[k]
, which induces the partition

M=\chi^{-1}(1)\cup\cdots\cup\chi^{-1}(k).

Given
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\}
a family of
n
subsets of
M
, the
discrepancy
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as

\disc(\mathcal{A},k):=\min_{\chi:M\to[k]}\max_{\ell\in[k]}\ \max_{i\in[n]}\left|\frac{1}{k}|A_{i}|-|A_{i}\cap\chi^{-1}(\ell)|\right|.

For all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
\disc^{\max}(n,k):=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\ \sup_{A\in(2^{M})^{n}}\ \disc(A,k).
We will use the following lower bound.

Lemma 1
.

[Corollary 3.3. in
[
11
]
].
For any
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 2
, we have

\textstyle\disc^{max}(n,k)\geq\sqrt{n-1}/16.

3
Proof of Theorem
1

The theorem follows from combining Lemma
1
with the following Lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\}
be a family of
n
subsets of
M
.
For each
i\in[n]
, consider a couple with two valuations

v_{i}^{1}(S):=|A_{i}\cap S|\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{i}^{2}(S):=|\overline{A_{i}}\cap S|,

where
\overline{A_{i}}:=M\setminus A_{i}
.
Assume there exists an allocation
M=S_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup S_{n}
, that is
\mathrm{EF}c
for some integer
c\geq 1
with respect to all valuations
\{v_{i}^{1},v_{i}^{2}:i\in[n]\}
.

\disc(\mathcal{A},n)\leq 6c.

Proof.

Let
\chi:M\to[n]
be the coloring inducing this partition, i.e.
S_{j}=\chi^{-1}(j)
. By the definition of discrepancy with
k=n
, the coloring
\chi
gives the following upper bound:

\disc(\mathcal{A},n)\leq\max_{j\in[n]}\max_{i\in[n]}\left|\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}-|A_{i}\cap S_{j}|\right|.

Hence it suffices to prove that for every
i,j\in[n]
,

\left|\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}-|A_{i}\cap S_{j}|\right|\leq 6c.

(1)

The following claim directly follows from the definition of EF
c
combined with the fact that the valuation functions are additive and binary.

Claim 1
.

For every
i,j\in[n]
and each
r\in\{1,2\}
,

v_{i}^{r}(S_{j})\leq v_{i}^{r}(S_{i})+c.

(2)

The first step of the proof is to show that all bundles size are within
2c
of each other.

Claim 2
.

For all
i,j\in[n]
,

\bigl||S_{i}|-|S_{j}|\bigr|\leq 2c.

Consequently, for every
i\in[n]
,

\frac{m}{n}-2c\leq|S_{i}|\leq\frac{m}{n}+2c.

(3)

Proof of Claim
2
.

Fix
i,j\in[n]
.
Apply (
2
) with the two agents of couple
i
comparing bundle
S_{j}
to its own
S_{i}
.
For
r=1
we get
v_{i}^{1}(S_{j})\leq v_{i}^{1}(S_{i})+c
, and for
r=2
we get

v_{i}^{2}(S_{j})\leq v_{i}^{2}(S_{i})+c
. Adding:

v_{i}^{1}(S_{j})+v_{i}^{2}(S_{j})\leq v_{i}^{1}(S_{i})+v_{i}^{2}(S_{i})+2c.

But for any set
S
,

v_{i}^{1}(S)+v_{i}^{2}(S)=|A_{i}\cap S|+|\overline{A_{i}}\cap S|=|S|.

Therefore
|S_{j}|\leq|S_{i}|+2c
.
Swapping the roles of
i
and
j
yields
|S_{i}|\leq|S_{j}|+2c
. Let
s_{\min}:=\min_{i}|S_{i}|
and
s_{\max}:=\max_{i}|S_{i}|
.
The first part gives
s_{\max}-s_{\min}\leq 2c
.
Since
\sum_{i}|S_{i}|=m
, the average
m/n
lies between
s_{\min}
and
s_{\max}
, proving (
3
).
∎

Fix
i,j\in[n]
. We prove (
1
). Suppose, for contradiction, that

\left|\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}-|A_{i}\cap S_{j}|\right|>6c.

(4)

There are two cases.

Case 1:
|A_{i}\cap S_{j}|<\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}-6c
.

Then

v_{i}^{2}(S_{j})=|\overline{A_{i}}\cap S_{j}|=|S_{j}|-|A_{i}\cap S_{j}|>|S_{j}|-\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}+6c.

Using the lower bound
|S_{j}|\geq\frac{m}{n}-2c
from (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle v_{i}^{2}(S_{j})

\displaystyle>\left(\frac{m}{n}-2c\right)-\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}+6c

\displaystyle=\frac{m-|A_{i}|}{n}+4c=\frac{|\overline{A_{i}}|}{n}+4c,

where we used
m-|A_{i}|=|\overline{A_{i}}|
.
By Claim
1
applied to valuation
v_{i}^{2}
,

v_{i}^{2}(S_{i})\geq v_{i}^{2}(S_{j})-c>\frac{|\overline{A_{i}}|}{n}+3c.

Therefore,

v_{i}^{1}(S_{i})=|A_{i}\cap S_{i}|=|S_{i}|-v_{i}^{2}(S_{i})<\left(\frac{m}{n}+2c\right)-\left(\frac{|\overline{A_{i}}|}{n}+3c\right)=\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}-c,

using the upper bound
|S_{i}|\leq\frac{m}{n}+2c
from (
3
) and

m-|\overline{A_{i}}|=|A_{i}|
.

On the other hand,

\sum_{t=1}^{n}v_{i}^{1}(S_{t})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}|A_{i}\cap S_{t}|=|A_{i}\cap M|=|A_{i}|.

Hence the average value of
v_{i}^{1}(S_{t})
over
t
is
|A_{i}|/n
, so there exists

\ell\in[n]
with
v_{i}^{1}(S_{\ell})\geq\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}.
Combining with
v_{i}^{1}(S_{i})<\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}-c
gives
v_{i}^{1}(S_{\ell})>v_{i}^{1}(S_{i})+c
, contradicting Claim
1
. Thus Case 1 is impossible.

Case 2:
|A_{i}\cap S_{j}|>\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}+6c
.

Then
v_{i}^{1}(S_{j})=|A_{i}\cap S_{j}|>\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}+6c
.
By Claim
1
applied to valuation
v_{i}^{1}
,

v_{i}^{1}(S_{i})\geq v_{i}^{1}(S_{j})-c>\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}+5c.

Therefore,

\displaystyle v_{i}^{2}(S_{i})

\displaystyle=|\overline{A_{i}}\cap S_{i}|=|S_{i}|-|A_{i}\cap S_{i}|=|S_{i}|-v_{i}^{1}(S_{i})

\displaystyle<\left(\frac{m}{n}+2c\right)-\left(\frac{|A_{i}|}{n}+5c\right)=\frac{m-|A_{i}|}{n}-3c=\frac{|\overline{A_{i}}|}{n}-3c,

again using (
3
).
As before,

\sum_{t=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2}(S_{t})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}|\overline{A_{i}}\cap S_{t}|=|\overline{A_{i}}|,

so there exists
\ell\in[n]
with
v_{i}^{2}(S_{\ell})\geq|\overline{A_{i}}|/n
.
Thus
v_{i}^{2}(S_{\ell})>v_{i}^{2}(S_{i})+3c
, contradicting Claim
1
. Therefore Case 2 is impossible.

Since both cases lead to contradictions, (
4
) is false, and therefore
(
1
) holds for all
i,j\in[n]
. Hence
\disc(\mathcal{A},n)\leq 6c
, as claimed.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\}
be a family of
n
subsets of
M
such that
\disc(\mathcal{A},n)\geq\sqrt{n-1}/16
. Such a family exists by Lemma
1
. By Lemma
2
, if an allocation is EF

, then
c\geq\disc(\mathcal{A},n)/6\geq\sqrt{n-1}/96
.
