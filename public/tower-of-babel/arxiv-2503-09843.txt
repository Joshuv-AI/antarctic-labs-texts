The Computation of the Disguised Toric Locus of Reaction Networks
Gheorghe Craciun, Abhishek Deshpande, Jiaxin Jin

Abstract
Mathematical models of reaction networks can exhibit very complex dynamics, including multistability, oscillations, and chaotic dynamics. On the other hand, under some additional assumptions on the network or on parameters values, these models may actually be toric dynamical systems , which have remarkably stable dynamics. The concept of “ disguised toric dynamical system” was introduced in order to describe the phenomenon where a reaction network generates toric dynamics without actually being toric; such systems enjoy all the stability properties of toric dynamical systems but with much fewer restrictions on the networks and parameter values. The disguised toric locus is the set of parameter values for which the corresponding dynamical system is a disguised toric system. Here we focus on providing a generic and efficient method for computing the dimension of the disguised toric locus of reaction networks. Additionally, we illustrate our approach by applying it to some specific models of biological interaction networks, including Brusselator-type networks, Thomas-type networks, and circadian clock networks.

Introduction

Mathematical models of biochemical reaction networks are commonly described by polynomial dynamical systems
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
Studying the dynamical properties of these networks is essential for understanding the behavior of chemical and biological systems
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
]
.
In general, analyzing these systems is a challenging problem. Classical nonlinear dynamical properties, such as multistability, oscillations, and chaotic dynamics, are difficult to investigate
[
6
,
1
]
.

There is a particular class of dynamical systems generated by reaction networks, known as
complex-balanced systems

[
7
]
(or
toric dynamical systems

[
4
]
), which are known for their remarkably robust dynamics.
In particular, all positive steady states in these systems are locally asymptotically stable
[
7
,
1
]
. Further, these systems admit a unique positive steady state within each affine invariant polyhedron; moreover, oscillations or chaotic dynamics are excluded for this class of systems
[
7
]
.

However, the classical theory of complex-balanced systems has a limitation: to obtain a large set of parameter values (i.e., choices of reaction rate constants) that result in a complex-balanced system, the reaction network must satisfy additional graphical properties, namely
weak reversibility
and
low deficiency
(see
[
1
]
for details).
This limitation motivates the study of the notion of
dynamical equivalence
, which facilitates a significant relaxation of both restrictions.
Dynamical equivalence is based on the idea that two different reaction networks can generate the same dynamics for appropriately chosen parameter values. This phenomenon has also been referred to as
macro-equivalence

[
7
]
or
confoundability

[
8
]
.

The concept of a
disguised toric locus
was first introduced in
[
9
]
.
The disguised toric locus of a reaction network
G
is the set of positive reaction rate vectors for which the corresponding dynamical system can be realized as a complex-balanced system by some network
G^{\prime}
.
In other words, this locus consists of positive reaction rate vectors
\boldsymbol{k}
such that the mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
is dynamically equivalent to a complex-balanced system
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
.
Systems with reaction rate vectors in the disguised toric locus are called
disguised toric systems
, as they are dynamically equivalent to complex-balanced systems.
Several general properties of the disguised toric locus have been established
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. For example, it was shown in
[
11
]
that the disguised toric locus is path-connected.
In a recent paper
[
10
]
, we derived a formula (see Theorem
3.9
) for the dimension of the disguised toric locus.

In this paper, we develop a detailed approach for efficiently computing this formula. This is important and valuable because the formula established in
[
10
]
is purely theoretical, and translating it into a computationally feasible method has remained unclear, especially when dealing with large reaction networks.
A key challenge in this context is computing the dimension of the term
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
within the formula. This term represents the set of reaction rate constants
\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}
for the network
G^{\prime}
such that the system
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
is both complex-balanced and
\mathbb{R}
-realizable on the original network
G
. Specifically, we express it as

\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)=\underbrace{\mathcal{J}(G^{\prime})}_{\text{complex-balanced}}\cap\ \underbrace{\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)}_{\text{$\mathbb{R}$-realizable on $G$}}.

Thus, computing the dimension of
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
requires careful consideration of two distinct types of restrictions. One of the main results of this paper, Theorem
4.4
, proves that these two restrictions are independent in terms of dimension. Moreover, we propose an algorithm (see Algorithm
4
) that efficiently computes the dimension of
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
. This algorithm streamlines the process, making it feasible to apply to arbitrarily large networks.
In conjunction with the classic results on complex-balanced systems, Theorem
4.4
provides a straightforward formula for computing the dimension of the disguised toric locus of an E-graph.

To summarize, our work advances the theoretical framework of the disguised toric locus, making it accessible for real-world applications in reaction networks across diverse fields. In the application section, we demonstrate the utility of our approach by applying it to several biological models, including Brusselator-type, Thomas-type, and circadian clock models.

Definition 2.1

(a)

A

Definition 2.2

Let

(a)

A set of vertices in

(c)

An E-graph

Furthermore, if

Let

\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}=\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}).

(2.1)

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
be a mass-action system (
2.1

A state

is called a

of the system if

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})}^{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y})=\mathbf{0}.

A positive steady state

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}}{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})}^{\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}.

If a mass-action system

Toric dynamical systems are known for their robust graphical and dynamical properties. In

Flux Systems and Flux Equivalence

Definition 2.11
.

Let

(2.2)

, such that for every
\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E

J_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}=k_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}}.

Further, define the flux vector
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})=(J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}})_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}

J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}=k^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}}.

Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

The mass-action systems
(G,\boldsymbol{k})

(b)

The flux systems

Disguised Toric Locus and

In this section, we introduce the key concepts of this paper: the disguised toric locus
\mathcal{K}_{\text{disg}}(G)
and the
\mathbb{R}
-disguised toric locus
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G)
.
Additionally, we outline the method for determining the dimensions of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G)
and

Definition 3.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an E-graph. A dynamical system

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}),

is said to be
\mathbb{R}
-realizable

on
G
, if there exists some
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
such that

\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}).

(3.1)

In addition, the dynamical system is said to be
realizable

on
G
if
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}_{>0}
in (
3.1
).

Definition 3.2

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an E-graph. The
toric locus
of
G
is defined as

\mathcal{K}(G):=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{|E|}\ \big|\ (G,\boldsymbol{k})\ \text{is a toric dynamical system}\}.

A dynamical system is said to be
disguised toric
(or has a
toric realization
) on
G
if it is realizable on
G
for some
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{K}(G)
.

We are now prepared to define the disguised toric locus and the
\mathbb{R}
-disguised toric locus.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be two E-graphs.
The set
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G,G^{\prime})
is defined as

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G,G^{\prime}):=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}\ \big|\ \text{the dynamical system }(G,\boldsymbol{k})\ \text{is disguised toric on }G^{\prime}\}.

The
\mathbb{R}
-disguised toric locus
of
G
is given by

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G):=\displaystyle\bigcup_{G^{\prime}\sqsubseteq G_{c}}\ \mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G,G^{\prime}).

Define
\mathcal{K}_{\text{disg}}(G,G^{\prime}):=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G,G^{\prime})\cap\mathbb{R}^{|E|}_{>0}
, the
disguised toric locus
of
G
is given by

\mathcal{K}_{\text{disg}}(G):=\displaystyle\bigcup_{G^{\prime}\sqsubseteq G_{c}}\ \mathcal{K}_{\text{disg}}(G,G^{\prime}).

The set
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G,G^{\prime})
consists of all rate vectors
\boldsymbol{k}
for which the system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
can be realized in
G^{\prime}
with reaction rate constants belonging to the toric locus of
G^{\prime}
.
The
\mathbb{R}
-disguised toric locus
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G)
is defined as the union of all sets
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G,G^{\prime})

G^{\prime}
is a weakly reversible subgraph of
G_{c}
.
Similarly, the set
\mathcal{K}_{\text{disg}}(G,G^{\prime})
and the
disguised toric locus

are defined analogously to
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G,G^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G)
, but with the additional requirement that the rate vectors satisfy
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}_{>0}

Remark 3.4
.

The restriction to graphs
G^{\prime}
satisfying
G^{\prime}\sqsubseteq G_{c}
when defining the disguised toric locus and the

-disguised toric locus is well-justified.
In principle,
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G)
is intended to include all vectors
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
such that the system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
has a toric realization with respect to any (arbitrary) graph
\tilde{G}
.
However, as shown in
[

that can also give rise to a toric realization of the same dynamical system.
Consequently, restricting

to weakly reversible subgraphs of

does not alter the definition of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{R}\text{-disg}}(G)

4
Computing the Dimension of the Disguised Toric Locus

Theorem
3.9
provides a formula for
\dim(\mathcal{K}_{\text{disg}}(G))
.
Definition
2.1
, along with Remarks
2.8
and
2.15
, offers a framework for computing
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{G^{\prime}})
,
\dim(\mathcal{D}_{\textbf{0}}(G))
, and
\dim(\mathcal{J}_{\textbf{0}}(G^{\prime}))
, respectively.
However, the computation of
\dim(\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))
in this formula remains unresolved, preventing the full determination of the disguised toric locus dimension.

In this section, we address this gap by providing a method for computing
\dim(\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))
, which is the main result of this paper. We begin by defining two sets as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be two E-graphs.

(a)

The set
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G)
is defined as

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G):=\{\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}\ \big|\ \sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in E}{J}_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E}{J}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\ \text{for every vertex }\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in V\}.

(b)

The set
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
is defined as

\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G):=\{\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}\ \big|\ \text{the flux system }(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime})\ \text{is $\mathbb{R}$-realizable on }G\}.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be two E-graphs.

(a)

Two sets
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
and
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
are both linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}
.

(b)

Consider
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
from Definition
3.5
. Assume that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\neq\emptyset
, then

\dim(\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))=\dim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big).

(4.1)

Proof.

(a)
By direct computation, Definition
4.1
shows that the set
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
forms a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}
.
We now prove that
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{1},\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
and two real values
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since both flux systems
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{1})
and
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{2})
are
\mathbb{R}
-realizable on G, Definition
3.5
shows that there exist
\boldsymbol{J}_{1},\boldsymbol{J}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
, such that for every vertex
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in V\cup V^{\prime}
,

\begin{split}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}\in E}J_{1,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0})&=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{1,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0}),\\
\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}\in E}J_{2,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0})&=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{2,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0}).\end{split}

(4.2)

Consider
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}=\alpha_{1}\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}
. From (
4.2
), we obtain that

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}\in E}(\alpha_{1}J_{1,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}}+\alpha_{2}J_{2,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}})(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{1,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0}).

Thus,
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
and
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}
.

(b)
From Definition
3.5
, we have

\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)=\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)\cap\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}_{>0}.

(4.3)

Part (a) shows both
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
and
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
are linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}
. This implies that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
is also a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}
.
From (
4.3
) and the assumption that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\neq\emptyset
,
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
is an open cone and thus we conclude (
4.1
).
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be a weakly reversible E-graph and let
G=(V,E)
be an E-graph.
Given a vector

\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}})_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E^{\prime}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G),

let
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be an arbitrary real vector. Consider the following expression:

\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}=(\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}})_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E^{\prime}}\ \text{ with }\ \boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}=\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}w_{i}.

(4.6)

Then, we have the following property:

(a)

\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
for any
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

(b)

\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
if and only if

\boldsymbol{w}\in\ker(\tilde{J})
, where
\tilde{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is the Kirchoff matrix defined as

\tilde{J}_{ij}=\begin{cases}-\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}}&\text{ if $i=j$},\\[5.0pt]
\quad J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{i}}&\text{ if $i\neq j$ and $\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in E^{\prime}$},\\[5.0pt]
\quad 0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4.7)

Proof.

(a)
Since
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
, from Definition
3.5
there exists some
\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
, such that for every vertex
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in V\cup V^{\prime}
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}\in E}J_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}).

This, together with (
4.6
), implies that for every vertex
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in V\cup V^{\prime}
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}\in E}J_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}).

Thus, we prove part (a).
Recall the notation in (
4.4
); this further indicates that

\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\ker\left(B=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\intercal}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\intercal}_{d_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\right)\ \text{ for any }\ \boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(4.8)

(b)
We now identify the set of vectors
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
.
From Definition
4.1
and assumption,
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
denotes the set of vectors
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}})_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E^{\prime}}
satisfying that for any
1\leq i\leq\ell
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in E_{i}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E_{i}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\ \text{ for every vertex $\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in V_{i}$}.

(4.9)

For each
1\leq i\leq\ell
, let
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{i}}
denote the components of
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
corresponding to the reactions within the

L_{i}
, and define the matrix
\tilde{A}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times r_{i}}
as follows:

\tilde{A}^{(i)}_{jk}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $\boldsymbol{y}_{i,j}$ is the source vertex in the $k$-th reaction},\\
-1&\text{ if $\boldsymbol{y}_{i,j}$ is the target vertex in the $k$-th reaction},\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

From (
4.9
),
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
is equivalent to the following condition:

\tilde{A}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}(i)}=\mathbf{0}\ \text{ for every $1\leq i\leq\ell$}.

(4.10)

Recall the Kirchhoff matrix
\tilde{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
defined in (
4.7
). The condition (
4.10
) can be rewritten as

\tilde{J}\begin{pmatrix}w_{1}\\
\vdots\\
w_{m}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{0}.

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
if and only if
\boldsymbol{w}\in\ker(\tilde{J})
.
∎

From Lemma
4.2
, the dimension of
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
is determined by the complex-balanced flux condition and the
\mathbb{R}
-realizable condition.
The following theorem establishes a key property: when computing
\dim(\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))
, these two conditions operate independently.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be a weakly reversible E-graph and let
G=(V,E)
be an E-graph.
Assume that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\neq\emptyset
, then

\codim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)=\codim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\big)+\codim\big(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big).

(4.11)

Proof.

By direct computation,

\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)^{\perp}=\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})^{\perp}+\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)^{\perp}.

(4.12)

This implies that

\codim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)\leq\dim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})^{\perp}\big)+\dim\big(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)^{\perp}\big)=d_{1}+d_{2}.

To prove (
4.11
), it suffices to show that the above inequality holds as an equality.
We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that

\codim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)<\dim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})^{\perp}\big)+\dim\big(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)^{\perp}\big).

This, together with (
4.12
), shows that

\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{d_{1}},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{d_{2}}\}\ \text{ are linearly dependent}.

(4.13)

On the other hand, recall the matrices
\tilde{A}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times r_{i}}
for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
, and the linear conditions (
4.9
) on
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
in Lemma
4.3
.
Kirchhoff’s junction rules indicate that every linkage class with
m_{i}
vertices imposes
(m_{i}-1)
independent constraints among the conditions in (
4.9
), implying that

\text{the row rank of $\tilde{A}^{(i)}$}=\rank(\tilde{A}^{(i)})=m_{i}-1\ \text{ for every $1\leq i\leq\ell$}.

Since
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
belongs to the left null space of
\tilde{A}^{(i)}
for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
, it follows that for every
1\leq i\leq\ell
,

\text{the first $(m_{i}-1)$ row vectors of $\tilde{A}^{(i)}$ are linearly independent}.

Moreover, Kirchhoff’s junction rules state that the constraints from distinct linkage classes are independent. This implies that the collection of these
(m_{i}-1)
row vectors of
\tilde{A}^{(i)}
for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
forms a basis for
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})^{\perp}
.
Without loss of generality, we select this collection of vectors as
\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\intercal}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\intercal}_{d_{1}}\}
, and thus

d_{1}=\sum\limits^{\ell}_{i=1}(m_{i}-1)=\sum\limits^{\ell}_{i=1}m_{i}-\ell.

(4.14)

From (
4.13
) and the fact that
\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{1}}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d_{2}}
form bases for
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})^{\perp}
and
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)^{\perp}
, respectively, we assume without loss of generality that

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{d_{1}}\text{ is linearly dependent with $\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{d_{1}-1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{d_{2}}\}$}.

(4.15)

Using (
4.8
) in Lemma
4.3
and (
4.15
), we conclude that
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\ker(A)
if and only if

\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\ker\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\intercal}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\intercal}_{d_{1}-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.16)

From (
4.7
) in Lemma
4.3
, the condition in (
4.16
) is equivalent to the following:

\tilde{J}_{(d_{1}-1)}\begin{pmatrix}w_{1}\\
\vdots\\
w_{m}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{0},

where
\tilde{J}_{(d_{1}-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{1}-1)\times m}
denotes the submatrix consisting of the first
(d_{1}-1)
row vectors of
\tilde{J}
. This, together with (
4.5
) and (
4.16
), shows that

\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\ \text{ if and only if }\ \boldsymbol{w}\in\ker(\tilde{J}_{(d_{1}-1)}).

(4.17)

From
\tilde{J}_{(d_{1}-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{1}-1)\times m}
and (
4.14
), it follows that

\dim\big(\ker(\tilde{J}_{(d_{1}-1)})\big)\geq m-(d_{1}-1)=\ell+1.

(4.18)

Recall from Lemma
4.3
that
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
if and only if
\boldsymbol{w}\in\ker(\tilde{J})
.
Since
G^{\prime}
is weakly reversible and consists of
\ell
linkage classes with
m
vertices, Kirchhoff’s junction rules show that there exists
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, such that

\ker(\tilde{J})=\spn\{\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}\}\ \text{ and }\ \dim\big(\ker(\tilde{J})\big)=\ell.

However, this further implies that
\rank(\tilde{J})=m-\ell
, and

\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\ \text{ if and only if }\ \boldsymbol{w}\in\spn\{\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}\}.

This contradicts the conditions specified in (
4.17
) and (
4.18
). Therefore, we conclude the theorem.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be a weakly reversible E-graph with
\ell
linkage classes and let
G=(V,E)
be an E-graph.
Assume that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\neq\emptyset
, then

\dim(\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))=\dim\big(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)-|V^{\prime}|+\ell.

(4.19)

Proof.

From Lemma
4.2
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})
and
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
are both linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}
. Thus,

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\ \text{is a linear subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}$}.

Together with (
4.11
) in Theorem
4.4
, this implies

\begin{split}\dim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)&=|E^{\prime}|-\codim\big(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)-\codim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\big)\\
&=\dim\big(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)-\codim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\big).\end{split}

(4.20)

From (
4.14
), we deduce that

\codim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\big)=|V^{\prime}|-\ell.

(4.21)

Using (
4.21
) and (
4.1
) in Lemma
4.2
, we obtain

\dim(\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))=\dim\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(G^{\prime})\cap\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)=\dim\big(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\big)-(|V^{\prime}|-\ell),

and thus we conclude (
4.19
).
∎

We conclude this section by outlining an algorithm to compute
\dim(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))
as given in (
4.19
) of Theorem
4.5
, followed by a proof of its correctness.

Algorithm 1
(Compute the dimension of
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
).

1:

Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be two E-graphs.
Assume that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\neq\emptyset
.

2:

For each vertex
\boldsymbol{y}\in V^{\prime}
, collect the reaction vectors in
G
with source vertex
\boldsymbol{y}
and compute their span, denoted by
S_{\boldsymbol{y}}
.

3:

Determine a basis for
S_{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\perp}
, denoted by
\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{p}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Construct the matrix:

A_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\intercal}_{1}\\[5.0pt]
\vdots\\[5.0pt]
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\intercal}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}.

4:

Collect the reaction vectors with source vertex
\boldsymbol{y}
in
G^{\prime}
, denoted by
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}\}
.
Construct the matrix:

B_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\ \ldots\ \boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}.

5:

Compute
\dim(\ker(A_{\boldsymbol{y}}B_{\boldsymbol{y}}))
. Iterate this process for all vertices in
V^{\prime}
, then

\dim(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\in V^{\prime}}\dim(\ker(A_{\boldsymbol{y}}B_{\boldsymbol{y}})).

Now we prove the correctness of Algorithm
4
via the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be two E-graphs.
Assume
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\neq\emptyset
, and follow the notation in Algorithm
4
. Then

\dim(\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\in V^{\prime}}\dim(\ker(A_{\boldsymbol{y}}B_{\boldsymbol{y}})).

(4.22)

Proof.

From Definitions
3.5
and
4.1
, any flux system
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime})
with
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
is
\mathbb{R}
-realizable on
G
. Since
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\neq\emptyset
, there exists some
\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
such that for every vertex
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in V\cup V^{\prime}
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}\in E}J_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0}).

(4.23)

Moreover, every vertex in
V\setminus V^{\prime}
yields an empty sum from the left-hand side of (
4.23
), so the vector
\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
must yield a zero sum from the right-hand side of (
4.23
).

Therefore, it suffices to identify
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E^{\prime}|}
that satisfies (
4.23
) for the vertices in
V^{\prime}
.
Moreover, for any vertex
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in V^{\prime}
, (
4.23
) is equivalent to

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0})\in S_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}.

(4.24)

Let
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}}=(\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}})_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}
denote the components of
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}
corresponding to the reactions with the source vertex
\boldsymbol{y}
. Thus, condition (
4.24
) can be rewritten as

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0})=B_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}\in\ker(A_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}).

(4.25)

This implies that
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}\in\ker(A_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}}B_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}})
.
Since
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}
is a collection of all components corresponding to the source vertices in
V^{\prime}
, and these components partition
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}
, it follows that (
4.22
) holds.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Given two E-graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
, the set
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
collects the flux vectors
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
such that
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime})
is
\mathbb{R}
-realizable on
G
. Thus, every vertex
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in V^{\prime}
satisfies that

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}\in E}J_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0}).

This implies that the
net flux vector
from every vertex in
V^{\prime}
needs to be
\mathbb{R}
-realizable on
G
.
The result of Lemma
4.6
can be interpreted geometrically. Due to dynamical equivalence and the differences in reactions between two E-graphs, each vertex in
V^{\prime}
possesses a certain degree of freedom regarding its realizability in
G
. The dimension of
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
represents the aggregate degrees of freedom across all vertices in
V^{\prime}
.

For example, consider the two E-graphs
G,G^{\prime}
in Figure
2
of Example
5.1
. It can be verified that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)\neq\emptyset
.

(a)

For vertices
X,3Y,X+2Y
, the reactions originating from them are identical in both
G
and
G^{\prime}
. Thus, the degrees of freedom in selecting vectors correspond to the total number of edges originating from these vertices (i.e.,
1+1+2=4
).

(b)

For vertex
Y
, there are two more reactions originating from it in
G^{\prime}
than in
G
, namely
Y\to 3Y
and
Y\to X
.
To ensure that the net flux vector from
Y
is
\mathbb{R}
-realizable on
G
, the reaction rate constants
k_{Y\to 3Y}
and
k_{Y\to X}
must satisfy a proportionality constraint. Consequently, the degrees of freedom in selecting vectors correspond to the number of edges from
Y
in
G^{\prime}
decreased by one due to the restriction (i.e.,
3-1=2
).

Finally, the dimension of
\mathfrak{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G)
is the sum of the degrees of freedom from all vertices, which gives
4+2=6
(see more details in Example
5.1
).

Applications

In this section, the results on the disguised toric locus are applied to examples motivated by biochemical and cellular processes.
Specifically, the dimension of the disguised toric locus is explicitly computed for these examples, revealing that it attains full dimension within the corresponding parameter space.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we have developed methods to compute the dimension of the disguised toric locus of a reaction network.
Our results build upon and advance prior work
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
Notably, Theorem
4.4
shows the independence of the two key factors (complex-balanced conditions and
\mathbb{R}
-realizable conditions) when determining
\dim(\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{R}}(G^{\prime},G))
.
To demonstrate the applicability of our methods, we applied it to several models, including Brusselator-type models, Thomas-type models, and Circadian clock models, all of which have significant biological relevance.

While this work focuses primarily on computation, the approach introduced here opens several directions for future research. One promising direction is to identify conditions under which the dimension of the disguised toric locus of a reaction network attains full dimension in the parameter space. Recent work
[
10
]
provided a formula for the dimension of the disguised toric locus. Building on this, we aim to establish the conditions on network structure that ensure the disguised toric locus forms an open subset in the parameter space.

Another potential research direction involves characterizing the conditions under which the disguised toric locus is a smooth manifold. In
[
39
]
, it was established that the toric locus of a reaction network is a smooth manifold.
Extending this result to the disguised toric locus can help in exploring the regularity of steady states within the disguised toric locus.
