Bell state measurements in quantum optics: a review of recent progress and open challenges
Luca Bianchi, Carlo Marconi, Davide Bacco

Abstract
Bell state measurements, which project bipartite qubit systems onto the maximally entangled Bell basis, are central to a wide range of quantum information processing tasks, including quantum teleportation, entanglement swapping, and fusion-gate quantum computation. In photonic quantum platforms, where information is encoded in optical degrees of freedom, the realization of efficient Bell state measurements is particularly challenging, especially when constrained to linear optical elements. In this review, we provide a comprehensive examination of existing proposals for implementing Bell state measurements, highlighting their fundamental limitations and the strategies developed to overcome them. Additionally, we survey recent advances in Bell state measurements for high-dimensional systems, an area of growing interest due to its relevance in scalable quantum networks and high-capacity quantum communication.



I
Introduction

The first quantum revolution gave rise to technologies such as transistors, lasers, and integrated circuits, fundamentally transforming modern society. Building on this foundation, the second quantum revolution aims to exploit uniquely quantum phenomena, such as entanglement, to unlock new possibilities in information processing and communication.
Indeed, the role of entanglement as a resource in quantum information is evident across a wide range of protocols, including quantum teleportation
Bennett et al. 1993
;
Pirandola et al. 2015
, superdense coding
Bennett and Wiesner 1992
;
Mattle et al. 1996
, and quantum key distribution
Bennett and Brassard 2014
;
Pirandola et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2020
, among others. Like any other resource, entanglement can be quantified using appropriate measures
Plenio and Virmani 2014
, which naturally leads to the notion of maximally entangled states, providing the greatest utility for certain tasks. For multipartite systems, identifying such states is a challenging task, as different entanglement measures may not agree on which states are the most entangled
Dür et al. 2000
;
Acín et al. 2001
. However, a striking simplification arises in the bipartite case, where all entanglement measures coincide
Vidal 2000
. This feature allows for a unique definition of maximally entangled states in bipartite systems which, up to local unitary transformation, correspond to the so-called
Bell states

Nielsen and Chuang 2001
.
For qubit systems, the four Bell states are defined as

\left|\Psi^{\pm}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\left|00\right\rangle_{L}\pm\left|11\right\rangle_{L})\penalty\ ,\qquad\left|\Phi^{\pm}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\left|01\right\rangle_{L}\pm\left|10\right\rangle_{L})\penalty\ ,

(1)

where
\left|i\right\rangle_{L}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
denotes a logical qubit state, with
i=0,1
. Moreover, such states form an orthonormal basis for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, so that any two-qubit quantum state can be expressed as a linear combination of the Bell states in Eq.(
1
).
A Bell state measurement (BSM)
Bertlmann and Zeilinger 2002
is a joint measurement on a pair of qubits that projects them onto the Bell basis. The ability to perform a complete and efficient BSM is a fundamental requirement in several quantum information protocols.
For instance, in
quantum teleportation
, BSM is used to transfer an unknown quantum state from one party to another using shared entanglement and classical communication
Boschi et al. 1998
;
Pirandola et al. 2015
. In
entanglement swapping

Żukowski et al. 1993
, BSM allows two previously unentangled particles to become entangled, thereby extending the reach of entanglement across quantum networks
Pan et al. 1998
. This principle underlies the operation of quantum repeaters, which are essential for long-distance quantum communication. Moreover, in the context of
fusion-gate
quantum computation
Ralph et al. 2002
;
Bartolucci et al. 2023
, Bell measurements enable the construction of large-scale entangled states by merging smaller entangled building blocks. Finally, in the context of
quantum key distribution
, BSMs lie at the core of protocols that allow for establishing a secret key even when the measurement devices cannot be trusted
Lo et al. 2012
.
In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of BSMs, exploring both their theoretical foundations and experimental realizations, while emphasizing their crucial role in advancing quantum technologies. Given the versatility of photonic platforms for encoding quantum states, this review focuses on quantum optical implementations of BSMs, examining the advantages and limitations of various approaches and highlighting ongoing advances aimed at improving their efficiency.

This review is structured as follows. In section
II
, we recall some basic aspects of qubit systems and their representation in quantum optics. Section
III
provides an overview of the main techniques for implementing BSMs in qubit systems, discussing the theoretical limitations on the success probability and detailing various strategies to overcome these constraints. In section
IV
, we shift our focus to qudit systems, discussing the most common photonic degrees of freedom used for their encoding. Section
V
then reviews existing techniques for performing efficient high-dimensional BSMs. Since the previous sections focus on discrete-variable encodings, section
VI
addresses the case of continuous-variable systems. In section
VII
, we explore key applications of BSMs in quantum information science, with an emphasis on recent experimental advances. Finally, section
VIII
presents some concluding remarks.

II
Qubit systems

In the framework of quantum optics, photonic states can be described using two different encodings. In the so-called
single-rail
encoding (see e.g.,
Furusawa and Van Loock 2011
), the state of a qubit is represented within a single optical mode, e.g. in its Fock basis
\{\left|0\right\rangle,\left|1\right\rangle\}
, and a generic photonic state takes the form
\left|\psi\right\rangle=\alpha\left|0\right\rangle+\beta\left|1\right\rangle
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
satisfy the normalization constraint
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
. However, this encoding is not particularly convenient for quantum optics, since it would require nonlinear interactions to implement simple transformations on single qubits.
A more suitable representation of the state of a photonic system is given by the
dual-rail
encoding, where two orthogonal modes are used. In this sense, common choices are the polarization modes (e.g., vertical and horizontal) or the two spatial modes of a beamsplitter. Hence, using this type of encoding, the logical qubit states take the form
\left|0\right\rangle_{L}=\left|10\right\rangle,\left|1\right\rangle_{L}=\left|01\right\rangle
, so that the Bell states of Eq. (
1
) can be expressed as

\left|\Psi^{\pm}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\left|1010\right\rangle\pm\left|0101\right\rangle)\penalty\ ,\qquad\left|\Phi^{\pm}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\left|1001\right\rangle\pm\left|0110\right\rangle\penalty\ .

(2)

In the following, unless explicitly stated otherwise, all photonic states will be expressed in dual-rail encoding.
Finally, we recall that dual-rail encoded photonic states can be equivalently expressed in terms of a set of bosonic operators
\{\hat{a}_{k},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\}
such that
[\hat{a}_{k},\hat{a}_{k^{\prime}}]=[\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k^{\prime}}]=0
and
[\hat{a}_{k},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k^{\prime}}]=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}
, where
\hat{a}_{k},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}
denote the operators annihilating and creating a photon on mode
k
, respectively. Hence, Eq.(
2
) can be cast as

\displaystyle\left|\Psi^{\pm}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{3}\pm\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{4})\left|\text{vac}\right\rangle\penalty\ ,\qquad\left|\Phi^{\pm}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{3}\pm\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{4})\left|\text{vac}\right\rangle\penalty\ ,

(3)

where
\left|\text{vac}\right\rangle
denotes the total photonic vacuum state.

III
Bell state measurements for qubit systems

The first proposal for a BSM in quantum optics appeared in
Braunstein and Mann 1995
;
Weinfurter 1994
and was subsequently implemented in
Bouwmeester et al. 1997
, where it was used to realize a quantum teleportation protocol.

Figure 1:
Scheme for BSM

Typically, the experimental implementation of a measurement in the Bell basis exploits the Hong-Ou-Mandel (HOM) effect
Hong et al. 1987
, by superimposing two initially uncorrelated photons on a
50/50
beam splitter. Then, single-photodetection measurements are performed at the output of the beamsplitter. A coincidence measurement between the two detectors signals a successful BSM, corresponding to the collapse of the quantum state into one of the Bell states of Eq.(
1
). However, this simple scheme can distinguish only two of the four Bell states, thus resulting in an overall
50\%
efficiency for this discrimination task.
For some time, it remained unclear whether the inherently probabilistic nature of this process was a consequence of the specific experimental implementations or, instead, was rooted in a deeper physical limitation. This ambiguity was finally resolved in
Lütkenhaus et al. 1999
;
Vaidman and Yoran 1999
, where it was demonstrated that a complete BSM (that is, with
100\%
success probability) cannot be achieved using only linear-optical components. This naturally raises the question of whether there exists a fundamental limit on the success probability. This question was answered in
Calsamiglia and Lütkenhaus 2001
, where the authors showed that, in the case of a dual-rail encoding, the success probability of a BSM is bounded from above by
50\%
, thereby confirming the optimality of earlier experimental schemes.
Nevertheless, the above limitations can be circumvented by resorting to auxiliary photons, nonlinear interactions, and hyper-entanglement
Lütkenhaus et al. 1999
. In the following, we inspect these strategies, providing a detailed analysis of the benefits and the main limitations related to each of them.

III.1
Boosting the success probability of qubit Bell-state measurements

III.1.1
Auxiliary states

The first proposal to use auxiliary photons in the context of BSM for qubit systems appeared in
Grice 2011
. In that work,
N-1
entangled photonic states
\left|\Gamma_{j}\right\rangle
, with
j=1,\dots,N-1
, were employed alongside a standard Bell measurement setup to identify an unknown Bell state
\left|\zeta\right\rangle
. The core of this approach is to introduce additional quantum correlations via auxiliary photons, enhancing the efficiency of the measurement scheme in the discrimination of all four Bell states. Each of the
N-1
auxiliary photons is described by an entangled state of the form

\left|\Gamma_{j}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{H,2^{j}+1}\dots\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{H,2^{j+1}}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{V,2^{j}+1}\dots\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{V,2^{j+1}}\right)\left|\text{vac}\right\rangle\penalty\ ,

(4)

where
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{X,k}
is the bosonic creation operator for a photon in mode
k
with polarization
X\in\{H,V\}
.
Since each
\left|\Gamma_{j}\right\rangle
contributes an even number of photons, all with identical polarization, the overall parity of the global input state
\left|\zeta\right\rangle\left|\Gamma_{1}\right\rangle\cdots\left|\Gamma_{N-1}\right\rangle
remains conserved. As a consequence, the Bell states
\left|\Psi^{\pm}\right\rangle
and
\left|\Phi^{\pm}\right\rangle
, which can already be distinguished without auxiliary photons from the parity of their detection patterns, can still be discriminated by counting the number of horizontally (
n_{H}
) and vertically (
n_{V}
) polarized photons. Specifically, odd values of
(n_{H},n_{V})
indicate the Bell states
\left|\Psi^{\pm}\right\rangle
, while even values correspond to the states
\left|\Phi^{\pm}\right\rangle
. Furthermore, the increase in success probability is related to the quantity
n_{H}-n_{V}
: certain click patterns, which are uniquely associated to
\left|\Phi^{+}\right\rangle
, yield even values of
n_{H}-n_{V}
, whereas others, uniquely identifying
\left|\Phi^{-}\right\rangle
, result in odd values. Perfect discrimination fails only in the degenerate case when
n_{H}-n_{V}=\pm 2^{N}
, which occurs with a total probability of
1/2^{N}
. Thus, the overall success probability scales as
P_{s}=1-1/2^{N}
, demonstrating an exponential improvement with the number
N
of auxiliary entangled photons. Hence, for
N=2
, a success probability of
75\%
was achieved, well beyond the fundamental limit imposed by standard linear optics.
A detailed analysis of the success rate of the above protocol under realistic conditions has been conducted in
Wein et al. 2016
, where several experimental parameters, such as photon losses, detector dark counts, and detector efficiency, have been taken into account.

One of the main limitations of the method proposed in
Grice 2011
is the requirement that the auxiliary photons need to be prepared in a highly entangled state of the form of Eq.(
4
). In fact, although such states can be easily prepared through spontaneous parametric down conversion (SPDC), their generation remains probabilistic, thus limiting the effectiveness of the protocol. One way to overcome this drawback was presented in
Ewert and van Loock 2014
, where it was shown that a
75\%
success probability can be reached using as a resource two single-photon identical states undergoing a balanced beam splitter transformation. Then, the following auxiliary state is obtained through HOM effect:

\left|\Gamma\right\rangle=\frac{\left|20\right\rangle+\left|02\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\penalty\ .

(5)

Following a scheme similar to the one in
Grice 2011
, the authors of
Ewert and van Loock 2014
then generalized the above scheme to the case of an arbitrary number of auxiliary photons of the form

\left|\Gamma_{j}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}2^{2j-2}}\left[\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=2^{j}+1\\
k\;\text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{2^{j+1}}\left(a^{\dagger}_{k}\right)^{2}+\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=2^{j}+1\\
k\;\text{even}\end{subarray}}^{2^{j+1}}\left(a^{\dagger}_{k}\right)^{2}\right]\left|\text{vac}\right\rangle\penalty\ ,

(6)

which can similarly be implemented by repeated HOM effects on a network of beam splitters.
The success probability in this case is given by
P_{s}=1-1/2^{N+1}
, where
N
is the number of auxiliary states, which approaches to unity when
N\rightarrow\infty
, thus allowing for an asymptotic deterministic BSM.
We emphasize that, although both schemes exhibit the same scaling with the number of auxiliary photons, the primary advantage of the present proposal over that of
Grice 2011
lies in the less demanding resources required to generate the auxiliary state.
Recently, a successful experimental implementation of this scheme for
N=1
was reported in
Bayerbach et al. 2023
, and was subsequently employed for quantum teleportation
D’Aurelio et al. 2025
.

Along a similar direction, in
Olivo and Grosshans 2018
, the optimality of several auxiliary-based BSM schemes was investigated. Through numerical optimization techniques, it was provided evidence that the approach presented in
Grice 2011
, as well as other small-scale schemes, are indeed optimal within a particular set of experimental constraints.
Finally, we conclude this paragraph by presenting an alternative approach, based on fusion gates
Browne and Rudolph 2005
, to implement a qubit BSM starting from high-dimensional systems
Yamazaki and Azuma 2024
. Here, the concept of
pairwise fusion gate
is introduced as a tool to project high-dimensional states of local dimension
d
, onto two-dimensional Bell states. In particular, the resulting success probability is given by
P_{s}=1-1/d
without the use of auxiliary photons, and can be improved to
P_{s}=1-1/d^{k+1}
when employing
N=2(2^{k}-1)
auxiliary states. Notice that such probability is independent of the particular choice of the auxiliary states, the chosen linear optical scheme and the encoding scheme, thus highlighting the versatility of this proposal.

III.1.2
Nonlinear optics

Introducing nonlinear optical interactions offers another promising route to enhance the success probability of BSM schemes.
One of the first proposals in this direction was presented in
Paris et al. 2000
, where a nonlinear interferometric approach for a polarization-encoded BSM was proposed. Their method relies on a third-order nonlinear interaction known as
cross-Kerr effect
, which allows to obtain a complete BSM, whose success probability is limited only by the efficiency of the nonlinear process. However, since such rates are generically low, in
Barrett et al. 2005
an alternative protocol was proposed, based on weaker cross-Kerr effects.
Among other types of nonlinearities, squeezing stands out as one of the most versatile due to its wide applicability and experimental feasibility.
The first proposal to exploit it for improving BSM efficiency was presented in
Zaidi and van Loock 2013
, where it was shown that, similarly to the case of auxiliary photons, squeezing increases the number of interference terms among initially uncorrelated photons. This enhancement leads to a higher success probability in BSM, for both single- and dual-rail encodings.

In particular, considering a dual-rail polarization encoding, the setup involves an input Bell state impinging on a
50:50
beam splitter, followed by two polarizing beam splitters. Single-mode squeezers are then applied to each mode before detection with photon-number-resolving detectors (PNRDs). For the single-rail case, the scheme consists of a beam splitter, two single-mode squeezers, and a second beam splitter before the PNRDs.
In both cases, the improvement in the success probability is a function of the squeezing parameter
\zeta
, reaching a maximum
64.3\%
at
\zeta\simeq 5.719\;\text{dB}
for dual-rail encoding, and
62.5\%
at
\zeta\simeq 6.270\;\text{dB}
for single-rail.
The main advantage of this approach is that it eliminates the need for auxiliary states, thus removing the dependence on deterministic single-photon sources or complex entangled resources, which remain significant practical challenges.
A detailed analysis of this scheme, including realistic considerations such as detector noise, limited resolution, and phase instability, was conducted in
Kilmer and Guha 2019
, where these imperfections were shown to reduce efficiency under experimental conditions. Additionally, hybrid teleportation schemes have combined discrete-variable (DV) and continuous-variable (CV) techniques to overcome the intrinsically probabilistic nature of BSM, as demonstrated, for instance, in the experiments reported in
Takeda et al. 2015
;
Takeda et al. 2013
.

An alternative approach was proposed in
Kim et al. 2001
, where a BSM was implemented as part of a quantum teleportation protocol using a nonlinear process known as sum-frequency generation (SFG). In SFG, two photons of different frequencies interact within a nonlinear medium to produce a single photon whose frequency equals the sum of the input ones. Remarkably, in
Kim et al. 2001
, there is no fundamental theoretical upper bound on the success probability, and the only limitations derive from the efficiency of the SFG process itself.
A similar technique was also employed in
Sangouard et al. 2011a
to implement an entanglement swapping protocol.
Despite its advantages, this approach has notable drawbacks. First, nonlinear interactions in optical systems often introduce additional noise, which can degrade the fidelity of the teleported state
César et al. 2009
. Furthermore, while this method is well-suited for polarization-encoded qubits, extending it to other types of encoding is non-trivial, mainly due to the dependency on the power of the pump.

We conclude this section by presenting another instance of nonlinear effects arising from the so-called
light–matter
interaction. Up to this point, atomic systems have been treated using an effective description, where their influence on the photonic system is incorporated through coupling constants associated with specific nonlinear effects. However, a more comprehensive and accurate approach involves treating light and matter on equal footing, modeling the matter component as a collection of quantum two-level systems. In this fully quantum picture, a common theoretical framework is given by the quantum Rabi model
Xie et al. 2017
, and more specifically, by the Jaynes–Cummings model
Greentree et al. 2013
, which stands at the core of cavity quantum electrodynamics (QED)
Walther et al. 2006
. Based on this,
several techniques for deterministic BSMs have been proposed
Lloyd et al. 2001
;
Specht et al. 2011
, supporting the construction of scalable quantum photonic platforms
Duan and Kimble 2004
;
Reiserer and Rempe 2015
. Recently, experimental demonstrations of cavity QED–based BSMs have been reported in
Nölleke et al. 2013
;
Welte et al. 2021
;
Kamimaki et al. 2023
.

III.1.3
Hyper-entanglement

Another strategy to increase the success probability of a BSM is to embed the qubit systems within a larger Hilbert space. This idea stands at the core of the concept of
hyper-entanglement
, where a composite system is entangled with respect to multiple degrees of freedom. Along this line, in
Kwiat and Weinfurter 1998
, two proposals were presented, where a two-qubit system was entangled in polarization and an extra degree of freedom, namely time-energy or momentum. In this way, a complete BSM can be implemented by using photon-number-resolving (PNR) detectors. A similar approach was presented in
Walborn et al. 2003
, where the need for PNR detectors was replaced by an exchange of classical communication between the two parties, which allows for the implementation a BSM nonlocally.
The main drawback of this method is that it can be applied only to down-converted photons resulting from an SPDC process, and therefore cannot be directly applied to the case of quantum teleportation.
In
Schuck et al. 2006
, the first deterministic BSM based on hyper-entanglement in polarization and energy-time was achieved. Remarkably, their setup required only linear optical components, without the need for auxiliary photons. Expanding on this, in
Barbieri et al. 2007
, an alternative protocol for deterministic BSM based on polarization-momentum hyper-entanglement was presented.
Further works were devoted to explore how to distinguish hyper-entangled Bell states, i.e., tensor products of Bell states in two different degrees of freedom. In
Wei et al. 2007
it was shown that, in the case of
n=2
degrees of freedom, it is possible to distinguish only
7
out of the
16
hyper-entangled Bell states, when restricting to linear optics. Moreover, in the same work, the maximal number of mutually distinguishable such states was conjectured to be upper bounded by
2^{n+1}-1
when only one copy of the Bell state is available. Further, complete distinguishability of all
4^{n}
hyper-entangled Bell states is possible when two copies of the state are available. A general proof of this bound was found in
Pisenti et al. 2011
.
Other works, such as
Sheng et al. 2010
;
Wang and Wang 2016
investigated the use of nonlinear effects within the problem of complete discrimination of hyper-entangled Bell states.
While a complete survey of other applications of hyper-entangled states in quantum information processing is out of the scope of this review, we refer the interested reader to
Deng et al. 2017
.

IV
High-dimensional systems

Qudit systems, whose corresponding Hilbert space has dimension
d
, offer several advantages over their qubit counterparts. One immediate benefit of encoding information in high-dimensional (HD) states is the potential reduction in the size and complexity of quantum circuits
Gedik et al. 2015
;
Karácsony et al. 2024
.
Specifically, a
D
-dimensional system requires
N_{\text{qubit}}
qubits such that
2^{N_{\text{qubit}}}=D
, whereas only
N_{\text{qudit}}
qudits are needed, with
d^{N_{\text{qudit}}}=D
.
As a consequence, for large values of the local dimension
d
, fewer qudits are needed as compared to the qubit case, and the information storage capacity scales as
k=\log_{2}d
.
Moreover, fewer transformations and controls are needed to manipulate the same amount of information
Wang et al. 2020
.

Operational lifetimes of qudits are influenced by noise and decoherence. Since each quantum gate requires a finite amount of time to implement, using fewer gates becomes crucial when operating within limited coherence windows. Moreover, it has been shown that any operation can be approximated by a unitary operator in
SU(d^{N})
, with an accuracy that scales as
1/d^{2}

Luo et al. 2014
. This further reduces the number of gates required, enhancing overall coherence times.

From an experimental point of view, qudits have demonstrated superior information transmission rates as compared to qubits
Bacco et al. 2017
;
Bacco et al. 2019
. They also exhibit enhanced resilience to noise, which is particularly beneficial in quantum key distribution (QKD) protocols
Sheridan and Scarani 2010
. In this context, robustness is often quantified by the quantum bit error rate (QBER), defined as the ratio of erroneous to total received bits
Nielsen and Chuang 2001
. While a low QBER indicates reliable transmission, other metrics such as channel fidelity, gate error rates, and error-correction capabilities must also be considered. Notably, qudit systems exhibit higher error tolerance thresholds, allowing them to outperform qubits under equivalent QBER conditions
Bechmann-Pasquinucci and Tittel 2000
. Furthermore, increasing the local dimensionality enables higher secure key generation rates within a fixed spatial region, though often at the expense of transmission range
Cerf et al. 2002
.

Qudits also provide notable advantages for entanglement distribution
Ekert 1991a
. Using fewer high-dimensional systems instead of many low-dimensional ones has been shown to mitigate the effects of noise
Liu and Fan 2009
. Additionally, qudit dimension affects the fidelity of quantum cloning processes. For example, symmetric cloning of qudits achieves a fidelity of
1/2+1/(d+1)
, indicating that higher dimensions make it more difficult for adversaries to replicate quantum information accurately
Navez and Cerf 2003
;
Bruß and Macchiavello 1999
.

From a practical standpoint, photons represent the best candidates to implement HD quantum states. At low energies, photons can be regarded as non-interacting with electromagnetic fields, and their weak coupling with the environment leads to long coherence times, making them excellent for qudit transport in free space or optical fibers
O’brien et al. 2009
.

Several photonic platforms support HD state encoding. Orbital angular momentum (OAM) encoding exploits the helical phase structure of light, where each quantized OAM mode, characterized by a topological charge
l\in(-\infty,+\infty)
, forms a basis state in the qudit Hilbert space. This method offers an unbounded state space and can be realized using devices like spiral phase plates or q-plates, making it a promising candidate for free-space communication and HD-QKD
Bouchard et al. 2018
. However, OAM states are susceptible to atmospheric turbulence and experience significant mode dispersion in optical fibers, making it necessary the use adaptive optics to extend their transmission range
Ren et al. 2014
(for a review on OAM see
Erhard et al. 2018
;
Erhard et al. 2020
;
Mirhosseini et al. 2015
).
Path encoding is another widely adopted technique, wherein different spatial paths represent distinct qudit basis states. This encoding has found particular utility in integrated-photonics experiments, e.g., boson sampling
Spagnolo et al. 2014
. Common implementations include integrated waveguides
Yang et al. 2025
, interferometric networks
Khabiboulline et al. 2019
, and multicore fibers
Da Lio et al. 2021
. This approach enables high-fidelity state manipulation and is particularly well-suited to chip-scale integration, as demonstrated in universal photonic processors
Carolan et al. 2015
. However, implementing high-dimensional transformations requires increasingly complex optical networks
Ma et al. 2025
, limiting scalability.

Time-bin encoding
Brecht et al. 2015
, based on the photon’s arrival time, is implemented using interferometers with defined path-length delays. It offers excellent robustness against decoherence and has been successfully employed in long-distance fiber-based quantum communication
Vagniluca et al. 2020
. However, while time-bin qudits can travel through standard optical fibers with minimal loss, the approach demands precise timing resolution and offers limited gate speed due to inherent temporal constraints
Yu et al. 2025
.
An alternative option is represented by frequency encoding, where basis states are associated with discrete spectral components of photons
Lukens and Lougovski 2017
. This method benefits from compatibility with existing telecom infrastructure and allows efficient generation of entangled qudit states using standard optical tools
Jin et al. 2016
.
Each encoding approach offers specific strengths tailored to different applications: OAM allows for generating relatively easily qudit states of larger local dimension, path encoding suits integrated quantum processors, time-bin encoding is ideal for long-distance fiber-based systems, and frequency encoding aligns well with classical networks. However, each also has inherent limitations. To address these, hybrid encoding schemes have been proposed, where an increased versatility is reached at the cost of greater experimental complexity.
For instance, combining OAM and path encoding demonstrated enhanced state manipulation capabilities
Jo et al. 2019a
, while time-frequency entangled qudits have shown promise for robust quantum communication networks
Cheng et al. 2023
.

V
High-dimensional Bell state measurements

When the Hilbert space of the quantum system is enlarged to higher dimensions, the notion of Bell states should be properly generalized. To this end, two-qudit Bell states have been defined as
Bennett et al. 1993
;
Sych and Leuchs 2009

\left|\Psi_{lm}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\omega_{d}^{lk}\left|k_{L}\right\rangle\left|(k\oplus m)_{L}\right\rangle\penalty\ ,

(7)

where
l,m=0,\dots,d-1
,
\omega_{d}=\exp(2\pi i/d)
is the
d
-th root of the unity and
\oplus
denotes the sum modulo
d
. Different from the case of qubit systems, where all Bell states are either symmetric or anti-symmetric with respect to the exchange of the subsystems, in the high-dimensional scenario, there exist states that do not have a definite symmetry. More importantly,
Calsamiglia 2002
demonstrated that, under the constraints of linear optics, it is fundamentally impossible to perform an HD BSM even when ambiguous (i.e., probabilistic or non-deterministic) discrimination is allowed. Similarly to the qubit case, this no-go theorem can be circumvented by introducing nonlinear optical interactions or auxiliary photons. However, unlike in the qubit scenario—where such techniques are employed to enhance the success probability—they are strictly required in the high-dimensional case to achieve any nonzero success probability at all. This result was further strengthened in
Carollo and Palma 2001
, which showed that the inclusion of auxiliary photons can only enhance the success probability of ambiguous discrimination, although perfect discrimination remains impossible. In the following, we discuss in detail the use of several approaches to implement high-dimensional BSMs.

V.1
Boosting the success probability for high-dimensional Bell state measurements

V.1.1
Auxiliary states

The generalization of auxiliary-photon-based methods is explored in
Dušek 2001
, where the KLM protocol
Knill et al. 2001
is systematically applied to construct a non-deterministic—i.e., conditional—SWAP (CSWP) gate. This two-photon gate is a fundamental building block for performing Bell measurements in higher dimensions. However, its implementation requires a growing number of modes and entangled auxiliary photons as the system dimension dd increases. The overall success probability of the scheme is directly tied to the success rate of the non-deterministic operation and is lower bounded by

P_{s}\geq\left(\frac{M}{M+1}\right)^{2(d-1)^{2}}\penalty\ ,

(8)

where
d
is the dimension of the qudit system and
M
denotes a parameter defined by the number of auxiliary photons and modes used in the setup, corresponding to
2M
auxiliary photons and
4M
auxiliary modes, respectively.

Given the nondeterministic nature of the KLM protocol and the extremely low probability of generating highly entangled auxiliary states, the pursuit of near-deterministic Bell measurements was largely set aside in favor of more experimentally feasible solutions. In
Luo et al. 2019
, a scheme was proposed achieving a success probability scaling as
1/d^{2}
using only
d-2
auxiliary single photons. In this approach, the input uncorrelated particles and auxiliary photons are processed through a
d
-dimensional quantum Fourier transform, which acts as a multiport beam splitter
Żukowski et al. 1997
, and is supplemented by a non-unitary transformation defined on a
d+1
-dimensional space. By employing post-selection, the scheme enables unambiguous discrimination of a single Bell state out of the
d^{2}
possibilities. Moreover, it was shown that, within a teleportation protocol, additional operations on Bob’s side can further enhance the overall success probability. The method was analyzed theoretically for
d=3,4
and experimentally implemented in the qutrit case.

Despite the experimental challenges associated with this scheme—primarily due to the need for high-dimensional and sophisticated interferometric setups—it laid the foundation for the development of more advanced auxiliary-based strategies. In particular,
Bharos et al. 2024
introduced a significant improvement in high-dimensional Bell-state analysis by constructing an entangled-state analyzer specifically tailored for even-dimensional Hilbert spaces. In this approach, the auxiliary resource is an entangled state of the form:

\left|\text{aux}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d/2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\hat{a}_{j,0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j,1}^{\dagger}\dots\hat{a}_{j,d-3}^{\dagger}\left|\text{vac}\right\rangle\penalty\ ,

(9)

where
\hat{a}_{j,k}^{\dagger}
creates a photon in the mode
j
for the
k
-th auxiliary state. Since the state in Eq.(
9
) has Schmidt rank
2/d^{2}
, a BSM of all Bell states becomes now achievable with a success probability of
P_{s}=2/d^{2}
, thus providing a remarkable enhancement over previous methods relying on separable auxiliary photons.

V.1.2
Nonlinear optics

The proposal for a BSM for qubits based on a pre-detection squeezing technique, originally introduced in
Zaidi and van Loock 2013
, has recently been generalized to high-dimensional systems in
Bianchi et al. 2024a
. In this work, the success probability was numerically evaluated for dimensions
d=3,4,5
, showing an improvement over previous scaling laws that relied on auxiliary photons
Luo et al. 2019
. Notably, this protocol does not require auxiliary states, as it is provided evidence that their inclusion does not enhance the success probability. The primary limitation of this approach lies in the need for photon-number-resolving detectors.

In,
Sephton et al. 2023
proposes a nonlinear protocol for teleporting high-dimensional spatial information using sum-frequency generation (SFG) as a detection mechanism. In this scheme, a photon from an entangled pair generated via SPDC, is combined with a spatially encoded coherent state in a second nonlinear crystal. SFG occurs only when their spatial modes are matched, effectively acting as a filter and enabling heralded teleportation. The nonlinear detector functions as a high-dimensional, basis-independent spatial-mode filter and supports various encoding schemes, including OAM, Hermite-Gaussian, and arbitrary spatial modes. The authors also introduce a theoretical model describing the process as a quantum channel, relating its capacity, that is, the number of modes that can be faithfully transferred, to experimentally accessible parameters like pump beam width, demonstrating efficient operation up to dimension
d=14
.

Along similar lines, the SFG-based BSM proposed in
Kim et al. 2001
has been extended to arbitrary dimensions in
Bianchi et al. 2024b
. A key advantage of this method is its independence from the choice of encoding. To evaluate its performance, the fidelity of a quantum teleportation protocol was simulated under noisy conditions, confirming its robustness.
Finally, quantum light-matter interactions are also gaining attention in the context of high-dimensional states, particularly for the development of controlled gates that could overcome the probabilistic limitations of photon-photon interactions
Chen et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2025
.

V.1.3
Hyper-entanglement

In
Zhang et al. 2019
, the authors propose a qudit-based scheme for performing a complete BSM. Their method generalizes the concept of hyperentanglement introduced in
Walborn et al. 2003
by utilizing an auxiliary maximally entangled state in a separate Hilbert space—such as path, orbital angular momentum (OAM), or time-bin—to fully resolve the Bell basis in a primary high-dimensional space. The authors provide an explicit example of constructing a BSM for angular momentum–path qutrit systems and outline a methodology to generalize their scheme to arbitrary dimensions.

VI
Continuous-variable Bell state measurements

An alternative paradigm to exploit optical modes for information processing lies in the so-called
continuous-variable
(CV) regime
Lloyd and Braunstein 1999
;
Braunstein and Van Loock 2005
, where information is stored in the continuous degrees of freedom of the electromagnetic field, known as
quadratures
. For an optical mode described by the bosonic operators
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, the quadratures are formally defined as

\hat{x}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})\penalty\ ,\quad\hat{p}=\frac{1}{2i}(\hat{a}-\hat{a}^{\dagger})\penalty\ .

(10)

These observables have a continuous spectrum, meaning that measurement outcomes are real-valued rather than discrete. Moreover, they allow to establish a natural link between many classes of optical states, such as coherent and squeezed states, and the Heisenberg uncertainty relations.

Within the CV framework,
Gaussian states

Adesso et al. 2014
represent a fundamental resource for quantum information processing
Lami et al. 2018
, due to their mathematical simplicity and experimental accessibility. However, it has been shown that a genuine quantum advantage often requires the use of
non-Gaussian
resources
Bartlett et al. 2002
, sparking growing interest in non-Gaussian state engineering.

In general, most operations available in the discrete-variable (DV) regime can also be implemented in the CV setting, although the mapping between the two is not always direct
Andersen et al. 2015
. A fundamental difference lies in the choice of observables: DV systems typically involve measurements of the photon number operator, whereas CV protocols rely on quadrature operators.

The most common Gaussian measurement in CV-based quantum information is the
homodyne detection

Vogel and Welsch 2006
. This technique involves mixing the input state with a strong coherent state, known as the
local oscillator
, using a
50:50
beam splitter. Assuming both the input and the oscillator are single-mode, the two output modes are sent to photodetectors, and the difference of the resulting photocurrents is measured. By adjusting the phase of the local oscillator, one can select the quadrature to be measured. Specifically, if the local oscillator has complex amplitude
\alpha=|\alpha|e^{i\phi}
with
|\alpha|\gg 1
, then the measured quadrature takes the form

\hat{x}_{\phi}=\cos(\phi)\,\hat{x}+\sin(\phi)\penalty\ ,\qquad\hat{p}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{a}e^{-i\phi}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{i\phi})\penalty\ ,

(11)

where
\hat{a}
denotes the annihilation operator of the input state. The same technique can be used to measure the
\hat{p}_{\phi}
spectrum.

In the CV regime, quantum teleportation and entanglement swapping have been theoretically proposed and experimentally demonstrated in several works
Bowen et al. 2003
;
Pirandola and Mancini 2006
;
Yonezawa et al. 2004
. In a two-mode scenario, BSMs involve projections onto an infinite-dimensional entangled basis of the form
\left|\Psi_{r}\right\rangle=\hat{D}_{-r}\left|\xi\right\rangle
, where
\hat{D}_{r}=\exp{\left(ir^{\text{T}}\Omega r\right)}
is the displacement operator in the canonical quadrature variables
r=(x_{1},x_{2},p_{1},p_{2})
, and
\Omega
is the
4\times 4
symplectic matrix characterizing the optical phase space. The state
\left|\xi\right\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|j,j\right\rangle
represents an (unphysical) maximally entangled CV state with infinite Schmidt rank. Its nonphysical nature stems from the absence of a normalization constant and corresponds to the ideal limit of infinite squeezing.

The standard method for implementing this type of projection is known as
heterodyne detection
, which can be viewed as the CV analogue of the BSM qubit in the DV regime, with photon-counting replaced by appropriate homodyne measurements
Serafini 2023
. In this scheme, one measures a complete set of commuting quadrature observables, eliminating the probabilistic nature that characterizes DV-based BSMs. In particular, the simultaneous measurement of the two commuting operators
\hat{x}_{-}=\hat{x}_{1}-\hat{x}_{2}
and
\hat{p}_{+}=\hat{p}_{1}+\hat{p}_{2}
deterministically projects the input state onto the entangled basis, thereby rendering the BSM complete.

However, this ideal behavior holds only in the limit of infinite squeezing, where the projection is exact. In realistic scenarios with finite squeezing, the projection deviates from the maximally entangled basis, resulting in reduced fidelity for the teleported state and lower-quality entanglement in swapping protocols
Hofmann et al. 2000
. This trade-off illustrates a fundamental distinction between DV and CV approaches: DV systems generally offer high-fidelity operations at the cost of probabilistic success, whereas CV systems enable deterministic operations with inherently lower fidelity.

VII
Applications

As previously mentioned, BSMs are a fundamental component of many quantum information protocols. In this section, we focus on three key applications: quantum repeaters, fusion-based quantum computation, and quantum key distribution. For each, we review the current state of the art and discuss the main challenges in their implementation.

VII.1
Quantum repeaters

Quantum repeaters are essential for enabling long-distance quantum communication by overcoming the limitations imposed by photon loss and noise in optical channels. Unlike classical signal amplifiers, quantum repeaters cannot clone quantum states due to the no-cloning theorem
Wootters and Zurek 1982
. Instead, they utilize techniques such as entanglement distribution
Ekert 1991b
, purification
Bennett et al. 1996
, and swapping
et al. 1998
to extend the range of quantum networks while preserving quantum coherence. The basic principle involves dividing the communication channel into shorter segments, generating entanglement between neighboring nodes, and then performing entanglement swapping to establish end-to-end entanglement
Briegel et al. 1998
.

The development of quantum repeaters has progressed through several generations, each improving performance and scalability. For an extensive review on quantum repeaters, see
Azuma et al. 2023
. First-generation repeaters rely on entanglement swapping combined with probabilistic entanglement purification
Dür et al. 1999
. While these systems are close to experimental feasibility using quantum memories leveraging light-matter interactions
Van Loock et al. 2006
;
Ladd et al. 2006
;
Bergmann and van Loock 2019
, based on trapped ions (see e.g.
Sangouard et al. 2009
;
Krutyanskiy et al. 2023
) or atomic ensembles
Chen et al. 2007
;
Sangouard et al. 2008
;
Han et al. 2010
;
Sangouard et al. 2011b
, they are limited by finite memory coherence times and the resource demands of purification. Second-generation repeaters
Jiang et al. 2009
incorporate quantum error correction to protect against operational errors, enhancing noise robustness
Muralidharan et al. 2014
. These require high-fidelity gates and efficient error-correcting codes, which present significant experimental challenges. Third-generation repeaters
Azuma et al. 2015
, currently still theoretical, aim for full fault tolerance through topological error correction and deterministic entanglement swapping
Muralidharan et al. 2016
, demanding breakthroughs in hardware and control.

Several major challenges remain in realizing practical quantum repeaters. Quantum memories must preserve coherence long enough to complete entanglement distribution across network segments, often requiring millisecond-to-second storage times for long distances
Simon et al. 2010
. Photon loss in optical fibers and detector noise degrade performance, necessitating advanced error mitigation. On the CV side, many proposals were designed for quantum repeaters which rely on the deterministic nature of BSMs
Polkinghorne and Ralph 1999
;
Ide et al. 2001
;
Hoelscher-Obermaier and van Loock 2011
;
Hofer et al. 2013
;
Dias et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2022a
. Recent advances include hybrid approaches combining DV and CV systems to enhance efficiency
Takeda et al. 2015
;
Darras et al. 2021
. All-photonic repeaters, instead, eliminate the need for quantum memories by using graph states
Li et al. 2019
;
Shchukin and van Loock 2021
. Additional techniques, such as multiplexing to boost entanglement rates
Van Loock et al. 2008
and high-dimensional encodings to improve resilience to noise
Bacco et al. 2019
, are also topics under active investigation.

VII.2
Fusion-based quantum computation

Bell measurements also play a central role in fusion-based quantum computation
Nickerson et al. 2013
;
Gimeno-Segovia et al. 2015
;
Bao et al. 2023
, a promising paradigm for scalable photonic quantum computing. This approach leverages entangled resource states and adaptive measurements to perform universal quantum operations
Paesani et al. 2021
. Conceptually, fusion-based computation reflects the duality between the Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures, differing from traditional gate-based models by building large entangled states through probabilistic fusion of smaller states (e.g., Bell pairs or graph states).
Specifically, the HOM effect, occurring at the output ports of a beam splitter, is a key mechanism to generate photonic graph states, where BSMs play the role as entangling gates which “fuse” the particles together. Fusion-based quantum computation is particularly suited to photonic systems
Chan et al. 2025
;
Pettersson et al. 2025
, where deterministic entangling gates are challenging, but high-quality single-photon sources and detectors are available. A major advantage of fusion-based quantum computing is its intrinsic tolerance to photon loss and operational errors
Paesani and Brown 2023
. Recent experiments have demonstrated small-scale fusion gates using linear optics, marking important steps toward fault-tolerant, scalable architectures
Song et al. 2024
;
psi 2025
.

Cluster states are currently employed as a resource also in CV quantum information processing, see e.g.
Menicucci et al. 2006
;
Gu et al. 2009
;
Menicucci 2014
.
However, CV fusion-based computation faces limitations due to imperfections from finite squeezing, which introduce computational errors when measuring CV cluster states. For a concise overview of CV fusion-based schemes, see
Xin 2023
.

VII.3
Quantum key distribution

Quantum key distribution (QKD)
Pirandola et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2020
is a technique that enables the secure exchange of cryptographic keys between distant parties by exploiting the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics. Thanks to rapid technological progress, QKD has become one of the most mature and promising applications of quantum information science, with successful implementations achieving increasingly long transmission distances through both free-space
Aspelmeyer et al. 2003
;
Vallone et al. 2015
;
Liao et al. 2017
;
Cocchi et al. 2025
and optical fiber channels
Ding et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Mueller et al. 2025
;
rib 2023

However, a major challenge lies in the gap between the theoretical models of QKD protocols, often based on idealized assumptions, and their practical realizations. Discrepancies between theory and implementation can give rise to security loopholes, highlighting the need for frameworks that are robust against device imperfections. A significant advancement in this direction is provided by measurement-device-independent (MDI) QKD
Lo et al. 2012
, together with its recent version, twin-field (TF) QKD
Lucamarini et al. 2018
, both of which eliminate the need to trust measurement devices and thus mitigate vulnerabilities to so-called side-channel attacks
Lydersen et al. 2010
;
Xu et al. 2013
;
Jo et al. 2019b
.

In both MDI and TF QKD, the two legitimate parties (commonly referred to as Alice and Bob) send their encoded quantum states to an untrusted third party who performs a Bell state measurement (BSM). The outcome of this measurement enables the two parties to establish a shared key without requiring trust in the measurement process itself. Experimental demonstrations of these protocols have been successfully carried out in various platforms, both for MDI QKD
Tang et al. 2014
;
Tang et al. 2016
;
Yin et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2021
and for TF QKD (see
Wang et al. 2022
and references therein).

While MDI and TF QKD are compatible with current technological capabilities, they require a well-characterized and trusted quantum transmitter. A more comprehensive approach that also removes assumptions on the source is offered by device-independent (DI) QKD
Mayers and Yao 1998
;
Zapatero et al. 2023
, in which entangled photon pairs from an untrusted source are distributed to the users. In this setting, the security of the protocol is certified by the violation of a Bell inequality
Ekert 1991a
. However, a key assumption in DI QKD is the ability to close all relevant loopholes in a Bell test—an experimentally demanding task that has only recently been demonstrated
Nadlinger et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022b
. In this context, BSMs play a crucial role by enabling heralding mechanisms that identify successful entanglement distribution events without introducing detection loopholes (see, e.g.,
Zapatero et al. 2023
).

VIII
Conclusions

In this review, we provided a comprehensive overview of Bell state measurements, examining both their theoretical foundations as well as their most recent experimental implementations. Our discussion covered BSMs in the context of qubit systems, where they are most well understood and widely implemented, as well as in high-dimensional systems, which offer enhanced capabilities but pose additional experimental challenges. We analyzed the limitations related to achieving highly efficient BSMs in quantum optical platforms and surveyed the principal strategies developed to address these limitations, including the use of auxiliary photons, nonlinear interactions, and hyper-entanglement.

Finally, we reviewed the key applications of BSMs across several core protocols in quantum information science, highlighting their central role in enabling scalable and secure quantum communication. In particular, the ability to perform high-fidelity BSMs is crucial for the realization of quantum repeaters and, ultimately, the deployment of a quantum internet. As such, further advances in the theory and implementation of BSMs remain a critical requirement for the future of quantum technologies.

As such, the study of Bell state measurements remains an active and rapidly evolving field of research. Future progress, both theoretical and experimental, will be crucial in advancing quantum technologies and in realizing the full potential of quantum information processing on a global scale.
