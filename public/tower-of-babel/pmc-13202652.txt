Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi Inoculation Mediated Changes in Phytohormones Under Drought Stress
Authors: Murugesan Chandrasekaran, Iyyakkannu Sivanesan, Se Chul Chun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChanges in climatic conditions around the world have increased day by day which leads to droughts in agricultural lands. Nowadays various technologies and methodologies are being used to protect plants from drought stress. Among various methods, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) mediated alleviation of drought stress was found to be a more efficient one. Most of the AMF studies revealed their importance for water status, gas exchange, and nutrient uptake, but they also act as potential candidates for the regulation of phytohormones. At present, there is no comprehensive and quantitative evaluation of the phytohormonal changes under drought stress. Therefore, we carried out a random-effects model of meta-analysis to explore the influences of AMF inoculation on phytohormones under drought stress. Results showed that stress hormones increased by 2% but were non-significant, whereas growth hormones significantly increased by 24% in AMF-inoculated plants. Among phytohormones, indole acetic acid (IAA) content increased significantly by 37%. Also increased content of jasmonic acid (JA) (25%), salicylic acid (SA) (25%), gibberellins (24%), and brassinosteroids (11%) compared to non-mycorrhizal plants. Due to low sample size, we treated results with caution. However, abscisic acid (ABA) contents decreased by 6% in AMF-inoculated plants. Among growth hormones, IAA showed more positive effect sizes in AMF-inoculated plants under drought stress conditions. Among phytohormones, JA and SA had a positive effect size, whereas ABA showed a negative effect size. These results conclude that depending upon the level of stress, AMF species and plant species determined the accumulation of phytohormones under drought stress.

Introduction

Drought stress limits the functions of the essential mechanisms necessary for photosynthesis because of changes in stomatal opening and reduced water transport in plants [1]. Thus, drought stress weakens normal functions of plants and further changes the physiological and biochemical mechanisms involved in water limitations [2]. Drought-tolerant plants survive through the production of more fine roots, reduction in water loss (stomatal closure), and transpiration. Among various biochemical mechanisms, plant hormone-mediated cell signaling is found to be an important regulatory mechanism under drought stress conditions [3]. During stress conditions, changes in hormone ratios exhibit various changes in biochemical mechanisms and their physiological reactions, which in turn initiate changes in plant growth and performance [4]. Hence, alleviation of drought stress is of foremost importance for enhancing crop yield. There are two different types of hormones, such as growth hormones and stress hormones, which are essential for plant growth and stress tolerance [3–5].

Growth hormones such as indole acetic acid (IAA), gibberellins (GAs), cytokinin (trans-zeatin-riboside (ZR)), and brassinosteroids (BRs) are the most important hormones under drought stress conditions. Stress hormones such as jasmonic acid (JA), abscisic acid (ABA), and salicylic acid (SA) act as an indicator for drought stress resistance mechanisms [6]. Particularly, ABA and IAA are important for stomatal behaviors during stress conditions. ABA is produced in the shoot and transported to the root or root to shoot, thereby inducing stomatal closure [7,8]. Apart from drought stress tolerance, ABA also acts as a signaling molecule for the formation of branches of arbuscule (which is the site of nutrients and water exchange) and mycorrhizal colonization through regulation of others [9]. Which, in turn, promotes the symbiotic relationships between arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and the roots of plants to increase their growth and yield. Previous studies showed that the symbiotic association of soil microbes, such as AMF, with plants proved to be an efficient scavenger of soil nutrients and water [10]. Hence, recently, most of the research studied focused on symbiosis between AMF and plants as one of the drought alleviation strategies for sustainable agriculture. Previously published studies showed the importance of the AMF-mediated regulation of plant hormones [11–14].

Meta-analysis studies are needed for beginning deep interpretations for a complete view of the mycorrhizal–plant association, and a precise picture of the cumulative impact on the plant and sustainable agriculture. Recently, meta-analysis studies confirmed the importance of root morphological characteristics, biomass, and nutrient uptake under various levels of drought stress [15], and their importance for salt stress alleviation [16,17]. We also found positive effects on enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants in AMF-inoculated plants [18,19]. Therefore, we studied a meta-analysis to quantify the plant hormonal changes in plants under drought stress in AMF-inoculated plants. The present meta-analysis was designed based on the following questions: (i) How do the phytohormones respond to different drought intensities, growth forms, and AMF species? (ii) What are the relationships between different phytohormones like IAA and ABA during drought stress? (iii) What are the relationships between individual hormones? (iv) To confirm drought stress tolerance is related to plant hormonal changes in AMF-inoculated plants.

Materials and methods

Collection of data

To build a database, we did a literature survey in electronic databases for published articles (Google Scholar, Science, Nature, Elsevier, Science Direct, Springer, and Wiley & Blackwell) and in Web of Science® and the references cited in the publications retrieved. The search combinations entered were mycorrhiza* inoculation and drought stress/or under drought stress, Arbuscular Mycorrhiza* inoculation and drought stress/or under drought stress, Mycorrhiza*/or Arbuscular Mycorrhiza* plant hormones under drought stress, and Mycorrhiza/or Arbuscular Mycorrhiza* phytohormones under drought stress. The use of the Boolean truncation (*’) character ensured that the variations of the word such as mycorrhizae, mycorrhizas, and mycorrhizal were also included. These searches resulted in 450 published and unpublished online references, of which 155 were considered likely to contain significant information. To further limit the search to appropriate articles, the following inclusion criteria are used: (1) plants under drought stress, (2) would comprise both without AMF inoculation and with AMF inoculation, (3) the intensity of drought stress, and (4) results were published in international journals, (5) plant hormones; (6) crop plants (annual and/or perennial); and (7) the experiments had to be performed under a drought stress condition. Those studies that did not provide information on all of the above response variables and those that presented irrelevant data were excluded. Among the 155 references, 105 papers were rejected based on these criteria, and the list was refined to 50 studies (Figure S1). From these studies, we extracted 252 quantitative measures of variables identified as potentially meeting the selection criteria of presenting information on the effects of AMF inoculation under drought stress. From each published article gathered means, standard error (SEs) (converted into standard deviations (SDs))/deviations, and sample sizes were gathered. The meta-win calculator is used for the calculation of SD from SEs. Further, data were collected from graphs using Web Plot Digitizer v. 2.4 [20]. However, articles had to meet a set of inclusion criteria which included information on. AMF inoculation mediated changes in phytohormones under drought stress conditions.

Drought stress is divided into two levels based on the percentage of field capacity (FC) mentioned in the published articles, such as moderate stress (40–80% FC) and severe stress (0–40% FC). For each case study, we compiled the following moderators: AMF species, AMF inoculation (single or mixed), plant species, plant characteristics (plant group, life cycle, growth forms), and experimental conditions (greenhouse or field conditions). Based on exclusion and inclusion criteria, 50 publications (supplementary materials, meta-analysis references, and Appendix 1) were taken for studies, and 252 observations were collected based on our response variables (plant hormones) such as IAA, GA, ZR, BR, ABA, SA, and JA, and AMF-mediated drought stress.

Data collection

We also collected data from published figures using WebPlotDigitizer v4.0. We collected data from figures such as the number of replications or sample size (n), mean, SD, and SEs of the control (without AMF inoculation), as well as the treatment (with AMF inoculation) under drought stress, which are necessary for meta-analysis studies. Converted SEs into SD using the meta-win statistical calculator.

Categorical variables

Fixed factors related to the responses of plant hormones after AMF inoculation under drought stress conditions were categorically analyzed. Specific fixed factors were as follows:

Plant hormones were grouped into two different groups based on their majority of function for our analysis: AMF-inoculated plant groups were evaluated for significant differences in their growth hormones and stress hormones during drought stress.

Growth hormones were divided into three parameters: IAA, GA, ZR, and BR. This allowed the testing for significant differences under different drought stress conditions mediated by the inoculation of AMF.

The stress hormones include three parameters: ABA, JA, and SA.

AMF richness is divided into single and mixed levels.

The use of AMF belonging to only one species was categorized as “single species.” Co-inoculation with more than one AMF species was categorized as “mixed species.”

Plant species and plant family

There were about 25 plant host species included for analysis, spanning members of 15 different plant families.

The plant group comprises two levels: monocot and dicot.

Life cycle

Plants were categorized as annual or perennial for the plant life cycle.

Plant growth habit

This categorical variable was described as herbaceous, grass, and woody forms of plants.

Drought stress was defined in three categories: moderate and high (0–40% FC). Moderate drought stress ranged from 40 to 80% FC, and drought stress ranging from 0 to 40% FC was high drought stress.

The experimental condition comprised two levels: greenhouse, which included all experiments done under a protected and controlled set-up (i.e., pot trials); and field, containing all outdoor studies (i.e., soil trials).

Meta-analysis

Meta-win 2.1 software was used for the calculation of effect sizes (the natural log of response ratio (lnR)) to predict the response of AMF inoculation under drought stress [21,22]. One major assumption of the meta-analysis is that the results of different individual studies, or observations within the database, are independent of one another. If publications reported data from more than one study system/group, those systems were considered as independent data points/observations [21]. We used random meta-analytical methods for the calculation of effect size. The calculated effect size corresponds to variable effects of plant hormones under drought stress due to AMF inoculation compared to those of its control, i.e., without AMF inoculation. Non-significant response represents a 95% confidence interval that overlapped with zero values. Zero (0) effect sizes suggest no difference in effects between the experimental and control groups, negative values represent reduced AMF inoculation effects where the control group achieves a healthier response than the experimental group, and positive values represent increased effects in response to AM fungi inoculation where the experimental group reaches a healthier response than the control group. Positive effect size represents an increase in plant hormone production in AMF-inoculated plants, whereas a negative effect size confirms the decrease in plant hormone production.

Meta-analysis formula

We used the following formula for the calculation of effect size and AMF inoculation responses: ln R= ln (Xe/Xc)= ln Xi− ln Xc where Xe is the AMF inoculation (experimental) and Xc is the non-AMF inoculation (control).

Variance of the studies was calculated using the following formula: vlnR=(1/Ne)×(Stde/Xe)+(1/nc)×(Stdc/Xc) where Ne is the experimental sample size, Nc is the control sample size, Se is the standard deviations of the experiment, and Sc is the standard deviations of control. Categorical analyses were carried out to investigate AMF inoculation efficiency on individual plant species, AMF species, and intensities of drought. In the framework of our meta-analysis, under drought stress, response parameter observed after AMF inoculation was assessed by Spearman’s rho rank and Kendall’s tau correlation. We used the fail-safe number of the Rosenthal method to determine the number of non-significant, unpublished or missing studies, which would have to be added to our meta-analysis to nullify its overall effect size [22]. If this number was larger than 5n + 10 (n = number of studies), then publication bias was safely ignored. The percentage of plant hormones was calculated using the following formula: ( exp ( ln R)−1)×100%.

Results

Overall analysis

The overall effect size of AMF inoculation under drought stress for plant hormones was positive and significant (n = 252; LRR = 0.101; 95% CI, 0.0427–0.1502; Figure 1). Our results prove that AMF-inoculated plants significantly enhanced stress alleviation by 10% (Figure 1(a)). p Values associated with heterogeneity of the QT statistic showed that AMF inoculation under drought stress conditions for plant hormones significantly differed in effect (QT = 493.454; p Chi-square = 0.0000), and significant (p < 0.05) publication bias was observed using Kendall’s tau (p = 0.03) and Spearman’s rank correlation (p = 0.04). Moreover, fail-safe Rosenthal method (245,034) was larger than those of our analysis (1270); thus publication bias for our studies was safely ignored. Besides, there were differences in the AMF inoculation responses between stress and growth hormones. Levels of stress hormones increased by 2%, whereas growth hormones significantly increased by 24% under drought in AMF-inoculated plants. Our results confirmed that AMF found itself to be an efficient soil microbe for drought stress alleviation. Moreover, among growth hormones, IAA accumulation increased by 32%, followed by GA (24%), BR (11%), but ZR accumulation reduced by 18% (Figure 1(b)). For stress hormones, JA and SA significantly increased by 26%, whereas ABA decreased by 6% and was non-significant (Figure 1(c)).

Effect of level of stress on phytohormonal changes

Our analysis showed that moderate stress increased the content of plant hormones by 15%, whereas severe levels of drought stress reduced the accumulation of plant hormones by 2%, compared to those of non-AMF plants (Figure 2). Growth hormones increased by 21% and 32% in moderate and severe stress, respectively. Whereas, for stress hormones, moderate levels of stress showed increased levels of hormones by 11%, but severe stress showed a negative response to hormones, i.e., reduced by 15%. Under drought stress, some of the stress hormones increased by AMF inoculation, and some of them showed negative responses under drought stress. AMF inoculation increased IAA content by 39% and 34%, respectively, for moderate and severe levels of drought stress. For GA, under the moderate level of drought stress, AMF inoculation increased GA content by 14%, but for the severe level of drought stress, GA content increased by 47%. Whereas ZR content decreased by 22% and 9% for moderate and severe stress, respectively. We treated results with caution due to low sample size. BR content increased by 11% under a moderate level of stress, but due to low sample size, we did not analyze further.

Effect of drought intensities on growth and stress hormones. When confidence intervals overlap zero, the effect sizes are not significantly different from zero. Numbers above error bars represent the number of observations.

ABA activity decreased as levels of stress increased. Under a moderate level of drought stress, ABA increased by 8%, whereas a severe level of stress reduced it by 27%. Moreover, JA content increased by 20% and 162% for moderate and severe levels of drought stress, respectively. Another stress hormone, SA content, also increased by 13% and 54%, for moderate and severe levels of drought stress, respectively.

Identity of plant species on phytohormonal changes

Characteristics of plants varied under drought in AMF-inoculated plants. Among plant families, Solanaceae showed a significantly increased percentage (56%) of growth hormone accumulation, followed by Fabaceae (49%) and Poaceae family, whereas Rutaceae family showed a non-significant response to both growth and stress hormones (Figure 3(a)). For stress hormones, we did not find any significant results for plant families. Among plant species, Solanum lycopersicum (tomato) showed significant effects for both growth and stress hormones (Figure 3(b)), but all other species showed non-significant effects for plant hormone accumulation. Due to low sample sizes of individual plants, we treated results with caution. Monocot and dicot plants showed different effects under drought stress for stress hormones, in which monocots increased by 11%, whereas dicots decreased by 2%. Monocot and dicot plants increased growth hormone levels by 31% and 23%, respectively (Figure 4(a)). Among growth forms, herbaceous plants increased growth hormones by 65%, followed by grass and woody plants by 11% and 6%, respectively. Further, herbaceous plants showed the highest reduction (9%) for stress hormones, but grass and woody plants increased by 11% and 8%, respectively (Figure 4(b)). Most of the studies are carried out in greenhouse conditions; very few studies are carried out in field conditions (Figure 5(a)). We treated the results of field conditions with care due to their low sample size. In the future, we need more studies to compare the results of experimental conditions. For the life cycle, growth hormones increased in both annual and perennial plants by 47% and 10%, respectively. For stress hormones, annual and perennial plants slightly increased by 3% and 2%, respectively (Figure 5(b)).

Type of AM fungi on drought stress alleviation

Species of AMF and types of AMF inoculation showed different effects for plant hormones under drought stress. For AMF inoculation, growth hormone production increased by 46% for mixed inoculation, but single species inoculation increased by 17% (Figure 6(a)). For stress hormones, both single-species inoculation and mixed-species inoculation slightly increased by 3%. The highest plant hormone production was achieved in F. mosseae, followed by R. irregularis (Figure 6(b)). For stress hormones, F. mosseae inoculation increased stress hormones by 7%, whereas R. irregularis inoculated plants reduced stress hormone production by 6%.

Categorical analysis of AMF species and AMF inoculation. When confidence intervals overlap zero, the effect sizes are not significantly different from zero. Numbers above error bars represent the number of observations.

We also further analyzed their effects on individual plant hormones. For IAA, single species inoculation decreased the IAA content more than that of mixed AMF species inoculation, whereas it was non-significant for ABA content under drought stress. The pattern of increase in plant hormone levels also varies for AMF species. For IAA content, the highest accumulation was found in R. irregularis rather than in F. mosseae. In contrast, for ABA, R. irregularis showed a more negative response than that of F. mosseae. It indirectly influenced the overall stress hormonal levels under drought stress in AMF-inoculated plants. Overall stress hormonal analysis showed that R. irregularis had the highest negative response compared to those of F. mosseae. But for the growth hormones, both AMF species showed equal and positive effects under drought stress in AMF-inoculated plants. Our analysis confirmed that changes in plant hormone levels in AM fungi-inoculated plants depend on the type of AM fungal species under drought stress conditions. Thus, the efficacy of AM fungi on host plants varied and differed, corresponding to the percentage of mycorrhizal colonization and symbiosis. These results clearly confirmed that hormonal cross-talks are very important for stress alleviation in AMF-inoculated plants.

Discussion

In this study, we shed light on the plant hormone regulation under drought stress when an AM fungus is present in plant roots. Our previous studies on antioxidants confirmed the importance of AMF on drought stress alleviation in plants [18,19]. Now, we go further on this AMF-mediated stress alleviation mechanism by studying for the first time the effect of plant hormone regulation under various levels of drought stress in AMF-inoculated plants by meta-analysis. Plant hormones such as IAA, GA3, ABA, ZR, and so on, act as a signaling for arbuscule formation and mycorrhizal symbiotic relationship with plants [23–25]. AMF and host plants’ root association promotes drought stress alleviation through plant hormonal changes [26]. Drought stress-induced changes in physiological mechanisms promote hormonal regulation and accumulation essential for crop growth and productivity [27–31]. So, it is necessary to obtain an ecologically friendly methodology, such as AM fungi-mediated alleviation of drought stress. Previous studies emphasized the significance of AM fungi symbiosis on plant hormone accumulation and drought stress alleviation [30,32–37].

Our analysis showed that AM fungi-inoculated plants showed both positive and negative effect sizes for hormone accumulation under drought stress. First, we analyzed the changes in stress levels. We found that AM fungi significantly reduced the plant hormone levels by 2% under severe drought stress, whereas moderate levels of drought stress increased the levels of plant hormones by 15%. These results confirm that if the level of stress increases, the production of plant hormones decreases in AMF-inoculated plants, but it depends on the type of plant hormones. Moreover, AMF-inoculated plants increased the amount of growth hormones at all levels of drought stress. But stress hormones increased under moderate stress, whereas they were reduced under severe drought stress. Furthermore, changes in stress hormone accumulation determine the changes in drought stress alleviation.

Among growth hormones, IAA is found to be an important hormone not only because of the development of root and shoot, but also because it is essential for arbuscule formation [38,39]. Naturally, in plants, drought stress reduced the concentration of auxins (IAA). Whereas AMF symbiosis increased the content of IAA under drought stress. Our analysis showed that AMF inoculation increased the content of IAA at all levels of drought stress (Figure 2). A previous study also showed that AMF inoculation increased the content of IAA in maize plants under drought stress [39]. Thus, AMF symbiosis maintains auxin content and regulates AMF symbiosis for drought stress mitigation. AMF inoculation augmented IAA in leaves and roots, as reported earlier in trifoliate orange seedlings [29,40–42]. Another study also showed that F. mosseae inoculated S. constrictum had an increased level of auxin in plants [43]. Our results showed an increase in the content of IAA and GA3 levels, but reduced zeatin (ZT, a cytokinin); similar results were also found in previous studies in plants, exposed to drought stress conditions [41,44,45]. It suggests that AMF may possibly delay leaf senescence and trigger a quick adjustment to drought stress by changing the IAA signaling mechanism.

Another important stress hormone, ABA, is also associated with AM fungal symbiosis with roots of the plants and arbuscule formation, water, minerals, and nutrient exchange [46–48]. ABA is an influential stress hormone under drought stress because of its role in opening and closing of stomata. Some of the previous reports stated that ABA content should be increased to induce stomatal closure, thereby reducing water loss during drought stress conditions in plants [37,41,49]. However, contrary to the above-mentioned findings, our analysis showed reduced levels of ABA under drought stress, which is consistent with other findings [50,51]. Chitarra et al. [31] also confirmed a reduction in ABA content in AM fungi-inoculated tomato plants under drought stress. In another study, AMF-inoculated C. bungei plants had lower levels of ABA under drought stress [50,51]. AMF colonization showed lower leaf ABA content than non-colonized plants under severe water deficit. The inoculation of corn plants with AM fungi indicated reduced ABA levels during drought stress conditions [52]. AMF inoculation has shown reduced ABA in Capsicum annuum plants compared to those of non-AMF plants. It was proposed for further insights into stomatal closure, stomatal conductance, and ABA levels [51]. Nevertheless, host plants might produce higher ABA and transport it to the leaves for the regulation of stomatal closure under drought stress [7]. However, ABA levels in roots are higher than in shoots suggest root–shoot signaling modulation, and intricate studies are necessitated [53].

Our study showed that for MeJA, AMF colonization had increased MeJA levels of plants under severe levels of drought stress conditions, but the difference was not significant, and the sample size was also very small; we treated the data with caution. Whereas in AMF inoculation increased MeJA content by 20% under moderate levels of drought stress conditions (Figure 2). Recently, exogenous application of MeJA increased the root hydraulic conductivity of R. irregularis-inoculated Phaseolus vulgaris plants under drought stress; it also regulates other hormones like IAA and SA [47]. More studies are still needed to conclude the efficacy of MeJA in AMF-inoculated plants for both colonization and drought stress tolerance. AMF-colonized plants also recorded increased levels of GA by 14% and 47% under moderate and severe levels of drought stress, respectively (Figure 2). Wang et al. [41] study also showed that AMF inoculation significantly increased the content of gas under both well-watered and drought stress conditions. Our analysis also confirmed that some of the plant hormones had higher accumulation when a single species of AMF was inoculated, and some of them had increased effect sizes in mixed species of inoculation. These findings conclude that AMF inoculation plays a major role in plant hormone accumulation under drought stress. Hence, the type of mycorrhizal inoculation and species of AMF influencing the hormonal accumulation and drought stress alleviation efficiency need more field studies to understand the deeper perspectives. Moreover, hormonal interactions and their regulatory mechanism for arbuscule formation and stress alleviation were undoubtedly of immense impact in plants under drought stress for sustainable agriculture.

Conclusions

In our meta-analysis, we weighed the influences of several individual variables on AMF-mediated accumulation of hormones under drought stress by various categorical analyses. Notably, ABA levels decreased, and IAA levels increased with AMF inoculation under drought stress conditions. Also found increased levels of GA, JA, and SA accumulation and decreased levels of ZR accumulation with AMF inoculation under drought stress; due to low sample size, we treated the results with caution. More studies are needed for future analysis. Overall analysis showed that mixed AMF inoculation was found to be more effective in increasing the accumulation of growth hormones under drought stress. Moreover, growth hormone accumulation increased under both moderate and severe levels of drought stress in AMF-inoculated plants. Among AMF species, R. intraradices and F. mosseae were effective in the accumulation of plant hormones and alleviation of drought stress; notably, F. mosseae was found to be more efficient than R. intraradices. Therefore, our analysis suggests that the selection of specific AMF species for specific plant species could help increase plant performance under drought stress. Therefore, plant growth-promoting AMF-mediated alleviation of drought stress would be an eco-friendly approach for sustainable agriculture.
