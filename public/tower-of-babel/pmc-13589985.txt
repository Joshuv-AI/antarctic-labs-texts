Trajectories of Psychological Detachment and Associated Factors Among Family Caregivers of Patients with Breast Cancer Receiving Postoperative Chemotherapy
Authors: Jing-En Dai, Yan Zhang, Cheng-Jin Ma, Chen-Rong Mao, Xian-Wen Li, Ling Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to identify latent trajectory classes of psychological detachment and examine factors associated with class membership among family caregivers of patients with breast cancer (BC) undergoing postoperative chemotherapy.MethodsA convenience sample of 220 family caregivers of patients with BC undergoing postoperative chemotherapy was recruited from the breast surgery departments of two tertiary Grade A hospitals in Suzhou Municipal Hospital between December 2023 and December 2024. Baseline data were collected from caregivers prior to the first chemotherapy cycle (T0). Follow-up assessments were conducted using the Psychological Detachment Scale (PD) at three time points: after the first chemotherapy cycle (T1), at the midpoint of chemotherapy (T2), and after completion of chemotherapy (T3). Growth Mixture Modeling was applied to identify latent trajectory classes of psychological detachment. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed to assess factors associated with trajectory class membership.ResultsA total of 200 caregivers completed the full follow-up assessment. At T0, T1, T2, and T3, the mean PD scores among caregivers were 6.74 ± 1.30, 5.75 ± 0.96, 9.14 ± 1.49, and 11.85 ± 1.66, respectively. Three distinct trajectory classes were identified: a high-level detachment group (69.0%), a moderate-level detachment group (25.5%), and a low-level slow-increase detachment group (5.5%). Patient age, tumor stage, molecular subtype, caregiver burden, social support, and fear of cancer recurrence were significantly associated with psychological detachment trajectory class membership (p < 0.05).ConclusionPsychological detachment trajectories differed among family caregivers of patients with BC undergoing postoperative chemotherapy, indicating population heterogeneity. Targeted interventions tailored to trajectory class characteristics and associated factors may support improvements in psychological detachment among caregivers. Given the small size of the low-level group, further validation in larger samples is warranted.

Introduction

In 2022, the global incidence of breast cancer (BC) reached 11.6%, with 2.3 million new cases reported, ranking second among all cancers worldwide and first among cancers in women.1 Surgery and chemotherapy represent primary treatment modalities for patients with BC; however, these interventions not only reduce patient quality of life but also impose a substantial psychological burden on caregivers.2 With the advancement of positive psychology, psychological detachment has received increasing research attention. Psychological detachment is defined as a state in which family caregivers mentally and physically disengage from patient-related thoughts and stressors during periods away from caregiving, thereby facilitating rest and recovery.3 Adequate physical and psychological recovery enables caregivers to maintain energy, deliver higher-quality care, and support patient recovery.4 Unlike burnout or emotional exhaustion—which reflect chronic depletion—psychological detachment specifically captures the recovery process during off-caregiving periods, representing a distinct and potentially modifiable resource for caregiver well-being. Current research on psychological detachment among family caregivers has been predominantly cross-sectional, with limited longitudinal evaluation of changes during chemotherapy.5,6 The postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy phase represents a distinct and prolonged stressor period, during which caregivers face cumulative physical and emotional demands that may differentially affect their capacity to detach. Guided by the cancer family caregiving experience model, this study longitudinally followed family caregivers of patients with BC undergoing postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy, and assessed psychological detachment across three domains: cancer trajectory, contextual factors, and stress processes.7 Four time points (T0–T3) were selected to capture both acute responses to chemotherapy initiation and longer-term adaptation patterns across the treatment course. Therefore, the goal of this study was to identify latent trajectory classes of psychological detachment and examine associated factors among family caregivers using a growth mixture model (GMM).

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

Family caregivers who met the inclusion criteria were recruited from the breast surgery departments of two tertiary Grade A hospitals in Suzhou Municipal Hospital between December 2023 and December 2024. The patient inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed BC and completion of surgery;8 (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) a confirmed chemotherapy regimen involving at least six cycles. The family caregiver inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years, serving as the primary caregiver during chemotherapy (including parents, spouses, and children) and being aware of the patient’s diagnosis; (2) provision of at least four hours of caregiving per day; (3) absence of cognitive impairment and adequate ability to understand and communicate. Patients who had completed surgery were specifically selected to establish a uniform psychological baseline, as the postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy phase represents a distinct and prolonged stressor period for caregivers. A minimum of six chemotherapy cycles was required to ensure a sufficient duration for longitudinal follow-up across the four predefined assessment time points.

The patient exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe psychiatric disorder or psychological illness; (2) severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction or other serious physical illness; (3) history of other malignancies. The family caregiver exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) recent exposure to major stressful life events (eg, divorce, bereavement); (2) concurrent caregiving for another patient. The withdrawal criteria were as follows: (1) patient death during follow-up; (2) voluntary withdrawal of the family caregiver during follow-up.

The sample size was calculated using the sample size table for single-group repeated measures designs, with four measurement time points per participant, a mean correlation coefficient of r = 0.5, an effect size of f = 0.14, a significance level of α = 0.05, and statistical power of 1 − β = 0.8, resulting in a required sample size of 142.9 To account for potential attrition and ensure the reliability of the results, the sample size was increased by 20% to 178. The final sample size was 220. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Suzhou Municipal Hospital (approval number: K-2024-066-K01). Participant recruitment began in December 2023. The temporal discrepancy between study initiation and formal ethics approval was subsequently reviewed by the institutional ethics committee, which provided retrospective confirmation for the use of the collected data.

Instruments

General Information Questionnaire

The general information questionnaire was developed based on the objectives of the present study. Patient-related data collected included age, comorbidities, tumor stage, type of surgery, and molecular subtype. Family caregiver–related data included age, sex, place of residence, education level, and monthly household income per capita.

Psychological Detachment Scale

The Psychological Detachment Scale (PD) was originally developed by Sonnentag et al and subsequently adapted by Hou et al for use among oncology caregivers.10,11 The scale is unidimensional and comprises four items. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (5 points). The total score is obtained by summing the four items, resulting in a possible range of 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating a greater level of psychological detachment. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale in the current sample was 0.77.

Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview

The Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview (ZBI) was originally developed by Zarit et al in the 1980s and subsequently adapted for use in China by Wang et al in 2006.12,13 The ZBI comprises two dimensions (personal strain and role strain) and includes a total of 22 items. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from “never” (0), “rarely” (1), “sometimes” (2), “frequently” (3), to “always” (4). A total score of less than 21 is considered indicative of no apparent or mild burden; scores between 21 and 39 indicate moderate burden; and scores of 40 or above indicate severe burden. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale in the current sample was 0.87. The ZBI has been widely used in China to assess caregiver burden among cancer caregivers.

Social Support Rating Scale

The Social Support Rating Scale was developed by Xiao Shuiyuan in 1986, based on prior international research.14 The scale comprises 10 items across three dimensions: subjective support, objective support, and support utilization. Items 6 and 7 are scored using a multiple-response format: if “no source” is selected, a score of 0 is assigned; if “the following sources” is selected, the score is calculated cumulatively according to the number of sources endorsed. The remaining items are scored using a single-choice format, with each response option assigned a corresponding score. The maximum total score is 66, with higher scores indicating a greater level of social support received. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale in the current sample was 0.858.

Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form/Caregiver Version

The Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form/Caregiver Version (FoP-Q-SF/C) was developed by Hu et al based on the Chinese spousal version of the FoP-Q-SF developed by Chinese members of the study team.15,16 The questionnaire comprises three dimensions (health and symptoms, interpersonal support, and occupational concerns) and includes a total of 12 items. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from “never” to “always,” with higher response categories corresponding to higher scores. The total score ranges from 12 to 60. A score of 34 or above is considered indicative of the presence of fear of progression in the respondent. Higher scores reflect greater fear of disease progression. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale in the current sample was 0.919. The FoP-Q-SF/C has been used by Li et al to assess fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) among caregivers of patients with colon cancer.17

Data Collection

Data collection was conducted by research personnel who had received standardized training, in accordance with established survey procedures, among all family caregivers who met the inclusion criteria. Prior to data collection, the purpose, methods, potential benefits, and risks of the study were explained to participants, confidentiality was assured, and informed consent was obtained. From previously published longitudinal studies examining psychological and related outcomes among family caregivers of cancer patients during chemotherapy, as well as the cancer trajectory component of the cancer family caregiving experience model, four time points were designated for data collection:7,18,19 before the first chemotherapy cycle (T0), after the first chemotherapy cycle (T1), at the midpoint of chemotherapy (T2), and after completion of chemotherapy (T3). To minimize response fatigue, the total survey duration was strictly limited to 10–15 minutes.

Questionnaire completion was closely monitored by research personnel to enhance data completeness and reliability. In cases of missing data, family caregivers were promptly asked to provide the required information. The proportion of missing data was minimal, and complete-case analysis was applied. Participants who could not be contacted after three consecutive attempts were classified as lost to follow-up. Following completion of data collection, double data entry and verification were performed independently by two qualified data management personnel to ensure data accuracy. The verified data were subsequently imported into the database for statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.7. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were described using the mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and proportions. Between-group differences were assessed using analysis of variance or the chi-square test, as appropriate. GMM was conducted to identify trajectory patterns of psychological detachment among family caregivers, with models specifying one to five latent classes. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion, and sample size–adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion, with lower values indicating a better model fit. Entropy values ranged from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 reflecting greater classification accuracy. The Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) was used as the primary criterion for selecting the number of classes, as it provides a robust comparison between models with k and k−1 classes.20 Linear slopes were specified as a pragmatic choice to capture the overall upward trend in the present analysis. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine factors associated with latent trajectory class membership of psychological detachment among family caregivers. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Discussion

Trajectories of Psychological Detachment Among Family Caregivers

The results of this study indicated that psychological detachment among family caregivers of patients with BC undergoing postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy was heterogeneous, with three latent trajectory classes identified: the high-level detachment group (69.0%), the moderate-level detachment group (25.5%), and the low-level slow-increase detachment group (5.5%). This finding is consistent with that reported by Huang et al in a longitudinal study examining benefit finding among caregivers of colorectal cancer patients undergoing postoperative chemotherapy.21 The identification of distinct trajectory classes indicates that levels of psychological detachment vary according to characteristics of patients and family caregivers, indicating that different latent classes may require distinct intervention approaches. While the associations between psychological outcomes and factors such as caregiver burden or fear of cancer recurrence align with established psycho-oncology literature, the present study adds longitudinal depth by mapping how these factors are associated with distinct developmental trajectories over the course of chemotherapy.

Although all three trajectory classes demonstrated an upward trend over time (p < 0.05), only the high-level detachment group exceeded the Chinese normative score of 11 for the PD by the end of chemotherapy, whereas the moderate-level and low-level slow-increase detachment groups remained below this threshold.11 These findings suggest that caregivers in the moderate- and low-level detachment groups should be identified at an early stage. Interventions should be implemented at appropriate time points according to trajectory type and tailored to class-specific characteristics to improve psychological detachment effectively. Future interventional studies should explore strategies tailored to these specific trajectory characteristics. Concrete clinical strategies to improve psychological detachment include mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), encouraging scheduled “time-outs” involving brief physical respites from caregiving environments, and engaging caregivers in immersive hobbies. However, as these recommendations are derived from observational data, their effectiveness requires empirical testing in future intervention studies.

Factors Associated with Psychological Detachment Trajectories Among Family Caregivers

Patient Age

This study demonstrated that, when compared with the high-level detachment group, caregivers of middle-aged patients were more likely to be classified in the low-level slow-increase detachment group, which is consistent with the findings reported by Tao et al22 Most caregivers in this study were spouses. Middle-aged cancer patients and their family caregivers are typically primary income earners and key sources of emotional support within the family, and therefore may experience greater psychological, financial, and social burdens, which are associated with lower levels of psychological detachment.22,23 Middle-aged patients experiencing substantial declines in self-care ability and significant reductions or loss of family and social roles may have difficulty adapting to these changes. Such circumstances may be associated with increased psychological stress responses and a higher likelihood of negative emotional states. Given the bidirectional relationship between patient and caregiver psychological well-being, caregivers of middle-aged patients may experience elevated levels of negative affect and may therefore be more likely to be classified in the low-level detachment group.23 These findings indicate that enhanced psychological screening and assessment should be conducted for family caregivers of middle-aged patients. For caregivers with lower levels of psychological detachment, technology-assisted mobile health interventions may be considered to improve psychological detachment. Such interventions have demonstrated potential in reducing negative affect among caregivers.24,25

Tumor Stage and Molecular Subtype

This study demonstrated that family caregivers of patients with stage I–II disease, luminal subtype, or HER2-positive BC were more likely to be classified in the high-level detachment group, consistent with findings reported by Zhao et al26 These patterns may be attributable to the generally more favorable prognoses associated with stage I–II disease, luminal subtype, and HER2-positive BC. In contrast, stage III–IV disease or triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is associated with poorer prognostic outcomes and an increased risk of adverse clinical events, including treatment failure or rapid disease progression, which may contribute to anticipatory grief among family caregivers.27 These findings indicate that particular attention should be directed toward family caregivers of patients with stage III–IV disease or TNBC. A family-centered dyadic psychoeducational intervention may be considered to address negative emotional responses in both patients and their family caregivers, thereby facilitating improvements in caregiver psychological detachment.28

Caregiver Burden

Caregiver burden is defined as the physical, psychological, emotional, and social pressures experienced during the provision of care to patients.29 This study indicated that, compared with the high-level detachment group, family caregivers with greater caregiver burden were more likely to be classified in the moderate-level detachment group or the low-level slow-increase detachment group, consistent with findings reported by Lu et al30 This association may be explained by the prolonged cohabitation of family caregivers with patients and their primary responsibility for caregiving, which may result in cumulative impacts across psychological, physical, financial, and social domains, thereby limiting psychological detachment. It has been reported that the effect of caregiver burden on family caregiver well-being is more strongly influenced by long-term psychological and social resources than by sociodemographic or disease-related factors.31 These findings indicate that, in clinical nursing practice, supportive care programs delivered through smartphone-based applications may be implemented for family caregivers with high caregiver burden to reduce burden levels.32

Social Support

Social support is defined as assistance that can be perceived, recognized, or received by an individual from others.33 This study indicated that family caregivers with higher levels of social support were more likely to be classified in the high-level detachment group, consistent with findings reported in prior studies.5,34 According to the stress-buffering hypothesis, higher levels of social support are associated with attenuation of stress responses, thereby reducing adverse effects on physical and psychological well-being and facilitating positive emotional experiences.35 Karahan et al reported that the extent of physical and psychological responses to stressful events is significantly influenced by the level of social support.36 Collectively, these findings indicate that, among family caregivers of patients with BC undergoing postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy, higher levels of social support are associated with improved stress adaptation, reduced negative emotional distress, and enhanced psychological detachment. These findings also suggest that, in clinical practice, the level of social support among family caregivers should be monitored. For caregivers with lower levels of social support, nurse-led peer support interventions may be implemented to enhance social support and improve the quality of care.37

Fear of Cancer Recurrence

FCR is defined as fear, worry, or concern regarding the possibility of cancer returning or progressing.38 This study indicated that, compared with the high-level detachment group, family caregivers with higher levels of FCR were more likely to be classified in the moderate-level detachment group or the low-level slow-increase detachment group, consistent with findings reported by Zhang et al6 Prolonged fear and anxiety may be associated with activation of caregivers’ stress response systems, resulting in sustained elevations in stress-related hormones such as cortisol; these physiological changes may adversely affect psychological status and hinder the attainment of psychological detachment.39 According to the theory of cognitive adaptation, exposure to physical, psychological, and social stressors may be associated with maladaptive psychological responses, including anxiety, depression, and reduced self-esteem, which may exacerbate negative affect and further impede psychological detachment.40,41 These findings indicate that web-based psychological support programs may be considered by healthcare professionals to reduce FCR levels among family caregivers, thereby promoting improvements in psychological detachment.42

Caregiver Age

Caregiver age was not significantly associated with psychological detachment trajectory class membership in the present study. This may be related to the disease stage and care needs of the patients. First, most patients with breast cancer in this study were at an early disease stage and therefore generally had a favorable prognosis.43 Second, patients at an early disease stage tend to retain greater self-care ability, resulting in a relatively lower caregiving burden for family caregivers, which is consistent with findings from an overseas study.44 Consequently, caregiver age may not have exerted a significant influence on psychological detachment in this cohort.

Relationship to the Patient

In the present study, relationship to the patient was not significantly associated with psychological detachment trajectory class membership, which is consistent with the findings of Zhang et al6 A possible explanation is that most caregivers in this study were parents or spouses. These caregivers generally had close relationships and strong emotional bonds with the patients and were often willing to undertake substantial caregiving responsibilities, consistent with the findings of Breuning et al45 Accordingly, caregivers with different relationships to the patient should receive comparable attention in clinical practice rather than differentiated support solely on the basis of family role.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from only two tertiary Grade A hospitals using convenience sampling, which limits the external validity and generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, although the three-class GMM model demonstrated optimal fit indices, the low-level slow-increase detachment group comprised only 5.5% of the sample (n=11). This small subgroup size may compromise the stability of the classification and the reliability of the multinomial regression estimates involving this class. Consequently, the findings related to this specific trajectory class should be interpreted with caution, and future research with larger samples is required to conduct sensitivity analyses and validate the clinical meaning and stability of this subgroup. Third, while the longitudinal design strengthens our understanding of psychological detachment trajectories, the reliance on self-report instruments introduces a potential risk of social desirability bias and response fatigue, particularly given that each session required participants to complete multiple scales. Fourth, as this study was observational in nature and conducted during a specific period of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy, the results may not be fully applicable to patients undergoing different treatment regimens (eg, neoadjuvant chemotherapy or endocrine therapy alone). Additionally, causal relationships cannot be inferred from the observed associations.

Conclusion

In summary, a GMM was used to characterize trajectories of psychological detachment among family caregivers of patients with BC undergoing postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy, and three latent trajectory classes were identified. Patient age, tumor stage, molecular subtype, caregiver burden, social support, and FCR were identified as factors associated with caregiver psychological detachment trajectories. These findings indicate that heterogeneity among family caregivers should be considered in clinical practice. The distinct trajectories identified in this study provide an empirical basis for the future development and testing of targeted interventions tailored to trajectory class characteristics. However, given the observational nature of this study and the small size of the lowest trajectory class, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to enhance the generalizability and external validity of the results.
