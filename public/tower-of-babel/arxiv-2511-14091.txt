State-Space Representation of INGARCH Models and Their Application in Insurance
Jae Youn Ahn, Hong Beng Lim, Mario V. Wüthrich

Abstract
Integer-valued generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedastic (INGARCH) models are a popular framework for modeling serial dependence in count time-series. While convenient for modeling, prediction, and estimation, INGARCH models lack a clear theoretical justification for the evolution step. This limitation not only makes interpretation difficult and complicates the inclusion of covariates, but can also make the handling of missing data computationally burdensome. Consequently, applying such models in an insurance context, where covariates and missing observations are common, can be challenging. In this paper, we first introduce the marginalized state-space model (M-SSM), defined solely through the marginal distribution of the observations, and show that INGARCH models arise as special cases of this framework. The M-SSM formulation facilitates the natural incorporation of covariates and missing data mechanisms, and this representation in turn provides a coherent way to incorporate these elements within the INGARCH model as well. We then demonstrate that an M-SSM can admit an observation-driven state-space model (O-SSM) representation when suitable assumptions are imposed on the evolution of its conditional mean. This lifting from an M-SSM to an O-SSM provides a natural setting for establishing weak stationarity, even in the presence of heterogeneity and missing observations. The proposed ideas are illustrated through the Poisson and the Negative-Binomial INGARCH(1,1) models, highlighting their applicability in predictive analysis for insurance data.



Keywords:
Count time-series model, INGARCH, state-space model, observation-driven state-space model, Bühlmann credibility.

Introduction

1.1
INGARCH model

In insurance, claims counts modeling is an important topic in insurance pricing and risk management.
While random-effect models
(
32
)
are central for capturing policyholder-level variation across time and deriving fair premiums, it has been increasingly recognized that accounting for temporal dependence is equally essential for reflecting the dynamic structure of claims histories
(
33
;
6
;
22
)
.
Alongside the integer-valued autoregressive (INAR) model
(
4
)
,
the
integer-valued generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedastic
(INGARCH) model
has emerged as a popular framework for representing such temporal dependence in counts processes.
Introduced by
16
and extended by
19
, it combines an autoregressive recursion for the conditional mean with a specified conditional distribution, often Poisson or
negative-binomial (NB) for claims counts.
In particular, the INGARCH models were inspired by analogous specifications for continuous-valued data, including the generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedastic (GARCH) model for Gaussian observations
(
7
)
, the autoregressive conditional duration (ACD) model for Exponential or Gamma-distributed durations
(
15
)
, and related Gamma autoregressive models
(
26
)
.
These parallels indicate that the INGARCH approach belongs to a broader class of conditional mean models applicable across different data types.

In the INGARCH setting, the one-step-ahead conditional mean is a linear combination of a constant term, the latest observation(s), and the preceding conditional mean(s), allowing the model to capture a wide range of temporal patterns in counts data.
Its parameters can be estimated directly via the conditional log-likelihood, avoiding the need for simulation-based procedures.
This combination of modeling flexibility and computational simplicity has led to applications in various areas, including climate science
(
25
)
, actuarial science
(
34
)
, epidemiology
(
18
)
, and many other domains where count data exhibits dynamic dependence.
Well-known special cases are the Poisson-INGARCH model
(
16
)
and its NB extensions
(
39
)
.

When applying INGARCH models in insurance practice, two issues may emerge.
First, bringing exogenous covariates into an INGARCH specification can be delicate.
With an identity link, the linear predictor must remain non-negative, which typically imposes sign or range restrictions on coefficients or regressors
(
11
)
.
A log-link removes this positivity constraint and allows a GLM-style specification for the covariates, but the dynamics then operate on the log-means, thus, the conditional means are no longer an affine function of past counts and stability conditions require a different interpretation
(
20
)
.
Second, because the INGARCH recursion depends directly on the past observations, any missing value breaks the
recursion; the conditional log-likelihood must either integrate over the unobserved counts or rely on explicit
imputation, both of which increase computational burden and can hinder interpretation
(
10
)
.

1.2
Observation-driven state-space models

Observation-driven state-space models (O-SSMs) form a broad class of dynamic models in which the latent state is updated by a deterministic function of past observations and past latent variables. The distinction between observation-driven and parameter-driven state-space models, introduced in the seminal paper by
12
, lies in how the state evolution is specified. In parameter-driven models, the state process follows a stochastic evolution with its own noise term, not impacted by the specific observations. A key challenge with parameter-driven state-space models is that the conditional distribution of the latent states given the data is typically not available in closed form. This is largely due to the difficulty of choosing noise distributions that preserve conjugacy, which often forces reliance on numerical approximation.
In contrast, an O-SSM defines the state update equation explicitly, enabling greater control over the predictive distribution of the latent variables. This is especially important in conjugate prior settings to ensure the predictive distribution is available in closed form, hence circumventing the computational and interpretational challenges of parameter-driven models.

An O-SSM is typically specified through two components, chosen so that the likelihood function can be evaluated analytically:

i.

an
observation equation
, which specifies the conditional distribution of the response given the current latent state;

ii.

an
evolution equation
, which deterministically updates the state from the previous latent state and the latest observation.

This computational tractability, combined with the ability to incorporate rich dynamics through the update function, has made the O-SSM widely used in insurance
(
5
;
1
;
3
)
, but also in other fields such as econometrics, environmental science, epidemiology where modeling time-varying behavior is important
(
28
;
29
)
.

1.3
From the INGARCH model to the M-SSM and the O-SSM

Although INGARCH models and O-SSMs share structural similarities, they have largely evolved in separate strands of the time-series literature. The INGARCH framework specifies a single-step recursion for the conditional mean of the observed process, whereas an O-SSM separates this into two distinct steps: an observation equation and a deterministic state-update equation.
While the single-step formulation of INGARCH models is often easier to specify and interpret, the two-step structure of the O-SSM offers a distinct advantage in insurance applications by allowing for greater flexibility in incorporating explanatory variables, handling heterogeneity, and systematically addressing missing observations.

In this paper, we show that the INGARCH(1,1) model can be represented as a particular case of an O-SSM. First, Section
3
introduces the notion of a
marginalized state-space model
(M-SSM), defined as a framework specified solely through the marginal distribution of the observations. We further demonstrate, in Sections
4
and
5
, that INGARCH models arise as a special case of the M-SSM framework, which provides a theoretical foundation for addressing heterogeneity and missing observations within INGARCH models.

We then examine the weak stationarity of the INGARCH(1,1) model. This property is essential for reliable inference and prediction in short time-series settings, which are commonly encountered in insurance applications
(
8
;
30
)
. Section
3.2
shows that a M-SSM admits an O-SSM representation whenever certain convex-order conditions on the conditional mean in the evolution step are satisfied. This lifting from a M-SSM to an O-SSM is essential for analyzing the weak stationarity property: in the presence of heterogeneity and missing observations, stationarity can only be meaningfully defined through the state-space process rather than the observation process.
However, because the M-SSM (and thus the INGARCH model) does not uniquely determine the underlying state-space dynamics, it does not allow identification or analysis of the covariance structure of the latent process; see Remark
1
in Section
3
. By contrast, the state-space dynamics are well defined in the O-SSM framework.

Sections
4
and
5
illustrate the proposed approach for two widely used special cases, the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) and the NB-INGARCH(1,1) models.
Section
6
further demonstrates how heterogeneity can be accommodated through explanatory variables and how missing observations can be systematically handled within these models.
We also discuss the weak stationarity properties of the Poisson and the NB-INGARCH(1,1) models together with these extensions, and we present a brief real-data analysis from an insurance application to illustrate the practical implementation of the models.
Finally, Section
7
concludes the paper by highlighting that our results establish a conceptual bridge between INGARCH models and O-SSMs, and provides a set of tools for extending, estimating, and interpreting such models within a unified state-space framework, particularly in the context of insurance applications.

In the next section, we begin by introducing the formal definitions of the O-SSM and the parameter-driven state-space model (P-SSM), along with their linearized restricted versions.

Dynamic random effect models

(O-SSM)

As explained in Corollary

Model 2

(O-SSM with linear evolution)
.

If we equip Model
1
with the additional property

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+1}\mid\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t}\right]=\Delta_{t}\Theta_{t}+c_{t},

(2.4)

for some constants
\Delta_{t},c_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, the model is called an
observation-driven linear state-space model
(O-LSSM).

An O-LSSM has the property that the observations
Z_{\leq t}
do not impact the conditional means (
2.4
), but they only potentially impact the higher conditional moments of the states
\Theta_{t+1}
, given
(\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t})

Our main interest in this manuscript lies in these O-LSSMs and we analyze their behavior up to their second moments. Under an O-LSSM assumption, for
k\geq 1
we have the recursion

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+k}\mid\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+k}\mid\Theta_{\leq t+k-1},Z_{\leq t+k-1}\right]\mid\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t}\right]

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t+k-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+k-1}\mid\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t}\right]+c_{t+k-1}.

Recursively expanding, we have for O-LSSMs

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+k}\mid\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t}\right]=\Theta_{t}\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{t+j}+c_{t:t+k-1}^{*},

(2.5)

where we set

c_{t:t+k-1}^{*}\;=\;\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}c_{t+r}\prod_{j=r+1}^{k-1}\Delta_{t+j},

with an empty product set to one.
As a result, applying the tower property, we have for O-LSSMs

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+k}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]\,\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{t+j}+c_{t:t+k-1}^{*},

and the serial covariance structure

{\rm Cov}\left(\Theta_{t},\Theta_{t+k}\right)={\rm Cov}\left(\Theta_{t},{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+k}\mid\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t}\right]\right)={\rm Cov}\left(\Theta_{t},\Theta_{t}\,\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{t+j}\right)={\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{t}\right)\,\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{t+j}.

(2.6)

Another special case of the O-SSM of Model
1
is obtained by restricting the evolution equation to be independent of the past responses.
This leads to a fully probabilistic state evolution, and it facilitates flexibility in state dynamics modeling.

Model 3

(P-SSM)
.

The stochastic process of response variables
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and state-spaces
(\Theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a
parameter-driven state-space model
(P-SSM) if, for all

, it satisfies the observation equation (
2.1
) and the evolution equation

{\Theta}_{t+1}\mid(Z_{\leq t},\Theta_{\leq t})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\rm(d)}}}\Theta_{t+1}\mid\Theta_{t}.

(2.7)

Theorem 1
.

Under the P-SSM dynamics of Model
3
, we have the decomposition

f\!\bigl(Z_{\leq t}\mid\Theta_{\leq t}\bigr)\;=\;f(Z_{\leq t-k}\mid\Theta_{\leq t-k})\,\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}f\!\bigl(Z_{t-j}\mid\Theta_{t-j}\bigr),\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }k\geq 1.

Proof.

Assume the P-SSM dynamics of Model
3
holds. Using the autoregressive representation of the joint distribution, we have

\displaystyle f(Z_{\leq t}\mid\Theta_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=f(Z_{\leq t},\Theta_{\leq t})/\pi(\Theta_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=f(Z_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t-1},\Theta_{\leq t})\,\pi(\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t-1},\Theta_{\leq t-1})\,f(Z_{\leq t-1},\Theta_{\leq t-1})/\pi(\Theta_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=f(Z_{t}\mid\Theta_{t})\,\pi(\Theta_{t}\mid\Theta_{t-1})\,f(Z_{\leq t-1}\mid\Theta_{\leq t-1})/\pi(\Theta_{t}\mid\Theta_{\leq t-1})

\displaystyle=f(Z_{t}\mid\Theta_{t})\,f(Z_{\leq t-1}\mid\Theta_{\leq t-1}).

Recursive iteration completes the proof.
∎

The previous theorem proves an alternative representation of the P-SSM.

{\Theta}_{t+1}\mid(Z_{\leq t},\Theta_{\leq t})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\rm(d)}}}\Theta_{t+1}\mid\Theta_{t}.

3
Marginalized state-space models

3.1
Analysis of the state-space dynamics

When dealing with O-SSM dynamics, in general, the interest lies in the distribution of the responses
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
without the need of explicitly knowing the full joint distribution
(Z_{t},\Theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
. For example, for model fitting, since typically only values of the response variables are observed, MLE requires computing the marginal likelihood of the observations. If the states are involved (i.e., if integrating them out does not result in a closed form), model fitting usually relies on numerical schemes such as the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm. Since this can be inefficient, the time-series literature often directly makes assumptions on the distribution of
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
without an underlying full specification of the state-space dynamics. The most popular example for counts data is the INGARCH model. We define such models in general.

Model 5

(M-SSM)
.

The stochastic process consisting of observed responses
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and latent states
(\Theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, satisfying
(
2.1
) for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, is defined as a
marginalized state-space model
(M-SSM) if the following specifications are given:

i.

The observation equation

\pi(Z_{t}\mid\Theta_{t}),\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z}.

ii.

The marginal evolution equation

\pi({\Theta}_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}),\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z}.

(3.1)

If we furthermore assume

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]=\Delta_{t}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]+c_{t},

(3.2)

for some constants
\Delta_{t},c_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, we call this model a
marginalized linear state-space model
(M-LSSM).

The crucial difference to the previous specifications is the marginal evolution equation
(
3.1
). In this specification, there is no need to track the past states
\Theta_{\leq t}
because we can directly simulate the next state
\Theta_{t+1}
by just having knowledge about the past responses
Z_{\leq t}
. Of course, this is a major advantage because it does not require integrating out the hidden states in (
3.1
).

The following result shows that the joint distribution of the observations
Z_{\leq t}
of a M-SSM dynamics according to Model
5
is well defined.

Lemma 2
.

Under Model
5
, we have the following properties:

i.

The one-step filtering update is given by

\pi(\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t+1})\;\propto\;f(Z_{t+1}\mid\Theta_{t+1})\,\pi(\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}).

(3.3)

ii.

The marginal density of
Z_{\leq t}
can be written as

\displaystyle f(Z_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=f(Z_{\leq 0})\,\prod_{j=1}^{t}f(Z_{j}\mid Z_{\leq j-1})=f(Z_{\leq 0})\,\prod_{j=1}^{t}\int f(Z_{j}\mid\theta_{j})\,\pi(\theta_{j}\mid Z_{\leq j-1})\,{\rm d}\nu(\theta_{j}).

where
\nu
is the
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathbb{R}
for which the probability density
\pi
has been defined.

When the marginal evolution equation is chosen under a conjugate specification, the filtering
distribution
\pi(\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t+1})
admits a closed-form expression.
Moreover, the corresponding predictive distribution

f(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t})\;=\;\int f(Z_{t+1}\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta\mid Z_{\leq t})\,{\rm d}\nu(\theta),

is also available in closed form under the standard natural exponential family-conjugate
setup, where the observation equation belongs to a natural exponential family and the
marginal evolution equation is the associated conjugate prior with a tractable normalizing constant
(
14
;
23
;
9
)
.

Remark 1
.

By construction, integrating out the latent states of any given O-SSM results in a M-SSM. The marginal evolution equation in (
3.1
) is precisely the conditional marginal that results from such an integration. Importantly, the O-SSM corresponding to a well-defined M-SSM is
not
unique. Specifically, while the M-SSM dynamics specifies only the conditional marginals

\pi(\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t})\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}),

any bivariate (conditional) copula (dependence structure) defining the joint conditional distribution of

(\Theta_{t},\Theta_{t+1})
, given
Z_{\leq t}
, i.e.,

\pi(\Theta_{t},\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t})=\pi(\Theta_{t+1}\mid\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t})\,\pi(\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}),

could underlie these dynamics. Thus, this model lacks the explicit definition of this conditional dependence structure and, therefore, is not fully identifiable in terms of the state-space process
(
2
)
.
This joint conditional distribution naturally defines the evolution equation of the O-SSM

\pi(\Theta_{t+1}\mid\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t})=\frac{\pi(\Theta_{t},\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t})}{\pi(\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t})}.

Consequently,
any O-SSM that shares these conditional marginals – but differ in their
copula – induce exactly the same M-SSM.
Since the copula linking
\Theta_{t}
and
\Theta_{t+1}
, conditional on
Z_{\leq t}
, is left unspecified, the conditional covariance

{\rm Cov}\left(\Theta_{t+1},\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right),

is not identified within the M-SSM framework. Consequently, the unconditional decomposition

{\rm Cov}\left(\Theta_{t+1},\Theta_{t}\right)\;=\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm Cov}\left(\Theta_{t+1},\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right)\right]\;+\;{\rm Cov}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right],\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]\right),

also does not identify the covariance
{\rm Cov}\left(\Theta_{t+1},\Theta_{t}\right)
. Hence, a weak stationarity statement for the latent state process
(\Theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is out of scope in this modeling approach as it can have any structure.

This under-identification also affects the observation process: when there are prior differences (time-varying or unit-specific) or when observations are missing, a coherent weak-stationarity analysis of the observation process
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
depends on the latent dependence structure which is unspecified under the M-SSM assumptions. This is our main motivation to work within the framework of the O-SSM/O-LSSM model formulation, i.e., it restores the identifiability of the covariances, and it enables us to handle heterogeneity through covariates and the missing data problem.

3.2
Lifting M-LSSM to O-LSSM

Our next goal is to lift a M-LSSM satisfying the marginal linear relation (
3.2
) to an O-LSSM that satisfies the linear conditional-mean property (
2.4
). As discussed in Remark
1
, one can lift any M-SSM to an O-SSM by specifying the copula. However, if one additionally specifies the condition

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+1}|Z_{\leq t}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[m_{t}(\Theta_{t})|Z_{\leq t}\right],

(3.4)

as in (
3.2
) where
m_{t}(x)=\Delta_{t}x+c_{t}
, such a lift need not to exist. We hence seek conditions under which such a lift exists. The next result shows that a necessary requirement is the conditional convex ordering

m_{t}(\Theta_{t})\mid Z_{\leq t}\;\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\;\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t},\qquad\text{a.s.}

If this convex ordering fails to hold on a set of histories
z_{\leq t}
, with positive probability, then no O-SSM lift with the conditional mean as in (
3.4
) exists. In particular, since the convex ordering implies the variance ordering, provided second moments exist, we also have the following conditional variance inequality

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left(m_{t}(\Theta_{t})\mid Z_{\leq t}\right)\;\leq\;{\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right),\qquad\text{a.s.}

Lemma 3
.

Consider the stochastic process of observed responses
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and latent states
(\Theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
in the M-SSM of Model
5
satisfying the conditional mean condition (
3.4
) for given measurable functions
m_{t}
. Assume there exists an O-SSM (Model
1
) with processes
(Z_{t}^{*})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
(\Theta_{t}^{*})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that

Remark 2
.

We provide some insight into why convex ordering is the relevant ordering for the existence of the desired coupling. First, note that by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\phi(X)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\phi({\mathbb{E}}\left[X\right]),

i.e., we always have

Equivalently, it implies that if one redistributes the mass of a degenerate distribution in a way which preserves its mean – also known as a mean-preserving distribution – the convex order will be non-decreasing. Thus, convex order can be viewed as a measure of dispersion, and this is naturally obtained by the martingale construction of computing conditional expectations w.r.t. a given filtration of an integrable random variable.

Coming back to our above theorem, the requirement

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{*}|X^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=X^{*},

essentially requires that

be a mean-preserving distribution of the point mass at

to be satisfied, it is necessary for

4
Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) models

Given exogenous series

(\beta_{t}^{[0]})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq[0,\infty),\quad(\beta_{t}^{[1]})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq(0,\infty)\quad\text{and}\quad(\beta_{t}^{[2]})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq(0,\infty),

(4.1)

we define the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model
(
16
;
19
)
for
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
by the following specification:

i.

Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\;\sim\;\operatorname{Poi}\bigl(M_{t+1\mid t}\bigr)
,
where
M_{t+1\mid t}=M_{t+1\mid t}(Z_{\leq t})>0
is a function of
Z_{\leq t}
, and

ii.

M_{t+1\mid t}\;=\;\beta_{t}^{[0]}\,+\,\beta_{t}^{[1]}Z_{t}\,+\,\beta_{t}^{[2]}M_{t\mid t-1}
.

Using the Binomial-Poisson mixture representation, Example
1
below presents a M-LSSM whose induced observation process coincides with the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model, as will be established in Proposition
1
, below.

Example 1
.

Let

(\Delta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq[0,\infty),\quad(c_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq[0,\infty)\quad\hbox{and}\quad(p_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq(0,1)

be exogenous sequences, excluding the case
(\Delta_{t},c_{t})=(0,0)
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
(to guarantee
\mu_{t+1|t}>0
).
We define the M-LSSM with the following specifications:

i.

Observation equation
:

Z_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t-1},\Theta_{\leq t}\;\overset{(d)}{=}\;Z_{t}\mid\Theta_{t}\;\sim\;\operatorname{Binom}(\Theta_{t},p_{t}).

ii.

Marginal evolution equation
:

\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\;\sim\;\operatorname{Poi}\left(\mu_{t+1\mid t}\right),

where
\mu_{t+1\mid t}=\mu_{t+1\mid t}(Z_{\leq t})
is a predetermined positive function of
Z_{\leq t}
.

iii.

Linear evolution
: (
3.2
) holds.

We compute the dynamics under these assumptions.
Since, conditionally on
Z_{\leq t-1}
,
Z_{t}
is a binomial-thinning of
\Theta_{t}
with probability
p_{t}
, we have the following properties:

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle\perp(\Theta_{t}-Z_{t})|Z_{\leq t-1},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}-Z_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t-1}

\displaystyle\sim\operatorname{Poi}\left((1-p_{t})\mu_{t\mid t-1}\right),

\displaystyle Z_{t}|Z_{\leq t-1}

\displaystyle\sim\operatorname{Poi}\left(p_{t}\mu_{t\mid t-1}\right).

Hence, we have the following filtering distribution

\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\rm(d)}}}Z_{t}+K_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t},\quad\hbox{where}\quad K_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\sim\operatorname{Poi}\left((1-p_{t})\,\mu_{t\mid t-1}\right).

(4.3)

This concludes the example.

This example motivates the following result.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model

Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\sim\operatorname{Poi}\left(M_{t+1\mid t}\right),\qquad M_{t+1\mid t}=\beta_{t}^{[0]}+\beta_{t}^{[1]}Z_{t}+\beta_{t}^{[2]}M_{t\mid t-1},

with exogenous sequences given in (
4.1
).
There exists a unique M-LSSM which induces this Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model.

Proof.

We start with the proof of existence.
Let
(Z_{t},\Theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}}
be the M-LSSM of Example
1
. Using property (
4.2
) implies the recursion

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]

\displaystyle=p_{t+1}\mu_{t+1\mid t}=p_{t+1}\left(\Delta_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]+c_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{t}p_{t+1}+\Delta_{t}p_{t+1}Z_{t}+\Delta_{t}\frac{1-p_{t}}{p_{t}}\,p_{t+1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t-1}\right],

the second equality uses the linear evolution equation (
3.2
), and the third one uses (
4.3
) together with

{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t-1}\right]=p_{t}\mu_{t\mid t-1}
.
Comparing with the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) recursion, we identify

\beta_{t}^{[0]}=p_{t+1}c_{t},\qquad\beta_{t}^{[1]}=p_{t+1}\Delta_{t},\qquad\beta_{t}^{[2]}=p_{t+1}\Delta_{t}\left({1}/{p_{t}}-1\right),\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{Z},

(4.4)

thus, the M-LSSM of Example
1
induces the stated model. This proves existence.

Next, we prove uniqueness.
From
\beta_{t}^{[2]}/\beta_{t}^{[1]}=1/p_{t}-1
, we obtain

p_{t}=\frac{\beta_{t}^{[1]}}{\beta_{t}^{[1]}+\beta_{t}^{[2]}}\in(0,1),\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{Z}.

Thus, the sequence
(p_{t})_{t}
is uniquely determined by the INGARCH coefficients.
Since the same relationship holds for
p_{t+1}
, in conjunction with (
4.4
) we also get that the following terms

\Delta_{t}=\frac{\beta_{t}^{[1]}}{p_{t+1}}>0,\qquad c_{t}=\frac{\beta_{t}^{[0]}}{p_{t+1}}\geq 0,\qquad\mu_{t+1\mid t}=\frac{M_{t+1\mid t}}{p_{t+1}}>0

are uniquely determined for each
t
, hence the induced M-LSSM is unique.
∎

The above result addresses the equivalence between the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model and the M-LSSM in Example
1
. The following result, in combination with Theorem
4
, shows that the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model can be naturally extended to an O-LSSM.

Theorem 5
.

Consider the setting of Example
1
. For fixed
\Delta_{t}>0
and
c_{t}\geq 0
, let

m_{t}(x)=\Delta_{t}x+c_{t}
. For
every

\mu_{t\mid t-1}>0
, we have the
conditional convex-order relation

m_{t}(\Theta_{t})\mid Z_{\leq t}\;\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\;\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t},

(4.5)

if and only if

0<\Delta_{t}\leq 1.

(4.6)

In case
\Delta_{t}=0
and
c_{t}>0

4.5

Proof.

Consider the setting of Example
1
.
By (

\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\;\overset{(d)}{=}\;Z_{t}+K_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad K_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\sim\operatorname{Poi}\left((1-p_{t})\,\mu_{t\mid t-1}\right).

As a result, the linear evolution equation (
3.2

\mu_{t+1\mid t}\;=\;\Delta_{t}\bigl(Z_{t}+(1-p_{t})\,\mu_{t\mid t-1}\bigr)+c_{t}.

(4.7)

Define

X=\Delta_{t}(Z_{t}+K_{t})+c_{t}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad Y=\Theta_{t+1}.

Note that

m_{t}(\Theta_{t})\mid Z_{\leq t}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{{\rm(d)}}}\;X\mid Z_{\leq t}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad Y\mid Z_{\leq t}\sim\operatorname{Poi}(\mu_{t+1\mid t}).

We first prove the sufficient part. Let
\Delta_{t}>1
. Set

D(s;Z_{\leq t})=\log{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{sY}|Z_{\leq t}\right]-\log{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{sX}|Z_{\leq t}\right].

Clearly, if we have
X\preceq_{cx}Y|Z_{\leq t}
, then given the convexity of the functions
\phi_{s}(x)=e^{sx}
we must have
D(s;Z_{\leq t})\geq 0
for all

, a.s. in
Z_{\leq t}
. Note that the relevant expectations are given by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{sX}|Z_{\leq t}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl\{s(\Delta_{t}Z_{t}+c_{t})\bigr\}\,\exp\bigl\{(1-p_{t})\mu_{t\mid t-1}\bigl(e^{\Delta_{t}s}-1\bigr)\bigr\},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{sY}|Z_{\leq t}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl\{\mu_{t+1\mid t}\bigl(e^{s}-1\bigr)\bigr\}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle D(s;Z_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=\mu_{t+1|t}(e^{s}-1)-(1-p_{t})\mu_{t|t-1}(e^{\Delta_{t}s}-1)-s(\Delta_{t}Z_{t}+c_{t})

\displaystyle=e^{s}(\mu_{t+1|t}-(1-p_{t})\mu_{t|t-1}e^{(\Delta_{t}-1)s})+(1-p_{t})\mu_{t|t-1}-\mu_{t+1|t}-s(\Delta_{t}Z_{t}+c_{t}).

Since
\Delta_{t}-1>0
,

as

. Hence, we must have

for a large enough

. In particular, we may find such an

for any realization of
Z_{\leq t}
, proving sufficiency.

Now, we prove the necessary part. First, define
K_{t}^{\prime}
and
Z_{t}^{\prime}
as follows:

\displaystyle K_{t}^{\prime}|K_{t},Z_{\leq t}\sim{\rm Binom}(K_{t},\Delta_{t})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad Z_{t}^{\prime}|K_{t},Z_{\leq t}\sim{\rm Poi}(\Delta_{t}Z_{t}+c_{t}).

By the Poisson thinning, we have that
K_{t}^{\prime}|Z_{\leq t}\sim{\rm Poi}(\Delta_{t}(1-p_{t})\mu_{t|t-1})
; since

depends only on

and
Z_{t}^{\prime}\perp K_{t}|Z_{\leq t}
, we also have
Z_{t}^{\prime}\perp K_{t}^{\prime}|Z_{\leq t}
, such that
Z_{t}^{\prime}+K_{t}^{\prime}|Z_{\leq t}\sim{\rm Poi}(\mu_{t+1|t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y|Z_{\leq t}
.

Further,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{t}^{\prime}+K_{t}^{\prime}|X,Z_{\leq t}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{t}^{\prime}+K_{t}^{\prime}|K_{t},Z_{\leq t}\right]=X
. Thus, by Theorem

.
∎

In particular, the latter part of the proof of Theorem
5
gives an explicit construction for the desired O-LSSM. This representation of the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model closely resembles the dynamic
N
-mixture model
(
35
;
13
)
used in ecology and the INAR hidden Markov chain model
(
17
)
used in public health. Although differing slightly in formulation, these approaches are built on the same double-thinning mechanism: the latent state evolves through the binomial survival mechanism combined with a Poisson recruitment, and the observed counts arise from an additional binomial thinning. These models are popular because they provide a natural way to capture unobserved populations or hidden processes, and they are intuitive in explanation. The crucial distinction to our derivation is that our formulation yields an explicit INGARCH representation in which every conditional distribution is available in closed form, so the model estimation can be performed directly from the conditional likelihood without any need of integration. By contrast, dynamic
N
-mixtures and INAR hidden Markov chain models require numerical methods to integrate over the latent states or the hidden regimes, which makes their fitting procedure substantially more computationally intensive.

Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\sim\operatorname{NB}\bigl(\kappa_{t+1\mid t},\,\pi_{t+1\mid t}\bigr),

where the parameters of the NB distribution are given by

\kappa_{t+1\mid t}=a_{t+1\mid t}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\pi_{t+1\mid t}=\frac{1}{1+b_{t+1\mid t}}.

In particular, this provides us with the conditional expectation

{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]\;=\;\frac{a_{t+1\mid t}}{b_{t+1\mid t}}\;=\;\frac{\kappa_{t+1\mid t}\,\pi_{t+1\mid t}}{1-\pi_{t+1\mid t}}.

The resulting recursion is of the form

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]

\displaystyle=~{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]~=~\Delta_{t}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]+c_{t}

\displaystyle=~\Delta_{t}\,\frac{a_{t\mid t-1}+Z_{t}}{\,b_{t\mid t-1}+1\,}+c_{t}~=~c_{t}+\frac{\Delta_{t}}{b_{t\mid t-1}+1}\,Z_{t}+\Delta_{t}\,\frac{b_{t\mid t-1}}{b_{t\mid t-1}+1}\,M_{t\mid t-1},

where the second equality uses the linear evolution equation (
3.2
), the third uses the Gamma-Poisson conjugate update.
Comparing with the NB-INGARCH(1,1) recursion, we identify

\beta_{t}^{[0]}=c_{t}\geq 0,\qquad\beta_{t}^{[1]}=\frac{\Delta_{t}}{b_{t\mid t-1}+1}>0,\qquad\beta_{t}^{[2]}=\Delta_{t}\,\frac{b_{t\mid t-1}}{b_{t\mid t-1}+1}>0,\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{Z}.

(5.1)

Thus, the M-LSSM in Example
2
induces the stated model. This proves existence.

Now, we prove uniqueness.
From (
5.1
) we obtain

b_{t\mid t-1}={\beta_{t}^{[2]}}/{\beta_{t}^{[1]}}>0,

and

\Delta_{t}=\beta_{t}^{[1]}(b_{t\mid t-1}+1)=\beta_{t}^{[1]}+\beta_{t}^{[2]}>0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad c_{t}=\beta_{t}^{[0]}\geq 0.

Henceforth,
(b_{t\mid t-1},\Delta_{t},c_{t})
are uniquely determined by the INGARCH coefficients.
Finally, we have
a_{t+1\mid t}=\kappa_{t+1\mid t}>0
. Therefore, the induced M-LSSM is unique.
∎

The above result address the equivalence between the NB-INGARCH(1,1) model and the M-LSSM of Example
2
. The following result in combination with Theorem
4
shows that NB-INGARCH(1,1) models can be naturally extended to O-LSSMs.

5.2
Existence of an O-LSSM representation

In this subsection, we establish an equivalence condition for the existence of an O-LSSM corresponding to the M-LSSM described in Example
2
.
Equivalently, by the representation result for the NB-INGARCH(1,1) model given in Proposition
2
,
we provide an equivalence condition under which an NB-INGARCH(1,1) model admits an O-LSSM formulation.

Theorem 6
.

Consider the setting of Example
2
. For fixed
\Delta_{t}\geq 0
and
c_{t}\geq 0
, let

m_{t}(x)=\Delta_{t}x+c_{t}
. For
every

a_{t\mid t-1},b_{t\mid t-1}>0
, we have the
conditional convex-order relation

m_{t}(\Theta_{t})\bigm|Z_{\leq t}\;\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\;\Theta_{t+1}\bigm|Z_{\leq t},

(5.2)

if and only if

0\leq\Delta_{t}\frac{b_{t+1\mid t}}{b_{t}}\leq 1.

(5.3)

Proof.

The proof for
\Delta_{t}=0
is trivial. Therefore, we focus on the case
\Delta_{t}>0
.
First, we have

\mathbb{E}[m_{t}(\Theta_{t})\mid Z_{\leq t}]=\Delta_{t}\,\frac{a_{t}}{b_{t}}+c_{t},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\Theta_{t+1}\mid Z_{\leq t}]=\frac{a_{t+1\mid t}}{b_{t+1\mid t}},

and

\frac{a_{t+1\mid t}}{b_{t+1\mid t}}=\Delta_{t}\,\frac{a_{t}}{b_{t}}+c_{t}.

(5.4)

Set

X=m_{t}(\Theta_{t})=\Delta_{t}\Theta_{t}+c_{t},\qquad Y=\Theta_{t+1},\qquad q_{t}=\frac{b_{t+1\mid t}}{b_{t}}>0.

We first prove the sufficient part. With the mean restriction (
5.4
), the conditional moment generating functions are

{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{sX}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]=e^{c_{t}s}\Bigl(1-\frac{\Delta_{t}s}{b_{t}}\Bigr)^{-a_{t}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{sY}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]=\Bigl(1-\frac{s}{q_{t}b_{t}}\Bigr)^{-q_{t}(\Delta_{t}a_{t}+c_{t}b_{t})}.

Let
D(s;Z_{\leq t})=\log{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{sY}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]-\log{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{sX}\mid Z_{\leq t}\right]
. Then,

\displaystyle D(s;Z_{\leq t})=a_{t}\log\left(1-\frac{\Delta_{t}s}{b_{t}}\right)-c_{t}s-q_{t}(\Delta_{t}a_{t}+c_{t}b_{t})\log\left(1-\frac{s}{q_{t}b_{t}}\right).

Suppose, contrary to the assumption, that
q_{t}>1/\Delta_{t}
. Then, as
s\to(b_{t}/\Delta_{t})^{+}
,

\displaystyle D(s;Z_{\leq t})

\displaystyle\to a_{t}\log 0^{-}-c_{t}\frac{b_{t}}{\Delta_{t}}-q_{t}(\Delta_{t}a_{t}+c_{t}b_{t})\log\left(1-\frac{1}{\Delta_{t}q_{t}}\right)=-\infty.

Hence, for
s_{\epsilon}=b_{t}/\Delta_{t}-\epsilon
, with
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, we must have
D(s_{\epsilon};Z_{\leq t})<0
. Since we can find such an
\epsilon>0
for any realization of
Z_{\leq t}
, the sufficiency is proved.

Now, we prove the necessary part. First, note that

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}\Theta_{t}|Z_{\leq t}\sim{\rm Gamma}(a_{t},b_{t}/\Delta_{t}).

Let
X^{\prime}=\Delta_{t}\Theta_{t}+V_{t}
, where
V_{t}|\Theta_{\leq t},Z_{\leq t}\sim{\rm Gamma}(c_{t}b_{t}/\Delta_{t},b_{t}/\Delta_{t})
. By construction, we have
\Delta_{t}\Theta_{t}\perp V_{t}|Z_{\leq t}
, hence
X^{\prime}\sim{\rm Gamma}(a_{t}+c_{t}b_{t}/\Delta_{t},b_{t}/\Delta_{t})
. Next, note that by the mean restriction (
5.4
), and by the assumption
\Delta_{t}b_{t+1|t}/b_{t}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle a_{t+1|t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{t}b_{t+1|t}}{b_{t}}(a_{t}+c_{t}b_{t}/\Delta_{t})\leq a_{t}+c_{t}b_{t}/\Delta_{t}.

Hence, let
Q_{t}|\Theta_{t},V_{t},Z_{\leq t}\sim{\rm Beta}(a_{t+1|t},a_{t}+c_{t}b_{t}/\Delta_{t}-a_{t+1|t})
, and set

\displaystyle Y^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{(b_{t}/\Delta_{t})Q_{t}X^{\prime}}{b_{t+1|t}}.

By a Beta-thinning,
Q_{t}X^{\prime}|Z_{\leq t}\sim{\rm Gamma}(a_{t+1|t},b_{t}/\Delta_{t})
, such that
Y^{*}|Z_{\leq t}\sim{\rm Gamma}(a_{t+1|t},b_{t+1|t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}Y|Z_{\leq t}
. We have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{*}|X,Z_{\leq t}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{*}|\Theta_{t},Z_{\leq t}\right]=\frac{(b_{t}/\Delta_{t}){\mathbb{E}}\left[Q_{t}|Z_{\leq t}\right]}{b_{t+1|t}}\,(\Delta_{t}\Theta_{t}+{\mathbb{E}}\left[V_{t}|Z_{\leq t}\right])=\Delta_{t}\Theta_{t}+c_{t}=X,

hence by Theorem
3
, we have
X|Z_{\leq t}\preceq_{cx}Y^{*}|Z_{\leq t}
, which also implies
X|Z_{\leq t}^{\prime}\preceq_{cx}Y|Z_{\leq t}
. This completes the proof of the necessary part.
∎

Again, the proof of the necessary part of Theorem
6
provides an explicit construction of the desired O-LSSM.

In Example
2
, two parametrizations of
(a_{t+1\mid t},b_{t+1\mid t})
recover well-known NB-INGARCH(1,1) specifications from the literature. The next result shows that, under either parametrization, the convex-order condition holds, and therefore an O-LSSM representation exists.

Corollary 2
.

For given exogenous sequences

(\Delta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\in[0,1]\quad\hbox{and}\quad(c_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\in[0,\infty),

assume the setting of Example
2
holds. Then, we have the following results:

i. (parametrization of
38
)

Suppose that

b_{t+1|t}=b,\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z},

(5.5)

for some constant
b>0
. Then, for every
a_{t|t-1}>0
, we have

\Delta_{t}\Theta_{t}+c_{t}\,\mid\,Z_{\leq t}\ \preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\ \Theta_{t+1}\,\mid\,Z_{\leq t}.

ii. (parametrization of
39
)

Suppose that

a_{t+1|t}=a,\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z},

(5.6)

for some constant
a>0
.
Then, for every
b_{t\mid t-1}>0
, we have

\Delta_{t}\Theta_{t}+c_{t}\,\mid\,Z_{\leq t}\ \preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\ \Theta_{t+1}\,\mid\,Z_{\leq t}.

.

Proof.

When
\Delta_{t}=0
, the proofs are straightforward in either case, and we only consider the case
\Delta_{t}\in(0,1]
in this proof.

We first prove part i.
Assume (
5.5
):
b_{t+1\mid t}\equiv b>0
. Then,

\frac{b_{t+1\mid t}}{b_{t}}\;=\;\frac{b_{t+1\mid t}}{b_{t|t-1}+1}\;=\;\frac{b}{b+1}\;<\;1\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\Delta_{t}},

so, by Theorem
6
,
the convex-order condition holds for every
a_{t\mid t-1}>0
.

Now, we prove part ii.
Assume (
5.6
):
a_{t+1\mid t}\equiv a>0
. Then,

a_{t}=a+Z_{t}.

By the mean identity (
5.4
), we have

\frac{a}{b_{t+1\mid t}}\;=\;\Delta_{t}\left(\frac{a+Z_{t}}{b_{t}}\right)+c_{t}\geq\Delta_{t}\frac{a}{b_{t}}.

Multiplying by
\dfrac{b_{t+1|t}}{a\Delta_{t}}>0
gives us
1/\Delta_{t}\geq b_{t+1|t}/b_{t}
for every
b_{t\mid t-1}>0
, and Theorem
6
completes the proof.
∎

6
Practical considerations of INGARCH models in insurance applications

6.1
Missing data

Within the framework of O-SSMs and M-SSMs, there are two popular mechanisms of missing data. There is
unawareness missing
, which is of a similar nature as described in
36
, and there is
no-exposure missing
, as described in
24
.

Unawareness missing means that the response
Z_{t}
of a certain time point
t
has not been recorded. Therefore, it is missing in the past observations
Z_{\leq s}
,
s\geq t
. A typical example in insurance is a car insurance cover of a giving insurance policyholder. Assume this policyholder purchases insurance cover with an insurance company A in calendar year
t-1
, with an insurance company B in calendar year
t
, and again with insurance company A in calendar year
t+1
. Then, insurance company A will not observe the claim
Z_{t}
for calendar year
t
, and it will be missing from their records under the assumption that no information is transferred between companies across jurisdictions.

No-exposure missing means that the experiment has not been carried out in period
t
, therefore
Z_{t}
is not observable/available. Coming back to our car insurance example, consider an insurance policyholder who is insured with the same insurance company A for the calendar years
t-1,t,t+1
. For calendar year
t
, this insurance policyholder may decide to suspend driving, i.e., there is no insurance exposure in that calendar year and, obviously, the insurance claim cannot occur, i.e., it will be
Z_{t}=0
, a.s.

For the modeling of unawareness missing, we equip the responses
Z_{t}
with binary indicator variables
U_{t}
that indicate whether the responses have been recorded or not.
Thus,
Z_{t}
gives the recorded response if
U_{t}=1
, and otherwise,
U_{t}=0
, if the response at time
t
has not been recorded (is not available). For simplicity, we focus for this unawareness missing only on period
t
, and we assume that
Z_{t}
has not been recorded, i.e.,
U_{t}=0
. In the case of an O-SSM, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Theta_{t+1}\in\cdot\mid\Theta_{\leq t},Z_{\leq t-1},Z_{t}U_{t},U_{t}=0\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\Theta_{t+1}\in\cdot\mid\Theta_{\leq t},Z_{\leq t-1},U_{t}=0\right),

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\Theta_{t+1}\in\cdot\mid\Theta_{\leq t},Z_{\leq t-1}\right),

assuming that
\Theta_{t+1}
and
U_{t}
are conditionally independent, given
\{\Theta_{\leq t},Z_{\leq t-1}\}
.
That is,
U_{t}
acts as a mask cancelling out the past observation
Z_{t}
. Naturally, the same applies for a M-SSM

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Theta_{t+1}\in\cdot\mid Z_{\leq t-1},Z_{t}U_{t},U_{t}=0\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\Theta_{t+1}\in\cdot\mid Z_{\leq t-1}\right),

assuming that
\Theta_{t+1}
and
U_{t}
are conditionally independent, given
Z_{\leq t-1}
. These are reasonable assumptions in the above insurance example.
Thus, the case of unawareness missing can be handled by integrating out the missing variable
Z_{t}
, giving a mathematically proper treatment of this data issue.

The no-exposure missing is different in nature.
Under the additive form of the exponential dispersion family (EDF)
(
27
)
, we equip the distributions of the responses
(Z_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
with an external sequence
(w_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\subset[0,\infty)
, where
w_{t}\geq 0
describes an exposure measure for period
t
(also called case weights). The major change then concerns the observation distributions in all of our models, see
(
2.3
). Namely, the density of the observations
Z_{t}
, given the state
\Theta_{t}
, is assumed to explicitly depend on the exposure
w_{t}\geq 0

f_{w_{t}}(Z_{t}\mid\Theta_{t}):=f(Z_{t}\mid\Theta_{t},w_{t}),

(6.1)

which is assumed to be a non-degenerate distribution for
w_{t}>0
, and degenerate at
Z_{t}=0
for
w_{t}=0
.
Hence, no-exposure missing (
w_{t}=0)
now means

{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z_{t}=0\mid\Theta_{t},w_{t}=0\right)=1.

All the remaining considerations from above are ceteris paribus under this inclusion of the weights.

In an insurance context, we would set
\ast=0
, saying that there is no claim
Z_{t}=0
under a zero exposure
w_{t}=0
. In this case of a zero exposure
w_{t}=0
, one has for the O-SSM

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Theta_{t+1}\in\cdot\mid\Theta_{\leq t},Z_{\leq t-1},Z_{t}=\ast\right),

and for the M-SSM

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Theta_{t+1}\in\cdot\mid Z_{\leq t-1},Z_{t}=\ast\right),

which differs from the unawareness missingness in the sense that we do not need to integrate out the missing observation
Z_{t}
, but rather this observation
Z_{t}=\ast
does not carry any information that impacts
\Theta_{t+1}
. That is, these two types of missingness lead to different state-space evolutions. As we shall illustrate for the Poisson and NB-INGARCH models, below, a zero-exposure missing observation essentially translates to using
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{t}|Z_{\leq t}\right]=\mu_{t|t-1}
in the evolution step, i.e., the filtering step is skipped altogether, whereas in the unawareness case the unobserved observation persists as a mixing parameter in the means of future time steps, which usually does not result in a closed form log-likelihood.
Notably, the form of missingness only matters for
future
states, and does not impact the filtering distribution for the current state: in either case we have
f(Z_{t}|\Theta_{t})=1
, such that
\pi(\Theta_{t}|Z_{\leq t})\propto 1\cdot\pi(\Theta_{t}|Z_{\leq t-1})
.

Only in the P-SSM, i.e., in the state-space model where the state-space dynamics is (purely) parameter-driven, response data missingness does not impact the state-space dynamics. By equality of the complete-data likelihood, we get in this case

\displaystyle\pi(\Theta_{t+1}|Z_{\leq t-1},Z_{t}=\ast)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\Theta_{t+1},Z_{t}=\ast,Z_{\leq t-1})}{P(Z_{t}=\ast|Z_{\leq t-1})f(Z_{\leq t-1})}=\frac{f(\Theta_{t+1},Z_{\leq t-1})}{f(Z_{\leq t-1})}=\pi(\Theta_{t+1}|Z_{\leq t-1}).

6.2
Practical considerations for the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model and weak stationarity

We now discuss several practical aspects of our proposed model by revisiting Example
1
.
In insurance applications, a time-series is typically anchored at time zero, and the different time periods
t
may exhibit distinct prior rates through the parameter
p_{t}\in(0,1)
.
Furthermore, we introduce exposure indicators
w_{t}
, which is either 1 to represent full exposure, or 0 to represent periods with no exposure.
Finally, we extend the model to a multi-policyholder setting for
i=1,\ldots,n
(panel data).
The following example presents this extended version of the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model introduced in Example
1
. As we will show, anchoring leads to the first latent state having a strictly smaller variance than all subsequent latent states in this model. Discarding even one observation is undesirable in an insurance context, where time-series tend to be short, with length of 10 years or less (typically on a calendar year scale). Hence, to ensure stationarity for
(\Theta_{t})_{t\geq 1}
, it is necessary to augment the model with the unobserved time point
t=0
.

Example 3
.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let

\mu>0,\quad(w_{i,t})_{t\geq 0}\subseteq\{0,1\},\quad(\Delta_{i,t})_{t\geq 0}\subseteq[0,1]\quad\hbox{and}\quad(p_{i,t})_{t\geq 0}\subseteq(0,1),

be exogenous sequences.
We define the M-LSSM with the following specifications:

i. Anchoring at
t=0
:

Assume

\Theta_{i,0}\;\sim\;\operatorname{Poi}\!\left(\mu_{i,0\mid-1}\right)

with
\mu_{i,0\mid-1}=\mu>0
.

ii. Observation equation:

Z_{i,t}\mid Z_{i,0:t-1},\Theta_{i,0:t}\;\overset{d}{=}\;Z_{i,t}\mid\Theta_{i,t}\;\sim\;\operatorname{Binom}(\Theta_{i,t},p^{*}_{i,t}=w_{i,t}p_{i,t}).

iii. Evolution equation:

\Theta_{i,t+1}\mid Z_{i,0:t}\;\sim\;\operatorname{Poi}\!\left(\mu_{i,t+1\mid t}\right),

where
\mu_{i,t+1|t}=\Delta_{i,t}\mu_{i,t}+c_{i,t}

with
\mu_{i,t}:={\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{i,t}\mid Z_{i,0:t}\right]
and
c_{i,t}=(1-\Delta_{i,t})\mu.

iv.

The family of processes

\bigl\{(Z_{i,t},\Theta_{i,t})_{t\geq 0}:i=1,\dots,n\bigr\}

is assumed to be mutually independent across
1\leq i\leq n
.

The above model gives the latent predictive-mean recursion

\mu_{i,t+1\mid t}~=~\Delta_{i,t}\left(Z_{i,t}+\left(1-w_{i,t}p_{i,t}\right)\mu_{i,t\mid t-1}\right)+(1-\Delta_{i,t})\mu.

(6.2)

Interpreting this in terms of the recursive mean structure, we observe that we have two (weighted) averages, corresponding to the filtering step and the evolution step, respectively. The filtering step updates
\mu_{i,t|t-1}
by weighting it with the latest observation
Z_{i,t}
, where the weight of the latter corresponds to the thinning ratio
w_{i,t}p_{i,t}
— this is to ensure stationarity. This updated mean then evolves to become a weighted average of itself (with weight
\Delta_{i,t}
) and the prior mean
\mu
.
Thus, one can interpret the filtering and evolution steps as acting in opposite directions, with the former updating the mean to be more data-centric and the latter bringing it back in line with the prior assumption. Hence, the induced means
\mu_{i,t+1|t}
will only meaningfully differ from the prior mean
\mu_{i,0|-1}=\mu
for
\Delta_{i,t}
sufficiently close to 1 and
p_{i,t}
sufficiently large.

In view of the recursive mean structure (
6.2
), it is now also clear how to handle the no-exposure missingness
w_{i,t}=0
, i.e., by simply setting
Z_{i,t}=w_{i,t}=0
. In this case, the first weighted average reduces to bringing forward
\mu_{i,t|t-1}
, i.e., a zero-exposure translates to skipping the filtering step for this time point, and proceeding directly to the evolution step.

On the other hand, for the unawareness missingness case, for
s\geq t+1
, we see the unobserved
Z_{i,t}
appearing as a mixing parameter in the distributions of
\Theta_{i,s}
and hence
Z_{i,s}
, whence the resulting distributions are a Poisson mixture of Poissons, thus, do not lead to a closed form. However, as long as we are willing to accept that the uncertainty in the value of
Z_{i,t}
is not being modeled, we may impute
Z_{i,t}={\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{i,t}|Z_{i,1:t-1}\right]=w_{i,t}p_{i,t}\mu_{i,t|t-1}
, which in particular aligns it with the no-exposure case. Given that the choice of the distributions in the state-space model is explicitly to admit closed forms, based on the above argument our recommendation is to simply treat unawareness missing as no-exposure missing, with explicit acknowledgement of the limitations of such an assumption. We recommend this choice for the NB-INGARCH model discussed later on as well.

Example
3
is identical to Example
1
, except for the anchoring and instead using
p^{*}_{i,t}=w_{i,t}p_{i,t}
in place of
p_{t}
. Hence, where
w_{i,t}=1
, the INGARCH representation of Proposition
1
applies, while for
w_{i,t}=0
, one simply uses
M_{i,t+1|t}={\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{i,t+1}|Z_{i,0:t}\right]=w_{i,t+1}p_{i,t+1}(\Delta_{i,t}\mu_{i,t|t-1}+(1-\Delta_{i,t})\mu)
.

Given that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{i,t+1}|Z_{i,0:t}\right]=w_{i,t+1}p_{i,t+1}\mu_{i,t+1|t}
, one avenue to incorporate (possibly time-varying) covariates is via a logit link on the parameters
p_{i,t+1}
. However, one should note the following properties of such an approach:

1.

Unlike under a log link – the standard in frequency modeling – coefficients under a logit link do not have a multiplicative effect, making them less straightforward to interpret.

2.

p_{i,t}
not only controls the expected value, but also the weight placed on
Z_{i,t}
in the filtering step.

Finally, we investigate the property of weak stationarity of this model. Given the possible heterogeneity over time of
Z_{i,t}
, it is more feasible to instead enforce stationarity in
(\Theta_{i,t})_{t}
. However, since the conditional dependence of
(\Theta_{i,t},\Theta_{i,t+1})\mid Z_{i,0:t}
is left unspecified under the M-LSSM specification, discussing second-order stationarity requires us to first lift it to an O
-
LSSM. By Theorem
5
(or equivalently by Theorem
4
), such a lift exists whenever
0\leq\Delta_{i,t}\leq 1
for all
t
. Furthermore, while the O-LSSM specification is not unique, Remark
3
guarantees the uniqueness of the covariance structure, hence for the purposes of deriving second moments we can consider
(Z_{i,t},\Theta_{i,t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
as following the O-LSSM.
The following results provide the unconditional moments of the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model in Example
3
.

Proposition 3
.

Under the setting of Example
3
, we have for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
t\geq 1
:

i.

The mean of the latent state is given by
\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{i,t}\right]=\mu.

ii.

We have

{\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t}\right)={\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,1}\right)
for all
t\geq 1

if and only if
\Delta_{i,t}^{2}=\frac{\Delta_{i,0}^{2}w_{i,0}p_{i,0}}{\Delta_{i,0}^{2}w_{i,0}p_{i,0}+w_{i,t}p_{i,t}}\in[0,1]
. Hence, if
w_{i,t}p_{i,t}=w_{i,1}p_{i,1}
for all
t\geq 1
, then
(\Theta_{i,t})_{t\geq 1}
is weakly stationary.

iii.

The mean of the observation is given by
\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{i,t}]=w_{i,t}p_{i,t}\,\mu.

iv.

The variance of the observation is given by

{\rm Var}\left(Z_{i,t}\right)\;=\;w_{i,t}p_{i,t}\,\mu\;+\;(w_{i,t}p_{i,t})^{2}\,\big({\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t}\right)-\mu\big).

v.

For
k\geq 1
, the auto–covariance of the observations is

{\rm Cov}\left(Z_{i,t},Z_{i,t+k}\right)\;=\;w_{i,t}p_{i,t}\,w_{i,t+k}p_{i,t+k}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{i,t+j}\right){\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t}\right).

Proof.

The proof of part i follows from the tower property. For part ii, the first part follows from the recursion for the variance of the latent state

{\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t+1}\right)\;=\;\Delta_{i,t}^{2}{\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t}\right)\;+\;\mu\Big(1-\Delta_{i,t}^{2}\big(1-w_{i,t}p_{i,t}\big)\Big),\qquad\text{ for all $t\geq 0$.}

(6.3)

The second part follows by requiring
\Delta_{i,t}=\Delta_{i,1}
, and from equation (
2.6
). The proofs of parts iii and iv follow from the tower property. Finally, part v follows from Lemma
1
with
\lambda_{i,u}=w_{i,u}p_{i,u}
.
∎

Finally, we discuss the necessity of anchoring the series at
t=0
instead of
t=1
. Note that the variance stationarity in part ii of Proposition
3
is defined only for
t\geq 1
. By (
6.3
), if
{\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t}\right)={\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,0}\right)=\mu
, then
{\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t+1}\right)=\mu+\mu\Delta_{i,t}^{2}w_{i,t}p_{i,t}\geq\mu
, with equality only if
\Delta_{i,t}^{2}w_{i,t}p_{i,t}=0
. Hence,
{\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t}\right)\equiv{\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,0}\right)
holds only in the degenerate case where,

\displaystyle\text{for all $t\geq 0$: }\>\text{either }\Delta_{i,t}=0\text{ or }w_{i,t}p_{i,t}=0.

Of course, modeling the full observed time-series as a stationary sequence therefore requires integrating out
Z_{i,0}
in the likelihood, which undermines the simplicity of the model in the first place.

6.3
Practical considerations for the NB-INGARCH(1,1) model and weak stationarity

The need for integration to ensure weak stationarity of the latent process, as well as the need for the logit link to incorporate covariates, makes the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model difficult in practical use. As we shall see in this subsection, this contrasts with the NB-INGARCH(1,1) model of Example
2
. We begin by extending the NB-INGARCH(1,1) model in Example
2
to panel data and to accommodate no-exposure missingness and time-varying covariates.

Example 4
.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let

(w_{i,t})_{t\geq 1}\subset\{0,1\},\quad(\lambda_{i,t})_{t\geq 1}\subseteq(0,\infty),\quad a_{1|0}>0\quad\hbox{and}\quad(\Delta_{i,t})_{t\geq 1}\subseteq[0,1],

be exogenous (sequences).
We define the M-LSSM with the following specifications:

i. Anchoring at
t=1
:

Assume

\Theta_{i,1}\;\sim\;\operatorname{Gamma}\bigl(a_{i,1\mid 0},\,b_{i,1\mid 0}\bigr),

with
a_{i,1|0}=b_{i,1|0}=a_{1|0}>0
.

ii. Observation equation:

Z_{i,t}\mid\Theta_{i,t}\;\sim\;\operatorname{Poi}(w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}\Theta_{i,t}).

iii. Evolution equation:

\Theta_{i,t+1}\mid Z_{i,1:t}\;\sim\;\operatorname{Gamma}\bigl(a_{i,t+1\mid t},\,b_{i,t+1\mid t}\bigr),

where

b_{i,t+1\mid t}=q_{i,t}\left(b_{i,t|t-1}+w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}\right)\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,a_{i,t+1\mid t}=\Delta_{i,t}q_{i,t}\left(a_{i,t|t-1}+Z_{i,t}\right)+(1-\Delta_{i,t})b_{i,t+1\mid t}\mu_{i,1|0},

and where

q_{i,t}\;=\;\dfrac{1}{\,\Delta_{i,t}^{2}+(1-\Delta_{i,t}^{2})\,\dfrac{b_{i,t|t-1}+w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}}{a_{i,1|0}}\,}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{i,1|0}=1.

(6.4)

Hence, we have
\mu_{i,t+1|t}=\Delta_{i,t}\mu_{i,t}+c_{i,t}

with

\mu_{i,t}:={\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{i,t}\mid Z_{i,1:t}\right],\quad\mu_{i,t+1|t}:={\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{i,t+1|t}\mid Z_{i,1:t}\right]\quad\hbox{and}\quad c_{i,t}:=(1-\Delta_{i,t})\mu_{i,1|0}.

iv.

The family of processes

\bigl\{(Z_{i,t},\Theta_{i,t})_{t\geq 0}:i=1,\dots,n\bigr\}

is assumed to be mutually independent across
1\leq i\leq n
.

The choice of
q_{i,t}
is to ensure stationarity in the variance; see
2
. Under the setting of Example
4
, the Poisson-Gamma mixing implies the negative-binomial property

Z_{i,t+1}\mid Z_{i,1:t}\;\sim\;\operatorname{NB}\!\big(\kappa_{i,t+1\mid t},\,\pi_{i,t+1\mid t}\big),\qquad\kappa_{i,t+1\mid t}:=a_{i,t+1\mid t},\quad\pi_{i,t+1\mid t}:=\frac{w_{i,t+1}\lambda_{i,t+1}}{\,b_{i,t+1\mid t}+w_{i,t+1}\lambda_{i,t+1}\,}.

Hence, we have

M_{i,t+1\mid t}\;:=\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{i,t+1}\mid Z_{i,1:t}\right]\;=\;w_{i,t+1}\lambda_{i,t+1}\,\mu_{i,t+1\mid t},\qquad\mu_{i,t+1\mid t}\;:=\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{i,t+1}\mid Z_{i,1:t}\right].

With the linear evolution and the Gamma-Poisson filtering update,
we have the following recursion

\mu_{i,t+1\mid t}~=~\Delta_{i,t}\left(\,\frac{Z_{i,t}}{\,b_{i,t\mid t-1}+w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}\,}\;+\;\,\frac{b_{i,t\mid t-1}}{\,b_{i,t\mid t-1}+w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}\,}\,\mu_{i,t\mid t-1}\right)+(1-\Delta_{i,t})\mu_{i,t}.

If
w_{i,t}>0
, this yields the INGARCH(1,1) with

M_{i,t+1\mid t}=\beta_{i,t}^{[0]}+\beta_{i,t}^{[1]}\,Z_{i,t}+\beta_{i,t}^{[2]}\,M_{i,t\mid t-1},

where

\beta_{i,t}^{[0]}:=w_{i,t+1}\lambda_{i,t+1}c_{i,t},\quad\beta_{i,t}^{[1]}:=\frac{w_{i,t+1}\lambda_{i,t+1}\Delta_{i,t}}{\,b_{i,t\mid t-1}+w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}\,},\quad\beta_{i,t}^{[2]}:=\frac{w_{i,t+1}\lambda_{i,t+1}\Delta_{i,t}}{\,b_{i,t\mid t-1}+w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}\,}\,\frac{b_{i,t\mid t-1}}{\,w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}\,}.

For
w_{i,t}=0
, we simply have

M_{i,t+1\mid t}=w_{i,t+1}\lambda_{i,t+1}\,\mu_{i,t+1\mid t}=w_{i,t+1}\lambda_{i,t+1}\,\bigl(c_{i,t}+\Delta_{i,t}\,\mu_{i,t\mid t-1}\bigr).

Similarly to the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model, towards investigating weak stationarity, we first lift the M-LSSM of Example
4
to an O-LSSM, for which we apply Theorem
6
. This requires that
0\leq\Delta_{i,t}b_{i,t+1|t}/b_{i,t}\leq 1
, which can be shown to hold; see Lemma
4
in
A
. Similarly to the Poisson-INGARCH case, Remark
3
guarantees the unique covariance structure given by (
2.6
). It hence remains to obtain the unconditional moments, which are given in Proposition
4
.

Proposition 4
.

Under the setting of Example
4
, we have for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
t\geq 1
:

i.

The mean of the latent state is given by
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{i,t}\right]=1
.

ii.

The variance of the latent state is given by

{\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t}\right)\;=\;1/a_{1|0}.
Hence, weak stationarity of
\Theta_{i,t}
holds provided that
\Delta_{i,t}=\Delta_{i,1}
for all
t\geq 1
.

iii.

The mean of the observation is given by

{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{i,t}\right]=w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}
.

iv.

The variance of the observation is given by

{\rm Var}\left(Z_{i,t}\right)\;=\;w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}\;+\;\frac{(w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t})^{\,2}}{a_{1|0}}.

v.

For
k\geq 1
, the auto-covariance of the observation is given by

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\left(Z_{i,t},Z_{i,t+k}\right)

\displaystyle=\;w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}\,w_{i,t+k}\lambda_{i,t+k}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{i,t+j}\right){\rm Var}\left(\Theta_{i,t}\right).

Proof.

The proofs of items i, iii, iv and the first part of item ii are results taken from
2
. The second part of item ii follows from (
2.6
), and part v follows from Lemma
1
with
w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}
in place of
\lambda_{t}
.
∎

Thus, the advantages of the NB-INGARCH(1,1) over the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model are at least twofold. First, weak stationarity on latent states underlying the full observed series is possible by anchoring at
t=1
rather than
t=0
, hence removing the need for a tedious integration. Secondly, time-varying covariates may instead be incorporated via
\lambda_{i,t}
using the (standard) log-link, allowing for easier interpretation.

Unfortunately, as in the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) model, the weight on
Z_{t}
in the filtering update,
w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t}/(b_{i,t|t-1}+w_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t})
, does still depend on the size of
\lambda_{i,t}
. However, at the very least the weights decrease over time as it can be shown that
b_{i,t|t-1}
increases over time. This comports better with intuition in insurance pricing since the mean should be updated less as information accumulates; this contrasts with the Poisson-INGARCH(1,1) weights, which are exactly
w_{i,t}p_{i,t}
regardless of the length of accumulated history.

7
Conclusions

This paper bridges INGARCH models and O-SSMs by demonstrating how the former can be represented within the latter framework and by deriving explicit O-SSM formulations for the Poisson and the NB-INGARCH(1,1) cases. The two-step state-space representation of the O-SSM enhances interpretability, allows the inclusion of covariates, and provides a convenient treatment of missing observations within the INGARCH modeling paradigm.

Future research may extend this framework to the broader class of the exponential dispersion family, where conjugate marginal evolutions could again yield closed-form filtering and predictive distributions. Furthermore, a systematic investigation of weak stationarity within these extensions, especially under time-varying covariates and panel data settings, would further strengthen the practical relevance of the proposed framework in insurance applications.

Appendix A
Miscellaneous Results

Lemma 4
.

Under the setting of Example
4
, we have

\Delta_{i,t}\,\frac{b_{i,t+1\mid t}}{b_{i,t}}\ \leq\ 1,

(A.1)

for every
i=1,\ldots,n
and
t\geq 1
.

Proof.

Since
\Delta_{i,t}\leq 1
, it suffices to show that
b_{i,t+1|t}/b_{i,t}=q_{i,t}\leq 1
. In turn, it suffices to show that
b_{i,t}/a_{i,1|0}\geq 1
for all
t
, since if this holds,

\displaystyle q_{i,t}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}_{i,t}+(1-\Delta^{2}_{i,t})\frac{b_{i,t}}{a_{i,1|0}}\geq\Delta^{2}_{i,t}+(1-\Delta^{2}_{i,t})=1.

We prove this via induction as follows. First, this holds for
t=1
since
a_{i,1|0}=b_{i,1|0}\leq b_{i,1|0}+w_{i,1}\lambda_{i,1}=b_{i,1}
.
Next, given that
b_{i,t}/a_{1|0}\geq 1
for some
t
,

\displaystyle\frac{b_{i,t+1}}{a_{1|0}}

\displaystyle=q_{i,t}\frac{b_{i,t}}{a_{1|0}}+w_{i,t+1}\lambda_{i,t+1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{b_{i,t}/a_{1|0}}{\Delta^{2}_{i,t}+(1-\Delta^{2}_{i,t})(b_{i,t}/a_{1|0})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}_{i,t}(b_{i,t}/a_{1|0})^{-1}+(1-\Delta^{2}_{i,t})}\geq 1.

This completes the proof.
∎
