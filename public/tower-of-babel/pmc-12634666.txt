Immunological characterization of hepatitis B core antigen antibody among vaccinated adults lacking hepatitis B surface antigen in ethiopia: A multicenter facility-based study
Authors: Adane Adugna, Desalegn Abebaw, Zigale Hibstu Teffera, Yonatan Kindie, Wubetu Yihunie Belay, Getachew Tilaye Mihiret, Altaseb Beyene Kassaw, Mihiret Bogale Abera, Gashaw Abebe, Addis Alem, Zeleke Geto, Mohammed Abdu Seid, Hassen Ahmed Yesuf, Mulu Shiferaw Asfaw, Mohammed Jemal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major public health challenge in Ethiopia, particularly among high-risk adult populations such as health professionals. Despite vaccination efforts, the prevalence and determinants of anti-hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc) seroreactivity among vaccinated adult health professionals lacking hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) are not well characterized. Objective This study aimed to determine the prevalence and predictors of anti-HBc seroreactivity among HBsAg-negative vaccinated adult health professionals in hospitals of the northwestern region of Ethiopia. A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 399 adult health professional who completed HBV vaccination series and were HBsAg negative from April to September 2024. Data on sociodemographic, clinical, and vaccine-related characteristics were collected via questionnaire. Serum samples were tested for HBsAg and anti-HBc using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits at Debre Markos Blood Bank Laboratory. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify significant predictors of anti-HBc seroreactivity. In this study, the overall anti-HBc seroreactivity among HBsAg-negative vaccinated adult healthcare professionals was 25/399 (6.3%, 95% CI: 4.0%-9.0%). Participants born in rural areas had an anti-HBc seropositivity rate of 8.1%. Additionally, midwives exhibited a seropositivity rate of 11.7%. Among participants who had undergone organ transplantation, 66.7% showed anti-HBc seroreactivity. Moreover, among participants with a family history of liver disease, the prevalence of anti-HBc was 30.0%. Moreover, a previous history of chronic kidney disease (AOR: 9.1, 95% CI: 7.56–11.98, P < 0.014) and obesity (AOR: 6.9, 95% CI: 4.91–8.63, P < 0.003) were significant predictors of anti-HBc seroreactivity. Anti-HBc seroprevalence among HBsAg-negative vaccinated adult healthcare professionals was notable. This finding underscores the critical need for post-vaccination testing to identify HBV infections. Introduction

The burden of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major public health challenge globally and specifically in Ethiopia1. Worldwide, approximately more than 296 million people are living with chronic HBV infection, with the highest prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa and East Asia, regions that include Ethiopia2. Despite vaccination efforts, HBV causes significant morbidity and mortality, with about 820,000 deaths annually attributed to HBV-related liver disease, including cirrhosis and liver cancer3,4.

In Ethiopia, despite widespread vaccination programs have reduced new infections, breakthrough infections persist5. This is evidenced by the presence of antibodies to the hepatitis B core antigen (anti-HBc) among vaccinated adults who lack detectable surface antigen (HBsAg)6. Anti-HBc positivity in such individuals suggests prior or ongoing viral exposure despite immunization7. This seems plausible since approximately 5% of vaccinated individuals remain at risk of infection despite the vaccine’s high overall efficacy. However, failure of vaccine effectiveness may occur primarily in cases of infection by HBV/HBsAg mutants, which are less susceptible to the widely used vaccine8,9. Anti-HBc serves as important markers for past or ongoing HBV infection, as they develop after exposure to the virus itself10.

Host immune status, including factors such as overweight and chronic diseases like chronic kidney disease (CKD), can impair the immune response to hepatitis B vaccination, resulting in anti-HBc positivity despite full vaccination11. Conditions like CKD cause disturbances in T-lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells, which are crucial for effective vaccine-induced immunity12. Overweight individuals may also experience altered immune responses, affecting antibody production and durability13. These immunological impairments can lead to incomplete protection, allowing viral exposure or breakthrough infections, which manifest as anti-HBc positivity even when vaccination is complete14.

In Ethiopia, data are limited on the prevalence and determinants of anti-HBc seroreactivity among hepatitis B vaccinated adults without HBsAg, particularly across multiple healthcare facilities15. Most existing research has focused on children or single-center studies, with little information about adult populations or multicenter facility-based contexts. Furthermore, gaps remain in evaluating factors that contribute to breakthrough infections, including variability in vaccine response, levels of protective antibodies, and exposure risks such as multiple sexual partners, hospitalization, blood transfusions, needle exposure, contact with body fluids, body piercings, and tattoos16,17. This lack of comprehensive data compromises the ability to fully understand vaccine effectiveness and HBV transmission dynamics at the population level in Ethiopia18. Justifying this research, investigating the anti-HBc among vaccinated adults who are HBsAg-negative can provide critical insights into the immune status and possible viral exposure routes in this population19. Moreover, this study’s multicenter facility-based design allows for a broader, epidemiologically robust assessment that can support national efforts to control HBV infection and achieve the WHO elimination goals by 203020. Moreover, addressing these research gaps is essential for tailoring HBV prevention policies and improving long-term health outcomes in Ethiopia21.

In other words, while hepatitis B vaccination has significantly reduced the infection burden, the detection of anti-HBc among vaccinated, HBsAg-negative Ethiopian adults remains underexplored and inadequately characterized. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of anti-HBc seroreactivity and its predictors among HBsAg-negative vaccinated adult health professionals in all hospitals of the East Gojjam zone, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and period

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among HBsAg-negative vaccinated, adult health professionals in all hospitals of East Gojjam, located in the northwestern region of Ethiopia, from April to September, 2024.

Study population

The study population consisted of adult health professionals working in all hospitals within the East Gojjam zone, who have been fully vaccinated against hepatitis B virus and tested negative for HBsAg.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included adult health professionals (aged 18 years and above) employed in hospitals of the East Gojjam zone and who provided informed consent to participate. On the other hand, individuals, currently undergoing treatment for hepatitis B infection, unable or unwilling to provide informed consent, on prolonged leave or absent during the study period were excluded from the study.

To account for potential non-response, a 10% margin was added, resulting in an additional 38 participants. Therefore, the final calculated sample size was 422 participants. Then, the total sample size was proportionally distributed among each hospital, and participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique. Laboratory analysis of all 422 collected samples using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) identified 23 participants who tested positive for HBsAg; these individuals were excluded from the study. The remaining 399 participants, confirmed to be HBsAg-negative, proceeded to anti-HBc testing, establishing the final sample size for this study as 399.

Data collection and laboratory test procedures

A structured questionnaire was employed to gather socio-demographic, clinical and vaccine-related information from each study participant. Additionally, 5 to 7 milliliters of venous blood were drawn by venipuncture from every participant. All blood samples collected from the hospitals across the East Gojjam Zone were transported in cold boxes with ice packs to Debre Markos Blood Bank Laboratory (DBBL) to maintain proper storage conditions. Serum was separated from the blood samples through centrifugation and subsequently stored at −80 °C until laboratory analysis.

Screening for HBsAg was conducted at the DBBL using a qualitative ELISA kit [Monolisa™ HBs Ag ULTRA, Bio-Rad, France] on 100 µL serum samples. The absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at wavelengths of 450/620–700 nm. Samples that tested negative for HBsAg by ELISA were further analyzed for the presence of antibodies to hepatitis B core antigen (anti-HBc) using the Monolisa™ Anti-HBc PLUS ELISA kit.

Quality control

To ensure laboratory quality control throughout the processes of collection, storage, and ELISA testing for HBsAg and anti-HBc, a comprehensive step-by-step approach was applied. Initially, thorough training was provided to data collectors to ensure that questionnaires were consistently administered, thereby minimizing interviewer bias and errors. During blood collection, sterile and standardized venipuncture equipment was used, proper techniques were followed to avoid hemolysis or contamination, and samples were accurately labeled with unique identifiers to prevent mix-ups. Subsequently, temperature control was maintained during sample transport by using cold boxes with fresh ice packs, which were regularly monitored, and transport time between collection sites and the laboratory was minimized.

At the processing stage, samples were promptly centrifuged using calibrated centrifuges, serum was carefully separated to avoid cellular contamination, and storage at −80 °C was consistently maintained with a temperature log kept. Regarding analytical quality control for ELISA testing, reagents were handled carefully by using kits within their expiration dates and freshly preparing reagents to avoid cross-contamination. Each ELISA plate was loaded with positive controls, negative controls, and blank wells to monitor assay performance, with optical density values of controls being verified to fall within specified ranges. Equipment such as spectrophotometers and centrifuges were regularly calibrated and maintained.

Furthermore, laboratory personnel were adequately trained and proficiency in ELISA procedures was ensured, with strict adherence to standard operating procedures (SOPs) throughout sample handling and testing. Reliability was enhanced by running samples in duplicate or as recommended, and repeat testing was performed for borderline or inconsistent results. Detailed documentation and record-keeping were maintained, covering sample receipt, processing times, reagent lot numbers, control results, final test outcomes, and any deviations encountered during testing. In the post-analytical phase, data entry accuracy was verified, periodic data audits were conducted to identify discrepancies.

Data analysis

The data were initially entered into EpiData software version 3.1 and then exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Findings were displayed using in text and tables. Binary logistic regression was conducted to evaluate the relationships between the dependent and predictor variables. In the bivariate analysis, variables with a p-value below 0.25 were chosen as candidates for inclusion in the multivariate logistic regression. The multivariate analysis provided adjusted odds ratios (AORs) alongside 95% confidence intervals, with statistical significance determined at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval statement

The study was granted ethical clearance by the Institutional Research Ethics Review Committee (IRERC) of the College of Health Sciences at Debre Markos University, adhering to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (protocol number: R/C/S/D/317/09/2024). In addition, this study was approved by the IRERC of each institution at the study site. Permission letters were also obtained from each institution at the study site. Furthermore, written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study.

Furthermore, more than half of the participants (52.9%) were married, followed by 43.1% who were single, and 4.0% who were divorced. Discussion

The limited data on anti-HBc seroprevalence among vaccinated Ethiopian adults highlights the need for comprehensive multicenter studies to better understand HBV transmission dynamics and improve control strategies22. Strengthening vaccination programs, improving screening, and addressing exposure risks are crucial steps for reducing HBV infection burden and moving towards WHO’s 2030 elimination goals in Ethiopia and globally23. Additionally, infections with HBsAg mutant strains can occur; these mutants alter the HBsAg’s structure, making it undetectable by standard assays24.

This study investigated the prevalence and predictors of anti-HBc seroreactivity among HBsAg-negative vaccinated adult health professionals in hospitals of the northwestern region of Ethiopia. In this study, the overall seroprevalence of anti-HBc was 25/399 (6.3%, 95% CI: 4.0%−9.0%). This prevalence is lower than that reported in a study from Poland25, which found a rate of 10.5% among healthcare workers. In contrast, countries with well-established vaccination programs and lower HBV endemicity, such as parts of Europe and North America, generally report anti-HBc seroprevalence below 5% in healthcare workers, highlighting the effectiveness of vaccination and stringent infection control measures2,26,27. Conversely, in regions with higher HBV endemicity and lower vaccination coverage, particularly in many African countries, healthcare workers tend to exhibit higher rates of HBV exposure and infection, with seroprevalence sometimes exceeding 10%28–30.

The observed variation in anti-HBc seroprevalence across different studies and regions can be attributed to several factors. Differences in HBV endemicity fundamentally influence exposure risk, with higher endemic regions naturally exhibiting greater seroprevalence. In addition, in regions with a high prevalence of HBV infection, the likelihood of mutant strains emerging is greater than in low-prevalence areas31. Consequently, the risk of horizontal transmission persists even among individuals vaccinated against the wild-type virus, as these mutants can evade the neutralizing effects of vaccination-induced antibodies32. Furthermore, the presence of such mutants increases the risk of undetectable HBsAg in standard assays33.

Moreover, vaccination coverage and the timing of vaccine introduction are also critical determinants: countries with longstanding, well-implemented vaccination programs and effective infection control tend to have lower seroprevalence among healthcare workers34, reflecting both reduced new infections and protection against chronic carriage.

Conversely, in regions where vaccination coverage is incomplete or recent, such as many African countries, exposure rates remain higher. Additionally, variations in occupational exposure risks35, including frequency of needle-stick injuries and adherence to standard precautions, affect infection rates36. Methodological differences, such as the sensitivity of assays for anti-HBc detection and study population characteristics (age, work experience, prior infection status), further contribute to disparities37–39.

Therefore, the 6.3% seroprevalence in this study falls between the lower rates seen in well-vaccinated, low-endemic populations and the higher rates observed in regions with ongoing transmission and variable vaccination uptake.

A notable finding was that anti-HBc seroreactivity was observed across various socio-demographic strata, with slightly higher proportions in younger participants (≤ 30 years) compared to older ones (>30 years), as well as among females compared to males. This contrasts somewhat with other studies where older age groups tend to have higher rates of HBV exposure due to cumulative lifetime risk18,40. The relatively higher seroreactivity in younger age groups in our study could be attributed to recent occupational exposures or varying immunological responses to vaccination within this demographic. Moreover, this may be explained by several factors, including horizontal transmission through sexual contact41; gaps in vaccine coverage due to non-vaccination or incomplete immunization outside the recommended schedule42; infection with HBsAg mutants, especially in regions with high prevalence; false-positive anti-HBc test results; or previous HBV infection occurring prior to vaccination43.

Similarly, the seroreactivity differences between males and females appear modest but align with some literature noting gender differences in HBV exposure risks and immune responses, possibly influenced by behavioral, hormonal, or genetic factors44. The socio-demographic backdrop, including more urban-born participants and health workers predominantly holding undergraduate or higher education levels, reflects a population with likely better vaccine access but also intense occupational exposure risks.

In addition, midwives exhibited higher anti-HBc positivity compared to other healthcare professionals such as anesthetists, among whom no seroreactivity was detected in this cohort. This pattern may relate to greater frequency and intensity of blood and body fluid exposure in certain medical roles. Consistent with this, exposures to needles and body fluids were associated with increased seroreactivity in bivariate analyses, reinforcing known transmission routes for HBV45.

The seroreactivity rate of 21.4% among participants vaccinated within the last 12 months was higher compared to those with longer periods since vaccination. This may seem paradoxical since recent vaccinates would be assumed to have better protection. It’s also possible that these individuals were previously infected before completing vaccination. Similar findings have been reported by other studies in sub-Saharan Africa indicating incomplete vaccine efficacy or waning immunity in real-world conditions46.

Participants with a family history of liver disease exhibited markedly elevated anti-HBc seroreactivity (30.0%), which is consistent with genetic or environmental clustering of HBV infection risk. Familial transmission, either vertical (from mother to child) or horizontal in childhood, remains a significant route of HBV spread worldwide47.

This association warrants emphasis on family screening and early immunization interventions.

The history of HIV infection among participants showed compelling anti-HBc positivity (90%), reflecting the immunosuppressive effects of HIV and increased susceptibility to co-infections including HBV48. HIV-HBV coinfection is known to complicate immune control of HBV, hastening liver disease progression49. This finding highlights the critical need for integrated HBV screening and vaccination strategies within HIV care programs in Ethiopia.

In this study, among clinical comorbidities, history of obesity and chronic kidney disease (CKD) were independently and significantly associated with anti-HBc seroreactivity after multivariate adjustment. The adjusted odds ratios of 6.9 for obesity and 9.1 for CKD suggest these conditions play a role in vulnerability or persistence of HBV infection markers. Obesity represents a state of chronic low-grade inflammation and altered immune responsiveness50. Impaired immune responses in obese individuals may reduce vaccine efficacy or promote viral persistence, possibly explaining this association51. Furthermore, chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation seen in obesity also adversely affect liver health and may facilitate HBV reactivation or interfere with clearance, as supported by prior immunological studies52,53.

Chronic kidney disease patients often exhibit immune dysfunction, reduced vaccine responsiveness, and frequent healthcare exposures such as dialysis, which increase HBV infection risk54,55. In other words, this impairment is related to the degree of kidney failure, resulting in lower seroconversion rates, reduced peak antibody titers, and shorter duration of protective immunity compared to healthy individuals56,57. Thus, CKD patients have a higher likelihood of inadequate antibody response due to both immune dysfunction associated with kidney disease and suboptimal vaccination timing or dosing, necessitating early and possibly intensified vaccination strategies to improve protection against HBV infection in this high-risk group58,59.

Limitation

Although this multisite research contributes valuable knowledge about anti-HBc seroreactivity among HBsAg-negative vaccinated adult health professionals, it has certain limitations, including its cross-sectional design, which does not allow for causal conclusions. Moreover, HBeAg (hepatitis B e antigen) and anti-HBe antibody status were not evaluated in this study, leaving markers of viral replication and infectivity unknown. Additionally, the study depended solely on serological markers without confirming HBV DNA presence, thereby restricting the ability to identify occult infections. Future longitudinal investigations that incorporate molecular diagnostic methods and track vaccine-induced antibody titers over time are needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of vaccine efficacy and the dynamics of HBV transmission.

Furthermore, the hepatitis B virus infection status of the study participants before vaccination was unknown due to a lack of available information.

Conclusion and recommendations

The current study showed a notable prevalence of anti-HBc seroreactivity among HBsAg-negative vaccinated adult health professionals, suggesting that this may be influenced by individual variations in host immune status despite completed vaccination, as well as breakthrough infections possibly related to HBV mutants. It is recommended to implement routine post-vaccination serologic testing for healthcare professionals to assess protective anti-HBs antibody levels and identify non-responders. Strengthening HBV vaccination programs is essential by ensuring full adherence to recommended schedules and considering booster doses for individuals with waning immunity or at higher risk. Additionally, enhancing surveillance for HBV mutants that may evade vaccine-induced immunity will be crucial. Moreover, promoting comprehensive infection control practices and regular education on occupational exposure risks can further reduce HBV transmission among healthcare workers.

Furthermore, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and obesity were significant determinants of anti-HBc seroreactivity due to their detrimental effects on immune response. CKD patients often exhibited impaired immunity, leading to lower seroconversion rates, reduced antibody titers, and shorter duration of protection following HBV vaccination. Similarly, obesity induced chronic low-grade inflammation and immune dysregulation, impairing vaccine-induced antibody production and resulting in weaker and less sustained immune responses. Consequently, both conditions increased the risk of insufficient protective immunity despite vaccination. Hence, early and intensified vaccination strategies were recommended for CKD patients, including vaccination at earlier disease stages, use of higher vaccine doses, or additional booster doses to improve antibody response. For obese individuals, personalized vaccination protocols and regular monitoring of antibody levels were also advised to ensure adequate protection.
