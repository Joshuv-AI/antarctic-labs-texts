Open Cholecystectomy in a Patient With Situs Inversus Totalis and Symptomatic Cholelithiasis: A Case Report
Authors: Syeda Farwa Parihan Gillani, Muhammad Hassaan Javaid, Rao Nouman Ali, Wajiha Irfan, Sohaib Irfan, Raghabendra Kumar Mahato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSitus inversus totalis (SIT) reverses normal anatomical landmarks used during cholecystectomy, requiring careful preoperative imaging, a mirror‐image surgical strategy, and strict achievement of the critical view of safety before ductal or vascular division. Despite visceral transposition, open cholecystectomy via a left subcostal (mirror‐image Kocher) incision can be performed safely.

Introduction

Situs inversus totalis (SIT) is a rare congenital anomaly where there is a complete reversal of the thoracic and abdominal organs, with the prevalence rate being 1 per 6000–20,000 births (0.01% of the population) [1, 2]. The anomaly develops due to the malfunctioning of the laterality determination process in the development of the embryo leading to dextrocardia and the presence of the liver, gallbladder, and hepatoduodenal ligament on the left side [3]. About a third of patients with SIT develop primary ciliary dyskinesia, which forms Kartagener's syndrome (bronchiectasis, sinusitis, and situs inversus) [2].

The incidence of cholelithiasis among SIT cases is similar to the general population but the anatomical reversal results in changed signs, symptoms, and treatment modalities [4, 5]. The abdominal pain might occur in the left upper quadrant, not in the right, and the reversed Murphy's sign might be elicited at a different location, which often results in misdiagnosis [6, 7]. Due to the fact that most of the surgeons come across SIT very rarely (1–2 times in their whole practice), each case described adds to their experience [3].

As of now, around 40 cases of open cholecystectomy and more than 100 cases of laparoscopic cholecystectomy in patients with SIT have been described in the literature [8]. Although laparoscopic cholecystectomy is considered the gold standard for treatment, the open approach has its own indications, such as lack of laparoscopic access, need for conversion to open approach during surgery, or refusal of minimally invasive surgery by the patient [9, 10]. The current report describes a 24‐year‐old man with SIT and cholelithiasis managed by open cholecystectomy through a left subcostal approach with special attention to the anatomical problems faced and adaptation during surgery to get a critical view of safety in mirror‐image anatomy.

Case Presentation

A 24‐year‐old male was seen at the surgery Outpatient Department complaining of left upper quadrant and epigastric colicky pain that was occurring intermittently over a period of 3 months and had a pain score of 6–7/10. The pain was always associated with eating fatty food, nausea, and sometimes vomiting. No other complaints like jaundice, fever, chills, itching, acholia, dark‐colored urine, hematemesis, melena, or any unintentional weight loss were reported. The patient has no comorbid conditions and has never had an abdominal surgery, no family history of congenital anomalies, and denied tobacco and alcohol use.

On physical examination, all vital signs were within the normal range: heart rate, 82 beats per min; blood pressure, 118/76 mmHg; respiratory rate, 16 per min; and temperature, 98.4°F (36.9°C). Abdomen showed slight deep tenderness over the left hypochondrium and epigastrium with no guarding or rigidity. The reverse Murphy's sign was noted over the left subcostal area. On examination of the cardiovascular system, the most crucial finding was that the cardiac apex was seen to beat from the right side, palpable maximum at the fifth right intercostal space in mid‐clavicular line, raising immediate clinical suspicion for dextrocardia and SIT.

Investigations

No remarkable findings on laboratory investigation were made. Complete Blood Count showed no leukocytosis or anemia. The serum liver function test, which includes total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, ALT, AST, ALP, and GGT, showed no elevation, and therefore, there was no biochemical evidence for biliary obstruction, hepatocellular damage, and cholestasis. Amylase and lipase levels were not elevated; thus, no concurrent pancreatitis. Creatinine and electrolytes were normal. Coagulation profile (prothrombin time, INR) was normal.

The 12‐lead ECG showed dextrocardia with typical findings of right axis deviation, inverted P waves in lead I and aVL, positive P waves in aVR and poor R wave progression in the precordial leads [6]. Chest X‐ray revealed a right‐sided heart shadow, the apex pointing to the right side, right‐sided gastric air bubble and left‐sided liver shadow, indicative of SIT (Figure 1).

Chest radiograph and CECT mediastinum (axial section) demonstrating rightward cardiac apex (dextrocardia) confirming situs inversus totalis.

CECT abdomen (coronal section) demonstrating left‐sided liver and gallbladder (yellow arrows) and right‐sided spleen (blue arrows) consistent with situs inversus totalis.

The abdominal USG helped make the diagnosis of SIT because the liver and gallbladder were found in the left hypochondrium and the spleen was seen in the right hypochondrium. Calculi (the biggest one is 12 mm) were seen in the gallbladder, while there was no pericholecystic fluid or thickening of the gallbladder wall (> 3 mm) to indicate acute cholecystitis. The common bile duct (CBD) had normal dimensions (4 mm) and there was no dilatation of the intrahepatic biliary radicles (IHBRD). The head of the pancreas was seen on the left side without peripancreatic changes.

Further evaluation of the biliary anatomy and exclusion of CBD stones before surgery was carried out by means of contrast‐enhanced CT scan of the abdomen, pelvis, and mediastinum (Figures 1, 2, 3). CT scan showed complete inversion of all thoracic and abdominal viscera: liver, gallbladder, and portal vein on the left side; stomach, pancreas (tail on the right side), and spleen on the right side. The structure and content of the hepatoduodenal ligament were mirror‐imaged. There was no biliary dilation, choledocholithiasis, or peripancreatic pathology visualized at cross‐sectional imaging. The CBD was 4 mm on CT. With regard to normal liver biochemistry, no biliary dilation was visualized at USG and CECT scan, and the absence of symptoms of CBD stones (jaundice, cholangitis, or pancreatitis) led to the conclusion that the probability of choledocholithiasis was considered low, and further cholangiography was deferred per institutional practice and consultant assessment.

CECT abdomen (axial section) confirming left‐sided liver (yellow arrows) and right‐sided spleen (green arrows).

Diagnostic Assessment

Symptomatic cholelithiasis with situs inversus totalis was diagnosed based on the patient's history, presence of reverse Murphy's sign, sonographic findings of cholelithiasis on the left side without biliary obstruction, and confirmation of visceral transposition using cross‐sectional imaging.

Operative Management

Post multidisciplinary consultation with obtaining informed consent, cholecystectomy was decided. In view of (a) the non‐availability of facilities for performing laparoscopic surgery that can handle mirror‐image anatomy at the place of treatment, and (b) the patient's refusal to be transferred to another place where laparoscopic surgery could be attempted, an open cholecystectomy was chosen as the first choice of surgery. Laparoscopic surgery was not attempted. It was done under general anesthesia in the supine position.

Left subcostal, or mirror‐image Kocher's, incision was performed, extending from the midline to left anterior axillary line at around 2 cm below the costal margin (Figure 4). This offered a direct view of the left lateral lobe of the liver, the undersurface of the left lobe of the liver where the gallbladder fossa is situated, and structures of the hepatoduodenal ligament transposed to the left side.

Left subcostal (mirror‐image Kocher) incision for open cholecystectomy in situs inversus totalis.

As soon as the organs inside the peritoneal cavity were exposed, it became evident that all the visceral organs were mirrored. The gallbladder was situated on the inferior side of the left hepatic lobe. The mirror‐image Calot's triangle was visualized; the cystic duct originated from the gallbladder neck on the left side and ran medially toward the common bile duct, which was situated on the right side of the cystic duct. The inferior border of the left liver made up the superior border of the hepatocystic triangle. The left common bile duct formed the medial border, whereas the inferior border consisted of the cystic duct. The cystic artery originated from the right hepatic artery, which entered the Calot's triangle from the surgeon's left.

Incision of peritoneum covering the hepatocystic triangle and dissection with great precision to obtain CVS included: (i) clearance of hepatocystic triangle of fat and fibro‐areolar tissues, (ii) mobilization of the lower third of the gallbladder from the liver surface, and (iii) confirmation that only two structures entered the gallbladder: cystic duct and cystic artery. The common bile duct was observed throughout the dissection and it remained intact. The cystic duct was doubly clipped proximally and singly distally, and then it was divided between the clips. Similarly, the cystic artery was doubly clipped and divided. Fundus‐first dissection was performed to dissect the gallbladder off its hepatic bed on the left side with minimum bleeding (< 50 mL). The total operative time was 75 min. A closed suction drain was inserted in the subhepatic space (left side) due to some ooze from the gallbladder bed, and the abdomen was sutured in layers (Figure 5).

Postoperative wound closure with closed‐suction drain in situ.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

There were no complications during the postoperative period. The patient was able to tolerate oral intake on postoperative day one and regular food on postoperative day two. There was a decrease in drainage of fluid, which was serosanguinous, less than 20 mL per day on postoperative day two, and the drain was then removed before discharge. The patient was then discharged from the hospital in stable condition on postoperative day three. On histopathology of the cholecystectomy, there was chronic calculous cholecystitis with cholesterolosis but no dysplastic or malignant cells. There was no evidence of infection, seroma, or hematoma of the wound at 2 weeks postoperatively. The patient's complaints of pain had resolved.

Discussion

Herein lies the clinical importance of this case in three main areas: (i) the diagnosis of gallstone disease in SIT, (ii) preoperative biliary investigations to rule out choledocholithiasis, and (iii) intraoperative considerations necessary to perform a safe cholecystectomy in patients with total visceral transposition. All three of these areas have educational significance independent of just the rarity of SIT.

SIT patients do not have an increased prevalence of cholelithiasis when compared to the rest of the population [4, 5]; nevertheless, the laterally inverted visceral anatomy causes changes to both symptoms and signs that emerge from the physical examination. Patients suffering from left‐sided cholelithiasis usually suffer from either left upper quadrant pain or epigastric pain, and Murphy's sign should be checked for in the left lower part of the rib cage, or in what is referred to as “reverse Murphy's sign” [6, 7]. It is worth mentioning that around 10% of SIT patients suffering from cholelithiasis may experience right‐sided pain due to incomplete remapping of the visceral pain pathway by the central nervous system [11]. Familiarity with this clinical heterogeneity is essential to avoid diagnostic delay.

In the current case, the incidental discovery of a right‐sided apex beat while conducting a routine physical examination provided the critical clue for further investigations. This emphasizes the necessity of proper examination of the cardiovascular system and abdomen in cases where there is unexplained abdominal pain, as SIT may accidentally be discovered when conducting surgery.

An important point before any surgery in patients with cholelithiasis is the exclusion of stones in the common bile duct. In the case of the patient, the process was carried out in an organized way. The presence of normal serum bilirubin, ALT, ALP, and GGT levels along with CBD diameter of 4 mm on USG and CECT without the presence of IHBRD served as negative predictors for the presence of choledocholithiasis, thus meeting the requirements of low‐risk criteria (the absence of jaundice, cholangitis, pancreatitis, dilated CBD, and CBD stones visualized) [12]. According to the EASL and ASGE stratification system, the patient belongs to a low probability group of having CBD stones; therefore, MRCP examination before surgery and intraoperative cholangiography were not required [6, 12, 13]. However, in institutions where it is possible, MRCP serves as a gold standard non‐invasive test for CBD evaluation, while intraoperative cholangiography is available in all cholecystectomy surgeries regardless of SIT because of the mirror biliary anatomy, which increases the chance of injury.

Cholecystectomy has long been accepted as the standard of care for symptomatic cholelithiasis, and this remains true even in the case of SIT; more than 100 cases of laparoscopic cholecystectomy in SIT have been documented in the literature, with successful results [14, 15, 16]. But the use of laparoscopy in SIT presents certain challenges to the surgeon; the procedure involves performing the major dissection maneuvers in an ergonomically sound manner from the right side of the body while working on a left‐sided gallbladder, meaning that the non‐dominant hand (left) needs to be used for procedures such as the traction on Hartmann's pouch that normally are done using the dominant right hand [10, 17, 18, 19]. Other positions that have been described in the literature include “French position,” single port surgery, and robotic‐assisted surgery [18, 20, 21, 22].

In the current scenario, there were two reasons for the open cholecystectomy, both of which were occurring at the same time: the lack of availability of laparoscopic facilities in the treatment hospital and an informed refusal by the patient to move to another institution where laparoscopy is possible. More importantly, there was no effort made for laparoscopy, nor was any conversion done during the surgery. The open cholecystectomy was the predetermined plan, and the left subcostal mirror‐image Kocher incision offered an ample amount of visualization for the transposed hepatobiliary anatomy and facilitated bimanual dissection without the ergonomic constraints imposed by laparoscopic instrumentation.

The main difficulty of cholecystectomy surgery in SIT patients lies in identifying the mirror‐image structures inside Calot's triangle since they lack any kind of recognizable landmarks. The specific intraoperative changes in the present case included the following points: First, 3D reconstruction of the mirror images in preoperative planning on CT was done by the surgical team before performing the first incision. As a result, the surgical team knew in advance that the CBD would be located on the right side relative to the surgical field instead of the left one.

The second approach, which is the critical view of safety (CVS) technique as described by Strasberg et al. [23] was carefully performed using its mirror image. The hepatocystic triangle was entirely separated from fat and connective tissue, the lower gallbladder was carefully separated from the liver bed, and there were only two structures identified to be entering into the gallbladder before the division. The achievement of CVS remains the single most vital intraoperative protection against bile duct injury, and its use is not lessened in any way due to the anatomical reversal.

Third, the vascular pattern of Calot's triangle was seen to be normal (e.g., cystic artery coming off the right hepatic artery, now on the left side of the patient), in keeping with previous literature showing that SIT usually doesn't cause any additional anomalies within the liver vasculature other than the laterality shift [10, 24]. This again confirms the previously stated idea that although spatial orientation has been changed in SIT, the organization of hepatobiliary vasculature follows a predictable pattern. Fourth, a retrograde gallbladder dissection approach (fundus‐first) was used after clamping the cystic duct and artery for an added layer of security owing to the unfamiliar orientation.

This successful outcome of uneventful recovery is proof of the appropriateness of this method. In this case, it can be seen that the skill needed for the safe removal of the gall bladder in SIT does not involve the learning of a new technique but involves proper spatial orientation and abidance by the safety principles applicable in normal cholecystectomy.

Conclusion

These particular learnings from this case include the following: First, there is a possibility of diagnostic delay because of localization of symptoms in the left upper quadrant instead of the right; an exhaustive physical examination with a high degree of suspicion is necessary. Second, preoperative bile duct evaluation should undergo a stratified process to rule out choledocholithiasis; if there are no signs on biochemistry or ultrasound of biliary obstruction, MRCP may not be needed, but its use is highly recommended in resource‐rich institutions in light of the possibilities of mirrored anatomy complicating identification of the CBD intraoperatively. Third, open cholecystectomy through the mirror‐image Kocher incision, with diligent application of the critical view of safety and careful identification of the mirror‐image Calot's triangle anatomy, will result in the same success rate of cholecystectomy surgery as the conventional method. As institutions increasingly encounter SIT patients, familiarity with these principles, rather than empirical improvisation, will determine operative safety.
