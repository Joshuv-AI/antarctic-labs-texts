How Long to Treat to Achieve Desired Treatment Outcomes with Baricitinib 4 mg in Severe Alopecia Areata: Evidence-based Approach
Authors: Brett King, Maryanne Senna, Manabu Ohyama, Rodney Sinclair, Najwa Somani, Na Lu, Yun-Fei Chen, Jakub Jedynak, Andrew Buchanan, Bianca Maria Piraccini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionScalp hair regrowth in severe alopecia areata (AA) can occur rapidly or take up to 2 years in some patients. Baseline factors influence the likelihood and/or timing of treatment response with Janus kinase inhibitors. We evaluated initial evidence of improvement (Severity of Alopecia Tool30) and subsequent treatment success (SALT score ≤ 20) in four subgroups defined by baseline disease severity and AA episode duration to inform the treatment length required before assessing failure, based on disease presentation.MethodsIn a post hoc analysis of BRAVE-AA1/2 (NCT03570749/NCT03899259), baricitinib 4 mg treatment subgroups were assessed for SALT30 and subsequent SALT score ≤ 20 over time.ResultsIn severe AA (SALT score 50–94), SALT30 occurred in 70% of those with short duration and 61% with long duration by Week 24, leading to 75% and 65% achieving treatment success (SALT score ≤ 20) by Week 52. Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune disease that causes nonscarring hair loss that can affect any hair-bearing area. It can present with limited patches to complete loss of hair on the scalp and/or body [1]. The Janus kinase (JAK) pathway is a key pathway involved in the pathogenesis of AA, and therapies that block this pathway, including associated driver cytokines such as interferon-γ and interleukin (IL)-15, have been recently approved for the treatment of severe AA [2–4].

Like other cutaneous inflammatory conditions such as psoriasis or atopic dermatitis, long-term treatment of disease in severe AA is required to maintain response among those patients who achieve treatment success. However, unlike in other cutaneous inflammatory conditions, where benefits of treatment are often observed over 1–4 months [5], hair regrowth in severe AA often takes 6–12 months and sometimes longer [6, 7]. The timing of response onset and proportion of patients who achieve treatment success has been shown to vary based on baseline disease characteristics, namely baseline disease severity [8] and the duration of the current hair loss episode [7]. If dermatologists do not understand the time required for hair regrowth, patients may be prematurely discontinued or switched from therapy, ultimately resulting in poor treatment outcomes.

The oral selective reversible JAK inhibitor baricitinib, taken at a dose of 2 mg or 4 mg once daily, is approved for and efficacious in regrowing eyebrow, eyelash and scalp hair in adults with severe AA [1, 9]. While response to treatment in the BRAVE-AA clinical trials was observed as soon as weeks for some and within the first few months for many other patients, with continued improvement over 52 weeks, some patients took as long as 2 years to achieve a SALT score ≤ 20 (80% scalp coverage), the primary endpoint in clinical trials [7, 10]. Previous BRAVE-AA analyses identified early, gradual and late responder subgroups based on achieving a 30% improvement in baseline SALT score (SALT30) [7, 11]. Duration of the current hair loss episode influenced likelihood of response, while baseline disease severity influenced whether a patient was an early, gradual or late responder (and also influenced the likelihood of response) [7]. These analyses demonstrated that the timing of SALT30 response can predict when near-complete or complete hair regrowth is likely to occur, informing length of treatment to achieve response based on disease presentation.

In the present analysis, we evaluated initial improvement (SALT30) and subsequent scalp treatment success (SALT score ≤ 20) across subgroups defined by baseline severity (severe AA, SALT score 50–94; very severe AA, SALT score 95–100) and chronicity of scalp hair loss (duration of the current severe AA episode: < 4 years, short duration; ≥ 4 years, long duration) to estimate the time required to treat patients based on their disease presentation before assessing treatment failure. These data inform an evidence-based tailored approach to determining how long to treat a patient to achieve successful treatment outcomes.

Methods

Patients and Study Design

BRAVE-AA1 (NCT03570749) and BRAVE-AA2 (NCT03899259) are two Phase 3, randomized, double-blind, parallel-group, placebo-controlled studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of baricitinib in the treatment of severe AA (≥ 50% scalp hair loss). Eligibility criteria and trial protocol details have been previously published [2]. Briefly, participants were randomized to placebo, baricitinib 2 mg or baricitinib 4 mg and treated for 36 weeks. All participants randomized to either baricitinib dose continued treatment through Week 52, at which point responders (SALT score ≤ 20) underwent a re-randomization to stay on assigned treatment, down-titrate or withdraw from treatment. Non-responders to baricitinib 4 mg (SALT score > 20) who had an eyebrow or eyelash response at Week 52 were allowed to stay on their assigned dose past Week 52 [10].

The studies adhered to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and the research protocols were approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each center. All patients provided written informed consent.

This post hoc analysis consisted of patients from BRAVE-AA1 and BRAVE-AA2 who were randomized to receive baricitinib 4 mg treatment through Week 52 (n = 515). Four subgroups were assessed according to baseline disease characteristics of severity category (severe vs very severe) and duration of the current episode within each of the two severity categories (Fig. 1). Hair Regrowth Assessments

Patient scalp hair loss was assessed using the SALT score. A SALT score ≤ 20 indicates ≤ 20% scalp hair loss, while a score of 100 means total scalp hair loss. SALT30 is defined as a 30% improvement from baseline in the SALT score [1, 11], which has been previously used in clinical trials to define initial scalp hair regrowth and subsequently shown to predict timing and magnitude of treatment success [7], as defined by achieving a SALT score ≤ 20. SALT scores between 50 and 94 define severe AA, and SALT scores between 95 and 100 define very severe AA (consistent with alopecia totalis/universalis) [12]. EB/EL hair loss was measured with the ClinRO EB/EL scale [13], where 3 denotes total loss, 2 large gaps, 1 minimal gaps and 0 no loss. EB/EL response was marked by achieving a score of 0 or 1 with ≥ 2-point improvement from baseline on the ClinRO scale, indicating minimal or no hair loss at Week 52 among patients with significant EB/EL involvement at baseline.

Statistical Analysis

Data collected after permanent treatment discontinuation were censored. Outcomes for scalp response are presented as nonresponder imputation for missing data up to Week 52. Summary statistics, mean (standard deviation [SD]) and % were calculated for the demographics and baseline disease characteristics. Response rates of SALT30 over 52 weeks were calculated for the four subgroups. SALT score ≤ 20 response rates were calculated for patients who achieved SALT30 at Week 24 for severe subgroups and Week 52 for very severe subgroups. An additional supplementary analysis of SALT score ≤ 20 response rates at Week 104, irrespective of baseline AA severity, was performed for patients who were scalp non-responders at Week 52 (SALT score > 20) but had achieved SALT30 and were allowed to continue on baricitinib 4 mg because of EB or EL response.

Among patients with very severe AA, SALT30 responses increased steadily to Week 52 and did not reach a plateau. Among 42% and 25% of patients, respectively, with short and long durations of the current episode, who achieved SALT30 at Week 52, 58% and 43% also achieved SALT scores ≤ 20 at Week 52, respectively. Analysis of likelihood of future treatment success for patients with very severe AA who had achieved SALT30 by Week 52 was not possible because of the BRAVE-AA study design wherein patients who had not achieved either SALT score ≤ 20 or EB or EL ClinRO score of 0/1 were discontinued from treatment at Week 52.

Analysis past Week 52 was possible in a subgroup of patients [10] who were scalp non-responders but had an eyebrow or eyelash response at Week 52 (N = 78) and were permitted to continue treatment with baricitinib 4 mg. Approximately 90% of these patients had very severe AA at baseline. In this supplementary analysis, 53% (n = 41) of these patients had achieved SALT30 at Week 52, none of whom had achieved SALT ≤ 20 at this time point. Of those patients achieving SALT30, 51% (n = 21) went on to achieve SALT score ≤ 20 treatment success with an additional year of treatment at Week 104.

Discussion

Historically, severe AA has been challenging to treat, and hair can take considerable time to regrow [6, 7]. Given the potentially long delay between treatment initiation and treatment response in some patients, these data address key clinically relevant questions about (1) how long it takes to observe discernible improvement (SALT30) following treatment initiation and (2) how long it takes to subsequently achieve clinically meaningful response (SALT score ≤ 20) with treatment. Factors that increase the probability of treatment success (SALT score ≤ 20) with JAK inhibition include lower baseline severity [8, 14], shorter duration of the current episode [7], higher treatment dose [1, 9], evidence of improvement with treatment within 24 weeks [7] and sufficient time on treatment [6, 7, 10].

A 30% improvement from baseline (SALT30) has been previously used to define initial evidence of improvement in Phase 2 clinical trials. Subsequent work showed that SALT30 can predict future treatment success [7] and that this improvement may occur early, gradually or late depending on patient baseline disease characteristics [7]. Baseline AA severity and duration of the current scalp hair loss episode are important factors that influence the likelihood of treatment response to JAK inhibitors in adults. These characteristics can be used to define patient subgroups that, as demonstrated by this analysis, respond differently to treatment. Understanding these response differences can be informative for both the clinician and the patient in terms of setting appropriate treatment expectations and making management decisions on how long to wait before considering to stop or switch therapy.

Most patients presenting at baseline with severe AA (SALT score 50–94) achieved SALT30 by 6 months, with 65% of those with short duration (n = 75/115) and 57% of those with long duration (n = 29/51) achieving ultimate treatment success, respectively. In these patients, treatment to 1 year resulted in clinically meaningful regrowth (SALT score ≤ 20) in 75% and 65% of patients with short and long duration, respectively. While episode duration influenced the likelihood of treatment success, it did not affect the timing of the response given that most patients achieved SALT30 by 6 months regardless of episode duration subgroup.

Conversely, patients presenting with totalis-like AA (SALT score 95–100) required more time on treatment to see initial improvement (SALT30). By 6 months, 42% and 25% of patients with short and long episode duration, respectively, achieved SALT30, and this proportion continued to increase through the observation period without plateauing by Week 52 (61% short and 36% long duration), indicating that continued treatment would result in additional patients achieving SALT30. Among those who achieved SALT30 at Week 52, 58% and 43% also achieved a SALT score ≤ 20. Similarly to severe AA, while episode duration influenced the likelihood of response, it did not influence the speed of response.

It was not possible to determine the impact of continued treatment beyond 1 year on response rates among the very severe AA cohort defined in this analysis because of the study design, which mandated re-randomization of baricitinib responders at Week 52. However, among scalp non-responders at Week 52 who had an EB/EL response, almost 90% had very severe AA at baseline, and 53% achieved a SALT30 by Week 52. Of those who achieved SALT30 at Week 52, 51% achieved treatment success (SALT score ≤ 20) after an additional 1-year of treatment. Therefore, these findings suggest that both SALT30 and/or EB/EL regrowth may be indicators of future treatment success in this cohort.

The patient subgroups described in this analysis were shown to have differences in onset of response and treatment success rates. These subgroups correspond to patient populations that are easily identifiable in the clinic setting based on visual assessments and patient history. Identifying and considering whether a patient falls within one of these subgroups can help inform treatment expectations and timelines to ensure each patient has the optimal opportunity to achieve treatment success and that treatment is not prematurely switched or discontinued. To our knowledge, this is the first analysis presenting an evidence-based approach to determining how long to treat patients with a JAK inhibitor based on disease presentation at baseline. However, the findings are consistent with analyses of ritlecitinib [6] demonstrating different patterns of clinical response and supporting continued treatment beyond 1 year for some patients, particularly those with alopecia totalis or universalis.

Limitations to this study relate to applicability within clinical practice. In the BRAVE-AA trials, concomitant use of adjuvant therapies for the treatment of severe AA was prohibited [2]. In routine practice, clinicians often use adjuvant therapies such as topical steroids [15], methotrexate [16] and topical or oral minoxidil [17, 18]. There is limited evidence suggesting adjuvant therapy may increase response rates compared to monotherapy with JAK inhibitors, but how it impacts timing of response and ultimate treatment success remains unknown [19]. This should be considered when assessing individual response to treatment. The impact of baseline severity and AA episode duration on treatment success has been observed with other JAK inhibitors used for treatment of AA. However, whether these evidence-based findings on length of treatment to achieve response based on disease characteristics are applicable to treatment with JAK inhibitors other than baricitinib is not known.

Conclusion

In summary, while other data have established that treatment must be continued indefinitely and must not be withdrawn to maintain scalp hair regrowth among those who achieve treatment success, this analysis addresses an evidence-based approach to determining the length of treatment required to reach initial evidence of improvement and treatment success, an important topic given that for most patients, hair regrowth takes time. This study highlights four patient populations based on the severity and duration of the current episode that determine the proportion of patients who respond and time to both initial response (SALT30) and treatment success (SALT score ≤ 20). Patients with severe AA typically require 6 months to 1 year to initial evidence of response and treatment success, respectively, regardless of duration of the current episode. The data also suggest that patients with alopecia totalis/universalis may require treatment for more than a year to reach initial evidence of response and treatment success, a finding supported by ritlecitinib analyses showing that patients who required > 1 year of treatment to achieve response were more likely to have alopecia totalis/universalis than those who responded by week 24 [6]. These data describe treatment responses with baricitinib 4 mg from pivotal clinical trials, and real-world experiences may vary with dose and use of adjuvant therapies.
