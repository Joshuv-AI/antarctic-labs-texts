An analysis of the decomposition and driving force of carbon emissions in transport sector in China
Authors: Jing Gu, Shuhua Jiang, Jing Zhang, Jinde Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChina’s transport carbon emissions are increasing quickly and the issue of emission reduction is urgent. This article aims to calculate and decompose China’s transport carbon emissions during 2001–2019. It first calculates the China’s transport carbon emissions by IPCC carbon emission factor method, and then applies the Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) model for decomposition analysis. The conclusion indicates that: (1) Diesel, gasoline, kerosene, and fuel oil are the major energy sources used in China’s transport sector, with the combined consumption of diesel and gasoline exceeding 70%, and the annual growth rate of energy consumption reached 8.92% during 2000–2019. Among them, natural gas and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) have the fastest growth rate, while the only one showing a downward trend is raw coal, indicating that China’s transportation energy structure is being optimized. (2) Although China’s transport carbon emissions have been increasing, the growth rate has declined since 2013. The proportion of carbon emissions from kerosene, diesel, natural gas, and LPG increased from 2000 to 2019, while that of raw coal, gasoline, and fuel oil decreased. This suggests that the use of clean energy, air transportation, and large-scale transportation is increasing, while the use of heavily polluting fuels and small-scale road transportation is decreasing. (3) Per capita GDP is the driving factor that has the most influence on the increase of China’s transport carbon emissions. Population positively influences transportation carbon emissions too, but issues such as aging, low fertility rates, and insufficient labor force may change the direction of the impact in the next 30 years. (4) The negative effect of energy intensity on transport carbon emissions is the greatest, followed by industrial structure and energy structure. The development of highways, new energy vehicles, railway electrification, multimodal transportation, third-party logistics, and logistics information technology in China has improved the energy structure, reduced energy intensity, and brought China’s transport sector into an important stage of innovation driven and pursuit of coordinated development with the environment.Keywords: Transportation sector, CO2 emissions, Decomposition analysis, LMDISubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Environmental economics, Environmental impact, Sustainability

Introduction

The transport sector accounts for one fourth of the global carbon emissions. It is one of the prime reasons of global climate change. For China, the transport sector accounts for about 10% of the country’s total carbon emissions and has been increasing quickly1. The issue of emission reduction is urgent. At present, low-carbon transport develops slowly in China, and has great potential in reducing carbon emissions. Therefore, analyzing the transport sector’s energy consumption characteristics, carbon emissions changes, and their driving factors is key for China to fulfill its commitments to the international community.

Due to industrialization, China’s transport carbon emissions are still increasing. The situation of controlling carbon dioxide emissions is severe. What factors have resulted in the growing of transport carbon emissions in China? This issue is worth further exploration. In the research area of environmental economics, decomposition analysis, as a theoretical framework for studying the changing characteristics of things, has been increasingly applied. Decomposing carbon emissions into multiple factors and quantitatively analyzing their impact on emissions changes has become an effective means for studying such issues. Index Decomposition Analysis (IDA) and Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) are two most widely used decomposition tools. Compared to the SDA method, which requires the use of input-output tables as data sources, the IDA method only needs the use of department aggregated data, making it appropriate for models with time series data and fewer factors. Generally speaking, the IDA method mainly includes two types: Laspeyres Index and Divisia Index. The Laspeyres Index Decomposition Analysis does not have zero value problem during the decomposition process, but the decomposition results have residuals. On the basis of the original Laspeyres Index Decomposition Analysis, Sun put forward an improved decomposition model, which can effectively solve the residual problem2. However, when the decomposition model has more than three influencing factors, the calculation process becomes very complex3. The Divisia Index decomposition method mainly includes the Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) and the Arithmetic Mean Divisia Index (AMDI), which using logarithmic mean and arithmetic mean as weights, respectively. AMDI contains residuals and is unable to solve the problem of zero values in the data. On the contrary, LMDI not only successfully solves the problem of residuals and zeros, but also satisfies other conditions of the “perfect decomposition method”, making it one of the most popular carbon emission decomposition methods in empirical analysis.

The research and development process of LMDI is detailed in the literature4. Vinuya et al. (2010) discussed the carbon emissions of various states in the U.S. during 1990–2004 by LMDI method. The conclusion reveals that improving energy efficiency, reducing carbon intensity, and optimizing energy structure can help offset the increase in carbon emissions caused by economic development and population growth5. Timilsina and Shrestha (2009) studied transport carbon emissions in 20 countries from Latin America and the Caribbean between 1980 and 2005 using the LMDI multiplication form. They believed that economic development and energy intensity were the two major driving factors for the increase of transport carbon emissions6. Zhang Ming et al. (2011) analyzed the China’s transport energy consumption between 1980 and 2006 using the LMDI method, and pointed out that 99.43% of the transport energy consumption increase was caused by transportation demand growth, while technological progress suppressed 6.08% of the increase7. William Chung et al. (2013) applied the LMDI decomposition method to analyze China’s transport energy consumption during 2003–2009, and the results reveals that diesel is the main factor leading to the increase in energy consumption8. Mraihi et al. (2013) investigated the factors affecting road traffic energy consumption in Tunisia from 1990 to 2006 by LMDI method, and discovered that fuel intensity, per capita GDP, vehicle intensity and urban factors were worth paying attention to9. Andreoni et al. (2012) delved into water and air transport carbon emissions in Europe by LMDI method, and concluded that economic growth is the principal one10. Kim (2019) analyzed greenhouse gas emissions from South Korea’s transport sector during 1990–2013, and pointed out that economic growth was the dominant factor in carbon emissions growth, followed by population11.

From the above research, it is found out that the driving factors of transport carbon emission are receiving global attention. However, research on transport carbon emissions in developing countries is not as mature as research on carbon emissions in other industries. Therefore, this article dedicated to conduct a decomposition analysis of China’s transport carbon emissions during 2001–2019 using LMDI method, with the aim of providing theoretical support for the Chinese government to develop emission reduction policies and build a green transport system. It is also a supplement to existing research on transport carbon emissions in developing countries represented by China. Compared to previous studies, the article selects economic output, carbon intensity, energy intensity, industrial structure, energy structure, and population size as driving factors according to the true state of China and literature analysis. The research results indicate that economic and population growth have a promoting effect on the increase of transport carbon emissions, while energy intensity, energy structure, and industrial structure have a negative impact on transport carbon emissions, with energy intensity having the greatest negative impact.

Literature review

Research on carbon emission calculation

At present, there are three main methods for calculating greenhouse gas emissions: actual calculation method based on microscale, mass balance method based on mesoscale, and carbon emission factor method. The actual calculation method mainly calculates carbon emissions from agriculture and forest ecosystems. The mass balance method is mainly applicable to specific enterprises or industries. The carbon emission factor method is internationally recognized as a method for calculating carbon emissions. It multiplies various energy consumption with carbon emission factors. Kurokawa et al. (2013)12, Jorgenson et al. (2017)13and Kone and Buke (2019)14respectively calculated the carbon emission levels of China, India and other Asian countries, 50 states of the United States, the District of Columbia and Turkey using the carbon emission factor method. Fang et al. (2015)15and Sun et al. (2019)16 calculated carbon emissions in China’s provinces and provincial capitals, respectively.

IPCC has proposed calculation methods based on the characteristics of the transport sector, including top-down and bottom-up approaches. The “top-down” method calculates transport carbon emissions by multiplying various energy consumption with corresponding carbon emission coefficients. Sanjuan Delmas et al. (2014) developed a top-down approach to calculate CO2 emissions from water transportation in Spain17. Salvatore Saija et al. (2002) used an improved top-down approach to calculate road transport carbon emissions in Italy18. Han et al. (2018) applied a top-down approach to calculate China’s transport carbon emissions and further explored its relationship with economic growth19.

The “bottom-up” method calculates total energy consumption based on driving mileage, vehicle possession, and energy consumption per unit mileage of various transportation modes, and then multiplies it by the carbon emission coefficient to get the carbon emissions. Palencia et al. (2017) used the bottom-up approach to calculate the carbon emissions of light buses in Japan20. Alam et al. (2017) adopted the method to calculate road transport carbon emissions in Ireland21. Tang et al. (2017) used the method to calculate the carbon emissions of tourism transportation in China22. Peng et al. (2018) found through the “bottom-up” approach that the total direct demand for oil and related greenhouse gas emissions from road transportation in China will reach 508 million tons and 1.5 billion tons respectively by 202323. Suryati et al. (2018) used this method to study the emission loads generated by motor vehicles and aircraft24.

There are also scholars who combined the two calculation methods to analyze and calibrate the relevant data. Zhang et al. (2019) analyzed the application of the “top-down” model and the “bottom-up” model in the calculation of road traffic carbon emissions and found that different factors have different impacts on road traffic25. Liu et al. (2019) also combined the two methods to calculate China’s transport carbon emissions26. At present, the “top-down” method is more commonly applied to calculate the carbon emissions in a certain region or industry, while the “bottom-up” method is more likely to be adopted to calculate the carbon emissions generated by a certain transportation mode. The transport carbon emission calculated in this article belong to the overall carbon emissions calculation within a certain region, and accurate results can be obtained through the calculation of energy end-use. Consequently, this article chose the “top-down” method for carbon emission calculation.

Research on the driving factors of transport carbon emissions

Decomposition analysis and regression analysis are two common approaches for researching the driving factors of carbon emissions. Mazzarino (2000) used a decomposition model to analyze Italy’s carbon emissions during 1980–1995, and found that GDP growth was primarily responsible for the carbon emissions growth27. Based on transport carbon emissions data from Asia countries selected during 1980–2005, Timilsina and Shrestha (2009) utilized the LMDI approach to find out that Per capita GDP, energy intensity and population were the primary factors effecting transport carbon emissions28. Houda Achour and Mounir Belloumi (2016) studied transport carbon emissions in Tunisia during 1985–2014 by LMDI approach and found that energy intensity played a crucial role in reducing transport carbon emissions, while improving transportation intensity has a significant effect on low-carbon transportation29,30. Solaymani (2019) investigated the driving factors of transport carbon emissions in 7 major carbon emitting countries by LMDI approach. It was found that during 2000-15 carbon intensity was the major reason for the carbon emissions decrease, while economic output effects and electricity structure were the main reason for the increase in carbon emissions31. Mine Isik and Kemal Sarica (2020) investigated the driving factors of Turkey’s transport CO2 emissions during 2000–2017 by LMDI approach and found that economic and population growth played a crucial role32. Wang et al. (2020) discussed the relationship between transport sector development and CO2 emissions in countries along “the Belt and Road” using the Tapio model and LMDI. The study suggested that energy intensity was crucial for reducing carbon emissions in developing countries, while transport intensity was the main contributor to carbon emissions in developed countries33.

With the development of econometrics, using econometric models for regression analysis has become one of the commonly used approaches to research the driving factors of carbon emissions. Shim et al. (2006) explored the linkage between transport energy consumption and urban morphology in South Korea, and proposed suggestions for decentralized urban development and new road construction34. Liddle and Lung (2010) found that among the 17 developed countries studied, the impact of population size in different age groups on transport carbon emissions varies35. Okada (2012) analyzed the relationship between the proportion of elderly population and road transport carbon emissions using the panel data from OECD countries. The result implies that the increase in elderly population was related to the reduction of road traffic carbon emissions when the proportion of elderly population exceeds 16%36. Saboori et al. (2014) analyzed the relationship among road transport energy consumption, CO2 emissions, and economic growth in OECD countries by FM-OLS model and found that the bi-directional relationship among the three was positive37. Alshehry and Belloumi (2016) found that the causal relationship between economic growth and transport carbon emissions is long-term in Saudi Arabia38. Lin and Omoju (2017) used eight Asian countries as research subjects to construct a STIRPAT model and found that the increase in income and population will promote transport carbon emissions, while technological progress, private sector investment, and more rail infrastructure will reduce transport carbon emissions39. Andres and Padilla (2018) established a STIRPAT model to explore the driving factors of GHG emissions such as population, transport volume and mode, economic activity, and energy intensity in the EU transport activity. The results indicated that population and energy intensity were the two major factors causing carbon emissions increase40. Liu and Sun (2016) explored the relationship among volume of traffic, GDP, and transport carbon emissions using Tapio coefficients based on the panel data from provinces in China during 1994–2012. It was found that there was a weak coupling relationship between traffic volume and economic growth41.

Commentary on the literature

Through the above research, it can be found that there is an increasing emphasis on the research of transport carbon emissions worldwide, and the decomposition methods and conclusions are becoming more diverse. However, compared to other high emission sectors such as industry and electricity, research on transport carbon emissions is not yet very rich, especially for developing countries such as China. Therefore, this article adopts the most commonly used carbon emission factor method to calculate China’s transport carbon emissions, and then utilize the LMDI approach to explore the driving factors of carbon emissions in the transport sector, as a supplement to existing research on transport carbon emissions of developing countries represented by China. Compared to previous research, the influencing factors selected in this article include carbon intensity, energy intensity, energy structure, industrial structure, economic output, and population, which are more in line with the situation of developing countries represented by China. In addition, in the discussion of empirical results, this article’s analysis is more in-depth.

Data collection

The data of transport energy consumption used in the research are derived from the provincial data list of the CEADs from 2000 to 2019. The data of resident population, regional gross domestic product (GDP), and EVA of transport sector all comes from the China Statistical Yearbook from 2000 to 2019. In addition, to eliminate the influence of price factors, the GDP and EVA of transport sector in each province are calculated at constant prices in 2000.

Results and analysis

China’s transport energy consumption

China’s transport energy consumption during 2000–2019 is listed in Table 3. The energy consumption of the whole country increased from 69.93 to 354.56 million tons of standard carbon in the studying period, with an annual growth rate of 8.92%, indicating a quickly growing in energy consumption. Since 2015, the consumption of raw coal has decreased steadily, indicating that China’s transport energy structure is being optimized.

Analysis of driving factors of transport carbon emissions in China

The LMDI method is applied to analyze the driving factors of transport carbon emissions in China, and the results are depicted in Table 6. It can be discovered that the total effect value from 2001 to 2019 is basically positive, implying that the overall effect of various factors on transport carbon emissions is positive. But the added value of transport carbon emissions is decreasing every year, especially after 2015, the decline has been rapid, as shown in Fig. 5. The added value of transport carbon emissions in 2019 is 1.97 million tons, a decrease of 94.02% compared to 2001. Per capita GDP is the factor that has the greatest impact on the increase of carbon emissions from transportation, causing an increase in carbon emissions from 12.96 million tons in 2001 to 42.17 million tons in 2019. Population also has a positive impact, causing an increase in carbon emissions from 1.22 million tons in 2001 to 2.43 million tons in 2019. Energy structure, energy intensity, and industrial structure have a negative influence on transport carbon emissions, with energy intensity and industrial structure having the greatest negative impact. Figure 6 shows a regional comparison of the effect of various factors on transport carbon emissions. The effect of energy intensity, energy structure, and industrial structure on transport carbon emissions in each region tends to converge in oscillations. The energy structure and energy intensity have maintained a negative influence on transport carbon emissions in all regions since 2014 and 2016, respectively. The influence of per capita GDP on transport carbon emissions is positive in all regions, with the effect in the east much greater than that in the central and west, and currently the annual effect value tends to stabilize. The population has a positive influence on transport carbon emissions in the east and west, with the effect value in the east showing a significant downward trend and the gap with the west gradually decreasing. The population in central China has had a negative influence on transport carbon emissions since 2012. From the overall effect, the annual transport carbon emissions in various regions are generally increasing, but the magnitude of the increase is gradually decreasing. From 2001 to 2019, the annual added value of transport carbon emissions in the central, east, and west decreased by 77.61%, 95.51%, and 98.31%, respectively.

The effect of energy structure on transport carbon emissions

According to the LMDI decomposition results, the effect of energy structure on transport carbon emissions has been negative since 2013, implying that the transport energy consumption structure has been optimized. Although diesel and gasoline are still the major energy sources for transport consumption, accounting for about 70%, the proportions of raw coal, gasoline, and fuel oil decreased by 8.81%, 7.72%, and 1.17% respectively from 2001 to 2019, while the proportion of natural gas increased by 5.25%. This indicates that clean energy is gradually replacing heavily polluting fuels. With the increase of environmental awareness and the decrease in dependence on fossil fuels, natural gas, as a clean energy source, is gradually becoming the new favorite in the field of transportation. Natural gas has multiple advantages such as environmental protection, economy, and safety. Compared with traditional fuels, vehicles using natural gas can reduce carbon dioxide emissions by more than 70%. This is not only beneficial for improving urban air quality, but also helps to reduce global greenhouse gas emissions. China’s natural gas vehicles have developed quickly over recent years, with a total ownership of 6.64 million vehicles by the end of 2022. Compressed natural gas (CNG) taxis have been widely used in western cities such as Chongqing and Xi’an, while Liquefied natural gas (LNG) heavy-duty trucks and LNG intercity buses are being promoted in provinces such as Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia. It is expected that LNG trucks will keep on growing in the future, with the possibility of reaching 1–1.2 million units by 2025. Ships using LNG instead of diesel on the Yangtze River and Grand Canal have also begun operating. The Ministry of Transport pointed out that LNG is a national priority for promoting clean energy and the application of LNG in the water transport is a good way to realize emission reduction and energy structure optimization.

Electricity is another clean energy used in China’s transport sector. Beijing, the capital city, took the lead in implementing an unrestricted policy for new energy vehicles. Besides, a series of measures such as high subsidies and exemption from purchase taxes were promoted, which led to a boom in the electric vehicle industry. In 2018, China declared the lifting of limitation on foreign investment in the manufacturing of new energy vehicles. After the new regulations were released, Tesla declared the opening of a super factory in Shanghai. The entry of foreign new energy vehicle enterprises into China has driven the development of the whole industry. According to statistics from TrendForce Consulting, worldwide sales of new energy vehicles in 2022 was about 10.65 million, with China and Western Europe being the two main markets, accounting for 63% and 29% of the market share, respectively. The sales of new energy vehicles in China in 2023 was 9.495 million, a year-on-year increase of 37.9%, and the market share reached 31.6%. Among them, pure electric vehicles are the focus of the new energy market. For the sake of advancing the substitution of clean energy in the transport sector and accelerating the construction of green freight transportation in many regions, supporting policies for producers and users have been introduced to promote the electrification of freight vehicles. In 2022, the sales rate of pure electric trucks in the top ten major cities has all reached over 10%, with Chengdu, Foshan, and Guangzhou approaching 30%, and Shenzhen reaching 50%. The Energy Foundation has released the “China Carbon Neutrality Comprehensive Report 2022: Electrification Special Report”, stating that it is expected that the direct electrification rate of the passenger transportation sector will reach 56–64% by 2060, and the average electrification rate of freight transportation will reach 22–39%. It can be inferred that the booming development of new energy vehicles will result in a further decline in the use of traditional fossil fuels.

The effect of energy intensity on transport carbon emissions

Energy intensity reflects the dependence of economic development on energy. As listed in Table 6, energy intensity is one of the major factors affecting transport carbon emissions in China, and has shown a significant negative effect since 2016. Figure 7 displays the changes in transport energy intensity during 2000–2019 in China. The energy intensity showed a significant upward trend from 2000 to 2005, remained stable from 2005 to 2012, and decreased year by year after 2012, forming an inverted U-shaped curve.

Changes in China’s energy intensity from 2000 to 2019.

Looking back at the 40 years development process of reform and opening up, China’s transport sector has roughly undergone three major transformations, reflecting the different requirements of socio-economic development stages for transportation. At the beginning of reform and opening up, China’s transportation capacity was seriously insufficient, and various factors could not flow smoothly. Transportation not only unleashes the vitality of its own, but also provides support for the other factors. “To be rich, build roads first” has become a true portrayal of this period. The second stage is the expansion of transportation investment. The continuous expansion of investment scale in transportation infrastructure has increasingly shown its driving effect on the economy, becoming an important stabilizer of economic growth. Since the mid to late 1990s, large-scale investment in transportation has been ongoing for about a decade. At this stage, transport energy intensity continues to rise. The third stage is the stage of improving transportation services. With the improvement of China’s production organization and people’s pursuit of a better life, transportation needs to transform towards better services. The precision requirements for transportation layout and project investment are increasing, and energy intensity is gradually decreasing.

Reducing energy intensity is the first choice for energy conservation policies in various countries. John Holdren, a science advisor to Obama and a physicist at Harvard University, believes that the cheapest and fastest way to save energy is to improve the energy efficiency of the construction, industrial, and transport sectors. For some developed countries, due to their relatively clean energy structure, low-carbon and energy-saving are closely related. However, for China, it is currently in a stage of joint promotion of urbanization and industrialization, and its energy demand has obvious rigid characteristics. While reducing energy intensity, it is also necessary to optimize the energy structure and improve the application of clean energy.

Road transport is the largest energy consuming sector in China. Against the backdrop of the country’s promotion of “transit to rail” and “transit to water”, as well as the development of multimodal transportation, the transportation structure of the freight industry is constantly being optimized. The proportion of road freight volume has shown a slow downward trend with fluctuations since 2013. In 2022, the proportion of road freight volume was 72.04%, a decrease of 3.02% compared to 2013. But road freight is still the category with the largest proportion of freight volume. Many factors can affect energy consumption of road transport, including road conditions, traffic conditions, vehicle characteristics, vehicle speed, and so on. The better the road conditions, the lower the energy consumption of vehicles. In 1988, the first expressway in China, Shanghai Jiaxing Expressway, was completed and opened to traffic, ending the history of no expressway in Chinese Mainland. By the end of 2021, the total length of expressways opened to traffic in China had been 169,100 km, ranking first in the world in terms of scale. The improvement of road conditions has reduced transportation energy consumption. The rapid development of third-party logistics has greatly increased the intensification of transportation. Digital freight logistics platforms have achieved intelligent matching of vehicle and cargo, improving vehicle loading rates and energy utilization efficiency. In the application of new energy, the railway electrification rate has increased to over 70%. New energy buses account for more than 66%. The Ministry of Transport promotes the use of shore power by ships in the Yangtze River Economic Belt and Bohai Bay. Overall, the decrease in energy intensity has promoted the reduction of China’s transport carbon emissions.

The effect of industrial structure on transport carbon emissions

According to data in Table 6, the effect of industrial structure upon transport carbon emissions has been basically negative since 2003. From Fig. 8, it can be observed that the proportion of transport sector in GDP has been decreasing from 6.73% in 2001 to 4.01% in 2020, indicating that China’s economic development is weakening its dependence on transport sector, and GDP growth rate is greater than that of the transport sector.

The proportion of transport sector in GDP from 2000 to 2020.

From 1993 to 2002, China’s transportation investment and financing platform, led by the toll road sector, gradually expanded to all areas of transportation infrastructure construction, forming a diversified investment platform that absorbs social capital. During this period, the focus was on accelerating transportation infrastructure construction and fully leveraging its role in promoting the economy. In 2003, for the sake of strengthening the environmental protection management of transportation construction projects, the Chinese Ministry of Transport issued the “Measures for Environmental Protection of Transportation Construction Projects”, which stipulated the procedures for environmental impact assessment of transportation construction projects. After 2003, the government continued to implement market mechanism reforms in various fields of transportation, and an open transportation market was basically established. The vitality of various market entities was effectively stimulated, which better met the freight and travel needs. After 2013, railways implemented the separation of government and enterprises, and industry management responsibilities were merged into the Ministry of Transport. The independent management of various transportation modes was completely changed, laying the foundation for top-level design of comprehensive transportation. In February 2019, “Opinions on Accelerating Development of Logistics to Form a Powerful Domestic Market” was issued, proposing measures such as constructing a high-quality logistics infrastructure network. Driven by the above policies, China’s transport sector has entered a new stage of innovation driven and quality-oriented development, with the pursuit of coordinated economic and environmental development becoming a more important goal.

The effect of economic output on transport carbon emissions

From the LMDI decomposition results in Fig. 5, it can be found that economic output is the most significant factor affecting transport carbon emissions, and it is the prime reason for the continuous increase of China’s transport carbon emissions. The increase in transport carbon emissions brought about by economic development in 2001 was 12.96 Mt, and in 2019 it was 42.17 Mt. During this period, the per capita GDP also increased from USD1, 112 to USD9, 866. However, during the continuous growth of per capita GDP, the increase in transport carbon emissions caused by economic development has remained stable since 2007. Analyzing the development history of economy and transportation, it can be discovered that the economic structure, industrial structure, and product structure directly affect the transportation system. With the changes in economic and industrial structures, labor is shifting from the primary industry to the secondary industry. In the process of industrialization and rapid expansion of market size, the demand for raw materials has sharply increased, resulting in an average growth rate of freight volume approaching or exceeding that of the national economy. With the further development of industrialization, the average value of transported goods has significantly increased, while the volume and weight have decreased. In this process, the growth rate of freight volume slows down and gradually lower than that of the national economy. Due to changes in product structure, higher requirements have been placed on transportation quality, such as speed, convenience, and safety. Road transport and air transport have begun to flourish, with significant changes in transportation scale and structure. These changes reflect the changes in economic structure, industrial structure, product structure, and productivity layout, as well as the advancement of the transportation system.

Economic transformation is a common phenomenon. Daniel Bell believes that the typical feature of a post-industrial society is the transition from an industrial economy to a service economy. At that time, the United States had already entered the post-industrial society, followed by Germany, France, Japan, and South Korea. After more than 30 years of economic growth, the proportion of China’s industrial added value in GDP began to decline after 2008. Meanwhile, the proportion of added value in the tertiary industry is increasing, indicating that the transformation from industrial economy to service economy has begun. Transportation has become an integral part of the logistics system and must meet the needs of flexible manufacturing system, zero inventory, and global production systems for special transportation, containerized transportation, and door-to-door international multimodal transportation. Air freight has maintained a growth trend while the total volume of transportation has decreased. In terms of passenger transportation, more reliance has been placed on public transportation such as civil aviation and high-speed railways, and the development of new energy vehicles has also resulted in a decrease in the growth rate of transport carbon emissions.

The effect of population on transport carbon emissions

The population has a positive effect on China’s transport carbon emissions, but the effect has remained relatively stable, as shown in Fig. 5. Since 2010, the growth in transport carbon emissions caused by population has been decreasing. In 2020, the total population of China reached 1.41 billion, and the growth rate has significantly slowed down compared to ten years ago. The weakening of population growth momentum is becoming an outstanding feature of China’s population development. On the basis of the world population forecast for 2019, the total population size of China will peak around 2030 and then start to decline. Analyzing the spatial characteristics of the population, from 2010 to 2020, the population growth momentum in the east decreased while that in the central and west increased, which will improve the uneven distribution of China’s population with more in the southeast and less in the northwest. In terms of urban and rural population composition, China’s urban population accounted for 63.89% in 2020, surpassing 56.11% of rural population for the first time. This marks a breakthrough in the transformation of China’s population from rural to urban areas. Urban populations usually have a higher demand for transportation than rural populations, so urbanization will bring about an increase in transport carbon emissions. As illustrated in Fig. 9, the growth rate of urbanization in China began to decline after 2011, which will cause a reduction in future transport carbon emissions increment. In the aspect of the composition of the working-age population, the percentage of the working-age population aged 15–59 in 2020 was 63.35%, a decrease of 6.79% from ten years ago. On the other hand, the percentage of elderly people aged 60 and above increased from 13.26% in 2010 to 18.70% in 2020, an increase of 5.44%, and the trend of population aging continues to intensify. The aging population may have lower demand for transportation, thereby reducing carbon emissions. China’s ability to maintain an annual economic growth rate of 9.6% for over 20 years is closely related to demographic dividends such as a high proportion of working-age population, lower total dependency ratio, and a large influx of rural population into cities. But currently, the promoting effect of these factors on the economy is weakening, causing the increase in transportation carbon emissions to gradually decrease. 2018 was supposed to be the peak year for newborns in China after the implementation of “the two-child” policy, but in many regions, the number of newborns has decreased year-on-year, resulting in a weakened impact of population growth on carbon emissions.

Discussion

Analysis of factors affecting transport carbon emissions in China

This article selects six factors, consisting of carbon intensity, energy structure, energy intensity, industrial structure, economic output, and population as the main influencing factors of transport carbon emissions through literature review and the theoretical analysis of China’s transportation development. An LMDI model is established for decomposition analysis. LMDI decomposition analysis is one of the primary methods for analyzing carbon emissions, and is used by scholars from various countries.

From the perspective of the impact of economic development on transport carbon emissions, a study conducted in Latin American and Caribbean countries from 1980 to 2005 found that economic growth and transportation energy intensity were the main factors affecting the increase in transport carbon emissions. In order to reduce transport carbon emissions, it is necessary to decouple the growth of carbon dioxide emissions from economic growth. Policy tools can be used to promote fuel conversion, mode conversion, and reductions in energy intensity in the transport sector (Timilsina, Shrestha, 2009)6. A study on the European water and air transportation industry from 2001 to 2008 found that economic growth has been the main factor contributing to the increase in carbon emissions from water and air transportation activities in the 27 EU countries (Andreoni, Galmarini, 2012)10. The research in Turkey from 2000 to 2017 shows that the carbon emissions of the transport sector are mainly driven by economic growth, followed by population and emission intensity (Mine Isik, Kemal Sarica, 2020)32. A study conducted in the U.S. from 1960 to 2008 found that economic growth and higher car ownership rates largely resulted in a more than threefold increase in transport carbon emissions (L Schipper, C Saenger, A Sudardshan, 2011)44. The above research is consistent with the conclusions of this article. Regardless of the stage of socio-economic development, economic growth has always been the major influencing factor for the transport carbon emissions increase. Currently, the energy consumption of China’s transport sector accounts for about 10% of the country’s final energy consumption, while the proportion in developed countries is usually between 20% and 40%. With economic development, the transport energy consumption in China is bound to increase and the growth of transport carbon emissions is inevitable.

Population growth also has a positive effect on transport carbon emissions, which is consist with a study in South Korea from 1990 to 2013 (Kim, 2019)11. China’s population growth has been declining, and first time of negative growth happened in 2022. In the next 30 years, issues such as aging and insufficient labor force will become prominent, which may have an inhibiting effect on the growth of transport carbon emissions.

Energy intensity is considered a prime factor in reducing carbon emissions in developing countries (Wang C, Wood J et al., 2020)33, and a study in Tunisia from 1985 to 2014 reveals that energy intensity plays a crucial role in reducing transport carbon emissions (Houda Achour, Mounir Belloumi, 2016)29. The research in this article also confirms this viewpoint that energy intensity is the factor with the greatest negative impact on China’s transport carbon emissions. The development of China’s graded highways, railway electrification, new energy vehicles, third-party logistics, multimodal transportation, and logistics information technology has reduced energy intensity and improved energy utilization efficiency.

Compared with foreign scholars, Chinese scholars started their study on the relationship between carbon emissions and industrial structure relatively late. Watanabe (2004) analyzed the effect of industrial structure adjustment on carbon emissions and found that the degree of impact is 20%45. Talukdar and Meisner’s (2001) empirical study on developing countries from 1987 to 1995 found that primary industry’s development reduced a country’s carbon emissions, while secondary industry’s development increases carbon emissions46. Under.

certain technological levels, different industrial structures have varying levels of carbon emissions. Therefore, this study incorporates the proportion of the transport sector in the national economy as an influencing factor into the decomposition analysis. The research results indicate that the growth rate of China’s transport sector is slower than the GDP, and it gradually shows a negative effect on the transport carbon emissions. With the improvement of marketization in the transport sector and emphasis on environmental protection, China’s transport sector has entered a new stage of innovation driven and quality-oriented development.

The continuous consumption of fossil fuels will increase carbon emissions and worsen the environment (Ahmed, 2006)47. The adjustment of energy structure is beneficial for reducing transport carbon emissions. Compared to gasoline powered vehicles, electric vehicles are generally considered a good substitute to alleviate environmental pollution pressure (Sovacool, 2019), which can reduce carbon emissions and address global warming48. Spangher et al. (2019) conducted a quantitative analysis of the influence of electric vehicle sales on greenhouse gas emissions49. Karmaker’s (2020) study found that the widespread use of charging stations utilizing biogas resources can reduce carbon emissions50. This research found that the development of electric vehicles, train electrification, and natural gas vehicles and ships in China has effectively improved the energy structure and reduced transport carbon emissions.

Limitations and future research

This study used LDMI model to analyze the factors affecting China’s transport carbon emissions. Four factors were selected from the original Kaya equation, including carbon intensity, energy intensity, economic output, and population. Another two factors, energy structure and industrial structure, were chosen through literature analysis and practical observations. Although the selection of influencing factors follows the principles of scientificity, systematicity, and importance as much as possible, some micro influencing factors are still inevitably overlooked due to data availability issues. In future research, the selection of micro influencing factors is worth further investigation.

Conclusion and suggestions

Conclusion

This article first uses a top-down approach to calculate China’s transport carbon emissions, and then analyzes its main affecting factors by the LMDI model. The paper selected a total of six main influencing factors, among which four factors, carbon intensity, economic output, energy intensity, and population, were derived from the original Kaya equation. The remaining two factors, energy structure and industrial structure, were selected through literature analysis and understanding of China’s actual development situation. The results indicate that:

The energy consumption of China’s transport sector has been growing rapidly, from 69.93 in 2000 to 354.56 in 2019 (in million tons of standard carbon), with an annual growth rate of 8.92%. Among them, LPG and natural gas have the fastest growth rates, and the only one showing a downward trend is raw coal, indicating that China’s transportation energy structure is being optimized.

The natural gas consumption in the central and west is higher than that in the east, which is mainly related to the fact that China’s natural gas is mostly produced in the central and west.

Suggestions

Transportation is a heavily polluting industry compared to other sectors. With the development of the transport sector, its carbon emissions continue to rise, with an annual growth rate of over 8% and accounting for about 10% of the country’s total emissions, becoming one of the fastest-growing areas for greenhouse gas emissions. Therfore, rising emissions need to be mitigated.

According to the research in this article, economic development is the most significant factor affecting the increase in carbon emissions in China’s transport sector and will not change in the short term. Transportation is an important material production sector in the national economy, which organically links social production, distribution, exchange, and consumption, and is a prerequisite for the normal conduct of socio-economic activities. Economic growth and the development of transport sector are interdependent. The development of transport sector can promote the growth of national economy, and the growth of national economy will also drive the development of transport sector. The transport sector will increase its energy consumption during its development, and relying solely on fossil fuels will inevitably lead to a rapid increase in carbon emissions. Therefore, it is necessary to adjust the energy structure and increase the use of clean energy, such as electricity, natural gas, solar energy, hydrogen energy, etc.
