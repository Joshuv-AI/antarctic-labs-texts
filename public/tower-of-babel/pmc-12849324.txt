Depression and poisoning in adolescents and young adults: a retrospective study
Authors: Junyi Wang, Xiaohua Xia, Hua Yuan, Jin Ma, Feng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent poisoning, particularly deliberate self-poisoning, represents a serious public health issue with significant implications for mental health.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 203 cases of poisoning in individuals aged 14–24 who presented to the emergency department of the Affiliated Kunshan Hospital of Jiangsu University between January 2017 and December 2023. The study aimed to identify key predictors of suicidal poisoning, with a specific focus on the relationship between documented depression history and suicide. Intent (suicidal vs. accidental [non-suicidal]) was classified based on clinician documentation and discharge coding; depression history was defined by a previously documented diagnosis in the electronic medical record (EMR).ResultsAmong the 203 cases, 85.2% were attributed to suicide attempts, with females comprising 61.1% of the sample. The majority of the patients resided in urban areas (97.0%), and 57.1% had a documented history of depression. Notably, all patients with a documented history of depression were in the suicidal group; in univariate ROC analysis, depression showed the highest discrimination for suicidal poisoning (AUC: 0.835). Other variables, such as age and gender, had substantially weaker predictive power.ConclusionsThese findings support targeted screening and prevention strategies (including means restriction and safe medication practices) for adolescents and young adults at risk, while emphasizing the need for cautious interpretation and prospective confirmation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Poisoning may be accidental, resulting from unintended exposure to toxic substances, or intentional, as an act of self-harm or a suicide attempt [1–6]. From a public-health perspective, poisoning is particularly actionable due to the potential for means-restriction strategies (e.g., safe storage and dispensing limits, and poison-control education). Among adolescents, the use of poisoning as a means of suicide is particularly concerning. Understanding the demographic characteristics and underlying causes of poisoning in adolescents is essential to developing targeted prevention and intervention strategies [7].

Depression represents a major public health concern among adolescents, contributing significantly to self-harm and suicide attempts globally according to WHO Global Health Estimates (2019), underscoring the importance of understanding method-specific risks in youth [8–16]. It is estimated that 26.17% of children and adolescents have depressive symptoms [17, 18]. Adolescent poisoning is a critical global health issue, posing serious risks to physical and mental well-being [19–24]. In China, meta-analytic evidence indicates a substantial burden of depressive symptoms among children and adolescents [25]. Studies have consistently demonstrated that depression is a strong predictor of suicide among adolescents [26, 27]. A study conducted in Italy revealed that 71.1% of adolescent poisoning patients had at least one comorbid psychiatric disorder [28]. Conceptually, depression may contribute to suicidal poisoning through emotional dysregulation, hopelessness, and impulsivity. However, less is known about the specific patterns and risk factors of suicidal poisoning in adolescents and whether prior depression independently predicts suicidal poisoning after taking into account sociodemographic factors. Several studies have identified demographic factors such as gender and age as potential predictors of suicide attempts in cases of poisoning [24, 29–31]. In China, hospital-based data indicate substantial poisoning burdens among youth [32]. However, the association of a documented history of depression with suicidal poisoning in hospital-based Chinese samples remains under-characterized [32]. Consistent with WHO definitions (adolescents: 10–19 years; youth: 15–24 years), our hospital-based cohort includes adolescents and young adults (ages 14–24), and we use this terminology accordingly. We included up to age 24 to reflect our ED’s youth/young-adult service catchment and the WHO youth category, capturing a transitional developmental period with distinct risk patterns and medication access.

This study aims to address this gap by conducting a retrospective analysis of poisoning cases in adolescents presenting to the emergency department of Affiliated Kunshan Hospital of Jiangsu University between January 2017 and December 2023. Specifically, this study evaluates the association between demographic variables (age, gender, residence) and documented depression history in relation to suicidal poisoning. Our objective is to characterize associations and discriminative performance rather than to make causal claims or establish independence of effects.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Kunshan Hospital of Jiangsu University (No. 2024-03-002-K01). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. This was a retrospective chart review following STROBE principles for observational studies. All patient data were de-identified prior to analysis, with access restricted to authorized investigators; data were exported from the EMR by hospital IT, stored on encrypted, access-controlled servers, and safeguarded with routine audit logs.

This retrospective observational study was conducted in the emergency department of Affiliated Kunshan Hospital of Jiangsu University, and included patients aged 14 to 24 years who presented with acute poisoning between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2023. Patients were included in the study if they were within the specified age range, presented with acute poisoning due to ingestion, inhalation, or dermal exposure to toxic substances, and had complete medical records documenting demographic details, cause of poisoning, substance involved, and clinical outcomes. Only cases with complete essential fields (demographics, cause, substance, and outcome) were included; no imputation was performed. We excluded patients with incomplete or missing data, particularly those lacking essential information on the cause or outcome of the poisoning incident.

We collected data from the hospital’s electronic medical records system, focusing on key variables including age, gender, place of residence (urban or rural), the substance involved (e.g., medications, alcohol, chemicals), cause of poisoning (suicidal or accidental [non-suicidal]), and depression history. Poisoning was defined as exposure (ingestion, inhalation, dermal) to either prescribed medications or non-medication toxic agents (e.g., pesticides, carbon monoxide, daily chemicals, rodenticides, mushrooms). History of depression was defined as a previously documented clinical diagnosis in the EMR by psychiatry or another treating clinician (e.g., recorded on the problem list, in prior notes, or as a discharge/ED diagnosis). We did not infer depression solely from the ingested drug (e.g., antidepressants). Psychiatry consultations, when available, were considered, but were not mandated by the study protocol. Intent (suicidal vs. accidental [non-suicidal]) was classified based on treating clinician documentation and discharge coding in the EMR; the study team did not independently re-adjudicate intent. In ambiguous cases, we retained the clinician-documented classification. At our institution, clinician documentation is supported by discharge coding consistent with ICD-10; where available, coding supported the intent classification. The primary outcome measure was the identification of whether the poisoning was a suicide attempt or an accidental event. Clinical outcomes, including full recovery, ongoing treatment, or death, were also recorded. The EMR medical history field reliably captured depression, cardiovascular disease, and cancer; other psychiatric comorbidities were not consistently recorded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Both median (range) and mean (SD) for age were reported to provide complementary measures of central tendency and dispersion. To assess the relationship between potential risk factors—gender, age, residence, and history of depression—and the likelihood of suicidal poisoning, t-tests were used for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. ROC analyses are univariate and descriptive; AUC values < 0.5 indicate inverse discrimination (e.g., younger age associated with suicidal poisoning). In addition, we performed a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to evaluate the predictive performance of the variables that were significant in univariate analyses. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to assess each variable’s ability to distinguish between suicidal and non-suicidal poisoning cases. An AUC value closer to 1 indicates stronger predictive performance, while an AUC of 0.5 suggests no discriminative power. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0, and a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 203 adolescent and young adult aged 14 to 24 years who presented with poisoning between January 2017 and December 2023 were included in the study. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 19 years (range: 14–24), with a mean age of 19.11 ± 3.17 years. The majority of the patients were female (n = 124, 61.1%), and the remaining 79 patients (38.9%) were male. Regarding medical history, 116 patients (57.1%) had a documented history of depression, while only a small number of patients had cardiovascular disease (n = 1, 0.5%) or cancer (n = 1, 0.5%). Notably, 85 patients (41.9%) did not have any significant medical history. The vast majority of patients resided in urban areas (n = 197, 97.0%), with only 6 patients (3.0%) coming from rural regions. Intentional self-poisoning with the intent of suicide was the predominant cause of poisoning, accounting for 173 cases (85.2%), while 30 cases (14.8%) were classified as accidental (non-suicidal) poisonings. The outcomes were overwhelmingly positive, with 202 patients (99.5%) surviving, and only 1 patient (0.5%) succumbing to poisoning. Because case-level narratives were variably documented and sensitive, we did not present a detailed description of the single death.

Regarding the substances involved, antidepressant medications were the most common agents, involved in 63 cases (31.0%). Combination poisoning with both antidepressants and sleeping pills occurred in 32 cases (15.8%), while antidepressants in combination with other substances were used in 5 cases (2.5%). Other substances involved included pesticides (n = 33, 16.3%), carbon monoxide (n = 17, 8.4%), sleeping pills (n = 14, 6.9%), alcohol (n = 10, 4.9%), and daily chemical products (n = 8, 3.9%). A smaller number of poisonings were related to anti-inflammatory drugs (n = 9, 4.4%), other drugs (n = 10, 4.9%), rat poison (n = 1, 0.5%), and poisonous mushrooms (n = 1, 0.5%). To avoid double counting, substance categories were defined as mutually exclusive single-agent or combination groups; ‘sleeping pill’ as a single agent is distinct from ‘antidepressants & sleeping pills’.

Comparison of suicidal and non-suicidal poisoning patients

Suicidal poisoning was significantly associated with younger age, with the mean age of patients in the suicide group being 18.80 ± 3.13 years compared to 20.93 ± 2.83 years in the accidental (non-suicidal) group (p = 0.001) (Table 2). Additionally, there was a significant gender difference. Among suicidal cases, 64.2% were female (111/173) and 35.8% were male (62/173). Within-sex proportions who presented with suicidal poisoning were 89.5% for females and 78.5% for males (p = 0.031).

Comparison of suicidal and non-suicidal patients

A striking finding was the association between depression and suicide. All poisoning patients with a history of depression (n = 116, 100%) had attempted suicide, while none of the patients in the accidental (non-suicidal) group had a history of depression (p < 0.001). Place of residence also showed a significant association, with the majority of suicidal poisonings occurring in urban residents (n = 171, 86.8%), while accidental (non-suicidal) poisonings were more frequent among rural residents (n = 4, 66.7%) (p < 0.001). Notably, given the small number of rural cases, these comparisons should be interpreted descriptively.

ROC curve analysis

ROC curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive value of age, gender, place of residence, and depression for identifying suicidal poisoning. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) values are presented in Table 3. Depression showed the highest discrimination for suicidal poisoning, with an AUC of 0.835 (95% CI: 0.780–0.890, p < 0.001), indicating high discriminative ability (Fig. 1). In contrast, age showed inverse discrimination, with an AUC of 0.307 (95% CI: 0.208–0.405, p = 0.001). Gender had an AUC of 0.604 (95% CI: 0.493–0.715, p = 0.069) and place of residence had an AUC of 0.439 (95% CI: 0.320–0.558, p = 0.287), indicating no significant predictive value. Notably, ROC analyses were univariate and descriptive; results should not be interpreted as causal.

Discussion

This retrospective study examined 203 cases of adolescent and young adult poisoning over a seven-year period, aiming to identify key factors associated with suicidal intent. Our findings revealed that the majority of poisonings in this age group were intentional suicide attempts, with depression showing the highest discriminative ability for suicidal poisoning in our data. This result underscores the association between adolescent depression and suicide.

This strong association is consistent with previous studies. Hawton et al. noted that adolescents with psychiatric disorders were significantly more likely to engage in self-harm or attempt suicide [10]. A cohort study from Victoria, Australia, involving 2,008 adolescents demonstrated a significant association between adolescent depression and anxiety symptoms and incidents of self-harm in early adulthood (HR: 5.9, 95% CI: 2.2–16) [33]. Although the causes of this phenomenon remain uncertain, it is speculated that young individuals who engage in self-harm may rely on this behavior to alleviate underlying distress [33]. Previous emotional regulation models of self-injury have confirmed that negative emotions often precede most self-harm incidents, while reductions and relief of negative emotions occur afterward [34]. Conceptually, depression may elevate risk via emotional dysregulation, hopelessness, and heightened impulsivity, which could make accessible methods such as poisoning more likely.

The role of impulsivity in adolescent suicidal behavior may also explain the high prevalence of poisoning as a method of suicide in this population [35–38]. Adolescents, particularly those with depression, often exhibit increased impulsivity. Poisoning, particularly with medications like antidepressants or readily available substances such as pesticides, may be seen as an accessible and less violent means of self-harm. In our cohort, many suicide attempts involved antidepressants, either alone or in combination with other substances, consistent with prior literature that emphasizes access to medications as a key driver [29]. However, this overlap also introduces potential confounding by access to means, which could partly inflate the observed association between depression and suicidal poisoning, even though depression was not inferred from the ingested drug in our data abstraction.

Our findings also show a significant gender difference, with female adolescents accounting for a larger proportion of suicide attempts. This aligns with existing literature [30, 31]. Possible explanations include differences in help-seeking, method selection, socialization, and the accessibility of medications; however, our dataset does not include the psychosocial covariates needed to evaluate these hypotheses. Thus, our results reflect the need for gender-sensitive approaches in both mental health treatment and suicide prevention strategies.

Importantly, approximately one-third (57/173; 32.9%) of those with suicidal poisoning had no documented depression, underscoring that other unmeasured factors (e.g., psychosocial stressors, trauma, family history, substance use, prior attempts) likely contribute. Moreover, because classification of intent relied on clinician documentation, there is potential for circularity: documented depression may have influenced judgments of suicidal intent. This is reflected statistically by complete separation (all depressed cases were suicidal), which precludes valid parameter estimates from standard logistic regression and cautions against strong causal interpretations [39].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, the generalizability of the findings to broader populations may be limited. The sample was overwhelmingly urban (97%), likely reflecting the hospital’s urban catchment and differential access to care; rural cases were few and may represent more severe presentations or different care pathways. Second, beyond age, sex, residence, and depression history, the dataset contained minimal clinical/social predictors (e.g., trauma history, substance use, family history, social stressors, prior attempts), limiting contextual interpretation. Third, classification bias is possible if prior depression influenced staff judgment of intent; intent was determined from clinician documentation and discharge coding, without independent re-adjudication. Fourth, overlap between depression (predictor) and access to antidepressants (a common means) may inflate the observed association despite our attempt to define depression independent of the ingested agent. Finally, other psychiatric comorbidities were not systematically available in the EMR abstraction and could not be analyzed, which limits clinical interpretation.

In conclusion, these findings emphasize the need for early identification and intervention in adolescent and young adult depression to prevent suicide attempts. Policy and clinical implications include integrating routine screening in acute care, means-restriction strategies (safe storage of medications, limiting dispensed quantities to at-risk youth), and poison-control education and counseling on access to means. Prospective, multi-center studies with standardized, blinded assessments of psychiatric history and intent, and inclusion of broader psychosocial variables, are needed to estimate adjusted associations robustly and to inform prevention.
