A Computer Vision Problem in Flatland
Sameer Agarwal, Erin Connelly, Annalisa Crannell, Timothy Duff, Rekha R. Thomas

Abstract
. When is it possible to project two sets of labeled points lying in a pair of projective planes to the same image on a projective line? We give a complete answer to this question and describe the loci of the projection centers that enable a common image. In particular, we find that there exists a solution to this problem if and only if these two sets are themselves images of a common pointset in projective space.



Imagine you are a robot equipped with a camera, exploring a new world. The images that your camera captures, depend on your position and orientation as well as the scene that you are looking at. Generally speaking, if you are looking at two different scenes with your camera in two different locations, then the images will also be different, but this begs the question, when is this not the case? i.e., when are the images of two different scenes from two different cameras the same? For simplicity we will assume that the world being explored is two dimensional (also known as a flatland), and the images are one dimensional. In this idealized setting we consider the following question:

Given two sets of
n
labeled points
\{x_{i}\}
and
\{y_{i}\}

in the plane, when is there a pair of flatland cameras, the first camera imaging
\{x_{i}\}
and the second camera imaging
\{y_{i}\}
, such that they produce the same image? If such a pair of cameras exists, then describe the set of all such camera pairs.

To answer this question, we first need to formalize it.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{k}
denote the
k
-dimensional real projective space.
A point in
\mathbb{P}^{k}
may be represented by a nonzero
(k+1)
-vector, and two such vectors
x_{1},x_{2}
represent the same point if there exists a nonzero scalar
\lambda
with
x_{1}=\lambda x_{2},
in which case we write
x_{1}\sim x_{2}.

Abusing notation slightly, we write
A
for any
(k+1)\times(l+1)
matrix representing a linear projection
1
1

1

The notation
\dashrightarrow
indicates that
A
is a
rational map
—it may be undefined at certain points in its domain (namely, the points in the right nullspace of
A
).

A:\mathbb{P}^{l}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{k}
.
When
\textup{rank}(A)=k+1=l,
the
center
of the projection
A
is the unique point
a\in\mathbb{P}^{l}
in the right nullspace of
A
(at which the projection is undefined). A
flatland camera
is a rank 2 linear projection from
\mathbb{P}^{2}
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. If the camera pair
(A,B)
projects
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
to the same image in

We can now state our questions precisely.

Question

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}

\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}

\mathbb{P}^{2}

I.

When are there linear projections

\forall i

Our first main result (

Surprisingly, the answer is that

\mathbb{P}^{2}
that have the same image in
\mathbb{P}^{1}

While the answer to Question I only requires elementary projective geometry and linear algebra, answering Question II calls upon tools from algebraic geometry and invariant theory. The answers themselves come in the
form of explicit algebraic equations and synthetic geometric constructions that describe the loci of centers.

Remark 1
.

It is worthwhile to clarify the nature of constructions involving
plane algebraic curves—specifically, conics and cubics—as they are key players in our story.
It is a consequence of elementary Galois theory that plane conics other than circles cannot be constructed using only a straightedge and compass.
On the other hand, if we are given five generic points on a conic, then we may construct as many additional points on the conic as desired using the classical
Braikenridge-Maclaurin Theorem
(a converse to Pascal’s Theorem, see
[
5
, Thm. 9.22]
.)
Similarly, if we are given a rational point on a rational conic, we may generate infinitely many more rational points using stereographic projection; conics with this property appear in our analysis of
n=7
points.
For the cubics
C_{x},C_{y}
appearing in
Theorem

1
, it is known from algebraic geometry that no rational parametrization exists.
Nevertheless, it is possible to test whether or not ten points lie on a common cubic with a straightedge and compass—see
[
17
]
for this recent result and many interesting related questions.

Answer to Question I

We first motivate a more careful formulation of Question I.
If we take any scalar
\lambda

\mathbf{a}

\mathbf{b}

\lambda\end{bmatrix}.

So, in a trivial sense, the answer to Question I is always “yes.”
Thus, going forward we exclude rank-one projections.
For any

we define a

Lemma 3
.

Fix
l\geq 2,
let
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{l}
be sets of labeled points, and set

A=\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l},\,B=\left[I\,|\,0\right]_{l},\quad A^{\prime}=\left[I\,|\,0\right]_{l+1},\,B^{\prime}=\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l+1}.

We have
\forall i,\,Ax_{i}\sim By_{i}
if and only if there exists a set of points
\mathcal{Z}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{l+1}
, none lying on the line connecting the centers
a^{\prime}=[0:0:\cdots:1],

b^{\prime}=[1:\cdots:0:0]\in\mathbb{P}^{l+1},
such that

\forall i,\,x_{i}\sim A^{\prime}z_{i}\text{ and }y_{i}\sim B^{\prime}z_{i}.

Proof.

To simplify notation, fix a pair of points with the same label and their representatives in homogeneous coordinates,
x=[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{l}]\in\mathcal{X}
and
y=[y_{0}:\cdots:y_{l}]\in\mathcal{Y}
.
We first prove the forward direction. Suppose there exists a scalar
\lambda
such that
\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l}x=\lambda\left[I\,|\,0\right]_{l}y\neq 0.

Consider the point

z=[x_{0}:x_{1}:\cdots:x_{l}:\lambda y_{l}]=[x_{0}:\lambda y_{0}:\cdots:\lambda y_{l-1}:\lambda y_{l}]\in\mathbb{P}^{l+1}.

Points on the line spanned by
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
are of the form
[\mu_{1}:0:\cdots:0:\mu_{l+1}]
.Consequently,
\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l}x=[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{l}]\neq 0
implies that
z
does not lie on the line spanned by
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
. Moreover,

\left[I\,|\,0\right]_{l+1}z=x,\quad\text{ and }\quad\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l+1}z=\lambda y\sim y.

Thus, if
A=\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l}
and
B=\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l}
project
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
to the same image, then
z
,
A^{\prime}=\left[I\,|\,0\right]_{l+1}
and
B^{\prime}=\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l+1}
provide the necessary reconstruction.
For the converse, note that if
z\in\mathbb{P}^{l+1}
satisfies both

\left[I\,|\,0\right]_{l+1}z\sim x
and
\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l+1}z\sim y,

then
\left[0\,|\,I\right]_{l}x\sim\left[I\,|\,0\right]_{l}y\sim[z_{1}:\cdots:z_{l}]\in\mathbb{P}^{l-1}
.
∎

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
be two sets of labeled points in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. There exist flatland cameras
A:\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashedrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
B:\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashedrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
with centers
a
and
b
such that
\forall i,\ Ax_{i}\sim By_{i}
if and only if there exists a
3\times 3
matrix
F
of rank 2 such that

Fa=F^{T}b=0,\,\,\text{and }\,\,\forall i,\,\,y_{i}^{T}Fx_{i}=0,\quad Fx_{i}\neq 0,\quad F^{T}y_{i}\neq 0.

Proof.

We first establish the forward direction.
Starting from flatland cameras

A=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{a}_{2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

we define
F=\mathbf{b}_{2}\mathbf{a}_{1}^{T}-\mathbf{b}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{2}^{T}.

Clearly
Fa=F^{T}b=0.

Moreover, for any
i,

y_{i}^{T}Fx_{i}=\det\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1}^{T}x_{i}&\mathbf{b}_{1}^{T}y_{i}\\
\mathbf{a}_{2}^{T}x_{i}&\mathbf{b}_{2}^{T}y_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\det\begin{bmatrix}Ax_{i}&By_{i}\end{bmatrix}=0,

since
Ax_{i}\sim By_{i}.

If
F
were not rank
2,
this would imply that
\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\top}
was a multiple of
\mathbf{a}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\top}
, or vice-versa; supposing for instance that
\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\top}=\lambda\mathbf{a}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\top}
for some scalar
\lambda,
we could write
\mathbf{a}_{1}=\lambda(b_{1i}/b_{2i})\mathbf{a}_{2}
for some

contradicting the fact that
A
is full rank.
Since
F
is rank 2, the matrices
A
and
F
have the same nullspace, so

implies
Fx_{i}\neq 0
; similarly,
\textup{null}(B)=\textup{null}(F^{T})
gives
F^{T}y_{i}\neq 0.

For the reverse direction, note first that the rank-
2

F
can be factorized as

(1)

\displaystyle F=UV^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&u_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}^{\top}\\
v_{2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

where

and

are

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}-v_{2}^{\top}\\
v_{1}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }B=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\\
u_{2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

we may readily verify
\textup{null}(A)=\textup{null}(F),

\textup{null}(B)=\textup{null}(F^{T}),
and that
\forall i\,\,Ax_{i}\sim By_{i}.

Once one camera center is fixed, the other is uniquely determined. Thus, the camera centers variety
\mathcal{C}_{7}
is a finite set of three points,
d_{(0,0)}(\mathcal{C}_{7})=3,
and there are at most
3
choices for the pair
(a,b).

As noted in
[
4
, Remark 3]
, the
2\times 2
minors of the matrix
M(a)
define a quadratic Cremona transformation
\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
sending
x_{i}\to y_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,7.

The three base points of this transformation are the three possible camera centers in
\pi_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{7})=\cap\omega_{a,i}.

Moreover, each conic
\omega_{a,i}
is the pre-image of the line
\langle e_{j},e_{k}\rangle\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{y}
, where
j,k\neq i
, under this transformation.

When the data
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are defined by sufficiently generic
rational numbers
, the three distinguished points
f_{1i}\times f_{2i}
,
i=1,\ldots 3
that arise in the proof of
Theorem

36
are also rational.
This in turn implies that the conics
\omega_{a,i}
and
\omega_{b,i}
appearing in
Theorem

36
contain infinitely many rational points.
This can be proved by
stereographic projection
: if
\ell
is any rational line passing through the distinguished point that is not tangent to the conic, then its points of intersection with the conic are determined by the roots of a quadratic polynomial: since one of the roots corresponding to the distinguished point is rational, it follows that the other root (and hence the other point of intersection) must also be.
Thus, we may construct infinitely many rational points on the conics
\omega_{a,i},\omega_{b,i}.
