Mild-Sunlight-Activated Safe Photodynamic Therapy Using On-Off Polymer Photosensitizers in Wearable Microneedle Patch
Authors: Yufu Tang, Chunxu He, Zhen Li, Quli Fan, Bin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is promising but limited by its dependence on specialized photosources, clinic-based administration, and prolonged post-treatment light avoidance due to phototoxicity side effects. Here, we report mild-sunlight­activated PDT (SunPDT) microneedle patches incorporating polymeric photosensitizers with an intrinsic “on-off” reactive oxygen species (ROS)-generating mechanism, enabling bio-safe, deep-tissue, and self-administered PDT without fixed clinic visits and strictly prolonged light avoidance post-treatment. Rationally designed photosensitizers generate robust ROS under low-intensity mild sunlight (12 mW cm-2) excitation, and feature an intrinsic “on-off” ROS generation mechanism that confines ROS generation to lesions, even when photosensitizers diffuse into surrounding healthy tissues. Microneedle-enabled deep photosensitizer delivery and excitation by the near-infrared component of sunlight together allow self-administered treatment of deep-seated lesions without scheduled clinic visits. Importantly, the SunPDT patch’s triple-safety design, low-intensity mild sunlight excitation, local delivery of photosensitizer by microneedles, and an intrinsic “on-off” ROS generation mechanism, eliminate post-treatment prolonged light avoidance, ensuring bio-safe PDT. Using female psoriatic mice as a proof-of-concept model, our “on-off” SunPDT microneedle patches achieve high therapeutic efficacy and patient-friendly, self-administered treatment, and outperform clinical Protoporphyrin IX patches, which demonstrate weaker effects and require prolonged light shielding. Therefore, this study establishes a promising design for a lifestyle-integrated PDT platform.

Introduction

Traditional photodynamic therapy (PDT), which uses photosensitizers (PSs), light, and oxygen (O₂) to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS)1–9, is a valuable clinical treatment for a variety of lesions like infection, cancer, and psoriasis10–17. However, PDT translation is limited by two major challenges: First, the requirement for specialized light sources and trained medical personnel limits the assessibility of PDT (Fig. 1a)18–21, particularly in resource-limited regions. Meanwhile, concentrated light sources may cause localized pain or other discomfort, seriously impacting patient experience. Second, biosafety concerns substantially restrict the broader application of PDT22,23. The limited penetration of topically applied PSs across the stratum corneum necessitates systemic administration for deep lesions, raising concerns regarding long-term toxicity22–26. Moreover, residual PSs can generate excessive ROS upon light exposure, requiring prolonged post-treatment light avoidance. Although “off-on” PSs reduce initial phototoxicity, activation converts them into effectively “always-on” PSs for sustained ROS generation, posing risks upon redistribution to normal tissues1. Therefore, a new PDT paradigm is needed to eliminate reliance on specialized light sources, skilled personnel, intravenous PS delivery and prolonged post-treatment light avoidance, enabling treatment in a comfortable, relaxed environment.Fig. 1Design and mechanism of wearable “on-off” SunPDT microneedle patch.Schematic illustration of a traditional PDT with scheduled visits at fixed clinics and post-therapy light avoidance and b the proposed wearable “on-off” sunlight-driven photodynamic therapy (SunPDT) microneedle patch in daily use without post-therapy light avoidance. c The working mechanism of the SunPDT microneedle patch is composed of diblock copolymer (PNT) PSs. d Rationally designed PNT PSs generate robust ROS under low-intensity mild sunlight and feature an intrinsic “on–off” mechanism that confines ROS generation to lesions, preventing generation even when photosensitizers are diffused into healthy tissue. Sunlight, an abundant and sustainable light source, is composed of 4% UV (280-400 nm), 43% visible light (400-700 nm), and 53% near-infrared (NIR) light (700-2500 nm)27. The high NIR light ratio enhances deep-seated lesion treatment and minimizes side effects, offering a promising solution to the limitation of specialized light source dependence of traditional PDT (Fig. 1b)9. In this regard, sunlight-driven PDT (SunPDT) shows great potential to illuminate large areas, making it particularly effective for treating extensive or multiple lesions simultaneously, reducing treatment sessions. Outdoor treatments in natural settings can create a more relaxed and pleasant experience for patients compared to clinical environments of traditional PDT11,13. Nevertheless, direct sunlight intensity reaches approximately 100 mW cm⁻² at noon on a clear day, it diminishes to 10-30 mW cm⁻² under cloudy days, or on sunny mornings and evenings and shade28. To avoid UV skin damage from direct sunlight, mild sunlight (10-30 mW cm⁻²) represents a more practical and effective approach to performing SunPDT. However, most existing NIR PSs require high-power-density laser ( > 100 mW cm-2) excitation9, and for divergent sunlight, an even higher power density may be needed. More importantly, almost all the existing PSs show a narrow absorption spectrum, which is poorly matched to the NIR part of the sunlight spectrum, making it impossible to harness mild sunlight for PDT of deep-seat lesions8. Therefore, PSs with broad NIR absorption and high ROS yield to ensure effective activation even under mild sunlight (10-30 mW cm⁻²) are highly desirable yet remain unexplored.

To address the second challenge of traditional PDT, microneedle patches penetrate the stratum corneum, enabling painless, localized PS delivery into the dermis or deep lesion sites24,29–37. This approach avoids the systemic side effects associated with intravenous administration of large doses of PSs. In addition, the SunPDT microneedle patch enables simple, self-administered PDT in non-clinical settings, which eliminates the need for skilled medical personnel, complex equipment and frequent clinic visits, broadening the practical applications of PDT. Unfortunately, the main challenge in developing practical SunPDT microneedle patches is that locally high-concentration PSs delivered via microneedle patches could diffuse into normal tissue, causing severe phototoxicity1,22,23. Traditional PDT requires strict avoidance of bright sunlight for weeks post-treatment to prevent phototoxicity, which significantly limits patient compliance11,33. An effective strategy to minimize phototoxicity is to develop “on-off” PSs in wearable microneedle patches that stay active (ROS “on” state) in diseased tissue during treatment but quickly degrade (ROS “off” state) after fulfilling their therapeutic role and migrating to normal tissue, a goal yet to be achieved.

Psoriasis, a chronic immune-mediated skin disease affecting all age groups globally, was chosen as the model due to its significant individual and societal burden, including associated psychosocial comorbidities like anxiety and depression38–40. Psoriasis is characterized by widespread erythematous, scaly plaques, and itching. PDT-generated ROS could destroy hyperproliferative keratinocytes, modulate abnormal immune responses, and inhibit neovascularization in lesions, thereby providing a promising avenue for psoriasis therapy39–41.

In this work, we report a mild-sunlight­activated PDT (SunPDT) microneedle patch incorporating polymeric photosensitizers with an intrinsic “on–off” ROS-generating mechanism, enabling bio-safe, deep-tissue, and self-administered therapy without fixed clinic visits or post-treatment restrictions (Fig. 1c). SunPDT microneedle patch consists of hyaluronic acid and a biodegradable diblock copolymer (PNT) PS. PNT PSs, tailored through rational PS skeleton design and precise side-chain engineering, generate 11.6 times higher ROS than clinically approved Protoporphyrin IX (PpIX) PS, under the same mild sunlight (12 mW cm⁻²). Unlike PpIX, whose visible-light absorption allows only shallow tissue penetration, PNT’s broad NIR absorption (650–1100 nm) aligns with sunlight’s NIR part, which enables deep tissue penetration, allowing ROS generation to be detected even 5 mm deep in tissue. The combination of microneedle-mediated deep tissue PS delivery and excitation by the near-infrared component of sunlight together allows self-administered treatment of deep-seated lesions without scheduled clinic visits. Unlike the previous PDT patch, where PSs diffuse into normal tissue, causing serious phototoxicity, our “on-off” PNT PSs in wearable microneedle patches stay active (ROS “on” state) in diseased tissue during treatment but quickly deactivate (ROS “off” state) through biodegradation after fulfilling their therapeutic role without obvious biodistribution in normal tissue, ensuring no phototoxicity in healthy tissues. Therefore, SunPDT patch’s triple-safety design, low-intensity mild sunlight excitation, microneedle-delivered low-dose photosensitizers, and an intrinsic “on-off” ROS generation mechanism, eliminate post-treatment prolonged light avoidance, ensuring bio-safe PDT. As a proof-of-concept using psoriasis as a disease model, our “on-off” SunPDT microneedle patches achieved high-efficiency and self-administered treatment without requiring post-treatment light avoidance, whereas clinical Protoporphyrin IX patches showed only weak efficacy and required prolonged light avoidance. Therefore, this study establishes a promising design guideline for practical and biosafe PDT, bringing it from a clinic-dependent procedure to relaxed daily life use, enhancing accessibility, affordability, and clinical potential.

Results

Design, preparation and characterization of PSs

To prepare SunPDT microneedle patches, the mild sunlight-driven PSs were first designed. PSs with efficient ROS generation are crucial. We initially selected monomer 5,5’-bis(trimethylstannyl)−2,2’-bithiophene (TT) as an electron donor, and (N, N’-bis(7-glycol)−2,6-dibromonaphthalene-1,4,5,8-bis(dicarboximide) (NDIgly) (80%) as the electron acceptor via Stille coupling to construct the polymer PS backbone due to their high ROS generation efficiency9. However, this type of PSs is not suitable for mild sunlight-driven PDT because its absorption in the NIR region of the sunlight spectrum is relatively weak, and it lacks the capability for “on-off” controllable ROS generation. To broaden the absorption of the PS backbone, 1,4-dibromo-2,5-bis((6-bromohexyl)oxy)benzene (PBM) (20%) as an electron donor is doped into the PS backbone, followed by trimethylamine modification to form diblock copolymer PNT PSs. In this design, the incorporation of PBM yields TT-PBM as the electron donor and TT-NDIgly as the electron acceptor, thereby promoting the intramolecular charge transfer (CT) effect driven by donor–acceptor interactions, broadening the polymer’s absorption into the longer wavelengths in the NIR region. More importantly, the glycol side chains on NDIgly and the quaternized PBM segments enhance the water dispersity of the polymer PNT in water, which enhances the accessibility of disease-associated ROS and enzymes to the PS backbone, promoting the PS biodegradation42. Wearable microneedle patches incorporating rapidly biodegradable PNT PSs that remain active (ROS “on” state) in diseased tissues but rapidly degrade and switch ROS off (ROS “off” state) after completing their therapeutic mission enable precise control over ROS generation with an “on-off” switching capability. The PNT polymers were fully characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance to show the right structure with high purity (Supplementary Fig. S1-8). Gel permeation chromatography analysis revealed that the number-average molecular weight (Mn)/polydispersity index (PDI) of PNT was 10.9 kDa/1.36. Due to its amphiphilic nature, PNT forms nanoparticles in aqueous media. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images show that PNT PS nanoparticles are spherical in water (Fig. 2a), and dynamic light scattering (DLS) shows that PNT PS nanoparticles have an average diameter of 24.3 ± 1.8 nm (Fig. 2b). As shown in Fig. 2c, the absorption spectrum of PNT PSs spans visible to NIR range with prominent peaks at 412 and 715 nm and an extended absorption edge reaching 1100 nm, which matches well with the sunlight spectrum, especially in the NIR window. Furthermore, the fluorescence spectrum of PNT PSs shows a clear emission peak at 595 nm with extending fluorescence up to 800 nm under 488 nm excitation (Supplementary Fig. S9). To study the ability to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), PNT PSs were irradiated by mild sunlight (12 mW cm−2) emitted from a sunlight simulator, using a clinically approved PS, PpIX as the control43. PpIX is one of the main forms of PS produced after the metabolism of 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) in the body. Its absorption spectrum is from 300 to 700 nm with a peak at 350 nm (Fig. 2c). The generation of ¹O₂, •O₂⁻ and •OH was assessed using 9,10-anthracenediyl-bis(methylene) dimalonic acid (ABDA), dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR 123) and 4-hydroxyphenyl-fluorescein (HPF), respectively, under mild sunlight irradiation (12 mW cm⁻², 3 min). ABDA undergoes a reduction in absorption upon reacting with ¹O₂. DHR 123 reacts with •O₂⁻ to emit green fluorescence peaking at 525 nm, while HPF interacts with •OH to produce fluorescein with an emission maximum at 515 nm under 490 nm excitation. As shown in Fig. 2e and S10, the absorption spectrum of the ABDA solution remained unchanged under only mild sunlight, PNT PSs or PpIX. By contrast, ABDA absorption is reduced for PNT PSs or PpIX under mild sunlight. As depicted in Fig. 2f, g and S11-12, the fluorescence of DHR 123 and HPF for PNT PSs shows a more obvious increase than that of PpIX. As shown in Fig. 2h, PNT PSs exhibited superior ROS generation with remarkable enhancements of 11.6-fold overall, 2.21-fold for ¹O₂, 6.42-fold for •O₂-, and 2.97-fold for •OH, than that of PpIX, demonstrating their much higher efficiency under mild sunlight (12 mW cm⁻²). In addition, comparative assays against the commonly used hematoporphyrin (HPIX) PSs in skin diseases (Supplementary Fig. S13) also showed that PNT PSs achieved an overall 10.43-fold higher ROS production, including 3.13-fold for ¹O₂, 4.85-fold for •O₂-, and 2.45-fold for •OH, underscoring their superior ROS generation efficiency under 12 mW cm⁻² sunlight irradiation. Furthermore, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was employed to investigate ROS under simulated sunlight using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) to trap •O₂- and •OH and 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy (TEMPO) to capture ¹O₂ (Fig. 2i)44–47. The ESR spectra showed no signals for PNT PSs or sunlight, however, characteristic signals for •O2-, •OH, and ¹O₂ were observed from PNT PSs with sunlight, aligning well with the preceding findings in Fig. 2e–g. As shown in Fig. 2j, the PNT PSs showed strong ROS generation ability even under 5 mm chicken breast tissue. By contrast, under the same condition, hardly any ROS generation was observed for PpIX.

Preparation and characterization of SunPDT microneedle patches

Next, we explored the potential of using PNT PSs with microneedle patches to develop SunPDT microneedle patches. To achieve biodegradability, hyaluronic acid (HA) was used as a dopant to fix the microneedle geometry. Initially, a solution of PNT PSs was homogeneously mixed with a 30% HA solution (Fig. 3a), which was then injected into a microneedle mold under vacuum conditions. Then, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was used to form a backing layer. After a drying process, SunPDT microneedle patches embedded with the functional material (PNT PSs and HA) were successfully fabricated using a demolding technique (Fig. 3a). Stereomicroscopic images of the SunPDT microneedle patch revealed an array of needles arranged in a 15×15 grid with uniform and well-ordered conical tips (Fig. 3b and c). Fluorescence microscopy further confirmed that PNT PSs, with an emission maximum of 595 nm under 488 nm excitation, were evenly encapsulated within the SunPDT microneedle patch and distributed uniformly across the supporting base (Fig. 3d). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that each needle of the SunPDT microneedle patch exhibited uniform distribution morphology and conical tips with a base length of 360 μm and a height of 800 μm (Fig. 3e). The mechanical strength of the SunPDT microneedle patch was tested using an electronic universal material testing machine, showing a compression force of 0.8 N per needle at 0.2 mm sensor movement (Fig. 3f). To assess the PNT PS release properties of the SunPDT microneedle patch, the patch was inserted into a Strat-M® skin penetration bionic membrane and immersed in a PBS solution. Fluorescence intensity was monitored at various time points. The results demonstrated that over 50% of the PNT PSs encapsulated in the SunPDT microneedle patch were released within the first 24 hours, while the remaining PNT PSs continued to be gradually released over the following 48 hours (Fig. 3g), highlighting the capability of SunPDT microneedle patch for sustained PS delivery.

Next, the transdermal PS delivery potential of the SunPDT microneedle patch was tested through an in vitro skin penetration assay. The SunPDT microneedle patches were similarly inserted into the Strat-M® skin penetration bionic membrane and immersed in PBS solution. Then, the PBS was added Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG, 1O2 indicator), HPF (·OH indicator) and DHR123 (O2- indicator), respectively, and exposed to the sunlight simulator for 15min. The PBS solution was then sampled, placed in the letter mold of “SunPDT” and photographed under 488nm excitation to collect images (Fig. 3h). The typical green fluorescence observed from SOSG (1O2 indicator), HPF (·OH indicator) and DHR123 (O2- indicator) indicates that PNT PSs released from the SunPDT microneedle patch produce high levels of 1O2, ·OH and O2-·, contributing to the high-efficiency SunPDT. Upon insertion of the SunPDT microneedle patch into fresh porcine skin (Fig. 3i), visible micropores were formed in the skin, confirming that the patches could penetrate the skin. Histological analysis of porcine skin sections showed that the needle tips remained within the tissue with a penetration depth of approximately 425 μm (Fig. 3j). Such depth can easily penetrate the stratum corneum and bypass the physiological barrier of skin cuticles to reach the upper dermis, making it possible to deliver PSs to deeper layers of the skin. This is highly advantageous for the PDT of deep-seated tissue lesions. Notably, within just 2min of skin insertion, the SunPDT microneedle patch exhibits a complete loss of its tips, accompanied by a substantial decrease in needle height and rounding (Fig. 3k), suggesting the rapid dissolution of the SunPDT microneedle patch to release the PNT PSs. These results demonstrate that the SunPDT microneedle patch exhibits simplicity, efficiency, and rapid PNT PS release, showing great promise as a transdermal PS delivery system.

SunPDT microneedle patches for psoriasis therapy

Psoriasis is a chronic dermatological condition characterized by the abnormal proliferation and differentiation of keratinocytes. In individuals suffering from psoriasis, the rate of skin cell proliferation is markedly accelerated, leading to an accumulation of cells in the stratum corneum and the formation of scaly lesions. Normal Human Epidermal Keratinocytes (NHEK) and the immortalized human keratinocyte line (HaCaT) are commonly used models for psoriasis research. This study investigates the PDT efficacy of PNT PSs under irradiation of mild sunlight with a power density of 12 mW cm-2 in targeting hyperproliferative keratinocytes. An antiproliferative assay was first conducted on NHEK and HaCaT cells co-incubated with PNT PSs. Meanwhile, PpIX is conducted on NHEK and HaCaT cells as a control. Therefore, given that clinical applications of PpIX rely on cellular self-synthesis from externally supplied 5-ALA, we adopted the same approach in this study. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S14, PNT PSs did not significantly affect cellular viability (IC50 > 40 μM in NHEK cell, IC50 > 45 μM in Hacat cell), indicating good biocompatibility and biosafety. The impact of mild sunlight exposure alone was found to be negligible. However, after exposure to mild sunlight irradiation, cells co-incubated with PNT PSs exhibited a pronounced cytotoxic effect, with a significant reduction in IC50 to 2.65 ± 0.14 μM for NHEK cells and 3.31 ± 0.21 μM for Hacat cells, respectively. In contrast, the clinically used PS 5-ALA showed IC50 values of 31.62 ± 0.68 μM for NHEK cells and 33.11 ± 0.72 μM for Hacat cells and failed to achieve effective therapeutic outcomes under mild sunlight irradiation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S15, brighter red fluorescence of PI was observed from NHEK and HaCaT cells treated with mild sunlight and PNT PSs as compared to the cells treated with mild sunlight or 5-ALA, suggesting the stronger killing effect of PNT PSs on cells. Flow cytometry was subsequently employed to quantify apoptosis (Supplementary Figs. S16 and 17). After irradiation with mild sunlight, the apoptosis rate of the PNT PS group reached 75.7%, while 5-ALA-induced apoptosis remained minimal, consistent with the previous findings. These results suggest that under mild sunlight irradiation, PNT PSs exhibit promising PDT effects, which can effectively eliminate hyperproliferative keratinocytes in the stratum corneum of psoriasis. To further evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of SunPDT microneedle patches, the penetration depth of PNT PSs was first evaluated. To simulate in vivo light attenuation, a 5-mm-thick chicken breast tissue was placed between the light source and psoriasis-related NHEK and HaCaT cells during CCK-8 viability assays. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S18, PNT PSs maintained strong photodynamic cytotoxicity under these conditions, indicating that the sunlight intensity used is sufficient to activate PNT PSs at clinically relevant tissue depths. Next, an imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis-like mouse model was employed. Mice were randomly assigned to five groups: I: PBS, II: Sunlight, III: SunPDT microneedle patch, IV: PpIX microneedle patch + Sunlight, V: SunPDT microneedle patch + Sunlight (Fig. 4a). Each group underwent distinct treatments beginning on 3 post-model induction and lasting for 6 days. The microneedle patches were refreshed every two days with each mouse exposed to 30 min of mild sunlight daily at a power density of 12 mW cm⁻². Body weight and Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores were recorded daily. As shown in Fig. 4b, the dorsal psoriasis skin of groups II (Sunlight) and III (SunPDT microneedle patch) show similar results with significant acanthosis, erythema, and scaling compared to mice of group I (PBS). By contrast, group V (SunPDT microneedle patch + Sunlight) exhibited remarkable improvement of skin lesions, marked by noticeable reductions in acanthosis, scaling, and erythema, indicating the optimal therapeutic effect of SunPDT microneedle patches under mild sunlight exposure. However, group IV (PpIX microneedle patch + sunlight) only exhibited weak therapeutic improvement. This limitation primarily stems from the low ROS yields of PpIX PSs and their visible spectrum absorption characteristics that fundamentally constrain tissue penetration depth. Furthermore, longitudinal assessment of psoriasis progression through Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores (desquamation, erythema, and thickness; Fig. 4c–e) across mice groups demonstrated marked suppression of psoriasiform inflammation in Group V. The SunPDT microneedle patch + Sunlight group significantly inhibited psoriasis-like dermatitis, aligning with previous therapeutic findings. Histological analysis of H&E-stained skin sections revealed typical psoriatic histopathological features. The PBS, Sunlight and SunPDT microneedle patch groups exhibited obvious keratin layer accumulation and microabscesses (Fig. 4f, red arrow), accompanied by pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration predominantly within the dermal layer (Fig. 4f, blue arrow). By contrast, these pathological changes, including epidermal thickening and inflammatory infiltration, were alleviated to varying degrees in the SunPDT microneedle patch + Sunlight group, whereas only minimal improvement was observed in the PpIX microneedle patch + Sunlight group. The epidermal thickness in group I (PBS) was measured at 262.9 µm, which decreased to 41.63 µm in group V (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. S19), indicating that SunPDT demonstrated significant attenuation of hyperkeratotic thickness in the stratum corneum. Therapeutic biosafety study of SunPDT microneedle patches

Enhancing therapeutic biosafety is vital for patient compliance and experience, making it a key focus in the advancement of the wide application of PDT. Microneedle patches painlessly deliver PSs into deep lesions, reducing the systemic side effects of traditional intravenous PSs to a certain extent. However, local PSs delivered through microneedle patches rapidly diffuse into normal tissue, which often causes potential long-term toxicity and phototoxicity of normal tissue24. Developing high ROS yield to enable lower dosages while ensuring rapid biodegradation holds significant potential for enhancing the biosafety of SunPDT microneedle patches. Since nanoparticles administered in vivo are subject to continuous clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), we first evaluated the biodegradation potential of the PNT PS under phagocyte-mimicking in vitro conditions. Myeloperoxidase (MPO), an enzyme abundantly expressed in phagocytes that catalyzes the production of hypochlorous acid (HClO) from hydrogen peroxide and chloride ions, was employed to induce oxidative stress on the backbones of the PNT PSs. Mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. S21a) and infrared spectra (Supplementary Fig. S22) was performed to analyze segments after biodegradation. The possible degradation mechanism of the PNT PSs was proposed (Supplementary Fig. S21b), suggesting the naphthalenediimide and thiophene unit as the vulnerable moiety toward oxidation. To study biodegradability, PNT PSs were investigated by incubating them with RAW264.7 cells for 12 h, washing them with PBS to remove free PNT PSs, and then stimulating the cells with lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Then the cells were collected, and the intracellular PNT PS concentration was measured by absorbance and fluorescence intensity. The maximum absorbance at 412 nm in LPS-treated cells was 0.13 (Fig. 5a), significantly lower than the 0.41 observed in untreated cells, while the maximum fluorescence intensity at 595 nm decreased by 2.8-fold (Fig. 5b), confirming biodegradability of PNT PSs at the cellular level. Confocal laser scanning microscopy visually depicted the uptake and degradation process of PNT PSs within the cells. Confocal fluorescence imaging showed a distinct red fluorescence signal in RAW264.7 cells 12 hours after PNT PS treatment, indicating substantial PNT PS uptake. As LPS stimulation and time increased, the fluorescence intensity within the cells gradually decreased until it almost disappeared after 36 h (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. S23), indicating the rapid biodegradability of PNT PSs. A similar phenomenon was observed when DHR123 was used to study the ROS production of PNT PSs within the cells (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. S24). To confirm that PNT PSs also degrade rapidly in psoriatic tissue without diffusing into normal tissue, we used a PpIX microneedle patch as a control. As shown in Figs. 5d and 5e, PpIX fluorescence remained detectable in normal tissue up to 120 hours after patch removal. By contrast, the PNT PS fluorescence of mice of SunPDT microneedle patch group decreased sharply within 12 hours, nearly returning to the background level before patch application (-0.5 h in Fig. 5e). Furthermore, ex vivo fluorescence imaging showed that major organs treated with the PpIX microneedle patch displayed markedly stronger fluorescence than those treated with the SunPDT patch at 120 h post-treatment (Fig. 5f). These data indicate that traditional clinically approved PpIX could diffuse from psoriatic tissue into normal tissue, potentially causing long-term toxicity and phototoxicity. By contrast, the fluorescence of PNT PSs is barely detectable in normal tissue at 12 h, likely due to their rapid biodegradation. Furthermore, the pharmacokinetics of PNT PSs in the bloodstream also revealed minimal systemic exposure (Supplementary Fig. S25), further confirming negligible distribution of PNT PSs in normal tissues. Barely detectable PSs in normal tissues show great potential for minimizing long-term toxicity and phototoxicity. More importantly, the effective SunPDT window is crucial for in vivo therapy, given the rapid degradation of PNT PSs. To further study the biosafety of SunPDT microneedle patch, no intentional light avoidance was implemented within 15 days after treatment. On the 15th day, the mice were euthanized, and their major organs were collected for histological analysis using H&E staining. Meanwhile, healthy mice with no treatment were used as controls. Compared to healthy mice, no significant histopathological abnormalities or lesions were observed in the kidneys, livers, and spleens of all groups (Fig. 5g). These results demonstrate that the “on-off” PNT PSs in wearable microneedle patches stay active (ROS “on” state) in diseased tissue during treatment but quickly deactivate (ROS “off” state) through biodegradation after fulfilling their therapeutic role without obvious biodistribution in normal tissue, ensuring bio-safe PDT.

We next evaluated the in vivo safety profile of the SunPDT microneedle patch. Hematological, serum biochemical, and histopathological analyses were performed at 1-, 7-, and 30-days post-administration. Mice treated with the SunPDT patch exhibited body weights comparable to those of saline-treated controls (Supplementary Fig. S27a) and showed no observable alterations in behavior or appearance, including feeding, drinking, or coat condition (Supplementary Fig. S27b). Hematological parameters remained largely unchanged across all time points relative to controls, indicating the absence of systemic infection or inflammation (Supplementary Fig. S28). Similarly, serum biochemical markers revealed no signs of liver, kidney, or hematological toxicity (Supplementary Fig. S29). Histological examination of major organs further confirmed the lack of structural abnormalities or lesions (Supplementary Fig. S30). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the SunPDT microneedle patch is well tolerated in vivo, without evident toxicity.

Discussion

Traditional PDT uses PSs applied to the skin or administered via intravenous injection to generate ROS under specialized light source exposure for disease treatment48–55. However, widespread clinical adoption of traditional PDT faces two major challenges: (1) Dependence on specialized light sources and experienced medical personnel; and (2) bio-safety concerns from the long-term toxicity and phototoxicity of PSs. These challenges increase patient burden, hinder compliance, and limit the treatment environment, ultimately diminishing the overall treatment experience. Here, we present a “on-off”, wearable microneedle patch with PNT PSs for PDT under mild sunlight (12 mW cm⁻²). PNT PSs, tailored through rational PS skeleton design and precise side-chain engineering, offer broad NIR absorption, high ROS yield, and rapid biodegradation. Therefore, our SunPDT microneedle patch offers significant advantages over traditional PDT: (1) self-administered, wearable microneedle patches and mild-sunlight irradiation eliminate the need for experienced medical personnel, specialized light sources, and intravenous PS injections in traditional PDT. This innovation enhances affordability and accessibility, even in areas with poor health care and electricity, while improving convenience, compliance, and the overall treatment experience. SunPDT further enables large-area treatment, efficiently targeting multiple lesions, ideal for psoriasis or burns. (2) PNT PSs absorb NIR light to generate ROS, while microneedle patches could deliver PSs into deep tissues for effective deep treatment. (3) The combination of mild intensity (12 mW cm⁻²) irradiation, low-dose PSs, rapid PS biodegradation at the lesion site after treatment, and microneedle-mediated local PS delivery effectively minimizes side effects, enhancing comfort and safety. Although promising, a limitation of this system is the lack of preferential binding of the photosensitizer to pathological tissue at the cellular or molecular level. The apparent targeting therefore arises primarily from localized delivery via the microneedle patch rather than active molecular recognition. Future studies may address this limitation through the incorporation of targeting ligands or activatable photosensitizer designs to enhance disease specificity. Collectively, our SunPDT concept not only represents a promising strategy for treating psoriasis but also provides a promising design guideline to complete the PDT in a relaxed, pleasant and daily life scene, such as outdoors or leisure activities, reducing patient anxiety and stress and enhancing the treatment experience and bringing PDT closer to more practical applications.

Methods

Female BALB/c mice (4 weeks old) were purchased from Jiangsu Collection Medicine Kang Biotechnology Co., LTD (SYXK (SU) 2019-0056). Animal procedures were conducted in strict compliance with the Animal Management Rules of the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China and were approved by the Science and Technology Ethics Committee of Nanjing University (NO. IACUC-2502013). Mice were shaved backs for 2 × 3 cm. All the mice were treated with 65 mg commercial IMQ cream (5%, w/w, Sichuan Mingxin Pharmaceutical Co., LTD) once a day for 3 days to establish a psoriasis model. To induce psoriasis-like skin lesions, a topical application was applied to the shaved skin. The materials and methods used in this study are described in detail in the Supplementary information, including descriptions of the photosensitizer used in this study, the imaging method and considerations.

Fabrication of SunPDT microneedle patch

PNT PSs (1 mg) or PPIX (1 mg) and HA solutions (1 mL, 30 wt%) were added to the PDMS mold and centrifuged at 1800 g for 10 min. After the drying and detaching processes, the PNT PS-based SunPDT microneedle patch was obtained. For the characterization of the microneedle patch, bright-field images were obtained using a depth field 3D microscope (MIV-IN) to record the morphology of the microneedle patches. The surface morphology of microneedle patches was obtained using SEM (ULTRA55).

Cell culture

Normal human epi-dermalkeratinocyte (NHEK), human immortalized epidermal cells (HaCaT) cell, RAW 264.7 cell were sourced from the Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were cultivated in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin). The cells were grown in a 37 °C incubator supplied with 5% carbon dioxide.
