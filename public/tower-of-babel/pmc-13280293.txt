HBV Serological Profiles and Vaccination Status in People with HIV in the Transition to a Tenofovir-Sparing Era: Insights from a Large HIV Cohort
Authors: Alberto Foncillas, Lorena De La Mora, Leire Berrocal, Elisa de Lazzari, Júlia Calvo, Abiu Sempere, Ivan Chivite, Alexy Inciarte, Maria Martínez-Rebollar, Berta Torres, Ana González-Cordón, Jose Luís Blanco, José. M. Miró, Esteban Martínez, Montserrat Laguno, Josep Mallolas, Juan Ambrosioni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe increasing adoption of tenofovir (TXF)-sparing antiretroviral therapy (ART) raises concerns regarding hepatitis B virus (HBV) susceptibility and reactivation risk among people with HIV (PWH). We characterized HBV serological profiles and vaccination status according to ART composition in a real-world cohort.MethodsA cross-sectional study of all PWH in active follow-up at Hospital Clínic, Barcelona, as of 30 June 2025. ART regimens were categorized as TXF-containing or TXF-sparing, with or without lamivudine (3TC). HBV serological patterns were classified as chronic infection, serologically resolved infection, isolated HBV core antibody (anti-HBc), no exposure/immunity, and vaccine-induced immunity. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared using nonparametric and chi-squared/Fisher’s tests.ResultsAmong the 6437 participants included (82% cisgender men; median age 48 years [IQR 39–58]), 3519 (55%) received TXF-containing and 2918 (45%) TXF-sparing regimens, of whom 1702/2918 (58%) were with 3TC. HBV serological distribution was: 2% chronic infection, 26% serologically resolved infection, 5% isolated anti-HBc, 52% vaccine-induced immunity, and 15% without exposure/immunity (50% documented prior vaccination attempts, 32% nonresponders, and 31% with prior anti-HBs detection). Overall, 1280 (20%) lacked protective HBV immunity (isolated anti-HBc or negative serology for all markers), including 530 (41%) on TXF-free regimens. TXF recipients were younger (47 versus 50 years, p < 0.001), more often migrants (58% versus 49%, p < 0.001), had lower suppression rates (91% versus 97%, p < 0.001), a higher proportion of previous virological failure(s) (26% versus 21%, p < 0.001), and a lower number of prior ART regimens (median 3 versus 4, p < 0.001).ConclusionsOne in five PWH lacked effective HBV immunity, including 41% of whom were receiving TXF-sparing strategies. In the context of increasing use of TXF-sparing strategies, improvements in systematic HBV screening, vaccination, and risk-based monitoring are essential to prevent HBV-related morbidity.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) share transmission routes, representing HIV–HBV co-infection a significant clinical challenge with substantial global and regional implications [1]. Co-infected individuals face greater morbidity and mortality due to synergistic pathogenesis, increasing the risk of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, liver failure, and liver-related mortality compared with those with either infection alone [2–5]. Moreover, despite widespread vaccination efforts, people with HIV (PWH) consistently exhibit lower HBV vaccine response rates and antibody durability than the general population [6, 7].

Recent meta-analyses report an estimated global prevalence of active HIV–HBV co-infection around 7.6–8.2% overall, ranging considerably from ~6% to 7% in low prevalence regions, such as North America and Europe, to higher rates in endemic areas such as the Western Pacific and Sub-Saharan Africa (> 10%) [8, 9]. In Spain, the co-infection rate is low and remains stable at approximately 3% according to local studies [10], although up to 30% of PWH have evidence of previous HBV exposure [11]. These individuals, identified by HBV core antibody (anti-HBc) positivity, are at risk of HBV reactivation (HBVr) in the context of immunosuppression because of virus genome persistence in latent forms, particularly covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), despite resolved infection.

Traditionally, antiretroviral therapy (ART) consisted of the combination of two nucleos(t)ide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) and a third agent. These combinations often contained lamivudine (3TC), emtricitabine (FTC), and tenofovir (TXF)—either disoproxil fumarate or alafenamide (TDF/TAF)—all of which have activity against HBV; however, only TXF provides robust, durable HBV suppression owing to the rapid emergence of HBV resistance with 3TC or FTC when used without TXF [12].

However, the landscape of HIV treatment has shifted toward simplified ART regimens—dual therapies such as dolutegravir/lamivudine (DTG/3TC), dolutegravir/rilpivirine (DTG/RPV), and long-acting injectables such as cabotegravir/rilpivirine (LA-CAB/RPV)— many of which lack effective anti-HBV activity [13, 14]. Emerging agents (e.g., lenacapavir, islatravir, ulonivirine, newer integrase inhibitors, or monoclonal antibodies against HIV) also do not provide HBV coverage. This therapeutic shift raises concerns about HBVr in PWH with past HBV exposure and incident HBV infection (HBVi) risk in nonimmune individuals, particularly in the context of immunosuppression [15].

Despite this, data on the real-world risk of HBVr or HBVi among PWH switching to TXF-sparing regimens remain scarce and inconsistent. Existing evidence largely comprises case reports and small series [16–20], along with retrospective studies [21–32], a scoping literature review [33], and a single prospective cohort in the context of LA-CAB/RPV [34], reporting heterogeneous results.

This study aims to characterize HBV immune profile and vaccination status among PWH and its association with ART regimens, with a particular focus on TXF-containing versus TXF-sparing strategies. By examining the epidemiological and HIV-related characteristics, this research seeks to provide a real-world risk map to guide screening, vaccination, and ART selection in the era of treatment simplification and long-acting therapy, ultimately informing future ART strategies in settings with prior HBV exposure or absent immunity.

Methods

Design, Setting, and Study Population

We conducted a unicentric, cross-sectional study with retrospective data at the HIV Unit of Hospital Clínic, Barcelona, Spain, as of 30 June 2025. The study population included all adults with HIV infection in active follow-up, defined as those who were receiving ART regimens and had at least one blood sample taken within the year prior to the cross (database closure).

Patients were eligible if they had at least one HBV serology result available at the time of database lock. No other exclusion criteria were applied.

Study Assessments

ART regimens at the time analysis were recorded and categorized as: (1) TXF-containing regimens, (2) TXF-sparing regimens with 3TC, and (3) TXF and 3TC-sparing regimens.

We retrospectively obtained HBV serological markers including HBV surface antigen (HBsAg), HBV core antibody (anti-HBc IgG), and HBV surface antibody (anti-HBs; categorized as positive [≥ 10 mIU/mL] or negative [< 10 mIU/mL]) from the electronic medical record system of the PWH cohort at Hospital Clínic. For patients with multiple serologic results, the most recent determination was used for analysis. Patients were categorized according to serologic pattern into: (1) chronic HBV infection (HBsAg-positive, anti-HBc-positive, anti-HBs-negative), (2) serologically resolved HBV infection (HBsAg-negative, anti-HBc-positive, anti-HBs-positive), (3) isolated anti-HBc (HBsAg-negative, anti-HBc-positive, anti-HBs-negative), (4) without previous HBV exposure or immunity (HBsAg-negative, anti-HBc-negative, anti-HBs-negative), and (5) vaccine-induced immunity (HBsAg-negative, anti-HBc-negative, anti-HBs-positive).

We defined “at-risk HBV serologic profiles” as either absence of immunity (HBsAg-negative/anti-HBc-negative/anti-HBs-negative) or isolated anti-HBc positivity, representing susceptibility to HBVi or potential HBVr risk. Although resolved infection with low anti-HBs titers (e.g. < 100 mIU/mL) may also represent a subgroup at risk in some clinical contexts, this category was not included in our primary definition.

Demographic and immunovirological data including most recent available viral load (VL) and CD4+ T-cell count, nadir CD4+ T-cell count, and history of previous virological failure (VF) (defined as two consecutive VL ≥ 50 copies/mL assessed over the entire available follow-up period) were retrieved from the HIV Unit electronic medical records.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Comparisons between groups (e.g., TXF-containing versus TXF-sparing regimens) were performed using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, and Wilcoxon rank sum tests for continuous variables. When comparing the distribution of HBV serological status groups between TXF-containing and TXF-sparing regimens, Bonferroni correction was applied to the pairwise comparisons.

The tests were two-tailed, and the significance level was set at 5%. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 19 [35].

Ethics

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (protocol no. HCB/2025/0752) and was conducted according to the Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of the study, its potential utility for society, and in accordance with the relevant legislation (Articles 6.1.e and 9.2j of the General Data Protection Regulation [GDPR], as well as Additional Disposition 17.2.d of LOPD-GDD 3/2018), the Ethics Committee of the Hospital Clinic of Barcelona waived the requirement for written informed consent.

Results

Of 6604 PWH engaged in care at our center, 6550 were in active follow-up and receiving ART. Among them, 6437 with available HBV serology were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The cohort was predominantly cisgender men (82%) with 54% migrants, and a median age of 48 years (IQR 39–58). Overall, 71% identified as men who have sex with men (MSM), and sexual transmission accounted for 92% of HIV acquisition. Of the 6437 participants, 141 (2%) had chronic HBV infection, 1671 (26%) showed serologically resolved infection, 300 (5%) had isolated anti-HBc, 980 (15%) had no evidence of HBV exposure or immunity, and 3332 (52%) presented vaccine-induced immunity. In total, 13 participants (< 1%) were unclassifiable owing to indeterminate data. Overall, 1280 (20%) participants lacked protective HBV immunity (defined as isolated anti-HBc or negative serology for all HBV markers); 530 (41%) of whom were receiving TXF-sparing regimens, including 392/530 (74%) with no exposure/immunity and 138/530 (26%) with isolated anti-HBc. Among 437/1971 (22%) HBsAg-negative/anti-HBc-positive individuals who had HBV DNA testing available at any time during follow-up, 13/437 (3%) had detectable HBV DNA at some point, consistent with occult HBV infection. Among the 13 confirmed cases, 8 presented with detectable but nonquantifiable HBV DNA levels (< 10 IU/mL) of uncertain clinical significance (1/8 was not receiving ART at the time of HBV DNA assessment, 4/8 were on TXF-sparing regimens—including one with 3TC, and 3/8 were on TXF-containing regimens). The remaining 5/13 individuals had detectable and quantifiable HBV DNA (3/5 were not on ART at the time of HBV DNA assessment, while 2/5 were receiving TXF-sparing regimens—including one with 3TC).

Regarding vaccination, 492/980 (50%) participants without prior exposure/immunity (HBsAg-negative, anti-HBc-negative, anti-HBs-negative) had documented previous HBV vaccination attempts with conventional alum-adjuvanted vaccines but lacked detectable anti-HBs at the time of assessment, with a median (IQR) of 4 doses (2–6), and 156/492 (32%) having received ≥ 6 doses, thus presenting nonresponse to vaccination. Furthermore, among these triple-negative individuals, 307/980 (31%) reported previous anti-HBs positivity at any time in their available medical records, with a median (IQR) time since last documented anti-HBs positivity of 7 years [5–11].

Participants on TXF-containing regimens were significantly younger (47 versus 50 years, p < 0.001) and more frequently migrants (58% versus 49%, p < 0.001), particularly from Latin America (44% versus 35%, p < 0.001).

Regarding HIV-related data, 6037/6437 (94%) were virologically suppressed. However, virologic suppression was lower among TXF-containing participants (91% versus 97%; p < 0.001) and a history of previous VF was higher among TXF-containing regimen participants (26% versus 21%; p < 0.001). The median CD4 count at last visit was lower in the TXF-containing group (714 versus 752 cells/µL, p < 0.001), but nadir CD4 counts did not differ significantly between groups (301 versus 317 cells/µL, p = 0.10). Participants on TXF-containing regimens had a lower number of prior ART regimens (median 3 versus 4; p < 0.001).

The distribution of HBV serologic profiles differed significantly according to TXF use (p < 0.001). Participants receiving TXF-containing ART more frequently had completely negative serology (17% versus 13%; p < 0.001), whereas a vaccine-induced immunity profile was more common in the TXF-sparing group (56% versus 49%, p < 0.001). The prevalence of resolved infection (26% in both groups; p = 0.175) and isolated anti-HBc (5% in both groups; p = 0.167) did not differ. Chronic HBV infection was observed almost exclusively among individuals receiving TXF-containing regimens (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this large real-world cohort, nearly one in five PWH exhibited “at-risk” HBV serologic profiles (isolated anti-HBc or triple-negative serology) consistent with potential vulnerability to HBVi or HBVr, underscoring an emerging gap in HBV protection as ART therapy shifts toward simplified and TXF-sparing strategies. To our knowledge, this is the largest European cohort analysis examining HBV immune profile and vaccination status specifically in the TXF-sparing era.

Specifically, 15% of participants lacked all HBV immunity markers, and an additional 5% exhibited an isolated anti-HBc pattern, a profile associated with occult infection and HBVr risk [36]. These “at-risk” profiles were frequently observed among individuals receiving TXF-sparing regimens, identifying a clinically relevant population lacking HBV-active coverage. Notably, nearly half of participants receiving TXF-sparing regimens were on 3TC-containing therapies, which, although suboptimal for HBV protection when used as monotherapy, may still confer partial antiviral activity against HBV [12, 29]. Moreover, Salpini et al. showed that TXF withdrawal in HBsAg-negative/anti-HBc-positive PWH was associated with a progressive HBV replication increase, particularly in those with low anti-HBs titers (< 100 mIU/mL) or cryptic HBV-DNA, suggesting that risk may extend beyond individuals lacking anti-HBs to a broader population with prior HBV exposure but low anti-HBs titers [28]. However, this subgroup was not included in our operational definition of “at-risk” serologic profiles.

Although reactivation is rare, isolated anti-HBc remains a surrogate marker of occult HBV infection with important clinical and public health implications, as HIV–HBV co-infection accelerates liver damage and increases liver-related morbimortality and hepatocellular carcinoma risk [37–39]. Our relatively low prevalence of isolated anti-HBc (~5%) compared with the ~20–25% reported in other cohorts [1, 8–11] may reflect the specific epidemiological profile of our population (predominantly MSM with sexually acquired HIV), as well as the long-standing universal HBV vaccination program in Spain, and the increasing adoption of standarized vaccination in isolated anti-HBc individuals.

A noteworthy finding was the age distribution of TXF prescribing. Younger PWH were more frequently prescribed TXF, which may seem counterintuitive given the increasing adoption of dual, TXF-sparing regimens. Several factors likely contribute to this pattern. First, chronic HBV infection was concentrated in the TXF group, and younger participants (particularly migrants) were more likely to have HBV exposure, making TXF clinically appropriate. In contrast, older individuals more frequently presented with renal or bone comorbidities, which may prompt clinicians to avoid TXF. Moreover, a higher proportion of migrants were receiving TXF-containing regimens. Many originated from countries where TDF/3TC/DTG (or “TLD”) is the preferred first-line regimen [40] and, in the absence of complete ART history, clinicians may be reluctant to simplify these individuals to TXF-sparing regimens, owing to concerns about archived resistance and potential virologic failure. Finally, despite strong evidence supporting dual regimens [13, 14], prescribers may still prefer TXF in younger or newly diagnosed individuals owing to its perceived higher genetic barrier to resistance and anti-HBV activity.

From an HIV perspective, virological suppression was high among participants, but significantly lower in TXF recipients. Moreover, individuals receiving TXF presented a more frequent history of prior VF and lower current CD4 counts, whereas those on TXF-sparing regimens had a greater number of previous ART lines. These findings likely reflect different resistance profiles, ART history, and/or poor perceived adherence, driving prescribers’ preference for maintaining triple therapy in these patients, and using dual therapy for treatment simplification.

Overall, our findings emphasize that ART regimen allocation is not random but shaped by factors such as age, comorbidities, HBV status, migration background, and perceived adherence. Although significant associations were observed between HBV serologic status and TXF prescription patterns, these likely reflect confounding influences—particularly clinician prescribing preferences, patient origin, and age distribution—rather than a direct effect of ART itself. This underscores the need for cautious interpretation of cross-sectional data and highlights the importance of adjusted analyses in future studies to clarify causal relationships.

Importantly, our data confirm that HBV immunity remains suboptimal in many PWH despite longstanding vaccination programs. Reduced vaccine response and waning antibody titers are well-documented in PWH, especially in those with historical immunosuppression or low CD4 counts [41–45]. In our cohort, half of individuals with negative HBV serology had previously received vaccination, and nearly a third of these had completed ≥ 6 doses, indicating that anti-HBs absence often reflects impaired response or antibody waning rather than lack of vaccination. Supporting this, almost one-third of triple-negative individuals had prior anti-HBs positivity, with a median interval of seven years since last detectable antibodies, indicating a probable loss of measurable humoral immunity over time. Newer vaccine strategies may help overcome these gaps. The CpG-adjuvanted recombinant HBV vaccine HEPLISAV-B (HepBCpG; Dynavax) has shown particularly promising results, with seroconversion rates as high as 99% in PWH who previously failed vaccination, and up to 78.1% reaching anti-HBs titers > 1000 mIU/mL [46], representing an effective rescue strategy in nonresponders. While increasingly recommended internationally [13, 14], broader implementation and commercialization are still needed.

These findings emphasize the need for integrating periodic reassessment of HBV serostatus, vaccine history documentation, and targeted revaccination, especially when initiating or switching to regimens without anti-HBV activity. Vaccination circuits simplification (e.g., same place and day administration at routine HIV visits) may improve coverage in some settings.

Although this study was not designed to measure incidence or reactivation events, the high prevalence of “at-risk” profiles (isolated anti-HBc and triple-negative serology), many on regimens lacking HBV coverage, highlights a potentially underestimated gap in clinical management. Previous studies have reported widely variable HBVr rates (< 1–15%) depending on ART regimen, HBV immune status, definition of reactivation, and follow-up duration [16–34]. Our findings call for prospective evaluations and standardized surveillance protocols in this population.

This study benefits from a large, well-characterized cohort, capturing the full spectrum of contemporary ART regimens, and essential clinical data for HIV management nowadays. Limitations include its cross-sectional design, limited availability of HBV DNA testing, and absence of longitudinal follow-up to evaluate clinical outcomes such as HBVi/HBVr or liver-related events. In addition, incomplete vaccination histories may have led to misclassification of immune status in some individuals. Anti-HBs titers were analyzed using the conventional ≥ 10 mIU/mL threshold, and higher protective thresholds (e.g. ≥ 100 mIU/mL) were not specifically evaluated.

Conclusions

Our results reveal a significant proportion of PWH receiving TXF-sparing ART regimens who remain at risk of HBVi or HBVr, owing to either absent immunity or isolated anti-HBc status. Notably, 31% of participants without exposure/immunity had previously documented protective anti-HBs titers. These findings support systematic HBV serological reassessment before initiating or switching to ART regimens without HBV activity, particularly prior to treatment simplification or long-acting ART strategies lacking HBV coverage. Further prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the incidence of HBVr in this population and to inform evidence-based guidelines on ART simplification and optimal periodicity of vaccine-induced immunity assessment in the context of HIV–HBV co-infection.
