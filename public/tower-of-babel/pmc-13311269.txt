Global surveillance of anthrax outbreaks in domestic animals and humans: A retrospective secondary analysis of reported cases over two decades (2005–2024)
Authors: Irshad Ul Ala, Benjamin Er, Joanna Khoo, Joanne Sheot Harn Chan, Kyaw Thu Aung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthrax, caused by Bacillus anthracis, remains a significant concern in animal and public health globally. This study examines anthrax outbreak data from 2005 to 2024, utilizing reports from the World Animal Health Information System (WAHIS) and ProMED. Our analysis reveals a general decreasing trend in global anthrax incidents, with persistent endemicity in regions of Central Asia, Africa, and Southern Asia. Despite this overall decline, Türkiye maintains similar levels of anthrax incidents in livestock, while six countries demonstrate increasing trends during the studied period, a finding broadly supported by sensitivity analysis. Additionally, unexpected emergence in historically low-incidence regions demonstrates the disease's unpredictable nature. The study identifies cattle and sheep as primary hosts, underscoring their role in disease ecology. Importantly, we observe differences in reporting patterns between countries with high livestock incidence and those documenting human cases, which may reflect differential surveillance capacity, underreporting of human cases, or genuine epidemiological variation in zoonotic transmission dynamics. Notable differences between WAHIS and ProMED reports potentially reflect that animal surveillance systems and human health alert networks capture different aspects of anthrax outbreaks, underscoring the value of cross sectoral data synthesis to achieve a more comprehensive epidemiological understanding to inform risk mitigation measures.Keywords: Anthrax, Bacillus anthracis, Disease surveillance, Zoonotic disease, One health, Epidemiology, Global health

Introduction

Anthrax is a highly infectious bacterial disease caused by the bacterium Bacillus anthracis. B. anthracis has been linked to numerous outbreaks related to infections in animals and humans [1], [2]. Anthrax has had a long history of causing epidemics and is a significant concern in various regions around the world, particularly in areas with livestock and in countries with a history of biological warfare [3]. It originates from vegetation, soil or water that is often ingested by ruminant herbivores such as antelopes, cows, deers, goats, and sheep [4]. Among the affected, cows and sheep are typically the most commonly impacted livestock [5]. Humans are not primary targets of anthrax infection, but can become infected through contact with contaminated animals or animal products, most often occurring in agricultural regions within sub-Saharan Africa, Central and South America, Central and Southwestern Asia, and Southern and Eastern Europe. Antarctica is the only continent with no record of an anthrax outbreak event [3].

B. anthracis spores persist in soil for decades, with outbreaks often triggered by environmental disturbances such as flooding or drought. The global prevalence of anthrax has varied over time, influenced by factors such as climate, livestock populations, vaccination prevalence, and human activities. Anthrax is more prevalent in regions with extensive livestock farming, with more opportunities for the bacteria to spread from animals to humans [6]. The disease is particularly prevalent in countries with large livestock populations, such as India, Türkiye, and parts of Africa, where the risk of human infection from animals is high.

Anthrax is typically endemic [7]. Livestock anthrax risk is likely to be highly localised [8], with the extent of areas of endemicity appear to be well defined by various factors that drive human-host contact. Additionally, the reciprocal relationship between anthrax spores and local environmental factors appears to drive the predominantly localised pattern observed in anthrax outbreak occurrence.

Anthrax is rarely spread from animal to animal or person to person. Instead, transmission typically occurs between different species [9], [10]. Carnivores and humans usually become infected by consuming meat from an infected animal. Ungulates on the other hand, typically contract anthrax by ingesting spores in the soil or in their feed [11]. This often leads to a rapid death, while their respective predators feeding on them are more resistant.

Anthrax poses significant challenges to global veterinary and public health. Recent human cases reported around the world underscore the need for a comprehensive analysis of its prevalence and distribution. By analyzing data from multiple countries over time, we seek to identify temporal trends in the disease's occurrence and key areas of concern such as geographic hotspots with persistent transmission, species-specific risk patterns, and discrepancies between animal and human surveillance data.

Methodology

This study employed a retrospective analysis of global anthrax outbreak data from two complementary surveillance systems to examine temporal and spatial trends in disease occurrence from 2005 to 2024. ProMED data (from 2021 to 2024) and WAHIS data (from 2005 to 2024) were analyzed. The differing time periods reflect data availability constraints: while WAHIS maintains comprehensive historical archives accessible for the full study period, ProMED email alerts are only available from the date of subscription initiation, limiting our access to reports from January 2021 onwards when data collection commenced.

Data sources

ProMED surveillance data (2021–2024)

We analyzed anthrax outbreak reports from the Program for Monitoring Emerging Diseases (ProMED), an informal global surveillance network that captures disease events generating public health concern [12]. ProMED reports were accessed through RSS feed subscription, filtering specifically for anthrax-related incidents from January 2021 to March 2024. The different analysis period for ProMED data corresponds to the duration of our RSS feed subscription, as ProMED's RSS system only provides real-time alerts from the point of subscription onwards, with no access to historical archives through this method. This dataset provided insights into recent human-implicated anthrax cases and outbreak contexts that may not be captured through formal reporting channels.

From each ProMED report, we extracted standardized variables including outbreak timing, geographic location, affected animal species, and incident frequency (Table 1). This approach enabled analysis of anthrax emergence patterns in diverse epidemiological contexts beyond routine livestock surveillance.

WAHIS dataset (2005 to 2023)

We obtained comprehensive animal anthrax data from the World Animal Health Information System (WAHIS), the official mandatory reporting database of the World Organisation for Animal Health [13]. WAHIS represents systematic surveillance data submitted by member countries through designated national veterinary services, providing standardized reporting of notifiable diseases including anthrax.

The WAHIS dataset encompassed 20 years of global anthrax surveillance (2005–2024) with detailed epidemiological variables including temporal occurrence, geographic distribution, affected species, outbreak magnitude, and control measures implemented (Table 2). Our analysis focused on five key variables: year, world region, country, species, and new outbreak occurrences to examine long-term epidemiological trends.

Data preparation

WAHIS data underwent standardization to ensure analytical consistency. Missing values and dashes (‘-’) in disease occurrence fields were coded as zeros to enable quantitative analysis. This approach is justified by WAHIS's mandatory reporting framework, where member countries have legal obligations under the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) to report notifiable diseases including anthrax. Under this active surveillance system, dashes in disease occurrence fields typically represent administrative artifacts of zero values rather than absence of surveillance, as countries are required to submit reports even when zero cases occur. The logic follows that if anthrax were present, countries would be mandated by international standards (Terrestrial and Aquatic Animal Health Codes) to report quantitative data; therefore, empty fields are more likely to represent lack of identification of disease than unreported outbreaks.

This zero-coding approach was applied exclusively to disease occurrence variables (New Outbreaks). Population and susceptible animal count fields containing dashes were excluded from prevalence calculations, as dashes in these fields indicate ‘data not collected’ rather than zero animals present, making the assumption of zero cases epidemiologically sound for analytical purposes.

To characterise the extent of missing data across the dataset, reporting completeness was assessed prior to zero-coding by calculating the proportion of country-year observations containing quantitative outbreak records. Overall reporting completeness was high across all world regions, ranging from 93.1% in Asia to 98.3% in Europe, suggesting that regional trend comparisons are not substantially confounded by differential missingness at the regional level. However, temporal completeness declined notably in the most recent years of the study period, from above 95% consistently during 2005–2019 to 78.9%, 80.5%, and 72.5% in 2021, 2022, and 2023 respectively, alongside a reduction in the number of contributing countries from approximately 60 during the earlier study period to as few as 34 in 2020, recovering partially to 40–41 by 2022–2023. This decline likely reflects reporting lags inherent to mandatory surveillance systems, where countries submit data retrospectively, as well as potential disruptions to surveillance infrastructure during the COVID-19 pandemic period. Consequently, trend estimates for the 2020–2024 period should be interpreted with greater caution than those for earlier periods.

To assess the robustness of findings to this zero-coding assumption, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using complete case analysis, excluding country-year observations where outbreak records were missing or zero, and requiring a minimum of five non-missing observations per country for inclusion in trend modeling. This approach allowed direct comparison of trend detection outcomes under alternative missing data assumptions.

To address the epidemiological heterogeneity within the broad “Asia” regional classification, we developed sub-continental groupings based on shared climatic, ecological, and livestock management characteristics that influence anthrax transmission dynamics. Countries were classified as follows: North Asia (China, Mongolia, Russia, Korea, Japan), Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Vietnam, Philippines, Myanmar, Laos, Singapore, Malaysia), Southern Asia (Afghanistan, India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, Maldives), Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan), and Middle East (Georgia, Iran, Israel, Jordan, Syria, Türkiye, Iraq, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Palestine, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, United Arab Emirates, Oman, Yemen, Saudi Arabia, Lebanon). Countries in Africa, the Americas, and Europe retained their original classifications from the “World.region” variable.

Statistical and geospatial analysis

All analyses were conducted using R Studio (version 2024.04.2 build 764) with echarts4r and echarty visualization libraries. We employed time-series analysis to examine temporal patterns in anthrax incidence, creating line charts and stream plots to visualize trends across sub-continents, countries, and animal species. Spatial analysis utilized choropleth maps to display geographic distribution patterns across four distinct time periods.

Fisher's g test was applied to assess periodicity as it is specifically designed to detect dominant periodic components in time series data without requiring a priori assumptions about cycle length. To assess temporal periodicity in global anthrax occurrence, we applied Fisher's g test to annual WAHIS data. Cross-dataset validation was performed by identifying countries reporting anthrax in both surveillance systems (2021–2024) and comparing species distribution patterns to evaluate surveillance system complementarity.

Temporal trend analysis employed linear regression modeling to assess the significance of outbreak trends over time, with statistical significance evaluated at α = 0.05. Linear regression was selected to quantify directional trends in outbreak incidence over time, as it provides interpretable slope estimates suitable for comparing rates of change across countries. Annual rates of change in outbreak incidence were calculated using fitted linear models for each country with sufficient data points. Residual autocorrelation diagnostics were performed using the autocorrelation function (ACF) to assess the independence assumption of linear regression models across all country-specific trend analyses. Where significant autocorrelation was detected, robust standard errors were applied using the Heteroskedasticity and Autocorrelation Consistent (HAC) estimator to ensure valid statistical inference. To examine longer-term patterns, data were aggregated into consecutive 5-year periods (2005–2009, 2010–2014, 2015–2019, 2020–2024) for comparative analysis.

To assess regional differences in temporal trends, we employed a two-step analytical approach. First, we fitted separate linear regression models for each world region to calculate individual regional trend slopes (outbreaks per year), with regional outbreak totals calculated by aggregating country-level data within each world region by year. Linear trend coefficients were extracted from these region-specific models to quantify the annual rate of change for each region, with positive values indicating increasing trends and negative values indicating declining trends. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust p-values from these individual regional trend analyses for multiple comparisons.

ANOVA with an interaction term (World.region × Year) was employed to formally test whether temporal trends differed significantly across regions, rather than simply describing regional patterns descriptively. Therefore, we performed analysis of variance (ANOVA) with interaction terms to test whether these regional slopes differed significantly from each other. The ANOVA model included world region, year, and their interaction term (World.region × Year) to test the null hypothesis that all regions have identical temporal trends. The significant interaction term indicates that regional slopes are statistically different from each other.

For cross-dataset validation, we identified countries reporting anthrax outbreaks in both WAHIS and ProMED systems during the overlapping period (2021–2024). Species distribution patterns were calculated as percentages of total reported cases within each surveillance system, enabling comparison of surveillance system coverage and species detection capabilities.

Comparative surveillance analysis

We conducted systematic comparison of WAHIS and ProMED reporting patterns to assess how different surveillance approaches capture anthrax epidemiology. This included analysis of geographic coverage, species involvement, and outbreak characteristics to understand the relative strengths and limitations of formal versus informal disease surveillance networks.

Some countries exhibited fluctuating patterns. Türkiye's maintenance of consistently elevated outbreak burden across all four study periods, in contrast to the declining trajectory observed globally, suggests the presence of structural or ecological drivers that have proven resistant to the control measures effective elsewhere (see Fig. 3). Possible explanations include the country's large and geographically dispersed livestock population, ecological conditions in Anatolia that favour spore persistence, or gaps in vaccination coverage in smallholder farming systems — factors that warrant targeted investigation.

Regional comparison analysis revealed significant differences in temporal trends between world regions (ANOVA: F₄,₈₇ = 11.25, p < 0.001 for interaction term). The ANOVA model demonstrated significant main effects for both world region (F₄,₈₇ = 28.30, p < 0.001) and year (F₁,₈₇ = 26.89, p < 0.001), with the critical interaction term (World.region × Year: F₄,₈₇ = 11.25, p = 2.10 × 10−7) confirming that temporal trends differ significantly across regions. While all regions demonstrated declining trends, the rate of decline varied significantly: Africa showed the steepest annual reduction (−61.9 outbreaks/year, adjusted p < 0.01), followed by Asia (−12.2 outbreaks/year, adjusted p = 0.179), Americas (−7.6 outbreaks/year, adjusted p < 0.001), Europe (−2.3 outbreaks/year, adjusted p < 0.001), and Oceania (−0.56 outbreaks/year, adjusted p = 0.01). After Bonferroni correction, all regions except Asia maintained statistical significance.

Sensitivity analysis excluding missing and zero-coded observations identified eight countries with statistically significant increasing trends, compared to six in the primary analysis. Five of the original six countries retained statistical significance under the sensitivity analysis, with Rwanda, Syria and Kazakhstan emerging as additional countries demonstrating increasing trends. Pakistan did not retain statistical significance in the sensitivity analysis, suggesting its primary analysis result may be partially influenced by the zero-coding assumption. The consistency of findings across five of six primary analysis countries, alongside the emergence of additional increasing trend countries, suggests that the primary analysis results are broadly robust to the handling of missing data, while indicating that the zero-coding approach may have masked genuine increasing trends in countries with intermittent but real outbreak reporting.

Cross dataset comparison (2021–2024)

For countries with a relatively high record of incidents within the WAHIS and ProMED datasets, cattle and sheep appeared as the most affected species with regards to anthrax.

Species distribution analysis revealed consistent patterns across both surveillance systems during 2021–2024. WAHIS data showed cattle as the most frequently affected species (84.78%), followed by sheep (8.7%), with the remaining cases distributed among goats, equids, and other domestic animals. ProMED reports demonstrated a similar pattern with cows representing the majority of cases (42.86%), together with cattle (14.28%). This combined total made for a majority compared to sheep (21.43%) (See Fig. 4). Despite differences in reporting mechanisms, both systems identified cattle and sheep as the primary species involved in anthrax outbreaks, accounting for over 50% of all reported cases in each dataset.

Discussion

The significant global reduction in reported anthrax cases over the study period likely reflects the cumulative impact of sustained improvements in agricultural infrastructure, veterinary capacity and public health systems across endemic regions. Widespread implementation of livestock vaccination programmes has fundamentally reduced animal reservoir populations serving as the primary source of human exposure [14], while enhanced biosecurity measures and regulated carcass disposal protocols have reduced environmental contamination and spore persistence [15], [16], [17]. These systemic improvements, rather than any single intervention, most plausibly account for the magnitude and geographic breadth of the observed decline.

Despite overall global decline, local persistence in some countries highlight the complex, region-specific nature of anthrax trends

The persistence of increasing trends in a subset of countries despite global decline suggests that local factors — including inadequate vaccination coverage, unregulated livestock movement, limited veterinary infrastructure and ecological conditions favouring spore persistence — can override broader global improvements in disease control. Türkiye's consistently high outbreak burden across all four study periods is particularly notable, suggesting structural or ecological drivers that have proven resistant to the control measures effective elsewhere. The modest absolute rates of increase observed across all six countries, while statistically robust, indicate that these represent slow-burning epidemiological shifts rather than acute deteriorations, affording a window for targeted intervention before trends accelerate.

Sensitivity analysis further validated these findings, with five of the six countries retaining statistical significance when missing values were excluded from analysis. The loss of significance for Pakistan under complete case analysis warrants cautious interpretation of its primary analysis result, as its apparent increasing trend may reflect the influence of zero-coded missing observations rather than a genuine epidemiological shift. Conversely, the emergence of Rwanda, Syria and Kazakhstan as additional countries with significant increasing trends in the sensitivity analysis suggests that zero-coding may have attenuated trend detection in countries with genuine but intermittently reported outbreaks, indicating that the primary analysis likely represents a conservative estimate of the number of countries experiencing increasing anthrax burden.

Statistical testing using Fisher's g test revealed no significant periodicity in the global anthrax outbreak data (p = 0.5453), despite apparent visual patterns in our stream plot

The absence of significant global periodicity likely reflects the fundamentally localised nature of anthrax ecology, where outbreak timing is governed by region-specific interactions between environmental conditions, livestock management practices and vaccination coverage rather than universal climatic or seasonal drivers. This finding has important implications for surveillance strategy, suggesting that global-level monitoring alone is insufficient to capture the heterogeneous temporal dynamics of anthrax risk, and that sub-national surveillance systems capable of detecting localised cyclical patterns may be more informative for outbreak prediction and prevention.

The significant regional heterogeneity in temporal trends (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2) reflects the complex, region-specific factors influencing anthrax control effectiveness. Africa's steepest decline likely reflects substantial improvements in livestock vaccination programs and veterinary infrastructure development over the study period, starting from higher baseline incidence rates. The more modest declines in Europe and Oceania may indicate these regions had already achieved relatively effective control measures by 2005, resulting in smaller absolute reductions from lower baseline levels.

The loss of statistical significance for Asia after Bonferroni correction likely reflects the high heterogeneity within this broad regional classification, where countries with divergent trends — such as increasing outbreaks in Pakistan and Indonesia alongside sharp declines in Afghanistan and India — attenuate the overall regional signal. This underscores the limitation of broad regional aggregation for anthrax trend analysis, and supports the sub-continental groupings employed in this study.

WAHIS and ProMED provide integrated surveillance for anthrax, combining systematic livestock monitoring with detection of zoonotic transmission events in humans

The concordance in species distribution across two fundamentally different surveillance systems — one mandatory and agriculturally focused, the other informal and public health oriented — provides strong validation that cattle and sheep represent genuinely high-risk populations rather than surveillance artifacts. This consistency likely reflects the biological reality of their grazing behaviour, which maximises soil contact and therefore spore exposure, combined with their numerical dominance in global livestock systems. From a practical standpoint, this finding supports targeting cattle and sheep populations as the primary focus of vaccination programmes and biosecurity interventions, regardless of the surveillance system used to monitor disease burden.

Furthermore, the integration of WAHIS and ProMED surveillance data reveals the complementary strengths of these two monitoring systems for comprehensive anthrax surveillance. WAHIS, as the official mandatory reporting system of WOAH, provides systematic agricultural surveillance focused on livestock populations through designated national veterinary services. This system excels at capturing routine disease burden in domestic animals and tracking long-term epidemiological trends within established reporting frameworks [13]. In contrast, ProMED operates as an event-based surveillance system that primarily captures anthrax incidents with human health implications, including direct human cases, zoonotic transmission events, and animal outbreaks that pose public health risks [12].

The complementary value of these systems is evident in their different surveillance objectives: WAHIS provides comprehensive livestock-focused data with standardized reporting protocols for animal health management, while ProMED captures human-implicated anthrax events that may involve diverse species but are notable primarily due to their zoonotic transmission potential or direct human exposure risks. ProMED's human health focus enables detection of cross-species transmission events, occupational exposures, and outbreak situations that create public health concerns beyond routine agricultural disease monitoring.

The present study identified notable differences in reporting patterns whereby countries such as Türkiye and China reported multiple livestock anthrax cases through WAHIS whilst no corresponding human cases were documented in ProMED during the equivalent temporal period. This pattern may reflect several non-mutually exclusive explanations: differential surveillance sensitivity between animal and human health systems, underreporting of human cases in countries with limited public health infrastructure, genuine variation in zoonotic transmission risk due to differences in occupational exposure or cultural practices involving livestock, or the possibility that robust veterinary surveillance and biosecurity measures in these countries may be effectively limiting zoonotic spillover. While the precise drivers of this pattern cannot be determined from surveillance data alone, it underscores the importance of integrated One Health approaches that systematically link animal and human health reporting to better characterise zoonotic transmission dynamics.

The recent emergence of anthrax in historically low-incidence regions exemplifies the disease's unpredictable nature and challenges conventional risk assessment approaches

In May 2025, Thailand experienced a notable anthrax cluster in Mukdahan Province near the border with Lao PDR, comprising four confirmed cutaneous cases (three males and one female, aged 36–58 years), including one fatality [18]. The outbreak was linked to the slaughter and distribution of two cattle during a communal merit-making event, where direct contact with infected carcasses and contaminated meat was identified as the source of infection. The index patient presented with rapidly progressive lesions, seizures, and lymphadenopathy, and died within hours of hospitalization, while the remaining three patients were successfully treated with levofloxacin and doxycycline. In response, over 600 at-risk individuals were screened, post-exposure prophylaxis was administered, 1222 cattle were vaccinated, and quarantine and movement controls were enforced within a five-kilometer radius. This was Thailand's first anthrax-related death since 1994.

These cases indicate that various environmental and anthropogenic factors, including modifications in agricultural practices, climatic variability, or altered livestock movement patterns, may serve as potential triggers for disease re-emergence in regions with prolonged periods of epidemiological quiescence [18], [19], [20], [21]. This pattern reinforces the challenge of predicting where anthrax might next emerge, even with comprehensive historical data. Such unexpected emergence underscores the importance of maintaining integrated surveillance systems that can detect and respond to outbreaks regardless of a region's historical risk profile [18], [19], [20], [21], [22].

Species-specific anthrax patterns highlight the need for surveillance systems beyond routine livestock monitoring to detect wildlife involvement and environmental contamination

Our study also highlighted that Nigeria, previously recognised for its relatively effective anthrax control measures, experienced a significant multi-state outbreak in 2023 [23]. The outbreak spanned Niger and Lagos States, affecting 173 susceptible animals with test positivity rates exhibiting considerable geographical and demographic variation, ranging from 0.3% to 52.6% depending on location and animal age cohorts. Epidemiological analysis of 1201 examined animals revealed that 85.6% were recently imported from neighbouring countries, with an overall anthrax incidence of 4.0%. Notably, incidence rates demonstrated a marked age-related disparity, with older animals exhibiting significantly higher rates (47.9%) compared to newly introduced livestock (1.3%). Of public health concern, appropriate carcass disposal protocols were implemented for only 18.8% of anthrax-positive animals, whilst the remainder were processed for human consumption, thereby creating substantial zoonotic transmission pathways. This outbreak exposed critical deficiencies in border surveillance systems, unregulated cross-border livestock movements, and suboptimal vaccination coverage, highlighting systemic vulnerabilities in country's anthrax prevention framework [22].

These species-specific epidemiological patterns carry significant implications for surveillance strategy development. Wildlife involvement indicates environmental contamination and suggests potential reservoir maintenance mechanisms operating independently of domestic animal populations, whilst carnivore infections provide evidence of active circulation of contaminated materials within the ecosystem. The expanded taxonomic diversity documented through ProMED surveillance demonstrates the critical value of event-based surveillance systems in detecting atypical epidemiological patterns that may be overlooked by conventional livestock monitoring programmes, which are primarily designed to optimise agricultural productivity rather than provide comprehensive cross-species disease surveillance.

A notable constraint of the present study is its reliance on surveillance systems structurally oriented toward domestic animal and human health, with limited capacity to capture wildlife involvement or environmental reservoir dynamics. WAHIS reporting is largely confined to domestic livestock submitted through national veterinary services, while ProMED captures events generating public health concern, which typically involves human or domestic animal cases. Consequently, the role of wildlife species — including herbivores such as zebra and wildebeest known to sustain anthrax transmission in endemic ecosystems, as well as carnivores such as lions that acquire infection through scavenging [11] — is likely substantially underrepresented in our dataset. Studies in Etosha National Park and Zimbabwe have documented significant anthrax exposure in wildlife populations operating independently of domestic animal transmission cycles [21], [24], suggesting that domestic animal surveillance alone provides an incomplete picture of the ecological drivers sustaining anthrax endemicity. Similarly, environmental contamination through soil spore banks — which can persist for decades and serve as the primary infection source for grazing animals — is not captured by either surveillance system. Future surveillance frameworks should consider integrating environmental sampling and wildlife health monitoring alongside domestic animal reporting to achieve a more ecologically complete understanding of anthrax transmission dynamics.

Conclusion

This study, leveraging global data from WAHIS and ProMED surveillance systems, examines the temporal and spatial trends of anthrax epidemiology from 2005 to 2024. Overall, there is a decreasing trend in reported incidents worldwide, suggesting progress in global disease control efforts. Our analysis demonstrates that while most regions show declining anthrax incidence, persistent endemicity remains in Central Asia, parts of Africa, and Southern Asia, with increasing trends observed in six countries including Türkiye and Haiti, illustrating the heterogeneous nature of anthrax risk globally. The study also highlights the predominance of cattle and sheep as primary hosts across both datasets, underscoring their critical role in the disease's ecology and transmission dynamics. In addition, the consistent identification of cattle and sheep as primary hosts across both surveillance systems provides information for veterinary and public health professionals, while the observed differences in reporting patterns between countries with high livestock incidence and those documenting human cases may reflect differential surveillance capacity or genuine variation in zoonotic transmission dynamics, highlighting the need for more integrated cross-sectoral surveillance.

This study enhances awareness among global health practitioners and policymakers that comprehension of spatial-temporal epidemiological patterns can inform evidence-based decision-making regarding vaccination programme implementation, livestock management protocols, and transboundary disease surveillance initiatives. The documented occurrence of unexpected disease re-emergence in historically low-incidence countries in the regions such as Thailand demonstrates the necessity for sustained epidemiological vigilance, even in areas with previously exemplary disease control records. Furthermore, the persistent endemicity and increasing trends observed in several lower-income countries underscore the ethical imperative for equitable distribution of veterinary resources, vaccination support, and surveillance capacity-building to regions where anthrax burden remains disproportionately high relative to available control infrastructure.

Limitations of the study highlight important considerations for interpreting global anthrax surveillance data

The assumption that missing values in WAHIS data represent zero cases, while justified by the mandatory reporting framework of WOAH, introduces potential bias particularly in countries with weaker surveillance infrastructure. Quantitative assessment of reporting completeness revealed that while regional completeness was consistently high across the study period (93–98% across all world regions), temporal completeness declined substantially in the most recent years, reaching 72.5% in 2023, alongside a reduction in contributing countries to as few as 34 in 2020. This suggests that the apparent stabilisation or continued decline in outbreak numbers observed in the 2020–2024 period may partly reflect incomplete data submission rather than a genuine epidemiological signal, and findings for this period should be interpreted accordingly. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that this assumption influences results for individual countries, most notably Pakistan, whose significant increasing trend in the primary analysis was not retained under complete case analysis. Future analyses should consider multiple imputation approaches to more rigorously account for missing data uncertainty.

Future surveillance strategies must recognise that effective anthrax monitoring requires combining data from multiple systems. Data aggregation across diverse climatic zones and agricultural systems naturally obscures underlying seasonal patterns, as peak risk periods vary considerably between regions. Fundamental differences between surveillance systems present inherent analytical limitations: WAHIS provides mandatory, standardized reporting through official veterinary channels, whilst ProMED captures informal, media-driven outbreak reports potentially subject to reporting biases toward severe or unusual cases. These systems exhibit differential detection sensitivities, with WAHIS potentially underreporting in regions with limited veterinary infrastructure and ProMED potentially overrepresenting outbreaks generating public attention.
