Dynamic task offloading for sports training monitoring in MEC-assisted smart wearable device systems
Authors: Yusheng Zhang, Desheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the development of Internet of Things (IoT) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies, smart wearable devices (SWDs) are regarded as a promising approach for sports training monitoring. However, such monitoring generates a large number of computation-intensive tasks, which SWDs struggle to process in real-time due to limited battery capacity and computing power. Fortunately, the emergence of multi-access edge computing (MEC) offers an effective solution, allowing smart wearable devices to offload tasks to edge servers at the network edge for low-latency, energy-efficient processing. This paper investigates the dynamic task offloading problem in MEC-assisted wearable device systems. By jointly optimizing the offloading decisions, CPU frequency, offloading power of SWDs, and the CPU frequency of edge servers, we aim to minimize the total energy consumption of SWDs while maintaining the queue backlog. Through the Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty optimization framework, the long-term stochastic optimization problem is decoupled into a series of deterministic single-slot subproblems. A lightweight server selection algorithm is proposed to enable adaptive switching to alternative servers during overloads with negligible computational and signaling costs. Then, the problem is further decomposed into multiple sub-problems that can be solved in parallel. Based on this, we propose the Sports Training Monitoring (STM) algorithm to achieve efficient online solutions. Theoretical analysis and experiments indicate that STM can effectively reduce the energy consumption of SWDs while maintaining system performance.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Faced with increasingly fierce international competition in competitive sports, high-level sports training methods are shifting towards technology and technological innovation1. Recent advancements in sports monitoring have greatly benefited from emerging artificial intelligence (AI) and Internet of Things (IoT) technologies, enabling more precise and efficient data collection and analysis2–4. Compared to traditional visual monitoring methods, non-visual monitoring does not require specific angles or shooting environments, and is not limited by the layout of the venue or the installation of equipment, making it highly adaptable to various sports scenarios5. The use of smart wearable devices (SWDs), such as smartwatches, smart bracelets, smart clothing, etc, effectively facilitates non-visual monitoring in sports training, which is better suited to diverse and dynamic environments, allowing athletes to train effectively in various settings without being constrained by cameras or fixed monitoring systems6,7. Additionally, non-visual monitoring with SWDs can track key metrics such as heart rate, respiratory rate, movement patterns, and fatigue levels, providing a more comprehensive understanding of an athlete’s performance and health condition8,9.

However, although the lightweight nature of SWDs provides more possibilities for sports monitoring, their size limits their battery capacity and performance10. As a result, it is difficult for SWDs alone to handle the large amount of computation-intensive monitoring tasks generated during sports activities, especially when some SWDs integrate multiple physiological indicators with motion data and apply machine learning algorithms for in-depth analysis. The traditional approach is generally to use cloud computing to supplement the performance of SWDs. However, due to its high latency, it is challenging to continue real-time processing for latency-sensitive tasks. Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC)11, as a new computing paradigm, provides a promising solution to this problem. MEC deploys edge servers at the network edge, allowing SWDs to offload tasks to these edge servers, effectively solving the issue of limited computing and communication resources in SWDs and improving the quality of service for users12–14.

Utilizing MEC-assisted SWDs in sports monitoring helps overcome the computing resource and battery limitations of SWDs, which enables SWDs to focus on continuous data collection and basic processing, while the edge servers handle more complex computations, ensuring that the SWDs remain lightweight and efficient15. Furthermore, MEC is well-suited for large-scale sports scenarios where multiple athletes need to be monitored simultaneously. The ability to offload and process data from numerous devices at the edge allows for coordinated and comprehensive monitoring across large groups, which is particularly useful in large-scale sports events16.

Nevertheless, the utilization of MEC-assisted smart wearable devices in sports monitoring still faces several challenges. First, environmental information during sports activities exhibits high randomness and uncertainty, making accurate predictions difficult. Consequently, optimization decisions must be made without prior knowledge of specific tasks. Second, there is an inherent conflict between battery capacity and computing performance, necessitating a balanced task offloading and resource allocation strategy under the constraints of limited device capabilities. Furthermore, the sports monitoring process is continuous and dynamic, and long-term constraints on SMDs should be considered to ensure system stability while optimizing energy consumption. Finally, although enabling server switching can enhance system robustness in extreme situations, frequent handovers incur additional overheads that can completely negate the associated benefits.

This paper studies the task offloading problem for MEC-assisted SWDs. We consider a MEC system that includes multiple SWDs and multiple edge servers. The SWDs can choose to process tasks locally or offload partial tasks to nearby available edge servers. Our optimization objective is to minimize the total energy consumption of SWDs while ensuring system queue stability. Our decision variables include the offloading decision of SWDs, CPU frequency, offloading power, and the CPU frequency of the edge servers. We define the problem as a multi-time slot stochastic optimization problem and transform it into a deterministic optimization problem using stochastic optimization techniques. By decoupling different variables, it is transformed into several sub-problems. To solve these subproblems efficiently online, we propose the Sports Training Monitoring (STM) algorithm. Specifically, STM incorporates a lightweight server selection mechanism to enable adaptive switching during overloads with negligible costs, and further decomposes the resource allocation into multiple parallel sub-problems. Additionally, we rigorously prove the gap between the algorithm and the optimal solution as well as the algorithm’s complexity. The main contributions of the paper are as follows:

Related work

In recent years, many studies have focused on using SWDs for sports training monitoring. This discussion proceeds from reviewing related use cases of SWDs in sports monitoring, to energy optimization efforts for these devices, and finally to energy-efficient task offloading solutions that consider dynamic uncertainties. We have also discussed the similarities and differences between our work and existing research.

H. Jiang et al.17 considered applying edge computing to mobile health systems for health monitoring and diagnosis. They proposed a non-interactive privacy-preserving system based on a naive Bayes model, which improves model accuracy while reducing computation and communication overhead. Lauren et al.7 used wearable devices to accurately monitor the quantity and quality of movements in neuromuscular training. They designed a custom peak detection algorithm to extract the repetition count from accelerometer signals during running, jumping, and leaping, which allowed them to assess movement quantity and quality. Alessio et al.18 utilized SWDs to measure various metrics such as the positioning of soccer players, and employed machine learning techniques to build an injury predictor, thereby assessing the relationship between injury risk and training performance. J. Yang et al.19 designed and optimized a sports training system with wireless sensing devices and developed a cloud service computing solution that provided computational resources through cloud computing. D. Sun et al.20 applied SWDs in track and field training to improve and optimize athletic performance, demonstrating that SWDs can assist in enhancing athletic training levels through monitoring. However, none of these works specifically addressed the optimization of SWDs, particularly in situations where SWDs alone cannot support ML-assisted training monitoring tasks.

Various studies have been conducted to address energy efficiency issues in SWDs. M. Xu et al.21 proposed a deep learning framework for wearable devices that strategically offloads DL tasks to other devices to reduce computation energy consumption while preserving user privacy. Y. Chen et al.22 applied MEC and SWD technologies in health monitoring systems, using wearable sensors to receive human parameters. They used a DDPG-based algorithm to address computation offloading issues in healthcare services, significantly reducing transmission delay and energy consumption. Z. Song et al.23 explored collaborative and resource allocation problems for fog computing-assisted wearable health devices, proposing a quantum-inspired bean optimization method to optimize spectrum and computing resources. C. Wang et al.24 studied distributed wearable devices with edge intelligence for health monitoring. They designed a device collaboration offloading framework and introduced a graph-based DAG algorithm to reduce energy consumption for wearable devices. Considering the benefits of invoking vehicular resources in highly dynamic load environments, H. Jiang et al.25 designed a novel vehicle-assisted VEC network architecture and further proposed VaCo to support cooperative vehicular caching.

G. Fang et al.26 proposed an MEC offloading method for SWDs. They used a combinatorial optimization framework and developed the OEA algorithm for high-efficiency task offloading, focusing on whether task processing periods were determined. J. Mei et al.27 investigated the task offloading problem in MEC systems with multiple energy-harvesting (EH) devices and designed a Lyapunov-based maximum computation task offloading algorithm to maximize system throughput. H. Han et al.28 investigated task offloading in MEC-assisted IoT networks, considering energy and computational limitations of IoT devices, and proposed an energy-efficient algorithm that balances energy consumption and latency. Yang et al.29 studied task offloading for MEC systems supported by NOMA, taking into account system dynamics and group-level interference under SCN technology. They proposed the CADTO algorithm to reduce overall system carbon emissions while maintaining stable performance. L. Xu et al.30 explored dynamic task offloading for MEC systems assisted by energy harvesting (EH). They designed an energy prediction model based on an LSTM algorithm, effectively improving system energy efficiency. L. Dai et al.31 addressed the problem of efficient task offloading in MEC environments and proposed LyFOTO, a Lyapunov-guided federated DRL online task offloading algorithm, which can adaptively regulate system throughput according to communication conditions.

Although these studies have made significant contributions to sports training monitoring, SWD applications, and energy-efficient task offloading, they do not adequately address the dynamic task offloading challenges faced by MEC-assisted SWD systems. Lightweight server selection algorithm

It is worth noting that a single edge server may experience sudden workload bursts not only due to random task arrivals, but also because the physical movement of athletes can cause multiple SWDs to cluster near the same server in a short time. A straightforward way to alleviate this problem is to switch the offloading target to another nearby edge server. However, many Lyapunov-based MEC task-offloading studies either ignore server switching or simplify the problem by assuming that each device offloads to a fixed edge server. By contrast, some DRL-based methods obtain a server-selection decision in every time slot and then treat that decision as a constant in the subsequent resource-allocation process. Although such a design is flexible, it often relies heavily on retraining when the environment changes, which limits its effectiveness in highly dynamic sports training scenarios.

Moreover, server switching in practical MEC systems is not free. Handover usually involves signaling exchange, context migration, and connection re-establishment, which introduce extra latency and non-negligible energy consumption. To mitigate these practical costs, we incorporate a lazy switching mechanism into the server selection algorithm. As shown in Algorithm 1, the SWD strictly maintains its current server association as long as the queue backlog of the current server does not exceed a preset safety threshold. Only when the current server becomes congested does the device trigger lightweight probing, scan the nearby available edge servers, and select a new candidate with low latency and acceptable backlog. In this way, the proposed framework minimizes unnecessary handovers at the mechanism level while still retaining the ability to adapt to dynamic workload variations.

Effect of the weighting factor V on algorithm performance

The purpose of this experiment is to examine how the control parameter V affects the energy-performance tradeoff of the proposed STM algorithm. Within the stochastic optimization framework, the size of V can be adjusted to balance the relationship between queue backlog and energy consumption optimization. We simulate a MEC-assisted smart wearable device system with 100 SWDs and 5 ESs running for 3000 time slots, and we test the effect of V on queue backlog and energy consumption optimization using values ranging from to . By observing fig.2 and fig. 3, we can see that as V increases, the energy consumption of the device gradually decreases, which aligns with our reasoning. As V acts as the weight coefficient for energy consumption E(t), an increase in V implies that the algorithm tends to favor an energy-saving state. This, however, inevitably results in an increase in queue backlog to some extent. Nonetheless, as the queue backlog reaches a certain threshold, it gradually converges. By observing fig. 2 and fig. 3, we can select different V values to meet various scenario requirements.

Impact of scalability on algorithm performance

To adapt to different sports training monitoring tasks, the algorithm needs to be evaluated with various scales, specifically including variations in task arrival rates and device counts. We conduct a series of experiments to demonstrate how environmental scale impacts algorithm performance.

As shown in Fig. 4, we use data from the Sport Database as the baseline task arrival rate per time slot, simulating scenarios with 80%, 100% and 120% arrival rates. When sports training monitoring tasks arrive at different rates, the algorithm consistently converges after a certain number of time slots. This demonstrates that our algorithm can handle varying levels of task arrival rates, and the size of tasks does not significantly affect convergence speed. Furthermore, a higher task arrival rate inevitably results in greater energy consumption by SWDs. As depicted in Fig. 5, changes in task arrival rate also influence the degree of queue backlog, increasing or decreasing accordingly. However, after some time, the algorithm is able to stabilize the queue backlog, keeping it controlled at a constant level.

Comparison experiment

To validate the overall advantage of STM against representative baseline and learning-based methods, the comparison experiments are conducted. It shows that STM not only reduces the energy consumption of SWDs, but also preserves queue stability and achieves a better balance between efficiency and performance.

In this subsection, we compare the performance of the STM algorithm with four other algorithms in the same environment. Each algorithm is tested over 1600 time slots to evaluate their effectiveness in reducing energy consumption and maintaining queue stability. As can be seen from the experimental results in Fig. 8, the energy consumption of the CLO and OO algorithms is significantly higher than that of the other three, due to the cubic increase in energy as CPU frequency cycles increase. Excessively assigning tasks to either side leads to a rapid surge in energy consumption. Among the three low-energy algorithms, the proposed STM and CTOTO algorithms clearly outperform the RC algorithm.

From the experimental results in Fig. 9, it can also be observed that the OO and RC algorithms cannot effectively ensure queue stability in our experimental setup, as the computing performance provided by these two methods is insufficient to handle the tasks arriving in each time slot in a timely manner. Additionally, although the CLO algorithm can maintain a very small queue backlog, it results in nearly 8 times the energy consumption of the STM algorithm, which is hardly acceptable in practical applications. The queue backlog of the DTOTO algorithm is similar to that of the proposed STM algorithm, but its energy consumption is higher than that of the proposed algorithm. Ultimately, we choose STM algorithm as the recommended algorithm, as it performs best in reducing energy consumption and controlling the queue. In summary, the comparative experiments demonstrate that the STM algorithm can effectively reduce device energy consumption while ensuring system stability.

Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed a dynamic task offloading problem for sports training monitoring in MEC-assisted smart wearable device systems. Considering the randomness and dynamic nature of the environment and channel conditions, we reformulated the original problem as a stochastic optimization problem. We employed stochastic optimization techniques to transform the stochastic problem into a deterministic one and decomposed it into three subproblems. We proposed a lightweight algorithm to derive optimal offloading decisions. By jointly optimizing the CPU frequencies of SWDs and edge servers, as well as their offloading power, we developed the STM algorithm to minimize total energy consumption while maintaining system performance. Theoretical analysis and experimental results demonstrate that STM outperforms other state-of-the-art algorithms by effectively reducing energy consumption and maintaining queue stability. Thus, STM serves as a promising solution for enhancing the energy efficiency and performance of smart wearable devices in real-time sports training scenarios. Although the proposed STM algorithm has demonstrated good real-time decision-making capability and practical applicability through theoretical analysis and simulation results, its deployment in real sports training environments still requires further investigation. In future work, we will implement the proposed approach on practical smart wearable platforms and MEC-enabled testbeds, and further evaluate its performance under realistic factors.
