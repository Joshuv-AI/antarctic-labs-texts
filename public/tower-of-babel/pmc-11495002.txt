Mobile phone addiction and self-injury in adolescents with MDD: mediation by self-esteem and depression, and the failure of usage restrictions
Authors: Xiaoqian Liu, Nan Yan, Lan Wang, Kongliang He, Wei Zhang, Xuankun Zhang, Tian Lan, Jiesi Wang, Yongjie Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have suggested a significant association between mobile phone addiction (MPA) and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in general adolescents. However, limited research has analyzed this relationship in clinical populations, such as those with major depressive disorder (MDD), and the potential mediation mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study analyzed data from 2343 adolescents with MDD (77.9% females; mean age = 14.99 years, SD = 1.65). Using mediation models, we explored the roles of self-esteem and depression severity (measured by Patient Health Questionnaire-9) in the relationship between MPA and NSSI. Additionally, we evaluated the associations between existing school-implemented mobile phone usage policies and levels of MPA, self-esteem, depression symptoms, and NSSI.ResultsMPA was significantly associated with NSSI, with low self-esteem and increased depression severity almost entirely mediating this effect (log-odds = 0.016, 95%CI = 0.02–0.013). Subdomains of MPA, including inability to control craving, feelings of anxiety, and productivity loss, followed similar mediation models, while the subdomain of withdrawal/escape had a partial effect mediated by depression severity. Additionally, enforced restrictions on mobile phone usage in schools did not show positive effects on related variables and even exacerbated MPA by increasing cravings and reducing productivity.ConclusionOur study suggests that MPA in adolescents with MDD may be an important risk factor for NSSI occurrence, and that reducing the negative impact of mobile phone usage on self-esteem and depressive symptoms, rather than simply restricting usage, may be crucial for effective intervention.Keywords: Non-suicidal self-injury, Mobile phone addiction, Self-esteem, Depression, Adolescent, Mobile phone usage restriction

Background

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) refers to the intentional self-infliction of injury without suicidal intent, typically through cutting or scratching the skin, pulling hair, or hitting oneself [1]. Adolescents are a high-risk population for NSSI; according to a meta-analysis conducted in 2023, the average prevalence rate of self-injury among general adolescents was 16%, with a higher rate of 19.4% observed in Asian populations [2]. In clinical adolescents, such as those with major depressive disorder (MDD) or bipolar disorder, prevalence rates range from 30 to 80% [3–5]. Intervention methods for NSSI, particularly among adolescents, remain limited [4]. Although several theories attempt to explain the occurrence of NSSI [6], and many factors—from genetic to societal to psychological—have been linked to NSSI [6–8], the relationships among different risk factors and the underlying pathological mechanisms remain unclear.

Mobile phone addiction (MPA), also known as 'mobile phone dependence' or 'problematic mobile phone use,' refers to excessive mobile phone use and typical addictive behaviors, such as withdrawal, cravings, anxiety, and loss of control when the phone is unavailable [9, 10]. Despite the convenience mobile phones offer, their problematic use has become a global public health issue [11, 12], with over 30% of adolescents excessively reliant on mobile phones [12]. Several studies have indicated that MPA is significantly associated with NSSI occurrence in adolescents [13–16], but the underlying reasons for this association are not well understood. The association of MPA with NSSI may arise from deficits in emotion regulation and stress coping, both of which are maladaptive strategies [16]. Moreover, MPA may have multiple negative impacts on mental health, potentially increasing the risk of NSSI [12, 15].

MPA may impact adolescent mental health in multiple ways. First, MPA may induce emotional problems, such as depression, anxiety, and disturbed sleep quality [17, 18], which may further worse symptoms in patients with mental disorders. Second, MPA often accompanies unhealthy social comparison [19], which may decreased self-esteem [20, 21], a crucial factor for mental well-being. Third, MPA-induced excessive phone use reduces face-to-face interactions and meaningful social connections, potentially leading to social isolation and loneliness [22, 23]. Finally, MPA creates psychological dependency, leading to stress and withdrawal symptoms, such as anxiety, when phone use is restricted [9, 16]. According to the theories of NSSI [24, 25], several potential reasons for NSSI occurrence include emotion dysregulation, social dysregulation and self-punishment desires. The negative effects of MPA mentioned above may fuel these potential reasons or reduce barriers. For example, MPA-induced depression, anxiety or loneliness may worsen emotion regulation; lower self-esteem and productivity loss may induce self-punishment; and disrupted social connections due to MPA may aggravate the social dysregulation. However, whether the association of MPA with NSSI is mediated by these potential paths remains under-investigated.

In the present study, we investigated the relationship between MPA and NSSI, along with potential mediational factors, in a large multi-center population (n = 2343) of adolescent patients (ages 12 to 18) with MDD. Previous studies on this association have mainly focused on healthy populations. However, clinical adolescents exhibit higher rates of MPA and NSSI, yet related studies are still limited. In this population of adolescents with MDD, we first confirmed the associations among MPA, NSSI, self-esteem, and depression severity. Second, we used a sequential mediation model to analyze whether self-esteem and depression severity mediate the effect of MPA on NSSI. Finally, we investigated whether limiting mobile phone usage through school enforcement regulations could benefit MPA and NSSI.

Methods

Participants

A total of 2343 adolescent patients with MDD were recruited from 16 psychiatric hospitals across different cities in China. All patients were diagnosed by two psychiatrists using the Chinese version of the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID). Due to the non-systematic nature of the recruitment process employed in this study, the participation rate had not been measured. The mean age of the patients was 14.99 ± 1.65 years (range, 12–18 years). Of these patients, 77.9% identified as female, reflecting the demographics of adolescent depression patients. Additionally, 1,071 individuals (45.7% of the total) were residential students who self-reported that they could not use mobile phones daily due to enforcement by boarding school regulations.

Measures

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI)

The Functional Assessment of Self-Mutilation (FASM) [26, 27] was used to assess the history of NSSI within the past year and the specific NSSI behaviors engaged in. The FASM is a self-report instrument with two sections: (1) a list of 11 common methods of NSSI (e.g., cutting, burning, banging/hitting self), including their frequency and the need for medical treatment; and (2) a list of different functions of NSSI (e.g., to punish yourself, to get help, to stop feeling bad). Only Sect. 1 of the FASM was used in the current study. Participants with a history of NSSI in the past year were identified by endorsing or denying (“yes” or “no”) the item “Have you ever engaged in nonsuicidal self-injury within the past year?”.

Mobile phone addiction (MPA)

The tendency for mobile phone addiction was assessed using the Chinese version of the Mobile Phone Addiction Index (MPAI), originally developed by Leung in 2008 [10] and translated and adapted by Huang et al. in 2014 [28]. The MPAI is a self-report questionnaire consisting of 17 items across four sub-dimensions: losing control and receiving complaints (ICC), feeling anxious and lost (FAL), withdrawal/escape (WE), and productivity loss (PL). Participants rated each item on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (always). Total MPAI scores (ranging from 17 to 85) and scores for the four sub-dimensions were calculated, with higher scores indicating greater intensity of mobile phone use. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the total score was 0.89, and the coefficients for the four sub-dimensions were 0.83, 0.77, 0.71, and 0.77, respectively.

Self-esteem

Self-esteem was assessed using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES), a self-report scale with 10 items (Rosenberg, 1965) [29]. Participants rated each item on a four-point Likert scale, choosing from 1 (completely inconsistent) to 4 (completely consistent). After reverse coding the negatively worded items (item 8 was not reversed in Chinese version [30]), higher scores indicated higher self-esteem. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.90.

Depressive severity

Depressive severity was evaluated by the scale of Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [31, 32]. This scale is a 9-item self-report scale, with 4-point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day). Responses on the 9 items are summed and a higher score indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.90.

Statistical analysis

The data for continuous variables were described as means and standard deviations (SD), while the data for categorical variables were described as numbers and percentages. Differences between groups were compared using t-tests or one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-square (χ2) tests for categorical variables.

The relationships between pairs of study variables in the two groups (NSSI and no NSSI) were assessed by Pearson’s r coefficient, and the normal distribution of continuous variables was checked using Quantile–Quantile plots. A mediation regression analysis with 5000 bootstrapped bias-corrected samples, using the PROCESS v4.3 macro in SPSS (Hayes, 2018), was conducted to test the mediating effects of self-esteem and depressive severity on the association between total scores of MPA and its four sub-dimensions on NSSI. All analyses using PROCESS or ANOVA were adjusted for the potential confounding effects of age and sex.

IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for all statistical analyses. All calculated P values were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at a level of < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and psychological variables between NSSI group and non-NSSI group

In this study, a total of 1782 (76.1%) participants reported a history of NSSI within the past year, which identified as NSSI group, the others identified as None-NSSI group. The most common method of NSSI was cutting or carving (87.0%), followed by hitting on purpose (55.4%), banging (50.3%), biting (47.9%), and scraping skin (44.4%). Table 1 presents the demographic and psychological characteristics of the NSSI and no NSSI groups. In terms of demographics, based on the results from independent t-tests or chi-square tests, the NSSI group had a significantly higher proportion of females (χ2 = 77.09, p < 0.001) and a lower mean age (t = -7.22, p < 0.001) compared to the no NSSI group. The groups did not differ significantly in terms of education level, ethnicity, or residence.

Regarding psychological characteristics, the t-test results indicated that all variables showed significant differences between the groups. Individuals with NSSI exhibited higher severity of depression (PHQ-9 score, t = 13.9, p < 0.001), lower self-esteem (t = -15.15, p < 0.001). NSSI group also showed higher level of MPA, as represented by the more individuals meeting the MPA criteria (χ2 = 9.125, p = 0.003), higher MPA total score (t = 5.88, p < 0.001), and more high-level PMA individuals (χ2 = 26.197, p < 0.001). Additionally, all MPA subscale scores were higher in NSSI group compare to non-NSSI group, including inability to control craving (ICC, t = 5.06, p < 0.001), feeling anxious & lost (FAL, t = 7.67, p < 0.001), withdrawal/escape (WE, t = 3.45, p < 0.001) and productivity loss (PL, t = 1.67, p < 0.001).

Correlations between variables

Table 2 presents the partial correlation coefficients (Pearson R) between severity of depression, self-esteem, and MPA scores in the non-NSSI group and NSSI group, after controlling for age and sex. In both groups, the PHQ-9 scores were significantly positively correlated with MPA, including the total score and four subscale scores (all p < 0.001). In contrast, self-esteem was significantly negatively correlated with the MPA total score, as well as the ICC, FAL, and PL subscale scores (all p < 0.05), but not with the WE subscale, in both groups. The PHQ-9 scores and self-esteem also showed strongly negative correlation in both groups.

The sequence mediation effect of self-esteem and depression from total MPA to NSSI

The model specifying the association between total MPA and NSSI occurrence via self-esteem and depression severity was significant (ModelLL = 315, p < 0.001; Fig. 1). MPA, as the independent variable (X), was significantly associated with the first mediator (M1), self-esteem (β = -0.115, p < 0.001), and the second mediator (M2), PHQ-9, after controlling for M1 (β = 0.097, p < 0.001). M1 was also significantly associated with M2 after controlling for MPA (β = -0.661, p < 0.001). Additionally, both mediators were significantly associated with the dependent variable (Y), NSSI (Self-esteem: log-odds = -0.084, p < 0.001; PHQ-9: log-odds = 0.039, p < 0.001). As showed in Table 3, the three indirect paths (Ind1: X → M1 → Y (a1*b1); Ind2: X → M2 → Y (a2*b2); Ind3: X → M1 → M2 → Y (a1*d21*b2)) were all significant; the direct effect of MPA on NSSI occurrence is not statistic significant. Thus, self-esteem and depression severity fully mediated the relationship between total MPA and NSSI occurrence.

The sequence mediation effect of self-esteem and depression from MPA sub-dimensions to NSSI

To gain further insight into the specific associations between MPA and NSSI occurrence, the sequence mediation models of four sub-dimensions of MPA on NSSI were conducted. As showed in Fig. 2, all the mediation models were significant (ICC: ModelLL = 314.39, p < 0.001; FAL: ModelLL = 317.18, p < 0.001; WE: ModelLL = 318.66, p < 0.001; PL: ModelLL = 316.26, p < 0.001). The independent variables, including ICC, FAL, and PL, but not WE, were significantly associated with self-esteem (M1) (ICC, FAL, PL, p < 0.001; WE, p = 0.212). Additionally, all these subscales were significantly associated with PHQ-9 (M2) after controlling for M1 (all p < 0.001). M1 was also significantly associated with M2 after controlling for different MPA subscales (all p < 0.001). Moreover, both mediators were significantly associated with the dependent variable, NSSI, in all four mediation models (all p < 0.001). As shown in Table 3, only the WE subscale showed a significant direct effect on NSSI beyond covariances with M1 and M2. The total indirect effect and the Ind2 path (X → M2 → Y) were significant in all models, while the Ind1 (X → M1 → Y) and Ind3 (X → M1 → M2 → Y) paths were significant in models with ICC, FAL, and PL, but not WE. These results suggest that self-esteem and depression severity fully mediated the relationship between the MPA sub-dimensions ICC, FAL, and PL, and NSSI occurrence; however, for the WE sub-dimension, only depression severity showed a partial mediation effect.

The effect of mobile phone usage restrictions on psychological variables and NSSI

To explore the potential impact of mobile phone usage restrictions on MPA and related outcomes, we further analyzed whether controlling daily mobile phone usage by school had any effects. In present study, there were 1,071 individuals (45.7% of the total) were residential students who could not return home daily, and the schools-imposed regulations deprived their mobile phone usage during school, which resulting the inability to use phones every day. As shown in Table 4, after controlled with age and sex, controlling phone usage did not have a significant effect on PHQ-9 scores, self-esteem, or the ratio of NSSI occurrence. Moreover, surprisingly, phone usage restrictions significantly increased the level of MPA, including the increased proportion of individuals meeting the MPA criteria (χ2 = 8.463, p = 0.004), the total score (F = 8.952, p = 0.003), ICC subscale (F = 12.586, p < 0.001), and PL subscale (t = 8.785, p = 0.002). These results suggest that limited daily mobile phone usage by school authorities seems to be unhelpful for MPA and related psychological issues, and even potentially detrimental for MPA.

Discussion

In a population of 2343 adolescents with MDD, we found that MPA was significantly associated with NSSI occurrence. According to the results of the mediation analysis, MPA-induced low self-esteem and increased depression severity almost entirely mediated the effect of MPA on NSSI occurrence. This mediation effect also existed in three subdomains of MPA: inability to control cravings, feelings of anxiety and loss, and productivity loss. The subdomain of withdrawal/escape did not show a significant effect on self-esteem, and its effect on NSSI was partially mediated by depression severity. Additionally, we found that limiting mobile phone usage through school regulations moderately increased MPA but did not significantly influence self-esteem, depressive symptoms, or NSSI occurrence.

In the current study, 76.1% of patients reported having engaged in NSSI behavior more than once during the past year, which is much higher than the rates reported in healthy adolescent populations—approximately 19% in the Asian population [2] and 24.7% in mainland China [33] according to recent meta-analyses. This may be because, as previous studies have indicated, depressive symptoms increase the risk of NSSI [34, 35]. Our mediation analysis results also support this finding. Meanwhile, in the current population, 47.8% of patients were identified as having MPA according to the survey scale. Although there is no common standard for identifying MPA, the prevalence may vary depending on the scale used. Our results indicate a high level of MPA compared to previous studies on healthy adolescents, which report prevalence rates ranging from 6 to 30% [36, 37]. This high level of MPA may also reflect the significant association between MPA and depression [17]. Additionally, our results further indicated that MPA may significantly impact NSSI occurrence. According to the mediation analysis results, combining the direct and indirect effects (total log-odds = 0.02, OR = 1.02), each additional unit of the MPA total score increases the odds of NSSI occurrence by approximately 2%. This means that individuals with high-level MPA (total score ≥ 65) have odds of NSSI occurrence that are at least 2.014 times greater than those with low-level MPA (total score < 30). The effects of the four subdomains on NSSI were also all significant.

Our mediation model also indicated that self-esteem and depressive symptoms almost entirely mediated the effect of MPA on NSSI occurrence, with only the subdomain of withdrawal/escape showing some degree of direct effect. These findings suggest that the self-esteem and depressive symptoms may be the main underlying meditators for the increase in NSSI caused by MPA. Interestingly, our results also indicated that the path of In1 (X—> self-esteem—> Y) had the largest effect in the mediation model, except for the withdrawal/escape subscale. When considering the effect of In3 (X—> self-esteem—> depression severity—> Y), these results suggest that MPA-induced lower self-esteem may be the core problem. This is largely because self-esteem had a much larger impact on NSSI than depression severity, as indicated by the log-odds of self-esteem on NSSI (-0.082 to -0.085) compared to the log-odds of depression severity (0.037 to 0.043). Low self-esteem has been identified as a crucial risk factor for NSSI occurrence in non-clinical adolescents by previous studies [38–40]. Our results further indicate that even in adolescents with MDD, the impact of self-esteem on NSSI seems greater than that of depressive symptoms. MPA may reduce self-esteem through multiple pathways, such as unhealthy social comparison [21], body image dissatisfaction [41, 42], reduced academic achievement [43], and disrupted social connections [44]. Meanwhile, it also cannot be ruled out that the relationship between MPA and self-esteem or depression severity may be influenced by other confounding factors, such as environmental or social stress, which could simultaneously lead to an increase in MPA and a decrease in self-esteem. Nevertheless, these results still implied that it may be more effective to intervene in these issues rather than simply trying to decrease screen time, especially since our results showed no positive effect of limiting mobile phone usage through school regulations.

Reducing screen time has been suggested as an important method to mitigate the negative impact of mobile phone usage, especially for adolescents [45, 46]. Hundreds of apps and platforms have been developed methods to control screen time, particularly for children and adolescents [47]. However, the effectiveness of these methods remains contentious [45, 48]. Some previous studies have shown that reducing screen time or social media usage in various ways provides some benefits for mental health [42, 48]. However, there are limited studies investigating the effect of reducing phone usage in clinical adolescents. In most Chinese schools, regulations are enforced to limit mobile phone use, especially in boarding schools, where students are significantly restricted from using their phones daily. However, our results showed that for adolescents with MDD, these enforced regulations did not show any positive effects on MPA or related mental health issues. In fact, the patients from boarding school exhibited a higher degree of MPA, characterized by increased cravings for phone usage and reduced productivity. While these differences may be attributed to factors such as diminished social connections with family between boarding and day schools, this still suggest that enforced reductions in mobile phone usage may not be an effective way to resolve the MPA problem. Alternatively, schools might consider implementing exercise interventions or increasing physical activities of students. Previous studies have shown that physical exercise can effectively reduce MPA [49], boost self-esteem [50] and lower levels of depression [51].

There are also some limitations in the present study. First, as a cross-sectional study, the cause-and-effect relationships suggested by our mediation model should be interpreted cautiously. Because these results can only serve to support our hypothesis and cannot proved the causal relationships between the variables, and the associations between variables may also be driven by other cofounders, which need to be verified by further cohort or intervention studies. Second, our results were based on an adolescent population with MDD, which may differ from healthy populations. Third, the variables used in this study rely entirely on self-reported data from participants, which may be subject to bias. For example, some participants may not have accurately reported their mobile phone usage.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that MPA in adolescents with MDD may be an important risk factor for NSSI occurrence, and that reducing the negative impact of mobile phone usage on self-esteem and depressive symptoms, rather than simply restricting usage, may be crucial for effective intervention. For the clinical treatment with adolescent NSSI, we recommend considering MPA as a target for integrated assessment and behavioral therapy. Additionally, psychoeducation for patients’ families should emphasize that addressing MPA requires more supportive and proactive involvement, rather than solely relying on usage restrictions.
