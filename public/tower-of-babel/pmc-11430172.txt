Isolation, Molecular Characterisation, and Pathogenicity Analysis of a Novel Recombinant ALV‐J Strain Isolated From Chinese Hetian Chickens
Authors: Tianyu Lei, Liyun Zhuang, Tingting Dai, Qun Niu, Yinli Bao, Weiming Lin, Cuiqin Huang, Xintian Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAvian leukosis virus subgroup J (ALV‐J) primarily affects poultry, particularly chickens, leading to tumourigenesis and immunosuppression, which results in substantial economic losses. It is important to note that ALV‐J is commonly found in indigenous chicken breeds in China, and the virus's vertical transmission characteristics present a significant threat to the preservation of local chicken breeds.ObjectivesThe study aimed to investigate the characteristics and effects of the recombinant ALV‐J strain LY2021J, with a focus on its genetic composition and its potential influence on virulence and pathogenicity.Methods LY2021J was isolated using DF‐1 cells and validated by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and IFA. The proviral genome was amplified using segmented PCR and then spliced together using DNASTAR software. Genome‐wide genes, including gag, pol, gp85, and long terminal repeat (LTR), were compared. Recombination sites were analysed using RDP5 and SimPlot software. Pathogenicity was evaluated by monitoring symptoms and conducting examinations on SPF chickens.ResultsThe outbreak of ALV‐J in China has caused significant economic losses in the poultry industry. Although largely controlled in white‐feather broilers and egg‐laying chickens, ALV‐J has spread to yellow‐feather broilers and local breeds. A strain, LY2021J, isolated from Hetian chickens, showed lower mortality despite severe dysplasia. Genetic analysis revealed high similarity between LY2021J and the Chinese strains JS14NT01 and NX0101, suggesting a shared origin. Recombination with strain ev‐1 and specific 3′ UTR deletions may explain LY2021J's reduced virulence. Continued monitoring and prevention strategies are essential to mitigate ALV‐J's impact.Keywords: avian leukosis virus subgroup J (ALV‐J), China, pathogenicity, recombinant, viral isolation

Introduction

Avian leukosis virus (ALV) is classified within the genus Alpha‐retroviruses of the family Retroviridae (Fadly 1997). It can be transmitted both vertically and horizontally and is known to induce tumours, immunosuppression, reduced productivity and even mortality in chickens raised in commercial poultry operations (Zhou, Xue, and Zhang 2019). ALV is categorized into different subgroups on the basis of the characteristics of its viral envelope protein. It can also be classified as either exogenous or endogenous viruses depending on the transmission model. Exogenous ALV includes subgroups ALV‐A, ALV‐B, ALV‐C, ALV‐D, ALV‐K and ALV‐J, with ALV‐A, ALV‐B and ALV‐J being the primary subgroups responsible for avian neoplastic diseases worldwide (Zhang, Su, and Zhang 2020). Avian leukosis has caused significant economic losses to the poultry industry, and there are currently no effective means for prevention or treatment. Both attenuated and inactivated vaccines have proven ineffective in preventing ALV infection (Feng and Zhang 2016).

ALV is a typical retrovirus with a lipid envelope, belonging to the RNA virus group. ALV particles exhibit a nearly spherical structure and typically measure 80–120 nm in diameter (Bova, Manfredi, and Swanstrom 1986). The ALV genome is approximately 7.5 kb in size and consists of three main coding genes–gag, pol and env–as well as the non‐coding 5′ UTR and 3′ UTR regions at both terminals (Payne 1992). The gag gene encodes the viral matrix (MA), capsid (CA) and nucleoproteins (NC), whereas the pol gene encodes functional proteins that are relatively stable. The env gene encodes a glycoprotein that plays a crucial role in determining host susceptibility and virus neutralization and forms the basis for classifying ALV subgroups. The envelope protein of ALV‐J, encoded by the env gene, is divided into gp85 and gp37 subunits (Venugopal 1999). The gp85 protein is particularly prone to mutations (Chen, Wang, and Fang 2020). The 5′ UTR and 3′ UTR of ALV include the long terminal repeats (LTRs), which, despite not encoding proteins (Navarro, Mallen, and Hueso 2021), are highly mutation‐prone regions involved in the synthesis and integration of ALV proviral cDNA into the host genome (Wu, Li, and Chen 2022).

Avian leukosis is widespread among commercial broilers, egg‐laying hens and local chicken breeds in China, with infection and incidence rates increasing annually (Li, Wang, and Lin 2019). In addition to causing tumour‐induced mortality, most infected chickens exhibit subclinical symptoms that adversely affect production performance. These symptoms include delayed sexual maturity, reduced egg production, lower fertilization and hatching rates and immunosuppression, which can lead to secondary bacterial infections and other viral diseases. Consequently, this impacts the biosecurity of poultry farming (Deng, Li, and He 2021). Currently, ALV‐J is detected in nearly all regions and various indigenous chicken breeds across China (Chesters, Smith, and Nair 2006). However, data on ALV‐J in Fujian are notably scarce, similar to other avian diseases. The Hetian chicken, a distinctive traditional breed from Fujian with unique characteristics, is of significant importance for resource evaluation and utilization (Cui, Du, and Zhang 2003). Investigating ALV infection in Hetian chickens, as with other indigenous breeds, offers a valuable area of research. This report presents our preliminary findings on virus isolation and sequencing, which are anticipated to enhance our understanding of the fundamental characteristics of ALV in Hetian chickens.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Samples

Suspected cases of avian leukosis were sampled from a Riverine chicken farm in Longyan City, Fujian Province, China. The affected chickens were predominantly 30 days old, with an incidence rate of 70%. Clinical symptoms included stunted growth, weight loss, pale combs and abdominal swelling. Post‐mortem examination of the affected chickens revealed tumours on the surface of the liver and necrotic lesions in the spleen. Livers and spleens were collected for PCR assays and virus isolation. The tissues were thoroughly homogenized, then stored at −80°C and subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles. After the addition of 1 mL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) buffer, the samples were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane (Sangon Biotech, China) and used for virus isolation, whereas the pellet was utilized for genomic DNA extraction.

Virus Isolation

In brief, the supernatant from homogenized liver tissue of diseased chickens was inoculated into DF‐1 cells in a 96‐well plate. Two hours after inoculation at 37°C, the supernatant was replaced with Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 1% bovine serum. The DF‐1 cells were then cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 7 days (Li, Fu, and Cui 2017).

Forty minutes prior to testing, the infected DF‐1 cells were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles and then tested for viral presence using an antigen capture enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with an ALV p27 antigen test kit (Ruifan, China). Additionally, the virus was assessed by an immunofluorescence assay. DF‐1 cells were fixed in cold acetone (−20°C) for 20 min and subsequently incubated with a gp85 G2 mouse monoclonal antibody (Bioss, China) specific to ALV‐J for 45 min at 37°C. Following this, the cells were washed three times with (PBS, pH 7.2) and then stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG (Bioss) at a 1:100 dilution. After air‐drying, the cells were examined under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) at 200× magnification. Plates were washed with PBS (Sangon Biotech) before viewing. Samples exhibiting specific cytoplasmic fluorescence were considered positive.

PCR Identification

Total viral RNA was extracted from cell supernatant (Pricella, China) and reverse transcribed to cDNA using a Hiscript III Reverse Transcriptase kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subtypes of the positive samples were detected by PCR, and primers are listed in Table 1. PCR conditions were 5 min at 95°C followed by 30 cycles of 45 s at 95°C, 30 s at 55°C, 20 s at 72°C and a final elongation for 10 min at 72°C. The PCR products were visualized via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and ethidium bromide staining. Samples with a 350 bp band were considered positive.

Genome Amplification, Cloning and Sequencing

PCR was performed with specific primers to amplify the LTR, gag, pol and env regions of ALV‐J (Table 2). The PCR was performed in a reaction mixture containing 10 µL of ALV‐J DNA, 1 µL of each primer (50 pmol), 10 µL of 5× buffer (12.5 mM MgCl2), 2 µL of dNTP mix, 2 µL of enzyme mix (Taq polymerase) and 24 µL of distilled water. The 35 cycles of 95°C for 45 s, 50°C for 45 s and 72°C for 1 min were broken up by a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Then, 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and ethidium bromide staining were used to visualize the PCR results. According to the manufacturer's instructions, all PCR products that had been purified using the gel extraction kit were ligated into the pGEM‐T Easy vector system (Promega, USA). The recombinant vectors were transformed into amplified Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells. Positive clones were confirmed by bacterial PCR and were sent to Sangon Biotech for sequencing.

Sequence Analysis

The DNASTAR package's SeqMan program was used to splice the genomic nucleotide sequences of the gene fragments. Complete genomic segments were multiple‐sequence aligned with other ALVs using the NCBI BLAST software. The MEGA version 11.0 programme was used to construct a neighbour‐joining phylogenetic tree for the two isolates using 1000 bootstrap replicates. The same programs were used to investigate the isolates' LTR, pol, env and gag genes. The reference ALV strains are presented in Table 3 along with several strains from the other six ALV subgroups, including the prototype strain HPRS‐103 as well as American, Chinese, Russian and Nigerian strains. To evaluate the reliability of recombination events in the LY2021J nucleotide sequence, the Kimura two‐parameter model was applied with 100 bootstrap replicates. The recombination events were then mapped and assessed for accuracy. Recombination detection programme version 5 (RDP5) and the SimPlot software (V3.5.1, USA) were used to further confirm the presence of potential recombination.

Pathogenicity Experiment

The animal experiment was performed following the guidelines for experimental animals of the Ministry of Science and Technology (Beijing, China). The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Longyan University (Permit No. LY2021009x). Forty 2‐day‐old SPF chickens with negative ALV test results were selected and randomly divided into two groups of 20 chickens each. In the first group, 0.5 mL of virus isolate containing 105 TCID50 was injected intraperitoneally into each chick as a challenged group, and in the second group, 0.5 mL of physiological saline was injected intraperitoneally into each chick as a control group. Body weight was measured weekly, and blood was drawn for viraemia testing and blood smear preparation (Wu, Li, and Chen 2022).

Preparation of Blood Smears

To make blood smears, blood was drawn from chickens in the challenged group throughout their culture phase, at 4 and 7 weeks of age; a drop of fresh blood was taken, applied evenly to a clean slide, stained with Wright–Giemsa (Solarbio, China) and examined by optical microscope.

Gross Lesion Observation and Histopathological Examination

Post‐mortem examinations were performed after the chickens were euthanized at 7 wpi. Eight chickens from the infected group and four chickens from the control group were randomly selected for necropsy to observe the pathogenicity and oncogenesis of LY2021J. Furthermore, for histological examination, the organs of infected and control chickens, including the liver, spleen, kidney and heart, were collected under sterile conditions and fixed in 10% buffered formalin. Following fixation, the tissue samples were dehydrated using a series of increasing concentrations of ethanol (e.g. 70%, 80%, 90% and 100%). After dehydration, the samples were cleared using xylene, which facilitates the removal of alcohol and promotes tissue transparency. All samples were embedded in paraffin wax, sectioned into 4–5 µm slices and stained with haematoxylin and eosin. Histopathological lesions of these samples were examined using an optical microscope.

Statistical Analysis

The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and were analysed using the software SPSS Statistics 25. One‐way ANOVA was used followed by multiple comparisons via the Tukey post hoc test to compare weight differences.

Results

Subgroup Verification of LY2021J

The tissue samples were analysed for ALV nucleotides using multiplex PCR. The results revealed a 350 bp fragment specific to ALV‐J, whereas no bands corresponding to other subgroups were detected (Figure 1).

Virus Isolation

DF‐1 cells were inoculated with positive liver tissues. The supernatants of infected DF‐1 cells yielded a positive result using the ALV group‐specific antigen (p27) ELISA (7 days after inoculation). After being fixed with cold acetone and stained with the G2 monoclonal antibody, specific fluorescence was detected in the cytoplasm of ALV‐infected cells (Figure 2). Molecular Characterisation of the Genome

The full‐length provirus genome of LY2021J (GenBank Access No. OP918846) was 7293 bp in length with a typical genetic structure of a replicable C‐type retrovirus, consisting of a gag‐pol‐env sequence and an LTR at both ends. Phylogenetic analysis of the env gene revealed that LY2021J was in the same branch as the ALV prototype HPRS‐103 (Figure 3A). In addition, the genes and functional regions (LTR, gag, pol) of LY2021J and HPRS‐103 were found in different branches of their respective phylogenetic trees (Figure 3A). Analysis of recombination events showed that isolate LY2021J recombined with strain ev‐1 using strain ALV‐J NX0101 as the primary parent and as the viral backbone. The main recombination sites were located at the ends of the LTR gene and the gag gene at 1452–2063 bp at both ends of the sequence (Figure 3B).

LY2021J gp85 Gene Sequence Analysis

The sequence similarity between the gp85 gene of LY2021J and the ALV‐J reference strains ranged from 91.4% to 98.2%. In contrast, the similarity with subgroups A–E and K was only 46.8% to 48.1%, 47.5%, 47.5%, 48.3%, 47.1% to 47.3% and 46.8%, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that LY2021J exhibited the highest similarity of 98.2% with the strain JS14NT01, placing it in the same clade (Figure 3C). Comparison of the gp85 amino acid sequence of LY2021J with that of the ALV‐J reference strain revealed that mutations were predominantly located in variable region 2 (Vr2), variable region 3 (Vr3) and hypervariable regions 1 (hr1) and 2 (hr2) (Figure 4).

Sequence Analysis of the 3′ UTR Gene

The 3′ UTR of LY2021J was 217 bp in length, containing the rTM region, direct repeat 1 (DR1) and the E element. Comparison of the nucleotide sequence of the 3′ UTR region with HPR‐103 and other ALV‐J reference strains revealed a 175 bp deletion in the rTM progenitor of LY2021J, consistent with many ALV‐J strains that may induce haemangioma and myeloma. In addition, the E element was also partially missing (Figure 5). Research demonstrated that early Chinese strains exhibited a nearly indistinguishable 127 bp deletion in the E component, akin to US strain 4817 and other isolates observed globally. It is probable that the 3′ UTR with the 127 bp deletion in the E element of subgroup J viruses was derived from a shared endogenous viral ancestor through recombination and has existed for an extended period (Li, Wang, and Lin 2019). Similarly, there have been reports suggesting that an intact E element is not essential for ALV‐J to induce tumours (Chesters, Smith, and Nair 2006).

Clinical Symptoms

From week 3, chickens in the infected group showed signs of depression and loose feathers and weighed significantly less than those in the control group (p < 0.05). Furthermore, from week 4 to week 7, the infected group weighed significantly less than the control group (p < 0.01), suggesting that infection with this strain had a suppressive effect on body weight in SPF chickens (Figure 6).

Growth inhibition in sick chickens infected with LY2021J. Effect of LY2021J strain infection on body weight of SPF chickens (X ± SD g, * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01).

Blood Analysis

The blood smear analysis of 4‐week‐old SPF chickens indicated the breakdown of lymphocytes in their bloodstream (Figure 7A), whereas the blood smear of 7‐week‐old chickens revealed the existence of myeloid tumour cells that underwent significant lysis (Figure 7B). These tumour cells were characterized by their large, spherical shape, round or oval nuclei positioned on one side and a significant number of eosinophilic granules present in the cytoplasm.

Discussion

The outbreak of ALV‐J in China has resulted in massive economic losses in the poultry industry (Cui, Du, and Zhang 2003). Although avian leukosis in white‐feather broilers and egg‐bearing chickens in China has been largely controlled and cleared following strict breeding purification, ALV‐J has been introduced into yellow‐feather broilers and various local breeds of chickens reared in large numbers throughout China, and there is relatively little specific information on ALV‐J in local chicken breeds (Li, Fu, and Cui 2017). As a result, an ALV‐J strain was isolated from Hetian chickens in Fujian, China, and some typical ALV‐J cases were effectively replicated in chickens in this study. We observed that hepatic haemangiomas with white nodules and significant severe dysplasia in the recurrent cases. Isolated LY2021J showed lower mortality.

The ALV gp85 gene, recognized as the most variable gene within the ALV genome, serves as the primary basis for defining ALV subgroups. The homology among chicken subgroups A through E and K exceeds 75%; however, the homology between subgroup J and other subgroups is only 30%–40% (Wang, Li, and Li 2021). Despite significant variation among strains within subgroup J, the minimum homology is approximately 90%. In this study, the gp85 region of LY2021J exhibited an amino acid sequence indicative of ALV‐J and demonstrated the highest similarity to the Chinese Heilongjiang strain JS14NT01. The gag and env genes of LY2021J were most similar to the Chinese strain NX0101, whereas the pol gene was most comparable to the ALV‐J strain JS14NT01 from Heilongjiang Province, China.

It is hypothesized that LY2021J may originate from the strain JS14NT01. The gp85 and pol genes of LY2021J exhibit a high degree of similarity to those of the Chinese Heilongjiang strain JS14NT01, particularly in the gp85 region where the amino acid sequence showed the highest similarity to JS14NT01. These genetic similarities indicate that LY2021J could have originated from JS14NT01 or share a common ancestor with it.

Genetic evolution analysis of the LTR sequences of LY2021J and recombination analysis (Figures 3A and 3B) revealed that LY2021J underwent recombination with strain ev‐1, with strain ALV‐J NX0101 serving as the primary parent and viral backbone. We hypothesize that the reduced virulence of LY2021J is attributable to the recombination of LTRs, which suggests a novel mutational mechanism for ALV‐J emerging in China (Wu, Li, and Chen 2022).

A substantial regulatory sequence in the 3′ UTR region of ALV‐J can alter viral gene expression, which is important for viral pathogenicity and replication (Shi, Liu, and Zhao 2011). Many ALV‐J reference strains have 3′ UTR deletions as demonstrated by (Han, Hao, and Liu 2015). LY2021J shares a 175 bp deletion from the 3′ end of the rTM region through 30 bp of the DR‐1 region, as well as a 142 bp deletion in E(XSR) with the majority of Chinese ALV‐J reference strains. Consecutive deletions in rTM and DR‐1 regions of the 3′ UTR have been shown to promote the pathogenicity and oncogenicity in ALV‐J in SPF chickens (Ma, Yu, and Chang 2020; Gao, Yun, and Qin 2012).

ALV‐J is known for its high mutation rate and has progressively adapted to various chicken types, including egg‐laying hens, yellow‐feathered broilers and numerous local breeds, over the last decade of its global spread. This adaptation has led to increased diversification in pathogenesis, particularly in the types of tumour cells involved, as well as a marked rise in transmissibility and pathogenic tumourigenicity, which are closely linked to viral evolution (Zhang, Su, and Zhang 2020). The observed recombination of ALV‐J with ALV‐E may have reduced the lethality of ALV‐J but has resulted in more severe dysplasia. This recombination complicates virus identification and poses significant potential economic losses to the poultry industry, underscoring the critical need for effective eradication strategies. Molecular biological analyses and pathogenicity assays of the ALV‐J strain LY2021J, obtained from Chinese Hetian chickens, are anticipated to aid in monitoring mutation trends, investigating ALV‐J prevalence in local chicken breeds and developing preventive measures.
