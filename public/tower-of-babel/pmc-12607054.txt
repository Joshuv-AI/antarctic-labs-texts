Safety of spinal anaesthesia in the East African population: assessment of spinal cord termination level at and above L2 vertebra in adults
Authors: David O. Nekyon, Sheila Waa, Samuel Nguku Gitau, David S. Odaba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile spinal anaesthesia is a widely used technique, failure to accurately identify the L3-L4 intervertebral space (IVS) during the procedure may expose a patient to a needlestick injury to the spinal cord.ObjectivesThis study aimed to determine the proportion of Conus Medullaris Terminations (CMT) at or above the L2 vertebra in a black East African population and identify any age or gender-based variations.MethodologyA prospective observational study of lumbar spine MRI was conducted on 331 patients who had no vertebral deformities or prior spine surgeries. Two radiologists assessed the CMT level by consensus, referencing the nearest vertebral segment divided into thirds and the intervertebral disc.ResultsThe median CMT was at Lower third of first lumbar vertebra (LL1)[UT12-LL3], with non-significant variation due to gender (p = 0.057), and age (p = 0.272). The proportion of CMT at or above the L2 vertebra was 97.9% with no significant variation due to age (p = 0.983) or gender (p = 0.977) and, CMT at the L1 vertebra and above was 66.5%, with significant variation with age (p = 0.084), but not with gender (p = 0.265). With the risk of a spinal needle being inaccurately placed 1 or 2 intervetertebral spaces (IVS) above the target, 2.1% (L2-L3 IVS) and 33.5% (L1-L2 IVS) of spinal cords may be exposed to potential neuraxial injury respectively. Risk of exposure to neuraxial injury at L2-L3 IVS was double that seen in other studies of subjects without vertebral deformities.ConclusionThe increased risk of iatrogenic neuraxial injury during spinal anaesthesia highlights the need for thorough training and supervision of healthcare providers, especially non-anaesthetists, performing diagnostic lumbar punctures.

Introduction

For patient safety, it is critical to consider the termination level of the conus medullaris (CMT) during the performance of lumbar punctures.

The advent of Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) made it possible to quickly and safely determine the CMT level in live patients, which on average, is at higher levels than had previously been determined [1]. This difference may be due to the draw backs of using cadavers: post-mortem morphological changes, the ‘unselected nature’ of the study subjects, and body positioning during examination [1].

A Kenyan cadaveric study determined that the median CMT was at the upper third of the L2 vertebra (UL2) with 94.5% of patients having their CMT at or above the lower third of the L2 vertebra, resulting in a recommendation that extra caution should be practiced when performing lumbar puncture in African populations [2].

An early spinal MRI study revealed a median CMT position at the middle third of the L1vertebra (ML1) in both genders [1]. Subsequent studies have confirmed variations in the CMT.(3–5) With imaging performed on living subjects rather than cadavers, the results were considered more accurate [1].

There are conflicting findings regarding the impact of age and gender on the CMT level. One study found no significant difference attributable to either factor, while others indicated that older patients exhibit a lower termination level than younger ones and that females have a slight but significantly lower termination level than males [3–6].

MRI studies conducted in African populations, which involved relatively small sample sizes, have since reported CMTs at higher levels than seen in cadavers by Gatonga et al., with conflicting evidence on the impact of age on the results [2, 7, 8]. Potentially, there may be regional variations amongst ethnically similar populations. However, none of these studies mentioned CMT levels at sites of potential importance to spinal anaesthesia and lumbar punctures. Furthermore, there are currently no MRI studies that compare the CMT levels across different racial groups.

Neuraxial blocks are widely utilized in regional anaesthesia, especially in surgeries involving the lower extremities and the abdomen, as well as for post-operative pain relief through lumbar and thoracic epidural blocks. Additionally, diagnostic lumbar puncture for extracting cerebrospinal fluid is a common clinical procedure [9].

These neuraxial procedures are performed with care taken to avoid any iatrogenic trauma to the spinal cord that could can lead to acute and chronic pain, as well as motor and sensory impairments, which may be persistent [10–12]. Considering the variation in termination levels across the population, the L3-L4 or L4-L5 intervertebral space (IVS) has been recommended as the target for safe spinal anaesthesia [13].

Critically, even when the L3-L4 IVS is targeted, the evidence shows that a significant number of anaesthetists indicated 1–2 IVS more cephalad than this and thus may inadvertently place the spinal needle near the conus medullaris [13, 14]. Similar studies amongst non-anaesthesia practitioners have yet to be conducted.

Lumbar punctures are frequently performed outside operating rooms, often by junior doctors or medical students with non-standardised training in the procedure, who may lack adequate supervision [15]. These challenges may be more notable in a resource limited setting.

Safe spinal needle insertion during regional anaesthesia and lumbar puncture is heavily dependent on accurate knowledge of the CMT level. To minimize the risk of iatrogenic spinal cord injury, it is essential to have population-specific data. This study addresses an important knowledge gap by characterizing CMT anatomy in an African population, providing valuable insights that can guide safer clinical practices in regional anaesthesia and other diagnostic procedures.

This study aimed to determine the CMT levels in a living Black East African population using MR imaging; with the primary objective of determining the percentage of patients with conus medullaris positioned at or above L2 vertebra, and secondarily, at and above L1 vertebra, and explore any correlation with gender or age.

Materials and methods

This was a prospective sequential observational study of magnetic resonance images. It was conducted from August 2019 to September 2020 at the Department of Diagnostic Radiology of the Aga Khan University Hospital, a tertiary-level regional referral academic hospital in Nairobi, Kenya. The study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki as amended by the 75th world medical association general assembly, Helsinki, Finland, October 2024. Approval was obtained from the Aga Khan University Institutional Ethics Review Committee (Ref: 2018/REC-85) before commencement of the study.

Patients over 18 years old who self-identified as black African in their medical records and were seeking routine lumbar spine MRI scans were eligible for the study. Exclusion criteria encompassed vertebral developmental abnormalities, fractures, degenerative changes, spinal tumor, and history of spine surgery.

Patients were assessed for eligibility as they presented for routine MRI scans of the lumbar spine. The primary clinical indications for imaging were lower back pain and lower limb neuropathies. Eligible patients who provided written informed consent were sequentially enrolled in the study until the target sample size was achieved.

The sample size (n) was determined using the Cochrane formula for a one-sample proportion at a 95% confidence level: n0 = z^2pq/d^2. Using a level of significance of 1.96, an expected proportion of patients (p) at 0.5 (naïve proportion), an expected proportion of patients without the condition under study (q = 1-p) of 0.5, and a margin of error of 5% (d = 0.05), a sample size of 384 was calculated. Considering an estimated 1382 lumbar spine MRI scans in adult black African patients in 2017 at the study site, a population correction using this finite population resulted in a calculated value of 331, including an allowance for a 10% non-response.

The MRI examinations were performed on a 1.5T GE MRI scanner and images archived within the hospital’s Patient Archiving and Communication System (PACS) Two consultant radiologists with 12- and 6-years experience independently assigned the level of the CMT.

The assessment focused on T2-weighted sagittal images of the thoracolumbar spine, with the patient positioned supine. The most distal point of the spinal cord, CMT, was identified. Following the methodology delineated by Saifuddin et al.,[3] each vertebra was categorized into three distinct segments: upper, middle, and lower. Furthermore, each intervertebral disc was treated as a separate region for analysis (Fig. 1). A line was drawn perpendicular to the sagittal view of the vertebral column at the identified level of the conus medullaris to accurately assign it to the corresponding vertebral level. Any discrepancies in the segment assignments were addressed and resolved through a consensus process among the evaluating radiologists.Fig. 1Vertebral subdivision into four segments (upper third, middle third, lower third and intervertebral disc) of L1and the determination of the CMT

The CMT data collected was entered onto a Microsoft Excel (version 16.30) spread sheet by each respective radiologist and the data sets were cleaned and merged into an anonymized master list. The demographic data (age and gender) was recorded for each patient.

A statistical analysis was conducted using R version 4.3.2 to examine CMT levels across eight age-groups (18–19, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, 80–89 years). Descriptive statistics were calculated (median [Interquartile range]), and Kruskal-Wallis analysis and Mann-Whitney U test were used to assess variations in distribution of CMT among these age groups and across genders respectively.

The proportion of subjects with CMT at or above L1and L2 vertebral levels by age and gender was calculated. A Kruskal-Wallis analysis was performed to explore the relationship between age and these proportions, using binary classifications of termination levels (at or above L1 and L2 vertebra) as independent variables and age as the dependent variable. Gender differences in these proportions were analyzed with Pearson’s chi-square tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

It has been found in several studies in patients without vertebral abnormalities, including ours findings, that the median termination level of the spinal cord typically falls around the L1 vertebra and the distribution we found closely mirrored these studies [1, 3–5, 8]. Equally notable is the number of studies showing subjects with termination levels that reach as low as the L3 vertebra[3, 4, 7, 16], as seen in our study, but not consistently among populations of African descent [8]. This may point to variations in ethnically similar, but geographically separated populations.

In the same population (ethnically and geographically) as our study, a cadaveric study showed a CMT level range similar to the one observed in our MRI-based study [2]. However, the median level (UL2) in this study was 2 segments lower and notably intersected an IVS (between L1-L2) and with a much lower proportion of subjects with a CMT at or above the L2 vertebra than our findings show [2].

When targetting the placement of the spinal needle at the L3-L4 IVS[13], our findings suggest that the likelihood of causing cord injury in our study population is minimal if the targeted IVS is accurately located. This conclusion is supported by the absence of CMT below the L3 vertebra, as observed in several studies involving patients without spinal vertebral deformities [1, 7, 16–20].

Touffier’s line has been highlighted as an unreliable landmark for locating the L3-L4 IVS[13, 14, 21]. Actual needle placement was noted to range from 1 to 3 intervertebral spaces (IVS) higher than the anaesthesia provider thought, with an accuracy of 29–52% amongst experienced providers, and this may be complicated by obesity [13, 14, 22]. Consequently it is essential to review CMT levels in this regard.

In published MRI-based studies, the proportion of subjects with a CMT at or above the L2 vertebra ranged over 99% among adults in the absence of vertebral pathology [7, 16, 17, 19, 20, 23]. Our findings are lower than this, with 2.1% of conus medullaris potentially exposed to a needlestick injury at the L2-L3 IVS, almost double the number seen in previous studies, but less than half found in the cadaveric study in a similar population.

Our study population demonstrates a lower proportion of CMT at or above the L1 vertebra compared to most other studies in subjects without vertebral deformities, with only one exception[7, 8, 17–20]. Consequently, the risk of neuraxial injury from unintentional spinal needle placement at the L1-L2 IVS is elevated, affecting 33.5% of individuals.

Though experienced anaesthetists are still more likely to achieve procedural accuracy[14, 24], anaesthesia practice often includes individuals of all grades and skill levels who will conduct spinal anaesthetics. It becomes even more of a concern when one considers that diagnostic lumbar punctures are conducted in the same way, but more likely by non-anaesthetists and possibly of junior standing [15].

Effect of gender

Our findings align with multiple studies that indicate no significant difference in the median CMT levels between genders in the absence of vertebral deformities [3, 7, 8, 16, 18, 20]. Conceivably, the low number of males in our study had minimal impact on the results. Furthermore, we observed no significant gender-related differences in the proportions of CMT at or above the L2 and L1 vertebral levels.

Effect of age

The effect of age on the median CMT level in subjects with normal vertebral anatomy has produced contradicting results from numerous studies with some stating that there is correlation[7, 17, 19, 25], correlation amongst females[23], and similar to our study no correlation with age [3, 17]. However, with advancing age in the general population, degenerative changes to the morphology of the vertebra and inter-vertebral disc may cause the CMT to drop more caudally[4, 5, 20], and potentially show a lower proportion with CMT at or above the L2 in that population than seen in our findings. This discrepancy may not be unexpected, considering the relatively younger demographic of our sample population.

Furthermore, a recent study that included individuals with various age-related vertebral pathologies still demonstrated lower CMT exposure at the respective IVS compared to our findings [6]. A more inclusive study may be necessary to determine if the risk of neuraxial injury in our population may have been underestimated due to the exclusion of these subjects from our study.

Conclusion

The proportion of the conus medullaris terminating at or above the L2 and L1 vertebra is lower in the black East African population compared to other populations. This difference may increase the potential risk of inadvertent neuraxial injury during spinal anaesthesia and lumbar puncture in this demographic. To mitigate this risk, it is essential to implement rigorous training for healthcare providers, coupled with strong supervision for junior staff [15]. Simulation of lumbar punctures has shown promising results among final-year medical students[26], and should be considered for inclusion in the undergraduate curriculum.

Additionally, there is a need for more accurate methods to locate the targeted intervertebral space for the insertion of a spinal needle. Techniques such as ultrasound and fluoroscopy guidance have shown potential benefits including increasing first attempt success and reducing number of attempts to achieve success[22, 24, 27–30]. However these benefits are most evident amongst experienced practitioners due to the steep learning curve associated with these methods, but may be effectively addressed through simulation training[27, 31].

Limitations

The research was conducted at a single centre, which may limit the external validity of the findings. The exclusion of subjects with vertebral deformities may have limited the perceived risk of neuraxial injury and a more inclusive study may be required to determine this. In contrast to our study, which examined subjects in a neutral (supine) position, spinal anaesthesia is typically performed with the vertebral column flexed. However, Liu et al. reported no statistically significant differences in the CMT level when varying patient positions. [25]
