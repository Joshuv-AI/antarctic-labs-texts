Serum Neuropeptides in Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria: Associations With Disease Activity and Clinical Phenotypes
Authors: Zhenrui Shi, Xinyi Deng, Jieru Ren, Siyao Lu, Guozhen Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropeptides have been implicated in chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), but clinical data remain limited. We measured serum calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), neuropeptide Y (NPY), vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP), and stem cell factor by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay in 80 patients with CSU and 40 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. CSU patients were categorized as flare or non-flare at sampling according to the presence or absence of wheals on the day of blood collection, and disease activity was assessed using the Urticaria Activity Score and its 7-day version (UAS7). Median serum CGRP levels were significantly higher in CSU patients than in healthy controls (263.86 pg/mL vs. 235.13 pg/mL, P < 0.001). In the CSU group, there was a non-significant trend toward higher CGRP levels in the non-flare group compared to the flare group (283.74 pg/mL vs. 251.01 pg/mL, P = 0.062). Serum VIP levels correlated positively with UAS7 (r = 0.238, P = 0.035), whereas NPY showed no clear group differences. Stem cell factor was below the detection limit in most samples. These findings support a possible role of CGRP and VIP in CSU and VIP as a candidate biomarker of disease burden that warrants validation in larger, longitudinal, multi-center studies.Keywords: Chronic spontaneous urticaria; neuropeptide; biomarker, urticaria activity score, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

INTRODUCTION

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a mast cell–driven disorder characterized by recurrent wheals, angioedema, or both for at least 6 weeks in the absence of a consistent external trigger.1 Although its prevalence is only about 1%, CSU is clinically relevant because it often persists, disrupts sleep, and is associated with anxiety, depression, and impaired work performance. Second-generation H1-antihistamines are recommended as first-line treatment, yet many patients remain symptomatic even at up to fourfold doses.2,3 This incomplete response highlights the need to better elucidate CSU pathophysiology and to identify biomarkers that more accurately reflect disease burden and support individualized management.

Autoimmune mechanisms are thought to drive CSU in a substantial subset of patients via histamine-releasing autoantibodies against the high-affinity immunoglobulin E (IgE) receptor or, less commonly, IgE itself. In others, the triggers of mast cell degranulation are less clear, but may involve IgE to autoantigens, complement activation, and neuroimmune mediators such as neuropeptides.4 Neuropeptides are small, bioactive peptides released from sensory and autonomic nerve fibers to mediate crosstalk between the nervous and immune systems.5 In the skin, they modulate vascular permeability, itch, and immune cell activation. Several neuropeptides, including substance P (SP), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP), and neuropeptide Y (NPY), can activate mast cells and induce histamine and proinflammatory mediator release, positioning them at the neuro–immune interface that is central to CSU.6

SP is the best-characterized circulating neuropeptide in CSU, with elevated serum levels that correlate with disease activity and increased SP/neurokinin-1 receptor expression on basophils linked to enhanced histamine release and immune cell recruitment.7,8 By contrast, data on other neuropeptides such as CGRP, VIP, NPY, and stem cell factor (SCF) are limited and partly inconsistent. For example, Basak et al.9 reported higher SP and SCF levels in chronic urticaria but no significant differences in CGRP, VIP, or NPY compared to controls. Whether these neuropeptides fluctuate between flare and non-flare states and how they relate to clinical activity remains largely unknown, and clarifying their roles may provide novel diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers in CSU.

In this context, we set out to characterize serum levels of CGRP, NPY, VIP, and SCF in patients with CSU compared with healthy controls and to examine their relationship with Urticaria Activity Scores (UASs) and clinical disease state.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient selection

Between December 2020 and October 2021, we enrolled 80 adults (20 men and 60 women) with CSU who were attending the dermatology outpatient clinic at Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hospital. CSU was diagnosed according to current guidelines. Patients were categorized as flare (presence of wheals or angioedema) or non-flare at sampling, yielding 40 patients in each subgroup (10 men, 30 women per group).

Patients with chronic inducible urticaria, other inflammatory skin diseases, active infectious/autoimmune/systemic disorders, psychiatric disease or use of centrally acting drugs, or pregnancy/breastfeeding were excluded. Patients continued their usual treatment; antihistamines and other medications, including corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, were neither ruled out nor routinely withheld before sampling, and H1-antihistamine use at the time of sampling was recorded as yes/no.

Forty age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers served as controls. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hospital (SYSYW-2024-015-01), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Assessment of CSU severity

Disease activity was assessed using the UAS7. UAS (0–6) rates wheal and pruritus severity (0–3 each) on the day of evaluation and was recorded only for patients with a flare at sampling. UAS7 was categorized as: 0 = no activity; 1–6 = well-controlled; 7–15 = mild; 16–27 = moderate; and 28–42 = severe disease, in line with current international urticaria guidelines.10

Sample collection and neuropeptides measurement

Venous blood (5 mL) was collected from all participants, allowed to stand at 4°C for 30 minutes, and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C. Serum was separated and stored at −80°C until analysis. Serum CGRP, NPY, VIP, and SCF were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Wuhan Yunclon Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions; all samples were run in duplicate and concentrations calculated from kit-derived standard curves.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of serum neuropeptide concentrations and continuous clinical parameters was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All were non-normally distributed. Therefore, non-parametric tests were used for group comparisons and correlation analyses. Categoric variables were compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, and continuous variables using the Mann-Whitney U test. Correlations between serum neuropeptides and UAS/UAS7 were assessed with Spearman’s rank correlation. Multivariable linear

regression was used to explore factors associated with serum CGRP, NPY, VIP levels and the positivity of SCF, with each neuropeptide as the dependent variable and flare status, antihistamine use, immunosuppressive drugs, systemic corticosteroids, age, sex, disease duration, and UAS7 as covariates, model assumptions were checked by inspection of residuals. All tests were two-tailed with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Correlation of neuropeptides with disease activity

In patients with a flare at sampling, serum VIP showed a moderate but an insignificant correlation with UAS (r = 0.303, P = 0.057), whereas CGRP and NPY did not correlate with UAS (Fig. 2A-C). UAS7 data were available for 79 of 80 CSU patients. In this cohort, serum VIP levels were modestly but significantly correlated with UAS7 (r = 0.238, P = 0.035), while no association was observed for CGRP or NPY (Fig. 2D-F).

Among the 79 patients with total IgE measurements, 39 had elevated and 40 had normal IgE levels, but serum CGRP, NPY, and VIP concentrations did not differ between these groups (Supplementary Table S1). Similarly, in the 69 patients tested for thyroid autoantibodies, neuropeptide levels were comparable between thyroid peroxidase antibody–positive and –negative patients and between thyroglobulin antibody–positive and –negative patients (Supplementary Table S2).

To account for potential confounding by medication, we fitted multivariable linear regression models including flare status, antihistamine use, immunosuppressive drugs, systemic corticosteroids, age, sex, disease duration, and UAS7. None of the medication variables significantly predicted CGRP, NPY, VIP levels or SCF detectability, whereas CGRP levels were positively associated with age, and NPY levels differed by sex (Supplementary Table S3). In exploratory analyses, we compared serum CGRP, NPY, and VIP levels across UAS7-defined disease activity categories (well-controlled/mild, moderate, severe CSU) and healthy controls. Analysis of variance showed significant overall differences for CGRP and VIP but not for NPY. Post hoc comparisons indicated that CGRP levels were highest in patients with mild CSU and significantly higher than in moderate and severe CSU and in healthy controls, while VIP levels were significantly higher in patients with severe CSU than in those with mild CSU and in healthy controls (Supplementary Table S4).

DISCUSSION

Our findings showed that serum CGRP levels were significantly higher in CSU patients than in healthy controls, supporting a potential contribution of CGRP to disease pathophysiology. CGRP is a 37–amino acid neuropeptide released from peripheral and central neurons that can induce vasodilation, nociception, and neurogenic inflammation, and it can activate mast cells via CGRP receptors to promote histamine release and neuroimmune crosstalk.11 Histological studies have reported increased CGRP+ and vascular endothelial growth factor + cells in lesional skin of CSU and other inflammatory dermatoses, with co-localization to dermal blood vessels, implicating a vascular–inflammatory axis in wheal formation.12 In our cohort, there was an insignificant trend toward higher serum CGRP levels in the non-flare group than in the flare group, a finding which is considered hypothesis generating. Given the cross-sectional design and lack of kinetic data, we cannot determine whether CGRP is transiently depleted during flares or upregulated after their resolution. Longitudinal studies with repeated sampling are needed to define CGRP dynamics and clarify its mechanistic role in CSU.

VIP is a 28–amino acid neuropeptide of the secretin/glucagon superfamily, widely expressed in both central and peripheral nervous systems. Through VIP receptor (VPAC)1 and VPAC2 that are expressed on multiple immune cell types, including mast cells, VIP exerts diverse immunoregulatory functions.13 Notably, VIP can directly activate mast cells in a dose-dependent manner, inducing degranulation and promoting the release of histamine, prostaglandins, and various cytokines.14 It also inhibits the differentiation of Th1 cells and favors the expansion of Th2 cells through various non-excluding mechanisms that involve regulation of DC functions.13 Our observation that VIP correlated with cumulative UAS7 but not with the single-day UAS reflects that serum VIP levels integrate disease activity over several days, whereas a single-day UAS is more vulnerable to short-term fluctuations. Thus, UAS7 may better capture the overall disease burden that is biologically linked to VIP.

Several studies have examined whether neuropeptides change with treatment and predict therapeutic response in CSU, but results are inconsistent. Antihistamines such as loratadine and cetirizine have been reported to reduce serum SP and VIP in parallel with symptom improvement,9 whereas another study found that SP and Mas-related G protein-coupled receptor X2 correlated with disease activity but were not significantly modified by antihistamine therapy.15 Wang and Zhao16 showed that baseline SP, VIP, and NPY levels were elevated and correlated with symptom scores in chronic urticaria with allergic rhinitis, and that lower pretreatment levels predicted a better response to imipramine. In our study, multivariable models did not identify medication use as an independent predictor of neuropeptide levels. However, residual confounding related to dosing and timing, together with the cross-sectional design, precludes firm conclusions about treatment effects. Longitudinal studies with standardized medication protocols are needed to determine whether neuropeptide dynamics reflect true clinical improvement and whether baseline levels can reliably predict treatment response.

Taken together, this study assessed serum CGRP, NPY, VIP, and SCF in CSU with a focus on flare versus non-flare at sampling, and found higher CGRP levels in CSU than in controls with an insignificant trend toward higher levels in non-flare patients, while VIP showed a modest positive correlation with UAS7. These exploratory findings support CGRP and VIP as candidate neuroimmune mediators in CSU. However, several limitations should be noted: the single-center, cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not capture intra-individual dynamics; patients were not treatment-naïve and medication exposure was only recorded as use vs. non-use, so residual confounding by concomitant drugs cannot be excluded; several neuropeptides, particularly SCF, were frequently below the detection limit, limiting quantitative analyses; and serum measurements, which showed marked inter-individual variability, may not fully reflect local neuropeptide activity. The modest sample size further constrained subgroup analyses. Larger, preferably treatment-naïve, longitudinal studies using more sensitive assays are needed to validate and extend these findings.
