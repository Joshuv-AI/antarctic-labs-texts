Prevalence and pattern of cognitive impairment among neurological outpatients in Kano, Nigeria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Muhammad Aliyu Abba, Saad Suleiman, Buhari Hassan, Aliyu Zakariyya Inuwa, Aisha Shittu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive impairment is a progressive neurodegenerative syndrome and a major contributor to disability among individuals with neurological disorders. Despite its growing burden, cognitive impairment remains understudied in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in Nigeria, where hospital-based data are scarce. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and pattern of cognitive impairment among neurological outpatients in Kano, Nigeria, and to examine associations with selected sociodemographic and clinical characteristics.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among 75 adult neurological outpatients attending two tertiary hospitals in Kano. Cognitive impairment was assessed using the Mini-Cog. Prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and associations between cognitive impairment and sociodemographic or clinical factors were analyzed using Chi-square tests and Cramer’s V. The Blessed Dementia Rating Scale (BDRS) was used for severity staging only.ResultsThe prevalence of screening-detected cognitive impairment was 72.0% (95% CI: 60.9–82.0) using the Mini-Cog. Cognitive impairment was more frequent among stroke survivors compared with other neurological conditions. Significant associations were observed with gender (p = 0.027, V = 0.26), employment status (p < 0.001, V = 0.42), and type of neurological disorder (p = 0.017, V = 0.34).ConclusionScreening-detected cognitive impairment is highly prevalent among neurological outpatients in Kano, Nigeria, particularly among stroke survivors. Routine cognitive screening should be integrated into neurological care in this setting. However, findings should be interpreted as screening-based estimates requiring clinical confirmation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05111-0.

Background

Cognitive impairment is a progressive neurodegenerative syndrome characterized by cognitive decline and loss of independence, affecting millions worldwide and posing a major public health challenge [1]. Globally, over 55 million people live with dementia, and this figure is projected to triple by 2050 [2]. The majority of cases are accounted for by Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia, although other forms such as Parkinson’s disease dementia and frontotemporal dementia also contribute [3–5]. Importantly, dementia is now recognized as a leading cause of disability among older adults, and it frequently exceeds the burden of other chronic conditions such as stroke and heart disease [6, 7]. In sub-Saharan Africa, however, the prevalence is rising largely due to population aging and increased life expectancy [8]. Specifically in Nigeria, dementia cases have increased dramatically from around 63,512 in 1995 to 318,011 in 2015, which underscores the urgent need for early diagnosis and preventive care [9].

Rehabilitation professionals play a critical role in addressing dementia-related functional decline, as balance deficits, gait impairments, and postural control problems are common and directly raise the risk of falls and fractures [10, 11]. Nevertheless, cognitive screening remains rarely conducted in Nigerian hospitals, highlighting the need for routine assessment alongside tailored rehabilitation strategies [12]. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of cognitive impairment and its association with sociodemographic and clinical factors among patients attending neurological outpatient clinics in two tertiary hospitals in Kano, Nigeria. To our knowledge, no published hospital-based study has specifically examined cognitive impairment in a mixed neurological outpatient population in Kano, though community-based studies exist. This study may provide essential evidence for clinical practice and policy development.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted to determine the prevalence of cognitive impairment and its associated factors among patients with neurological disorders. The study was carried out at Murtala Mohammed Specialist Hospital and Mohammed Abdullahi Wase Teaching Hospital, both tertiary healthcare centers recognized for their specialized neurological care services in Kano, Nigeria.

Participants and sampling

The study population included adult outpatients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with neurological conditions such as stroke, facial nerve palsy, or traumatic brain injury. All facial nerve palsy cases were idiopathic (Bell’s palsy) and confirmed by a neurologist to be lower motor neuron lesions, excluding post-stroke central causes. Patients were recruited from the neurology outpatient departments of the selected hospitals between January and March 2022. Patients with severe cognitive or communication impairments that interfered with the ability to provide informed consent were excluded from the study.

A consecutive sampling technique was employed, whereby every eligible patient presenting at the clinics during the data collection period was invited to participate until the desired sample size was achieved. This method was chosen to minimize selection bias and ensure a representative sample of the outpatient population.

Where N represents the estimated total number of eligible patients available during the data collection period (approximately 95 patients, based on clinic attendance records). Applying this correction yielded a sample size of 73. Therefore, 75 patients were enrolled in the study, and all provided complete data for analysis.

Data collection tools and procedure

Data were collected using standardized, interviewer-administered tools:

Sociodemographic and Clinical Data Form: This form was used to record participants' demographic variables (e.g., age, sex, education level, marital status, employment status) and clinical information (e.g., type and duration of neurological condition). The full form is available as Supplementary Material.

Mini-Cog Test: The Mini-Cog is a brief cognitive screening tool that combines a three-word recall test with a clock-drawing task (CDT). It is scored out of 5, with a score of ≤ 2 indicating probable cognitive impairment. The clock-drawing task was administered by asking participants to draw a clock showing “ten past eleven.” Scoring followed the standard 0–2 point system (normal clock = 2 points). The three-word recall was scored 0–3 points. The three recall words were adapted to culturally familiar items (“red,” “honesty,” “bicycle”) after pilot testing with 10 non-participant outpatients to ensure comprehension. The tool has demonstrated high sensitivity (76–99%) and specificity (89–93%) for detecting cognitive impairment in diverse populations, including low-literacy settings [13, 14]. It was chosen for its brevity and minimal language bias. The Mini-Cog assessment form used in this study is available as Supplementary Material.

Blessed Dementia Rating Scale (BDRS): The BDRS is a clinician-rated instrument that evaluates the severity of dementia based on observed changes in daily functioning, habits, and personality. It assesses performance on everyday tasks such as eating, dressing, and completing household chores. Higher scores indicate greater impairment. In this study, the BDRS was used only for severity staging among participants who screened positive on the Mini-Cog, not as a separate prevalence estimate [15]. The BDRS used in this study is available as Supplementary Material.

Language Support: To ensure comprehension among participants, all assessments were administered in Hausa, the predominant local language in Kano, by research assistants who were fluent in both English and Hausa. Where necessary, explanations were provided in Hausa to clarify questions and ensure accurate responses.

Procedure: All assessments were administered face-to-face in a private clinic setting. The sequence began with the sociodemographic questionnaire, followed by the Mini-Cog evaluation. To maintain consistency, two trained research assistants (fluent in both English and Hausa) conducted all interviews. Each assessment session lasted approximately 20–25 minutes.

Data analysis

All data were entered and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 20.0. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequencies, and percentages) were used to summarize participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics. The Chi-square test was used to examine the association between the presence of cognitive impairment (as measured by the Mini-Cog) and categorical variables such as age group, gender, educational level, and type of neurological condition. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Cramer’s V was used to assess the strength of associations. Results were presented in tabular and graphical formats for enhanced clarity and interpretation.

Discussion

This hospital-based study revealed a high prevalence of screening-detected cognitive impairment among neurological outpatients in Kano, Nigeria. Specifically, 72.0% of the participants screened positive for cognitive impairment using the Mini-Cog test. This rate is considerably higher than global population estimates and suggests a possible under-recognition of cognitive impairment in similar specialist care settings [16]. The predominance of male participants (58.7%) aligns with previous reports from Nigerian neurological clinics [17], although globally dementia is more common in women, largely due to longer life expectancy and biological factors [18]. This discrepancy may reflect cultural or health system barriers in Northern Nigeria, where women may have limited access to specialized hospital care [19, 20]. Most participants were unemployed (86.7%) and had low educational attainment. These are well-recognized risk factors for cognitive impairment, as limited education and occupational engagement reduce cognitive reserve and resilience against neurodegenerative processes [21, 22]. This finding reinforces the importance of public health policies that encourage lifelong learning and occupational opportunities to reduce cognitive vulnerability [23, 24].

Stroke emerged as the most common neurological diagnosis (65.3%), consistent with evidence linking it to vascular dementia and post-stroke cognitive impairment in sub-Saharan Africa [4, 25]. The strong association between type of neurological disorder and cognitive impairment observed in this study underscores the contribution of vascular risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes, which are increasingly prevalent in Nigeria [20, 26]. These results highlight the need for aggressive stroke prevention and post-stroke cognitive rehabilitation [12, 27]. The high rates of cognitive impairment detected using the Mini-Cog suggest that this instrument is feasible for use in Nigerian hospital settings, though formal validation in Northern Nigeria is still needed [13, 28]. Routine use of such screening tools would support early detection, timely medical intervention, caregiver education, and planning [29, 30]. Early detection is especially critical in low-resource settings where access to specialized neurology or psychiatry services is often delayed [31].

This study also highlights the need for capacity-building among healthcare providers in Northern Nigeria. Training physiotherapists, neurologists, and general practitioners to identify early signs of cognitive decline is essential, in line with the WHO Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) [32]. Integration of cognitive assessment into neurorehabilitation services could improve patient outcomes by addressing both motor and cognitive health [32, 33]. Furthermore, comprehensive geriatric care should be prioritized as Nigeria’s aging population continues to grow. Proactive strategies for screening and management of cognitive impairment at outpatient and community levels are urgently needed [19, 24].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is that it provides one of the first hospital-based estimates of cognitive impairment among neurological patients in Northern Nigeria, thereby filling an important knowledge gap. The use of a validated screening tool with appropriate language support strengthens the reliability of our findings.

However, several major limitations warrant caution. First, the Mini-Cog has not been formally validated in Northern Nigeria. The Clock Drawing Test (CDT) component is known to be influenced by low educational level, prior drawing experience, and motor deficits (e.g., post-stroke hemiparesis), which are common in our population. It also has poor sensitivity for mild cognitive impairment and can produce false positives in individuals with low literacy or visuospatial impairment from other causes. Second, while assessments were conducted in Hausa, a formal forward-backward translation process was not performed; verbal translation by fluent assistants may have introduced semantic inconsistencies. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Fourth, the consecutive sampling method, while minimizing selection bias, may still limit generalizability to the wider community. The hospital-based population also means findings cannot be extrapolated to community-dwelling adults without neurological conditions. Fifth, some potential contributors to cognitive decline, such as comorbidities, socioeconomic status, and nutritional status, were not fully assessed [30]. Sixth, screening tools, while useful, are not substitutes for formal diagnostic evaluation, and our prevalence figures may represent an overestimate of true clinical dementia. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as screening-detected cognitive impairment requiring confirmatory clinical evaluation.

Future directions

Further research should adopt longitudinal and community-based designs to obtain more representative estimates of cognitive impairment in Nigeria. Formal validation of cognitive screening tools in Northern Nigerian populations, including proper forward-backward translation, is urgently needed. Studies examining cultural beliefs, stigma, caregiver burden, and health system barriers would also provide valuable insights, as emphasized in international dementia action frameworks [34].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a high prevalence of screening-detected cognitive impairment among neurological outpatients in Kano, Nigeria, particularly among stroke survivors. Gender, employment status, and type of neurological disorder were significantly associated with cognitive impairment. These findings emphasize the need for routine cognitive screening in neurology clinics, early intervention, and integration of cognitive care into neurorehabilitation services in Nigeria. However, due to the methodological limitations noted above, these findings should be considered screening-based estimates requiring clinical confirmation.
