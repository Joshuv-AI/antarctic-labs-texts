A PDE approach for the invariant measure of stochastic oscillators with hysteresis
Lihong Guo, Harry L. F. Ip, Mingyang Wang

Abstract
This paper presents a PDE approach as an alternative to Monte Carlo simulations for computing the invariant measure of a white-noise-driven bilinear oscillator with hysteresis. This model is widely used in engineering to represent highly nonlinear dynamics, such as the Bauschinger effect. The study extends the stochastic elasto-plastic framework of Bensoussan et al. [SIAM J. Numer. Anal. 47 (2009), pp. 3374–3396] from the two-dimensional elasto-perfectly-plastic oscillator to the three-dimensional bilinear elasto-plastic oscillator. By constructing an appropriate Lyapunov function, the existence of an invariant measure is established. This extension thus enables the modelling of richer hysteretic behavior and broadens the scope of PDE alternatives to Monte Carlo methods. Two applications demonstrate the method’s efficiency: calculating the oscillator’s threshold crossing frequency (providing an alternative to Rice’s formula) and probability of serviceability.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the following system of the white-noise-driven bilinear elasto-plastic oscillator (BEPO)

\ddot{X}(t)+\mathbf{F}(t)=\mathfrak{f}(X(t),\dot{X}(t))+\sigma\dot{W}(t),

Formulations of the model

To illustrate the BEPO model formulations from both physical and mathematical perspectives,
we introduce the following notation.
Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times(-b,b),\hskip 40.00006pt

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{D}}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times[-b,b],

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{-}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\{-b\},\hskip 40.00006pt

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{+}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\{b\},\quad b>0,

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(t)=(X(t),Y(t),Z(t)),\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z).

Additionally, let
C_{b}(\bar{\mathcal{D}})
represent the space of continuous and bounded functions on

(\bar{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{B})
,
where
\mathcal{B}
denotes the Borel
\sigma
-field on
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
.

2.1
Physical formulation of the BEPO

As mentioned before, the white-noise-driven BEPO is formally described by

\displaystyle\ddot{X}(t)+\mathbf{F}(t)=\mathfrak{f}(X(t),\dot{X}(t))+\sigma\dot{W}(t),

(2.1)

with the initial displacement and velocity

X(0)=x
and
\dot{X}(0)=y
respectively.
Here,

\dot{W}
denotes a white noise random forcing in
the sense that
W
is a Wiener process,
\sigma>0
is the noise intensity, and

\mathfrak{f}(x,y)
is a Lipschitz function from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}
, representing all non-elastoplastic deterministic forces.
The restoring force
\mathbf{F}(t)
is a functional that depends on
the entire trajectory
\{X(s),0\leq s\leq t\}
up to time
t
,
its non-linearity comes from the switching of regimes from
an elastic phase to a plastic one, or vice versa.
In this model, the irreversible (plastic) deformation
\Delta
and
the reversible (elastic) deformation
Z
at time
t
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Z(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}X(t),\quad\mathrm{d}\Delta(t)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mbox{in elastic phase},

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Delta(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}X(t),\quad\mathrm{d}Z(t)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mbox{in plastic phase},

while
X(t)=Z(t)+\Delta(t)
.
Typically, plastic phase occurs when
|Z(t)|=b
and
elastic phase occurs when
|Z(t)|<b
,
here
b>0
is an elasto-plastic bound.
We will consider the case in which the force
\mathbf{F}(t)
is
a linear combination of the elastic and plastic deformations.
This force is a linear function of
Z

(while
\Delta=X-Z
remains constant) in the elastic phase and
a linear function of
X
(while
Z
remains constant at
\pm b
) in the plastic phase as follows:

\mathbf{F}(t)=k(1-\alpha)Z(t)+k\alpha X(t),\quad 0\leq\alpha\leq 1,\quad k>0.

(2.2)

Note that when
\alpha=0
, the system (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) reduces to the classical EPPO model.

2.2
Mathematical formulation of the BEPO

Consider the triple

\{(X(t),Y(t),Z(t)),\>t\geq 0\}

solution of the following SVI

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X(t)=Y(t)\mathrm{d}t,\\
\mathrm{d}Y(t)=[\mathfrak{f}(X(t),Y(t))-k(1-\alpha)Z(t)-k\alpha X(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W(t),\\
(\mathrm{d}Z(t)-Y(t)\mathrm{d}t)(\xi-Z(t))\geq 0,~\forall|\xi|\leq b,~|Z(t)|\leq b,\end{cases}

(2.3)

with initial condition

(X(0),Y(0),Z(0))=(x,y,z)\in\bar{\mathcal{D}}
.
The SVI (
2.3
) is a concise and rigorous way to express the elasto-plastic dynamics,
shown in Section
2.1
, as it governs the alternance between elastic and plastic regimes.
It is a well-posed problem, see
[
30
]
Chapter 4.

Without loss of generality,
we take
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}
as an example to illustrate the process generated by SVI (
2.3
).
Recall from

]

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\theta_{n+1}=\inf\{t>\tau_{n}~|~|Z(t)|=b\},\\
\tau_{n+1}=\inf\{t>\theta_{n+1}~|~\mathrm{sign}(Y(t))=-\mathrm{sign}(Z(t))\},\end{cases}

(2.4)

where
\tau_{0}=\theta_{0}=0
and

.
To show the behavior of the restoring force

with respect to

,

.

corresponds to each entry in the plastic regime,

(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}

When
t\in(\tau_{n},\theta_{n+1})

and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X(t)=Y(t)\mathrm{d}t,\\

\mathrm{d}Z(t)=0.\end{cases}

3
Invariant probability measure

In this section,
we first derive the backward Kolmogorov equations,
then we reveal the existence of the invariant probability measure.
Moreover, we provide an approximation to this measure.
As the proofs are classic, we place them in Appendix
A
for the reader’s convenience.

3.1
Backward Kolmogorov equations

Let
\beta(x,y,z)=\mathfrak{f}(x,y)-k(1-\alpha)z-k\alpha x
,
define the operators
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}_{+}
, and
\mathbf{B}_{-}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\triangleq\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{y}+y\partial_{x}+\beta(x,y,z)\partial_{y}+y\partial_{z},

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{+}\triangleq\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{y}+y\partial_{x}+\beta(x,y,b)\partial_{y}+\min(0,y)\partial_{z},

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{-}\triangleq\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{y}+y\partial_{x}+\beta(x,y,-b)\partial_{y}+\max(0,y)\partial_{z}.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
solve SVI (
2.3
),
let
g\in L^{2}(\bar{\mathcal{D}})
, and consider

\displaystyle\psi(t,\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{X}(t))|\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}],

(3.1)

then

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\psi-\mathbf{A}\psi=0,~~\text{in~}(0,T]\times\mathcal{D},\\
\partial_{t}\psi-\mathbf{B}_{\pm}\psi=0,~~\text{in~}(0,T]\times\mathcal{D}^{\pm},\\
\psi(0,\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x}).\end{cases}

(3.2)

3.2
Existence of an invariant probability measure

By selecting an appropriate Lyapunov function,
an energy estimate for the SVI (
2.3
) is established in Theorem
3.3
.
Subsequently, the existence of an invariant probability measure for
\mathbf{X}(t)
is deduced from Theorem
3.4
.
To compute this invariant measure,
an approximation scheme based on a PDE approach is presented in Theorem
3.6
.
To show these, the following assumptions are needed.
For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle y\mathfrak{f}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq-c_{0}y^{2}+c_{1}xy+c_{2},~\text{with}~c_{0}>0,~c_{1}\leq k\alpha,~c_{2}\geq 0,

(3.3)

\displaystyle x\mathfrak{f}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq-d_{0}xy+d_{1}x^{2}+d_{2},~\text{with}~d_{0}\geq c_{0},~d_{1}<k\alpha,~d_{2}\geq 0.

(3.4)

Remark 3.2
.

Assumptions (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) contain the case
\mathfrak{f}(x,y)=-c_{0}y
from EPPO

[
5
]
, where
c_{0}>0
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let

V(x,y)\triangleq(k\alpha+\frac{c_{0}d_{0}}{2}-c_{1})x^{2}+y^{2}+c_{0}xy
.
For any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(X(t),Y(t))]\leq V(X(0),Y(0))+C/C_{1},

where
C=\sigma^{2}+2c_{2}+c_{0}d_{2}+(kb(1-\alpha))^{2}\left(\frac{2}{c_{0}}+\frac{c_{0}}{2(k\alpha-d_{1})}\right)

and

C_{1}=\min\left(\frac{c_{0}}{3},\frac{c_{0}(k\alpha-d_{1})}{2k\alpha+c_{0}d_{0}+c^{2}_{0}-2c_{1}}\right)
.

Next, we define the probability law
\mu(t)
on
(\bar{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{B})
associated with
\mathbf{X}(t)
by

\displaystyle\mu(t)(\phi)\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\phi(\mathbf{X}(t))]=\mu(0)(P_{t}\phi),~~\text{for}~\phi\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathcal{D}}).

The operator
P_{t}
is defined by

P_{t}\phi(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\phi(\mathbf{X}(t))|\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}]
,
for
\phi\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathcal{D}})
.

Theorem 3.4
.

The process
\mathbf{X}(t)
generated by SVI (
2.3
)
with initial condition
\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}\in\bar{\mathcal{D}}

admits an invariant measure
\mu
, i.e.,

\mu(0)=\mu
implies
\mu(t)=\mu
,
\forall t\geq 0.

Such an invariant measure
\mu
for
\mathbf{X}

must annihilate the infinitesimal generator of
P_{t}
on any test function,
as shown in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.5
.

The measure
\mu
satisfies the ultra-weak formulation

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbf{A}\varphi(x,y,z)\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathcal{D}^{+}}\mathbf{B}_{+}\varphi(x,y,b)\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathcal{D}^{-}}\mathbf{B}_{-}\varphi(x,y,-b)\mathrm{d}\mu=0,~~\forall\varphi\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathcal{D}}).

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
g\in L^{2}(\bar{\mathcal{D}})
.
The unique solution
u_{\lambda}
bounded by

\frac{||g||_{\infty}}{\lambda}
solves the following PDEs

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\lambda u-\mathbf{A}u=g,~~\text{in~}\mathcal{D},\\
\lambda u-\mathbf{B}_{\pm}u=g,~~\text{in~}\mathcal{D}^{\pm},\end{cases}

(3.5)

satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lambda u_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{X}(t))|\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}]=\int_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}}g\mathrm{d}\mu,~\forall\mathbf{x}\in\bar{\mathcal{D}}.

(3.6)

Remark 3.7
.

As noted in
[
5
]
,
(
3.6
) is numerically significant in that it allows for the calculation
of
\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{X}(t))|\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}]
without solving a time-dependent problem.
Furthermore,
the resulting limit is independent of the initial position
\mathbf{x}
.

Remark 3.8
.

The uniqueness of the invariant measure remains a challenging issue,
as the system exhibits degenerate noise and non-smooth drift due to hysteresis,
which precludes the application of standard ergodic theory for diffusions.
Nevertheless, in numerical experiments,
we compare the “spatial average” derived from the invariant measure (via PDE methods) with
the “temporal average” obtained from long-term simulations (via Monte Carlo).
These comparisons strengthen our confidence in the ergodicity assumption.

4
Numerical method for the invariant measure

For numerical computation,
the unbounded domain
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
is truncated by

\mathcal{D}_{XY}\triangleq[-\bar{x},\bar{x}]\times[-\bar{y},\bar{y}]\times[-b,b]
,
where
\bar{x}

and
\bar{y}
are chosen sufficiently large that the probability of finding the
underlying process outside
\mathcal{D}_{XY}
is negligible.
Note that the small parameter
\lambda
in PDEs (
3.5
) poses a challenge
for the finite difference method:
to preserve numerical accuracy,
an extremely fine mesh must be employed,
which incurs high computational costs.
To address this efficiency bottleneck, we introduce the scaling transformation:

\tilde{x}=\lambda x
,
\tilde{y}=\lambda y
,
\tilde{z}=\lambda z
,
and let
v(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z})\triangleq\lambda u(\frac{\tilde{x}}{\lambda},\frac{\tilde{y}}{\lambda},\frac{\tilde{z}}{\lambda})
.
As a consequence, the truncated domain changed to

[-\lambda\bar{x},\lambda\bar{x}]\times[-\lambda\bar{y},\lambda\bar{y}]\times[-\lambda b,\lambda b]
.
The numerical approximation is built on the following three-dimensional finite difference grid

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\triangleq\left\{(\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{i}},\tilde{y}_{\mathrm{j}},\tilde{z}_{\mathrm{k}})=(-\lambda\bar{x}+(\mathrm{i}-1)\delta_{\tilde{x}},-\lambda\bar{y}+(\mathrm{j}-1)\delta_{\tilde{y}},-\lambda b+(\mathrm{k}-1)\delta_{\tilde{z}})\right\}_{1\leq\mathrm{i}\leq I,1\leq\mathrm{j}\leq J,1\leq\mathrm{k}\leq K},

where
\delta_{\tilde{x}}\triangleq\frac{2\lambda\bar{x}}{I-1}
,

\delta_{\tilde{y}}\triangleq\frac{2\lambda\bar{y}}{J-1}
,

\delta_{\tilde{z}}\triangleq\frac{2\lambda b}{K-1}
,

I,J
, and
K
are odd integers greater than 1.
Let
v_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}
denote the numerical approximation of
v(\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{i}},\tilde{y}_{\mathrm{j}},\tilde{z}_{\mathrm{k}})
,
let
\bm{v}
be a vector whose elements are
v_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}
.
For the sake of completeness,
we provide the following notations of the difference symbols with respect to
\tilde{x}
,
analogous definitions apply to
\tilde{y}
and
\tilde{z}
.

•

The first-order forward and backward finite differences:

(\partial^{\mathrm{f}}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\triangleq(v_{\mathrm{i}+1,\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}-v_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}})/\delta_{\tilde{x}}
,

(\partial^{\mathrm{b}}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\triangleq(v_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}-v_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}})/\delta_{\tilde{x}}
.

•

The second-order centered finite difference:

(\partial^{2}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\triangleq(v_{\mathrm{i}+1,\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}-2v_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}+v_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}})/\delta^{2}_{\tilde{x}}
.

•

The second-order forward and backward finite differences:

(\partial^{\mathrm{ff}}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\triangleq(-3v_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}+4v_{\mathrm{i}+1,\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}-v_{\mathrm{i}+2,\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}})/(2\delta_{\tilde{x}})
,

(\partial^{\mathrm{bb}}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\triangleq(3v_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}-4v_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}+v_{\mathrm{i}-2,\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}})/(2\delta_{\tilde{x}})
.

•

The second-order upwind difference:

(c(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z})\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\triangleq\max(0,c_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}})(\partial^{\mathrm{ff}}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}+\min(0,c_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}})(\partial^{\mathrm{bb}}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}
.

The discretization of the scaled version of PDEs (
3.5
) follows the form:

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}-(L\bm{v})_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}=\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}},

(4.1)

where

(L\bm{v})_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\triangleq(L_{\tilde{y}}\bm{v})_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}+S_{\tilde{x}}+S_{\tilde{z}}
,
with
(L_{\tilde{y}}\bm{v})_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}=\frac{\lambda\sigma^{2}}{2}(\partial^{2}_{\tilde{y}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}+(\tilde{\beta}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z})\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{y}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}
,

\tilde{\beta}
and
\tilde{g}
are the scaled version of the functions
\beta
and
g
.
The terms
S_{\tilde{x}}
and
S_{\tilde{z}}

are determined by the region types and boundary indices, as specified below:

•

Interior (black triangles, shown in Fig.

S_{\tilde{x}}=(\frac{\tilde{y}}{\lambda}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}},\quad S_{\tilde{z}}=(\frac{\tilde{y}}{\lambda}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{z}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}
.

•

Boundaries (blue/red squares and diamonds, shown in Fig.

\displaystyle S_{\tilde{x}}=\begin{cases}\max(0,\frac{\tilde{y}_{\mathrm{j}}}{\lambda})(\partial^{\mathrm{ff}}_{\tilde{x}}v)_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}},&\text{if}~\mathrm{i}=1,\\

5
Numerical results

In seismology, statistical physics, and engineering,
it is necessary to investigate the frequency of threshold crossing and the probability of the risk of failure,
especially for risk assessment and schematic design.
For instance, in building and bridge structural design,
quantifying the frequency and amplitude of ground motion
at specific sites is essential to ensure the seismic resilience of constructions.

5.1
Frequency of crossing threshold

In this subsection,
we focus on empirical approaches to compute the crossing frequency of SVI (
2.3
) on a unit time interval.
One of the powerful tools to compute this quantity is the Rice’s formula:

\displaystyle\nu(a_{1})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|y|p(a_{1},y)\mathrm{d}y,

where
p(\cdot,\cdot)
is the joint density of
X(0)
and
\dot{X}(0)
,

a_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
is a given threshold.
For details, refer to Appendix
C
and
[
21
]
.
Generally, the exact expression of the invariant probability density function of (
2.3
) is not easy to obtain.
Therefore, we cannot use Rice’s formula directly.
Instead, we provide an approximation to this quantity.
Specifically,
Theorem
3.6
implies that

\displaystyle\nu(a_{1})=\int_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}}g(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\lambda u_{\lambda},

where
u_{\lambda}
is the solution of (
3.5
) with
g(\mathbf{x})=|y|\delta(x-a_{1})
.
In practice,
g(\mathbf{x})
can be approximated by

\frac{|y|}{\sqrt{2\pi}\varepsilon_{0}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-a_{1})^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}\right)

for a small
\varepsilon_{0}>0
.

Another direct approach is through Monte Carlo simulation.
Let
\delta_{t}>0
be the time step,
and take a sufficiently large final time
T=N\delta_{t}
.
Define the discrete times
t_{n}=n\delta_{t}
for
n=0,1,\ldots,N
.
We then construct a sequence
\{(X_{n},Y_{n},Z_{n}),0\leq n\leq N\}
.
For each
n
,
(X_{n},Y_{n},Z_{n})
denotes an approximation of
(X(t_{n}),Y(t_{n}),Z(t_{n}))
.
By using the property of Dirac delta function, we proceed with the following approximation

\displaystyle\nu(a_{1})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|Y(t)|\delta(X(t)-a_{1})\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|Y(t)|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{j:X_{j}=a_{1},Y_{j}\neq 0\}\end{subarray}}\frac{\delta(t-t_{j})}{|Y_{j}|}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{j}=a_{1},Y_{j}\neq 0\}}.

We compare the results computed using the finite difference approach to PDEs (
3.5
)
with the results obtained by Monte Carlo simulations.
The settings of parameters are listed in Appendix
D
.
As shown in Fig.
4
, the results based on Monte Carlo are close to the PDE results.

5.2
Probability of the serviceability limit state

Unlike elastic deformation,
plastic deformation is permanent and cumulative.
The deformation
\Delta(t)=X(t)-Z(t)
can result either from a single large event or
from the accumulation of many smaller ones,
the latter being a direct consequence of the ratchet effect.
In this subsection, we study the probability

\mathbf{P}(a_{2})\triangleq\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}[|X(t)-Z(t)|\leq a_{2}~|~\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}]
.
By Theorem
3.6
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(a_{2})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\{|X(t)-Z(t)|\leq a_{2}\}}(\mathbf{X}(t))~|~\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\lambda u_{\lambda},

where
u_{\lambda}
is the solution of (
3.5
) with

g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{1}_{\{|x-z|\leq a_{2}\}}(\mathbf{x})
,

a_{2}\geq 0
.
For the probabilistic numerical scheme,
we use the Monte Carlo method, and the settings are the same as in Example 1.
Then,
\mathbf{P}(a_{2})
can be approximated by

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(a_{2})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{j}-Z_{j}|\leq a_{2}\}}.

The numerical results obtained from PDE and Monte Carlo approaches are shown in Fig.

.
The settings of parameters can be found in Appendix
D
.

5.3
Empirical convergence rate from the numerical case studies

Since the exact solution is not always available,
we employ numerical solutions computed on different meshes to estimate the empirical convergence rate
of the PDE-based approach.
To be precise,
let
\mathcal{U}^{h}
be the numerical solution on a uniform mesh with spacing
h
and

\mathcal{U}^{\text{exact}}
the exact solution,
assume
p
is the convergence rate for numerical schemes, then

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{U}^{h}-\mathcal{U}^{\text{exact}}\|_{\infty}\leq Ch^{p},

where
C
is a constant independent of
h
.
Moreover, assume that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{U}^{h}-\mathcal{U}^{\text{exact}}\|_{\infty}=Ch^{p}+O(h^{p+\epsilon}),\quad\text{for~some~}\epsilon>0,

then

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathcal{U}^{h}-\mathcal{U}^{h/2}\|_{\infty}}{\|\mathcal{U}^{h/2}-\mathcal{U}^{h/4}\|_{\infty}}\approx 2^{p}+O(h^{\epsilon}).

With such a relation in mind, we test the convergence rate of the numerical scheme by considering

\displaystyle p(h)\triangleq\log_{2}\left(\frac{\|\mathcal{U}^{h}-\mathcal{U}^{h/2}\|_{\infty}}{\|\mathcal{U}^{h/2}-\mathcal{U}^{h/4}\|_{\infty}}\right).

For more details, refer to
[

,

]
.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we designed a PDE-based numerical method for computing the invariant measure
and steady-state statistics of a white-noise-driven bilinear hysteresis oscillator.
The approach offers an alternative to Monte Carlo simulations.
By establishing a theoretical extension of the EPPO model and
providing an appropriate Lyapunov function,
we demonstrated the existence of an invariant probability measure,
although the uniqueness remains a challenging problem beyond the scope of this paper.
Applications of the proposed framework,
including the calculation of threshold crossing frequency and serviceability probability,
illustrate its practical utility for analyzing high-dimensional nonlinear and nonsmooth dynamical systems.
Numerical results confirm the efficiency and promise of our strategy
for broadening the scope of alternatives to traditional Monte Carlo methods.

Proofs of theorems

Firstly, let
\mathbf{X}
solve SVI (
2.3
),
we note that for a smooth enough function
F
,

\displaystyle F(t,\mathbf{X}(t))=

\displaystyle~F(0,\mathbf{X}(0))+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{s}F(s,\mathbf{X}(s))\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}F(s,\mathbf{X}(s))\mathrm{d}X(s)+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{y}F(s,\mathbf{X}(s))\mathrm{d}Y(s)

(A.1)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial^{2}_{y}F(s,\mathbf{X}(s))\mathrm{d}s+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{z}F(s,\mathbf{X}(s))\mathrm{d}Z(s),}_{=\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{z}F(s,\mathbf{X}(s))Y(s)\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{z}F(s,\mathbf{X}(s))\mathrm{d}\Delta(s)}

where
Z(t)=X(t)-\Delta(t)
and

\mathrm{d}\Delta(t)=\mathbf{1}_{\{Z(t)=b\}}\max(0,Y(t))\mathrm{d}t+\mathbf{1}_{\{Z(t)=-b\}}\min(0,Y(t))\mathrm{d}t
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We prove that if
\psi
is a solution of (
3.2
),
then
\psi
satisfies (
3.1
).
For any
t,\tau\in[0,T]
,
by Itô’s formula (regard
t
as a constant and refer to (
A.1
)),

\displaystyle\psi(t-\tau,\mathbf{X}(\tau))

\displaystyle=\psi(t,\mathbf{X}(0))-\int_{0}^{\tau}\partial_{s}\psi\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{\tau}\partial_{x}\psi\mathrm{d}X(s)+\int_{0}^{\tau}\partial_{y}\psi\mathrm{d}Y(s)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\partial^{2}_{y}\psi\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle~~+\int_{0}^{\tau}Y(s)\partial_{z}\psi\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{\tau}\partial_{z}\psi\mathbf{1}_{\{Z(s)=b\}}\max(0,Y(s))\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{\tau}\partial_{z}\psi\mathbf{1}_{\{Z(s)=-b\}}\min(0,Y(s))\mathrm{d}s.

Applying

\displaystyle 1=\mathbf{1}_{\{|Z(s)|<b\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{Z(s)=b\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{Z(s)=-b\}},

\displaystyle Y(s)-\max(0,Y(s))=\min(0,Y(s)),

and noting that
\psi
solves (
3.2

\displaystyle\psi(t-\tau,\mathbf{X}(\tau))=\psi(t,\mathbf{X}(0))+\text{mean-zero~term}.

Since
\tau
is arbitrary, let
\tau=t
,
take the expectation conditional on
\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\psi(0,\mathbf{X}(t))|\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{E}[\psi(t,\mathbf{X}(0))|\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}],

that is

\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{X}(t))|\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}]=\psi(t,\mathbf{x})
. ∎

Especially, if the function
g
in Theorem
3.1
is replaced by an indicator function,
we can obtain the following corollary.

Corollary A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Borel
\sigma
-field on
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
,
a function
u(s,\mathbf{x})
given by

\displaystyle u(s,\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{X}(t))|\mathbf{X}(s)=\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{P}_{(s,\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathcal{B}),

with
0\leq s\leq t
and
\mathbf{x}\in\bar{\mathcal{D}}
, is a solution of the equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{s}u+\mathbf{A}u=0,~~\text{in~}[0,t)\times\mathcal{D},\\
\partial_{s}u+\mathbf{B}_{\pm}u=0,~~\text{in~}[0,t)\times\mathcal{D}^{\pm},\\
u(t,\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{x}).\end{cases}

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

We consider the system in the elastic regime, for the plastic regime, the same result can be obtained.
By Itô’s formula,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[X^{2}(t)]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}[X(t)Y(t)],

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}(t)]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}[Y(t)\mathfrak{f}(X(t),Y(t))-k(1-\alpha)Y(t)Z(t)-k\alpha X(t)Y(t)]+\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[X(t)Y(t)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}(t)+X(t)\mathfrak{f}(X(t),Y(t))-k(1-\alpha)X(t)Z(t)-k\alpha X^{2}(t)].

Recall that

\displaystyle V(x,y)=\left(k\alpha+\frac{c_{0}d_{0}}{2}-c_{1}\right)x^{2}+y^{2}+c_{0}xy.

By using the assumptions (
3.3
) and (
3.4
),
and the Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[V(X(t),Y(t))]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[-c_{0}Y^{2}(t)-c_{0}(k\alpha-d_{1})X^{2}(t)-2k(1-\alpha)Y(t)Z(t)-c_{0}k(1-\alpha)X(t)Z(t)]+\hat{C}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{c_{0}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(k\alpha-d_{1})X^{2}(t)+Y^{2}(t)]+C,

where
\hat{C}=\sigma^{2}+c_{0}d_{2}+2c_{2}
,

C=\hat{C}+(kb(1-\alpha))^{2}\left(\frac{2}{c_{0}}+\frac{c_{0}}{2(k\alpha-d_{1})}\right)
.
Moreover, since

\displaystyle V(x,y)\leq\left(k\alpha+\frac{c_{0}d_{0}}{2}+\frac{c^{2}_{0}}{2}-c_{1}\right)x^{2}+\frac{3}{2}y^{2},~\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R},

then

\displaystyle C_{1}V(x,y)\leq\frac{c_{0}}{2}[(k\alpha-d_{1})x^{2}+y^{2}],

where
C_{1}=\min\left(\frac{c_{0}}{3},\frac{c_{0}(k\alpha-d_{1})}{2k\alpha+c_{0}d_{0}+c^{2}_{0}-2c_{1}}\right)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[V(X(t),Y(t))]+C_{1}\mathbb{E}[V(X(t),Y(t))]\leq C,

which implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(X(t),Y(t))]

\displaystyle\leq V(X(0),Y(0))e^{-C_{1}t}+C(1-e^{-C_{1}t})/C_{1}

\displaystyle\leq V(X(0),Y(0))+C/C_{1},~~~\forall t>0.

∎

Define the probability law
\mu_{{}_{T}}
on
(\bar{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{B})
associated with
T>0
by

\displaystyle\mu_{{}_{T}}(\phi)\triangleq\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\phi(\mathbf{X}(t))]\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mu(0)(P_{t}\phi)\mathrm{d}t,~~\text{for}~\phi\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathcal{D}}).

In fact,
\mu_{{}_{T}}
corresponds to the ergodic mean.
(For the definition of ergodic, refer to
[
17
]
, p. 154.)

To prove Theorem
3.4
, the following lemma is necessary.

Lemma A.2
.

The sequence
\{\mu_{{}_{T_{n}}}\}_{n\geq 1}
is tight, for any
T_{n}\uparrow\infty
.

Proof.

We need to prove that for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
there exists a compact set
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{B}
,
such that

\displaystyle\mu_{{}_{T_{n}}}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}})\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Notice that

\displaystyle\mu_{{}_{T_{n}}}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}})=\frac{1}{T_{n}}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{X}(t))]\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{T_{n}}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon})\mathrm{d}t.

Therefore,

\displaystyle 1-\mu_{{}_{T_{n}}}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}})=\frac{1}{T_{n}}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}(t)\notin\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon})\mathrm{d}t.

We then show that the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\triangleq\left\{(x,y,z):\mathfrak{h}|x|+\Big|\frac{c_{0}}{2}x+y\Big|\leq\frac{1}{\delta},|z|\leq b\right\}

is appropriate, where
\delta=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak{c}}}
,

\mathfrak{c}=V(X(0),Y(0))+C/C_{1}
, and

\mathfrak{h}=\left(k\alpha+c_{0}d_{0}/2-c_{1}-c_{0}^{2}/4\right)^{1/2}
.
To see this, by applying Theorem
3.3
and Chebyshev’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}(t)\notin\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathfrak{h}|X(t)|>\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|\frac{c_{0}}{2}X(t)+Y(t)\Big|>\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\delta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{h}^{2}|X(t)|^{2}+\Big|\frac{c_{0}}{2}X(t)+Y(t)\Big|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4\delta^{2}\mathfrak{c}=\varepsilon,

which implies the result.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

For any
\mu(0)
,
\{\mu_{{}_{T_{n}}}\}_{n\geq 1}
is tight according to Lemma
A.2
,
hence there exists a subsequence
\{\mu_{{}_{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}}\}_{\mathrm{k}\geq 1}
that converges weakly to a certain measure
\mu
,
i.e., for any
\phi\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathcal{D}})
,

\displaystyle\mu_{{}_{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}}(\phi)\xrightarrow[\mathrm{k}]{}\mu(\phi).

Then
\mu
is invariant, since

\displaystyle\mu(P_{t}\phi)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mathrm{k}}\mu_{{}_{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}}(P_{t}\phi)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mathrm{k}}\frac{1}{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}\int_{0}^{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}\mu(0)(P_{s}P_{t}\phi)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mathrm{k}}\frac{1}{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}\int_{0}^{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}\mu(0)(P_{s+t}\phi)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mathrm{k}}\frac{1}{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}\int_{t}^{t+T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}\mu(0)(P_{s}\phi)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mathrm{k}}\frac{1}{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}\int_{0}^{T_{n_{\mathrm{k}}}}\mu(0)(P_{s}\phi)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\mu(\phi).

∎

Proof of Corollary
3.5
.

By the definition of invariant measure (refer to
[
1
]
, pp. 55-56),
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}}\Lambda(\varphi)\mathrm{d}\mu=0,~~\forall\varphi\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathcal{D}}),

where
\Lambda
is the infinitesimal generator of
P_{t}
.
That is also

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbf{A}\varphi(x,y,z)\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathcal{D}^{+}}\mathbf{B}_{+}\varphi(x,y,b)\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathcal{D}^{-}}\mathbf{B}_{-}\varphi(x,y,-b)\mathrm{d}\mu=0.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

(i) Consider the Laplace transformation to the solution of (
3.2
) in
\mathcal{D}
,
denoted as
u_{\lambda}
:

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}t,~~\text{for}~\lambda>0.

Integration by parts gives that

\displaystyle\lambda u_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-e^{-\lambda t}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})\Big|_{t=0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\partial_{t}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\psi(0,\mathbf{x})+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbf{A}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}t.

Note that the operator
\mathbf{A}
is independent of
t
, we get

\displaystyle\lambda u_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{A}u_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}).

Using the same way, on the boundaries
\mathcal{D}^{\pm}
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda u_{\lambda}(x,y,\pm b)-\mathbf{B}_{\pm}u_{\lambda}(x,y,\pm b)=g(x,y,\pm b).

The Laplace transformation implies the boundedness:

\displaystyle|u_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\sup_{[0,T]\times\bar{\mathcal{D}}}|\psi(t,\mathbf{x})|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}}{\lambda}.

(ii) Thanks to the final value theorem for the Laplace transformation,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\lambda u_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\psi(t,\mathbf{x}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\lambda u_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{X}(t))|\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}]=\int_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}}g\mathrm{d}\mu,~\forall\mathbf{x}\in\bar{\mathcal{D}}.

∎

Appendix C
Rice’s formula

Before introduce the Rice’s formula, we need the following definition.

Definition A.1

(Upcrossing and downcrossing
[
22
]
)
.

For an almost sure (a.s.) continuous process
\zeta=\{\zeta(t),t\geq 0\}
,
we say it has a upcrossing of
a\in\mathbb{R}
at
t_{0}>0
,
if for some
\varepsilon>0
,
\zeta(t)\leq a
in
(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0})
and

\zeta(t)\geq a
in
(t_{0},t_{0}+\varepsilon)
.
The downcrossing of the level
a
is similarly defined by reversing inequalities.

For the a.s. differentiable process
\zeta
, it has a upcrossing (downcrossing) of
a
at
t_{0}>0
,
if
\dot{\zeta}(t_{0})>0
(
\dot{\zeta}(t_{0})<0
).

Next, we introduce the Rice’s formula.
Let
\eta=\{\eta(t),t\geq 0\}
be a continuous and strictly stationary process.
The distribution of
\eta(0)
, denoted by
F(x)=\mathbb{P}(\eta(0)\leq x)
,
is assumed to be a continuous function.
For a fixed
a\in\mathbb{R}
, let
N^{+}(a)
be the number of upcrossing of level
a
by
\eta
on
[0,1]
,
the intensity of upcrossing can be evaluated using the relation

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(a)\triangleq\mathbb{E}[N^{+}(a)]=\lim_{q\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{P}(\eta(0)<a<\eta(q)).

The proof can be found in
[
21
]
Lemma 7.2.2.
If the process
\eta
is Gaussian and the derivative
\dot{\eta}(t)
exists in a quadratic mean,
then Rice’s formula states that

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(a)=\int_{0}^{\infty}yp(a,y)\mathrm{d}y,

where
p(a,y)
is the joint density of
\eta(0)
and
\dot{\eta}(0)
(refer to
[
21
]
Theorem 7.2.4).
A more general form of Rice’s formula for the intensity of crossing a given level
a
:

\displaystyle\nu(a)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|y|p(a,y)\mathrm{d}y,

where
\nu(a)=\nu^{+}(a)+\nu^{-}(a)
, and
\nu^{-}(a)
is the downcrossing intensity.
