Variable efficacy of the non-covalent KRASG12D inhibitor (MRTX-1133) with obesity in murine pancreatic cancer
Authors: Sujith Sarvesh, Holly Stephens, Pia Muri, Henry Nnaemeka Ogbonna, Jianqing Zhang, Karen Aikhionbare, Dhana Sekar Reddy Bandi, Cherlene Hardy, Ganji Purnachandra Nagaraju, Bassel F. El-Rayes, Lyse A. Norian, Jeremy B. Foote
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous studies indicate that Diet-induced-obesity (DIO) promotes pancreatic tumor progression, immunosuppression, and resistance to chemotherapy. Here, we evaluated the impact of DIO on efficacy of KRASG12D-specific inhibitor MRTX1133 in syngeneic mouse PDAC cell lines implanted into lean and DIO mice. MRTX1133 induced regression in the 2838c3 syngeneic murine PDAC model irrespective of body condition. However, DIO promoted recruitment of gMDSC and reductions in cytotoxic T cells within the 2838c3 T-cell inflamed TME, correlating with loss of control of microscopic disease. These findings suggest that DIO reduces the potency of targeted KRASG12D inhibition in T cell-inflamed PDAC.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is currently the third leading cause of cancer-associated mortality in the US1 with a 5-year survival rate of 13%1. Hallmark features of PDAC associated with poor outcomes include mutant KRASG12D/V/R-driven oncogenesis in the majority of PDAC patients, an abundant fibrotic stroma enriched with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) that promotes tumor progression and immune suppression, an abundance of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and M2 polarized macrophages, which promote immune suppression and exclusion of cytotoxic T and NK cell migration into the tumor microenvironment (TME)2,3.

Obesity is a major risk factor for PDAC4–6 as it accelerates tumorigenesis, enhances oncogenic KRASG12D signaling7, and induces metabolic reprogramming in genetically engineered mouse models of PDAC8. Diet-induced obesity (DIO) is linked to the development of hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes, increasing the risk of developing PDAC9. Furthermore, DIO promotes recruitment of immunosuppressive macrophages and MDSCs9,10, exclusion of T and NK cells and reductions in their cytotoxic activities in preclinical models of PDAC and other tumor types3,11–13. Obesity-driven changes in myeloid infiltration within tumors are related to cytokine and chemokine changes within the microenvironment that favor the recruitment of immature and mature MDSCs13.

Recent advances in targeting oncogenic KRAS signaling began with development of G12C specific inhibitors and extending within the last several years to G12D and pan-KRAS inhibitors which have the potential to exhibit broad activity in PDAC reaching over 90% of patients due to mutations in KRAS involving the glycine 12 residue, specifically missense mutations to aspartic acid (42%), valine (31%), and arginine (17%), cysteine (<2%)14. Overall, G12C, G12D, and pan-KRAS inhibitors have demonstrated potent anti-tumor activity and the ability to reprogram the PDAC TME from a myeloid-enriched, immunosuppressive type to a TME enriched with functional cytotoxic T cells and pro-inflammatory macrophages that support anti-tumor immunity in models of PDAC, NSCLC, and CRC in animals with a lean body mass15–20.

Given the impact of obesity on both oncogenic KRASG12D signaling and suppression of anti-tumor immunity, it was our intent to conduct a proof-of-concept study with the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 using syngeneic mouse PDAC cell lines bearing different levels of T cell infiltration21 to better understand whether DIO would impact both efficacy of oncogenic KRAS inhibition in the PDAC TME and the anti-tumor immune responses critical for control of microscopic disease16.

MRTX1133 inhibits RAS-MAPK signaling, inducing cell cycle arrest and tumor regression

As in previous studies15,16, MRTX1133 treatments in vitro reduced expression of phosphorylated Erk1/2 (pERK1/2), a downstream readout for RAS-MAPK activity (Fig. 1a, b), and induced G0-G1 phase cell cycle arrest (Fig. 1c–f)16 and apoptosis (Fig. 1g, h) in 2838c3 and 6491c5 PDAC cell lines. These findings are consistent with a growth-inhibitory role of MRTX113322. To study this in vivo, lean (mean = 22.58 g) and DIO (mean = 39.65 g) mice were generated (Fig. 2a, outlined in “Methods”) by feeding diets high or low in fat for 3-months (Fig. 2b). Secondary alterations in fasted blood glucose values (Fig. 2c) were noted in DIO mice, consistent with the development of a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced pre-diabetic state23. Lean and DIO C57BL/6 mice were then randomized into groups and implanted with tumor lines that generate PDAC models with either low (6419c5) or high (2838c3) T-cell abundance. T cell infiltration differences in the two models are due to epigenetic regulation of Cxcl1 gene expression downstream of c-Myc overexpression in T cell-deficient 6419c5 tumors21. When 2838c3 and 6419c5 tumors reached 50 - 70 mm3, lean and DIO mice were treated with MRTX1133 for seven days (Fig. 2d; outlined in methods). MRTX1133 induced regression of established 2838c3 tumors in both lean and DIO recipient mice (Fig. 2e, f). However, in DIO mice, recurrence was common after cessation of MRTX1133 treatment: 89% (8/9 mice) of DIO vs. 31% of lean (4/13) 2838c3 tumor-bearing mice exhibited gross disease by Day 60 (Fig. 2g). In mice with T cell-deficient, 6419c5 tumors, the response to MRTX1133 was attenuated, with MRTX1133 inducing growth arrest in LFD recipients and slow growth in DIO recipients (Fig. 2h, i). Upon termination of MRTX1133 treatment, 6419c5 tumors regrew immediately in both lean and DIO (Fig. 2j) mice, reaching endpoint tumor volumes by Day 20. Thus, T cells are important for controlling gross and microscopic disease, consistent with previous findings for mutation-specific16,19 and Pan-KRAS inhibitors24. DIO increases granulocytic MDSCs (gMDSCs) infiltration into the MRTX1133-treated, 2838c3 TME

Recurrence of tumors in the DIO 2838c3 model suggested a role for obesity-induced suppression of T cell control of microscopic disease. Host obesity is known to alter the immune composition of the TME, increasing recruitment of immunosuppressive gMDSCs and excluding cytotoxic CD8+ T cells25 in mouse models of PDAC, other cancers13, and in non-tumor-bearing obese mice25 through induction of chronic inflammation and release of cytokines and growth factors that alter hematopoiesis, leading to increased mobilization of immature myeloid cells into the circulation.

Therefore, we evaluated changes in immune cell infiltration, focusing on myeloid and T cell subsets in lean versus DIO mice with established 2838c3 and 6419c5 tumors after completion of MRTX1133 treatment. To do this, we adjusted the dosing schedule from our previous methods to ensure sufficient residual tumor tissue (cells and mRNA) for the downstream applications focusing on evaluation of changes in immune cell recruitment and function (Fig. 3a; outlined in “Methods”). Alterations in dosing frequency did not significantly impact the efficacy of MRTX1133 in larger tumors (TV = 250–500 mm3) with either model (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c) and reduced pERK1/2 in vivo in both models, with some minor differences including partial pERK1/2 expression in MRTX1133-treated 6419c5 tumors from DIO recipients (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e), implying modest resistance. Consistent with gross evidence of regression, CD45- YFP+ tumor cells exhibited a decrease in viability based on ex vivo analysis of LIVE/DEAD Aqua staining in MRTX1133-treated LFD and HFD cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 1f). Evaluation of myeloid cell infiltration into the TME revealed a significant increase in Ly6CintLy6G+ gMDSCs in 2838c3 but not 6419c5 tumors (Fig. 3b), which exhibit higher baseline abundances of gMDSCs21. Immunohistochemistry revealed a similar pattern of gMDSCs infiltration into 2838c3 tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), confirming a role for DIO in the recruitment of gMDSCs into the 2838c3 TME. Phenotypic analyses revealed significant increases in PD-L1 and CD206 expression on gMDSCs within 2838c3 DIO tumors in response to MRTX1133 (Fig. 3c), suggesting polarization toward a tumor-promoting, immunosuppressive N2 neutrophil cell type26–28. Although no significant changes were noted in frequencies of CD11b+ Ly6G/C- F4/80+ macrophages in either model by flow cytometric anlaysis (Fig. 3d), increases in F4/80 expression within the tumor were noted by IHC in 2838c3 (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d), consistent with previous findings where MRTX1133 and the pan-RAS(ON) inhibitor RMC7977 induced expansion of macrophages in the PDAC TME16,24. Furthermore, MRTX1133 promoted M1 macrophage polarization in both models, most notably in 2838c3 and 6419c5 tumor-bearing LFD mice (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 2e, f), which is consistent with the ability of mutant KRAS inhibitors to promote M1 macrophage polarization in a range of preclinical tumor models with KRASG12C/D mutations, including colon20, lung19, and pancreas16,24.

DIO reduces T cell infiltration and cytotoxic effector activity of T cells post MRTX1133 treatment

Previous work indicates a correlation between increasing gMDSCs infiltration into PDAC TME and a reduction in CD8+ T cell abundance and function25. Therefore, we evaluated the impact of DIO on the T cell response to MRTX1133 treatment within the TME of 2838c3 and 6419c5 tumors, which, in our hands, have significant differences in the frequencies of intratumoral T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2g), consistent with previous published findings21. Analysis of CD3+ T cell infiltrate within TME revealed significant reductions in total intratumoral CD3+ T cells in DIO mice after MRTX133 treatment in 2838c3 but not 6419c5 (Supplementary Fig. 2h, i). Flow cytometry analysis revealed reductions in CD4+ Foxp3− (Fig. 3f, left panel) and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3h, left panel), but not CD4+ Foxp3+ (Fig. 3g, left panel) in MRTX1133-treated, 2838c3 tumors from DIO mice, however; no changes in T cell abundances were noted in 6419c5 tumors (Fig. 3f, g, right panels and Supplementary Fig. 2i, right panel). These findings were consistent with reductions in overall CD3+ T cell abundance in the 2838c3 TME of DIO mice treated with MRTX133, suggesting that alterations in myeloid infiltration blunt recruitment of T cells into the T cell-inflamed 2838c3 model. Prior studies in DIO breast cancer models and mouse PDAC models fed a normal diet revealed increased activation and Fas-ligand-induced cell death of CD8 T cells through interactions with tumor-infiltrating gMDSC13,17. In these models, Fas-ligand expression did not change on gMDSCs or other immune cell populations; however, analyses of T cell activation revealed significant increases in PD-1 (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and CD69 (Supplementary Fig. 3b), trending increases of CD44hiCD62L− T cell (Supplementary Fig. 3c) abundance, and significant increases in Ki67+ proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 3d) within CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from the 2838c3 TME. These data support a shift to an effector T cell phenotype in the 2838c3 T cell-inflamed model, consistent with previous findings in a DIO breast cancer model13. Functional characterization of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells isolated from MRTX1133-treated, 2838c3 TME from DIO mice revealed significant reductions in IFNγ in CD8+ and CD4+ T cells after in vitro stimulation of 2838c3 (Fig. 3i) but not 6419c5 (Fig. 3j) tumor-derived lymphocytes, indicating dysfunction. These findings were supported by reductions in mRNA for genes (Cd2, Cd3d, Cd3g, Cxcr3, Cxcr6, Dpp4, Lck, Ltb, Nkg7, and P4ha1) associated with T cell abundances, trafficking, retention, and Th1 effector activities (Fig. 3k) consistent with the hypothesis that increased gMDSCs infiltration into the TME impacts T and NK cell infiltration, function, and most likely turnover13 resulting in reductions in intratumoral T cell mediated control of microscopic tumor growth. Pathway and gene ontology analyses revealed downregulation of pathways associated with positive regulation of leukocyte activation, migration, and NK cell immunity (Supplementary Fig. 3e). These findings provide insight into the factors that facilitate the regrowth of tumors in the DIO 2838c3 model after cessation of MRTX1133 treatment, despite earlier gross evidence of regression of palpable tumors. In regard to the slow regrowth, several possibilities need to be considered. First, MRTX1133 induces exit from the cell cycle as demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 1g. Second, in in vitro studies, treatment with high levels of MRTX1133 induces a state of senescence in 2838c3 cells (Sarvesh et al. manuscript in preparation) implying that re-entry into cell cycle may be further delayed in comparison to 6419c5, which does not undergo senescence in vitro, re-enters cell cycle after 48 h treatment with MRTX1133 (data not shown), and displays some resistance to MRTX1133 induced inhibition of RAS-MAPK signaling in DIO mice. Lastly, despite the increase in MDSC and reductions in T cell infiltration and function, T cells with albeit reduced functionality are present in the TME, which contrast with a previous study where their depletion after cessation of MRTX1133 treatment results in rapid re-growth of tumors, implying complete loss of control occurs only when T cells are absent from the TME16.

DIO increases mRNA expression of pathways critical to lipid metabolism and immune suppression post MRTX1133 treatment

To begin to understand the impact of DIO on innate cell changes in the DIO 2838c3 TME, preliminary analysis of differential gene expression in 2838c3 lean and DIO models treated with MRTX1133 were performed using NanoString mRNA profiling. Differential gene expression analyses revealed significant increases in mRNAs for Cd36, Anglpt4, Col11a1, and Serpinb5 (Fig. 3k, l) within the DIO MRTX1133 tumor cohort (n = 3 samples/group). These genes are involved directly in promoting tumor proliferation, migration, stem cell-like properties29, metastasis30–32, EMT29, and metabolic alterations associated with lipid metabolism31. CD36 in particular is of interest as a potential adaption to MRTX1133 under conditions of DIO, as it is expressed on a wide range of cells within TME, including tumor, stromal, regulatory T cells, CAFs, and immunosuppressive myeloid cells33, however, findings in patients with regard to the TCGA and protein expression have yielded conflicting information. Analysis of TCGA data revealed that increased CD36 mRNA expression correlated with improved survival, despite protein upregulation in advanced tumor stages34. Analysis of protein expression in a Japanese patient cohort revealed that increased expression correlated with decreased overall survival, increased vascular invasion, and resistance to chemotherapy, implying a negative prognostic role for CD36 in PDAC35. Previous studies support a role for CD36 in recruitment, metabolic reprogramming, and immunosuppressive activities of gMDSCs36,37 and direct suppression of cytotoxic T cells through lipid peroxidation38, suggesting that the effects of DIO in MRTX1133-treated 2838c3 tumors may involve CD36 signaling.

Overall, these findings indicate a role for obesity-driven inflammation in dampening T cell-mediated control of residual microscopic disease after targeted KRASG12D inhibition that may negatively impact the efficacy of parenteral administration of MRTX11333 in obese patients, resulting in greater rates of disease recurrence. Additional studies on the molecular machinery involved, in particular CD36 and ANGPTL4, as promoters of metabolic reprogramming of cancer and immune cells, SERPINB5 and COL11A1, as modulators of stromal responses to treatment and tumor progression, and the impact of MDSC depletion and PD-1 ICB in combination with MRTX1133 in obese models of PDAC, are warranted.

Methods

Animals

Eight-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (000664) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Female mice were maintained under SPF conditions (mouse parvovirus 1, mouse parvovirus 2, minute virus of mice, parvovirus nonstructural protein-1, mouse hepatitis virus, Theiler’s murine encephalomyelitis virus, epizootic diarrhea of infant mice virus, sendai virus, pneumonia virus of mice, reovirus, mycoplasma pulmonis, lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, mouse adenovirus, ectromelia virus, k virus, polyoma virus) and preferentially used for these studies specifically to reduce incidence of aggression and injury seen in male mice when fed high fat diets as this would have complicated our subcutaneous modeling of tumor growth. Animals were monitored 3-days per week after diet changes and tumor implantation/treatments and clinically ill (excessive weight loss >15% of starting body weight, tumor ulceration, lethargy, anorexia, dehydration) animals were removed from study as needed. All animals were humanely euthanized by approved methods (C02 followed by cervical dislocation) prior to tissue collection. All animal experiments and procedures were performed in accordance with guidelines from the University of Alabama at Birmingham’s Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee (IACUC) using an approved IACUC protocol (APN22661).

Diet

Following 1 week of acclimation, animals were randomized to one of two matched, purified-ingredient diets: a low-fat diet (LFD; Research Diets 12450 J with 10% kcal from fat and matching sucrose to 12492) or high-fat diet (HFD; Research Diets 12492 with 60% kcal from fat) for 12–14 weeks, thereby generating lean (LFD) or diet-induced obese (DIO, HFD) mice. Mice were weighed weekly and fasted for 24 h followed by an assessment of blood glucose levels. Prior to use, individual mice were identified as DIO if their final body weight was >3 s.d. above the mean of age-matched lean (LFD-fed) mice within the same cohort, as per our reported methods31. Before tumor cell implantation and initiation of in vivo studies, we established mice with the appropriate body conditions by feeding either low fat diet (LFD; 10% kcal from fat) or high fat diet (HFD, 60% kcal from fat) for 12 weeks13. After this, mice were weighed and classified as either lean (LFD, final body weight <25 g) or DIO (HFD, final body weight >3 s.d. above the LFD mean) based on established metrics13,25.

Cell lines

Murine PDAC cell lines purchased from Kerafast (Boston, MA) were derived by Li et al.20 from tumor-bearing KrasLSL-G12D/+; Trp53LSL-R172H/+; Pdx1-Cre; Rosa26YFP/YFP (6419c5, 2838c3) mice and cultured in DMEM complete media as previously indicated21.

Tumor cell implantation

A single-cell suspension of murine PDAC cells was prepared in PBS and kept on ice until injection. Tumor cells (1 × 106) were injected subcutaneously into the flank regions of 8-week-old, female C57BL/6 mice.

MRTX1133 formulation

For in vitro treatments, MRTX1133 (Chemitek, Indianapolis IN) was dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 25 μM and diluted to 10, 100, 200, 500, 1000 ηM concentrations in DMEM complete media. PDAC tumor cell lines (2838c3 and 6419c5) were plated at 1.5 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated in working concentrations of MRTX1133 above for 3 h. Cells were then washed 3x in PBS and lysates were generated using RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors for downstream immunoblotting.

For in vivo studies MRTX1133 was formulated in 10% research grade Captisol (CyDex Pharmaceuticals) in 50 mmol/L citrate buffer pH 5.0. Once formulated, MRTX1133 was protected from light and stored at 4 °C for a 1-week treatment course. MRTX1133 was administered at 30 mg/kg via i.p. injection with b.i.d. dosing either daily (tumor growth curves) after tumors reached an average volume of 50–70 mm3 or in a modified dosing schedule (1 day SID, 3 days BID, 1 day rest, 1 day SID at endpoint) which was consistent with previous methodology studying the impact of MRTX1133 on 2838c3 and 6419c5 growth in mice fed normal diet16. After terminating treatment with MRTX1133 at day 8, MRTX1133-treated 2838c3 lean and DIO cohorts were monitored for 60 days using bi-weekly measurements of tumors. Mice with tumor implantation sites containing no palpable evidence of tumor mass (<3 × 3 mm) were considered to lack gross tumor burden (complete regression).

For FACS and NanoString mRNA analyses we modified the dosing and treatment regimens to ensure sufficient 2838c3 tumor tissue was present for downstream analysis after termination of MRTX1133 treatment. The following changes to the MRTX1133 treatment regimen were used with YFP-expressing 2838c3 and 6419c5 cells: (i) tumors were grown for a longer period of time (~14 days) to generate larger tumors (~ 300–700 mm3) prior to treatment and (ii) the treatment schedule was abbreviated to an initial PM dose on day 1, AM/PM dosing on days 2–4, a day of rest on day 5, followed by an AM dose on day 6, exactly 3 h prior to tissue collection for FACS, NanoString, and histology.

NanoString and gene ontology analysis of tumor immune microenvironment

At day 20 experimental endpoint, 2838c3 and 6419c5 PDAC tumors were excised and a portion was preserved in RNAlater. RNA was batch isolated and submitted to the UAB NanoString facility for interrogation using the nCounter PanCancer Immune Profiling Panel (XT-CSO-MIP1-12) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data analysis was performed using the Rosalind Bioinformatics Analysis Platform for NanoString nCounter Gene Analysis. Differentially expressed genes were identified as those with a p value < 0.05 and fold change > ±1.5. Due to the exploratory nature of our study and intent for validation, we used raw p values and did not adjust p values for multiple comparisons.

The NanoString tumor immunogenetic profiling dataset expression matrix was composed of n = 12 mice bearing 2838c3 tumors consisting of the following groups: (i) vehicle treated, lean body mass, (ii) MRTX1133 treated, lean body mass, (iii) vehicle treated, DIO, and (iv) MRTX1133 treated, DIO mice. Differentially expressed genes were separated into groups of upregulated or downregulated genes and subjected to Gene Ontology enrichment analysis using the PANTHER classification system to identify associated biological processes (http://geneontology.org/).

Immunoblotting

Tumor or cell lysates were generated after treatment with RIPA lysis buffer (Thermoscientific, IL, USA) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Milipore, MA, USA) and sonicated for homogenization and equal protein concentrations were loaded and run on a 4–20% SDS-PAGE. Gels were transferred to a PVDF membrane, which were blocked with or 5% BSA in TBS-T (TBS with 0.1% Tween 20) depending on the blocking agent used for the primary antibodies, for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C on a rocker with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) for 45 min at room temperature on an orbital shaker. Membranes were then washed to remove excess secondary antibody and developed using SuperSignal West Enhanced Chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged using a Odyssey XF (Li-Cor). Representative whole blots (pERK, tERK, βactin) for 2838c3 (Supplementary Fig. 4) and 6419c5 (Supplementary Fig. 5) at 3 h post incubation in vehicle/MRTX1133 are included.

Immune-fluorescent Analysis

Mouse tumor specimens were fixed in 4% Paraformaldehyde (ThermoFisher, Waltham MA) overnight at 4 °C then incubated in 30% sucrose gradient overnight at 4 °C followed by flash freezing in OCT. Frozen, 5 μM sections were cut and antigen retrieval (Citrate buffer pH 6 at 100 °C for 5 min) was performed. Slides were blocked in 10% normal goat serum (ThermoFisher, Waltham MA) for 30 min at room temperature followed by overnight staining with antibodies specific for anti-mouse CD3ξ (clone 145-2C11, BioLegend, San Diego CA) anti-mouse F4/80 PE (clone BM8, BD Pharmingen, San Jose CA) and anti-mouse Ly6G (clone IA8, BioLegend, San Diego CA), phospho-ERK1/2 (clone, Cell Signal Technologies, Danvers, MA). For detection of CD3 ξ, streptavidin Alexa Flour 555 (ThermoFisher, Waltham MA) was used (1/500 dilution) and for detection of phosphor-ERK1/2, goat-anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Flour 555 (ThermoFisher, Waltham MA) was used (1/1000 dilution) and incubated for 30 min at RT in the dark. Slides were then washed and cover slipped with Fluoromount G + DAPI (ThermoFisher, Waltham MA) and imaged. IF images were acquired with a C5810 series digital color camera (Hamamatsu Photonic System, Bridgewater, NJ). Images were processed with Adobe Photoshop and IP LAB Spectrum software (Signal Analytics Software, Vienna, VA). For analysis, images were placed in RGB stack, gray-scaled then thresholded in red (CD3, F4/80, Ly6G) or blue pseudo-colored channels (DAPI), which provided the best contrast to quantify area and number of positive particles.

Multiparameter flow cytometry

Mouse tumors were digested in a 1 mg/mL collagenase IV and 0.1 mg/mL DNAase 1 (Worthington Biochemical, Lakewood, NJ) in HBSS for 45 min at 37 °C with intermittent shaking. Samples were then washed with RPMI containing 10% FBS and filtered through a 70 μM strainer generating single cell suspensions. Cells were labeled with primary fluorophore-conjugated antibodies and a live/dead stain (Supplementary Table 2) for 30–60 min at 4 °C, washed and re-suspended in flow buffer(2% Fetal bovine serum in DPBS). Cytokine expression from single cell suspensions of spleen and tumor was quantified as follows: cells were plated at a concentration of 106 cells in 1 mL of RP-10(RPMI + 10% FBS) media and stimulated for 5 h at 37 °C using a 1x solution of a Cell Activation Cocktail containing PMA ionomycin, and Brefeldin A (Biolegend, San Diego, CA). Conversely, cells were cultured for 5 h at 37 °C using a solution containing 5 μg of Brefeldin A (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) to serve as an unstimulated control. After cell surface staining, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 45 min at 4 °C then washed with 1x Perm/Wash (BD, 554723). For intracellular staining in tumor and spleen samples, cells were stained with an antibody cocktail specific intracellular cytokines (IL-2, IFNγ, TNFα, IL-17A, and granzyme B) in Perm/Wash solution for 60 min at 4 °C. Data were acquired on a Symphony A5 flow cytometer, with analysis performed using FlowJo version 10.7.2.

BMI and CD36 proteomic expression analysis in human PDAC

The PDAC cohort consisting of proteomic, transcriptomic, and clinical metadata was retrieved from Cao et al. (Cell 2021) study39. Data was accessed using cBioPortal (CPTAC Cell 2021, n = 140). The data were filtered to include only patients with complete records including survival and BMI measurements (n = 128). To study the relationship between CD36 protein expression and BMI, CD36 expression levels were stratified by BMI category (Underweight (<18.5 kg m−2), Normal (18.5 to <25 kg m−2), Overweight (25 to <30 kg m−2), Obese (>30 kg m−2)) and Kruskal–Wallis with pairwise Wilcoxon test was performed as a statistical test. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to study the linear correlation of CD36 expression levels and BMI.

Statistical analyses

Analyses of longitudinal data of tumor growth will be assessed using mixed effects models to evaluate significant differences (p < 0.05) between the slopes of each curve. We will apply best fit statistics to determine covariance. Each animal experiment will be carried out with a minimum of 8 animals per group/genotype, with a focus on female mice, which exhibit similar incidence of PDAC and are easier to manage on a high-fat diet. To account for attrition, we included an additional 2 mice/group for a total of 10 mice/group. For a continuous response, with n = 8 mice per group, we will have 81% power to detect a 2-fold difference between two groups. GraphPad Prism (version 9.1.2) was used for statistical analyses and graphical representation with data are presented as either means ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). Two-tailed Student’s t test (one group, single variable), One-way (4 groups, single variable) and two-way (4 groups, multiple independent variables) analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the appropriate tests with multiple corrections (Tukey’s and Bonferroni method) to correct for type 1 error were performed for determination of statistical significance between groups.
