Economic Burden of Overweight and Obesity in Sweden: Current Estimates and Future Projections
Authors: Adam Fridhammar, Jovanna Dahlgren, Marije Galavazi, Lena Hjelm, Jonatan Dereke, Katarina Steen Carlsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsOverweight and obesity are major public health challenges and well‐established risk factors for cardiovascular disease, cancer and premature mortality. We estimated the economic burden of overweight and obesity in Sweden in 2023 and projected the future economic burden in 2040 under alternative scenarios of increasing prevalence.Materials and MethodsA prevalence‐based cost‐of‐illness approach was used to estimate the economic burden of overweight and obesity in Sweden in 2023 from a societal perspective, including healthcare, social services, pharmaceuticals, productivity losses and informal care. The population attributable fraction method was used to estimate the additional cost burden from overweight and obesity compared to normal weight.ResultsThe total economic burden was estimated at approximately EUR 7 billion (EUR 664 per capita), with EUR 2.6 billion from overweight and EUR 4.4 billion from obesity. Productivity losses accounted for 71% of total costs, healthcare costs 21% and pharmaceuticals, informal care and social services 8%. Projections to 2040 suggest that demographic changes alone could increase the burden to EUR 7.2 billion, while rising prevalence could raise it to EUR 8.6 billion. A decrease of one percentage point in obesity prevalence would counteract the demographic effects, resulting in a reduction to EUR 7.1 billion in the projection to 2040.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the urgent need for effective obesity interventions. Societal strategies promoting healthy lifestyles, targeted prevention and improved access to obesity surgery and evidence‐based treatments may reduce the rising prevalence of obesity. Policymakers should weigh short‐term costs against long‐term savings.

Introduction

Overweight and obesity have become major global health issues. In 2022, 890 million adults had obesity (defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) and an additional 1.6 billion were overweight (defined as BMI ≥ 25–29.9 kg/m2) according to the World Health Organization [1]. The prevalence of obesity in the EU was 14.8% in 2022 [2]. In Sweden, as in other European countries, the prevalence of overweight and obesity has steadily increased over the recent decades, now present in more than half of the adult population, and in the last 10 years, the proportion of adults with obesity has increased by more than a third, from 13.6% in 2014 to 18.2% in 2024 [3]. Notable from a public health perspective is the increase in the proportion of Swedish children, adolescents and young adults with obesity (e.g., 6–9 years from 4.4% in 2015 to 7.2% in 2021 [3]).

Overweight and obesity are well‐established risk factors for many chronic diseases, including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, musculoskeletal diseases, respiratory diseases, mental illnesses and cancer, as well as for premature mortality [4]. Obesity and obesity‐related diseases pose a substantial burden on individuals and society at large, partly due to an increased need for healthcare and partly through productivity losses.

The economic burden of obesity in Europe has recently been estimated for Denmark, England, Finland, Germany, Greece, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain and the UK [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Three of the studies (England, Finland and Portugal) also included the economic burden of overweight. The total annual economic burden was reported for Denmark, Finland, Italy and Portugal and the lifetime economic burden for Germany, Greece, Spain, the Netherlands and the UK, while England included both annual and lifetime estimates.

The most up‐to‐date Swedish estimate of the economic burden of overweight and obesity was based on data from 2016 and focused on adults [11]. The study included costs for healthcare, pharmaceuticals, social services, productivity losses and informal care. It relied on international studies examining the relationship between BMI and obesity‐related diseases. More recently, two larger studies from Sweden (including over 4 million individuals) [12] and Finland (including nearly 115 000 individuals) [13] have been published, providing updated evidence on the increased risk for diseases associated with overweight and obesity.

The rising prevalence of obesity in Sweden and new evidence on obesity‐related diseases highlight the need for updated estimates of the economic burden. The aim of this study was to estimate the economic burden of overweight and obesity among adults and children in Sweden in 2023 and to project the future economic burden in 2040 under alternative scenarios.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

The study used a prevalence‐based cost‐of‐illness method to estimate the economic burden of overweight and obesity in 2023. The analysis was conducted from a societal perspective, including all relevant costs: healthcare (primary, specialised outpatient and inpatient), social services, pharmaceuticals, productivity losses and informal care, i.e., unpaid care provided by relatives. Healthcare costs included costs with obesity as the primary diagnosis in children and adults. In adults, they also included costs for obesity‐related diseases. Productivity losses included unemployment, short‐term sick leave, sickness benefits, disability benefits and productivity losses due to premature mortality.

The population attributable fraction (PAF) method was used to estimate the economic burden of obesity‐related diseases [14]. This method quantifies the proportion of an outcome that can be attributed to a specific risk factor, in this case overweight and obesity, respectively. The PAF method combines information on the prevalence of the risk factor (exposure) and the associated relative risks to estimate the share of the economic burden attributable to that exposure. The approach is widely used in studies of the economic burden of obesity [9, 10, 11, 15, 16], and provides a consistent basis for comparing economic impacts across settings. The following PAF formulas, accounting for multiple exposure levels (i.e., normal weight, overweight and obesity), were used: PAFOW=POW×RROW−1PNW+POW×RROW+POB×RROB PAFOB=POB×RROB−1PNW+POW×RROW+POB×RROB where PAFOW is the PAF for overweight, PAFOB is the PAF for obesity, P NW is the prevalence of normal weight, P OW is the prevalence of overweight, P OB is the prevalence of obesity, RROW is the relative risk for overweight and RROB is the relative risk for obesity.

Relative risks for obesity‐related diseases were used to estimate the PAF for each disease associated with overweight and obesity, respectively, which were multiplied by the total costs for healthcare, social services, pharmaceuticals and informal care for these diseases to calculate the cost associated with overweight and obesity.

For productivity losses, relative risks directly linking overweight and obesity to unemployment, short‐term sick leave, sickness benefits, disability benefits and premature mortality were used to calculate the PAF associated with overweight and obesity. These were then multiplied by the total productivity losses for each outcome to calculate the productivity losses associated with overweight and obesity.

Risk Estimates

All obesity‐related cancer risks were derived from a Swedish study of over 4 million individuals, most of whom had BMI measured at military conscription or during weeks 8 to 10 of pregnancy [12]. The corresponding obesity‐related non‐cancer disease risks came from a Finnish study of nearly 115 000 individuals with self‐reported BMI [13]. Only statistically significant risk increases from these sources were included in the current study. Data Sources

The most recent available data on prevalence, resource use and productivity loss at the time of data collection were used. All registry‐based data were from 2023. All costs were standardised to 2023 price levels and converted from Swedish krona to euro. The data sources are listed in Table S3.

The prevalence of overweight and obesity in 2024 for the adult population stratified by age and gender (Figure 1) was based on a National Public Health Survey conducted by the Public Health Agency of Sweden [3]. Corresponding data for children and adolescents were based on registry data from child healthcare centres and school healthcare services. The prevalence for intermittent age groups (3, 5 and 10 years) was extrapolated from existing data.

Prevalence of overweight and obesity in Sweden in 2024, showing (a) overweight and (b) obesity.

Healthcare costs were obtained from the Cost Per Patient (KPP) database at the Swedish Association of Local Authorities and Regions (SALAR), which provides national cost data for primary care and specialised out‐ and inpatient care, stratified by primary diagnosis, age and gender [22]. However, not all healthcare contacts are recorded in the database. The coverage in 2023 was 30% for primary care, 74% for specialised outpatient care and 94% for inpatient care. Recorded costs were therefore adjusted to reflect full coverage, assuming the same distribution of costs for unrecorded episodes.

Costs for social services related to coronary heart disease (CHD) and cerebrovascular disease (CVD) were based on a recently published international study covering 54 countries, including Sweden [23]. Official statistics on resource use in social services are not disease‐specific, and provision is based on the assessment of functional needs. Social services costs for the other obesity‐related diseases were not identified.

Medications used within hospital‐based care are included in the costs reported in the KPP database. Cost data related to prescription drugs for obesity [24], diabetes [25], CHD [23] and CVDs [23] were available and were included in a separate category. For drugs with multiple indications, it was not feasible to allocate prescription drug costs across obesity‐related diseases.

For productivity losses, the prevalence of unemployment was obtained from Statistics Sweden [26]. Short‐term sick leave refers to the first 14 days of illness, during which compensation is paid by the employer and was based on the total number of sick days from Statistics Sweden [27]. Long‐term sick leave encompasses both sickness benefits and disability benefits. Sickness benefits refer to sick leave from Day 15 onward, at which point financial responsibility shifts to the Swedish Social Insurance Agency. Disability benefits reflect long‐term or permanent productivity loss, including both disability pension and activity compensation. Data were obtained from the Swedish Social Insurance Agency, in 2023 [28]. Productivity losses from premature mortality were estimated using all‐cause deaths from the Cause of Death Register [29].

Future productivity losses were discounted at an annual rate of 3%, in accordance with Swedish guidelines [30]. Lost productivity was valued using the human capital approach, where losses were measured by the market value of wages and employer social contributions.

Informal care was included for CHD and CVD [23] as well as for breast and colorectal cancer, taken from two Finnish studies [31, 32]. Data on informal care for other obesity‐related diseases were not identified. Informal care costs were estimated using the replacement cost method, which values unpaid caregiving at the cost of equivalent professional care [33].

Alternative Analysis, Including Mental Illness

The main analysis did not include mental illness, due to a potential bidirectional relationship with overweight and obesity [34]. However, in the Swedish study of conscripted men, overweight and obesity were associated with significantly higher risks of disability benefits due to mental illness [20]. Accordingly, productivity losses attributable to mental illness were included in an alternative analysis, using relative risks derived from that study (Table S2).

Results

Total Economic Burden

The total economic burden of overweight and obesity in Sweden in 2023 was estimated at approximately EUR 7 billion, corresponding to EUR 664 per capita, of which EUR 2.6 billion (EUR 245 per capita) was attributable to overweight and EUR 4.4 billion (EUR 419 per capita) to obesity (Table 2 and Figure 2). Productivity losses accounted for the largest share of the economic burden (71%), followed by healthcare costs (21%). Pharmaceuticals, informal care and social services together represented the remaining 8% of the total economic burden of overweight and obesity.

Productivity Losses

The total productivity losses associated with overweight and obesity were estimated at EUR 5 billion (EUR 472 per capita), of which 62% was attributable to obesity and 38% to overweight. Disability benefits accounted for EUR 1.4 billion, followed by unemployment at EUR 1.3 billion. Sickness benefits amounted to EUR 1 billion, productivity losses due to premature mortality totalled EUR 718 million and short‐term sick leave was estimated at EUR 520 million.

Direct Healthcare Costs

Healthcare costs totalled EUR 1.5 billion (EUR 139 per capita), with the largest share coming from inpatient care, which accounted for EUR 590 million. Primary care costs were EUR 468 million and specialised outpatient care contributed EUR 405 million. Across all care settings, costs were consistently higher for obesity than for overweight. Care provided for obesity as the primary diagnosis accounted for EUR 68 million in healthcare costs, of which EUR 19 million was attributable to children and EUR 49 million to adults.

Among obesity‐related diseases, cardiovascular diseases represented the largest share of healthcare costs (28%), followed by diabetes (18%) and musculoskeletal disorders (15%). Cancer accounted for 6% of total costs, obesity‐specific treatments 5% and the remaining 29% was attributable to other obesity‐related diseases. A detailed breakdown of the economic burden by disease is provided in Table S4.

Alternative Analysis, Including Mental Illness

In an alternative analysis that included productivity losses due to mental illnesses, the total economic burden of overweight and obesity increased to EUR 8 billion, representing an additional EUR 1 billion compared with the main analysis. Total productivity losses rose from EUR 5 billion to EUR 6 billion, corresponding to 75% of the total economic burden.

Projections of the Economic Burden in 2040

Figure 3 shows the projected changes in the economic burden up to 2040. Under Scenario 1, with overweight and obesity prevalence held constant at 2023 levels, the total economic burden is projected to increase by 3.3%, reaching EUR 7.2 billion. This increase is entirely driven by demographic changes, including population growth and ageing, which result in more individuals at risk of overweight or obesity.

Projected changes in the economic burden of overweight and obesity in Sweden up to 2040.

In Scenario 2, assuming a continued increase in prevalence following trends observed over the past decade, the effect of rising prevalence alone amounts to nearly a 23% increase of the total economic burden. Combined with demographic change, the total burden is projected to rise to EUR 8.6 billion, as demographic changes and increasing prevalence together amplify the economic burden.

Scenario 3 assumes a reduction in obesity prevalence by one percentage point relative to 2023. The decrease in prevalence alone would reduce the economic burden by 2.6%, but demographic changes contribute an increase of 3.3%, resulting in a net increase to EUR 7.1 billion. This scenario illustrates how demographic shifts can counteract the gains from reduced prevalence.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the societal economic burden of overweight and obesity in Sweden in 2023, highlighting both the direct costs associated with healthcare, social services and pharmaceuticals and the indirect costs related to productivity losses and informal care. The total societal costs were estimated at approximately EUR 7 billion, with obesity alone accounting for more than EUR 4.4 billion.

Productivity losses were the main cost driver, consistent with previous research showing that the economic burden of overweight and obesity is primarily due to reduced workforce participation and productivity. Notably, including mental illnesses increased the total economic burden to EUR 8 billion, emphasising the importance of considering psychosocial factors in economic evaluations of obesity. Healthcare costs associated with overweight and obesity were estimated at approximately EUR 1.5 billion, with only a small fraction (EUR 68 million) attributable to provided care with obesity as the primary diagnosis. A recent survey to Swedish primary care physicians found that 44% saw lifestyle modification as the most effective treatment for severe obesity, and that 9 out of 10 respondents considered weight loss the patient's responsibility [36].

Although direct healthcare costs for children with obesity as the primary diagnosis were relatively modest (EUR 19 million), this is likely a conservative estimate of the economic burden of early‐life obesity, as it excludes the potential impact of comorbidities before adulthood. The long‐term consequences may be considerable, as childhood obesity increases the risk of obesity in adulthood, chronic diseases [37] and productivity losses, and can negatively affect educational outcomes, contributing to a higher risk of unemployment [38]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis found that children and adolescents with obesity are approximately five times more likely to have obesity in adulthood compared with those without obesity in childhood [39], highlighting the importance of early preventive interventions to reduce future economic and health burdens.

To enable comparison across countries, cost per capita was estimated from reported total costs and population data, with all costs converted to 2023 price levels. From a societal perspective, the estimated economic burden per capita of overweight and obesity in Sweden (€664) is slightly lower than in Finland at €730 [7]. When restricted to obesity, the economic burden per capita (€419) is substantially higher than in Italy (€260) [9]. For healthcare costs, the estimated €139 per capita for overweight and obesity is comparable to Portugal (€130) and England (€160) [6, 10]. In contrast, Denmark reports substantially higher estimated healthcare costs per capita for obesity (approximately €440), based on an extrapolation from hospital‐diagnosed cases [5], which may limit comparability.

Comparisons with earlier Swedish studies suggest that the economic burden of obesity has increased over the past two decades, from approximately EUR 2.0 billion in 2003 [40, 41] to EUR 6.6 billion in 2016 [11] (both adjusted to 2023 prices), with the estimated cost for 2023 reaching EUR 7.0 billion. This increase is likely driven largely by the rising prevalence of obesity, although some of the difference reflects methodological variations and access to more data sources. Notably, the 2003 study did not include primary care costs, productivity losses due to unemployment or short‐term sick leave, or informal care, while the 2016 study accounted for productivity losses due to mental illness, which was not included in the current analysis but excluded unemployment and short‐term sick leave. For a more direct comparison with the 2016 study, the alternative estimate of EUR 8.0 billion, which includes productivity losses due to mental illness, may be considered.

The economic burden of overweight and obesity in Sweden exceeds that of cancer according to a recent figure presented by the Swedish Cancer Society [42]. The estimated burden is similar to that of dementia [43]. In terms of national economic output, costs associated with overweight and obesity correspond to approximately 1.3% of the Swedish gross domestic product (GDP) in 2023 [44], which highlights a non‐negligible impact of overweight and obesity on the economy as a whole. This figure is broadly comparable to the 1.5% of GDP reported for Finland [7].

This study has several notable strengths. First, healthcare costs were based on comprehensive national registry data, capturing not only in‐ and outpatient hospital care but also primary care [22]. Second, the relative risks for obesity‐related diseases were obtained from large, recently published Scandinavian studies that cover a wide range of obesity‐associated conditions [12, 13]. Third, the analysis included children and adolescents in addition to adults, providing a more complete estimation of the economic burden across all age groups. Finally, the study not only estimates the total economic burden of overweight and obesity but also shows how these costs are distributed across different sectors, providing valuable insights for policy and resource allocation.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The prevalence of overweight and obesity in the adult population is based on self‐reported BMI [3]. Evidence from Sweden suggests that self‐reported BMI underestimates the true obesity prevalence, with estimates of 14% based on self‐reported BMI compared with 19% based on measured BMI among men and 20% compared with 25% among women [45]. This likely led to an underestimation of the economic burden, as prevalence affects all costs in the analysis. At the same time, the relative mortality risks were derived from a large meta‐analysis of 230 studies that included studies using self‐reported height and weight [21], which may have led to upward bias [46]. However, mortality‐related costs only represented 10% of the total economic burden, suggesting that any upward bias likely had a limited effect.

Costs associated with social services, pharmaceuticals and informal care are underestimated, as data were only available for a subset of obesity‐related diseases: social services for two diseases (CHD and CVD), pharmaceuticals for four diseases (obesity, diabetes, CHD and CVD) and informal care for four diseases (CHD, CVD, breast cancer and colorectal cancer).

The PAF method used to estimate the economic burden is based on relative risks. However, the data used to estimate healthcare, social services, pharmaceutical and informal care costs were reported as hazard ratios. These reflect instantaneous risk over time and are not directly interchangeable with relative risks, which measure cumulative risk over a defined period [47], and their use may therefore introduce bias in the estimated PAF. In addition, an aggregate hazard ratio was reported for diabetes. While obesity is strongly associated with Type 2 diabetes [48], other forms, particularly Type 1, are not causally linked to obesity. Applying this pooled estimate to all diabetes‐related costs underestimates the contribution of obesity to Type 2 diabetes, while overestimating the contribution to Type 1 diabetes.

Despite these limitations, the hazard ratios were obtained from recent cohort studies conducted in Sweden and Finland [12, 13], selected due to their relevance to the Swedish setting. Both studies used individual‐level data with adjustment for age, gender and education, supporting the validity of the estimates. As healthcare, social services, pharmaceutical and informal care costs accounted for less than one‐third of the total economic burden, any resulting bias is likely to have had a limited impact on the overall results.

The relationship between obesity and mental illnesses is likely bidirectional [34]. For this reason, the approximately EUR 1 billion due to productivity losses attributable to mental illnesses was included only in an alternative analysis, despite evidence of a significantly increased risk of disability pensions due to mental illnesses among Swedish men with overweight or obesity [20]. The main analysis therefore focuses on other obesity‐related diseases, providing conservative estimates of productivity losses while acknowledging that early overweight and obesity may also contribute to substantial later‐life productivity losses due to mental illnesses.

The projections of the economic burden in 2040 were based on the estimated burden in 2023 and were influenced only by demographic changes and projected future prevalence of overweight and obesity. However, prices, healthcare utilisation, productivity levels and treatment technologies are likely to change over time. Nevertheless, these projections are useful for illustrating how the future economic burden may change under different scenarios for overweight and obesity prevalence.

The findings emphasise the urgent need for effective interventions to prevent and manage obesity. Societal strategies aimed at promoting healthy lifestyles across the population, such as government policies to shift diets from unhealthy to healthy foods and early targeted prevention for individuals at increased risk of developing obesity, could help curb the rising prevalence of obesity. These also need to be accompanied by broad cost‐effective societal initiatives, including schools, workplaces and other arenas of daily life. Healthcare interventions, including greater access to obesity surgery and evidence‐based pharmacological treatments, may also reduce obesity‐related health impacts. Policymakers in health care and across society should weigh short‐term costs against long‐term benefits, as preventive and therapeutic measures often require upfront investments but can lead to considerable savings over time.
