Three-loop banana integrals with three equal masses Preprint: BONN-TH-2025-32
Claude Duhr, Sara Maggio

Abstract
We obtain and solve the canonical differential equations for the three-loop banana integrals in dimensional regularisation when three of the four masses are equal. The K3 surface associated with the maximal cuts factorises into a product of two elliptic curves. This allows us to express the differential forms in the canonical differential equations in terms of meromorphic modular forms. We present a rigorous proof that these differential forms only have simple poles and that they define independent cohomology classes. We also present explicit results for all master integrals in terms of iterated integrals of meromorphic modular forms (and integrals thereof). This is the first time that it was possible to express the results of a Feynman integral associated with a K3 geometry and depending on two dimensionless ratios in terms of functions that have previously been studied in the literature.

3
Analytic results for three-loop banana integrals with three equal masses

In the previous section we have reviewed modular parametrisations of the corresponding periods and mirror map. In this section we show how we can combine this result with the canonical differential equations to obtain full analytic expressions for all master integrals in terms of iterated integrals of modular forms.

4
Conclusion

In this paper we have presented for the first time fully analytic results for all the master integrals for the three-loop banana integrals with three equal masses in
D=2-2\varepsilon
dimensions. While analytic results for the three-loop banana integrals with four different masses have recently appeared
60
;
104
, the main novelty of our result is that the master integrals can be expressed in terms of iterated integrals of meromorphic modular forms (and integrals thereof). While meromorphic modular forms have already appeared in the context of the equal-mass banana integral
30
;
35
;
106
, this is the first time that it was possible to express a Feynman integral depending on two dimensionless ratios in terms of this class of functions.

The appearance of meromorphic modular forms has some practical advantages, because this class of functions is relatively well studied in the literature. In particular, we have used our computation to study the properties of our results. We were able to rigorously prove that our canonical differential equations satisfy the properties conjectured in ref.
58
, and we could show that the differential forms in the canonical differential equation have only simple poles and define independent cohomology classes. We stress that all our results rely very profoundly on various recent advances in our understanding of the mathematics underlying Feynman integrals associated with K3 surfaces, in particular their period geometry
64
;
61
, the corresponding canonical bases
77
;
108
;
107
;
106
;
104
;
58
;
92
;
28
;
27
and their properties
105
;
62
;
59
, and the mathematics underlying iterated integrals of meromorphic modular forms
94
;
39
;
97
;
35
;
37
.

For the future, it would be interesting to see if the approach developed here to solve Feynman integrals associated with K3 surfaces that are products of two elliptic curves can be applied also to other cases. For example, in ref.
55
it was observed that a certain family of integrals appearing in the context of gravitational wave scattering involves a K3 surface whose periods can be written as products of complete elliptic integrals. We expect that it is possible to express those results in terms of iterated integrals of modular forms as well.

Appendix A
Review of modular forms

A.1
Modular forms in one variable

Let
\Gamma
be a subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
(of finite index). It acts on the complex upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}=\big\{\tau\in\mathbb{C}:\textrm{Im}\,\tau>0\big\}
via Möbius transformations,

\gamma\cdot\tau=\tfrac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\,,\qquad\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma\,.

(69)

A meromorphic modular form of weight
k
is a meromorphic function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

f(\gamma\cdot\tau)=(c\tau+d)^{k}\,f(\tau)\,,\qquad\textrm{for all }\gamma\in\Gamma\,.

(70)

A modular function for
\Gamma
is a meromorphic modular form of weight 0.
We denote by
\cal M_{k}(\Gamma)
the vector space generated by all meromorphic modular forms for
\Gamma
, and
M_{k}(\Gamma)
is the subspace by all (holomorphic) modular forms.
Note that
M_{k}(\Gamma)
is non-trivial only for
k>0
.

We will only be interested in cases where
\Gamma
has genus zero, by which we mean that the Riemann surface
Y_{\Gamma}=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
has genus zero. The field of meromorphic functions of
Y_{\Gamma}
can be identified with the field of modular functions
\cal M_{0}(\Gamma)
. Since
Y_{\Gamma}
has genus zero, its field of meromorphic functions has a single generator, called a
Hauptmodul
for
\Gamma
. Said differently, every modular function can be expressed as a rational function in the Hauptmodul.

Throughout this paper, we encountered modular forms for the congruence subgroups
\Gamma_{1}(6)
and
\Gamma_{1}(12)
.

(80)

.
We can then consider the field
\cal G:=\cal F(\mathbb{I})
whose elements are rational functions in
\mathbb{I}
with coefficients in
\cal F
. Note that since
\partial_{x}\mathbb{I}=\xi_{N}\in\cal F
,
\cal G
is also differentially closed, and its field of constants is still
\mathbb{C}
.

Consider the gauge transformation

\boldsymbol{J}(x)=e^{-A_{N}\mathbb{I}(x)}\boldsymbol{I}(x)\,.

(88)

In this gauge, the differential equation takes the form

\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{J}(c)=e^{-A_{N}\mathbb{I}(x)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(x)e^{A_{N}\mathbb{I}(x)}\boldsymbol{J}(x)\,,\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(x)=\sum_{p=1}^{N-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\,\omega_{p}\,,

(89)

and the differential equation matrix is given by

e^{-A_{N}\mathbb{I}(x)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}e^{A_{N}\mathbb{I}(x)}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{p=1}^{N-1}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\operatorname{ad}_{A_{N}}^{r}(A_{p})\,\omega_{p}^{(r)}\,,

(90)

with
\operatorname{ad}_{A_{N}}(A_{p})=[A_{N},A_{p}]
and

\omega_{p}^{(r)}:=\mathbb{I}(x)^{r}\omega_{p}\,.

(91)

Our goal is to show that the differential forms
\omega_{p}^{(r)}
,
r\geq 0
and
1\leq p<N
, which are the letters that appear in the iterated integrals in the path-ordered exponential that solves eq. (
88
), define independent cohomology classes with respect to
\cal G
, i.e., whenever there are
c_{rp}\in\mathbb{C}
and
g\in\cal G
such that

\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{p=1}^{N-1}c_{rp}\,\omega_{p}^{(r)}=\mathrm{d}g\,,

(92)

then necessarily
c_{rp}=0
and
g
is a constant.
To see that this is true, we integrate eq. (
92
) over the path
\gamma
used to define
\mathbb{I}
. We obtain

\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{p=1}^{N-1}c_{rp}\,I_{\gamma}\big(\omega_{p}^{(r)}\big)=g(x)-g(x_{0})=:\tilde{g}(x)\,,

(93)

and
I_{\gamma}(\omega)
denotes the iterated integral of length one, cf. eq. (
12
). Using the fact that iterated integrals form a shuffle algebra, we find

\begin{split}I_{\gamma}\big(\omega_{p}^{(r)}\big)&\,=\int_{\gamma}\omega_{p}^{(r)}=\int_{\gamma}\omega_{p}\,\mathbb{I}(x)^{r}=\int_{\gamma}\omega_{p}\left(\int_{\gamma}\omega_{N}\right)^{r}=r!\int_{\gamma}\omega_{p}\underbrace{\omega_{N}\cdots\omega_{N}}_{r\textrm{ times}}\\
&\,=r!\,I_{\gamma}(\omega_{p},\underbrace{\omega_{N},\ldots,\omega_{N}}_{r\textrm{ times}})=r!\,I_{\gamma}(\omega_{p},\omega_{N}^{r})\,,\end{split}

(94)

where in the last equality we introduced the simplified notation

I_{\gamma}(\omega_{p},\omega_{N}^{r}):=I_{\gamma}(\omega_{p},\underbrace{\omega_{N},\ldots,\omega_{N}}_{r\textrm{ times}})\,.

(95)

Since
g\in\cal G
, also
\tilde{g}\in\cal G
, and so we can write

\tilde{g}=\frac{P(\mathbb{I})}{Q(\mathbb{I})}\,,

(96)

where
P
and
Q
are polynomials,

\begin{split}P(\mathbb{I})&\,=\sum_{k=0}^{d_{P}}\frac{p_{k}}{k!}\,\mathbb{I}^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{d_{P}}p_{k}\,I_{\gamma}(\omega_{N}^{k})\,,\\
Q(\mathbb{I})&\,=\sum_{k=0}^{d_{Q}}\frac{q_{k}}{k!}\,\mathbb{I}^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{d_{Q}}q_{k}\,I_{\gamma}(\omega_{N}^{k})\,,\end{split}

(97)

with
p_{k},q_{k}\in\cal F
. Equation (
93
) can then be cast in the form

\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{p=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=0}^{d_{Q}}\,r!\,c_{rp}\,q_{k}\,I_{\gamma}(\omega_{N}^{k})I_{\gamma}(\omega_{p},\omega_{N}^{r})=\sum_{k=0}^{d_{P}}p_{k}\,I_{\gamma}(\omega_{N}^{k})\,.

(98)

Using the shuffle algebra structure of iterated integrals, we can write
I_{\gamma}(\omega_{N}^{k})I_{\gamma}(\omega_{p},\omega_{N}^{r})
again as a linear combination (with integer coefficients) of iterated integrals of length
k+r+1>0
. We then see that both sides of eq. (
98
) are linear combinations with coefficients in
\cal F
of iterated integrals made from the letters
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N}
.Every iterated integral on the left-hand side has length at least 1 and involves exactly one letter
\omega_{p}
with
p\neq N
, while those on the right-hand side only involve the letter
\omega_{N}
, except for the integral with
k=0
, which has length 0. Since we know that these iterated integrals are linearly independent over
\cal F
, both sides must vanish independently. In particular this means
p_{0}=\ldots=p_{d_{P}}=0
, and so
P=0
. This implies
\tilde{g}=0
, or equivalently
g
must be a constant. Equation (
93
) then takes the form

\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{p=1}^{N-1}c_{rp}\,I_{\gamma}\big(\omega_{p}^{(r)}\big)=\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{p=1}^{N-1}r!\,c_{rp}\,I_{\gamma}(\omega_{p},\omega_{N}^{r})=0\,.

(99)

The linear independence of the
I_{\gamma}(\omega_{p},\omega_{N}^{r})
then implies
c_{rp}=0
, which finishes the proof.

C.2
Independence of the differential forms

We now turn to the proof that the differential forms
\omega_{ij}
that appear in the canonical differential equation in section
3
define independent cohomology classes.

We start by assuming that
\tau_{2}
has been fixed to some constant generic value, and we only consider the one-forms
\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\,\omega_{ij}^{1}
. We know from appendix
B
that the
\omega_{ij}^{1}

\varepsilon

\mathbb{I}_{k}(\cdot,\tau_{2})

. From the explicit expression in appendix
B
, we see that all relevant meromorphic modular forms for

have positive weight (seen as a modular form in
\tau_{1}
), and we can group them into three classes:

•

Class I:
Functions of class I have at most simple poles:

1\,,\phi_{3,-1}\,,\phi_{4,-2}\,,\phi_{2,0}^{a}\,,\phi_{3,1}^{a}\,,\phi_{0,2}^{b}\,,\phi_{2,0}^{b}\,,\phi_{0,2}^{c}\,,\phi_{2,0}^{c}\,.

•

Class II:
Functions of class II have at least double poles double or at most triple poles:

\phi_{2,2}\,,\phi_{4,0}\,,\phi_{4,2}\,,\phi_{3,1}^{b}\,.

(101)

•

Class III:
Functions of class III do not enter the
\omega_{ij}^{1}
directly, but only through the
\varepsilon
-functions in eqs. (
38
) and (
38
):
8
8

8

We of course take the derivative in
\tau_{2}
before fixing
\tau_{2}
to a constant value.

\partial_{\tau_{2}}\phi_{3,-1}(\cdot,\tau_{2})\,,\qquad\partial_{\tau_{2}}\phi_{4,-2}(\cdot,\tau_{2})\,,\qquad\partial_{\tau_{2}}^{2}\phi_{4,-2}(\cdot,\tau_{2})\,.

(102)

We know from the discussion in section
3
that these functions have double or triple poles, respectively.

By inspection, we see that the pole orders of these meromorphic modular forms satisfy the criterion to lie in the vector space
\widetilde{\cal M}(\Gamma^{1}(12))
defined in appendix
A
. We denote by
\widetilde{\cal N}_{1}\subseteq\widetilde{\cal M}(\Gamma^{1}(12))
the linear span of these meromorphic modular forms. From eq. (

) we see that

\widetilde{\cal N}_{1}\cap\partial_{\tau_{1}}\mathcal{QM}(\Gamma^{1}(12))=\{0\}\,.

(103)

Said differently, if we pick a basis
\{f_{p}\}
for
\widetilde{\cal N}_{1}
, then the differential forms
\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\,f_{p}(\tau_{1})
define independent cohomology classes (seen as functions of

only).

So far, we have only considered meromorphic modular forms. However, the functions of class III do not appear directly in the definition of the
\omega_{ij}^{1}
, but they only enter through the
\varepsilon
-functions
\mathbb{I}_{k}(\cdot,\tau_{2})
. Applying the result from appendix
C.1
to the functions of class III one at the time, we can eliminate them one after and replace them with the
\varepsilon
-functions. We consider the vector space
\widetilde{\cal N}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
generated by products of
\varepsilon
-functions with a function of class I and II.

9

9

Note that along the way we also introduce products of functions of class III and
\varepsilon
-functions. Those, however, do not appear in the differential forms from section

.
Equation (

) together the analysis from appendix

then implies that

\widetilde{\cal N}_{1}^{\varepsilon}\cap\partial_{\tau_{1}}\mathcal{QM}(\Gamma^{1}(12))(\mathbb{I}_{1},\mathbb{I}_{2},\mathbb{I}_{3})=\{0\}\,,

(104)

where the elements of
\mathcal{QM}(\Gamma^{1}(12))(\mathbb{I}_{1},\mathbb{I}_{2},\mathbb{I}_{3})
are rational functions in

Let
\widetilde{\cal N}_{1}^{\omega}
denote the linear span of the functions

\widetilde{\cal N}_{1}^{\omega}\cap\partial_{\tau_{1}}\mathcal{QM}(\Gamma^{1}(12))(\mathbb{I}_{1},\mathbb{I}_{2},\mathbb{I}_{3})=\{0\}\,.

We can of course repeat exactly the same construction starting from the

We are now in the position to prove that the differential forms
\omega_{ij}
define independent cohomology classes. Assume that there are constants

\sum_{ij}c_{ij}\,\omega_{ij}=\mathrm{d}F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\,,

(108)

10

10

Such a set is easy to obtain, by simply resolving trivial linear relations among the

Equation (

) is equivalent to the system

\sum_{ij}c_{ij}\,\omega_{ij}^{k}=\partial_{\tau_{k}}F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\,,\qquad k=1,2\,.

We focus on

\mathrm{d}F=0

\sum_{ij}c_{ij}\,\omega_{ij}=0\,,

(110)

and so

from the linear independence of the
