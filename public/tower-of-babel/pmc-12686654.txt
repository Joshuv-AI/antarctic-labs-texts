Global pattern, trend, and cross-country inequality of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension from 1990 to 2021, with projection from 2022 to 2060
Authors: Xing Liu, Xiaoyang Wang, Yuxin Liang, Hongli Li, Jiazheng Chen, Zhijun Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension pose a serious threat to global health. This study analyzed trends and future projections of hypertension-related cardiovascular diseases burden using Global Burden of Disease 2021 data.MethodsWe estimated mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) via DisMod-MR 2.1, analyzed regional disparities by socio-demographic index (SDI), and projected trends to 2060 using the Bayesian age-period-cohort model. Health inequalities were evaluated via the slope index of inequality and concentration index.ResultsGlobally, the number of DALYs and deaths due to cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension increased in 2021, reaching 214,518,341 (95% uncertainty interval [UI]: 180,418,055–247,570,720) and 10,376,441 (95% UI: 8784,052–12,032,762) respectively, while the age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR) and age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) decreased. In high SDI regions, the number of DALYs and deaths decreased, while in the remaining regions, they increased. However, the ASDR and ASMR in all SDI regions decreased. In 21 major regions, the number of DALYs and deaths increased. East Asia had the highest values, and Oceania had the lowest. Only in Southern Sub-Saharan Africa did the ASDR and ASMR increase. Regarding age, DALYs rates increased with age from 15–74 years, and mortality increased with age from 15–84 years, and decreased after 75 and 85 years, respectively. It is predicted that DALYs rates and mortality continue to decline and stabilize by 2060.ConclusionThe burden of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension varies markedly across the globe, with declining rates in high SDI regions but rising trends in low and low-middle SDI regions. These findings indicate growing inequalities and the need for region-specific interventions.

Introduction

Hypertension represents a significant modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), playing a critical role in the development of ischemic heart disease, stroke, and various other vascular disorders [1,2]. Despite progress in both prevention and treatment modalities, the global incidence of hypertension-related CVDs continues to rise, driven by factors such as population aging, lifestyle modifications, and the increasing prevalence of metabolic risk factors, including obesity and diabetes [3].

Beyond its direct health effects, hypertension imposes a significant economic and social burden [4]. High-income regions have experienced significant reductions in cardiovascular disease mortality, attributable to early detection, enhanced clinical management, and population-wide preventive measures [5,6]. Conversely, low- and middle-income regions are witnessing an escalating disease burden, influenced by rapid urbanization, constrained healthcare infrastructure, and inadequate hypertension control [7,8]. This trend not only affects the quality of life of individuals but also has long-term implications for the workforce and economic productivity [9].

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 provides an important data source and analytical framework for comprehensively understanding the prevalence of diseases, disease burden, and their changing trends [10]. By analyzing GBD data, we can deeply explore the distribution characteristics and changing patterns of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension among different regions and populations globally, providing a scientific basis for formulating targeted prevention and control strategies.

Despite the well-recognized contribution of hypertension to global cardiovascular disease burden, prior studies remain fragmented in scope and often limited to specific outcomes or regions. Variations in definitions and analytic approaches have hindered standardized global assessment, constraining cross-national comparisons and evidence-based policymaking. Moreover, few analyses have systematically evaluated global patterns, temporal trends, health inequalities, and long-term projections of hypertension-attributable cardiovascular diseases burden, evidence that is essential for formulating equitable prevention strategies [11].

In addition, the distribution of hypertension-related cardiovascular diseases is influenced by wide variations in socio-demographic conditions, healthcare access, and epidemiologic transitions. Therefore, assessing these disparities using a composite measure such as the socio-demographic index (SDI) and forecasting future trends using advanced modeling techniques such as the Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model provides a unique opportunity to generate nuanced, policy-relevant evidence. However, few studies have employed these methods simultaneously in a global context to explore long-term patterns and inequality in the cardiovascular disease burden attributable to hypertension, representing a critical methodological gap in the current literature.

This study aimed to utilize GBD 2021 data to comprehensively assess the trends and projections of the burden of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension, providing a basis for formulating precise prevention and control strategies at the global and regional levels. By combining comprehensive data, advanced statistical modeling, and inequality assessment, this study provides an up-to-date, globally comparable overview of the evolving burden of hypertension-related cardiovascular diseases. The findings are expected to inform resource prioritization, support the design of targeted interventions, and facilitate progress toward global cardiovascular health equity.

Methods

Data source

The GBD 2021 was released by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) in the United States (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2021) [10]. This database covers 204 countries and regions and has conducted systematic and scientific assessments of the disease burden caused by 371 diseases and injuries, as well as 88 risk factors, from 1990 to 2021 [12]. The GBD framework systematically identifies risk factors for each disease through literature reviews and expert consultations, ensuring that the identified risk factors are scientifically valid and clinically relevant. All data used in this study were sourced from publicly available databases, and ethical approval was not required for their use.

Data collection

DisMod-MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta-regression framework, was used to model GBD data and simulate the outcomes of cardiovascular diseases [10]. DisMod-MR 2.1 integrates data from multiple sources, including national health surveys, epidemiological studies, and clinical datasets, providing reliable estimates of the prevalence and association of hypertension with cardiovascular diseases. This framework generates preliminary mortality estimates and adjusts for key covariates, including age, gender, and SDI. Additionally, the model incorporates behavioral risk factors, such as smoking, diet, and alcohol consumption, as these are critical determinants of cardiovascular disease. The model also accounts for the prevalence of hypertension, a direct driver of the disease burden.

The DALYs is an indicator used to measure the burden of diseases. It combines years of life lost due to premature death with years of life lost due to disability caused by diseases. This indicator enables a comprehensive assessment of the impacts of diseases and medical interventions, which is particularly useful for disease prevention and control.

SDI

The SDI is a composite measure of social and economic conditions influencing health outcomes in each region. It reflects overall socioeconomic development, incorporating factors such as educational attainment, per capita income, and fertility rate [13]. The SDI ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a higher level of development. Based on the SDI, the 204 countries and regions are classified into five categories: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high [14].

Health inequality analysis

The slope index of inequality (SII) and the concentration index are standardized indicators for measuring absolute and relative gradient inequalities, respectively. These indices are used to assess the distributional inequality of the burden of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension among countries. The SII is calculated by performing regression analysis on mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) rates for the national population, based on their relative positions according to SDI. The relative position is defined as the midpoint of the cumulative population range ranked by SDI. A weighted regression model is applied to account for heteroscedasticity. The concentration index is derived by numerically integrating the area under the Lorenz concentration curve, based on the cumulative distribution of mortality and DALYs rates and the population ranked by SDI.

Predictive analysis

The BAPC model, combined with the integrated nested Laplace approximation, was used to predict the future trends of the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) and the age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR) of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension [15].

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the trends of the age-standardized rates (ASR) of mortality and DALYs for cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension, the ASR per 100,000 population was calculated according to the following formula [16]:ASR=∑i=1Aaiwi∑i=1Awi×100000where ai: the age-specific rate of the ith age group; W: the number of people in the corresponding ith age group in the standard population; A: the number of age groups.

The regression line is fitted to the natural logarithm of the ratio, y = α+βx+Ɛ, where y = ln (ASR) and x = calendar year. The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) is calculated as EAPC = 100 × [exp(β)-1], and its 95 % confidence interval (CI) can be obtained from the linear regression model [17].

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). Final figure modifications were completed using Adobe Illustrator (version 2023).

Results

Global, regional, and national burden of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension

Globally, compared with 1990, both the DALYs cases and deaths cases due to cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension increased in 2021, reaching 214,518,341 (95 % uncertainty interval [UI]: 180,418,055 to 247,570,720) and 10,376,441 (95 % UI: 8784,052 to 12,032,762), respectively. However, the ASDR and the ASMR both declined, with values of 2513.43 (95 % UI: 2115.3 to 2902.8) and 125.33 (95 % UI: 106.26 to 145.46), respectively. The corresponding EAPCs were −0.69 (95 % CI: −4.15 to 2.88) and −0.76 (95 % CI: −3.11 to 1.65), respectively. In five different SDI regions, compared with 1990, the number of DALYs and deaths in high SDI regions in 2021 were 23,650,184 (95 % UI: 19,424,473 to 27,375,421) and 1366,494 (95 % UI: 1094,812 to 1609,656), respectively, showing a downward trend. In contrast, both DALYs cases and death cases in other SDI regions were on the rise. Nevertheless, the trends of ASDR and ASMR in all five SDI regions were similar, showing a decline. The EAPC values in high SDI, high-middle SDI, and middle SDI regions were negative, while those in low-middle SDI and low SDI regions were positive. Among the 21 GBD regions, compared with 1990, the number of DALYs and deaths due to cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension increased in most regions by 2021. Among them, East Asia had the highest number of DALYs and deaths in 2021, with 56,130,200 (95 % UI: 43,448,394 to 70,727,236) and 2925,917 (95 % UI: 2284,210 to 3673,853), respectively. Oceania had the lowest number of DALYs and deaths in 2021, with 290,099 (95 % UI: 214,843 to 368,233) and 10,246 (95 % UI: 7630 to 13,037), respectively. In most regions, the ASDR and ASMR showed a declining trend, except for Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, where both ASDR and ASMR increased to 3391.28 (95 % UI: 2924.35 to 3824.99) and 169.92 (95 % UI: 145.44 to 191.84) in 2021, respectively. The corresponding EAPC values were 0.2 (95 % CI: −3.34 to 3.88) and 0.31 (95 % CI: −2.09 to 2.77), respectively (Table S1). For the perspective of regional differences in disease burden among the 21 GBD regions, within cardiovascular diseases, the impact of hypertension risk on hypertensive heart disease, stroke, and ischemic heart disease was greater. Among them, the ASDR of lower extremity peripheral arterial disease in Andean Latin America was the lowest both in 1990 and 2021, with the corresponding ASDR being 0.414 and 0.674 respectively. The ASMR of lower extremity peripheral arterial disease in Andean Latin America, Oceania, Southeast Asia, and East Asia was the lowest both in 1990 and 2021. Specifically, Andean Latin America had the lowest ASMR (0.006) in 1990, while Oceania had the lowest ASMR (0.014) in 2021. The ASDR and ASMR of ischemic heart disease in Eastern Europe and Central Asia were the highest both in 1990 and 2021. The burden of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension by age and sex group

In 2021, for cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension among the age group of 15 - 74 years, the DALYs rates increased with the increase in age. Specifically, within the age group of 70–74 years, the number of DALYs for ischemic heart disease and stroke reached 12,407,814.73 and 13,457,565.13 respectively, and the DALYs rates were 191.57 and 207.78 respectively. After the age of 75, as age increased, the DALYs rates decreased, and the changes in hypertensive heart disease, stroke, and ischemic heart disease were more pronounced. In 2021, for cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension within the age group of 15 - 84 years, the mortality increased with age. In the age group of 80 - 84 years, the number of deaths for ischemic heart disease and Stroke were 749,137.88 and 666,771.91 respectively, and the mortality were 13.314 and 11.850 respectively. After the age of 85, as age increased, the mortality decreased, and the changes in hypertensive heart disease, stroke, and ischemic heart disease were more pronounced (Fig. 4).Fig. 4The burden of cardiovascular diseases DALYs (a) and deaths (b) caused by hypertension by age groups in 2021.Fig 4

Sex-specific disparities were identified in hypertension-attributable cardiovascular deaths and DALYs, with clear age-dependent patterns. At younger ages (20–44 years), females showed lower death cases than males, whereas in older age groups, female deaths exceeded those of males. Peak deaths from ischemic heart disease and stroke occurred in females aged 80–84 years (400,011.48 and 362,247.50, respectively), while males reached their highest ischemic heart disease deaths at the same age (349,126.40). DALYs were consistently higher in males across most age groups. The highest burden from ischemic heart disease was observed in males aged 65–69 years (7853,940.16), while females had peak stroke-related DALYs at 70–74 years (6116,944.63) (Supplementary Figure S1).

Temporal trends of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension

For cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension, both the number of DALYs and deaths increased year by year as the years, while the ASDR and ASMR decreased over time. Among them, the changes in hypertensive heart disease, stroke, and ischemic heart disease were more noticeable (Fig. 5). In 2021, the number of deaths for stroke, hypertensive heart disease, and ischemic heart disease reached 4212,696.92, 1331,780.048, and 4692,863.213 respectively. Health inequality analysis

The analysis of health inequalities reveals that as the SDI level increases, both the DALYs rates and the mortality decline, indicating that in regions with a higher SDI, the DALYs rates and mortality of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension are lower. Compared with 1990, the SII of the DALYs rates and the mortality decreased significantly in 2021, suggesting that global health inequalities in DALYs rates and mortality have gradually diminished. When comparing 1990 with 2021, the concentration index of the DALYs rates increased from −0.1 to 0, and the concentration index of the mortality increased from −0.17 to −0.06. The changes in the concentration index tend to approach 0, indicating that the health inequalities of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension have gradually weakened on a global scale (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Health inequality analysis for cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension in 1990 and 2021. Projections of the burden of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension

It can be observed from 1990 to the projected year of 2060 that, as the years progress, both ASDR and ASMR have been decreasing year by year and are expected to gradually stabilize (Fig. 7). Among the cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension, the changes in stroke, ischemic heart disease, and hypertensive heart disease are more noticeable. The corresponding ASDR are projected to stabilize at 0.01587, 0.01796, and 0.00264 per 100,000, respectively, by 2060. Future projections indicate a declining trend in ASMR and ASDR for cardiovascular diseases attributable to hypertension across all age groups. For instance, in individuals aged 95–99 years, ASDR is projected to decrease from 98,408.08 in 2021 to 74,082.67 in 2060, while ASMR is expected to decline from 11,610.90 to 8344.65 per 100,000. Despite the overall decline, the age-related pattern remains consistent in 2060, with ASMR and ASDR increasing progressively with age (Supplementary Figure S2). Sex-specific projections also reveal higher ASMR and ASDR in males compared to females throughout the study period. From 2022 to 2060, both indicators show a continuous downward trend. By 2060, male ASMR and ASDR are estimated at 183.56 and 4213.37 per 100,000, respectively, while female rates are projected at 115.25 and 2540.60 per 100,000 (Supplementary Figure S3).

Discussion

In 2021, the number of DALYs and deaths due to cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension increased significantly compared with 1990. However, the ASDR and ASMR showed a downward trend. This phenomenon is closely related to the changes in the global population structure. With the intensification of population aging, the number of patients has increased. Meanwhile, the improvement of medical care and the strengthening of preventive measures have reduced the relative burden of the disease [18].

Different SDI regions presented different trends. The number of DALYs and deaths decreased in high SDI regions, while it increased in other regions. Nevertheless, the ASDR and ASMR decreased in all SDI regions, reflecting the important role of the level of socioeconomic development in the changes in disease burden [19] . High SDI regions usually have more complete medical systems, higher health awareness, and healthier lifestyles, which contribute to reducing the disease risk, consistent with previous research results. In contrast, regions with low SDI may still have a heavy disease burden due to limited medical resources and insufficient health awareness [20].

At the regional and national levels, the DALYs cases and deaths cases increased in most of the 21 GBD regions. East Asia had the highest disease burden, while Oceania had the lowest. This disparity can be attributed to multiple factors, including genetic susceptibility, lifestyle, environmental factors, and the accessibility of healthcare resources [21]. For example, East Asia has a dense population. In some areas, there are unhealthy lifestyle habits such as high-salt diets and a high smoking rate, and the aging process is rapid, resulting in a persistently high disease burden [20]. In contrast, Oceania has a relatively small population and better healthcare conditions, leading to a relatively low disease burden [22,23].

Meanwhile, the burden of different cardiovascular diseases varies across regions. For instance, the ASDR and ASMR of ischemic heart disease are high in Eastern Europe and Central Asia, where lifestyle factors such as high consumption of saturated fats, tobacco use, and excessive alcohol intake remain pervasive [24]. Emerging evidence supports the role of region-specific modifiable risk factors as key drivers of hypertension-related cardiovascular burden. In East Asia, excessive sodium intake, averaging more than 10 g/day in countries such as China, remains a leading contributor to elevated blood pressure, far exceeding the WHO recommended level of <5 g/day [25]. In Eastern Europe and Central Asia, smoking prevalence among men remains above 40 % in several countries, and harmful alcohol use is a major public health concern [26]. Meanwhile, in South Asia, a combination of unhealthy diets and ambient air pollution, the latter ranking among the top five risk factors for CVD DALYs in India and Bangladesh, may play a central role in increasing vascular risk [27]. While our study focused on describing global trends in overall burden, these observations highlight the importance of future region-specific quantitative analyses. Methods such as comparative risk assessment or causal mediation modeling could help disentangle the relative contributions of behavioral, environmental, and structural factors across regions. Such evidence is essential for designing context-sensitive prevention strategies, particularly in settings where rapid epidemiologic transitions are occurring.

In terms of age distribution, the DALYs rates increased with age from 15–74 years, and the mortality increased with age from 15–84 years, then decreased after the ages of 75 and 85, respectively. This indicates that the burden of cardiovascular diseases caused by hypertension is relatively heavy in middle-aged and pre-elderly populations. Therefore, disease prevention and early intervention for these populations should be strengthened [28]. For example, carrying out health education to enhance the awareness of hypertension and its hazards among this age group, promoting the adoption of healthy lifestyles, and strengthening the screening and management of hypertension can help reduce the disease burden [29].

The analysis of health inequalities indicated that the SDI was negatively correlated with the DALYs rates and mortality, and the relevant inequality indices improved in 2021. This implies that regions with a higher level of socioeconomic development have a relatively lower disease burden, and global health inequalities are gradually diminishing. However, regions with a low SDI still require attention. These regions have heavy disease burden and are the key areas for future prevention and control efforts. It is necessary to increase resource investment in low SDI regions, improve their healthcare conditions, and enhance health awareness to narrow the global health gap [30].

The prediction results showed that both the ASDR and ASMR are expected to continue to decline and stabilize by 2060. The situation presents a certain degree of promise for future disease prevention and control undertakings. However, we still cannot afford to be complacent. The changing trends of different cardiovascular diseases were different, suggesting that targeted planning should be carried out according to the characteristics of different diseases when formulating prevention and control strategies [31]. For example, for major diseases such as stroke, ischemic heart disease, and hypertensive heart disease, differential prevention and treatment plans are needed, including the control of risk factors, the promotion of early diagnosis, and treatment technologies [32,33].

This study also has certain limitations. Firstly, while the GBD 2021 dataset provides a broad global perspective, its reliance on modeling techniques such as DisMod-MR 2.1 to fill data gaps, especially in regions with sparse health data (e.g., Sub-Saharan Africa), introduces uncertainties. These models depend on assumptions about disease prevalence, covariate relationships, and imputation methods, which may not fully capture local epidemiological nuances. In areas without robust vital registration systems, hypertension-related cardiovascular disease mortality estimates could be sensitive to model priors [34]. Although the GBD framework quantifies uncertainty through 95 % UI, findings in low-SDI regions should be interpreted cautiously due to potential model biases. Future research should prioritize validation efforts in underrepresented regions and incorporate localized data to improve model calibration. Secondly, the study did not explore the impact of gene-environment interactions or emerging risk factors (e.g., new environmental exposures or dietary patterns) on cardiovascular disease burden. These factors warrant further investigation to better understand disease etiology. Additionally, spatial and environmental determinants, such as population density, may influence cardiovascular risk through pollution exposure, healthcare access, and social disparities. These should be considered in future studies [35]. Additionally, as an observational ecological study using secondary data from GBD 2021, this analysis can only identify associations and temporal trends in the burden of hypertension-related cardiovascular diseases. Causal inferences cannot be made, and potential confounders not fully accounted for in the modeling process may influence observed patterns. Future studies incorporating individual-level data or Mendelian randomization anlysis are needed to explore the causal pathways between hypertension and cardiovascular outcomes in different contexts. Finally, future research should consider incorporating spatial and environmental determinants such as population density, which may influence cardiovascular risk through pathways including pollution exposure, healthcare accessibility, and social infrastructure disparities.

Conclusion

Using GBD 2021 data, our study revealed that while global ASMR of hypertension-related cardiovascular diseases declined, the total burden rose, especially in low SDI regions. Mortality and DALYs rates trends correlated with socioeconomic factors, with low SDI regions facing accelerated burden growth. For low SDI regions, including parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, and Latin America, face significant challenges due to limited resources and infrastructure. To effectively address these challenges, we propose a multi-faceted approach that includes enhancing surveillance through robust data collection and monitoring systems, improving healthcare infrastructure by investing in facilities and training healthcare professionals, and implementing community-based programs focused on early detection, treatment adherence, and lifestyle modifications. By prioritizing these strategies in low SDI regions, it is expected that the disease burden can be significantly reduced and finally to advance global health equity.
