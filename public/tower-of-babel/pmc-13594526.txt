Life-history traits associated to West Nile virus seroprevalence in wild birds: Lessons from the recent outbreaks in Spain
Authors: Ana Irina Martín-López, Josué Martínez-de la Puente, Sergio Magallanes, Jesús Veiga, Martina Ferraguti, Ramón Soriguer, Francisco Llorente, Pilar Aguilera-Sepúlveda, Miguel Ángel Jiménez-Clavero, Jordi Figuerola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
West Nile virus (WNV) is a vector-borne zoonotic pathogen maintained in a bird–mosquito cycle, occasionally infecting humans and horses. It is considered a re-emerging pathogen in Europe, with recurrent outbreaks recently reported in Spain. However, the ecological and evolutionary factors driving spatio-temporal variation in WNV incidence remain unclear. To address this, we analysed a long-term dataset of the presence of WNV neutralizing antibodies in 19,105 serum samples from 48 avian species. Blood samples were collected from birds in southern Spain over two decades, between 2003 and 2024, encompassing periods with varying human WNV incidence. Using phylogenetically informed models, we analysed the ecological and life-history traits associated with WNV seroprevalence in wild birds. Age, phylogeny, locality, and year explained significant variation in seroprevalence. Among first-year individuals, urban-associated species showed higher WNV seroprevalence than species inhabiting forest or shrubland habitats, and it varied significantly based on primary lifestyle. In adults, seroprevalence also correlated positively with body mass and negatively with beak length. Notably, WNV seroprevalence in urban birds increased significantly during years with human outbreak, whereas no such association was found in birds from non-urban habitats. These results suggest that the complexity of WNV cycles may be partly unravelled by incorporating data on avian ecology and phylogeny. In addition, we provide evidence of active virus circulation inside the cities during WNV human outbreaks and suggest the potential of urban bird species as epidemiological sentinels for human outbreaks. Overall, this study underscores the importance of a One Health approach to understand vector-borne zoonotic pathogen dynamics and to inform effective surveillance and control strategies.

Introduction

West Nile virus (WNV) is a widely distributed emerging zoonotic flavivirus (family Flaviviridae), primarily maintained in a bird–mosquito transmission cycle [1]. Mosquitoes can also transmit WNV to horses and humans, although these mammals are dead-end hosts [2]. In humans, infections are usually asymptomatic [3], while about 20% of cases show mild, short-duration symptoms such as headache and fever. Less than 1% of infections may develop severe neurological symptoms, including encephalitis or meningoencephalitis, with fatalities in roughly 10% of these severe cases [3].

In Europe, WNV is considered a re-emerging pathogen due to its increasing and highly variable incidence [4]. Since the early 2000s, WNV activity has increased, peaking in 2018 [5]. In Spain, evidence of WNV circulation dates to at least 2003, supported by seroconversion in resident birds [6], [7], [8] and horses [9], [10], and WNV detection in mosquitoes [11]. Human infections were extremely rare until 2020, with only six reported cases between 2004 and 2019. An unusual outbreak in 2020 caused 77 confirmed human cases and eight fatalities, mainly in southern Spain [12]. Following the 2020 season, further infections were documented, with a significant increase in 2024 (158 cases, 20 deaths) [13].

These temporal and spatial variations in WNV prevalence are influenced by ecological and biological factors. Biotic factors, including avian community composition, play a key role in transmission dynamics [14]. In Europe, these patterns remain poorly studied [15]. Identifying bird species with higher susceptibility is therefore essential for understanding virus amplification [16]. In this context, a functional traits approach can provide insight into the ecological and life-history factors underlying WNV exposure, improving monitoring efficiency [17].

Exposure to mosquito bites varies among bird species probably linked to interspecific differences in physical traits and eco-ethological characteristics, including habitat use and behaviour [14]. Larger-bodied avian species show higher WNV seroprevalence [18], probably because they release more mosquito attractants such as CO₂, heat, and olfactory cues [17], [19]. Nevertheless, host species with larger body size typically have also longer lifespans, making it necessary to control for age to separate body size from cumulative exposure effects [18]. Mosquito feeding success is also influenced by exposed skin and host anti-mosquito behaviour, often more effective in smaller individuals [19].

Seasonality and migration further influence vector–host interactions by influencing the spatio-temporal interactions between infected mosquitoes and birds, with important epidemiological consequences [20]. Habitat type may also influence WNV exposure, likely reflecting differences in mosquito abundance and diversity [20], and avian communities [21]. Urban species such as house sparrows (Passer domesticus) and common blackbirds (Turdus merula) have been suggested as crucial reservoirs for WNV, sustaining virus circulation and increasing human outbreak risk [22], although no study has compared yet WNV seroprevalence in these urban species with other avian groups.

Despite growing WNV research, most studies have focused on short sampling periods, single host traits, or have not explicitly accounted for phylogenetic relationships among host species, limiting our understanding of the key ecological drivers of WNV exposure [23]. This is specially the case in Europe, despite the growing public health relevance of WNV in recent years [4]. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, the potential link between long-term patterns of WNV circulation in wild birds with the occurrence of human outbreaks has been little explored. Therefore incorporating bird life-history traits is essential for understanding WNV transmission dynamics, identifying changes associated with human outbreaks, and designing targeted surveillance, particularly in urban areas prone to human spillover [24]. To fill this knowledge gap, here we use a 21-year dataset of WNV seroprevalence in wild birds from southern Spain to examine how bird species phylogeny, life-history traits, and habitat use shape interspecific and temporal variation in WNV exposure.

Materials and methods

Bird sampling and WNV antibody detection assay

Wild birds were sampled between 2003 and 2024 at 30 localities from Huelva, Seville, Cadiz and Malaga provinces in southern Spain (Fig. 1). Sampling was conducted in different seasons each year, although capture effort and species composition varied among them. Capture methods differed among species and included mist nets, walk-in traps and other standard techniques. After capture, birds were ringed and aged following Euring codes [25], [26]. Birds were classified as first-year inidividuals (Euring codes 1 and 3, or code 5 when captured between January–March) or adults (codes 4, 6, 7, 8, or code 5 when captured between April–December). For individuals captured more than once (based on ring number), a single observation was randomly selected for analysis. Blood samples, never exceeding 1% of body mass, were kept in a portable icebox in the field and transported to the laboratory the same day. Two methods were used for WNV antibody detection: ELISA and virus neutralization test (VNT). Bird sera collected in 2003 and2004 were analysed by VNT against WNV (strain E101, Genbank accession number AF260968) to measure neutralizing antibody (NT Ab) titres [27]. When a monoclonal antibody–based blocking ELISA (INgezim West Nile Compac, Gold Standard Diagnostics, Spain) became commercially available [28], it was used as the initial screening method. This procedure was applied to all samples collected from 2005 onwards. Because ELISA cannot distinguish antibodies against WNV from those against other closely related flaviviruses circulating in the area, all positive or doubtful ELISA samples were subsequently confirmed by VNT. Since 2007, all samples analysed by VNT were also tested in parallel against Usutu virus (USUV) (strain SAAR 1776, Genbank accession number AY453412) and Bagaza virus (BAGV) (strain Spain/RLP-Hcc1/2010; Genbank accession number KR10824) to determine antibody specificity [27]. Serum samples with neutralizing titres ≥1:10, which completely neutralized the cytopathic effect in cell monolayers, were considered positive, and antibody specificity was assigned when VNT titres for one virus were at least four times higher than for the other tested viruses [29]. Otherwise, samples were classified as “positive for an undetermined flavivirus.” Samples analysed only by VNT against WNV were considered WNV-specific, as they were collected before the first local detection of other flaviviruses in the study area: 2009 for USUV [11] and 2010 for BAGV [30].

Bird life-history traits

Six life-history trait variables were selected from the 20 available in the AVONET database [31] based on their potential association with WNV exposure in birds. In this database, variables are defined at the species level rather than individual level. The selected species traits were: i) typically occupied habitat (forest, urban, shrubland, wetland), ii) migratory strategy (sedentary, short-distance migrant, Trans-Saharan migrant, originally classified as resident, partial migratory and migratory), iii) primary lifestyle (aerial, aquatic, generalist, insessorial, terrestrial), iv) beak length culmen, v) tarsus length, and vi) body mass. For this study, bird species categorized as “forest” and “woodland” in the AVONET database [31], based on to their typically occupied habitat, were combined into a single category due to their ecological similarity. In addition, although in the original AVONET database “human-modified habitats” species were defined as those from “urban landscapes, intensive agriculture, and gardens”, all bird species classified within this category in our dataset were captured in urban environments. Thus, we renamed this category as “urban” bird species. Furthermore, although common blackbirds (T. merula) is categorized as a “forest” species in the AVONET database, we reclassified this species as “urban” because all individuals included in this study were captured in urban environments. In spite of that, analyses yielded qualitatively similar results when blackbird was considered a forest species. Finally, migratory species were further reclassified into two categories: short-distance migrant and Trans-Saharan migrant. Definitions of each category are provided in Supplementary Table S1, and the full list of traits assigned to each species is shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analyses

Statistical models included WNV serological status as the dependent variable, coded as a binomial variable (1: positive vs. 0: negative). WNV-negative birds included individuals without WNV antibodies and those positive for USUV, BAGV, or “undetermined flavivirus”. Analyses were restricted to WNV because the number of USUV-positive individuals was too low for detailed statistical analyses. To ensure reliable prevalence estimates, only bird species with a sample size ≥15 individuals captured in at least two localities were included, accounting for potential spatial heterogeneity in virus transmission [32].

A phylogeny for the species included in this study was obtained as a consensus tree from 10,000 trees from BirdTree database [33], using a Hackett tree distribution. A 50% majority-rule consensus tree was produced in Geneious v2023.0.4 [34], following Rubolini et al. [35]. To identify life-history traits associated with WNV seroprevalence, multivariate generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were fitted using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques. This framework allows the inclusion of multiple random factors, including the phylogenetic relationships as a covariance matrix, without introducing parameter bias or relying in strict normality assumptions for binary data [36], [37]. Weakly informative inverse-gamma priors (V = 1, ν = 0.002) were used to reduce prior impact on posterior estimates [37]. Sensitivity analyses using alternative weakly informative priors yielded qualitatively identical results, indicating that posterior estimates were robust to prior specification. WNV serological status was the dependent variable, and the bird species life-history traits were included as fixed factors, while year of sampling, sampling locality, bird species identity, and bird phylogeny were included as random factors.

Four parallel MCMC chains were run for 180,000 iterations, with a burn-in of 30,000 and a thinning interval of 100. Output values were calculated as the mean of the four estimates. Phylogenetic signal was estimated as an equivalent to Pagel's λ model [38], that ranges between 0 and 1 according to the degree traits from closely related species resemble relative to the expectations of a Brownian motion process. Additional models, with the same permutation parameters but excluding phylogeny, were run to assess whether some life-history traits correlated with WNV seroprevalence independently of phylogenetic effects. Separate models were fitted for i) the complete dataset, including the interaction between age and each independent variable and factor, and ii) first-year individuals only, since antibodies in adults may reflect cumulative exposure, whereas first-year individuals reflect infections acquired in the current season [39]. For temporal consistency, each year was defined as April to March, as first-year individuals sampled between January to March represent infections acquired in the first year of life. Model validation included inspection of trace and density plots, which showed stationary mixing and unimodal distributions across chains; effective sample sizes exceeding 1000 for all parameters (minimum = 1664); satisfactory Heidelberg and Welch convergence diagnostics; and Gelman–Rubin diagnostics with values <1.05 across four independent MCMC chains. Potential collinearity among fixed effects was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with all values below 4, indicating no problematic collinearity among predictors [40].

To assess the relationship between WNV seroprevalence in birds and human WNV outbreaks, we fitted a GLMM with a binomial distribution and logit link using the dataset including only first-year birds. In this analyses, WNV antibody status was the dependent variable, while season, years with human outbreaks in Andalusia (1 for those years with human outbreaks which included 2010, 2016, 2020–2024; 0 for other years), bird habitat (urban vs. non-urban), and the interaction between human outbreak years and habitat were included as fixed factors. In this analysis, we considered only two categories in the bird habitat variable to account for differences between bird species typically found in urban areas with respect to the non-urban species, which include species initially catalogued as forest, shrubland and wetland. Sampling locality and bird species were included as random intercepts. Fixed factors were tested with type III ANOVA chi-square tests, followed by Tukey post-hoc tests. DHARMa and Q-Q plot diagnostics were used to check residuals, and Pearson statistics indicated no significant overdispersion.

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2) using the packages: MCMCglmm, ape, plotMCMC, coda, phytools, geiger, phylolm, lme4, MuMIn, DHARMa.

Results

Bird life-history traits and WNV antibodies

Phylogenetic MCMC GLMM using the complete dataset including adults and first-year individuals showed significant associations between WNV seroprevalence and species beak length, body mass, migratory behaviour, and habitat type, with differences between age groups. Tarsus length and primary lifestyle were not associated with WNV seroprevalence (Fig. 4a). A strong significant phylogenetic signal on WNV seroprevalence was detected (post-mean = 0.767, 95% C.I. = 0.583 – 0.890). In adults, species beak length was negatively associated with WNV seroprevalence (post-mean = −1.836, 95% C.I. = −3.603 – -0.055), whereas species body mass was positively associated (post-mean = 3.663, 95% C.I. = 1.124 – 6.218). In first-year individuals, neither species beak length (post-mean = −1.079, 95% C.I. = −2.707 – 0.504) nor species body mass (post-mean = 1.279, 95% C.I. = −1.568 – 4.088) were related to WNV seroprevalence. Migratory behaviour was associated with WNV seroprevalence in first-year individuals, with short-distance migrants showing higher seroprevalence than sedentary species (post-mean = 1.874, 95% C.I. = 0.714 – 3.145). Regarding habitat, adults of species from urban habitats (post-mean = 1.658, 95% C.I. = 0.457 – 2.964) and shrublands (post-mean = 1.379, 95% C.I. = 0.163 – 2.602) showed higher WNV seroprevalence than forest. When phylogeny was excluded, only species body mass, migratory behaviour, and habitat type remained significant predictors of WNV seroprevalence (Fig. S1a). In adults, species body mass was positively associated with WNV seroprevalence (post-mean = 2.959, 95% C.I. = 0.512 – 5.552). In first-year individuals, short-distance migratory species had higher WNV seroprevalence than sedentary species (post-mean = 1.521, 95% C.I. = −0.228 – 2.868). Regarding habitat, adults from shrubland (post-mean = 1.662, 95% C.I. = 0.159 – 3.262) and wetland habitats (post-mean = 1.820, 95% C.I. = 0.431 – 3.206) showed higher seroprevalence than those from forest species, with no significant differences among other habitat types.

Analyses restricted to first-year individuals confirmed habitat and primary lifestyle as significant predictors of WNV antibodies (Fig. 4b). A strong phylogenetic signal was again detected (post-mean = 0.887, 95% C.I. = 0.766 – 0.960). Bird species of urban habitats showed higher WNV seroprevalence than those from forest (post-mean = 3.062, 95% C.I. = 0.364 – 5.672) and shrubland habitats (post-mean = 2.690, 95% C.I. = 0.003 – 6.009), with no differences among other habitat categories. Additionally, generalist (post-mean = 3.018, 95% C.I. = 0.781 – 5.493) and insessorial bird species (post-mean = 3.858, 95% C.I. = 1.112 – 7.009) had higher WNV seroprevalence than terrestrial bird species.

When phylogeny was excluded from the analyses, habitat type and primary lifestyle remained significantly associated with WNV seroprevalence (Fig. S1b). Birds species from wetlands showed higher seroprevalence than those from forest (post-mean = 4.116, 95% C.I. = 1.789 – 6.985) and shrubland habitats (post-mean = 3.048, 95% C.I. = 0.541 – 5.706), with no differences among other habitat categories. Aquatic species showed lower seroprevalence than generalist (post-mean = −4.053, 95% C.I. = −7.889 – -0.671) and insessorial species (post-mean = −4.390, 95% C.I. = −8.566 – -0.664), with no associations among other lifestyle categories.

WNV seroprevalence and the occurrence of human outbreaks

WNV seroprevalence was significantly associated with the occurrence of human outbreaks (χ2 = 28.39, df = 1, p < 0.0001), but not with habitat (χ2 = 0.38, df = 1, p = 0.536). However, the interaction between human outbreak occurrence and habitat was significant (χ2 = 62.65, df = 1, p < 0.0001). Urban bird species showed lower WNV seroprevalence in years without human outbreaks than in years with reported human cases (Est = −2.589, S.E. = 0.264, Z = –9.809, p < 0.0001; Fig. 5), whereas non-urban species showed no significant differences (Est = 0.475, S.E. = 0.290, Z = 1.638, p = 0.357; Fig. 5). Discussion

Prior to 2020, only six human WNV cases had been reported in Spain [10], [12], [41]. In 2020, a major outbreak occurred, mainly affecting populations in the Guadalquivir Basin in southern Spain [22]. However, WNV circulation has been documented in the area since at least 2003 [6], [7], [8]. Our results confirm this pattern, with seropositive first-year sedentary birds detected annually over the past 21 years, despite interannual variation in seroprevalence, as well as differences among bird species. In the case of analyses restricted to first-year individual birds, seasonal variation was also observed, with a higher WNV seroprevalence in autumn. This pattern suggests that infections occurred during summer and early autumn overlapping with mosquito activity, and thus higher WNV transmission risk, result in seroconversion by the time of autumn [8], [9], [18]. For example, during the 2020 outbreak in the study area, WNV infection intensity in mosquitoes peaked at the end of July with the last positive Cx. perexiguus pool detected by the end of September [22]. We also reported the first detection of USUV circulation in vertebrates in southern Spain in 2009, coinciding with first detections in mosquitoes [11]. In subsequent years, USUV circulation has also been reported in birds [7], [8], [22] and horses [9].

All bird orders examined here were exposed to WNV, with the highest WNV seroprevalences found in Charadriiformes, Gruiformes and Passeriformes. Studies in the USA show that WNV affects many species, although exposure varies substantially [14]. Previous studies have also reported high WNV seroprevalence in Passeriformes, Charadriiformes, Falconiformes, Gruiformes, Accipitriformes, and Piciformes [18], [42]. Among them, Charadriiformes, Gruiformes and Passeriformes are considered potential competent reservoirs for WNV [41]. We also found a strong phylogenetic signal in both the full dataset and the dataset including only first-year individuals, indicating that host phylogeny influences WNV infection probability [43]. Therefore, these taxa deserve particular attention during WNV surveillance, as they may provide reliable information on virus circulation with smaller sample sizes. However, experimental studies are needed to assess host competence and the role of these species in WNV transmission, as seropositivity reflects exposure and survival to infection but does not necessarily indicate host competence [44]. Despite the valuable large dataset included in this study, the bird species sampled were not uniform across sampling years and localities. This could result in an uneven representation of some bird taxa, potentially affecting our results. However, the consistency of the phylogenetic signal after accounting for year and locality as random effects suggests that the observed patterns are unlikely to be solely driven by sampling heterogeneity.

We found strong evidence that different bird characteristics and host species traits predict WNV seroprevalence, independently of phylogenetic relatedness. Among them, bird age had a significant effect on WNV seroprevalence, with relationships differing between adults and first-year individuals. This likely reflects the prolonged exposure of adults versus the annual snapshot of first-year birds [39]. In addition, species body mass was positively associated with WNV seroprevalence, as previously reported [15], [18]. The mechanisms underlying this association have not been yet fully identified, but this association is likely driven by a higher host attractiveness and exposure to mosquito vectors [17]. For instance, larger bird species emit more CO₂ and olfactory cues, potentially increasing their attractiveness to mosquitoes [17]. In addition, smaller species may show more agile anti-mosquito behaviours, potentially reducing bite risk [19]. Beak length was negatively related to WNV seroprevalence, suggesting reduced defensive ability in short-billed species for preening or pecking [19]. Furthermore, we found higher WNV seroprevalence in short-distance migratory species than in sedentary ones. While residents confirm local circulation, higher seroprevalence in migrants suggests exposure in other regions [45], such as northern Africa, where WNV antibodies have been detected in resident birds in Morocco [46], and south of the Sahara where high seroprevaleces were reported in horses, specially in the Sahel area [47]. Similarly, in a previous study conducted in southwestern Spain, migratory birds had higher WNV exposure likelihood than native and exotic bird species [21]. However, literature reports contrasting results, with migrants also showing lower [48], or no significant differences [15] relative to residents, possibly due to regional migratory routes, the timing of sampling in relation to migratory movements or infection–migration trade-offs. Finally, differences in WNV seroprevalence in birds of different habitat categories or primary lifestyles likely reflect variation in exposure to WNV-infected mosquitoes, driven by differences in mosquito abundance and community composition among habitats [20]. We found higher WNV seroprevalence in generalist and insessorial species than in terrestrial species, while urban birds showed higher WNV seroprevalence than forest or shrubland species. Generalist and insessorial birds may encounter mosquitoes across a wider range of microhabitats, whereas terrestrial birds may have more limited vector contact [42]. Unfortunately, in our study we cannot determine the specific contribution of mosquito communities (e.g., mosquito abundance or infection rates) to the observed seroprevalence patterns because bird captures were not associated to mosquito surveillance. Future studies integrating long-term WNV surveillance in birds with vector monitoring are therefore necessary to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the ecological drivers of WNV circulation.

Finally, we observed an interaction between habitat type and years of WNV human outbreaks in Andalusia. Urban species showed higher seroprevalence in outbreak years (2010, 2016, 2020–2024) compared to non-outbreak years, with no differences in non-urban species. This is consistent with evidence that human outbreaks are shaped by various environmental, biotic, and sociological factors [49]. A high WNV seroprevalence was found in urban species such as house sparrows and blackbirds, during a recent human outbreak in Andalusia, confirming that WNV was circulating within urban areas [22]. Our results support the role of urban bird species in the epidemiology of WNV, suggesting that the outbreaks often involve localized amplification in cities rather than uniform increases in virus circulation across regions. The present study has identified some of the avian species more exposed to WNV infection in Spain, but it is also necessary to assess their potential as competent reservoirs of the virus to completely understand their role in WNV amplification [42]. Moreover, this highlights the need for targeted mosquito control in urban breeding sites, such as buildings and drainage infrastructures [50]. Such spatially focused transmission linked to anthropogenic factors may explain outbreak dynamics better than interannual virus variation alone [51].

Conclusions

Over the past two decades, WNV has become endemic in southern Spain, reporting large temporal and interspecific variation in seroprevalence in animals and human incidence. Our results highlight the role of certain wild bird species in WNV transmission from a functional traits approach. Body mass, migratory behaviour, and habitat were important predictors of WNV seroprevalence, with variation by age categories, likely reflecting a differential exposure to mosquito vectors. Since urban bird species had higher WNV seroprevalence during human outbreaks, unlike non-urban birds, these species could be incorporated into targeted surveillance programs as early indicators of increased WNV circulation. Likewise, vector control efforts should prioritise urban and peri-urban mosquito breeding sites during periods of increased transmission risk, integrating bird surveillance with entomological and human surveillance approach within a One Health framework. As vector-borne diseases like WNV remain a global concern, such coordinated surveillance could improve early warning systems and support timely interventions to reduce the risk of WNV transmission to humans and animals.
