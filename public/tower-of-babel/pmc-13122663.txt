Introduction

Nutcracker syndrome (NCS) refers to compression of the left renal vein (LRV), most commonly between the abdominal aorta and the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), resulting in impaired renal venous drainage. The anatomical basis of this condition was first described by El-Sadr and Mina in 1950, who detailed LRV entrapment between the aorta and SMA, though the clinical syndrome was not yet recognized at that time.1 The term “nutcracker” was later coined by de Schepper in 1972 to describe this vascular compression, and is now widely used to refer to the syndrome characterized by symptomatic LRV entrapment.2 The classic configuration involves LRV compression between the SMA and aorta (anterior NCS), though a posterior variant, compression between the aorta and vertebral column, is also recognized.2, 3, 4.

Clinically, NCS may manifest with hematuria, flank pain, orthostatic proteinuria, pelvic congestion syndrome in females, and varicocele in males.3 Despite its potential morbidity, NCS remains underdiagnosed due to its heterogeneous symptomatology and the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria.3,5 The nutcracker phenomenon is the term used when anatomical compression is demonstrated on imaging in the absence of symptoms, whereas nutcracker syndrome implies the presence of clinical manifestations.5

Diagnosis of NCS requires careful correlation of clinical features with radiological findings. Initial evaluation commonly employs Doppler ultrasonography, CT angiography, or MRI, which can reveal anatomical compression of the LRV and help differentiate anterior from posterior subtypes.4,6 However, imaging alone is not definitive, as similar compressions may be found in asymptomatic individuals.5 The gold standard remains retrograde renal venography with direct measurement of the renocaval pressure gradient, with a gradient ≥3 mmHg considered significant.3,7 Nevertheless, the invasive nature of this test limits its use to selected cases.7

Cystoscopy and ureteroscopy (URS) are not required in the standard diagnostic algorithm for NCS but may be employed to exclude other causes of hematuria when imaging or clinical suspicion persists.8 Endoscopic approaches such as URS have been described anecdotally to localize unilateral renal bleeding or to rule out upper tract urothelial carcinoma in complex cases, though they are not part of routine evaluation.8,9 In this context, URS may be used to obtain diagnostic clarification for certain patients and could assist in decisions regarding surgical intervention or conservative management.10

This case report highlights a rare instance in which URS helped identify the source of hematuria in a patient with suspected nutcracker syndrome and demonstrates how it can serve as a useful diagnostic tool in selected clinical situations.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old female with no prior medical history or previous surgeries presented with symptoms of a gross hematuria two to three times a month that would last one to two day which she described as painless and associated with clots since June 2022. The hematuria was unrelated to her menses and was not accompanied by symptoms of flank pain, dysuria, or urinary frequency. The patient also stated that she has never experienced a urinary tract infection, kidney stones, trauma to the urinary tract or a coagulopathy. Additionally, there was no relevant family history of renal or autoimmune disorders. The patient was single and did not smoke.

Physical exam revealed the patient to be hemodynamically stable, with a BMI of 22.9 kg/m2. Both abdominal and genitourinary exams were unremarkable. Lab studies showed mild anemia with a hemoglobin of 10.7 g/dl, normal renal function with a serum creatinine of 51 μmol/L, and normal coagulation parameters with an INR of 0.97. Urine analysis showed >4000 RBCs/high power field with negative cytology for malignant cells.

Imaging

A contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis during the hematuria episode with no filling defect during delayed film demonstrated anterior compression of the left renal vein between the SMA and the aorta, with an aortomesenteric angle of 35.8°, suggestive of anterior NCS. No renal masses, hydronephrosis, or ureteric calculi were identified (Fig. 1, Fig. 1A and B).

Ureteroscopy

Revealed active bloody efflux from the left ureteric orifice, with a normal-appearing bladder mucosa (Fig. 2).

Ureteroscopic view of the proximal left ureter demonstrating an intraluminal blood clot adherent to the ureteral wall, with no visible mucosal lesion or mass.

Retrograde pyelography (RGP)

Showed patent bilateral ureters with lateral deviation but no filling defects or hydronephrosis.

Diagnostic ureteroscopy (URS)

Advanced to the proximal left ureter, URS revealed a patent lumen with an isolated intraluminal blood clot. No strictures, masses, or mucosal abnormalities were visualized.

Nephrology workup

Autoimmune testing (ANA, ANCA, anti-GBM, dsDNA, C3, C4) was negative. No proteinuria was detected.

Outcome

The patient was managed conservatively post-URS without additional interventions. At serial follow-ups over one year, the patient remained asymptomatic and repeat urinalyses were negative for hematuria. Follow-up renal ultrasonography at 6 and 12 months was unremarkable.

Discussion

Nutcracker syndrome (NCS) is a rare but important cause of hematuria, particularly in young, thin individuals, often females, without other identifiable urologic pathology.3,5,11 The underlying pathophysiology is renal venous hypertension caused by compression of the left renal vein (LRV), leading to the formation and rupture of thin-walled venous collaterals into the collecting system.5,11 Clinical manifestations are heterogeneous, ranging from microscopic or gross hematuria and flank or abdominal pain, to symptoms of pelvic venous congestion, orthostatic proteinuria, and varicocele.3,5 Notably, many patients undergo extensive urologic workup before NCS is considered, as standard investigations often fail to identify intrinsic urinary tract disease.3

Diagnostic imaging & variable Accuracy

Doppler ultrasonography is widely used as an initial screening tool. Its sensitivity for NCS ranges from 69 to 90%, while the specificity ranges from 89 to 100%.6 Key diagnostic metrics include the diameter ratio of the LRV at the hilar portion versus the compressed segment, and peak velocity ratios. These ratios have been validated through numerous classical Doppler studies.6 Cross-sectional imaging modalities such as CT or MR angiography are frequently employed when Doppler results are equivocal or more detailed anatomical assessment is needed.8A pre-compression to compression diameter ratio of >2.25 on CT is strongly suggestive of significant venous compression, but thresholds vary among studies and are not universally agreed upon.8 Although CT/MRI can clearly show the presence of vascular compression and related collateral vessels, these modalities cannot determine the location of bleeding or definitively exclude other potential causes of hematuria (such as tumor or stone), which would need to be evaluated independently.4,8,12 The imaging literature supports that each modality (US, CT/MRI, Venography) has different advantages and disadvantages for evaluating NCS, and therefore, a multi-modality approach should be utilized.12

Retrograde venography as gold standard

Retrograde renal venography with direct measurement of the renocaval pressure gradient remains the gold standard for NCS diagnosis.8,12 A pressure gradient ≥3 mmHg between the LRV and the inferior vena cava (IVC) is widely accepted as diagnostic, though this threshold may overlap with normal variants.8 The invasive nature of venography limits its use to cases where noninvasive modalities are inconclusive or when definitive confirmation is required before proceeding with intervention.8,12

Ureteroscopy (URS) in persistent hematuria & NCS

Cystoscopy and ureteroscopy (URS) are not routinely indicated in the diagnosis of NCS, but may be employed when imaging is inconclusive or when it is necessary to exclude other causes of hematuria such as urothelial carcinoma or stones.8 In selected patients, endoscopy may demonstrate unilateral bleeding from the left ureteral orifice during active hematuria, suggesting renal origin.8 However, endoscopic evaluation does not establish the diagnosis of vascular compression itself and should be reserved for persistent or unexplained hematuria after imaging.8,13 Management strategies are tailored to symptom severity; conservative management is appropriate for mild or intermittent hematuria, while surgical intervention (such as LRV transposition) is considered for recurrent or severe cases.14

Hematuria resolution & speculative therapeutic effect

Spontaneous remission of hematuria is well-documented in NCS, particularly in younger patients managed conservatively.15 While observation is often appropriate, the variable natural history of NCS and the lack of standardized treatment criteria necessitate caution when attributing clinical improvement to specific interventions.15 This raises the possibility of a therapeutic effect, such as clot dislodgment or a local washout. However, because ureteroscopy has not been established as a therapeutic modality in the management of NCS, any such association remains speculative.15

Limited literature on URS in NCS

The published literature on URS in NCS is sparse, and URS is generally described only as an adjunctive diagnostic tool, not as a standard part of the diagnostic algorithm.8 Most expert reviews emphasize that the diagnostic workup of NCS should focus on clinical–imaging correlation, and that endoscopy is reserved for excluding other causes of hematuria or for clarifying the bleeding source in complex cases.8,12 Direct visualization via URS or cystoscopy may occasionally demonstrate blood at the left ureteral orifice, but does not confirm vascular compression. 8The present case illustrates how URS can contribute to diagnostic clarity in selected patients with persistent hematuria, but it should not be interpreted as definitive treatment for NCS.

Conclusion

Nutcracker syndrome should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unexplained hematuria, especially in young individuals with negative cytology and imaging suggestive of LRV compression. While not a standard diagnostic modality, ureteroscopy can be a valuable adjunct in selected cases to localize bleeding and exclude intraluminal lesions. The possible therapeutic impact of URS, as observed in this case, warrants further exploration.
