Superb microvascular imaging versus CT angiography for assessing disease activity in pediatric Takayasu arteritis: a comparative study with high consistency
Authors: Qiqiong Tang, Chenxi Jia, Jiashuo Wu, Yunlu Tao, Zhenghu Wei, Mingyu Xia, Zankai Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo compare the value and consistency of superb microvascular imaging (SMI) and CT angiography (CTA) for assessing disease activity in pediatric Takayasu arteritis (TA), and to explore the feasibility of SMI as a non-invasive screening tool.MethodsForty-seven children with TA treated at our hospital between May 2019 and January 2025 were included. Disease activity was determined using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) criteria, and patients were classified into active (n = 26) and inactive (n = 21) groups. All patients underwent both SMI and CTA. The sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and area under the ROC curve (AUC) of SMI grading and CTA for diagnosing TA activity were analyzed. Kappa tests were used to assess consistency with NIH criteria. Inter-observer agreement for SMI grading was also evaluated.ResultsSMI grading was significantly higher in the active group than in the inactive group (Z = 5.388, p < 0.001). Using SMI grade ≥ II as the cutoff, SMI had a sensitivity of 84.62%, specificity of 95.24%, and accuracy of 89.36%. CTA had a sensitivity of 88.46%, specificity of 90.48%, and accuracy of 89.36%. The AUC values were 0.885 for SMI and 0.895 for CTA. SMI showed good consistency with NIH criteria (Kappa = 0.788), as did CTA (Kappa = 0.786). Inter-observer agreement for SMI grading was good (Kappa = 0.865, 95% CI: 0.777–0.953), with 43 of 47 cases (91.49%) showing complete concordance.ConclusionsSMI and CTA demonstrate comparable diagnostic performance and high consistency in assessing disease activity in pediatric TA. Given its non-invasive nature, absence of radiation, no need for contrast agents, and repeatability, SMI is recommended as the preferred screening tool for long-term follow-up of disease activity in pediatric TA, with CTA reserved for cases where SMI suggests activity or when precise vascular assessment is needed.Keywords: Superb microvascular imaging, CT angiography, Takayasu arteritis, Disease activity, Pediatric, Screening tool

Background

Takayasu arteritis (TA) is a chronic progressive large-vessel vasculitis and the third most common pediatric vasculitis after IgA vasculitis and Kawasaki disease [1]. TA involves the aorta and its major branches, leading to vessel wall thickening, stenosis, and even occlusion, resulting in ischemic symptoms in corresponding organs [2]. The clinical presentation of pediatric TA is often atypical, and young children may have difficulty describing their symptoms accurately, making early diagnosis and disease activity assessment challenging.

Accurate determination of TA disease activity is crucial for treatment decisions—active disease requires aggressive immunosuppressive therapy, while inactive disease focuses on managing complications [3]. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) criteria are currently the primary clinical tool for assessing TA activity. However, these criteria rely heavily on subjective symptoms and laboratory markers, with inherent limitations [4]. Therefore, imaging plays an increasingly important role in TA activity assessment.

Studies have shown that children with TA experience the most severe damage and slowest remission among all pediatric vasculitides [5]. This highlights the urgent need for better tools to monitor disease activity and guide timely treatment adjustments. Recent advances in imaging technology have opened new avenues for TA evaluation. Cong et al. [6] demonstrated that carotid vasa vasorum detected by super-resolution ultrasound microvascular imaging correlates significantly with disease activity in TA (sensitivity 42.3%, specificity 93.2%), providing a foundation for the use of novel ultrasound techniques in this setting.

CT angiography (CTA) can clearly demonstrate vessel wall thickening, enhancement, and other active inflammatory changes, making it a valuable imaging modality for assessing TA activity [7]. However, CTA has significant limitations for pediatric use, including radiation exposure, the need for iodinated contrast agents, and sedation requirements in young children. Children are particularly sensitive to radiation, and the cumulative risk from repeated CTA examinations is substantial [8]. Moreover, TA is a chronic disease that extends from childhood into adulthood, requiring decades of long-term follow-up—frequent CTA use is impractical and potentially harmful.

Superb microvascular imaging (SMI) is an innovative non-invasive ultrasound technique that sensitively detects low-velocity blood flow signals within vessel walls, indirectly reflecting inflammatory activity through the presence of neovascularization [9, 10]. Compared with CTA, SMI offers several advantages: no radiation, no contrast agent requirement, non-invasive, portable, low-cost, and excellent repeatability. These features make SMI theoretically ideal for long-term follow-up of pediatric TA. However, the consistency and diagnostic value of SMI compared with CTA in pediatric TA activity assessment have not been systematically studied.

This study aimed to compare the diagnostic performance and consistency of SMI and CTA for assessing disease activity in pediatric TA and to evaluate the feasibility of SMI as a screening tool for routine follow-up. We explicitly recognize that SMI is not intended to replace CTA as the diagnostic gold standard, but rather to serve as a complementary, radiation-free screening instrument to identify children who may safely defer CTA during long-term surveillance, thereby providing a safer and more cost-effective imaging strategy for the long-term management of pediatric TA.

Methods

Patients

All 47 patients who met the inclusion criteria and underwent both SMI and CTA examinations were consecutively enrolled. No additional exclusions were made after enrollment.

Superb microvascular imaging (SMI)

SMI was performed using a Canon Aplio i900 ultrasound system with a PLT-805AT linear array probe (frequency range: 5–11 MHz). All examinations used the same parameters in mSMI mode, with the following settings: imaging depth 2–3 cm; ROI size adjusted to cover the entire thickened carotid artery wall. The thickest segment of the common carotid artery was evaluated bilaterally, and the highest grade from either side was recorded for analysis.

Based on the visualization of microvascular flow within the thickened carotid artery wall, SMI findings were classified into three grades (Fig. 1):

Examples of SMI grading of the thickened carotid artery wall. (A) Grade 0: no detectable blood flow signal within the thickened carotid artery wall. (B) Grade I: 1–4 punctate or 1–2 short linear blood flow signals. (C) Grade II: ≥5 punctate or ≥ 2 short linear blood flow signals. SMI: superb microvascular imaging

SMI grading was independently assessed by two deputy chief radiologists who were blinded to each other’s readings, the patients’ clinical data, and the NIH scores. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Inter-observer agreement for SMI grading was evaluated using the Kappa test.

The SMI grading scale was based on previously published criteria by Wang et al. [10], which were adapted from contrast-enhanced ultrasound grading systems for assessing neovascularization in TA.

CT angiography (CTA)

CTA was performed using a Siemens SOMATOM Sensation 64 CT scanner. Scanning parameters: tube voltage 100–120 kV, tube current 200–394 mAs, rotation time 0.28 s, collimation width 0.6 mm × 20. Scanning was performed from the mandible level to the femoral head. Children were instructed to hold their breath and raise their arms during scanning.

Contrast agent (ioversol, 370 mg I/ml) was injected via the antecubital vein at a dose of 1.5 ml/kg body weight and an injection rate of 4–5 ml/s, followed by 30 ml of saline at the same rate. Automatic triggering was used, with the trigger point set at the proximal ascending aorta (threshold: 120 HU). Scanning began 6 seconds after the threshold was reached. Venous phase data were acquired 90 seconds after contrast injection.

All images were transferred to a Siemens workstation for post-processing, including multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) and volume rendering (VR). CTA findings indicative of active TA included: arterial wall thickness > 3 mm, wall enhancement ratio > 1.2, presence of a low-density ring sign, irregular vessel wall, and increased perivascular fat density.

All patients underwent SMI and CTA examinations within a mean interval of 3.5 ± 1.8 days (range: 1–7 days), minimizing the possibility of disease activity changes between the two examinations.

Outcome measures

The following parameters were compared between the active and inactive groups: sex, age, clinical symptoms, pulse status, vascular bruits, ESR, CRP, SMI grade, and CTA results. Diagnostic performance (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and AUC) was calculated for both SMI grading and CTA using NIH criteria as the reference standard.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25.0. Continuous data (normally distributed) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the t-test. Categorical data were expressed as n (%) and compared using the chi-square test. Ordinal data were compared using the rank-sum test. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using a 2 × 2 table and ROC curves. The Kappa test was used to assess agreement between SMI grading and NIH criteria, between CTA and NIH criteria, and between the two readers for SMI grading. Kappa values < 0.4 indicated poor agreement, 0.4–0.75 indicated moderate agreement, and ≥0.75 indicated good agreement. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample size estimation: Based on an expected AUC of 0.80, a two-sided α of 0.05, and a power (1−β) of 0.80, a minimum of 30 patients was required using the method of Hanley and McNeil. Accounting for a potential 10% dropout rate, we aimed to enroll 33 patients; ultimately, 47 patients were included, providing adequate statistical power for the primary analyses.

Discussion

Pediatric TA is a chronic progressive disease that extends from childhood into adulthood, requiring decades of long-term follow-up and multiple imaging assessments. Throughout this prolonged disease course, clinicians face a practical challenge: balancing diagnostic accuracy with examination safety, particularly regarding cumulative radiation exposure in children. This study compared the diagnostic performance and consistency of SMI and CTA for assessing disease activity in pediatric TA, providing evidence to optimize long-term follow-up strategies.

Comparison of diagnostic performance between SMI and CTA

In this study, using NIH criteria as the reference standard, SMI (grade ≥ II) had a sensitivity of 84.62%, specificity of 95.24%, and accuracy of 89.36% for diagnosing active TA. CTA had a sensitivity of 88.46%, specificity of 90.48%, and accuracy of 89.36%. The AUC values were 0.885 for SMI and 0.895 for CTA, with no statistically significant difference. These findings demonstrate that SMI and CTA have comparable diagnostic performance in assessing pediatric TA activity, supporting the potential of SMI as an alternative screening tool.

The high specificity of SMI (95.24%) has important clinical implications—a negative SMI finding (grade < II) strongly suggests inactive disease, making it safe to defer CTA. This feature is particularly valuable for children with TA who require long-term follow-up, as it allows clinicians to confidently monitor disease status without unnecessary radiation exposure.

High consistency between SMI and CTA

SMI showed good consistency with NIH criteria (Kappa = 0.788), as did CTA (Kappa = 0.786). Both values exceed the threshold for “good agreement” (Kappa ≥ 0.75). This finding suggests that SMI grading and CTA produce highly consistent results for assessing TA activity, further supporting the feasibility of using SMI as a CTA alternative for routine surveillance.

Notably, the inter-observer agreement for SMI grading was good (Kappa = 0.865, 95% CI: 0.777–0.953), with 43 of 47 cases (91.49%) showing complete concordance between the two readers. This indicates that SMI grading criteria are clear, reproducible, and suitable for use by different operators—a critical requirement for any clinical screening tool that may be used across different centers and practitioners.

Comparison with other imaging modalities

Recent studies have explored various imaging techniques for assessing TA activity. Cong et al. [6] demonstrated that carotid vasa vasorum detected by super-resolution ultrasound microvascular imaging correlates significantly with TA disease activity, supporting the biological basis of SMI technology. Sopa et al. [13] systematically reviewed imaging modalities for monitoring large vessel vasculitis, highlighting the utility of ultrasound, MRI, and PET/CT, each with distinct advantages depending on the clinical context.

Sözeri et al. [14] reported the value of FDG-PET/CT for assessing disease activity in childhood large vessel vasculitis. Compared with PET/CT, SMI offers several advantages: no radiation, no need for radiopharmaceuticals, lower cost, and bedside operability, making it more suitable for routine follow-up. PET/CT may be reserved for cases where SMI findings are equivocal or when whole-body assessment is required.

Clinical implications: from “replacement” to “tiered management”

We wish to emphasize that our study was not designed to propose SMI as a replacement for CTA. Rather, we aimed to evaluate whether SMI could serve as a practical screening instrument for long-term follow-up. We fully acknowledge that CTA remains the gold standard for comprehensive vascular mapping and initial diagnosis.

Based on the high consistency between SMI and CTA (Kappa = 0.788) and the comparable diagnostic performance of the two modalities, we propose a tiered management strategy for assessing disease activity in pediatric TA:

Tier 1 - Routine Surveillance: Perform SMI every 3–6 months. If SMI grade < II, inactive disease is highly likely, and continued follow-up without CTA is safe.

Tier 2 - SMI Positive: If SMI grade ≥ II, active disease should be suspected, and CTA is recommended for confirmation and detailed vascular assessment.

Tier 3 - CTA Confirmation: CTA remains the gold standard for initial diagnosis, confirmation when SMI suggests activity, and comprehensive vascular evaluation prior to interventional procedures.

This tiered approach can significantly reduce the number of CTA examinations in children with TA while maintaining diagnostic accuracy. Given that children are more sensitive to radiation than adults, and that TA requires lifelong follow-up, the health economic and safety benefits of this strategy are substantial.

Comparison with previous studies

Previous studies have established the value of SMI in assessing TA activity. Wang et al. [10] reported that SMI correlated well with contrast-enhanced ultrasound for evaluating TA activity. Chen et al. [15] further demonstrated that SMI grading correlated with serum levels of MMP-3 and MMP-9, suggesting that SMI can reflect the degree of vascular wall inflammation. Building on these findings, our study is the first to directly compare SMI with CTA in a relatively large pediatric cohort (47 patients) and to propose a specific clinical management algorithm with practical cutoff values.

Chitkara et al. [16] reported a case of pediatric TA complicated by left ventricular thrombus, highlighting the severe cardiovascular complications that can occur in this population. This underscores the importance of accurate disease activity monitoring to prevent such adverse outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the single-center retrospective design may have introduced selection bias. Second, while inter-observer agreement was good, SMI grading remains somewhat subjective and requires adequate training. Third, we acknowledge that the NIH criteria include imaging findings, which introduces a risk of circularity when used as the reference standard for validating CTA. However, in our study, the NIH score was assessed by a pediatric rheumatologist based primarily on clinical and laboratory data, while CTA criteria were evaluated by independent blinded radiologists. Nevertheless, we recognize this as a limitation and suggest that future studies consider pediatric-specific activity tools such as PVAS (Pediatric Vasculitis Activity Score). Fourth, although the sample size (47 patients) is relatively large for a rare pediatric disease, multicenter prospective studies are needed for validation. Fifth, the SMI grading scale used in this study is semi-quantitative and has not been formally validated; further studies are needed to establish standardized criteria. Sixth, this study did not evaluate the dynamic changes of SMI during treatment monitoring, which is an important direction for future research.

Xu et al. [17] recently developed a prediction model for poor prognosis in childhood-onset TA. Future studies could incorporate SMI parameters into such prognostic models to enhance their predictive value.

Despite these limitations, the practical screening algorithm derived from this study offers a clinically useful framework for monitoring disease activity in children with TA, pending validation in larger prospective cohorts.

Conclusion

SMI and CTA demonstrate comparable diagnostic performance and high consistency (Kappa = 0.788) in assessing disease activity in pediatric TA. Given its non-invasive nature, absence of radiation, no need for contrast agents, excellent repeatability, low cost, and good inter-observer agreement (Kappa = 0.865), SMI is recommended as a preferred screening tool for long-term follow-up of disease activity in pediatric TA. CTA can be reserved for initial diagnosis, confirmation when SMI suggests activity, and pre-intervention vascular assessment. This “SMI screening → CTA confirmation” tiered management strategy has the potential to significantly reduce unnecessary radiation exposure in children with TA while maintaining diagnostic accuracy.
