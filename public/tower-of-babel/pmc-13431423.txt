Knowledge, Practice, and Health System Challenges in the Management of Pre-Eclampsia: A Cross-Sectional Study of Midwives at Princess Christian Maternity Hospital
Authors: Amidu Gibril Torto, Dibor Alfred Chukwudubem, Joy B Johnson, Abdulai Kanu, Alhaji Alpha Kamara, John Bendu, Aminn Alhassan Conteh, Kadlene Graff, Adama Oury Toure, Sahr E Gbomor, Nuhu Bah, Tamba Buffa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPre-eclampsia (PE) is a pregnancy-related hypertensive disorder and the leading cause of worldwide maternal deaths with the greatest burden in sub-Saharan Africa. Pre-eclampsia is one of the leading causes of maternal mortality in Sierra Leone, where the rate is the highest globally, primarily due to hypertensive disorders. The purpose of this study was to gain insight into the level of knowledge and practice of managing PE among midwives working in a tertiary referral hospital (Princess Christian Maternity Hospital) in Freetown.MethodsA cross-sectional survey of 50 midwives was conducted using a structured, valid questionnaire. The data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests, with p<0.05, indicating a statistically significant association.ResultsThe majority of midwives (90%) were female and had obtained a midwifery diploma (62%). All of the midwives were able to link eclampsia with convulsions; however, there were significant gaps in their knowledge. Only 42% of midwives were able to identify the placenta as a cause of PE, 42% identified proteinuria as a sign of PE and only 10% recognized epigastric pain as a symptom of PE. Bachelor’s degree qualifications were significantly associated with a greater level of knowledge regarding PE (p=0.004). Although the vast majority of midwives (90%) recognized magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) as the first line treatment for seizures due to PE, 30% of the midwives reported that they had never given MgSO4 and that the level of their education was significantly associated with having given MgSO4 to a woman (p=0.012). Major barriers identified by the midwives included insufficient staffing (92%) and inadequate blood pressure monitoring equipment (16%).ConclusionThere are significant gaps in knowledge and experience regarding MgSO4 among midwives at PCMH and those with the highest level of education had a greater number of correct answers regarding the physiological aspects of managing PE. There is an urgent need for targeted in-service training and for systemic health system barriers to be addressed in order to improve maternal outcomes.

Introduction

Pre-eclampsia is a multisystem disorder unique to pregnancy, characterized by new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks’ gestation.1 It results from endothelial dysfunction and vasospasm, potentially leading to severe maternal and fetal complications. The onset of seizures in a woman with pre-eclampsia signifies eclampsia, a major cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality globally.2 Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are the second leading direct cause of maternal death, accounting for an estimated 14% of such deaths worldwide, with the greatest burden observed in low- and middle-income countries.2

Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate share of this burden, with an estimated 25,000 maternal deaths annually from eclampsia and pre-eclampsia.3 Sierra Leone reflects this crisis, recording one of the highest maternal mortality ratios globally at 857 deaths per 100,000 live births.4 Despite government initiatives like the 2010 Free Health Care Initiative, progress in maternal health has been slow.5 Effective management of pre-eclampsia/eclampsia is frequently hampered by delayed diagnosis, unavailability of magnesium sulfate, lack of adequate monitoring equipment, and chronic shortages of trained personnel.6

Midwives are the backbone of maternity care in Sierra Leone, often serving as the first point of contact for pregnant women. They are trained and regulated by the Nursing and Midwifery Board of Sierra Leone. Their knowledge and practices in the detection and management of pre-eclampsia have a direct impact on maternal outcomes. However, limited published information exists detailing the competencies of midwives in managing this condition in the Sierra Leonean context.7,8 This study therefore sought to evaluate the knowledge and practices of midwives at Princess Christian Maternity Hospital (PCMH) in managing pre-eclampsia, with the aim of identifying gaps to inform targeted interventions for improving maternal care.

Methods

Study Design and Duration

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted at PCMH from October to November 2023.

Study Setting

The research was carried out at Princess Christian Maternity Hospital (PCMH) in Freetown. PCMH is the main specialist maternity hospital and a tertiary referral center for Sierra Leone, managing high-risk pregnancies from across the country. It has a bed capacity of 168 and admits approximately 850 patients’ monthly.4 Hypertensive disorders, including pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, are a leading cause of maternal admissions and mortality at this facility.

Sample Size and Sampling Method

A purposive non-probability sampling technique was used to enroll all available registered and enrolled midwives working at PCMH during the study period, yielding a sample of 50 participants.

Data Collection Instrument

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was developed by the author team based on a review of relevant literature and World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines on pre-eclampsia management. It consisted of three sections: (A) demographic information, (B) knowledge of pre-eclampsia, and (C) clinical practices. Content validity was established through review by a panel of three senior obstetricians. The questionnaire was then pilot-tested among 10 midwives at a different maternity unit to assess face validity and clarity. Reliability testing yielded good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: knowledge items α = 0.82, practice items α = 0.79, overall α = 0.81).

Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28). Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to summarize the data. The Chi-square test (χ2) was utilized to compare categorical variables, specifically to examine the association between professional qualification levels (certificate, diploma, bachelors) and (a) comprehensive knowledge of pre-eclampsia, and (b) experience in administering MgSO4. Fisher’s Exact Test was used when more than 20% of expected cell counts were less than 5. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The evaluation of midwives’ pre-eclampsia and eclampsia management practices and knowledge, through analyzing the baseline data collected during the PCMH’s pilot study in Sierra Leone, identified large deficiencies in both the theoretical and practical knowledge of midwives and that their qualification level, along with the systemic health-related issues within the country, significantly impacted their management of pre-eclampsia and eclampsia. While eclampsia was correctly identified by all midwives based upon its convulsive feature, which is similar to Ethiopian midwives,9 the understanding of the underlying causes of eclampsia, based on their pathophysiology, was limited. The majority of midwives (42%) identified placental dysfunction as the main cause of eclampsia, and there was poor recognition of many eclampsia symptoms, especially epigastric pain (10%). In contrast, more than 50% of midwives in Ethiopia recognized epigastric pain as an important early symptom of eclampsia,9 which indicates a significant deficiency because if midwives do not recognize this prodromal symptom of severe disease, then women may progress to eclampsia. A significant positive correlation was found between a midwife’s level of education (Bachelor’s) and their knowledge score (p=0.004), which indicates that greater levels of education lead to greater knowledge of pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, similar to findings from Uganda.10

Using magnesium sulfate is one area where there is a significant practice gap. While an overwhelming majority (90%) could identify it as an appropriate medication, nearly 1/3 of them had never given it. This “Know - Do” Gap is very concerning and likely results from multiple factors. It appears that qualification level has a significant relationship with experience in administering magnesium sulfate (p=0.012); all bachelor’s degree midwives said they had given magnesium sulfate as opposed to fewer than ½ of the certificate midwives, suggesting that lower cadre (certificate) staff were either excluded from this vital task or lacked confidence. Additionally, 32% of the midwives surveyed reported inconsistent availability of magnesium sulfate, indicating a failure of the supply chain, which is commonly found in low-income countries11,12, that fails to provide midwives with the opportunity to develop and maintain competency. The reliance on personal experience (18%) rather than on nationally recognized standardized guidelines/protocols (WHO Guidelines 200) demonstrates that there is a lack of compliance with SOPs and the need for ongoing protocol-based training/supervision13,14; that is also what differentiates developing countries from developed countries such as Sweden where the rate of adherence to the guidelines/protocols is nearly 100%.15

Barriers identified by midwives at the systems level were also quite clear; 92% of midwives surveyed reported that a lack of adequate staffing was a significant barrier to providing quality care. Additionally, it was reported that 16% of midwives who worked with the other respondents to administer magnesium sulfate claimed that they did not have adequate functional equipment to do so. Due to this environment, even skilled and knowledgeable midwives are unable to perform to their best ability. These results reflect results from the WHO studies that identified both human and material resource deficiencies as leading challenges in providing safe maternal care services in sub-Saharan Africa.1 System-level barriers were stark, with 92% of midwives citing staff shortages as a major impediment to quality care. This, coupled with inadequate functional equipment (16%), creates an environment where even knowledgeable and skilled midwives cannot perform optimally. These findings echo WHO reports identifying human and material resource deficiencies as principal challenges to safe maternal care in sub-Saharan Africa.16

This study had several limitations. Its single-center design at a tertiary hospital may limit the generalizability of findings to lower-level facilities. The sample size, while capturing most available midwives, was relatively small. The use of a self-reported questionnaire is subject to social desirability bias, potentially overestimating actual practice. Finally, while qualification was a key variable, we did not analyze the impact of years of clinical experience, which could be a confounder. Future research should explore this.

Conclusion

This study finds that while midwives in the Primary Care Maternal Health Program have some knowledge about pre-eclampsia, there are significant limitations to this knowledge, namely: gaps in the pathophysiological understanding of pre-eclampsia and atypical presentations of pre-eclampsia (such as epigastric pain), and minimal hands-on practice administering MgSO4. Individual provider level gaps are not uniform and are associated with the midwife’s level of training; midwives with certificate training are the least prepared. These provider level factors, as well as critical system level factors, such as chronic staffing shortages and unreliable access to drugs and equipment, contribute to poor maternal and fetal outcomes and increased rates of mortality due to eclampsia in Sierra Leone.

To significantly reduce maternal mortality due to eclampsia in Sierra Leone will take a two-pronged approach. First, targeted in-service training will need to be conducted so that pathophysiology knowledge is strengthened, all warning signs are universally recognized, and hands-on training can be completed in safe administration of MgSO4 through simulation drills. Second, health system capacity will need to be urgently improved so that reliable access to essential medications and functioning monitoring equipment is available, and addressing critical staffing shortages of skilled providers occurs. Only through a dual focus on both the competency of the provider as well as the capacity of the institution will sustainable improvement be realized.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

By systematically collecting and analyzing (quantitively) midwives’ competencies in a high burden, understudied context, this study fills an important gap in the literature. Use of a validated questionnaire increases the reliability of findings.

Limitations

As a single site study, results may not be generalizable to other sites (or hospital/healthcare systems) in Sierra Leone. Data collection was cross-sectional (one point in time), thus, it cannot establish changes over time in practice or knowledge (ie, does not measure validity of these variables). Self-reported knowledge/practice can be affected by social desirability bias. The study did not measure the influence of years of clinical experience on knowledge/practice (assuming this may have an impact).
