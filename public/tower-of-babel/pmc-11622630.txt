The Hidden Link between Chronic Kidney Disease and Lung Injury
Authors: Elizabeth F. Redente
Published: 2024

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), a progressive condition affecting millions globally, is characterized by the irreversible loss of kidney function. This results in a decreased ability to excrete phosphate, leading to hyperphosphatemia, a condition marked by systemically elevated phosphate concentrations in the blood (1). Hyperphosphatemia is associated with increased inflammation and cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases, including fibrotic interstitial lung diseases (2). Historically, however, the focus of CKD management has been on restoring kidney function and on the involvement of the cardiovascular system, often overlooking the involvement of the respiratory system (3, 4). Clinical studies in patients with CKD have increasingly reported higher incidences of restrictive lung diseases, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) (5, 6), indicating a need for further investigation into the kidney–lung axis. In this issue of the Journal, Bollenbecker and colleagues (pp. 659–676) hypothesize that CKD-driven hyperphosphatemia is sufficient to induce lung injury and fibrosis (7). Using a mouse model of CKD that uses an adenine-rich diet to simulate hyperphosphatemia, the authors observed that these mice developed inflammation and subsequent lung fibrosis, suggesting a direct link between high phosphate concentrations and lung injury. In addition, administration of a high-phosphate diet followed by bleomycin treatment exacerbated lung inflammation at Day 17 and fibrosis by Day 21, even in the absence of kidney damage (7). The authors show that a high concentration of phosphate itself is a critical mediator of lung injury, independent of other CKD-related factors, through activation of ERK 1/2 (extracellular signal related kinase 1/2) and PKB/AKT (protein kinase B) signaling (7). As a whole, their findings represent a paradigm shift in our understanding of the pulmonary effects of elevated systemic phosphate associated with CKD. By linking hyperphosphatemia to lung fibrosis through the MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinases)/AKT signaling pathway, the study not only broadens the scope of CKD research but also offers hope for new cross-organ therapeutic strategies.

The PI3K (phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase)/AKT signaling pathway is vital in regulating cell growth, motility, metabolism, and survival. Class I PI3Ks, including PI3Kγ, are involved in lung diseases, with PI3Kγ overexpression and increased activation seen in IPF tissue (8, 9). AKT1/2 activation by PI3K has been shown to play multiple roles in pulmonary fibrosis. AKT1-mediated mitophagy was required for fibrosis development by increasing the apoptosis resistance of alveolar macrophages (10), and AKT2-deficient mice were protected from bleomycin-induced fibrosis (11). Injured alveolar epithelial cells activate the PI3K/AKT pathway, releasing TGF-β and inducing fibroblast proliferation, differentiation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and activation of downstream pathways, including mTOR, HIF-1α, and FOX family proteins (12–16). Increased levels of PI3Kγ have been measured in IPF fibroblasts (16–18), and PI3Kγ-deficient mice are also protected from bleomycin-induced fibrosis (8). Evidence also suggests that overexpression of α-SMA (α-smooth muscle actin) in lung fibrosis is linked to PI3K/AKT activation (19).

Although increased MAPK signaling has been shown to play a key role in pulmonary fibrosis (20–23), a significant finding is that direct activation of MAPK signaling in the lung can be initiated and exacerbated by elevated serum phosphate concentrations. This also resulted in the activation of sodium-phosphate cotransporters and FGFR1 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 1). Primary human normal and IPF lung fibroblasts responded to increase phosphate with increased expression and production of IL1β, IL6, and CXCL8, as well as Acta2. Moreover, in vitro studies showed that pharmacological inhibition of MAPK/AKT, FGFR1, and sodium-phosphate cotransporters effectively prevented this proinflammatory response. The studies by Bollenbecker and colleagues also determined the in vivo response to excess phosphate using two preclinical models. In the first, they demonstrated that hyperphosphatemia in the context of CKD is sufficient to induce lung injury and increased proinflammatory cytokine production and fibrosis. In the second, they show that increased levels of phosphate alone (without kidney involvement) during bleomycin-induced lung injury were also sufficient to increase the proinflammatory profile and MAPK signaling in the lungs, resulting in an exacerbated fibrotic response disease (7). These data are supported by published studies using transgenic mice that overexpress TGF-α (tumor necrosis factor alpha) under the CCSP (club cell secretory protein promoter) and demonstrate that the activation of MEK/ERK is increased in mesenchymal cells during the development of spontaneous fibrosis (24). Therapeutic inhibition using the MEK inhibitor ARRY-142886 prevented fibrosis progression, lung cell proliferation, collagen increase, extra cellular matrix (ECM) gene production, and lung function decline (24). A recent publication by Fortier and colleagues found that MKP1 (MAPK phosphatase 1), which regulates MAPK activity, promoted myofibroblast dedifferentiation and restored apoptosis sensitivity by dephosphorylating p38α MAPK. Lineage-specific deletion of MKP1 in Col1a2+ fibroblasts after the peak of bleomycin-induced fibrosis prevented spontaneous resolution from occurring. Normal homeostatic fibrosis resolution was restored after treatment with the p38α inhibitor VX-702 (25). This suggests that MKP1 plays an antifibrotic role by inhibition of p38α during fibrosis resolution.

Together, these studies convey the importance of the MAPK and AKT pathways in lung fibrosis development and resolution. Based on the significance of MAPK signaling in the pathogenesis of IPF, clinical trials have begun to reposition PI3K/AKT inhibitors originally used for cancer treatment for the treatment of IPF (16), although current drugs are still in phase I trials (26). The authors have now shown that additional systemic factors, like hyperphosphatemia, can exacerbate lung fibrosis via these pathways. Although this study offers significant insights, it also opens up several avenues for further research. For instance, the exact mechanisms by which phosphate interacts with FGFs and downstream pathways in fibroblasts and other lung cells [for example transitional CCSP+KRT8+ (keratin 8) epithelial cells, endothelial cells, or macrophages] will need to be elucidated. Targeting these signaling molecules offers potential therapeutic avenues, whether by inhibiting MEK, enhancing MKP1 activity, or controlling phosphate levels to prevent CKD-related lung injury. However, without a targeted approach, care should be taken because of the multifaceted nature and broad actions of the MAPK pathway in pulmonary fibrosis (4, 7, 16, 24, 25). Further confirmatory studies are also needed to validate these findings and assess the efficacy of phosphate-lowering therapies in clinical settings. Long-term studies involving patients with CKD with varying degrees of lung disease could help in developing targeted treatment protocols that can be integrated into standard care practices, while also providing novel insights into treatments for patients with interstitial lung diseases.
