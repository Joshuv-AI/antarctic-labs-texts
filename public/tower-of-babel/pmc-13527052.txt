Assessing Suicidal Ideation in a Community of Bangladesh: Validation of a Suicidal Ideation Scale
Authors: Md Omar Faruk, Md Abdul Awal, Kamal Uddin Ahmed Chowdhury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Surpassing the global and Bangladesh averages, Jhenaidah, a southwestern region in Bangladesh, remains one of the most prone districts for suicidal ideation and suicide rates. However, despite the higher prevalence, there is no psychometric measure available to assess the degree of suicidal ideation in this region. The present study aimed to validate the Bangla version of the Suicidal Ideation Scale (B-SIS) in Jhenaidah.Methods:We recruited 300 participants (male 50.87% and female 45.13%) via purposive sampling from six sub-districts, with an overall mean age of 23.26 (SD = 2.96). We used the B-SIS, the World Health Organisation-5 Well-being Index and the Symptom Rating Scale.Results:The scale yielded a one-factor structure with 55.58% of the total variance supported by multiple indices in confirmatory factor analysis (RMSEA = 0.061, CFI = 0.981, TLI = 0.971, SRMR = 0.035). The intraclass correlation coefficient (two-way random-effects model, absolute agreement) for average measures was 0.90 (95% CI, [0.84, 0.95]; F[29, 551] = 10.11; p < .001), indicating high score stability across administrations. The scale also demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, reliability and construct validity.Conclusions:The psychometric properties suggest that the B-SIS is a suitable measure to assess suicidal ideation in Jhenaidah. The study is expected to advance scholarly work in suicidology by taking Jhenaidah into account.Keywords: Assessment, Bangladesh, community, suicide ideation, validation

The World Health Organisation’s (WHO) estimates indicate that every year more than 700,000 people die by suicide and approximately 77% of global suicides take place in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). 1 Suicide has been identified as the fourth leading cause of death among 15–29-year-olds. 1 Estimates also indicate that the suicide attempt rate is approximately 10–15 times higher than the suicide rate, 2 with evidence suggesting that the incidence of suicide attempts is highest in people aged 18–34 years of age. 3 Data also suggest that the rates of suicide attempts are seen as higher among females than their male counterparts, 4 with other contradictory estimates suggesting the rates are two to three times higher in males than in females. 3 Despite the recent data demonstrating that the global rates of suicide have decreased, suicide remains a major topic of discussion, consequently drawing widespread attention in research as well as public awareness over the past decade.5,6 Suicide refers to a deliberate behaviour, self-injurious in nature, with the intention of ending one’s life,7,8 whereas suicidal ideation is defined as a thought about ending a life with or without a clear plan. 3 Incidents of suicide follow an unprecedented level of individual and collective tragedy with the potential to affect family, friends, healthcare professionals and broader communities.9,10 Considering the gravity, suicide and suicidal attempts have been counted as an important public health problem worldwide,1,3 including Bangladesh, one of the LMICs in the South Asian region. 11

Bangladesh reports that every year, more than 10,000 people die by suicide, and the average rate of suicide ranges from 7.3 to 14 per 100,000 population.11–13 The rate of suicide has been observed disproportionately in rural and urban areas, with a 17-fold higher suicide rate in rural areas.13,14 Despite being a serious public health concern, incidents of suicide remain largely underreported due to the socio-religious structure of Bangladesh, a country where suicidal acts are considered criminal offences.11,15 Besides, Bangladesh lacks a systematic and comprehensive suicide surveillance system.15–17 Data related to suicide are primarily collected from published reports in the media, which are often found to be inconsistent and contradictory.16,18,19 Despite growing evidence suggesting higher rates of suicide, including suicide ideation, in rural areas,13,14 currently, there is no psychometric measure available to assess suicidal ideation in rural areas. We take Jhenaidah as an example—a southwestern administrative district of Bangladesh that reports a staggering number of suicide related incidents in Bangladesh. A study conducted in 2020 showed that approximately 23,000 suicide attempts were observed, whereas the number of suicide deaths was 3,152 between 2010 and 2018. 20 The study also reported that the rate of suicide attempts and suicide rate was 136.35 and 20.6 per 100,000, respectively. Jhenaidah Sadar (sub-district) topped the list on the incidence of suicide attempts (38.09%) and suicide deaths (33.47%) in comparison to other regions. 20 The higher prevalence of suicide in Jhenaidah 21 led to the inception of this study, where the Suicidal Ideation Scale (SIS) could serve as a means to assess suicidal ideation amid the dearth of measures validated in this context. Many studies investigating suicidal ideation among different target populations used a set of questions instead of validated measures.22–25 It has been recommended that measures used to study a specific population should be validated to avoid incorrect interpretation of the findings. 26

The Rationale for the Study

Despite having measures available in Bangla related to suicidal ideation and severity of suicidality,27,28 we chose to validate the SIS for several reasons. First, the SIS was believed to be very useful for minimising this difficulty, as it requires only a low reading and literacy level. 29 Second, existing scales may not be suitable for a particular population or context. 26 For example, a scale validated for an age group (the Bangla version of the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation [BSS] was validated for participants aged 13–23 years) may not be appropriate for individuals with specific cultural backgrounds (i.e., Jhenaidah). In addition, the BSS includes items (i.e., 5, 10, 11 and 19) that were not significantly associated with the scale’s total score. 30 Furthermore, Items 11 and 13 were shown to be psychometrically problematic, 31 with Item 11 demonstrating misrepresentation of true beliefs. 32 In addition, the Bangla version of the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale Lifetime/Recent–Clinical version 27 assesses multiple dimensions (e.g., severity, intensity and behaviour). It requires trained clinical administration, which often impedes immediate understanding of severity determination regarding suicidal ideation, consequently delaying accessibility to mental health services.

Given the issues embedded in the BSS and the need to develop a new scale tailored to the target population or context to enhance measurement accuracy, the SIS was chosen for the present study. Besides, the new scale may be able to capture a different or more nuanced aspect of the construct of interest that existing scales do not adequately address. This can contribute to conceptual advancement in the field. Finally, the items and the rating system in the SIS are easy to understand and respond to in both clinical and research applications.33,34 Being shorter and less time-consuming than other scales, such as the BSS. 35 The SIS does not require an expert or trained person.35,36 Therefore, the present study aimed to validate the Bangla version of the Suicidal Ideation Scale (B-SIS) in the context of Jhenaidah with the following two objectives:

To examine the factor structure of the B-SIS and evaluate its model fit using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

Methods

Study Design and Sample

With an estimated area of 1949.62 km2, Jhenaidah is one of the 64 districts in Bangladesh, having six sub-districts. Jhenaidah is predominantly a Muslim-majority region with numerous prominent religious structures (e.g., mosques). The majority of people live in rural areas and resort to agriculture as their primary profession. 37 As the district contains prominent rivers in southwestern Bangladesh, it is one of the agriculturally dependent districts. Like many rural districts, Jhenaidah faces challenges, including limited access to health care, particularly mental health care, limited infrastructure development and limited employment opportunities outside agriculture. The district is prone to natural disasters; therefore, governmental and non-governmental organisations run activities related to climate and livelihoods aimed at poverty alleviation, notably by providing microcredit loans. Despite adhering to broader Bangladeshi cultural and linguistic norms, Jhenaidah has its own regional variations and nuances (e.g., different dialects, local festivities and Baul music, a type of folk music). As noted earlier, Jhenaidah has long been plagued by higher rates of suicide in Bangladesh. 21

We recruited participants from six sub-districts of Jhenaidah using a three-stage sampling technique. In the first stage, we randomly selected six unions, each representing one sub-district. The second stage involved randomly selecting six villages from these six unions. Finally, participants were purposively selected from six villages with equal representation of males and females. The inclusion criteria were residents of randomly selected villages who were ≥18 years of age. No screening instruments for stress, anxiety or well-being were used during participant selection. Participants were excluded only based on predefined exclusion criteria, including major psychiatric conditions. Therefore, no participants were excluded based on stress, anxiety or well-being scores, minimising the risk of selection bias related to these constructs. A total of 300 participants aged 18–45, with an overall mean age of 23.48 and SD = 3.58 (male: 50.87%; mean age = 24.04; SD = 2.684; female: 49.13%; mean age = 22.44; SD = 3.028), were selected based on the inclusion criteria. Participants were not provided with a non-binary or ‘other’ gender option, nor with an option for ‘not religious or none’ for religion. In a rural context of Bangladesh, such omissions might be attributed to cultural norms and societal attitudes, in which non-binary genders or non-religious affiliations may not be widely recognised or accepted. Therefore, the survey design was influenced by traditional gender and religious classifications prevalent in the region.

We aimed to assess the factor structure of the scale and evidence suggested that for factor analysis, a sample size of 200 is fair and 50 participants per factor is considered adequate.38,39 Other researchers suggested that the sample-to-item ratio should not be less than 5:140,41 while a 20:1 ratio has also been suggested. 42 Validation studies of the SIS conducted in other contexts33,36 demonstrated a two-factor structure; therefore, we hypothesised that the present study would require 200 participants. We, however, recruited 300 participants to ensure more participation from the region. Of those recruited, the number of suicides within and outside of the family (having a distant relationship) was found to be 59 (20.41%) and 116 (40.14%), respectively. Tools

The SIS

Originally developed by Rudd (1989) on a sample of university students, the SIS was later tested on a large military clinical sample. 33 The construct underlying the scale is suicidal ideation, conceptualised as thoughts, wishes or plans related to ending one’s life, which is widely recognised as a key indicator within the continuum of suicide risk. The B-SIS is based on a reflective measurement model, in which individual items represent observable manifestations of the underlying latent construct of suicidal ideation.

Designed to provide information about the presence or absence of suicidal thoughts, the frequency of thoughts and the history of previous suicide attempts, the 10-item SIS is an assessment and a screening scale. The scale assesses feelings and behaviours over the past year using a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (never or none of the time) to 5 (always or a great many times). The total score ranges from 10 to 50 and higher scores represent greater suicidal ideation, with a total score of ≥15 considered serious suicidal ideation. The SIS demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.86). 34 The later study by Luxton et al. (2011) also demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.91) for the overall SIS.

The Bangla validated version 43 was used in the present study. The scale showed acceptable psychometric properties (α = 0.80, test–retest reliability = 0.89) and acceptable convergent and divergent validity. The SIS was translated into many languages35,36,44 and was used in a variety of target populations33,45–49 across the world, including gender and sexually diverse people in Bangladesh. 43

The WHO-5 Well-being Index

The Bangla version of the WHO-5 Well-being Index 50 was used to assess the divergent validity of SIS. The Bangla version of the scale measures the levels of well-being with a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (at no time) to 5 (all of the time). The score ranges from 0 to 25, with higher scores indicating greater well-being. The Bangla version of the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.75), test–retest reliability (0.71), divergent validity (−0.44) and convergent validity (0.54). Cronbach’s α for the present study was found to be acceptable, with α = 0.8351–53 and inter-item correlation ranging from 0.30 to 0.78. In addition, for the current study, the unidimensional factor structure demonstrated factor loadings ranging from 0.50 to 0.87, with Item 5 yielding the lowest factor loading value (0.50). The WHO-5 Well-being Index has been used as a screening tool for depression as well as an outcome measure across a wide range of study fields. 54

The SRQ

The Symptom Rating Scale (SRQ) is a 20-item scale developed in 1994 55 and has also been validated in Bangladesh. 56 In a yes/no response format, the scale uses ≥8 to screen for mental health symptoms. The scale reported acceptable sensitivity (81%–90%) and specificity (58%–95.2%) with a view to screening people with and without psychological morbidity across the world, including Italy, Brazil, Nicaragua and Kenya. 55 The Kuder– Richardson Formula 20 (KR 20) for the scale was found to be acceptable in the current study, with a KR 20 value of 0.85.57,58

Procedure

We aimed to collect data from all six sub-districts of Jhenaidah. The six sub-districts contain a total of 67 unions with 1,136 villages. We utilised a list of all unions within each of the six sub-districts of Jhenaidah. We obtained the list of unions and villages from a local organisation [blinded for peer review] tasked with recording the fatal and non-fatal suicide attempts in the district. It should be noted that the organisation [blinded for peer review] has been working relentlessly since its inception in 1999, liaising with government and non-governmental stakeholders. 20 We randomly selected six unions via lottery, each representing a sub-district. Before the draw, we assigned each union a number from 1 to 67. We continued the process until six unique unions were selected. Of these 67 unions, six villages were selected by simple random sampling. Each village was assigned a unique identification number starting from 1 to 1,136. We used an online random number generator to select six unique numbers within the range. The villages corresponding to the six numbers were those selected for participant recruitment. The third stage involved recruiting participants through purposive sampling. We utilised a local health workers network, teachers and community members (e.g., local government representatives) identified by the organisation [blinded for peer review]. Six experienced volunteers from the organisation [blinded for peer review], who had extensive experience in gathering data on both fatal and non-fatal suicide attempts in this district, conducted the data collection via door-to-door visits. However, the research team arranged training before data collection. The training was designed to provide comprehensive guidance to ensure ethical, effective and sensitive data collection. The contents of the training included suicide awareness and understanding (e.g., overview of suicide, prevalence, risk factors and warning signs), ethical guidelines (e.g., inform consent, confidentiality and right to withdraw), communication skills (e.g., empathetic, non-judgemental and active listening sensitively and respectfully), safety protocols (e.g., we provided participants with a list of referral directory solely dedicated to preventing suicide with contact details), cognitive interviewing, cultural sensitivity (e.g., adapting communication and approaches to respect diverse cultural backgrounds), data security and self-care. Each volunteer was randomly assigned to a village and they approached potential participants for a face-to-face interview after obtaining verbal and written consent, with clear and comprehensive information about the study. The volunteers approached both male and female residents of households who met specific inclusion criteria, including being >18 years old and residing in the respective village. Only one response was collected from each household. To ensure participants covered every possible score point from 10 to 50 on the scale, we selected participants from other settings, such as healthcare facilities (e.g., upazila health complex) and educational institutions (e.g., college), within the respective villages. However, not all villages had access to these facilities. Therefore, data collection was limited to households in villages without healthcare facilities or colleges. In addition, we also recruited participants from diverse demographic backgrounds (e.g., age, socioeconomic status and clinical characteristics such as exposure to suicide related death). This diversity was thought to capture the variability in suicidal ideation across the population.

At the outset of the questionnaire, there were items designed to assess cognitive impairment through functional assessments. Questions included activities of daily living (e.g., maintaining personal hygiene) and instrumental activities of daily living (e.g., managing finances and transportation). Volunteers also considered communication skills and language during initial interactions with the participants as additional criteria for determining cognitive impairments.

To evaluate test–retest reliability over a 3-week interval, we collected 30 phone numbers to reach out to participants for follow-up data collection. We fully briefed the participants on why their phone numbers were being collected and secured their consent both verbally and in writing. To ensure confidentiality, phone numbers were stored in an encrypted document within a protected OneDrive directory, where they were assigned to participant IDs. This measure was taken to prevent any disclosure of contact details within the dataset. Moreover, only the research team members directly involved in the study were granted access to this contact information, ensuring stringent control over its use.

The scale was administered to a sample of 15 participants to assess the comprehensibility of each item and difficulties in responding to items as part of a pilot study before the final data collection. We found no items that posed difficulties regarding comprehensibility or understandability. Data were collected from 11 May to 28 May 2023.

Ethical Considerations

The study was part of a pilot aimed at testing the effectiveness of a community-based suicide prevention training programme in Jhenaidah. The intervention study and the current study were approved by the Ethical Review Committee Approval Ref. No.: IR230501. Written informed consent was obtained from participants. The consent form clearly outlined the advantages and potential risks involved. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point. Participation was completely voluntary; therefore, we provided no compensation of any kind to the participants. No participants declined to participate in the study. Participants were provided with a list of referral directories that include contact details of mental health service agencies across the country.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using PASW Statistics version 25 59 and AMOS version 22. 60 We checked multivariate outliers with Mahalanobis distance (MD) (p < .001). 61 A maximum MD greater than the critical chi-square value for df = 7 with a critical α value of 0.001 suggests the existence of possible multivariate outliers. We removed 11 data points because they exceeded the suggested MD value (24.32) for a study containing seven variables. 62 Therefore, we conducted analyses on 289 data points, removing 11 cases.

We used the maximum likelihood (ML) method to analyse the variance-covariance matrices and conduct a CFA of the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) solutions. Several indices were used to assess model fit in the CFA. The chi-square statistic (χ2) was used to assess overall model fit. In addition, the ratio of chi-square to df (χ2/df), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI) and the standardised root mean square residual (SRMR) were considered to determine the adequacy of model fit. We used χ2 with p ≥ .01, χ2/df ≤ 2, RMSEA ≤ .06, CFI ≥ .95, TLI ≥ .95, GFI ≥ .93 and SRMR ≤ .08 as criteria for model fit.63–65

We used Cronbach’s alpha for assessing internal consistency reliability, with an α value of >0.70 as the criterion for evaluating internal consistency. 66 The test–retest reliability was assessed with Pearson correlation, with r > 0.50 as the strength of the correlation. 67 The construct validity of SIS was assessed through divergent and convergent validity. Data suggest that the majority of suicides, including suicidal ideation, are associated with psychological problems and the rate of suicide has been estimated to be 5%–8% for mental health problems such as depression, among others.68–70 We used the Symptoms Rating Scale as a convergent measure, suggesting that suicidal ideation and perceived mental health symptoms would be positively correlated. Moreover, we also hypothesised that the SIS would differentiate people with and without mental health problems. In addition, evidence also suggested that well-being has a negative relationship with suicidal ideation.71,72 In line with existing evidence, we hypothesised that the SIS would show a negative correlation with the WHO-5 Well-being Index. In other words, the SIS would also discriminate against people with higher and lower levels of well-being.

Results

Exploratory Factor Analysis

The EFA was performed using ML. Unlike studies that yielded a two-factor structure,33,44 the present study demonstrated a one-factor structure. The one-factor structure was also evident in another validation study conducted in Malaysia. 35 No rotation method was used as the scale demonstrated a single factor. The suitability of factor analysis was determined using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. KMO and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were found to be 0.903 and χ2 = 1656.252 (p < .001), respectively. With an eigenvalue of one, the EFA yielded one factor explaining 55.58% of the total variance. The KMO measure of sampling adequacy was found acceptable according to the rule of thumb. 73 All items except for Item 4, with a factor loading of 0.55, demonstrated a factor loading of >0.60 (ranging from 0.65 to 0.85). Evidence suggested that a factor loading <0.32 is recommended for removal. 61 The decision to retain Item 4 was based on the criteria suggested by Tabachnick et al. (2013). Communalities extraction values ranged from 0.30 to 0.72. Item 4, with the extraction value of 0.30, was considered acceptable, as evidence suggested that an item having an extraction score of <0.20 is generally recommended for removal. 74 The factor loadings and communality extraction values for each item are presented in Table 2.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

The one-factor structure was tested in CFA. The indices indicated poor model fit. However, the model showed acceptable fit indices after correlating the error terms of Items 1 and 2, 5 and 6 and 6 and 7. Correlating error terms can improve the model’s fit to the data and account for shared variance not explained by the specified latent factor. 75 It is important to acknowledge that correlated error terms can also lead to overfitting. Therefore, the justification for correlating error terms in CFA should be based on theoretical, empirical or practical reasons, particularly for a suicide ideation scale. Suicidal ideation is a complex and multifaceted construct that a single latent factor may not fully capture. Numerous dimensions, such as passive and active ideation, frequency, intensity and planning, may be involved and play a crucial role. Additionally, measurement error or unique sources of variance in individual items can contribute to the complexity. By allowing correlated error terms among conceptually related items or those sharing a common source of error, it is possible to better account for this complexity and improve the model’s fit to the data. Error terms of Items 1 and 2 were correlated because the Bangla translation of these items indicates behavioural actions for suicide (thinking of ways for suicide, which indicates a covert action, whereas the other item demonstrates an overt action that the person has already informed others about the intention). Items 5, 6 and 7 revolve around how life was generally perceived. Therefore, the error terms for these items were correlated, resulting in a better fit of the model. Internal Consistency Reliability

Internal consistency reliability was assessed through Cronbach’s alpha. The Cronbach’s alpha for the B-SIS was found to be 0.90. The mean for the B-SIS was 14.42 (SD = 5.120). We calculated composite reliability (CR) by dividing the sum of squares of factor loadings by the sum of the error variances. The CR of the 10-item scale was 0.95, indicating high internal consistency and reliability. This value is well above the commonly accepted threshold of 0.70, suggesting that the items on the scale effectively measure the same underlying construct.

Test–Retest Reliability

The scale demonstrated a strong correlation between the scores from the two administrations separated by 3 weeks. Pearson’s r correlation was used to assess test–retest reliability, yielding r = 0.74 (p < .01), consistent with other SISs. 76 Values ranging between 0.40 and 0.75 are considered good, while values above 0.75 are generally excellent.77,78 The reliability of the test–retest scores was also assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The analysis revealed an average ICC of 0.90, with a 95% confidence interval ranging from 0.841 to 0.946, F(29, 551) = 10.110, p < .001. This indicates a high level of consistency between the test and retest scores.

Construct Validity

We assessed construct validity (including convergent, divergent and known-groups validity), internal consistency reliability and test–retest reliability. Concurrent validity was partially supported through significant correlations between the B-SIS and theoretically related measures (SRQ and WHO-5) administered during the same assessment period. Predictive validity was not examined because the study did not include longitudinal follow-up data. 79 The correlation coefficient between the B-SIS and total score of SRQ is statistically significant at the 0.01 level (two-tailed), with a bootstrapped correlation coefficient of approximately 0.60. The 95% confidence interval for this correlation coefficient ranges from approximately 0.455–0.642. On the other hand, results indicate that the correlation coefficient between the total scores of B-SIS and WHO-5 is also statistically significant at the 0.01 level (two-tailed), with a bootstrapped correlation coefficient of approximately −0.48. The 95% confidence interval for this correlation coefficient ranges from −0.560 to −0.407.

Independent-samples t tests on B-SIS scores showed that the scale could discriminate between people with and without mental health problems [t(287) = −7.981, p < .001] and between lower and higher levels of well-being [t(287) = −7.490, p < .001]. The discrimination was evident by a large effect size, calculated with Cohen’s d 67 (in both cases 0.94 with respect to the Symptoms Rating Scale and the WHO-5 Well-being Index) (see Table 4).

Discussion

Amid the lack of psychometric measurements in the face of a higher prevalence of suicide and suicide attempts in Jhenaidah, the present study was conceived to produce a measure that would enable clinicians and researchers to assess the degree of suicide ideation in the region.

The B-SIS scale demonstrated a one-factor structure, unlike other studies33,36,80 and studies conducted among different targeted populations. 43 However, other studies have also yielded a one-factor structure, 35 including the original study. 34 Potential factor structure differences in the cross-cultural validation of a SIS can be discussed in light of several key considerations. Suicidal ideation can be influenced by cultural variabilities such as cultural factors, including norms, values and expressions of distress. Experience and expression of thoughts related to suicide may be different across cultures. As such, it is reasonable to expect that the factor structure of a scale assessing suicidal ideation may vary across cultures. Adapting a scale to a different cultural context requires translation and cultural adaptation. Differences in language and cultural nuances may lead to variations in item interpretation and response patterns, thereby yielding different factor structures. In addition, the characteristics of the study sample in each cultural context can influence the factor structure. For example, variations in demographics, mental health prevalence or cultural subgroups within the sample can lead to differences in the observed factor structure. It is imperative to note that SIS validation studies conducted on a clinical sample33,36 demonstrated two factors, while one-factor was found in studies conducted on a non-clinical sample. 35 The theoretical framework and conceptualisation of suicidal ideation may not be consistent across cultures. Future studies should explore potential reasons why the B-SIS demonstrated a one-factor structure in the context of Jhenaidah.

In the EFA, one item (i.e., Item 4) demonstrated a communality value of 0.30 and a factor loading value less than 0.60 (i.e., 0.55). However, the item passed the suggested values for factor loading and communality value.61,74 Unlike the present study, the item demonstrated comparatively higher loading values in earlier studies.33,36 However, the item also displayed the lowest factor loading in the CFA. 33 The item conveys a direct message about whether suicidal attempts have been made. This might seem threatening to a group of people or may lead to social desirability bias. This can lead to substantial variability in responses, thereby reducing factor loadings. In the current study, Item 4, including those with factor loadings <0.70 (Items 1, 3 and 5), was retained based on content validity, theoretical considerations, clinical relevance and assessment context. Item 1 is critically important to consider despite the lower factor loading, as this relates to the thinking of ways to kill oneself in a setting (i.e., Jhenaidah) where the common method (i.e., poisoning) of suicide attempt for males and females remains the same. 20 Therefore, the item covers a specific aspect of suicidal ideation in this region and across the world. 81 On the other hand, Items 3, 4 and 5 refer to the belief that life will end in suicide, previous attempts and feelings of worthlessness—all of which are significant risk factors of suicidal intent and behaviour.81,82 These factors may comprise the theoretical framework underpinning the scale, further necessitating the inclusion of these items to capture the construct of suicidal ideation fully. In addition, the items might be of clinical relevance as they essentially form the symptom trajectories or warning signs of suicidal ideation. With that in mind, we recommend that researchers scrutinise their psychometric properties across different contexts or populations.

The CFA model showed acceptable fit indices, except for χ2/df and RMSEA, which were slightly above the recommended cut-off values. χ2/df and RMSEA are sensitive to sample size and characteristics; 26 therefore, evidence of close fit with an RMSEA value less than 0.06 is sometimes a subjective judgment 60 and often produces incorrect decisions. 64 Other researchers suggested that an RMSEA value can be considered a fair fit if the value falls within the 0.05–0.08 range.64,83,84 A cross-cultural validation study of the SIS conducted in Korea also found that an RMSEA value of 0.08. 36 A value of χ2/df ranging from 2 to 1 or 3 to 1 indicates an acceptable fit 85 while some researchers suggest that the ratio <2 or as high as five can be considered a reasonable fit. 86 Studies also suggested that there is no single fit index that can be considered as a definitive marker of optimum model fit.64,87 Considering other fit indices, the overall B-SIS model provides sufficient evidence of model fit.

We used the same dataset to perform EFA and CFA. While the EFA and CFA are suggested to be performed in different datasets as a general and recommended practice, researchers also suggested that using different datasets in cross-cultural validation studies might result in a lack of correspondence between EFA and CFA. 88 Because without any prior assumptions, EFA explores potential factor solutions while following a hypothesis or theory, CFA tests the specific factor models, 89 implying the importance of correspondence between EFA and CFA. Using the same dataset suggests that the identified factor structure is based on the same data used in the CFA, allowing for consistency between the CFA model and the factor structure identified in the exploratory period. The use can also be justified on efficiency grounds, as the same dataset can save time and resources. Finally, CFA involves assessing the goodness of fit of the hypothesised model to the data; therefore, using the same dataset allows for a direct evaluation of how well the model fits the data, which is essential for model validation.

The psychometric indices, including internal consistency reliability, test–retest reliability and validity, were found to be acceptable. However, the correlation between well-being and suicidal ideation was found to be low and negative.90,91 The relationship between suicidal ideation and well-being is multifaceted and complex. Suicidal ideation is influenced by numerous factors, including mental health conditions, life stressors and social support, while well-being may vary over time and can fluctuate based on life events. Besides, the correlation between suicidal ideation and well-being may vary across cultures as well as contextual settings. Cultural norms, stigma and attitudes toward mental health can also influence this relationship. As a result, the correlation may be weaker due to the intricate nature of these relationships. Despite the low correlation, it may still have significant implications for suicide screening, mental health intervention and prevention efforts.

Based on the findings and their significance, the B-SIS can be considered a contextually valid and reliable measure to assess suicidal ideation in Jhenaidah. Acceptable psychometric properties, such as reliability and validity, as well as CFA fit indices, indicate that the B-SIS is suitable for people in Jhenaidah.

Strengths

The study is the first attempt to offer an SIS specific to the people of Jhenaidah in Bangladesh. The study will open opportunities for further research on suicidal ideation and related factors, including research aiming to prevent suicide in Jhenaidah. Integrating the SIS into routine healthcare systems in Jhenaidah may ensure that mental health assessment becomes a standard practice within the healthcare services. This enables early detection and intervention for individuals at risk, reducing the burden on mental health services in the long term. Data collected through the scale may inform the development of mental health policies and programmes at the regional and national levels. This can lead to improved allocation of resources and implementation of evidence-based strategies to prevent suicide and support mental well-being in Jhenaidah and beyond.

Limitations

We acknowledge that the complete randomisation of participants during recruitment could have informed a rigorous research method. Collecting data from purposely recruited participants might involve selection bias. Participants may not be fully representative of the broader population. This could mean that participants in the study might have different characteristics or experiences than those not included. Additionally, participants may feel social desirability bias, in which they might respond in a socially acceptable or expected way instead of expressing true feelings and/or experiences, especially on a sensitive topic such as suicidal ideation. Biases inherent to purposive sampling (e.g., social desirability bias and selection bias) may limit the generalisability of the findings. Therefore, we recommend that future studies employ random sampling to ensure the generalisability of the findings.

Employing questionnaires rather than standardised tests to determine cognitive impairment provides only indirect insights into cognitive functioning, as it typically relies on subjective assessments of cognitive capabilities. The present study could have strengthened its methodology by conducting comprehensive interviews with relatives and caregivers to gather data on the cognitive functioning of the selected participants. Subsequent research endeavours should incorporate standardised assessment tools to ensure precise evaluations of cognitive abilities.

The sample size of 289 participants may seem relatively small for a scale validation study and failure to do so may result in range restriction, with certain score ranges underrepresented or absent in the sample. This also reduces generalisability and may affect the validity of the results. Therefore, we recommend reassessing the scale’s validity using a large sample in future studies.

We also note that the Cronbach’s α of 0.80 for the Bangla version of the scale might seem lower than the >0.90 often aspired in clinical assessment, except for a few rating scales; 92 The Suicide Screening Questionnaire-Observer Rating-Factor 2 with α 0.75 93 and Revised Suicide Trigger Scale-Subscale 3 with α 0.75. 94 Potential reasons for a lower alpha might include cultural and linguistic nuances (e.g., subtle cultural differences in valuing particular expressions or concepts may not always be possible to capture the tool’s original intent), the nature of the population (e.g., demographic characteristics and small sample size) and cultural attitudes toward suicidal ideation (e.g., stigmatisation of suicide). It should be noted that the alpha of the original scale was 0.86. 34 To address the lower alpha, future research could focus on enhancing the scale’s reliability through methods such as focus group discussions or cognitive interviews to deepen understanding of item interpretations.

Conclusions

The current study evaluated the psychometric properties of the B-SIS among adults in Jhenaidah. Findings supported a unidimensional factor structure and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, excellent test–retest reliability and construct validity. These results indicate that the B-SIS is a reliable and valid measure for assessing suicidal ideation in this population. Given its briefness and ease of administration, the scale may be useful in both research and clinical settings to facilitate the assessment of suicidal ideation in Jhenaidah, Bangladesh.
