k k -Homogeneous Equiangular Tight Frames
Emily J. King

Abstract
Equiangular tight frames (ETFs) are optimal packings in projective space which also yield useful decompositions of signals. Paley ETFs are constructed using number theory. In this article, we present the doubly homogeneous automorphism groups of the Paley ETFs of prime order. We also prove some properties of doubly homogeneous frames, namely that they are always ETFs and that their short circuits—i.e., dependent subsets of minimum size—form a balanced incomplete block design. We additionally characterize all k k -homogeneous ETFs for 3 ≤ k ≤ 5 3\leq k\leq 5 . Finally, we revisit David Larson’s AMS Memoirs Frames, Bases, and Group Representations [ HL00 ] coauthored with Deguang Han and Wandering Vectors for Unitary Systems and Orthogonal Wavelets [ DL98 ] coauthored with Xingde Dai with a modern eye and focus on finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Keywords : equiangular tight frames, ETFs, k k -homogeneous, k k -transitive, Paley difference sets, t t -designs, matroid circuits

1.1
Equiangular Tight Frames

For any separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and any orthonormal basis
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j\in J}
, Parseval’s equality yields that for all
x\in\mathcal{H}

\left\|x\right\|^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}\left|\langle x,\varphi_{j}\rangle\right|^{2}.

What might be surprising is that there are collections of vectors
\Phi
which are not orthonormal bases which also satisfy that equality exactly (
Parseval frames
), in a scaled version (
tight frames
), or in a loosened version (
frames
). In infinite dimensions, even being a frame requires significant structure, and there is an immense amount of research about such vectors (see, e.g.,
[
Chr16
]
).

Definition 1.1
.

For a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j\in J}
in
\mathcal{H}
,
\Phi
is a
tight frame
if there exists
A>0
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{H}

A\left\|x\right\|^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}\left|\langle x,\varphi_{j}\rangle\right|^{2}.

A
is called the
frame bound
.

In finite dimensions, any spanning set is a frame, and one often asks for additional structure, like being an equiangular tight frame. We will use the notation
\mathbb{F}
to mean
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
throughout the paper.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{d\times n}
. We call
\Phi

equiangular
if there exist
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta
such that

\left|\langle\varphi_{j},\varphi_{k}\rangle\right|=(\beta-\alpha)\delta_{j,k}+\alpha.

If additionally,
\Phi
is a tight frame, then we call
\Phi
an
equiangular tight frame (ETF)
or a
(d,n)
-ETF
.

In
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, it is common to employ the abuse of notation using
\Phi
to both mean the frame itself and the short fat matrix with columns the frame vectors. With that notation,
\Phi
is a tight frame precisely when
\Phi\Phi^{\ast}=AI
.
Equiangular tight frames are optimally robust to noise and up to two erasures
[
GKK01
,
SH03
,
HP04
]
, are important in quantum information theory
[
Zau99
,
Zau11
]
, and provide optimal projective codes
[
JKM19
]
. As mathematical objects which interact with a variety of fields, there are a number of conjectures about their existence or the lack thereof: Zauner’s conjecture
[
Zau99
,
Zau11
]
, the Gillespie conjecture
[
Gil18
]
, the
(d,2d)
conjecture
[
FI23
,
IJM24
]
, and the Fickus-Jasper conjecture
[
FJ19
]
.

For many choices of
d
,
n
,
\mathbb{F}
, there does not exist an ETF. (See, e.g,
[
FM16
]
.) When
n=d
, the ETFs are simply equal-norm orthogonal bases. When
n=d+1
, they span lines which go through vertices of a regular simplex.

Definition 1.3
.

If
\Phi
is an ETF of
d+1
vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, we call it a
simplex ETF
. If further, the inner product of any two distinct vectors in a simplex ETF is negative (necessarily the same negative number since they are equiangular), then we call it a
regular simplex ETF
.

In a certain sense, every simplex ETF is equivalent to a regular simplex ETF. Let
\operatorname{U}(d)
denote the
d\times d
orthogonal matrices when
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
d\times d
unitary matrices when
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. Then there is always a
U\in\operatorname{U}(d)
and a choice of scalars
\eta_{j}\in\operatorname{U}(1)
such that

\left(U\varphi_{j}\eta_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{d+1}

have real entries and point to vertices of a regular
d
-simplex centered at the origin. In general, one may ask if two sets of vectors are fundamentally “the same”, i.e., span the same set of lines up to a global change of basis. Fortunately, there is a relatively simple test.

Definition 1.4
.

Assume that
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n},\Psi=(\psi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{d\times n}
. We say that the vectors are
switching equivalent
(or
projectively unitarily equivalent
or
isometrically isomorphic
) if there exists a

U\in\operatorname{U}(d)
and a choice of scalars
\eta_{j}\in\operatorname{U}(1)
such that

\psi_{j}=U\varphi_{j}\eta_{j},\quad j=1,2,\ldots,n.

The test for switching equivalence has been rediscovered in different forms over many years (e.g.,
[
Sei76
,
GP77
,
Bre90
,
AFF11
,
CW16
]
)

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n},\Psi=(\psi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{d\times n}
be ETFs. They are switching equivalent if and only if for
j\neq k\neq\ell\neq j

\left\langle\varphi_{j},\varphi_{k}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{k},\varphi_{\ell}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{\ell},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle\psi_{j},\psi_{k}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{k},\psi_{\ell}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{\ell},\psi_{j}\right\rangle.

We will use the notation
\TP(j,k,\ell)=\left\langle\varphi_{j},\varphi_{k}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{k},\varphi_{\ell}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{\ell},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle
, where
\Phi
will be clear from context.

For
d\neq n
, there exists a
(d,n)
-ETF over
\mathbb{F}
if and only if there exists a
(n-d,n)
-ETF over
\mathbb{F}
. Such frames are called
Naimark complements
of each other. Naimark complements of Parseval frames (not in general equiangular and allowing infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces) were first studied by Han and Larson
[
HL00
]
, where they were called
complementary frames
. It was later realized that these results followed from Naimark’s dilation theorem concerning the lifting of positive operator valued measures
[
Neu43
]
.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
\Phi
be a
(d,n)
-ETF over
\mathbb{F}
with frame bound
A
. Then any
\Psi\in\mathbb{F}^{(n-d)\times n}
that satisfies

\Psi^{\ast}\Psi=AI-\Phi^{\ast}\Phi

is an
(n-d,n)
-ETF over
\mathbb{F}
with frame bound
A
called a
Naimark complement
of
\Phi
.

Note that the inner products of distinct vectors of a Naimark complement are simply the negatives of the corresponding vectors in the first frame. Thus, by Proposition
1.5
, characterizing switching equivalence of an ETF is equivalent to solving the problem for Naimark complements. Hence, it suffices to study ETFs for which
n\geq 2d
.

Given the historical connection of frames to Fourier bases
[
DS52
]
, it is perhaps not surprising that Fourier-related concepts arise in constructions of various “nice” frames, like frames in
L^{2}(\mu)
for
\mu
a fractal measure (see, e.g.,
[
DHSW11
]
) or Gabor frames for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see, e.g.,
[
Grö01
]
). Discrete Fourier transform matrices may also be used to define “nice” frames in finite dimensions.

Definition 1.7
.

Consider the integers mod
m

\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and a primitive
m
th root of unity
\zeta
. The
Fourier matrix
of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
is the
m\times m
matrix with
(i,j)
-entry
\zeta^{ij}
, where indexing is
0,1,\ldots,m-1
. For
m_{1},\,m_{2},,\,\ldots,\,m_{k}\in\{2,\,3,\,\ldots\,\}
, the
Fourier matrix
of
\mathbb{Z}_{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{m_{k}}
is the Kronecker product of the Fourier matrices of the cyclic factors.

Note that this construction via Kronecker products yields a natural way to view the rows of the Fourier matrix as being indexed lexicographically by the elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{m_{k}}
. By the fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups, the character table of any finite abelian group is (up to possible permutation of entries) a Fourier matrix. Any
n\times n
Fourier matrix is a unitary matrix scaled by
\sqrt{n}
; thus, any
d
-row all-column (i.e.,
n
-column) submatrix
\Phi
of an
n\times n
Fourier matrix satisfies
\Phi\Phi^{\ast}=nI_{d}
. So, such submatrices always yield tight frames. We can completely characterize when such submatrices yield ETFs using combinatorial design theory (see, e.g.,
[
CD07
]
).

Definition 1.8
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group of size
v
written additively. A
(v,k,\lambda)
-difference set
is a subset
X\subseteq G
of size
k
such that the multiset of differences contains each non-identity element exactly
\lambda
times.

Example 1.9
.

Consider
\{1,2,4\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{7}
. Then the difference table is

\begin{array}[]{c||c|c|c}-&1&2&4\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr 1&0&1&3\\
\hline\cr 2&6&0&2\\
\hline\cr 4&4&5&0\end{array}

That is, the multiset of differences contains each non-identity element of
\mathbb{Z}_{7}
exactly once. Thus
\{1,2,4\}
is a
(7,3,1)
-difference set.

In any abelian group of size
v
, any subset of size
1
,
v-1
, or
v
trivially forms a difference set. The difference set in Example
1.9
is the smallest non-trivial difference set. In particular, not all abelian groups have a nontrivial difference set.
Examples of the construction of ETFs using difference sets appeared in
[
SH03
,
DF07
,
GR09
]
, while the following theorem which completely characterizes the construction may be found in
[
XZG05
]
.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group with Fourier matrix
F
. Let
\Phi
be a all-column submatrix of
F
, with rows indexed by
X
. Then
\Phi
is an ETF if and only if
X
is a difference set in
G
.

1.2
The Geometric Structure of ETFs

One way to further understand ETFs is by characterizing their geometric and combinatorial properties via their symmetries and linear dependencies. We introduce the former concept in this section and the latter in the next section.

Definition 1.11
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
be a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. We call
U\in\operatorname{U}(d)
a
vector automorphism of
\Phi
if there exists a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
such that
U\varphi_{j}=\varphi_{\sigma(j)}
for
j=1,\ldots n
. We call
U\in\operatorname{U}(d)
a
line automorphism of
\Phi
if there exists a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
such that
U\varphi_{j}\varphi_{j}^{\ast}U^{\ast}=\varphi_{\sigma(j)}\varphi_{\sigma(j)}^{\ast}
for
j=1,\ldots n
. The group of vector automorphisms is the
vector automorphism group
G
of
\Phi
and the quotient group of the line automorphisms modulo
\operatorname{U}(1)
is the
line automorphism group
[G]
of
\Phi
.

By abuse of notation, we will work with elements of
\operatorname{U}(d)
rather than
\operatorname{PU}(d)
when analyzing the line automorphisms. In particular,
\operatorname{U}(1)
plays no role in what permutation of the lines is induced.

Example 1.12
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group with
n\times n
Fourier matrix
F
. Let
\Phi
be a
d
-row, all-column submatrix of
F
, which is necessarily a tight frame. Further, define
M_{j}
for each frame index
j
as the
d\times d
diagonal matrix with diagonal entries the entries of
\varphi_{j}
. Then
\{M_{j}\}_{j}
is a group in
\operatorname{U}(d)
which is a representation of
G
. Also,
F
is the orbit of the all-ones vector
\mathbbm{1}
under
\{M_{j}\}_{j}
, where
M_{j}\mathbbm{1}=\varphi_{j}
.

We will often ask when the automorphism groups have extra structure.

Definition 1.13
.

An action of a group
G
on a set
X=\left\{\,x_{i}\,\,\middle|\,\,i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\,\right\}
is
k
-transitive
if for each ordered
k
-tuple with no repeated elements
(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}})
and each permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
, there exists a
g\in G
such that (as ordered tuples)

(g\cdot x_{i_{1}},\,g\cdot x_{i_{2}},\,\ldots,\,g\cdot x_{i_{k}})=(x_{\sigma(i_{1})},\,x_{\sigma(i_{2})},\,\ldots,\,x_{\sigma(i_{k})}).

The action is
k
-homogeneous
if for each set of
k
elements of
X
with no repeated elements
\{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}}\}
and each permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
, there exists a
g\in G
such that (as sets)

\{g\cdot x_{i_{1}},\,g\cdot x_{i_{2}},\,\ldots,\,g\cdot x_{i_{k}}\}=\{x_{\sigma(i_{1})},\,x_{\sigma(i_{2})},\,\ldots,\,x_{\sigma(i_{k})}\}.

A frame is
k
-transitive
(respectively,
k
-homogeneous
) if its line automorphism group is
k
-transitive (respectively,
k
-homogeneous).

Clearly,
k
-transitivity implies both
(k-1)
-transitivity and
k
-homogeneity. However,
k
-homogeneity does not nest. Consider a set of size
k
acted on by the trivial group. Then the group action is
k
-homogeneous but not
j
-homogeneous for any
1\leq j<k
.

We make a note of a result from
[
CW18
]
(cf. Proposition
1.5
) that we will leverage to prove a few results and which was also the basis of the code provided by Joey Iverson that was used to test some ideas.

Theorem 1.14
.

[
CW18
]

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
be an ETF. Then there is a
g\in G
inducing a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
of the vectors if and only if for all
j\neq k

\left\langle\varphi_{j},\varphi_{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\varphi_{\sigma(j)},\varphi_{\sigma(k)}\right\rangle.

Similarly, there is a
g\in[G]
inducing a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
of the lines if and only if for all
j\neq k\neq l\neq j

\TP(j,k,\ell)=\TP(\sigma(j),\sigma(k),\sigma(\ell)).

Since this result relies solely on the inner products, one can see that both the vector and the line automorphism groups of an ETF and a Naimark complement of an ETF are the same.

1.3
The Combinatorial Structure of ETFs

Understanding the linear dependencies of vectors in ETFs rose to prominence in the search for so-called deterministic restricted isometry property (RIP) measurements in compressed sensing (see, e.g.,
[
BFMW13
]
). However, it was realized that many ETFs have in some sense worst possible linear dependence properties
[
FMT12
]
.

Definition 1.15
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{d\times n}
. The
spark
(or
girth
) of
\Phi
,
\operatorname{spark}(\Phi)
, is the size of the smallest subset of linearly dependent vectors in
\Phi
.

“Spark” is the term from compressed sensing literature
[
DE03
]
, while “girth” is a more classical term from matroid theory (see, e.g.,
[
Oxl11
]
). RIP concerns subsets up to a certain size of columns of a matrix having condition number close to
1
; so, small subsets of columns being linearly dependent means that the matrix is far from being RIP.

Example 1.16
.

Consider an orthonormal basis
(u_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
for
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Then

•

\operatorname{spark}\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}&\ldots&u_{d}&\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}\end{pmatrix}=d+1
,

•

\operatorname{spark}\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}&\ldots&u_{d}&u_{1}\end{pmatrix}=2
, and

•

\operatorname{spark}\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}&\ldots&u_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}=1
,

where the singleton set consisting of the zero vector is counted as a linearly dependent set of size
1
, while the rank of each of the vector collections above is
d
.

Trivially, the largest that the spark of
n
vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
may be is
d+1
, which happens when any subset of
d
vectors is linear independent. When this happens, we say the vectors are
full spark
. Full spark frames (respectively, tight frames) of
n
vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
form an open dense set in the space of frames (respectively, tight frames) of
n
vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{d}

[
Cah09
,
ACM12
,
CMS17
]
. However, the same cannot be said when restricting to ETFs. Applying a now classical result from the compressed sensing literature
[
DE03
]
to ETFs, we have the following result.

Proposition 1.17
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
be a
(d,n)
-ETF. Then

\operatorname{spark}(\Phi)\geq 1+\sqrt{\frac{d(n-1)}{n-d}}.

The infinite class of ETFs constructed in
[
FMT12
]
all saturate this worst-case bound for spark. Inspired by this, the author of this paper and coauthors characterized all ETFs which saturate this bound
[
FJKM18
]
.

Theorem 1.18
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
be a
(d,n)
-ETF. Then

\operatorname{spark}(\Phi)=1+\sqrt{\frac{d(n-1)}{n-d}}

if and only if
\Phi
contains a subset of vectors which form a simplex ETF for their span.

This lead to the following definition
[
FJKM18
]
.

Definition 1.19
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
be an ETF. The
binder
of
\Phi
is the set of subsets of
\Phi
(or their corresponding indices) which form simplex ETFs for their span.

If the binder is non-empty, then each of the elements of the binder (which are subsets of
\Phi
) have the same size. In certain cases (e.g., Gabor Steiner frames
[
BK19
]
), the binder forms a type of combinatorial design call a BIBD. For more on combinatorial designs see, e.g.,
[
CD07
]
.

Definition 1.20
.

A
t
-
(v,k,\lambda)
-design
or
block design
is an ordered pair
(V,\mathcal{B})
, where
V
is a set of size
v
and
\mathcal{B}
is a collection of subsets of
V
called
blocks
such that each block
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
has the same size
k
and any subset of
t
distinct elements in
V
is contained in precisely
\lambda
blocks. When
t=2
, the design is called a
balanced incomplete block design (BIBD)
.

Note that
t
-designs are also
s
-designs for any
1\leq s\leq t
.

Example 1.21
.

Consider the following subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{7}
which result from shifting the difference set in Example
1.9
by each element of the group:

\left\{\{1,2,4\},\,\{2,3,5\},\,\{3,4,6\},\,\{0,4,5\},\,\{1,5,6\},\,\{0,2,6\},\,\{0,1,3\}\right\}.

Note that
\{1,2\}
is only in the first block,
\{1,3\}
only in the seventh,
\{1,4\}
only in the first, and so on. This is a
2
-
(7,3,1)
-design a.k.a. a
(7,3,1)
-BIBD.

The binder, however, of most ETFs is empty. This does not mean that the ETF is full spark, only that it does not have worst case spark. So, one may ask to study the properties of the smallest dependent sets. This leads us to introduce a new definition.

Definition 1.22
.

Let
\Phi
be an ETF with spark
s
. The
bender
1
1

1

“Bender” is in honor of the smack-talking robot on Futurama, a show which is filled with math jokes.
of
\Phi
is the set of subsets of
\Phi
(or their corresponding indices) which are size
s
and linearly dependent. The elements of the bender are called
short circuits
.

In matroid theory, minimally dependent subsets of vectors, i.e., those for which any proper subset is independent, are called
circuits
. The size of the smallest circuit(s) is the girth, i.e., the spark. Hence the name “short circuit.”

If
\Phi
has a non-trivial binder, then the bender is the binder and the short circuits are all simplex ETFs for their span. On the other extreme, if
\Phi
is full spark
d+1
, then the bender is the set of all subsets of size
d+1
of the vectors.

It was noted in
[
FL23
]
that the binder of an ETF with a doubly transitive line automorphism group is a BIBD. However, folklore in combinatorial design theory (see, e.g.,
[
CD07
, Remark 4.29]
) means a much more general result holds.

Proposition 1.23
.

Let
\Phi
be an ETF with
k
-homogeneous line automorphism group. Then the bender forms a
k
-design.

Proof.

Clearly, acting on a short circuit with an element of the line automorphism group results in a short circuit since unitaries do not change linear dependency. For any two different sets
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
of
k
frame vectors, there is a least one element of the line automorphism group mapping
X_{1}
to
X_{2}
, meaning that the size of the set of short circuits containing
X_{1}
is the same as the size of the set of short circuits containing
X_{2}
. Thus, the bender is a
k
-design.
∎

2
Paley ETFs

We will apply the tools from Sections
1.2
and
1.3
to combinatorially and geometrically characterize one infinite class of ETFs.

We begin by reviewing some basic facts about finite fields. If
q=p^{s}
for some prime
p
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the finite field of size
q
, then
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}=\mathbb{F}_{q}\backslash\{0\}
is a cyclic group under multiplication. We denote the quadratic residues and non-residues as

\displaystyle\operatorname{QR}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\displaystyle=\left\{\,a^{2}\,\,\middle|\,\,a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\,\right\}

\displaystyle\operatorname{NR}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\backslash\operatorname{QR}.

Thus,
\mathbb{F}_{q}=\operatorname{QR}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cup\operatorname{NR}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cup\{0\}
. When the underlying field is clear from context, we will often simply write
\operatorname{QR}
and
\operatorname{NR}
. Note that
\operatorname{QR}
is thus a subgroup of the multiplicative cyclic group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
of index
2
, meaning it has size
(q-1)/2
and is also cyclic. When
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
,
\operatorname{NR}=-\operatorname{QR}
; that is, the non-quadratic residues are precisely the negations of the quadratic residues. This fact implies the following (see, e.g.
[
CD07
]
).

Theorem 2.1
.

When
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
,
\operatorname{QR}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
forms a difference set in the additive group of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Example 2.2
.

We have that
\operatorname{QR}(\mathbb{F}_{7})=\{1,2,4\}
since in
\mathbb{F}_{7}

1^{1}=1,\quad 2^{2}=4,\quad 3^{2}=2,\quad 4^{2}=2,\quad 5^{2}=4,\quad 6^{2}=1,

i.e., precisely the difference set in
\mathbb{Z}_{7}
(i.e., the additive group of
\mathbb{F}_{7}
) in Example

.

Thus, it follows from Theorem
1.10
that if

,

is the Fourier transform of the additive group of

, and

must be an ETF, which we call a

. If

this corresponding ETF.

We note that some authors (see, e.g.,
[

) use the terminology “Paley ETF” to refer to

-ETFs which are also constructed leveraging number theory about quadratic residues.

Example 2.3
.

The smallest non-trival Paley ETF is

\Phi_{7}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\zeta&\zeta^{2}&\zeta^{3}&\zeta^{4}&\zeta^{5}&\zeta^{6}\\
1&\zeta^{2}&\zeta^{4}&\zeta^{6}&\zeta&\zeta^{3}&\zeta^{5}\\
1&\zeta^{4}&\zeta&\zeta^{5}&\zeta^{2}&\zeta^{6}&\zeta^{3}\end{pmatrix}

. Further, other than the

\Phi_{7}=\left\{\,M^{k}\mathbbm{1}\,\,\middle|\,\,k\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,6\}\,\right\},\quad\textrm{where}\quad M=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta^{2^{0}}&0&0\\
0&\zeta^{2^{1}}&0\\
0&0&\zeta^{2^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mathbbm{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\\

The properties seen in Example

generalize. First, we define two special operators.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
p>3
be a prime with

and
\zeta
be a primitive

be a generator of
\operatorname{QR}(\mathbb{F}_{p})

is the

M_{\{p\}}=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta^{a^{0}}&0&\ldots&0\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

For any

d\times d

(cyclic) translation matrix

For a given

and

Proposition 2.5
.

Let

be a prime with

and

be a primitive

be a generator of

\Phi_{p}=\left\{\,M_{\{p\}}^{k}\mathbbm{1}\,\,\middle|\,\,k\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,p-1\}\,\right\}.

Proof.

This trivially follows from Example

\operatorname{QR}(\mathbb{F}_{p})=\left\{\,a^{\ell}\,\,\middle|\,\,\ell\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,(p-3)/2\}\,\right\}.

∎

Note that, unlike in Example

, the ordering of rows indexed using the construction above are not necessarily the ordering as a subsequence of
(1,\,\ldots,\,p-1)
. Proposition
2.5
also implies that other than

(p-1)/2

In order to use Theorem

, we need to characterize the triple products of the Paley ETF vectors.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
p>3
be a prime with
p\equiv 3\bmod 4
and consider the Paley ETF
\Phi_{p}
. Further let
\zeta
be the primitive
p
th root of unity and
a
the generator of
\operatorname{QR}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
used to generate
\Phi_{p}
. Finally, set

\alpha=\sum_{\ell=0}^{(p-3)/2}\zeta^{a^{\ell}}.

Then

\TP(j,k,\ell)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\alpha^{3};&j-k,k-\ell,\ell-j\in\operatorname{QR}\\[2.0pt]
\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\alpha;&\textrm{ two of $j-k,k-\ell,\ell-j\in\operatorname{QR}$}\\[2.0pt]
\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\overline{\alpha};&\textrm{ one of $j-k,k-\ell,\ell-j\in\operatorname{QR}$}\\[2.0pt]
\overline{\alpha}^{3};&\ j-k,k-\ell,\ell-j\in\operatorname{NR}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

First, we note that

\left\langle\varphi_{j},\varphi_{k}\right\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{(p-3)/2}\zeta^{ja^{\ell}}\zeta^{-ka^{\ell}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{(p-3)/2}\zeta^{(j-k)a^{\ell}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}(p-1)/2&j=k\\
\alpha&j-k\in\operatorname{QR}\\
\overline{\alpha}&j-k\in\operatorname{NR}\end{array}\right.

since
(j-k)a^{\ell}\in\operatorname{QR}
if and only if
j-k\in\operatorname{QR}
and also
\operatorname{NR}=-\operatorname{QR}
. So, for distinct
j,k,\ell
, the desired result immediately follows.
∎

We prove one more lemma before getting to the main result.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
p>3
be a prime with
p\equiv 3\bmod 4
and consider the Paley ETF
\Phi_{p}
. Let
\alpha
be as in Lemma
2.6
. Define
\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}
to be any
(p-1)/2\times(p-1)/2
matrix consisting of the columns of
\Phi_{p}
corresponding to the quadratic residues. Similarly define
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}
for the non-quadratic residues. Then the row sums of
\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}
are
\alpha
,
\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}
is invertible, and the row sums of
\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}^{-1}
are
1/\alpha
. Similarly, the row sums of
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}
are
1/\overline{\alpha}
,
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}
is invertible, and the row sums of
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}^{-1}
are
1/\overline{\alpha}
.

Proof.

The fact that
\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}
and
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}
have the claimed row sums follows from the proof of Lemma
2.6
.
\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}
and
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}
are invertible due to a result by Chebotarëv about the invertibility of square submatrices of prime order Fourier transform matrices
[
SL96
]
. For the remaining claim, we note that for
n\times n

A=B^{-1}
where
B
has constant row sum
\beta
, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{i,j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{i,k}B_{k,j}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{i,k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{k,j}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{i,k}\beta=\beta\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{i,k},

as desired.
∎

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
p>3
be a prime with
p\equiv 3\bmod 4
. Define
M=M_{\{p\}}
and
T=T_{(p-1)/2}
. Then

\displaystyle\left\{\,M^{k}T^{\ell}\,\,\middle|\,\,k\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,p-1\},\,\,\ell\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,(p-3)/2\}\,\right\}

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{(p-1)/2}\cong\left\{\,f\in\operatorname{AGL}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\,\,\middle|\,\,f(x)=mx+b,\,\,m\in\operatorname{QR}(\mathbb{F}_{p}),\,\,b\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\,\right\}

is the automorphism group of both the vectors and the lines of the Paley ETF
\Phi_{p}
. The group acts double-homogeneously but not double-transitively on the vectors and the lines.

A note of warning for those accustomed to thinking of an affine group as a translation group semidirect product a scaling group, in particular those with a background in wavelet theory: The group of matrices
\{T^{\ell}\}
which translate the entries of the vectors act like dilations in the correspondence to
\operatorname{AGL}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
.

Proof.

First, we note that the order of
M
is
p
and the order of
T
is
(p-1)/2
. This also means that raising
M
to elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is well-defined when interpreted mod
p
. Further, for
a
the given generator of the quadratic residues used to define
M
,
k\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,p-1\}
, and
\ell\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,(p-3)/2\}
, we have

T^{\ell}M^{k}T^{-\ell}=M^{ka^{-\ell}}

Thus,

\left\{\,M^{k}T^{\ell}\,\,\middle|\,\,k\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,p-1\},\,\,\ell\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,(p-3)/2\}\,\right\}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{(p-1)/2},

with corresponding group operation

\left(k,\ell\right)\left(\tilde{k},\tilde{\ell}\right)=\left(k+\tilde{k}a^{-\ell},\ell+\tilde{\ell}\right),

which may be rewritten as

\left(k,a^{\ell}\right)\left(\tilde{k},a^{\tilde{\ell}}\right)=\left(k+\tilde{k}a^{-\ell},a^{-\ell}a^{-\tilde{\ell}}\right),

which is isomorphic to

\left\{\,f\in\operatorname{AGL}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\,\,\middle|\,\,f(x)=mx+b,\,\,a\in\operatorname{QR}(\mathbb{F}_{p}),\,\,b\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\,\right\}.

By Proposition 3.1 in
[
Kan69
]
, since
\{M^{k}T^{\ell}\}
is isomorphic to the given subgroup of
\operatorname{AGL}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, it suffices to show that it is the automorphism group and that it acts transitively as the automorphism group in order to show that it acts double-homogeneously but not double-transitively on either the vectors or the lines.

Recall that we denote the automorphism group of the vectors as
G
and the automorphism group of the lines as
[G]
. Since we will implicitly show that the line automorphisms are
G\times U(1)
, we will treat elements of
[G]
as being in
\operatorname{U}((p-1)/2)
rather than
\operatorname{PU}((p-1)/2)
.

Note that Proposition
2.5
implies
G
and
[G]
are transitive since the vectors in
\Phi_{p}
are the orbit of
\mathbbm{1}
under
\{M^{k}\}
. We now show that
G
contains
\{M^{k}T^{\ell}\}
. Trivially, the subgroup
\{T^{\ell}\}
fixes
\mathbbm{1}
. Thus, for an arbitrary vector
M^{\kappa}\mathbbm{1}
in
\Phi_{p}
, we have

M^{k}T^{\ell}M^{\kappa}\mathbbm{1}=M^{k}M^{\kappa a^{-\ell}}T^{\ell}\mathbbm{1}=M^{k+\kappa a^{-\ell}}\mathbbm{1}\in\Phi_{p}

From this it follows that
[G]\supseteq G\supseteq\{M^{k}T^{\ell}\}
.

Now we want to show that there is nothing else in
[G]
. The stabilizer of
\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}
in
\{M^{k}T^{\ell}\}
is just
\{T^{\ell}\}
. Since
[G]
is transitive, by the orbit stabilizer theorem, the index of the stabilizer of
\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}
in
[G]
, denoted
[G]_{\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}}
, is
p
. The index of
\{T^{\ell}\}
in
\{M^{k}T^{\ell}\}
is
p
. Thus,
[G]
strictly contains
\{M^{k}T^{\ell}\}
if and only if the stabilizer group of
\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}
strictly contains
\{T^{\ell}\}
. Let
U\in[G]_{\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}}
. We seek to show that it is
T^{\ell}
for some
\ell
and thus
[G]=\{M^{k}T^{\ell}\}
, which would then imply that
G=\{M^{k}T^{\ell}\}
. Let
\sigma\in S_{0,1,\ldots,p-1}
be the permutation of the
\varphi_{j}\varphi_{j}^{\ast}
induced by
U
(which necessarily fixes
0
). By Theorem
1.14
, we know that such a
\sigma
must map triple products with
\TP(0,k,\ell)
to those
\TP(0,\sigma(k),\sigma(\ell))
of the same value. Plugging
j=0
into Lemma
2.6
, we have

\TP(0,k,\ell)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\alpha^{3}&k\in\operatorname{NR},\ell\in\operatorname{QR},k-\ell\in\operatorname{QR}\\[2.0pt]
\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\alpha&(k,k-\ell\in\operatorname{NR},\,\ell\in\operatorname{QR})\textrm{ or }(k,\ell\in\operatorname{NR},\,k-\ell\in\operatorname{QR})\textrm{ or }(k,\ell,k-\ell\in\operatorname{QR})\\[2.0pt]
\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\overline{\alpha}&(k,k-\ell\in\operatorname{QR},\,\ell\in\operatorname{NR})\textrm{ or }(k,\ell\in\operatorname{QR},\,k-\ell\in\operatorname{NR})\textrm{ or }(k,\ell,k-\ell\in\operatorname{NR})\\[2.0pt]
\overline{\alpha}^{3}&\ k\in\operatorname{QR},\ell\in\operatorname{NR},k-\ell\in\operatorname{NR}\end{array}\right.

For case
\alpha^{3}
, for any choice of
k\in\operatorname{NR}
, there is always at least one
\ell\in\operatorname{QR}
such that
k-\ell\in\operatorname{QR}
and vice versa. Thus,
\sigma
must map
\operatorname{QR}
to
\operatorname{QR}
and
\operatorname{NR}
to
\operatorname{NR}
. Now consider arbitrary
k\in\operatorname{QR}
and
\ell=-k\in\operatorname{NR}
. The triple product is
\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\overline{\alpha}
precisely when
k-\ell=2k\in\operatorname{QR}
, i.e., when
2\in\operatorname{QR}
. This happens when
p\equiv-1\bmod 8
. Otherwise, when
p\equiv 3\bmod 8
, the triple product is
\overline{\alpha}^{3}
. Either way, once the ordering of
\operatorname{NR}
is fixed to be the ordering of negative the
\operatorname{QR}
,
\sigma
induces the same permutation of the two sets (of order at most
(p-1)/2
). To that end, let
\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}
be the
(p-1)/2\times(p-1)/2
circulant matrix that has the
\operatorname{QR}
columns in order

M^{1}\mathbbm{1},\,M^{(p-3)/2}\mathbbm{1},\,M^{(p-5)/2}\mathbbm{1},\,\ldots,\,M^{3}\mathbbm{1},\,M^{2}\mathbbm{1}.

Define
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}=\overline{\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}}
, the component-wise complex conjugation. Then
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}
is also circulant and each column corresponds to the negative of the parameter of the column in
\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}
. By Lemma
2.7
,
\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}
and
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}
are invertible and their inverses have constant row sums. Let
\Pi
denote the
(p-1)/2\times(p-1)/2
permutation matrix representing the permutation of the quadratic residues (respectively, the non residues). We would like to show that
U
is this permutation (up to unimodular constant). On the vector level, in order for
U
to induce the given permutations, there must exist diagonal unitary matrices
D_{\operatorname{QR}}
and
D_{\operatorname{NR}}
such that

U=\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}D_{\operatorname{QR}}\Pi\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}^{-1}=\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}D_{\operatorname{NR}}\Pi\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}^{-1}

Since
U\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}U^{\ast}=\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}
,
D_{\operatorname{QR}}
and
D_{\operatorname{NR}}
must be unimodular multiples of the identity and can be ignored. Thus, we have

\left(\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}^{-1}\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}\right)\Pi=\Pi\left(\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}^{-1}\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}\right).

As product of circulant matrices,
\Phi_{\operatorname{NR}}^{-1}\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}
is circulant and diagonalizable by the Fourier matrix for
\mathbb{Z}_{(p-1)/3}
. Since
\Pi
commutes with it, it must also be diagonalizable by the Fourier matrix and hence circulant. The only circulant permutation matrices are of the form
T^{\ell}
and the product commutes:

U=\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}T^{\ell}\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}^{-1}=\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}\Phi_{\operatorname{QR}}^{-1}T^{\ell}=T^{\ell},

as desired.
∎

This theorem should not be incredibly surprising, given that Paley designs are the only
2
-homogeneous but not
2
-transitive Hadamard designs
[
Kan69
]
. As we will see in Theorem
3.6
, Paley skew-conference matrices also yield an infinite class of ETFs which are
3
-homogeneous but not
3
-transitive.
In
[
Kan69
]
, it is also noted that if a set has a
2
-homogeneous automorphism group that is not
2
-transitive, represented as a subgroup of
\operatorname{AGL}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
, then the stabilizer of
0
(for us
\mathbbm{1}
or
\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top})
permutes elements corresponding to the quadratic residues and elements corresponding the non-quadratic residues, which is what we observed.

3
k
-Homogeneous ETFs

In this section, we characterize
k
-homogeneous ETFs. Throughout this section, we assume
d>1
. In particular, this means that the ETFs cannot trivially contain repeated elements.

The following result is generally known, but we include a proof here for completeness.

Lemma 3.1
.

The line automorphism group of orthonormal bases and simplex ETFs with
n
vectors is isomorphic to
S_{n}
. If an ETF is a regular simplex ETF or an orthonormal basis, then the vector automorphism group is also isomorphic to
S_{n}
.

Proof.

Given any two ordered orthonormal bases
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
U_{2}U_{1}^{-1}
is a unitary matrix that maps the basis
U_{1}
to the basis
U_{2}
. Thus, the automorphism group of the vectors and in turn the lines is
S_{n}
. A
(d,n)
-ETF has
n=d+1
vectors if and only if all of the triple products are negative
[
FJKM18
]
. Thus, by Theorem
1.14
, any permutation of the lines is induced by some element of the automorphism group of the lines. Finally, if the ETF is a regular simplex ETF, then the inner products are all negative and thus any permutation of the vectors is possible (see, e.g.,
[
CW16
]
).
∎

We also include the following converse.

Lemma 3.2
.

The only ETFs with
3
-transitive line automorphism groups are orthonormal bases and simplices. The only ETFs with
2
-transitive vector automorphism groups are orthonormal bases and regular simplex ETFs. Finally, the only real ETFs with
2
-homogeneous vector automorphism groups with are orthonormal bases and regular simplex ETFs.

Proof.

The first result is Theorem II.6 in
[
Kin19
]
. The second comes from the fact that if the vector automorphism group is
2
-transitive, then any inner product of distinct vectors is mapped to any other inner product. The third comes from the fact that for real vectors the inner product is symmetric, meaning that
2
-homogeneity implies that all of the inner products between distinct vectors are the same.
∎

Note that this gives an alternate proof that the vector automorphism group of the prime order Paley ETFs
\Phi_{p}
is not
2
-transitive. We note that the following immediately follows from the definition of homogeneity, but we record it here as we will use it frequently.

Lemma 3.3
.

If a group acts
k
-homogeneously on a set of size
n
, then it also acts
(n-k)
-homogeneously.

Contrast this with transitivity, where every
k
-transitive group action is also
(k-1)
-transitive and where the larger
k
is, the less likely it is that a group acts
k
-transitively.
We now have a final lemma before stating and proving the main result of this section.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that
\Phi
is an ETF with
n>3
vectors where the inner products of any two distinct vectors is imaginary. Then the line automorphism group of
\Phi
is
3
-homogeneous. Conversely, if
n\notin\{d,d+1,2d\}
,
\Phi
cannot have
3
-homogeneous automorphism group.

Proof.

Let
j,k,\ell
be distinct indices. Then note that

\displaystyle\overline{\TP(j,k,\ell)}=\overline{\left\langle\varphi_{j},\varphi_{k}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{k},\varphi_{\ell}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{\ell},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\varphi_{k},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{\ell},\varphi_{k}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{j},\varphi_{\ell}\right\rangle=\left\langle\varphi_{j},\varphi_{\ell}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{\ell},\varphi_{k}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{k},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle=\TP(j,\ell,k).

If the inner products are all imaginary, for any set
\{j,k,\ell\}
of distinct indices there is some ordering of the triple products which is a positive real number times
i
. It now follows from Theorem
1.14
that the automorphism group of the lines is
3
-homogeneous.

Similarly, we note that for any ETF and any set
\{j,k,\ell\}
of distinct indices, there are only two possible triple products regardless of order,
\TP(j,k,\ell)
and
\overline{\TP(j,k,\ell)}
. Thus, if the triple products are not all complex conjugates of each other, there will be two sets of three distinct indices which cannot be mapped to each other. This means that the triple products of a
3
-homogeneous frame are either real or imaginary. Note that if the triple products are all positive then the vectors are switching equivalent to a set with all positive inner products and thus have an invertible Gram matrix. But the only non-redundant ETFs are orthonormal bases, a contradiction. Thus, the triple products are all
-\alpha
or all
\pm i\alpha
for some
\alpha\geq 0
. A necessary condition for an ETF to have purely imaginary inner products (see, e.g.,
[
ET00
,
Str08
]
) is that
n=2d
. Simplices and orthonormal bases uniquely yield triple products which are all negative or
0
, respectively.
∎

In
[
BP05
,
HS12
]
, it was proven that redundant equiangular tight frames which are in a certain sense optimally robust to three erasures (called
3
-uniform
) must be a simplex or have purely imaginary inner products, which suggests a connection to being
3
-homogeneous.

In
[
IM22
]
, it was proven that any redundant spanning set which has a doubly transitive line automorphism group must be an ETF. However, the result holds for doubly homogeneous ETFs with minor changes to the proof.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{d\times n}
be a frame (i.e., spanning set) with
n>\max\{2,d\}
. If
\Phi
has a doubly homogeneous line automorphism group, then
\Phi
is an ETF.

Proof.

For any
U
in the line automorphism group, there exists
\sigma\in S_{n}
and
(\eta_{j})^{n}_{j=1}\in\operatorname{U}(1)^{n}
such that

U\varphi_{j}=\eta_{j}\varphi_{\sigma(j)},\quad j=1,2,\ldots,n.

By
[
LW65
, Theorem 2A]
, if
n\geq 2k
, then any
k
-homogeneous group action on a set of size
n
is
(k-1)
-transitive. Thus, if
n\geq 4
, then the doubly homogeneous line automorphism group must act
1
-transitively, meaning that the vectors are all equal norm. If
n=3
, then the doubly homogeneous line automorphism group must act
(3-2)
-homogeneously, i.e.,
1

A frame is tight if and only if

if and only if the Gram matrix

is

times an orthogonal projection. This is the formulation we will use. Let

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\varphi_{k},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle U\varphi_{k},U\varphi_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\eta_{k}\varphi_{\sigma(k)},\eta_{j}\varphi_{\sigma(j)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\eta_{k}\overline{\eta_{j}}\left\langle\varphi_{\sigma(k)},\varphi_{\sigma(j)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\eta_{j}}\eta_{k}\Gamma_{\sigma(j),\sigma(k)}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\overline{\eta_{j}}\eta_{k}\Gamma_{m,\ell}\\[2.0pt]
\overline{\eta_{j}}\eta_{k}\Gamma_{\ell,m}\end{array}\right.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\left(\Gamma^{2}\right)_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{k},\varphi_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle U\varphi_{i},U\varphi_{j}\right\rangle\left\langle U\varphi_{k},U\varphi_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\eta_{i}\varphi_{\sigma(i)},\eta_{j}\varphi_{\sigma(j)}\right\rangle\left\langle\eta_{k}\varphi_{\sigma(k)},\eta_{i}\varphi_{\sigma(i)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\eta_{j}}\eta_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varphi_{\sigma(i)},\varphi_{\sigma(j)}\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi_{\sigma(k)},\varphi_{\sigma(i)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\eta_{j}}\eta_{k}\left(\Gamma^{2}\right)_{\sigma(j),\sigma(k)}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\overline{\eta_{j}}\eta_{k}\left(\Gamma^{2}\right)_{m,\ell}\\[2.0pt]
\overline{\eta_{j}}\eta_{k}\left(\Gamma^{2}\right)_{\ell,m}\end{array}\right.

Thus, the off-diagonal of

is a constant multiple of the off-diagonal of
\Gamma
. Also,

\left(\Gamma^{2}\right)_{j,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}\rangle\right|^{2}=\beta^{2}+(n-1)\alpha^{2}=\frac{\beta^{2}+(n-1)\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\Gamma_{j,j}

Hence, there exists

such that

is a scalar multiple of an orthogonal projection and hence

is a tight frame. Putting everything together, we have that

is an ETF.
∎

Assume that
