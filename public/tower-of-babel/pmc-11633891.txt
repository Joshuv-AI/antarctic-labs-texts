The amino acid metabolism pathway of peripheral T lymphocytes and ketamine-induced schizophrenia-like phenotype
Authors: Peipei Wang, Linzhi Jiang, Junmei Hu, Zihan Jiang, Yu Zhang, Congliang Chen, Yanchen Lin, Mi Su, Xia Wang, Linchuan Liao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The intricate interplay between peripheral adaptive immune cells and the central nervous system (CNS) has garnered increasing recognition. Given that alterations in cell quantities often translate into modifications in metabolite profiles and that these metabolic changes can potentially traverse the bloodstream and enter the CNS, thereby modulating the progression of mental illnesses, we sought to explore the metabolic profiles of peripheral immune cells in a ketamine-treated mouse model of schizophrenia.Methods:We used flow cytometry to scrutinize the alterations in peripheral adaptive immune cells in a ketamine-induced schizophrenia mouse model. Subsequently, we implemented an untargeted metabolomic approach with ultra-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) to detect the metabolite profiles of peripheral abnormal lymphocytes and identify differential metabolites present in plasma. We then employed targeted metabolomics using UPLC-MS/MS to quantify the common differential metabolites detected in mouse plasma.Results:Flow cytometry analysis detected a notable increase in the count of peripheral CD3+ T cells in a ketamine-induced schizophrenia mouse model. Subsequent untargeted metabolomics analysis revealed that the amino acid metabolism pathway underwent substantial alterations. A detailed quantification of 22 amino acid profiles in the peripheral plasma indicated significant elevation in the levels of glycine, alanine, asparagine, and aspartic acid.Limitations:Our ongoing research has yet to conclusively identify the precise amino acid metabolism pathway that serves as the pivotal factor in the manifestation of the schizophrenia-like phenotype induced by ketamine.Conclusion:The peripheral amino acid metabolism pathway is involved in the ketamine-induced schizophrenia-like phenotype. The metabolic profile of peripheral immune cells could provide accurate biomarkers for the diagnosis and treatment of psychiatric diseases.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a common mental illness that affects around 1% of the world’s population.1–3 However, given the challenges in modelling a complex and heterogeneous condition in preclinical models, little is known about the pathogenesis and psychopathology of schizophrenia.

Ketamine, a novel pharmacological agent, is primarily employed in clinical settings as an anesthetic, analgesic, and, more recently, as an antidepressant.4–6 However, its misuse can lead to severe addiction and may trigger mental health disorders.7 Research has consistently demonstrated that ketamine-induced behavioural alterations closely mimic the symptomatic presentation of schizophrenia among healthy human volunteers8 and in rodent models.9–12 Consequently, establishing an animal model of schizophrenia using ketamine can be used to gain a deeper understanding of schizophrenia’s pathophysiology and explore novel therapeutic avenues.13

As the field of neurologic and psychiatric disorders increasingly focuses on the intricate interplay between the immune and nervous systems, particularly the newly appreciated role of peripheral adaptive immune cells within the central nervous system (CNS),14 researchers have embarked on an exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying psychiatric disorders from the unique perspective of peripheral immune cells.

Metabolomics, a comprehensive spectral analysis approach, offers invaluable insights into the endogenous metabolic landscape and can be used to explore molecular mechanisms of disease and to facilitate the discovery of biomarkers.15 Some research has identified metabolic markers associated with ketamine administration models,16–19 but the sensitivity and specificity of these markers still pose challenges for their translation and practical application. In recent years, researchers have shifted their focus to the realm of cellular metabolomics. For instance, a study reported that purines produced by peripheral CD4+ T cells enter the CNS and act on oligodendrocytes, ultimately leading to anxiety-like behaviour in mice.20

To gain a deeper insight into the mechanisms and biomarkers associated with ketamine-induced schizophrenia, we sought to employ an advanced metabolomics approach to precisely identify salient metabolite alterations in both peripheral lymphocytes and blood samples. Furthermore, we aimed to identify the differential metabolites ranging from the cellular level, through the peripheral circulation, to the hippocampus. By jointly analyzing these metabolic pathways, we aimed to provide not only more precise biomarkers for ketamine-induced schizophrenia but also establish a crucial foundation for uncovering the underlying mechanisms of these diseases.

Methods

Drugs and reagents

We obtained ketamine hydrochloride from the Yunnan Provincial Public Security Department. We dissolved ketamine hydrochloride in physiologic saline, converted into ketamine dose, and administered it intraperitoneally (0.1 mL/10 g body weight). The fixed viability stain 780 (FVS780) dye, allophycocyanin cyanine 7 (APC-Cy7), anti-mouse CD16/CD32 monoclonal antibodies, anti-mouse CD45–fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), CD3–brilliant violet (Bv) 510 or CD3–FITC, CD19–Bv605, CD11c–phycoerythrin (PE), and NK1.1–Bv650 were all purchased from BD Biosciences.

Animals

We purchased 5-week-old male C57BL/6J mice from Chengdu Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology. Animals were housed in groups for 1 week under controlled conditions (temperature 23° ± 2°C, 12-h light–dark cycle, humidity 50% ± 5%, free access to water, and standard mouse chow).

Ketamine-administered mouse model

We based our experimental design on our previous study using ketamine for establishing a schizophrenia mouse model.19,21 We randomly divided animals into ketamine and control groups. The animals in the control group received 0.9% saline and those in the ketamine group received 50 mg/kg ketamine, both administered intraperitoneally, twice daily for 8 days (Figure 1).

Behavioural tests

As per our previous study, we selected the open field test, the elevated plus maze, and the Y maze to verify anxiety symptoms and cognitive impairment.19,21

Analysis of peripheral immune cells

After completion of behavioural tests, we employed flow cytometry to scrutinize alterations in the composition of immune cells within the mouse spleen. Specifically, we analyzed the quantities of T cells (CD3+), B cells (CD19+), natural killer (NK) cells (NK1.1+), and dendritic cells (CD11c+), all within the CD45+ leukocyte population.

We anesthetized the mice by 3% isoflurane inhalation. We removed spleen tissue, ground it into a single-cell suspension, and treated it with lysis red. We adjusted cell counts to 106 cells per tube, and used 1 mL Dulbecco phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to resuspend cells. According to manufacturer instructions, we added 1 μL of FVS780 dye, incubated the suspension at room temperature for 15 minutes to distinguish dead or live cells, followed by washing twice and resuspending with 100 μL PBS. We added 2.5 μL of anti-mouse CD16/CD32 monoclonal antibody, incubated at 4°C for 20 minutes to block nonspecific antibody binding, washed twice, and resuspended with 100 μL PBS. We added 2.5 μL of anti-mouse CD45, CD3, CD19, CD11c, or NK1.1, incubated at 4°C for 30 minutes, washed twice, and resuspended with 500 μL PBS. We then transferred the suspension to rotary tubes and conducted flow cytometry using FACS Celesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), selecting the fluorescence channel as FVS780–APC-Cy7, CD45–FITC, CD3–Bv510, CD19–Bv605, CD11c–PE, or NK1.1–Bv650, as appropriate. We analyzed results using FlowJo version 10 (BD Biosciences).

CD3+ T cell sorting

After anesthetizing the mice, we took spleen tissue, ground it into a single-cell suspension, and treated it with lysis red. We resuspended cells with 1 mL Dulbecco PBS (around 107 cells per tube), added 1 μL FVS780 dye, incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes to distinguish dead or live cells, washed twice, and resuspended with 600 μL PBS. We added 10 μL of anti-mouse CD16/CD32 monoclonal antibody, incubated at 4°C for 20 minutes to block nonspecific antibody binding, washed twice, and resuspended with 600 μL PBS. We then added 10 μL of anti-mouse CD3 antibody, incubated at 4°C for 30 minutes, washed twice, and resuspended with 3 mL PBS. We transferred the suspension to a rotating tube and used FACSMelody flow cytometry (BD Biosciences) for sorting by selecting the fluorescence channel as FVS780-APC-Cy7 and CD3–FITC. We collected the sorted CD3+ cells using a flow cytometry tube that contained serum, washed the collected cells thoroughly with PBS twice, quickly froze the solution in liquid nitrogen, and stored it at −80°C until analysis.

Peripheral plasma collection

After anesthesia, we took around 1 mL of cardiac blood and added it to an anticoagulant tube. We left it to stand at room temperature for 2 hours, centrifuged at 3000 rpm, and incubated at 4°C for 15 minutes. We collected around 200 μL of upper plasma and stored it at −80°C until analysis.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

We performed untargeted metabolomics analysis using ultra-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) sorted CD3+ T cells and mouse plasma, and conducted the experimental procedures described by Suzhou PANOMIX Biomedical Tech (Figure 1). The mobile phase and detailed gradient elution conditions can be found in Appendix 1, Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, available at www.jpn.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/jpn.240038/tab-related-content.

Targeted metabolomics analysis of amino acids

We conducted a quantitative, targeted metabolomics analysis of 22 amino acids in mouse plasma using UPLC-MS/MS and the experimental procedures outlined by Suzhou PANOMIX Biomedical Tech. The parameters and standard curves for each amino acid are shown in Appendix 1, Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

We described all data using means and standard deviations. We used GraphPad Prism 9 software to analyze the data and generate graphs. We compared groups using the Student t test, with 0.05 as the significance threshold for p values. To gain a holistic understanding of the metabolomic profiles of CD3+ T cells in our 2 experimental groups and to identify any potential outliers, we initially applied principal component analysis (PCA). We also used partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) for a more in-depth examination of differences. To identify differential metabolites in CD3+ T cells between the ketamine and control groups, we applied stringent criteria, setting VIP values greater than 1.0 and p values less than 0.05 as thresholds. We used volcano maps and conducted pathway analysis, focusing on both concentration and topology, to explore differential metabolites and metabolic pathways.

Ethics approval

We followed the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in all experimental methods. The Medical Ethics Committee of Sichuan University approved this study (no. KS2020261).

Results

We included 8 mice each in the ketamine and control groups. When subjected to the open field test, the ketamine group exhibited notably lower total distance, time spent in the centre area, and number of entries in the centre (Figure 2A). Furthermore, the elevated plus maze revealed that the ketamine group displayed significantly less time spent in and fewer entries into the open arms than the control group (Figure 2B). The Y maze test showed significant alterations in spontaneous alternation behaviour (Figure 2C). Collectively, these findings indicated the successful construction of a ketamine-induced schizophrenialike experimental model in mice.

(C) Spontaneous alternation in the Y maze test, with the ketamine group showing significantly less spontaneous alternation. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.001. SD = standard deviation.

Peripheral immune cell changes

Upon successfully establishing a ketamine-induced schizophrenia-like mouse model, we employed flow cytometry to scrutinize alterations in the composition of immune cells within the mouse spleen. As depicted in Figure 3, our findings revealed no statistically significant variations in the proportions of CD19+, NK1.1+, or CD11c+ cells within the CD45+ population. In contrast, we observed a notable and significant increase in the proportion of CD3+ T cells within the CD45+ leukocyte subset. These results underscore the involvement of peripheral T cells in the manifestation of the ketamine-induced schizophrenialike phenotype.

Metabolic profiling of peripheral splenic CD3+ T cells

Having confirmed the substantial elevation of peripheral T cells in a ketamine-induced schizophrenia-like mouse model, we proceeded to isolate CD3+ T cells from the spleens. As shown in Appendix 2, Supplementary Figure S1, available at www.jpn.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/jpn.240038/tab-related-content, we confirmed the significantly higher number of CD3+ T cells in the ketamine group compared with the control group. Subsequently, we employed UPLC-MS/MS for untargeted metabolomic profiling of these CD3+ T cells. The characteristic metabolic profiles of CD3+ T cells, captured in both positive- and negative-ion modes, are presented in Appendix 2, Supplementary Figure S2.

The PCA score plots, presented in Appendix 2, Supplementary Figure S3 for both positive- and negative-ion modes, revealed a discernible trend of separation between the CD3+ T cell profiles of the control group and the model group samples. The PLS-DA analysis showed that, in both modes, Q2 values were greater than 0.5 and R2Y values were close to 1, indicating that the data of this model were effective, were reliable, and had good predictive ability (positive-ion mode, ketamine v. control: R2Y = 0.995, Q2 = 0.775; negative-ion mode, ketamine v. control, R2Y = 0.999, Q2 = 0.730). The PLS-DA score plots demonstrated a pronounced separation between the control and ketamine groups (Figure 4A and 4B), indicating that the ketamine-induced schizophrenia mouse model significantly affected the metabolic profile of peripheral CD3+ T cell.

Our rigorous screening process unveiled alterations in a total of 16 metabolites under both positive- and negative-ion modes. Of these, 6 metabolites displayed increased levels, while 10 showed decreased levels compared with the control group. Volcano maps visually represented the overall landscape of these differential metabolites (Figure 4C). Appendix 1, Supplementary Table S4 provides a comprehensive list of these metabolites, along with their detailed information. Further metabolic pathway analysis revealed that the perturbed pathways primarily involved tyrosine metabolism; adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase signalling; biosynthesis of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan; lysine degradation; and cysteine and methionine metabolism. These findings are summarized in Figure 4D; Appendix 1, Supplementary Table S5 offers detailed insights into the pathway analysis. Notably, most differential metabolites enriched in these pathways were amino acids, highlighting the profound impact of ketamine-induced schizophrenia on amino acid metabolism in peripheral CD3+ T cells.

Metabolic profiling of peripheral plasma

To further explore the hypothesis that the differential metabolites of significantly increased peripheral CD3+ T cells from the ketamine-induced schizophrenia mouse model would enter circulation, we also tested the metabolic profile of peripheral plasma under this model. This approach enabled us to capture the representative metabolic signatures of the peripheral plasma in both positive- and negative-ion modes, which are presented in Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure S4.

The results of PCA analysis are shown in Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure S5, with some overlap between the control group and the ketamine group. As depicted in Figure 5A, the PLS-DA analysis showed that the 2 groups of samples could be effectively distinguished (cross-validation model variable results, positive-ion mode: R2Y = 0.999, Q2 = 0.91; negative-ion mode, R2Y = 0.992, Q2 = 0.707).

Differential metabolites were rigorously screened based on stringent criteria (VIP > 1.0 and p < 0.05). Compared with the control group, a comprehensive analysis of both positive-and negative-ion modes in the model group yielded a total of 39 differential metabolites, with 31 metabolites exhibiting increased levels and 8 metabolites displaying decreased levels (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table S6). We employed volcano maps to illustrate the overall patterns and characteristics of these differential metabolites (Figure 5C). Further analysis of the metabolic pathways associated with these differential metabolites — summarized in Appendix 1, Supplementary Table S7 — revealed that they were primarily involved in lipid and amino acid metabolism. Specifically, affected pathways included those involved in sphingolipid signalling; fatty acid synthesis; arachidonic acid metabolism; phenylalanine metabolism; biosynthesis of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan; lysine degradation; tyrosine metabolism; and arginine and proline metabolism (Figure 5D). These findings suggested that the abnormal amino acid metabolites detected within CD3+ T cells in the model group were highly likely to disseminate into the circulation.

Absolute quantitative analysis of amino acids in peripheral plasma

To delve deeper into the alterations in amino acid metabolism within the context of the ketamine-induced schizophrenia-like mouse model, we conducted a targeted metabolomics analysis on peripheral plasma samples. The findings from this analysis revealed intriguing changes in amino acid profiles. Notably, compared with the control group, 3 amino acids — γ-aminobutyric acid (Figure 6), glutamine, and histidine — exhibited decreased levels in the model group, with the exception of homocysteine, which was undetected. Conversely, the levels of 18 amino acids were found to be elevated. Among these, 4 amino acids — glycine, alanine, asparagine, and aspartic acid — displayed particularly significant increases (Figure 6, Figure 7, Figure 8, and Figure 9).

Discussion

We constructed a ketamine-induced schizophrenia mouse model to explore characteristic biomarkers. Building on our previous research endeavours,19,21 we refined the experimental protocol for constructing the model by adjusting the dosing regimen to 50 mg/kg, administered via intraperitoneal injection twice daily over an 8-day period. The results of behavioural experiments unanimously supported the successful establishment of a ketamine-induced experimental schizophrenia mouse model.

With the growing interest in the intricate interplay between the immune system and the nervous system in the context of neurologic and psychiatric disorders,22–24 the pivotal role of peripheral adaptive immune cells in the CNS has garnered substantial recognition.14 Research has illuminated that soluble cytokine from the periphery can traverse the blood–brain barrier and modulate CNS functions.25 Furthermore, during certain pathological conditions, peripheral lymphocytes — including T cells, B cells, and their subsets — have been observed to infiltrate the CNS.26,27 Notably, studies have demonstrated that immunodeficient mice exhibit compromised spatial learning and memory capabilities, which can be remediated through the administration of exogenous T lymphocytes, underscoring the indispensability of peripheral adaptive immunity for cognitive function.28 Given this compelling evidence, we explored changes in key peripheral immune populations — namely T, B, NK, and dendritic cells — within our established ketamine-induced experimental schizophrenia mouse model. Our findings revealed a pronounced elevation in the number of peripheral T lymphocytes, hinting at a potential pivotal role for these cells in ketamine misuse–induced schizophrenia, which raises the question of how augmented peripheral T lymphocytes contribute to the condition’s progression.

The prolonged misuse of ketamine, a relatively novel drug, invariably results in severe deterioration of the blood–brain barrier. In such circumstances, peripherally activated lymphocytes capitalize on the compromised barrier, traversing it and infiltrating the CNS, thereby exacerbating disease progression.26,27 Concurrently, soluble cytokines liberated by these peripheral lymphocytes gain easier access to the CNS, where they execute regulatory functions.25 Moreover, alterations in peripheral lymphocytes trigger shifts in the body’s metabolic landscape, with these metabolites potentially circulating into the CNS to modulate the course of related disorders.29 Metabolomics, as the ultimate response of biological systems, emerges as a valuable tool for understanding phenotypic changes.30 Previous investigations have predominantly focused on metabolic perturbations at the organismal level, including those within localized brain tissue, urine, and blood, but relatively little attention has been given to metabolic changes occurring at the cellular level. It is crucial to acknowledge that long-term drug misuse not only elicits changes in T lymphocytes but also profoundly alters the body’s metabolic profile. These metabolites, carried through circulation, may reach the CNS and contribute to the manifestation of behavioural symptoms associated with ketamine misuse.29

After confirming a significant increase in the number of peripheral T lymphocytes in the ketamine-induced schizophrenia model, we isolated CD3+ T cells from the peripheral spleen and employed an untargeted metabolomics approach using UPLC MS/MS to identify differential metabolites within these CD3+ T cells. The PLS-DA score plot revealed a notable separation between the control and ketamine groups, indicating substantial differences in the metabolites of peripheral CD3+ T cells in the ketamine-induced experimental schizophrenia mouse model. Furthermore, the pathway enrichment map indicated that the metabolic pathways of amino acids were the most substantially affected.

Amino acids are vital substances for the body, playing a crucial role in metabolic processes such as protein synthesis, energy metabolism, and neurotransmission.31 Research has demonstrated that amino acids are closely linked to cellular carbon metabolism and signal transduction processes related to T-cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune function. This further confirms that an increase in peripheral T cells can disrupt amino acid metabolic pathways. Studies have reported that abnormal amino acid metabolism is associated with various neurologic and psychiatric disorders, including autism spectrum disorders, intellectual disabilities, epilepsy, and schizophrenia.32–34

Previously, we described the comprehensive metabolic characteristics of hippocampal tissue in a ketamine-induced schizophrenia mouse model.19 The results indicated that pathways with substantial metabolic changes in the hippocampus tissue were also involved in amino acid metabolism. Based on this, we hypothesize that long-term ketamine administration overactivates peripheral T lymphocytes, leading to disorders in their amino acid metabolic pathways. The abnormal amino acid products may then circulate into the brain, causing changes in local brain tissue–related metabolites and, ultimately, participating in the regulation of ketamine-induced schizophrenia symptoms.

To further validate our hypothesis, we employed an untargeted metabolomics approach to detect metabolite changes in the plasma of ketamine-induced schizophrenia mice. Our findings revealed that the affected pathways primarily involved amino acid metabolism. Subsequently, we quantified 22 amino acids in mouse plasma and observed significant increases in glycine, alanine, asparagine, and aspartic acid, particularly asparagine and aspartic acid, which were also upregulated in the metabolic profiles of peripheral CD3+ T cells and nontargeted peripheral plasma (Appendix 1, Supplementary Tables S1 and S3). Research has reported that increased levels of asparagine and aspartic acid are associated with various psychiatric disorders. For instance, levels of asparagine, glutamine, proline, and arginine have been found to be significantly higher among patients with major depressive disorder.35 In a rat model of chronic unpredictable mild stress and depression, concentrations of glutamine and glycine decreased, while aspartic acid was upregulated, in association with depressive-like behaviour.36 In addition, increased L-aspartic acid has been identified as a potential biomarker of depression.37 Taken together, these data further support our hypothesis that abnormal amino acid products in peripheral T lymphocytes may circulate into the brain, causing changes in local brain tissue–related metabolites.

Limitations

Although our research has yet to conclusively identify the precise amino acid metabolic pathway that serves as the pivotal factor in the ketamine-induced schizophrenialike phenotype, this remains a crucial focus of our future experiments. Nevertheless, our study detected alterations in peripheral immune cells in the ketamine-induced schizophrenia model mice. This work offers a fresh perspective on the underlying mechanisms of mental disorders and has the potential to yield more precise biomarkers for the identification and diagnosis of mental health conditions associated with ketamine and other novel drugs.

Conclusion

During the progression of ketamine-induced schizophrenia in mice, the quantity of peripheral T lymphocytes significantly increases, leading to substantial changes in metabolic profiles. The differential metabolic products are mainly enriched in the amino acid metabolic pathway; abnormal amino acid metabolic pathways are also found in peripheral blood. We postulate that the amino acid metabolic pathway of peripheral T lymphocytes plays a pivotal role in the progression of ketamine-induced schizophrenia. Our targeted metabolomics analysis of peripheral plasma specifically highlighted the significant upregulation of 4 key amino acids, namely glycine, alanine, asparagine, and aspartic acid. Notably, asparagine and aspartic acid exhibited particularly robust increases, which were consistent with their upregulation in the metabolic profiles of peripheral CD3+ T cells and untargeted plasma analysis. These findings strongly suggest that the amino acid metabolic pathway of peripheral T lymphocytes is intricately linked to the ketamine-induced schizophrenia-like phenotype in mice. Aspartic acid is a promising candidate biomarker for ketamine-induced schizophrenia, offering a potential diagnostic tool and therapeutic target for future research endeavours.
