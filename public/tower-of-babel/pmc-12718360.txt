SPNS1 is an essential cellular factor for EV-A71 by acting as a transporter of viral pocket factor
Authors: Yu-Zhi Fu, Fang-Fang Luo, Liu Yang, Yu-Xia Zhang, Jing-Yang Li, Su-Yun Wang, Yong Zhang, Yan-Yi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHuman enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) is a major cause of hand, foot and mouth diseases which poses a global threat to human health. Despite intensive investigations, the cellular factors that are critical for EV-A71 infection remain enigmatic, hampering the understanding of viral pathogenesis and the development of anti-EV-A71 therapies. This study reveals the lysosomal protein SPNS1 acts as a transporter for release of EV-A71 pocket factor into the cytosol, a process required for release of the viral genome from the lysosomes to the cytosol and its subsequent replication. Studies in mice indicate that SPNS1 is important for EV-A71 infectivity and pathogenesis. Therefore, SPNS1 may serve as a potential drug target for developing antivirals against EV-A71 and a group of enteroviruses.

Human enterovirus 71 (EV-A71) is a single positive-strand RNA virus that belongs to the enterovirus A genus of Picornaviridae (1). EV-A71 infection is a major cause of hand, foot and mouth disease (HFMD) that is characterized by herpes on the hands, feet, buttocks, and mouth and could progress to fatal encephalitis in infants and children (2). The virion of EV-A71 is a nonenveloped icosahedral capsid with a diameter of ~20 to 30 nm. The viral capsid is composed of VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4, with VP1, VP2, and VP3 on the outer surface and VP4 attached to the inner surface of the capsid. On the icosahedral virion, a circular depression called “canyon” is present around each fivefold axis of symmetry, where VP1 is localized. Below the floor of the canyon lies a pocket domain of VP1, which is bound by a hydrophobic “pocket factor” that has been shown to be a natural lipid (sphingolipid). It has been demonstrated that the pocket factor plays an essential role in maintaining EV-A71 particle stability (3–7).

Entry of EV-A71 can be initiated by binding of the virus to its cellular receptors. Human scavenger receptor B2 (hSCARB2) and P-selectin glycoprotein ligand 1 (hPSGL-1) have been shown to be entry receptors for EV-A71 (8, 9). Upon EV-A71 infection, receptors bind to VP1 at the canyon, resulting in receptor-mediated endocytosis. Subsequently, the virus-containing endosomes fuse with lysosomes to form endosomes/lysosomes. In the acidic environment, the receptor undergoes conformational changes, which results in collapse of a pocket in VP1 immediately below the floor of the canyon and subsequently expulsion of the pocket factor from VP1. Removal of the pocket factor triggers destabilization of the capsid, leading to exposure of VP4 and the N terminus of VP1, and subsequent formation of a pore on the endo/lysosomal membrane by VP4 and the N terminus of VP1, eventually resulting in release of the viral genome to the cytosol (2). Therefore, release of the viral pocket factor functions as a switch to initiate EV-A71 uncoating and release of its genomic RNA into the cytosol. While its importance is well established, how viral pocket factor is released into the cytosol is unknown.

Sphingolipid transporter 1 (SPNS1) is a ubiquitously expressed membrane associated protein mainly localized in lysosomes. It functions as a lysophospholipid transporter which mediates extravasation and circulation of lysophospholipids such as lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) from lysosomes to the cytosolic phospholipid pool (10–13). In this study, we screened for host factors required for EV-A71 infection by the CRISPR-Cas9 method, and these efforts led to identification of SPNS1. Our findings demonstrate that SPNS1 acts as a transporter of EV-A71 pocket factor, which is essential for EV-A71 uncoating and replication.

Results

Identification of SPNS1 as an Essential Cellular Factor for EV-A71 Infectivity.

It has been well established that EV-71 infection results in cell death in various cells (8). To identify host factors required for EV-A71 infection and replication, we established a genome-wide knockout library in HeLa cells with the CRISPR-Cas9 method and infected the cells with EV-A71 (H strain) (MOI of 0.1). EV-A71 infection resulted in death of most cells at 5 d postinfection (dpi). The survived cells were cultured and infected with EV-A71 again (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). The survived cells from the second-round infection were subjected to next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis, which identified multiple candidate genes that are required for EV-A71 life cycle and for infection-induced cell death (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Among the candidate genes, scavenger receptor class B member 2 (SCARB2) and solute carrier family 35 member B2 (SLC35B2) have been reported as a receptor and a cellular factor for EV-A71, respectively (8, 14), which validated the effectiveness of our screens. We then validated the candidate genes for their roles in the life cycle of EV-A71. RT-qPCR results indicated that knockdown of SCARB2, SLC35B2, SPNS1 (10, 11, 15), SNAP associated protein (SNAPIN) (16) and components of the HOPS complex (VPS33A, VPS39, VPS18, and VPS41), but not the other candidates such as transmembrane protein 170A (TMEM170A), glutathione-disulfide reductase (GSR), methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase (NADP+ dependent) 2 like (MHFD2L), calcium binding protein 7 (CABP7) or protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type U (PTPRU), markedly inhibited EV-A71 replication (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Meanwhile, we used RT-qPCR to measure the editing efficiency of the gRNA targeting the candidate genes. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1D, most genes were successfully edited, with only a few exceptions. In these experiments, knockdown of SPNS1, SNAPIN, and the HOPS complex components showed little effects on the replication of examined nonenteroviruses including respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), rift valley fever virus (RVFV), chikungunya virus (CHIKV), or vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), suggesting a specific role of these proteins in EV-A71 replication.

In this study, we focused on the function of SPNS1 in the EV-A71 life cycle. SPNS1 is a 528 aa, highly conserved and ubiquitously expressed lysophospholipid transporter with most of its mass embedded in the lysosomal membrane (10, 11). Several studies have demonstrated that SPNS1 is involved in lysosomal phospholipid salvage (10, 11), as well as tumor suppressor genes Beclin-1- and p53-regulated development and aging (17). SPNS1 is an essential gene that its null mutation is embryonically lethal in mice (15). To validate the involvement of SPNS1 in the EV-A71 life cycle, we edited SPNS1 by the CRISPR-Cas9 method with three individual sgRNAs that target different sites in the SPNS1 coding sequence and then examined their effects on EV-A71 infection. As shown in Fig. 1A, following EV-A71 infection, compared with control cells, the expression of viral VP1 was significantly reduced in SPNS1-edited HeLa cells in which SPNS1 was undetectable. Similarly, GFP-positive cells were easily detected in control but dramatically reduced in SPNS1-edited HeLa cells following infection with a GFP-tagged EV-A71 (Fig. 1B). SPNS1 deficiency also markedly reduced the levels of EV-A71 5’UTR RNA after infection of EV-A71 (MOI of 0.01 or 0.1) for 24 or 48 h (Fig. 1C). Additionally, SPNS1-editing impaired viral RNA levels after EV-A71 infection in human embryonic rhabdomyoma cells (RD), human glioblastoma U-87 MG cells (U-87) and human hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1E), suggesting that SPNS1 plays an important role in promoting EV-A71 infection in different types of cells.

The Lysosomal-Located SPNS1 Is an Evolutionarily Conserved Cellular Factor for EV-A71.

To further determine the role of SPNS1 in EV-A71 infection, we generated HeLa SPNS1-knockout clones (HeLa-ΔSPNS1), in which mutation of SPNS1 was confirmed by sequencing and deficiency of SPNS1 expression was confirmed by immunoblotting analysis (Fig. 2A). As expected, expression of EV-A71 VP1 protein (Fig. 2A), cell death (Fig. 2B), and production of progeny viruses (Fig. 2C) following EV-A71 infection were impaired in HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells in comparison with control cells. Trans-complementation of HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells with a cDNA encoding human SPNS1 reversed the effects of SPNS1 deficiency in these experiments (Fig. 2 A–C). These results suggest that SPNS1 is required for the completion of EV-A71 life cycle.

Previously, it had been reported that SPNS1 is a lysophospholipid transporter associated with lysosomal phospholipid salvage (11). There are four reported isoforms of human SPNS1. The isoform 1 encodes full-length SPNS1, whereas the other three isoforms encode truncated versions of SPNS1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Confocal microscopy indicated that only isoform 1, but not 2, 3, or 4, was localized on lysosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). To determine which isoforms of SPNS1 support EV-A71 infectivity, we reconstituted HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells with each of the 4 isoforms. We found that only isoform 1, but not isoform 2, 3, or 4, restored the infection of EV-A71 as determined by viral RNA levels in infected cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). These results suggest that the lysosomal localization of SPNS1 is related to its ability to support EV-A71 infection.

Previous literature reports have indicated that, in comparison with HeLa cells, although mouse fibroblast L929 cells are relatively less susceptible to EV-A71 infection, they are not completely nonpermissive (8, 18, 19). Therefore, we further verified the susceptibility of L929 cells to EV-A71. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C, L929 cells can be infected by EV-A71 and develop CPE. We next investigated whether murine SPNS1 can support EV-A71 infectivity in L929 cells. As shown in Fig. 2D, knockout of SPNS1 in L929 cells dramatically reduced viral RNA levels following EV-A71 infection compared with control cells. Trans-complementation of HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells with either human or murine SPNS1 restored both viral RNA replication (Fig. 2E) and production of progeny viruses (Fig. 2F) after EV-A71 infection. These results suggest that the lysosomal-located SPNS1 plays an evolutionarily conserved role for completion of the life cycle of EV-A71.

SPNS1 Is a Cellular Factor for Pan-Enteroviruses.

We next determined whether SPNS1 is an essential cellular factor for other enteroviruses. Eleven enteroviruses, including those from the EVA group (EV-A71, CVA16, CVA4, and CVA10), EVB group (CVB3, CVB5, ECHO3, ECHO11, and CVA9), EVC group (CVA24), and EVD group (EV-D68) were used to infect control and HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells and viral RNA levels were measured by RT-qPCR. The results indicated that SPNS1 deficiency reduced viral RNA levels of 9 out of 11 examined enteroviruses including EV-A71, CVA16, CVA4, CVA10, ECHO3, ECHO11, CVA9, CVA24, and EV-D68 compared to control cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Trans-complementation of HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells with SPNS1 restored replication of these viruses (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). In contrast, SPNS1 deficiency had no marked effects on replication of CVB3 and CVB5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). In these experiments, SPNS1 deficiency also inhibited replication of the nonenveloped encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) but had no marked effects on replication of the nonenveloped Seneca virus A (SVA), the enveloped RVFV and CHIKV, and the DNA viruses Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Collectively, these results suggest that SPNS1 is an important cellular factor for certain enteroviruses and nonenteroviruses.

The sphingolipid transporter family contains three members including SPNS1 (SLC63A1), SPNS2 (SLC63A2), and SPNS3 (SLC63A3). We next determined whether these members are functionally redundant to support replication of CVB3, CVB5, and CVA9, which did not require SPNS1 for replication. The results indicated that knockdown of either SPNS2 or SPNS3, or double knockdown of SPNS2 and SPNS3, had no marked effects on the replication of CVB3, CVB5, and CVA9 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C–E), which retain infectivity in SPNS1-deficient cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). These results suggest that replication of CVB3 and CVB5 is independent of any of the three SPNS proteins, whereas replication of CVA9 is partially supported by SPNS1 but not SPNS2/3. Additionally, knockdown of SPNS1, but not SPNS2 and SPNS3, dramatically reduced viral RNA levels after EV-A71 infection in HeLa cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F), suggesting that EV-A71 only utilizes SPNS1 but not SPNS2 or SPNS3 for replication.

It has been shown that SCARB2 serves as a receptor for EV-A71 but not EV-D68, ECHO3, and ECHO11 (5, 8, 20, 21). Knockdown of SCARB2 inhibited replication of EV-A71 but not EV-D68, ECHO3, and ECHO11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). In light of our earlier observations showing that knockdown of SPNS1 inhibited replication of EV-A71, EV-D68, ECHO3, and ECHO11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), these data suggest that SPNS1 is required for replication of certain viruses that do not utilize SCARB2 as an entry receptor.

SPNS1 Deficiency Reduces EV-A71 RNA Replication at the Early Stage.

We next investigated how SPNS1 is required for EV-A71 infectivity. We first tested whether SPNS1 is essential for EV-A71 binding to the cell surface and subsequent internalization. The results indicated that SPNS1 deficiency did not affect EV-A71 attachment to cells or its subsequent internalization (Fig. 3A). We then infected control or HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells with EV-A71 and measured viral RNA levels at various time points postinfection. As shown in Fig. 3B, viral RNA levels in HeLa-ΔSPNS1 and control cells were comparable at 3 h after infection. However, viral RNA levels were markedly reduced in HeLa-ΔSPNS1 compared to control HeLa cells at 6 h after infection and this reduction was more dramatic at 24 h after infection. These results suggest that SPNS1 is required for EV-A71 RNA replication after its entry and internalization.

It has been shown that once released from the endosomes into the cytosol, the EV-A71 genomic RNA (gRNA) is used as a template to synthesize negative-strand RNA (nsRNA) and double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) intermediates, then the nsRNAs serve as templates to further produce a large amount of viral gRNAs (2). Validated primers (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) were used for RT-qPCR experiments and the results showed that SPNS1 deficiency dramatically impaired the production of EV-A71 nsRNAs (Fig. 3C) and dsRNA intermediates (Fig. 3D) at 8 h post–EV-A71 infection. Additionally, we transfected EV-A71 gRNA directly into the cytosol of control and HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells, which bypasses the processes of viral entry and uncoating, and then measured replication of EV-A71 gRNA. Interestingly, SPNS1 deficiency did not inhibit the synthesis of viral nsRNA and EV-A71 genomic RNA translation up to 12 h after transfection (Fig. 3E). In addition, we have also evaluated the role of SPNS1 on EV-A71 translation. As shown in Fig. 3F, SPNS1 deficiency showed no effects on the translation of transfected EV-A71 genomic RNA, as indicated by comparable VP1 levels in the control and SPNS1-deficient cells. Collectively, these results suggest an essential role of SPNS1 in viral uncoating and releasing of viral gRNA from the endo/lysosomes to the cytosol for its replication upon EV-A71 infection.

It has been demonstrated that acidic condition of the late endo/lysosomes is important for uncoating and viral genome release (5, 20, 22). We treated HeLa-ΔSPNS1 and control HeLa cells with basic lysosomal inhibitor NH4Cl and then examined whether the function of SPNS1 is dependent on acidic condition of lysosomes. The results showed that, in mock-treated cells, at 6 or 8 h after infection, EV-A71 replication was dramatically lower in HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells than in control cells (Fig. 3G). Treatment of wild-type cells with NH4Cl reduced EV-A71 replication to a lower degree than the effects of SPNS1 knockout, and knockout of SPNS1 did not further enhance the inhibitory effects of NH4Cl on EV-A71 replication (Fig. 3G). These results imply that acidification of the endo/lysosomes is a prerequisite for SPNS1-mediated EV-A71 gRNA release.

To further investigate whether SPNS1 is important for the release of the viral genome from the endo/lysosomes to the cytosol, we isolated endo/lysosomes and examined whether SPNS1 deficiency affects the amount of viral RNA in the late endo/lysosomes at early phase (<3 h) of viral infection. The results showed that while viral RNA in late endo/lysosomes of control and HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells were comparable at 0.5 and 1 h after EV-A71 infection, viral RNA levels in the late endo-lysosomes of HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells were markedly higher than that of control cells at 2 and 3 h after infection (Fig. 3H). These results suggest that SPNS1 is required for the release of viral gRNA from the endo/lysosomes into the cytosol upon EV-A71 infection.

SPNS1 Is a Transporter of Viral Pocket Factor.

It has been reported that binding to the cellular receptor SCARB2, as well as release of a viral sphingosine pocket factor, leads to uncoating of EV-A71 in the late endo/lysosomes and subsequent release of viral gRNA into the cytosol (5, 7). Since SPNS1 is a lysophospholipid transporter which could mediate salvage of sphingosine (10, 11), we next investigated whether SPNS1 acts as a transporter of viral pocket factor from the endo/lysosomes into the cytosol, which initiates uncoating of EV-A71.

First, we conducted RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses to investigate the transcription and translation levels of VP1. As shown in Fig. 4A, in the early stages of EV-A71 infection (2 to 6 h postinfection), VP1 mRNA transcription remains at a low level. A significant upregulation of VP1 mRNA synthesis is only observed after 8 h postinfection (Fig. 4A). However, between 2 and 8 h following infection, no substantial increase in VP1 protein levels is detected (Fig. 4B). These data indicated that the VP1 protein observed during this period is likely attributable to residual viral particles from the initial infection rather than de novo synthesis.

Coimmunoprecipitation experiments indicated that SPNS1 was constitutively associated with SCARB2. Upon EV-A71 infection, viral VP1 formed a complex with SCARB2 and SPNS1 (Fig. 4C). However, pull-down experiments indicated that SPNS1 does not directly bind to VP1 in vitro (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Therefore, we speculate that their interaction exists under physiological conditions during viral infection. Moreover, we also investigated whether the knockout of SPNS1 would affect the localization of SCARB2 on lysosomes. As shown in Fig. 4D, the results showed that knockout of SPNS1 did not interfere with the colocalization of SCARB2 on lysosomes. Further research demonstrated that regardless of whether VP1 was ectopically expressed or not, SCARB2 and SPNS1 were both colocalized at the early endosomes, late endosomes, and lysosomes (Fig. 4 E and F). Similarly, confocal microscopy experiments also showed that VP1 colocalized with SCARB2 and SPNS1 at the early endosomes, late endosomes, and lysosomes after EV-A71 infection (Fig. 4F).

We next investigated whether the transporter activity of SPNS1 is required for its support of EV-A71 replication. It has been shown that arginine 76 (R76), glutamate 164 (E164), and histidine 427 (H427) of SPNS1 are critical residues for its transporter activity and mutation of any one of these residues abolishes its ability to mediate salvage of lysosomal LPC and LPE (11). We performed computational docking with SPNS1 and sphingosine and found direct binding of sphingosine into a putative substrate-binding pocket within SPNS1 (Fig. 5A). This model pinpointed R76 and H427—but not E164—as key residues potentially contacting sphingosine. We then generated the alanine substitutions at these sites and examined the roles of these residues in EV-A71 replication and pocket factor release. Consistent with the structural prediction, reconstitution of SPNS1-deficient HeLa cells with wild-type SPNS1 and SPNS1E164A but not SPNS1R76A or SPNS1H427A restored EV-A71 infectivity as determined by levels of VP1 protein (Fig. 5B) and viral RNA (Fig. 5C). Confocal microscopy results indicated that similar as wild-type SPNS1, these mutants were localized at the late endo/lysosomes and colocalized with SCARB2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). The simplest explanation for the differential role of E164 is that while the residue is important for the transport of LPC and LPE, it plays a less critical function in the transport of the EV-A71 sphingosine pocket factor.

We then investigated whether SPNS1 can mediate expulsion of the viral pocket factor. We prepared EV-A71N3-SP viruses in which the sphingosine pocket factor in the capsid was azido-labeled. Confocal microscopy results showed that N3-sphingosine was detected at the plasma membrane after 1 h of EV-A71N3-SP incubation at 4 °C (which allows viral attachment to the cell but not internalization) in both the control and HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, to track the intracellular trafficking of the azido-labeled pocket factor, we performed confocal microscopy at different time points after EV-A71N3-SP infection. The results showed that, 1 h postinfection, N3-sphingosine was localized in the lysosomes in all cell lines tested, including the control HeLa cells, HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells as well as HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells reconstituted with SPNS1, SPNS1E164A, SPNS1R76A, or SPNS1H427A (Fig. 5E). However, 3 h after EV-A71N3-SP infection, the pocket factor N3-SP was released from the lysosomes into the cytosol in wild-type HeLa cells and HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells reconstituted with wild-type SPNS1 or SPNS1E164A, but remained in the lysosomes of HeLa-ΔSPNS1 cells, or HeLa-ΔSPNS1 reconstituted with SPNS1R76A or SPNS1H427A (Fig. 5E). In these experiments, the uninfected or infected unlabeled EV-A71 control groups showed no detectable signals from azido-labeled sphingosine, suggesting the specificity of azide-alkyne click chemistry reaction (Fig. 5E). These results suggest that SPNS1 is required for uncoating of EV-A71 virions by mediating the release of viral pocket factor.

SPNS1 Is Required for Lethal EV-A71 Infection in Mice.

It has been reported that EV-A71 infection results in high viral loads and significant pathologic changes in the skeletal muscles and spinal cords in newborn mice (23). Since homozygous mutation of Spns1 is lethal, we prepared Spns1 conditional knockout mice in which the expression of Spns1 is specifically deficient in skeletal muscles (Spns1f/f Myf5-Cre). We infected newborn control Spns1f/+Myf5-Cre or Spns1f/f Myf5-Cre mice with EV-A71 (Shan Dong strain) intramuscularly. Five days postinfection, skeletal muscles and spinal cords were collected for analyses of viral loads and histopathology (Fig. 6A). The results indicated that EV-A71 RNA levels were ~100- and 1,000-fold lower in skeletal muscles and spinal cords of Spns1f/f Myf5-Cre mice than that of the control mice, respectively (Fig. 6A). Consistently, viral titers of EV-A71 in the skeletal muscles and spinal cords of Spns1f/f Myf5-Cre mice were ~10,000- and 1,000-fold lower than that in the control mice, respectively (Fig. 6B). Histopathological analysis demonstrated that the skeletal muscles of the control mice exhibited severe myositis with highly disrupted skeletal muscle fibers accompanied by severe leukocyte infiltrations after EV-A71 infection, whereas Spns1f/f Myf5-Cre mice had much lower degrees of skeletal muscle injury (Fig. 6 C, Left panels). The spleens of Spns1f/+Myf5-Cre mice infected with EV-A71 exhibited massive necrosis and the boundary between the white and red pulps was ambiguous, whereas Spns1f/f Myf5-Cre mice had clearly visible boundary between the white and red pulp and much reduced necrosis in the spleen (Fig. 6 C, Right panels). Immunohistochemical analysis showed dramatically reduced number of EV-A71 VP1-positive cells in the skeletal muscles and spleens of the Spns1f/f Myf5-Cre mice comparing to the Spns1f/+Myf5-Cre mice (Fig. 6C). In addition, severe symptoms such as hind limb paralysis at 5 dpi were observed in Spns1f/+Myf5-Cre but not Spns1f/f Myf5-Cre mice (Fig. 6 D, Left panels). Spns1f/f Myf5-Cre mice exhibited significantly higher survival rates compared with the control mice after EV-A71 infection (Fig. 6 D, Right panels). Collectively, these results suggest that Spns1 plays an important role in EV-A71 infection and pathogenesis in mice.

Discussion

EV-A71 is a pathogen that causes HFMD and dominates HFMD severe cases and death in children under 5 y old. Entry of EV-A71 into host cells is critical for its infection, which includes key steps such as attachment, endocytosis, and uncoating. Despite several receptors such as SCARB2, PSGL-1, SLC35B2, and Nucleolin have been shown to mediate attachment and endocytosis of EV-A71, host factors regulating the process of viral uncoating and genomic RNA release from the endo/lysosomes to the cytosol remain enigmatic. In this study, we identified SPNS1 as an essential factor required for release of the viral sphingosine pocket factor packed in the capsid, which is a prerequisite for EV-A71 uncoating and subsequent replication.

Several lines of evidence suggest that SPNS1 plays a critical role in release of the pocket factor. First, SPNS1 deficiency markedly inhibited EV-A71 infection and production of the progeny viruses, indicating its essential role in the viral life cycle. Second, only the endo/lysosomal-localized isoform of SPNS1 is capable to facilitate EV-A71 infection. Moreover, inhibition of endo/lysosome acidification diminished the effects of SPNS1 on EV-A71 infection, indicating that the function of SPNS1 is associated with late endo/lysosomes. Third, while SPNS1 deficiency inhibited replication of the viral genome after EV-A71 infection, it had no marked effects on the replication of viral gRNA directly transfected into the cytosol, suggesting an essential role of SPNS1 in the uncoating and release of the viral genome. Fourth, SPNS1 interacted with both EV-A71 VP1 and the entry receptor SCARB2, making it proximal to the canyon of the viral particles where the pocket factor is located. Fifth, experiments with EV-A71 that bears azido-labeled sphingosine pocket factor showed that while the pocket factor was released into cytosol at 3 h postinfection in wild-type cells, it is accumulated in the endo/lysosomes of SPNS1-deficient cells. Finally, computational docking suggests direct binding of sphingosine into a putative substrate binding/transporting pocket of SPNS1, mutation of the predicted key residues in the binding cavity of SPNS1, such as R76 and H427, resulted in loss of ability to facilitate viral pocket release from the lysosomes. These results collectively demonstrate that SPNS1 acts as a transporter of the viral pocket factor, which is required for uncoating of EV-A71 in the endo/lysosomes and release of viral gRNA into the cytosol for replication. Based on our study, we propose a model on how SPNS1 is required for the completion of EV-A71 life cycle. Upon trafficking into the endo/lysosomes, the acidic environment causes conformational changes of the SCARB2–VP1 complex, resulting in the binding of VP1 to SPNS1 and exposure of the pocket factor that is normally concealed within the pocket of VP1. Subsequently, SPNS1 acts as a transporter for release of the viral pocket factor into the cytosol. Once the viral pocket factor is released into the cytosol, the viral particle becomes unstable and enlarged. This structural change exposes VP4 and N terminus of VP1, which anchor to the endo/lysosomal membrane, forming a pore through which the viral genome is released into the cytosol.

In addition to EV-A71, SPNS1 deficiency also abolished replication of CVA16, which also utilizes SCARB2 as an entry and uncoating receptor (8). In these experiments, SPNS1 deficiency also dramatically reduced replication of the enteroviruses CVA10, ECHO11, EV-D68, CVA4, CVA24, and ECHO3, partially reduced replication of CVA9 and had no marked effects on the replication of CVB3 and CVB5. The molecular mechanisms of viral pocket factor release and uncoating vary among different enteroviruses. CVB3 and CVB5 utilize coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CAR) as the entry receptor. It has been demonstrated that, for CAR-mediated coxsackievirus entry, pocket factor release and uncoating is mediated by binding of viral particles to the receptor CAR under neutral pH conditions at the cell membrane (24), while SPNS1 is primarily localized in endosomes and lysosomes. For CVA9, previous studies have reported that CVA9 uncoating can occur in multivesicular bodies MVBs (derived from endosomes) or via cation-dependent mechanisms, which proceed efficiently under neutral pH conditions (25, 26). In contrast, it has been reported that SPNS1 exhibits better transporter activity under acidic conditions and the transporting efficiency is significantly reduced in neutral environments (11, 13), which may explain why deficiency of SPNS1 only showed limited influence on CVA9 replication. Knockdown of the other two SPNS family members, SPNS2 and SPNS3, either individually or simultaneously, had no marked effects on the infection of CVB3, CVB5, or CVA9, suggesting that SPNS2 and SPNS3 are not involved in infectivity of these viruses. SPNS2 is primarily localized on the plasma membrane and is responsible for the transport of sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) from the intracellular to the extracellular compartment (27), while SPNS3 has been found to express only in some immune cells such as Jurkat or THP1. Overall, since both EV-A71 and CVA16 utilize SCARB2 as a receptor and SCARB2 constitutively associates with SPNS1 as shown in our study, while the other examined enteroviruses utilize different receptors, it is possible that different viral receptors and pocket factors may dictate which transporters are used to support the uncoating process of different enteroviruses.

The function of SPNS1 in EV-A71 infection is also conserved in human and mouse cells. Animal infection experiments with Spns1-conditional knockout mice confirmed its critical role for EV-A71 infection and pathogenesis. In summary, we identified SPNS1 as a host factor for EV-A71, which plays a critical role in the transport/release of viral sphingosine pocket factor, a process required for uncoating of viral particles and release of the viral genome into the cytosol. Previous studies have reported that small molecules competing with native pocket factors for binding to the pocket of VP1 and preventing the viral uncoating process have antiviral effects against EV-A71 (28). In light of these observations, small molecules targeting SPNS1 may serve as antivirals to a wide range of enteroviruses and other viruses that utilizes SPNS1 as a viral pocket factor transporter.
