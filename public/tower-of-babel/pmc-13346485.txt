Age-related differences in whole-body muscle synergies during overarm throwing in children
Authors: Takuya Murakami, Ayane Kusafuka, Taishi Okegawa, Daiki Yamasaki, Yuto Sakakibara, Nadaka Hakariya, Naotsugu Kaneko, Hiroki Saito, Atsushi Sasaki, Kimitaka Nakazawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Overarm throwing is a uniquely human skill that requires precise whole-body coordination. Although throwing behavior emerges early in childhood, the neuromuscular mechanisms that support its development remain poorly characterized. Here, we provide novel evidence for the age-related differences of whole-body muscle synergies during maximum-effort throwing in preschool-aged (PS) and school-aged (SA) children. Electromyography was recorded from 16 muscles, and non-negative matrix factorization was applied to extract muscle synergies. We compared ball speed, number of synergies, synergy structure, and temporal consistency between groups. Ball speed was significantly higher in SA than PS (p = 0.0059). Yet, the number of synergies did not differ (p = 0.6227), suggesting that the dimensionality of synergies is largely established by the preschool years. Instead, developmental improvements were driven by structural and temporal reorganization. Trunk and upper-limb synergies merged into a single synergy in SA, reflecting improved postural integration. Moreover, the temporal variability of synergy activation was reduced in SA (p = 0.0006), indicating that movement sequences became more precise and stable. These findings reveal that early gains in throwing arise not from expanding synergy number but from reorganizing their structure and temporal coordination, offering mechanistic insight into how complex whole-body skills are refined during childhood.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50611-1.

Introduction

The ability to throw objects with speed and accuracy is a uniquely human skill that depends on sophisticated whole-body muscle coordination. Throwing has played a pivotal role throughout human history, from hunting in ancestral foraging societies to serving as a central skill in modern sports such as baseball and cricket1. Interestingly, throwing behavior emerges spontaneously in early childhood and develops even without formal instruction2, suggesting deeply embedded neural foundations with evolutionary significance in the human motor system. Overarm throwing, therefore, serves as an ideal model for investigating how humans develop and refine whole-body coordination. Understanding its developmental trajectory may provide insight into the maturation of neuromotor control.

To execute such complex movements, the central nervous system (CNS) must generate precisely coordinated motor commands across hundreds of muscles and thousands of motor units. A prevailing hypothesis is that the CNS simplifies this high-dimensional control problem through a modular strategy, commonly described as muscle synergies: coordinated patterns of muscle activation that reduce dimensionality of motor control3. These muscle synergies can be extracted from electromyographic (EMG) data using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), which reveals the underlying muscle coordination structures involved in task execution4. Developmental studies of locomotion demonstrated that muscle synergies follow a staged trajectory: newborns express two basic synergies, toddlers acquire two additional ones, and these four modules are refined but largely conserved through adulthood5. Running similarly shows an increase in modular complexity, from approximately six synergies in preschoolers to seven in sedentary adults6.

While developmental changes in locomotor muscle synergies are well characterized, much less is known about how coordination emerges and is refined across development during complex upper-limb tasks such as throwing. A recent study focusing only on upper-limb muscles reported that the number of throwing-related synergies remained stable from preschool to school age, but their structure reorganized across development7. However, throwing fundamentally requires precise integration of upper-limb, trunk, and lower-limb actions to generate power and transfer energy through the kinetic chain8. Importantly, the transition from preschool (typically under 6–7 years of age) to the early school years represents a pivotal developmental window when coordination and fundamental motor skills become more organized9. Indeed, classic observational work showed that the fundamental throwing pattern emerges by around six and a half years of age, with further refinements in speed and coordination continuing through approximately twelve years2. Thus, examining whole-body muscle synergies during this stage is critical to uncovering how children reorganize neuromuscular modules to achieve efficient throwing. By extending muscle synergy analysis beyond the upper limb, the present study addresses a key gap and provides evidence for how whole-body coordination, including the integration of upper-limb, trunk, and lower-limb modules, is refined during development. This whole-body perspective provides a powerful framework for quantifying developmental changes, as it captures the low-dimensional modular organization underlying high-dimensional, whole-body movements.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate age-related differences in whole-body muscle synergies during overarm throwing by comparing preschool-aged and elementary school-aged children. Based on locomotor studies showing both early establishment of basic modules5 and later increases in module number during adolescence6, as well as prior evidence of structural reorganization in throwing synergies7, we hypothesized that (i) the number and structure of muscle synergies would differ between the two age groups and these age-related differences may be associated with differences in throwing performance (i.e., ball speed), and (ii) synergy activation timing would become more consistent across trials. By characterizing age-related differences in throwing-related motor modules, this study aims to advance understanding of the neural mechanisms that may support the development of motor skills and provide a basis for evidence-based training and intervention strategies in pediatric populations.

Results

Throwing performance and the number of muscle synergies

We recorded maximum-effort overarm throws from 11 preschoolers (PS; mean ± s.d. age = 62.2 ± 8.0 months) and 9 school-aged children (SA; mean ± s.d. age = 100.3 ± 14.4 months). None of the participants had prior experience in organized sports involving overarm throwing (e.g., baseball or softball). Participants threw a ball toward a net 2 m away while surface EMG was simultaneously recorded from 16 muscles spanning the upper limbs, trunk, and lower limbs. All subsequent statistical analyses were conducted with n = 11 for PS and n = 9 for SA. Detailed analytical procedures are provided in the Methods section.

Ball speed, derived from high-speed camera tracking, was significantly higher in SA (mean ± s.d.: 33.6 ± 10.2 km/h) than in PS (mean ± s.d.: 21.4 ± 6.2 km/h) (t (18) = 3.12, p = 0.0059, d = 1.40, Fig. 1A), reflecting age-related improvements in throwing performance.

Roberton’s developmental sequence score, an observational index of overarm-throwing technique based on component-wise developmental stages, obtained by summing the steps of the five throwing components (Minimum score = 5, Maximum score = 17)10, was significantly higher in SA (mean ± s.d.: 11.0 ± 2.5) than in PS (mean ± s.d.: 7.6 ± 2.5) (t (18) = 3.63, p = 0.0019, d = 1.63, Table S1), indicating that SA children had progressed to a more mature stage of throwing technique development than PS children. Individual component scores for each participant are also provided in the Supplementary data (Table S1).

To evaluate underlying coordination, EMG signals were processed (band-pass filtered, rectified, low-pass filtered, normalized, and time-interpolated) and decomposed using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to extract low-dimensional muscle synergies; only solutions achieving ≥ 90% variance accounted for (VAF) were retained. The average number of muscle synergies did not differ significantly between PS (mean ± s.d.: 6.0 ± 0.6) and SA (mean ± s.d.: 6.2 ± 1.3) (t (18) = 0.50, p = 0.6227, d = 0.23, Fig. 1B). This indicates that the overall complexity of motor coordination, as reflected by the number of synergies, remains stable between the two groups, despite differences in throwing performance.

The achieved power values (α = 0.05, two-tailed) were 0.84 for ball speed, 0.97 for Roberton’s developmental sequence score, and 0.08 for the number of synergies, which should be considered when interpreting this nonsignificant result.

Age-related differences in muscle synergy structure

Clustering analysis was applied to characterize differences in synergy structure between the two groups. This revealed eight muscle synergies in both PS and SA. Among them, four were common synergies, while four were specific to each PS and SA (Fig. 2A). By NMF, synergies were extracted separately for each participant. Most children expressed only a subset of the synergy patterns identified in the analysis. Since these subsets differed across children, pooling and clustering all individual synergies yielded a group-level set of distinct patterns. Thus, the “eight synergies” refer to the number of synergy clusters identified across participants, not what is necessarily present in every child. To visualize inter-individual variability in the expression of group-level synergy patterns, we report the occurrence of each synergy cluster across participants in Table S2. Synergies were classified based on whether pairwise cosine similarity exceeded 0.9.

We further examined cross-group reorganization using cosine similarity and reconstruction analyses. Two patterns of developmental reorganization emerged (Fig. 2B):

In SA, a new muscle synergy (SA-5) emerged through the merging of two PS modules, PS-5 (rectus abdominis-dominant) and PS-6 (pectoralis major-dominant), indicating integration of trunk and upper-limb coordination. The reconstruction coefficients for PS-5 and PS-6 were 0.6353 and 0.4336 respectively, and a cosine similarity between the reconstructed synergy and SA-5 was 0.9548. One more synergy (SA-7) emerged through the merging of two PS modules, PS-7 (right tibialis anterior-dominant) and PS-8 (right rectus femoris-dominant), reflecting a more integrated lower-limb coordination pattern in SA. The reconstruction coefficients for PS-7 and PS-8 were 0.7466 and 0.2556 respectively, and a cosine similarity between the reconstructed synergy and SA-7 was 0.9027.

These findings demonstrate that, although the number of muscle synergies remained unchanged, age-related differences were observed in their qualitative structural organization.

Temporal consistency of synergy activation

To evaluate the timing stability of each synergy activation, we calculated the centroid of activity (CoA) for each temporal activation profile. The standard deviation of CoA across trials was significantly lower in SA (mean ± s.d.: 8.0% ± 2.3%) than in PS (mean ± s.d.: 11.2% ± 1.7%) (t (18) = 4.16, p = 0.0006, d = 1.87, Fig. 3B). This reduction in variability indicates that synergy activation timing became more consistent across trials in the school-aged group. The achieved power for this comparison was 0.98. Representative activation profiles illustrate this effect, with narrower dispersion of CoA in SA compared to PS (Fig. 3A).

These results suggest that developmental improvements in throwing performance were supported not by increased synergy number but by structural reorganization and enhanced temporal precision.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we examined age-related differences in whole-body muscle coordination during overarm throwing by analyzing EMG activity from 16 upper-limb, trunk, and lower-limb muscles in PS and SA. Using NMF, we extracted low-dimensional muscle synergies and compared their number, structure, and temporal organization across groups. The main findings were: (i) throwing performance, as indexed by ball speed, was higher in SA than PS; (ii) throwing skill development, as indexed by Roberton’s developmental sequence score, was more advanced in SA than PS; (iii) the number of muscle synergies did not differ between groups; (iv) between-group differences in synergy structure were observed, including patterns consistent with module merging; and (v) synergy activation timing was more consistent in SA. Together, these age-related differences suggest that older children achieve higher throwing performance alongside higher throwing skill level. This improvement appears to be associated less with an increased number of synergies and more with differences in synergy structure, potentially supporting a more advanced technique, and with more consistent temporal coordination.

Age-related differences in muscle synergy structure

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, the number of muscle synergies did not differ significantly between PS and SA. This suggests that a neuromuscular coordination capacity for throwing is already present by the preschool years. Importantly, however, the present study focused on a relatively narrow developmental window from preschool to school age. Thus, the absence of group differences in synergy number should not be taken to imply that the repertoire remains fixed throughout life. It remains possible that synergy number increases later in development, particularly during adolescence, when musculoskeletal growth, neural maturation, and structured training may further expand the motor repertoire. Indeed, studies of locomotion have reported such developmental increases: toddlers initially express four basic locomotor synergies, which are subsequently refined and expanded into the adult repertoire5, while the number of running synergies increases from approximately six in preschool children to seven or more in sedentary adults6. Our results therefore provide a cross-sectional snapshot showing that, despite similar synergy numbers, the two age groups differed qualitatively in synergy structure.

One notable reorganization observed in the present study was the merging of trunk and upper-limb modules: the rectus abdominis-dominant synergy (PS-5) and the pectoralis major-dominant synergy (PS-6) combined into a single module in school-aged children (SA-5). This integration reflects improved trunk and upper-limb coordination, a critical factor for generating greater ball speed11,12. The more integrated trunk and upper-limb co-activation pattern observed in SA may be consistent with maturation of task-specific posture-action coordination during throwing. This interpretation is also consistent with the higher Roberton’s developmental sequence trunk-component scores in SA (Table S1), indicating more advanced trunk involvement in the throwing technique. Moreover, in PS, the right tibialis anterior-dominant synergy (PS-7) and right rectus femoris-dominant synergy (PS-8) were independently activated. In SA, these synergies may merge and work more coordinately (SA-7), leading to a more integrated pattern during the landing phase and possibly facilitating energy transfer. This interpretation is also consistent with the higher Roberton’s developmental sequence foot-component scores in SA, indicating more advanced landing and foot-action patterns (Table S1).

Together, these findings demonstrate that module merging may contribute to age-related differences in synergy organization and may be associated with higher throwing performance.

Inferred functional role of each synergy

Despite these reorganizations, four synergies were commonly observed in both the preschool and school-aged groups. This persistence may support the notion, consistent with earlier studies2, a neural capacity supporting throwing is available by early childhood, while also reflecting shared task demands and practice environments. Since we did not collect detailed kinematic/kinetic measures, the functional interpretations below should be considered inferential. Below, we describe the possible functional roles of the observed synergies, excluding those described above, using conventional throwing phases as a reference framework rather than implying that children executed these phases in the same manner as skilled adult throwers. PS-1/SA-1, involving the deltoid, infraspinatus, and trapezius, appears to relate to humeral elevation, scapulothoracic control, and eccentric stabilization during arm raising and deceleration13,14. PS-2/SA-2 (left rectus femoris) and PS-3/SA-3 (left tibialis anterior) may reflect stride-leg stabilization and braking after landing, potentially contributing to energy transfer along the kinetic chain15. PS-4/SA-4 dominated by the right soleus may be engaged from initial contact to landing, potentially contributing to ankle stabilization and control during weight acceptance. PS-6/SA-6, comprising the erector spinae and left biceps femoris, may suggest a back-side support strategy that provides a stable base and braking support for efficient pelvis–trunk rotation and energy transfer immediately before ball release16–18. SA-8, dominated by left soleus, may reflect an independent neural drive to the soleus to adjust the interaction with the ground. This pattern is also consistent with a potential role of the soleus in modulating ankle/foot stiffness around stride-foot contact, which could help stabilize the distal segment and support effective braking/force transmission16,19. Taken together, these modules appear to underlie essential functions such as drive-leg stabilization, scapular and humeral control, trunk–pelvis coupling, and stride-leg braking. Their persistence suggests that a neural capacity supporting throwing is already present by early childhood, within strong task and biomechanical constraints, and serves as a basis for later refinement.

Refinement of temporal coordination

A key finding was the reduction in variability of synergy activation timing (i.e., CoA) in SA. This result contrasts with a previous study focusing solely on upper-limb muscle, which reported minimal age-related timing differences7 and underscores the importance of analyzing whole-body coordination. In preschoolers, immature sequencing of trunk, limb and postural actions likely contributes to trial-to-trial variability20,21. For example, if trunk rotation begins too early or too late relative to stride-foot contact, or if arm acceleration is not well synchronized with trunk motion, the overall sequence becomes less consistent across trials, leading to broader temporal dispersion of synergy activation. Alternatively, the higher timing variability in PS may reflect an exploratory coordination strategy during skill development.

Several factors may account for the observed age-related differences in CoA. First, maturation and task-specific experience promote tighter synchronization of support actions, trunk rotation, and arm acceleration with key mechanical events such as stride-foot contact, peak trunk rotation, and ball release, thereby reducing the range of timing solutions expressed across trials20,21. Second, the timing patterns of pelvic and trunk rotation, as well as lower-limb contribution, vary with both skill level and developmental stage, but with increasing maturity, these patterns converge toward a more stereotyped temporal sequence22,23. Consequently, school-aged children demonstrate more reproducible coordination, with synergy more precisely aligned to critical mechanical events24,25.

This reduction in timing variability likely reflects a developmental shift toward a more stable and repeatable organization of whole-body synergy timing. Importantly, since we did not directly quantify movement efficiency or accuracy, the present findings should be interpreted as changes in within-subject, trial-to-trial consistency rather than performance quality. In this context, the higher CoA variability in PS may indicate a less stabilized or more exploratory coordination strategy during skill acquisition, whereas the lower variability in SA suggests more consistent timing of muscle recruitment across trials.

Neural mechanisms underlying synergy reorganization

Muscle synergy organization can be flexibly reconfigured or fine-tuned to improve performance during development5,6, adaptation26–28, and skill learning29,30. The reorganization observed here likely reflects converging influences of neural maturation, motor experience, and biomechanical growth. Neural factors such as myelination, increased synaptic efficiency, and refinement of corticospinal pathways, enable more selective control of muscle subgroups within existing synergies31,32, supporting both structural modifications and sharpening temporal activation patterns6,33. Experience through spontaneous play and repeated throwing likely promotes effective temporal sequences via trial-and-error learning and Hebbian-like plasticity, biasing synergy merging or fractionation toward task-effective compositions34,35. In parallel, biomechanical scaling during growth enhances stability and mechanical leverage, reducing the need for compensatory timing adjustments36. Together, these processes drive selective synergy reorganization while preserving the core modular repertoire.

Limitations

Since our design is cross-sectional, cohort differences (PS vs. SA) may reflect age-related differences but do not directly demonstrate within-individual reorganization or a developmental trajectory. Future longitudinal studies are required to confirm whether similar changes occur within the same individuals across development. Moreover, our clustering analysis summarizes recurring synergy patterns across participants, but it does not imply that all children shared an identical set of synergies. Some clusters were observed in only a small subset of participants. Therefore, the reported cluster repertoire should be interpreted as group-level diversity.

Conclusions

Whole-body muscle synergies supporting overarm throwing are established early in childhood but undergo structural reorganization and temporal refinement between preschool and school age. These changes, particularly the integration of upper-limb and trunk synergies, the integration of lower-limb synergies, and improved consistency of activation timing, likely underlie age-related differences in throwing performance. Although the number of synergies remained unchanged in this developmental window, later stages may bring quantitative increases. Overall, early throwing skill enhancement depends less on expanding the number of synergies and more on refining their structure and timing, representing an important stage in the broader trajectory of motor development.

Materials and methods

Participants

11 preschoolers (PS; 5 males, 6 females; mean ± s.d. age = 62.2 ± 8.0 months, range 47–77 months) and 9 school-aged children (SA; 4 males, 5 females; mean ± s.d. age = 100.3 ± 14.4 months, range 85–131 months) participated in this study. None had current musculoskeletal injuries or a history of neurological impairment within the past year, or prior experience in sports involving overarm throwing (e.g., baseball or softball). Although hand anthropometrics were not directly measured in the present study, since the participants ranged in age from approximately 4 to 11 years, we additionally characterized age-appropriate hand size using published normative data for Japanese children37. Based on these reference data, wrist-to-middle-fingertip length is approximately 11 cm at 4 years of age and approximately 15.5 cm at 11 years of age. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all procedures were approved by the local committee in the University of Tokyo (reference number: 895-6). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all participants.

Experimental tasks

All experiments were conducted in an indoor laboratory. Participants were instructed to throw a ball with maximum effort toward a net located 2 m away. After a brief warm-up and familiarization, each participant performed 8 to 11 throws. The diameter of the ball was 7 cm.

Data recordings

Ball kinematics were recorded with two synchronized high-speed cameras (DSC-RX10M4, SONY, Japan; 960 fps) placed behind and to the side of the participant. Synchronization was achieved with an LED flash triggered by a commercial device (FA-WRC1M and FA-WRR1, SONY, Japan). The center coordinates of the ball were extracted from the recorded images without physical markers, using the same methods as in a previous study38. Each recording window lasted ~ 1000 ms, covering the throwing motion from initiation to several tens of milliseconds after ball release. Trials were excluded if the ball moved out of the camera’s view or if the LED light flash occurred outside the data acquisition window due to motion variability.

We simultaneously recorded EMG activity from 16 muscles spanning the upper limbs, trunk, and lower limbs using wireless sensors (Pico EMG, Cometa Srl, Italy). Recorded muscles included pectoralis major (PM), middle deltoid (MD), infraspinatus (ISP), and middle trapezius (MT) on the throwing side; rectus abdominis (RA), erector spinae at the T10 vertebral level (ES/T10), rectus femoris (RF), biceps femoris (BF), tibialis anterior (TA), and soleus (SOL) bilaterally. Accordingly, surface EMG electrodes were placed on representative muscles that are known to be predominantly active during baseball pitching as a whole-body movement involving coordinated contributions of the upper limb, trunk, and lower limb13,15,39. EMG signals were sampled at 2000 Hz. Synchronization with video data was achieved using the LED flash signal.

Kinematic analysis

Ball tracking in the high-speed camera images was performed using DeepLabCut40, a deep learning-based automatic image recognition system. The tracking models were trained using manually labeled images from one trial per participant. For each trial, 20 frames were automatically selected by built-in frame extraction algorithm of DeepLabCut, which uses the k-means method to identify the most informative frames for manual labeling. Separate models were developed for each camera view and trained for 100,000 iterations. These trained models were used to automatically track and digitize the center of the ball in all remaining trials. This tracking procedure was identical to that used in previous studies involving throwing tasks38,41. The accuracy of this method has been validated against conventional methods, with the absolute distances in coordinates average ranging from 15.5 to 29.4 mm, and correlation coefficients between the two methods ranging from 0.932 to 0.99941.

Two points during the throwing movement, Start and Ball release, were detected using the position coordinates of the ball, and the analysis window was defined as the phase from Start to Ball release. Hand kinematic data were low-pass filtered at 15 Hz. Following prior reaching studies that define movement onset using a percentage threshold of the speed profile, Start was defined as the time point when the throwing-hand speed exceeded 10% of its peak value within each trial42. Ball release was determined by qualitative observation of the images from the side-view camera. Ball speed was calculated as the magnitude of the velocity vector at the moment of ball release. MaxHTD was defined as the time point when the throwing-side hand was farthest from the net (target), identified as the frame with the maximum hand-to-target distance within the analysis window.

Throwing skill assessment

Throwing skill development was assessed using Roberton’s developmental sequence for overarm throwing. Five throwing components were scored from the video recordings according to established criteria: (1) arm preparation backswing, (2) humerus action, (3) forearm action, (4) trunk action, and (5) foot action43. Each component was assigned a developmental step score, and the total Roberton’s developmental sequence score was calculated by summing the five component scores (minimum score = 5, maximum score = 17), with higher scores indicating a more advanced developmental stage of throwing skill. Higher scores have also been associated with better throwing performance10.

EMG processing

EMG data were processed using custom MATLAB script (R2024a; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Signals were band-pass filtered between 40 and 450 Hz, demeaned, full-wave rectified, and low-pass filtered at 10 Hz (Finite Impulse Response filter, or FIR)6,44. Subsequently, each muscle’s amplitude was normalized to its peak value within each trial45,46. Clustering muscle synergies

To characterize differences in synergy structure between the two groups, representative synergy vectors were first identified within each group using k-means clustering. Clustering was performed in MATLAB using the k-means function (Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox) with the squared Euclidean metric. Initial cluster centroid positions were selected uniformly at random from the synergy vectors to be clustered. For each candidate number of clusters (k = 2–12), the clustering was repeated 3000 times with different initial centroid estimates and, the replicate with the smallest within-cluster point-to-centroid sum of squares was retained6. The optimal number of clusters was determined as the value of k that maximized the average silhouette score28,51, and the centroid of each cluster was interpreted as a representative synergy pattern within the group. When multiple synergies from the same participant were assigned to the same cluster, only the synergy with the highest cosine similarity to the cluster centroid was retained, and the remaining synergies (lower cosine similarity) were removed52. Differences in the distribution of synergy types between the PS and SA groups were then examined to explore developmental changes in muscle coordination.

Muscle synergy similarity

To quantitatively assess the similarity of muscle synergies between the PS and SA groups, we calculated pairwise cosine similarity between synergy vectors53. Each synergy vector was normalized to unit length prior to comparison to ensure that only the direction (i.e., muscle weighting pattern) was considered independent of magnitude. where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{i}$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{j}$$\end{document}represent two synergy vectors and denotes the dot product. A similarity value of 1 indicates perfect similarity, whereas values closer to 0 indicate dissimilarity. To identify shared muscle synergy patterns between developmental stages, we compared synergy vectors across groups (PS vs. SA). Synergy pairs with a cosine similarity ≥ 0.9 were considered to reflect similar muscle coordination structures57,58. The proportion of highly similar synergy pairs was then calculated to evaluate the extent of similarity between the two groups.

Merging of muscle synergies

To evaluate developmental changes in synergy structure, we analyzed the merging of muscle synergies between groups54. In this study, merging was defined as the case in which a synergy vector observed in the SA group could be reconstructed as a linear combination of two or more synergy vectors from PS group. If two or more PS synergies met this criterion and the reconstructed vector showed high similarity to the original SA synergy (cosine similarity ≥ 0.9), the case was classified as a merging event. The number and proportion of such merged synergies were calculated to quantify the extent of integration. This merging process was interpreted as a developmental consolidation of motor modules, potentially reflecting increased efficiency in neuromuscular control.

Temporal analysis of synergy activation

For each synergy identified in each participant, the centroid of activity (CoA) defined as the center of gravity of the corresponding temporal pattern was calculated using the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CoA\:=\:\frac{\sum\:_{t=1}^{201}t*C\left(t\right)}{\sum\:_{t=1}^{201}C\left(t\right)}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C\left(t\right)$$\end{document} represents the temporal pattern at normalized time frame t (201 frames per trial). To facilitate interpretation, CoA values were linearly rescaled from frame indices to a percentage scale (0–100%), such that 0% corresponds to Start and 100% to Ball release time.

This metric was employed to objectively characterize the temporal distribution of each synergy55. By weighing each time point according to its activation magnitude, CoA reflects the overall timing and structure of activation throughout the movement phase, rather than focusing on a single peak value. Furthermore, by calculating the standard deviation of CoA across repetitions, we were able to assess the consistency of activation timing, which serves as an index of movement stability. We quantified timing stability at the participant level as SD of CoA, defined as the mean of the per-synergy standard deviations of CoA calculated across repetitions within that participant.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB (R2024a; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare ball speed, the number of extracted muscle synergies, and the standard deviation of the CoA between groups. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed), and exact p values are reported. Effect size (ES) was calculated using Cohen’s d56.

We recruited 11 participants in PS and 9 in SA without a priori power analysis. We instead conducted a sensitivity analysis in G*Power, which indicated that an effect size of 1.33 would be necessary to obtain a power of 80%. Post-hoc (achieved) power was computed in G*Power (α = 0.05, two-tailed) based on the observed effect sizes.
