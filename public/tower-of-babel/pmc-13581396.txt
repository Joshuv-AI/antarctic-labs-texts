Associations between specific non-sustained ventricular tachycardia characteristics and sudden cardiac death in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Yu Zhang, Fang Qin, Yunfan Guan, Jie Wang, Lutong Pu, Yu Xia, Channa Zhang, Dong Wang, Xueyi Wu, Mo Zhang, Limei Wang, Wen Jiang, Lianming Kang, Lianjun Xu, Yucheng Chen, Lei Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsNon-sustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT) is a well-established risk factor for sudden cardiac death (SCD) in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). However, its characteristics have been overlooked. This study evaluates the prognostic value of NSVT characteristics, particularly coupling intervals, for HCM risk stratification.Methods and resultsThis retrospective, multicentre study included 2625 HCM patients. Twenty-four-hour Holter recordings were assessed for NSVT and its characteristics (frequency, duration, and coupling intervals). The primary endpoint was SCD; the secondary endpoints were cardiovascular and all-cause death. Two thousand five hundred and eighty-four patients were analysed (49.6 ± 15.2 years; 62.4% male). Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia occurred in 320 (12.4%). During the median follow-up of 4.4 years, there were 221 all-cause deaths (8.6%), 159 cardiovascular deaths (6.2%), and 64 SCDs (2.5%). Among characteristics, the second coupling interval best predicted adverse outcomes (optimal cut-off = 394 ms). Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia frequency and duration were not prognostic. In univariable and multivariable analyses, a second coupling interval < 394 ms markedly increased the risk of SCD [hazard ratio (HR) 8.36, 95% confidence interval (CI) 4.33–16.12, P < 0.001], cardiovascular death (HR 3.64, 95% CI 2.07–6.37, P < 0.001), and all-cause death (HR 3.26, 95% CI 1.97–5.41, P < 0.001) vs. absence of NSVT, whereas a second coupling interval ≥ 394 ms did not. Replacing NSVT with malignant NSVT (NSVT with short second coupling interval) significantly improved SCD risk algorithms.ConclusionA short second coupling interval of NSVT poses a higher SCD risk in HCM than a long one. Detailed dissection of NSVT characteristics may improve decision-making for implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation in HCM.Keywords: Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia, Coupling interval, Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Sudden cardiac death

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common inherited cardiovascular disease. It is characterized by significant left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy, cardiac fibrosis, and impaired diastolic function.1,2 The prevalence of HCM in the general adult population is reported to be 1 per 200–500 people,3 and the incidence of sudden cardiac death (SCD) can reach as high as 0.5–2% per year in people with HCM.1,2 Risk stratification for SCD in people with HCM is crucial, as it aids decision-making for SCD prevention, including implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) implantation. However, the risks associated with ICD implantation, such as vascular damage and thrombosis, also indicate the importance of meticulous selection of candidates for ICD intervention.

Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT) is a well-established independent risk factor for SCD in patients with HCM, and it has been incorporated into the existing SCD risk models.1,4 However, these models simply consider the presence or absence of NSVT, overlooking the weight of its specific characteristics, including its frequency, duration, and rate, which may largely affect patient prognosis.5,6 Previous studies on the associations of NSVT characteristics with SCD risk in patients with HCM have reached conflicting conclusions.7–10 Current guidelines have also not reached a consensus on the prognostic value of the characteristics of NSVT.1,5 Specifically, the European guidelines state that there is no evidence that NSVT characteristics influence SCD risk in HCM,1 whereas the American guidelines recommend greater emphasis on NSVT episodes that are frequent, longer, or faster during monitoring.5 This discrepancy highlights the complexity of the relationship between NSVT characteristics and SCD risk. Therefore, a more comprehensive analysis of these NSVT characteristics is warranted.

Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia coupling intervals, defined as R-R intervals between the first NSVT beat and the preceding sinus beat (referred as the first coupling interval) or between two consecutive NSVT beats, offer more precise information of NSVT rate. Previous research has identified the significant role of the NSVT coupling interval for predicting prognosis in other cardiac diseases11; however, its predictive value in patients with HCM remains unknown. This study aims to comprehensively investigate the value of NSVT characteristics, with a particular focus on the NSVT coupling interval, for predicting SCD risk in patients with HCM.

Methods

Study population

This multicentre retrospective study recruited 2625 consecutive patients with HCM who underwent baseline Holter monitoring at three tertiary medical centres (Fuwai Hospital, West China Hospital, and The Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University) in China between 1999 and 2023. All patients underwent a systematic clinical evaluation, the data of which were collected from each patient at enrolment. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy was confirmed by echocardiographic and/or cardiac magnetic resonance demonstration of LV hypertrophy with a maximal LV wall thickness of ≥15 mm (or ≥13 mm in patients with a family history of HCM) in the absence of any other cardiac or systemic disease capable of producing such a magnitude of hypertrophy (e.g. uncontrolled hypertension, cardiac valve disease, and phenocopies). A total of 2584 patients were successfully followed at least once and were included in the final analysis, while 41 patients (1.56%) were lost to follow-up. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital, West China Hospital, and The Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Written informed consent was waived because this study was retrospective.

Holter electrocardiogram monitoring

Three-channel 24 h Holter electrocardiogram monitoring was performed in all patients with HCM using a digitized Holter analyser. Holter electrocardiogram monitoring is routinely performed, often within the first 2 days of hospital admission. Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia was defined as three or more consecutive ventricular beats with the fastest heart rate ≥ 120 b.p.m., lasting for <30 s. All Holter data were digital; therefore, a detailed evaluation of NSVT and its characteristics was possible. For each confirmed NSVT episode, the coupling intervals were initially measured automatically by the system software and were subsequently reviewed and confirmed by experienced electrophysiologists. During Holter monitoring, the following NSVT characteristics were collected for each patient: (i) the frequency (number of episodes) of NSVT; (ii) the longest duration (measured by number of beats) of NSVT episodes; and (iii) the coupling interval (ms) of the first, second, third, and the last, second-to-last, and third-to-last NSVT beats (in cases of multiple NSVT episodes, we evaluated the fastest episode). All automated measurements were reviewed and confirmed by experienced electrophysiologists.

Follow-up and outcomes

The primary endpoint of the study was SCD, defined as witnessed sudden and unexpected death with or without documented ventricular fibrillation within 1 h of new symptoms or as nocturnal death with no antecedent history of worsening symptoms. Resuscitation from cardiac arrest and appropriate ICD shock therapy for ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation were considered to be equivalent to SCD. The secondary endpoints were cardiovascular death and all-cause death. All events were obtained by telephone interviews, follow-up letters, clinic visits, or mortality surveillance system. All events were carefully recorded, checked, and verified by an independent group of clinicians.

Sudden cardiac death model improvement analysis

The 2014 HCM Risk-SCD model was used as the reference model in the model improvement analysis. The model result generated using the published coefficients of the 2014 HCM Risk-SCD model is provided in Supplementary material online, Figure S1. We aimed to investigate whether incorporating more specific NSVT characteristics could enhance the performance of the HCM Risk-SCD model. However, modifying an internal metric and then directly applying the original model coefficients would be statistically inappropriate; therefore, we recalibrated both the original and updated models using our cohort data while maintaining the original model structure. Specifically, the original model was refitted using all seven original variables of the HCM Risk-SCD model; the updated model was refitted using the same six original variables, with NSVT variable replaced by modified ‘malignant NSVT’ definition. The coefficients for all variables were recalibrated to reflect the characteristics of our study population.

We also investigated the value of malignant NSVT across different AHA/ACC ICD implantation recommendation classes. Our analysis focused on the Class 2a group and the Class 2b candidate group. The Class 2a group was defined according to the original guideline definition, whereas the Class 2b candidate group was defined as patients who did not meet Class 2a criteria and with no evidence of extensive LGE (those who were potential candidates for Class 2b indication based primarily on the condition of NSVT).

Genetic testing

As previously described,12 a total of 621 patients underwent genetic testing. A total of eight sarcomeric genes (MYBPC3, MYH7, MYL2, MYL3, TNNT2, TNNI3, TPM1, and ACTC1), which are highly associated with HCM pathogenicity, were evaluated in this study. Variant pathogenicity was determined according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. Variants classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), while categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage). Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. One-way analysis of variance and the Kruskal–Wallis test were used to compare continuous variables among patients with different NSVT characteristics. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The follow-up time for each patient was calculated from the date of their enrolment to the date of the occurrence of the study endpoint, death from other causes, or the most recent follow-up, whichever came first.

Among the clinical variables included in the analysis, left atrial diameter (22 missing, 0.9%), NYHA functional class (62 missing, 2.4%), and late gadolinium enhancement (644 missing, 25.0%) were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations.

Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and to evaluate the associations of NSVT characteristics with outcomes. Multivariable model Ⅰ was adjusted for age and sex. The multivariable model Ⅱ was fitted using a backward stepwise selection approach, with all baseline variables (age, sex, syncope, NYHA functional class, atrial fibrillation, medical history, family history, maximal LV wall thickness, left atrial diameter, LV ejection fraction, LV outflow tract gradient, LV apical aneurysm, late gadolinium enhancement, and treatment strategy) initially included. Linearity assumption for continuous variables was assessed in the Cox regression models. For predictors showing evidence of nonlinearity, a quadratic term was incorporated into the Cox regression models. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the predictive performance of the NSVT characteristics for primary endpoint, and the C-statistics and cut-off values were calculated.

In the model improvement analysis, time-dependent analyses were performed to evaluate the prediction of 5-year SCD risk. The C-statistics of the original and updated SCD models were calculated and compared using DeLong’s test. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated for both models. Model calibration was assessed by evaluating the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes using calibration plot and calibration slope.

All statistical analyses were performed and all figures were drawn using R software (v. 4.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

A total of 2584 patients with HCM who underwent Holter electrocardiogram monitoring were analysed, with a mean age of 49.6 ± 15.2 years. Of these, 1611 (62.4%) were male. Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia was documented in 320 patients (12.4%). The baseline characteristics of the patients stratified by the presence or absence of SCD are presented in Table 1. Compared with the patients without SCD, those with SCD had a significantly higher prevalence of NSVT, more frequent family history of both HCM and SCD, greater LV wall thickness, and lower LV ejection fraction. Patients who underwent SRT were less likely to experience SCD.

Comparison of the predictive value of non-sustained ventricular tachycardia characteristics in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

In this cohort of patients with HCM, the presence of NSVT was associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes (see Supplementary material online, Tables S1–S3). To obtain a more nuanced understanding of the prognostic value of NSVT, the first, second, third, and the last, second-to-last, and third-to-last coupling intervals of NSVT on Holter monitoring were analysed. Sudden cardiac death occurred in 17 of 320 patients (5.3%) with NSVT. The frequency [1.0 (1.0–2.0) vs. 2.0 (1.0–3.0), P = 0.827], duration [5.0 (4.0–8.0) vs. 4.0 (3.0–7.0), P = 0.239), first coupling interval (620.7 ± 200.3 vs. 678.5 ± 209.5 ms, P = 0.264), the second-to-last coupling interval (417.8 ± 103.2 vs. 459.6 ± 154.9 ms, P = 0.086), and the last coupling interval (830.2 ± 319.1 vs. 898.9 ± 386.6 ms, P = 0.512) of NSVT were comparable between the SCD and non-SCD groups. The second coupling interval (393.4 ± 86.3 vs. 486.4 ± 135.3 ms, P < 0.001), third coupling interval (386.1 ± 66.1 vs. 446.1 ± 120.6 ms, P = 0.002), and the third-to-last coupling interval (438.0 ± 89.7 vs. 587.6 ± 280.0 ms, P = 0.015) were significantly shorter in the SCD group than in the non-SCD group (Figure 2). The ROC curve analysis showed that the C-statistics of the second coupling interval of NSVT to predict SCD (0.763, 0.645–0.881) is higher than that of the other characteristics of NSVT (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2).

The secondary endpoint analysis showed the same results. Specifically, the second coupling interval of the NSVT beats exhibited better prediction ability for cardiovascular death (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3) and all-cause death (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4) than the other NSVT characteristics.

Associations between the second coupling interval and outcomes

The ROC curve analysis demonstrated an optimal cut-off value of 394 ms (sensitivity: 0.706; specificity: 0.805) for the second coupling interval of NSVT for predicting SCD (Figure 3). According to this cut-off value, the study population was divided into three groups: non-NSVT (n = 2264), NSVT with a long second coupling interval (≥394 ms, n = 249), and NSVT with a short second coupling interval (<394 ms, n = 71). Patients with NSVT with a short second coupling interval were more likely to present with syncope and atrial fibrillation, and they had a greater LV wall thickness and a larger left atrial diameter (Table 2).

During the median follow-up period of 4.4 years, 221 patients (8.6%) died from all-cause death, 159 (6.2%) from cardiovascular death, and 64 (2.5%) from SCD. The univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses showed that patients with NSVT with a short second coupling interval had a markedly higher risk of SCD than those without NSVT (univariable model: HR 9.83, 95% CI 5.20–18.56, P < 0.001; multivariable model I: HR 10.79, 95% CI 5.66–20.54, P < 0.001; multivariable model II: HR 8.36, 95% CI 4.33–16.12, P < 0.001). However, the risk of SCD was similar between patients with NSVT with a long second coupling interval and patients without NSVT (univariable model: HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.41–2.56, P = 0.968; multivariable model I: HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.45–2.87, P = 0.789; multivariable model II: HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.31–2.03, P = 0.623) (Table 3; Supplementary material online, Table S4). The survival analysis for cardiovascular death and all-cause death showed the same results (Table 3; Supplementary material online, Tables S5 and S6). The results of the survival analysis suggested that the presence of a short second coupling interval in NSVT was highly malignant, while a long second coupling interval may not provide important prognostic information in patients with HCM. The Kaplan–Meier analysis also revealed a significantly higher incidence of SCD, cardiovascular death, and all-cause death among patients with a short second coupling interval (Figure 4). Additionally, while numerically higher crude rates of non-cardiovascular and non-SCD cardiovascular deaths were observed in patients with NSVT with second coupling interval < 394 ms, survival analysis confirmed no statistically significant associations between the second coupling interval and these endpoints (see Supplementary material online, Tables S7 and S8). Sensitivity analysis was performed in patients with complete clinical data, and the results remained consistent (see Supplementary material online, Tables S9–S14).

Optimizing the non-sustained ventricular tachycardia variable improved the guideline-based sudden cardiac death risk algorithms

Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia is included as a variable in both the 2014 HCM Risk-SCD and 2024 AHA/ACC algorithms. In the present study, we investigated whether optimizing the NSVT variable would enhance the performance of these guideline-based SCD risk algorithms.

As only patients with a short second coupling interval (<394 ms) were considered to be malignant based on the above results, we defined NSVT with a second coupling interval of <394 ms as ‘malignant NSVT’. Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia with a long second coupling interval (≥394 ms) was considered as absence of malignant NSVT along with the non-NSVT group, as these patients shared a similar prognosis.

In the 2014 HCM Risk-SCD model, we modified the original NSVT variable from simply indicating the presence or absence of NSVT to identifying the presence or absence of malignant NSVT. The C-statistic of the updated model increased to 0.775 (95% CI: 0.702–0.848) after modifying the NSVT variable, which is significantly higher than that of the original calibrated model, for which the C-statistic was 0.749 (95% CI: 0.674–0.825, P = 0.003) (Figure 5). Calibration slope was of 1.200 (95% CI 1.093–2.209) for the updated model and 1.022 (95% CI: 0.773–2.872) for the original model. Calibration plots are presented in Supplementary material online, Figure S5. Detailed model performance is shown in Supplementary material online, Table S15.

For patients in the intermediate-risk ‘grey zone’ (4% to 6%) defined by original 2014 HCM Risk-SCD model, 134 individuals in our cohort fell within this range. With the updated model, 19 patients were reclassified as high-risk (≥6%), among whom 1 experienced SCD during follow-up; 25 patients were reclassified as low-risk (<4%), with no SCD events recorded.

In the 2024 AHA/ACC SCD risk algorithms, although the presence of NSVT was associated with a significantly higher risk of SCD among Class 2a patients (P < 0.001), SCD risk did not differ significantly between patients with or without conventional NSVT in Class 2b candidate group (P = 0.400) (see Supplementary material online, Figure S6). Patients classified as Class 2a patients and Class 2b candidates in indications for ICD implantation demonstrated a higher risk of SCD when presenting with malignant NSVT (NSVT with a short second coupling interval), compared to those with NSVT exhibiting a long second coupling interval or patients without NSVT (Figure 5). Subgroup analyses by age showed that in AHA/ACC ICD Class 2a group, the presence of malignant NSVT was associated with a significantly higher risk of SCD in both younger (<40 years) and older (≥40 years) patient subgroups, compared with those with long second coupling intervals or without NSVT, while in Class 2b candidate group, this significance only appeared in the younger patient subgroup. This may suggest that the malignant NSVT has stronger influence on younger patients (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7).

These results suggest that assessing the presence of malignant NSVT, rather than simply assessing the presence of NSVT, is more appropriate when evaluating SCD risk in patients with HCM.

Genetic factors contribute to the presence of malignant non-sustained ventricular tachycardia

Among 621 patients who underwent genetic testing, 208 (33.5%) carried at least one P/LP variant in the eight sarcomeric genes. Significant differences in the yield rate of P/LP variants were observed across groups (P = 0.041): patients with malignant NSVT had the highest yield rate of P/LP variants of sarcomeric genes (50.0%), compared with those who had long second coupling interval of NSVT (46.2%) and those without NSVT (31.8%) (see Supplementary material online, Table S16).

Discussion

Sudden cardiac death is one of the main causes of death among patients with HCM, and the presence of NSVT has been recognized as a significant independent risk factor for SCD.4,13,14 However, the prognostic value of NSVT characteristics remains to be studied further. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the value of the NSVT coupling interval for predicting SCD in HCM. The main results are as follows: (i) the second coupling interval of NSVT carried the best predictive value for adverse outcomes (SCD, cardiovascular death, and all-cause death) when compared with the first, third, and the three-to-last coupling intervals, while the frequency and duration of coupling intervals of NSVT showed no association; (ii) a short second coupling interval of NSVT was demonstrated to be highly malignant, while a long second coupling interval was not associated with an increased risk of SCD, cardiovascular death, and all-cause death in patients with HCM; and (iii) the modification from conventional NSVT to the malignant NSVT definition significantly improved the predictive performance of the 2014 HCM Risk-SCD model and the discriminative ability for SCD of the 2024 AHA/ACC risk algorithm. This study offers a thorough and accurate understanding of the prognostic value of NSVT characteristics, thereby providing new evidence for risk stratification of SCD in patients with HCM.

Previous studies evaluating NSVT characteristics have not conceived satisfying conclusions on the prognostic value of these characteristics in HCM. Two previous studies from Maron BJ and McKenna WJ both showed that the duration, frequency, and rate of NSVT were not related to adverse outcomes in unselected HCM populations.9,10 In contrast, in two studies that enrolled patients with ICDs, the associations of the frequency and duration of NSVT with ICD-treated ventricular arrhythmias in patients with HCM were identified, while the prognostic value of the NSVT rate remained conflicting.7,8 These discrepancies may be partly due to the different populations and the small sample sizes of the studies. Additionally, the lack of more accurate features to better describe NSVT should also be considered as a potential contributing factor. The NSVT coupling interval is a more specific characteristic than the NSVT rate, and the former has been proven to be a significant predictor of prognosis in other cardiac diseases. A study focusing on outflow tract ventricular tachycardia found that a progressively shorter second coupling interval in NSVT may be associated with a more malignant disease phenotype.11 The present study investigated the prognostic value of detailed NSVT characteristics in a large cohort of 2584 unselected patients with HCM. Our results indicated that the frequency and duration of NSVT had no relationship with prognosis in HCM, which is consistent with the findings of Maron BJ and McKenna WJ.9,10 However, detailed analysis of the NSVT coupling interval revealed that the second coupling interval of NSVT was significantly associated with adverse outcomes in patients with HCM and exhibited better predictive ability than the first and third coupling intervals.

Further analysis of the second coupling interval of NSVT led us to define ‘malignant NSVT’ for the first time. We found from the survival analysis that the risk of adverse outcomes was comparable between patients with a second NSVT coupling interval of ≥394 ms and those without NSVT, whereas NSVT with a second coupling interval of <394 ms was associated with a significantly increased risk of adverse outcomes in patients with HCM. Therefore, we proposed that NSVT with a second coupling interval of ≥394 ms may not provide significant prognostic information in patients with HCM, while cases with a shorter second coupling interval were deemed malignant.

Importantly, we re-evaluated the guideline-based SCD risk algorithms based on our results. The modification of conventional NSVT variable to the malignant NSVT definition significantly enhanced the predictive performance of the 2014 HCM Risk-SCD model and also showed improved discriminative ability for SCD within the framework of 2024 AHA/ACC SCD risk algorithm. Furthermore, the malignant NSVT definition enabled more precise risk stratification among AHA/ACC Class 2a patients and more effectively identified individuals who appropriately met Class 2b criteria. Notably, malignant NSVT appeared to exert a stronger prognostic impact in younger patients.

Additionally, we observed that most patients experienced SCD within the first few years after the initial recording of malignant NSVT, while SCD events were rare in patients with NSVT with a long second coupling interval. This interesting finding suggests that the decision-making process on whether to perform ICD implantation in patients with HCM should start early once malignant NSVT is detected. Furthermore, in-depth profiling of NSVT is encouraged because it may offer superior guidance compared with a simple ‘yes or no’ determination of NSVT for the monitoring and intervention of adverse outcomes in patients with HCM.

The presence of NSVT in patients with HCM can primarily be attributed to abnormal calcium handling, which may result from the formation of re-entrant circuits, ion channel dysfunction, and decreased myofibrillar ATPase activity.15 A short coupling interval reflects the extent of calcium overload and an electrophysiological environment conducive to triggering activity and re-entrant circuits, such as myocardial scarring, fibrosis, or dispersion of refractoriness.2,16–19 This instability can lead to rapid and disorganized electrical activity, increasing the risk of fatal arrhythmias. Specifically, the first coupling interval of NSVT primarily represents its triggering activity, which corresponds to the initiation of NSVT.20 In contrast, the second coupling interval represents re-entry, a more severe electrophysiological disturbance leading to the persistence of NSVT21; therefore, the second coupling interval is considered to be more reflective of the behaviour of NSVT than the first. Interestingly, the third coupling interval also showed certain predictive significance in the ROC curve analysis, but its predictive value was notably less than that of the second coupling interval, suggesting that the electrophysiological characteristics of the initial re-entry may be more clinically determinative.

Further genetic analysis showed that sarcomeric genes are associated with the presence of malignant NSVT. As sarcomeric genes are known to promote myocardial fibrosis, these variants may be responsible for the substrate of a short second coupling interval.22,23 The specific mechanisms require further investigation.

Although automated algorithms for NSVT detection may vary across Holter device manufacturers and potentially affect initial waveform recognition and noise handling, all Holter systems used in this study were clinically validated and approved for hospital use, ensuring a high baseline standard of automated detection accuracy. Moreover, the final diagnosis of NSVT and quantitative measurements of coupling intervals relied on standardized manual review, which also minimized the impact of such technical variability on our core findings.

This study has some limitations that should be considered. First, while our exploratory results suggested that a short second coupling interval in NSVT is associated with a poor prognosis in HCM, a definite cut-off value for the second coupling interval requires further validation. Second, the findings lacked external validation, and future studies are needed to confirm the prognostic significance of the second coupling interval of NSVT for predicting SCD in HCM. Third, the duration of Holter monitoring was limited to 24 h, whereas 48 h monitoring is usually recommended, which may have affected detection of NSVT events. Fourth, the missing data of late gadolinium enhancement and genetic testing may introduce some bias, studies specifically designed for these variables may provide stronger evidence. Finally, NSVT may exhibit substantial daily variability. Recent data have demonstrated limited diagnostic reliability of short-term monitoring for premature ventricular complexes due to daily variability.24 Although this evidence relates primarily to premature ventricular complexes, similar variability may apply to NSVT.

Conclusion

This study focused on the value of detailed NSVT characteristics as prognostic predictors in patients with HCM. The second coupling interval of NSVT demonstrated superior value for predicting SCD, cardiovascular death, and all-cause death in HCM compared with other NSVT characteristics. Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia with a short second coupling interval was highly malignant and responsible for the increased risk of SCD in patients with NSVT, while NSVT with a long second coupling interval did not convey prognostic information in HCM. Consideration of the second coupling interval of NSVT was associated with improved risk stratification for SCD. These findings suggest that measuring the second coupling interval of NSVT may improve risk stratification, especially SCD prediction, in patients with HCM.
