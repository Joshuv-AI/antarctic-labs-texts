An investigation of the Bernstein’s strengths Scale: factorial validity and network analysis of attention-deficit/hyperactivity symptoms, mental health, and the strengths of the healthy adult self
Authors: Mónika Miklósi, Kornél Vajsz, Stella Oláh, Vivien Nagy, Brigitta Szabó
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBernstein’s heuristic model of personal strengths describes 16 positive attributes of the healthy adult self, grouped into four higher-order factors: self-directedness (identity, self-reflection, self-confidence, self-assertion, imagination/creativity), self-regulation (emotional balance, resilience, self-control, self-care, reality testing), connection (empathy, compassion, humour, responsibility), and transcendence (gratitude and wisdom). The study aimed to explore the factorial validity of Bernstein’s Strengths Scale developed for the assessment of these constructs. Furthermore, we explored the association of strengths with attention-deficit hyperactivity (ADHD) symptoms and mental health in a nonclinical sample using a network approach.MethodsFive hundred and twenty-eight adults filled out an online questionnaire, including Bernstein’s Strengths Scale (BSS), the Mental Health Continuum – Short Form (MHC-SF), and the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS).ResultsThough our data showed an acceptable fit to the 16-factor model, the Self-control subscale and item 28 of the Compassion subscale were excluded because of reliability issues. The final model with 15 factors and 44 items showed excellent fit, χ2(797) = 1130.149, p < .001, RMSEA = 0.028, 90% CI [0.024, 0.032], SRMS = 0.049, CFI = 0.993, TLI = 0.991. Principal component analysis revealed three instead of four second-order factors. In the network model, Self-confidence and Wisdom emerged as the most influential nodes. ADHD symptoms were negatively connected to Responsibility, Self-care, Identity, Emotional balance, Self-confidence, and mental health, while they had positive edges with Humor, Self-reflection, and Imagination/creativity. Mental health was positively related to several strengths, with the strongest association being between mental health and Gratitude.ConclusionThe BSS has acceptable factorial validity and is a reliable tool for assessing the personal strengths of the healthy adult self. ADHD symptoms in adults may be associated with deficits in personal strengths, but protective factors could also be identified. Addressing personal strengths in psychosocial interventions for ADHD in adults may improve patients’ emotional well-being and everyday functioning.

Introduction

Since the beginning of the twenty-first century, there has been a change in the direction of psychological research and practice. While previously the focus was almost exclusively on the identification and elimination of deficits, in the last few decades, there has been significant interest in ways to build on positive attributes and personal strengths [1, 2]. Strengths are behavioural, cognitive, or emotional capacities that empower optimal functioning and performance in achieving valued outcomes [3]. For improving personal strengths, many positive psychology interventions have been developed, and their effectiveness in increasing well-being and reducing stress, anxiety and depression has been supported by several meta-analyses [4–10]. A tendency toward a stronger focus on patients’ existing strengths can also be observed in traditional psychotherapeutic approaches, e.g., psychodynamic and cognitive‒behavioral therapy [11].

More recently, David Bernstein [12] developed a concept of personal strengths that uses schema therapy as a positive psychological intervention to strengthen the healthy adult self. It is based on Young’s schema theory [13, 14] but focuses on adaptive rather than maladaptive functioning. As defined by Claassen and Pol [15], the healthy adult self empowers the individual to use adaptive capacities to become an autonomous and responsible person in a positive relationship with the self and the environment. Building on earlier theories and research [2, 15–21], Bernstein described 16 strengths of the healthy adult self, which are grouped into four higher-order factors. The self-directedness factor includes strengths that empower individuals to determine their life path, i.e., identity, self-reflection, self-confidence, self-assertion, and imagination/creativity. Strengths in the self-regulation factor (emotional balance, resilience, self-control, self-care, and reality testing) enable the effective control of emotions, thoughts, and behaviour. The third connection factor includes strengths such as empathy, compassion, humour, and responsibility, which serve to establish meaningful reciprocal relationships. The fourth factor is transcendence, which is related to higher-order human goals or meaning in life and social relationships. Two strengths are listed here: gratitude (thankfulness) and wisdom [12]. It has been emphasized that people tend to underuse their strengths [11], especially when faced with stress [12]. The focus of Bernstein’s work is therefore to strengthen the healthy adult self by recognizing and appreciating its qualities and to take small steps towards the use of personal strengths to adaptively meet the individual’s emotional needs [12].

The Bernstein’s Strengths Scale, a 48-item self-report questionnaire, has also been developed as a measure of this concept [12]. Although it is available in more than 25 languages, to our knowledge, until now, there has been no research on the factorial validity of the measure. Such research would also take a step forward in the validation of the underlying model.

Previous attempts to integrate a positive psychology approach into cognitive behaviour therapy have focused mainly on emotional disorders [11]. However, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD [22]), , which is one of the most prevalent neuropsychiatric disorders in children and adolescents, may be of particular interest in this context. ADHD continues into adulthood in 4–77% of cases [23] with a worldwide prevalence of 2–3% [24–26] and high rates of comorbidities reaching 70 to 75% [27]. The core symptoms of inattention and impulsivity [28], as well as emotional dysregulation [29], disturbed sleep patterns [30] and executive function deficits [31, 32], are characteristics of adult ADHD that lead to impaired functioning [28, 33], higher levels of perceived stress [34], and impaired interpersonal relationships [35, 36] .

In adults with ADHD, executive dysfunction may lead to deficits in personal strengths and difficulties in mobilising existing strengths [37], therefore, a positive psychological analysis is particularly warranted. In line with this, lower levels of self-esteem [37, 38], self-efficacy, self-confidence, sense of control [37], sense of mastery [39], and self-compassion [40, 41] have been reported in ADHD, and deficits in personal strengths mediated the relationship between ADHD symptoms and mental health [42], comorbidity [39], and impaired functioning [43]. More specifically, in a sample of undergraduate students, Burr and LeFevre [44] found that ADHD symptoms were negatively related to self-confidence, which was a mediator in the relationship between ADHD symptoms and math performance. In a more recent study, difficulties in emotion regulation and self-esteem mediated the relationship between ADHD symptoms and procrastination in a sample of college students [43]. Furthermore, emotion regulation strategies served as mediators between ADHD symptoms and internalizing symptoms, as well as impairments in the interpersonal domain [42]. On the other hand, internal resources may serve as protective factors. In a qualitative study, cognitive dynamism, courage, energy, humanity, resilience and transcendence emerged as factors promoting favorable outcomes in adults with ADHD [45]. A more recent qualitative study assessed positive aspects of ADHD; five main themes emerged (creativity, flexibility, being dynamic, socioaffective skills, higher-order cognitive skills), of which creativity was the most frequently emerging theme [46].

Cognitive behaviour therapy is the second evidence-based treatment after medication in adult ADHD [45–48]. There is a growing literature on the usefulness of the schema therapeutic approach as well [34, 49–54]. However, Bernstein’s model of personal strengths has not yet been evaluated within this context. To fill this gap, we aimed to explore the factorial validity of Bernstein’s Strengths Scale and the connections between personal strengths, mental health, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity symptoms in a community sample of young adults. First, we assessed the fit of the suggested factor structure to our data by employing confirmatory factor analysis. We hypothesized that the original 16-factor model would fit our data. Then, using a network approach, we explored the structure of personal strengths and their connection to ADHD symptoms and mental health to determine the relative importance of personal strengths in relation to adult ADHD symptoms and mental health, as well as to identify strengths that bridge ADHD symptoms and mental health.

Method

Sample and procedure

Data from 528 young adults (mean age = 21.41 years, SD = 2.29, range: 18 – 28, female: N = 488, 92.4%, with one missing case) were analysed. Further demographic characteristics of the sample are shown in Table 1.

The Institutional Ethical Committee of the Eötvös Loránd University, Psychological Institute approved the study (Nr. 2022/344). Participants were recruited online through Mélylevegő Projekt (Deep Breath Project), a Hungarian psychoeducational social media page aimed at younger generations.

Mélylevegő Projekt was established during the 2020 COVID-19 lockdowns by psychology students at Eötvös Loránd University, Budapest, Hungary. It serves as a mental health promotion initiative to increase awareness about symptoms of depression and anxiety, as well as working to reduce the stigma associated with mental disorders. The project initially focused on providing psychoeducational materials to young people affected by lockdowns but has continued its activity since. Participants gave their informed consent before completing the questionnaire.

Instruments

Bernstein’s Strengths Scale (BSS [12]) assesses 16 personal strengths with 48 Likert-type items. Respondents are asked to judge what strengths they can rely on in difficult situations by indicating on a 5-point Likert scale how true each statement is (from 1 = not true, to 5 = extremely true). To our knowledge, psychometric properties have not yet been reported. The Hungarian version was created with the approval of the author using a back-translation process.

The Adult ADHD Self-Report (ASRS [55, 56]) is a reliable and valid measure of adult ADHD symptoms according to the DSM-IV. On a five-point Likert scale, respondents indicate how often they experienced each symptom over the past six months (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, 5 = very often). Good psychometric properties of the Hungarian version have been reported [57, 58]. We used the total score: we added up 18 item scores without using individual cut-off scores because this procedure produced a distribution that was more appropriate for network analysis. Because of the strong intercorrelation of the Inattention and Hyperactivity/Impulsivity subscales (Pearson’s r = .64), we used the total score in the analyses (Cronbach’s α = 0.89). Higher scores represent higher levels of ADHD symptoms.

The Mental Health Continuum Short Form (MHC-SF [59]) assesses emotional, psychological and social well-being with 14 Likert-type items measuring the frequency with which respondents experience indicator of positive mental health (from 1 = never to 6 = every day). Higher scores represent higher levels of mental health. Cronbach’s alpha of the original version was 0.74 [59] and that of the Hungarian version was 0.89 [60]. In our data, Cronbach’s alpha of the total score was 0.85.

Statistical analyses

For the analyses we used the JASP statistical software [61]. Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to assess the fit of the suggested factor structure in our data. Comparative Fit Indices (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Indices (TLI) with a minimum acceptable threshold of 0.900 [62], Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) with a maximum acceptable threshold of 0.07 [63], and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) with a maximum acceptable threshold of 0.08 [64] were calculated. Internal consistencies of the subscales were described by means of Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s ω and the Spearman-Brown coefficient. Item-rest correlations are also reported. Principal component analysis with oblimin rotation was conducted to assess second-order factors. To explore the structure of personal strengths and their specific connections to ADHD symptoms and mental health, we conducted a network analysis with a Gaussian graphical model using graphical lasso based on extended Bayesian inference criterion estimation [65] using JASP statistical software [61]. Centrality indices [66] were used to find the most influential nodes in the joint BSS-ASRS-MHC-SF network. The robustness of the network estimates was examined using the R package bootnet [67]. Nonparametric bootstrapping with 2500 samples was performed to compute 95% confidence intervals of edge-weight accuracy for the edges, and the centrality stability correlation coefficient was calculated (> 0.70 very good, > 0.50 good, > 0.25 acceptable [67].

Results

Factorial validity of the BSS

We examined the original factor structure [12] on the present sample using confirmatory factor analysis. The Shapiro-Wilk Test for Multivariate Normality and the Mardia’s coefficients for multivariate skewness and kurtosis were significant (p < .001); we used the Diagonally Weighted Least Squares (DWLS) estimator [68]. The model with 16 factors and 48 items showed an acceptable fit, χ2(960) = 2004.989, p < .001, RMSEA = 0.045, 90% CI [0.043, 0.048], SRMS = 0.062, CFI = 0.980, TLI = 0.977. However, with the addition of the four second-order factors, goodness of fit indices tended to decrease, χ2(1055) = 32729.008, p < .001, RMSEA = 0.055, 90% CI [0.052, 0.057], SRMS = 0.073, CFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.966; therefore, we rejected this hierarchical factor solution.

Next, we explored the internal consistencies of the scales by means of Cronbach’s alphas. However, the acceptance of Cronbach’s alpha depends on the number of items [69, 70]; a lower alpha can also be acceptable in the case of short subscales. Therefore, we also calculated McDonald’s ω. As McDonald’s ω can be calculated for subscales with a minimum of three items, we also reported the Spearman-Brown coefficient [71]. All but two subscale showed acceptable internal consistencies (Table 2). However, the Self-control subscale had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.505, the McDonald’s ω coefficient was 0.509, and the Spearman-Brown coefficient was still not acceptable, 0.542. The average interitem correlation of the three items of the Self-control subscale was also weak, r = .256 [0.198 – 0.309]. Therefore, we excluded this subscale from the model. Similarly, the Compassion subscale did not have acceptable reliability (α = 0.565, ω = 0.589, Spearman-Brown coefficient = 0.576), but it could be improved by dropping out item 28 (α = 0.717). The final model with 15 factors and 44 items showed excellent fit, χ2(797) = 1130.149, p < .001, RMSEA = 0.028, 90% CI [0.024, 0.032], SRMS = 0.049, CFI = 0.993, TLI = 0.991.

Second-order factors

Next, we conducted a principal component analysis with oblimin rotation on the 15 factors to find a possible second-order factor solution. Parallel analysis based on factors resulted in three components with eigenvalues of 7.059, 1.881, and 0.821. The model explained 65.1% of the total variance, from which Component 1 accounted for 47.1% and Components 2 and 3 accounted for 12.5% and 5.5% of the variance, respectively. Five primary factors loaded exclusively on the first component, i.e., Emotional balance, Resilience, Self-confidence, Humor and Reality testing. Compassion and Empathy loaded exclusively on the second component, while Self-reflection loaded only on the third component. However, all other subscales had high (> 0.3) cross-loadings, with Gratitude, Imagination, Self-care, Self-assertion, and Wisdom having the largest coefficients on the first, Responsibility on the second component, while Identity had the largest coefficient on the third component (Table 3).

Network analysis

We conducted network analysis with a Gaussian graphical model using graphical lasso based on extended Bayesian inference criterion estimation [65]. Each of the 17 nodes corresponded to the subscale scores of the BSS and the total score of the ASRS and the MHC-SF (Fig. 1). The edges denote the partial correlations between the scores of the factors. The sparsity of the network was 0.346. Out of a total of 136 possible edges, 89 edges were observed to have nonzero values. This suggests that the network exhibited a moderate level of connectivity, with a considerable number of existing connections between nodes. Edge stability (0.750), network stability for expected influence (0.750) and strengths centrality (0.750) were very good.

Discussion

In the last few decades, emerging research has suggested that not only the presence of negative factors but also the absence of positive factors is important in understanding psychopathology [73]. Accordingly, several theoretical models of personal strengths and positive attributes emerged (e.g [2, 74, 75]), and traditional psychotherapy approaches have recognized the need to integrate positive traits and protective factors into their models [11]. Bernstein’s heuristic model of personal strengths is one of these efforts integrating the views of positive psychology with the schema therapy approach [12]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the validity of this model and to explore the association of strengths with symptoms of ADHD within this context.

Factorial validity of the BSS

First, we explored the factorial validity of Bernstein’s Strengths Scale. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the original 16-factor structure [12]. However, the Self-control subscale did not have acceptable internal reliability; therefore, we excluded it from the model. Further research should explore the psychometric characteristics of this subscale in other samples. Furthermore, we left out item 28 in order to improve the Compassion subscale’s reliability. The final model with 15 subscales and 44 items showed excellent fit to our data. The results are in line with the large amount of research confirming that different strengths can be identified [73]. On the other hand, our analysis did not support the four higher-order factors suggested by Bernstein [12]. The principal component analysis resulted in three components corresponding mainly to the original self-regulation, connection, and self-directedness factors. However, Self-confidence loaded in the self-regulation instead of the self-directedness factor, suggesting that self-confidence may be mainly related to perceptions of the adaptive functioning of the self. Furthermore, Humour, Gratitude, Imagination, Self-assertion, and Wisdom also had the highest loadings on the self-regulation factor, indicating that these strengths are highly relevant for managing an individual’s emotions and behaviour. However, the strengths of the original transcendence factor did not form a separate higher-order factor. Wisdom had the highest loading on the self-regulation factor and had high cross-loadings on the other two factors as well, while Gratitude had the highest loading on the self-regulation factor with a high cross-loading on the connection factor, indicating that they have both intrapersonal and interpersonal relevance. The three-factor solution is in line with studies on other models of personal strengths, of which the most well-known is the VIA Classification of Strengths and Virtues [2]. Peterson and Seligman [2] identified 24 character strengths that are morally valued and contribute to personal fulfilment and the benefit of others in a cross-cultural context. Although in their theoretical model, these strengths were classified into six general dimensions of virtues reflecting cultural conceptions of desirable functioning [76], factor analytic studies mainly resulted in three higher-order factors [77]. The first factor, labelled caring, sociability or interpersonal strengths [73, 77], included strengths such as kindness, love, and forgiveness, while gratitude and humour also had high loadings on that factor in several samples [77]. This factor corresponds to the connection factor in Bernstein’s model. Strengths in this factor enable the individual to fulfil the core emotional need of secure attachment by forming meaningful, mutual relationships [12]. The second factor that emerged from factor-analytic studies of virtues was labelled self-control, conscientiousness, cautiousness, or temperance, with strengths such as self-regulation, prudence, perseverance, or modesty [73, 74]. This factor corresponds to the self-regulation factor of Bernstein’s model, which includes intrapersonal strengths aiming at the regulation of emotions, impulses, thoughts and behaviour [12]. The third factor that emerged from previous research on virtues was labelled inquisitiveness, vitality, or personal agency in different studies [73, 74] and included strengths such as creativity, curiosity, learning, perspective, bravery, hope, judgement, and social intelligence. In our analysis, Self-reflection and Identity loaded in the self-directedness factor, and Wisdom, Imagination/Creativity, Self-care and Self-reflection had high cross-loadings on it, suggesting that seeking truth, knowledge and the ability to learn from experiences, using one’s creative capacities, as well as recognizing and communicating the individual’s emotional and physical needs are related to strengths that enable the individual to have a clear sense of self and to set the person’s life course [12]. It is important to note that cultural differences may exist in the structure of strengths and virtues [73]; therefore, our results may not be generalizable for other cultural contexts; more research is needed in that field.

The network analysis provided further information about the structure of strengths. The results revealed that strengths are highly interconnected, while Wisdom and Self-confidence emerged as the most influential nodes in the network. It is important to note that these strengths showed a strong bivariate correlation but a zero edge in the network, indicating a nonsignificant partial correlation. These results together suggest that their relationship may be mediated by other strengths. Wisdom had numerous positive edges with strengths from all higher-order factors, while Self-confidence was placed between the Self-directedness and Self-regulation factors, bridging them with several positive connections to strengths from both. These findings underline that seeking knowledge and the ability to learn from experiences, as well as the confidence of the individual in his abilities, qualities, and judgements, may be central to the adaptive functioning of the healthy adult self. This is in line with previous research in that wisdom was shown to be associated with better overall health, well-being, happiness, life satisfaction, and resilience across the lifespan (see [78] for a review), while self-confidence was found to be strongly and positively related to self-efficacy, negatively associated with neuroticism [79] and was the best noncognitive predictor of academic achievement [80].

Strengths, mental health, and ADHD symptoms

In the network model, we also explored the relationships between personal strengths, mental health, and ADHD symptoms. According to our analyses, mental health was strongly related to several strengths, from which the strongest edge connected mental health and Gratitude. A recent meta-analysis [81] based on 158 independent samples found a positive association of medium effect between dispositional gratitude and psychological well-being; our results extended these findings by showing that this positive relationship also remained significant in a multivariate model after controlling for the effect of several other strengths.

Similar to the results of other studies (e.g [34, 42]), we found that ADHD symptoms were associated with lower levels of mental health. Furthermore, higher levels of ADHD symptoms were related to lower scores on the Responsibility, Self-care, Emotional balance, Identity, and Self-confidence subscales of the BSS. In line with previous studies [37–42], our results suggest that adults with ADHD may have deficits in specific personal strengths, as well as difficulties utilizing existing strengths in challenging situations. This may add to the functional impairments associated with ADHD in adults and may undermine the individuals’ psychological well-being [33, 43, 44, 82]. While some of these deficits may be directly related to executive dysfunction (e.g., difficulty taking responsibility may be associated with deficits in working memory and organization skills), weaknesses in other strengths, such as identity (i.e., a clear sense of self) or self-confidence, may be rooted in a person’s general negative self-perception. According to narrative reviews, the self-concept of adults with ADHD could be characterized by maladaptive beliefs about the self, i.e., failure, impaired self-control, being different from others and a sense of inadequacy [52, 83], resulting from the interaction of the symptoms and environmental challenges in forming the individual’s views of the self and the world from the person’s formative years [47, 49]. These dysfunctional beliefs were shown to mediate the relationship between ADHD and comorbid conditions, especially emotional disorders [84].

Importantly, in the multivariate model, ADHD symptoms were positively associated with Humour, Self-reflection, and Imagination/creativity. These strengths may serve as protective factors in adults with ADHD. For example, Schei and colleagues [85] found that individual and environmental protective factors mediated the relationship between emotional and conduct problems and quality of life in a sample of adolescents with ADHD. A recent review [86] reported high rates of creative abilities/achievements among both clinical and subclinical groups and concluded that strengths of ADHD may lead to an increase in positive self-views and quality of life of the patients. The findings underline the importance of focusing on positive aspects in the understanding of ADHD both in research and clinical practice.

Limitations and suggestion for further research

The results can be viewed considering the limitations of the study. Though our results supported the overall validity of Bernstein’s model, we had to exclude the original Self-control factor because of reliability issues. Self-control is a complex phenomenon [87], sometimes even suggested to be a second-order factor [73]. The three items of the BSS Self-control subscale assess inhibition (I stop and think before acting), delay gratification (I can put off my immediate wishes) and distress tolerance (I can handle frustrations). The three items showed low inter-item correlations, indicating that they reflect different constructs, which do not form a single factor. A narrower definition of self-control as the ability of the individual to pursue long-term goals despite external or internal obstacles [88], and the selection of items accordingly would result in a more reliable subscale.

The cross-sectional design does not allow us to explore causality. The long-term effect of strengths and ADHD symptoms on mental health should be explored by longitudinal studies. Self-reports of ADHD symptoms and personal strengths may also be biased by social desirability, insight, and cognitive abilities. Women were overrepresented in the sample; therefore, our results cannot be generalized to the population. Cultural differences may also influence the findings, and more research is needed in different cultural contexts.

Implications for practice

Despite these limitations, our results have important implications for clinical practice. The primary focus of current CBT approaches in the treatment of adult ADHD is the development of executive self-management skills [48, 89], but the importance of focusing on maladaptive cognitions has also been emphasized [34, 52, 90]. Our results on the connection between ADHD symptoms and personal strengths suggest that the positive psychology approach may also have special relevance in psychosocial interventions focusing on adult ADHD, as has already been suggested in other age groups [91, 92]. More specifically, empowering the healthy adult self in schema therapy by helping patients recognize and access their strengths [12] may help adults with ADHD to build a more positive self-concept and to master more adaptive coping skills.

Conclusion

The aim of this study was twofold. First, we aimed to assess the factorial validity of Bernstein’s Strengths Scale. Our results suggest that the BSS has good psychometric properties and may be a useful tool for assessing the personal strengths of the healthy adult self. Second, we explored the relationship between ADHD symptoms and personal strengths using the network approach. Our results revealed that ADHD symptoms in adults are associated with deficits in some but not all strengths; protective factors were also identified. Targeting personal strengths in psychosocial interventions may add to the improvement of patients’ functioning in everyday life.
