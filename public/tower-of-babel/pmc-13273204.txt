ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAdvances in paediatric intensive care have resulted in a growing population of survivors exposed to long-term morbidity. Paediatric post–intensive care syndrome (PICS-p) encompasses new or worsened physical, cognitive, psychological/psychiatric, and socio-familial impairments that may affect children and their families after discharge from the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU). Despite increasing recognition of PICS-p, post-PICU follow-up remains highly heterogeneous worldwide, and systematic screening and management strategies are rarely integrated into routine care. These guidelines were issued at the initiative of the French national health authority (Haute Autorité de Santé: HAS) to improve the prevention, identification, and management of PICS-p.DesignClinical practice guidelines developed following the HAS standardized methodology (Recommandations de Bonne Pratique), including systematic literature review, multidisciplinary expert elaboration, formal external review, and HAS board approval. Reporting follows the AGREE II framework.MethodsClinical questions were predefined by HAS project leads. A systematic literature search ([dates]) was conducted across Embase, EmCare, Medline, and the Cochrane Library, prioritising guidelines, meta-analyses, and systematic reviews. Two field experts drafted initial recommendations, graded A (established evidence) to Expert Opinion (absence of evidence). An 18-member multidisciplinary working group refined the draft through two in-person meetings. A 52-member external review panel formally voted on agreement for each recommendation. The final version was approved by the HAS board.ResultsFourty recommendations were issued: 5 with a moderate level of evidence (grade B), 8 low level (grade C) and 27 very low (expert opinion). Strong agreement by the panel of experts was achieved for all recommendations except for one (moderate). The recommendations define PICS-p as a prevalent, multidimensional condition and emphasize systematic, repeated screening of children and families from PICU admission through the first year after discharge. Key preventive strategies include implementation of ABCDEFGH bundle, early mobilization, optimized analgesia and sedation, delirium prevention, and family-centred psychological support. Structured care pathways and pragmatic screening tools are proposed to ensure continuity of care.ConclusionThese guidelines emphasize the importance of structured, multidisciplinary, and family-centred strategies, to improve the prevention, early identification, and management of PICS-p and optimize long-term outcomes for children and their families.Keywords: PICS-p, Guidelines, Follow-Up, Screening, Management, Family-Centred care

Introduction

Advances in paediatric intensive care have led to a substantial and growing population of children surviving critical illness [1]. However, survival alone is no longer a sufficient measure for successful recovery. Increasing evidence demonstrates that many children experience new or worsened physical, cognitive, emotional, and social impairments following discharge from the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU), a constellation now recognized as Paediatric Post-Intensive Care Syndrome (PICS-p) [2]. These morbidities may persist for months or years, adversely affecting health-related quality of life, school performance, family functioning, and long-term societal participation [3].

Despite the growing recognition of PICS-p, post-PICU follow-up practices remain highly heterogeneous worldwide [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. A large scoping review of the literature showed that when follow-up is performed, assessments most commonly occur at PICU discharge and at 3, 6, and 12 months, while follow-up beyond one year is reported in fewer than one-fifth of studies [9]. Importantly, the majority of these follow-up assessments are conducted within research settings rather than as part of routine clinical care, underscoring the lack of systematic integration into healthcare systems [10]. National and regional surveys further highlight marked variability in the availability of structured post-PICU follow-up programs. In France, approximately one-thirds of PICUs reported offering post-PICU follow-up consultations focusing on PICS-p, most often led by paediatric intensivists [4]. Other surveys from the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia, and New Zealand indicate that formal post-PICU services are inconsistently available or largely absent, with significant barriers related to staffing, funding, and institutional prioritization [5,6]. In North America, only a limited number of centres have established dedicated post-PICU clinics, often focused on selected high-risk populations such as children with neurological injury or prolonged critical illness [8]. Collectively, these data suggest that most children worldwide do not receive standardized or comprehensive follow-up after PICU discharge, despite their known vulnerability to long-term sequelae [10]. This lack of uniformity reflects not only resource constraints but also the absence of consensus on diagnostic criteria, screening strategies, and care pathways for PICS-p. At present, children at risk are frequently identified late, inconsistently, or not at all, resulting in missed opportunities for early intervention during a critical period of neurodevelopmental plasticity [10]. Given the accumulating evidence of the high prevalence, multidimensional nature, and long-term impact of PICS-p, there is a compelling need to move beyond descriptive studies toward structured, pragmatic recommendations.

Thus, the purpose of these recommendations was the development of guidelines for healthcare professionals, with the aim of improving the prevention and the management of paediatric patients with PICS-p in terms of the following: definition, risk factors, diagnosis, prevention and early and long-term management including health-care pathways.

Methods

These guidelines were clinical practice guidelines defined in the health field as systematically developed statements to assist practitioner and patient decisions about appropriate healthcare for specific clinical circumstances [11]. They were developed on the initiative of the French national health agency (Haute Autorité de Santé (HAS)), following it’s standardized Recommandations de Bonne Pratique (RBP) methodology. Guideline development and reporting followed the AGREE II framework.

Scope and target population

These guidelines concerned all paediatric patients admitted to a PICU or following a hospital stay in a PICU in whom PICS-p is suspected or diagnosed, and any patient. They are intended for all healthcare professionals who treat or are likely to treat children with PICS-p, including: general practitioners, physical and rehabilitation physicians, internal medicine specialists, pneumologists, intensive care physicians, psychiatrists, neurologists, school doctors, ear-nose and throat (ENT) specialists, infectious disease specialists, follow-on care and rehabilitation physicians, skin and cosmetic surgeons, clinical psychologists, speech therapists, nurses, physiotherapists and occupational therapists.

Step 1: Field scoping and literature review

Clinical questions and literature search topics were predefined by the HAS project leads in agreement with field experts prior to the search. A systematic literature search was conducted in March 2025 across the Embase, EmCare, and Medline databases and the Cochrane Library. Clinical guideline repositories and relevant professional society websites were additionally consulted. Searches were restricted to publications in English and French. Full search algorithms and strategies are detailed in Appendix 1. Evidence was prioritised in the following hierarchical order: (1) national or international clinical practice guidelines, (2) meta-analyses, (3) systematic reviews. Two bibliographers independently conducted the search and initial screening.

Step2: Evidence appraisal and initial draft

Two field experts (JC and MP) reviewed and analysed the selected literature, and drafted an initial working document and a first set of recommendations. Each recommendation was graded according to the HAS four-level grading system (Appendix 2):

Step 3: Multidisciplinary working group review

The draft recommendations were reviewed and refined through two separate in-person meetings of an 18-member multidisciplinary working group, comprising representatives of relevant professional organisations and patient and user associations. This process produced the first formal draft of the guidelines.

Step 4: External review panel

A separate external review panel of 52 members was then consulted to provide feedback on the proposed recommendation. Panel members formally voted on their level of agreement for each recommendation (score from 1 (least) to 9 (best agreement). Agreement was defined as strong when ≥90% of reviewers agreed with a score of at least 5/9, and moderate when less than 90% of reviewers agreed with a score of at least 5, requiring working group rediscussion.

Step 5: Final version and HAS board approval

Comments from the review panel were systematically analysed by the working group. The final version of the guidelines was produced incorporating this feedback, and subsequently submitted to and approved by the HAS board prior to publication.

Results

The process resulted in the development of 40 recommendations. The level of evidence was moderate for 5 recommendations (grade B), low for 8 recommendations (grade C) and a very low (expert opinion) for 27 recommendations. Strong agreement by the panel of experts was achieved for all recommendations except one (moderate). EXPERT OPINION/ STRONG AGREEMENT

Rationale:

This recommendation is based on the position paper published by Manning et al. in 2018 [2]and on four narrative literature reviews without meta-analysis [3,[12], [13], [14]] exploring the spectrum and prevalence of post–intensive care impairments in children. Symptoms may appear at different time points during follow-up: some are evident at hospital discharge, while others emerge weeks, months, or even years later. This delayed presentation is particularly relevant in paediatrics, as ongoing development may mask or reveal deficits over time.

The physical domain of PICS-p includes a broad spectrum of impairments, and prospective epidemiological data are scarce for most of these outcomes. Neuromuscular impairment primarily refers to ICU-acquired weakness. While its incidence is well described in adults, paediatric data are limited, and diagnosis is challenging due to the lack of validated assessment tools, especially in infants [12]. Long-term motor sequelae persist in a subset of children, although partial recovery over time is common [3], and a significant proportion (20%) require physical rehabilitation at PICU discharge [13].

Osteoarticular and cutaneous sequelae are not specifically addressed in paediatric systematic reviews. Joint range-of-motion limitations may result from prolonged immobilization, abnormal positioning, or tissue retraction, potentially leading to functional motor impairment. Cutaneous sequelae, such as scars or pressure injuries related to invasive devices and prolonged bed rest, may contribute to pain and mobility limitations.

General physical symptoms—including fatigue, chronic pain, and paediatric feeding disorders—are frequently reported but poorly characterized epidemiologically [3], as they are often embedded within global quality-of-life measures.

Organ-specific physical sequelae are also integral to PICS-p. Respiratory impairments are primarily described in children who experienced paediatric ARDS, with pulmonary function abnormalities reported in a substantial proportion at 3 months and persisting in some cases up to one year or longer, justifying structured follow-up [15]. Renal sequelae are informed by expert consensus rather than paediatric reviews [16]; acute kidney injury during PICU stay is associated with increased post-discharge morbidity and a higher risk of progression to chronic kidney disease, warranting long-term nephrological follow-up.

Cognitive impairment is described as a global neurological dysfunction affecting psychomotor development, cognitive acquisition, memory, attention, executive functions, language, learning abilities, and school performance. Reported prevalence varies widely (3–73%), reflecting substantial heterogeneity in study designs, assessment tools, age groups, and methodological quality. Systematic reviews and cohort studies [[17], [18], [19], [20]] consistently report new or worsened global functional and cognitive impairment at PICU discharge in a significant proportion of children, with incomplete recovery over time. Meta-analytic data [21] demonstrate lower intelligence quotient scores (-7,1 points in IQ scores (IC 95 % [0,55–0,40], p < 0,01) in PICU survivors compared with controls, while domain-specific deficits in memory and attention frequently persist months to years after discharge. Cognitive outcomes are influenced by illness severity, neurological diagnoses, mechanical ventilation, and length of PICU stay. Long-term data remain limited, baseline neurodevelopmental status is rarely reported, and standardized paediatric neuropsychological assessments are lacking. Consequently, longitudinal developmental and educational follow-up is recommended, particularly for children at increased risk of sequelae.

Psychological and psychiatric symptoms mainly include depressive symptoms, anxiety disorders, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with possible psychosomatic manifestations. In young children, these symptoms often present as irritability, sleep disturbances, separation anxiety, or behavioural changes. Narrative reviews [2,22,23] and meta-analyses [24] report a prevalence of psychological or psychiatric impairment ranging from 10% to 35%, reaching up to 60% in some studies. Data on depressive symptoms are limited and heterogeneous, with reported prevalence between 0% and 13%. Anxiety symptoms affect approximately one quarter of children within three months after PICU discharge. PTSD is the most extensively studied outcome, with prevalence ranging from 3% to 37%, peaking between three months and one-year post-discharge and varying according to assessment methods. Longer PICU stays and peri-admission traumatic events increase risk. Screening tools are useful for detection but require confirmation through specialized psychological or child psychiatric follow-up.

Socio-familial symptoms are a key dimension of PICS-p affecting parents, siblings, and the wider family network. Families are exposed to intense stress during and after PICU hospitalization, related to illness severity and the risk of death. The most frequently reported outcomes include parental anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), as well as social difficulties such as disrupted employment, schooling, childcare, and family functioning. Narrative reviews [2,[25], [26], [27]] report parental anxiety in 15–50%, depressive symptoms in 8–30%, and PTSD in approximately 10–40% of cases, with persistence for months to years and higher risk among mothers. Psychological distress has also been described in other family members, while post-traumatic growth may coexist with negative emotional outcomes [28]. Although social and economic consequences are poorly quantified, socio-familial impairment can directly affect child recovery, supporting early and sustained family-centred psychosocial support starting in the PICU.

EXPERT OPINION/ STRONG AGREEMENT

Rationale:

Thirteen literature reviews [2,[12], [13], [14],17,[19], [20], [21],[23], [24], [25],28,29] were identified to assess risk factors for PICS-p. Interpretation of the available evidence is limited by the predominantly observational nature of the included studies and by substantial heterogeneity in definitions, outcome measures, and study populations. Consequently, no single consensual or exhaustive list of risk factors can be established emphasizing the need for a high index of suspicion and proactive screening for PICS-p when one or more risk factors are present. Risk factors can be grouped into three categories: pre-admission, during the PICU stay, and post-discharge. The heterogeneity of findings reinforces the importance of systematic, longitudinal, and family-centred follow-up for all children exposed to PICU.

Rationale:

International guidelines and expert consensus consistently identify early mobilisation as a key strategy to prevent physical sequelae in children during PICU stay [30,31,36].

Early mobilization, when initiated within 24–72 hours of PICU admission in clinically stable children, has been shown to be feasible and safe and is associated with improved functional outcomes and preservation of age-appropriate motor skills [30].

Recent international guidelines emphasize that rehabilitation and mobilization should be integrated even in severe critical illness, provided that interventions are adapted to clinical stability and safety criteria [32]. Effective implementation requires a coordinated multidisciplinary approach and active involvement of parents or caregivers, supporting both functional recovery and developmental needs [31].

GRADE B /STRONG AGREEMENT

Rationale:

In the paediatric population, delirium is the main potentially modifiable risk factor for cognitive manifestations of PICS-p. Measures related to sedation and analgesia are complementary to delirium prevention strategies.

Three clinical practice recommendations [31,32,34] and five literature reviews were identified regarding the prevention of cognitive symptoms of PICS-p [19,[37], [38], [39], [40]]. International guidelines [31,34] consistently emphasize the importance of early delirium screening using validated assessment tools, as well as the implementation of preventive strategies, including: promotion of a regular circadian rhythm (sleep–wake cycle); minimization of benzodiazepine-based sedation whenever feasible; use of protocols aiming at optimizing sedation and analgesia dosing; active involvement of the family in care and management, with optimization of communication; promotion of early mobilization; careful attention to the light and noise environment. Recent reviews provide additional insights: the prevalence of delirium is estimated at approximately 30% of PICU admissions [38,40]; the use of physical restraints represent an additional risk factor [38]; and, finally, education and training of healthcare professionals and parents may represent a promising strategy to improve delirium screening and prevention [37].

Question 2.4: How can we prevent psychological/psychiatric symptoms?

R 2.4.1: Experts suggest the prevention of psychological/psychiatric symptoms in children is closely intertwined with the prevention of similar symptoms in their parents, focusing on three key elements: communication, care environment, and psychosocial support.

EXPERT OPINION/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.4.1.1: Regular communication with patients, regardless of their age or developmental stage, and with their parents is recommended: on a daily basis, at the patient’s bedside, including when the patient is sedated, and through formalized medical interviews, facilitated by a key intermediary (e.g., the referring physician).

EXPERT OPINION/STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.4.1.2: Communication methods tailored to the child’s age, developmental level, and communication abilities should be used.

GRADE B/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.4.2: The patient’s environment should be carefully managed to promote sensory well-being, including optimization of noise and lighting, access to appropriate facilities, and active parental involvement in care.

EXPERT OPINION/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.4.3: Interventions should be implemented to reduce anxiety in patients and their relatives during and after PICU hospitalization. Organization of PICUs, should both geographically and functionally, allow parents to be present with their child 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.

GRADE B/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.4.3.1: The presence of parents, their substitutes, or other support arrangements (e.g., mentoring programs, associations, or relatives) should be encouraged to minimize the amount of time the child is left alone.

EXPERT OPINION/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.4.3.2: Regular psychological support should be provided to patients and their parents, beginning with an early preventive consultation with a psychologist dedicated to the PICU, and, if necessary, a child psychiatrist. Patients at highest risk should undergo targeted screening to prevent the development of post-traumatic stress disorder.

GRADE B/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.4.3.3: Visits by siblings should be facilitated, as they can benefit not only the patient but also the siblings and their families. The initial visit should be carefully planned, with siblings accompanied, ideally, by a psychologist.

EXPERT OPINION/ STRONG AGREEMENT

Rationale:

Exposure to invasive procedures, altered consciousness, and poor understanding of events during intensive care may contribute to the development of traumatic memories. Parental psychological distress during and after the PICU stay has been shown to be associated with adverse psychological outcomes in children, highlighting the interdependence between child and family mental health [31,41]. These data underline the importance of a family-centred and developmentally appropriate approach to care to reduce psychological risk.

Preventive interventions should focus on reducing perceived stress and trauma for both the child and their caregivers through clear, repeated, and age-appropriate communication, emotional support for families, and facilitation of parental presence and involvement in care may mitigate anxiety and promote adaptive coping mechanisms [31]. Although the level of evidence remains limited, these low- risk interventions align with trauma-informed care principles increasingly promoted in PICU.

In France, like in other countries, this approach is reinforced by national regulations, affirming the right of hospitalized children to continuous parental presence [45]. Beyond ethical considerations, sustained parental involvement promotes emotional security, improves communication, and supports psychological well-being.

Overall, available evidence supports early identification of at-risk children and families, enhanced family involvement, optimized communication, and integration of psychosocial support throughout the PICU stay as feasible strategies to prevent psychological symptoms of PICS-p.

R 2.5.1: Experts suggest the prevention of symptoms in family and relatives is closely interconnected with the prevention of symptoms in children.

GRADE C/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.5.1.2: Parents should also be allowed to be present during intensive care procedures (including cardiopulmonary resuscitation, intubation and catheter insertion) when appropriate to the clinical context and accompanied by a dedicated professional. They should furthermore be involved as fully as possible in their child's care.

GRADE C/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.5.1.3: Parents should be offered early and regular psychological support during hospitalization and afterwards.

GRADE C/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.5.1.4: Appropriate, timely and repeated communication regarding their child's clinical condition is an essential component in preventing post-traumatic stress among parents.

GRADE C/ STRONG AGREEMENT

R 2.5.2: The need for social work support should be assessed for all families, regardless of socioeconomic status, to facilitate access to available resources and assist parents during and after hospitalization in the PICU.

EXPERT OPINION/ STRONG AGREEMENT

Rationale:

Prevention of psychosocial symptoms in PICS-p is closely linked to family, particularly parental, psychological well-being. Parental distress is a major determinant of adverse psychosocial outcomes in children after PICU hospitalization, highlighting the family system’s central role in the child’s recovery [41,43].

Family presence and involvement during the PICU stay may protect against psychosocial morbidity by reducing parental helplessness and traumatic perceptions of care. Family-centred care models that encourage parental participation in daily care activities support coping and may lessen long-term psychological distress [42,44].

Effective communication is another central component of psychosocial risk prevention. Timely, clear, and repeated information regarding the child’s clinical status and care trajectory is associated with reduced parental uncertainty and distress [44].

Although high-quality evidence remains limited, psychological support for parents during and after hospitalization is widely recommended [41]. Reviews of psychosocial interventions indicate that early identification of parental distress, access to psychological support, and structured follow-up after discharge may improve coping strategies and reduce persistent psychological symptoms [42,43].

Social vulnerability also influences psychosocial outcomes. Routine assessment of social support needs and facilitation of access to resources may alleviate practical stressors and indirectly improve psychological well-being for both children and parents [42,44].

EXPERT OPINION/ STRONG AGREEMENT

Rationale:

Evidence guiding the screening and diagnosis of PICS-p is limited, with no paediatric-specific studies or guidelines available. Current HAS recommendations therefore rely on extrapolation from adult guidelines [46] and expert consensus (SCCM) [47]. Early identification of risk factors and symptoms is recommended shortly after PICU admission, with repeated assessments throughout the PICU stay and renewed screening before transfer to the general ward, a critical transition period for patients and families. Standardized discharge and handover protocols are advocated to ensure continuity of information and care. During conventional hospitalization and before discharge, families should be informed about potential delayed PICS-p symptoms. Post-discharge follow-up should be iterative, typically beginning within 1–3 months and extending up to one year or longer. Primary care paediatricians and general practitioners play a central role in early detection, provided communication with PICU teams is optimized.

R 3.4: Experts underline that no individual symptom is pathognomonic of PICS-p. However, persistent symptoms, or those that significantly affect the daily life of the child or their family, should be seen as warning signs that may indicate PICS-p.

EXPERT OPINION/ MODERATE AGREEMENT

Rationale:

A wide range of scales and questionnaires have been proposed to support the screening and diagnosis of PICS-p. These tools are intended to complement—but never replace—systematic clinical assessment and physical examination, which remain essential for identifying PICS-p symptoms. Overall, the evidence base is limited by heterogeneity in tool selection, age validity, feasibility, availability (language, cost), and psychometric properties, particularly in young children.

The main paediatric reference is the international expert consensus under the auspices of the SCCM [48], which established a core Paediatric Intensive Care Outcome Measurement Set (PICU-COMS) through a Delphi process. Among 140 instruments reviewed, ten tools reached expert consensus based on paediatric validation, feasibility, reproducibility, and accessibility, covering physical, cognitive, psychological/psychiatric, social, family, and global quality-of-life domains. Additional instruments are commonly used in the literature for domain-specific screening, including functional status scales (PCPC/POPC), developmental assessments, mental health and PTSD screening tools, and quality-of-life measures. However, few instruments are validated for children under three years of age, and many require age-specific adaptation or specialist involvement.

PICS-p screening extends beyond the child to include parents, with a specific focus on psychological, psychiatric, and socio-familial symptoms. Adult guidelines [46] recommend brief, validated self-administered instruments for parental screening (Table 3).

Based on this framework and the paediatric literature, the working group established a pragmatic list of screening scales covering physical, cognitive, psychological/psychiatric, socio-familial, and quality-of-life domains. Tool selection was guided by validity, clinical use, feasibility, and accessibility (including availability of validated French versions). All instruments were designated for screening purposes only and are intended to complement—rather than replace—clinical history and examination. Given the scarcity of validated paediatric cognitive tools, particularly in young children, clinical assessment remains central. The proposed screening framework is therefore adaptable, risk-oriented, and designed to support early identification and referral within a structured follow-up pathway. Given the limited paediatric-specific evidence, recommendations extrapolated from adult data [46] and expert consensus support raising awareness among conventional hospital staff and primary care professionals about PICS-p, delirium, cognitive and psychological symptoms, nutritional needs, and safe medication management.

The randomized controlled trial [49] addressed the issue of information transfer to primary care, particularly for paediatric patients with chronic diseases. The authors demonstrated that a dedicated consultation program, involving interaction between hospital-based and specialist practitioners, reduced readmissions, PICU admissions, and total length of hospital stay. However, this study was conducted at a single centre, which considerably limits its external validity. Similarly to the adult setting [46], it appears reasonable to offer PICS-p awareness programs to healthcare professionals who are likely to care for children discharged from the PICU.

Conclusion

PICS-p represents a frequent, multidimensional, and often under-recognized consequence of paediatric critical illness, with lasting effects on children and their families. The HAS clinical practice guidelines address an important challenge in paediatric critical care by providing a structured, pragmatic framework for the prevention, early identification, and management of PICS-p across the continuum of care. By emphasizing systematic screening, risk-informed follow-up, family-centred interventions, and multidisciplinary coordination, these recommendations aim to shift post-PICU care from fragmented practices toward an integrated and anticipatory model.

Although the current evidence base is limited and largely observational, strong expert consensus supports the clinical relevance and feasibility of the proposed strategies. Importantly, the guidelines underscore that absence of high-level evidence should not delay implementation of low-risk, developmentally appropriate interventions during a critical window of recovery and neurodevelopmental plasticity. Future research should prioritize prospective evaluation of care pathways, validation of paediatric-specific outcome measures, and assessment of long-term educational, psychosocial, and societal outcomes. Ultimately, improving survival after paediatric critical illness must be accompanied by sustained efforts to optimize long-term health, functioning, and quality of life for children and their families.
