Beyond the hashtag: a content analysis of #womenshealth posts on Instagram
Authors: Brooke Nickel, Mali Dunn, Emma G Gram, Tessa Copp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWomen increasingly rely on social media for health information, yet concerns persist about the accuracy, transparency, and commercial influence shaping this content. This content analysis of the Instagram posts using #womenshealth aimed to map women's health topics and interventions discussed and assess the presence of health claims, financial conflicts of interest, and reference to scientific evidence. Of 472 included posts, 297 (63%) were created by individual influencers including 162 from other or alternative health professionals and 105 from medical doctors. 264 (56%) posts focused on general health information related to a disease, condition, issue or symptom, followed by 75 (16%) posts which promoted drugs, supplements, or devices. Hormonal and reproductive health-related posts were the most common topic with 226 (48%) posts, including 58 posts specifically about menopause and/or perimenopause. 324 (69%) posts had explicit health claims, with only 34 (7%) posts referring to scientific evidence. Financial interests were common; 241 (51%) of account holders had a clear financial interest and 186 (39%) posts directly sold an intervention. Women’s health-related Instagram content reveals an environment dominated by non-expert voices and commercially oriented messaging, blurring the line between women’s health education and advertising. These findings highlight the risk of misleading and commercialized women’s health information and underscore the need for stronger regulatory oversight and greater engagement from qualified health professionals in online spaces. More broadly, improving the quality of women’s health discourse requires addressing the structural drivers of online health narratives and strengthening trust across women’s information environments.

Background

Women's health has received increasing public attention in recent years, driven by recognition of evidence gaps in research and inadequate care for many conditions affecting women. At the same time, social media platforms have become an important and prominent source of health information, with many women turning to them for advice on symptoms, wellbeing, and treatments (Chen and Wang 2021, Anderer 2024, Pedroso et al. 2026). Health influencers, including both lay people and healthcare professionals and practitioners, build an audience and create and/or share health advice, often to raise awareness and reduce perceived stigma for certain conditions and their treatments (Olsson et al. 2024). However, concerns have been growing about the trustworthiness of the information shared on social media and the potential influence of financial interests (Ershov et al. 2025, Nickel et al. 2025b, 2026). Posts often rely on anecdotes or emotional personal experiences over scientific evidence (Nickel et al. 2025b), raising questions about the accuracy of the information presented and the health claims made, and whether commercial interests are shaping advice in ways that do not always support women’s best interests.

These concerns can be understood through the emerging framework of digital determinants of health which recognize that digital environments, such as social media, can shape health behaviours (Kickbusch and Holly 2023). They also reflect longstanding scholarship examining how media and cultural narratives shape the representation and communication of women’s health issues, influencing how health, bodies, and illness experiences are understood within broader social and commercial contexts (Hinnant 2009, Lupton 2012, 2020, Beijbom et al. 2023), with social media platforms now extending these processes into new digital environments.

Studies have examined social media content related to specific and predetermined women’s health topics such as pregnancy, nutrition, body image and beauty standards, polycystic ovary syndrome, and menopause, and have recognized the presence of misleading information (George et al. 2023, Jones et al. 2024, Jiménez-García et al. 2025). However, to our knowledge, there is limited research examining the broader landscape of women’s health content shared on social media, leaving little understanding of which topics are most prevalent and what health issues women are likely to encounter in their feeds. We therefore conducted a content analysis to inductively map the major women's health topics and interventions being discussed and also to assess the presence of health claims, financial interests, and use of scientific evidence on social media.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a content analysis of Instagram (Meta) posts about women’s health. Content analysis (Drisko and Maschi 2015) is a widely used research method to systematically analyse and quantify various types of data including written, verbal, and visual content. The study reporting adheres to the STROBE Statement guidelines (von Elm et al. 2007). A waiver of consent from The University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee was obtained (2025/HE000515).

Data collection

We sampled posts from #womenshealth on Instagram. The use of #womenshealth reflects the way users, creators, and commercial actors collectively organize and make visible women’s health-related content on Instagram, while recognizing that hashtag-based approaches may capture certain forms of content and engagement more readily than others. The hashtag included >8.8M posts at the time of searching and we included the first appearing 500 posts. Posts were eligible if they included the #womenshealth and were in English. Text-only posts, images, infographics, articles, and recorded videos including reels and audio-only posts were included and analysed. Live stories were excluded due to their time-limited presence. There was no exclusion on number of followers the influencer or account holder had.

The posts were sampled from a university VPN in November 2025 from a newly created Instagram account, noting the gender as female and age as 29, as the mean age of the largest demographic of Instagram users (25–34). Instagram was chosen as it is one of the most popular and fast-growing platforms for shortform content and is widely used across women of all ages (Kemp 2025). Data from the caption, on-screen text, and audio and video were extracted into an Excel spreadsheet and included in the analysis. URLs were saved and returned to for additional data analysis.

Analysis

The analysis largely took a deductive approach using a pre-specified coding framework in line with our aims to examine the health topics and interventions. Codes, tested in our previous research (Nickel et al. 2024, 2025a, 2025b) were used and adapted. This included the account holder and post characteristics (e.g. followers, gender, self-stated credentials, and date of the post); engagement and virality cues (e.g. views, likes, and comments); the overarching health category addressed in the post: general health information/disease awareness/drug or supplement/device (test or treatment)/none i.e. unrelated to women’s health; whether a health claim (i.e. a statement suggesting that a product, intervention, behaviour, or practice could influence a health outcome) was made; financial interests of the account holder (e.g. disclosure—presence or absence including ad, paid partnership or sponsorship, discount, company clearly promoting or selling an intervention); direct sale of an intervention; and reference to scientific evidence. The specific women's health topics discussed were also inductively captured.

Coding of the data was undertaken independently by one researcher (M.D.), with key attributes including the overarching health category addressed in the posts, the specific health topics and/or interventions discussed, health claims, financial interest and scientific evidence, double coded by a second (B.N.). Where posts addressed multiple categories, the most prominent topic or primary focus of the post’s content was used for coding purposes. Inter-rater reliability across all key attributes was 96.98% for crude agreement and 0.94 for Cohen’s kappa. Any discrepancies in double coding were discussed and resolved. Quantitative data were collated in Excel and analysed in Stata SE Version 18.0.

Results

Of the 500 captured posts, 28 were excluded during data analysis as posts were no longer available or were duplicates, leaving 472 posts in total. Posts were created between 6 May 2024 and 15 November 2025, with the median date of 10 July 2025. The account holders of the included posts had a combined total of 78 707 322 followers (median of 67 100 followers) at the time of data analysis. Comments were visible in 50% of the posts with a median of 32 comments per post and likes were available in 17.5% of the posts with a median of 1192 likes.

Account holders were predominantly individual influencers (297 posts, 63%), with 91% being females. Of the individual influencers, 162 (55%) were other or alternative health professionals (e.g. health coaches, naturopaths) and 105 (35%) were medical doctors. The remaining posts were from company accounts (76 posts, 16%), community organizations (71 posts, 15%), media organizations (6 posts, 1%), or non-governmental organizations (6 posts, 1%). A final three posts were from ‘other’ accounts. 254 (54%) posts were from verified accounts.

The posts were exclusively categorized into six overarching pre-specified health categories: 264 (56%) posts related to general health information of a disease, condition, issue or symptom; 75 (16%) posts related to the promotion of drugs, supplements or devices; 44 (9%) posts related to nutrition; 19 (4%) posts related to fitness; 58 (12%) posts related to other women’s health topics including spirituality and weight loss; and 12 (3%) posts were completely unrelated to women’s health. Discussion

Our study provides a snapshot of women’s health-related content on social media, highlighting a high-volume, high-reach information environment dominated by non-expert voices and commercially oriented messaging mostly without reference to evidence. Most posts originated from individual female influencers, with limited representation from medical doctors suggesting that much of the health information encountered by women on social media is likely shaped by personal branding and experiential narratives rather than clinical expertise.

Broadly, our findings add to a growing body of research that raises important concerns about the conflicts of interest of widely disseminated health content on social media platforms (Suarez-Lledo and Alvarez-Galvez 2021), including recent concerns around content discussing and promoting non-evidence-based health interventions (Nickel et al. 2025a, 2025b). Specifically, our study aligns with other recent research examining narrower areas of women’s health on social media, which has similarly identified the prominence of commercially driven narratives, health advice framed through personal experience, and the translation of complex health topics into accessible but potentially oversimplified content (de Moel-Mandel et al. 2025, Pfender and Fowler 2025, Eubanks and Shvartsman 2026, Marks and Jones 2026). These findings also align with literature on Hashtag Publics (Rambukkana 2015) through which #womenshealth represents a digital public where lived experience, expertise, and commercial interests intersect to shape the visibility and circulation of women’s health information online.

The predominance of posts focused on general health information, particularly hormonal and reproductive health, indicates that such content may constitutes a large amount of the current women’s health-related information on social media. Notably, menopause and perimenopause accounted for a substantial proportion of this content, reflecting growing public interest in these historically under-discussed topics (Orgad and Rottenberg 2024, Barbosa and Amorim 2025). However, this apparent increase in visibility of menopause-related content is accompanied by a rise in companies trying to fill the space (Wood et al. 2025, Marsh 2026). This highlights how social media may facilitate the commercialization of health information by increasing the visibility of commercially driven health narratives (Kickbusch and Holly 2023). The commercially driven discussion and promotion in our sample of posts is clear, with over half of account holders having an explicit financial interest, and nearly 40% of posts having a direct link to purchase or access related health products or interventions. This convergence of accessible women’s health information and marketing of women’s health products blurs the boundary between education and advertisement, similar to that shown in traditional diseases awareness campaigns (Leonardo Alves et al. 2014, Johansson et al. 2024), which is likely to influence women’s health behaviours in ways that are not evidence based.

Of additional concern is the low use of scientific evidence in our sample despite the high prevalence of explicit health claims made, many by those with financial interests. Recent data indicate that, although most adults recognize that health content on social media may be false or misleading, many continue to engage with these platforms for health advice (Pedroso et al. 2026). This emphasizes a critical gap between the widespread consumption of health information on social media and the evidentiary basis supporting the claims being made. Consistent with research on reproductive health misinformation, misleading online health content often relies on subtle exaggeration and persuasive framing, making it difficult for users to distinguish evidence-based information from commercially or ideologically driven narratives (Malki et al. 2023). The limited reference to evidence in our sample may also reflect broader challenges in translating scientific knowledge about health-related topics into formats that are both accurate and accessible for social media audiences. Although prior work has shown that evidence-based communication can be achieved and can meaningfully improve public understanding (Powell and Pring 2024, Roberts-Lewis et al. 2024, Michel et al. 2025, Graham et al. 2026), our findings indicate that, at present, women’s health content online remains at clear risk of being misleading and oversimplified (Denniss and Lindberg 2025).

While the commercialization of women’s health content on social media raises important concerns and may lead to the purchase and/or consumption of unnecessary medical products and interventions, it is also important to recognize that these platforms can provide valuable opportunities for peer support, community building, and the sharing of lived experiences (Chen and Wang 2021). Parasocial relationships, whereby followers develop feelings of familiarity and trust with influencers, can make health advice feel more relatable and accessible, even when formal expertise is limited (Heiss et al. 2025). For some women, particularly those who have experienced barriers to accessing healthcare, dismissal of their health concerns, or broader failures within health systems, narratives promoted on social media may therefore resonate with women and represent an accessible space to seek information and validation. These factors may contribute to the appeal and influence of health-related content online and should be considered alongside concerns regarding commercial interests and the quality of health information being shared.

Our study provides a novel overview of women’s health information on social media. Using an inclusive approach without a predetermined disease focus, we captured a broad snapshot of the content women may encounter on social media. Whilst the findings reflect a single time point and may not capture the rapidly evolving nature of social media, our analysis identifies broader thematic patterns that are likely to remain relatively stable over time, even as specific topics, products, or trending health conversations shift e.g. injectable peptides (Piatkowski et al. 2026). The findings are also shaped by Instagram’s specific algorithms, including factors such as the user age we set and geographical location, which may limit generalizability to other platforms. Finally, it is important to note that we did not verify the health claims being made, nor the evidence being cited. Future research could build on our study and other work examining women’s health in digital environments by exploring how women engage with, interpret, and act on health information encountered on social media, including how commercially driven narratives may influence health beliefs and behaviours. Evaluation of the accuracy and impact of online health claims, alongside research identifying approaches to support evidence-based communication while preserving the benefits of peer support and lived experience sharing within digital health communities, is also needed.

Conclusion

Taken together, our findings point to a complex social media landscape in which women’s health information may be widely accessible but not consistently reliable. The dominance of commercially motivated, non-expert voices, coupled with frequent health claims and limited reference to scientific evidence, underscores the immediate need for stronger regulatory oversight, clearer standards for transparency of content and account holders vested interests, and greater involvement from qualified health professionals in online spaces (Gram et al. 2025a, 2025b). However, to truly improve the discourse around women’s health we need to move beyond these measures and recognize the commercial drivers of these health narratives in order to re-build trust across women’s information ecosystems (Purnat and Clark 2025, Purnat and Scales 2025).
