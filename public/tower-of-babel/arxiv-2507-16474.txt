Stability for multiple Lamb dipoles
Ken Abe, In-Jee Jeong, Yao Yao

Abstract
. In the class of nonnegative vorticities on the half-plane, we establish the Lyapunov stability of finite sums of Lamb dipoles under the initial assumptions that the dipoles are sufficiently separated and that the faster dipoles are positioned to the right of the slower ones. Our approach combines sharp energy estimates near the Lamb dipoles with a Lagrangian bootstrapping scheme, enabling us to quantify the exchanges of circulation, enstrophy, impulse, and energy between various parts of the solution. The strategy of the proof is robust, and we present several potential extensions of the result.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we are concerned with long-time dynamics of
nonnegative vorticities
in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}=\left\{x=(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{2}\geq 0\right\}
, evolving by the incompressible Euler equation:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=0,&\\
u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega,&\end{aligned}\right.

where
\nabla^{\perp}=(-\partial_{x_{2}},\partial_{x_{1}})^{\top}
and
\Delta^{-1}
is the Dirichlet Laplacian inverse in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. We shall always assume that the initial data
\omega_{0}=\omega(t=0)
belongs to
L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, so that there is a unique global-in-time solution by Yudovich theory
[
63
]
.

Although global well-posedness is well-known, the long-time dynamics of (
1.1
) is a highly challenging problem: while the vorticity seems to generally concentrate on just a few points as
t\to\infty
(
[
52
,
5
,
32
,
24
]
), at the same time, it is known that complicated quasi-periodic motion can persist (
[
48
,
4
,
25
,
34
,
40
,
38
]
). Regarding this problem, the setting of nonnegative vortices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
could offer a simplification, thanks to the following monotone behavior (
[
45
,
44
]
):

(1.2)

\begin{split}c_{0}(\omega_{0})\leq\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{1}\omega(t,x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}u_{1}(t,x)\omega(t,x)\,dx\leq C_{0}(\omega_{0})\end{split}

where
c_{0}(\omega_{0})\leq C_{0}(\omega_{0})
are strictly positive constants (unless
\omega_{0}
is identically zero) depending only on
\omega_{0}
. That is, on the vorticity support, the “fluid particles” are drifting to the right in average with
O(1)
speed: then it is conceivable that if the vorticity consists of a few separated vortices, the faster ones will escape to spatial infinity earlier than the slower ones, making the interaction smaller as
t\to\infty
. In the current work, we present several definite results in this direction, namely forward-in-time stability and spatial separation of multi-vortex solutions.

Let us recall a few important conserved quantities for (
1.1
). Any
L^{p}
norm (
p\in[1,\infty]
), as well as the distribution function of
\omega(t,\cdot)
is preserved in time, since the velocity is divergence-free. In relation to the Lamb dipoles, the
enstrophy

K[\omega(t,\cdot)]:=\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}^{2},

which is simply the square of the
L^{2}
norm, plays a particularly important role. Furthermore, we introduce the
impulse
(the
L^{1}
norm with weight
x_{2}
)

\mu[\omega(t,\cdot)]:=\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}_{*}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{2}|\omega(t,x)|dx,

and the
kinetic energy

\begin{split}E[\omega(t,\cdot)]:=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega(t,x)(\Delta^{-1}\omega)(t,x)dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|u(t,x)|^{2}dx.\end{split}

The kinetic energy is conserved by (
1.1
). Furthermore, the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}x_{2}\omega(t,x)dx
is also conserved by (
1.1
), which coincides with our convention of the impulse under the sign assumption
\omega(t,\cdot)\geq 0
.

1.1.
Lamb dipoles and the main result

The collection of Lamb dipoles constitutes a two parameter family of traveling waves to (
1.1
).
We set the
normalized Lamb dipole
to be

(1.3)

\overline{\omega}_{Lamb}(x):=\frac{-2c_{L}}{J_{0}(c_{L})}\,J_{1}(c_{L}r)\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}(r)\sin(\theta)

in the polar coordinate system
(r,\theta)
.
Here,
J_{m}(r)
is the
m
-th order Bessel function of the first kind and
c_{L}=3.83\dots
is the first positive zero of
J_{1}(r)
. While we defer the details to §
2.3
,
\overline{\omega}_{Lamb}(x-t\mathbf{e}_{1})
is a compactly supported solution to (
1.1
) whose impulse, enstrophy, and energy are given by
\pi,\pi c_{L}^{2}
, and
\pi
, respectively. See Figure
1
for an illustration of the vorticity and streamline of a normalized Lamb dipole in the moving frame.

.

A two-parameter family of Lamb dipoles is obtained by scaling (

\kappa,\mu>0
, the Lamb dipole with enstrophy

where

\overline{\omega}_{Lamb}=\omega_{Lamb}^{\sqrt{\pi}c_{L},\pi}

N\geq 1
, positive

\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}}=\{\bar{\kappa}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

\mathbf{\bar{p}}=(\bar{p}_{1},\cdots,\bar{p}_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

N
Lamb dipoles:

(1.5)

\begin{split}\overline{\omega}^{\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}},\boldsymbol{\bar{\mu}}}_{Lamb,\mathbf{\bar{p}}}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{i},\bar{\mu}_{i}}(x-\bar{p}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}).\end{split}

The velocity of the
i

\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{i},\bar{\mu}_{i}}

\bar{\kappa}_{i}/(\sqrt{\pi}c_{L})
(note that

\bar{V}_{i}

\bar{V}_{1}>\bar{V}_{2}>\bar{V}_{3}
.

Given two norms
X,Y
defined for functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we shall write
\|f\|_{X\cap Y}:=\|f\|_{X}+\|f\|_{Y}
.

requires the initial condition to be close to a superposition where the velocities are ordered and the centers are far apart.)

Theorem A

(Stability of linear superposition of
N
Lamb dipoles)
.

Fix
M>0
,
N\geq 1
, and positive
N
-vectors
\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}},\boldsymbol{\bar{\mu}}
, with
\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}}
satisfying
\bar{\kappa}_{1}>\bar{\kappa}_{2}>\cdots>\bar{\kappa}_{N}.
Then, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
depending only on
N,M,\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}},\boldsymbol{\bar{\mu}}
with the property that for any
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, there exist
\delta_{0},D_{0}>0
depending on
\varepsilon,N,M,\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}},\boldsymbol{\bar{\mu}}
such that as long as

\begin{split}\bar{p}_{i}>\bar{p}_{i+1}+D_{0}\qquad\mbox{for all}\qquad 1\leq i\leq N-1,\end{split}

we have the following stability result. For any initial data
\omega_{0}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
with

(1.6)

\begin{split}\left\|\omega_{0}-\overline{\omega}^{\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}},\boldsymbol{\bar{\mu}}}_{Lamb,\bar{{\bf p}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\leq\delta_{0},\quad\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\leq M,\quad\text{ and }\quad|{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega_{0})|\leq M,\end{split}

the corresponding solution
\omega(t,\cdot)
to
\omega_{0}
satisfies

(1.7)

\begin{split}\left\|\omega(t,\cdot)-\overline{\omega}^{\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}},\boldsymbol{\bar{\mu}}}_{Lamb,\mathbf{p}(t)}\right\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\leq\varepsilon\qquad\mbox{for all}\qquad t>0\end{split}

for some time-dependent vector
\mathbf{p}(t)=(p_{1}(t),\cdots,p_{N}(t))
satisfying the estimate

(1.8)

\begin{split}|p_{i}(t)-\bar{p}_{i}-\bar{V}_{i}\,t|\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2}(1+t),\qquad 1\leq i\leq N,\end{split}

for some
C=C(M,N,\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}},\boldsymbol{\bar{\mu}})>0
.

Remark 1.1
.

A few remarks are in order.

•

When
N=1
(single Lamb dipole case), global-in-time stability was obtained in
[
1
]
, and we shall discuss this case in more detail in §
1.2.1
. The “shift estimate” of the form (
1.8
) was obtained later in
[
19
]
, with improvements in
[
47
]
. In the case
N=1
,
\varepsilon_{0}
does not depend on
\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}
. For
N\geq 2
, the
L^{\infty}
bound of
\omega_{0}
is necessary for estimating
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
(cf. Lemma
2.2
), since our proof is dynamical in nature. (To this end, a bound on
\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{p}}
for any
p>2
suffices.)

•

When
N\geq 2
, the statement is nontrivial even when the initial data is given
exactly
as the linear superposition of Lamb dipoles. In fact, the proof in this case is not any simpler. Note that the velocity generated by each Lamb dipole is not compactly supported; it pushes down ones in the back, and lifts up ones in the front (cf. Figure
1
). Furthermore, it is not difficult to see that the condition
\bar{\kappa}_{1}>\bar{\kappa}_{2}>\cdots>\bar{\kappa}_{N}
is essential. Specifically, if a faster Lamb dipole is initially located to the left of a slower one, then by assuming (
1.7
) for all
t\geq 0
, it can be deduced that the faster Lamb dipole will eventually catch up to the slower one and “collide,” which would contradict (
1.7
).
2
2

2

In this contradiction argument, (
1.8
) does not need to be assumed.
Switching the initial order of the Lamb dipoles has little effect on the coercive conserved quantities of the Euler equations, clearly indicating that Theorem
A
cannot be proved solely through frozen-time variational considerations.

•

Note that the condition
\bar{\kappa}_{1}>\bar{\kappa}_{2}>\cdots>\bar{\kappa}_{N}
only says the enstrophy (which is proportional to velocity) of the Lamb dipoles is ordered; we do not require their amplitude, impulse or radii of support to be ordered. In particular, a Lamb dipole with a larger radius is not necessarily faster than another one with a smaller radius; see the relation in (
1.4
).

•

The norm
L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}
in (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) can be replaced by
L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}\cap L^{1}
. In particular, any initial data
\omega_{0}
satisfying the assumptions above gives a nontrivial example where the rescaled vorticity
t^{2}\omega(t,tx)
converges in the sense of a measure as
t\to\infty
, a theory developed by Iftimie–Lopes Filho–Nussenzveig Lopes
[
42
]
. See §
1.3.1
for details.

•

In the pioneering work of Martel–Merle–Tsai
[
51
]
, a similar statement which also includes the asymptotic stability was obtained for linear superpositions of the gKdV solitons. Unfortunately, the asymptotic stability for the Lamb dipoles is not known even in the case
N=1
. One notable difference is that the family of Lamb dipoles (as well as any other traveling waves for Euler) comes in a more than two-parameter family, while gKdV solitons are just parameterized by the speed. This presents serious complications, and of course the Euler dynamics is very different from that for gKdV; see §
1.2.4
for the main challenges arising in our case.

The strategy we develop is quite robust, and we believe that Theorem
A
can be generalized in several ways, in particular replacing each Lamb dipole with another orbitally stable one. We shall discuss potential generalizations below in §
1.3
, after explaining the ideas and difficulties of proof. To demonstrate flexibility of the method, let us present a simple result which says that one can “separate out” a Lamb dipole from the solution, with the sole assumption that the other part is initially slower than the Lamb dipole under all possible rearrangements.

To state the result, given an essentially bounded
\omega\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we define
V_{max}[\omega]
as the maximum velocity under any rearrangement of
\omega
, namely

\begin{split}V_{max}[\omega]=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{R}[\omega]}\|\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}(g)\|_{L^{\infty}}\end{split}

where
\mathcal{R}[\omega]
is the set of rearrangements of
\omega
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
:

(1.9)

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}[\omega]=\left\{g\,:\,\left|\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\,:\,g(x)>\lambda\}\right|=\left|\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\,:\,\omega(x)>\lambda\}\right|\mbox{ for all }\lambda>0\right\}.\end{split}

Theorem B

(Separating out a Lamb dipole from slower part)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0,\alpha\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
and
M>0
, there exist
\delta_{0},D_{0}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon,\alpha,M
, such that the following holds.

Consider any initial data
\omega_{0}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
of the form
\omega_{0}=\omega_{0l}+\omega_{0r}
, where
\omega_{0r}
satisfies the following for some
D>D_{0}
:

\|\omega_{0r}-\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(\cdot-D\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\leq\delta_{0},\quad\|\omega_{0r}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\leq M,\quad\text{ and }\quad|{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega_{0r})|\leq M,

and
\omega_{0l}
satisfies
{\mathrm{supp}}\,\omega_{0l}\subset\{x_{1}<x_{2}\cot\alpha\}
, and
V_{max}[\omega_{0l}]<\sin\alpha
.
Then, with
V_{avr}
and
\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)
defined by

\begin{split}V_{avr}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{V_{max}[\omega_{0l}]}{\sin\alpha}\right)<1\quad\text{ and }\quad\omega_{r}(t,\cdot):=\omega(t,\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}>x_{2}\cot\alpha+V_{avr}t\}},\end{split}

we have the following stability: for some
p(t)
satisfying
|p(t)-t-D|\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2}(1+t)
,

\|\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)-\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(\cdot-p(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }\quad t\geq 0.

Remark 1.2
.

We give a few remarks regarding Theorem
B
.

•

The point of this result is that we do not require any “detailed” information on the part
\omega_{0l}
for separating out a Lamb dipole to the right. It is expected that if furthermore
\omega_{0l}
is close to any of the “locally energy maximizing” dipole, then this information also propagates for all
t\geq 0
; we leave this extension to a future work.

•

This result is stated for simplicity with the normalized Lamb dipole
\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}
defined in (
1.3
), and a similar result holds for all the other Lamb dipoles by appropriately rescaling the assumptions.

•

The condition
V_{max}[\omega_{0l}]<\sin\alpha
is sharp in view of particle trajectories: any fluid particle starting from the support of
\omega_{0l}
is not able to reach the line
\{x_{1}=x_{2}\cot\alpha+V_{avr}t\}
for
t\geq 0
by the velocity that
\omega_{0l}
and its all possible rearrangements generate.

•

It is well-known that

V_{max}[\omega]\leq C_{0}\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/2}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}}^{1/2}
holds
for some universal constant
C_{0}>0

[
42
,
44
]
. Therefore, Theorem
B
is applicable if
\omega_{0l}\in L^{\infty}
is close to a vortex patch
\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{0}}
with
\Omega_{0}\subset\{x_{1}<x_{2}\cot\alpha\}
and
|\Omega_{0}|
sufficiently small. This also shows that for Theorem
B
to hold, it is not necessary for the slower part
\omega_{0l}
to be located to the “left” of the Lamb dipole.

1.2.
Overall strategy and difficulties

1.2.1.
Stability of a single Lamb dipole

The first ingredient in our proof is the stability for a single Lamb dipole from
[
1
]
. To describe the setup, we introduce the admissible class

(1.10)

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\kappa,\mu}:=\left\{\omega\geq 0\mbox{ on }\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\,:\,\,\|\omega\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\leq\kappa,\,\,\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\leq\mu\right\}.

Note that
\omega_{Lamb}^{\kappa,\mu}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\kappa,\mu}
by definition, and the main result of
[
1
]
(see Theorem
2.7
below) says that
\omega_{Lamb}^{\kappa,\mu}
is the energy maximizer in the class (
1.10
), unique up to shifts along the
x_{1}
-axis. This variational characterization of the Lamb dipoles implies Lyapunov stability for each of them. In this work, we are going to use the following version, which says that under tight
L^{2},L^{1}_{*}
bounds, any vorticity having similar energy with the corresponding Lamb dipole must be close to it.

Proposition 1.3
.

Fix some
\kappa,\mu>0
and
M>0
. Then, for any
\varepsilon_{1}>0
, there exists
\delta_{1}=\delta_{1}(\kappa,\mu,M,\varepsilon_{1})>0
such that the following holds: if
\omega\geq 0
,
\|\omega\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\leq M
satisfies

\begin{split}\omega\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\kappa+\delta_{1},\mu+\delta_{1}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad E[\omega]\geq E[\omega_{Lamb}^{\kappa,\mu}]-\delta_{1},\end{split}

then we have

\begin{split}\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\|\omega-\omega_{Lamb}^{\kappa,\mu}(\cdot-\tau\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\leq\varepsilon_{1}.\end{split}

The proof of this proposition is given in
[
22
, Proposition 2.6]
, but again, this is just a reformulation of the compactness statement from
[
1
, Theorems 1.3 and 1.5]
.

1.2.2.
Decomposing the solution into
N
pieces

To prove Theorem
A
, we shall decompose the solution
\omega(t,\cdot)
appropriately into
N
disjoint pieces, and apply Proposition
1.3
to each of them. There does not seem to be a “canonical” way to decompose the solution, and we simply used hard cutoffs: we take (for the precise definition, see §
3.2
)

(1.11)

\begin{split}\omega(t,\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_{i}(t,\cdot),\qquad\omega_{i}(t,\cdot):=\omega(t,\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\frac{P_{i}(t)+P_{i+1}(t)}{2}\leq x_{1}<\frac{P_{i-1}(t)+P_{i}(t)}{2}\right\}}(\cdot)\end{split}

where
P_{i}(t)=\bar{p}_{i}+C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}t=\bar{p}_{i}+\bar{V}_{i}t
is the “expected location” of the
i
-th Lamb dipole for
1\leq i\leq N
when interactions are absent, and
P_{0}:=+\infty
and
P_{N+1}:=-\infty
. We shall set
K_{i}(t):=\|\omega_{i}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
,
\mu_{i}(t):=\|\omega_{i}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{*}}
, and
E_{i}(t):=E[\omega_{i}(t)]
for simplicity. Note that enstrophy and impulse are both
additive
: we have

(1.12)

\begin{split}K_{1}(t)+\cdots+K_{N}(t)=\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mu_{1}(t)+\cdots+\mu_{N}(t)=\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{*}}\end{split}

for all
t
. On the other hand, the energy has a positive “interaction” term
E_{inter}(t)
(see §
2.2.3
for the precise definition), so that the total energy satisfies

E_{0}=E(t)=E_{1}(t)+\cdots+E_{N}(t)+E_{inter}(t)>E_{1}(t)+\cdots+E_{N}(t).

For Proposition
1.3
to be applicable to each of
\omega_{i}(t,\cdot)
, we need
global-in-time
bounds

(1.13)

\begin{split}K_{i}(t)\leq K_{i}(0)+C\delta_{0},\quad\mu_{i}(t)\leq\mu_{i}(0)+C\delta_{0},\quad E_{i}(t)>E_{i}(0)-C\delta_{0}\end{split}

for all
1\leq i\leq N
, with some time-independent
C>0
. We emphasize that
\delta_{0}
-smallness is needed (which is from initial data) and not just
\varepsilon
-smallness, which comes from the conclusion (
1.7
).
3
3

3

Even in the single Lamb dipole case
N=1
, the quantitative relation between
\delta_{0}
and
\varepsilon
is unknown, and in any case we expect
\delta_{0}\ll\varepsilon
, so we cannot rely on this
\varepsilon
-smallness to close estimates of the form (
1.13
).
The immediate difficulty we face in applying Proposition
1.3
with each
\omega_{i}(t,\cdot)
is that all of
K_{i},\mu_{i},E_{i}
are time-dependent due to interactions. In fact, as we shall explain below, there are competing effects which could make each of
K_{i}(t)
and
\mu_{i}(t)
grow or decay.

1.2.3.
Point vortex case

To see whether (
1.13
) has any chance to work, it is natural to first consider the case when each Lamb dipole is replaced by a point vortex. This was indeed considered in the pioneering work
[
42
]
. To be concrete, take an initial data of the form

(1.14)

\begin{split}\omega_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}\delta_{X_{i}(0)}\mbox{ on }\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},\quad X_{i}(0)=(p_{i}(0),h_{i}(0)),\end{split}

with
\Gamma_{i},h_{i}(0)>0
and
p_{1}(0)>\cdots>p_{N}(0)
. In the absence of interactions, the
i
-th vortex translates horizontally to the right with uniform speed
V_{i}:=\Gamma_{i}/(4\pi h_{i}(0))
. Similarly with the assumptions of Theorem
A
, we take

(1.15)

\begin{split}V_{1}>V_{2}>\cdots>V_{N},\qquad p_{i}(0)-p_{i+1}(0)>D_{0}\gg 1,\quad 1\leq i\leq N-1.\end{split}

If we now let (
1.14
) evolve according to the
N
vortex system on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
(Kirchhoff–Helmholtz equation) under the assumptions (
1.15
), then it can be proved that the corresponding solution

\begin{split}\omega(t,\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}\delta_{X_{i}(t)}\qquad X_{i}(t)=(p_{i}(t),h_{i}(t)),\end{split}

is global in time and satisfies

(1.16)

\begin{split}|h_{i}(t)-h_{i}(0)|\ll 1,\qquad\frac{1}{t}|p_{i}(t)-p_{i}(0)-V_{i}t|\ll 1\end{split}

for all
1\leq i\leq N
and
t\geq 0
. Therefore, an analogue of (
1.13
) holds for sufficiently separated point vortices, although we do not really have a concept corresponding to the enstrophy in this case. The key observations which give (
1.16
) are: if we take
D_{i}(t)
to be the smallest distance from
X_{i}(t)
to the other point vortices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we have the interaction velocity decay
\lesssim(D_{i}(t))^{-2}
, as well as linear in time separation
D_{i}(t)-D_{i}(0)\gtrsim t
, which makes the integrated (from
t=0
to
+\infty
) effect of mutual interactions small, as long as
D_{i}(0)
is large. This type of velocity decay and linear separation, whose precise version is given in Lemma
2.1
below, is indeed the key mechanism behind our main results as well.

1.2.4.
Enstrophy, Impulse, and Energy

We are in a position to discuss the key technical issues in establishing (
1.13
), most of which are present already in the case
N=2
. In this simplest case, we use the borderline
L(t):=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{p}_{1}+\bar{p}_{2}+(\bar{V}_{1}+\bar{V}_{2})t)
to decompose
\omega(t,\cdot)
as

\begin{split}\omega(t,x)=\omega_{1}(t,x)+\omega_{2}(t,x):=\omega(t,x)\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}\geq L(t)\}}+\omega(t,x)\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}<L(t)\}},\end{split}

and the goal is to obtain (
1.13
) for
i=1,2
.

Evolution of enstrophy and impulse
. In the simplest case of
N=2
, thanks to the conservation law (
1.12
), it suffices to prove
\int_{0}^{\infty}|\dot{K}_{1}(t)|dt,\int_{0}^{\infty}|\dot{\mu}_{1}(t)|dt\lesssim\delta_{0}
, where
\dot{}
denotes the time derivative. A direct computation yields

\begin{split}\dot{K}_{1}(t)=-\int_{\{x_{1}=L(t)\}}(\dot{L}-u_{1}(t,x))(\omega(t,x))^{2}dx_{2}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\dot{\mu}_{1}(t)=-\int_{\{x_{1}=L(t)\}}(\dot{L}-u_{1}(t,x))x_{2}\omega(t,x)dx_{2}+\int_{\{x_{1}>L(t)\}}(u_{2})_{2}(t,x)\omega_{1}(t,x)dx,\end{split}

and the bootstrap assumptions give that
\dot{L}\gg|u_{1}|
on the line
\{x_{1}=L(t)\}
for all
t\geq 0
. (Here,
(u_{2})_{2}
is the second component of the velocity generated by
\omega_{2}
.) Therefore, the first terms in the right-hand sides are always negative, which represents flux of enstrophy and impulse from right to left through the border
\{x_{1}=L(t)\}
.

Filamentation and variational principle for two Lamb dipoles
. These flux terms capture the effect of
filamentation
, or creation of long tails, behind a Lamb dipole.
4
4

4

This behavior is not specific to Lamb dipoles and widely observed in experiments and simulations
[
49
,
53
]
. Some rigorous results in formation of filaments are given in
[
19
,
20
,
33
,
47
]
.
Filamentation occurs
generically
because, in the reference frame moving with a Lamb dipole, its velocity field advects vorticity perturbations from the front to the rear outside the dipole, see Figure
1
. This is the immediate difficulty we face, compared to the point vortex case.

Even in the single Lamb dipole case (
N=1
), the only known way to rule out large filamentation is through the energy conservation and appealing to the energy maximizing character of a single Lamb dipole. Assuming for a moment that the last term in the equation for
\dot{\mu}_{1}
is negligible, for
N=2
, we need to have a statement that large filamentation from
\omega_{1}
to
\omega_{2}
is
energy unfavorable
, namely it decreases the total energy of the solution. To be more concrete, one may introduce the admissible class involving two ‘‘infinitely separated’’ vortices, for instance
5
5

5

This type of admissible classes were used for both construction and stability of “multi-soliton” solutions in certain situations in incompressible and compressible flows; see for instance
[
46
,
15
]
.

\begin{split}\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(2)}:=\left\{\omega=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}\,:\,\mathrm{dist}\,({\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega_{1}),{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega_{2}))\gg 1,\,\omega_{1}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}},\,\omega\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{(\bar{\kappa}_{1}^{2}+\bar{\kappa}_{2}^{2})^{1/2},\bar{\mu}_{1}+\bar{\mu}_{2}}\right\}.\end{split}

Somewhat surprisingly, this approach simply cannot work, because
\bar{\omega}_{1}+\bar{\omega}_{2}
with
\bar{\omega}_{i}:=\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{i},\bar{\mu}_{i}}
is not necessarily a local energy maximizer in
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(2)}
, even when
\bar{\kappa}_{1}>\bar{\kappa}_{2}
. Local energy maximizing property holds only if we assume additionally
{\bar{\kappa}_{2}}/{\bar{\mu}_{2}}>{\bar{\kappa}_{1}}/{\bar{\mu}_{1}}
. In other words, when
\bar{\kappa}_{1}>\bar{\kappa}_{2}
and
{\bar{\kappa}_{2}}/{\bar{\mu}_{2}}\leq{\bar{\kappa}_{1}}/{\bar{\mu}_{1}}
, we are in the “energy favorable” regime, in the sense that pure backward transfer of enstrophy and impulse either preserves or even increases the total energy! This suggests the possibility that over time,
\omega_{2}
may accelerate and eventually surpass
\omega_{1}
, breaking the stability without violating any of the conserved quantities.

Relating energy variation with impulse variation
. It turns out that multiple Lamb dipole stability holds even in the above “energy favorable” regime: our key observation is that the individual energy tends to vary much slowly relative to the corresponding impulse: The time derivative of
E_{1}(t):=E[\omega_{1}(t,\cdot)]
satisfies a similar identity with that of
\dot{\mu}_{1}
, and its negative term (due to filamentation) turns out to be much smaller than the corresponding negative term in
\dot{\mu}_{1}
.
6
6

6

This estimate quantifies the well-known tendency of incompressible fluids, at least within this specific setting: once energy becomes concentrated in a region, it tends to remain localized.
With some algebraic computations, this observation rules out stability breaking by large filamentation.

The “lift-up” effect and Lagrangian analysis of Gain–Error interaction
. We still need to show that the last term in the equation for
\dot{\mu}_{1}
is small, after integrated in time from
0
to
\infty
. This last term is actually nonnegative:
(u_{2})_{2}(t,x)>0
pointwise on
\{x_{1}>L(t)\}
, namely all the fluid particles from
\omega_{2}
are “lifting up” those in
\omega_{1}
, increasing the impulse of
\omega_{1}
.
7
7

7

This can be also understood from the streamlines depicted in Figure
1
.
Strictly away from the borderline
L(t)
, this lift-up effect decays with rate
t^{-2}
in time, which makes the time-integrated effect small for initially far away Lamb dipoles. This is indeed the main lesson from the case of point vortices.

However, what may happen is that, close to the borderline
L(t)
, the part of the vorticity that
\omega_{2}
gains from
\omega_{1}
via filamentation may continuously transfer its impulse back to the “adjacent” part which is still contained in
\omega_{1}
. We shall refer to this scenario as
Gain–Error interaction
: controlling this is the most technically involved part of the current paper. Let us summarize the key issues here:

•

Error
is defined by the part of
\omega_{1}
which is relatively close to the borderline with
\omega_{2}
; its smallness is given in Lemma
4.4
.

•

Gain
is defined by the part of
\omega_{2}
which came originally from
\omega_{1}
; its smallness is deduced in Lemma
4.3
. To control the Gain–Error interaction, we need to have the
L^{1}
control over the gain part–when two fluid particles are very close, the kernel defining the impulse exchange is singular, and cannot be directly controlled either by the enstrophy nor impulse.

To deal with this issue of having singular kernel, we switch to Lagrangian coordinates: to get uniform smallness integrated over time, we track the whole trajectory of each “gain particle” and explicitly show that it can make a nontrivial contribution only for a relatively short period of time during its “lifetime,” which is sufficient since the total circulation (
L^{1}
norm) of gain particles is small (Lemma
4.4
). This key Lagrangian computation is presented in Lemma
5.4
. In the end, what we manage to show is that the total impulse gain (integrated from
t=0
to
\infty
) of
\omega_{1}
from
\omega_{2}
can be bounded in terms of the total impulse loss of
\omega_{1}
to
\omega_{2}
via filamentation.

Lagrangian bootstrapping scheme and the shift estimate
. The previous strategy needs to be carried out carefully through a set of bootstrap assumptions: we assume (
1.13
) for some
C\delta_{0}
over an interval of time
[0,T]
and proceed to show, using these bootstrap assumptions, that (
1.13
) actually holds for
C\delta_{0}/100
, say.
8
8

8

We remark again that we do not have a quantitative relation between
\varepsilon
and
\delta_{0}
, and in particular
\varepsilon
-closeness of the solution to a sum of Lamb dipoles (which is the conclusion of Theorem
A
) can be used only in a very limited way.
In this process, we also need to ensure that each piece
\omega_{i}
defined by (
1.11
) “contains” the corresponding Lamb dipole near the expected location. This does not follow from energy considerations, and the estimate which tracks the approximate location of the Lamb dipole is based on another Lagrangian bootstrapping scheme itself. In this work, we follow the strategy from
[
47
]
for this part of the argument.

Functional inequalities involving enstrophy and impulse
. Since our bootstrap assumptions provide sharp bounds on the enstrophy and impulse, it is very important to control the solution based on these two quantities as much as possible. For instance, Proposition
2.4
shows that the interaction energy can be bounded purely in terms of the enstrophy and impulse. While the proof is elementary, such a bound seems to be new.

In the very end, the reason why Theorem
A
works is because the linear separation between the Lamb dipoles is sufficiently fast to prevent filaments from gaining and exchanging enough circulation, enstrophy, impulse, and energy with each other. The proof of Theorem
B
is more or less similar, with a slight difference in the choice of the bootstrap assumptions.

1.3.
Dynamics in the upper half-plane

Let us review relevant works regarding dynamics of the Euler equations in the upper half-plane, to put our main results in context.

1.3.1.
General results on the upper half-plane

There are a few results concerning the general long time dynamics of nonnegative vortices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}

[
43
,
45
,
42
,
44
]
. In
[
43
]
, Iftimie proved that for any nontrivial nonnegative
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, there exists
c_{0}>0
such that for all time,

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{1}\omega(t,x)dx\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{1}\omega_{0}(x)dx+c_{0}t\end{split}

where
\omega(t,\cdot)
is the corresponding Yudovich solution.
9
9

9

The linear rate of growth is sharp since there is a uniform in time bound for
\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
. While this result does not imply that the support of the vorticity in the
x_{1}
-coordinate can also grow linearly in time, this was proved later in
[
19
]
by filamentation from a single Lamb dipole.
In the same paper and in
[
42
]
, it was further proved that for any
t\geq 0
,

\begin{split}x_{2}\leq C(t\log(2+t))^{1/3},\qquad x_{1}\geq-D(t\log(2+t))^{1/2}\qquad\mbox{for all}\quad x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega(t,\cdot)),\end{split}

with the additional assumption that
\omega_{0}
is compactly supported, where
C,D>0
depend only on
\omega_{0}
. To the best of our knowledge, it is still not known whether these inequalities are sharp. Furthermore,
[
42
]
obtained a
scattering result
upon rescaling: defining
\widetilde{\omega}(t,x):=t^{2}\omega(t,tx)
(this preserves the
L^{1}
norm for each
t>0
), it was proved that if
\widetilde{\omega}(t,\cdot)
(viewed as a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
) weakly converges to a measure
\mu\otimes\delta_{0}
as
t\to\infty
, then
\mu
must be the sum of an at most countable set of Dirac deltas, which can possibly accumulate only at 0. That is (we refer to
[
42
]
for the precise statement),

\begin{split}t^{2}\omega(t,tx)\rightharpoonup\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}\delta(x_{1}-a_{i})\otimes\delta(x_{2})\end{split}

where
1\leq N\leq\infty
,
\Gamma_{i}>0
and
a_{i}\in[0,C]
where
C=\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u_{1}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
.

One can see that the solutions of Theorem
A
give a nontrivial limiting measure, where for each
1\leq i\leq N
,
\Gamma_{i}
and
a_{i}
are explicitly given as the
L^{1}
norm and the traveling speed of the
i
-th Lamb dipole, up to an
o(1)
error uniform in
t>0
. Given that the result of
[
42
]
does not exclude the case of countably many Dirac deltas accumulating at the origin, it is tempting to speculate stability for a countable sequence of rescaled Lamb dipoles, summable in the
L^{1}
norm. However, this seems to be a challenging problem for now.

1.3.2.
Traveling waves on the upper half-plane

There is a vast literature on the study of traveling wave solutions of (
1.1
) on the upper half-plane, many of which concern
existence
, the Lamb dipole being a rare example with an explicit formula. There are relatively few results proving the nonlinear stability of such traveling waves defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}

[
60
,
10
,
1
,
62
,
16
,
57
,
58
,
12
,
11
,
23
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. These stability results are largely based on the expectations of Kelvin
[
56
]
, Arnol’d
[
2
]
, and Benjamin
[
3
]
that stable traveling waves are those who maximize the kinetic energy under the impulse constraint. This variational principle can be supplemented with additional constraints which are conserved by the Euler flow, e.g. the
L^{p}
norm of the vorticity and more generally the distribution function. The stability result for such traveling waves with general distribution function was obtained by Burton, Nussenzveig Lopes, Lopes Filho in
[
12
]
, with relatively recent improvements in
[
11
,
62
]
. These works concern the case when the vortex is separated from the boundary of the half-plane. More recently, stability for a larger class of traveling waves including the Lamb dipole was obtained in
[
1
]
. Furthermore, the
uniqueness
of the maximizer under appropriate constraints is open, except for the case of Lamb dipoles
[
10
,
1
]
and the “large impulse regime” where the vortex is asymptotically circular
[
16
]
. Without uniqueness, the stability needs to be stated for the whole set of maximizers.

Numerical and experimental works
[
54
,
55
,
37
,
49
,
59
,
35
]
demonstrate the existence and stability of even a larger class of traveling waves than what is known.

We expect that the methods used here could be generalized to obtain stability of linear superpositions of other traveling waves, under the conditions that (i) each traveling wave is orbital stable in an appropriate sense, (ii) faster traveling wave is always located to the right relative to slower ones, and (iii) they are initially sufficiently far apart. In particular, a natural generalization is to consider the Sadovskii vortex
[
41
,
21
,
23
]
, which is a traveling wave patch that admits a similar variational characterization to the Lamb dipole. Still, many technical challenges will be present, especially when each of them is not related to the other by rescaling of some common profile.

Although we only consider 2D Euler on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
in this work, an analogous problem is to consider single-signed vortex rings in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which are axisymmetric solutions to the 3D Euler equations
[
61
,
9
,
18
,
14
]
. This is indeed the original setup for Kelvin and Benjamin, which motivated the study of traveling waves on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
as a model problem.

Lastly, we note that there are several recent works which concern long time dynamics of vortices in the strongly interacting regime, most notably the leapfrogging motion, both in two and three dimensions
[
39
,
29
,
28
,
13
,
31
]
.

1.3.3.
Case of the quadrant: additional odd symmetry

In the half-plane, one may consider vorticities that are odd in
x_{1}
, which reduces the motion to the first quadrant
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
. If one further assumes that the vorticity is nonnegative on
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
, then one obtains monotonicity (
[
45
]
)

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{1}\omega(t,x)dx>0>\frac{d}{dt}\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}}x_{2}\omega(t,x)dx.\end{split}

Under these symmetry and sign assumptions, the stability (
t\geq 0
) for two odd-symmetric Lamb dipoles moving away from each other was obtained in
[
22
]
. As this formula shows, the vorticity impulse restricted to
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
decreases with time. In particular, one does not need to worry about impulse gain, which is the most challenging issue in the current work.

Based on a detailed asymptotic information for sharply concentrated traveling waves in the half-plane (
[
27
]
), the work
[
26
]
obtained the existence of a concentrated vortex in
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
, which is asymptotic to a traveling wave in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
as
t\to+\infty
. It would be an interesting problem to obtain the Lamb dipole in this asymptotic limit as well.

1.3.4.
Viscous traveling waves

Another very important issue is to understand the dynamics of slightly viscous traveling waves, see numerical computations in
[
49
]
. Very recently,
[
30
]
obtained a long time approximation of a viscous traveling wave with a pair of Dirac deltas on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as the initial condition; see
[
36
]
for the case of a single vortex ring.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect several useful inequalities which will be used later in the proofs. Most of the inequalities are well-known (cf.
[
44
,
42
,
6
]
), but the ones in Proposition
2.4
are new, to the best of our knowledge.

2.1.
Notations and conventions

For a point
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we write
\bar{x}=(x_{1},-x_{2})
. With this notation, for two points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
|x-\bar{y}|=|\bar{x}-y|\geq|x_{2}+y_{2}|
. The integrals and norms will be taken in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, unless otherwise specified. The general bounds for the stream function, velocity, and energy do not require that
\omega
is nonnegative, and we shall mention it explicitly when the assumption
\omega\geq 0
is necessary.

2.2.
Bounds for stream function, velocity, and energy

2.2.1.
Stream function

Given a vorticity
\omega
, the corresponding stream function
\psi=\Delta^{-1}\omega
is given explicitly by

(2.1)

\begin{split}\psi(x)&=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\log\left(1+\frac{4x_{2}y_{2}}{|x-{y}|^{2}}\right)\,\omega(y)\,dy=:-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}{\bf G}(x,y)\,\omega(y)\,dy.\end{split}

The kernel
{\bf G}
is nonnegative for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and therefore if
\omega\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we have that
\psi\leq 0
. Furthermore, for any
0<\alpha\leq 1
, there is a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
depending only on
\alpha
such that

(2.2)

\begin{split}0\leq{\bf G}(x,y)\leq C_{\alpha}\frac{(x_{2}y_{2})^{\alpha}}{|x-{y}|^{2\alpha}}\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},x\neq y.\end{split}

In particular, if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
satisfies
x_{2}>0
, and
x
is separated from the support of
\omega
by a distance
D(x)=\inf_{y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega)}|x-{y}|>0
, we can apply the above inequality for
\alpha=1
and obtain

\begin{split}\frac{|\psi(x)|}{x_{2}}\leq\frac{C}{D^{2}(x)}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}}.\end{split}

2.2.2.
Velocity

The velocity
u=(u_{1},u_{2})
is defined by
u(x)=\nabla^{\perp}\psi=-\nabla^{\perp}(-\Delta)^{-1}\omega
. For convenience, we introduce the kernel
{\bf K}(x,y)=({\bf K}_{1}(x,y),{\bf K}_{2}(x,y))=-\nabla^{\perp}_{x}{\bf G}(x,y)
so that

(2.3)

\begin{split}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}{\bf K}(x,y)\omega(y)dy.\end{split}

We present a simple lemma for the velocity.

Lemma 2.1
.

The velocity kernel
{\bf K}
satisfies

(2.4)

\begin{split}|{\bf K}(x,y)|\leq\frac{Cy_{2}}{|x-y||x-\bar{y}|}.\end{split}

If
x\in\mathbb{R}_{2}^{+}
is separated from
{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega)
by a distance
D(x):=\inf_{y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega)}|x-y|>0
, then
u(x)
satisfies

(2.5)

\begin{split}|u(x)|\leq\frac{C}{D^{2}(x)}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}},\end{split}

and its second component further satisfies the estimate

(2.6)

\begin{split}\frac{|u_{2}(x)|}{x_{2}}\leq\frac{C}{D^{3}(x)}\min\left\{{\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}}},D(x)\|\omega\|_{L^{1}}\right\}.\end{split}

Proof.

We write

\begin{split}u_{1}(x)&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(\frac{x_{2}-y_{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}-\frac{x_{2}+y_{2}}{|x-\bar{y}|^{2}}\right)\omega(y)dy\\
&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\frac{4x_{2}^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}|x-\bar{y}|^{2}}y_{2}\omega(y)dy+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}+\frac{1}{|x-\bar{y}|^{2}}\right)y_{2}\omega(y)dy\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\frac{2(x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+2(y_{2}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})}{|x-y|^{2}|x-\bar{y}|^{2}}y_{2}\omega(y)dy\end{split}

Similarly,

(2.7)

\begin{split}u_{2}(x)&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(\frac{x_{1}-y_{1}}{|x-y|^{2}}-\frac{x_{1}-y_{1}}{|x-\bar{y}|^{2}}\right)\omega(y)dy=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\frac{4(x_{1}-y_{1})x_{2}}{|x-y|^{2}|x-\bar{y}|^{2}}y_{2}\omega(y)dy.\end{split}

Therefore, (
2.4
) follows using that
|x-\bar{y}|\geq\max\{x_{2}+y_{2},|x-y|\}
. From (
2.4
), the bound (
2.5
) readily follows. (
2.6
) directly follows from (
2.7
).
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

We have

(2.8)

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}\left(1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\|\omega\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}}}{\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}^{3}}\right)\right).\end{split}

As a consequence, for any
0<\alpha<1
, there exists a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
such that

(2.9)

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{\alpha}\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}^{1-\alpha}\left(\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}^{\alpha}+\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2\alpha/3}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}}^{\alpha/3}\right).\end{split}

Remark 2.3
.

These upper bounds hold also for
|\psi(x)/x_{2}|
, since

\begin{split}\frac{\psi(x)}{x_{2}}=-\frac{1}{x_{2}}\int_{0}^{x_{2}}u_{1}(x_{1},z)dz.\end{split}

Proof.

Let us apply (
2.4
) to the integral (
2.3
) for the velocity, and split the integration domain into regions (i)
\{|x-y|<l\}
, (ii)
\{l\leq|x-y|<L\}
, and (iii)
\{L\leq|x-y|\}
where

\begin{split}l:=\frac{\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}}{\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}},\qquad L^{2}:=\frac{\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}}}{\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}}.\end{split}

When
l\geq L
, we do not need to estimate the integral in the intermediate region (ii), which corresponds precisely to the case when the argument of
\log_{+}
is less than 1 in (
2.8
).

For the regions (i), (ii), and (iii), we may bound the integrals by

\begin{split}Cl\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}},\quad C\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}\log_{+}\left(\frac{L}{l}\right),\quad CL^{-2}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}},\end{split}

respectively, where the bound for (iii) is a direct consequence of Lemma
2.1
. Plugging the definitions of
L
and
l
into above and taking the sum finishes the proof.
∎

2.2.3.
Kinetic energy

For
\omega
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, its kinetic energy
E=E[\omega]
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
is defined by

(2.10)

\begin{split}E[\omega]=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\psi(x)\omega(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|u(x)|^{2}\,dx.\end{split}

Alternatively, the energy can be expressed in terms of the double integral

\begin{split}E[\omega]=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega(x){\bf G}(x,y)\omega(y)dxdy\end{split}

where
{\bf G}
was defined in (
2.1
). We also define the
interaction energy
between two vorticities as follows:

(2.11)

\begin{split}E_{inter}[\omega,\widetilde{\omega}]:=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega(x){\bf G}(x,y)\widetilde{\omega}(y)dxdy=E_{inter}[\widetilde{\omega},{\omega}].\end{split}

Since
{\bf G}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we have

E[\omega]\geq 0
for any
\omega\geq 0
, as long as it is finite. Furthermore, when
\omega,\widetilde{\omega}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we have
E_{inter}[\omega,\widetilde{\omega}]\geq 0
.

Below we prove various bounds on the interaction energy. These inequalities are inspired by
[
1
, Proposition 2.1]
, but we do not require the vorticity to be bounded in
L^{1}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\omega,\widetilde{\omega}\in L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and
\psi,\widetilde{\psi}
be their corresponding stream functions. We have

(2.12)

\begin{split}\left|E_{inter}[\omega,\widetilde{\omega}]\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\|x_{2}^{-1}\widetilde{\psi}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega))}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})},\|x_{2}^{-1}{\psi}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathrm{supp}}\,(\widetilde{\omega}))}\|\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\right\},\end{split}

(2.13)

\begin{split}\left|E_{inter}[\omega,\widetilde{\omega}]\right|\leq C(\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\|\omega\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\|\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\|\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})})^{1/2},\end{split}

and

(2.14)

\begin{split}|E[\omega]-E[\widetilde{\omega}]|\leq C(\|\omega-\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\|\omega-\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\|\omega+\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\|\omega+\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})})^{1/2}.\end{split}

If there is a positive distance
D
between
{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega)
and
{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\widetilde{\omega})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, then we have

(2.15)

\begin{split}E_{inter}[\omega,\widetilde{\omega}]\leq\frac{C}{D^{2}}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}}\|\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{1}_{*}}.\end{split}

Proof.

To begin with, to obtain (
2.12
), we just note that

\begin{split}E_{inter}[\omega,\widetilde{\omega}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega)}(-\widetilde{\psi})\omega\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega)}(-x_{2}^{-1}\widetilde{\psi})\,(x_{2}\omega)\,dx.\end{split}

Let us now prove (
2.13
): cut the integral into regions
\{|x-y|\geq L\}
and
\{|x-y|<L\}
for some
L>0
to be determined. In the former, use the linear bound on the log to get

\begin{split}\iint_{\{|x-y|\geq L\}}\omega(x){\bf G}(x,y)\widetilde{\omega}(y)dxdy\leq\iint_{\{|x-y|\geq L\}}\omega(x)\frac{Cx_{2}y_{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\widetilde{\omega}(y)dxdy\leq CL^{-2}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}}\|\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{1}_{*}}.\end{split}

To bound the latter, we first observe
\|x_{2}^{1/3}\omega\|_{L^{3/2}}\leq\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}}^{1/3}\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}^{2/3}.
Then, we apply (
2.2
) with
\alpha=\frac{1}{3}
to bound

\begin{split}{\bf G}(x,y)\mathbf{1}_{\{|x-y|<L\}}\leq\frac{C(x_{2}y_{2})^{1/3}}{|x-y|^{2/3}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|x-y|<L\}}=:(x_{2}y_{2})^{1/3}\tilde{{\bf G}}_{L}(x-y)\end{split}

and use Young’s convolution inequality to obtain

\begin{split}\left|\iint x_{2}^{1/3}\omega(x)\widetilde{{\bf G}}_{L}(x-y)y_{2}^{1/3}\widetilde{\omega}(y)dxdy\right|\leq\|\widetilde{{\bf G}}_{L}\|_{L^{3/2}}\|x_{2}^{1/3}\omega(x)\|_{L^{3/2}}\|y_{2}^{1/3}\widetilde{\omega}(y)\|_{L^{3/2}}\end{split}

with
\|\widetilde{{\bf G}}_{L}\|_{L^{3/2}}=CL^{2/3}
to derive the bound

\begin{split}\left|\iint_{\{|x-y|<L\}}\omega(x){\bf G}(x,y)\widetilde{\omega}(y)dxdy\right|\leq CL^{2/3}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}}^{1/3}\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}^{2/3}\|\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{1}_{*}}^{1/3}\|\widetilde{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2/3}.\end{split}

Optimizing in
L
gives the claimed bound. The proof of (
2.14
) directly follows from (
2.13
), by noting that
E[\omega]-E[\widetilde{\omega}]=E_{inter}[\omega-\widetilde{\omega},\omega+\widetilde{\omega}]
.

For the last statement, one may just bound

\begin{split}\log\left(1+\frac{4x_{2}y_{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{Cx_{2}y_{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{D^{2}}\end{split}

for
x\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega)
and
y\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\widetilde{\omega})
.
∎

Simply taking
\omega=\widetilde{\omega}
gives the following energy inequality.

Corollary 2.5

(Energy inequality)
.

Let
\omega\in L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. Then, its kinetic energy is bounded and satisfies

(2.16)

\begin{split}E[\omega]\leq C\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\|\omega\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\end{split}

with a universal constant
C>0
.

Remark 2.6
.

A similar computation gives a kinetic energy bound for
L^{p}\cap L^{1}_{*}
vorticity for
p>1
:

\begin{split}E[\omega]\leq C(p)\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}^{\theta}\|\omega\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}^{2-\theta},\qquad\theta=\frac{4(p-1)}{3p-2}.\end{split}

This inequality should be useful in extending Theorem
A
to the case of energy maximizers with the
L^{p}\cap L^{1}_{*}
constraint.

2.3.
Lamb dipoles

The Lamb dipole was introduced by
H. Lamb
[
50
, p. 231]
and independently by S. A. Chaplygin
[
17
]
. We have fixed the normalized Lamb dipole to be

\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(x)=\frac{-2c_{L}}{J_{0}(c_{L})}\,J_{1}(c_{L}r)\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}(r)\sin(\theta)

in the polar coordinate system
(r,\theta)
. The function
\frac{-2c_{L}}{J_{0}(c_{L})}\,J_{1}(c_{L}r)
is strictly positive for
r<1
, and in particular
\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. Furthermore, it vanishes when
r=1
, and therefore
\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}
is supported in
|x|\leq 1
and Lipschitz continuous.

The stream function
\bar{\psi}_{Lamb}
of the Lamb dipole can be explicitly computed, by writing it in the form
\Delta(\bar{\psi}_{Lamb})=\Delta(f(r)\sin(\theta))=\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}
and solving the ODE in
r
for
f(r)
. We have

(2.17)

\begin{split}\bar{\psi}_{Lamb}(x)=-r\sin(\theta)+\frac{2J_{1}(c_{L}r)}{c_{L}J_{0}(c_{L})}\sin(\theta),\qquad r\leq 1\end{split}

and we can observe the following
stream-vorticity relation

\begin{split}\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}=(c_{L})^{2}(-\bar{\psi}_{Lamb}-x_{2})_{+}\end{split}

where
(f)_{+}:=\max\{f,0\}
. This relation implies that
\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}
is a traveling wave solution to (
1.1
) with velocity
\mathbf{e}_{1}
:
\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(x-t\mathbf{e}_{1})
solves (
1.1
).

We can explicitly compute the enstrophy, impulse, and energy of
\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}
. To begin with, the enstrophy is given by

\begin{split}K[\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}^{2}(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{-2c_{L}}{J_{0}(c_{L})}\,J_{1}(c_{L}r)\right)^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)\,rdrd\theta=\pi c_{L}^{2}.\end{split}

The impulse is

\mu[\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{2}\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}r\sin(\theta)\,\frac{-2c_{L}}{J_{0}(c_{L})}\,J_{1}(c_{L}r)\sin(\theta)\,rdrd\theta=\pi.

Lastly, the energy can be computed using the stream-vorticity relation (
2.17
):

\begin{split}E[\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}]&=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\bar{\psi}_{Lamb}(x)\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(x)dx\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(-\bar{\psi}_{Lamb}(x)-x_{2})\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(x)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}x_{2}\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(x)dx=\pi.\end{split}

Combining the main theorem of Abe–Choi
[
1
, Theorems 1.3 and 1.5]
with Corollary
2.5
gives the following
sharp energy inequality
.

Theorem 2.7
.

For any
\kappa,\mu>0
, the rescaled Lamb dipole
\omega_{Lamb}^{\kappa,\mu}
defined in (
1.4
) is the unique maximizer, up to shifts in the
x_{1}
-coordinate, of the energy in the class
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\kappa,\mu}
defined in (
1.10
). Furthermore, we have the following sharp energy inequality for
\omega\geq 0
belonging to
L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}
:

(2.18)

\begin{split}E[\omega]\leq C_{L}\|\omega\|_{L^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\|\omega\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})},\qquad C_{L}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}c_{L}}.\end{split}

and the equality holds if and only if
\omega
is an
x_{1}
-translate of
\omega_{Lamb}^{\kappa,\mu}
for some
\kappa,\mu
.

Proof.

The first statement follows from Abe–Choi
[
1
, Theorems 1.3 and 1.5]
; see also
[
22
]
. Therefore, it suffices to prove (
2.18
), but this is an immediate consequence of this variational principle and Corollary
2.5
, simply because the constant
C>0
in the corollary should be achieved by plugging in a Lamb dipole into the inequality.
∎

The initial data

\overline{\omega}^{\boldsymbol{\bar{\kappa}},\boldsymbol{\bar{\mu}}}_{Lamb,\mathbf{p}(0)}

In what follows, we let the constant

3.3.
Bootstrap assumptions

For

\begin{split}K_{\leq i}(t)\leq K_{\leq i}(0)+\delta,\qquad 1\leq i\leq N-1,\end{split}

4.1.
Approximating by Lamb dipoles

In this section, under the bootstrap assumptions (

Lemma 4.1
.

If the bootstrap assumptions (
B1
)–(
B3
) hold on
[0,T]
, then for all
t\in[0,T]
we have

(4.1)

\begin{split}|K_{i}(t)-\bar{K}_{i}|+|\mu_{i}(t)-\bar{\mu}_{i}|+|E_{i}(t)-C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\bar{\mu}_{i}|\leq C\delta,\end{split}

(4.2)

\begin{split}\|\omega_{i}(t)-\bar{\omega}_{i,Lamb}(t)\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{N},\qquad\text{where}\quad\bar{\omega}_{i,Lamb}(t,x):=\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{i},\bar{\mu}_{i}}(\cdot-p_{i}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})\end{split}

for some
p_{i}(t)
satisfying

(4.3)

\begin{split}|p_{i}(t)-\bar{p}_{i}-\bar{V}_{i}\,t|\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2}(1+t)\end{split}

for all
1\leq i\leq N
.

Proof.

In the proof, we first prove (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) for
i=1,\dots,N
using an inductive argument, starting with the

.

Shift estimate
.
At this point, by (
4.4
), we know that for all
t\in[0,T]
,
\omega_{i}(t,\cdot)
is
{\varepsilon_{1}}
-close to the Lamb dipole
\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{i},\bar{\mu}_{i}}
after some shift
\tau_{i}(t)
, for each

. Under this condition, we want to define

that satisfies (

) and (

Step 1. Estimation on the oscillation of
\tau_{1}
.
Unfortunately,
\tau_{1}(t)
defined by the property (
4.4
) may not be unique, and is not necessarily continuous in
t
. Still, we will show that it cannot jump too much within any time interval of length
T_{1}:=\min\{T,\frac{R_{1}}{10N\bar{V}_{1}}\}
, where
R_{1}:=R(\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1})
is the support radius of the corresponding Lamb dipole defined in (
1.4
). Namely, for all
0\leq t<t^{\prime}\leq T
, we will show

(4.8)

\begin{split}|\tau_{1}(t)-\tau_{1}(t^{\prime})|\leq R_{1}\qquad\text{ if }0<t^{\prime}-t<T_{1}.\end{split}

To see this, we first note the global-in-time pointwise bound for the velocity
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 5N\bar{V}_{1}
, which holds even when one “stacks up” all the Lamb dipoles at a single point. (The maximum velocity of a Lamb dipole with traveling speed
\bar{V}_{1}
is bounded by
4\bar{V}_{1}
.) For any
t^{\prime}\in[t,t+T_{1}]
, assume towards a contradiction that
\tau_{1}(t^{\prime})>\tau_{1}(t)+R_{1}
. Due to the global velocity bound, the fluid particles starting from
x_{1}\leq\tau_{1}(t)
at time
t
(which contains almost half of the
L^{2}
norm from
\omega_{1}(t,\cdot)
) lies to the left of
x_{1}=\tau_{1}(t)+5N\bar{V}_{1}(t^{\prime}-t)\leq\tau_{1}(t)+R_{1}/2
at time
t^{\prime}
. Therefore, if
\tau_{1}(t^{\prime})>\tau_{1}(t)+R_{1}
, the shifted Lamb dipole
\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(\cdot-\tau_{1}(t^{\prime})\mathbf{e}_{1})
is clearly going to miss all of them, which contradicts the defining property of
\tau(t^{\prime})
in (
4.4
). We can use a similar argument to rule out the possibility of
\tau_{1}(t^{\prime})<\tau_{1}(t)-R_{1}
.

Step 2. Definition of the displacement
p_{1}(t)
.
In the following, we will define
p_{1}(t)
in an implicit way, by slightly
adjusting the argument in
[
47
, §3.2]
.
Let
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be a smooth test function that is odd, monotone increasing on
[-4R_{1},4R_{1}]
, and satisfies

g(x_{1})=\begin{cases}x_{1}&\text{ for }|x_{1}|\leq 3R_{1}\\
4R_{1}&\text{ for }4R_{1}\leq x_{1}\leq D_{0}/5\\
0&\text{ for }x_{1}\geq D_{0}/4\end{cases}.

We then introduce the function
H(t,p)
for
t\geq 0
and
p\in\mathbb{R}
as

(4.9)

\begin{split}H(t,p):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega(t,x+p\,{\bf e}_{1})g(x_{1})dx,\end{split}

which is differentiable in
t
and
p
since
g
is smooth.

Using such function
H
, we will define
p_{1}(t)
and show its properties through an induction argument, each time advancing by time
T_{1}
until we hit time
T
. Namely, assuming at some
t_{0}\in[0,T]
we have

(4.10)

\tau_{1}(t_{0})-L_{1}(t_{0})>\frac{D_{0}}{3},

we will show the following holds for all
t\in[t_{0},\min\{t_{0}+T_{1},T\}]
:

(a) There exists a unique
p_{1}(t)\in(\tau_{1}(t)-2R_{1},\tau_{1}(t)+2R_{1})
that satisfies

(4.11)

\begin{split}H(t,p_{1}(t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega(t,x+p_{1}(t){\bf e}_{1})g(x_{1})dx=0.\end{split}

Furthermore, such
p_{1}(t)
satisfies

(4.12)

|p_{1}(t)-\tau_{1}(t)|\leq C\varepsilon_{1},

and

(4.13)

\|\omega_{1}(t)-\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(\cdot-p_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\leq C\varepsilon_{1}.

(b) The function
p_{1}(t)
defined above is differentiable in
t
for
t\in[t_{0},\min\{t_{0}+T_{1},T\}]
, and satisfies

(4.14)

\begin{split}\Big|\frac{d}{dt}p_{1}(t)-\bar{V}_{1}\Big|\leq C\varepsilon_{1}^{1/2}.\end{split}

Note that (
4.10
) holds at
t_{0}=0
, since
|\tau_{1}(0)-\bar{p}_{1}|<R_{1}
(where
\bar{p}_{1}
is from the assumption of Theorem
A
), and
\bar{p}_{1}-L_{1}(0)>\frac{D_{0}}{2}
by definition of
L_{1}(0)
. Assuming (
4.10
) holds at some
t_{0}\in[0,T]
, once we prove that (b) holds, it implies

\frac{d}{dt}(p_{1}(t)-L_{1}(t))\geq\frac{\bar{V}_{1}-\bar{V}_{2}}{2}-C\varepsilon_{1}^{1/2}\quad\text{ for all }t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+\min\{t_{0}+T_{1},T\}].

If
t_{0}+T_{1}<T
, we integrate the above in
[t_{0},t_{0}+T_{1}]
and combine it with (
4.12
) to obtain

\begin{split}\tau_{1}(t_{0}+T_{1})-L_{1}(t_{0}+T_{1})&\geq p_{1}(t_{0}+T_{1})-L_{1}(t_{0}+T_{1})-C\varepsilon_{1}\\
&\geq p_{1}(t_{0})-L_{1}(t_{0})+\frac{\bar{V}_{1}-\bar{V}_{2}}{2}T_{1}-C(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}^{1/2}T_{1})\\
&\geq\tau_{1}(t_{0})-L_{1}(t_{0})+\frac{\bar{V}_{1}-\bar{V}_{2}}{2}T_{1}-C(2\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}^{1/2}T_{1})\\
&\geq\tau_{1}(t_{0})-L_{1}(t_{0})\geq\frac{D_{0}}{3},\end{split}

thus (
4.10
) also holds at
t_{0}+T_{1}
. We can then do induction to conclude that (a) and (b) holds for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Step 3. Existence and uniqueness of
p_{1}(t)
.
Let us first justify the induction step (a). For any
t\in[t_{0},\min\{t_{0}+T_{1},T\}]
, combining (
4.10
) with (
4.8
), we know
\tau_{1}(t)-L_{1}(t)>D_{0}/4
.
Using the decomposition
\widetilde{\omega}_{i}(t):=\omega_{i}(t)-\omega_{i,Lamb}(t)
and (
4.4
), we have

\begin{split}H(t,\tau_{1}(t))&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega_{1}(t,x+\tau_{1}(t){\bf e}_{1})g(x_{1})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega_{\geq 2}(t,x+\tau_{1}(t){\bf e}_{1})g(x_{1})dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega_{1}(t,x+\tau_{1}(t){\bf e}_{1})g(x_{1})dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(x)g(x_{1})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\widetilde{\omega}_{1}(t,x+\tau_{1}(t){\bf e}_{1})g(x_{1})dx.\end{split}

Here the second equality follows from the fact that
\omega_{\geq 2}(t,\cdot+\tau_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})
are supported on
\{x_{1}<L_{1}(t)-\tau_{1}(t)\}
, whereas
g
is supported on
\{x_{1}>-D_{0}/4\}
; these supports are disjoint since
\tau_{1}(t)-L_{1}(t)>D_{0}/4
. For the two integrals on the right hand side, note that the first integral is zero by odd symmetry of
g
in
x_{1}
, and the second integral is bounded by
C\varepsilon_{1}
using
\|\widetilde{\omega}_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}
, with
C>0
depending on the size of the support of
\widetilde{\omega}_{1}
. The above discussion yields

(4.15)

\begin{split}\left|H(t,\tau_{1}(t))\right|\leq C\varepsilon_{1}.\end{split}

Now we compute, for points
(t,p)
satisfying
|p-\tau_{1}(t)|\leq 2R_{1}
,

\begin{split}(\partial_{p}H)(t,p)&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\omega_{1}(t,x+p\mathbf{e}_{1})g^{\prime}(x_{1})dx\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(x+(p-\tau_{1}(t))\mathbf{e}_{1})dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\widetilde{\omega}_{1}(t,x+p\mathbf{e}_{1})g^{\prime}(x_{1})dx\\
&=-\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(x)dx}_{=:m_{1}}-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\widetilde{\omega}_{1}(t,x+p\mathbf{e}_{1})g^{\prime}(x_{1})dx.\end{split}

Here the first equality is again due to fact that the contribution from
\omega_{\geq 2}
is zero, and the second equality follows from the fact that
g^{\prime}=1
on
|x_{1}|<3R_{1}
and
|p-\tau_{1}(t)|\leq 2R_{1}
. Note that the last integral involving
\widetilde{\omega}_{1}
can again be bounded by
C\varepsilon_{1}
, using
\|\widetilde{\omega}_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}
, with
C>0
depending on the size of the support of
\widetilde{\omega}_{1}
. Therefore we arrive at

(4.16)

|\partial_{p}H(t,p)+m_{1}|\leq C\varepsilon_{1}\quad\text{ for all }\quad|p-\tau_{1}(t)|\leq 2R_{1}.

In particular, by choosing
\varepsilon_{1}
sufficiently small, (
4.16
) implies

(4.17)

\partial_{p}H(t,p)\leq-\frac{1}{2}m_{1}\quad\text{ for all }\quad|p-\tau_{1}(t)|\leq 2R_{1}.

Combining (
4.15
) and (
4.17
), for any
t\in[t_{0},\min\{t_{0}+T_{1},T\}]
, there exists a unique
p_{1}(t)
in
(\tau_{1}(t)-2R_{1},\tau_{1}(t)+2R_{1})
satisfying (
4.11
), and it also satisfies (
4.12
).
In particular, at
t=0
, this gives
|p(0)-\bar{p}_{0}|\leq C\varepsilon
since
\tau_{1}(0)
can be chosen as
\bar{p}
.

Putting (
4.12
) together with the triangle inequality and Lipschitz continuity of
\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}
, we obtain (
4.13
):

\begin{split}\|\omega_{1}(t)-\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(\cdot-p_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}&\leq\|\omega_{1}(t)-\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(\cdot-\tau_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\\
&\qquad+\|\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(\cdot)-\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(\cdot-(\tau_{1}(t)-p_{1}(t))\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\\
&\leq C\varepsilon_{1}+C|\tau_{1}(t)-p_{1}(t)|\leq 2C\varepsilon_{1}.\end{split}

Step 4. Estimating derivative of
p_{1}(t)
.
To justify the induction step (b), we proceed similarly as in
[
47
, §3.2]
, which we briefly sketch below. (The only difference is that
[
47
, §3.2]
considers the equation in the moving frame with velocity
\bar{V}_{1}
, whereas we consider it in the original frame.)

Recall that
p_{1}(t)
is defined to satisfy
H(t,p_{1}(t))=0
. Since
H(t,p)
is differentiable in
t,p
, and
\partial_{p}H(t,p_{1}(t))
does not vanish by (
4.17
), we have
p_{1}(t)
is differentiable for
t\in[t_{0},\min\{T_{1},T\}]
, with its derivative given by

(4.18)

\frac{d}{dt}p_{1}(t)=-\frac{\partial_{t}H(t,p_{1}(t))}{\partial_{p}H(t,p_{1}(t))}.

Note that we already have the estimate (
4.16
) for the denominator. For the numerator, we have

\partial_{t}H(t,p_{1}(t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}u_{1}(t,x+p_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})\,\omega_{1}(t,x+p_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})\,g^{\prime}(x_{1})dx.

In (
4.13
), we have shown
\omega_{1}(t,x+p_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})
is close to
\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}
. In the above integral, if we approximate
\omega_{1}(t,x+p_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})
and
u_{1}(t,x+p_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})
by
\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}
and
(u_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}})_{1}
respectively, the integral would become

\begin{split}I_{approx}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}(u_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}})_{1}(x)\,\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(x)\,g^{\prime}(x_{1})dx=\bar{V}_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}(x)g^{\prime}(x_{1})dx=\bar{V}_{1}m_{1}.\end{split}

Here the first equality is due to the fact that
(u_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}-\bar{V}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1})\cdot\nabla\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}=0
(recall
\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}
is a traveling wave solution with velocity
\bar{V}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}
); testing this equation by
g(x_{1})
and applying the divergence theorem gives the first equality. The second inequality follows from the definition
m_{1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}dx
, and the fact that
g^{\prime}=1
in
|x_{1}|<3R_{1}
.

It remains to estimate the difference
\partial_{t}H(t,p_{1}(t))-I_{approx}
. By (
4.13
),
\|\omega_{1}(t,x+p_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})-\omega_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon_{1}
. Using the Biot–Savart law gives
\|u(t,x+p_{1}(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})-u_{Lamb}^{\bar{\kappa}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\varepsilon_{1}^{1/2}
. These estimates (together with the fact that
\omega_{1}
has compact support) gives

|\partial_{t}H(t,p_{1}(t))-\bar{V}_{1}m_{1}|=|\partial_{t}H(t,p_{1}(t))-I_{approx}|\leq C\varepsilon_{1}^{1/2}.

Plugging this and (
4.16
) into (
4.18
) gives (
4.14
).

Finally, given
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, we may take
\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon}{10CN}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Velocity on the borders

As a consequence of (
4.3
), we obtain smallness of the velocity everywhere between the two “borders”
L_{i}^{\pm}(t)
. In particular, since such estimate also holds for
x_{1}=L_{i}(t)
, the smallness of velocity (as compared to
\dot{L}_{i}
) implies that it is only possible for a fluid particle to cross a “border”
L_{i}(t)
from the right to the left, but never the other way.

Lemma 4.2
.

If the bootstrap assumptions (
B1
)–(
B3
) hold for
[0,T]
, then for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
1\leq i\leq N-1
, we have

(4.19)

\begin{split}\sup_{\{x_{1}\in[L_{i}^{-}(t),L_{i}^{+}(t)],x_{2}\geq 0\}}|u(t,x)|\leq\varepsilon^{1/2}\dot{L}_{i}.\end{split}

Proof.

From Lemma
4.1
, we have that

(4.20)

\begin{split}\Big\|\omega(t,\cdot)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bar{\omega}_{j,Lamb}(t,\cdot)\Big\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\leq\varepsilon.\end{split}

We decompose the velocity
u=:u_{Lamb}+u_{rem}
, where
u_{Lamb}
denotes the velocity generated by
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bar{\omega}_{j,Lamb}(t,\cdot)
; here
\bar{\omega}_{j,Lamb}(t,\cdot)
is defined by (
4.2
). When
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
satisfies
x_{1}\in[L_{i}^{-}(t),L_{i}^{+}(t)]
for some
i
, the support of
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bar{\omega}_{j,Lamb}(t,\cdot)
is separated from
x
by at least
D_{0}/10
by (
4.3
). Applying (
2.5
),

\begin{split}|u_{Lamb}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{D_{0}^{2}}.\end{split}

Next, applying (
2.9
) with
\alpha=1/2
and (
4.20
), we obtain

\begin{split}|u_{rem}(x)|\leq C(M^{1/3}+\varepsilon^{1/3})\varepsilon^{2/3}\leq C\varepsilon^{2/3}\end{split}

where we let
C
also depend on
M
. Taking
\varepsilon_{0}
smaller, if necessary, relative to all
\dot{L}_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq N-1
) and
M
and then taking
D_{0}
larger in a way depending on
\varepsilon_{0}
, we obtain that (
4.19
) holds for all
x_{1}\in[L_{i}^{-}(t),L_{i}^{+}(t)]
.
∎

4.3.
Bounds on the gain

Lemma 4.3
.

If the bootstrap assumptions (
B1
)–(
B3
) hold for
[0,T]
, then for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
2\leq i\leq N
, we have the following bounds for
\omega_{\geq i,gain}
:

(4.21)

\begin{split}\qquad\|\omega_{\geq i,gain}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\delta,\quad\quad\|\omega_{\geq i,gain}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\delta^{1/2}.\end{split}

Proof.

Recall that in (
4.2
), we have shown that fluid particles can only cross a “border”
L_{i}(t)
from right to left, but not the other way. Since
\omega
is conserved along the particle trajectory,
\omega_{\geq i,gain}(t,\cdot)
is purely contributed by the fluid particles who have crossed
L_{i}
from the right to the left before time
t
(i.e. the fluid particles who left
\sum_{j<i}\omega_{j}
by time
t
). Using this fact together with (
B1
),
we immediately have the following bound on
\|\omega_{\geq i,gain}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
:

\begin{split}\|\omega_{\geq i,gain}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{j<i}\left(K_{j}(0)-K_{j}(t)\right)\leq C\delta.\end{split}

The
L^{1}
bound then follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the fact that
\omega_{0}
has a compact support, whose measure is conserved in time:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\omega_{\geq i,gain}(t,x)dx\leq\|\omega_{\geq i,gain}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}|{\mathrm{supp}}\,\omega_{0}|^{1/2}\leq C\delta^{1/2}.\qed

4.4.
Bounds on the errors

We recall from (
3.8
)–(
3.9
) that we have defined

\begin{split}\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)}=\omega_{i}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{L_{i}(t)\leq x_{1}<L_{i}^{+}(t)\}},\,\,\omega_{i,cen}=\omega_{i}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{L_{i}^{+}(t)\leq x_{1}<L_{i-1}^{-}(t)\}},\,\,\omega_{i,err}^{(r)}=\omega_{i}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{L_{i-1}^{-}(t)\leq x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}\end{split}

for all
1\leq i\leq N
. We have
\omega^{(r)}_{1,err}=0=\omega^{(\ell)}_{N,err}
. We denote the corresponding stream functions by
\psi_{i,err}^{(\ell)},\psi_{i,cen}
, and
\psi_{i,err}^{(r)}
.

Lemma 4.4

(
\delta
-smallness of errors)
.

If the bootstrap assumptions (
B1
)–(
B3
) hold for
[0,T]
, then for all
t\in[0,T]
, we have

(4.22)

\begin{split}\|\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)}\|_{L^{1}_{*}}+\|\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\omega_{i,err}^{(r)}\|_{L^{1}_{*}}+\|\omega_{i,err}^{(r)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\delta.\end{split}

Proof.

Let us denote
\omega_{i,err}:=\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)}+\omega_{i,err}^{(r)}
, and denote by
\psi_{i,err}
its corresponding stream function. So (
4.22
) is equivalent with

\|\omega_{i,err}\|_{L^{1}_{*}}+\|\omega_{i,err}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\delta.

For simplicity, we set
\omega_{i,rem}(t):=\omega_{i}(t)-\bar{\omega}_{i,Lamb}(t)
, which satisfies (
4.2
). (Note that
\omega_{i,rem}
contains the left and right errors
\omega_{i,err}
, as well as the error in the central part.)
For any
x\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\omega_{i,err}
, we have
x_{1}\in(L_{i}(t),L_{i}^{+}(t))\cup(L_{i-1}^{-},L_{i+1}(t))
, therefore

\begin{split}|\nabla\psi_{i}(t,x)|\leq|\nabla\psi_{i,rem}(t,x)|+|\nabla\psi_{i,Lamb}(t,x)|<\frac{C_{L}}{10}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\quad\text{ for }x\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\omega_{i,err}\end{split}

using that
\|\omega_{i,rem}\|_{L^{2}}<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
by (
4.2
), and that
\bar{\omega}_{i,Lamb}(t)
is far from
L_{i}^{+}(t)
and
L_{i-1}^{-}(t)
by (
4.3
).

Similarly, using
\|\omega_{i,err}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\omega_{i,err}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, we also have

|\nabla\psi_{i,err}(t,x)|<\frac{C_{L}}{10}\bar{\kappa}_{i},

and subtracting the above two inequalities gives

|\nabla\psi_{i,cen}(t,x)|<\frac{C_{L}}{5}\bar{\kappa}_{i}
for
x\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\omega_{i,err}
. Since all these stream functions are zero on the
x_{1}
-axis, we have

(4.23)

\psi_{i,err}(t,x)<\frac{C_{L}}{10}\bar{\kappa}_{i}x_{2}\quad\text{ and }\psi_{i,cen}(t,x)<\frac{C_{L}}{5}\bar{\kappa}_{i}x_{2}\quad\text{ for }x\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\omega_{i,err}.

We now decompose and estimate
E_{i}(t)
as follows: writing
\mu_{i,cen}=\|\omega_{i,cen}\|_{L^{1}_{*}},\kappa_{i,cen}=\|\omega_{i,cen}\|_{L^{2}}
, we use the above estimates to obtain

(4.24)

\begin{split}E_{i}(t)&=-\frac{1}{2}\int\omega_{i,err}\left(\psi_{i,err}+2{\psi}_{i,cen}\right)dx-\frac{1}{2}\int{\omega}_{i,cen}{\psi}_{i,cen}dx\\
&\leq\frac{1}{4}C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\|\omega_{i,err}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{*}}+C_{L}{\mu}_{i,cen}(t){\kappa}_{i,cen}(t)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{4}C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\|\omega_{i,err}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{*}}+C_{L}(\bar{\mu}_{i}+C\delta-\|\omega_{i,err}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{*}})(\bar{\kappa}_{i}+C\delta)\\
&\leq C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\bar{\mu}_{i}-\frac{3}{4}C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\|\omega_{i,err}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{*}}+C\delta.\end{split}

Now combining this with (B3)
i
gives

\begin{split}C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\bar{\mu}_{i}-C\delta<E_{i}(0)-\gamma_{i}\delta<E_{i}(t)\leq C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\bar{\mu}_{i}-\frac{2}{3}C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\|\omega^{(\ell)}_{i,err}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{*}}+C\delta\end{split}

This gives
\|\omega_{i,err}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{*}}\leq C\delta
. Plugging this back into the second inequality of (
4.24
) and combining it with (B3)
i
again, we have

C_{L}\bar{\kappa}_{i}\bar{\mu}_{i}-C\delta<E_{i}(t)\leq C_{L}\bar{\mu}_{i}\kappa_{i,cen}(t)+C\delta.

This yields
\kappa_{i,cen}(t)\geq\bar{\kappa}_{i}-C\delta

\|\omega_{i,err}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\delta
.
∎

4.5.

Lemma 4.5
.

If the bootstrap assumptions (

\begin{split}0\leq E[\omega]-\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}E[\omega_{i}]\leq C\delta^{3/2}.\end{split}

Proof.

We take

and decompose

\begin{split}E[\omega]&=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}E_{inter}[\omega_{i},\omega_{j}]=\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}E[\omega_{i}]+2\sum_{1\leq i\leq N-1}E_{inter}[\omega_{i},\omega_{i+1}]+\sum_{i,j:|i-j|>1}E_{inter}[\omega_{i},\omega_{j}].\end{split}

Each term on the right-hand side is nonnegative. Then, we may bound using (

\begin{split}\sum_{i,j:|i-j|>1}E_{inter}[\omega_{i},\omega_{j}]\leq\frac{C}{D_{0}^{2}}\leq C\delta^{2}.\end{split}

Similarly, for

and

\begin{split}E_{inter}[\omega_{i},\omega_{i+1}]=E_{inter}[\omega_{i}-\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)},\omega_{i+1}]+E_{inter}[\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)},\omega_{i+1}-\omega_{i+1,err}^{(r)}]+E_{inter}[\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)},\omega_{i+1,err}^{(r)}]\end{split}

and bound again using (

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq i\leq N-1}\left(E_{inter}[\omega_{i}-\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)},\omega_{i+1}]+E_{inter}[\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)},\omega_{i+1}-\omega_{i+1,err}^{(r)}]\right)\leq\frac{100C}{D_{0}^{2}}\leq C\delta^{2}.\end{split}

Finally, we use (
2.13
) to bound

\begin{split}E_{inter}[\omega_{i,err}^{(\ell)},\omega_{i+1,err}^{(r)}]\leq C\delta^{3/2},\end{split}

where we have applied (

∎

5.1.
Enstrophy, impulse, and energy change

Given a time-dependent function
g(t,x)
that is transported by the velocity field
u
on
\mathbb{R}_{2}^{+}
, it is convenient to introduce the notation
\mathbf{F}_{i}[g](t)
to denote the flux of
g
across the moving line
\{x_{1}=L_{i}(t)\}
(with the leftward direction defined as positive flux):

\begin{split}\mathbf{F}_{i}[g](t):=\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i}(t),x_{2}\geq 0\}}\left(\dot{L}_{i}-u_{1}(t,x)\right)g(t,x)dx_{2}.\end{split}

This expression is interpreted as
0
for
i=0
and
N
;
L_{0}=+\infty
and
L_{N}=-\infty
were introduced just for convenience.

Proposition 5.1
.

We have the following formulae for the enstrophy, impulse, and energy change:

(5.1)

\begin{split}\dot{K}_{i}(t)&=\mathbf{F}_{i-1}[\omega^{2}](t)-\mathbf{F}_{i}[\omega^{2}](t),\end{split}

(5.2)

\begin{split}\dot{K}_{\leq j}(t)&=-\mathbf{F}_{j}[\omega^{2}](t),\end{split}

(5.3)

\begin{split}\dot{\mu}_{i}(t)&=\sum_{j\neq i}\int u_{j,2}(t,x)\omega_{i}(t,x)dx+\mathbf{F}_{i-1}[x_{2}\omega](t)-\mathbf{F}_{i}[x_{2}\omega](t),\end{split}

(5.4)

\begin{split}\dot{\mu}_{\leq j}(t)&=\int(u_{\geq j+1})_{2}(t,x)\,\omega_{\leq j}(t,x)dx-\mathbf{F}_{j}[x_{2}\omega](t),\end{split}

and

(5.5)

\begin{split}\dot{E}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j\neq i}\int-\nabla\psi_{i}(t,x)\cdot(u_{j}\omega_{i})(t,x)\,dx+\mathbf{F}_{i-1}[-\psi_{i}\omega](t)-\mathbf{F}_{i}[-\psi_{i}\omega](t).\end{split}

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of this proposition.

5.1.1.
Enstrophy

We compute

\begin{split}\dot{K}_{i}(t)&=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}\omega^{2}(t,x)dx\\
&=\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i-1}(t)\}}\left(\dot{L}_{i-1}-u_{1}(t,x)\right)\omega^{2}(t,x)dx_{2}-\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i}(t)\}}\left(\dot{L}_{i}-u_{1}(t,x)\right)\omega^{2}(t,x)dx_{2}.\end{split}

This gives (
5.1
). Summation in
i
gives (
5.2
).

5.1.2.
Impulse

We compute

\begin{split}\dot{\mu}_{i}(t)&=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}x_{2}\omega(t,x)dx\\
&=\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}u_{2}(t,x)\omega(t,x)dx\\
&\qquad+\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i-1}(t)\}}\left(\dot{L}_{i-1}-u_{1}(t,x)\right)x_{2}\omega(t,x)dx_{2}-\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i}(t)\}}\left(\dot{L}_{i}-u_{1}(t,x)\right)x_{2}\omega(t,x)dx_{2}\\
&=\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}u_{j,2}(t,x)\omega_{i}(t,x)dx\\
&\qquad+\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i-1}(t)\}}\left(\dot{L}_{i-1}-u_{1}(t,x)\right)x_{2}\omega(t,x)dx_{2}-\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i}(t)\}}\left(\dot{L}_{i}-u_{1}(t,x)\right)x_{2}\omega(t,x)dx_{2}\end{split}

where we used that

\begin{split}\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}u_{i,2}(t,x)\omega_{i}(t,x)dx=0\end{split}

by anti-symmetry of the Biot–Savart kernel. Therefore, we obtain (
5.3
). Summation in
i
gives (
5.4
), with the observation

\begin{split}\int u_{j,2}(t,x)\omega_{i}(t,x)dx=\int(\partial_{x_{1}}\Delta^{-1}\omega_{j})(t,x)\omega_{i}(t,x)=-\int u_{i,2}(t,x)\omega_{j}(t,x)dx.\end{split}

5.1.3.
Energy

Recall that the energy
E_{i}(t)
is given by

E_{i}(t)=E[\omega_{i}(t)]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<y_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}\omega(t,x)\omega(t,y)\mathbf{G}(x,y)dxdy.

When taking its time derivative, due to the symmetry between
x
and
y
, we can apply the derivative only to
\omega(t,x)
and the integral limits of
x
, and remove the coefficient
1/2
. This yields the following, where we also use the definition
\psi_{i}(t,x):=-\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<y_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}\omega(t,y)\mathbf{G}(x,y)dy
:

(5.6)

\begin{split}\dot{E}_{i}(t)&=\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}-\psi_{i}(t,x)(\partial_{t}\omega)(t,x)dx+\\
&\qquad+\dot{L}_{i}(t)\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i}(t)\}}\psi_{i}(t,x)\omega(t,x)dx_{2}-\dot{L}_{i-1}(t)\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i-1}(t)\}}\psi_{i}(t,x)\omega(t,x)dx_{2}.\\
\end{split}

For the first integral on the right-hand side, we have

\begin{split}&\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}-\psi_{i}(t,x)(\partial_{t}\omega)(t,x)dx=\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}\psi_{i}(t,x)\nabla\cdot((u\omega)(t,x))\,dx\\
&\quad=\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}-\nabla\psi_{i}(t,x)\cdot(u\omega_{i})(t,x)\,dx\\
&\qquad-\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i}(t)\}}\psi_{i}(t,x)(u_{1}\omega)(t,x)dx+\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i-1}(t)\}}\psi_{i}(t,x)(u_{1}\omega)(t,x)dx\end{split}

and we further note that,

\begin{split}\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}-\nabla\psi_{i}(t,x)\cdot(u\omega_{i})(t,x)\,dx=\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}-\nabla\psi_{i}(t,x)\cdot(u_{j}\omega_{i})(t,x)\,dx\end{split}

holds, using a similar cancellation to the impulse case.

Plugging the above into (
5.6
) yields

\begin{split}&\dot{E}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\{L_{i}(t)<x_{1}<L_{i-1}(t)\}}-\nabla\psi_{i}(t,x)\cdot(u_{j}\omega_{i})(t,x)\,dx\\
&\qquad+\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i-1}(t)\}}(\dot{L}_{i-1}(t)-u(t,x))(-\psi_{i}\omega)(t,x)dx_{2}-\int_{\{x_{1}=L_{i}(t)\}}(\dot{L}_{i}(t)-u(t,x))(-\psi_{i}\omega)(t,x)dx_{2}.\end{split}

This gives (
5.5
).

5.3.
Closing the assumption on impulse

The following proposition is at the heart of the proof, where we aim to control the impulse change for all times. The proof starts with an explicit expression of the time derivative of impulse, where we decompose the vorticity left to border into the “gained” part and the original part, and decompose the vorticity right to border into the “error” that is close to border, and the centered part that is further away.

Out of these combinations, the most difficult case is the interaction between the “gained” part on the left and the “error” part on the right, since their support are not separated apart – both parts can be arbitrarily close to the border. Note that the smallness of these two terms themselves is not sufficient: we need to show the
time integral
(in an arbitrarily long time interval) contributed by the interaction is small. This key estimate is done in Proposition
5.4
, where we switch to a Lagrangian viewpoint to control the time integral of the contribution from a “gained” particle.

.

To begin with, we write
F:=(u_{1,err}^{(\ell)})_{2}
and make an area preserving change of variables
x\mapsto\Phi(t,x)
to rewrite the integral as

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{x_{1}\geq L_{1}(0)\}\cap\{\Phi_{1}(t,x)<L_{1}(t)\}}\omega_{0}(x)\,F(t,\Phi(t,x))\,dx\,dt,\end{split}

where we use the definition of
{\omega}_{\geq 2,gain}(t,\cdot)
: recall that every fluid particle in its support originates on the right of
L_{1}(0)
at time
0
, and has crossed to the left of
L_{1}(t)
by time
t
. Using Fubini’s theorem, the integral equals

\begin{split}\int_{\{x_{1}\geq L_{1}(0)\}}\omega_{0}(x)\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau(x)\leq T\}}(x)\left[\int_{\min\{\tau(x),T\}}^{T}F(t,\Phi(t,x))\,dt\right]dx,\quad\tau(x):=\inf\{t\geq 0:\Phi_{1}(t,x)<L_{1}(t)\}.\end{split}

We set
\tau(x)=+\infty
if
\Phi_{1}(t,x)\geq L_{1}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
.

We have a uniform in time bound for
F
, applying (
2.9
) with
\alpha=3/4
together with (
4.22
):

(5.13)

\begin{split}\|F(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\delta^{3/8}.\end{split}

Furthermore, we observe that once a “fluid particle” starting initially at
x
crosses the line
\{x_{1}=L_{1}(t)\}
from the right to left at time
t=\tau(x)
(i.e.
\Phi_{1}(\tau(x),x)=L_{1}(\tau(x))
, by Lemma
4.2
, its distance to the borderline
L_{1}(t)
increases linearly:
we have that

\begin{split}|L_{1}(t)-\Phi_{1}(t,x)|=L_{1}(t)-\Phi_{1}(t,x)\geq\frac{1}{10}|\bar{V}_{1}-\bar{V}_{2}|(t-\tau(x))\qquad\text{ for all }t\geq\tau(x).\end{split}

Hence, for
T>t>\tau(x)
, we have a pointwise estimate from (
2.5
):

(5.14)

\begin{split}F(t,\Phi(t,x))\leq C\|\omega_{1,err}^{(\ell)}\|_{L^{1}_{*}}|t-\tau(x)|^{-2}\leq C\delta|t-\tau(x)|^{-2},\qquad t\in(\tau(x),T).\end{split}

Equipped with two bounds (
5.13
) and (
5.14
), we can now integrate for each
x
:

\begin{split}\left|\int_{\min\{\tau(x),T\}}^{T}F(t,\Phi(t,x))\,dt\right|\leq C\int_{\min\{\tau(x),T\}}^{T}\min\left\{\delta^{3/8},\delta|t-\tau(x)|^{-2}\right\}dt\leq C\delta^{11/16}.\end{split}

Finally, we recall the
L^{1}
bound (
4.21
) from Lemma
4.3
:

\begin{split}\int_{\{x\geq L_{1}(0)\}}\omega_{0}(x)\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau(x)<T\}}(x)dx=\|\omega_{\geq 2,gain}(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\delta^{1/2}.\end{split}

Therefore,

\begin{split}\int_{\{x\geq L_{1}(0)\}}\omega_{0}(x)\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau(x)<T\}}(x)\,\left[\int_{\min\{\tau(x),T\}}^{T}F(t,\Phi(t,x))\,dt\right]\,dx\leq C\delta^{5/8+1/2}\leq\delta^{9/8},\end{split}

which finishes the proof.
∎

5.4.
Closing the assumption on energy

Proposition 5.5
.

For all
1\leq i\leq N
, we have

(5.15)

\begin{split}\inf_{t\in[0,T]}E_{i}(t)\geq E_{i}(0)-\frac{\delta}{100}.\end{split}

Proof.

We recall the formula (
5.5
):

\begin{split}\dot{E}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j\neq i}\int-\nabla\psi_{i}(t,x)\cdot(u_{j}\omega_{i})(t,x)\,dx+\mathbf{F}_{i-1}[-\psi_{i}\omega](t)-\mathbf{F}_{i}[-\psi_{i}\omega](t).\end{split}

We first take
1\leq i\leq N-1
, and let us prove a stronger statement, namely

(5.16)

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|E_{i}(t)-E_{i}(0)\right|\leq\frac{\delta}{1000N},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N-1.\end{split}

Note that from (
4.1
) and (
5.8
), we actually have for all
1\leq i\leq N-1
that

\begin{split}0\leq\int_{0}^{T}{\bf F}_{i}[x_{2}\omega](t)dt\leq C\delta.\end{split}

Now the key observation is that

\begin{split}\left|\mathbf{F}_{i}[-\psi_{i}\omega](t)\right|\leq\sup_{x_{1}=L_{i}(t)}\left|\frac{\psi_{i}(t,x)}{x_{2}}\right|{\bf F}_{i}[x_{2}\omega](t)\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2}{\bf F}_{i}[x_{2}\omega](t),\end{split}

where the smallness estimate on
\frac{\psi_{i}(t,x)}{x_{2}}
follows along the same lines as the proof of Lemma
4.2
. In particular, by taking
\varepsilon_{0}
smaller, we can guarantee that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\mathbf{F}_{i}[-\psi_{i}\omega](t)\right|dt\leq\frac{\delta}{10000N},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N-1.\end{split}

Therefore, it suffices to obtain a similar bound for

\begin{split}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int-\nabla\psi_{i}(t,x)\cdot(u_{j}\omega_{i})(t,x)\,dxdt\right|,\qquad i\neq j,1\leq i\leq N-1.\end{split}

Bounding this by
o(\delta)
is somewhat simpler than the proof of Proposition
5.3
, the factor
-\nabla\psi_{i}
giving an additional smallness compared to the corresponding integral for the impulse change. Therefore we omit the proof.

Finally, when
i=N
, we use (
5.16
), conservation of energy
E[\omega(t,\cdot)]=E[\omega_{0}]
, together with (
4.25
) and initial closeness (
3.6
) to obtain (
5.15
) for
i=N
, by taking
C_{0}
larger if necessary (
\delta_{0}=\delta/C_{0}
). This finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Since (
B1
)–(
B3
) hold on
[0,T]
with
\delta
replaced by
\delta/100
, by continuity in time, this concludes that (
B1
)–(
B3
) hold with
\delta/100
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. In particular, Lemma
4.1
is applicable for all nonnegative times, which establishes Theorem

6.
Separating out a Lamb dipole

In this section, we sketch the proof of Theorem
B
, which is largely parallel to the one of Theorem
A
. For convenience, let us recall the notation and assumptions from the statement. The initial data
\omega_{0}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
is decomposed into
\omega_{0}=\omega_{0l}+\omega_{0r}
:

•

\omega_{0r}
satisfies
\|\omega_{0r}-\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(\cdot-D\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}\leq\delta_{0}
and
\|\omega_{0r}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\leq M
,

•

\omega_{0l}
satisfies
{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega_{0l})\subset\{x_{1}<x_{2}\cot\alpha\}
and
V_{max}[\omega_{0l}]<\sin\alpha
.

We have defined

\begin{split}V_{max}[\omega_{0l}]=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{R}[\omega_{0l}]}\|\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}(g)\|_{L^{\infty}},\quad V_{avr}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{V_{max}[\omega_{0l}]}{\sin\alpha}\right)<1,\end{split}

where
\mathcal{R}[\omega_{0l}]
is the set of all possible rearrangements of
\omega_{0l}
(
1.9
).

6.1.
Decomposition and bootstrap assumptions

Let
\omega(t,\cdot)
be the solution corresponding to
\omega_{0}
, and let us decompose

(6.1)

\begin{split}\omega_{r}(t,\cdot):=\omega(t,\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}>L(t,x_{2})\}},\quad L(t,x_{2}):=x_{2}\cot\alpha+V_{avr}t,\quad\omega_{l}(t,\cdot):=\omega(t,\cdot)-\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)\end{split}

and set

\begin{split}K_{r}(t)=\kappa_{r}^{2}(t)=\|\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad\mu_{r}(t)=\|\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}_{*}},\quad E_{r}(t)=E[\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)].\end{split}

Similarly, we define
K_{l},\kappa_{l},\mu_{l}
, and
E_{l}
. We now introduce the bootstrap assumptions: for a sufficiently small
\delta>0
depending only on
\varepsilon,\alpha,M
, we take

•

(B1’)
\sup_{\{x_{1}=L(t,x_{2})\}}(u_{1}(t,x)-\cot\alpha\,u_{2}(t,x))\leq V_{avr}
.

•

(B2’)
\mu_{r}(t)\leq\mu_{r}(0)+\delta
.

•

(B3’)
E_{r}(t)\geq E_{r}(0)-\delta
.

Note that (B2’) is exactly identical to (
B2
) for the
N=2
case. (B3’) is identical to (
B3
) (for
i=1
only). Here we do not need to control
E_{l}(t)
, since the bootstrap assumption (
B3
) for
i=N
is only needed to show that
\omega_{N}
stays close to a Lamb dipole; whereas in Theorem
B
we do not need to prove anything on
\omega_{l}
.

The condition (B1’) says that the fluid particles can cross
\{x_{1}=L(t,x_{2})\}
only from right to left, and compared to the case of Theorem
A
, this is the only difference in the set of bootstrap assumptions. We shall see that (B1’) implies the enstrophy bound
K_{r}(t)\leq K_{r}(0)
and that (B1’) is propagated in time by the assumption on
\omega_{0l}
involving the rearrangements. Given a sufficiently small
\delta>0
, we are going to choose
\delta_{0}=\delta/C_{0}
with some large
C_{0}=C_{0}(\varepsilon,\alpha,M)>0
, and prove that the bootstrap assumptions propagate for all
t\geq 0
.

6.2.
Consequences of the bootstrap assumptions

We assume that the bootstrap assumptions hold for a time interval
[0,T]
and obtain their consequences, during the same time interval.

6.2.1.
Upper bound on enstrophy

To begin with, we compute the enstrophy change:

\begin{split}\dot{K}_{r}(t)&=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\{x_{1}>L(t,x_{2})\}}\omega^{2}(t,x)dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\left(u_{1}-\cot\alpha u_{2}-V_{avr}\right)\omega^{2}\right)(t,L(t,x_{2}),x_{2})\,dx_{2}\end{split}

and this gives
K_{r}(t)\leq K_{r}(0)
from (B1’).

6.2.2.
Approximating by Lamb dipole

Using
K_{r}(t)\leq K_{r}(0)
with (B2’) and (B3’), we may apply Proposition
1.3
to the case of normalized Lamb dipole:

\begin{split}\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\|\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)-\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(\cdot-\tau\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{1}_{*}}<\varepsilon.\end{split}

As in §
4.1
, we can show that the shift
\tau(t)
can be chosen to satisfy

\begin{split}|\tau(t)-t-D|\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2}(1+t).\end{split}

For convenience, we take
\omega_{r,Lamb}(t,\cdot)=\bar{\omega}_{Lamb}(\cdot-\tau(t)\mathbf{e}_{1})
and define
\omega_{r,rem}(t,\cdot)=\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)-\omega_{r,Lamb}(t,\cdot)
. Furthermore, by combining the upper bounds on
K_{r}(t)
,
\mu_{r}(t)
with

(6.2)

\begin{split}C_{L}\mu_{r}(0)\kappa_{r}(0)-C\delta<E_{r}(t)\leq C_{L}\mu_{r}(t)\kappa_{r}(t),\end{split}

we deduce lower bounds

(6.3)

\begin{split}\mu_{r}(t)>\mu_{r}(0)-C\delta,\qquad K_{r}(t)>K_{r}(0)-C\delta.\end{split}

6.2.3.
Smallness of the gain and error

As in (
3.10
)–(
3.11
), we define
\omega_{l,gain}(t,\cdot)
to be the part of
\omega_{l}(t,\cdot)
which came from
\omega_{r}
during the time interval
[0,t]
:

(6.4)

\begin{split}\omega_{l,gain}(t,\cdot):=\omega_{l}(t,\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{\{\Phi^{-1}_{1}(t,x)\geq L(0,\Phi^{-1}_{2}(t,x))\}}(\cdot),\qquad\omega_{l,*}(t,\cdot):=\omega_{l}(t,\cdot)-\omega_{l,gain}(t,\cdot).\end{split}

Here,
\Phi^{-1}(t,x)=(\Phi^{-1}_{1}(t,x),\Phi^{-1}_{2}(t,x))
is the inverse of
\Phi
, where
\Phi
is the flow map associated with
u(t,x)
;
\Phi(t,\Phi^{-1}(t,x))=x=\Phi^{-1}(t,\Phi(t,x))
.

Lemma 6.1
.

We have

\begin{split}\|\omega_{l,gain}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\delta,\qquad\|\omega_{l,gain}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\delta^{1/2}.\end{split}

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to that of Lemma
4.3
, using (
6.3
).
∎

We then decompose
\omega_{r}
in a similar way as in the Eulerian decomposition in §
3.4.1
, into the “error term” which is relatively close to the borderline and the rest:

(6.5)

\begin{split}\omega_{r,err}(t,\cdot):=\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}\leq L^{+}(t,x_{2})\}},\quad L^{+}(t,x_{2}):=x_{2}\cot\alpha+\big(V_{avr}+\frac{1}{10}\big)t+\frac{D}{2}\end{split}

and we take
\omega_{r,cen}(t,\cdot):=\omega_{r}(t,\cdot)-\omega_{r,err}(t,\cdot)
. These two further decomposition of
\omega_{l}
and
\omega_{r}
are illustrated in Figure

.

We have

(6.6)

\begin{split}\|\omega_{r,err}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}_{*}}+\|\omega_{r,err}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\delta.\end{split}

6.3.
Closing the bootstrap assumptions

We now close the bootstrap assumptions: on
[0,T]
where they are valid, we will prove that (B1’) holds with the strict inequality, and (B2’) and (B3’) hold with
\delta/100
instead of
\delta
. This would complete the proof of Theorem
B
.

Closing (B1’)
.
We show that on
[0,T]
, we actually have the strict inequality in (B1’). We estimate the velocity at time
t
on
\{x_{1}=L(t,x_{2})\}
by the sum of velocities coming from the following decomposition:

\begin{split}\omega=\omega_{l,*}+\omega_{l,gain}+\omega_{r,cen}+\omega_{r,err}.\end{split}

We denote the corresponding velocities by
u=u_{l,*}+u_{l,gain}+u_{r,cen}+u_{r,err}.
To begin with, smallness of
u_{l,gain}
and
u_{r,err}
comes from Lemma
6.1
and
6.2
, respectively. Next, the smallness of
u_{r,cen}
in
\{x_{1}=L(t,x_{2})\}
follows from (
2.5
) in Lemma
2.1
, using the fact that the support of
\omega_{r,cen}
is separated from this line by
cD
with some
c>0
. Lastly, smallness of
u_{l,*}
comes from the observation that
\omega_{l,*}(t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{R}[\omega_{0l}]
and in particular
\|u_{l,*}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq V_{max}[\omega_{0l}]<\sin\alpha
. More precisely, for
v(x):=u_{l,*}(t,x)
, we have for every
x
the inequality

\begin{split}|v_{1}(x)-\cot\alpha v_{2}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{\sin\alpha}|v(x)|\leq\frac{1}{\sin\alpha}V_{max}[\omega_{0l}]<V_{avr}.\end{split}

Since the last inequality is strict, we may take
\varepsilon
smaller and
D
larger if necessary, to make sure that (B1’) holds again with the strict inequality.

Closing (B2’) and (B3’)
.
The proofs for replacing
\delta
by
\delta/100
in (B2’) and (B3’) are parallel to the case of Theorem
A
for the rightmost dipole. That is, the arguments to close (B2’) and (B3’) are almost identical to the proofs of case
i=1
in Proposition
5.3
and Proposition
5.5
.
