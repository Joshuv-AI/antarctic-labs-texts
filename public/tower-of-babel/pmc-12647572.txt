Changes in blood concentration of tranexamic acid in dialysis and non-dialysis patients undergoing cardiac surgery: a prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Takahiro Tamura, Tatsuro Yokoyama, Tsuyoshi Nakai, Yasuhiro Miyagawa, Kimitoshi Nishiwaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pharmacokinetics of tranexamic acid (TXA), used during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) surgery, in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis remain unclear. This prospective study compared serum tranexamic acid (TXA) concentrations following bolus administration and subsequent intensive care unit (ICU) admission in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), including those receiving maintenance hemodialysis and those not on dialysis. Participants received two TXA boluses: 1000 mg (500 mg for patients receiving hemodialysis) at procedure initiation and another 1000 mg (500 mg for patients receiving hemodialysis) after CPB withdrawal. Blood samples were collected at 30, 60, and 120 min, and after CPB weaning after the first dose; following the second dose; upon ICU admission; and at 4, 8, and 12 h post-ICU admission to measure TXA concentrations. A renal function-based simulation model was developed to optimize TXA dosing. Among 82 patients (74 without dialysis and 8 receiving hemodialysis), serum TXA concentrations were similar between groups until ICU admission. Following CPB withdrawal, patients receiving hemodialysis exhibited higher TXA concentrations than non-dialysis patients, despite receiving only half the total dose. TXA concentrations remained significantly higher in hemodialysis patients after ICU admission. Bolus administration effectively maintained antifibrinolytic TXA levels (> 10 µg/mL). Introduction

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is a widely used antifibrinolytic agent in cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), known to reduce postoperative bleeding and transfusion requirement1. Recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that even low-dose TXA (e.g., 20 mg/kg) is effective in minimizing blood loss and reducing the incidence of postoperative seizures2. Additionally, recent findings indicate complete inhibition of fibrinolysis at a TXA concentration of 10 µg/mL and with a 500 mg dose3. Although TXA is considered highly effective4, significant side effects have been reported, including dose-dependent seizures following cardiac surgery5. Therefore, to optimize its use, recent studies have begun examining the pharmacokinetic profile of TXA in different patient populations6,7.

A previous study estimated the population pharmacokinetics of TXA in patients weighing 70 kg and undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB8. However, this study did not account for renal function, which influences TXA excretion due to its kidney-based elimination pathway. As TXA excretion varies with renal function and there is currently no simulation model that considers renal function in relation to TXA blood levels, understanding the changes in TXA concentrations post-administration is essential for planning optimal dosing. Given the reported side effects and the lack of consideration for renal elimination, optimizing TXA dosing in patients undergoing cardiac surgery is crucial. Additionally, TXA is usually administered as a continuous infusion and reports describing bolus administration of TXA remain rare. In Japan, there are only a few facilities that use continuous infusion, and bolus infusion is generally used. The drug approval for TXA in Japan allows the use of up to 2.5 g per day. Therefore, in normal surgery, two bolus doses of 1000 mg are sufficient to maintain the blood concentration of TXA the next morning, rendering this as a simple method in clinical practice while avoiding the use of syringe pump required for continuous administration.

Therefore, we designed this study to address the gaps in literature and to evaluate TXA concentrations over time in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB under our hospital’s routine TXA bolus protocol, both intraoperatively and post-ICU admission, and including patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis (HD) and those not receiving HD to assess the impact of renal function on TXA concentrations in this clinical setting. The primary objective of this study was to establish a population pharmacokinetic model of TXA in this setting. The secondary objective was to determine whether a single bolus administration of TXA could maintain antifibrinolytic concentrations throughout the perioperative period, even in patients receiving HD.

Methods

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB #2021-0250, dated September 7, 2021). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocols were conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This manuscript has been prepared in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Patients and study design

This prospective observational study was conducted at Nagoya University Hospital, Japan. Patients aged 20–90 years scheduled for elective cardiac or aortic surgery with CPB between September 2021 and August 2022 were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included unforeseen circumstances preventing study continuation. Patients who underwent CPB and received TXA were included. Among them, patients with end-stage renal disease undergoing maintenance HD (HD group) received a reduced TXA dose of 500 mg at the start of surgery and again immediately after CPB withdrawal (total 1000 mg). Patients without maintenance dialysis dependence (non-HD group) received 1000 mg at both instances (total 2000 mg). After TXA administration, blood samples were collected at approximately 30, 60, and 120 min, and after weaning from CPB; at ICU admission; and at 4, 8, and 12 h after ICU admission. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to separate plasma, which was subsequently transferred to other tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Plasma phospholipids and proteins were removed by adding 300 µL of 1% formic acid in acetonitrile to 100 µL plasma and centrifuging at 3000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was extracted using a solid-phase extraction cartridge (GL Sciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan), diluted fifty-fold (5 mL) with 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, and prepared for analysis by adding 100 µL sample to 20 µL internal standard (IS).

Demographic and clinical data—including sex, age, body weight, height, and body mass—were obtained from patients’ electronic medical records (HOPE/EGMAIN-GX; Fujitsu, Tokyo, Japan) and anesthesia recording system databases (ORSYS and ACSYS; Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands). Preoperative serum creatinine levels, surgery type, and intraoperative bleeding volume were recorded. Body surface area and creatinine clearance (CLcr) were calculated using the DuBois formula9 and Cockcroft–Gault equation10, respectively. Renal function was classified according to the estimated glomerular filtration rate criteria for Japanese patients (Supplementary Table S1).

General anesthesia procedure

Standard monitoring was conducted for all patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery. General anesthesia was induced using intravenous fentanyl (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and midazolam (Sandoz, Basel, Switzerland), supplemented with remifentanil (Daiichi Sankyo, Tokyo, Japan) and rocuronium (Merck Sharp & Dohme K.K., Tokyo, Japan) to facilitate tracheal intubation, and maintained with air, oxygen, remifentanil, and inhaled sevoflurane (Maruishi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan). Porcine heparin (300 U/kg) (Mochida Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was administered before cannulation for CPB, with additional boluses (50 U/kg) administered as needed to maintain an activated coagulation time > 480 s. In our institution, HD patients do not receive intermittent or continuous renal replacement therapy using dedicated dialysis machines during CPB, but rather undergo intraoperative HD integrated into the CPB circuit. In particular, the Hemocrystal® membrane (MERA Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) is routinely incorporated into the CPB setup for all patients, including both HD and non-HD, to facilitate fluid and solute management during surgery. Protamine (3 mg/kg) (Mochida Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was administered to reverse heparin effects post-CPB. Red blood cells were transfused as needed to maintain hemoglobin levels > 7–8 mg/dL during CPB. Blood sampling and the FibCare device (Atom Medical, Tokyo, Japan) were used to monitor fibrinogen levels during CPB withdrawal11. Transfusion management was guided by thromboelastography algorithm following CPB withdrawl and in the ICU12,13. Patients were intubated upon admission to the ICU.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis

Plasma TXA concentrations were measured using ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC)-tandem mass spectrometry (Waters ACQUITY UPLC® system with ACQUITY TQD detector) following established protocols14,15. Chromatographic separation of TXA and IS was performed using an ACQUITY UPLC® BEH HILIC column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) maintained at 40 °C. Gradient elution was performed with eluents A and B—0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water and methanol, respectively—under the following conditions: 99.9% eluent A (linear gradient, v/v) at 0 min, 85% at 2 min, 30% at 4 min, and finally returning to 99.9% at 6 min, with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Each 20 µL injection had a total runtime of 6 min. Quantification was performed using multiple reaction monitoring with electrospray ionization in positive mode. The multiple reaction monitoring transitions for TXA were from m/z 157.9 to 122.8, 99.7, 68.8, 50.8, and 40.1, and for IS, from m/z 143.9 to 108.8, 81.0, 80.7, 40.8, and 40.4.

Statistical analyses

In this cohort study, we analyzed the population pharmacokinetics of TXA in patients undergoing CPB while considering renal function. We have recently developed a population compartment pharmacokinetic model16 using a NLME model, wherein we analyzed all plasma concentration-time data of TXA using Phoenix NLME software, version 8.4.0 (Sirtara, Princeton, NJ). A target sample size of 100 patients was set, with model fitting planned for 80 patients; the study was terminated once satisfactory model fitting was achieved, and no formal sample size analysis was performed.

Descriptive statistics and univariate comparisons

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed variables, data were presented as means ± standard deviations and compared using Student’s t-test (equal variances) or Welch’s t-test (unequal variances after Levene’s test). For non-normally distributed variables, data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests when expected cell frequencies were < 5.

Effect sizes and confidence intervals

Effect sizes were calculated for all comparisons. Cohen’s d was calculated for parametric tests, and rank-biserial correlation (r) was calculated for the Mann–Whitney U test. 95% confidence intervals were computed for all effect sizes using established methods.

Multiple comparison correction

Given the multiple time points analyzed (8 comparisons), the Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple testing. Adjusted p-values are reported alongside raw p-values, with statistical significance defined as adjusted p < 0.05.

Longitudinal data analysis

To account for within-subject correlation and time trends in the repeated measures data, mixed-effects models were fitted using the restricted maximum likelihood method. The base model included fixed effects for dialysis status, time, and their interaction, along with random intercepts for individual patients. Model selection was performed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Model diagnostics included assessment of residual normality (Shapiro–Wilk test), identification of outliers (standardized residuals > 3), and calculation of pseudo-R2.

Assumptions testing

Statistical assumptions were formally tested for all analyses. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, homoscedasticity using Levene’s test, and outliers were identified using the IQR method (values beyond Q1-1.5×IQR or Q3 + 1.5×IQR).

All analyses were conducted using Python, version 3.12 (https://www.python.org/)17, with the scipy, statsmodels, pandas, and numpy libraries. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for adjusted pvalues, with all tests being two-tailed.

Three patients were excluded due to receipt of a different TXA dose (one patient) or undergoing surgery without CPB (two patients). Additionally, three patients were excluded due to excessive missing data that prevented TXA level tracking over time. This resulted in a final cohort of 8 patients on HD and 74 non-HD patients. Patient characteristics

Baseline characteristics were comparable across groups, with no statistically significant differences after correction for multiple comparisons (Table 1). The HD group had a median age of 73.5 years (IQR: 65.0–78.0) compared to 69.5 years (IQR: 58.0–77.0) in the non-HD group (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.244, effect size r = 0.25, 95% CI −0.07–0.57). Body mass index was also comparable between groups; 21.6 kg/m2 (IQR: 19.6–24.7) in HD group versus 23.8 kg/m2 (IQR: 21.2–27.1) in non-HD group (p = 0.190, effect size r = −0.29, 95% CI −0.61–0.04). No significant differences were observed in surgical variables, including duration of.

anesthesia, surgery, or cardiopulmonary bypass.

Mixed-effects model analysis

To account for within-subject correlation and temporal trends, mixed-effects models were applied to the longitudinal data. The optimal model included fixed effects for dialysis status, time, and their interaction, along with random intercepts for patients (AIC = 2847.3).

The model revealed significant main effects for:

Dialysis status: F(1,74) = 12.4, p < 0.001.

Time: F(7,498) = 89.7, p < 0.001.

Dialysis status × Time interaction: F(7,498) = 8.2, p < 0.001.

This interaction indicates that the temporal pattern of TXA concentration differed significantly between HD and non-HD patients. Model diagnostics confirmed adequate fit (pseudo-R2 = 0.73), with normally distributed residuals (Shapiro–Wilk p = 0.082) and minimal outliers (2.1% of observations with standardized residuals > 3).

Discussion

This study presents two novel findings. First, an effective blood concentration of TXA can be sustained with intermittent bolus administration of 2000 mg. Second, we propose the development of a TXA blood concentration simulation model that accounts for renal function.

Previous studies have shown that TXA administration reduces blood transfusion rates similarly at doses less than 2 g and between 2 and 10 g4. Fiechtner et al.18 reported that, in 21 cardiac surgery patients on CPB, an initial TXA dose of 10 mg/kg administered intravenously over 20 min, followed by 1 mg/kg/h via a central line until 20 h post-ICU admission, maintained effective serum TXA concentrations above 10 µg/mL at all time points measured (5 min after the first bolus; 5, 30, and 60 min after CPB initiation; at discontinuation; and 1 h post-discontinuation). Based on these findings and those by Yoshii et al.3, lower TXA doses may be favorable for maintaining antifibrinolytic activity. Given the risk of convulsions at higher doses, clinicians might prefer to administer the minimum dose necessary to maintain effective TXA levels in circulation. Clinically, it is essential to determine if a low intraoperative TXA dose, such as 2 g, can sustain antifibrinolytic activity, as this may be sufficient to achieve the target concentration of 10 µg/mL in most patients. Additionally, the approved maximum TXA dose in Japan is 2,500 mg/day, making the question of whether 2 g can provide effective antifibrinolytic action particularly relevant. Our study shows that even a 2-g bolus can sustain effective TXA blood concentrations at or above 10 µg/mL in most patients.

Previous studies have explored whether continuous infusion offers any benefit over bolus administration. Imtiaz et al.19 found no significant differences in blood loss, ICU stay, morbidity, or mortality between bolus and continuous infusion groups following coronary artery bypass grafting. Conversely, another study on abdominal tumor surgery reported that TXA reduced postoperative blood loss more effectively when administered as a bolus followed by a 4-hour continuous infusion compared to a single bolus20. However, continuous infusion is rarely employed in Japan, where bolus administration is more common due to its simplicity and regulatory constraints (maximum dose 2.5 g/day). Given our findings that a 2 g bolus maintains therapeutic concentrations even in HD patients, a bolus-based approach may be both effective and practical in routine CPB practice.

At our hospital, dialysis membranes are used during CPB, resulting in similar TXA concentration trends regardless of renal function status. However, after CPB withdrawal, TXA retention patterns varied by renal function, with patients undergoing HD retaining higher TXA concentrations than those with normal renal function, even at half the dose. This observation highlights the role of renal excretion in TXA clearance. After CPB, TXA levels tended to be progressively higher at each time point as renal function declined. These findings emphasize the importance of considering renal function in TXA dosing, underscoring the need for a blood concentration simulation model that accounts for renal excretion to guide dosing adjustments.

Previously, no simulation model for TXA concentrations accounted for renal function. To address this, we developed a population compartment pharmacokinetic model16 using a nonlinear mixed-effects (NLME) model and analyzed all plasma concentration-time data of TXA using Phoenix NLME software, version 8.4.0 (Sirtara, Princeton, NJ). Population pharmacokinetic parameters and their variability were estimated using the first-order conditional estimation with the extended least squares method. The two-compartment model with combined errors was identified as the best fit. The final model accounted for the effects of body weight (BW) and CLcr, summarized as follows: V1 (L) = 12.77 × (BW/61.4)0.910, V2 (L) = 6.857, CL1 (L/h) = 3.263 × (CLcr [L/h] / 61.0)0.752, and CL2 (L/h) = 2.85916. One-compartment models were parameterized by clearance (CL1) and volume of distribution of the central compartment (V1). In the two-compartment models, additional parameters, such as intercompartment clearance (CL2) and volume of distribution of the peripheral compartment (V2), were used. Assuming a body weight of 61.4 kg (the median weight) and a CLcr of 60.0 mL/min, our model estimated CL1 as 3.223 L/h (53.72 mL/min) and V1 as 12.77 L. These values indicate that CL1 was notably lower and V1 was higher than those reported in the product information (CL1: 110–116 mL/min; V1: 9–12 L). Such pharmacokinetic alterations are frequently observed in CPB patients receiving hydrophilic drugs, such as TXA and cephalosporins, which have a smaller volume of distribution and are primarily eliminated via renal excretion. Several factors associated with CPB can influence drug pharmacokinetics, including drug sequestration by the CPB circuit, increased volume of distribution, changes in protein binding, tissue distribution, and hemodilution. Dowd et al.21 reported that CPB led to a decrease in CL1 and an increase in V1 for a 70-kg patient undergoing cardiac surgery, suggesting that CPB impacts the pharmacokinetics of TXA. These findings suggest that dose adjustments tailored to CPB conditions are necessary to achieve therapeutic TXA levels in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB.

In another study by Jerath et al.22, the pharmacokinetics of TXA were explored in patients with chronic renal dysfunction (CRD) undergoing cardiac surgery. This study provides crucial insight into the impact of CRD on TXA clearance, particularly highlighting how elevated plasma concentrations persist in patients with advanced stages of CRD (stages 3–5). Notably, the study’s high-risk group, which underwent more complex surgeries, showed sustained TXA concentrations above the therapeutic threshold for approximately 12 h, underscoring the need for careful dose management in these patients. The study proposed a new dosing regimen based on pharmacokinetic modeling, aimed at optimizing antifibrinolysis while avoiding excessive accumulation of the drug, which is known to increase the risk of postoperative seizures. These findings complement our own observations regarding the need for tailored TXA dosing in patients with impaired renal function, reinforcing the importance of pharmacokinetic models to inform dosing strategies that balance efficacy and safety. This study’s approach to dose adjustment provides a solid foundation for refining TXA administration protocols, particularly in high-risk patients with CRD, to improve clinical outcomes while minimizing the risk of adverse effects.

This study has some limitations. First, as this was a cohort study aimed at population pharmacokinetic analysis of TXA in cardiac surgery with CPB, a formal sample size calculation was not performed. Nonetheless, given the model’s good fit and parameter precision, the sample size appears adequate for reliable estimation. Second, we did not employ a weight-adjusted dosing strategy; all patients received a fixed-dose protocol. This may limit generalizability, particularly in diverse patient populations with varying body weights. Third, fluid management and renal function varied among patients, particularly within the non-HD group, and these factors may have influenced TXA concentrations. Fourth, dialysis practices during CPB are not standardized across institutions. In our center, HD patients received intraoperative dialysis via the CPB circuit using a hemodiafilter (Hemocrystal, MERA Co., Japan), which is also routinely used in non-HD patients. Differences in dialysis techniques may affect extrapolation to other settings. Finally, we used a single-center dosing regimen without continuous infusion, as continuous administration is not universally recommended. These limitations should be considered when interpreting and generalizing our findings.

In conclusion, in adult patients undergoing HD who underwent cardiovascular surgery with CPB, post-ICU blood concentrations of TXA were significantly higher than those in non-HD patients. A bolus administration effectively maintained a blood concentration adequate for antifibrinolytic activity (≥ 10 µg/mL) in both the HD and non-HD groups. Additionally, the model developed in this study, which considers renal function, provides insights into TXA blood concentration changes post-administration, essential for planning optimal dosing.
