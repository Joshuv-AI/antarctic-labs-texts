Atomic-Scale Characterization of Impurity Enrichment to High-Purity Alumina Lattice Defects
Authors: Tommaso Tacchetto, Allan Costine, Mark Pearce, Zakaria Quadir, Steven M. Reddy, David Saxey, Denis Fougerouse, Xiao Sun, Dimitrios Dimitriou, Martijn Woltering
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-purity alumina (HPA) is a cornerstone material in advanced energy and electronic technologies necessary for the global energy transition. Traditionally, HPA is routinely studied using bulk analytical techniques, which overlook nanoscale inhomogeneities. However, as industrial demand shifts toward increasingly higher purity standards, a fundamental understanding of the structural and chemical nanoscale characteristics of HPA becomes essential. In this study, electron microscopy and atom probe tomography are used to reveal that crystallographic defect structures within HPA act as major sinks for bulk impurities. These impurities are preferentially accumulated via dislocation-mediated diffusion. These findings challenge the conventional homogeneous view of pure bulk materials and show that HPA can be chemically and structurally inhomogeneous at the nanoscale level. The results highlight the critical role of nanoscale characterization of high-end materials and reveal new opportunities to develop innovative pathways for precision design and manufacturing of next-generation high-performance materials.

Introduction

HPA is a purified form of synthetic aluminum oxide (i.e., corundum) characterized by a concentration ≥99.99% of α-Al2O3. At room temperature, α-Al2O3 is the thermodynamically stable phase, refractory, and with a strong resistance to mechanical abrasion. Unlike lower grade alumina, mostly adopted in bulk ceramics and refractories, HPA finds rapid growing applications in advanced technologies for energy transition such as LEDs, synthetic sapphire production, and lithium-ion battery separators where exceptional purity, chemical stability, consistency, and performance are essential. ,

The end use of HPA in industrial applications is strongly dependent upon its trace impurities. In particular, metal impurities can significantly affect its performance such as increased dielectric loss or thermal stability. Importantly, when used as a coating in Li-ion battery separators, the leaching of impurities into the electrolyte can compromise ionic and thermal conductivity, promote dendrite formation, and increase the risk of thermal runaway and short circuits (e.g., refs and and references therein). Contrarily, targeted doping of Rare Earth Elements (REE) is widely used to engineer grain boundaries in sintered ceramics to either strengthen or reduce grain boundary cohesion. , It follows that the chemistry has strong influence on the characteristics of HPA, hence its market applications.

Given the role of trace impurities in determining the performance of HPA, it is therefore important to understand its fundamental structure across multiple length scales. While the relationships between impurities and crystallographic properties is well-known in bulk ceramics (e.g., refs and and references therein), there is a significant knowledge gap in understanding the significance of subtle nanometre heterogeneities within single crystals of HPA. For example, nanoscale processes such as pipe diffusion along dislocations can facilitate transport of dopants across the crystal lattice. This knowledge gap is largely due to the limitations of conventional bulk characterization approaches, which often overlook the localized chemical variations and structural defects that might occur at the nanoscale. For example, because of its extreme level of purity, the routine quantification of low trace impurities is resolved by bulk analytical techniques (e.g., Inductively Coupled Plasma- Mass Spectrometry), while crystallographic and morphology information are obtained by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) or Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (e.g., refs and ). As a result, the crystallographic-chemistry correlation at the scale of a single crystal is lost, hindering a deeper understanding of the fundamental mechanisms that govern material performance.

In this work, the three-dimensional geometry and composition of nanoscale features (e.g., dislocations) within HPA are investigated using a cross-correlative approach of electron microscopy and atom probe tomography (APT). This study reveals how impurities can localize along intrinsic dislocations within single crystals of HPA, advancing our understanding of impurities distribution in synthetic high-purity materials. These results demonstrate the role of crystallographic defects in mediating the mobility of trace elements that will inform future strategies for improvement of HPA performance in electronic and energy applications.

Materials and Methods

The ≥99.99% pure α-Al2O3 used for this study was sourced from Sumitomo Chemicals, Japan. The bulk impurities concentrations are Si (22 ppm), Na (3 ppm) and Fe (2 ppm). This product was selected from available market options for its relatively large average grain size (>2 μm), compared to most commercially available HPA powders. The material was used as received with no additional treatment.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization of the powdered sample was performed using a Zeiss Ultra Plus Field Emission-SEM at CSIRO Mineral Resources, Perth (Australia). Secondary Electron-SEM (SE-SEM) imaging was conducted by dispersing a small amount of HPA over a double-sided carbon tape mounted on a metal stub and then carbon coated. For Electron Backscattered Diffraction (EBSD) orientation mapping the material was embedded in epoxy resin and polished with colloidal silica. Diffraction data were collected at 100 nm step-size using an Oxford symmetry detector. The post processing of the EBSD data was done using AztecCrystal v. 3.1 and involved noise reduction by the removal of “wild spikes” and a six-nearest neighbor zero solution removal.

A transmission electron microscopy (TEM) foil was prepared from the polished epoxy round mount using a TESCAN Lyra3 Ga+ Focused Ion Beam (FIB)-SEM housed at Curtin University (Australia). The milling was performed at 30 kV while a 5 kV final polishing was used to reduce the damage induced by the high-energy ions. TEM, Scanning-TEM (STEM) imaging, and STEM-EDS mapping were conducted using a FEI Talos FS200X at Curtin University (Australia). In STEM mode, images were collected in Bright-Field (BF), Dark Field (DF) and High-Angle Annular Dark-Field (HAADF) with the instrument operating at 200 kV.

Four specimens for APT were prepared using the same TESCAN Lyra3 FIB-SEM. Targeting of the volume of interest used the electron beam deposition Pt fiducials method (Figure S1). The annular milling was performed using 30 kV acceleration voltage of the ion-gun. A final step of 5 kV polishing was performed to minimize the Ga implantation and residual Pt from the protective layer. The specimens were analyzed on a CAMECA LEAP 4000X HR at the Geoscience Atom Probe Facility, Curtin University. The instrument was operated at a base temperature of 60K in laser-assisted mode with a UV laser (λ = 355 nm) pulsed at 200 kHz and a pulse energy of 450 pJ. The ion detection rate was kept constant at 0.005 ions/pulse. The mass spectrum and the 3D visualization of the data set were processed using CAMECA’s AP Suite 6.3 software. 3D atom maps were obtained using average atomic density of corundum (0.00847 nm3/atom) and the evaporation field determined empirically by Fougerouse et al., 2022. The reconstructions included both voltage and tip profile protocols, the latter based on low kV SE-SEM images of the specimens. In the heterogeneous specimens (SU-12, SU-13), concentration analysis was performed by extracting the mass spectrum of constant volume regions of interest both inside (interface) and outside (matrix) the enriched regions, respectively. Calculations of the Gibbsian interfacial excess was performed following the method of Theska and Primig (2024).

Results

Localization of the Defects

At the SEM, the as-obtained powder material consists of discrete, micrometre-scale polyhedral grains of aluminum oxide, with occasional agglomerated grains being observed (Figure ). The individual grains do not exceed ca. 7 μm in diameter (Figure a), are highly faceted and characterized by well-ordered growth step-patterns (Figure b).

EBSD orientation mapping indicates that individual grains are randomly oriented with respect to each other (Figure c). High-spatial resolution EBSD maps indicate the presence of subtle linear misorientations (<5°) within some of the grains (Figures d,e). Disorientation axis analysis indicates that these small misorientations are not randomly distributed but broadly align with the trace of the linear features (Figure S2). Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM) variations seen at the edges of grain result from minor topographical differences that develops between the softer embedding resin and the HPA crystals during mechanical polishing (Figure e). These features are interpreted as artifacts and are not considered further.

TEM imaging indicates that three crystals of HPA were captured within the lamella, separated by a thin film of epoxy resin (Figure a). The cross-section confirms the faceted morphology of the single crystals of HPA. STEM-BF imaging reveals the presence of ubiquitous darker domains clustered at the grain corners (Figure a) as well as elongated features that crosscut the grains, structures not apparent in SEM observations (Figure a). At higher magnifications, the edge regions exhibit both linear and circular geometries (red and white arrows in Figure b). STEM-BF observations indicate that the circular geometries likely correspond to localized strain associated with crystal distortion. Instead, the darker, evenly spaced (<20 nm) linear features, represent classical illustration of dislocations. The contrast originates from the diffraction of the electron beam from the lattice distortion around the dislocation cores. This was confirmed by performing stage tilting observations. Coexistence and intersection of dislocations create complex intricate polygonal structures (Figure b, Figure S3). In STEM-HAADF mode (Figure c, Figure S3), these linear features appear brighter in greyscale. Since HAADF provides Z-contrast images (e.g., brighter gray scale indicates atoms with higher atomic mass), this contrast may indicate compositional heterogeneity between the host and defects or, alternatively, an effect of electron channelling typical of dislocations (e.g., refs and ).

Composition of the Defects

STEM-EDS analyses conducted on the areas containing the crystallographic features indicate a homogeneous distribution of Al and O with no distinguishable chemical heterogeneity associated with the defects (Figure d).

The mass-to-charge ratio spectra obtained from APT analysis of three specimens (SU-12, SU-13, SU-14) indicate that the bulk composition is predominantly composed of Al (ca. 41 at%) and O (ca. 58 at%), present both as single and multiple oxide molecular ions (Figure S4, Table ). The APT data are consistent with the analysis of alumina (e.g., ref ). Oxygen concentrations lower than stoichiometric values is likely related to neutral oxygen not being detected in the APT analysis of oxides (e.g. refs − ). Compared to the local background, Si and Mg are common traces in specimens SU-12 and SU-13, whereas a minor peak of Na is recorded only in SU-13 (Table ).

Three-dimensional reconstruction of SU-12 and SU-13 reveal that discrete structural features are captured within the data set and highlighted by higher concentrations of Si (Figures a,b). Except for the nanoscale Si-enriched domains observed in SU-12 and SU-13, all other Si in the atom maps stems from background counts. This is demonstrated by comparing the mass spectra of two equivalent regions of interest (i.e., interface and matrix) within specimen SU-13 (Figure S5). In SU-12, Si enrichments decorate two linear features (Figure b). Similarly, SU-13 displays a vertically oriented planar domain along one side of the specimen, within which elongated linear features can be distinguished, oriented perpendicularly to the vertical axis of the specimen (Figure a, Supplementary Video). Si-concentration 2D profiles across this planar region confirms the presence of equally spaced compositionally distinct domains (Figure c). Compositionally, the enriched domains in the analyzed specimens are characterized by elevated Si contents (up to ca. 3 atom %), with Mg (up to 0.04 atom %) and Na (up to 0.01 atom %) in specimen SU-13 being present. This corresponds to reduced Al and O compositions (Table ). This chemical heterogeneity is visually confirmed by a 1D-concentration profiles across the interface of sample SU-13 (Figure ). Interfacial excess calculation yield ca. 19 nm–2 excess Si, 0.09 nm–2 Mg and only 0.003 nm–2 Na (Figure S6). However, the large errors in the Mg and Na estimates arise from the very low ion counts detected for these species (Figure S6).

Discussion

Correlating Nanostructures to Nanochemistry

Dislocations represent one-dimensional crystallographic defects in which the perfectly ordered arrangement of atoms of crystal lattice is locally distorted along a line. , A high-density of dislocations can further evolve into more extensive bulk defects, such as planar defects or grain boundaries. In this study, EBSD mapping identifies the occurrence of subtle linear changes in misorientation (<5°) with disorientation axis lying subparallel to the elongation direction (Figures d,e) consistent with a low angle tilt-boundary geometry (e.g. ref ). TEM observations revealed clustering of linear defects at the edges of three crystals of HPA as well as planar interfaces that transect individual grains (Figure ). These regions of the HPA grains were specifically targeted for APT specimen preparation (Figure S1), enabling direct correlation between the structural and chemical data sets.

The APT reconstructions reveal equally aligned, chemically enriched, linear and planar features in three dimensions. The planar structure captured within sample SU-13 exhibits a series of parallel, evenly spaced linear features that closely match the spacing measured in sample SU-12, as identified by the 2D profile (Figure c). Because low-angle grain boundaries consist of an array of dislocations, the planar geometry is interpreted to be a low-angle grain boundary plane, internally composed of equally spaced dislocations, consistent with both EBSD and TEM observations. Taken together, the correlations across EBSD, TEM and APT data sets provide a coherent framework demonstrating that trace element heterogeneities in the HPA crystals are closely associated with crystallographic defects.

Trace Element Segregation to High-Purity Alumina Crystal Defects

Crystallographic defects and grain boundaries influence the mechanical, electrical and conductivity properties of materials (e.g., refs − and references therein). These defects and interfaces are favorable sites for solute segregation due to their elevated free-energy state (e.g., ref ), hence playing a critical role in dopant distribution and phase stability. An example of this behavior is observed during the synthesis of yttria-stabilized alumina, where the reduced solubility of Zr and Y dopants on the formation of α-Al2O3 from a transition alumina precursor can lead to their expulsion from the crystal lattice and localization at grain boundaries (e.g., refs and ). In polycrystalline Al2O3 ceramics, APT studies have previously resolved localized segregation of dopants to grain boundaries and dislocations providing key insights to defect-mediated segregation as an active mechanism for the preferential mass transport of cations during ceramic processing. ,,,−

The APT results reveal that lattice defects are chemically distinct and dominated by Si enrichment, with minor Mg and Na locally detectable. Notably, although Fe is listed as a nominal impurity, its concentration in the analyzed volume remains below the background of the APT measurement. The results of this study are consistent with previous observations. Using atomic-resolution STEM to analyze purposely doped and thermally joined HPA bicrystals, Tochigi et al. (2017) indicate that dislocation cores can preferentially host solute species. The APT results of this study demonstrate that trace impurity enrichment can be present at intrinsic lattice defects of synthetic HPA without externally added dopants or sintering-induced redistribution. By incorporating APT into HPA research, this study has advanced previous qualitative approaches enabling to resolve in three-dimensions the internal structure of a boundary plane and the quantification of impurity segregation at the scale of a single dislocation. Importantly, the high Gibbsian interface excess of aliovalent Si implies that the defects in HPA may act as an active site for solute accumulation exceeding one atomic monolayer requiring a severe charge compensation of the local arrangement around the defect. This non-stoichiometric environment aligns with previous studies, indicating that defects in HPA may be chemically and structurally more complex than previously assumed.

A growing number of APT studies applied to a wide range of materials, supports the role of dislocations and grain boundaries as preferential sites of chemical segregation particularly in response to gradient of diffusion or deformation. ,,− In natural corundum, Jin et al. (2024) documented the presence of nanoscale chemical inhomogeneity in the form of nanoclusters developed during crystallization. In particular, the authors conclude that the clustering reflects a process of element incorporation driven by fractionation. In this study, given that no other changes in microstructures (e.g., deformation) or composition (e.g., doping) were applied before the investigation, strain-induced segregation is not a likely mechanism to account for the observed impurity distribution within the investigated HPA. Instead, the process of segregation is likely to be related to a secondary process of solid-state diffusion during crystal growth. Step-bunching and step-faceting are well-known processes occurring during the growth of corundum, which leads to formation of atomic terraces and defects (e.g., refs − ). In this study, SEM investigation of the powder samples indicates the presence of surface steps, which closely resemble terrace-step morphology typical of overlapping {0001} faces in corundum (e.g., refs and ). Although it was not possible to verify the crystallographic orientation of these facets in the analyzed crystals of HPA, based on these observations we speculate that the formation of crystallographic defects is likely to be linked to the reorganization of the hexagonal packed lattice during the growth of α-Al2O3 crystal. Because of volumetric restrictions at the edges, vacancies might therefore condense to accommodate for the localized distortion, as indicated by the strain clouds observed in the TEM data.

Segregation of Si to dislocations or grain boundary has been previously documented in polycrystalline ceramics studies. ,, In particular, previous studies speculated that Si fractionation within alumina might be influenced by the presence of crystallographic defects such as dislocations or facets. , For example, Swiatnicki et al. (1995) demonstrated that Si exhibits a strong affinity for boundary planes parallel to the basal (0001) plane. In this study, based on the spacing of the Si concentration in the APT data set, it is reasonable to interpret that the observed Si, Mg and Na impurities are likely mobilized along dislocation cores (likely through pipe-diffusion) during the crystallization and the growth of the defects. The presence of crystallographic defects allows for a preferential pathway of diffusion of impurities to compensate for the charge balance given the energy excess at the interfaces.

Conclusions

This study reveals nanoscale chemical inhomogeneities in HPA, challenging the conventional assumption of uniform purity and underscoring the limitations of bulk analytical methods. Understanding how and where impurities localizesuch as at dislocations or grain boundariescan inform strategies to minimize defect formation and control impurity migration. For applications like battery separators, where alkali mobility poses risks to electrolyte stability, it is essential not only to quantify impurity concentrations but also to assess their spatial distribution. Dislocations may act as sinks that immobilize impurities, preventing their migration, or conversely, as fast diffusion pathways (via pipe-diffusion) enhancing leaching. These dual roles highlight the importance of mapping impurity-dislocation interactions at the atomic scale. Beyond HPA, the approach presented here offers a framework for uncovering hidden chemical complexities in other oxide systems and guiding new frontiers in functional material design.
