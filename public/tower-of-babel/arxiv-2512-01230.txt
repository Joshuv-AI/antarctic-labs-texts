The Entropy Flow of a Laser Beam Journal: Results in Physics
Howard M. Wiseman

Abstract
A laser beam is often modelled by a pure coherent state. In fact its state is mixed, even if it has coherent-state photon-number statistics (Poissonian), because the phase must vary. We consider such an ideal laser beam, with phase diffusion rate ℓ \ell , equal to its (Lorentzian) spectral width. We show that the beam entropy is extensive, with an entropy flow of S ˙ = N ˙ ​ ℓ \dot{S}=\sqrt{\dot{N}\ell} , where N ˙ \dot{N} is the number flow. We give an intuitive explanation for this remarkably simple result, and compare it to a unidirectional thermal beam.



1
Introduction

Since the invention of the laser, its output has been recognized for, and found myriad applications due to, its superior coherence properties compared to other light sources
[
13
]
. For many purposes, a laser beam may be described as a perfectly coherent state
|{\alpha}\rangle
, with
\alpha
constant in time, and in some quantum optics textbooks it is so described. However, more careful treatments show that even a laser with with no technical noise has quantum noise leading to phase diffusion, at some rate
\ell
, which is also equal to the FWHMH (full-width-at-half-maximum-height) spread in frequencies
[
11
,
9
]
. Since a laser beam is thus not in a pure state, this raises the question: what is its entropy? Surprisingly, to the author’s knowledge, this has never been calculated. This paper rectifies that.

2
Problem statement and solution

A free-space light-beam which is unidirectional, approximately monochromatic, polarized, and fully transverse-coherent is described by a 1-parameter family of bosonic lowering operators
\hat{b}(t)

[
11
,
1
]
. Here,
t
can be understood as the time since that infinitesimal beam-segment was emitted from the source, so that, at clock-time
\tau
, the field a distance
z
from the laser is described by
\hat{b}(\tau-z/c)
. With commutation relations
[\hat{b}(t),\hat{b}(s)^{\dagger}]=\delta(t-s)
, the operator
\hat{b}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{b}(t)
is the
photon flow
operator. We denote the mean photon flow, assumed time-independent, as
\dot{N}=\left\langle{\hat{b}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{b}(t)}\right\rangle
(rather than
{\cal N}
as in Ref.
[
1
]
).

An ideal laser beam can be modelled as a constant-amplitude but wandering-phase coherent state. That is, an eigenstate of
\hat{b}(t)
, with eigenvalue
\sqrt{\dot{N}}e^{i\phi(t)}
, where
\phi(t)
is a real stochastic process. Separating the beam into small segments of length
\delta t=T/K
, we can write this state over a duration
[0,T)
as

\rho_{[0,T)}=\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\left[\bigotimes_{k=0}^{K-1}\left|{re^{i\phi(k\delta t)}}\right\rangle\left\langle{re^{i\phi(k\delta t)}}\right|\right],

(1)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\bullet}
denotes the ensemble average over
\bullet
, and the ket
|{re^{i\varphi}}\rangle
is a single-mode coherent state of mean photon number
r^{2}=\dot{N}\delta t
. We will consider pure phase diffusion,

\phi(t)=\omega_{0}t+\sqrt{\ell}W(t),

(2)

where
\omega_{0}\gg\ell
is the centre-frequency and
W(t)
is a Wiener process. This well describes quantum-limited phase noise, or other homogenous noise giving rise to a Lorentzian lineshape of FWHMH
\ell

[
9
]
. We also restrict to the usual regime of laser beams where the beam coherence
\mathfrak{C}=4\dot{N}/\ell
— the number of photons per coherence time
[
1
]
— is extremely large.

We wish to calculate the entropy flow of the beam,

\dot{S}=\lim_{T\to\infty}T^{-1}S[\rho_{[0,T)}],

(3)

which is independent of
\phi(0)
and
T
. Because the von Neumann entropy,
S_{1}
, is too difficult to calculate, we use the Rényi 2-entropy,
S_{2}
. For the asymptotic-in-
T
results, the beam effectively comprises many statistically independent identical segments of length
\approx 1/\ell
, so these entropies will differ at most by a constant factor
[
2
]
.
Thus, the quantity to be calculated is

\dot{S}_{2}=\lim_{T\to\infty}T^{-1}\left\{{-k_{\rm B}\log{\rm Tr}\left[(\rho_{[0,T)})^{2}\right]}\right\}

(4)

As we prove below, Eq. (
4
), the entropy flow of a laser beam of linewidth
\ell
much less than its photon flow
\dot{N}
, equals

\dot{S}_{2}=k_{\rm B}\sqrt{\dot{N}\ell}.

(5)

3
Proof

Being careful to use independent Wiener processes, say
W(t)
and

V(t)
, for the two copies of
\rho
in Eq. (
4
), we have

{\rm Tr}\left[(\rho_{[0,T)})^{2}\right]=\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{W,V}\left[{\prod_{k=0}^{K-1}\left|{\left\langle{re^{i\sqrt{\ell}W(k\delta t)}}\right|\left.{re^{i\sqrt{\ell}V(k\delta t)}}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}}\right].

Evaluating the inner product of these coherent states
[
11
]
, and defining a new Wiener process
X=(W-V)/\sqrt{2}
, we find

{\rm Tr}\left[(\rho_{[0,T)})^{2}\right]=\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[{\Pi_{k=0}^{K-1}e^{-4r^{2}\sin^{2}\left\{{\sqrt{\ell/2}\,X(k\delta t)}\right\}}}\right].

(6)

Using
r^{2}=\dot{N}\delta t
, and taking the limit so
\delta t\to{\rm d}t
, gives

{\rm Tr}\left[(\rho_{[0,T)})^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[{e^{-4\dot{N}\int_{0}^{T}\sin^{2}\left\{{\sqrt{\ell/2}\,X(t)}\right\}{\rm d}t}}\right].

(7)

This can be attacked by the Feynman–Kac technique
[
6
]
. Let
X(0)=x
and define the right-hand-side of Eq. (
7
) as
u(x,T)
. Then it can be shown
[
3
,
7
]
that this function is the unique solution, satisfying
u(x,0)=1
, of

-\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}={\cal L}u,\textrm{ where }{\cal L}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+4\dot{N}\sin^{2}(\sqrt{\ell/2}\,x).

(8)

Now, the differential operator
{\cal L}
is equivalent to the 1D Schrödinger Hamiltonian for a
\sin^{2}
potential, so clearly has a positive spectrum, meaning
u
decays monotonically over time. The lowest eigenvalue,
\lambda_{0}>0
, has a corresponding eiegenfunction
u_{0}(x)
that is non-zero almost everywhere.
Thus, in the long-time limit, for almost every
x
,
u(x,T)\to c_{0}u_{0}(x)e^{-\lambda_{0}T}
. So, integrating over a uniform distribution for
x
[
i.e.
averaging over all
\phi(0)
], Eq. (
4
) equals
k_{\rm B}\lambda_{0}
. The same is true for setting
x=0
[
i.e.
taking
\phi(0)
as given], as
u_{0}(0)\neq 0
by symmetry.

Now, to find
\lambda_{0}
, define a small parameter
\epsilon=(4\mathfrak{C})^{-1/4}=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\ell/\dot{N})^{1/4}

and change variables to
y=\epsilon x
. Then

{\cal L}=2\sqrt{\dot{N}\ell}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon^{2}}\sin^{2}(\epsilon y)\right].

(9)

In the desired high coherence limit,
\mathfrak{C}\gg 1
, the term in square brackets can be approximated by the dimensionless harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian, with minimum eigenvalue of
1/2
. Thus
\lambda_{0}=\sqrt{\dot{N}\ell}
, and we obtain Eq. (
5
).

4
Discussion of Eq. (
5
)

First, while such an expression has never been given before, in hindsight it is quite reasonable. The only parameters that define the ideal laser beam are three rates:
photon flow
\dot{N}
, linewidth
\ell
, and mean frequency
\omega_{0}
. The entropy of the mixture of coherent states is independent of
\omega_{0}
.
The entropy flow must go to zero as
\dot{N}\to 0
(zero photon flow means a vacuum state, which is pure) and as
\ell\to 0
(zero linewidth means a constant phase coherent state, which is pure). Therefore
\dot{S}_{2}=\sqrt{\dot{N}\ell}
is the simplest expression we could hope for.

Second, we can understand Eq. (
5
) by thinking of the number of distinguishable states in the ensemble (
1
). Each random walk of the phase through time gives a
different
state of the beam, but these are not necessarily distinguishable. They are if the coherent states
|{re^{i\phi(k\delta t)}}\rangle
, in some segment
k
, are close to orthogonal. For this we must consider
K
finite so that the modulus of the coherent amplitude
r=\sqrt{\dot{N}\delta t}
is non-infinitesimal. Let
\delta t=1/\sqrt{\dot{N}\ell}
, so that
r=(\dot{N}/\ell)^{1/4}
. Over this time, the random change in the phase is, roughly,
\pm\sqrt{\ell\delta t}=\pm(\dot{N}/\ell)^{-1/4}
. Since this is small, the complex coherent amplitude is displaced randomly (on the ring of radius
r
) by
\pm r\sqrt{\ell\delta t}=\pm 1
. This is just the displacement needed to make two coherent states roughly orthogonal. So, every time
\delta t
, a phase-path diverges into two distinguishable phase-paths. This increases the entropy by roughly
k_{\rm B}\log(2)
, giving
\dot{S}\approx k_{\rm B}/\delta t
, in agreement with Eq. (
5
).

Third, for a ideal standard quantum-noise-limited laser
[
12
]
,
\ell=\kappa/2\mu
and
\dot{N}=\kappa\mu
, where
\mu
is the mean number of
intracavity
photons and
\kappa
is the bare cavity intensity-damping rate. This gives
\dot{S}_{2}=\kappa/\sqrt{2}
, clearly limited by quantum noise.

Fourth, it is useful to rewrite Eq. (
5
) as

\dot{S}_{2}=k_{\rm B}\sqrt{{P}/{\hbar}}\sqrt{{\ell}/{\omega_{0}}},

(10)

where
P=\hbar\omega_{0}\dot{N}
is the laser beam power, to compare with a thermal beam. A unidirectional polarised one-dimensional thermal beam at temperature
\Theta
has a power
[
13
]

P^{\Theta}=\dot{U}^{\Theta}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}\omega}{2\pi}\,\hbar\omega\,\bar{n}^{\Theta}_{\omega}=\frac{\pi}{12}\frac{(k_{\rm B}\Theta)^{2}}{\hbar},

(11)

where
\bar{n}^{\Theta}_{\omega}=(e^{\hbar\omega/k_{\rm B}\Theta}-1)^{-1}
. Its Rényi-2 entropy flow is

\dot{S}_{2}^{\Theta}=k_{\rm B}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}\omega}{2\pi}\,\log(2\bar{n}^{\Theta}_{\omega}+1)=k_{\rm B}\frac{\pi}{8}\frac{k_{\rm B}\Theta}{\hbar},

(12)

since a single-mode thermal state has
{\rm Tr}[\rho^{2}]=1/(2\bar{n}+1)
. Equation (
12
) is just a factor of
3/4
smaller than the von Neumann entropy flow,
\dot{S}^{\Theta}_{1}
, well known from 1+1–conformal field theory
[
4
]
.
Thus, combining Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
), we have

\dot{S}_{2}^{\Theta}=k_{\rm B}\sqrt{{P^{\Theta}}/{\hbar}}\sqrt{{3\pi}/{16}}.

(13)

Comparing to Eq. (
10
), we see that, for the same power, the laser beam has an entropy smaller by a factor of roughly
Q^{-1/2}
, where
Q=\omega_{0}/\ell
is the Q-factor of the beam. Many commercial solid-state lasers have
Q\approx 10^{10}
or better
[
14
]
.
To give a numerical value,
\dot{S}_{2}\approx 10^{-12}
W/K for
Q=10^{10}
and
P=500
mW.

5
Summary and conclusions

Calculating the entropy flow for a laser beam fills a gap in knowledge. The result is remarkably simple, and has an intuitive explanation.
It gives a new insight into the nature of laser light and confirms, in a new form, its important differences from (unidirectional) thermal light
[
13
]
. Since a laser is like the pendulum for optical clocks
[
5
]
, the result may also shed light on the question of irreversibility in clocks
[
8
,
10
]
.
