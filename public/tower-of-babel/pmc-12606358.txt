Determining the optimal inoculant rate to maximize soybean productivity in the Guinea Savanna agro-ecological zone of Ghana
Authors: Abdul-Latif Abdul-Aziz, Bashiru Haruna, Jacob Ulzen, Issah Alidu Abukari, Abdulai Haruna, Rashidatu Abdulai, Alhassan Yamyolya Baako, Ibrahim Salifu Yusif
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoybean productivity in sub-Saharan Africa is often constrained by low soil fertility and limited biological nitrogen fixation (BNF). This study evaluated the effects of different rhizobial inoculant rates (0, 5, 7.5, 10, and 12.5 g kg−1 seed) on growth, yield, and biological nitrogen fixation (BNF) of three soybean varieties, Afayak, Favor, and Jenguma, using a split-plot design with variety as the main plot and inoculant rate as the subplot. Significant effects of variety and inoculant rate were observed for key agronomic traits. Compared to the uninoculated control, grain yield increased (p < 0.001) in 2023 by 27.3%, 78.8%, 45.9%, and 146.5%, and in 2024 by 47.6%, 54.2%, 87.9%, and 180.6% at 5, 7.5, 10, and 12.5 g kg−1 seed, respectively. The 10 g kg−1 rate yielded the highest nitrogen fixation, at 51.5 kg ha−1 in 2023 and 56.4 kg ha−1 in 2024. Jenguma responded best to inoculation, followed by Afayak and Favor. Economic analysis, using partial budgeting and the marginal rate of return (MRR), identified the most profitable rates as exceeding a 100% MRR threshold. Regression analysis revealed a curvilinear yield response to inoculant rate, with an optimum of about 1.9 g per plot, equivalent to 10 g kg−1 seed for yield stability or 7.5 g kg−1 for a more economical option. Results underscore the importance of variety-specific inoculation strategies to enhance nitrogen use efficiency and sustainable soybean production in the region.Keywords: Rhizobial inoculant, Biological nitrogen fixation (BNF), Soybean varieties, Economic analysis, Sub-Saharan Africa agricultureSubject terms: Plant sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max L. Merrill) has become an important cash and rotational crop in Ghana’s northern savannah regions, where it plays a vital role in household food security, rural livelihoods, and soil health through biological nitrogen fixation (BNF)1,2. Its value goes beyond human consumption, serving as livestock feed and contributing to industrial processing, thus making it a key part of Ghana’s agricultural development plans3,4. Recognizing its multiple benefits, soybean farming has grown significantly across the Guinea and Sudan savanna zones of West Africa5,6. To enhance these benefits, rhizobial inoculation has been promoted as a low-cost, climate-smart method to improve nodulation and nitrogen input, especially in nitrogen-poor soils typical of tropical areas7,8. Many studies in sub-Saharan Africa, including Ghana, have shown notable increases in nodulation, nitrogen fixation, and grain yield after inoculation with Bradyrhizobium japonicum, particularly on soils with low native rhizobia populations1,9,10. However, the level of these improvements varies significantly and is often limited by site-specific factors like soybean variety, soil nutrients, compatibility between the inoculant strain and the plant, and environmental conditions8,10,11. Recent research highlights how native rhizobia populations influence inoculation outcomes in Ghana. While these local strains are widespread, they are often ineffective or only moderately effective at fixing nitrogen. They tend to outcompete introduced inoculant strains for nodule space, especially when inoculant application rates are low or when the strains are poorly adapted to the environment3,8,9,12. This microbial competition greatly reduces inoculation effectiveness and limits expected agricultural benefits. Despite over ten years of promotion, current inoculant use guidelines in Ghana are mostly generic, often based on manufacturer recommendations that don’t consider local ecological variations or different soybean varieties13,14. Such “one-size-fits-all” rates may not work well across the different soil and microbial environments used by Ghanaian farmers. New field trials in West Africa suggest that soybean responses to inoculants are not always linear and that the best rates differ depending on the variety and location. Both excessive and too little application can lead to poor nodule colonization, low nitrogen fixation, and reduced economic returns1,9,12,13. Moreover, the widespread use of inoculants among smallholder farmers in Ghana remains limited. Major barriers include poor availability and distribution of quality inoculants, improper storage that damages rhizobia, lack of farmer awareness, and insufficient extension services and training on proper inoculation techniques2,3,5,7. These challenges hinder turning research success into real productivity improvements. Given these issues, this study aims to address a significant knowledge gap by examining how commonly grown soybean varieties respond to different inoculant rates in field conditions within Ghana’s Guinea Savanna zone. The specific goals are to: (i) find the best Bradyrhizobium inoculant rate for improving nodulation and yield, and (ii) evaluate the economic feasibility of these rates to support evidence-based recommendations for sustainable soybean farming among smallholders.

Materials and methods

Description of experimental sites

The experiment was conducted at the Savannah Agriculture Research Institute (SARI) field in the Tolon District of Northern Ghana. This site is in the Guinea Savannah Agroecological Zone, between 9° 25′ N and 00° 58′ W. The rainfall pattern is unimodal, occurring from May to October, with peaks in August and September. From Fig. 1, rainfall varied significantly between years. In 2023, peak rainfall was observed in August (254.3 mm) and October (238.1 mm), whereas in 2024, September recorded the highest rainfall (330.3 mm), followed by August (225.1 mm). Several months in 2024, including March, May, November, and December, recorded no rainfall, contrasting sharply with the 2023 values of 5 mm, 126 mm, 140.1 mm, and 13.2 mm, respectively. June and July in 2024 received 149.1 mm and 85.2 mm, respectively, both exceeding the amounts in 2023 (97.3 mm and 107.1 mm), indicating a shift in rainfall onset and intensity. Average temperatures across both years were relatively stable, with minor fluctuations (Fig. 2). May was the warmest month in both years, registering 30.2 °C in 2023 and 29.1 °C in 2024. August was the coolest month, averaging 26.4 °C in 2023 and 26.8 °C in 2024. Overall, temperatures in 2024 were slightly lower than in 2023 for most months, particularly in May, October, and November, suggesting a modest cooling trend during the growing season. These climatic differences between 2023 and 2024 may have directly affected crop growth stages, water availability, and the efficiency of soil amendments such as inoculants.

Characterization of soil at experimental sites

Before the application of treatments, composite soil samples were collected from the top 0–20 cm layer across the experimental field to characterize baseline soil fertility. The samples were air-dried, gently crushed, and passed through a 2-mm sieve for the analysis of physicochemical properties. Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 soil-to-water suspension using a digital pH meter, following the procedure described by McLean15. Organic carbon content was determined using the Walkley–Black wet oxidation method, as outlined by Nelson and Sommers 16. Total nitrogen was quantified using the micro-Kjeldahl digestion method, according to Bremner and Mulvaney (1982). Available phosphorus was extracted using the Bray-1 method17, which is suitable for acidic soils, and quantified colorimetrically with the molybdenum blue method. Exchangeable potassium was extracted with 1 M ammonium acetate (NH₄OAc) at pH 7.0 and measured using a flame photometer, as described by Thomas18. The initial soil characteristics summarized in Table 1 provide essential information on the baseline fertility status of the experimental site before treatment application. The soil pH of 6.5 reflects a moderately acidic environment, which, while not severely limiting, may still influence nutrient availability and the activity of symbiotic rhizobia. As noted by Brady and Weil19, rhizobial efficiency tends to decline in soils where pH drops below 5.5, although the current pH remains within an acceptable range for soybean cultivation. The organic carbon content (1.58 g kg−1) and total nitrogen (0.65 g kg−1) are both low, indicating poor organic matter levels and limited nitrogen reserves—characteristics commonly observed in degraded tropical soils20. These deficiencies highlight the need for biological amendments such as rhizobial inoculants to enhance nitrogen fixation. Furthermore, the available phosphorus level (6.98 mg kg−1) falls significantly below the critical threshold of 15 mg kg−1 required for optimal nodulation and legume growth21, suggesting a constraint on phosphorus-dependent processes. The exchangeable potassium concentration (0.65 cmol (+) kg−1) is also suboptimal, potentially limiting plant metabolic functions and yield potential. Collectively, these baseline values underscore the necessity of soil fertility enhancement strategies to improve crop productivity under such conditions.relatively moderate but may still limit yield potential when coupled with other nutrient constraints. These baseline conditions justify the use of rhizobial inoculants as a strategy to enhance biological nitrogen fixation and support soybean productivity. They also help contextualize the crop’s response to the treatments applied.

Experimental design and treatments

The experiment was conducted using a split-plot design with three replications. The main plots consisted of three soybean (Glycine max L.) varieties: Afayak (TGx1834-5E), Favor (TGx1844-22E), and Jenguma (TGx1448-2E). The sub-plots received five inoculant rates: 0 (control), 5, 7.5, 10, and 12.5 g kg−1 seed. These rates were selected based on the manufacturer’s recommendation (5 g kg−1 seed), previous research findings8,22, and prevailing farmer practices in northern Ghana to ensure both biological effectiveness and cost efficiency. Each experimental plot measured 4 × 4 m and was separated by a 1 m alley to minimize edge effects. A maize plot was included as a non-nodulating reference crop to facilitate the estimation of Biological Nitrogen Fixation (BNF). The inoculant used was NoduMax, a commercial peat-based formulation of Bradyrhizobium japonicum (developed by IITA), consisting of 50% active rhizobial culture and 50% sterile peat carrier. Control plots received no inoculant, and no chemical fertilizer was applied to any plot during the study.

Soybean varieties and sowing details

The soybean varieties used, Afayak, Favor, and Jenguma, are improved promiscuous cultivars developed by IITA and released by CSIR-SARI for cultivation in Ghana. They were selected based on their prevalence among smallholder farmers and their different responses to inoculation under savanna conditions: Afayak (TGx1834-5E): early-maturing (90–100 days), drought-tolerant, with a yield potential of 2.0–2.5 t ha−1. Favor (TGx1844-22E): early- to medium-maturing (95–105 days), highly adaptable, with yield potential up to 2.8 t ha−1. Jenguma (TGx1448-2E): medium- to late-maturing (100–110 days), widely adopted, with consistent nodulation and yields of 2.5–3.0 t ha−1. All three varieties are promiscuous and can nodulate with various indigenous Bradyrhizobium strains, making them suitable for low-input systems. Sowing was done manually at a 50 cm × 10 cm spacing, establishing a population density of 200,000 plants ha−1, in accordance with regional agronomic recommendations (CSIR-SARI, 2020). Two seeds were sown per hill, then thinned to one plant 10 days after emergence.

Data collection

Data collection was collected on the growth and yield including nodulation, root and shoot biomass, pod yield, grain yield, and 100-seed weight. Biomass was sampled at the full pod stage (R4 stage) to determine shoot and root biomass, and nodulation). After carefully cutting the root systems of ten plants, they were gently washed on a 2-mm mesh sieve under running tap water. The nodules were then detached and counted, while the fresh shoot, root, and nodule samples were oven-dried at 65 °C for 48 hours to determine their dry biomass. During harvesting, the pods from ten plants were removed and counted to determine the pod load (i.e., pod number per plant). The pods of five plants weresub-sampled from the ten harvested and oven-dried at 65 °C for 48 h, and their weights were recorded. After threshing the pods harvested from each treatment net-plot, the grains were adequately sun-dried on a concrete platform and weighed on an electronic balance. One hundred seeds from each treatment were randomly selected and weighed. This procedure was replicated three times, and the average weight of 100 seeds was determined. Harvest index was determined by dividing the grain yield by the total above-ground biomass and multiplying by 100 to express it as a percentage.

Estimation of N2-fixed

The technique used to estimate N fixation was the Total Nitrogen Difference (TND) method. This was done by comparing the total nitrogen of the legume with that of a non-legume23. The amount of N fixed was calculated by subtracting the total nitrogen of the reference crop (maize) from that of the legume (soybean), and the difference value is assumed as N derived by BNF (N2 fixed).

Economic analysis of treatments

The economic benefits associated with inoculation rates for soybean varieties were assessed using partial budgeting, as outlined in Soha24 and Biratu et al.25. All variable input costs were considered, along with the seasonal average operational costs applicable to all treatments during the cropping season in the research area. The amounts farmers paid for clearing land, planting, purchasing supplies such as seed, and hiring labour for weeding, harvesting, and transporting farm products to their homes were all considered variable costs. Subsequently, the difference between each treatment’s gross income and total production costs was calculated to determine its value or net return per hectare. The average annual net returns over the study period were used to compute the mean net returns. There were no levies on capital expenses, including land, capital interest, farm equipment depreciation, or other overhead costs. After dividing the net benefit by the operational cost, the benefit ratio for each treatment was established. This is the difference between the total income obtained from selling the crop and the total cost of producing it.

Statistical analysis

All data collected were subjected to statistical analysis using GenStat Discovery Edition 10. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed following the split-plot design structure, with soybean variety as the main plot factor and inoculant rate as the subplot factor. In addition to the standard ANOVA, a linear–quadratic regression model, Y = β0 + β1 Rate + β2 Rate2, was fitted to assess the nature of the response of each variety to the inoculant rate. Polynomial contrasts [POL(Rate,2)] were used in GenStat to partition the rate effect into linear and quadratic components, and their interactions with variety were also tested. All treatment means were compared using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) at a 5% significance level.

Results and discussions

Nodule number and nodule dry weight

Nodule numbers were significantly influenced by soybean variety, inoculant rate, and their interaction, especially in the 2023 season (p < 0.01), but not in 2024 (Table 2). In 2023, nodule numbers ranged from 9 to 51 per plant, with the highest in Afayak at 12.5 g of inoculant. This indicates a dose-dependent response, consistent across Favor and Jenguma, though with different magnitudes. These results agree with Zahran26, who observed increased nodulation with inoculation, particularly in soils with low native rhizobial populations. Across both seasons, significant main effects of variety and inoculant rate were noted (Table 3). In 2023, Afayak and Favor produced the highest nodule numbers (p < 0.001), demonstrating superior genetic compatibility with Rhizobium11,27. Inoculation at 5, 7.5, 10, and 12.5 g/plant increased nodulation by 66%, 160%, 147%, and 160%, respectively, compared to the control. However, responses plateaued beyond 7.5 g, supporting findings from Dakora & Keya28 and Tamiru & Girma29 that increasing inoculant beyond optimal levels does not guarantee further benefits. In 2024, varietal effects remained significant (p < 0.001), with Afayak again outperforming others. Inoculation increased nodule numbers by 45%, 127%, 143%, and 174%, respectively (Table 2). The 5 g rate was statistically like the control, indicating it was insufficient to promote effective nodulation. These findings confirm the dose-responsive nature of inoculation, with 7.5–12.5 g emerging as effective application rates22,30. Soil phosphorus was low (6.98 mg/kg), which can limit early root development and nodulation26,31.

Nodule dry weight, an indicator of nitrogen fixation efficiency, was significantly affected by variety, inoculant rate, and their interaction across both seasons (p < 0.01, Table 2). In 2023, the highest dry weight (1937 mg/plant) was recorded in Afayak at 12.5 g, followed by Jenguma at 10 g. This pattern persisted in 2024, showing consistent positive responses to increasing inoculant rates. The increased biomass likely reflects improved rhizobial colonization and symbiotic activity11,32. Significant main effects of variety and inoculant were also observed (p < 0.001, Table 3). In 2023, Afayak and Favor recorded the highest nodule dry weights, suggesting effective Rhizobium compatibility 27. Inoculation at 5, 7.5, 10, and 12.5 g increased nodule biomass by 75%, 90%, 208%, and 176%, respectively. Although 12.5 g produced the highest values, it did not significantly differ from 7.5 and 10 g, indicating diminishing returns beyond 10 g29,30. In 2024, Favor recorded the highest nodule dry weight, with Afayak and Jenguma performing similarly. This shift may be due to seasonal differences in rainfall and soil microbial dynamics33. Inoculant applications at 5, 7.5, 10, and 12.5 g increased dry weight by 85%, 95%, 206%, and 181%, respectively, over the control (Table 2). Again, the 10 g rate was statistically like 12.5 g, suggesting it as the most efficient dose for maximizing biomass without wasting input. These results confirm that both host genotype and inoculant rate are key to nodulation success. While Afayak showed consistent performance in nodule number, Favor excelled in nodule biomass across seasons. The 10 g inoculant rate appears optimal, balancing effectiveness and resource efficiency.

Although P supplements were not tested in this study, phosphorus deficiency (initial soil analysis recorded available P at 6.98 mg kg⁻1) may have limited the full expression of inoculant benefits. Future work should therefore investigate the co-application of phosphorus with inoculants. Seasonal variations in nodulation likely reflected environmental differences, particularly rainfall and temperature. For example, March, May, November, and December 2024 recorded no rainfall, compared with 5 mm, 126 mm, 140.1 mm, and 13.2 mm, respectively, in 2023. Conversely, June and July 2024 received 149.1 mm and 85.2 mm, both higher than in 2023 (97.3 mm and 107.1 mm), suggesting a shift in rainfall onset and intensity. Temperatures in 2024 were slightly lower than in 2023 during most months, especially May, October, and November, indicating a modest cooling trend in the growing season. In addition, native rhizobial populations, though not characterized in this study, may also have influenced inoculant performance, as their abundance and competitiveness can affect symbiotic outcomes33,34. Future studies should therefore include assessments of indigenous rhizobia to better optimize inoculant effectiveness.

Shoot and root dry weight

Shoot and root dry weights are essential indicators of plant growth, nitrogen fixation efficiency, and varietal adaptability under different farming conditions. This study revealed significant effects of soybean variety, inoculant rate, and their interaction on shoot and root dry weights, with notable seasonal variations (Table 2). Shoot dry weight was affected by both inoculant rate and variety in both seasons, with an interaction effect seen in 2023 (p < 0.01) but not in 2024. Higher inoculant rates consistently increased shoot biomass. The 10 g and 12.5 g rates produced the highest shoot weights across varieties, although the difference between them was not statistically significant, indicating a threshold beyond which more inoculation offers limited benefits, a trend supported by Bala and Giller22 and Hungria et al.30. In 2023, Afayak (4613 kg/ha) and Jenguma (4520 kg/ha) had the highest shoot biomass at 12.5 g and 10 g rates, respectively. Favor, while responsive to inoculation, showed relatively lower shoot dry weights, possibly due to reduced nitrogen fixation efficiency under current conditions. Inoculation increased shoot biomass by up to 121% compared to the control, with the largest gains at 12.5 g. In 2024, similar patterns continued. Afayak remained the top performer, demonstrating its strong adaptability. Increases in shoot biomass over the control ranged from 106% at 5 g to 218% at 12.5 g, though gains leveled off between 10 and 12.5 g (Table 2). This supports using a 10 g inoculant rate as both effective and cost-efficient. The significant interaction between variety and inoculant rate for shoot dry weight (p < 0.01) suggests that responses to inoculation vary by genotype, likely due to differences in nitrogen translocation from root nodules to shoots, as previously reported by Herridge et al.27. These findings align with those of Salvagiotti et al.35, Savala et al.36, and Thilakarathna and Raizada11, which emphasize the role of inoculation in enhancing aboveground biomass through improved nitrogen uptake.

Root dry weight also responded significantly to inoculant rate and variety (p < 0.01; Table 2), without an interaction effect observed in both seasons (p > 0.05). In 2023, concerning the main varietal differences, Afayak and Jenguma produced significantly higher root dry weights than Favor, suggesting more vigorous root systems with greater potential for nodulation and nutrient absorption. Bradyrhizobium inoculation improved root biomass across all rates. Compared to the control, increases in root dry weight in 2023 were 4. 75%, 20.33. 33%, 37. 09%, and 41. 69% for 5 g, 7.5 g, 10 g, and 12.5 g, respectively (Table 2). However, these increases were more moderate than those observed for shoot biomass, indicating that root growth is somewhat less responsive to inoculant rate. In 2024, Favor unexpectedly produced the highest root dry weight, indicating strong genotype-by-environment interactions. Root biomass increased by 17. 84–62. 52% across increasing inoculant rates, again showing a trend of diminishing returns beyond 10 g. These observations suggest environmental factors, such as moisture availability and microbial activity, strongly influence varietal performance, as also noted by Giller34 and Zengeni et al.33. No significant interaction between variety and inoculant rate was observed for root dry weight (p > 0. 05), implying that although there are varietal differences, the benefits of inoculation on root development were consistent across genotypes. This finding reinforces the broad effectiveness of Bradyrhizobium inoculation for root growth.

Overall, both shoot and root biomass were significantly improved by Bradyrhizobium inoculation, with optimal responses at 10–12. 5 g per 1 kg of seed. Afayak consistently exhibited superior shoot biomass across seasons, while root biomass showed more variability depending on genotype and season. These results highlight the importance of matching inoculant rates with varietal traits and environmental conditions to enhance biological nitrogen fixation and optimize soybean growth.

Pod number and pod dry weight

Pod number and pod dry weight are key yield components in soybeans, reflecting reproductive efficiency and nutrient utilization, especially from biological nitrogen fixation (BNF). In both seasons, Bradyrhizobium inoculant rate and soybean variety significantly influenced these parameters, while interactions between variety and inoculant rate were not significant (p > 0.05; Table 3), indicating that inoculation benefits were consistent across genotypes. In both cropping seasons, varietal differences notably affected pod number. In 2023, Afayak and Jenguma produced the highest pod numbers (p < 0.001), with Favor showing slightly lower but comparable values to Jenguma. This highlights genotypic variability in reproductive potential, supported by Thilakarathna and Raizada11, who linked pod formation to nodulation efficiency and nitrogen use. Genotype contributes to pod formation in soybeans through its influence on nodulation efficiency, nitrogen fixation capacity, assimilate partitioning, and reproductive duration. Genotypic differences determine how effectively plants establish symbiosis with Bradyrhizobium, which in turn affects nitrogen availability for reproductive development11.Varieties that fix more nitrogen and allocate greater assimilates to reproductive organs generally produce more pods, while differences in flowering time, reproductive duration, and tolerance to stresses such as drought and pests also play critical roles in pod initiation and retention30,36. Ultimately, the genetic regulation of pod set and abortion underlies variability among genotypes, leading to differences in pod number and yield potential27. Bradyrhizobium inoculation significantly increased pod number (p < 0.001; Table 3). In 2023, pod number increased by 12%, 36%, 84%, and 84% with 5 g, 7.5 g, 10 g, and 12.5 g inoculant rates, respectively, over the control. The 10 g and 12.5 g rates performed similarly, indicating a response plateau. These findings align with Hungria et al.30 and Savala et al.36, who reported improved pod formation with higher inoculant doses up to an optimal level. In 2024, Afayak again led in pod count (p < 0.05), confirming its yield stability across seasons. Favor and Jenguma had similar but lower pod numbers. Inoculation continued to significantly boost pod formation (p < 0.001), with increases of 104% to 106% across all rates compared to the control. However, differences among inoculant levels were minimal, suggesting a ceiling effect under favorable environmental conditions, as previously observed by Herridge et al.27.

Pod dry weight also responded significantly to variety and inoculant rate (Table 3), though no interaction effects were found across seasons (p > 0.05). In 2023, concerning the main varietal effect, Jenguma recorded the highest pod dry weight (p < 0.01), while Afayak and Favor were statistically similar. Inoculation increased pod dry weight by 31.7%, 64.1%, 68.3%, and 91.7% at 5 g, 7.5 g, 10 g, and 12.5 g, respectively. The 10 g and 12.5 g rates were statistically comparable, suggesting a saturation point beyond which increases become marginal, consistent with Bala and Giller22. In 2024, no significant varietal differences were observed for pod dry weight, likely due to environmental factors such as rainfall or microbial dynamics masking genotypic expression. However, inoculation remained highly significant (p < 0.001), with increases of 40.8%, 77.3%, 108.6%, and 171.8% at 5–12.5 g, respectively. The 12.5 g rate significantly outperformed all others, indicating a strong dose–response relationship under favorable conditions, in line with Dakora and Keya28 and Hungria and Vargas32.

These results underscore that while varietal differences exist, Bradyrhizobium inoculation significantly enhances pod number and pod dry weight across genotypes and seasons. The 10 g rate offers an optimal balance between performance and efficiency, although 12.5 g may confer additional benefits under ideal conditions. The lack of significant interaction suggests broad adaptability of the inoculant across tested varieties, reinforcing its utility in improving soybean productivity in the Guinea Savanna zone.

Grain yield, 100-seed weight, and harvest index

Grain yield, 100-seed weight, and harvest index (HI) are important indicators of soybean productivity and efficiency. Their responses were significantly affected by variety, Bradyrhizobium inoculation rate, and season, although the effects varied in magnitude (Tables 3). In 2023, grain yield was significantly influenced by variety, inoculation rate, and their interaction (p < 0.01), but in 2024, only inoculation rate had a significant effect. In 2023, Jenguma achieved the highest grain yield (5722 kg/ha at 12.5 g), showing strong compatibility with Bradyrhizobium japonicum. Favor experienced the greatest relative increase, from 1278 kg/ha (control) to 3861 kg/ha (12.5 g), indicating high responsiveness to inoculation. Afayak reached its peak at 7.5 g (3278 kg/ha) and slightly declined to 10 g before recovering at 12.5 g. These varietal trends highlight different optimal inoculant rates, possibly due to genotype-specific nitrogen fixation efficiency or biomass partitioning capacity. In the main varietal effect, Jenguma consistently recorded the highest grain yield, while Afayak and Favor had statistically similar yields.

Its superior performance may stem from better adaptation to the Guinea Savanna, higher photosynthetic efficiency, and stronger compatibility with Bradyrhizobium japonicum.

Previous studies in Ghana and Nigeria8,37 similarly report Jenguma’s advantage in yield and nodulation efficiency over other varieties.

Afayak and Favor’s comparable yields likely reflect similar genetic yield potential under the given environmental and soil conditions. Throughout the 2023 season in the main effect, inoculation increased grain yield by 27% to 147%, with 12.5 g surpassing all other rates (Table 5). In 2024, environmental stress- characterized by lower rainfall and higher temperatures-reduced overall yields, minimized varietal differences, and decreased nodulation. Still, inoculation significantly boosted yields by 47–181%, with 12.5 g again showing the highest gain. This suggests the consistent effectiveness of Bradyrhizobium inoculation in improving yield, even under less favorable conditions, consistent with findings from Abaidoo et al.22 and Hungria et al.32.

Seed weight varied significantly with the main variety and inoculant rate in 2023 but not in 2024 (Table 3). Afayak consistently produced the largest seeds, likely due to its genetic potential for seed development. Inoculation increased 100-seed weight by up to 16. 7% at 12.5 g, with all rates outperforming the control. However, variety × inoculant interactions were not significant, indicating that seed size response was mostly independent of variety. In 2024, no significant effects from variety or inoculant rate were observed on 100-seed weight, likely due to environmental stress limiting seed fill. This underscores the sensitivity of seed development to seasonal variation and the importance of favorable conditions for inoculant effectiveness38.

Harvest index (HI) responded significantly to inoculant rate and the variety × inoculant interaction in 2023, but not in 2024 (Table 3). HI ranged from 0.5 to 0.9, with the highest values recorded for Jenguma at 12.5 g and Favor at 12.5 g, indicating better biomass partitioning to seeds at higher inoculant levels. Afayak’s HI remained stable (0.8%) at lower inoculant rates but declined at 10 g, suggesting potential physiological limits or microbial competition at higher doses. In 2024, HI differences were not significant, and no inoculant effect was observed. This suggests that under unfavorable conditions (e.g., drought or heat), the inoculant’s impact on biomass partitioning is diminished. Nevertheless, the 2023 results support the view that inoculation enhances not only total yield but also the efficiency of assimilate allocation, particularly when genotype-inoculant compatibility is high35.

Across seasons, Bradyrhizobium inoculation significantly enhanced grain yield and seed weight, especially at the 12.5 g rate, although optimal responses varied by variety. Jenguma showed the strongest overall performance, while Favor was highly responsive, and Afayak exhibited a lower optimal threshold. Harvest index improvements were inoculant-dependent and more pronounced under favorable conditions. Seasonal variation, particularly in rainfall and temperature, moderated inoculant effectiveness and varietal expression. These findings underscore the need for variety-specific and climate-adaptive inoculation strategies to maximize soybean productivity in low-input systems.

Amount of N2 fixed

The amount of nitrogen fixed by soybeans was significantly influenced by both variety and inoculant rate, though no significant interaction was observed between the two in either 2023 or 2024 (p = 0.975 and 0.908, respectively, Figs. 3 and 4). This indicates that the response to inoculation was consistent across varieties, a trend also reported by Abaidoo et al.22 in West African soils. Among the varieties, Jenguma consistently fixed the highest N₂, 51.22 kg/ha in 2023 and 55.97 kg/ha in 2024, significantly outperforming Afayak and Favor, which had similar but lower values. Jenguma’s superior fixation likely reflects its better nodulation and compatibility with Bradyrhizobium strains, aligning with findings by Kermah et al.39 and Okogun & Sanginga40. Inoculant rate also significantly influenced N₂ fixation. The 10 g/kg seed rate resulted in the highest N₂ fixed (51.52 kg/ha in 2023 and 56.41 kg/ha in 2024), significantly exceeding lower rates and the control. The 12.5 g/kg rate showed no additional benefit, suggesting a plateau effect, possibly due to microbial competition or limited nodule occupancy, as observed by Herridge et al.27 and Thilakarathna and Raizada11. Fixation levels were slightly higher in 2024 across treatments, likely due to improved environmental conditions or cumulative effects of previous inoculations. This supports the view of Peoples et al.41 that seasonal factors have a strong influence on BNF effectiveness in legumes.

Economic analysis

Table 4 presents the partial budget analysis of inoculant application rates across the three soybean varieties. Inoculant cost was the only variable expense, with all other cultivation costs held constant. All treatments generated positive net benefits, confirming that the added returns from inoculation exceeded the associated costs. For Afayak, the 7.5 g/kg seed rate yielded the highest net benefit of GHS 81,875, outperforming the 10 g and 12.5 g rates, which incurred higher costs with reduced returns. For the Favor variety, the 12.5 g/kg rate generated the highest net benefit of GHS 96,400. Similarly, for Jenguma, the 12.5 g/kg rate produced the highest net benefit of GHS 142,925, although the 7.5 g/kg rate was more profitable than the 10 g/kg rate. Since all treatments were economically viable, a marginal rate of return (MRR) analysis was conducted using the data in Table 5, with all dominated treatments excluded. For Afayak, the 7.5 g/kg rate yielded the highest MRR, exceeding the minimum acceptable threshold of 100%, making it the most economically beneficial. In the case of Favor, the 12.5 g/kg rate offered both the highest net benefit and MRR and is therefore recommended. Likewise, for Jenguma, the 12.5 g/kg rate resulted in the highest MRR and net benefit, making it the most economically optimal rate. These findings align with the recommendation criteria of Soha24 and Biratu et al.25, highlighting the importance of optimizing inoculant rates based on variety-specific economic performance.

Regression analysis

Polynomial regression analysis showed significant effects of inoculant rate on grain yield for both harvests (Table 6 and Fig. 5). For the season (Grain yield 1), the quadratic component of the inoculant rate response was not significant (p = 0.358) when analyzed across varieties, but the Rate 2 × Variety interaction was marginally significant (p = 0.057), indicating some variation in curvature among varieties. The overall model explained 54% of the variance, with significant main effects of variety (p = 0.010) and inoculant rate (p < 0.001). Jenguma tended to produce higher yields than Afayak (p = 0.040), while Favor did not differ significantly from Afayak. For the second season (Grain _ yield 2), the quadratic term was highly significant (p = 0. 006), accounting for a large portion of the variation (67% of the variance explained). However, neither variety nor the interactions between rate and variety were significant (p > 0.05), suggesting a similar curvilinear response across varieties. The fitted quadratic equation for Grain yield 2 was: Ŷ = 698 − 53. 5 (Rate) + 13. 97 (Rate 2), where yield is in kg ha⁻1 and Rate is the inoculant application rate in g per plot. This model predicts the maximum yield at approximately 1.9 g per plot for the pooled data across varieties. The significant quadratic term for grain yield in the second season confirms that the yield response to inoculation rate is not purely linear but peaks at an optimal point before declining. This pattern aligns with the well-documented diminishing returns of rhizobial inoculants beyond the rate needed to saturate nodulation potential26,42. For the first season, the marginal Rate 2 × Variety interaction suggests that different varieties have distinct inoculant rate- yield relationships. Jenguma, for example, had a more pronounced positive quadratic coefficient, indicating a higher optimal rate than Afayak or Favor. Similar genotype-specific responses have been reported by Ulzen et al.8 in northern Ghana, where varieties differed in nodulation efficiency and response to inoculation rate due to differences in root architecture and compatibility with Bradyrhizobium japonicum strain.

In the pooled analysis for the second season, the optimal rate was estimated at approximately 1.9 g per plot, beyond which yield declined. This aligns with findings from Yusuf et al.37 and Ronner et al.43, who reported that increasing inoculant application beyond the agronomic optimum may not boost yield and can sometimes reduce it due to resource competition or excessive nodulation diverting assimilates away from grain filling. The lack of significant variety effects in the second harvest could be because of more uniform soil conditions and reduced environmental variability at the later harvest stage, supporting observations by Danso and Eskew23 that environmental factors can often overshadow genotype-specific responses in nodulating legumes under field conditions. Practically, the results suggest that while inoculation is beneficial, rates should be tailored to the variety and growing conditions to maximize returns. Excessive rates not only waste resources but may also decrease yield potential. Adopting an optimal rate also aligns with the principles of 4R Nutrient Stewardship, applying the right source, at the right rate, right time, and right place44, which is crucial for sustainable soybean production in the Guinea Savanna.

Conclusion

The study highlights the critical role of optimized inoculation practices in enhancing soybean productivity and profitability. Regression analysis revealed a curvilinear yield response to inoculant rate, with an optimum of about 1.9 g per plot, equivalent to 10 g kg−1 seed for yield stability or 7.5 g kg−1 for a more economical option. Tailoring rates to specific varieties can enhance sustainability, particularly in areas with low native Rhizobium and poor soil fertility. Large-scale adoption will require raising farmer awareness, ensuring access to quality inoculants, maintaining proper storage, and providing practical training to translate research into lasting improvements in soil fertility and food security.
