Explicit non-free tensors
Maxim van den Berg, Matthias Christandl, Vladimir Lysikov, Harold Nieuwboer, Michael Walter, Jeroen Zuiddam

Abstract
Free tensors are tensors which, after a change of bases, have free support: any two distinct elements of its support differ in at least two coordinates. They play a distinguished role in the theory of bilinear complexity, in particular in Strassen’s duality theory for asymptotic rank. Within the context of quantum information theory, where tensors are interpreted as multiparticle quantum states, freeness corresponds to a type of multiparticle Schmidt decomposition. In particular, if a state is free in a given basis, the reduced density matrices are diagonal. Although generic tensors in ℂ n ⊗ ℂ n ⊗ ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n} are non-free for n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 by parameter counting, no explicit non-free tensors were known until now. We solve this hay in a haystack problem by constructing explicit tensors that are non-free for every n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . In particular, this establishes that non-free tensors exist in ℂ 3 ⊗ ℂ 3 ⊗ ℂ 3 \mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{3} , where they are not generic. To establish non-freeness, we use results from geometric invariant theory and the theory of moment polytopes. In particular, we show that if a tensor T T is free, then there is a tensor S S in the GL \mathrm{GL} -orbit closure of T T , whose support is free and whose moment map image is the minimum-norm point of the moment polytope of T T . This implies a reduction for checking non-freeness from arbitrary basis changes of T T to unitary basis changes of S S . The unitary equivariance of the moment map can then be combined with the fact that tensors with free support have diagonal moment map image, in order to further restrict the set of relevant basis changes.

Introduction

Generalizing the notion of a matrix, a
k
-tensor can be described as a multidimensional array of numbers with
k
indices
T=(T_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}})_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}}
, where the
i_{\ell}
range from
1
to some natural number
n_{\ell}
.
They play a pivotal role in algebraic complexity theory (bilinear complexity), quantum information theory (quantum states), combinatorics (cap sets and related problems).
In this paper we will restrict our attention to tensors of order
3

We call any subset
S\subseteq[n]^{3}

free
if any two distinct elements of

differ in at least two coordinates.
For any tensor

denote its support, consisting of the multi-indices at which

We call

has free support after possibly changing the basis on each of the tensor factors.
In particular, the tensor is sparse in this basis, in that it has at most

The class of free tensors play a particularly important role in several parts of the theory of moment polytopes and asymptotic spectra
[
Fra02
,
Str05
]

Generic tensors are not free, as can be seen by a dimension argument
[

, Remark 4.19]

In

]
the dimension of the Zariski-closure of the set of free tensors in

This implies that for every

However, no explicit non-free tensors were known until now.

Corollary 1.2
.

There exist non-free tensors in

.

In format

There exists a concise free tensor

Of vital importance in this criterion is the

.
This is a map

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose
T
is a free tensor, and let
S\in\overline{\textnormal{GL}\cdot T}\setminus\{0\}

\lVert\mu(S)\rVert
is minimal.
Then there exists
k\in\textnormal{U}
such that

has free support.

To prove this theorem we rely on classical results of geometric invariant theory, in particular results of Ness
[
NM84
]

However, we also depend on more modern (analytically-minded) approaches, in particular the one taken by Georgoulas, Robbin and Salamon in

In particular we heavily rely on properties of the gradient flow of the Kempf–Ness function, combined with an observation regarding moment map evaluations for tensors with free support

]

Motivation

We now elaborate on the role of freeness in the theory of moment polytopes and asymptotic spectra
[
Fra02
,
Str05
]
.

We first make the link between freeness and the moment map explicit.
The moment map
\mu\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\Herm_{n,n,n}
has the following concrete description.
If
T
is normalized, then the
(i,i^{\prime})
-th entry of first component of
\mu(T)
records the Frobenius inner product between slices
i
and
i^{\prime}
of
T
. The second and third components of
\mu
are given by inner products of “row-slices” and “column-slices”, respectively.
In particular the diagonal entries of each component of
\mu(T)
are simply given by the squared
\ell_{2}
-norm of the corresponding slice, row or column of
T
(after normalizing
T
).
Freeness of the support of a tensor implies that distinct slices/rows/columns have disjoint support, hence have zero inner product, leading to the following result
[
Sja98
,
Fra02
]
:

Proposition
.

If
\supp(T)
is free, then
\mu([T])
is a triple of
diagonal
matrices.

Freeness of tensors plays a role in giving inner approximations of
moment polytopes

[
Sja98
,
Fra02
]
.
The moment map is in fact a moment map in the symplectic geometry sense for the natural action of
\textnormal{U}=\U_{n}\times\U_{n}\times\U_{n}
on the projective space
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})
.
For
[T]\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})
, the image of
\mu
on
\overline{\textnormal{GL}\cdot[T]}\subseteq\mathbb{P}(V)
becomes a convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
after restricting the image to diagonal matrices with weakly increasing diagonal entries.
This polytope is called the moment polytope of
T
, and we will denote it by
\Delta(T)
.
(We will more formally define the moment map and moment polytope in the main text.)

Franz used the notion of freeness to determine the
3\times 3\times 3

Kronecker polytope
: this is the largest possible moment polytope for tensors of shape
3\times 3\times 3
, and can alternatively be defined as the closure of all normalized triples
\frac{1}{k}(\lambda,\mu,\nu)
such that
\lambda,\mu,\nu\vdash_{3}k
are partitions of
k
into at most
3
parts, with the condition that the Kronecker coefficient
g_{\lambda,\mu,\nu}\neq 0
.
He established an alternative description of the moment polytope method, which lends itself well to obtaining outer approximations.
Next, he verified tightness of his outer approximation of the
3\times 3\times 3
Kronecker polytope by constructing all its vertices using tensors with free support.
Franz’s method for obtaining outer approximations on moment polytopes is also highly relevant to our paper.
Sjamaar
[
Sja98
]
considered moment polytopes in the general setting of a reductive group acting on a representation, and used a slightly more general definition (equivalent to freeness in the case of the tensor action) to provide inner approximations for the image of the moment map.
By acting with triples of invertible diagonal matrices on
T
(which does not affect the support), one can achieve the following lower bound on
\Delta(T)
:

Proposition

(
[
Sja98
, Lem. 7.1]
)
.

If
T
has free support, then
\Delta(T)
contains
p_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\searrow$}}
for every
p\in\conv\{(e_{i}\mid e_{j}\mid e_{k})\mid(i,j,k)\in\supp(T)\}
, where
p_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\searrow$}}
is obtained from
p=(p_{1}\mid p_{2}\mid p_{3})
by putting each
p_{\ell}
in non-increasing order.

For free tensors it is known that two classes of functions that are central in the theory of asymptotic spectra (Strassen’s dual theory to asymptotic rank) the support functionals and quantum functionals, coincide
[
CVZ21
]
.
The quantum functionals are given by the maximum value of certain entropic quantities over the moment polytope of a tensor, whereas the support functionals are given by optimizing the same entropic quantities over all distributions on supports that a tensor can attain upon basis changes.
It is not known whether the quantum and support functionals agree for non-free tensors.
Better understanding of the class of non-free tensors can help settle this question, and in particular explicitly constructed non-free tensors could potentially be used for separations.
We do note that the non-free tensors we construct do not appear to yield such a separation, based on numerical evidence for
n=3,4
.

Moment map and moment polytopes

Notation

For natural numbers
n\geq 1
we write
[n]=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
.
We abbreviate
\GL_{n}=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.
We will work with tensors in
\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{c}
for
a,b,c\geq 1
.
There are a number of natural groups acting on this vector space.
For us of primary importance are products of the general linear group
\GL_{a}\times\GL_{b}\times\GL_{c}
, which we will denote by
GL
when
a,b,c
are clear from context, and its subgroup
\U\coloneqq\U_{a}\times\U_{b}\times\U_{c}\subseteq\GL
consisting triples of unitary matrices.
The action of
(A,B,C)\in\textnormal{GL}
on
T\in\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{c}
is given by

(A,B,C)\cdot T\coloneqq(A\otimes B\otimes C)T.

The space of Hermitian
a\times a
matrices is denoted by
\Herm_{a}
, and we write

\Herm_{a,b,c}\coloneqq\Herm_{a}\times\Herm_{b}\times\Herm_{c}.

Moment map and moment polytopes

We now discuss the moment map associated to the action of
GL
on tensors.
We begin with the geometric characterization of the moment map (see also
[
BFG+18
,
BFG+19
]
for an alternative introduction).
Recall that we defined the subgroup of unitary matrices as
\textnormal{U}=\U_{a}\times\U_{b}\times\U_{c}\subseteq\textnormal{GL}
.
We will write
V=\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{c}
, and endow it with the
U
-invariant inner product
\langle T,S\rangle=\sum_{i\in[a],j\in[b],k\in[c]}\overline{T_{i,j,k}}S_{i,j,k}
(note that our inner products are
\mathbb{C}
-linear in the second argument, and conjugate-linear in the first argument).

Definition 2.1

(Moment map for tensors)
.

The moment map
\mu\colon V\setminus\{0\}\to\Herm_{a,b,c}
is defined as follows.
Consider
T\in V\setminus\{0\}
as a map
T_{1}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{a})^{*}\to\mathbb{C}^{b}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{c}
, and identify
(\mathbb{C}^{a})^{*}\cong\mathbb{C}^{a}
using the standard (complex) inner product, and similar for
\mathbb{C}^{b}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{c}
.
Then

\mu_{1}(T)=\frac{1}{\lVert T\rVert^{2}}T_{1}^{*}T_{1}^{\phantom{*}}\in\mathbb{C}^{a\times a},\qquad\text{i.e.}\quad\mu_{1}(T)_{i,\ell}=\frac{1}{\lVert T\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j\in[b],k\in[c]}\overline{T_{i,j,k}}T_{\ell,j,k},

(2.1)

and similarly for the other two components.

Remark 2.2
.

The moment map is invariant under non-zero scalar multiplication of the argument: for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
,
\mu(\lambda T)=\mu(T)
.
Therefore
\mu
also induces a well-defined map
\mu\colon\mathbb{P}(V)\to\Herm_{a,b,c}
on the projective space
\mathbb{P}(V)
consisting of lines through the origin in
V
.

Remark 2.3
.

The moment map as defined above is a moment map in the sense of symplectic geometry for the
\U
-action on
\mathbb{P}(V)
, see e.g.
[
NM84
,
BGO+18
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For every
(A,B,C)\in\textnormal{U}
,
\mu\big((A,B,C)\cdot T\big)=(A\mu_{1}(T)A^{-1},B\mu_{2}(T)B^{-1},C\mu_{3}(T)C^{-1})
.

Proof.

The first component of
\mu
is given by
T_{1}^{*}T_{1}/\lVert T\rVert^{2}
.
Clearly
\lVert T\rVert^{2}
is
U
-invariant, and
((A,B,C)T)_{1}=(B\otimes C)T_{1}A^{*}
, so that

((A,B,C)T)_{1}^{*}((A,B,C)T)_{1}=AT_{1}^{*}(B\otimes C)^{*}(B\otimes C)T_{1}A^{*}=AT_{1}^{*}T_{1}A^{-1}

since
A,B,C
are unitary matrices.
The proof is analogous for the other components of
\mu
.
∎

We now state a connection between freeness of tensors and the moment map.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
Sja98
, Lem. 7.1]
,
[
Fra02
, Prop. 2.2]
)
.

Suppose

is a tensor with free support.
Then
\mu(T)
is a tuple of diagonal matrices.

Proof.

We establish that
\mu_{1}(T)
is diagonal, the proof for
\mu_{2}(T)
and
\mu_{3}(T)
is analogous.
Observe that for
i,j\in[a]
, expanding
Eq.

2.1
yields

(\mu_{1}(T))_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\lVert T\rVert^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{b}\sum_{\ell=1}^{c}\overline{T_{i,k,\ell}}T_{j,k,\ell}.

If
T
has free support, then
\overline{T_{i,k,\ell}}T_{j,k,\ell}=0
whenever
i\neq j
, because then
(i,k,\ell)
and
(j,k,\ell)
differ in exactly one coordinate.
This shows that
(\mu_{1}(T))_{i,j}=0
whenever
i\neq j
, implying
\mu_{1}(T)
is a diagonal matrix.
∎

q=(u_{n}\mid u_{n}\mid u_{n})+c\frac{h}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}.

(5.3)

For any vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we say that
v
is non-negative if
v_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in[d]
, we say that
v
is positive if
v_{i}>0
for all
i\in[d]
, and we say that
v
is non-increasing if
v_{1}\geq v_{2}\geq\cdots\geq v_{d}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
.

(1)

The vector
q=(q_{1}\mid q_{2}\mid q_{3})
is the orthogonal projection of
(u_{n}\mid u_{n}\mid u_{n})
onto the hyperplane
\{p\mid\left\langle\ignorespaces p,h\right\rangle=c\}
.

(2)

For every
i\in[3]
, the vector
q_{i}
is non-negative and non-increasing, with
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(q_{i})_{j}=1
.

Proof.

(1)
The vector
h
is a normal vector for the hyperplane, so the orthogonal projection adds a scalar multiple of
h
.
We see that
\langle(u_{n}\mid u_{n}\mid u_{n}),h\rangle=\langle u_{n},h_{1}\rangle+\langle u_{n},h_{2}\rangle+\langle u_{n},h_{3}\rangle=0
, since each
h_{i}
has entries summing to
0
. Therefore,
q=(u_{n}\mid u_{n}\mid u_{n})+c\frac{h}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}
.

(2) For every
i\in[3]
,
q_{i}
is sorted non-increasingly, because
u_{n}
and
h_{i}
are, and
c=1/n\geq 0
.
Therefore we just have to establish that the last entry of
q_{i}
is non-negative.
Note that
(h_{i})_{n}\geq-\lVert h_{i}\rVert\geq-\lVert h\rVert
.
Therefore,

(q_{i})_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(1+\frac{(h_{i})_{n}}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}\Bigr)\geq\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\lVert h\rVert}\Bigr).

(5.4)

We also have

\lVert h_{1}\rVert^{2}=\lVert h_{2}\rVert^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{n-1-2k}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{12}n(n^{2}-1),

and
\lVert h_{3}\rVert^{2}=(1/n)^{2}(n-1)+((1/n)-1)^{2}=(n-1)/n
.
Therefore,
\lVert h\rVert^{2}=n(n^{2}-1)/6+(n-1)/n
, and then from
n\geq 2
it follows that
\lVert h\rVert\geq 1
.
Combining this with (
5.4
) gives
(q_{i})_{n}\geq 0
.
Lastly,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(q_{i})_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{n})_{j}+(h_{i})_{j}=1+0=1
for
i\in[3]
.
∎

The non-negativity and normalization in

in particular imply that

has the potential to appear in the image of the moment map
\mu
(recall that we identify points in

with triples of diagonal matrices).
These two lemmas will later be used through the following corollary, allowing us to verify the conditions of

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
T\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
be such that
\supp(T)\subseteq\Gamma_{n}
and
\mu(T)=q
. Then
q
is the minimum-norm point of
\Delta(T)
.

Proof.

First observe that, since
q\in\mathcal{D}
by
Lemma

5.3
and
\mu(T)=q
,
q\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mu(\overline{\textnormal{GL}\cdot[T]})=\Delta(T)
.

Next, we will show that for every
p\in\Delta(T)
,
\lVert p\rVert^{2}\geq\lVert q\rVert^{2}
.
We first compute

\lVert q\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert(u_{n}\mid u_{n}\mid u_{n})+c\frac{h}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert(u_{n}\mid u_{n}\mid u_{n})\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{c^{2}}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}=\frac{3}{n}+\frac{c^{2}}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}.

Now we aim to show that every
p\in\Delta(T)
satisfies
\lVert p\rVert^{2}\geq\lVert q\rVert^{2}
.
Every
p=(p_{1}\mid p_{2}\mid p_{3})\in\Delta(T)
satisfies
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(p_{i})_{j}=1
for
i\in[3]
, i.e.,
\langle p_{i},u_{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{n}
.
There exists a unique decomposition
p_{i}=p_{i}^{\top}+p_{i}^{\perp}
where
p_{i}^{\perp}\perp u_{n}
for every
i\in[3]
and
p_{i}^{\top}=\langle p_{i},u_{n}\rangle u_{n}/\lVert u_{n}\rVert^{2}
; then

\lVert p_{i}\rVert^{2}=\lVert p_{i}^{\top}\rVert^{2}+\lVert p_{i}^{\perp}\rVert^{2}=\frac{\lvert\langle p_{i},u_{n}\rangle\rvert^{2}}{\lVert u_{n}\rVert^{2}}+\lVert p_{i}^{\perp}\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{n}+\lVert p_{i}^{\perp}\rVert^{2}.

We also have the inequality
\langle p,h\rangle\geq c
for every
p\in\Delta(T)
(
Lemma

5.2
). Since
h=(h_{1}\mid h_{2}\mid h_{3})
is such that
h_{i}\perp u_{n}
for every

, we find

c^{2}\leq\lvert\langle p,h\rangle\rvert^{2}=\lvert\langle p^{\perp},h\rangle\rvert^{2}\leq\lVert p^{\perp}\rVert^{2}\lVert h\rVert^{2},

where the inequality is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
Therefore for every
p\in\Delta(T)
,

\lVert p\rVert^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lVert p_{i}\rVert^{2}=\lVert p^{\perp}\rVert^{2}+\frac{3}{n^{2}\lVert u_{n}\rVert^{2}}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}+\frac{3}{n^{2}\lVert u_{n}\rVert^{2}}=\frac{c^{2}}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}+\frac{3}{n}=\lVert q\rVert^{2},

establishing that
q
is the minimum-norm point of
\Delta(T)
.
∎

Technical properties

Before we begin with the proof proper, we first establish various properties of the

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
n\geq 2
.

(1)

For
j\in[n-1]
we have
b_{j}>0
, and
b_{n}=0
.

(2)

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}b_{j}=(q_{3})_{n}
.

(3)

Writing
b_{0}=0
, we have
(q_{2})_{j}-b_{j}=(q_{1})_{n+1-j}-b_{j-1}
for
j\in[n]
.

(4)

For
j\in[n]
,
0\leq(q_{2})_{j}-b_{j}<\frac{1-(q_{3})_{n}}{n-1}
.

Proof.

(1)
Recall that
q=(u_{n}\mid u_{n}\mid u_{n})+c\frac{h}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}

where
c=\frac{1}{n}
and
h=(h_{1}\mid h_{2}\mid h_{3})
is as defined in
Eq.

5.2
.
Therefore
b_{j}\geq 0
reduces to

\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(h_{2})_{\ell}-(h_{1})_{n+1-\ell}\geq 0

for
j\in[n]
.
This quickly follows from
h_{1}=h_{2}=(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}-1,\dotsc,-\frac{n-1}{2})
: we have
\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(h_{2})_{\ell}\geq 0
and
\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(h_{2})_{\ell}=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(h_{1})_{n+1-\ell}
.
The inequality also becomes strict when
j\in[n-1]
.

(2)
Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}b_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(h_{2})_{\ell}-(h_{1})_{n+1-\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\big(n-1-2(\ell-1)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j(n-j)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{6}(n+1)n(n-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}\Big(\lVert h\rVert^{2}-\frac{n-1}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{c}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}(h_{3})_{n}=(q_{3})_{n}

using that
\lVert h\rVert^{2}=n(n^{2}-1)/6+(n-1)/n
and
(h_{3})_{n}=1/n-1
.

(3)
The equality

b_{j}-b_{j-1}=(q_{2})_{j}-(q_{1})_{n+1-j}

.

Finally we prove the bounds on
(q_{2})_{j}-b_{j}
; first we evaluate

\displaystyle(q_{2})_{j}-b_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\frac{\frac{n-1}{2}-2(j-1)}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}((q_{2})_{\ell}-(q_{1})_{n+1-\ell})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\frac{\frac{n-1}{2}-2(j-1)}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}j(n-j)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\frac{\frac{n-1}{2}-2(j-1)-j(n-j)}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}.

(4) We now prove
(q_{2})_{j}-b_{j}\geq 0
.
It suffices to have

\frac{n-1}{2}-2(j-1)-j(n-j)\geq-\lVert h\rVert^{2}=-\left(\frac{1}{6}(n+1)n(n-1)+\frac{n-1}{n}\right).

The left-hand side is minimized for

where it attains the value
\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{4}(n+1)^{2}
.
Rewriting the above shows that the inequality is equivalent to

\frac{n^{4}}{6}-\frac{n^{3}}{4}-\frac{2n^{2}}{3}+\frac{3n}{2}-1\geq 0.

Equality holds for
n=2

and

\frac{2m^{3}}{3}+\frac{m^{2}}{4}-\frac{17m}{12}+\frac{3}{4}

which is non-negative for

\frac{2m^{3}}{3}\geq\frac{17m}{12}

(q_{2})_{j}-b_{j}\geq 0
.

Lastly we show
(q_{2})_{j}-b_{j}<\frac{1-(q_{3})_{n}}{n-1}

Recall that
(q_{3})_{n}=\frac{1}{n}+c\frac{(h_{3})_{n}}{\lVert h\rVert^{2}}=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1/n-1}{n\lVert h\rVert^{2}}

Lemma 5.8
.

Let

(2)

given by

for
i\in[n]
.

In particular, (2) implies that

has diagonal entries given by

(\lambda,\dotsc,\lambda,0)
since

are isospectral.

Proof.

Let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be given by
w_{i}=\sqrt{\lambda-d_{i}}

Note that

: not all

can be
\lambda

0\leq d_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=(n-1)\lambda
.
Let
H=\lambda I_{n}-ww^{*}

Then

is Hermitian, and its

\lambda-(\lambda-d_{i})=d_{i}
.
Moreover
\lVert w\rVert^{2}=n\lambda-\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=\lambda
, and so the eigenvalues of
H
are given by
\lambda
with multiplicity
n-1
and
0
with multiplicity
1
.
The kernel is spanned by
w
.
Let
w^{1},\dotsc,w^{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an orthonormal basis of the orthogonal complement of
w
.
Set
W=\sqrt{\lambda}[w^{1}\mid\dotsc\mid w^{n-1}]
.
Then
W^{*}W=\lambda I_{n-1}
by virtue of orthogonality of the

, and
H=\lambda I_{n}-ww^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda w^{j}(w^{j})^{*}=WW^{*}
.

Remark 5.9
.

The existence of
W
as in
Lemma

5.8
is a special case of the Schur–Horn theorem
[
Hor54
]
.
It states that there exists a
n\times n
Hermitian matrix
H
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dotsc\geq\lambda_{n}
and diagonal entries
d_{1}\geq\dotsc\geq d_{n}
if and only if
(d_{1},\dotsc,d_{n})
is majorised by
(\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{n})
, that is

\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}.

In our setting we need an
H=WW^{*}
with diagonal entries
(d_{1},\dotsc,d_{n})
and eigenvalues
(\lambda,\dotsc,\lambda,0)
.
Here, a solution can also be explicitly constructed.
It is not known how to do this (efficiently) in general.

Lemma 5.11

For every

, we have
\mu(T^{W})=q
, and
q
is the minimum-norm point of
\Delta(T^{W})
.

Proof.

Observe first that
\lVert T^{W}\rVert=\lVert W\rVert^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lvert a_{i}\rvert^{2}=1-(q_{3})_{n}+(q_{3})_{n}=1
, where the second inequality follows from
\lvert a_{i}\rvert^{2}=b_{i}
and
Lemma

5.7
(2)
.
The support of
T^{W}
is contained in
\Gamma_{n}
.
The definition of the moment map (
Eq.

2.1
) implies that

\mu_{3}(T^{W})=W^{*}W\oplus[(q_{3})_{n}]=\diag(q_{3}).

It remains to check that
\mu_{1}(T^{W})=\diag(q_{1})
and
\mu_{2}(T^{W})=\diag(q_{2})
.
The support structure and
Eq.

2.1
already imply that
\mu_{1}(T^{W})
and
\mu_{2}(T^{W})
are diagonal matrices, so it remains to check that the diagonal entries are correct.
To obtain
\mu_{2}(T^{W})=\diag(q_{2})
, recall that its
j
-th diagonal entry is the squared
2
-norm of the
j
-th row of
T^{W}
, so

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(T^{W})_{j,j}=\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\lvert T^{W}_{i,j,k}\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lvert a_{j}\rvert^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lvert W_{j,k}\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lvert a_{j}\rvert^{2}+(WW^{*})_{j,j}=(q_{2})_{j},

by definition of
WW^{*}
and
\lvert a_{j}\rvert^{2}=b_{j}
.

Next, the
i
-th diagonal entry of
\mu_{1}(T^{W})
is the squared
2
-norm of the
i
-th slice of
T^{W}
, hence given by

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\lvert T^{W}_{i,j,k}\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lvert a_{n-i}\rvert^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lvert W_{(n+1-i),k}\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lvert a_{n-i}\rvert^{2}+(WW^{*})_{(n+1-i),(n+1-i)}

\displaystyle=(q_{1})_{i}.

where we note that we have set
a_{0}=0
.
For the last equality we used that

(WW^{*})_{(n+1-i),(n+1-i)}=(q_{2})_{n+1-i}-\lvert a_{n+1-i}\rvert^{2}=(q_{1})_{i}-\lvert a_{n-i}\rvert^{2}

by
Lemma

5.7
(3)
and
\lvert a_{j}\rvert^{2}=b_{j}
.

Finally, since
T^{W}
has support contained in
\Gamma_{n}
and
\mu(T^{W})=q
,
Corollary

5.4
implies that
q
is the minimum-norm point of
\Delta(T^{W})
.
∎

Non-freeness of the tensor

We now prove that
T^{W}
is not free.

Lemma 5.12
.

For every
W\in\mathcal{W}_{n}
, the tensor
T^{W}
is not free.

Proof.

Suppose
T^{W}
is free.
As
\mu(T^{W})=q
is the minimum-norm point of
\Delta(T^{W})
by
Lemma

5.11
,

Theorem

1.4
implies that there exists
k\in\U_{n}\times\U_{n}\times\U_{n}
such that
k\cdot T^{W}
has free support.
Therefore every component of
\mu(k\cdot T^{W})
is diagonal by
Lemma

2.5
.

We now further simplify
k
.
As
\mu
is equivariant with respect to the unitary group action, the components of
\mu(k\cdot T^{W})
and
\mu(T^{W})
are isospectral; but both are tuples of diagonal matrices, hence the diagonal matrices are related by permutations.
By additionally acting with some
\sigma\in(S_{n})^{3}\subseteq\U_{n}^{3}
, we may assume
\sigma k\cdot\mu(T^{W})=\mu(\sigma k\cdot T^{W})=\mu(T^{W})
.
We note here that local permutations do not affect freeness.
In other words, without loss of generality we may assume that
k
is in the stabilizer of
\mu(T^{W})
.
Now we use that
\mu(T^{W})=q
: both
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are strictly decreasing, and
q_{3}
is of the form
(\frac{1-(q_{3})_{n}}{n-1},\dotsc,\frac{1-(q_{3})_{n}}{n-1},(q_{3})_{n})
with
(q_{3})_{n}<\frac{1}{n}
.
This implies that the stabilizer is given by the subgroup
\U_{1}^{n}\times\U_{1}^{n}\times(\U_{n-1}\times\U_{1})\subseteq\U_{n}^{3}
.
By further acting with an element of
\U_{1}^{n}\times\U_{1}^{n}\times(\{I_{n-1}\}\times\U_{1})
, we may assume
k
only acts with some unitary
u\in\U_{n-1}
on the first
n-1
columns of
T^{W}
; this again does not affect the freeness of the support of
T^{W}
.
Then we have

k\cdot T^{W}=T^{Wu^{\top}}

where
Wu^{\top}
is the usual matrix product of
W
with the transpose of
u
.
Next, note that
Wu^{\top}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}
as
\bar{u}W^{*}Wu^{\top}=\frac{1-(q_{3})_{n}}{n-1}\bar{u}u^{\top}=\frac{1-(q_{3})_{n}}{n-1}I_{n-1}=W^{*}W
and
Wu^{\top}\bar{u}W^{*}=WW^{*}
. Here
\bar{u}
denotes the entrywise complex conjugate of
u
.
Therefore we may as well assume that we initially chose
W
such that
T^{W}
has free support.

The matrix
W
contains at least one non-zero element in every column, as
W^{*}W
has non-zero diagonal entries.
Since
W
has
n-1\geq 2
columns, there exist
i,i^{\prime}
such that
W_{i,1}\neq 0
and
W_{i^{\prime},2}\neq 0
.
In order for
T^{W}
to have free support, it must be the case that every row of
W
contains at most one non-zero element.
Therefore
i\neq i^{\prime}
, and the
i
-th and
i^{\prime}
-th rows of

have zero inner product.
But every off-diagonal entry of
WW^{*}=\frac{1-(q_{3})_{n}}{n-1}I_{n}-ww^{*}
is non-zero by virtue of

being entrywise positive (
Lemma

5.7
(4)
); in particular, the inner product between the
i
-th and
i^{\prime}
-th row cannot be zero.
This is a contradiction, and we conclude that
T^{W}
is not free.
∎

We are now in a position to prove
Theorem

5.6
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.6
.

There exists
W\in\mathcal{W}_{n}
by
Lemma

5.10
.
The tensor
T^{W}
is not free by
Lemma

5.12
.
The conciseness of

follows from

5.11

has no zero entries; combined with the definition of

has full-rank flattenings, that is,

is concise.
This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
General moment polytope criterion for non-freeness

We discuss here a general version of the criterion introduced in
Section

3
.
For a general overview of the terminology used here, we refer to
[
Wal17
,
GRS21
]
.
Let
G
be a connected complex reductive group,
K
a maximal compact subgroup, and suppose
\pi\colon G\to\GL(V)
is a rational representation of
G
on a finite-dimensional complex vector space
V
.
We will write
g\cdot v:=\pi(g)v
for
g\in G
and
v\in V
.

Assume
V
is endowed with a
K
-invariant inner product.
We denote the Lie algebra of
K
(resp.,
G
) by
\Lie(K)
(resp.,
\Lie(G)
), and assume that
\Lie(K)
is endowed with a (real) inner product invariant under the adjoint action of
K
.
Such an inner product can be extended to a complex inner product on
\Lie(G)
by using the decomposition
\Lie(G)=\Lie(K)\oplus i\Lie(K)
, which we will denote by
\left\langle\ignorespaces\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
.
The space of linear maps
\Lie(G)\to\mathbb{C}
is denoted by
\Lie(G)^{*}
.

The action of
G
on
V
induces a Lie algebra action of
\Lie(G)
on
V
, given by

X\star v:=\partial_{t=0}(\exp(tX)\cdot v)

for
v\in V
and
X\in\Lie(G)
. Here
\exp\colon\Lie(G)\to G
is the exponential map for
G
.

We define the moment map in this general setting as follows.
For
v\in V\setminus\{0\}
, consider the Kempf–Ness function
F_{v}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
F_{v}(g)=\log\lVert\pi(g)v\rVert
.

Definition A.1

(Moment map)
.

The moment map
\mu\colon V\setminus\{0\}\to i\Lie(K)

is defined as the unique element of
i\Lie(K)
satisfying for all
X\in i\Lie(K)

\left\langle\ignorespaces\mu(v),X\right\rangle=\partial_{t=0}F_{v}(\exp(tX))=\frac{\left\langle\ignorespaces v,X\star v\right\rangle}{\lVert v\rVert^{2}}.

Remark A.2
.

As the inner product on
V
is
K
-invariant, the Kempf–Ness function is also invariant:
F_{v}(kg)=F_{v}(g)
for all
k\in K
and
g\in G
.
As a consequence,
\partial_{t=0}F_{v}(\exp(tX))
vanishes for
X\in\Lie(K)\subseteq\Lie(G)
.

Remark A.3
.

The moment map as defined in
Definition

A.1
yields a moment map in the sense of symplectic geometry for the
K
-action on
\mathbb{P}(V)
(endowed with the Fubini–Study form), see
[
NM84
, Cor. 1.2.1]
.

Let
D_{K}\subseteq K
be a maximal torus
4
4

4
