Association between serum uric acid to albumin ratio and all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with sepsis: a retrospective study
Authors: Jinhui Zhang, Yang Liang, Chao Song, Zhenkui Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe serum uric acid to albumin ratio (UAR) is a recently recognized composite inflammatory biomarker that has not received sufficient attention in relation to sepsis. This study aimed to investigate the association between UAR and the risk of all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with sepsis.MethodsWe conducted an analysis using a retrospective database of sepsis patients who were admitted to the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University between January 2015 and November 2023. The patients were divided into four groups based on UAR quartiles. The primary outcome was in-hospital all-cause mortality. The Kaplan-Meier curve and log-rank tests were used to evaluate all-cause mortality among the four groups. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis and restricted cubic splines were performed to examine the association between UAR and clinical outcomes in patients with sepsis. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the impact of other variables on the relationship between UAR and all-cause mortality.ResultsA total of 1123 patients (63.0% males) were included in this study. The in-hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) mortality rates were 33.9% and 31.7%, respectively. There was a significant association between higher UAR levels and an increased cumulative incidence of 30-/60-day mortality (log-rank test, P < 0.001). Cox proportional hazards regression analysis demonstrated that the UAR was independently associated with a higher risk of in-hospital mortality [per SD increase in the UAR: hazard ratio (HR) (95%CI): 1.186 (1.067–1.319); P = 0.002] and ICU mortality [per SD increase in the UAR: HR (95%CI): 1.190 (1.068–1.327); P = 0.002]. Restricted cubic spline regression analysis illustrated a linear relationship between UAR and the risks of in-hospital and ICU mortality.ConclusionsElevated UAR levels were strongly associated with all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with sepsis, suggesting their potential as predictors of poor outcomes. However, further studies are needed to validate our findings and investigate the underlying causal factors that could contribute to sepsis prevention and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12134-4.Keywords: Sepsis, Uric acid, Albumin, Uric acid to albumin ratio, All-cause mortality

Introduction

Sepsis, which refers to organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated immune response to infection, impacts about one in three patients in intensive care units (ICUs) [1, 2]. It leads to 11 million deaths each year, accounting for 20% of all fatalities. Besides its high mortality rate, sepsis also causes significant short- and long-term morbidity and is linked to higher resource utilization and healthcare costs [3–5]. Despite extensive research efforts, sepsis remains one of the difficult challenges for the healthcare system [6]. Thus, identifying the risk factors associated with sepsis outcomes is essential for implementing early prevention strategies.

Currently, utilizing a combination of multiple rapidly available and reliable biomarkers is considered more clinically significant for diagnosing sepsis than relying on a single biomarker [7–9]. Growing evidence suggests that inflammation and oxidative stress play crucial roles in the progression of sepsis [10, 11]. Uric acid, which is the final product of purine metabolism by xanthine oxidase in liver cells, can act as an antioxidant at physiological concentration, whereas hyperuricemia has been associated with endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and oxidative stress [12, 13]. Numerous studies found that elevated serum uric acid, as an inflammatory mediator, independently predicted poor outcomes in sepsis patients [14, 15]. Additionally, albumin (Alb) possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antithrombotic properties, and decreased serum Alb levels have been associated with an increased inflammatory response [16, 17]. Several studies indicated that lower levels of Alb upon admission were linked to a higher risk of adverse outcomes in patients with sepsis [18, 19].

The uric acid to albumin ratio (UAR) is a novel metric that combines measurements of uric acid and Alb to provide an indication of the inflammatory and oxidative stress status in the body. This metric has been recognized as an innovative inflammatory marker that can predict adverse cardiovascular events and was associated with mortality in patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) [20, 21]. However, there is currently a lack of research investigating the relationship between UAR and sepsis outcomes. Therefore, our study aimed to investigate whether UAR could serve as a predictive tool for assessing the risk of all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with sepsis.

Methods

Study population

A retrospective study was conducted on a total of 1269 patients with sepsis admitted to the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University between January 2015 and November 2023. The inclusion criteria for this study were patients diagnosed with sepsis according to the sepsis 3.0 diagnostic criteria, combined with infection and a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of ≥ 2 [22]. In cases where patients had multiple hospital admissions, only the initial admission was considered for this study. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) patients under 18 years old; (2) patients who stayed in the ICU for less than 24 h; (3) patients with chronic kidney disease, hepatic cirrhosis, or hyperuricemia. (4) patients deemed comfort care, palliative care, or hospice. Ultimately, a total of 1123 patients were enrolled in this study and divided into four groups based on the quartiles of the UAR. The detailed inclusion and exclusion process was illustrated in Fig. 1. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for their participation in this study. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University (No. KY2023K1007).

Variable extraction

Data regarding age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, and SOFA score were extracted from the electronic medical recording system for the first 24 h of ICU admission. Other relevant information, such as comorbidities, infection pathogens, laboratory test, treatments, and clinical outcomes, were also collected. With the exception of uric acid and Alb, the lowest values of the mentioned laboratory markers within 24 h of ICU admission were recorded. Follow-up for each patient began on the date of ICU admission and ended on the date of death or discharge. The UAR was calculated using the following formula: [uric acid (µmol/L)] / [albumin (g/L)]. The average values of uric acid and Alb within the first 24 h after ICU admission were used in the calculation of UAR. Subsequently, all patients were divided into four categories based on their UAR quartile range: Quartile 1 group (UAR ≤ 6.60, ≤ 25th), Quartile 2 group (6.60 < UAR ≤ 10.23, 25th-50th), Quartile 3 group (10.23 < UAR ≤ 14.85, 50th-75th), and Quartile 4 group (UAR > 14.85, > 75th).

Primary outcome and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome measure for this study was all-cause mortality during the hospital stay. This included deaths that occurred in the ICU as well as on the general ward. The secondary outcomes assessed in the study were the occurrence of AKI and length of stay in ICU and hospital. AKI was defined according to the guidelines provided by Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [23].

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0, GraphPad Prism 10.0, MedCalc 20 and R software version 4.1.3. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), while those without a normal distribution were expressed as median and interquartile range [M (Q1, Q3)]. Variables with a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance were compared between the two groups using Student’s t-test; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U-test was used. Categorical data were presented as total number and composition ratio [n (%)], and differences between groups were analyzed. Spearman’s analysis was performed to assess correlations between the UAR and severity of illness scores. Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis, classified by UAR quartiles, was conducted followed by the log-rank test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of the UAR for all-cause mortality. To evaluate influencing factors related to the risk of all-cause death, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed. Confounding variables included variables selected based on p value < 0.05 in univariate analysis, and clinically relevant and prognosis-associated variables were also enrolled in the multivariate model. Covariates were included in the models in three stages: Model 1 included only the UAR without any adjustments. In Model 2, age, gender, BMI, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil (Neu), lymphocyte (Lym), platelet (PLT), C-reactive protein (CRP), and lactate were included as adjustment variables. The fully adjusted model (Model 3) included all adjustment variables, including age, gender, BMI, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, WBC, Neu, Lym, PLT, CRP, creatinine, D-dimer, lactate, APACHE II score, and SOFA score. The UAR was included in separate regression equations as both categorical variables and continuous variables, and was converted to a z-score to determine the increase in the risk of the outcome per standard deviation (SD) increase. A restricted cubic spline analysis with four knots positioned at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles was conducted to explore potential nonlinear relationships between UAR and in-hospital mortality and ICU mortality. Subgroup analysis was performed based on age (≤ 65 or > 65), gender (male or female), smoking (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), diabetes (yes or no), BMI (< 24.0 or ≥ 24.0), and AKI (yes or no). P-values for interaction were calculated to assess the effect of each subgroup on the outcome. Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was used for association between UAR with length of hospital stay and length of ICU stay.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In this study, a total of 1123 critically ill patients with sepsis were enrolled. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were listed in Table 1. The study included 707 males (63.0%) and 416 females (37.0%), with a median age of 75 (IQR: 65–84) years. The median levels of uric acid and Alb were 286.9 (IQR: 192.3-411.7) µmol/L and 28.2 (IQR: 24.2–33.2) g/L, respectively. The median UAR was 10.23 (IQR: 6.60-14.85). The in-hospital and ICU mortality rates were 33.9% and 31.7%, respectively.

Patients in the UAR Q4 group were compared to those in the other groups, revealing that the former had a higher prevalence of diabetes and coronary artery disease. Additionally, patients with higher UAR levels exhibited elevated WBC, Neu, CRP, total bilirubin (Tbil), alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), glucose, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid, D-dimer, potassium, and lactate. UAR and in-hospital and ICU mortality

The UAR levels in the non-survivor group were significantly higher than those in the survivor group (12.04 vs. 9.46, P < 0.001). The distribution of UAR stratified by the mortality status of in-hospital death and ICU death was shown in Figure S2. The Kaplan-Meier curve analysis demonstrated a significant difference among various UAR groups for 30-day (log-rank P < 0.001, Fig. 3A) and 60-day mortality (log-rank P < 0.001, Fig. 3B). As shown in Figure S3, the ROC analysis indicated that the AUC of the UAR for predicting in-hospital mortality and ICU mortality was 0.658 (95%CI: 0.624–0.693) and 0.648 (95%CI: 0.613–0.683), respectively. Furthermore, the results demonstrated that the UAR outperformed uric acid (P for comparison = 0.017 for in-hospital death; = 0.024 for ICU death) and Alb (P for comparison < 0.001 for in-hospital death; < 0.001 for ICU death) when predicting both outcomes. To identify prognostic predictors in patients with sepsis, we conducted univariate Cox regression analysis (Table S2). Variables that showed significance in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05) or were potentially associated with clinical outcomes were included as independent variables for Cox regression analysis. In three multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, the UAR showed a significant and positive correlation with in-hospital death and ICU death, whether increased by 1 unit or 1 SD (Table 2). The UAR was further categorized into quartiles, with the Q1 group serving as the reference to evaluate the association between other quantile groups and in-hospital and ICU mortality. Patients in the highest quartile groups were significantly associated with a higher risk of hospital death in the three established Cox proportional hazards models: unadjusted model [HR (95%CI): 2.279 (1.728–3.006); P < 0.001], partly adjusted model [HR (95%CI): 1.839 (1.371–2.465); P < 0.001], and fully adjusted model [HR (95%CI): 1.665 (1.199–2.314); P = 0.002], compared to patients in the lower group (Table 2). Similar results were observed in the multivariate Cox proportional risk analysis of the UAR and ICU mortality (Table 2). Additionally, the risk of in-hospital mortality and ICU mortality demonstrated a consistent upward trend with increasing UAR, with all trend p-values below 0.05 (Fig. 4A and B). Moreover, the restricted cubic spline regression model revealed that the risks of in-hospital mortality and ICU mortality increased linearly with increasing UAR (Fig. 5A and B).

Subgroup analysis

To assess the potential correlation between UAR and in-hospital mortality, our study performed subgroup analysis (Table 3). Patients were categorized based on age (≤ 65 or > 65), gender (male or female), smoking (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), diabetes (yes or no), BMI (< 24.0 or ≥ 24.0), and AKI (yes or no). There was no statistically significant interaction between age, gender, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, AKI and UAR (all P-values for interaction ≥ 0.05). Interestingly, the UAR seemed to have more prominence of its predictive value in patients with BMI ≥ 24.0 kg/m2 [HR (95%CI) with BMI ≥ 24.0 kg/m2 1.074(1.049–1.098) vs. with BMI < 24.0 kg/m2 1.032(1.018–1.047), P for interaction = 0.007]. Similar results were obtained in stratified analyses of the UAR and ICU death (Table 4).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to examine the association between UAR and all-cause mortality in sepsis patients. The primary findings of this study indicated that elevated UAR levels may serve as independent risk factors for all-cause mortality in sepsis patients, even after accounting for various clinical and laboratory variables. Therefore, UAR had the potential to be a promising decision-making tool for clinicians and its assessment could refine prognosis evaluation for these patients.

Sepsis is characterized by various pathophysiological features, including neutrophil extracellular trap formation, formation of inflammatory bodies, cytokine-induced cell death, apoptosis, and endothelial cell dysfunction [24–26]. Although the exact mechanisms underlying sepsis are not yet fully understood, its progression is associated with an imbalance between proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses [27, 28]. Numerous studies had reported that inflammatory factors were the most prominent markers throughout the progression of sepsis [10, 29]. Li et al. demonstrated that elevated CRP levels independently predicted the severity of neonatal sepsis [30]. Moreover, oxidative stress is recognized as playing a significant role in the pathophysiology of sepsis [31–34]. Oxidative stress activation results in the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), along with the recruitment of inflammatory cytokines, which contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction in cells and subsequent multi-organ failure [35–37]. A large number of studies had indicated that oxidative stress occured during sepsis, characterized by decreased levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and increased levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) [31, 38].

Serum uric acid and Alb play significant roles in the inflammatory response and oxidative stress, and their involvement in the development and progression of sepsis is crucial [14, 18]. Uric acid, the final product of purine nucleotide breakdown in humans, is derived from both endogenous and exogenous sources [13]. As a cell signaling molecule, uric acid influences intracellular signal transduction by promoting the production of ROS and the expression of inflammatory mediators. This process leads to vascular damage, exacerbating the advancement of sepsis [39–41]. In a study involving 954 patients, hyperuricemia was associated with an increased risk of 90-day all-cause mortality in ICU patients with sepsis [15]. Alshehri et al. found that hyperuricemia in sepsis patients was associated with worse clinical outcomes such as higher ICU and hospital mortality [42]. Akbar et al. suggested that elevated serum uric acid levels were linked to unfavorable sepsis prognoses and increased the risk of AKI and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [43]. Another study demonstrated that elevated serum uric acid levels in sepsis patients also promoted the mobilization of endothelial progenitor cells, leading to AKI [44]. Furthermore, Chuang et al. discovered a positive correlation between elevated serum uric acid levels, total antioxidant capacity, and APACHE II scores in sepsis patients. This finding suggested that uric acid may significantly contribute to total antioxidant capacity, and hyperuricemia could potentially serve as an early predictive marker for poorer clinical outcomes in sepsis patients [41].

Alb, an essential protein within the body, performs various roles such as transportation and binding of ions, lipids, and metabolites. During an inflammatory response, Alb functions as a negative acute phase reactant, leading to reduced levels due to increased inflammation [45]. Additionally, Alb plays a vital role as an antioxidant by effectively neutralizing free radicals in the bloodstream, thereby reducing potential damage to the vascular endothelium [46]. Knoshita et al. demonstrated a negative correlation between serum Alb levels and oxidative stress [47]. Moreover, a retrospective cohort study indicated that Alb might predict the severity of the severity of abdominal sepsis in adult patients [48]. Sheng et al. showed that for sepsis patients with AKI undergoing CRRT, an increase of 1 g/dL in Alb level corresponded to a 25% and 27% reduction in mortality risk at 28 and 90 days, respectively [19]. Furthermore, Arnau-Barrés et al. found that strong predictors of mortality such as Alb could be a stronger predictor of mortality in elderly patients with sepsis [18]. Another study revealed that the level of serum Alb could serve as a marker of the higher risk of death in patients with prolonged sepsis [49].

UAR, a composite of serum uric acid and Alb, tends to rise with increasing uric acid or decreasing Alb, both of which are linked to elevated inflammation and oxidative stress. High uric acid levels had been correlated with increased levels of pro-inflammatory markers like hs-CRP [50]. Moreover, intracellular serum uric acid could trigger an inflammatory response by promoting the generation of reactive oxygen species and activating Cox-2 [51], and a reduced Alb levels suggested compromised immune regulation and heightened stress levels [52]. Therefore, we hypothesized that a higher UAR could potentially serve as a more effective risk assessment tool for sepsis. However, to date, no study had demonstrated the association of UAR with outcomes in sepsis patients. Various clinical studies have investigated the relationship between elevated UAR and increased morbidity and mortality in both the general population and other patient groups [53–55]. Chen et al. found that UAR may be a simple, cost-effective, and reliable predictive marker for all-cause and cardiovascular death in U.S. diabetic patients [56]. Kalkan et al. showed that UAR may be a prognostic factor for mortality in ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients and an easily accessible parameter to identify high-risk patients [57]. Meanwhile, Yin et al. reported that elevated UAR may be an independent and effective biomarker for predicting poorly-developed coronary collateral circulation development in non-ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) patients [58]. Özgür et al. observed a significantly higher mortality rate and shorter survival time in patients with AKI who had high UAR levels [21]. Wang et al. showed that UAR in patients with acute type A aortic dissection may be used as a new independent risk factor for long-term mortality [59]. Additionally, in patients after catheter ablation, UAR may serve as a promising predictor for atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence [60]. Another study involving 1893 participants suggested that increased UAR correlated with a heightened risk of short-term death or hospital readmission in Chinese individuals suffering from heart failure [61]. These findings indicate the potential association between UAR and clinical outcomes in critically ill patients. In our study of 1123 sepsis patients, we demonstrated that UAR can be used to assess the clinical value in predicting all-cause mortality. Although ROC curve analysis indicated that the UAR had a moderate predictive value for ICU or hospital mortality in sepsis patients, possibly due to insufficient sample size, we observed that the AUC of the UAR was superior to that of uric acid or Alb alone. Thus, we concluded that uric acid or Alb alone was weaker than UAR as predictors of all-cause mortality among sepsis patients. Furthermore, our findings demonstrated a positive correlation between UAR and disease severity scores, suggesting that UAR might reflect the severity of sepsis. In terms of in-hospital events, an elevated UAR proved to be a valuable inflammatory marker for evaluating the length of hospital stay in sepsis patients. These results indicated that UAR could be a more effective indicator to enhance clinicians’ capacity to predict outcomes for patients with sepsis. Therefore, routine assessment of UAR could play a role in reducing future major adverse clinical outcomes in critically ill patients.

In this study, we investigated the link between UAR and both in-hospital and ICU mortality within various subgroups. Our subgroup analysis indicated that the predictive value of UAR for in-hospital and ICU mortality did not statistical significance when stratified by age, gender, and smoking. Additionally, when categorizing by comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes, the findings remained statistically insignificant. It was possible that patients with these conditions received suitable treatments or embraced healthier lifestyle practices, which might reduce these risks. Furthermore, our study revealed that the predictive power of UAR appeared to be more pronounced in individuals with a BMI ≥ 24.0 kg/m2, suggesting that higher BMI could influence the predictive accuracy of UAR for all-cause mortality. This finding may be linked to the conventional understanding that higher BMI was generally considered an adverse prognostic factor in sepsis outcomes [62]. Another notable discovery from our research was the higher incidence of AKI in patients with elevated UAR levels, with a more substantial association between UAR and all-cause mortality observed in sepsis patients with AKI. It was imperative to closely monitor UAR indicators to prevent or ameliorate unfavorable outcomes in sepsis patients with AKI. Additionally, our study identified a significant linear correlation between UAR and in-hospital mortality or ICU mortality, suggesting that UAR could serve as a valuable tool for identifying high-risk mortality cases among critically ill sepsis patients. Consequently, monitoring UAR levels may aid healthcare providers in pinpointing patients with sepsis at heightened risk for poor outcomes, particularly within the ICU setting.

Our study presented several limitations. Firstly, it was important to note that this research was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. In order to validate the accuracy of our findings, conducting multicenter studies with larger sample sizes was necessary. Secondly, as an observational study conducted in a single center, we cannot definitively establish causality. Despite our efforts to control for confounding variables through multivariate adjustment and subgroup analyses, there may still be residual confounding factors that could potentially impact the prognosis. Thirdly, our study solely focused on investigating the predictive value of UAR upon admission, and the dynamic alteration of UAR over time remains unknown. Future studies should aim to explore the additional informative value of the UAR trajectory over time in predicting adverse outcomes. Lastly, the generalizability of our findings to other countries or populations was limited since the majority of our participants were from China. Further studies were warranted to validate our results in diverse populations and countries. Nonetheless, this study marked one of the initial attempts to evaluate the potential use of UAR as a biomarker for assessing the risk of adverse outcomes in sepsis patients, and the reliability of our findings had been confirmed through various regression models.

Conclusion

In our current study, we found a significant correlation between elevated UAR levels and increased all-cause mortality in sepsis patients. This suggested that UAR could serve as an easily measurable and appropriate serum biomarker for aiding early risk stratification and management decisions. It is important to emphasize that further validation of these findings is crucial, and additional cohort studies or randomized controlled trials are urgently required.
