Coordination-Driven Pyridine Capture in a Solid-State Zn(II) Complex for Rapid and Reversible Turn-On Vapoluminescence
Authors: Sotaro Kusumoto, Kazuki Nakahama, Kouta Karube, Yuudai Iwai, Ryo Ohtani, Fumiya Kobayashi, Kosaku Ohishi, Teruki Motohashi, Yang Kim, Yoshihiro Koide
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A Zn­(II) hydrazone complex undergoes reversible, coordination-driven pyridine capture in the solid state, producing rapid vapochromic and turn-on vapoluminescent responses. Interconversion between ZnL 2 and ZnL 2(Py) increases the photoluminescence quantum yield from 5.86% to 25.4% by inducing rigidity and suppressing nonradiative decay. This work demonstrates that reversible guest coordination is a powerful strategy for controlling solid-state luminescence and for designing VOC-responsive sensing materials.

Vapochromic and vapoluminescent materials have attracted considerable attention as environmentally relevant sensing platforms for volatile organic compounds (VOCs) because they exhibit reversible, visually detectable changes in color and/or emission upon exposure to vapor. , These responses have been widely reported in coordination systems based on Pt­(II), Au­(I), Ir­(III), Ru­(II), Pd­(II), Ni­(II), Cu­(I/II), , and Zn­(II). The underlying mechanisms typically involve modulation of intermolecular interactions, changes in metal–metal contacts, and/or structural reorganization of aggregated states, including crystal-to-crystal transformations and phase transitions.

Among VOCs, nitrogen-containing species such as pyridine are of particular concern because of their environmental and health impacts. In this context, coordination-driven capture offers a distinct advantage over second-sphere adsorption by providing stronger, potentially more selective binding, thereby enhancing sensitivity and stability. In addition, coordination-induced structural changes may amplify optical responses, including luminescence modulation. While vapor-induced quenching (“turn-off”) of emission is relatively common, systems exhibiting turn-on luminescence remain comparatively rare yet highly desirable for their superior signal-to-noise characteristics.

Herein, a Zn­(II) Schiff base complex incorporating a fluorene-based ligand (HL) is presented. It undergoes reversible coordination with pyridine in the solid state. This process induces a pronounced turn-on luminescence response, providing a rare example of coordination-driven emission enhancement and demonstrating a practical strategy for pyridine sensing.

The ligand HL was synthesized by condensing salicylhydrazide with 2-acetylfluorene in ethanol, yielding a yellow solid. Treatment of HL with ZnCl2 in methanol afforded ZnL 2 as a pale-yellow powder, in which the deprotonated ligand L –, (E)-2-(1-(9H-fluoren-3-yl)­ethylidene)-1-(2-hydroxybenzoyl)­hydrazin-1-ide, coordinates to Zn­(II) (Scheme ). In the presence of pyridine, an analogous reaction produced the pyridine-coordinated complex ZnL 2 (Py). Single-crystal X-ray diffraction of ZnL2 was not performed because suitable single crystals could not be obtained. Nevertheless, the PXRD pattern of ZnL 2 differs from that of the free ligand HL, confirming the formation of a distinct crystalline phase (Figure S1). Furthermore, the IR spectrum of ZnL 2 showed a pronounced shift of the CO stretching band from 1671 cm–1 in HL to approximately 1600 cm–1, providing further evidence for coordination of the hydrazone ligand to the Zn­(II) center (Figure S2). In contrast, suitable single crystals of ZnL2(Py) were obtained, and ZnL2(Py) crystallized in the monoclinic space group I2/a (Table S1). The Zn­(II) center adopts a discrete five-coordinate trigonal bipyramidal geometry, coordinated by two bidentate L – ligands through imine nitrogen and carbonyl oxygen donors and by one pyridine molecule. The axial positions are occupied by two imine nitrogen atoms, while the equatorial plane consists of two oxygen donors and the pyridine nitrogen. The O–Zn–O and N–Zn–O angles in the equatorial plane are 118.8° and 120.6°, respectively, consistent with a trigonal bipyramidal arrangement (Figure a). Within each L – ligand, two intramolecular hydrogen bonds form between the phenolic hydrogen and the imide nitrogen, creating six-membered rings. The crystal packing of ZnL 2 (Py) is stabilized by intermolecular C–H···O and C–H···π interactions, which form one-dimensional chains that further assemble into a three-dimensional supramolecular network (Figures S3 and S4).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) shows that ZnL 2 exhibits no significant weight loss up to 300 °C, indicating the absence of lattice solvent. In contrast, ZnL 2 (Py) undergoes a mass loss of approximately 9.0% near 180 °C, corresponding to the release of one pyridine molecule, consistent with its crystal structure (Figure b). Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) shows that ZnL 2 and ZnL 2 (Py) have distinct packing arrangements (Figure c). Thermal annealing of ZnL 2(Py), which removes the coordinated pyridine ligand, yields a PXRD pattern identical to that of ZnL 2. Re-exposure of the annealed sample to pyridine vapor for 24 h restores the original ZnL 2(Py) pattern (Figure c). This reversible transformation persists over multiple cycles, demonstrating robust, repeatable coordination/decoordination behavior in the solid state. Time-resolved PXRD measurements were conducted to monitor structural changes during pyridine uptake (Figure d). Upon exposure to pyridine vapor under a nitrogen atmosphere, the characteristic reflections at 2θ = 5.35° and 7.09° shift rapidly to lower angles (4.85° and 6.77°) within 30 s, indicating immediate host–guest interaction and lattice expansion (Figure S5). After 3 min, the peaks were observed at 4.84° and 6.76°, showing little further shift (Figure d). After 6 min, the low-angle peak gradually disappeared, and by 9 min, the structural phase transition was almost complete. Although single-crystal X-ray analysis was not feasible, the systematic low-angle shifts clearly indicate rapid expansion of the interlayer spacing, suggesting that the layers first separate to create space for guest uptake, followed by coordination of pyridine molecules to the metal centers. These results highlight the dynamic and reversible structural response of ZnL 2, supporting its potential as a solid-state sensor for pyridine vapor.

The Zn K-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra, specifically the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES), were obtained to probe the local coordination environment of the Zn­(II) centers (Figure ). The absorption edge positions of ZnL 2 (Py) synthesized, and ZnL 2 (Py_vapor) exposed to pyridine vapor are essentially identical. Their spectra overlap nearly perfectly across the entire measured range, indicating that both compounds possess an equivalent local coordination geometry. Thus, ZnL 2 (Py_vapor) can be assigned the same five-coordinate, trigonal bipyramidal structure as ZnL 2 (Py). In contrast, the spectrum of ZnL 2 shows notable differences in both the white-line intensity and the postedge region. Specifically, ZnL 2 exhibits a significantly enhanced white-line feature at ∼ 9667 eV and a diminished shoulder at ∼ 9673 eV compared with the pyridine-coordinated analogues. These variations are significant because the white-line intensity and postedge features in Zn K-edge XANES are highly sensitive to coordination number and to local geometric factors, including symmetry and distortion. Taken together, these spectral differences indicate that ZnL 2 , which adopts a four-coordinate geometry in its pristine form, undergoes a substantial change in its coordination environment upon exposure to pyridine vapor. Given that four-coordinate Zn­(II) complexes typically adopt a tetrahedral geometry, the observed spectral evolution strongly supports a reversible structural transformation between tetrahedral and trigonal bipyramidal geometries.

The diffuse reflectance spectra (Figure a) are dominated by ligand-centered transitions. For the free ligand HL, the shoulder at ∼ 362 nm is attributed to an intraligand charge-transfer (ILCT) transition from the phenolic/imine donor (HOMO) to the π* orbitals of the fluorenyl acceptor (LUMO). Upon coordination with Zn­(II) to form ZnL 2 and ZnL 2 (Py), pronounced bathochromic shifts to ∼ 377 nm and ∼ 400 nm, respectively, are observed. These shifts arise from ligand deprotonation and chelation, which enhance π-conjugation and increase structural rigidity. Consequently, the HOMO is destabilized by increased electron density, while the LUMO is stabilized by coordination-induced delocalization, leading to a reduced HOMO–LUMO gap. Given the d10 electronic configuration of Zn­(II), metal-centered and metal-to-ligand charge-transfer (MLCT) transitions are negligible, and any ligand-to-metal charge-transfer (LMCT) contribution is expected to be minimal; thus, ILCT remains the dominant transition. To gain insight into the electronic transitions of ZnL 2 (Py), TD-DFT calculations were performed using the optimized geometry derived from the crystallographic structure as the initial coordinates. The HOMO and HOMO–1 are mainly localized on the phenolic/imino donor moieties, whereas the LUMO+1 and LUMO+2 involved in the dominant electronic transitions are distributed over the π* orbitals of the fluorenyl acceptor unit (Figure c). The intense absorption bands calculated at 345.04 and 379.51 nm are mainly assigned to HOMO–2 → LUMO+1 and HOMO–1 → LUMO+1 transitions with relatively large oscillator strengths (Table S1). These results indicate that the observed absorption band predominantly originates from intraligand charge transfer (ILCT) from the phenolic/imino donor moieties to the fluorenyl acceptor unit.

The emission spectra (Figure b) show a substantial red shift from 430 nm for HL to 518 nm for both Zn­(II) complexes, corresponding to a large Stokes shift characteristic of emission from a stabilized ILCT excited state. Coordination suppresses nonradiative decay pathways by rigidifying the framework and further stabilizes the excited state through extended conjugation. Notably, ZnL 2 and ZnL 2 (Py) display nearly identical emission maxima, indicating that pyridine coordination has a negligible effect on the emissive state. This observation confirms that both the frontier molecular orbitals and the emission process are predominantly ligand-centered. Overall, the spectral evolution is governed by coordination-induced narrowing of the HOMO–LUMO gap and stabilization of ILCT states within the chelated ligand framework.

Upon exposure to pyridine vapor, ZnL 2 exhibits a ∼ 5-fold increase in emission intensity, consistent with formation of ZnL 2 (Py) (Figure ). This enhancement is visually corroborated by markedly increased brightness in the corresponding emission photographs after vapor treatment. In agreement with these observations, the absolute photoluminescence quantum yield (ΦPL) rises from 5.86% for ZnL 2 to 25.4% for ZnL 2 (Py), corresponding to an enhancement factor of ∼ 4.5. These results clearly indicate that pyridine coordination effectively promotes radiative decay, likely through increased structural rigidity and suppression of nonradiative pathways. Consequently, ZnL 2 can be regarded as a promising pyridine-responsive luminescent material, in which pyridine coordination induces conversion to ZnL 2(Py), accompanied by a substantial amplification of emission intensity.

To further evaluate the vapor selectivity of ZnL 2, the complex was exposed to various organic and inorganic vapors, and the resulting solids were characterized by PXRD (Figure S6). Exposure to EtOH, H2O, DCM, hexane, and THF caused no significant changes in the PXRD pattern of ZnL2, indicating that the original crystalline phase was retained. In contrast, clear changes in the diffraction patterns were observed after exposure to NH3 and DMF, suggesting the formation of distinct crystalline phases. The structural transformations induced by NH3 and DMF are likely attributable, at least in part, to their relatively strong coordination abilities toward the Zn­(II) center. Although the detailed structures of the NH3- and DMF-exposed phases remain undetermined, these results suggest that the vapor response of ZnL 2 is governed by the coordination ability of the guest molecules, supporting the coordination-driven nature of the present vapor-responsive system.

A Zn­(II) Schiff base complex capable of reversible pyridine coordination has been developed, exhibiting pronounced turn-on vapoluminescence in the solid state. Structural interconversion between a four-coordinate ZnL 2 and a five-coordinate ZnL 2 (Py) phase, as supported by PXRD and XAFS analyses, enables rapid, repeatable sensing behavior. The emission enhancement arises from coordination-induced rigidification and stabilization of ligand-centered excited states, accompanied by suppression of nonradiative decay pathways. This system is a relatively rare example of coordination-driven luminescence enhancement and highlights an effective strategy for designing selective and responsive VOC sensing materials.
