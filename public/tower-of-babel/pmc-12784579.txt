My risk perception; perspectives from youth at familial risk for bipolar disorder: a qualitative study
Authors: Yusef Ahmed, Freja Pind Pilegaard, Stine Pedersen, Maj Vinberg, Christina Wagner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOffspring of parents with bipolar disorder (BD) are at increased risk of developing mental health difficulties. While research has documented elevated rates of psychiatric symptoms in this group, less is known about how young people themselves perceive their parent’s illness, their own vulnerability, and the ways in which family relationships shape these experiences. Limited attention has been given to how they understand heredity and how they view preventative support. Greater insight into their lived experiences is essential for designing family-sensitive interventions that address both risks and resources.MethodsThis qualitative study included 15 young people aged 15–24 years who had at least one parent diagnosed with BD. Semi-structured interviews explored experiences of growing up with a parent with BD, perceptions of personal mental health and heredity, and expectations of support and research, which were the four predefined themes. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using qualitative content analysis. In addition, participants completed short self-report measures of mood and family functioning, which were used to provide contextual background for interpreting the qualitative findings.ResultsThe analysis generated several subthemes within each of the four predefined categories. Participants described living with unpredictability, heightened emotional vigilance, and in some cases role reversal, compounded by silence and stigma surrounding the illness. At the same time, they highlighted resources such as emotional awareness, strong sibling bonds, supportive peers, and curiosity about mental health research. Preventative support was welcomed, particularly when tailored to developmental stage and preferences.ConclusionGrowing up with a parent with BD involves both burden and resilience. Young people emphasized secrecy, responsibility, and emotional strain, but also loyalty, adaptability, and motivation to learn. These findings underscore the need for preventive interventions that are age-appropriate and attentive to relational resources, while also addressing vulnerabilities. Including offspring perspectives more systematically in clinical care and research may strengthen prevention for this high-risk yet often overlooked group.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07693-4.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Offspring, Lived experience, Qualitative research, Familial risk

Background

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a prevalent, highly heritable [1] mental health condition associated with early onset [2], recurrence [3] and reduced functioning and quality of life for both patients and their families [4]. Most cases of BD emerge before age 25 [5] and a strong genetic predisposition is known, with additional environmental factors contributing to its development [1]. Offspring of parents with BD have a markedly increased risk of developing BD - up to eleven-fold - as well as higher rates of depression and anxiety [6]. Risk factors such as changes in sleep, energy, and mood regulation often emerge several years before the onset of BD [7]. Improving communication within families may support earlier recognition of emerging symptoms and facilitate timely help-seeking among at-risk youth [8].

Children of parents with mental illness represent a well-documented risk group. Studies across diagnostic categories show that these children experience elevated levels of emotional distress, insecurity, role confusion, and increased caregiving responsibilities [9, 10]. They are often affected by stigma and silence within the family, which may contribute to heightened internalizing symptoms and difficulties in help-seeking [11]. While these issues are recognized across a range of parental mental illnesses, less is known about how they manifest specifically in the context of BD, where episodic mood shifts, behavioural changes, and unpredictability may create unique developmental challenges [12]. The combination of mood instability and high heritability makes the offspring of parents with BD a particularly important group for early support and intervention.

Our previous qualitative work shows that parents with BD often struggle to talk about the illness, which increases uncertainty for their children and underscores the need to explore offspring’s own experiences [12, 13].

In recent years, several preventive and early intervention approaches have been developed specifically for youth at familial risk for BD. Among the most established is Family-Focused Therapy for High-Risk Youth (FFT-HR) [14], which has shown promising effects on communication patterns, coping strategies, and early symptom trajectories.

Despite increased attention to early identification and intervention, limited research has explored how adolescents and young adults with a parent with BD experience being considered at risk. Little is known about how they interpret their parent’s diagnosis, how they understand heredity and familial vulnerability, and how they navigate concerns about their own mental health. Furthermore, few studies have examined what types of support these young people find meaningful, acceptable, and developmentally appropriate. Including their perspectives is essential for refining preventative interventions and ensuring that new clinical initiatives align with the lived experiences.

This study therefore aims to explore how offspring of parents with BD perceive their parent’s condition, how they experience being identified as at risk, and their perspectives on participating in preventive interventions.

Methods

Design

This study is a qualitative study based on semi-structured interviews, analysed by qualitative content analysis as described in the guidelines by Elo and Kyngäs [15] as conducted in previous qualitative studies by our group [13].

Participants

Participants in the study were offspring aged 15 to 25 who had at least one parent diagnosed with BD. Recruitment was conducted through the Affective Disorder Clinic at Mental Health Centre North Zealand, Denmark. Parents with BD were initially contacted and provided with information about the study by their primary therapist. If the parents consented, offspring were subsequently invited to participate, ensuring privacy of the family.

The parents did not participate in the interviews, and the interviews were kept confidential between the research team and the offspring.

Interviews

The interviews were conducted January-July 2025. A semi-structured interview guide (Supplementary 1) developed by the research team was used, which included questions about experiences of growing up with a parent with BD, perceptions of hereditary risk, personal mental health and perspectives on preventative support. During the sessions, the interviewers (YA, SP, or CW) actively facilitated the discussions by seeking clarification when needed, encouraging participants to elaborate on their perspectives, and steering the conversation back to central themes if it diverged [16]. The subthemes under each predefined theme reflect the aspects participants emphasized most strongly during the interviews.

All interviews were audio recorded and took place in a research room at Mental Health Centre North Zealand, Denmark or in some cases in the participants home, depending on the preferences of the participant. The interviews lasted between 17 and 68 minutes. All interviews were conducted by two members of the research team and were audio-recorded using encrypted digital recorders and were transcribed verbatim. Audio files and transcripts were stored on a secure, password-protected institutional server accessible only to the research team.

Additionally, the participants completed quantitative questionnaires (please see table S2) after the interview assessing mood and family dynamics [17–20]. All quantitative measures were self-report screening instruments. Measures provided contextual background only; no diagnostic interviews were conducted. In addition, participants were asked to self-report any diagnoses or mental health problems, but no systematic diagnostic assessment was conducted.

Data analysis

The analysis group consisted of four researchers - two medical scholar students (YA) and (FP), an MD and Postdoctoral Researcher, MD, PhD (CW), and a trained pedagogue and Master of Arts in Philosophy of Education (SP). YA, FP and CW brought experience in clinical psychiatry, while SP contributed with scientific skills within the humanities as well as lived experience as a patient with BD and a parent to an adolescent herself. This contributed to diverse perspectives in the analysis [21]. Data analysis was organized using the qualitative research software NVivo [22].

In accordance with previous studies by our group [23], the analysis followed a structured five-step process: 1) Open Coding: Each researcher independently reviewed the interview transcripts, identifying and marking all meaning units. A meaning unit was defined as a segment or phrase relevant to addressing the research questions. Each meaning unit was assigned a preliminary code summarizing its content. 2) Discussion of Codes: The analysis group collaboratively reviewed and refined the preliminary codes, reaching consensus on their meaning and relevance. 3) Code Merging: Preliminary codes were condensed and combined into new, overarching codes. This iterative process continued until no further meaningful merging was possible. 4) Categorization: Four overall categories were predefined based on the primary themes outlined in the interview guide. The content and meaning of all codes were then organized into these categories and their respective subthemes. 5) Synthesis and Review: YA drafted a detailed description of the categories based on team discussions. The draft was reviewed and revised by CW, SP and FP until consensus was achieved. The team worked collaboratively to ensure a shared understanding of the data, minimizing interpretation, and focusing on the participants’ original phrasing [24].

Analysis was conducted continuously throughout the interview process until thematic data saturation was reached.

Ethical considerations

The Scientific Ethics Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark declared that submission for ethical approval was not required for this project (Journal-nr.: H-24079197, date: 01/20-2025) and could therefore be initiated without further ethical permission according to §1, Sect. 4 of the Committee Act.

The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki [25] with written informed consent obtained from all participants (and from parents for those aged 15–17). SP, who has extensive family consultancy experience, attended all interviews with participants under 18 to ensure safeguarding and age-appropriate communication. Participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time.

Findings from the qualitative interviews

The findings are categorized into four main themes and subthemes (Fig. 1).

Overview of the main themes and subthemes emerged from the interviews

Navigating childhood with a parent with BD

I primarily remember it from when I was in high school, that I knew she was depressive at times, and it affected me. It was hard because it could be so unpredictable, changing from one day to the next. (Participant 1)

This quote highlights several of the challenges that come with being a child of a parent with BD; the unpredictable nature of the illness and the participant’s attempt to navigate and adapt to it.

Although participants described several interconnected experiences, the analysis separates these into distinct subthemes to capture different dimensions of growing up with a parent with BD.

The child’s observations and concerns

Unpredictability

Many became highly attuned to their parent’s emotional state, constantly assessing whether stability or an episode was ahead:” I always had to check in with myself before seeing her … Will it be calm, or will it turn into something else?” (Participant 1). Some avoided topics or social plans to manage the atmosphere: “I’d just feel her mood and adjust … it was like walking on eggshells.” (Participant 3). To cope, participants often withdrew emotionally or physically - not as rejection, but self-protection. “I love my dad, but I had to focus on my life too … if I didn’t pull back, I’d burn out.” (Participant 2). Yet distancing brought guilt when they couldn’t meet the parent’s needs: “I know he needs me … but I can’t carry it all” (Participant 6). Many felt frustration at the reversal of roles: “I’m the child … but when she’s in an episode, I must be the one who stays calm, plans, and picks up the pieces.” (Participant 5).

The anxiety of loss

Participants described how depressive or unstable states triggered concern, often unspoken: “It was uncomfortable to see him so down. I didn’t know what to say … I was scared that if I asked too much, it would make things worse.” (Participant 12). Others feared the consequences of severe episodes, with one noting: “I worried she might take her own life … it was in the back of my mind all the time.” (Participant 5). This fear persisted even when not living at home. Moving out sometimes intensified the worry: “Since I moved out … there’s more worry now - what if something happens and I don’t know?” (Participant 5).

Early responsibility and emotional strain

Responsibility

A significant emotional burden described by participants was the constant sense of responsibility they felt in relation to their parent:” He needed my help right away, and I didn’t know how long I had to assist … it felt unreasonable to expect that from me.” (Participant 2). Participants described trying to “read the room,” calm their parent during outbursts, or prevent escalating conflicts: “Let’s say he got into an argument with my siblings … then he completely blew up and got mad. I had to go over and talk to him, cool him down. I’ve done that a few times.” (Participant 10). For some, the responsibility was so embedded that they didn’t recognize it as unusual until later: “I only understood how much I carried when I moved out - and the silence felt strange.” (Participant 6).

Impact of involvement in illness and treatment

Beyond emotional support, some participants were also involved in more practical aspects of their parent’s treatment: “She gets ECT every week now. I know the schedule - I’ve followed it all closely.” (Participant 4). One person explained: “I had to explain to the doctor how she reacts to certain medications, because I’d seen it happen before. It’s like I know her file better than she does.” (Participant 1).

Emotional impact

Growing up with a parent with BD was overall described as having lasting emotional impact:” I didn’t want to burden anyone else, so I never talked about what was happening at home.” (Participant 15). Another said: “None of my friends knew. It felt easier that way” (Participant 9). Many described taking on a caretaker role that made them feel older than they were: “I always felt like I had to be the grown-up, even though I was just a kid.” (Participant 8).

Side effects and cognitive changes related to treatment

A participant described on the emotional toll of witnessing cognitive or behavioural changes in their parent following treatment – especially electroconvulsive therapy (ECT): “She’s not the same anymore. Her memory has clearly declined … we used to talk about complex things, and now she struggles to follow basic conversations.” (Participant 4). Others described feeling conflicted: “She’s more stable now, but she gets frustrated that she can’t remember things. That makes her sad, and it makes me sad too.” (Participant 1).

Unspoken sense of being different

Although several participants initially denied feeling different, their later descriptions of emotionally charged or atypical situations indicate a subtle, often unconscious, internalisation of difference: “When I see some of my dorm mates having visits from their parents … it does make me feel a bit different.” (Participant 10).

Offspring of a parent with BD as an adult child

The powerlessness of being a child of a parent with BD

Participants described how the responsibility they felt as adult children often coexisted with a sense of helplessness: “When she doesn’t have a partner, I feel that it increases my responsibility … I know I am her closest support person.” (Participant 14). Resentment often emerged in response to perceived inaction from the parent:” We are always here for her when she falls apart, she can’t even take the pill she agreed with her doctor to take.” (Participant 3).

Balancing own needs and boundaries

Despite the burden, many participants recognized the need to set boundaries. Yet doing so often came with guilt:” It wasn’t my responsibility to pick him up … but it was, wasn’t it?” (Participant 2). Another added: “When she calls, I pick up, even if I’m with a friend and can’t talk. Just so she knows I’m still here. (Participant 5). Balancing care with self-preservation was a recurring challenge: “I sometimes don’t answer, but then I feel awful afterwards. It’s like I’m choosing myself over her.” (Participant 15).

Language of BD

A recurring theme was the difficulty of developing a shared language about BD within the family: “It has been somewhat hidden … just explained as him being extra tired,” (Participant 2). Another added: “We’ve talked about it, but not as if it’s a mental illness. It’s more like, ‘that’s just who he is.” (Participant 12). Conversations often appeared only when participants initiated them:”We never really talked about it … it was something to be hidden from others.” (Participant 7).

Talking about BD outside the family was challenging: “When I talk to people who don’t know him, it’s hard to put into words what it actually is.” (Participant 8). Conflicting family beliefs further complicated matters, with one recalling debate with a brother who “doesn’t believe in mental illness.” (Participant 6).

The role of family, friends and partners

Mixed experiences with family

Many participants described siblings as central in managing emotional and practical challenges. “Our mother is not very good with things that cannot be fixed … That’s why I’ve talked much with my siblings about it.” (Participant 11). However, differences in awareness also emerged:” I don’t think my siblings have really been aware of it. It has been hidden from them, which is probably why I have thought about it more.” (Participant 5). Support from family members, such as aunts and uncles, varied considerably: “My aunts told me I could always come to them if it got too much.”(Participant 2). Another said:” Everyone probably knew something was going on, but they didn’t really know what it was or how much they should interfere.” (Participant 3).

Stigma, concealment, and lack of understanding

Some participants struggled with the decision to disclose their parent’s condition in professional settings: “I’ve been thinking about telling my boss for days. If he had been sick with something else, I probably would have said something. But with this … I don’t know what they would do with that information.” (Participant 2). Even when participants had access to support, some were reluctant to use it: “I know my friends care, but I also don’t want to be the one who always talks about heavy stuff.” (Participant 5).

Comfort despite illness

Many participants described experiencing a degree of comfort, security, and emotional stability despite the illness: “I was born into it, and it has just worked really well.” (Participant 5). Others echoed this:” We never felt unsafe. It was always where we preferred to be and where we felt the most comfortable.” (Participant 13).

Co-parent compensation

A key factor contributing to a sense of stability was the presence of a co-parent who actively took on responsibility: “Looking back, I think about how much my dad took care of us. He was really a superhero at home.” (Participant 4). Others confirmed similar experiences: “Even though my mom had her struggles, my dad made sure we still had a stable home.” (Participant 1).

Moments of joy from the parent with BD

Comfort also came from the parent with BD during periods of stability:” When my mom was doing well, she was the funniest, most loving, and energetic person. Our home was full of laughter.“ (Participant 4). Another shared: “I honestly feel like I had a more stable childhood than many of my friends, even though my mom was sometimes unwell.” (Participant 11).

Reflections on personal mental health and development

I think I am healthy and well, but I am much more conflict-averse than most people. I tend to prioritize keeping the atmosphere pleasant, sometimes at the expense of my own needs. I’ve learned to read the mood before reacting - at home, at work, and in social settings. But sometimes I catch myself doing it when I really shouldn’t. (Participant 6).

As this quote illustrates, many participants reflected on how growing up with a parent with BD had shaped their emotional responses, coping strategies, and sense of mental well-being. While some explicitly denied being negatively affected, their narratives often revealed subtle forms of psychological adaptation - particularly around conflict, control, and vulnerability.

Many participants spoke on the struggle to set boundaries and manage overstimulation: “I sometimes plan too many things and forget to consider my own needs. I don’t want to disappoint anyone, so I keep going until it becomes too much. Then I break down, hyperventilate, and cry for hours.” (Participant 2). Others shared:” I’ve always needed to feel in control. It’s not a fear of getting sick, exactly - it’s more about making sure no one ever must worry about me.” (Participant 12). Several felt confident in their ability to remain emotionally stable, even under pressure: “I’ve never felt like I was at risk for depression. Even when things have been rough, I always find ways to get through it.” (Participant 13).

Awareness of mental health risks often increased after their parent’s diagnosis: After my mom was diagnosed, I read up on what it means to be bipolar. I don’t think I have it, but I make a conscious effort to protect my mental health - whether that’s by going to therapy or just taking time for myself.” (Participant 1). Participants also reported long-term habits shaped by emotional vigilance. One noted: “I’m good at managing stress, but I also have a hard time letting go. It’s like I’m always scanning the room - checking, adjusting, preparing.” (Participant 3). Substance use and control were important concerns: “Seeing what alcohol did to my dad, I just don’t want to go there. Even when others partied, I stayed cautious. I know what can happen when someone loses control.” (Participant 6).

Overall, participants expressed a broad range of experiences. Some struggled with anxiety, overcommitment, or emotional sensitivity. Others had developed resilience and strong self-awareness. For many, their parent’s illness became not only a challenge to navigate, but a reason to stay mentally conscious and deliberate in their adult lives.

Reflections on heredity

Sometimes I catch myself thinking that I remind myself a lot of my father. I look like him, and in many ways, I behave like him. But then I stop and think, if my father did this, it would really annoy me. So, I try to change my behaviour. (Participant 8).

Several participants reflected on the possibility of inheriting traits or vulnerabilities from their parent with BD. For some, the topic of heredity had not been particularly present in their thoughts: “I actually didn’t even know bipolar could be passed on … until you just brought it up.” (Participant 12). Another noted that it had never been a major worry but acknowledged that “it makes sense that my mom’s been more worried about it than me.” (Participant 1).

For others, the lack of concern stemmed from a belief that their parent’s behaviour, rather than the diagnosis itself, was the more significant influence: “I’ve always known that I was sensitive and anxious, but I don’t believe that’s because of my mother’s diagnosis. If anything, my father’s behaviour and the environment we grew up in had a much bigger impact.” (Participant 14).

Some had reflected more consciously on heredity, particularly in relation to their future children: “I think more about it when I think about having kids myself. I’m not so worried about developing bipolar disorder, but I do think about whether I might pass on my anxiety.” (Participant 9). Another added: “Sometimes I wonder if I have some of the same traits, but it’s hard to tell if it’s because of who I am, or because of what I’ve seen.” (Participant 13). Family conversations also played a role in shaping participants’ thinking: My mom has said a few times that she’s glad we didn’t end up like her. So, I guess she’s thought about it more than I have.” (Participant 3).

Still, many participants placed greater weight on environmental and relational factors than on genetic ones:” Even if there were a genetic risk, I would never raise my kids in the kind of emotional environment I grew up in. That alone makes a difference.” (Participant 2). Another emphasized self-monitoring and prevention, saying: “I just try to manage stress and avoid going down the same path as my dad. That’s what I can control.” (Participant 10).

Though levels of concern varied, most participants emphasized a sense of agency. Heredity, while acknowledged, was rarely seen as something that defined their future. For many, the focus was not on fearing a genetic legacy, but on actively shaping their own outcomes through awareness, choice, and emotional responsibility.

Reflections on research participation, support and understanding

I think it would be great to have something like this. Just knowing there are others in the same situation and learning more about how to handle it would be valuable. (Participant 4).

Experiences of research participation and understanding the illness

Participants expressed a wide range of views about joining a study focused on offspring of parents with BD. For many, it evoked curiosity and hope, particularly about meeting others in similar situations: “It would make you feel like you’re not alone.” (Participant 4). Several felt it could provide earlier awareness and reassurance: “If you got invited to something like this five years ago … I would probably have been able to process things earlier.” (Participant 6).

Many noted that relatives are often left without guidance: “In the beginning it was really difficult … you just try to figure it out yourself.” (Participant 2). Others doubted more knowledge would help: “I’m used to it … I don’t think more information would change anything.” (Participant 15). A few worried that group interventions might increase anxiety, while flexibility was a recurring request from those with busy schedules.

Participants saw research participation to better understand BD:” I just knew something was difficult, but I never understood it was a form of illness.” (Participant 13). For some, understanding came much later: “It was really only recently … that I properly got to know what it was.” (Participant 10).

The absence of a label often brought confusion, and at times shame: “It’s not embarrassing to have a sick parent, but when you don’t know what it is, it can feel that way.” (Participant 8). Others pieced together the truth themselves: “He had mentioned being depressed, maybe even bipolar.” (Participant 7). Diagnosis frequently brought clarity: “It explained many of the conflicts we had” (Participant 5), but also new concerns: “It changed things … but it also accelerated her condition somehow. It made me worry more.” (Participant 1).

Greater understanding sometimes came through education or healthcare encounters: “Now that I’ve been diagnosed myself (with autism) and my dad has a diagnosis, I can better put words to it all.” (Participant 8). Yet gaps remained: “I’m not really sure what it means to be bipolar … but it makes sense in how he behaves.” (Participant 7).

Wishes and needs for preventative support

Participants shared their perspectives on preventative measures and early support. Many expressed a desire for interventions and targeted individual supports: “I attended a psychoeducation group, but it was only for spouses or parents of someone with BD. I felt out of place.” (Participant 15). Others wished for peer-specific spaces: “I don’t have any friends with a parent with a mental illness … I really would have liked to talk to someone who understands.” (Participant 7). Several participants linked their interest in early support to their understanding of hereditary risk, emphasising that clearer information about BD within the family might have helped them navigate concerns earlier.

Participants highlighted how services often focused on the diagnosed parent: “After my mom was discharged, we barely got any information about what she had gone through.” (Participant 5). Some linked this lack of support to wider challenges with openness: “I don’t know why I find it hard to tell my boss … I think many others feel the same way.” (Participant 2). Several emphasized that different forms of support work for different people: “Some things are better discussed with a professional, and others with someone who has gone through the same thing.” (Participant 14). The participants emphasized the importance of being able to choose if and how they would participate in preventative support, noting that interventions should feel voluntary and adaptable to their individual needs.

Overall, the participants highlighted that preventative support should be voluntary, flexible, and tailored to their needs and preferences, rather than focused solely on risk communication.

Discussion

This study examined how young people experience having a parent diagnosed with BD. First, participants found their parent’s mood swings emotionally destabilizing, requiring constant alertness and adaptation, though they also noted moments of closeness and normalcy. Second, many took caregiving roles early in life, which they viewed as burdensome and influential in shaping their identity, leading to a sense of emotional maturity. Third, reflections on their mental health revealed a mix of anxiety about vulnerability coupled with a keen desire to understand and discuss mental wellbeing on their own terms. Finally, support from siblings, partners, and peers was crucial in navigating their experiences, while silence around the illness, within the family often felt confusing or isolating. Participants also expressed a willingness to engage with research and preventive interventions that align with their developmental needs and their wish to have influence over if and how they engage in preventative support.

The findings both reaffirm and expand existing knowledge. Previous research has consistently documented how children of parents with BD face elevated emotional burdens [26], particularly related to the unpredictability of parental behaviour and early role confusion [12]. The present study confirms these patterns but also reveals how such challenges can produce unintended psychological resources. Several participants described heightened emotional awareness and a precocious ability to read social situations, suggesting that caregiving in this context is not purely a burden but also a site for growth, insight, and identity formation [27]. This duality complicates traditional notions of risk by illustrating how vulnerability and competence can co-exist. Patterns that may continue into adulthood, where early experiences of responsibility shape both strengths and potential vulnerabilities. For instance, while some participants felt more emotionally mature than peers, others described difficulties setting boundaries or asking for help. These findings suggest that the effects of role reversal may extend beyond adolescence and warrant further exploration e.g. as a preventative treatment target.

Secondly, silence around the parent’s illness emerged as a central tension. While many families avoided explicit discussion and sometimes minimizing language, participants rarely interpreted this as helpful. Instead, silence was linked to confusion, emotional detachment, and increased internal pressure to “figure things out” alone. Findings that align with studies showing that children are highly perceptive and often experience more distress when realities are obscured than when addressed openly. [13]. However, not all participants wanted full disclosure. Some noted their own discomfort with labels or feared social judgment. Similar findings have shown that fear of stigma often leads children to internalize silence, even when they seek understanding [9]. This ambivalence reflects a broader puzzle: that openness can offer relief, but also vulnerability, especially when adequate language or emotional scaffolding is lacking. The absence of shared language around the illness often left participants to construct their own explanations and sometimes leading to self-blame or distorted understandings. This points to a gap not only in communication, but also in the emotional learning available to young people. It raises questions about how families and clinicians might better equip youth to articulate complex experiences without resorting to silence or shame [11].

Thirdly, support systems, particularly siblings, but also peers, partners, and extended family were described as vital. These relationships provided not only emotional buffering but also opportunities to test and develop language around the illness [10]. Importantly, participants described these networks as involving friends or partners who initially misunderstood the illness sometimes became key sources of support over time. This suggests that while stigma remains a barrier, relational openness and mutual learning can foster more supportive social environments. Such findings speak to the importance of not only direct intervention, but also informal conversations and everyday forms of recognition [13].

Fourthly, participants’ reflections on heredity and their own mental health were marked by nuance. Some expressed anxiety about developing BD themselves, while others distanced from that possibility, which aligns with current studies [28]. What united them was a desire to understand their emotional lives - whether through self-reflection, conversation, or education. Interestingly, rather than resisting mental health discourse, many participants were open to being involved in research or support programs. They emphasized that timing, format, and tone mattered: forced participation or pathologizing language could be alienating, while respectful, developmentally appropriate support was viewed as helpful. This openness stands in contrast to assumptions that “at-risk” youth tend to withdraw or avoid engagement [29]. On the contrary, these young people articulated a strong wish to make sense of their experiences and to do so on their own terms. However, this openness also places a responsibility on the system - not only to offer support, but to do so in a way that recognizes young people’s autonomy and complexity. Participants did not want to be approached as passive recipients of care, but rather as collaborators in making sense of their lives [30]. This underlines the need for a shift in professional attitudes: from diagnosing or rescuing, toward listening and co-creating meaning.

Lastly, the findings suggest a broader rethinking of how adolescent development unfolds in contexts of parental mental illness. Rather than viewing these youth solely through the lens of inherited vulnerability, their experiences point to the role of family dynamics, communication, and social relationships in shaping outcomes [29]. The interplay of risk and resource, burden and belonging, is not a linear story of trauma or resilience, but one of complexity, ambivalence, and relational meaning-making.

The quantitative screening measures provide additional context for interpreting the qualitative findings. Although the family functioning measures did not indicate severe levels of dysfunction overall, several participants described relational strain, communication difficulties, and periods of instability in the qualitative interviews. This pattern suggests that standardised family environment measures may not fully capture the nuanced and fluctuating dynamics experienced in families affected by BD. Conversely, the moderate levels of stress and emotional symptoms reported by some participants are consistent with their qualitative accounts of uncertainty, worry, and increased caregiving responsibilities. Together, the qualitative and quantitative data highlight both the strengths and the vulnerabilities present in these families.

Implications

Young people with a parent with BD experience a nuanced interplay of emotional burden, premature responsibility, and a need for connection. Clinically, the study highlights the importance of supporting the entire family system, particularly siblings and co-parents, who provide stability [13]. Mental health professionals should guide family’s guidance on communication with children in age-appropriate and sensitive ways. Many participants found silence around the illness confusing, suggesting that family psychoeducation could help to reduce uncertainty and foster a shared language [31]. This aligns with previous studies on the benefits of psychoeducation and family-inclusive approaches for children of parents with mental illness [31].

The study also points to a general openness among participants toward preventive support and research participation, especially when such initiatives are developmentally appropriate and offer flexibility [29]. This implies that interventions targeting this group do not necessarily need to overcome resistance but rather should be designed in a way that respects young people’s preferences and lived experiences. Peer-based and digital formats may be particularly well-suited to increase accessibility and reduce stigma [29].

These findings highlight the need for more research that centres the voices of young people in families affected by mental illness [27]. Longitudinal studies could elucidate how early caregiving roles and emotional adaptations impact long-term outcomes, including mental health trajectories, identity development, and relationships. Additionally, integrating family-sensitive approaches in adult psychiatry is crucial, as parental roles are often overlooked. Overall, it is important to reframe how youth with a parent affected by mental illness are understood - not solely as individuals at risk, but as relationally embedded individuals recognizing both their challenges and capacities.

Strength and limitations

The study´s key strength is its qualitative, exploratory design, which offers a nuanced understanding of young people´s experiences with a parent diagnosed with bipolar disorder. The use of semi-structured interviews captured sensitive and complex topics in participants’ own words, allowing for emotional depth and individual variation.

The analysis involved a research team with diverse clinical and non-clinical backgrounds, enhancing interpretation in participants’ narratives. The sample included both genders and varied family dynamics, which provided a degree of variation in the data. Although the sample size of 15 was relatively small the analyses achieved thematic saturation, as no new insights emerged in the final interviews. However, caution is advised regarding qualitative saturation, as different methodologies could yield different emphases.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. All participants were recruited through a specialized affective disorder clinic, which may have resulted in a degree of self-selection bias. It is possible that families more open to mental health discourse - and young people with relatively high levels of reflection - were more likely to participate. The findings may therefore not fully capture the experiences of young people in more marginalized or less resourced families, or those not engaged with clinical services. Additionally, because the interviews relied on retrospective self-report, participants’ narratives may be influenced by memory, relational loyalty, or current mood.

Conclusion

This study provides insight into how young people experience growing up with a parent with BD. Their accounts illustrate how parental mood instability, periods of silence about the illness, and early caregiving responsibilities shaped their emotional lives, relationships, and developing sense of self. Although many described uncertainty, strain, and concern about their own mental health, they also demonstrated reflection, resilience, and the ability to make meaning of their experiences.

The findings underscore the need for developmentally appropriate, flexible forms of support that acknowledge young people’s preferences and give them a meaningful role in decisions about preventative or supportive interventions. Clearer communication within families, greater recognition of young people’s emotional and practical burdens, and family-sensitive clinical approaches may help reduce uncertainty and strengthen coping. This study contributes important knowledge for guiding future preventive initiatives and for ensuring that emerging interventions align with the lived experiences and needs of this often-overlooked group.
