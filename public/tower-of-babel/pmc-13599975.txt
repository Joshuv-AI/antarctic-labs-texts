Psychological experiences of syndactyly among youth aged 12–30: exploring mental health challenges and coping mechanisms in Islamabad – a qualitative study
Authors: Khansa Muhammad Sarwar, Shaheer Ellahi Khan, Babar Tasneem Shaikh, Zareef Uddin Khan, Syeda Hina Shah, Khola Raashid, Muhammad Jawad, Aqsa Elahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo explore the psychological and social experiences of youth aged 12–30 years living with congenital syndactyly in Islamabad.DesignA qualitative phenomenological design was employed. Semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted.Settings and participants12 participants aged 12–30 years with congenital syndactyly residing in Islamabad were recruited using purposive sampling from community organisations and special education centres. Interviews lasting 30–60 min were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and analysed using thematic analysis.ResultsFive major themes emerged: (1) limited understanding and concealment of the condition; (2) emotional burden including loneliness, intrusive thoughts and in some cases severe distress; (3) pervasive stigma manifesting through pity, moral and religious judgement, and dignity violations; (4) barriers to participation in education and everyday life; and (5) coping strategies involving faith, concealment and sometimes harmful responses alongside significant unmet support needs.ConclusionCongenital syndactyly was experienced as a complex psychosocial condition rather than solely a physical anomaly. The findings highlight the need for holistic, patient-centred care integrating psychological support, stigma reduction, inclusive education and community awareness. This study provides context-specific qualitative evidence to inform healthcare practice, policy and research in Pakistan.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, Young Adult, MENTAL HEALTH

Introduction

Congenital anomalies are structural or functional disorders arising during fetal development, present at birth or manifesting in early life and represent a significant global public health burden. The Global Burden of Disease 2024 study confirmed that congenital birth defects remain among the leading causes of mortality and disability in children under 20 years of age worldwide, with disproportionate impact in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where preventive and rehabilitative services remain inadequate.1 The WHO estimates that millions of newborns are affected annually, contributing substantially to childhood mortality, morbidity and long-term disability.2

Among congenital anomalies, differences affecting the limbs and hands carry particular significance due to the centrality of hand function in daily life—encompassing eating, writing, dressing, occupational performance and non-verbal communication. Syndactyly, one of the most common congenital hand anomalies, is defined by the fusion of two or more adjacent digits resulting from failure of complete digital separation during embryogenesis. Its severity ranges from simple soft-tissue webbing to complex fusion involving bone, nerves, blood vessels and nails.3 Recent population-based surveillance studies in China documented syndactyly as constituting approximately 4.3% of all reported congenital anomalies, with higher prevalence observed among males and urban populations.4 5

While syndactyly is typically managed surgically to optimise functional and cosmetic outcomes, the psychosocial dimensions of living with a visible congenital difference have received comparatively limited attention, particularly in LMICs. Growing evidence indicates that visible congenital hand differences are associated with stigma, emotional distress, impaired social relationships and challenges to self-concept—experiences that may persist regardless of surgical intervention.6 7 The Lancet Commission on ending stigma and discrimination in mental health identifies stigma as a driver of emotional distress, reduced help-seeking and diminished quality of life.8

Adolescence and young adulthood constitute a period of heightened psychosocial vulnerability for individuals with visible differences. Identity formation, peer acceptance, academic achievement and appearance-based social comparison are central developmental tasks during these years, rendering congenital differences potentially more psychologically impactful.6 Research suggests that youth with congenital hand anomalies may experience social anxiety, teasing, withdrawal and concealment behaviours, with socioeconomic disadvantage further compounding psychosocial outcomes.9

In Pakistan, research on congenital anomalies has predominantly focused on clinical and epidemiological characteristics, with limited attention to the mental health and social experiences of affected individuals. Local evidence from tertiary care hospitals in Islamabad suggests significant gaps in awareness and understanding of congenital and genetic conditions even among families attending specialist services.10 Cultural stigma, misconceptions regarding the aetiology of congenital conditions, and limited access to mental health services may further compound the psychosocial burden on affected youth and their families.

Islamabad, while offering relatively better access to healthcare and education compared with other Pakistani cities, presents a context where social comparison, academic pressures and appearance-related expectations coexist with traditional cultural norms. This combination may produce a distinct psychosocial environment for young people with visible congenital differences. To date, no qualitative study has examined the lived experiences, emotional impact, coping strategies or social challenges of youth with syndactyly in Pakistan.

This study explores the psychological and social experiences of youth aged 12–30 years living with congenital syndactyly in Islamabad. By generating context-specific, qualitative evidence, the study aims to inform the development of holistic, patient-centred approaches to care that integrate psychosocial support alongside surgical and physical management.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative phenomenological research design to explore the lived psychological and social experiences of adolescents and young adults with congenital syndactyly. Phenomenology is particularly suited to research that seeks to understand how individuals perceive, interpret and make meaning of personal experiences. Given that the psychosocial dimensions of syndactyly are complex, subjective and shaped by cultural context, a qualitative approach was most appropriate to generate the depth of insight that quantitative methods could not provide. The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).11

Research team and reflexivity

Interviews were conducted by KMS, a female researcher, an MSPH graduate with prior qualitative research experience and training during her coursework. She had no prior professional or personal relationship with any participant; rapport was built during the interviewing process. Participants were informed about the research goals, objectives and purpose of the researcher; KMS adopted a public health perspective instead of adopting a clinical perspective on this topic. The interview guide is available as online supplemental file 1: interview guide, which also contains questions about participants’ demographic characteristics. This research is grounded in an interpretivist (constructivist) epistemological stance, which holds that reality is subjectively experienced and socially constructed. This framework justifies the qualitative phenomenological design, as the study seeks to understand the lived experiences, personal meanings and contextual realities of youth with syndactyly in Islamabad.

Study setting and participant recruitment

The study was conducted in Islamabad, Pakistan, purposively selected as a research setting due to its demographic diversity and the presence of community organisations and special education centres providing care for individuals with congenital conditions. Participants were approached face-to-face and recruited from six non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and selected Special Education Centres operating within the city. Participants, visiting these centres for personal support, were approached in these organisations after seeking permission from the relevant authorities of these organisations.

Purposive sampling was employed to recruit participants who had direct lived experience of congenital syndactyly and could provide rich, contextually relevant information aligned with the study objectives. Participants were identified through healthcare providers, rehabilitation services, special education centres and community networks. Random or probability sampling was not appropriate, as the study aimed for depth of understanding rather than statistical generalisability.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were eligible if they were aged between 12 and 30 years, had a confirmed diagnosis of congenital syndactyly (simple or complex), were residents of Islamabad, and were able to communicate in Urdu or English. Adolescents and young adults were combined into one analytical group because they share the developmental stage of emerging adulthood, characterising identity information, social sensitivity and physical appearance differences. Individuals with acquired (non-congenital) digital fusion, significant cognitive impairments preventing meaningful interview participation, or critical illness were excluded.

Sample size

A total of 12 participants were included. Sample size was guided by the principle of data saturation, reached when no new themes or meaningful insights emerged from successive interviews—consistent with the conventions of phenomenological qualitative research, which prioritises depth over numerical representation.12

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews using an interview guide developed from a systematic review of relevant literature and aligned with the study objectives. The guide covered self-perception, emotional impact, stigma, social participation, coping mechanisms and support needs. Interviews were conducted between January and June 2025, lasting 30–60 min each, in Urdu or English according to participant preference. All interviews were audio-recorded and field notes were maintained with informed consent and transcribed verbatim to ensure accuracy and preserve the authenticity of participants’ narratives.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was conducted manually without using any software, following the six-phase framework of Braun and Clarke12: familiarisation with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the final report. Initial coding was conducted independently via an inductive approach by KMS, and data analysis was led by ZUK, SHS and KR, with a 33% (N=4 of 12) proportion of transcripts reviewed by the supervisor (SEK) to enhance credibility and reduce individual bias. Member checking was performed with a subset of participants (N=6 of 12) to verify the accuracy and resonance of emerging interpretations. An audit trail was maintained throughout to document analytical decisions by maintaining a reflexive journal and memos.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this research. The findings will be shared with participating organisations to inform service development.

Results

A total of 12 participants (mean age 20.8 years; six male, six female) were included. Simple syndactyly was present in eight participants (66.7%) and complex syndactyly in four (33.3%). Summary of themes and subthemes

Theme 1: understanding, knowledge and concealment

Participants consistently reported limited biomedical understanding of syndactyly, relying instead on simplified family explanations. This knowledge gap generated uncertainty and discomfort, particularly in social situations where explanations were sought. Many participants described concealment of their hand as both a practical and emotionally protective strategy.

Subtheme 1.1: limited understanding and informal explanations

The majority of participants reported that they had never received a clear medical explanation of their condition. Family narratives—often framed in simple or spiritual terms—filled this explanatory gap, but contributed to uncertainty rather than resolution.

My mother says it happened from birth. She never explained it properly. (R1)

I don't understand it fully, but I know it looks different from others’ hands. (R7)

This uncertainty about the cause and nature of their condition became itself a source of psychological burden, contributing to embarrassment and reduced social confidence, particularly when participants were asked to explain their hand to others.

Subtheme 1.2: concealment as a response to uncertainty and difference

Participants described concealment as a deliberate and emotionally motivated behaviour, used to avoid unwanted attention, questions and negative reactions in social and educational settings.

My hand isn’t like others. It feels strange, so I hide it. (R2, R3)

I hide it … it feels strange. (R4)

Concealment functioned as an anticipatory coping behaviour—a response to an environment perceived as likely to judge or scrutinise. While offering short-term protection from social discomfort, it also limited natural interaction and reinforced a sense of difference.

Theme 2: self-perception and emotional burden

Syndactyly was experienced not merely as a physical condition but as an emotional and psychological phenomenon shaped by social comparison, repeated scrutiny and internalised distress. Participants described a spectrum of emotional responses ranging from loneliness and reduced motivation to, in some cases, severe psychological distress.

Subtheme 2.1: feeling ‘different’ through teasing and unwanted attention

Social interactions—particularly teasing, staring and unsolicited comments—were central to how participants came to perceive themselves as different. This process of social evaluation transformed the physical appearance of the hand into a marker of undesirable difference.

I don’t understand it, but it looks strange… children make fun of me. (R7)

I don’t feel good going outside. People look at me in a strange way. (R9)

Subtheme 2.2: withdrawal, loneliness and intrusive thoughts

Many participants described persistent internal distress characterised by loneliness, social withdrawal and repetitive, intrusive thoughts about their condition and its social consequences. These experiences suggest patterns of rumination and internalised stigma rather than situational responses.

Thoughts come to mind. I feel alone. (R4, R5, R7, R8, R10)

I don’t feel good going outside … I prefer to stay at home. (R9)

Subtheme 2.3: hopelessness, low motivation and extreme distress

A subset of participants described feelings of hopelessness, diminished agency and resignation about the future. Importantly, one participant expressed language indicative of severe psychological distress:

I am helpless … I will just get through life. I don’t feel like doing anything now. (R10)

I get very angry … I feel like dying. (R6)

People can do everything, but I was left behind. (R10)

These expressions are clinically significant and highlight the need for routine psychological screening and safeguarding pathways within services caring for youth with congenital conditions.

Theme 3: stigma, moral judgement and violations of dignity

Stigma was experienced as a persistent and cumulative social process rather than a series of isolated incidents. Participants described multiple forms of stigmatising behaviour—from everyday pity to moral and religious attribution, outright rejection and in some cases violations of physical dignity.

Subtheme 3.1: staring and pity as everyday stigma

All participants reported being pitied, and most described being stared at in public settings. Although pity may be superficially sympathetic, participants experienced it as distancing and identity-reducing—a form of stigma that reinforced rather than bridged difference.

People look at me in a strange way. (R9)

Everyone pities us. (All respondents)

Subtheme 3.2: moral and religious blaming

A smaller group of participants described community narratives that attributed syndactyly to divine punishment or sinful behaviour. These moral framings of disability legitimised rejection and intensified shame, making it more difficult for participants to seek or expect social inclusion.

Some people say it is God’s punishment … a penalty for sins… and no one accepts us. (R7, R8)

Subtheme 3.3: rejection, being seen as a burden and dignity violations

Participants described experiences of interpersonal rejection and being perceived as a burden by family members or peers. Female participants additionally reported inappropriate touching and dismissive comments, highlighting gendered dimensions of dignity violation.

They left me because I didn’t have hands. (R4)

I was a burden. (R3, R4, R5, R6, R8)

They touch my hand in an inappropriate way … and say, “you don’t have a hand—what difference does it make to you?” (Female participant)

Theme 4: participation barriers in education and everyday life

Participation barriers were experienced across educational settings, daily routines and public life. These barriers were shaped by functional limitations alongside—and often more powerfully by—discouraging social attitudes, fear of humiliation and peer aggression.

Subtheme 4.1: everyday practical difficulties and discomfort

Mundane daily challenges served as repeated, concrete reminders of difference, generating frustration and emotional distress in situations others take for granted.

Sometimes it gets cold, and gloves don’t come in my size. (All respondents)

I just wish I had nails too. I feel like crying. (R9)

Subtheme 4.2: barriers to studying and school participation

Participants described the intersection of physical limitations and social discouragement as particularly disabling in educational settings, restricting opportunities for learning and academic engagement.

I want to study, but I cannot hold the pencil. They say I cannot study. (Multiple respondents)

Subtheme 4.3: avoiding public exposure and peer aggression

Fear of public visibility and experiences of peer aggression led participants to actively avoid settings in which their hand would attract attention—including school activities, social gatherings and public spaces.

When they call me on stage, I hide in the bathroom. (R3, R4, R5)

Sometimes it becomes difficult. These children hit me. (R1, R2, R3)

Theme 5: coping strategies, support gaps and the need for hope

Participants described coping as an ongoing and largely unsupported process. Faith-based strategies were the most commonly reported form of coping, though these coexisted with existential questioning and, in some cases, harmful self-directed responses to stigma. Unmet support needs were consistently articulated.

Subtheme 5.1: faith-based coping and existential questioning

Prayer and religious meaning-making were widely described as sources of emotional comfort, particularly in the absence of formal psychological support. However, participants also described unresolved questions and ambivalence that faith alone could not address.

When everything feels bad, we sometimes pray. (Multiple respondents)

Why did God make me like this? (Multiple respondents)

It is a gift from God, but sometimes it feels like the world takes advantage of people like us. (R9)

Subtheme 5.2: harmful coping in response to stigma

Some participants described self-directed anger and self-harm-like responses triggered by being labelled or stigmatised. These findings indicate that internalised stigma can produce active harm, and represent an important safeguarding consideration.

If someone says, “you are disabled,” then I hit myself. (R4, R7, R8, R9)

Subtheme 5.3: unmet support needs

Participants articulated clear and unmet needs for financial support, emotional understanding and hopeful communication about their future—needs that were consistently absent from their current care and social environment.

No one understands us. They say treatment is expensive. (Multiple respondents)

I never met anyone who gave hope that we can also become something. (R10)

Discussion

This qualitative phenomenological study provides the first context-specific qualitative evidence on the lived psychosocial experiences of youth with congenital syndactyly in Pakistan. The findings demonstrate that syndactyly is not experienced as a physical condition alone; it is shaped by limited knowledge, pervasive stigma, participation barriers and constrained support systems, producing a complex and often distressing psychosocial experience across adolescence and young adulthood.

Limited knowledge, informal explanations and concealment

Participants’ limited biomedical understanding of syndactyly—and their reliance on informal, family-based explanations—is consistent with existing literature on congenital hand differences. A past study found that children with congenital upper limb differences frequently relied on incomplete explanations, shaping their self-perception and social responses.13 Another study notes that patient education is often insufficient in congenital hand conditions, particularly in resource-limited settings.14 Local evidence further shows that awareness of congenital and genetic conditions remains limited even among families attending tertiary hospitals in Islamabad,10 suggesting systemic gaps in genetic counselling and patient education that extend beyond individual clinical encounters.

Concealment, widely described in this study, reflects anticipated stigma—participants adjusted behaviour to preempt negative social reactions.15 This aligns with international evidence documenting concealment behaviours such as hiding hands in pockets or avoiding public gestures.6 7 While adaptive in the short term, prolonged concealment may reinforce internalised stigma and restrict authentic social participation.

The significant emotional burden described by participants—encompassing loneliness, intrusive thoughts, reduced motivation, and in some cases expressions of hopelessness and suicidal ideation—is consistent with international literature on congenital hand differences.16 The spectrum of distress observed, from mild anxiety to clinically significant psychological crisis, underscores that the emotional impact of syndactyly is not uniform but shaped by social experiences, coping resources, and support availability. The expression ‘I feel like dying’ from one participant is particularly notable and highlights an urgent need for psychological screening and referral pathways in services providing care for this population.

Stigma emerged as one of the most powerful and pervasive determinants of psychosocial distress. Pity, moral and religious attribution, rejection and dignity violations collectively constituted a chronic and cumulative stigma experience rather than isolated incidents, aligning with a previous study, characterisation of stigma as a systemic social process.17 The framing of syndactyly as divine punishment—reported by a subset of participants—is consistent with broader evidence from South Asian contexts, where congenital conditions are sometimes attributed to fate or wrongdoing.18 Such narratives delegitimise claims to inclusion and intensify shame.

Female participants’ accounts of inappropriate touching and dismissive comments highlight the intersection of disability and gender-based vulnerability. International evidence demonstrates that women with disabilities are at elevated risk of harassment and dignity violations,19 a finding this study extends to young Pakistani women with visible congenital differences.

The participation barriers described align with the social model of disability, which locates disabling barriers in environmental and social structures rather than in the impairment itself. Wall et al20 found that children with congenital upper extremity differences from socioeconomically deprived backgrounds experienced worse psychosocial outcomes, partly through reduced social support and increased exclusion. The present study similarly demonstrates that discouragement, peer aggression and lack of accommodations were at least as disabling as any functional limitation. In the Pakistani educational context, where inclusive practices and assistive resources are limited, simple environmental adjustments—alternative writing tools, supportive teacher attitudes and anti-bullying policies—could substantially reduce participation barriers.21

Faith-based coping was the predominant strategy reported, reflecting the cultural and religious context of Pakistan, where religion serves as a primary framework for meaning-making in illness and adversity.10 While faith provided emotional regulation and comfort, it did not fully resolve distress—particularly in the continued presence of social stigma. More concerning were reports of self-directed harm in response to stigmatising labels, consistent with evidence linking internalised stigma to self-blame, self-harm and reduced help-seeking.22 These findings point to an urgent need for accessible psychosocial interventions. The absence of peer support groups, counselling services and hope-giving communication was consistently identified as a gap, echoing a previous study’s finding that psychosocial support and positive communication about the future are critical components of holistic care.23

Implications

The findings carry several implications for clinical practice, health policy and research. Clinically, healthcare providers should integrate psychosocial screening and referral pathways into the routine care of children and young people with syndactyly. Clear, age-appropriate and culturally sensitive explanations at diagnosis and follow-up may reduce uncertainty and concealment. Pre- and post-surgical counselling should address emotional expectations and self-image alongside functional outcomes. Psychological support services should be made accessible and destigmatised.

Educational institutions should adopt inclusive practices—including modified materials, anti-bullying policies and trained teachers—to reduce participation barriers. Community-level awareness campaigns, engaging religious leaders and community organisations, are needed to challenge moral attribution and stigmatising attitudes. At the policy level, congenital anomalies should be recognised as public health priorities encompassing psychosocial as well as physical dimensions, with coordinated investment in mental health, education, and community support systems.

Future research should employ longitudinal qualitative designs to examine how psychosocial experiences evolve across the life course, and quantitative studies with larger samples to estimate the prevalence of psychological distress. Intervention studies evaluating psychosocial support programmes, peer support groups, and school-based inclusion initiatives are particularly needed in LMICs.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides the first context-specific qualitative evidence on the psychosocial experiences of youth with syndactyly in Pakistan, generating insights not available from existing epidemiological or clinical literature. The phenomenological design enabled in-depth exploration of lived experience, and thematic analysis was conducted systematically with member checking and supervisor review to enhance credibility. Limitations include the small sample size confined to Islamabad, which may not represent experiences in other urban or rural Pakistani contexts. Social desirability bias may have influenced responses on particularly sensitive topics, and the phenomenological nature of the study precludes assessment of how experiences change over time.

Conclusion

This study reveals syndactyly as a deeply social and psychological experience for youth in Islamabad, shaped by stigma, participation barriers and inadequate support. Holistic care integrating psychological support, stigma reduction and inclusive education is urgently needed. These findings provide context-specific evidence to inform practice and policy in Pakistan and similar settings.
