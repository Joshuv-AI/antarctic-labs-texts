Acetaminophen-induced liver injury triggers short- and long-term neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier disruption, and behavioral deficits in a genetic mouse model of autism
Authors: Jakub Szabó, Andrej Feješ, Ladislav Bačiak, Gabriela Fujeríková, Emese Renczés, Daniel Gogola, Youssef Zaim Wadghiri, Daniela Ostatníková, Peter Celec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition with a strong genetic basis, yet its etiology is not fully understood. Neuroinflammation is emerging as a critical secondary factor. Since peripheral organ injury, such as acute liver failure, can induce neuroinflammation, we aimed to investigate whether liver injury could exacerbate ASD-like phenotypes in a genetically susceptible model. We used adult (12-month-old) Shank3−/− mice, a genetic model of ASD, and their wild-type (WT) counterparts. A single high dose of acetaminophen (APAP; 350 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally to induce acute liver injury. We assessed acute (24-h) and long-term (1-month) consequences on liver health, neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier integrity, and brain edema using plasma biomarkers, immunohistochemistry, and in-vivo MRI, as well as behavioral phenotypes, including sociability, repetitive behaviors and exploration. Acutely, APAP induced severe liver damage, cerebral edema, and neuroinflammation in Shank3−/− mice. One month later, despite liver recovery, both APAP-treated WT and Shank3−/− mice exhibited brain edema, elevated apparent transverse relaxation (R2*) rates, and lasting neuroinflammation, evidenced by microgliosis and reactive astrocytosis. These differences were more extensive in the Shank3−/− group. Behaviorally, this was accompanied by increased social deficits in both genotypes. Critically, only the APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice displayed significantly increased repetitive behaviors, and reduced exploratory activity. Here, we show that transient liver injury is associated with long-term neuroinflammation, markers of blood–brain barrier disruption, and behavioral deficits. These findings demonstrate that while peripheral organ damage can induce lasting behavioral changes in healthy animals, the severity and spectrum of these deficits are exacerbated by ASD-associated genetic predisposition. These findings support a preclinical gene-environment interaction framework relevant to ASD-associated vulnerability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-026-02385-w.

Background

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) involves a range of diverse neurodevelopmental disorders unified by common deficits in sociability and repetitive behaviors [1]. During recent years, ASD has been rising in incidence, and despite extensive research, its etiopathogenesis remains largely elusive [2, 3]. The heritability of ASD is estimated to be as high as 90%, which underscores the dominant influence of genetic factors [4]. Whole-genome studies identified a variety of genes associated with ASD, and the SH3 and multiple ankyrin repeat domains 3 (SHANK3) gene is one of the best researched to date [5]. The Shank3−/− mice display ASD-like phenotypes such as social deficits and self-injurious repetitive grooming [6]. With genetic factors broadly explored, research points to the secondary role of the environment in ASD etiology. While numerous environmental effects have been suggested, neuroinflammation, often triggered by environmental factors, has emerged as a key mediator in the underlying pathomechanisms [7].

Abnormal proinflammatory cytokine regulation is common in ASD patients [8] and persistent neuroinflammation is among the most prevalent comorbidities [9]. Indeed, postmortem examinations of ASD brains show microglial activation, reactive astrocytosis [10, 11], elevated concentrations of microglia-produced proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α [12], and markers of astrocytic dysfunction such as GFAP across the cortex [13, 14]. Patients with ASD also show elevated peripheral concentrations of GFAP and S100B [15, 16], a marker of disruptions to the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Animal models of ASD show similar findings. Namely, a CNTNAP2-deficient mouse model of ASD shows GFAP+ reactive astrocytosis in the hippocampus [17] and SHANK3-deficient non-human primates show similar changes across the cortex [18]. It has been shown that the degree of neuroinflammation might exacerbate the severity of the ASD phenotype by mechanisms such as synaptic pruning [19, 20]. However, current research lacks a reliable and translational preclinical model of neuroinflammation to study its effects [21].

Acute liver failure (ALF) is a severe hepatic dysfunctioncharacterized by coagulopathy, immune activation, and subsequent development of hepatic encephalopathy, a neuropsychiatric syndrome observed following liver dysfunction [22]. The necrotic and apoptotic hepatocytes promote excessive proinflammatory cytokine release [23] and failing liver function causes the accumulation of ammonia [24]. These can breach the BBB, infiltrating the brain, and trigger neuroinflammation, BBB disruptions, and the onset of cerebral edema [25]. While these mechanisms are the most recognized causes of hepatic encephalopathy, their pathophysiology remains to be fully elucidated. Acute manifestations of hepatic encephalopathy span from hypoactivity and nausea to mild cognitive deficits, ultimately culminating in coma [26]. Patients also experience long-term complications despite hyperammonemia resolving. In preclinical models, liver disease has also been linked to neuroinflammation, reduced brain plasticity, and functional deficits [27], as well as reported to exacerbate ASD-like phenotypes [28]. Although the role of neuroinflammatory disruption in the neurovascular unit has been suggested, experimental validation remains elusive [25].

The most common cause of ALF is drug-induced liver injury [29] and acetaminophen (APAP, N-acetyl-para-aminophenol, Paracetamol) is the most often used medication in such cases. APAP is an analgesic and antipyretic over-the-counter drug used globally. Previously, some concerns have been raised about a possible link between maternal APAP use during pregnancy, often related to treating infection and fever [30–32], and elevated risk of ASD in children [33]. While the link still draws considerable attention, the primary focus of APAP overdose remains on ALF and its pathology. Liver injury due to APAP overdose is characterized by the development of hepatocyte necrosis [34], and abnormal elevation of circulating aspartate and alanine aminotransferase (AST, ALT) enzymatic activity [35]. Mice are the most commonly used animals in preclinical APAP-induced ALF research, with males being the preferred sex as females are less susceptible to APAP hepatotoxicity [36]. The widespread availability of APAP, its common use, and the similarity of APAP-induced ALF pathophysiology in mice to human patients collectively make it an unexpectedly fitting and translational model of neuroinflammation.

To investigate the role of ALF-induced neuroinflammation in ASD symptomatology, we administered a single high dose of APAP to Shank3−/− mice and followed its short- and long-term effects. Acute development of cerebral edema, neuroinflammation, and hypoactivity was expected, while persisting brain inflammation, BBB disruptions, and exacerbation of the ASD behavioral phenotype were hypothesized long-term. In the case of lasting neuroinflammation and neurostructural damage, we presumed it to be associated with symptom severity.

Material and methods

Animals

Breeding pairs of heterozygous (HT) Shank3B mice (C57BL/6 background) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (JAX Stock No. #017688). Shank3B wild-type (WT) and knock-out (Shank3−/−) mutant mice were generated by crossing adult (3-month-old) HT males with age-matched HT females. Genotyping was conducted to determine the genotype of the offspring. Male WT (n = 25) and Shank3−/− (n = 30) adult (12-month-old) mice were used. Animals were group-housed (4 per cage) with their littermates and kept in a controlled environment of 24 ± 2 °C and 55 ± 10% humidity with ad libitum access to food and water on a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 0700, lights off at 1900 h). The experiment was performed in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments 2.0 (ARRIVE2.0) guidelines [37]. No animals were excluded from the study. Animals were randomly assigned into treatment groups, and following substance administration, all subsequent experiments and data analyses were performed by experimenters fully blinded to the experimental conditions. Handling and manipulation were carried out by the same researcher.

Genotyping and substance administration

The end-point PCR was used to confirm the genotype of the offspring animals. Tail snips (~ 0.2 cm) were collected at weaning, subsequently processed, and genomic DNA was isolated according to the instructions of the manufacturer (MyTaq™ Extract-PCR Kit, Bioline). After the extraction, 2 μl of the extract was amplified using standard Endpoint PCR and a combination of three primers designed to identify both the WT and KO alleles (P1-Common: GAG ACT GAT CAG CGC AGT TG; P2-WT: TGA CAT AAT CGC TGG CAA AG; P3-KO: GCT ATA CGA AGT TAT GTC GAC TAG G). Combinations of primers produced a 374-bp band for the WT allele, a 153-bp band for the KO allele, and both bands for the heterozygous allele. The PCR product was run on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with the GoodView™ Nucleic Acid Stain (Beijing SBS Genetech). At the start of the experiment, animals were put on food deprivation for 8 h and were subsequently intraperitoneally injected with a single dose of APAP (350 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, Taufkirchen, Germany) in a 100 mg/ml concentration or saline vehicle according to the previously used protocol [38]. Two separate forms of the study were conducted – in the short-term experiment, analyses were conducted up to 24 h after the APAP injection (WT Saline n = 9, Shank3−/− Saline n = 8, Shank3−/− APAP n = 8), and in the long-term experiment, analyses were conducted 1 month after the APAP injection (WT Saline n = 8, Shank3−/− Saline n = 8, WT APAP n = 8 Shank3−/− APAP n = 6). Administration was carried out by the same researcher in both experiments.

In vivo MRI and post-processing

In vivo MRI was performed on a 4.7 Tesla (4.7 T) animal imaging system with a 205-mm access horizontal bore superconducting magnet equipped with actively shielded gradient coil insert (SGRAD 205/120/HD/S, ID = 120 mm; Agilent Technologies UK, Berkshire, UK). The radiofrequency (rf) coil was a 25 mm inner diameter transmit/receive quadrature volume birdcage coil tuned to 200 MHz for signal acquisition (STARK contrast, Erlangen, GER). In vivo MRI scans were performed on all animals at 14 h (WT Saline = 9, Shank3−/− Saline = 8, Shank3−/− APAP = 8) and 1 month (WT Saline = 8, Shank3−/− Saline = 8, WT APAP = 8, Shank3−/− APAP = 6) following substance administration. Animals were anesthetized by 4% isoflurane and positioned prone into the magnet bore. During the scanning, anesthesia was maintained at 2% isoflurane, keeping the respiration rate of the animal at 80 BPM (ranging from 60–100 BPM). The ambient temperature in the magnet bore was maintained at 37 °C by warm air (SA Instruments). Twenty-one axial brain images were acquired using a two-dimensional (2D) multi-slice T2-weighted (T2-w) Fast-Spin-Echo (FSE) sequence (TEeff = 57 ms, TR = 3000 ms, 12 averages, FOV 25.6 × 25.6 mm2, 0.4 mm slice thickness, 0.4 mm gap, Matrix size 128 × 128, 200 μm in-plane resolution, turbo factor = 8, Time = 9.6 min.) to observe alterations in tissue contrast related to the transverse relaxation time T2, which manifest as hyperintensities in the presence of cerebral edema. Additionally, a 2D T2*-weighted Multi Gradient Echo pulse sequence (TE = 3.4 ms, TR = 740 ms, 8 echoes with 4 ms echo spacing, 10 averages, acquisition time ~ 10 min.) was acquired under the same slice coverage and in-plane resolution. This image acquisition facilitated the generation of a quantitative map of the apparent transverse relaxation rate R2* (1/T2*) from the 2D T2*-w echo train images (Firevoxel, build 368, https://firevoxel.org/). Notably, the R2* maps exhibit high sensitivity to the presence of iron (ferritin and hemosiderin) in the tissue [39], indicating intracellular iron deposition and potential signs of intracerebral microhemorrhage [40]. The 2D R2* maps were subsequently aligned and affinely registered using ITK-Snap v4.0.2 [41] to the Toronto Mouse Brain atlas [42], transformed using ANTs 2.0 [43], and overlaid with brain atlas-derived regions of interest to enable calculation of mean regional R2* values using Mrtrix3 [44]. Image acquisition, post-processing, and all quantitative analyses were performed by an experimenter blinded to the experimental groups.

Behavioral assessment

Behavioral testing was carried out 14 h and 1 month after the APAP injection, respectively. The testing was conducted in the PhenoTyper 4500 cage (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, The Netherlands), consisting of an open field arena (45 cm × 45 cm × 45 cm). Each test was performed in a dimly lit room with a room temperature of 24 ± 1 °C and the animals were habituated to the testing situation at least 30 min before the test. All animals were weighed after the behavioral testing period.

Sociability was tested in the previously used [45] Reciprocal interaction test (RCI), where the animals were randomly paired with a socially novel WT animal of the same sex and age (social partner) after 24 h of social isolation and were left to freely interact in a sawdust bedding-filled environment while being recorded for 10 min. The recording was subsequently scored by two trained observers blinded to the experimental design for social interaction (cumulative time spent nose-to-nose, nose-anogenital, and side-sniffing) and social disinterest (cumulative time spent self-grooming, digging, lying flat, freezing in contact or avoiding the social partner). The values measured by both observers were averaged and considered representative. The inter-observer variability was 3.1%.

In the Open field test (OF), animals were individually placed into the testing cage and left to freely explore the environment for 10 min while being recorded, according to the previously used protocol [45]. Recordings were scored manually by two trained observers blinded to the experimental design. The cumulative times of self-injurious grooming and rearing activity were scored as a measure of repetitive behavior and explorative behavior, respectively. The values measured by both observers were averaged and considered representative. The inter-observer variability was 4.6%. The distance traveled by the animals during the testing session was quantified as a measure of locomotor activity and the time the animals spent in the central zone was quantified as a measure of anti-anxiety behavior.

Cognitive performance (short-term memory) was assessed in the long-term experiment only and was measured using the Novel object recognition test (NOR) [46]. During the habituation period, animals were individually placed in the testing arena and were presented with two identical objects (plastic cylinders, placed in the opposite corners of the arena) to freely interact with them for 10 min. After a retention interval of 30 min, one of the initial objects was replaced (alternating sides every trial) by a novel object (metallic can), and the subject animal was placed back in the arena to freely move and interact with the objects for 10 min. Short-term memory was expressed in the form of a memory index, calculated using the formula [(novel_object_interaction_time – initial_object_interaction_time) / total_interaction_time], where values closer to -1 represent worse short-term memory performance, and values closer to 1 represent better short-term memory performance.

Blood collection, processing, and plasma biomarkers

Blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital sinus into the EDTA collection tubes (Microvette 500 EDTA K3E, Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) under 3% isoflurane anesthesia before euthanizing. The blood samples were centrifuged at 1600 g, 4 °C for 10 min and plasma supernatant was collected and stored at -20 °C until analysis. Plasmatic activity of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was quantified (Biolis 24i Premium instrument, Tokyo, Boeki, Japan) to indicate liver damage. To assess the degree of astrocytic injury and neuroinflammation, plasmatic concentrations of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) were measured by commercial ELISA kit (Mouse GFAP ELISA kit, ELK Biotechnology CO., Ltd., Denver, CO, USA) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. To evaluate BBB disruption, S100B protein was quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Human S100B DuoSet Elisa Kit, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). For all plasma biomarker assays, samples were anonymized before processing, and the analysis was conducted by an experimenter blinded to the experimental groups.

Euthanasia and tissue processing

Following the blood collection, anesthetized animals (3% isoflurane) were transcardially perfused with ice-cold 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and decapitated. The liver was dissected, weighed, and morphologically evaluated for signs of tissue damage. The brain was rapidly dissected on ice and split along the medial axis into two hemispheres. The right anatomical hemisphere was immediately put into 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and fixed overnight, then stored in cryoprotective solution (20% glycerol, 2% DMSO in 0.1 M PBS) at 4 °C until histological sectioning. The hippocampus was collected by micro-dissection from the left anatomical hemisphere, then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until homogenization. At the time of homogenization, hippocampal tissue was weighed and mixed with 0.01 M PBS (100 mg/ml). Samples were homogenized at 20 kHz for 2 × 1 min with stainless steel beads (TissueLyser II Bead Mill, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and subsequently centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min. The homogenized tissue supernatant was further used for biochemical analyses.

Biochemical and histological studies

The degree of astrocytic injury and neuroinflammation in the hippocampus was evaluated by GFAP concentrations quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Mouse GFAP ELISA kit, ELK Biotechnology, Denver, CO, USA) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. To evaluate microglia-promoted inflammation in the hippocampus, tissue homogenate concentrations of Tumor Necrosis Factor alpha (TNF-α) were analyzed using a commercial ELISA kit (Mouse TNF-α DuoSet Elisa Kit, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). To evaluate total protein concentration in the tissue homogenate, a bicinchoninic acid kit (BCA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used according to manufacturer instructions. Protein concentration was calculated based on the standard calibration curve. Each biochemical analyte was normalized to total protein concentration of the tissue homogenate. All biochemical analyses were performed by an experimenter fully blinded to the experimental group assignments.

Serial sagittal brain sections (25 µm) were prepared using Leica CM1860 UV cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA) with brain tissue fixed using PolyFreeze optimal cutting temperature medium (Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA) during cutting. The brain sections were stored in a cryoprotective solution for histological and immunohistochemical analyses. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) histological staining was carried out to assess structural abnormalities and cell swelling and Perls Prussian blue staining was performed to evaluate the presence of cerebral hemorrhage, indicative of the BBB disruption. Immunohistochemical staining was performed on free-floating samples using IBA1 (rabbit recombinant monoclonal, 1:1000, 4 °C overnight, Abcam) for the total microglia population, and GFAP (rabbit recombinant monoclonal, 1:1000, 4 °C overnight, Abcam) primary antibodies for astrocyte population. Antibody staining was reacted with 3,3-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) with nickel ammonium sulfate color intensification. Representative images were obtained using a DM2500 LED confocal microscope with a Leica EC3 digital color camera (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) at 10X and 40X magnification, and recorded using LAS EZ v3.2.0 software (Leica Microsystems, Switzerland). The tissue processing, slide preparation, imaging, and all subsequent evaluations were performed by an experimenter blinded to the experimental groups.

Statistical analysis

To determine the normality of data distribution, the Shapiro–Wilk test was utilized. Two-tailed Student t-test was conducted in the short-term phase of the experiment to evaluate treatment and genotype effects in group comparisons. One-way ANOVA, followed by the Bonferroni post hoc correction in case of a significant omnibus effect was used in the long-term experiment to compare the animal groups, for the number of each comparison. Since a sufficient understanding of the long-term consequences following APAP overdose is lacking, potential factor interactions were not assumed and all groups in the long-term experiment were considered independent. Chi-squared test was used to evaluate the association between treatment and the presence of T2-w FSE in vivo MRI findings. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (v3.1.9). Based on large effect sizes observed in our prior internal data on APAP-induced liver injury, with a power of 0.8, the required sample size was set to 23 and 28 animals for the short- and long-term experiments, respectively. Statistical analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.4.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Liver damage

Macroscopic analysis of liver damage revealed morphological signs of acute liver injury in the short-term experiment after APAP administration. Dissected liver samples of APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice indicate signs suggestive of liver fibrosis and early tissue necrosis (Fig. 1A), while the liver samples obtained from the saline-treated WT and Shank3−/− mice demonstrate normative liver morphology. This is further supported by a reduced liver-to-bodyweight ratio observed in APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice (3.07 ± 0.4) 24 h after the injection, as compared to saline-treated Shank3−/− mice [3.33 ± 0.9, t(14) = 2.36, p = 0.04, Fig. 1B]. No such effect was observed in comparison between saline-treated groups [t(15) = -1.7, p = 0.1]. At 24 h after the injection, APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice showed a threefold higher plasma AST enzymatic activity (6.89 ± 1.8 µKat/l) compared to the saline-treated Shank3−/− mice [1.72 ± 0.09 µKat/l, t(14) = -2.75, p = 0.016, Fig. 1C] and a 30-fold higher ALT enzymatic activity (22.6 ± 2.1 µKat/l) in comparison to the saline-treated Shank3−/− mice [0.76 ± 0.11 µKat/l, t(14) = -10.54, p < 0.001, Fig. 1D]. In the long-term experiment, APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice indicated persisting morphological liver damage upon macroscopic evaluation of the dissected tissue (Fig. 1E). The animal groups also differed in the liver-to-bodyweight ratio [F(3,26) = 3.27, p = 0.037, Fig. 1F]. The APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice showed reduced liver-to-bodyweight ratio (2.9 ± 0.04) compared to saline-treated Shank3−/− mice (3.2 ± 0.03, p = 0.04), but the APAP-treated WT mice did not (3.06 ± 0.06) in comparison to the saline-treated WT animals (3.2 ± 0.04, p = 0.27) and neither in comparison to the APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice (p = 0.37). The groups differed in the enzymatic activity of AST in plasma [F(3,26) = 4.6, p = 0.01, Fig. 1G]. This effect was manifested entirely by the APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice, which showed elevated AST enzymatic activity (1.65 ± 0.1 µKat/l) in comparison to both saline-treated Shank3−/− mice (1.48 ± 0.09 µKat/l, p = 0.002) and APAP-treated WT mice (1.05 ± 0.12 µKat/l, p = 0.004). In the enzymatic activity of ALT, animal groups differed as well [F(3,26) = 3.7, p = 0.025, Fig. 1H]. APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice showed elevated plasmatic ALT activity (1.04 ± 0.13 µKat/l) compared to the APAP-treated WT animals (0.63 ± 0.06, p = 0.01), but not in comparison to saline-treated Shank3−/− mice (0.92 ± 0.1 µKat/l, p = 0.47). Lastly, APAP-treated WT mice showed reduced ALT activity compared to their saline-treated WT controls (0.9 ± 0.6 µKat/l, p = 0.008).

Neuromorphological abnormalities

The T2-w FSE in vivo MRI qualitative assessment in the short-term experiment revealed areas of hyperintense signal in 60% of the APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice, but not in the saline-treated animals [χ2(2) = 13.3, p < 0.001, Fig. S1A]. Observed signal hyperintensities spanned approximately 400 µm along the dorsoventral axis in the axial anatomical orientation of diencephalic gray matter (Fig. 2A–C). Quantitative analysis of the MRI brain atlas-derived regional quantitative R2* indicated that in comparison to saline-treated Shank3−/− mice, APAP-treated Shank3−/− animals exhibit elevated values in the amygdala [t(14) = − 5.5, p < 0.001, 49.4 ± 1.6 vs. 31.2 ± 2.8, Fig. 2G], basal ganglia [t(14) = -6.6, p < 0.001, 49.2 ± 2 vs. 33.6 ± 1.3, Fig. 2H], corpus callosum [t(14) = − 10.1, p < 0.001, 54.5 ± 1.2 vs. 35.5 ± 1.4, Fig. 2I], dentate gyrus [t(14) = -6.9, p < 0.001, 48.9 ± 0.8 vs. 34.2 ± 1.9, Fig. 2D], globus pallidus [t(14) = − 5.2, p < 0.001, 44.5 ± 2.3 vs. 28.1 ± 2.1, Fig. 2E], hippocampus [t(14) = − 6.7, p < 0.001, 54.1 ± 1.1 vs. 37.3 ± 2.2, Fig. 2F], and striatum [t(14) = − 6.7, p < 0.001, 49.3 ± 3.4 vs. 26.1 ± 0.9, Fig. 2J]. No significant differences were observed in the mentioned regions between saline-treated Shank3−/− mice and saline-treated WT mice. The H&E histological assessment showed signs of cell swelling in the diencephalic gray matter tissue of APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice (Fig. 3C), but not in saline-treated groups (Fig. 3A, B), suggesting the presence of cytotoxicity. Both saline-treated groups showed physiological IBA1+ microglia morphology (Fig. 3G, H). Behavioral testing in the long-term experiment revealed complementary group differences in social interaction regardless of the APAP treatment and only affected by genotype [F(3,26) = 13.2, p < 0.001, Fig. 6G]. The APAP-treated Shank3−/−mice spent less than half of the time interacting with the social partner mouse (62.8 ± 9.2 s.) compared to the APAP-treated WT mice (132.2 ± 10 s., p < 0.001), and same was true for the saline-treated Shank3−/− mice (65.3 ± 7.5 s.), as compared to the saline-treated WT mice (135.5 ± 14.9 s., p < 0.001). Social disinterest in the long-term experiment showed group differences [F(3,26) = 16.3, p < 0.001, Fig. 6H] as well, with an apparent interactive effect of genotype and treatment. Repetitive behavior

In the short-term experiment, a distinct genotype effect could be observed as regardless of the treatment the Shank3−/−mice spent more time self-grooming (Shank3−/− APAP, 101.7 ± 27.2 s., Shank3−/− saline 69.4 ± 10.3 s.) than the saline-treated WT mice [t(15) = − 8.9, p < 0.001, 16.7 ± 4.2 s., Fig. 6C], but did not differ from each other (p = 0.28).

The group differences in the long-term experiment [F(3,26) = 33.94, p < 0.001, Fig. 6I] were mainly manifested by the APAP-treated Shank3−/−mice, which spent over twice as much time self-grooming (166.9 ± 17.4 s.) than the saline-treated Shank3−/−mice (74.4 ± 9.7 s., p < 0.001) and the APAP-treated WT mice (50.8 ± 9.6 s., p < 0.001). Regardless of the APAP treatment, the saline-treated Shank3−/−mice spent generally more time in repetitive self-injurious grooming than the saline-treated WT mice (17.6 ± 4.6 s., p = 0.003).

Anxiety-like behavior, exploration, locomotor activity and short-term memory

No substantial treatment or genotype effects were observed in anxiety-like behavior recorded during the short-term experiment (Fig. 6D). Despite some nominal differences, APAP-treated Shank3−/−mice did not spend significantly less time in the open central zone (16.9 ± 6.1 s.) than the saline-treated Shank3−/−mice [t(14) = 1.04, p = 0.31, 28.6 ± 9.3 s.], nor did the saline-treated Shank3−/−mice compared to the saline-treated WT animals [t(15) = 1.09, p = 0.25, 42.1 ± 8.1 s.]. A treatment effect was observed in explorative rearing during the short-term experiment with APAP-treated Shank3−/−mice spending less time exploring their environments (89.1 ± 8.4 s.) in comparison to the saline-treated Shank3−/−mice [t(14) = 3.61, p = 0.003, 131.9 ± 8.3 s., Fig. 6E]. No such differences were observed in saline-treated groups.

Consistent with results observed in the short-term experiment, no group differences were observed in anxiety-like behavior in the long-term experiment either [F(3,26) = 2.12, p = 0.12, Fig. 6J]. Nevertheless, group differences were observed in explorative rearing [F(3,26) = 9.54, p < 0.001, Fig. 6K]. This effect was entirely explained by the APAP-treated Shank3−/−mice, which were interested in exploring their environment less (78.9 ± 7.9 s.) than the saline-treated Shank3−/−mice (130.7 ± 8.8 s., p = 0.001), and APAP-treated WT mice (122.4 ± 7.6 s., p = 0.002). No further differences were observed. Despite minor differences, no substantial effects were observed in locomotor activity during the short-term phase of the project (Fig. 6F), nor in the long-term experiment [F(3,26) = 1.53, p = 0.23, Fig. 6L]. Lastly, no statistically significant differences were observed in short-term memory performance among the experimental groups [F(3,26) = 3.32, p = 0.35, Fig. S1A] within the long-term part of the experiment.

Discussion

Liver injury due to APAP overdose is fairly common and besides the obvious effects on liver function, it affects the brain by pathomechanisms such as in hepatic encephalopathy. Here, we show that liver injury can have more pervasive functional consequences long-term than initially thought and they may be associated with disruption of the neurovascular unit and brain inflammation. Importantly, these challenges seem to be progressively worse in co-occurrence with the SHANK3 deficiency, prominent ASD risk gene, exacerbating deficits in sociability, repetitive behaviors, and reduced interest one month after liver injury.

Short-term consequences of the acute APAP overdose in Shank3−/− mice

An acute APAP dose of 350 mg/kg was able to consistently induce liver injury in Shank3−/− mice 24 h after the administration. Macroscopic observations of liver samples indicate distinct morphological damage to the tissue, including signs of advancing hepatic necrosis. This is further confirmed by reduced relative liver weight and elevated enzymatic activity of AST and ALT in the plasma of the APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice, consistent with previous results [47]. Interestingly, the Shank3−/− mice showed reduced AST activity even without the APAP treatment. This difference, however, is within the physiological values and might be likely attributable to the affected SHANK3 expression in the liver [48]. Fourteen hours after the administration, we detected the presence of cerebral edema via the T2-w FSE in vivo MRI in 60% of APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice. The incidence of brain edema is commonly associated with progressed hepatic encephalopathy developed due to ALF in human patients and animal models following APAP intoxication [49]. Among other effectors, hyperammonemia is perceived to be the main cause of cytotoxic cerebral edema development [24]. However, some studies suggest the development of such brain edema might be not only cytotoxic but also vasogenic in nature. Rats receiving a single high dose of APAP (750 mg/kg) showed signs of cortical edema as early as 6 h after the administration with elevated ammonia concentrations and blood–brain barrier disruption [50]. A similar study demonstrated such edema might be mainly vasogenic at early-to-middle stages and as the ammonia increases, cytotoxic edema ensues [51]. Additionally, authors showed this dynamic might be brain region-specific and apparent specifically in the cerebellum and subcortical areas. All cases of edema observed during the short-term phase of the current study were recorded in the diencephalic gray matter and were accompanied by over a twofold elevation of R2* in six neighboring subcortical brain regions (amygdala, basal ganglia, corpus callosum, dentate gyrus, hippocampus, and striatum). As R2* is particularly sensitive to magnetic susceptibility effects of ferrous iron found in hemosiderin due to its larger particle size, this finding suggests minor disruptions in surrounding cerebral vasculature and hemosiderin extravasation, resulting in the development of brain edema 18 h after the APAP. Indeed, the focal Perls Prussian blue positivity across the diencephalic matter confirms the presence of microhemorrhages in APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice, giving support to potential BBB disruptions. However, a certain degree of cell swelling can be observed in the diencephalic cortex of APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice via the H&E histological staining as well. As intracellular water intake would drive the R2* signal down, it is reasonable to suggest at 18 h since the administration, the edema, potentially vasogenic in origin, might precede the development of cytotoxic cell swelling, histologically observed at the 24-h time-point. Put together, this evidence indicates a time-dependent and possibly overlapping effect of neurovascular disruptions and cytotoxicity, consistent with the previously suggested findings [51]. Furthermore, the diencephalic gray matter of APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice showed signs of IBA1+ microglia dystrophy, and the accumulation of GFAP+ astrocytes was observed in the hippocampus of APAP-treated animals, undetected in saline-treated groups. The analysis of hippocampal tissue homogenates showed twofold higher GFAP and manyfold higher TNF-α concentrations, whereas an eightfold higher GFAP, and 14-fold higher S100B plasma concentrations were measured in APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice 24 h after the treatment, indicating the involvement of extensive neuroinflammation and signs of BBB disruptions altogether. A similar involvement of neuroinflammation in brain abnormalities after ALF has been reported in previous animal studies as well [52]. Surprisingly though, during the behavioral testing 14 h after the administration, no functional deficits were observed in APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice in addition to the reduced sociability and elevated repetitions, generally associated with the SHANK3 deficiency. It is worth mentioning that the APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice with a positive T2-w FSE finding on the in vivo MRI have generally clustered towards the more abnormal phenotype in all recorded behavioral characteristics despite not producing a large enough effect in group comparison. A previous study showed a single low dose of APAP can promote social behaviors as early as after 1 h in the BTBR mouse model of ASD [53]. However, given the half-life of APAP in tissue falls under 5 h even after overdosing [54] and our behavioral testing being performed 14 h after the APAP administration, the results need to be discussed in the context of functional consequences of brain pathology due to the APAP overdose, rather than as in acute behavioral effects of APAP itself. In this line, a recent article demonstrated deficient behavior 12 h after ALF was induced via thioacetamide (TAA) administration [55]. TAA is known to produce a more direct impact on ALF induction compared to APAP [56], therefore we believe the absence of functional deficits 14 h after APAP in the current study might be due to the insufficient time of effect.

Long-term consequences of the acute APAP overdose in Shank3−/− and WT mice

Morphological signs of hepatic damage could still be observable 1 month after the APAP administration in both Shank3−/− and WT mice, mostly consisting of focal tissue scarring, suggestive of recovering liver fibrosis. Plasma enzymatic AST and ALT activity analyzed at this time point indicated liver regeneration as well and the recorded differences are likely only of a statistical nature and within physiological values. Nevertheless, liver regeneration seems less progressive in APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice. The SHANK3 deficiency has been shown to disrupt the inhibitory properties of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS [57] and one of the key regulators of hepatic metabolism [58]. It is thus plausible that Shank3−/− mice can face considerable difficulties recovering from an extensive liver insult. Surprisingly, even 1 month after the administration the presence of cerebral edema could be observed via the T2-w FSE in vivo MRI in 83% of the scanned APAP-treated Shank3−/−, and 50% of the APAP-treated WT mice. Similar to the short-term experiment, a large number of these findings were localized in the diencephalic gray matter, while some were recorded surrounding caudate putamen and nucleus accumbens as well. It is important to highlight the extent of the edema did not seem to change considerably (spanning approx. 400 μm along the dorsoventral axis in both short- and long-term experiments). Interestingly, the Shank3−/− mice showed a considerably higher incidence of brain edema 1 month after the APAP treatment than the WT animals. The observations of the in vivo brain structure of Shank3−/− mice are generally lacking in the literature, but the SHANK3 deficiency was shown to be associated with whole brain structural abnormalities [59], which could provide a partial explanation of the elevated cerebral edema incidence. The presence of brain edema was accompanied by a persistently twofold higher R2* in basal ganglia, dentate gyrus, globus pallidus, and hippocampus in both APAP-treated groups. This was further confirmed by the focal positivity of Perls Prussian blue in the diencephalic cortex of both APAP-treated groups, suggesting the presence of tissue microhemorrhage and possible disruptions to the BBB. Importantly though, the histological H&E examination demonstrated no detectable cell swelling in the diencephalic gray matter of APAP-treated WT and Shank3−/− mice. The absence of cytotoxic cell swelling would suggest the observed cerebral edema might be exclusively of vasogenic origin. A similar finding was not experimentally proved by far to the best of our knowledge. Furthermore, the presence of IBA1+ microglia dystrophy in the diencephalic cortex and GFAP+ reactive astrocytosis was indicated in the hippocampus of both APAP-treated groups, although arguably more extensive in Shank3−/− mice. This apparent exacerbation of brain pathology in APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice was additionally supported by the elevated concentrations of GFAP and TNF-α in hippocampus tissue homogenates. Even though the APAP-treated WT mice with a positive in vivo T2-w FSE MRI clustered toward the more abnormal concentrations of both analytes as well, the group as a whole demonstrated comparable values to control mice. Regarding plasma biomarkers, both APAP-treated groups showed approximately fivefold higher GFAP and S100B concentrations. These findings suggest the neuroinflammatory response to ALF induced by a single APAP injection might persist even 1 month after liver injury, can be detectable in the brain as well as in the periphery, and seems to be more extensive in the context of SHANK3 deficiency. Similar persistence and compartmentalization of the neuroinflammatory response is consistent with the neuroimmune cascades described across other neurodegenerative and neurovascular pathologies in both humans and animal models [21]. Taken together with a constant presence of detectable edema and elevated R2* in neighboring tissue, a cycle of chronic neuroinflammation preventing the micro disruptions of BBB from resolving and vice versa could be hypothesized, but additional investigations are required. To our knowledge, this mechanism has been discussed, but no other study demonstrated similar findings so far [21, 60, 61]. In contrast with the results of the short-term experiment, behavioral testing 1 month after the APAP administration revealed functional consequences. As expected, deficits in sociability were generally observed in Shank3−/− mice, however, APAP treatment was further associated with worse social disinterest, but not social interaction in both Shank3−/− and WT mice 1 month after the administration. To some degree, this discrepancy might be associated with brain region-specific functions. While rodent social interaction is primarily based on socially seeking behavior operated predominantly by the medial prefrontal cortex and cingulate area [62, 63], social disinterest is mostly based on social and contextual memory, social attachment, and bonding behavior, functionally placed best in rodent hippocampus, nucleus accumbens, and amygdala [64–66]. The cerebral edema detected in APAP-treated animals of our study was detected surrounding the latter brain regions, with the former showing no imaging abnormalities. This could suggest the recorded structural disruptions might be at least partially associated with the deficits observed in social disinterest, but further investigation is warranted.

While some evidence shows lasting social difficulties following acute liver injury in human patients [67], previous concerns raised about maternal APAP use during pregnancy and subsequent ASD symptomatology in children [32, 33] were not supported by a recent large-scale clinical study [68]. In preclinical research, the topic is largely understudied. To name a few, both a single high dose and a repeated low dose of APAP have been shown to reduce social empathy in rats after 2 weeks [69] previously. However, as social behaviors in rodents are rather intricate, drawing conclusions based on two socially very different rodent species is burdened by inaccuracy [70]. Recent evidence has been brought up suggesting social deficits could be associated with neuroinflammation [71]. Elevated inflammation in the brain could thus also affect APAP-treated WT mice and exacerbate social deficits in APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice of our study. In repetitive behaviors, the Shank3−/− mice exhibited a twofold elevated self-injurious grooming over their saline-treated controls, while only a nominal increase was found in APAP-treated WT mice. It has been previously demonstrated that repetitive behaviors might be associated with the loss of SHANK in the nucleus accumbens [72, 73]. It seems that the presence of a structural abnormality in the surrounding tissue such as edema could perhaps further exacerbate the behavior in SHANK3-deficiency. Persistent long-term anxiety has been reported in APAP-induced ALF survivors in human studies before [74, 75], but little evidence can be found in preclinical research. Consistent with this, our results do not indicate any abnormal anxiety-like behavior 1 month following APAP overdose. Survival of ALF in human patients is associated with long-term hospitalization and liver transplantation, both resulting in experiencing high stress or even developing post-traumatic stress disorder [74]. Arguably, the anxiety experienced after APAP-induced ALF might be related more to the psychosocial factors than to the drug or its liver- and brain-associated effects. Furthermore, the Shank3−/− mice in our study demonstrated a 50% reduction in explorative behavior 1 month after the APAP injection, with no effects on locomotor activity.

Excess APAP during overdose was previously suggested to be taken up by the dopaminergic cells, elevating the risk for dopamine-related behavioral deficits long-term [76]. Besides other functional hallmarks, APAP-affected dopaminergic signaling might undermine rodent exploratory behavior [77], but also locomotion [78]. Despite a minor reduction in exploration of APAP-treated WT animals of the current study, the attenuative effect seems to be dominantly mediated by the SHANK deficiency. A recent article demonstrated deleting SHANK3 in dopaminergic cells affects the explorative behavior [79], giving support to the argument that the additive effect of APAP-affected dopaminergic signaling could produce reduced exploration, but not locomotion, specific to the Shank3−/− mice in the current study. Lastly, ALF and associated hepatic encephalopathy have been extensively reported to result in cognitive impairment short- and long-term in both human patients [80] and experimental animals [81]. Our results are not in line with this, as neither APAP-treated animal group showed considerably reduced short-term memory 1 month after the administration. Despite no evident differences in group comparison, the APAP-treated Shank3−/− mice just barely failed to pass the threshold for statistical significance exhibiting a lower short-term memory index. Arguably, given the large variation and the sample size of 6 mice per group, the effect might just not have been large enough to manifest. The existence of this trend solely in the APAP-treated Shank3−/− group might indicate a form of gene-specific interaction with the APAP-induced ALF effects on the brain. After all, SHANK3 deficiency is mainly present in the Phelan-McDermid syndrome [82], a genetic disorder known for manifesting by intellectual disability and the development of additional neurostructural abnormalities might exacerbate the symptoms further.

The project, however, needs to be understood within the scope of its limitations. A primary limitation would be the exclusive use of male mice. While this choice was guided by a well-documented male bias in ASD prevalence, it prevents the evaluation of any potential sex-specific effects, not only within the ASD framework, but also in APAP metabolism. Furthermore, our study design prioritized the investigation of the under-researched long-term effects, building upon previous body of knowledge regarding the acute APAP effects in WT mice. While this allowed for a more effective use of the available resources, we acknowledge that the absence of the APAP-treated WT group is a limitation preventing further investigation in the short-term experiment. Additionally, the study was designed to characterize neuropathological effects rather than to define precise mechanistic pathways behind them. A more thorough investigation into specific inflammatory mediators, distinguishing resident immune cells from the infiltrating monocytes, or exploring the role of damage-associated molecular patterns in depth are logical next steps. Methodologically, our interpretations also carry some constraints. The R2* is an indirect marker, and while our findings are suggestive of iron deposition and microhemorrhage, further investigation is warranted. Correspondingly, while Perls’ staining confirms the presence of extravascular hemosiderin, its non-stoichiometric nature prevents reliable quantification of the injury extent. On that note, the IBA1 and GFAP assessment relies on a semi-quantitative, count-based method performed with available tissue samples, limiting its reach. Lastly, the inclusion of automated multi-animal tracking for social interaction tests, as well as including the analysis of cerebrospinal fluid samples for biomarker evaluation, despite being technically challenging, are important goals for the future research.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that murine liver injury, developed by an acute APAP overdose, might lead to brain edema, potentially vasogenic in origin, as early as within 18 h and is associated with profound neuroinflammatory response in subcortical regions by 24 h after administration in Shank3−/− mice. The brain edema and neuroinflammation persist in these mice even 1 month after APAP injection. Whether one could contribute to the persistence of the other remains to be investigated, but such association could subsequently help to understand the long-term functional consequences in WT mice and the exacerbated ASD-like phenotype in Shank3−/− mice. Current results give additional preclinical support to the hypothesized role of neuroinflammation in the effects of APAP-induced liver injury and suggest its potential long-term neuromorphological and behavioral consequences in the context of preclinical ASD research.
