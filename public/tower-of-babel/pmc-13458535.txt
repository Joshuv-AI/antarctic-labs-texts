Adaptation and validation of the Chinese version of the Service Quality Questionnaire for Internet hospital services in China
Authors: Tao Han, Sijia Xing, Zihan Zhao, Leshu Sun, Yutong Liu, Yang Yang, Zhiruo Zhang, Sisi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid growth of Chinese Internet hospitals resulted in an urgent need for systematic service evaluation to better supervise and guide their development and application. However, most previous studies have focused on patients’ evaluation of Internet hospital service quality, and did not consider healthcare professionals as users of Internet hospital services. The aims of this study are to evaluate the service quality of Internet hospitals from the perspective of healthcare professionals by validating the Chinese version of Service Quality Questionnaire (SERVQUAL-C), and to assess the impact of service quality on healthcare professionals’ satisfaction with Internet hospitals. A cross-sectional survey was administered through face-to-face or online interviews from June to September 2022. Participants were 646 healthcare professionals (248 males, 398 females) employed at Chinese hospitals. Participants reported their attitude toward using Internet hospitals and rated their expectations and perceptions of the service quality of these hospitals using the translated Chinese version of the SERVQUAL. The SERVQUAL-C exhibited acceptable psychometric properties. Content validity was supported by expert evaluation from 50 experts in healthcare management, hospital informatics, and psychometrics, while structural validity was further supported by confirmatory factor analysis (χ² / df ≤ 4.798, CFI ≥ 0.959, TLI ≥ 0.952, RMSEA ≤ 0.085, SRMR ≤ 0.020). The overall reliability was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α > 0.980). Multiple linear regression suggested that age, hospital grade and the perceived service quality of Internet hospitals were significantly associated with healthcare professionals’ satisfaction with Internet hospitals. Furthermore, the association between perceived service quality and healthcare professionals’ satisfaction was conditional upon expected service quality (B = 0.198, P = .033), with the positive association only being significant for those who held high expectations of these hospitals. Finally, the association between perceived service quality and healthcare professionals’ satisfaction differed across medical departments (Bs > 1.049, Ps < 0.048), with internal medicine, pediatrics, medical technology, and surgery showing greater associations than the Internet hospital administration and Internet technology department (i.e., the reference group). The SERVQUAL-C has satisfactory internal consistency and acceptable content validity, and may serve as an evaluation tool to assess healthcare professionals’ perspectives of the service quality of Internet hospitals. Introduction

Development of Chinese Internet hospitals

The concept of an Internet hospital emerged in 2014 in China, and generally refers to a service platform that integrates consultation, prescription, payment, and drug delivery based on brick-and-mortar hospitals with a focus on online follow-up and routine consultations1,2. In 2018, the Chinese government stipulated Internet hospitals must be established on the basis of physical hospitals focusing on follow-up medical servicesand routine clinical consultations. These hospitals have been growing rapidly in China, and have beenexpected to improve the national availability and accessibility of medical resources while controlling the costs of distant medical services3. Subsequently, the Chinese government implemented various political interventions to promote the development and expansion of Internet hospitals 4–6. The COVID-19 pandemic also accelerated the development and acceptance of remote medical care, for which Internet hospitals played a key role7,8.

To date, more than 1,700 Chinese Internet hospitals have been established that offer a range of services9, including: patient registration, appointments, guided triage, and referral at the pre-visit stage; online consultation, remote payment, and medical records enquiry across institutions during treatment; and chronic prescription management, drug delivery, patient management, and remote health education after treatment. These services are based on various digital platforms for Internet hospitals, such as smartphone applications or mini programs built into social media (e.g., WeChat or Alipay). The service quality of the Internet hospital platform directly impacted healthcare professionals’ actual operational experiences, which in turn affected the satisfaction of both healthcare professionals and their patients10. The rapid growth of Chinese Internet hospitals was accompanied by an urgent need for systematic service evaluation to supervise and guide their development and application. However, to our knowledge, little research attention has been directed towards such evaluation.

Previous studies in Western countries largely focused on patients’ evaluation of the service quality of Internet hospitals11, but did not consider healthcare professionals as users of Internet hospitals services (and associated platforms). However, healthcare professionals use Internet hospital platforms to set up on-call schedules, manage patients’ visits, deliver (chronic) prescriptions, and provide post-treatment healthcare. Healthcare professionals’ user experiences are therefore as important as those of patients, as their experiences determine their use of Internet hospital services and offer critical insights into the long-term development of the industry.

The Service Quality Questionnaire (SERVQUAL): a promising evaluation tool

The SERVQUAL12 offers a methodology and tool for analysis, development, and measurement of service quality at a functional rather than a technical level13. In 1982, Gronruth proposed the concept of perceived quality of service, and posited the perceived quality model in 1984. In 1985, Parasuraman, Berry, and Zeithaml proposed a gap analysis model based on this perceived quality model to explore possible causes of service quality problems and help managers improve service quality14. In 1988, those authors simplified the model into a 22-item questionnaire with five dimensions12. In 1991, SERVQUAL questionnairewas further revised by changing all negative expressions into positive expressions15.

The SERVQUAL questionnaire comprises five dimensions (22 items)12: Tangibles, such as physical facilities, equipment, and appearance of personnel; Reliability, namely the ability to perform the service accurately and dependably; Responsiveness, namley the willingness to help customers and provide prompt services; Assurance, which covers employees’ knowledge, courtesy, and ability to convey trust and confidence; and Empathy, namely the caring and individualized attention provided to customers. The SERVQUAL questionnaire measures customers’ expected and perceived service quality to examine a serving entity’s excellence and superiority. These two perspectives, namely the expectation and perception, can be measured simultaneously or independently depending on the specific research purpose. SERVQUAL scores reflect the customer’s attitude towards the service provider rather than the objective service quality. Furthermore, the SERVQUAL can reveal discrepancies (i.e., score gaps) between what customers believe should be provided (i.e., expectations) and what they believe they received (i.e., perceptions), which is crucial for futher survice quality improvement.

In previous research, the SERVQUAL was widely applied to evaluate service quality in health-related issues and was found to be suitable to assess the quality of medical services13,16,17. Therefore, this study adoptsed the SERVQUAL to gain insights regarding the status quo of the service quality of Chinese Internet hospitals from healthcare professionals’ perspectives. Identifying the discrepancies between expected and perceived service quality can facilitate the application of Chinese Internet hospitals, and consequently support the overall development of the remote healthcare industry in China. Additionally, digital health interventions are generally more applicable to chronic disease management (e.g., internal medicine department) than to acute treatment scenarios (e.g., surgical or emergency departments). Against this backdrop, this study attempted to address the research gap regarding the differentiated applicability which has long been overlooked in the existing literature.

The present study

To briefly summarize, the purpose of this study was threefold: (1) to translate the SERVQUAL into Chinese (i.e., SERVQUAL-C) and validate its psychometric properties; (2) to evaluate health professionals’ expectations and perceptions of the service quality of Internet hospitals using the SERVQUAL-C; and (3) to explore the associations between the expected and perceived service quality of Internet hospitals and overall satisfaction with associated medical platforms. We expected that the SERVQUAL-C could serve as a suitable assessment tool for Internet hospital service quality in Chinese medical settings, and could predict healthcare professionals’ satisfaction with Internet hospitals.

Methods

Study design

This study was a cross-sectional study. The criterion for the sample size was based on a rule of thumb that at least 10 participants for each scale item should be included18. To achieve a comprehensive assessment and considering the interdisciplinary nature of our research, we also included additional scales in our survey instrument. The full questionnaire, therefore, encompassed a total of 35 items. Therefore, the sample size included in the study was at least 350. Considering a data attrition rate of 20%, this study aimed at a total of 438 participants.

Recruitment

This study recruited 646 participants to complete the survey via either face-to-face or online interviews. Advertisements were posted on mainstream social media, and flyers were distributed across the hospitals.

Participants

In total, 646 participants (248 males, 398 females) from 114 Chinese hospitals covering tertiary, secondary and primary levels were recruited via convenience sampling. The inclusion criteria were medical workers who provided medical services in Chinese medical institutions. We excluded 25 responses from the analysis which were incomplete or with excessively short response time.

Procedure

This cross-sectional survey was conducted from June to September 2022. Each interviewer received training regarding participants’ privacy and interest protection before data collection began. All eligible participants who consented to participate were informed about the study’s purpose and procedures and asked to respond to the e-survey on their mobile device in a self-completion manner during the interview. Research team members conducted quality control on submitted responses based on (1) and (2) a shorter-than-expected response time duration. Responses that failed based on either of these two criteria were excluded.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Public Health and Nursing Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Reference number: SJUPN-202203). All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. All eligible participants who consented to participate were informed about the study’s purpose and procedures and were asked to respond to the e-survey on tablets during the interview. All personal identifiers have been removed from the data to ensure anonymization. Participants did not receive any material or monetary rewards for participation. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Materials

Participants responded to a battery of surveys including the SERVQUAL-C, satisfaction with Internet hospital services, information regarding their affiliated hospitals, and their professional and demographic information.

Hospital information

Participants reported information regarding the hospital and department in which they worked via an open-ended question (i.e., “Which medical institution and department are you working for?”). Hospital grades (1 = Primary level, 2 = Secondary level, and 3 = Tertiary level) were then coded using the latest version of the Chinese Hospital Classification19: Primary level hospitals are usually rural hospitals containing fewer than 100 beds; Secondary level hospitals tend to be affiliated with a medium-sized city, county, or district and contain 100–500 beds; and tertiary level hospitals are referral general hospitals at the municipal, provincial, or national level with more than 500 beds. Participants’ departments were coded into seven main categories based on traditional medical classification (1 = internal medicine, 2 = surgery, 3 = obstetrics and gynecology, 4 = pediatrics, 5 = medical technology, 6 = Internet hospital administration and Internet technology (IT), and 7 = others)19. Departments with insufficient responses (< 5; e.g., pharmacy, rehabilitation, and traditional Chinese medicine) were clustered as “others”.

Service quality of internet hospitals

The 22-item SERVQUAL questionnaire covers five dimensions each with four or five items that are scored from 1 = very poor to 5 = very good. The five dimensions are: Tangibles (e.g., “The aesthetic degree of Internet hospital platform page design”), Reliability (e.g., “The reasonable degree of working hours and intensity in the Internet hospital”), Responsiveness (e.g., “The clear degree of feedback channels for Internet hospital working problems”), Assurance (e.g., “The degree of trustworthiness of the Internet hospital in the mind of healthcare professionals”), and Empathy (e.g., “Internet hospital managers’ personalized care toward healthcare professionals”). The SERVQUAL yields three scores. The perception (P) and expectation (E) scores are computed by averaging the 22 items from each perspective, and a gap score computed by subtracting the latter from the former (i.e., P − E).

The Brislin translation model was adopted in the validation of the Chinese version of the SERVQUAL (SERVQUAL-C)20. The original English language version was translated into Chinese by a senior postgraduate student in public health, and then back-translated to English by two other senior postgraduate students. The translated versions were then compared with the original version to check for inconsistencies. Next, five experts in public health were independently consulted to review, and revise when necessary, the items in accordance with the characteristics of Chinese Internet hospitals. They also comprehensively evaluated the feasibility, accuracy, and readability of the translated scale within Chinese social and linguistic context. Revisions were made to five items to better reflect the characteristics of Chinese Internet hospitals. For example, one item was revised from “Appropriateness of working hours in Internet hospitals” to “Appropriateness of workload in Internet hospitals”, as experts agreed that work intensity is critical in the fragmented, high-concurrency online consultation setting of Chinese Internet hospitals. The revised version was then pilot-tested with 30 healthcare professionals (whose data was excluded from the following analyses) who provided further feedback on the readability and comprehension of the scale. Based on the pilot results, minor wording adjustments were made. For example, based on pilot feedback, one item was revised from “Trust in the Internet hospital platform” to “Trustworthiness of the Internet hospital platform” to improve readability and comprehension. This procedure was repeated until the translated and revised version was deemed satisfactory by all authors. Content validity was achieved by expert evaluation such that 50 experts from healthcare management, hospital informatics, and psychometrics provided qualitative evaluation (i.e., appropriate vs. not appropriate) regarding the relevance and suitability of the scale for the study purpose. All invited experts deemed the scale suitable for the study; therefore, content validity was supported.

Satisfaction with Internet hospitals

Participants reported their overall satisfaction with Internet hospitals in general by answering the question “All things considered, how satisfied are you with Chinese Internet hospitals in general?” (1 = not at all satisfied to 10 = extremely satisfied).

Sociodemographic information

At the end of the questionnaire, participants reported their sociodemographic information, including gender (1 = male, 2 = female), age (1 = ≤ 29 years, 2 = 30–39 years, 3 = 40–49 years, and 4 = ≥ 50 years), marital status (1 = unmarried, 2 = married), education (1 = below bachelor’s degree, 2 = bachelor’s degree, 3 = master’s degree, and 4 = doctorate), occupation (1 = doctor, 2 = nurse, 3 = medical technician, 4 = Internet hospital administration and IT staff, and 5 = others), and annual income (1 = ≤¥100,000 (or €12,674); 2 = ¥100,001–¥200,000 (or €12,674–€25,338); 3 = ¥200,001–¥300,000 (or €25,338–€38,013); 4 = ¥300,001–¥400,000 (or €38,013–€50,688); 5 = ¥400,001–¥500,000 (or €50,688–€63,375); and 6 = ≥¥500,001 (or ≥€63,375)).

Statistical analysis

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to examine the theoretical model of the SERVQUAL-C using the R lavaan package21,22 with maximum likelihood estimation. Multiple goodness-of-fit indices were calculated: χ2 (the smaller the better); the ratio of χ2 to its degree of freedom (cutoff ≤ 5)23; root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; cutoff < 0.080)24; normative fit index (NFI) and comparative fit index (CFI; cutoff ≥ 0.950)25,26; and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR; cutoff < 0.050)27. The average variance extracted (AVE; cutoff ≥ 0.500)28 was used to evaluate convergent validity across the five dimensions. Square-roots of AVE values larger than any correlation among any pair of latent constructs suggested good discriminant validity. Finally, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between service quality and healthcare professionals’ satisfaction with Chinese Internet hospitals across different departments.

Psychometric analysis: Validating the SERVQUAL-Cquestionnaire

CFA was used to examine the validity of the expectation and perception perspectives of the SERVQUAL-C. The fit indices of the research model are reported in Table 3, and parameter estimations are shown in Fig. 1. The fit indices for the expectation perspective were slightly higher than the recommended values (viz., χ² / df = 5.640, RMSEA = 0.085), and most fit indices for both perspectives exceeded the cutoff values, indicating that the model generally fit the data well. The AVE values in the model were all higher than the recommended cutoff value of 0.50, which supported the convergent validity. The factor loadings were generally satisfactory. In addition, both expectation and perception perspectives exhibited excellent internal consistency (Table, 4; Cronbach’s α 0.924 and McDonald’s ω 0.924). No significant ceiling or floor effects were detected for the overall or subscale scores.

Service quality and overall satisfaction with Internet hospitals

The SERVQUAL-C score was then applied to predict healthcare professionals’ overall satisfaction with the Internet hospitals. Three multiple linear regression models were examined with the entry method (Table 6). Dependent variable was overall satisfaction. Model 1 included gender, age, marital status, education, occupation, annual income, and hospital grade to examine whether these variables were statistically relevant to overall satisfaction as suggested by previous studies. Only statistically significant variables were further included as the control variables in subsequent models, namely gender, age, marital status, occupation, and hospital grade. Model 2 further included the SERVQUAL-C scores to evaluate the predictive aspect of the expected and perceived service quality. Finally, Model 3 examined whether the prediction of perceived service quality varied across different expectation levels and medical departments by including the corresponding interaction terms. All continuous predictors were centered before creating the interaction terms.

Model 1 revealed that among the included control variables, only gender, age, marital status, occupation and hospital grade were significantly associated with healthcare professionals’ overall satisfaction (Bs < 1.058, Ps < 0.05). These variables were retained in the subsequent analyses.

Model 2 (R2 = 0.468, F (13;632) = 44.7, P < .001; see Table 6, middle column) revealed that age, hospital grade, and perceived service quality were significantly associated with healthcare professionals’ satisfaction, after adjustment for the control variables. Specifically, healthcare professionals from tertiary level hospitals were significantly more satisfied with their hospitals than those from primary level hospitals, whereas no difference was observed between healthcare professionals from primary and secondary level hospitals. Importantly, overall satisfaction was positively associated with the perceived service quality of Internet hospitals (B = 1.988, standard error [SE] = 0.103, P < .001).

Model 3 (R2 = 0.474, F (26;619) = 23.3, P < .001; see Table 6, right column) further revealed that the association between perceived service quality and healthcare professionals’ satisfaction was conditional on expected service quality (B = 0.198, SE = 0.093, P = .033) and medical department (Bs > 1.049, Ps < 0.048). A decomposition (see Fig. 2) of the interaction effect between perceived and expected quality suggested that the positive association between perceived service quality and overall satisfaction was only significant for those who held high expectations of Internet hospitals (B = 0.932, SE = 0.430, P = .030) but not for those with low expectations (B = 0.641, SE = 0.457, P = .161). Another decomposition (see Fig. 3) suggested that the perceived service quality of Internet hospitals was positively associated with overall satisfaction to a greater extent for participants from the internal medicine, pediatrics, medical technology, and surgery (in descending order) departments than those from the Internet hospital administration and IT department (i.e., reference group).

Discussion

This study evaluated the service quality of Internet hospitals from the perspective of healthcare professionals by validating the Chinese version of the SERVQUAL, and predicted the impact of expected and perceived service quality on healthcare professionals’ satisfaction with Internet hospitals. To summarize, the SERVQUAL-C exhibited acceptable psychometric properties and perceived service quality was significantly associated with healthcare professionals’ satisfaction with Internet hospitals. This association was conditional on expected service quality and differed across medical departments.

Psychometric properties of the SERVQUAL-C

One of our purposes was to revise the SERVQUAL questionnaire for Chinese remote healthcare settings and examine its validity and reliability. In general, the SERVQUAL-C was found to be a robust and suitable assessment tool for evaluating the service quality of Chinese Internet hospitals (χ² / df ≤ 4.798, CFI ≥ 0.959, TLI ≥ 0.952, RMSEA ≤ 0.085, SRMR ≤ 0.020). It was noteworthy that the discriminant validity across the five dimensions was somewhat unsatisfactory, as suggested by high dimensional correlations and AVE values in participants’ perceptions. However, ignoring the dimensional structure did not benefit the fit of the measurement model. The lack of discriminate validity for the perception perspective likely reflects the nature of Internet hospital services, where the five quality aspects are not independent in users’ minds. For example, a platform with good “Tangibles” (e.g., clear layout) may simultaneously enhance perceptions of “Reliability” and “Responsiveness”. Moreover, healthcare professionals may not clearly differentiate between these dimensions when rating their experiences, especially in a digital environment where features overlap. Therefore, while the five-factor model is theoretically meaningful and empirically supported by fit indices, we caution against over-interpreting individual dimension scores. Instead, we recommend adopting the overall SERVQUAL-C score to reflect the comprehensive service quality, while dimensional scores serve as supplementary indicators to identify targeted areas for improvement. However, given the fit indices, the SERVQUAL-C was deemed a promising measure, although caution should be used in interpreting dimensional scores.

Expected and perceived service quality of Chinese Internet hospitals

The dimensional scores of the SERVQUAL-C showed thatall gap scores were negative, meaning perceived service quality did not match the expected levels; additionally, the largest and smallest discrepancies between expected and perceived service quality were in the Assurance and Empathy dimensions, respectively. Our results suggested the service quality of Chinese Internet hospitals as perceived by healthcare professionals had not yet met expectations, especially considering the status quo of Chinese medical demands and resources. In addition, Empathy was the dimension with the lowest scores for expectations, perceptions, and gaps. This suggested that Internet hospital administrators have not paid sufficient attention to healthcare professionals and failed to meet their needs, with this corroborated by the Assurance dimension having the largest gap score. To address these gaps, concrete interventions are needed, such as making in-depth needs analysis for key departments (e.g., internal medicine, pediatrics) to understand their specific workflow requirements and developing customized platform features that truly support their clinical practice. Furthermore, implementing specialized online communication training programs could help healthcare professionals better convey their expertise and empathy through digital channels, addressing the significant gaps in both Assurance and Empathy dimensions. Practices such as these could ensure staff voices are heard and allow different, novel opinions from Internet hospital development to create smoother and more trustworthy consultation experiences for patients29,30. In addition, enhancing the ease of use and effectiveness of Internet hospitals will help to increase healthcare professionals’ motivation to use telemedicine31. Considering the large Chinese population, high acceptance of remote diagnosis and treatment by healthcare professionals appears to be conducive to the development of Internet hospitals, which would play a key role in improving the relationship between supply and demand in Chinese medical resources.

Expected and perceived service quality and overall satisfaction

Our results revealed that perceived service quality was positively associated with healthcare professionals’ overall satisfaction with Internet hospitals, whereas expected service quality was not. Importantly, the effect of perceived service quality on overall satisfaction was only present for those who held high expectations of Internet hospitals. In other words, healthcare professionals’ perceptions of the service quality of Internet hospitals were partially determined by the “expectation lens” held. Any hospital that intends to develop its Internet hospital services should consider managing staff expectations of digital healthcare approaches in advance to ease the application process32.

Our findings further indicated that hospital grade was associated with healthcare professionals’ satisfaction with Internet hospitals. As regional medical centers in China, tertiary level hospitals typically benefit from greater financial investment, more advanced technical support teams, and superior IT infrastructure. These resources enable the development, maintenance and promotion of more robust, user-friendly, and well-integrated Internet hospital platforms, which in turn enhance the operational experience, user engagement and satisfaction for healthcare professionals.

Our results also revealed that the association between perceived service quality and overall satisfaction varied by medical department. These results suggested that the current service quality of Internet hospitals was only satisfactory for staff who had direct contact with patients. In contrast, the routine work of administrative and logistic staff is likely to not involve much application of Internet hospital services as the common features are largely focused on the treatment process or patients. However, this does not refute the possibility of application in those departments33. For example, inpatients could require room supplements directly via Internet hospital modules for room care services, report their hospital satisfaction rating via modules for patient-centered care, or file medical complaints via modules for unpleasantness management. Modules such as these offer promise in implementing Internet hospital services across the full medical process. Finally, the strongest association between perceived service quality and overall satisfaction was observed for the internal medicine department, which somewhat aligned with the authors’ expectations. In departments that have a strong requirement for chronic, systematic, and automatic patient management, Internet hospital services save healthcare professionals’ management efforts. Feedback from these specialties is expected to advance the development of Internet hospital services, and digital healthcare in general, to another level.

Limitations

While the study could provide valuable insights into the evaluation of Internet hospital services from the perspective of healthcare professionals, there remained several limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design of the study limited the opportunity to further examine the potential bi-directional relationship(s) between IH service quality and healthcare professionals’ satisfaction. Yet this bi-directional relationship could shed light on how IH development should be advanced according to healthcare professional’s satisfaction as the latter could reflect critical service gap(s). Future studies could benefit from longitudinal designs to establish the consistency of the SERVQUAL-C questionnaire over time, and to reveal the changes and associated factors in critical service perspectives. Secondly, although the sample size was large, its composition may somewhat limit the generalizability of the findings. The participants were primarily recruited from public hospitals using convenience sampling without planned stratification (e.g., regarding hospital grades or geographic distribution). Consequently, the results may be more representative of tertiary and secondary hospitals in the regions surveyed. Future replication should actively recruit participants from a wider range of institutions (including private hospitals and primary care centers) and broader geographical areas to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Thirdly, the current investigation tentatively suggested that IH service quality and healthcare professionals’ satisfaction differed across different hospital departments. Yet it remains unclear what factor(s) might set the root for these differences. Future study might consider future mixed-methods research design (e.g., focus group interview) to gain a richer, more nuanced understanding regarding these differences for better development, maintenance, and promotion of the IH services. Furthermore, content validity was assessed only qualitatively without quantitative indices such as I-CVI or S-CVI. Future studies should supplement with quantitative content validity measures to enhance methodological rigor.

Conclusions

The SERVQUAL-C questionnaire offers a robust and suitable assessment tool to evaluate the service quality of Chinese Internet hospitals from the perspective of healthcare professionals, as it had satisfactory internal consistency and acceptable content validity. This study revealed noteworthy gaps between the expected and perceived service quality of Chinese Internet hospitals from healthcare professionals’ perspectives, which comprises a frequently ignored user group. This study also noted the potential implications of using such an assessment tool to niche-target service demerits for the overall improvement of the remote, digital healthcare industry.
