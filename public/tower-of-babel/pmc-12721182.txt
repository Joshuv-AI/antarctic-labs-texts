Decoding auditory cognition: Unraveling the corticostriatal-SNr circuit’s role in frequency discrimination accuracy
Authors: Zhao-Qun Wang, Peng-Hui Chen, Hui-Zhong Wen, Tian-Tian Luo, Yun-Xiao Lou, Yan-Dong Zhao, Tong Zhu, Guang-Yan Wu, Ying Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe anterior auditory field (AAF) is critical for auditory discrimination, though underlying neural pathways remain unclear. Using viral tracing, optogenetics/pharmacogenetics, and behavior, we investigated the corticostriatal-substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) circuit’s role in frequency discrimination. AAF excitatory neurons, synapsing on caudate putamen (CPu) GABAergic neurons, were highly active during tasks. Pharmacogenetic suppression of AAF→CPuGABA projections impaired discrimination performance. We further identified an AAF→CPu D1-medium spiny neuron (MSN) pathway regulating discrimination; its pharmacogenetic/optogenetic inhibition caused deficits. Histology confirmed CPuD1→SNr projections. Critically, suppressing/ablating AAF→CPu→SNr circuit neurons severely disrupted accuracy. Fiber photometry revealed SNrGABA neuron activation during tasks. Together, AAF→CPuD1 projections and the AAF→CPu→SNr circuit are pivotal for frequency discrimination, advancing mechanistic insights into auditory processing circuits.Subject areas: Sensory neuroscience, Cognitive neuroscience

Introduction

Sound serves as a critical external cue for sensory perception across numerous species and may constitute a source of early neonatal information.1 Auditory discrimination is essential for animal survival, development, and notably, mother-infant communication. The embryo possesses the capacity to perceive, respond to, and learn acoustic signals from parents and the environment.2 In humans, fetal auditory experience significantly influences infants’ neuroprocessing and learning abilities.3 Fetal exposure to certain types of language, such as the mother’s voice, can affect fetal development and postnatal behavior. Studies have demonstrated that mammal can learn to recognize and discriminate familiar sounds in the external environment by habituating their hearing and that the effects of early auditory experiences may persist, thereby influencing developmental trajectory and adaptive potential of offspring.4,5,6 Nevertheless, the neural circuit mechanisms underlying auditory discrimination remain poorly understood.

The auditory cortex serves as a critical hub for auditory processing, playing a key role in the processing and integration of auditory information.7 Previous studies have demonstrated its involvement in frequency discrimination.8,9 The anterior auditory cortex (AAF) is a core region of the auditory cortex, playing a pivotal role in auditory processing.10,11,12,13 Our prior work revealed that AAF-striatum projections are functionally involved in regulating auditory frequency discrimination.14 The dorsal striatum (caudate putamen [CPu]) has been implicated in movement, perceptual decision-making, and auditory discrimination.15,16,17,18 The caudal tail region of the dorsal striatum, known as the auditory striatum, integrates inputs from auditory pathways and is strategically positioned within the basal ganglia to facilitate the integration of real-time auditory information and support action selection.8,9,19,20,21 Specifically, optogenetic and pharmacological inhibition of D1-receptor-expressing neurons in the auditory striatum has been shown to impair choice performance accuracy.22 These findings collectively suggest that downstream brain areas of AAF, such as the striatum, are involved in regulating auditory discrimination.

Notably, substantia nigra-auditory striatum projections have also been found to play a regulatory role in auditory discrimination,22 highlighting the complexity of the neural circuits involved. However, the precise functional contribution of the AAF-striatum projection, along with its associated brain regions and the specific cell types involved in auditory discrimination, remains incompletely understood.

In the present study, we employed a combination of viral tracing, optogenetic and pharmacogenetic manipulation, and caspase ablation to investigate the role of corticostriatal-substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) circuit in a frequency discrimination task. Our findings shed light on the neural circuits mediating auditory frequency discrimination and provide insights into the functional architecture of auditory processing networks.

Results

We adapted a fear conditioning paradigm to assess frequency discrimination. Mice were first habituated to the training chamber (100 s exploration). During training, they received five pairings of a 12-kHz tone (CS+, 20 s, 75 dB) with footshock (UCS, 2 s, 0.6 mA; interleaved with 60 ± 20 s random intervals), followed by 2-kHz tones (CS−) without reinforcement. Mice learned to associate CS+ (12 kHz) with aversive outcomes, showing significantly higher freezing to CS+ vs. CS− during testing. This paradigm, adapted from our prior work,14 allowed us to quantify frequency-specific discrimination.

AAF→CPuGABA neurons were activated during auditory frequency discrimination

Our previous research demonstrated that AAF pyramidal neurons, particularly those projecting to the striatum (AAF→CPu), play a crucial role in frequency discrimination behavior.14 To further investigate the functional involvement of AAF→CPu projection neurons in auditory discrimination, we employed a frequency discrimination behavioral protocol, which was previously established.14 Retrograde tracing combined with the c-Fos immunohistochemistry revealed that the majority of CPu-projecting neurons in the AAF were co-labeled with c-Fos-positive cells (Figures 1A–1D). Quantitative analysis showed that 91.52% of CTB+ neurons in the AAF were colocalized with c-Fos+ neurons (Figure 1C).

To further characterize the neuronal type of AAF→CPu projections, we employed the Cre-recombinase-based anterograde transsynaptic labeling system.23 Specifically, AAV2/1-Cre was microinjected into the AAF, along with co-injection of a viral mixture consisting of GAD67-DIO-tdTomato and EF1α-DIO-EYFP into the CPu (Figure 1E). The Cre recombinase system, in combination with chemogenetic and optogenetic tools, enables selective labeling and manipulation of genetically defined neuronal populations, making it a powerful approach for dissecting circuit-specific functions. The γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-synthesizing enzyme glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) exists as two functionally distinct isoforms: GAD65 is predominantly localized to presynaptic terminals of GABAergic neurons, where it associates with synaptic vesicles and mediates rapid neurotransmitter synthesis and release. In contrast, GAD67 is widely distributed throughout the neuronal cytoplasm, maintaining basal GABA levels and playing a pivotal regulatory role in synaptic plasticity.24 In this study, the GAD67 promoter drives expression specifically in GABAergic neuronal cell bodies, allowing for cell-type-specific identification during neural circuit tracing of the AAF→CPu pathway.

Four weeks after viral expression, anatomical tracing revealed that it was predominantly GABAergic neurons in the CPu that received input from the AAF (Figures 1F and 1G). Statistical results showed that 83.57% of GAD67+ neurons in CPu were colabeled with EYFP neurons. These results demonstrate that GABAergic neurons in CPu receiving AAF input were activated during auditory frequency discrimination.

Pharmacogenetic inhibition of AAF→CPuGABA neural circuit significantly impaired frequency discrimination

Next, in order to investigate the role of CPuGABA neurons in tone processing, a pharmacogenetic approach of designer receptors that are exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs)25,26 was applied to evaluate their function. We then microinjected a Cre-dependent recombined AAV into the CPu encoding the inhibitory DREADD receptor hM4Di (rAAV2/9-GAD67-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry or rAAV2/9-GAD67-DIO-mCherry) and the anterograde AAV expressing wheat germ agglutinin (WGA)-Cre recombinase27 (AAV2/1-CaMKIIα-WGA-Cre) bilaterally into the AAF (Figures 2A–2C). WGA facilitates specific and efficient anterograde trans-synaptic transmission in neural circuits. Expression of a WGA and Cre recombinase fusion protein using a viral vector technology in Cre-dependent transgenic animals allows to trace neuronal network connections.

Four weeks later, mice underwent frequency discrimination behavioral testing to assess the functional contribution of CPu GABAergic neurons receiving AAF input (Figures 2D–2K). As expected, in mice expressing the inhibitory DREADD hM4Di-mCherry in CPu neurons, CNO injection (2 mg/kg, intraperitoneally [i.p.]) significantly affected the ability to discriminate between the two frequencies (Figures 2D and 2H), while vehicle-injected mice exhibited clear differences in fear behavior associated with auditory frequency discrimination (Figures 2E and 2I). These results suggest that GABAergic neurons in the CPu that receive input from the AAF play a critical role in modulating auditory frequency discrimination.

Four weeks later, mice were subjected to frequency discrimination behavioral testing to evaluate the functional contribution of D1- and D2-expressing neurons in the CPu (Figures 3D–3G, 4D–4G). In mice expressing the inhibitory DREADD hM4Di-mCherry in CPu D1-MSNs, CNO injection (2 mg/kg, i.p.) significantly impaired their ability to discriminate between the two auditory frequencies (Figures 3D and 3F), while vehicle-injected mice exhibited clear differences in fear behavior associated with auditory frequency discrimination (Figures 3E and 3G). These findings suggest that D1 receptor-expressing neurons in CPu may play a selective role in modulating auditory frequency discrimination.

Optogenetic inhibition of AAF→CPuD1 neural circuit significantly impaired frequency discrimination

To further validate the role of D1-receptor-expressing neurons of CPu in auditory frequency discrimination, we used optogenetic stimulation to inhibit AAF-CPuD1 circuit by microinjecting an antegrade AAV expressing Cre recombinase (scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre) into the AAF and a Cre-dependent AAV (rAAV2/9-D1-DIO-eNpHR3.0-EGFP or rAAV2/9-D1-DIO-EGFP) into the right CPu bilaterally (Figures 5A and 5B). NpHR (halorhodopsin) is a light-driven ion pump that is an inhibitory photosensitive protein in the tools of optogenetics. The mechanism of action is that chloride ions are transported into the cell through yellow light activation, thereby inhibiting the activity of neurons or cells.29

Caspase ablation of AAF→CPuD1→SNr neuronal activity significantly impaired frequency discrimination

We then aimed to determine whether caspase-mediated ablation of neurons in AAF→CPuD1→SNr pathway affects auditory frequency discrimination. To selectively eliminate D1 receptor-expressing neurons within this circuit, we bilaterally co-injected scAAV2/1-Cre into the AAF, rAAV2/9-D1-ConFon-taCasp3-EGFP (encoding the pro-apoptotic fusion protein taCasp3-TEVp31) into the CPu, and AAV2/9-Flp into the SNr (Figures 8A–8C). This dual recombinase-dependent strategy ensures precise targeting of neurons projecting from the AAF to the CPu that subsequently innervate the SNr via D1-MSNs. Behavioral testing revealed that caspase-mediated ablation of D1-receptor-expressing neurons significantly impaired frequency discrimination performance (Figures 8D and 8E). No significant differences were observed in total travel distance or locomotor speed during the open field test (OFT; Figures 8F and 8G), indicating that the manipulation did not affect motor function but specifically disrupted auditory discrimination ability.

The SNr GABAergic neurons were activated during the discrimination of different frequencies

To trace the AAF→CPu→SNr circuit and assess functional engagement of SNr GABAergic neurons during auditory processing, we employed a dual-viral transsynaptic labeling strategy. High-titer anterograde AAV2/1-hSyn-Cre was injected into the AAF, and a Cre-dependent reporter virus (AAV2/9-DIO-mCherry) was delivered unilaterally into the CPu. In parallel, a mixture of vGAT-Cre AAV (AAV2/9-vGAT-Cre), Cre-dependent TVA receptor (AAV2/9-DIO-mCherry-TVA), and rabies glycoprotein (AAV2/9-DIO-RVG) was injected into the SNr. Three weeks later, EnvA-pseudotyped, glycoprotein-deleted rabies virus (RV-EnVA-ΔG-EGFP) was delivered to the same SNr region, and mice were perfused 7 days post-rabies injection (Figures 9A and 9B). Histological analysis revealed co-localization of mCherry and RV-EGFP in CPu neuronal somata, confirming that AAF-originating projections to the CPu predominantly target SNr-projecting GABAergic neurons (Figures 9C and 9D).

To further evaluate the functional activity of these SNr GABAergic neurons during auditory frequency discrimination, we performed fiber photometry to monitor intracellular Ca2+ dynamics in vivo (Figures 10A–10D). We microinjected rAAV2/9-D1-DIO-GcaMP7b or rAAV2/9-D1-DIO-EGFP into the right SNr and implanted an optical fiber above the injection site. Four weeks after virus injection, Ca2+ signals were recorded during presentation of two distinct auditory cues (CS+: 12 kHz; CS−: 2 kHz) known to elicit frequency discrimination behavior (Figure 10B). Fluorescence traces were aligned to tone onset, revealing a rapid increase in GCaMP7b signal following stimulus presentation (Figures 10E–10H). The increase in fluorescence signal was reliably accompanied by tone discrimination behavior and lasted for a few seconds. In contrast, no significant fluorescence change was detected in the EGFP control group (Figures 10I–10L). Statistical analysis showed a significant difference in the mean ΔF/F0 induced by 12-kHz tone between the GCaMP7b and EGFP groups (Figure 10F), whereas the mean ΔF/F0 induced by 2-kHz tone did not differ significantly (Figure 10J). Mice accurately discriminated between the two pure tones. Together, these results demonstrate that SNr GABAergic neurons exhibit activity correlated with auditory frequency discrimination.

Discussion

The AAF stands as a vital region within the auditory cortex, differing from the primary auditory cortex (A1) in its role in processing of sound and auditory discrimination tasks.32,33 Our prior research has highlighted the significance of the AAF and its projections to the striatum in auditory frequency discrimination.14 However, the contribution of specific striatal subregions and neuronal subtypes to this process remains poorly understood. Here, we systematically investigated the functional role of AAF→ CPuGABA, AAF→CPuD1, and AAF→CPuD1→SNr pathways in auditory discrimination using c-Fos staining, virus circuit tracing, optogenetic and pharmacogenetic manipulation, and caspase ablation. Our finding established that corticostriatal neurons originating in the AAF and their striatonigral projections—particularly the AAF→CPuD1→SNr circuit—play a critical role in regulating auditory frequency discrimination behavior.

Auditory discrimination of complex sounds involves the participation of multiple brain regions, including the auditory cortex.34,35,36 Using retrograde tracing combined with c-Fos immunofluorescence staining, we confirmed the involvement of AAF→CPu projections in auditory discrimination. Utilizing trans-synaptic viral tracing, we further demonstrated that the majority of projections from AAF target GABAergic neurons in CPu were GABAergic neurons (Figure 1), consistent with prior anatomical and functional studies.17,32,37,38 Pharmacogenetic inhibition of AAF→CPuGABA projections significantly impaired frequency discrimination performance (Figure 2), underscoring their functional relevance. Although cortical inputs may also engage striatal interneuronal microcircuits,39 which receive excitatory synaptic inputs, the contribution of these local circuits to auditory discrimination remains to be fully elucidated and warrants further investigation.

D1- and D2-expression MSNs, the two most principal subtypes of GABAergic neurons in the CPu, participate in diverse neural activities, including motor control, cognitive modulation, addiction, and auditory-related behavioral regulation.17,20,22,37,40,41,42 However, the specific contributions of AAF→CPuD1 and AAF→CPuD2 projections to auditory frequency discrimination were previously unknown. In this study, we found that pharmacogenetic inhibition of D1-MSN in the CPu originating from AAF significantly impaired frequency discrimination (Figure 3), whereas analogous inhibition of D2-MSNs had no significant effect (Figure 4). Furthermore, optogenetic inhibition of AAF→CPuD1 led to a marked attenuation of tone frequency discrimination behavior (Figure 5), indicating that D1-MSN within the AAF→CPu circuit play a pivotal role in modulating auditory frequency discrimination. These findings suggest that striatal D1-MSNs contribute not only to motor and learning/memory processes43,44 but also to higher-order auditory information processing.

The midbrain SNr, a key downstream target of the striatum, has been implicated in the regulation of auditory-related behaviors.22,41 Consistent with established circuitry, the CPu-SNr projection serves as a direct pathway involved in regulation of movement, cognition, and other functions.17,42,44,45 The striatum is a central hub for sensorimotor integration, with neural signals transmitted via distinct efferent pathways to downstream nuclei. The direct pathway originates from GABAergic spiny projection neurons (SPNs) expressing the D1 receptor, which project to SNr and the internal segment of globus pallidus (GPi).46,47,48 In this study, we confirmed the existence of direct CPu-SNr projections using both anterograde and retrograde tracing methods (Figure 6). By combining pharmacogenetic manipulation with Cre-dependent-caspase-mediated ablation, we functionally interrogated the role of the AAF→CPuD1→SNr circuit in auditory discrimination. Our results demonstrate that this cortico-striato-nigral pathway is critically involved in modulating auditory discrimination behavior (Figures 7 and 8). Nevertheless, the contribution of dopaminergic signaling to auditory discrimination remains incompletely understood and requires further investigation. While the caudal dorsal striatum (CDS) has been implicated in fear generalization,49 our data suggest that the anterior auditory striatum (AAF)-CPu-SNr circuit primarily mediates frequency discrimination. This distinction aligns with anatomical evidence showing AAF projections preferentially target CPu sensorimotor subregions involved in auditory processing, rather than CDS fear circuits. Future studies using intersectional genetics or optogenetic tagging could further disentangle circuit roles. Real-time calcium imaging revealed dynamic fluorescence changes in SNr GABAergic neurons during auditory discrimination tasks in mice, demonstrating their selective activation during this cognitive process (Figures 9 and 10). The dissociation between SNr Ca2+ activity and freezing behavior is evidence that SNr neurons are not necessary for the expression of freezing per se but may instead contribute to the sensorimotor transformation or contextual gating of defensive responses in a frequency-discrimination behavior. SNr neurons act as a “threat gate” integrating AAF→CPu inputs to amplify freezing only for CS+ (aversive) tones. CS−-evoked freezing, though behaviorally present, reflects non-specific arousal or downstream mechanisms, not SNr recruitment. This interpretation is supported by established literature on fear circuitry.50,51 Dopaminergic (DA) neurons are well established as key mediators of reward-based reinforcement learning.52 Notably, DA inputs to distinct striatal subregions exhibit marked functional heterogeneity.53,54,55 For instance, DA neurons projecting to the ventral striatum (VS) display activity patterns consistent with classical reward prediction error signaling, whereas those innervating the tail of the striatum respond to diverse stimuli including rewards, aversive cues, and neutral events.54,56 This contrasts sharply with VS-projecting DA neurons that respond selectively to rewards or reward-predictive cues. Such regional specialization implies that striatal dopamine signaling—and its downstream receptor systems (e.g., D1 and D2)—may exert distinct and context-dependent influences on diverse behavioral processes. In this study, the mice showed similar freezing responses to both CS+ and CS− tones after some circuit manipulations, also suggesting a dysregulation of fear generalization (i.e., over generalized). They showed CS+ and CS− responses were different in SNr; however, we did not manipulate the SNr neurons. It thus remains possible that silencing CPu-D1 neurons can affect a fear generalization circuit outside SNr (SNr is not the only target of CPu), which remains to be tested in the future.

Our study demonstrates that AAF→CPuGABA projections are functionally engaged in auditory frequency discrimination and that the AAF→CPu→SNr circuit plays a crucial role in mediating this behavior. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the precise neuronal circuits underlying auditory discrimination behavior.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides compelling evidence for the role of the AAF→CPu→SNr circuit in auditory frequency discrimination, several limitations should be considered. First, the behavioral assessment relied exclusively on a fear conditioning-based paradigm; whether these findings generalize to other forms of auditory discrimination or natural auditory behaviors remains to be tested. Second, although cell-type-specific manipulations were used, the potential influence of the experiments on collateral projections from the targeted neurons or local microcircuits cannot be entirely ruled out. Third, the study primarily employed loss-of-function approaches (inhibition, ablation); gain-of-function experiments could further strengthen the causal claims about circuit sufficiency. Finally, the research was conducted exclusively in male mice, and potential sex differences in the neural mechanisms of auditory discrimination were not explored. Addressing these aspects in future work will help to fully delineate the functional scope and specificity of this corticostriatal-SNr pathway.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

C57BL/6J mice (male, 6–8 weeks, 25–28 g) were provided by the Laboratory Animal Center at the Army Medical University. All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with institutional animal welfare guidelines and were approved by the Army Military Medical University Animal Care and Use Committee. Animals were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering.

Auditory frequency discrimination behavior

The auditory stimuli and electrical signals were generated using a SuperFcs system (XinRuan, Shanghai, China), following established protocols from our prior research.14 Behavioral freezing was quantified as the absence of movement (excluding respiration) persisting for ≥0.5 s, with cumulative freezing duration during sound presentation automatically calculated by the VisuFcs software.

Training

Following established protocols from our laboratory,14 mice were acclimated in a training chamber (28.5 × 32 × 52.5 cm) during a 100-s exploration period for environmental habituation. After this baseline interval, animals received five paired presentations consisting of: (1) a 20-s pure tone (75 dB SPL) serving as the conditioned stimulus (CS), and (2) a 2-s footshock (0.6 mA) as the unconditioned stimulus (UCS), with both stimuli terminating simultaneously. Pairings were delivered at randomized intervals of 60 ± 20 s. During training sessions, mice learned to associate specific sound frequencies (12-kHz pure tones) with the aversive stimulus. Following completion of five conditioning trials, mice were returned to their home cages using dedicated transfer enclosures.

Behavior test

In the initial conditioning phase, mice learned to associate a 12-kHz pure tone with an aversive footshock stimulus.14 On the third day, mice were transferred using a dedicated cage to a distinct testing environment (25 cm × 25 cm × 40 cm). The mice were allowed to explore the testing chamber for 100 s (baseline), after which the pure tone cue (CS+, 12 kHz,75 dB), the same as that used in the training session was delivered continuously for 100 s. After 30 min, a 100-s pure tone (2 kHz, 75 dB) that was not the conditioned stimulus (CS−) was delivered in a different, custom-made chamber (30 cm × 20 cm × 40 cm). To enhance environmental distinctiveness between testing contexts, the floor of each behavioral chamber was lined with either filter paper or crushed corncob bedding material.

Freezing time was measured when a lack of movement (except respiratory movements) lasted for 0.5 s or more based on detections by the SuperFcs system (XinRuan, Shanghai, China). The percentage of freezing time was calculated as the ratio between the total freezing time during the sound presentation and the duration of the entire sound presentation. The percentage was assessed based on the behavior of each animal, and the total percentage of freezing time was compared between groups.

Method details

Stereotaxic surgery and viral injections

The injection sites of AAF were carefully determined through a combination of extensive patch-clamp electrophysiology on the mouse auditory cortex and references to established literature. As previously validated in our lab, these coordinates align with both anatomical and functional characterization of AAF.11,12 Mice were maintained under surgical anesthesia using isoflurane (1–1.5% isoflurane/oxygen) and securely positioned in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China) as described previously.13,57,58 The Viral vectors or cholera toxin subunit B488 (CTB488, C34775, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) or cholera toxin subunit B594 (CTB594, C34777, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) was delivered through borosilicate glass micropipettes (10–20 μm tip diameter) using a computerized microinjection system (Nanoject Ⅲ, Drummond Scientific). Injection parameters were precisely controlled at 15 nL/min delivery rate, followed by a 5-min post-injection diffusion period before micropipette withdrawal. Viral injections were performed at the following stereotaxic coordinates: AAF, AP -2.30 mm, ML ±3.80 mm, DV 1.0 mm; CPu, AP -1.06 mm, ML ±2.93 mm, DV 2.50 mm; SNr, AP -3.27 mm, ML 1.25 mm, DV -3.90 mm.

Mice were microinjected with 150 nL of CTB488 (0.1%, w/v) into the right striatum to allow retrograde tracing of the AAF–CPu projections, and 14 days were allowed for retrograde tracer transport (Figure 1A).

To determine the cell type of the downstream region striatum of the AAF, mice were microinjected with 80 nL of ScAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre [titer: 1.04 × 1013 genome copies GC/mL, BrainVTA, China] into the right AAF; the mice were also unilaterally microinjected with 150 nL mixed viruses of rAAV2/9-EF1a-DIO-EYFP (titer: 5.93 × 1012 GC/mL, Obio Technology, China) and rAAV2/9-GAD67-DIO-tdTomato (titer: 3.05 × 1013 GC/mL, Obio Technology, China).

Mice were microinjected with 150 nL rAAV2/9-GAD67-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry (titer: 3.93 × 1012 GC/mL, Obio Technology, China) to pharmacogenetically inhibit striatum inhibitory neuron activity during auditory frequency discrimination behavior or 150 nL rAAV2/9-GAD67-DIO-tdTomato (titer: 3.05 × 1012 GC/mL, Obio Technology, China) as a control into the bilateral CPu. The mice were also bilaterally microinjected with 80 nL AAV2/1-CaMKIIα-WGA-Cre (titer: 1.24 × 1013 GC/mL, Obio Technology, China) into each side of the AAF (Figure 2B).

For pharmacogenetic inhibition of CPu D1 receptor neuronal activity during auditory frequency discrimination behavior, mice were microinjected with either 150 nL of rAAV2/9-D1-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry (titer: 5.69 × 1012 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China). The mice were also bilaterally microinjected with 80 nL ScAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre (titer: 1.04 × 1013 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into each side of the AAF (Figure 3B).

For optogenetic inhibition of CPu D1 receptor neuronal activity during auditory frequency discrimination behavior, mice were microinjected with either 150 nL of rAAV2/9-D1-DIO-eNpHR3.0-EGFP-WPRE-hGH polyA (titer: 5.34 × 1012 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China)) into the bilateral AAF (Figure 4B). The mice were also bilaterally microinjected with 80 nL ScAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre (titer: 1.04 × 1013 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into each side of the AAF (Figure 4B).

For pharmacogenetic inhibition of CPu D2 receotor neuronal activity during auditory frequency discrimination behavior, mice were microinjected with either 150 nL of rAAV2/9- D2-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry (titer: 5.12 × 1012 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into the bilateral AAF (Figure 5B). The mice were also bilaterally microinjected with 80 nL ScAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre (titer: 1.04 × 1013 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into each side of the AAF (Figure 5B).

For anterograde tracing of the CPu-SNr projections, mice were microinjected with 150 nL rAAV2/9-D1-EGFP (titer: 4.92 × 1012 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into the right CPu, and 4 weeks were allowed for anterograde tracer transport (Figure 6A). Mice were microinjected with 150 nL of CTB594 (0.1%, w/v) into the right SNr to allow retrograde tracing of the CPu–SNr projections, and 14 days were allowed for retrograde tracer transport (Figure 6D).

For pharmacogenetic inhibition of expressing D1 receptor neurons activity in AAF-CPu-SNr circuit during auditory frequency discrimination behavior, mice were microinjected with either 150 nL of rAAV2/9- D1-Con/Fon-hM4Di-mCherry (titer: 5.12 × 1012 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into the bilateral CPu (Figure 7B). The mice were also bilaterally microinjected with 80 nL ScAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre (titer: 1.04 × 1013 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into each side of the AAF and 100 nL rAAV2/R-hSyn-FLP-WPRE-hGH polyA (titer: 5.22 × 1012 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into each side of the SNr (Figure 7B).

For caspase ablation of expressing D1 receptor neurons in AAF-CPu-SNr circuit during auditory frequency discrimination behavior, mice were microinjected with either 150 nL of rAAV2/9-D1-Con/Fon-taCasp3-TEVp-P2A-EGFP-WPRE-hGH polyA (titer: 5.00 × 1012 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into the bilateral CPu (Figure 8B). The mice were also bilaterally microinjected with 80 nL ScAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre (titer: 1.04 × 1013 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into each side of the AAF and 100 nL rAAV2/R-hSyn-FLP-WPRE-hGH polyA (titer: 5.22 × 1012 GC/mL, BrainVTA, China) into each side of the SNr (Figure 8B).

Retrograde trans-monosynaptic tracing of the AAF→CPu→SNrGABA circuit was performed using rabies virus (RV). Eighty nanoliters (nL) of high-titer ScAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre (titer: 1.04 × 1013 GC/mL; BrainVTA, China) was injected into the right auditory association field (AAF). Concurrently, one hundred and fifty nL of the Cre-dependent virus rAAV2/9-DIO-mCherry (titer: 2.09 × 1012 GC/mL; Obio Technology, China) was injected into the ipsilateral caudate putamen (CPu). Subsequently, one hundred and fifty nL of a mixture of AAV helper vectors was injected into the right substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) at coordinates. The AAV helper mixture consisted of: rAAV2/9-VGAT1-Cre (titer: 2.74 × 1012 GC/mL; Obio Technology, China);rAAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-RVG (titer: 5.29 × 1012 GC/mL; BrainVTA, China), and rAAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-mCherry-F2A-TVA (titer: 5.84 × 1012 GC/mL; BrainVTA, China). At least 21 days post-injection of the AAV helpers, one hundred nL of RV-CVS-EnvA-ΔG-EGFP (titer: 2.5 × 108 IFU/mL; BrainVTA, China) was injected into the same SNr site (Figure 9B).

Fluorescence calcium activity in SNr GABAergic neurons during auditory discrimination was recorded using in vivo fiber photometry. In Vgat-Cre transgenic mice, 100 nL of the viral vector rAAV2/9-DIO-jGCaMP7b-WPRE-hGH-polyA (titer: 4.65 × 1012 GC/mL; BrainVTA, China) was unilaterally microinjected into the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) (Figure 10B).

Chemogenetic manipulations

To attain pharmacogenetic inhibition, mice expressing hM4Di-mCherry or mCherry (control) were injected intraperitoneally with 2 mg/kg clozapine N-oxide (CNO, HY-17366, MCE) [diluted with 5% DMSO (D5879, Sigma–Aldrich, Saint Louis, USA) and saline], and the behavioral test was implemented 30 min after CNO injection.

Optical fiber implantation

Mice were fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus after being anesthetized with isoflurane (1–1.5% isoflurane/oxygen). Then, for fiber photometry recording, the optical fibers (ceramic ferrule: diameter 2.50 mm; optical fiber: 100 μm core diameter, 0.37 NA) were implanted 200 μm above the viral injection site in the CPu, and two asymmetric cranial screws were fixed to the skull, while the skull surface was milled with a cranial drill for dental cement fixation. The optical fibers were fixed to the skull with dental cement immediately after the virus injection. The animals were allowed 4 weeks of recovery from the surgery and expression of the virus.

Optogenetic manipulation

The parameters of 594 nm laser for optogenetic activation were set as: 10 ms light pulse at 0.001 Hz with laser power 15 mW at fiber tip (NEWDOON, Aurora-200, China). The duration of optogenetic stimulation:210 s for the auditory discrimanation behavior test. Animals were sacrificed after completion of experiments to confirm specificity of viral expression and fiber placement.

Fiber photometry

The mice underwent fear conditioning training using the SuperFcs system (XinRuan, China). During training sessions, animals received paired presentations of a 12 kHz pure tone (75 dB; conditioned stimulus, CS+) with foot shocks. Following behavioral training, neural activity in the SNr was monitored using a fiber photometry system (Inper, China). The recording protocol employed dual-wavelength excitation: a 470-nm laser served as the primary excitation source for GCaMP fluorescence, while a 405-nm reference channel provided motion artifact correction and signal validation. Before recording sessions, animals were acclimated for 5 min in the test chamber following fiber-optic patch cable connection to the implanted cranial ferrule Neural responses were recorded during presentation of auditory stimuli: The conditioned tone (CS+: 12 kHz, 75 dB, 200 s duration) and a control tone (CS−: 2 kHz, 75 dB, 200 s duration) presented after a 30-min interstimulus interval.

The fiber photometry statistics were analyzed using InperDataAnalysis software (Inper, China). Fluorescence signal changes (ΔF/F0) was computed according to the standard formula: ΔF/F0 = (F – F0)/F0, where F represents the raw fluorescence signal at each time point (analyzed from 2 s pre-stimulus to 10 s post-stimulus onset), and F0 corresponds to the median fluorescence during the baseline period (−2 to 0 s relative to stimulus onset). The ΔF/F0 values are presented as heatmaps and plots with shaded areas that indicated the SEM to visualize the change in fluorescence. For quantitative analysis, we derived Mean response amplitude from the ΔF/F0 traces which were average ΔF/F0 value during the 0–4 s post-stimulus window.

Open field test

In order to evaluate the effect of neuronal apoptosis on the motor ability of animals, we used open field test to examine the total distance and speed of the animals in central district and surrounding areas. The OFT was performed in a square chamber (50 × 50 cm) with 45 cm high walls. The white floor of the chamber is made up of sixteen squares: the central four small squares are named the center zone, and the others are named peripheral zones. Before and after the test, the floor was cleaned with 75% alcohol, and during the test, each mouse was placed in the center zone and then allowed to freely move for approximately 5 min. The motion trials were recorded by a video camera during the experiment, and the data were acquired and analyzed by VisuTrack 2020 (Xinruan, Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence

For the assessment of endogenous c-Fos expression induced by auditory frequency discrimination behavior in the AAF, mice received auditory frequency training and test, and their brains were collected 90 min after testing (Figure 2A). The immunofluorescence staining protocol was designed as described below. First, the mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.01 mol/L, pH 7.4) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were stored in 4% PFA at 4°C for 24 h, and then transferred to a 15% sucrose solution in which they were stored at 4°C for 48 h. The brains were then cut into 30 μm frozen sections (CM1900, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) that were collected in PBS and rinsed three times in PBS for 15 min at room temperature, incubated in PBST (PBS +0.1% Triton X-100) with 3% normal bovine serum albumin for 1 h, and then incubated with primary antibody for 24 h at 4°C (rabbit anti-c-Fos, 1:500, 226003, Synaptic Systems, Goettingen, Germany). The sections then underwent three washing steps for 10 min each in PBS and were incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 h (goat anti-rabbit conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488, 1:500, Invitrogen). The sections were washed with PBS (thrice, 10 min) at room temperature, incubated with DAPI (1:2000, D9542, Sigma–Aldrich) for 15 min, then subjected to three more washing steps of 10 min each in PBS, followed by mounting and coverslipping on microscope slides. c-Fos expression was verified using a scanning laser microscope (SpinSR, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The number of c-Fos-positive neurons was analyzed by ImageJ (1.52a, Bethesda, USA).

Histology

After the behavioral test experiments, brain sections were collected using the methods described above in Immunofluorescence section. Sections 30 μm thick were washed with PBS (thrice, 10 min), incubated with DAPI (1:2000, D9542, Sigma–Aldrich) for 15 min, and then subjected to an additional 3 wash steps of 10 min each in PBS. Histological verification of virus expression and CTB488 and CTB594 tracing were obtained using a full slide scanning system (VS200, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and scanning laser microscope (SpinSR, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Prior to the use of parametric tests, the distribution of the data was tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data meeting the assumption of normality were analyzed with Student’s t-tests. Statistical significance was determined using the paired Student’s t test, unpaired Student’s test, or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test using SPSS software for Windows (v. 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, USA). All data are shown as the mean ± SEM; N.S., no significant difference, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
