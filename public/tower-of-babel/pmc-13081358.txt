Beyond the evidence: The cervical collar as cultural artefact
Authors: Tim Nutbeam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical collar application remains prevalent in trauma care despite the absence of evidence of neurological benefit from spinal immobilisation. Clinical behaviour in this area has proven resistant to change, and the persistence of the collar requires explanation beyond the evidential debate alone.The fear of cervical injury has origins that predate clinical practice. Neurobiological preparedness theory suggests that threat associations linked to survival-relevant stimuli are more rapidly acquired and more durably maintained than other learned fears; the cervical region is a plausible candidate for this form of prepared response. This disposition has been reinforced by centuries of cultural practice: capital punishment across civilisations has concentrated on the neck, and its apparatus has entered clinical language through terms such as the hangman's fracture and coup du lapin. The resulting cultural weight on cervical injury shapes illness behaviour, as demonstrated by natural experiments linking whiplash outcomes to compensation context. Cognitive biases compound this: pattern-based reasoning in high-stakes situations, defensive practice, and the availability heuristic all incline clinicians towards immobilisation regardless of the evidence. Effective change requires explicit engagement with the evolutionary, historical, linguistic, and cognitive determinants of cervical injury fear, alongside the evidential case for gentle patient handling.

Background

Spinal immobilisation has remained a defining element of trauma care for more than eight decades, yet contemporary evidence has consistently failed to demonstrate neurological benefit from its routine application [1, 2]. International trauma systems have begun to reflect this evidential shift, and the case for reconsidering rigid immobilisation is increasingly well made [1, 2]. Yet clinical behaviour in this area has proven resistant to change. The cervical spine continues to command a degree of anxiety that appears disproportionate to its contribution to the burden of injury. Understanding why this is the case, and what sustains this fear beyond the evidence that originally generated it, may prove as consequential to the reform of trauma practice as any new trial or review.

Main text

The fear of neck injury may have origins that predate clinical practice. Seligman described a neurobiological disposition in which certain fear associations are acquired more rapidly and persist more durably than others, not through conventional learning, but because they are aligned with conserved threat-response pathways [3]. More recent neuroscience supports this; fear responses associated with survival-relevant threats are resistant to extinction and are not readily corrected by rational argument or statistical evidence alone [4]. The neck is a plausible candidate for this kind of prepared fear. Ethological evidence across social mammals documents deliberate neck exposure as an appeasement gesture in submissive encounters; the cervical region represents the most efficient site of lethal attack. Cross-cultural human parallels, including the reflexive chin-tuck response to fright and the neck-exposure display in courtship, suggest that sensitivity to cervical threat is biological in origin.

This biological disposition has been reinforced by a pattern in human history that has received little attention in clinical contexts. Capital punishment across cultures has focused on the neck as the primary site of judicial killing. Hanging, decapitation and garrotting are technologies of cervical death, each practised publicly, and each designed to communicate as well as to kill [5]. The influence of this history on clinical language is more direct than is commonly acknowledged. The hangman's fracture, a bilateral C2 pars interarticularis injury produced by judicial long-drop hanging, carries its name from the executioner's technique, embedding the cultural apparatus of capital punishment within trauma nomenclature [6]. In French-language medical culture, soft-tissue whiplash injury is termed coup du lapin, a reference to the cervical dislocation used to dispatch a rabbit, encoding the imagery of lethal cervical injury within the name of what is typically a minor musculoligamentous sprain. Cross-cultural variation in both the naming and clinical course of whiplash provides independent evidence that the illness narrative around cervical trauma is not purely biomedical, but shaped by the cultural context in which it is received [7]. These are not etymological curiosities; they suggest that the cultural weight of neck injury has entered clinical language and, through it, shaped both practitioner expectation and patient illness behaviour.

The clinical consequences of this cultural loading are not theoretical. The nocebo literature demonstrates that negative expectation can shape physiological and functional outcome, and cervical soft-tissue injury represents a well-documented domain where this applies [8]. Cassidy and colleagues demonstrated in a natural experiment that whiplash outcomes in Saskatchewan improved substantially following the introduction of a no-fault insurance system, strongly implicating illness behaviour, shaped in part by the cultural weight of cervical injury, as a driver of prolonged disability [9]. This is consistent with evidence that clinical interventions that signal severity, including the application of a cervical collar in the emergency setting, may themselves perpetuate the disability they are intended to prevent through nocebo mechanisms acting on a pre-sensitised fear response [8, 10]. Benger and Blackham posed this question directly, asking why collars are routinely applied to conscious trauma patients when there is no evidence of benefit and documented physiological and psychological harms [10].

Taken together, these observations suggest a hypothesis relevant to the challenge of changing immobilisation practice. The cervical collar may function, at least in part, as an anxiety management device, and not only for the patient but for the clinician. Applying a collar is visible and immediate; it communicates to the patient, to bystanders and to receiving teams that a feared injury has been identified and addressed. Research on placebo effects demonstrates that the ritual of clinical intervention carries meaning beyond its mechanism of action, and that removing an established intervention may provoke resistance disproportionate to any loss of clinical benefit [11]. Croskerry demonstrated that in high-stakes situations, fast pattern-based reasoning tends to override evidence-based judgement [12]. The neck, for the reasons outlined above, is precisely the kind of domain where this is likely to occur. Kessler and McClellan add a further consideration: liability concern independently shapes clinical behaviour regardless of what the clinician consciously believes [13]. Tversky and Kahneman's availability heuristic compounds both: memorable cases of catastrophic cervical cord injury anchor clinical probability estimates well above their real epidemiological value, and this is not readily corrected by data alone [14]. In combination, these factors may explain why the collar has proven so resistant to removal from practice.

Conclusions

This analysis does not suggest that fear of cervical injury is irrational, nor that the clinical case for gentle handling requires cultural supplementation to be valid; the biomechanical and physiological evidence is sufficient on its own terms. The argument is, rather, that the resistance of immobilisation practice to revision may not be overcome by evidence translation alone. Grol and Grimshaw demonstrated that effective implementation of clinical change requires attention to the cognitive and social determinants of professional behaviour, not merely to evidence quality [15]. In a field where the fear in question has evolutionary roots, historical reinforcement, linguistic encoding and institutional expression in the collar itself, the communication of change will need to engage with these dimensions explicitly. To present the case for gentle handling as a purely evidential argument is to address the rational surface of a resistance that runs considerably deeper. Acknowledging that cervical injury fear has these deeper roots, across training, guideline development and clinical and public communication, may ultimately prove as important as the evidence itself in achieving the proportionate, patient-centred practice that current knowledge already supports.
