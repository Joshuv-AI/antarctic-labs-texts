Abstract
. This article aims at a proper definition and resolution of the parabolic Anderson model on Heisenberg groups 𝐇 n {\mathbf{H}^{n}} . This stochastic PDE is understood in a pathwise (Stratonovich) sense. We consider a noise which is smoother than white noise in time, with a spatial covariance function generated by negative powers ( − Δ ) − α (-\Delta)^{-\alpha} of the sub-Laplacian on 𝐇 n {\mathbf{H}^{n}} . We give optimal conditions on the covariance function so that the stochastic PDE is solvable. A large portion of the article is dedicated to a detailed definition of weighted Besov spaces on 𝐇 n {\mathbf{H}^{n}} . This definition, related paraproducts and heat flow smoothing properties, forms a necessary step in the resolution of our main equation. It also appears to be new and of independent interest. It relies on a recent approach, called projective, to Fourier transforms on 𝐇 n {\mathbf{H}^{n}} .



1.
Introduction

In
[
5
,
6
]
we have started a long term project aiming at studying parabolic Anderson models and polymer measures on geometric structures. The broader question for us is to determine if the geometric environment is likely to change the exponents of the model in a significant way. In this context Heisenberg groups (denoted by
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
in the sequel) are good test examples, due to their sub-Riemannian nature. The study in
[
6
]
was centered on stochastic PDEs in the Itô sense on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. We did find that the critical exponents obtained in that case were substantially different from the ones obtained in a flat Euclidean space. See also the wide variety of exponents obtained in fractal settings (
[
5
]
).

In the current contribution we go back to the Heisenberg group setting, but we now consider pathwise (Stratonovich) equations. This is crucial in order to define polymer measures in a straightforward way. Also notice that the Stratonovich setting relies on a set of tools which is completely distinct from the Itô setting. Before we turn to a summary of our findings, let us recall some basic geometric notation about
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
.

1.1.
General facts about Heisenberg groups

For a given
n\geq 1
,
the Heisenberg group
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the group multiplication defined for
(x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
:

(1.1)

\displaystyle(x,y,z)*(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}):=\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+2\omega((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))\right),

where
\omega:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\omega((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\prime}y_{i}-x_{i}y_{i}^{\prime}
is the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
The identity in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
is
e=(0,0,0)
and the inverse is given by
(x,y,z)^{-1}=(-x,-y,-z)
. In the sequel, for
q=(x,y,z)
and
q^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
, we will simply write
qq^{\prime}
for the product
q*q^{\prime}
.
Its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
with the Lie bracket given by

[(a,b,c),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})]=\left(0,0,2\omega((a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}))\right),\quad(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

Note that
\mathfrak{h}
can also be considered as the tangent space
T_{e}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
. One can then define a basis of left invariant vector fields on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
by

(1.2)

X_{i}(p)=\partial_{x_{i}}+2y_{i}\,\partial_{z},\quad Y_{i}(p)=\partial_{y_{i}}-2x_{i}\,\partial_{z},\quad Z(p)=\partial_{z},\quad i=1,\dots,n\,,

for any
p=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. The homogeneous norm on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
is given by

(1.3)

\lvert p\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}=(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}+|z|)^{1/2}\,.

We will also use the family of dilations
\{\delta_{\lambda};\lambda>0\}
defined as follows:

(1.4)

\delta_{\lambda}q=(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda^{2}z)\,,\quad\text{for}\quad q=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}.

The Heisenberg group
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
appears as a flat model of sub-Riemannian manifold. Its sub-Riemannian structure can be reflected by restricting diffusions on its horizontal distribution
\mathcal{D}:=\{\mathcal{D}_{p}\}_{p\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}
where

\mathcal{D}_{p}:=\mathrm{Span}\{X_{i}(p),Y_{i}(p),i=1,\dots,n\}.

A standard choice of sub-Riemannian metric is the inner product such that
\mathcal{H}\equiv\{X_{i}(p),Y_{i}(p),\linebreak i=1,\dots,n\}
is an orthonormal frame at
p
. This orthonormal frame is referenced to as horizontal. Related to this notion is the Carnot–Carathéodory distance on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, which will be abundantly used in the remainder of this paper. For
q_{1},q_{2}\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, it is defined as

(1.5)

d_{cc}(q_{1},q_{2})=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}|_{\mathcal{H}}dt;\ \gamma:[0,1]\to{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\ \text{horizontal},\gamma(0)=q_{1},\gamma(1)=q_{2}\right\}.

The Carnot–Carathéodory distance on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
is left-invariant i.e.

(1.6)

\displaystyle d_{cc}(pq_{1},pq_{2})=d_{cc}(q_{1},q_{2})

and satisfies the following scaling property

d_{cc}(\delta_{\lambda}q_{1},\delta_{\lambda}q_{2})=\lambda d_{cc}(q_{1},q_{2}).

It is then an easy consequence of the scaling properties of
d_{cc}
and
|\cdot|_{h}
that the induced norm of
d_{cc}
is equivalent to
|\cdot|_{h}
. Namely there exists a
c>1
such that for all
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
,

(1.7)

\frac{1}{c}|q|_{h}\leq d_{cc}(e,q)\leq c|q|_{h}.

The diffusion operator related to the sub-Riemannian metric, that is

(1.8)

\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}+Y_{i}^{2}\,,

is called the sub-Laplacian. It is essentially self-adjoint on the space of smooth and compactly supported functions
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
, and has a unique self-adjoint extension to the
L^{2}
space with respect to the Haar measure
\mu
. The sub-Laplacian also admits a heat kernel
p_{t}
, issued from
e
, which was explicitly computed in
[
9
]
: for any
q=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
we have

(1.9)

p_{t}(q)=\frac{1}{(2\pi t)^{n+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\frac{\lambda z}{t}}\left(\frac{2\lambda}{\sinh{2\lambda}}\right)^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{t}|(x,y)|^{2}\coth(2\lambda)\right)d\lambda,

where the norm
|(x,y)|
is defined by

(1.10)

|(x,y)|^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{n}^{2}\,.

Related to the heat kernel
p_{t}
, consider the heat semi-group

\{P_{t};\,t\geq 0\}
generated by the self-adjoint operator
\Delta
. Namely for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1},\mu)
we have

(1.11)

P_{t}f(p)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}}f(q)p_{t}(q^{-1}p)d\mu(q)\,,

where
\mu
designates the Haar measure on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
.
The insight on
P_{t}
provided by Fourier analysis is recalled and analyzed later in our paper.
For our SPDEs computations in Section
4
, we will also resort to resolvent kernels corresponding to the operator
(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}
where
0<\alpha<n+1
. Those kernels are given by

(1.12)

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}(p,q)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{\alpha-1}p_{t}(q^{-1}p)\,dt,\quad\text{for all}\quad p,q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}},

and were extensively studied in
[
6
]
to which we refer for further details.

1.2.
Main results

The scope of our paper is twofold. First we introduce a notion of weighted Besov space on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, which is compatible with heat flows. Then we apply this new notion to a parabolic Anderson model on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. Let us detail those two points separately.

The notion of weighted Besov space in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is clearly rooted in a proper partition of unity in Fourier modes (see for example
[
2
,
12
]
). In
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
this notion is far from being trivial. To start with, Fourier transforms in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
are traditionally seen as operator-valued. In order to get a more handy version of the transform, in this paper we resort to the recent approach introduced in
[
2
]
. Roughly speaking, this method is based on a series of projections onto rescaled Hermite functions. The Fourier variables are then elements of the finite dimensional set
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}=\mathbb{N}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
(although more complex structures are considered in
[
2
]
when taking closures). Once this new setting is accepted it remains to construct a convenient partition of unity, satisfying proper decay and support conditions. This step is based on delicate differential bounds detailed in Sections
2
and
3
. Overall our notion of weighted Besov space, although natural once the appropriate setting has been introduced, is an important part of our endeavor. We believe it is new and of independent interest.

Once the Besov space setting is established, we turn our attention to a parabolic Anderson model in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. Formally it can be written as the following linear equation over
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
:

(1.13)

\partial_{t}u_{t}(q)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u_{t}(q)+u_{t}(q)\,\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{t}(q).

In equation (
1.13
), the time integral has to be understood in the young (or Stratonovich) sense, while the product
q\mapsto u_{t}(q)\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{t}(q)
is interpreted as a distributional product. Our aim is to handle the case of a centered Gaussian noise
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
with a covariance function informally given by

(1.14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\dot{\mathbf{W}}_{t}^{\zeta,\alpha}(q_{1})\dot{\mathbf{W}}_{s}^{\zeta,\alpha}(q_{2})\right]=|t-s|^{-\zeta}\,G_{2\alpha}(q_{1},q_{2}),

where
G_{2\alpha}
is the kernel introduced in (
1.12
). Otherwise stated
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
behaves in time like the derivative of a fractional Brownian motion type processes, while in space one can write
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
as
(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}\dot{W}
(where
\dot{W}
stands for a white noise on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
). A more formal definition is provided in Section
4.3
. However, it should be clear from (
1.14
) that
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
is a distribution in both time and space. For this type of noise we prove that equation (
1.13
) admits a unique solution under the following condition on the coefficients
\zeta
and
\alpha
:

(1.15)

\zeta\in(0,1),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{n+1}{2}-(1-\zeta)<\alpha<\frac{n+1}{2}.

In order to achieve this result, we will solve a mild form of the stochastic heat equation (
1.13
). We resort to Young integration techniques in time and rely heavily on our weighted Besov space framework for the spatial component. Notice that weights are essential in this context, since a noise like
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
is spatially unbounded (regardless of the Besov scale
\alpha
). In fact we will solve (
1.13
) in a space of weighted processes which combines time and space regularity in a subtle way (see Definition
4.1
for a full description).

As mentioned above, one of our main goals is to quantify the influence of underlying geometric structures on relevant physical random systems (such as the parabolic Anderson model or directed polymers). Let us add a few comments about the exponents obtained in the current work:

(i)

In
[
6
]
we have established existence and uniqueness results for an equation of the form (
1.13
), albeit driven by a white noise in time. In
[
6
]
we also dealt with a stochastic integral interpreted in the Itô sense, as opposed to the Stratonovich-Young setting considered here. In this context we had established existence and uniqueness of the solution, under the condition

(1.16)

\frac{n}{2}<\alpha<\frac{n+1}{2}.

If we wish to compare (
1.16
) with our condition (
1.15
), one should recall that (formally) the white noise case in time corresponds to
\zeta=1
in (
1.15
). Hence in the white noise case hypothesis (
1.15
) becomes an incompatible condition

(1.17)

\frac{n+1}{2}<\alpha<\frac{n+1}{2}.

This phenomenon is not surprising. Indeed, we have also seen in
[
6
, Proposition 3.12]
that for
\alpha>\frac{n+1}{2}
, one can slightly modify the definition of
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
in order to get a function (versus a distribution for
\alpha<\frac{n+1}{2}
) in space. Hence condition (
1.17
) just tells us the following: if we wish to consider the stochastic heat equation (
1.13
) driven by a white noise in time, in the Stratonovich sense, then the noise should be a function in space. This is well known in other contexts, see for instance
[
17
]
.

(ii)

One can refer e.g to
[
10
]
for a comparison with equations on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the Stratonovich sense. We have argued in
[
6
]
that the noise in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which resembles most our noise
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
is based on Bessel kernels. This is a noise
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},\zeta,\alpha}
whose covariance function is formally similar to (
1.18
):

(1.18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\dot{\mathbf{W}}_{t}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},\zeta,\alpha}(x)\,\dot{\mathbf{W}}_{s}^{\mathbb{R}^{d},\zeta,\alpha}(y)\right]=|t-s|^{-\zeta}\,G^{\mathbb{R}^{d}}_{2\alpha}(x,y),

where
G^{\mathbb{R}^{d}}_{2\alpha}(x,y)
is the resolvent kernel for
(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{-2\alpha}
. Now let us translate the conditions in
[
10
, Proposition 5.22 and Hypothesis 4.8]
to the case of a Bessel kernel, for which the Fourier transform of the spatial covariance is
(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{-2\alpha}
. We let the reader check that we get

\alpha>\frac{d}{4}-(1-\zeta).

This is exactly our condition (
1.15
) in the Heisenberg case, once we realize that the relevant dimension in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
is the Hausdorff dimension
Q=2n+2
(as opposed to the topological dimension
2n+1
). As in
[
6
]
, this is a clear manifestation of the sub-Riemannian nature of
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
.

(iii)

The current article is restricted to equations defined in the Young sense. It would be natural to observe how our exponents are affected by more singular noises, recurring either to regularity structures or paracontrolled techniques. Notice that this type of development has been initiated by
[
11
]
. However the reference
[
11
]
focuses on a compact version of
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, which conveniently avoids the use of weighted spaces. We plan to delve deeper into this direction in future communications.

Our paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we recall the basic Fourier analysis setting which will prevail throughout the paper. Then we construct a Gevrey type class of functions in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, which is crucial step in the definition of weighted Besov space. This step culminates in a Bernstein type lemma in Section
2.4
. Section
3
is devoted to define Besov spaces and derive related smoothing effects of the heat semigroup, as well as a nontrivial notion of paraproduct. The application of this theory to stochastic PDEs is contained in Section
4
.

Notation.

Throughout this paper, the set
\mathbb{N}
stands for the family of integers
\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.
For any multi-index integer
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we use the notation
k!:=k_{1}!\cdots k_{n}!
and
|k|:=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}
. We extend the notation
|k|
to indices
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, by letting
|k|=|k_{1}|+\cdots+|k_{n}|
.

2.
Fourier transform on Heisenberg group

In this section we summarize some basic facts about Fourier transforms on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
as can be found in
[
3
]
. We will then study some properties of functions whose Fourier transform is compactly supported. Those properties will be crucial for a proper notion of Besov weighted space. Eventually we will define a notion of fractional Laplacian which is invoked for the description of our noisy inputs, and recall the notion of tempered distribution in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
.

2.1.
Fourier transform

It is well-known that all irreducible representations of the Heisenberg group
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
are unitary equivalent to the Schrödinger representations
(U^{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}
, that is the family of group morphisms
q=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to U_{q}^{\lambda}
between
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
and the unitary group of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined by

(2.1)

U_{q}^{\lambda}u(\xi)=e^{-i\lambda\left(z+2\langle y,\,\xi-x\rangle\right)}u\left(\xi-2x\right),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Based on those representations, the group Fourier transform of a function
f\in L^{1}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
is defined for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}=:\mathbb{R}^{*}
as the operator valued function on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
given by

(2.2)

\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)=\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}f(q)U_{q}^{\lambda}\,d\mu(q).

Clearly
\mathcal{F}(f)
takes values in the space of bounded operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. One of the important features of
\mathcal{F}
is its compatibility with the Laplace operator. Namely for a generic smooth function
f
we have

(2.3)

\mathcal{F}(\Delta f)(\lambda)=4\,\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)\circ\Delta^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{osc}}\,,

where the operator
\Delta^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{osc}}
is defined by
\Delta^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{osc}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}-\lambda^{2}|x|^{2}
and is called harmonic oscillator.

In this paper we will consider the Fourier transform from a projective point of view that was introduced by
[
3
]
. In order to introduce this notion, let
(\Phi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
be the family of Hermite functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is given by

(2.4)

\Phi_{0}(x)=\pi^{-\frac{n}{4}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{k}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{2^{|k|}k!}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}C^{k}\Phi_{0}(x)\,,\quad\text{for }k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\,.

In (
2.4
), we have set
C^{k}=\prod_{j=1}^{|k|}C_{j}^{k_{j}}
, where
C_{j}:=-\partial_{j}+M_{j}
denotes the creation operator and
M_{j}
is the multiplication operator such that for any function
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
M_{j}u(x)=x_{j}u(x)
. The family
(\Phi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the functions
\Phi_{k}
are the eigenfunctions of the harmonic oscillator
\Delta_{\mathrm{osc}}:=\Delta^{1}_{\mathrm{osc}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}-|x|^{2}
appearing in (
2.3
). Specifically, for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
we have

(2.5)

\Delta_{\mathrm{osc}}\Phi_{k}=-(2|k|+n)\Phi_{k}.

We now introduce a scaling factor which will account for the Fourier transform with respect to the
z
-variable in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. That is for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we consider the family of rescaled Hermite functions

(2.6)

\Phi_{k}^{\lambda}(x)=|\lambda|^{n/4}\Phi_{k}\left(\sqrt{|\lambda|}x\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,.

This family again forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and satisfies that

\Delta^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{osc}}\Phi^{\lambda}_{k}(x)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}-\lambda^{2}|x|^{2}\right)\Phi^{\lambda}_{k}(x)=-(2|k|+n)|\lambda|\Phi^{\lambda}_{k}(x).

We denote by
\|\cdot\|_{\textsc{hs}}
the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. Note that owing to the fact that
(\Phi^{\lambda}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

(2.7)

\|\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)\|_{\textsc{hs}}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\|\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)\Phi_{k}^{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}.

Identity (
2.7
) leads us to introduce the following projective definition of the Fourier transform that is convenient for defining the tempered distribution on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}:=\mathbb{N}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{*}
, whose generic element is denoted by
\hat{w}:=(m,\ell,\lambda)
. For any
f\in L^{2}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
we define the (projective) Fourier transform of
f
as the function
\hat{f}:\tilde{\bf H}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
given by

(2.8)

\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda):=\langle\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)\Phi_{m}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\ell}^{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,,

where we recall that the functions
\Phi_{k}^{\lambda}
are given by (
2.6
).

Notice that in (
2.8
), the Fourier transform is now complex valued as in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It can also be written as the following integral representation that is closely related to Wigner transforms of Hermite functions: for any
(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
,

(2.9)

\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\overline{e^{i\lambda z}K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q)}f(q)\,d\mu(q),

where for all
q=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, the kernel
K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q)
is given by

(2.10)

K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2i\lambda\langle y,\xi\rangle}\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}(x+\xi)\Phi^{\lambda}_{\ell}(-x+\xi)d\xi.

We now label some important properties of the projective Fourier transform which will be used in the sequel:

(i)

The Fourier inversion formula for
q=(x,y,z)
is obtained by integrating against the kernel
K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q)
. Namely recalling that we write
\hat{w}=(m,\ell,\lambda)
, it can be shown that

(2.11)

f(q)=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\sum_{m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\lambda z}K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q)\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda=:\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\int_{\tilde{\bf H}^{n}}e^{i\lambda z}K_{\hat{w}}(q)\hat{f}(\hat{w})d\hat{w},

where the second identity is a notation which will be used in the sequel without further mention.

In fact, it is shown in
[
3
]
that the Fourier transform as defined above can be extended as a bi-continuous isomorphism between
L^{2}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
and
L^{2}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
, and the following Plancherel formula holds,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}|f(q)|^{2}d\mu(q)=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\sum_{m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)^{2}|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda.

(ii)

The interaction between Laplacian and Fourier transform is easily read in projective mode.
Specifically combining (
2.3
) with (
2.5
) and(
2.8
), we can easily find that for any
(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
,

(2.13)

\widehat{\Delta f}(m,\ell,\lambda)=-4|\lambda|(2|m|+n)\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda).

Similarly for any
N\geq 1
, we have

(2.14)

\widehat{\Delta^{N}f}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\big(-4|\lambda|(2|m|+n)\big)^{N}\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda).

Relation (
2.13
) also enables to introduce fractional power of the Laplacian. Namely for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
we define
(-\Delta)^{\alpha}f
through its Fourier transform:

(2.15)

\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\alpha}f}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\big(4|\lambda|(2|m|+n)\big)^{\alpha}\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda).

(iii)

The Fourier transform of the Dirac distribution at the identity
e
is explicitly computed as:

\hat{\delta_{e}}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\langle\mathcal{F}(\delta_{e})(\lambda)\Phi_{m}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\ell}^{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\langle U_{e}^{\lambda}(\Phi_{m}^{\lambda}),\Phi_{\ell}^{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{m=\ell\}},

for all
(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. In the same way, let
p_{t}
be the sublaplacian heat kernel defined by (
1.9
). Then the projective Fourier transform of
p_{t}
satisfies

(2.16)

\hat{p}_{t}(m,\ell,\lambda)=e^{-4t|\lambda|(2|m|+n)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{m=\ell\}}.

(iv)

For any integrable functions
f,g
on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, the following convolution identity holds:

(2.17)

\widehat{f\star g}(m,\ell,\lambda)=(\hat{f}\cdot\hat{g})(m,\ell,\lambda)

where

(2.18)

(\hat{f}\cdot\hat{g})(m,\ell,\lambda):=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\hat{f}(m,j,\lambda)\hat{g}(j,\ell,\lambda).

We close this section by recalling from
[
3
]
that we can equip
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
with a metric
\widehat{d}
. That is for all
\hat{w}=(m,\ell,\lambda)
and
\hat{w}^{\prime}=(m^{\prime},\ell^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
, we define

(2.19)

\widehat{d}(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\prime})=|\lambda(m+\ell)-\lambda^{\prime}(m^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime})|_{1}+|(m-\ell)-(m^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime})|+n|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|

where
|\cdot|_{1}
denotes the Manhattan norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e. for
m\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
|m|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}|
.
Similarly, recall that the norm
|k|
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is given in the notation at the end of our introduction.
Taking limits
\hat{w}^{\prime}\to(0,0,0)
in relation (
2.19
), we also set

(2.20)

\widehat{d}_{0}(\hat{w})=|\lambda|(|m+\ell|+n)+|m-\ell|\,,

for all
\hat{w}=(m,\ell,\lambda)
in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
.

2.2.
Some differential operators on
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}

It is known that functions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with compactly supported Fourier transform have several regularity properties, which can be extended to weighted spaces. In the following subsections we shall study these properties in the Heisenberg group, extending
[
12
]
to our geometric setting. As a preparation to this task, in this section we introduce some
differentiations on
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. Notice that we borrow those differentiation operators from
[
3
]
. They nicely preserve the property of Fourier transform that converts multiplication in direct coordinates into differentiation in Fourier modes.

Definition 2.2
.

As in the general notation given in the introduction, we denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set
\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.
Recall that the space
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
is introduced in Definition
2.1
. We define a differential operator
\widehat{\Delta}
acting on functions

g:\tilde{\bf H}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
in the following way: for any
\hat{w}=(m,\ell,\lambda)
we set
\widehat{w}_{j}^{\pm}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(m\pm e_{j},\ell\pm e_{j},\lambda)
, where
e_{j}
is the element of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
with the
j
-th component
1
and all the other components
0
.
Then
\widehat{\Delta}g
is given by

(2.21)

\widehat{\Delta}g(\hat{w})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\\
-\frac{1}{2|\lambda|}(|m+\ell|+n)\,g(\hat{w})+\frac{1}{2|\lambda|}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\sqrt{(m_{j}+1)(\ell_{j}+1)}\,g(\hat{w}_{j}^{+})+\sqrt{m_{j}\ell_{j}}\,g(\widehat{w}_{j}^{-})\right).

If in addition
g
is differentiable with respect to
\lambda
, we also define a differentiation with respect to the
\lambda
variable:

(2.22)

\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}g(\hat{w})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}={\partial_{\lambda}g}(\hat{w})+\frac{n}{2\lambda}g(\hat{w})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\sqrt{m_{j}\ell_{j}}\,g(\hat{w}_{j}^{-})-\sqrt{(m_{j}+1)(\ell_{j}+1)}\,g({\hat{w}_{j}}^{+})\right).

Remark 2.3
.

The operator
\widehat{\Delta}
should be considered as a differentiation in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. One can justify this interpretation easily in two ways:

(a)

On the diagonal in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
, namely for
\hat{w}=(m,m,\lambda)
, it is readily checked that (
2.21
) becomes

(2.23)

\widehat{\Delta}g(m,m,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2|\lambda|}\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}\big(g(\widehat{w}_{j}^{-})-g(\hat{w})\big)+(m_{j}+1)\big(g(\widehat{w}_{j}^{+})-g(\hat{w})\big),

and the right hand side of (
2.23
) is easily interpreted as a Laplace type operator.

(b)

A second justification of
\widehat{\Delta}
as a Laplacian is the content of the technical Lemma
2.9
below. It states that multiplying by
|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}
in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
corresponds to applying
\widehat{\Delta}
in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. This is analogous to similar relations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
involving the Laplacian.

Notice that the same kind of observations are valid for the operator
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}
in (
2.22
).

Remark 2.4
.

In (
2.21
)-(
2.22
), we adopt the following convention: if there exists
j\in\{1,...,n\}
such that either
m_{j}=-1
or
\ell_{j}=-1
, then we set
g(m,\ell,\lambda)=0
. This allows us to define quantities such as
\widehat{\Delta}g(0,0,\lambda)
or
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}g(0,0,\lambda)
.

We now label some general analytic definitions on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
and
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
, which will be used throughout the paper. We first introduce some multiplication operators on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
.

Definition 2.5
.

On the Heisenberg group
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, we consider the following objects:

(i)

The Schwartz space of smooth functions with rapid decay on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
is identified with the Schwartz space on
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. It is denoted by
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
.

(ii)

The multiplication operators
M^{2}
and
M_{0}
on the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
: for any
q=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, we set

(2.24)

(M^{2}f)(q):=(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})f(q),\quad\text{and}\quad(M_{0}f)(q):=-izf(q).

Next we define what is meant by Schwartz space on the dual set
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. As expected, it is related to smoothness and decay properties.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
be the set introduced in Definition
2.1
. Then
\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
is the set of functions
\tilde{\phi}
on
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
such that

(i)

For all
(m,\ell)\in\mathbb{N}^{2n}
, the map
\lambda\mapsto\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda)
is smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{*}
.

(ii)

For all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the functions
\widehat{\Delta}^{N}\tilde{\phi}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\tilde{\phi}
decay faster than any expression of the form
d_{0}(\hat{w})^{-\kappa}
for all
\kappa>0
, where
d_{0}
is introduced in (
2.20
).

Note that the space
\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
can be equipped with a family of semi-norms

(2.25)

\lVert\tilde{\phi}\rVert_{N,\,\kappa,\,\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})}=\sup_{\hat{w}\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}}\left(1+d_{0}(\hat{w})\right)^{\kappa}\left(\widehat{\Delta}^{N}\tilde{\phi}(\hat{w})+\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\tilde{\phi}(\hat{w})\right).

Remark 2.7
.

Our Definition
2.6
is not completely exact, and we refer to
[
3
, Definition 2.5]
for the complete version. Specifically we have refrained from introducing the operator
\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}
and the completion
\widehat{\bf H}^{n}
, which play a prominent role in
[
3
]
, for sake of clarity. For our purposes it will be sufficient to consider the semi-norms (
2.25
).

One of the advantages of introducing the space
\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
is that we now have an isomorphism property for the Fourier transform. This result is borrowed from
[
3
, Theorem 2.6]
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
be the space given in Definition
2.5
and consider
\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
introduced in Definition
2.6
. Then the projective Fourier transform
f\to\hat{f}
is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
to
\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
, with inverse given by (
2.11
).

One of the main results contained in
[
3
]
is that the multiplication operators introduced in Definition
2.5
are compatible with the differentiations on
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. We label this result in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.9
.

Recall that the operators
\widehat{\Delta}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}
are respectively defined by (
2.21
) and (
2.22
).
The multiplications
M^{2}
and
M_{0}
are given in (
2.24
). Then for any
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
, we have

(2.26)

\widehat{M^{2}f}=-\widehat{\Delta}{\hat{f}},\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{M_{0}f}=\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}{\hat{f}}.

2.3.
Gevrey classes on the Heisenberg group

With the preliminaries of Sections
2.1
and
2.2
in hand, we are now ready to define the Gevrey classes on
(\tilde{\bf H}^{n},\widehat{d})
that characterize regularities of functions on
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. Recall that the Gevrey class on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with index
s
is defined as the set of infinitely differentiable functions that satisfies (for any multiindex
k
)

(2.27)

\sup_{K}|\partial^{k}f|\leq C^{|k|+1}(k!)^{s}\,,\quad\text{for any compact $K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$.}

The Gevrey classes
s\geq 1
interpolate between analytic functions and
C^{\infty}
functions. On Heisenberg groups, this kind of property will be quantified through the operators
\widehat{\Delta}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}
.

Definition 2.10
.

Consider the operators
\widehat{\Delta}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}
defined in (
2.21
) and (
2.22
), and a parameter
s\geq 1
. We define
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
to be the set of functions
\tilde{g}
on
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
such that:

(i)

For any
(m,\ell)
in
\mathbb{N}^{2n}
, the map
\lambda\mapsto\tilde{g}(m,\ell,\lambda)
is smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{*}
.

(ii)

For every compact set
K\subset(\tilde{\bf H}^{n},\widehat{d})
, there exists
C<\infty
such that for every integer
N\geq 0
,

(2.28)

\sup_{K}\left(\max\left(|\widehat{\Delta}^{N}\tilde{g}|,\,|\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\tilde{g}|\right)\right)\leq C^{2N+1}[(2N)!]^{s}.

In
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the exponential decay of a function
f
can be characterized by the fact that
\mathcal{F}f
is in a Gevrey class. We now show that the same is true on Heisenberg groups. We start with
the proposition below, which shows that decay of a function implies regularity of its Fourier transform in a Gevrey class.

Proposition 2.11
.

For
p=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, recall from (
1.3
) that
\lvert p\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}=(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}+|z|)^{1/2}
is the homogeneous norm of
p
. Consider a parameter
s\geq 1
and a function
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
satisfying the following relation:

(2.29)

|f(p)|\leq Ce^{-\varepsilon\lvert p\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{1/s}},\quad\text{for all}\quad p\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\,,

for some
C>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then the Fourier transform
\hat{f}
lies in
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
.

Proof.

We use the integral representation (
2.9
) of the Fourier transform
\hat{f}
. First, it is not difficult to see that the integral kernel
K_{m,\ell,\lambda}
satisfies

(2.30)

|{K}_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q)|\leq 1,\quad\text{for all $(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}$ and $q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}$.}

To this aim,
recall relation (
2.10
) defining the integral kernel
K_{m,\ell,\lambda}
. Bounding the complex exponential by
1
, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the right hand side of (
2.10
) and recalling that
(\Phi^{\lambda}_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
is an orthonormal family of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we easily get the estimate (
2.30
).

Next, let
N\geq 0
be an integer. Invoking relation (
2.26
) and the definition (
2.9
) of Fourier transform, we get

|\widehat{\Delta}^{N}\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)|=\left|\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}\overline{e^{i\lambda z}\,K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q)}\,(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})^{N}f(q)\,d\mu(q)\right|.

Plugging (
2.29
) and (
2.30
) into the equality above, we end up with the following relation for all
(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
:

(2.31)

|\widehat{\Delta}^{N}\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)|\leq C\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})^{N}e^{-\varepsilon\lvert q\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{1/s}}d\mu(q).

Using the exact same arguments, we also have, for all
(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
,

\displaystyle|\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}\overline{e^{i\lambda z}\,K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q)}\,|z|^{N}f(q)\,d\mu(q)\right|

(2.32)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}|z|^{N}e^{-\varepsilon\lvert q\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{1/s}}d\mu(q).

Summarizing (
2.31
) and (
2.32
), we have thus obtained that

(2.33)

M:=\sup_{\hat{w}\,\in\,\tilde{\bf H}^{n}}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\max\left(|\widehat{\Delta}^{N}\hat{f}\,(\hat{w})|,\,|\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\hat{f}\,(\hat{w})|\right)\right)\leq C\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\lvert q\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{2N}e^{-\varepsilon\lvert q\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{1/s}}d\mu(q).

We now resort to the elementary estimate
t^{2N}e^{-t}\leq(2N)!
, applied to
t=\frac{\varepsilon}{2s}\lvert q\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{1/s}
, in the right hand side of (
2.33
). We have

\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2s}\right)^{2N}|q|_{h}^{2N/s}\,e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2s}|q|_{h}^{1/s}}\leq(2N)!,

and raising both sides of the inequality to the power of
s
, we get

(2.34)

|q|_{h}^{2N}\,e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|q|_{h}^{1/s}}\leq C_{1}^{2N}[(2N)!]^{s},

where
C_{1}=(\varepsilon/2s)^{-s}
. Applying (
2.34
) to the right hand side of (
2.33
) gives

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot C_{1}^{2N}[(2N)!]^{s}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\lvert q\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{1/s}}d\mu(q).

Thus
M\leq C_{2}^{2N+1}[(2N)!]^{s}
, where
C_{2}=C_{1}+C\int e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\lvert q\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{1/s}}d\mu
. (Note that the constant
C_{2}
is independent of
N
.) This proves that
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
.
∎

We have just seen that if a function
f
defined on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
has a proper exponential decay, then its Fourier transform is in a space
\mathcal{G}^{s}
. We now examine the converse result: if a function
\tilde{\phi}
defined on
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
is in a class
\mathcal{G}^{s}
, then its inverse Fourier transform decays exponentially. This is detailed in the proposition below.

Proposition 2.12
.

Recall that the space
\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
is introduced in Definition
2.6
. Let
s\geq 1
and
\tilde{\phi}
be an element of
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})\cap\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
with bounded support in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. We call
\phi
its inverse Fourier transform, as defined in (
2.11
). Then there exist constants
C=C_{\phi}>0
and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\phi}>0
such that

(2.35)

|\phi(q)|\leq Ce^{-\varepsilon\lvert q\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{1/s}}\ \,\text{for all $q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}$}.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})\cap\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
. Owing to Proposition
2.8
, the inverse Fourier transform
\phi
of
\tilde{\phi}
is well defined.
Then it suffices to show that the above estimate (
2.35
) holds uniformly over
|q|_{h}\geq 1
. By (
2.26
) and (
2.11
), we have that for any integer
k\geq 1
and
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
,

\displaystyle|M^{2k}\phi(q)|=\left|\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\int_{\tilde{\bf H}^{n}}e^{i\lambda z}K_{\hat{w}}(q)((-\widehat{\Delta})^{k}\tilde{\phi})(\hat{w})\,d\hat{w}\right|.

Owing to (
2.30
), our assumption (ii) in Definition
2.10
and the fact that
\tilde{\phi}

has bounded support, we obtain that for all
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
,

(2.36)

|M^{2k}\phi(q)|\leq C^{2k+1}[(2k)!]^{s}.

Similarly we can also obtain that for all
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
,

(2.37)

\displaystyle|M_{0}^{k}\phi(q)|\leq C^{2k+1}[(2k)!]^{s}.

Given any integer
k\geq 1
, we choose
N
such that
2N-1\leq\frac{k}{s}\leq 2N
. Then for any
|q|_{h}\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle|q|_{h}^{k/s}|\phi(q)|\leq|q|_{h}^{2N}|\phi(q)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{2N}(|M^{2N}\phi(q)|+|M_{0}^{N}\phi(q)|)

(2.38)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}^{2N+1}[(2N)!]^{s},

where we have used (
2.36
) and (
2.37
) for the last inequality.
Since
(2N)!\leq 2^{2N}(2N-1)!
, one can bound the right hand side of (
2.38
) accordingly in order to get

(2.39)

\displaystyle|q|_{h}^{k/s}|\phi(q)|\leq C_{3}^{2N+1}[(2N-1)!]^{s}\leq C_{3}^{2N+1}[(2N-1)]^{s(2N-1)}\leq C_{4}^{k+1}k^{k}.

It then follows from (
2.39
) that there exists
C>0
such that uniformly in
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
,

(2.40)

e^{\varepsilon|q|_{h}^{1/s}}|\phi(q)|=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\varepsilon|q|_{h}^{1/s})^{k}}{k!}|\phi(q)|\leq C\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\varepsilon Ck)^{k}}{k!},

uniformly for some
C>0
. At last since
1/k!\leq(e/k)^{k}
, it suffices to choose
\varepsilon
so that
\varepsilon Ce<1
in the right hand side of (
2.40
) to obtain the desired estimate (
2.35
).
∎

Up to now we have seen general properties of Gevrey classes. We now produce an example of functions in
\mathcal{G}^{s}
with bounded support in Fourier modes. This proves in particular that the set of functions
\tilde{\phi}
satisfying the assumptions of Proposition
2.12
is nontrivial.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
f
be a function on
[0,\infty)^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following:

(a)

The support of
f
is such that
\textnormal{Supp}(f)\subset[0,\infty)^{n}\times\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

There exists
C=C_{f}<\infty
and
s\geq 1
such that for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}
, uniformly in
(x,\kappa,\lambda)
we have

(2.41)

|\partial_{x,\lambda}^{\alpha}f(x_{1},...,x_{n},\kappa,\lambda)|\leq C^{|\alpha|+1}(\alpha!)^{s}.

For
\hat{w}=(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
, we define a function
\Theta_{f}(\hat{w})
by

(2.42)

\Theta_{f}(\hat{w})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=f(|\lambda|R(m,\ell),m-\ell,\lambda),\quad\text{with}\quad R(m,\ell)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(m_{j}+\ell_{j}+1)_{1\leq j\leq n}.

Then
\Theta_{f}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
.

Proof.

Item (i) in Definition
2.10
is easily verified thanks to the fact that
f
is a smooth function. We will thus focus on checking relation (
2.28
). We will divide our proof in several steps.

Step 1: Estimates for
\widehat{\Delta}\Theta_{f}
.

Due to the fact that the support of
f
in the second variable is reduced to the singleton
\{0\}
, one can in fact restrict ourselves to variables of the form
\hat{w}=(m,m,\lambda)
. For notational simplicity, we thus set
R(m)=R(m,m)
and
f(|\lambda|R(m),\lambda)=f(|\lambda|R(m),0,\lambda)
. Then using the definition (
2.21
) of
\widehat{\Delta}
as well as relation (
2.42
), some elementary algebraic manipulations show that

(2.43)

(\widehat{\Delta}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)=-\frac{1}{2|\lambda|}\Big[(2|m|+n)f(|\lambda|R(m),\lambda)\\
-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big((m_{j}+1)f(|\lambda|R(m+e_{j}),\lambda)+m_{j}f(|\lambda|R(m-e_{j}),\lambda)\big)\Big].

Since we have assumed
|f|\leq C
, it follows easily from (
2.43
) that

(2.44)

\big|(\widehat{\Delta}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\big|\leq\frac{2|m|+n}{|\lambda|}\,C.

Moreover, iterating the estimate (
2.44
) one gets the following upper bound for
\widehat{\Delta}^{2}\Theta_{f}
:

\big|(\widehat{\Delta}^{2}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\big|\\
\leq\frac{1}{2|\lambda|}\left((2|m|+n)\,\frac{2|m|+n}{|\lambda|}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(m_{j}+1)\,\frac{2|m|+2+n}{|\lambda|}+m_{j}\,\frac{2|m|+n}{|\lambda|}\right)C\,,

which can be simplified as

(2.45)

\big|(\widehat{\Delta}^{2}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\big|\leq\frac{(2|m|+n)(2|m|+n+2)}{|\lambda|^{2}}\,C.

Therefore an induction procedure yields, for
p\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\big|(\widehat{\Delta}^{p+1}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2|m|+n)(2|m|+n+2)...(2|m|+n+2p)}{|\lambda|^{p+1}}\,C

(2.46)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2|m|+n+2p)!}{(2|m|+n-2)!}\,|\lambda|^{-(p+1)}\,C.

Recalling the elementary binomial relation
(j+k)!\leq j!\,k!\,2^{j+k}
, we thus have

(2.47)

\big|(\widehat{\Delta}^{p+1}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\big|\leq(2p+2)!\,2^{2|m|+n+2p}\,|\lambda|^{-(p+1)}\,C.

As mentioned above, due to the fact that
\Theta_{f}
is supported on the diagonal, (
2.47
) is enough to prove that for any compact set
K\subset\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
, there exists a
positive constant
C_{K}
such that for all
p\geq 0
we have

(2.48)

\sup_{K}\big|(\widehat{\Delta}^{p}\Theta_{f})\big|\leq C_{K}^{2p+1}[(2p)!]^{s}.

Step 2: Estimates for
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}\Theta_{f}
.
We proceed to prove an estimate of the form (
2.48
) for
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}
. First decompose the right hand side of (
2.22
) as

(2.49)

\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}=\partial_{\lambda}+\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda},

where the operator
\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}
is defined by

(2.50)

\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}g(\hat{w})=\frac{n}{2\lambda}g(\hat{w})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\sqrt{m_{j}\ell_{j}}\,g(\hat{w}_{j}^{-})-\sqrt{(m_{j}+1)(\ell_{j}+1)}\,g({\hat{w}_{j}}^{+})\right).

First let us bound the term
\partial_{\lambda}\Theta_{f}
from the right hand side of (
2.49
). Similarly to the estimates for
\widehat{\Delta}
, it is enough to focus our attention on diagonal points of the form
(m,m,\lambda)
in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. Also notice that we are estimating all our quantities on compact sets of
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. Those sets avoid the hyperplane
\lambda=0
, which means that we can consider
\lambda\mapsto|\lambda|
as a smooth function. Then it is readily checked from expression (
2.42
) that

(2.51)

\partial_{\lambda}\Theta_{f}(m,m,\lambda)=(\partial_{\lambda}f)(|\lambda|R(m),\lambda)+\text{sgn}\,(\lambda)\sum_{j=1}^{n}(2m_{j}+1)(\partial_{x_{j}}f)(|\lambda|R(m),\lambda).

Iterating this differentiation, it can be verified that
\partial_{\lambda}^{j}\Theta_{f}
satisfies the estimate

(2.52)

\big|\partial_{\lambda}^{j}\Theta_{f}(m,m,\lambda)\big|\leq(2|m|+n+1)^{j}C^{j+1}(j!)^{s}.

As far as
\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}
in (
2.50
) is concerned, we get

\displaystyle\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}\Theta_{f}(m,m,\lambda)

(2.53)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2\lambda}f(|\lambda|R(m),\lambda)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(m_{j}f(|\lambda|R(m-e_{j}),\lambda)-(m_{j}+1)f(|\lambda|R(m+e_{j}),\lambda)\big).

Let us now estimate iterates of
\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}\Theta_{f}
. Indeed
like in (
2.44
), one can simply bound
|f|
by
C
in (
2.53
). This enables to write

\big|(\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\big|\leq\frac{|m|+n}{|\lambda|}\,C.

One can then proceed as in (
2.45
) and get

\displaystyle\big|(\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}^{2}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2|\lambda|}\,\frac{|m|+n}{|\lambda|}\,C+\frac{1}{2|\lambda|}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(m_{j}\,\frac{|m|+n}{|\lambda|}\,C+(m_{j}+1)\,\frac{|m|+n+1}{|\lambda|}\,C\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(|m|+n)(|m|+n+1)}{|\lambda|^{2}}\,C.

Then similarly to (
2.46
), an induction procedure yields that for
k\geq 0
we have

(2.54)

\big|(\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}^{k}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\big|\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{k}}\,\frac{(|m|+n+k-1)!}{(|m|+n-1)!}\,C.

With (
2.54
) in hand, we can go back to the decomposition (
2.49
) of
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}
. According to this decomposition, if one wishes to upper bound
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{\,k}\Theta_{f}
, an estimation of derivatives of the following form is in order:

\partial_{\lambda}^{j}\left(\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}\right)^{k-j}\Theta_{f}.

We will omit details of those computations for sake of conciseness. However, a slight elaboration of (
2.53
) reveals that
that
(\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}^{k}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)
is the product of
\frac{1}{(2\lambda)^{k}}
and a sum of
N_{k}
terms of translates of
f
, where
N_{k}
satisfies an inequality similar to (
2.54
). Namely we have

(2.55)

N_{k}\leq 2^{k}\,\frac{(|m|+n+k-1)!}{(|m|+n-1)!}=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\,(2|m|+2n+2j).

Therefore, differentiating with respect to
\lambda
and putting together (
2.52
), (
2.54
) and (
2.55
), we get

(2.56)

\left|\frac{d^{j}}{d\lambda^{j}}(\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}^{k}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\right|\leq\sum_{p=0}^{j}\binom{j}{p}\frac{(k+p-1)!}{(k-1)!}\,\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{k+p}}\,(2|m|+2k+n+1)^{j-p}\\
\times\frac{(|m|+n+k-1)!}{(|m|+n-1)!}\,C^{j-p+1}((j-p)!)^{s}.

In (
2.56
) we now simply bound
((j-p)!)^{s}
by
(j!)^{s}\,/\,p!
and thus obtain

(2.57)

\left|\frac{d^{j}}{d\lambda^{j}}(\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}^{k}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\right|\leq\sum_{p=0}^{j}\binom{j}{p}\,\binom{k+p-1}{k-1}\,\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{k+p}}\,(2|m|+2k+n+1)^{j-p}\\
\times\frac{(|m|+n+k-1)!}{(|m|+n-1)!}\,C^{j-p+1}(j!)^{s}.

Exactly like we did for
(
2.47
)
, we now invoke the relation
(j+k)!\leq j!\,k!\,2^{j+k}
. This enables to write

\frac{(|m|+n+k-1)!}{(|m|+n-1)!}\leq 2^{|m|+n+k-1}k!,\quad\binom{j}{p}\leq 2^{j},\quad\text{and}\quad\binom{k+p-1}{k-1}\leq 2^{k+p-1}.

Plugging the above into (
2.57
), we obtain

\left|\frac{d^{j}}{d\lambda^{j}}(\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}^{k}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\right|\leq\sum_{p=0}^{j}2^{j}\,2^{k+p-1}\,\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{k+p}}\,2^{j-p}\,(|m|+k+n)^{j-p}\left(2^{|m|+n+k-1}k!\right)C^{j-p+1}(j!)^{s}.

Rearranging the terms in this expression, we discover that

(2.58)

\left|\frac{d^{j}}{d\lambda^{j}}(\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}^{k}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\right|\leq 2^{2j+2k+|m|+n}\,C^{j+1}\,k!\,(j!)^{s}\,\sum_{p=0}^{j}\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{k+p}}\,(|m|+k+n)^{j-p}.

Let us now return to the iterations of order
N\geq 1
for
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}
. Namely invoking (
2.49
) we write

\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\Theta_{f}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\binom{N}{j}\,\frac{d^{j}}{d\lambda^{j}}\,\widehat{\underline{\mathcal{D}}}_{\lambda}^{N-j}\Theta_{f}.

Gathering (
2.58
) into the above identity we obtain

(2.59)

\left|(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\right|\\
\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N}\binom{N}{j}\,2^{2N+|m|+n}\,C^{j+1}\,(N-j)!\,(j!)^{s}\sum_{p=0}^{j}\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{N-j+p}}\,(|m|+N-j+n)^{j-p}.

In addition one can simplify the binomial terms and rearrange terms in (
2.59
), which enables to get

\left|(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\right|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N}N!\,2^{2N+|m|+n}\,C^{j+1}\,(j!)^{s-1}\sum_{p=0}^{j}\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{N-j+p}}\,(|m|+N-j+n)^{j-p}.

Bounding trivially
j!
by
N!
and
C^{j}
by
C^{N}
above, this yields

(2.60)

\left|(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\right|\leq(N!)^{s}\,2^{2N+|m|+n}\,C^{N+1}\,\sum_{j=0}^{N}\sum_{p=0}^{j}\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{N-j+p}}\,(|m|+N-j+n)^{j-p}.

One can further simplify expression (
2.60
). Specifically, if
(m,m,\lambda)
lies in a compact set
K\subset\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
, we find that there exists a constant
C_{1}=C_{1,K}>1
such that

(2.61)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{K}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sup_{K}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\sum_{p=0}^{j}\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{N-j+p}}\,(|m|+N-j+n)^{j-p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N}\sum_{p=0}^{j}C_{1}^{N-j+p}(C_{1}+N-j)^{j-p}=C_{1}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\sum_{p=0}^{j}C_{1}^{-(j-p)}(C_{1}+N-j)^{j-p}.

We now resort to some very rough estimates for the right hand side of (
2.61
). Namely bound each
C_{1}^{-(j-p)}
by 1 and every term
(C_{1}+N-j)
by
C_{1}+N
. We obtain

\mathcal{A}_{K}\leq C_{1}^{N}N(N+1)\left(C_{1}+N\right)^{N}\,.

Invoking the elementary bound
N!\geq(N/3)^{N}
, we thus get the existence of a constant
C_{2}>C_{1}
such that

\mathcal{A}_{K}\leq C_{2}^{N}\,N!\,.

Plugging this inequality back into (
2.60
) and recalling that we are considering a parameter
s\geq 1
,
we conclude that for
N\geq 0
the following holds true:

(2.62)

\sup_{K}\left|(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\Theta_{f})(m,m,\lambda)\right|\leq(N!)^{s}\,C_{3}^{N}\,C^{N+1}\,2^{N+2}\,C_{2}^{N}\,N!\leq C_{4}^{2N+1}\,[(2N)!]^{s}.

Step 3: Conclusion.

Summarizing our considerations so far, we have obtained relations (
2.48
) and (
2.62
). It is now straightforward to see that (
2.28
) is fulfilled, which finishes the proof.
∎

2.4.
Bernstein’s lemma

As a key step in the definition of weighted Besov spaces, one would like to accurately quantify the effect of derivatives on functions whose support is bounded in Fourier modes. This kind of result is often referred to as Bernstein’s lemma, see
[
12
, Section 2.2]
. We will prove this result for
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
in the current section, beginning with a few preliminary notions.
Let us start by introducing a broad class of weights which will be used in the sequel.

Definition 2.14
.

We are given two functions
v,w:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, considered as weights on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. We say that
w
is
v
-moderate if for every
p,q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
,we have

(2.63)

w(pq)\leq v(p)w(q).

With the notion of
v
-moderate weight in hand, we now state a weighted version of Young’s inequality adapted to the Heisenberg group framework.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
v,w:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be two weight functions, and assume that
w
is
v
-moderate according to Definition
2.14
. Consider 3 parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in[1,\infty]
such that

\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{1}{\gamma}+1.

Then for every measurable functions
f,g:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

(2.64)

\|(f\star g)w\|_{L^{\gamma}}\leq\|f\,v\|_{L^{\alpha}}\|g\,w\|_{L^{\beta}}.

Proof.

This follows from a similar argument as in the case of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see, e.g.,
[
12
, Theorem 2.4]
). Recall that for all
p\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
we have

(f\star g)(p)=\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}f(pq^{-1})g(q)d\mu(q)=\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}f(q)g(q^{-1}p)d\mu(q).

Therefore it is readily checked that

|f\star g|(p)w(p)\leq\int|f(q)|\,|g(q^{-1}p)|w(p)\,d\mu(q)\,.

Next recast relation (
2.63
) as
w(p)\leq v(q)w(q^{-1}p)
. We obtain

|f\star g|(p)w(p)\leq\int|f(q)|\,|g(q^{-1}p)|v(q)w(q^{-1}p)\,d\mu(q)=[(|f|v)\star(|g|w)](p).

Our claim (
2.64
) then follows from Young’s inequality for functions on locally compact groups (see
[
2
, Lemma 1.4]
).
∎

We now introduce a pseudo-norm on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
which avoids some singularities near the origin
e
. We label its main properties in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.16
.

Recall that
the Carnot–Carathéodory distance is defined in (
1.5
). We define a pseudo-norm
|\cdot|_{*}
on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
by

(2.65)

|p|_{*}=\sqrt{1+d_{cc}(e,p)^{2}},\quad\text{for $p\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}$.}

Then
|\cdot|_{*}
satisfies the following properties:

(2.66)

|e|_{*}=1,\quad|p^{-1}|_{*}=|p|_{*}\qquad\text{for $p\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}$,}

as well as the triangular inequality

(2.67)

|pq|_{*}\leq|p|_{*}+|q|_{*}.

Proof.

Relation (
2.66
) is trivial. In order to prove (
2.67
), let us invoke the triangular inequality for
d_{cc}
and the left invariance property (
1.6
). This enables to write

|pq|^{2}_{*}=1+d_{cc}(e,pq)^{2}\leq 1+(d_{cc}(e,p)+d_{cc}(p,pq))^{2}=1+(d_{cc}(e,p)+d_{cc}(e,q))^{2}.

Expanding the right hand side above and resorting to the definition (
2.65
) of
|\cdot|_{*}
, it is thus easily seen that

\displaystyle|pq|^{2}_{*}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+d_{cc}(e,p)^{2}+d_{cc}(e,q)^{2}+2\,d_{cc}(e,p)d_{cc}(e,q)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(|p|_{*}+|q|_{*})^{2},

which finishes the proof of (
2.67
).
∎

The pseudo-norm
|\cdot|_{*}
is the main building block in order to introduce the exponential weights considered in this paper. We label the definition of those weights for further use.

Definition 2.17
.

Recall that
s
is a parameter such that
s\geq 1
.
Let
\eta
be a real number in
(0,1/s)
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
. We define the weight
w_{\nu}:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

w_{\nu}(q)=e^{-\nu|q|_{*}^{\eta}},

where we recall that
|q|_{*}
is defined in (
2.65
). We also denote by
L_{\nu}^{p}
the corresponding space
L^{p}({\mathbf{H}^{n}},w^{p}_{\nu}(q)\,d\mu(q))
, and by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\nu}
the inner product in
L^{2}_{\nu}
.

Remark 2.18
.

Our notation for weighted
L^{p}
-spaces differs slightly from the one introduced in
[
12
]
. Namely the spaces in
[
12
]
are of the form
L^{p}({\mathbf{H}^{n}},w_{\nu}(q)\,d\mu(q))
, in contrast with our version where we also raise
w_{\nu}
to a power
p
. While the main properties of the weighted spaces are not affected by this difference, our spaces are such that the weighted
L^{\infty}
-space can be seen as a limit of weighted
L^{p}
-spaces. This will be useful for the estimates involving the noise
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
in (
1.13
). It also simplifies the paraproduct computations in Section
3.3
below.

We now turn to a basic lemma stating that our exponential weights are moderate.

Lemma 2.19
.

For
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, recall that the weight
w_{\nu}
is introduced in Definition
2.17
, for a parameter
0<\eta<1/s
. Then for all
\nu\geq 0
,
w_{\nu}
is
w_{-\nu}
-moderate.

Proof.

Our objective is to show that the pair
w=w_{\nu}
,
v=w_{-\nu}
satisfies relation (
2.63
). Namely, for
p,q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
we wish to prove that

(2.68)

\frac{w_{\nu}(pq)}{w_{\nu}(q)}\leq w_{-\nu}(p).

To this aim, we first apply the mere definition of
w_{\nu}
in order to write

\frac{w_{\nu}(pq)}{w_{\nu}(q)}=\text{exp}(\nu(|q|_{*}^{\eta}-|pq|_{*}^{\eta})).

Next we invoke (
2.66
) and (
2.67
), which yields
|q|_{*}\leq|pq|_{*}+|p|_{*}
. Since we are considering a positive parameter
\nu
and
\eta<1/s\leq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{w_{\nu}(pq)}{w_{\nu}(q)}

\displaystyle\leq\text{exp}(\nu(|pq|_{*}+|p|_{*})^{\eta}-\nu|pq|_{*}^{\eta})

(2.69)

\displaystyle\leq\text{exp}(\nu|p|_{*}^{\eta})=w_{-\nu}(p)\,,

where we resort to the elementary relation
|a+b|^{\eta}-|a|^{\eta}\leq|b|^{\eta}
for the second inequality. Note that (
2.69
) is exactly our claim (
2.68
), which finishes the proof.
∎

It is known (see
[
14
, p. 23]
) that there exists a function
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with compact support such that
\varphi\equiv 1
on the unit ball centered at the origin (with respect to the Euclidean distance
|\cdot|_{2}
). We recall this important result in the lemma below.

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
s>1
and recall that the Gevrey space
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by (
2.27
). Then there exists a compactly supported function
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\varphi\equiv 1
on the unit ball
B_{1}(0)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x|_{2}<1\}
.

Proof.
It is a standard fact that the function
\psi(x)=\exp(-x^{-\kappa})\,\mathbbm{1}_{(x>0)}
, where
\kappa=1/(s-1)
, lies in
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R})
. Since Gevrey classes on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are stable under multiplication, the compactly supported function

\Psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\psi(1+x_{j})\psi(1-x_{j})

lies in
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then we take
\varphi
to be the convolution of the function
\Psi/\lVert\Psi\rVert_{L^{1}}
and the indicator function of
B_{2n}(0)
(see e.g.
[
14
, p. 22]
), so that
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
has compact support and
\varphi\equiv 1
on
B_{1}(0)
.∎

In the next lemma we extend Lemma
2.20
to the Heisenberg group. That is, we prove the existence of a Gevrey function
\phi\in\mathcal{G}^{s}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
with bounded support, such that
\phi\equiv 1
on the unit ball of
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
.

Lemma 2.21
.

Consider a parameter
s>1
and the Gevrey space
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
on the Heisenberg group
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
, as introduced in Definition
2.10
. Recall that the distance
\widehat{d}_{0}
is given by (
2.20
). Then there exists a function
\tilde{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
with bounded support such that
\tilde{\phi}\equiv 1
on the unit ball
B_{1}:=\{\hat{w}\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}:\widehat{d}_{0}(\hat{w})<1\}
.

Proof.

Consider the function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
constructed in Lemma
2.20
. For
(x,\kappa,\lambda)\in[0,\infty)^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
, we set

(2.70)

f(x,\kappa,\lambda)=\varphi(x)\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}},\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\Theta_{f}(m,\ell,\lambda),

where we recall the notation
\Theta_{f}
introduced in (
2.42
).
Our aim is to show that
\tilde{\phi}=\Theta_{f}
as defined above fulfills the conditions of our lemma.

(i)

Clearly the function
f
satisfies both conditions (a) and (b) in Proposition
2.13
. Therefore if we define
\Theta_{f}
as in (
2.42
), we directly get that
\Theta_{f}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
.

(ii)

Observe that if
f
is given as in (
2.70
), then
\Theta_{f}
can be written as

(2.71)

\Theta_{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\varphi(|\lambda|R(m,\ell))\mathbbm{1}_{\{m=\ell\}}.

Thanks to the relation above, one can show that the function
\Theta_{f}
is identically
1
on the ball
B_{1}
in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. To this aim, resorting to the expression (
2.20
), it is readily checked that if we wish to have
\widehat{d}_{0}(\hat{w})<1
then we need
m=\ell
. Hence one can describe
B_{1}
as

(2.72)

B_{1}=\{(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}:\,m=\ell,\,|\lambda|R(m,m)=|\lambda|(2|m|+n)<1\}.

From (
2.72
) and the fact that
\lvert|\lambda|R(m,m)\rvert_{2}\leq\lvert|\lambda|R(m,m)\rvert_{1}
, we have that

\tilde{\phi}\left(m,\ell,\lambda\right)=\Theta_{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\varphi\left(|\lambda|R(m,m)\right)\equiv 1\,,\ \quad\text{on}\ \,B_{1}.

This proves that
\tilde{\phi}\equiv 1
on the unit ball
B_{1}
.

(iii)

To see that the
\Theta_{f}
has bounded support, let
R>0
be such that
\varphi(x)=0
whenever
|x|\geq R
. Then
\Theta_{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)=0
whenever
||\lambda|R(m,\ell)|_{2}\geq R
. Due to the fact that
\lvert|\lambda|R(m,\ell)\rvert_{1}\leq n^{1/2}\lvert|\lambda|R(m,\ell)\rvert_{2}
, we see that

|\lambda|(|m+\ell|+n)\geq n^{1/2}R\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\big\lvert|\lambda|R(m,\ell)\big\rvert_{2}>R\,.

Hence it is readily seen that

\Theta_{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)=0\quad\text{whenever }\quad|\lambda|(|m+\ell|+n)\geq n^{1/2}R.

Noting that the support of
\Theta_{f}
is contained in the diagonal
\{m=\ell\}
, we then conclude that

(2.73)

\text{Supp}\,\Theta_{f}\subset\{(m,\ell,\lambda)\,:\,m=\ell,\,|\lambda|(2|m|+n)<n^{1/2}R\}.

At last it is clear from (
2.73
) that
\text{Supp}\,\Theta_{f}
is bounded with respect to the metric
\widehat{d}
.
Specifically, one can prove that if
\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\prime}
lye in
\text{Supp}\,\Theta_{f}
, then
\widehat{d}(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\prime})\leq M
for a large enough constant
M
. Namely for all
\hat{w}=(m,m,\lambda)
and
\hat{w}^{\prime}=(m^{\prime},m^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
such that
\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\prime}\in\text{Supp}\,\Theta_{f}
, according to the definition (
2.19
) of
\widehat{d}
we have

\displaystyle\widehat{d}(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=|\lambda(2m)-\lambda^{\prime}(2m^{\prime})|_{1}+0+n|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq|\lambda|(2|m|)+|\lambda^{\prime}|(2|m^{\prime}|)+n|\lambda|+n|\lambda^{\prime}|<2\,n^{1/2}R\equiv M,

where the last inequality follows from (
2.73
). We then obtain that the diameter of
\text{Supp}\,\Theta_{f}
is bounded by
M
.

Gathering the items (i)-(ii)-(iii) above, our claims are now proved.
∎

Before stating our version of Bernstein’s lemma, let us address some scaling properties that shall be used later.

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
\tilde{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
as be given in Lemma
2.21
. For any
\tau>0
, let
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda/\tau)
,
(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. Then
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}
is in
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
with bounded support and
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\equiv 1
on
B_{\tau}\cap D:=\{\hat{w}\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}:\widehat{d}_{0}(\hat{w})<\tau,\text{ and }m=\ell\}
. Moreover, let

g_{\tau}
be the inverse Fourier transform of
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}
, i.e.
\hat{g}_{\tau}=\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}
. Then for any
(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, we have

(2.74)

g_{\tau}(x,y,z)=\tau^{n+1}g_{1}(\tau^{1/2}x,\tau^{1/2}y,\tau z)=\tau^{n+1}g_{1}(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}\,}q),

where the dilation
\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}
is defined by (
1.4
).

Proof.

First from its definition and (
2.71
) we know that

(2.75)

\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\varphi\left(\frac{|\lambda|}{\tau}R(m,\ell)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{m=\ell\}}.

Similar to what we have proved in Lemma
2.21
, it is then easily seen that
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\equiv 1
on the set
B_{\tau}
.
To see (
2.74
), resorting to (
2.11
) we have

(2.76)

\displaystyle g_{\tau}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\sum_{m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}J_{m,\ell,\tau}(x,y,z),

where we have set

J_{m,\ell,\tau}(x,y,z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\lambda z}K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(x,y,z)\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,\ell,\lambda)|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda.

Next writing the definition (
2.10
) of
K_{m,\ell,\lambda}
and having the expression (
2.6
) of
\Phi_{k}^{\lambda}
in mind, we obtain

J_{m,\ell,\tau}(x,y,z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\lambda z}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2i\lambda\langle y,\,\xi\rangle}|\lambda|^{n/2}\Phi_{m}(|\lambda|^{1/2}(x+\xi))\\
\cdot\Phi_{\ell}^{\lambda}(|\lambda|^{1/2}(-x+\xi))d\xi\,\bigg)\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,\ell,\lambda)|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda.

Moreover, it is easily seen from (
2.75
) that
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\tilde{\phi}_{1}(m,\ell,\lambda/\tau)
for all
\tau>0
. Therefore one can apply the rescaling
\lambda\to\tau\lambda
and
\xi\to\tau^{-1/2}\xi
in order to get

J_{m,\ell,\tau}(x,y,z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\tau\lambda z}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2i\lambda\langle\tau^{1/2}y,\,\xi\rangle}|\lambda|^{n/2}\Phi_{m}(|\lambda|^{1/2}(\tau^{1/2}x+\xi))\\
\cdot\Phi_{\ell}^{\lambda}(|\lambda|^{1/2}(-\tau^{1/2}x+\xi))d\xi\,\bigg)\tilde{\phi}_{1}(m,\ell,\lambda)|\lambda|^{n}\tau^{n+1}d\lambda.

Invoking the definition (
2.10
) of the kernels
K_{m,\ell,\lambda}
again, the above expression for
J_{m,\ell,\tau}
can be recast as

\displaystyle J_{m,\ell,\tau}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\tau^{n+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\lambda(\tau z)}K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(\tau^{1/2}x,\tau^{1/2}y,\tau z)\tilde{\phi}_{1}(m,\ell,\lambda)|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda

\displaystyle=\tau^{n+1}J_{m,\ell,1}(\tau^{1/2}x,\tau^{1/2}y,\tau z).

Plugging this identity into (
2.76
), this easily implies (
2.74
).
∎

As a last preliminary step before we prove Bernstein’s lemma, we state a scaling property for functions of the form
\Delta^{k}g_{\tau}
for any positive integer
k
.

Lemma 2.23
.

For
\tau>0
, consider the function
g_{\tau}
given by (
2.74
). For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let us also recall that
\Delta^{k}
is defined in (
2.14
). We denote by
g^{(k)}_{\tau}
the dilated function of
\Delta^{k}g
, namely, for
q=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
,

(2.77)

\displaystyle g_{\tau}^{(k)}(q)=(\Delta^{k}g_{1})_{\tau}(q)=\tau^{n+1}(\Delta^{k}g_{1})(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}\,q),

where the dilations
\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}
are defined by (
1.4
). Also recall that the spaces
L^{p}_{\nu}
have been introduced in Definition
2.17
.
Then for any
\nu,\gamma>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(2.78)

\lVert g_{\tau}^{(k)}\rVert_{L^{\gamma}_{-\nu}}\leq C\tau^{(n+1)(1-\frac{1}{\gamma})}.

Proof of Lemma
2.23
.

We first prove that
g^{(k)}
in (
2.77
) has exponential decay, which is enough to see that
g^{(k)}_{\tau}\in L^{\gamma}_{-\nu}
. As a preliminary step in this direction, let us show that
\widehat{g^{(k)}_{1}}
sits in
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})\cap\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
and has bounded support.
To this aim, note that owing to (
2.14
) we have

(2.79)

\widehat{g^{(k)}_{1}}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\widehat{\Delta^{k}_{\mathcal{H}\,}g_{1}}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\Big(-4|\lambda|(2|m|+n)\Big)^{k}\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda).

Hence
\widehat{g^{(k)}_{1}}
has bounded support as
\tilde{\phi}
does. In addition, recall from (
2.70
) that
\tilde{\phi}=\Theta_{f}
with
f=\varphi\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}}
. Some elementary algebra using the definition (
2.42
) of
\Theta_{f}
shows that

(2.80)

\Big(-4|\lambda|(2|m|+n)\Big)^{k}\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\Theta_{f_{0}}(m,\ell,\lambda),

where
f_{0}(x,\kappa,\lambda)=(-4\sum x_{i})^{k}\varphi(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}}
.
In addition, since
h\equiv\varphi\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}}
verifies the assumptions of Proposition
2.13
and
f_{0}
is defined as a polynomial in
x
multiplied by
h
, it is readily checked that
f_{0}
also verifies the assumptions of Proposition
2.13
. Putting together (
2.79
) and (
2.80
) and applying Proposition
2.13
, we then obtain that

\widehat{g^{(k)}_{1}}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})\cap\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n}).

Therefore a straightforward application of Proposition
2.12
yields that
g^{(k)}_{1}
is of exponential decay. Otherwise stated, there exist
C>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

(2.81)

|g_{1}^{(k)}(q)|\leq Ce^{-\varepsilon\lvert q\rvert_{h}^{1/s}}\ \,\text{for all $q=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}$}.

Let us now turn to the scaling inequality (
2.78
). According to our definition (
2.77
) of
g^{(k)}
, we have for
q=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
,

g^{(k)}_{\tau}(q)=\tau^{n+1}g^{(k)}_{1}(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}\,q).

With our Definition (
2.17
) of inner products in weighted spaces and using a scaling

q=(x,y,z)\to\delta_{\tau^{1/2}\,}q=(\tau^{-1/2}x,\tau^{-1/2}y,\tau^{-1}z)
, we get

\displaystyle\lVert g_{\tau}^{(k)}\rVert_{L^{\gamma}_{-\nu}}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\tau^{\gamma(n+1)}\,|g_{1}^{(k)}|^{\gamma}(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}\,}q)\,e^{{\nu\gamma}|q|_{*}^{\eta}}\,d\mu(q)\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\tau^{(n+1)(1-\frac{1}{\gamma})}\left(\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}|g_{1}^{(k)}|^{\gamma}(q)\,e^{{\nu\gamma}|(\delta_{\tau^{-1/2}\,}q)|_{*}^{\eta}}\,d\mu(q)\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}.

Plugging (
2.81
) into the above inequality we thus obtain

(2.82)

\lVert g_{\tau}^{(k)}\rVert_{L^{\gamma}_{-\nu}}\leq C\tau^{(n+1)(1-\frac{1}{\gamma})}\left(\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}e^{{\nu\gamma}|(\delta_{\tau^{-1/2}\,}q)|_{*}^{\eta}-\varepsilon\lvert q\rvert_{h}^{1/s}}\,d\mu(q)\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}.

We now resort to the definition (
2.65
) of
|\cdot|_{*}
and relation (
1.7
). We also take into account that
\tau\geq 1
. We discover that there exists
c>0
such that

\displaystyle|\delta_{\tau^{-1/2}}\,q|_{*}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+d_{cc}(e,\,\delta_{\tau^{-1/2}}\,q)}\leq c(1+|q|_{h}).

Plugging this relation into (
2.82
) and recalling that in Definition
2.17
we have chosen
0<\eta<\frac{1}{s}
, we end up with

(2.83)

\lVert g_{\tau}^{(k)}\rVert_{L^{\gamma}_{-\nu}}\leq C\tau^{(n+1)(1-\frac{1}{\gamma})}\left(\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}e^{{\nu\gamma}c(1+|q|_{h})^{1/s}-\varepsilon\lvert q\rvert_{h}^{1/s}}\,d\mu(q)\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}.

We now choose
\nu_{0}={\varepsilon}/{2c\gamma}>0
and
0<\nu<\nu_{0}
in (
2.83
), so that
\nu\gamma c<\varepsilon/2
. We obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}e^{{\nu\gamma}c(1+|q|_{h})^{1/s}-\varepsilon\lvert q\rvert_{h}^{1/s}}\,d\mu(q)\leq\int_{|q|_{h}\leq 1}e^{2\varepsilon}d\mu(q)+\int_{|q|_{h}\geq 1}e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\lvert q\rvert_{h}^{1/s}}d\mu(q)\leq C_{1}

for some constant
C_{1}>0
.
Plug the above bound into (
2.83
) we then obtain the desired estimate (
2.78
).
∎

The preliminary results we have proved so far allow us to state the main result of this section. That is, we are ready to prove the aforementioned Bernstein lemma.

Proposition 2.24
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
, and consider parameters
\nu_{0}>0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta\in[1,\infty]
. Then there exists
C=C_{\nu_{0},\,\beta}
such that for every
\nu\leq\nu_{0}
,
\alpha\geq\beta
and
\tau\geq 1
, if we assume

(2.84)

\emph{Supp}\,\hat{f}\subset\{(m,\ell,\lambda):|\lambda|(2|m|+n)<\tau\},

then we have

(2.85)

\lVert\Delta^{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\leq C\tau^{k+(n+1)(\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{\alpha})}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\beta}_{\nu}},

where we recall that the spaces
L^{\alpha}_{\nu}
are introduced in definition
2.17
.

Remark 2.25
.

Condition (
2.84
) is interpreted as
\mathrm{Supp}\,\hat{f}
being contained in a ball in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. It is compatible with
[
1
, Definition 3.1]
.

Proof of Proposition
2.24
.

Recall the definition (
2.18
) of our dot product
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\cdot f
. Also recall from (
2.75
) that
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}
is supported on the diagonal
\{m=\ell\}
. Hence we have

(2.86)

(\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\cdot\hat{f})(m,\ell,\lambda)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,j,\lambda)\hat{f}(j,\ell,\lambda)=\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,m,\lambda)\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda).

One of the crucial points in our proof is thus the following: since we have assumed (
2.84
) and since we have obtained the relation
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\equiv 1
on
B_{\tau}\cap D
in Lemma
2.22
, it is readily checked that
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,m,\lambda)\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)
for all
(m,\ell,\lambda)
. Therefore, (
2.86
) can also be written as

(2.87)

(\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\cdot\hat{f})(m,\ell,\lambda)=\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda).

Taking inverse Fourier transforms on both sides of (
2.87
), resorting to (
2.17
) and recalling that we have set
\hat{g_{\tau}}=\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}
in Lemma
2.22
, this yields

(2.88)

g_{\tau}\star f=f.

Let us now evaluate
\Delta^{k}g_{\tau}
for
g_{\tau}
in the left hand side of (
2.88
). Resorting to (
2.77
), some easy scaling considerations entail that

(2.89)

\Delta^{k}g_{\tau}=\tau^{n+1}\Delta^{k}(g_{1}\circ\delta_{\tau^{1/2}})=\tau^{n+1+k}(\Delta^{k}g_{1})\circ\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}.

Recalling again that
g^{(k)}_{\tau}
is defined by (
2.77
), we have obtained

(2.90)

\Delta^{k}g_{\tau}=\tau^{k}g^{(k)}_{\tau}.

Hence applying
\Delta^{k}
on both sides of (
2.88
) and combining with (
2.90
) we get

(2.91)

\displaystyle\Delta^{k}f=\Delta^{k}g_{\tau}\star f=\tau^{k}g_{\tau}^{(k)}\star f.

We are now in a position to apply the weighted Young’s inequality in Proposition
2.15
to relation (
2.91
). Namely, for
\alpha\geq\beta
and
\gamma
such that
\gamma^{-1}+\beta^{-1}=1+\alpha^{-1}
, by invoking the fact that
w_{\nu}
is
w_{-\nu}
-moderate (see Lemma
2.19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\Delta^{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau^{k}\,\lVert(g^{(k)}_{\tau}\star f)w_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tau^{k}\lVert g_{\tau}^{(k)}\,w_{-\nu}\rVert_{L^{\gamma}}\lVert fw_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{\beta}}=\tau^{k}\,\lVert g_{\tau}^{(k)}\rVert_{L^{\gamma}_{-\nu}}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\beta}_{\nu}}.

Our claim (
2.85
) then follows from Lemma
2.23
and the relation
\beta^{-1}-\alpha^{-1}=1-\gamma^{-1}
.
∎

Remark 2.26
.

The results in Proposition
2.24
also holds for the weight function
\rho_{b}(q)=C(1+|q|_{*}^{b})
for some
C,b>0
. Namely for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha\geq\beta
and
\tau\geq 1
, if
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
satisfies (
2.84
), then there exists
C=C_{b,\,\beta}
such that

(2.92)

\lVert\Delta^{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\rho_{b}}}\leq C\tau^{k+(n+1)(\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{\alpha})}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\beta}_{\rho_{b}}}.

The proof follows the lines of the proof for Proposition
2.24
. One only need to use the facts that

\rho_{b}(p)=1+|p|_{*}^{b}\leq 1+(|q|_{*}+|q^{-1}p|_{*})^{b}\leq C_{b}(1+|q|_{*}^{b})(1+|q^{-1}p|_{*}^{b})=C_{b}\rho_{b}(q)\rho_{b}(q^{-1}p)

\displaystyle\lVert g_{\tau}^{(k)}\rVert_{L^{\gamma}_{\rho_{b}}}\leq C\tau^{(n+1)(1-\frac{1}{\gamma})}.

).

3.
Weighted Besov spaces

In this section we introduce a proper notion of weighted Besov space on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
and derive corresponding smoothing effects for the heat flow.

3.1.
Basic definitions

The blocks in Littlewood-Paley analysis are based on partitions of unity. Let us first construct those partitions in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. To this aim we start with a partition of unity in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As in
[
2
]
, this is defined in the following way.

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists a pair
\chi,\tilde{\chi}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that:

(i)

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
0\leq\chi(x),\tilde{\chi}(x)\leq 1
.

(ii)

The supports of
\chi,\tilde{\chi}
satisfy

(3.1)

\textnormal{Supp}\,\tilde{\chi}\subset B_{4/3}(0):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x|<4/3\},\quad\textnormal{Supp}\,\chi\subset\mathcal{C}^{*}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:3/4\leq|x|<8/3\}.

(iii)

We have

\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\chi_{k}(x)=1,\quad\text{where}\ \chi_{-1}=\tilde{\chi}\ \text{ and }\ \chi_{k}=\chi(\cdot/2^{k})\ \text{ for }\ k\geq 0.

In Proposition
2.13
, we used the existence of a function in
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in order to produce an example of function in
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
. We now proceed in the same way for the partition of unity on the diagonal of
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. Our result is summarized in the lemma below.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\{\chi_{k}:k\geq-1\}
be the partition of unity in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
produced in Proposition
3.1
. We set
f_{k}=\chi_{k}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}}
, and

(3.2)

\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\Theta_{f_{k}}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\chi_{k}(|\lambda|R(m,\ell))\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{m=\ell\}},

where we recall the notation
R
and
\Theta_{f_{k}}
from (
2.42
). Then we have

(i)

Every
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
is an element of
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
.

(ii)

The following relation holds true:

(3.3)

\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{m=\ell\}}.

Proof.

Item (i) is an immediate consequence of Proposition
2.13
. Item (ii) is derived trivially from Proposition
3.1
.
∎

With the partition of unity in hand we can now define the Littlewood-Paley blocks similarly to what is done in the flat case. This is formalized in the lemma below about Schwartz type functions.

Lemma 3.3
.

Consider
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
, where we recall that
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
is introduced in Definition
2.5
. For
k\geq-1
, we
define the block
\sigma_{k}f
of order
k
by its Fourier transform as

(3.4)

\widehat{\sigma_{k}f}=\tilde{\phi}_{k}\cdot\hat{f},

where
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
is defined in lemma
3.2
.
We also define
S_{k}f
as

S_{k}f=\sum_{i<k}\sigma_{i}f.

Then we have the pointwise convergence of
S_{k}f
to
f
.

Proof.

Owing to (
3.2
), every
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
is supported on the diagonal
\{m=\ell\}
in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\phi}_{k}\cdot\hat{f}\right)(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,j,\lambda)\hat{f}(j,\ell,\lambda)

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda).

Hence according to our definition (
3.4
), we get

\displaystyle\widehat{S_{k}f}(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<k}\tilde{\phi}_{i}(m,m,\lambda)\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda).

Now thanks to Proposition
2.8
we have that
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
. Thus
\sum_{i<k}\tilde{\phi}_{i}(m,m,\lambda)\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)
converges to
\hat{f}(m,\ell,\lambda)
, due to (
3.3
). Applying Proposition
2.8
again, we thus get the desired convergence of
S_{k}f
to
f
.
∎

We close this section by giving a formal definition of our weighted Besov spaces.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
. For
k\geq-1
consider the function
\sigma_{k}f
defined by (
3.4
).
For any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\nu>0
, and
\alpha,\beta\in[1,\infty]
, we define a norm

\|f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\gamma,\,\nu}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}=\left\{\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\left(2^{\gamma k}\|\sigma_{k}f\|_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\right)^{\beta}\right\}^{\frac{1}{\beta}},

where the spaces
L^{\alpha}_{\nu}
are those introduced in Definition
2.17
.
The weighted Besov space
\mathfrak{B}^{\gamma,\,\nu}_{\alpha,\,\beta}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
is defined as the completion of
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
with respect to this norm.

3.2.
Heat flow and Besov spaces

In this section we will quantify the smoothing effects of the heat semigroup in the weighted Besov spaces
\mathfrak{B}^{\gamma,\,\nu}_{\alpha,\,\beta}
. As a preliminary result, we first state a space-time decay property for functions with Fourier transforms supported in an annulus.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a Gevrey function supported in an annulus
\mathcal{C}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:R\leq|x|_{2}\leq R^{\prime}\}
. As in (
2.70
) and (
2.71
), we consider the function

(3.6)

\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\Theta_{f}(m,\ell,\lambda),\quad\text{where}\quad f(x,\kappa,\lambda)=\varphi(x)\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}}.

Also recall that the heat kernel
p_{t}
has been defined by (
1.9
). We denote
g=g_{1}
where
g_{\tau}
is introduced in Lemma
2.22
. Specifically we have
\hat{g}=\tilde{\phi}
. Then
there exist constants
C,c>0
such that

(3.7)

|(g\star p_{t})(q)|\leq Ce^{-ct-c\lvert q\rvert_{\mathrm{h}}^{1/s}}\ \,\text{for all}\ \,q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}.

Proof.

As stated in (
2.16
), we have

(3.8)

\hat{p}_{t}(m,\ell,\lambda)=e^{-4t|\lambda|(2|m|+n)}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{m=\ell\}}.

Moreover, it is readily checked that

\displaystyle\big(\widehat{g\star p_{t}}\big)(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\big(\tilde{\phi}\cdot\hat{p}_{t}\big)(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\tilde{\phi}(m,j,\lambda)\,\hat{p}_{t}(j,\ell,\lambda)

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda)\,\hat{p}_{t}(m,\ell,\lambda).

Gathering the expressions (
2.70
) and (
2.71
) for
\tilde{\phi}
and our formula (
3.8
) for
\hat{p}_{t}
, we get

\big(\widehat{g\star p_{t}}\big)(m,\ell,\lambda)=\varphi(|\lambda|R(m,\ell))\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{m=\ell\}}\,e^{-4t|\lambda|(2|m|+n)}.

Recalling again the definition (
2.42
) of
\Theta_{f}
, one discovers that
\widehat{g\star p_{t}}
can be written as

(3.10)

\big(\widehat{g\star p_{t}}\big)(m,\ell,\lambda)=\Theta_{f}(m,\ell,\lambda),\quad\text{with}\quad f(x,\kappa,\lambda)=\varphi(x)\,e^{-4t(x_{1}+...+x_{n})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}}.

With
(
3.10
) in hand, we will prove that
\widehat{g\star p_{t}}
satisfies (
3.7
) by a mere elaboration of Proposition
2.13
. Specifically we let the reader check that one is just reduced to prove an inequality similar to (
2.41
), weighted by the exponential term
e^{-ct}
. Namely for all
t\geq 0
,
x\in[0,\infty)^{n}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, it is sufficient to prove that for any compact set
K\subset\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
, there exists a
positive constant
C_{K}
such that

(3.11)

\sup_{K}\left(\max\left(|\widehat{\Delta}^{N}\Theta_{f}|,\,|\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{\lambda}^{N}\Theta_{f}|\right)\right)\leq C_{K}^{2N+1}[(2N)!]^{s}e^{-ct}\ \,\text{for all $N\geq 0$.}

Otherwise stated, going back to (
2.27
), we wish to show that the function

(3.12)

h(x)=\varphi(x)\zeta_{t}(x),\quad\text{where}\quad\zeta_{t}(x)=e^{-4t(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})},

is an element of
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Invoking the fact that
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and stability arguments for products in
\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we are further reduced to show that

(3.13)

\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\zeta_{t}(x)\leq C^{|\alpha|+1}(\alpha!)^{s}\,e^{-ct}

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
t\geq 0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. In addition, observe that in (
3.12
), the function
\zeta_{t}
is multiplied by
\varphi
, whose support is included in the annulus
\mathcal{C}
. Therefore we can content ourselves with a proof of (
3.13
) for
x\in\mathcal{C}
only. Recalling that
\mathcal{C}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:R\leq|x|_{2}\leq R^{\prime}\}
,
we set
r=R/2
. With all those preliminary reductions in hand, we now evaluate the left hand side of (
3.13
). An elementary computation shows that for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
\zeta_{t}
defined by (
3.12
), we have

(3.14)

\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\zeta_{t}(x)\right\rvert=(4t)^{|\alpha|}e^{-4t(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})}.

Moreover for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\geq 0
and an additional parameter
r\geq 0
, it is readily checked that

(4t)^{k}=r^{-k}(4rt)^{k}\leq r^{-k}\,k!\,e^{4rt}.

Plugging this rough estimate into (
3.14
), we end up with

\displaystyle\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\zeta_{t}(x)\right\rvert\leq r^{-|\alpha|}\,|\alpha|!\,e^{-4t(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}-r)}.

Now choose
r=R/2
and invoke the fact that
|x|_{2}\geq R
if
x\in\mathcal{C}
. Also, resort to the inequality
|\alpha|!/\alpha!\leq n^{|\alpha|}
, valid for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. This yields the existence of two constants
C,c>0
such that

\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\zeta_{t}(x)\right\rvert\leq C^{|\alpha|+1}\alpha!\,e^{-ct}.

Summarizing our considerations, we have just proved relation (
3.13
). Due to our series of reductions (
3.11
)–(
3.12
), this shows relation (
3.7
) and finishes our proof.∎

We turn to a first quantification of the smoothing effects of the heat semigroup for functions whose support is bounded in Fourier modes.

Proposition 3.6

(Smoothing effect of the heat flow)
.

For two constants
0<R_{1}<R_{2}
, consider the following region in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
:

(3.15)

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}=\{(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}:R_{1}\leq|\lambda|(2|m|+n)\leq R_{2}\}.

Recall that the heat semigroup
P_{t}=e^{t\Delta/2}
is defined by (
1.11
). Let also
\nu_{0}>0
be an additional parameter and we pick an arbitrary
\alpha\geq 1
. Then there exist constants
C,c>0
such that for every
0<\nu\leq\nu_{0}
,
t\geq 0
and
\tau\geq 1
,
F\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
, if we assume

(3.16)

\text{Supp}\,\hat{F}\subset\tau\tilde{\mathcal{R}}=\{(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}:\tau R_{1}\leq|\lambda|(2|m|+n)\leq\tau R_{2}\},

then the following inequality holds true:

(3.17)

\lVert P_{t}F\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\leq Ce^{-c\tau t}\lVert F\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a Gevrey function supported inside an annulus in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and such that
\varphi\equiv 1
on
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:R_{1}\leq|x|_{1}\leq R_{2}\}
. As in Lemma
3.5
and (
2.70
)–(
2.71
), we consider the function

(3.18)

\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\Theta_{f}(m,\ell,\lambda),\quad\text{where}\quad f(x,\kappa,\lambda)=\varphi(x)\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}}.

Then
\tilde{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
is a Gevrey function with compact support in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
and such that
\tilde{\phi}\equiv 1
on
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}\cap D
, where
D
is the diagonal
(m=\ell)
. Recall that for all our computations we are considering
\eta
such that
\eta<1/s
for the Definition
2.17
of our exponential weights
w_{\nu}
. As in Lemma
2.22
, we write
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}=\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda/\tau)
and
\hat{g_{\tau}}=\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}
, so that

(3.19)

\displaystyle g_{\tau}(q)=\tau^{n+1}g_{1}(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}\,q),\quad\text{for $q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}$.}

We have

\displaystyle\widehat{P_{t}F}(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\hat{p}_{t}(m,j,\lambda)\hat{F}(j,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\hat{p}_{t}(m,m,\lambda)\hat{F}(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\hat{p}_{t}(m,m,\lambda)\hat{F}(m,\ell,\lambda)\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,m,\lambda),

where we have used the fact that
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}=1
on
\tau\tilde{\mathcal{R}}\cap D
for the last identity. It follows that

\displaystyle\widehat{P_{t}F}(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\cdot\hat{p}_{t})(m,m,\lambda)\hat{F}(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=((\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\cdot\hat{p}_{t})\cdot\hat{F})(m,\ell,\lambda).

Taking inverse Fourier transforms above, we have thus obtained that

P_{t}F=(g_{\tau}\star p_{t})\star F.

Thus applying Proposition
2.15
, we get

(3.20)

\displaystyle\lVert P_{t}F\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}=\lVert((g_{\tau}\star p_{t})\star F)\,w_{\nu}\rVert_{L^{\alpha}}\leq\lVert(g_{\tau}\star p_{t})\,w_{-\nu}\rVert_{L^{1}}\lVert F\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

To establish (
3.17
), it then suffices to show that

(3.21)

\lVert(g_{\tau}\star p_{t})\,w_{-\nu}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq Ce^{-c\tau t}.

In the remainder of the proof we will focus on deriving the above inequality. Let us first use the identity (
3.19
) together with
\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}(qx^{-1})=\delta_{\tau^{1/2}q}(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}x)^{-1}
. This enables to write

(3.22)

\displaystyle(g_{\tau}\star p_{t})(q)=\tau^{n+1}\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}g_{1}(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}\,q(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}\,x)^{-1})p_{t}(x)d\mu(x).

We now perform the change of variable
\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}x=y
in (
3.22
), which yields

(3.23)

\displaystyle(g_{\tau}\star p_{t})(q)=\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}g_{1}((\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}\,q)\,y^{-1})p_{t}(\delta_{\tau^{-1/2}}\,y)d\mu(y).

In addition, it is readily checked from (
1.9
) that
p_{t}((\delta_{\tau^{-1/2}}\,q))=p_{\tau t}(q)
. Plugging this identity into (
3.23
) we end up with

(3.24)

(g_{\tau}\star p_{t})(q)=(g_{1}\star p_{\tau t})(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}\,q),\quad\text{for $q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}$.}

Thus using Lemma
3.5
and another change of variable
q:=\delta_{\tau^{1/2}q}
, we get

\displaystyle\lVert(g_{\tau}\star p_{t})\,w_{-\nu}\rVert_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-c\tau t}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}e^{-c\,\lvert\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}\,q\rvert^{1/s}_{h}}e^{\nu\,|q|^{\eta}_{*}}d\mu(q)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-c\tau t}\tau^{-(n+1)}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}e^{-c\,\lvert q\rvert^{1/s}_{h}}e^{\nu\,\lvert\delta_{\tau^{-1/2}}\,q\rvert^{\eta}_{*}}d\mu(q).

In addition, since
\tau\geq 1
, we have
|\delta_{\tau^{-1/2}\,}q|_{*}\leq|q|_{*}
. Reporting this information in the right hand side above, we end up with

\displaystyle\lVert(g_{\tau}\star p_{t})\,w_{-\nu}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq Ce^{-c\tau t}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}e^{-c\,\lvert q\rvert^{1/s}_{h}}e^{\nu\,\lvert q\rvert^{\eta}_{*}}d\mu(q).

Owing to the fact that we have chosen
\eta<1/s
we conclude that (
3.21
) holds. This finishes our proof.
∎

As a last preliminary result, we now state a proposition on time regularity for the action of the heat semigroup.

Proposition 3.7

(Time regularity of the heat flow)
.

For a parameter
\tau\geq 1
, we consider a function
F\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
such that

\emph{Supp}\,\hat{F}\subset\{(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}:|\lambda|(2|m|+n)<\tau\}.

Then for all
\alpha\in[1,\infty)
and
\nu\leq\nu_{0}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
t\geq 0
and
\tau\geq 1
, we have

(3.25)

\lVert(\emph{Id}-P_{t})F\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\leq C(t\tau\wedge 1)\lVert F\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

Proof.

Thanks to Proposition
3.6
, we have
\|P_{t}F\|_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\lesssim\left\|F\right\|_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}
. Hence in order to show (
3.25
), it is sufficient to prove

(3.26)

\lVert(\text{Id}-P_{t})F\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\leq Ct\tau\lVert F\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

In order to derive (
3.26
), let
\tilde{\phi}
be as in Lemma
2.21
, and let
\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\tilde{\phi}(m,\ell,\lambda/\tau)
. Since
\text{Supp}\,\hat{F}\subset B_{\tau}
, we have

\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\cdot\hat{F}=\hat{F}=\mathbbm{1}_{D}\cdot\hat{F}

where
D
is the diagonal in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
. Hence we can write

\displaystyle\widehat{(\text{Id}-P_{t})F}

\displaystyle=(\mathbbm{1}_{D}-(\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\cdot\hat{p}_{t}))\cdot\hat{F}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\phi}_{\tau}\cdot(\mathbbm{1}_{D}-\hat{p}_{t}))\cdot\hat{F}

\displaystyle=\widehat{(g_{\tau}\star H_{t})\star F},

where
\hat{H_{t}}=\mathbbm{1}_{D}-\hat{p}_{t}
. As in (
3.24
), we have

(3.27)

\displaystyle(g_{\tau}\star H_{t})(q)=(g_{1}\star H_{\tau t})(\delta_{\tau^{1/2}}\,q),\quad\text{for $q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}$.}

We now use some arguments which are directly imported from the proof of Lemma
3.5
. In order to make the comparison more transparent, let us focus on the case
\tau=1
in (
3.27
). We let the reader fill the details for a general
\tau>0
. First a series of elementary computations lead to the following relation that is equivalent to (
3.10
):

\big(\widehat{g\star H_{t}}\big)(m,\ell,\lambda)=\Theta_{f}(m,\ell,\lambda),\quad\text{with}\quad f(x,\kappa,\lambda)=\varphi\left(\frac{x}{\tau}\right)(1-e^{-4t|x|})\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}}.

Next similarly to (
3.12
) one can write

f(x,\kappa,\lambda)=t\varphi(x)\zeta_{t}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\kappa=0\}},

where the function
\zeta_{t}
is given by

\zeta_{t}(x)=\frac{1-e^{-4|x|t}}{t}.

It is now easy to see that
\zeta_{t}
verifies the series of inequalities (
3.13
). As in Lemma
3.5
, it is enough to assert

|(g_{\tau}\star H_{t})(q)|\leq Ct\tau e^{-c|q|_{h}^{1/s}},\quad\text{for $q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}$}.

Thus (
3.26
) follows as in the proof of Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Our desired smoothing effects for the heat semigroup are now direct consequences of Propositions
3.6
and
3.7
. Our findings are summarized below.

Proposition 3.8
.

For
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
,
\nu>0
and
\alpha,\beta\in[1,\infty)
, let
\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\nu}_{\alpha,\,\beta}
be the Besov spaces introduced in Definition
3.4
. We also consider a time horizon
\tau
and
\kappa^{\prime}>\kappa
. Then there exists a constant
C=C_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa^{\prime},\nu}
such that for all
t\in[0,\tau]
we have

(3.28)

\|P_{t}f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa^{\prime},\nu}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}\leq Ct^{-\frac{\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa}{2}}\|f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\,\nu}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}.

Moreover, if
\gamma
is an additional positive parameter lying in
(0,1)
, the following time regularity holds true:

(3.29)

\|(\mathrm{Id}-P_{t})f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa-2\gamma,\,\nu}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}\leq Ct^{\gamma}\|f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\,\nu}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}.

Proof.

According to (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), the function
\sigma_{k}f
is such that

\text{Supp}(\widehat{\sigma_{k}f})\subset\{2^{k}R_{1}\leq|\lambda|(2|m|+n)\leq 2^{k}R_{2}\},

for two constants
0<R_{1}<R_{2}
. Otherwise stated, according to our notation (
3.15
) and (
3.16
), we have
\text{Supp}(\widehat{\sigma_{k}f})\subset 2^{k}\tilde{R}
. Hence applying Proposition
3.6
, we obtain

(3.30)

\lVert P_{t}(\sigma_{k}f)\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\leq C_{0}\,e^{-c2^{k}t}\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

In addition, since both
\sigma_{k}f
and
P_{t}f
are obtained through multiplication operators in Fourier modes, it is clear that
\sigma_{k}(P_{t}f)=P_{t}(\sigma_{k}f)
. Therefore one can recast (
3.30
) as

(3.31)

\lVert\sigma_{k}(P_{t}f)\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\leq C_{0}\,e^{-c2^{k}t}\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

Consider now
\kappa^{\prime}>\kappa
as in our inequality (
3.28
). Thanks to (
3.31
), we have

\displaystyle 2^{\kappa^{\prime}k}\lVert\sigma_{k}(P_{t}f)\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\,t^{-\frac{\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa}{2}}\left[(2^{k}t)^{\frac{\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa}{2}}e^{-c2^{k}t}\right]2^{\kappa kt}\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

Moreover, some elementary calculus considerations show that
(2^{k}t)^{\frac{\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa}{2}}e^{-c2^{k}t}
is bounded by a constant which only depends on
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}
. Thus we discover that

(3.32)

2^{\kappa^{\prime}k}\lVert\sigma_{k}(P_{t}f)\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\leq C_{\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}}\,t^{-\frac{\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa}{2}}\,2^{\kappa kt}\,\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

We now easily get our claim (
3.28
) by taking
\ell^{\beta}
norms on both sides of (
3.32
).

In order to prove (
3.29
), we proceed very similarly to (
3.28
). Namely, we invoke the fact that
\text{Supp}(\widehat{\sigma_{k}f})\subset 2^{k}\tilde{R}
and the relation
\sigma_{k}(\text{Id}-P_{t})f=(\text{Id}-P_{t})\sigma_{k}f
. This allows to obtain, similarly to (
3.31
), that

(3.33)

\lVert\sigma_{k}((\text{Id}-P_{t})f)\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\lesssim(2^{k}t\wedge 1)\,\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

Now multiply both sides of (
3.33
) by
2^{(\kappa-\gamma)k}
. Some trivial algebraic manipulations show that

(3.34)

\displaystyle 2^{(\kappa-\gamma)k}\,\lVert\sigma_{k}((\text{Id}-P_{t})f)\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{(\kappa-\gamma)k}\,(2^{k}t\wedge 1)\,\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{\gamma}\left[(2^{k}t)^{-\gamma}(2^{k}t\wedge 1)\right]2^{\kappa k}\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

We now invoke the fact that
\gamma\in(0,1)
. In that case, the term
(2^{k}t)^{-\gamma}(2^{k}t\wedge 1)
is uniformly bounded. Therefore (
3.34
) becomes

(3.35)

2^{(\kappa-\gamma)k}\,\lVert\sigma_{k}((\text{Id}-P_{t})f)\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\lesssim t^{\gamma}2^{\kappa k}\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}.

Thus one can conclude that (
3.29
) holds by taking
\ell^{\beta}
norms of both sides of (
3.35
). This concludes our proof.
∎

3.3.
Paraproduct

In this section we define products of functions in our weighted Besov spaces
\mathfrak{B}^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\,\beta}
. More specifically we are interested in products of the form
f_{1}\cdot f_{2}
, with a function
f_{1}
in a Besov space such that
\gamma_{1}>0
and a distribution
f_{2}
in a Besov space with
\gamma_{2}<0
. This will allow us to deal with the noisy products in equation (
1.13
).

In order to state our main result, and still with equation (
1.13
) in mind, we will extend the class of exponentially weighted Besov spaces (introduced in Definition
2.17
) to a wider class of weights including polynomials.

Definition 3.9
.

In the sequel we will handle a class of
\mathcal{T}
of weights
w:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\mathcal{T}=\hat{\mathcal{T}}\cup\tilde{\mathcal{T}}
with

(3.36)

\hat{\mathcal{T}}=\big\{w:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+};\,w(q)=e^{-\nu|q|_{*}^{\eta}},\ \text{for}\ \nu\in\mathbb{R}\ \text{and}\ \eta\in(0,1/s)\bigg\}

and where
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}
is defined by

(3.37)

\tilde{\mathcal{T}}=\big\{\rho:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+};\,\rho(q)=|q|_{*}^{-b},\ \text{for}\ b\in\mathbb{R}\bigg\}.

We will write
w_{\nu}
(as in Definition
2.17
) for a generic element of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
, as well
\rho_{b}
for a generic element of
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}
.

The above definition leads to a natural extension of Definition
3.4
to general weighted Besov spaces. We label this definition here for further use.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
, and
w:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
a weight function in the class
\mathcal{T}
introduced in Definition
3.9
. We denote by
L_{w}^{\alpha}
the corresponding space
L^{\alpha}({\mathbf{H}^{n}},w(\cdot)\,d\mu(\cdot))
, and by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{w}
the inner product in
L^{2}_{w}
. For any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha,\beta\in[1,\infty]
, we define the norm

\|f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\gamma,\,w}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}=\left\{\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\left(2^{\gamma k}\|\sigma_{k}f\|_{L^{\alpha}_{w}}\right)^{\beta}\right\}^{\frac{1}{\beta}},

where
\sigma_{k}f
are defined by (
3.4
) for
k\geq-1
. The weighted Besov space
\mathfrak{B}^{\gamma,\,w}_{\alpha,\,\beta}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
is defined as the completion of
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
with respect to this norm.

We can now spell out our main result on distributional paraproduct, for our general class of weights
\mathcal{T}
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
w_{1}
,
w_{2}
be two weight functions in the class
\mathcal{T}
, and
\kappa_{1}>0>\kappa_{2}
such that
\kappa_{1}>|\kappa_{2}|
. Let
\alpha,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta\in[1,\infty]
be parameters satisfying

\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}.

Then the product map

(f_{1},f_{2})\in\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\,\beta}\times\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},\,w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\,\beta}\to f_{1}f_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},\,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\,\beta}

is continuous and the following inequality holds true:

\|f_{1}f_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},\,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}\leq\|f_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\,\beta}}\|f_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},\,w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\,\beta}}.

Remark 3.12
.

Note that Proposition
3.11
reads similarly to more classical paraproduct inequalities in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see e.g.
[
2
, Theorem 2.82]
). However, our method of proof has to depart from the flat case in very fundamental aspects. At the heart of the problem lies the following fact: in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for two Schwartz functions
\varphi,\psi
, we have

(3.38)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\varphi\,\psi)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\varphi)\star\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\psi).

This enables to prove that
\mathrm{Supp}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\varphi)\subset\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathrm{Supp}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\psi)\subset\mathcal{B}
imply that

(3.39)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Supp}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\varphi\,\psi)\subset\mathcal{C}_{2},

where
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are annulus and
\mathcal{B}
is a ball. Unfortunately, there is no equivalent of relation (
3.38
) in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. As a result, the road to establish an inclusion like (
3.39
) for the Heisenberg group is quite long. We shall describe this path in the next subsection.

We finish the statement of our main paraproduct results by giving an immediate consequence of Proposition
3.11
that will be used later.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{T}
and
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
be as given in Proposition
3.11
. Then it holds that

\|f_{1}f_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},\,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}\leq\|f_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}\|f_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},\,w_{2}}_{\infty,\,\beta}}.

Proof.

The conclusion follows from plugging
\alpha_{1}=\alpha
and
\alpha_{2}=\infty
in Proposition
3.11
.
∎

3.3.1.
Preliminary results

The paraproduct problem in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
has been addressed in
[
4
]
, albeit in a different Fourier setting. Our strategy consists in mapping their results to ours by comparing Fourier projections. In the process, radial functions and Laguerre calculus play a prominent role. We first recall some basic facts about those objects here. Let us start with a basic definition.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
f:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. We say that
f
is radial if there exists another function
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
q=(x,y,z)
we have

f(x,y,z)=\tilde{f}(|v|,z),

where we have set
v=(x,y)
and the norm
|v|
has been defined in (
1.10
).

Our next proposition features Laguerre polynomials, that we proceed to define now.

Definition 3.15
.

For
n,k\geq 0
, the Laguerre polynomial
L^{k}_{n}
of order
k
and type
n
is defined by

(3.40)

\displaystyle L_{k}^{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\begin{pmatrix}k+n\\
k-j\end{pmatrix}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}

We now state a result taken from
[
1
, relation (3.12)]
.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
g
be a radial function in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
, as given in Definition
3.14
. We consider the Fourier transform
\hat{g}
of
g
, as spelled out in Definition
2.1
. Then there exists
\tilde{g}:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
such that for
(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
we have

(3.41)

\displaystyle\hat{g}(m,\ell,\lambda)=\tilde{g}(|m|,\lambda)\delta_{m,\ell}

where for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have set

\tilde{g}(k,\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}k+n-1\\
k\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\overline{e^{iz\lambda}\mathcal{K}_{k,\lambda}(x,y)}g(q)d\mu(q),

and where the function
\mathcal{K}_{k,\lambda}
can be expressed in terms of the kernels
K
in (
2.10
) and Laguerre polynomials
L
in (
3.40
):

\mathcal{K}_{k,\lambda}(x,y):=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{n},|m|=k}K_{m,m,\lambda}(x,y)=e^{-|\lambda||v|^{2}}L_{k}^{n-1}\!\left(2|\lambda||v|^{2}\right),

where we recall that
v
stands for the couple
(x,y)
.

In the sequel we will need to consider radial test functions. The following lemma asserts that this type of functions exists.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
s>1
and recall that
{B}_{1}
is the ball in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
given by

{B}_{1}=\{\hat{w}\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n};\hat{d}_{0}(\hat{w})<1\}.

Let
\tilde{\phi}
be the function such that
\tilde{\phi}\equiv 1
on
{B}_{1}
, and
\tilde{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}^{s}(\tilde{\bf H}^{n})
, as constructed in Lemma
2.21
. We set
g=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\tilde{\phi})
. Then
g
is a radial function.

Proof.

The proof follows from considerations in
[
1
, page 17-18]
. Namely the function
\tilde{\phi}
constructed in Lemma
2.21
is supported on the diagonal
\{m=\ell\}
, In this situation, the Fourier inversion formula takes the form (recall that
g\equiv\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\tilde{\phi})
):

(3.42)

\displaystyle g(q)=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\lambda z}\omega_{\ell,\lambda}(v)\phi(\ell,\ell,\lambda)|\lambda|^{d}d\lambda,

where we have set
v=(x,y)
and
q=(v,z)
for a generic element
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, and where the function
\omega
is given by

\omega_{\ell,\lambda}(v)=e^{-|\lambda||v|^{2}}L_{\ell}^{(n-1)}(2|\lambda||v|^{2}).

Since
\omega_{\ell,\lambda}
is obviously radial, we deduce from (
3.42
) that
g
is radial as well.
∎

3.3.2.
Mapping Fourier settings

As mentioned at the beginning of Section
3.3.1
, our strategy towards a proper definition of paraproduct hinges on the Fourier setting from
[
4
]
. We shall first briefly recall this framework.

Lemma 3.18
.

Consider the state space

(3.43)

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n};\,d\xi\right)\,.

As in Definition
3.14
, we write
q=(x,y,z)=(v,z)
for a generic element of
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. Then a set of representations
\{\tilde{U}_{q}^{\lambda};\,q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}},\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}
is given by:

(3.44)

\tilde{U}_{q}^{\lambda}f(\xi)=\left(\frac{2|\lambda|}{\pi}\right)^{n/2}e^{-|\lambda||\xi|^{2}}\exp\left(\lambda\left(\imath z+2\left\langle\xi,v\right\rangle-{|v|^{2}}\right)\right)f\left(\xi-\bar{v}\right)\,.

The representations given in Lemma
3.18
give raise to a Fourier transform which is labeled below for further use. This is the Fourier transform used in
[
4
]
.

Definition 3.19
.

We will call
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
the Fourier transform based on the representations
\tilde{U}_{q}^{\lambda}
introduced in (
3.44
). That is similarly to (
2.2
), for a function
f\in L^{1}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
we set

(3.45)

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(f)(\lambda)=\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}f(q)\tilde{U}_{q}^{\lambda}\,d\mu(q).

Remark 3.20
.

In a somewhat non standard way, the state space in
[
4
]
was depending on the parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Namely for a given
\lambda
, the state space in
[
4
]
is

(3.46)

\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}=L^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{n};\,\left(\frac{2|\lambda|}{\pi}\right)^{n}e^{-2|\lambda||\xi|^{2}}d\xi\right)\,.

Then the representations in
[
4
]
are given by

(3.47)

V_{q}^{\lambda}F(\xi)=\exp\left(\lambda\left(\imath z+2\left\langle\xi,v\right\rangle\right)-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}\right)F\left(\xi-\bar{v}\right)\,,\quad\text{for all }F\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}\,.

However, it is easy to go from (
3.46
)-(
3.47
) to a single state space, as we did in (
3.43
)-(
3.44
).

The analysis in
[
4
, Proposition 2.1]
relies on a set of eigenfunctions that we now proceed to describe.

Lemma 3.21
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
define

(3.48)

F_{m}^{\lambda}(\xi)=\frac{\left(2|\lambda|\xi\right)^{m}}{(m{!})^{1/2}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\left(2|\lambda|\xi_{j}\right)^{m_{j}}}{(m_{j}!)^{1/2}}\,.

Then
\{F_{m}^{\lambda};\,m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
. Furthermore, whenever
f
is radial we have

(3.49)

\left\langle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}f(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda},F_{m^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right\rangle=\mathbbm{1}_{(m=m^{\prime})}\,R_{m}^{f,\lambda}\,,

where the scalar
R_{m}^{f,\lambda}
is defined by

(3.50)

R_{m}^{f,\lambda}=\binom{|m|+n-1}{|m|}^{-1}\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}f(q)e^{\imath\lambda z}L_{m}^{(n-1)}(2|\lambda||v|^{2})e^{-2|\lambda||v|^{2}}\,\mu(dq)\,,

and where the generic element of
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
is written
q=(v,z)
.

We now start to relate our notion of Fourier transform to the one put forward in
[
4
]
. To this aim, observe first that the representations (
3.43
)-(
3.44
) are obviously different from our representations
U_{q}^{\lambda}
in (
2.1
). However, the general representation theory in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
asserts that the two objects are equivalent. Let us specify this point in another lemma.

Lemma 3.22
.

The representations
\tilde{U}_{q}^{\lambda}
given in Lemma
3.18
are unitarily equivalent to (
2.1
). Otherwise stated, there exits a unitary operator
A:L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that for every
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}},\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
we have

(3.51)

\tilde{U}_{q}^{\lambda}=A^{*}U_{q}^{-\lambda}A,\quad\text{and}\quad U_{q}^{\lambda}=A\,\tilde{U}_{q}^{-\lambda}A^{*}.

In addition, one can choose the operator
A
so that

(3.52)

F_{m}^{\lambda}=A^{*}\Phi_{m}^{\lambda}.

Proof.

The existence of
A
such that (
3.51
) holds true is ensured by general representation theory considerations. Hence we also have

(3.53)

\mathcal{F}f(\lambda)=A\,\tilde{\mathcal{F}}f(-\lambda)A^{*}.

Apply the above relation to
\Phi_{m}^{\lambda}
. This yields

(3.54)

\left\langle\mathcal{F}f(\lambda)\Phi_{m}^{\lambda},\Phi_{m^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right\rangle=\left\langle A\,\tilde{\mathcal{F}}f(-\lambda)A^{*}\Phi_{m}^{\lambda},\Phi_{m^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right\rangle=\left\langle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}f(-\lambda)A^{*}\Phi_{m}^{\lambda},A^{*}\Phi_{m^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right\rangle.

Moreover, one can easily generate operators of the form
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}f(\lambda)
which are invertible by considering radial functions
f
.
Therefore invoking (
3.54
), we get
F_{m}^{\lambda}=A^{*}\Phi_{m}^{\lambda}
if one can prove that for a generic radial function
f
we have

(3.55)

\left\langle\mathcal{F}f(\lambda)\Phi_{m}^{\lambda},\Phi_{m^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right\rangle=\mathbbm{1}_{(m=m^{\prime})}\,R_{m}^{f,-\lambda},

where we recall that
R_{m}^{f,\lambda}
is defined by (
3.50
).
To verify (
3.55
), we just need to recall (
2.8
) and apply Lemma
3.16
. We obtain that for any radial function
f
,

\left\langle\mathcal{F}f(\lambda)\Phi_{m}^{\lambda},\Phi_{m^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right\rangle\\
=\mathbbm{1}_{(m=m^{\prime})}\begin{pmatrix}|m|+n-1\\
|m|\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}{e^{-iz\lambda}e^{-|\lambda|(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})}L_{|m|}^{n-1}(2|\lambda||(x,y)|^{2})}f(q)dq\,,

where
L_{k}^{n-1}
denotes the Laguerre polynomial of order
k
and type
n-1
as given in (
3.40
). This matches with (
3.50
) for
R^{f,-\lambda}_{m}
, which ends the proof.

∎

3.3.3.
Frequency localization of paraproducts

An important step in the definition of products is the following: show that the product of 2 functions which are localized in Fourier modes is still localized in Fourier modes. Below we prove a projective version of this fact, still inspired by
[
4
]
.

Proposition 3.23
.

Consider an annulus
\mathcal{C}_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}
and
j,l\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
f,g
be radial functions in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
such that for all

m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
we have

(3.56)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}=0\quad\text{for all }\lambda\not\in(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2j}\mathcal{C}_{0}

and

(3.57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(g)(\lambda)\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}=0\quad\text{for all }\lambda\not\in(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2l}\mathcal{C}_{0}.

Then the following holds true:

(1)

If
\ell>j+1
, then there exists an annulus
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(fg)(\lambda)\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}=0\quad\text{for all }\lambda\not\in(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2j}\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{0}

(2)

If
|\ell-j|\leq 1
, there exists a ball
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(fg)(\lambda)\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}=0\quad\text{for all }\lambda\not\in(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2j}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}.

Proof.

First combining (
3.53
) and (
3.52
) we have for any
h\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbf{H}^{n}}})
that

(3.58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}h(-\lambda)\Phi_{m}^{\lambda}=A\tilde{\mathcal{F}}h(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}.

Therefore the conditions (
3.56
) and (
3.57
) implies that

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}f(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}=\mathbbm{1}_{(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2j}\mathcal{C}_{0}}(\lambda)\,\tilde{\mathcal{F}}f(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}

and

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}g(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}=\mathbbm{1}_{(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2l}\mathcal{C}_{0}}(\lambda)\,\tilde{\mathcal{F}}g(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}.

As announced at the beginning of the section, we will now be able to transfer some of the results from
[
4
]
to our setting. Namely resorting to
[
4
, Proposition 4.2]
we obtain that

(i)

When
\ell-j>1
, there exists an annulus
\chi_{0}^{\prime}
such that

(3.59)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(fg)(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}=\mathbbm{1}_{(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2j}\chi^{\prime}_{0}}(\lambda)\,\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(fg)(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}.

(ii)

When
|\ell-j|\leq 1
, there exists a ball
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}
such that

(3.60)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(fg)(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}=\mathbbm{1}_{(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2\ell}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}}(\lambda)\,\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(fg)(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}.

Here it should be recalled that the simple localization formulae (
3.59
)-(
3.60
) are obtained in
[
4
]
thanks to the diagonalization property (
3.55
) for
\mathcal{F}f(\lambda)
, whenever
f
is radial. We now conclude by using (
3.58
) again. Namely we have

\mathcal{F}(fg)(-\lambda)\Phi_{m}^{\lambda}=A\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(fg)(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}.

Hence by symmetry of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, if
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(fg)(\lambda)F_{m}^{\lambda}=0
for
\lambda\not\in(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2j}\mathcal{C}_{0}
, the same is true for
\mathcal{F}(fg)(\lambda)\Phi_{m}^{\lambda}
. This yields (
3.56
). Relation (
3.57
) is obtained in the same way.
∎

Remark 3.24
.

Proposition 4.2 in
[
4
]
is proved by relating Fourier transforms in
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
to Fourier transforms in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
for specific situations. We could have adopted the same strategy in our situation. However, it looked simpler to just import the beautiful computations of
[
4
]
to our setting.

For a function
f\in L^{2}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
, the blocks are good examples of functions in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
. Therefore a direct application of Proposition
3.23
yields the following corollary, which mirrors the remark in
[
4
, page 92]
.

Corollary 3.25
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
. For
k\geq-1
, recall
\sigma_{k}f
and
S_{k}f
as defined in (
3.4
).
For
\varepsilon\in\{-1,0,1\}
and
k\geq 0
we set

h_{k}^{S}:=S_{k-1}f\sigma_{k}g,\quad\text{and}\quad h_{k}^{\sigma,\epsilon}=\sigma_{k-\epsilon}f\sigma_{k}g.

Then there exists an annulus
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\prime}
and a ball
\mathcal{B}_{0}^{\prime}
given as in Proposition
3.23
such that for all
(m,\ell,\lambda)\in\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{k}^{S}(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2k}\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{0}}(\lambda)\,\hat{h}_{k}^{S}(m,\ell,\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2k}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}}(\lambda)\,\hat{h}_{k}^{\sigma,\epsilon}(m,\ell,\lambda).

3.3.4.
Definition of paraproducts

With the preliminary notions introduced above, we can now turn to a definition of paraproduct. Notice that this can now be done similarly to the flat case, as in e.g
[
3
]
.

Definition 3.26
.

Let
f,g
be smooth functions in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
. We define the quantities
f\varolessthan g
and
f\varodot g
by

\displaystyle f\varolessthan g

\displaystyle=\sum_{j<k-1}\sigma_{j}f\ \sigma_{k}g=\sum_{k}S_{k-1}f\ \sigma_{k}g.

\displaystyle f\varodot g

\displaystyle=\sum_{|j-k|\leq 1}\sigma_{j}f\ \sigma_{k}g,

where we recall that
\sigma_{k}f
and
S_{k}f
are defined in (
3.4
). Then the product
fg
can be decomposed as

(3.61)

fg=f\varolessthan g+f\varodot g+f\varogreaterthan g,

where
f\varogreaterthan g=g\varolessthan f
.

Our main task in this section is to extend the product (
3.61
) to elements
f
and
g
in suitable Besov spaces (especially when
g
is a distribution). We shall do this by considering each term in the decomposition (
3.61
) separately. First we gather some relevant lemmas for Besov estimates of functions which are localized in Fourier modes. Those lemmas are elaborations of
[
2
, Lemma 2.23]

Lemma 3.27
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha,\beta\in[1,\infty]
, and let
\mathcal{C}\subset\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
be an annulus. Then there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that for any sequence
\{f_{k};k\geq 0\}
of functions in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
satisfying

(3.62)

\text{Supp}\hat{f}_{k}\subseteq 2^{k}\mathcal{C}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\left(2^{\kappa k}\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{\beta},

the function
f\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}f_{k}
is an element of
\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\nu}_{\alpha,\beta}
and the following holds true:

(3.63)

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\nu}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq C\left\lVert\left(2^{\kappa k}\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{\beta}}.

Proof.

Clearly there exists an
N_{0}>0
such that for all
|j-k|>N_{0}
it holds that

\sigma_{j}f_{k}=0.

Therefore for an arbitrary
j\in\mathbb{N}
one has

(3.64)

\displaystyle\|\sigma_{j}f\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}=\left\|\sum_{|j-k|\leq N_{0}}\sigma_{j}f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}\leq C\sum_{|j-k|\leq N_{0}}\left\|\sigma_{j}f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}.

In addition, recall from (
3.4
) that
\sigma_{j}f_{k}=\phi_{j}\star f_{k}
for a smooth function
\phi_{j}
. Hence applying Proposition
2.15
similarly to what we did in (
3.20
) and observing that
\|\phi_{j}w_{-\nu}\|_{L^{1}}
is uniformly bounded in
j
, relation (
3.64
) becomes

(3.65)

\displaystyle\|\sigma_{j}f\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}\leq C\sum_{|j-k|\leq N_{0}}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}.

With an easy algebraic manipulation, we end up with

(3.66)

\displaystyle 2^{\kappa j}\|\sigma_{j}f\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}\leq C\sum_{|j-k|\leq N_{0}}2^{\kappa k}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}},

where we recall that the generic constant
C
can change from line to line. The right hand side of (
3.66
) can now be interpreted as a discrete convolution. Indeed, setting
F(\ell)=C\mathbbm{1}_{[-N_{0},N_{0}]}(\ell)
and
G(k)=2^{k\kappa}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}
, one can recast (
3.66
) as

(3.67)

\displaystyle 2^{\kappa j}\|\sigma_{j}f\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}\leq(F\star G)(j).

Applying Young’s inequality to the
\ell^{\beta}
norm of the sequence
\{(F\star G)(j);j\geq 0\}
we thus get

(3.68)

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\nu}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq\|F\star G\|_{\ell^{\beta}}\leq C\|(2^{k\kappa}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{\ell^{\beta}},

where we have resorted to the
\ell^{1}
-norm of
F
(which is obviously finite) in our application of Young’s inequality.
Now relation (
3.68
) is our claim (
3.63
), and the proof is completed.
∎

When the Fourier modes of a function are localized in a ball, the estimates we obtain are not as good as in Lemma
3.27
(namely they will only hold for a regularity parameter
\kappa>0
). Nevertheless, the following lemma will be useful in order to define paraproducts in Besov spaces.

Lemma 3.28
.

As in Lemma
3.27
, consider a sequence
\{f_{k};k\geq 0\}
of functions in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{H}^{n}})
. We assume that
\kappa>0
and

(3.69)

\text{Supp}\hat{f}_{k}\subseteq 2^{k}\mathcal{B}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\left(2^{\kappa k}\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{\beta},

where
\mathcal{B}\subset\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
is a ball in
\tilde{\bf H}^{n}
with respect to the distance
\hat{d}_{0}
, and where
\alpha,\beta\in[1,\infty]
. Define
f
as
f=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}f_{k}
. Then

(3.70)

f\in\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\nu}_{\alpha,\beta}\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\nu}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq C\left\lVert\left(2^{\kappa k}\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{\nu}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{\beta}}.

Proof.

This proof goes along the same lines as for Lemma
3.27
. Details will thus be omitted.
Recall in the definition (
3.4
) of
\sigma_{j}f_{k}
that
\tilde{\phi}_{j}
is supported in an annulus
2^{j}\mathcal{C}
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is a ball, there exists an
N_{1}
such that for all
j\geq k+N_{1}
it holds that

\sigma_{j}f_{k}=0.

Similarly to (
3.64
)-(
3.66
), this implies that

\|\sigma_{j}f\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}=\left\|\sum_{k>j-N_{1}}\sigma_{j}f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}\leq C\sum_{k>j-N_{1}}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}.

Therefore like in (
3.66
) we obtain

2^{\kappa j}\|\sigma_{j}f\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}\leq C\sum_{k>j-N_{1}}2^{\kappa(j-k)}2^{\kappa k}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}.

Hence using the same discrete convolution arguments as in (
3.67
), we get

2^{\kappa j}\|\sigma_{j}f\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}\leq(F\star G)(j),

where
F(\ell)=C2^{\kappa\ell}\mathbf{1}_{[-\infty,N_{1}]}(\ell)
and
G(k)=2^{k\kappa}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}}
. Applying Young’s inequality we obtain that

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\nu}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq\|F\star G\|_{\ell^{\beta}}\leq C\|(2^{k\kappa}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{\alpha}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{\ell^{\beta}},

where we have resorted to the
\ell^{1}
-norm of
F
(which is finite whenever
\kappa>0
) in our application of Young’s inequality.
The proof is thus completed.
∎

We now turn to the main result of this section, giving the definition of paraproducts in our weighted Besov spaces.

Lemma 3.29
.

(1)
Suppose
\kappa,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta\in[1,\infty]
are such that

\kappa_{1}\neq 0,\quad\kappa=(\kappa_{1}\wedge 0)+\kappa_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}.

Then the mapping
(f,g)\mapsto f\varolessthan g
(defined for
f,g\in\mathcal{S}
) can be extended to a continuous bilinear map
\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}\times{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta}}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\beta}
, Moreover, there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that

(3.71)

\|f\varolessthan g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq C\,\|f\|_{B^{\kappa_{1},w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}\,\|g\|_{B^{\kappa_{2},w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta}}.

(2)
Suppose
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are such that
\kappa=\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}>0
, and let
\alpha,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta
be as above. Then the mapping
(f,g)\mapsto f\varodot g
can be extended to a continuous bilinear map
\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}\times{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta}}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\beta}
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that

(3.72)

\displaystyle\|f\varodot g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq C\,\|f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}\,\|g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta}}.

Proof.

We will prove the two items in our lemma separately.

Proof of item (1).
Recall that
f\varolessthan g
is defined as
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}h_{k}^{s}
, with
h_{k}^{s}=S_{k-1}f\sigma_{k}g
. Hence one can invoke Corollary
3.25
to assert that
\hat{h}_{k}^{s}
is supported in an annulus
(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2k}\chi^{\prime}_{0}
. Therefore we can apply a slight elaboration of (
3.63
) in Lemma
3.27
, which yields

(3.73)

\displaystyle\|f\varolessthan g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq C\left\lVert\left(2^{\kappa k}\lVert h_{k}^{s}\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{w_{1}w_{2}}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{\beta}}.

We now bound the right hand side of (
3.73
). First recalling that
h_{k}^{s}=S_{k-1}f\sigma_{k}g
and resorting to Hölder’s inequality we have

(3.74)

\displaystyle\lVert h_{k}^{s}\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{w_{1}w_{2}}}\leq\lVert S_{k-1}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{1}}_{w_{1}}}\lVert\sigma_{k}g\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{2}}_{w2}}.

In addition, from Definition
3.4
we have that

(3.75)

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma_{j}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{1}}_{w_{1}}}\leq 2^{-\kappa_{1}j}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}.

Hence summing over
j
we get

(3.76)

\displaystyle\lVert S_{k-1}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{1}}_{w_{1}}}\leq\sum_{j=-1}^{k-2}\lVert\sigma_{j}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{1}}_{w_{1}}}\leq\sum_{j=-1}^{k-2}2^{-\kappa_{1}j}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}\lesssim 2^{-(\kappa_{1}\wedge 0)k}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}.

Plugging (
3.75
) and (
3.76
) into (
3.74
), we then obtain

\lVert S_{k-1}f\ \sigma_{k}g\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{w_{1}w_{2}}}\lesssim 2^{-(\kappa_{1}\wedge 0)k}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}\lVert\sigma_{k}g\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{2}}_{w_{2}}}.

Therefore the right hand side of (
3.73
) can be bounded as follows:

(3.77)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(2^{\kappa k}\lVert S_{k-1}f\ \sigma_{k}g\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{w_{1}w_{2}}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}\left\lVert\left(2^{\kappa k_{2}}\lVert\sigma_{k}g\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{2}}_{w_{2}}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{\beta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}\lVert g\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},\,w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta}}.

This proves our claim (
3.71
).

Proof of item (2).
The proof of (
3.72
) follows closely the arguments of item (1). Namely one writes

\|f\varodot g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\beta}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\varepsilon\in\{-1,0,1\}}h_{k}^{\sigma,\varepsilon},

where
h_{k}^{\sigma,\varepsilon}=\sigma_{k-\varepsilon}f\sigma_{k}g
. Moreover, according to Corollary
3.25
we have
\hat{h}_{k}^{\sigma,\varepsilon}
supported in a ball
(2|m|+n)^{-1}2^{2k}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}
. Since we have assumed
\kappa>0
, Lemma
3.28
allows to write

(3.78)

\displaystyle\|f\varodot g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq C\sum_{\varepsilon\in\{-1,0,1\}}\left\lVert\left(2^{\kappa k}\lVert h_{k}^{\sigma,\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{w_{1}w_{2}}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{\beta}}

We now proceed to bound the right hand side of (
3.78
). That is applying Hölder inequality, for all
\varepsilon\in\{-1,0,1\}
and
k\geq 0
we have

(3.79)

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma_{k-\varepsilon}f\ \sigma_{k}g\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{w_{1}w_{2}}}\leq\lVert\sigma_{k-\varepsilon}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{1}}_{w_{1}}}\lVert\sigma_{k}g\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{2}}_{w_{2}}}.

Moreover, from Definition
3.4
it holds that

\lVert\sigma_{k-\varepsilon}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{1}}_{w_{1}}}\leq 2^{-\kappa_{1}(k-\varepsilon)}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},\,w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}},\quad\lVert\sigma_{k}g\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{2}}_{w_{2}}}\leq 2^{-\kappa_{2}k}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},\,w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta}}.

Hence similarly to what we did for (
3.77
), we end up with

\left\lVert\left(2^{\kappa k}\lVert\sigma_{k-\varepsilon}f\ \sigma_{k}g\rVert_{L^{\alpha}_{w_{1}w_{2}}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{\beta}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}\,\|g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta}}.

This establishes (
3.72
) and finishes our proof.
∎

We complete this section by giving a short proof of our product rules in Proposition
3.11
.

Proof of Proposition
3.11
.

We start from the decomposition (
3.61
) and bound the 3 terms
f\varolessthan g
,
f\varodot g
, and
f\varogreaterthan g
seperately. Now for
f\varolessthan g
, Lemma
3.29
yields

\|f\varolessthan g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq\|f\varolessthan g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,w_{1}w_{2}}_{\alpha,\beta}}\leq C\,\|f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{1},w_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}}\,\|g\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa_{2},w_{2}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta}}.

Similar estimates also hold for
f\varogreaterthan g
and
f\varodot g
. This concludes the proof.
∎

4.
Existence and uniqueness of pathwise solutions for a class of SPDEs

As an illustration of the use of weighted Besov spaces, this section addresses the existence and uniqueness of pathwise solutions to (
1.13
), for noises
\dot{\mathbf{W}}
in certain Besov spaces. For this purpose we shall replace first the noise
\dot{\mathbf{W}}
by
\mathcal{W}
, a nonrandom Hölder continuous function in time with values in a Besov space of distributions. Then we will prove that the noises
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
in (
1.14
) sit in convenient Besov spaces under proper condition on
\zeta,\alpha
.

4.1.
Paths in weighted spaces

We begin this section by introducing sets of Hölder functions in time, with values in weighted Besov spaces on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. This is the kind of noise
\mathcal{W}
which will drive Equation (
1.13
). Our basic spaces of Hölder functions are defined as

(4.1)

C^{\vartheta,\kappa,w}_{\alpha,\beta}:=C^{\vartheta}([0,T];\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\,w}_{\alpha,\,\beta}).

We shall also need elaborations of those spaces taking into account weights
w_{t}
decreasing with time. We label a definition in that sense below.

Definition 4.1
.

Consider three positive coefficients
\nu,b,\eta
and define a subset
\{w_{t};t\in[0,T]\}
of
\mathcal{T}
by setting

(4.2)

w_{t}(q)=e^{-(\nu+bt)|q|_{*}^{\eta}},\quad q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}.

We also consider the space of
\theta
-Hölder continuous functions on
[0,T]
that takes values in
\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}_{\alpha,\,\beta}
for each
t
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}_{\alpha,\beta}:

\displaystyle=\{f\in C([0,T]\times{\mathbf{H}^{n}}),\,\|f_{t}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}\leq c_{T,f},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\text{and}\ \|f_{t}-f_{s}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}\leq c_{f}|t-s|^{\theta},\ \text{for all }\ 0\leq s<t\leq T\}

We equip the space
\mathcal{D}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}_{\alpha,\beta}
with the Hölder norm

\|f\|_{\mathcal{D}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}_{\alpha,\beta}}:=\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\frac{\|\delta f_{st}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}_{\alpha,\,\beta}}}{|t-s|^{\theta}},

where here and in the sequel we write time increments as

(4.4)

\delta f_{st}=f_{t}-f_{s}.

4.2.
Existence and uniqueness for the SHE

The definitions in the previous section allow to give a precise meaning to the notion of solution to our rough PDE (
1.13
). Namely a solution is defined in the following way.

Definition 4.2
.

Consider the Hölder spaces
C_{\alpha,\beta}^{\vartheta,\kappa,w}
defined by (
4.1
). We are given a noise
\dot{\mathcal{W}}\in C_{\alpha_{2},\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}
for some
\gamma>0
, as well as a weight
\rho_{b}=c(1+|q|_{*}^{b})
. The initial condition for our system is a function
u_{0}
sitting in the Besov space
\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}^{\kappa,\,w}
from Definition
3.4
. Consider
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}
for some
\kappa>0
, where the space
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{1},\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}
is introduced in (
4.3
). We say that
u
is a mild solution to the equation (
1.13
) with initial condition

if it holds that

(4.5)

u(t,p)=P_{t}u_{0}(p)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}p_{t-s}(q^{-1}p)u(s,q)\dot{\mathcal{W}}(ds,q)d\mu(q),

where the time integral in (
4.5
) is understood as a Young integral, while the spatial product
u(s,q)\dot{\mathcal{W}}(ds,q)
is meant as in Proposition
3.11
.

Remark 4.3
.

In Definition
4.2
, we implicitly assume that
\vartheta
,
\kappa
,
w
,
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
,
\rho_{b}
,
\alpha_{2}
satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
3.11
. Also notice that in the sequel the parameter
\alpha_{2}
for the noise
\dot{\mathcal{W}}
will be chosen as
\alpha_{2}=\infty
.

Remark 4.4
.

We refer to
[
8
]
, for instance, for a proper definition of Young’s integrals. One way to understand the right hand side of (
4.7
) is to consider the time integral as a limit of Riemann sums. Namely consider a generic sequence of partitions
\{\Pi^{n}=(r_{j})_{j\leq n};n\geq 1\}
whose mesh goes to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Then one defines

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}

\displaystyle p_{t-s}(q^{-1}p)u(s,q)\dot{\mathcal{W}}(ds,q)d\mu(q)

(4.6)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}p_{t-r_{j}}(q^{-1}p)u(r_{j},q)(\delta\dot{\mathcal{W}})_{r_{j}r_{j+1}}(q)d\mu(q),

where we recall the convention (
4.4
) for the time increments.

Let us now state the main assumption on the noise
\dot{\mathcal{W}}
.

Hypothesis 4.5
.

The noise
\dot{\mathcal{W}}
driving equation (
4.5
) belongs to a space
C_{\infty,\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}
for a given

. In addition, we assume that
\vartheta
and

are two positive numbers in

such that

Given a
\dot{\mathcal{W}}
satisfying Hypothesis

on
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa}
that is given by

(4.7)

\Phi(v)(t,p):=P_{t}u_{0}(p)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}p_{t-s}(q^{-1}p)v(s,q)\dot{\mathcal{W}}(ds,q)d\mu(q).

Clearly a solution to (
4.5

. In the lemma below, we prove that

is a contraction on

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}
whenever the time interval is small enough.

Lemma 4.6

Fix a time interval

for

. Given an input distribution

that satisfies Hypothesis

Consider

We claim that for

Step 5: Conclusion.
Let us summarize our considerations so far. Combining (
4.24
) and (
4.29
) and picking
\theta=1-\vartheta+\epsilon
, we have obtained that

\|(P_{t-s}-\mathrm{Id})(L^{1,n,j}_{st}-L^{2,n,j}_{st})\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}}\leq\frac{C(t-s)^{\theta}}{(s-r_{2j+1})^{2(1-\vartheta+\epsilon)+\frac{\kappa+\gamma}{2}+b/\eta}}\|v^{12}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}}\|\dot{\mathcal{W}}\|_{C_{\infty,\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}}\left(\frac{s}{2^{n}}\right)^{1+\epsilon}.

Now, chose
\kappa=\gamma+2\epsilon
. By Hypothesis
4.5
we obtain that for sufficiently small
\epsilon,b>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that

(4.30)

\displaystyle\|(P_{t-s}-\mathrm{Id})(L^{1,n,j}_{st}-L^{2,n,j}_{st})\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}}\leq\frac{C(t-s)^{\theta}}{(s-r_{2j+1})^{1-\delta}}\|v^{12}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}}\|\dot{\mathcal{W}}\|_{C_{\infty,\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}}\left(\frac{s}{2^{n}}\right)^{1+\epsilon}.

Next we recall that
I^{n}_{st}-I^{n-1}_{st}
is defined in (
4.18
). Hence summing (
4.30
) over
j
and
n
we discover that

(4.31)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|I^{n}_{st}-I^{n-1}_{st}\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}}\\
\leq C(t-s)^{\theta}\|v^{12}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}}\|\dot{\mathcal{W}}\|_{C_{\infty,\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{s}{2^{n-1}}\right)^{\epsilon}\bigg(\frac{s}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n-1}-1}\frac{1}{(s-r_{2j+1})^{1-\delta}}\bigg).

The right hand side of (
4.31
) can be estimated thanks to elementary Riemann sums arguments. We thus get the following estimate:

(4.32)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|I^{n}_{st}-I^{n-1}_{st}\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}}\leq C(t-s)^{\theta}\|v^{12}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}}\|\dot{\mathcal{W}}\|_{C_{\infty,\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}}s^{\epsilon+\delta}.

The fact that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|I^{n}_{st}-I^{n-1}_{st}\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}}
is an absolutely convergent series in
\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}
allows to conclude that the limit of
I^{n}_{st}
exists. Specifically, taking into account that
I^{0}_{st}=0
we obtain the limit
I_{st}
in (
4.13
) as the following series in
\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}
:

I_{st}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(I^{n}_{st}-I^{n-1}_{st}).

For
s,t\in[0,\tau]
, relation (
4.32
) can thus be read as

(4.33)

\|I_{st}\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}}\leq C\tau^{\epsilon+\delta}(t-s)^{\theta}\|v^{12}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}}\|\dot{\mathcal{W}}\|_{C_{\infty,\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}}.

The upper bounds for the term
II_{st}
defined by (
4.12
) are obtained with very similar arguments. We let the patient reader check that we obtain the same bound as (
4.33
)

We are now ready to conclude about the announced contraction property. Namely putting together relation (
4.33
), the similar bound we have outlined for
II_{st}
and decomposition (
4.11
), it is readily checked that the term
\Phi^{12}
given by (
4.10
) fulfills

(4.34)

\|\Phi^{12}_{t}-\Phi^{12}_{s}\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}}\leq C\tau^{\epsilon+\delta}(t-s)^{\theta}\|v^{12}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}}\|\dot{\mathcal{W}}\|_{C_{\infty,\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}}\,,

for all
0\leq s<t\leq\tau
. We now pick
\tau
small enough, namely

(4.35)

\tau=\left(\frac{C}{2}\|\dot{\mathcal{W}}\|_{C_{\infty,\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\epsilon+\delta}}.

Then one can recast (
4.34
) as

\|\Phi^{12}_{t}-\Phi^{12}_{s}\|_{\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\kappa,\,w_{t}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}(t-s)^{\theta}\|v^{12}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}}

which is our desired contraction property.
∎

We now turn to the main existence and uniqueness result of this section.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\tau
be a fixed time horizon. Consider a noisy input
\dot{\mathbf{W}}
in a distribution space
C_{\infty,\beta}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}
satisfying Hypothesis
4.5
. Choose some exponents
\theta,\kappa
such that
\theta+\vartheta>1
and
\gamma<\kappa<1
. Then there exists a unique solution to equation (
4.5
), interpreted as in Definition
4.2
, sitting in the space
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\theta,\kappa,\nu,b}
from Definition
4.1
.

Proof.

The contraction properties from Lemma
4.6
yields existence and uniqueness on
[0,\tau]\times{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, where
\tau
is given by (
4.35
). Moreover, as one can immediately see from the right hand side of (
4.35
),
\tau
does not depend on the initial condition
u_{0}
. Therefore a classical patching argument allows to conclude. Further details are left to the reader for sake of conciseness.
∎

4.3.
Application to a family of Gaussian noises

In
[
6
]
we have defined a family of noises on
[0,\tau]\times{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
which were white in time, with a spatial covariance given by fractional powers of the Laplacian. In this paper, due to the fact that we are considering Stratonovich type equations, it is very natural to consider noises with a fractional Brownian motion type behavior in time. We briefly define this class of noisy inputs, referring to
[
6
]
for further details about the spatial covariance structure.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be a covariance function in time, satisfying

(4.36)

\displaystyle 0\leq\Gamma(t)\leq c_{\Gamma}|t|^{-\zeta},

for a constant
c_{\Gamma}>0
and
0<\zeta<1
. For a regularity coefficient
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n+1}{2})
, we consider a centered Gaussian family
\{\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\varphi);\varphi\in\mathcal{D}\}
for a certain subset of functions
\mathcal{D}:[0,\tau)\times{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}
. The covariance structure of this Gaussian family is given by

(4.37)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\varphi)\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\psi)\right)=\int_{[0,\tau)^{2}}\int_{({\mathbf{H}^{n}})^{2}}\varphi(s,q_{1})(-\Delta)^{-2\alpha}\psi(t,q_{1})\Gamma(s-t)\,d\mu(q_{1})dsdt

(4.38)

\displaystyle\hskip 65.04256pt=\int_{[0,\tau)^{2}}\int_{({\mathbf{H}^{n}})^{2}}\Gamma(s-t)G_{2\alpha}(q_{1},q_{2})\varphi(s,q_{1})\psi(t,q_{1})\,d\mu(q_{1})d\mu(q_{2})dsdt\,,

where
G_{2\alpha}
is the kernel defined by (
1.12
).

Remark 4.9
.

The identity between (
4.37
) and (
4.38
) is explained in
[
6
, Section 3]
. In addition, one can define
\mathcal{D}
in Definition
4.8
as the closure of smooth functions under the inner product given by (
4.38
). The time structure of this functional set is described in
[
13
]
, while the spatial component has been identified as the Sobolev space
W^{-\alpha,2}
in
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Remark 4.10
.

As defined in (
4.38
) the noise
\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}
is a distribution in both time and space. In order to be more consistent with a noise
\dot{\mathbf{W}}
being Hölder continuous in time like in Hypothesis
4.5
, let us introduce a new piece of notation. That is for
0\leq s\leq t\leq\tau
and
\varphi:{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}
smooth enough we set

(4.39)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{t}(\varphi)=\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[0,t)}\otimes\varphi),\quad\text{and }\quad\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st}(\varphi)=\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[s,t)}\otimes\varphi),

where
\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}
is still the noise as introduced in Definition
4.8
.

Our aim in this section is to prove that the noises
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
are proper inputs for (
4.5
). According to Theorem
4.7
, this amounts to prove that
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
satisfies Hypothesis
4.5
. We will thus have to place
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
in a space
C^{\vartheta,\kappa,w}
as defined in (
4.1
).
This involves weighted Besov spaces
\mathfrak{B}^{\kappa,w}
, for which block decompositions are useful. We start by computing this decomposition in the lemma below.

Lemma 4.11
.

For
\zeta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n+1}{2})
, let
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
be the noise given in Definition
4.8
and Remark
4.10
. For a distribution
f
defined on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, recall that the block
\sigma_{k}f
is defined by (
3.4
). Then for all
t\in(0,\tau)
and
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
, the random variable
\sigma_{k}\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{t}(q)
is given by the following Wiener integral:

(4.40)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{t}(q)=\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[0,t)}\otimes H_{k,q}),

where the function
H_{k,q}
is defined through the kernels
K_{m,\ell,\lambda}
in (
2.10
) in the following way:

(4.41)

H_{k,q}(q^{\prime})=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\int_{\tilde{\bf H}^{n}}e^{i\lambda(z-z^{\prime})}K_{\hat{w}}(q)\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)\overline{K_{\hat{w}}(q^{\prime})}\,d\hat{w},

where we have used the convention (
2.11
) for the integration over
\hat{w}
.

Proof.

The blocks are characterized through their Fourier transform according to (
3.4
):

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma_{k}\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\phi}_{k}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}})(m,\ell,\lambda)=\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)\widehat{\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}}(m,\ell,\lambda),

where the second identity is obtained similarly to (
3.5
). We now apply formula (
2.9
) for the Fourier transforms of
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
, which yields

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\sigma_{k}\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{t}}\right]\!(m,\ell,\lambda)=\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\overline{e^{i\lambda z}K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q)}\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(ds,dq)\right).

Therefore invoking the inverse Fourier transform (
2.11
), our definition (
4.41
) of the kernel
H_{k,q}
and a standard use of stochastic Fubini identities, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{t}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\int_{\tilde{\bf H}^{n}}e^{i\lambda z}K_{\hat{w}}(q)\left(\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{H}^{n}}\overline{e^{i\lambda z^{\prime}}K_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q^{\prime})}\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(ds,dq^{\prime})\right)d\hat{w}

(4.42)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}H_{k,q}(q^{\prime})\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(ds,dq^{\prime})=\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[0,t)}\otimes H_{k,q}).

Formula (
4.42
) proves relation (
4.40
) for fixed
t\in[0,\tau)
and
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. It remains to see that this formula is valid almost surely for all
t\in[0,\tau)
and
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. Since
k
is fixed, this easily stems from continuity arguments based on Kolmogorov’s criterion. We skip the details for sake of conciseness.
∎

We now delve deeper into the structure of the functions
H_{k,q}
. Our findings are summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.12
.

For
q,q^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
and
k\geq-1
, let
H_{k,q}(q^{\prime})
be the quantity given by (
4.41
). Recall that the inverse Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
is given by (
2.11
). Then we have

(4.43)

H_{k,q}(q^{\prime})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\tilde{\phi}_{k})(qq^{\prime-1})

where
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
is given as in (
3.2
).

Proof.

Let us explicit all the terms in our definition (
4.41
) of the kernel
H_{k,q}
. That is we use the fact, apparent from (
2.10
), that for
\hat{w}=(m,\ell,\lambda)
,

\overline{K}_{\hat{w}}(q^{\prime})=\overline{K}_{m,\ell,\lambda}(q^{\prime})=K_{m,\ell,-\lambda}(q^{\prime}).

Then we plug (
2.10
) into (
4.41
) and invoke the convention (
2.11
) on integration over
d\hat{w}
. We end up with the identity

\displaystyle H_{k,q}(q^{\prime})=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\sum_{m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,|\lambda|^{n}e^{i\lambda(z-z^{\prime})}\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}d\xi d\xi^{\prime}e^{2i\lambda\left(\langle y,\xi\rangle-\langle y^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}\rangle\right)}

(4.44)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}(x+\xi)\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}(x^{\prime}+\xi^{\prime})\Phi^{\lambda}_{\ell}(-x+\xi)\Phi^{\lambda}_{\ell}(-x^{\prime}+\xi^{\prime}).

We can simplify expression (
4.44
) by recalling (see Section
2.1
) that for every
\lambda
the family
\{\Phi_{m}^{\lambda};m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we thus have

f(x)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Phi_{\ell}^{\lambda}(y)f(y)dy\right)\Phi_{\ell}^{\lambda}(x),

from which it is easily seen that the following relation holds true in a distributional sense over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

(4.45)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\Phi^{\lambda}_{\ell}(-x+\xi)\Phi^{\lambda}_{\ell}(-x^{\prime}+\xi^{\prime})=\delta(x-x^{\prime}-(\xi-\xi^{\prime})).

Plugging (
4.45
) into (
4.44
) we thus obtain

\displaystyle H_{k,q}(q^{\prime})=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,|\lambda|^{n}e^{i\lambda(z-z^{\prime})}\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)

(4.46)

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\xi\,e^{2i\lambda\left(\langle y-y^{\prime},\xi\rangle+\langle y^{\prime},x-x^{\prime}\rangle\right)}\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}(x+\xi)\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}(\xi-x+2x^{\prime}).

In the above integral with respect to
\xi
, we perform the change of variable
\xi^{\prime}=\xi+x
. With relation (
2.10
) in mind, we realize that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle e^{2i\lambda\left(\langle y-y^{\prime},\xi\rangle+\langle y^{\prime},x-x^{\prime}\rangle\right)}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}(x+\xi)\Phi^{\lambda}_{m}(\xi-x+2x^{\prime})d\xi

(4.47)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=e^{-2i\lambda\langle y-y^{\prime},x^{\prime}\rangle}K_{m,m,\lambda}(x-x^{\prime},y-y^{\prime},z-z^{\prime}+2\langle y^{\prime},x\rangle-2\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle).

Gathering this information into (
4.46
) we get

\displaystyle H_{k,q}(q^{\prime})=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\,|\lambda|^{n}e^{i\lambda(z-z^{\prime}+2\langle y^{\prime},x\rangle-2\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle)}\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)

(4.48)

\displaystyle\times K_{m,m,\lambda}(x-x^{\prime},y-y^{\prime},z-z^{\prime}+2\langle y^{\prime},x\rangle-2\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle).

Now we simply recall from (
1.1
) that
(x-x^{\prime},y-y^{\prime},z-z^{\prime}+2\langle y^{\prime},x\rangle-2\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle)=qq^{\prime-1}
. Taking into account the fact that
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
is supported on the diagonal (
m=\ell
) and invoking relation (
2.10
) again, one can recast (
4.48
) as

(4.49)

\displaystyle H_{k,q}(q^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\lambda(qq^{-1})_{z}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}K_{m,m,\lambda}(qq^{\prime-1})\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda

(4.50)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}({\tilde{\phi}}_{k})(qq^{\prime-1}),

where
(qq^{-1})_{z}
stands for the
z
-coordinate of
qq^{-1}\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. This finishes the proof of our claim (
4.43
).
∎

In order to compute expectations in a convenient way, we will place our noise
\dot{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
in a specific class of Besov spaces. We label a proper definition below.

Definition 4.13
.

For
a\in[1,\infty)
,
\gamma>0
and a weight
\rho_{b}
defined as in (
3.37
), we denote by
\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}
the Besov space
\mathfrak{B}^{-\gamma,\rho_{b}}_{2a,2a}
. Recall from Definition
3.4
that the norm on
\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}
is given by

(4.51)

\|f\|_{\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}}^{2a}=\|f\|^{2a}_{\mathfrak{B}^{-\gamma,\,\rho_{b}}_{2a,\,2a}}=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\left(2^{-2\gamma ak}\|\sigma_{k}f\|^{2a}_{L^{2a}_{\rho_{b}}}\right).

In the lemma below we prove an embedding result for the Besov spaces.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}
be a Besov space as defined above. If
f\in\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}
for all
a\in[1,\infty)
. Then
f\in\mathfrak{A}_{\infty}^{-\gamma-\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

First we show that for any
0<\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}<\infty
and
0<\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}\leq\infty
it holds that

(4.52)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}^{-\gamma,\rho_{b}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}\subset\mathfrak{B}^{-\gamma-(n+1)(\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}),\rho_{b}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}}.

To this aim, note from (
2.92
) with
k=0
that the following holds true for all
\alpha_{2}\geq\alpha_{1}
:

\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{2}}_{\rho_{b}}}\leq C2^{k+(n+1)(\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}})}\lVert\sigma_{k}f\rVert_{L^{\alpha_{1}}_{\rho_{b}}},

given that
{\rm{Supp}}\,\mathcal{F}({\sigma_{k}f})\subset\{(m,\ell,\lambda):|\lambda|(2|m|+n)<C\cdot 2^{k}\}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{-\gamma-(n+1)(\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}),\rho_{b}}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\left(2^{-\gamma k-(n+1)k(\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}})}\|\sigma_{k}f\|_{L^{\alpha_{2}}_{\rho_{b}}}\right)^{\beta_{1}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}}

(4.53)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\left(2^{-\gamma k}\|\sigma_{k}f\|_{L^{\alpha_{1}}_{\rho_{b}}}\right)^{\beta_{1}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}}=C\|f\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{-\gamma,\rho_{b}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}}.

We then obtain (
4.52
) by using the fact that
\ell^{\beta_{1}}(\mathbb{Z})
is continuously embedded in
\ell^{\beta_{2}}(\mathbb{Z})
for all
0<\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}\leq\infty
.

Now using (
4.52
) with
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{1}=2a
and
\alpha_{2}=\beta_{2}=\infty
we have
f\in\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}
for all
a\in[1,\infty)
implies that
f\in\mathfrak{B}^{-\gamma-\frac{n+1}{2a},\rho_{b}}_{\infty,\infty}
for all
a\in[1,\infty)
. This then implies the desired conclusion.
∎

Our next lemma establishes how a noise with a given covariance function sits in spaces of the form
\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}
.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
be a noise as given in Definition
4.8
, with
\zeta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n+1}{2})
. Recall that we have defined a time-integrated noise
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
in (
4.39
) and that the spaces
\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}
are introduced in Definition
4.13
. Then for
a\geq 1
large enough,
\gamma>\frac{n+1}{2}-\alpha
and
\vartheta\in(0,1-\frac{\zeta}{2})
, there exists a random variable
Z
admitting moments of all orders such that the following inequality holds true:

(4.54)

\displaystyle\|\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st}\|_{\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}}\leq Z(t-s)^{\vartheta}.

As a consequence, we have that
{\mathbf{W}}^{\zeta,\alpha}
is in the space
C^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}_{\infty,\infty}
for any
\gamma>\frac{n+1}{2}-\alpha
and
\vartheta\in(0,1-\frac{\zeta}{2})
.

Proof.

Consider a generic
a\geq 1
and
\vartheta,\gamma
satisfying the conditions of our lemma. We will first bound the moments of the increments of
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
. Namely a simple application of Fubini’s identity to (
4.51
) yields

(4.55)

\mathbf{E}\left(\|\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st}\|^{2a}_{\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}}\right)=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}2^{-2\gamma ak}\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}\mathbf{E}\left(|\sigma_{k}(\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st})(q)|^{2a}\right)\rho_{b}(q)^{2a}\,d\mu(q).

We proceed to estimate the expected value in (
4.55
). To this aim recall from (
4.40
) that

\sigma_{k}\,\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st}=\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\otimes H_{k,q}).

Moreover, thanks to the fact that
\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}
is a Gaussian family, for every
a>1
there exists a constant
c_{a}
such that

(4.56)

\mathbf{E}\left(|(\sigma_{k}\,\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st})(q)|^{2a}\right)=c_{a}\bigg[\mathbf{E}\left(|\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\otimes H_{k,q})|^{2}\right)\bigg]^{a}.

We are thus reduced to estimate the variance of each random variable
\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\otimes H_{k,q})
. Next we resort to relation (
4.37
), the bound (
4.36
) and Plancherel’s identity (
2.12
), which enables to write

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(|\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\otimes H_{k,q})|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{[s,t]^{2}}\left(\int_{{\mathbf{H}^{n}}}|(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}H_{k,q}|^{2}\mu(dq)\right)\Gamma(u-v)dudv

(4.57)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}(t-s)^{2-\zeta}\sum_{m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,|\widehat{(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}H_{k,q}}|^{2}|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda.

In addition invoke expression (
4.43
) for
H_{k,q}
, relation (
2.14
) for the Laplace transform of
(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}H_{k,q}
and the fact that

|\left[\mathcal{F}f(q*\cdot)\right](\hat{w})|=|\mathcal{F}f(\hat{w})|
for a generic function
f
. We end up with

|\widehat{(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}H_{k,q}}(m,\ell,\lambda)|^{2}=4^{-2\alpha}|\lambda|^{-2\alpha}(2|m|+n)^{-2\alpha}|\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,\ell,\lambda)|^{2}.

Plugging this identity into (
4.57
) and recalling from (
3.1
) that
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
is supported on the diagonal
(m=\ell)
, we thus get the existence of a constant
c_{n,\alpha}
such that

(4.58)

\mathbf{E}\left(|\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\otimes H_{k,q})|^{2}\right)\leq c_{n,\alpha}(t-s)^{2-\zeta}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}J_{m,\ell,k},

where we have set

(4.59)

J_{m,\ell,k}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,|\lambda|^{-2\alpha}(2|m|+n)^{-2\alpha}|\tilde{\phi}_{k}(m,m,\lambda)|^{2}|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda.

Let us now take care of the integral
J_{m,\ell,k}
defined by (
4.59
).
Thanks to the definition (
3.2
) of
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
, it is readily checked that

J_{m,\ell,k}=(2|m|+n)^{-2\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\chi({2^{-k}|\lambda|R(m,m)})\right)^{2}|\lambda|^{n-2\alpha}d\lambda.

Moreover the function
\chi
given in Proposition
3.1
is supported in
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:3/4\leq|x|<8/3\}
. Therefore
J_{m,\ell,k}
is such that

J_{m,\ell,k}\leq(2|m|+n)^{-2\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}_{\left(|\lambda|<\frac{2^{k+2}}{(2|m|+n)}\right)}|\lambda|^{n-2\alpha}d\lambda.

We now integrate over
\lambda
in an explicit way, under the assumption
n-2\alpha>-1
(that is
\alpha<\frac{n+1}{2}
). This yields

(4.60)

\displaystyle J_{m,\ell,k}\leq\frac{c_{n,\alpha}}{(2|m|+n)^{2\alpha}}\left(\frac{2^{k+2}}{2|m|+n}\right)^{n-2\alpha+1}=c_{n,\alpha}\frac{2^{(k+2)(n+1-2\alpha)}}{(2|m|+n)^{n+1}}.

Gathering (
4.60
) and (
4.58
), we have thus obtained the upper bound

(4.61)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(|\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}(\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\otimes H_{k,q})|^{2}\right)\leq c_{n,\alpha}\,2^{k(n+1-2\alpha)}(t-s)^{2-\zeta}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\frac{1}{(2|m|+n)^{n+1}},

under the assumption
\alpha<\frac{n+1}{2}
. Eventually observe that the sum over
m
in (
4.61
) is finite, by elementary arguments. We have thus achieved the desired bound on the variance of the increments:

(4.62)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(|(\sigma_{k}\,\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st})(q)|^{2}\right)\leq c_{n,\alpha}2^{(n+1-2\alpha)k}(t-s)^{(2-\zeta)},\quad\text{for all }q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}.

We now conclude the lemma by using relation (
4.62
) in order to bound sums in
\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}
. That is combine (
4.62
) and (
4.56
) to obtain the following uniform estimate over
q\in{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
:

(4.63)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(|(\sigma_{k}\,\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st})(q)|^{2a}\right)\leq c_{n,\alpha,a}(t-s)^{a(2-\zeta)}2^{ak(n+1-2\alpha)}.

Now plug this inequality into (
4.55
). If
a
is chosen large enough (namely such that
ab>n+1
), the function
(\rho_{b})^{a}
becomes integrable on
{\mathbf{H}^{n}}
. Therefore combining (
4.63
) and (
4.55
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(\|\delta\,\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st}\|^{2a}_{\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}}\right)\leq c_{n,a,\alpha}(t-s)^{(2-\zeta)a}\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}2^{(n+1-2\alpha-2\gamma)ak}.

Hence if the coefficient
\gamma
satisfies our standing assumption
\gamma>\frac{n+1}{2}-\alpha
, the summation above is finite and for every
a>1
we have

(4.64)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(\|\delta\,\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st}\|^{2a}_{\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}}\right)\leq c_{n,a,\alpha,\gamma}(t-s)^{(2-\zeta)a}.

Our proof of (
4.54
) is now finished by standard arguments. Namely one goes from the moment inequality (
4.64
) to the almost sure relation (
4.54
) resorting to Garsia’s lemma
and Fernique’s inequality (recall that
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
is a Gaussian process). Details are omitted for the sake of conciseness.
Lastly, combining (
4.53
) and (
4.54
) we obtain that

(4.65)

\displaystyle\|\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st}\|_{\mathfrak{B}^{-\gamma-\frac{(n+1)}{2a},\rho_{b}}_{\infty,\infty}}\leq\|\delta\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}_{st}\|_{\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma}}\leq Z(t-s)^{\vartheta}

for all
\gamma+\frac{n+1}{2a}>\frac{n+1}{2}-\alpha
. Since the parameter
a
in (
4.65
) can be chosen as arbitrarily large, we get that
\mathbf{W}^{\zeta,\alpha}
sits in the space
C_{\infty,\infty}^{\vartheta,-\gamma-\epsilon,\rho_{b}}
for any
\gamma>\frac{n+1}{2}-\alpha
, for any
\epsilon>0
and
\vartheta\in(0,1-\zeta/2)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to state the main result of this section, ensuring existence and uniqueness for our stochastic heat equation (
4.5
) under proper conditions on the noise
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
be the noise given in Definition
4.8
and Remark
4.10
. We assume that the parameters
\alpha,\zeta
satisfy the conditions

(4.66)

\zeta\in(0,1),\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{n+1}{2}-(1-\zeta)<\alpha<\frac{n+1}{2}.

Then
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
verifies the conditions of Hypothesis
4.5
. Namely there exists
0<\gamma<1-\zeta
,
\frac{1+\gamma}{2}<\vartheta<1-\frac{\zeta}{2}
and
\rho_{b}(q)=c(1+|q|_{*}^{b})
for some
c,b>0
such that
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}\in C_{\infty,\infty}^{\vartheta,-\gamma,\rho_{b}}
.

Proof.

In Lemma
4.15
we have considered
\zeta\in(0,1)
,
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n+1}{2})
. Then we have proved that
\mathbf{V}^{\zeta,\alpha}
almost surely belongs to the space
C^{\vartheta}(\mathfrak{A}_{a}^{-\gamma})
for two parameters
\vartheta
,
\gamma
such that

(4.67)

\vartheta<1-\frac{\zeta}{2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\gamma>\frac{n+1}{2}-\alpha.

Now hypothesis
4.5
specifies that we should have
\vartheta>\frac{1+\gamma}{2}
, which can be recast as

(4.68)

2\vartheta>1+\gamma.

We can make (
4.67
) and (
4.68
) compatible iff
\alpha
and
\zeta
satisfy

2-\zeta<1+\frac{n+1}{2}-\alpha,

which is easily seen to be equivalent to

\alpha>\frac{n+1}{2}-(1-\zeta).

This finishes our proof.
∎
