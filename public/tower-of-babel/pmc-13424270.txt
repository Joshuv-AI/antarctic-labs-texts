Comment on “swedish massage versus hip strengthening exercises for pain and function in older adults with knee osteoarthritis: a randomized controlled trial”
Authors: Sahil Kumar, Sandeep Pattnaik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe results of the randomised controlled trial by Asgarimoghadam et al. (2026) on the use of Swedish massage and hip-strengthening exercises in older adults with knee osteoarthritis are quite informative regarding non-pharmacological management strategies. Although both interventions showed remarkable outcomes in alleviating pain and enhancing function and range of movement compared with the control, a few methodological issues should be considered. The lack of participant blinding with the use of subjective outcome measures is a potential risk of performance bias. Also, the sample size used is not very large, which raises doubts about the lack of statistical power to identify the differences between two active interventions contributing to an increased risk of a Type II error. The large screening-to-enrollment ratio also constrains the external validity of the results to more widespread clinical groups. The future research by addressing these limitations in prospective research with sound trial design, big sample size and enhanced external validity will go a long way to improve the evidence base associated with clinical decision-making in the geriatric rehabilitation field.

Dear Editor

We are very interested in reading the randomized controlled trial by Asgarimoghadam et al. [1] on the effects of Swedish massage (SM) and hip strengthening exercises (HSE) on pain, range of motion, and functional outcomes in older adults with knee osteoarthritis (KOA). These two points ought to be praised, because the authors have discussed a clinically significant issue and have carried out a realistic intervention at home.

Nevertheless, there are a number of methodological aspects that should be discussed. First, there is a possibility of performance bias as the participants were not blinded, especially since the outcome measures were subjective, including the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and KOOS-ADL. As is known, non-blinding can overstate the effect of treatment, particularly in non-drug treatments [2].

Second, as much as the research sought to compare the SM and HSE, no statistically significant differences existed between the two interventions. Although this could indicate similar efficacy, the relatively low sample size casts doubt on the lack of statistical power and the probability of Type II error [3]. A study specifically targeted as either a non-inferiority or an equivalence trial could have been more suitable to draw such conclusions.

Third, the large screening-to-enrolment ratio (710 screened, 75 included) indicates a very narrow sample, which can limit the generalizability of the results. Clinical trials with older adults can have difficulties in recruitment; nonetheless, the selectivity can diminish external validity and extrapolation to more typical clinical environments [4].

To sum up, although this study offers useful evidence in favour of non-pharmacological interventions to treat KOA, further studies must include the ability to minimize bias, guarantee sufficient statistical power, and improve the external validity. These enhancements would improve the quality of evidence on clinical decision-making in geriatric rehabilitation.
