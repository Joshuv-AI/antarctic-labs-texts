A dual pH/ROS-responsive hyaluronan-gated hollow mesoporous silica nanoplatform with CD44-associated cellular interaction improves curcumin retention under serum challenge and attenuates TNF-α–driven inflammatory outputs in rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts
Authors: Ni Yan, Elaheh Pilehvar, Sepehr Mehdizadeh, Xiaoyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis (RA) synovitis is driven in part by activated fibroblast-like synoviocytes derived from rheumatoid arthritis tissue (RA-FLS), which amplify inflammatory and oxidative pathways that contribute to progressive joint damage. Curcumin (CUR) is mechanistically relevant to these pathogenic processes; however, its therapeutic utility is constrained by poor aqueous solubility, low bioavailability, and non-selective exposure. Here, we developed a hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticle (HMSN)-based nanodelivery platform that integrates an HA outer gate with a dual pH/reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive interfacial design. Unlike passive HA-coated carriers, this system was designed to combine serum-phase CUR retention with condition-dependent HA gate remodeling and CD44-associated cellular interaction. The final HA-gated formulation preserved the hollow HMSN architecture, achieved high CUR incorporation (encapsulation efficiency 73.55 ± 3.06%; drug loading 18.39 ± 0.77% w/w), and showed reduced baseline leakage under neutral conditions with increased release under combined acidic/oxidative stimulation. HA shedding assays and bonding-mode controls further supported stimulus-selective gate detachment rather than simple adsorption-related loss. Under protein challenge, the HA-gated system reduced premature CUR leakage relative to ungated HMSNs. In RA-FLS, it increased cell-associated CUR signal in a CD44-associated manner and, at matched CUR-equivalent dosing, produced the strongest suppression of metabolic viability, DNA synthesis, TNF-α-induced inflammatory and matrix-associated mediators, and intracellular ROS, while showing improved tolerability in healthy synoviocytes compared with free CUR. These findings support this HA-gated dual pH/ROS-responsive HMSN system as an in vitro proof-of-concept nanodelivery platform for improving CUR retention and RA-FLS-directed functional activity under inflammatory microenvironment-relevant conditions.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Synovial fibroblasts, Hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles, Hyaluronan gating, Stimuli-responsive nanodelivery, pH/ROS-responsive release, Curcumin

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory disease that maintains synovial inflammation through immune activation, stroma remodelling, and stress of the microenvironment leading to progressive destruction of cartilage and bone. While rheumatoid arthritis is a systemic disease, much of its pathogenic effect is joint-specific and mediated via the effects of the synovial environment, in which hypoxia, metabolism adaptation, and acidification arise alongside inflammation and tissue invasion. Inflammation leads to reduction in synovial oxygen tension, which is positively correlated with synovitis; moreover, synovial fluid pH can become sub-physiological, indicating that mild acidity can be used as a disease-specific trigger during delivery [1], which are relevant for small molecule delivery since they result in faster degradation and allow for condition-specific release, when the carrier is protected against neutral conditions with protein present.

Within this inflammatory microenvironment, rheumatoid arthritis fibroblast-like synoviocytes (RA-FLS) function as active pathological effectors rather than merely passive structural cells. RA-FLS retain an aggressive, disease-associated phenotype even ex vivo, contributing to synovial hyperplasia, cartilage invasion, and persistent production of inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading proteases [2, 3]. TNF-α is a canonical upstream driver of RA-FLS activation and is widely used to model inflammatory effector programs in vitro, including induction of IL-6, COX-2/PTGS2, MMPs such as MMP-3, and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS/NOS2). Oxidative stress further amplifies this pathology by reinforcing inflammatory signaling, damaging cellular and matrix components, and sustaining pathogenic phenotypes across stromal and immune compartments [4, 5]. Accordingly, RA-oriented delivery systems should do more than suppress cell growth; they should attenuate inflammatory mediator production and oxidative burden in RA-FLS under conditions relevant to the stressed synovial microenvironment.

Curcumin (CUR) is an attractive anti-inflammatory and antioxidant small molecule with broad activity against inflammatory transcriptional programs and redox imbalance, but its therapeutic use is limited by poor aqueous solubility, rapid metabolism, and low bioavailability [6–8]. These limitations have repeatedly motivated nanoformulation approaches. However, simply placing CUR in a carrier is not sufficient for RA-relevant delivery. Conventional nanocarriers often face three major problems in inflamed joints: premature leakage from porous reservoirs, weak discrimination between isolated and combined pathological triggers, and protein corona formation that reshapes colloidal identity, uptake, and drug retention in serum-containing environments [9, 10]. For mesoporous systems, high loading often comes at the expense of stable retention, whereas ligand-directed strategies may lose functional advantage after protein adsorption. Thus, a useful RA delivery platform should preserve payload in protein-rich media, respond preferentially to the combined cues of inflamed synovium rather than to any single perturbation alone, and enhance interaction with RA-FLS through disease-relevant surface biology without introducing carrier-driven toxicity.

One of the key receptor axes in RA synovium is the CD44 receptor, which is one of the main receptors of hyaluronic acid that is expressed by synovial cells, both from the RA synovium and isolated synovial fibroblasts [11]. This property makes hyaluronic acid a common tool in the increase of the cellular interaction between CD44 receptors. Nevertheless, functionalization with HA alone does not provide a solution to the major challenge of drug delivery, as HA layer alone cannot serve as an active corona unless it is used within the gatekeeping system, where it can undergo leakage remodeling under pathological stimuli [12, 13]. MSN, especially HMSN, represent attractive materials for such use cases due to their high volume and tunable porosity, thus providing an effective mechanism for loading hydrophobic cargo [14, 15]. These features are directly relevant to the current platform, where the mesoporous/hollow architecture functions as a CUR reservoir and the external silica surface supports sequential construction of a responsive linker layer and HA gate. This reservoir–interface separation is advantageous for preserving structural integrity during multistep engineering while enabling regulated release and HA-associated cellular interaction.

The key unresolved question is whether a CUR-loaded HMSN can be engineered to resist premature leakage under protein-corona challenge while also showing enhanced gate remodeling and cargo release under combined acidic and oxidative conditions, while preserving colloidal integrity and enhancing RA-FLS association. In order to overcome this limitation, we have developed a CD44-targeted dual-response nanodelivery system wherein HMSNs act as the CUR carrier, the interface polymer/ linker provides pH response and facilitates HA attachment, while HA is attached using a responsive chemistry that will be shed on exposure to both acidic and oxidative stimuli. This design was chosen because combined acidic and oxidative exposure may better approximate key features of the inflamed synovial microenvironment than either condition alone. ROS-labile boronate/boronic-ester chemistries are well established for peroxide-responsive remodeling, and coupling this motif with pH-sensitive interfacial elements provides a plausible basis for condition-dependent gate destabilization under inflammatory conditions [16].

With this rationale in mind, our research hypothesis states that pH/ROS dual-responsiveness HA-gated HMSNs could reduce CUR leakage in high-protein environments while promoting disease-specific HA shedding, resulting in greater RA-FLS targeting and superior inhibition of TNF-α-induced inflammatory mediators as well as intracellular oxidative stress via equivalent CUR exposure. We aimed to achieve this via pursuing the following four goals: (1) to confirm progressive assembly of the nanoparticle structure and its maintenance, accompanied by consistent changes in the properties of the colloids, such as size and zeta potential; (2) to analyze responsiveness to stimuli by means of textural and payload analysis, release testing under pH/ROS changes, and HA-shedding assays along with bonding control experiments; (3) to study the stability of the colloid in the serum phase by monitoring its size, polydispersity, zeta potential, and premature CUR leakage in the presence of increasing amounts of proteins in the media; and (4) to study the connection between surface chemistry and functional response by measuring RA-FLS binding to the particle surface and blocking experiments for CD44 followed by CUR-equivalent comparison studies on viability, DNA synthesis, inflammatory factors, and oxidative stress according to the IC₅₀ concentration.

Materials and methods

Synthesis and surface engineering

Solid silica cores were synthesized by a Stöber method. Ethanol (200 mL, Merck), deionized water (20 mL, Milli-Q), and aqueous ammonia (10 mL, 28–30%, Sigma-Aldrich) were mixed, and TEOS (10 mL, Sigma-Aldrich) was added dropwise. After 6 h at 25 °C, the silica spheres were collected, washed with ethanol and water, and vacuum-dried at 40 °C.

Mesoporous core-shell particles were then prepared by dissolving etyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) (1.0 g, Sigma-Aldrich) in deionized water (480 mL) at 40 °C, followed by aqueous ammonia (7 mL, Sigma-Aldrich). Silica cores (200 mg), dispersed in ethanol, were added, and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) (3.0 mL, Sigma-Aldrich) was introduced dropwise. After 2 h at 40 °C, the particles were collected and washed with ethanol.

The CTAB template was removed by two reflux extractions in ethanol (200 mL, Merck) containing 1.0% v/v HCl (Merck), each for 2 h. Template-free core-shell particles were then etched in Na₂CO₃ solution (0.60 M, 100 mL, Sigma-Aldrich) at 60 °C for 60 min to generate HMSNs. The resulting HMSNs were washed to neutral pH, rinsed with ethanol, and vacuum-dried at 40 °C.

For aminosilanization, HMSNs (100 mg) were dispersed in anhydrous ethanol (40 mL, Merck), reacted with (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES) (1.0 mL, Sigma-Aldrich), and refluxed at 78 °C for 8 h to yield HMSNs–NH₂. For linker installation, 4-carboxyphenylboronic acid (CPBA) (120 mg, TCI) was activated in MES buffer (50 mM, pH 6.0, 20 mL, Sigma-Aldrich) using EDC·HCl (230 mg, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and NHS (140 mg, Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 min, then reacted with HMSNs–NH₂ (100 mg) for 12 h at 25 °C to produce HMSNs–NH₂–PBAE. For HA grafting, HA (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in PBS (pH 7.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 2.0 mg/mL, and HMSNs–NH₂–PBAE (100 mg) were incubated in the HA solution (50 mL) for 12 h at 25 °C. The resulting HMSNs–PBAE–HA were collected, washed with PBS and water, lyophilized, and stored at 4 °C protected from light.

CUR loading and final formulation

CUR (30 mg, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in ethanol (30 mL, Merck). HMSNs or HMSNs–NH₂–PBAE (100 mg) were dispersed in ethanol (20 mL, Merck), combined with the CUR solution, and stirred for 18 h at 25 °C in the dark. The particles were collected, and the post-loading supernatant together with all wash fractions was retained for UV–Vis mass-balance quantification. After washing with ethanol and PBS (pH 7.4), HA grafting was performed on CUR@HMSNs–PBAE as described above to obtain CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA. Final products were lyophilized and stored at 4 °C protected from light. A schematic summary of fabrication, surface engineering, and CUR loading is shown in Fig. 1.

Schematic illustration of HMSN fabrication, stepwise surface engineering, and CUR loading. The CTAB template was removed by repeated acid extraction under reflux (1% v/v HCl in ethanol), and the cores were subsequently selectively etched with Na₂CO₃ to generate HMSNs. Physicochemical characterization of nanoformulations

Nanoparticle morphology was examined by transmission electron microscopy using a JEOL JEM-2100 TEM (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 200 kV. Samples were prepared by depositing ethanol-dispersed particles (0.10 mg/mL) onto carbon-coated copper grids (Ted Pella) and air-drying before imaging. Representative micrographs were selected after inspection of at least five independent fields per sample.

Hydrodynamic diameter and dispersity were measured by dynamic light scattering using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) in filtered PBS (pH 7.4, 0.20 mg/mL) at 25 °C with number-weighted reporting. Each formulation was analyzed using three independently prepared dispersions, with three acquisitions per dispersion. PDI values were obtained from cumulants analysis. Zeta potential was measured by electrophoretic light scattering using the same instrument in PBS (pH 7.4, 0.20 mg/mL) at 25 °C with disposable capillary cells.

Surface chemistry was characterized by ATR-FTIR using a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a diamond ATR accessory. Dried powders were scanned over 4000–500 cm¹ using 32 scans at 4 cm¹ resolution. Spectra were baseline-corrected, normalized, and displayed with vertical offsets for clarity.

Thermal behavior was analyzed by TGA/DTG using a Q500 thermogravimetric analyzer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) under nitrogen (60 mL/min) using approximately 7 mg of sample. Samples were heated from 25 to 120 °C at 10 °C/min, held for 20 min, and then heated to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.

Porosity was evaluated by N₂ adsorption–desorption at 77 K using an ASAP 2020 surface area analyzer (Micromeritics, Norcross, GA, USA). Samples were degassed under high vacuum (< 10 µmHg) using chemistry-preserving conditions: HMSNs, 150 °C for 12 h; HMSNs–PBAE–HA, 80 °C for 10 h; and CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA, 70 °C for 10 h. BET surface area and BJH pore-size distributions were obtained using the instrument software.

Stimuli-dependent CUR release

Release studies were performed in four media to separate pH and oxidative effects: PBS (pH 7.4), acetate buffer (pH 6.5; 10 mM sodium acetate adjusted with HCl), PBS (pH 7.4) containing hydrogen peroxide (1.0 mM, Merck), and acetate buffer (pH 6.5) containing hydrogen peroxide (1.0 mM). All media were pre-equilibrated at 37 °C.

Equal CUR-equivalent doses of CUR@HMSNs, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE, and CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA were loaded into dialysis tubing (Spectra/Por, MWCO 12–14 kDa; donor volume 2.0 mL) and immersed in 50 mL receiver medium. Release was conducted at 37 °C with agitation at 120 rpm. At predetermined time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h), 1.0 mL aliquots were withdrawn and replaced with fresh pre-warmed medium. CUR in the receiver phase was quantified by UV–Vis at 425 nm.

HA shedding and bonding-mode specificity

HA shedding from HMSNs–PBAE–HA was evaluated at 37 °C under five conditions: PBS pH 7.4, acetate buffer pH 6.5, PBS pH 7.4 + 1.0 mM H₂O₂, acetate buffer pH 6.5 + 1.0 mM H₂O₂, and acetate buffer pH 6.5 + 10 mM H₂O₂. Before the shedding study, the total initial particle-associated HA content of the same formulation batch was determined by a carbazole-based uronic acid mass-balance assay using pooled post-grafting supernatants and wash fractions to quantify unbound HA. HA loading was expressed as µg HA per mg nanoparticles.

For shedding analysis, particles were incubated at 1.0 mg/mL and sampled at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. At each time point, particles were separated by centrifugation (20,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), and HA in the supernatant was quantified by carbazole-based uronic acid assay at 530 nm using a Synergy H1 plate reader (Agilent BioTek, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with matrix-matched HA standards prepared from HA (Sigma-Aldrich). To distinguish programmed cleavage from simple desorption or non-cleavable attachment, three groups were compared at 24 h under the same stimulus matrix: HMSNs–PBAE–HA, HMSNs + HA physical mixture, and covalently coupled HMSNs–HA prepared by EDC/NHS amide chemistry. All groups were tested using matched particle concentration, incubation volume, separation conditions, and assay chemistry.

Serum stability, corona-associated evolution, turbidity, and premature leakage

HMSNs–PBAE–HA, CUR@HMSNs, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE, and CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA were incubated at 37 °C in PBS (pH 7.4), complete growth medium containing 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific), or 50% serum prepared by mixing pooled human serum (Sigma-Aldrich) 1:1 with complete growth medium. Particle concentration was 0.20 mg/mL, and samples were analyzed at 0, 2, 6, 24, and 48 h.

At each time point, hydrodynamic diameter, PDI, and zeta potential were measured directly using the Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical). Turbidity was measured at 650 nm in a clear 96-well plate using the Synergy H1 plate reader (Agilent BioTek) and blank-corrected against the corresponding nanoparticle-free medium.

For premature CUR leakage, nanoparticles were separated by centrifugation (20,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), and CUR in the clarified supernatant was quantified by UV–Vis at 425 nm using matrix-matched standards. Leakage was expressed as the percentage of total initially loaded CUR. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent samples.

Cell culture quality and dosing framework

Primary human RA-FLS and healthy synovial fibroblasts were obtained from iCell Bioscience (Shanghai, China) and cultured in supplier-recommended synoviocyte growth medium supplemented with FBS and penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in humidified 5% CO₂. Cells were used at passage 4 throughout the study, and only mycoplasma-negative cultures verified by MycoAlert assay (Lonza) were used.

All CUR-containing groups were tested at CUR-equivalent 5 µM, defined from the 24 h IC₅₀ of free CUR in RA-FLS. Blank HMSNs–PBAE–HA was applied at a particle concentration matched to the corresponding CUR-loaded nanoparticle group.

Confocal microscopy and quantitative image analysis

RA-FLS were seeded on glass-bottom dishes at 30,000 cells per dish, allowed to attach for 24 h, and treated for 4 h with cells-only control, CUR@HMSNs, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA, or CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA with CD44 blockade, all at CUR-equivalent 5 µM. For blockade, cells were pre-incubated with anti-CD44 antibody (BioLegend, 10 µg/mL) for 30 min.

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich), stained with phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:200) and DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1 µg/mL), and imaged using an LSM 880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with a 63× oil objective. Imaging settings were kept constant within each experiment.

Quantification was performed in Fiji/ImageJ (NIH). Background subtraction used identically acquired cells-only controls. Mean fluorescence intensity was calculated per cell from at least five randomly selected fields per replicate. CUR-positive cells were defined using a threshold equal to the cells-only mean plus two standard deviations. Data are shown as mean ± SEM from three independent experiments.

IC₅₀, viability, proliferation, and inflammatory readouts

For IC₅₀ determination, RA-FLS were seeded in 96-well plates at 8,000 cells per well and treated with free CUR for 24 h. MTT was then added to 0.5 mg/mL, and the resulting formazan was dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich). Absorbance was read at 570 nm with 630 nm reference using the Synergy H1 (Agilent BioTek), and IC₅₀ was calculated by four-parameter logistic regression in GraphPad Prism.

For matched-dose comparisons, RA-FLS or healthy synoviocytes were treated for 24 h with Vehicle, blank HMSNs–PBAE–HA, free CUR, CUR@HMSNs, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE, or CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA at CUR-equivalent 5 µM. Formulation-only wells without cells were included as optical interference controls.

For EdU analysis, RA-FLS were treated for 24 h, labeled with 10 µM EdU (Thermo Fisher Scientific) during the final 2 h, and fluorescence was measured on the Synergy H1. Signal was background-corrected using no-EdU and formulation-matched controls. dsDNA from the same wells was quantified by PicoGreen (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and used for normalization. Results were expressed as percent of Vehicle.

For inflammatory gene expression, RA-FLS were seeded in 12-well plates at 120,000 cells per well, stimulated with TNF-α (PeproTech, 10 ng/mL), and treated concurrently for 24 h with Vehicle, blank carrier, free CUR, or nanoformulations at CUR-equivalent 5 µM. Total RNA was isolated using RNeasy (Qiagen), quantified by NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and reverse-transcribed using the High-Capacity Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qPCR was performed using PowerUp SYBR Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and expression was normalized to the geometric mean of GAPDH and ACTB using the 2ΔΔCt method, with TNF-α + Vehicle set to 1.

For IL-6 and MMP-3 ELISA, supernatants from the same treatment design were collected at 24 h, cleared by low-speed centrifugation, and analyzed using human IL-6 and MMP-3 ELISA kits (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Samples exceeding the calibration range were diluted and back-calculated. Measurements were performed in duplicate technical wells for each biological replicate.

Intracellular ROS assays

RA-FLS were treated for 24 h with Vehicle, blank HMSNs–PBAE–HA, free CUR, CUR@HMSNs, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE, or CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA at CUR-equivalent 5 µM in the presence of TNF-α (PeproTech, 10 ng/mL). Cells were then loaded with DCFH-DA (Sigma-Aldrich, 10 µM) for 30 min, washed, and fluorescence was measured at Ex 485 nm/Em 535 nm using the Synergy H1. Background correction included formulation-matched wells lacking DCFH-DA and wells containing formulations without cells.

For oxidative-challenge experiments, RA-FLS were pre-treated for 23 h under the same formulation conditions, then exposed to H₂O₂ (200 µM) for 1 h before DCFH-DA loading and fluorescence measurement. ROS index was expressed as percent of Vehicle within each panel.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, physicochemical data were obtained from three independent nanoparticle preparations. Cell experiments used three independent biological replicates, with technical duplicates or triplicates as specified. Data are presented as mean ± SD for physicochemical assays or mean ± SEM for biological assays. Statistical significance was evaluated using one- or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test (two-sided, α = 0.05) in GraphPad Prism v10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Significance thresholds were defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Physicochemical characterization and stimulus-responsive properties of the HA-gated nanoplatform

TEM analysis showed that pristine HMSNs had a well-defined hollow core-shell architecture with a continuous mesoporous shell and a clear electron-lucent cavity (Fig. 2A). This morphology was preserved after HA/PBAE surface functionalization in HMSNs–PBAE–HA and remained evident after CUR loading in CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA. In parallel, particle size increased progressively across the fabrication sequence. TEM-based size rose from approximately 135 nm for HMSNs to approximately 185 nm after HA/PBAE functionalization and to approximately 200 nm after CUR loading (Fig. 2A). Consistent with this trend, number-weighted DLS distributions showed a stepwise rightward shift after each modification step, and hydrodynamic diameter measurements increased sequentially across the preparation series (Fig. 2B, C). Despite these increases, dispersity remained controlled, with PDI values staying within a narrow range and number-based distributions remaining unimodal without evidence of a distinct large-particle population.

Scale bars, 100 nm. (B) Number-weighted DLS size distributions of intermediate and final formulations. (C) Hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) across fabrication steps. (D) Zeta potential of HMSNs, HMSNs–NH₂, HMSNs–PBAE–HA, and CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA measured in aqueous buffer at physiological pH. Data in panels C–D are shown as mean ± SD from independent measurements

Surface-charge analysis further tracked the successive modification steps. Pristine HMSNs showed a negative zeta potential of − 32.8 ± 1.2 mV, which shifted to + 21.3 ± 1.4 mV after APTES aminosilanization. Following installation of the HA-containing outer layer, the surface charge returned to a negative value in HMSNs–PBAE–HA (− 37.8 ± 1.1 mV), and this negative profile was maintained after CUR loading in CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA (− 40.3 ± 0.9 mV) (Fig. 2D).

FTIR spectra provided complementary evidence of stepwise surface engineering. HMSNs showed the expected silica-framework FTIR profile, dominated by the broad Si–O–Si stretching region around 1080–1100 cm¹, while subsequent surface modification was interpreted primarily from the emergence or strengthening of organic-region bands above 1000 cm¹ (Fig. 3A). After aminosilanization, HMSNs–NH₂ displayed increased intensity in the 3600–3000 cm¹ region, weak aliphatic C–H stretching bands around 2920–2850 cm¹, and a band in the approximately 1560–1650 cm¹ region. After PBAE installation, an ester carbonyl band appeared at approximately 1720–1730 cm¹ together with stronger aliphatic C–H signals. Following HA grafting, stronger carboxylate-associated bands were observed in the approximately 1600–1650 cm¹ and approximately 1400–1450 cm¹ regions, together with increased broad O–H-region intensity.

Thermogravimetric analysis also reflected the progressive addition of organic components. Bare HMSNs showed minimal total mass loss apart from low-temperature loss attributable to water/silanol-associated desorption, whereas HMSNs–PBAE–HA displayed a more pronounced mass-loss region mainly within approximately 200–600 °C, accompanied by a corresponding DTG profile (Fig. 3B, C). CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA showed a further increase in mass loss and DTG intensity relative to the corresponding blank HA-coated formulation. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption analysis showed that HMSNs retained a mesoporous isotherm profile, while accessible porosity decreased progressively across the series HMSNs > HMSNs–PBAE–HA > CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA (Fig. 4A). BJH pore-size distributions similarly showed reduced accessible pore volume after HA-gate installation and a further reduction after CUR loading. UV–Vis mass-balance analysis showed that the final HA-gated formulation, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA, achieved an encapsulation efficiency of 73.55 ± 3.06% and a drug loading of 18.39 ± 0.77% (w/w) (mean ± SD, n = 3) (Fig. 4B). The difference in magnitude between EE and DL is expected because EE is normalized to the input CUR mass, whereas DL is normalized to the total nanoparticle mass; thus, a formulation can retain a high fraction of the added drug while the drug still constitutes a smaller weight fraction of the overall carrier.

The release behavior of CUR was then evaluated under four environments: pH 7.4, pH 6.5, pH 7.4 + H₂O₂, and pH 6.5 + H₂O₂ (Fig. 4C1–C5). At pH 7.4, CUR@HMSNs showed the highest cumulative release, whereas CUR@HMSNs–PBAE released less and CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA showed the lowest release (Fig. 4C1). Under pH 6.5 and under pH 7.4 + H₂O₂, cumulative release increased for all formulations relative to pH 7.4 alone (Fig. 4C2,C3). Under the combined pH 6.5 + H₂O₂ condition, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA showed the highest release among its four environmental conditions (Fig. 4C4,C5). In parallel, carbazole-based uronic acid assay showed that the initial total particle-associated HA content was 123.3 ± 25.2 µg/mg nanoparticles (mean ± SD, n = 3). Time-resolved HA shedding remained low at pH 7.4 without H₂O₂, increased under acid-only and ROS-only conditions, and reached its highest level among the physiologically relevant conditions under pH 6.5 + H₂O₂ (Fig. 5A). The pH 6.5 + 10 mM H₂O₂ group showed the greatest overall shedding as a stress-test condition. In the 24 h bonding-mode comparison, the stimuli-cleavable HMSNs–PBAE–HA group showed greater HA release under triggering conditions than the covalent HA-control group, whereas the physical-mixture control showed higher HA loss across conditions consistent with weak association of adsorbed HA (Fig. 5B).

Serum stability, corona-associated evolution, and premature CUR leakage

The behavior of the formulations in protein-containing environments was examined by incubating them in PBS, 10% FBS-containing medium, or 50% serum for up to 48 h (Fig. 6). Hydrodynamic size changed in a time- and medium-dependent manner for all formulations, with the smallest changes observed in PBS and more pronounced shifts in protein-containing media, particularly in 50% serum (Fig. 6A). Across the tested groups, HA-coated systems showed smaller size increases than non-HA formulations under protein-rich conditions. PDI profiles remained relatively stable in PBS but increased over time in protein-containing media, with greater broadening in CUR@HMSNs and lower broadening in HA-coated systems (Fig. 6B). Zeta potential also evolved in a medium-dependent manner: in PBS, each formulation largely preserved its characteristic initial surface-charge pattern, whereas in 10% FBS and 50% serum, zeta-potential values gradually shifted toward a narrower range over time (Fig. 6C). Turbidity measurements showed minimal change in PBS for all groups, but OD₆₅₀ values increased gradually in 10% FBS and 50% serum, with the largest increase observed for CUR@HMSNs and lower values for CUR@HMSNs–PBAE and the HA-coated systems (Fig. 6D).

HMSNs–PBAE–HA, CUR@HMSNs, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE, and CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA were incubated at 37 °C in PBS, complete medium containing 10% FBS, or 50% serum. (A) Hydrodynamic diameter. (B) Polydispersity index (PDI). (C) Zeta potential. (D) Turbidity (OD₆₅₀). (E) Premature CUR leakage expressed as percentage of total loaded CUR. Premature CUR leakage showed the clearest formulation-dependent separation (Fig. 6E). In PBS, CUR@HMSNs exhibited progressive leakage reaching approximately 19% by 48 h, whereas CUR@HMSNs–PBAE reduced leakage to approximately 9–10%, and CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA remained below 5% at 48 h. This hierarchy became more pronounced in protein-rich media. CUR@HMSNs showed substantial leakage, reaching approximately 33% in 10% FBS and more than 40% in 50% serum by 48 h, whereas CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA remained below 12% even in 50% serum at the same time point. Together, these data show that HA gating preserved colloidal stability trends under protein exposure and most effectively limited premature CUR loss under increasingly stringent serum-challenge conditions.

Cellular association of the formulations was assessed in RA-FLS after 4 h incubation at CUR-equivalent 5 µM (Fig. 7). Confocal microscopy showed minimal baseline fluorescence in the cells-only control and detectable CUR-associated fluorescence in all CUR-loaded nanoparticle groups (Fig. 7A). The representative images showed a qualitatively stronger signal in the HA-functionalized group than in the non-HA comparators, while CD44 blockade reduced the apparent signal relative to the corresponding non-blocked CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA group.

Quantitative image analysis confirmed this overall pattern. CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA showed the highest mean cell-associated fluorescence intensity per cell (Fig. 7B) and the highest percentage of CUR-positive RA-FLS (Fig. 7C) under matched CUR-equivalent dosing. In both readouts, CD44 blockade significantly reduced the signal relative to the non-blocked HA-gated group. Together, these data support enhanced cellular association following HA functionalization and a CD44-associated contribution to this effect.

IC₅₀-based matched-dose evaluation of formulation effects on RA-FLS proliferation and inflammatory outputs

A free-CUR dose-response experiment in RA-FLS first established the working concentration for subsequent matched-dose comparisons. MTT analysis at 24 h showed a concentration-dependent reduction in metabolic viability, and nonlinear regression identified 5 µM as the IC₅₀ working concentration (Fig. 8A). Using this CUR-equivalent dose, formulation performance was then compared across groups. In the 24 h MTT assay, the blank carrier HMSNs–PBAE–HA did not differ from Vehicle, and free CUR and CUR@HMSNs produced comparable effects (ns vs. free) (Fig. 8B). In contrast, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE reduced viability further than free CUR (p < 0.05), and CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA produced the lowest viability among all CUR-containing groups, being lower than free CUR (p < 0.001) and lower than CUR@HMSNs–PBAE (p < 0.01).

At the transcript level, the blank carrier remained comparable to Vehicle across IL-6, PTGS2, NOS2, and MMP-3. At the protein level, secreted IL-6 and MMP-3 were likewise reduced by CUR-containing treatments, with the strongest suppression observed for CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA. In the manuscript text, the ELISA panel is described as showing significant reductions versus Vehicle and stronger inhibition by the HA-gated formulation than by free CUR for both IL-6 and MMP-3 (p < 0.01) (Fig. 8F).

Discussion

In this study, our research aims at solving the problem of enhancing CUR retention and efficiency in the protein-rich, oxidative, acidic, and activated fibroblast environment found within the joint space for rheumatoid arthritis nanodelivery applications [1, 4, 5, 17]. Among all the developed formulations tested in this study, the HA-protected and dual pH/ROS-responsive HMSN formulation showed the best performance profile across all the measurements. The final formulation of CUR@HMSNs-PBAE-HA exhibited well-preserved hollow structure, high payload capacity, minimal drug release at neutral conditions, increased release at acidic and oxidative stimuli, elevated binding affinity to RA-FLS cells, and comprehensive inhibition of proliferation-related, inflammatory, matrix-related, and oxidative biomarkers. Collectively, these results suggest that the superiority of the proposed HMSN-based drug delivery system is attributable to its design involving the incorporation of hollow mesoporous materials into an HA-based surface layer.

One advantage of the study is the biological results being corroborated by the internal consistency of the structure-function validation. The TEM, DLS, zeta potential, FTIR, TGA/DTG, and nitrogen sorption analysis demonstrates that the HMSN scaffold was structurally stable during the sequential steps of surface functionalization and drug loading; however, the interface chemistry changed as anticipated after each processing stage. The relevance of this issue is related to the fact that the effectiveness of mesoporous silica materials is determined primarily by the maintenance of the reservoir functionality on the inside, and the modification of surface chemistry to regulate permeability and biocompatibility [18–20]. From this perspective, the gradual decrease of pore accessibility after the gate layer of HA and loading of CUR is typical for the gated mesoporous silica delivery vehicles, and implies the formation of an actual barrier rather than random structural degradation. In this respect, the advantage of the HMSN platform in the present study lies not only in drug loading capacity, but in the ability to preserve a rigid reservoir during multistep gate construction and thereby couple internal cargo retention with externally engineered, condition-sensitive release behavior.

The release and HA-shedding datasets support a stimulus-responsive, condition-dependent behavior of the HA-gated formulation rather than a passive surface-coating effect. Across the tested environments, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA remained comparatively restrictive under neutral conditions, while showing higher release and greater HA detachment under acid-only or ROS-only challenge, with the greatest response observed under the combined pH 6.5 + H₂O₂ condition. A similar hierarchy was observed in the HA-shedding assay, and the inclusion of adsorption and covalent-bonding controls further indicates that the observed HA loss was not attributable solely to nonspecific desorption. Together, these findings are consistent with a condition-sensitive interfacial system whose barrier properties are relaxed more strongly under combined inflammatory-mimetic cues than under physiological-like neutral conditions. This interpretation is in line with previous reports showing that mesoporous silica carriers can regulate payload retention through surface-gating strategies, that ROS-responsive linkages can undergo oxidant-triggered remodeling, and that poly(β-amino ester)-containing interfaces can exhibit pH-sensitive destabilization under mildly acidic conditions [18, 21, 22].

The biological relevance of this behavior is supported by the rheumatoid synovial microenvironment, where extracellular acidification and oxidative stress are both established features of persistent inflammation. In that context, the stronger response observed under combined acidic/oxidative exposure suggests that the HA-gated construct is more closely aligned with the environmental stresses modeled in this study than ungated or partially engineered comparators. At the same time, the present assays define formulation-level differential responsiveness across the tested media, but they do not establish the kinetic basis of that behavior, identify cleavage products, or distinguish additive from synergistic trigger effects. The current evidence therefore supports condition-dependent HA-gated release and interfacial remodeling, while not extending to a quantitatively resolved cooperative or synergistic trigger mechanism [17, 18, 21].

Among the most relevant for translational aspects of this study, there is certainly a serum challenge dataset. Often enough, nanomaterials demonstrate impressive release and delivery properties when tested on low protein buffers, yet lose their functionality when introduced into a protein-rich environment [9, 10, 23, 24]. It is common knowledge today that the issue is due to the so-called biomolecular or protein corona formation. In the current study, all of the formulations demonstrated medium- and time-dependent modifications of their physicochemical properties while being incubated in 10% FBS and 50% serum, as it could be expected. What needs to be noted, though, is the smallest total drift and especially the lowest level of premature CUR leakage in CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA. This may very well play a role in interpreting further results at the cellular level. It is logical to assume that a greater degree of stability of the payload-carrier complex would provide more functional nanoparticles that can engage productively with cells.

The uptake-related findings indicate that HA functionalization increased cellular association of the formulation with RA-FLS and that CD44 contributed to this enhancement. Under matched CUR-equivalent conditions, CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA produced the strongest cell-associated CUR fluorescence and the highest percentage of CUR-positive cells, and both readouts were attenuated by CD44 blockade. These observations are biologically consistent with the documented expression of CD44 in rheumatoid synovium and cultured synovial fibroblasts and with the broader literature supporting HA-based design strategies for improving cellular interaction in CD44-relevant inflammatory systems [11, 25]. At the same time, the present assay format quantifies cell-associated fluorescence rather than directly resolving receptor-specific internalization, endocytic route, or intracellular trafficking fate. Accordingly, the current data support a CD44-associated enhancement of cellular interaction by the HA-functionalized surface, but do not establish exclusive receptor-mediated targeting or define the underlying internalization pathway [11, 25].

The functional data suggest that at the matched CUR-equivalent doses, the HA-gated system led to a wider and more pronounced inhibition of the activated RA-FLS phenotype compared to free CUR or the control nanostructures. This was apparent not only with regards to cell proliferation parameters such as viability or DNA content but also due to a simultaneous downregulation of IL-6, PTGS2, NOS2, and MMP-3, alongside the associated reduction in IL-6 and MMP-3 secretion. The observed effect, which spanned proliferation-related, inflammatory and matrix-related phenotypes, is especially noteworthy since it shows that the better delivery mechanism achieved not only higher concentration of CUR but also allowed for its effective action on different components of the RA-FLS phenotype, many of which may be intimately interconnected and serve as signaling pathways. Indeed, cytokine-mediated mechanisms play a crucial role in RA pathogenesis [26]. It is also consistent with previous research demonstrating anti-inflammatory action of CUR on synovial fibroblasts and arthritis [27] as well as metabolomic studies indicating CUR effects on FLS cells in RA patients [28].

Intracellular ROS results lend further credence to this same interpretation. Oxidative stress has been shown as a known causative factor in synovial inflammation and tissue damage in rheumatoid arthritis [4, 5]; therefore, the development of a drug delivery system which facilitates better intracellular inhibition of the oxidative burden could have relevance going beyond simple anti-proliferation effect. It can thus be seen from the current investigation that CUR@HMSNs-PBAE-HA exhibited the most effective inhibition of the oxidative burden measured in terms of reduced fluorescence intensity from DCFH-DA dye when exposed to both TNF-α-induced inflammation as well as an acute dose of hydrogen peroxide, whereas the control group was closer to the Vehicle treatment group. Nevertheless, caution needs to be exercised in the interpretation of the present results since the DCFH-DA dye used here measures cumulative fluorescence intensity from an oxidation-dependent signal, without any discrimination between different forms of ROS species [29].

Yet another promising aspect of the current dataset is the dissociation of effectiveness against RA-FLSs from tolerance in normal synoviocytes. As can be seen at CUR-equivalent doses of nanoformulations, especially CUR@HMSNs-PBAE-HA, the formulations exhibited significantly better tolerance in normal synoviocytes when compared to CUR. While these results cannot necessarily be transferred directly to living organisms at the moment, their importance lies in the fact that any delivery system for treating RA must help achieve better selectivity of cytotoxic effects in favor of affected cells rather than simply boost overall cell death rates. While the present dataset does not indicate real selectivity of activity in vivo, it does show how HA-dependent targeting might help shift exposure in favor of pathological fibroblasts at the expense of normal synoviocytes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were performed in vitro, and the current data therefore do not establish intra-articular retention, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, systemic safety, or therapeutic efficacy in rheumatoid arthritis. Second, the uptake results support CD44-associated interaction but do not fully define uptake pathways or intracellular trafficking. Third, the release and HA-shedding assays were conducted in simplified trigger environments and cannot reproduce the full biochemical complexity of arthritic synovial fluid and joint tissue. Fourth, the present study focuses on RA-FLS and does not address interactions with immune-cell populations that are central to RA pathogenesis. Finally, no histopathological or in vivo tissue-level evaluation was performed. Accordingly, the present work should be interpreted as a formulation-focused in vitro proof-of-concept study. Future studies should determine whether the observed serum-phase stability, cell-associated delivery, and anti-inflammatory/anti-oxidative effects translate into meaningful benefit in vivo.

Taken together, the present data indicate that the superior performance of CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA likely reflects the combined contribution of mesoporous reservoir function, condition-dependent HA-gated release behavior, and HA-associated cellular interaction, rather than any single formulation feature alone. This coordinated behavior was evident across both serum-phase and cellular readouts, suggesting that successful CUR nanodelivery in an RA-relevant microenvironment depends on preserving payload under protein challenge while enabling preferential activation under inflammatory stress conditions. Within the constraints of an in vitro system, the current findings therefore define a mechanistically coherent design framework for subsequent optimization and preclinical testing.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a dual pH/ROS-responsive, HA-gated hollow mesoporous silica nanoplatform for CUR delivery in an RA-relevant fibroblast model. The final CUR@HMSNs–PBAE–HA formulation preserved the hollow mesoporous architecture during multistep fabrication, achieved high CUR loading, reduced premature leakage under physiologic and serum-challenge conditions, and showed preferential activation under combined acidic and oxidative stimulation. At the cellular level, the HA-gated construct enhanced RA-FLS-associated CUR signal in a CD44-associated manner and produced the strongest overall suppression of proliferation-associated, inflammatory, matrix-associated, and oxidative readouts among the tested formulations. Collectively, these findings indicate that integrating a high-capacity hollow mesoporous reservoir with conditional HA gatekeeping can improve the in vitro functional profile of CUR delivery in an inflammatory synovial setting. Further in vivo studies will be required to determine whether these formulation-level and cellular advantages translate into therapeutic benefit in rheumatoid arthritis.
