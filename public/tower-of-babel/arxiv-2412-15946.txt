Securing the Management Plane in Intent-based Cellular Networks
Kashif Mehmood, Katina Kralevska, and Danilo Gligoroski

Abstract
Intent-based networking (IBN) is an emerging network management paradigm that allows automated closed-loop control and management of network devices and services. This paper addresses the security aspects in IBN systems by securing the management plane in IBN systems. We propose a novel security framework based on WireGuard that augments the existing standards to secure intent communication between intent stakeholders. The framework guarantees isolation through WireGuard tunnels and provides inherent authentication and access control mechanisms to avoid intrusion in IBN systems. Experimental results show the suitability and superiority of WireGuard compared to OpenVPN.

II
Related Work

II-A

IBN Architecture

IETF
defines the basic architecture for implementing intent processing and translation capabilities in traditional networks
[
8
]
. Clemm
et al.

[
9
]
present an abstract view of the intent lifecycle with mandatory processes such as intent definition, translation, and assurance. However, there is a lack of discussion on the management framework of intents in traditional networks and security requirements for different stakeholders.
TMForum
proposes an
intent management function (IMF)
as a basic building block for implementing the different parts of the intent lifecycle
[
1
]
. The processing and deployment of intents are based on the comprehension of available knowledge, decisions based on this knowledge, and the actions required to fulfill the objectives of the intent.

Similarly,
European telecommunications standards institute (ETSI)
proposes an implementation framework for
IBN
based on purely service-based architecture by treating each intent as a
management service (MnS)

[
6
]
. A
MnS
consumer generates the intent, whereas a
MnS
producer is responsible for fulfilling the intent objectives. For example,
CSC
generates an intent that must be fulfilled by the
CSP
, and
CSP
generates a more precise intent to be fulfilled by the
NOP
. The intents are categorized according to the scope as:

•

Intent-CSC
: Intent for CSP with specifications for the required service, for example,
“
Enable remote industrial control of Area A
.”

•

Intent-CSP
: Intent for NOP with specifications for the networking service, for example,
“
Provide a network capable of supporting industrial control between Area A and rest of the network
.”

•

Intent-NOP
: Intent for infrastructure or network equipment providers with information about specific network requirements, for example,
“
Provide a radio access network for Area A and core network capable of supporting ultra-reliable communications
.”

A
MnS
consumer owns a generated intent, and a
MnS
producer is the handler of the intent
[
10
]
. We utilize this terminology to develop a secure
IBN
framework.

IBN Security

Kim et al.

[
2
]
proposed Security-as-a-Service for cloud-based service orchestration using intents. Security intents are translated and mapped to relevant virtualized security functions available in the cloud platform. Similarly,
Wang et al.

[
3
]
utilize network slicing to isolate different security intents and map them onto slice-specific capabilities. The network slicing process handles the intents’ isolation and security along with the intents’ lifecycle.

The problem of secure design for
IBN

Ooi et al.

[
11
]
. The integration of security considerations within the intent lifecycle is investigated in different phases, from expression to deployment. Moreover,
Chowdhary et al.

[
12

propose a framework for defining a unified language model for security intent specification. However, the problem of securing the intent management plane is mainly unaddressed, leading to a considerable gap in understanding the security challenges.

III-C
1
Role of IBNSC

IBNSC
manages the intent interactions between stakeholders in an
IBN
system. This includes routing intents from consumers to producers modeled as individual management services within the management plane. However, the global state of the intent and its assurance is available to the
IBNSC
and utilized to measure the performance of the
IBN
stakeholders and
quality of service (QoS)
delivery.

IBNSC
also manages the security in
IBN
by initiating the necessary procedures for authentication, access control, and cryptographic key establishment with different intent stakeholders. In the proposed architecture,
IBNSC
acts as the authority that issues distinct authentication certificates to the intent stakeholders as
WG
peers for key exchange. We utilize specific security protocols for
Authentication
(Poly1305),
Key Exchange
(Curve25519),
Symmetric Encryption
(ChaCha20), and
Hashing
(BLAKE2s) to establish the
WG
encrypted tunnels for intent communication between intent stakeholders.

III-D
1
Step 1 - Key Generation

WG
uses asymmetric keys (static and ephemeral) and symmetric keys as implemented in Noise protocol
[
5
]
.
IBNSC
synchronizes public keys with its peers after the decentralized generation of asymmetric keys. This is established through a certificate authority at the
IBNSC
responsible for authentication and secure establishment of asymmetric keys between
IBNSC
and other intent stakeholders. The static keys
(s_{x},S_{x})
, where
x={I,R}
serve as the identity keys for authentication of different peers over a longer time. The ephemeral keys
(e_{x},E_{x})
, where
x={I,R}
are short-term session keys generated for each
WG
session during the handshake initiation stage by the initiator and responder, respectively. The symmetric keys are used for data encryption (intents in IBN) transferred through the
WG
encrypted tunnel.

III-D
2
Step 2 - Authenticated Key Exchange

The static public keys
S_{I},S_{R}
are exchanged using an
hypertext transfer protocol secure (HTTPS)
server running at the
IBNSC
after authentication is completed between
IBNSC
and other intent stakeholders (CSC, CSP, NOP, and VISP). This key exchange authenticates the intended peers of the
IBNSC
and establishes the
WG
handshake procedure.
The key exchange is performed using a modified Diffie-Hellman algorithm using Curve25519 cryptographic primitive.

3
Step 3 - Handshake and Session Establishment

Each intent stakeholder generates an asymmetric ephemeral key pair to initiate the handshake procedure with the
IBNSC
. For example,
CSC
sends a handshake initiation message that includes its ephemeral and static public keys in the encrypted message using a shared secret generated from its known information. The responder
IBNSC
sends the handshake response message with shared secrets using its ephemeral key pair and received keys. The handshake response message also includes the ephemeral public key of the
IBNSC
as shown in Algorithm
1

[
15
]
.

The key exchange consists of Diffie-Hellman exchanges and HMAC-Key Distribution Function (HKDF)
[
17
]
coupled with the hashing function BLAKE2s
[
18
]
. HKDF ensures strong encryption keys and uses the DH exchanges in the process.

III-D
4
Step 4 - Intent Communication

The
WG
encrypted tunnel is active only between the authenticated peers and the
IBNSC
. Intents can be communicated between different intent stakeholders via the
IBNSC
in a secure channel using the symmetric secret keys generated with the information shared between
WG
peers during the handshake procedure
[
15
]
.

IV
Performance Evaluation

IV-A

Experimental Setup

The proposed experimental testbed consists of different intent stakeholders (
CSC
,
CSP
,
NOP
,
VISP
) modeled as individual
virtual machines (VMs)
connected via a shared network using OpenStack
virtualized infrastructure manager (VIM)
. Each intent stakeholder is allocated 4 vCPUs, 16 GB of RAM, and 40GB of disk space. The connectivity between intent stakeholders and
IBNSC
is established as follows: one virtual tunnel interface each is implemented in
IBNSC
for connection using
WG
and OpenVPN. One physical interface is implemented to record performance benchmarks for
unencrypted
traffic without any established tunnels.

IV-B

Experimental Results

The measurement probes and monitors are installed in the
IBNSC
, and relevant metrics are recorded over several days using Prometheus. The experiments measure metrics for the resource (CPU and Memory) utilization of three individual interfaces: encrypted
WG
interfaces, encrypted OpenVPN interface, and unencrypted interface established between intent stakeholders. The round-trip-time (RTT) and total throughput between
IBNSC
and its peers are measured using iperf3.

IV-B
1
Throughput and RTT

The results show that the
WG
encrypted tunnel traffic provides higher throughput and lower RTTs than OpenVPN encrypted tunnel traffic between
IBNSC
and its peers as shown in Fig.
3
. The interface with
unencrypted
traffic achieves the highest useful data throughput between different intent stakeholders. However, it is followed closely by the
WG
encrypted tunnel statistics, outperforming the performance of OpenVPN encrypted tunnel interface. The exceptional performance of
WG
is attributed to several factors, including simplicity and lightweight design, cryptographic efficiency, stateless and optimized handshakes, and minimal overhead due to authentication and session establishment. These features provide the optimal security to communicate intents in
IBN
suitable for low-latency and high-reliability application services.

IV-B
2
Resource Utilization

The resource utilization for the intent stakeholder node and VPN protocols is depicted in Fig.
4
. The
node
level performance shows the overall resource utilization in individual intent stakeholders to serve as the baseline for the VPN services. The node level results also establish that the intent stakeholder VMs are not overloaded but operating under light load.

CSP has the highest observed load in the measurement duration with a peak CPU utilization of 9%.
WG
process in CSP has a maximum CPU utilization of around 1%, approximately lower than the observed CPU utilization of 2% for the OpenVPN process. A similar trend is observed, and
WG
outperforms OpenVPN by requiring fewer CPU cycles for its associated processes.

Memory usage by OpenVPN and
WG
follows an equivalent trend, with
WG
requiring less memory resources than OpenVPN. The observed memory utilization for
WG
remains below 0.01% for all intent stakeholders.

The functional aspects and security primitives used in
WG
via Noise_IK handshake pattern provide a simpler architecture with a 1-RTT handshake and low complexity, allowing minimal resource usage compared to other protocols
[
16
]
. Moreover, the low control overhead allows higher useful data throughput compared to OpenVPN encrypted tunnels between intent stakeholders.

V
Concusion

This paper investigates possible security vulnerabilities in the
IBN
management plane.
WG
is poised as a suitable candidate to enhance the security, integrity, and reliability amongst intent stakeholders. The experimental evaluation provides insights into the suitability of the
WG
protocol for
IBN
systems.
WG
achieved higher data rates and lower RTTs, corresponding to non-tunneled traffic. This efficiency is critical for ensuring low latency and high dependability services in IBN systems. The lightweight aspect of
WG
was demonstrated by resource utilization data, which showed much lower CPU and memory usage across all intent stakeholders. These results verify our approach of employing
WG
to secure the IBN management plane, providing a state-of-the-art solution that improves security without sacrificing performance.
