On the modeling of nonlinear wind-induced ice-drift ocean currents at the North Pole
Christian Puntini

Abstract
Starting from the governing equations for geophysical flows, by means of a thin-shell approximation and a tangent plane approximation, we derive the equations describing, at leading order, the nonlinear ice-drift flow for regions centered around the North Pole. An exact solution is derived in the material/Lagrangian formalism, describing a superposition of oscillations, a mean Ekman flow and a geostrophic current.

Introduction

The Arctic Ocean, centered over the North Pole, is a deep basin with maximum water depths exceeding
4000\,m
, surrounded by extensive continental shelves with maximum depths between
300\,m
and
400\,m
. This ocean basin connects to the Pacific Ocean via the shallow Bering Strait, with depths of less than
45\,m
, and to the Atlantic Ocean through the deeper Greenland Sea (maximum depths of
2600\,m
) and Denmark Strait (maximum depths of
650\,m
). Additionally, shallow channels (with maximum depths under
200\,m
) within the Canadian Arctic and Svalbard archipelagos provide further connections to the Atlantic. See Figure
1
.

Jakobsson et al. (2012)

Atmospheric circulation plays a dominant role in driving the surface circulation of the ocean. Anticyclonic (clockwise) atmospheric flow over the Arctic Ocean basin sets in motion the surface current circulation, represented by the Beaufort Sea Gyre and the Transpolar Drift. These surface currents transport Arctic Water, characterized by temperatures ranging from
-1.8^{\circ}C
to
3^{\circ}C
and salinities from
31\%
to
34\%
, across the Arctic Ocean basin. Most of this water is discharged into the North Atlantic Ocean via the East Greenland Current and the Baffin Current.

Sea ice is a dominant feature of polar seas, covering less than
10\%
of the world’s oceans, with approximately
40\%
of the world’s sea ice located within the Arctic Ocean basin.

The presence of sea ice in the Arctic Ocean and its marginal seas, including Baffin Bay, Hudson Bay, and the Barent Sea has several important effects on the physical oceanography of the region: the surface water temperature in this region remains near the freezing point dictated by its salinity, while the formation of sea ice expels salt in a process known as brine rejection, which increases surface water density and drives the thermohaline circulation. Additionally, winds transfer momentum from the atmosphere to the ocean surface through the sea ice cover, while the seasonal variable albedo of sea ice modulates the absorption and reflection of sunlight, affecting the energy exchange at the ocean surface and influencing the melting of the sea ice.

The northernmost part of the Arctic Ocean is entirely covered by a thin layer of sea ice, about
2\,m
thick during the winter, with summer sea-ice extent typically around one third of the winter extent. The winter maximum occurs in March, while the sea-ice minimum is in September (see
Timmermans and Marshall (2020)
and Figure
2
).

Given the evident importance of the Arctic Ocean, it is therefore crucial to understand the variety of phenomena characterizing this basin. The difficulty of physical observations (due to harsh meteorological conditions and to the presence of sea-ice) makes the theoretical approach based on the careful (asymptotic) analysis of the governing equations of fluid mechanics particularly important (and suitable) (see
Johnson (2022)
for a survey on this method). Such an approach was recently pursued for the study of the dynamics of the Beaufort Gyre (see e.g.
Constantin and Johnson (2023)
) and of the Transpolar Drift Current (see e.g.
Constantin and Johnson (2023)
,
Constantin and Johnson (2024)
or
Johnson (2024)
).

In this article, we study, adopting this aforementioned approach, the nonlinear flows under the sea-ice cover induced by wind (which exerts a stress on the ice sheet, in turn inducing a reduced stress on the water surface), extending the work in
Constantin (2022a)
and
Constantin (2022b)
, which was limited to regions outside the Amundsen Basin (where the North Pole is located, thus avoiding the singularity issue of the convergence of the meridians at the pole linked to the use of classical spherical coordinates). To avoid this inconvenience, we will adopt the rotated spherical coordinates (having the singularity at the Equator) developed in
Constantin and Johnson (2023)
and
Constantin and Johnson (2024)
.

At latitudes away from the equator, the oceanic flow below a depth of about
100\,m
typically features a geostrophic balance. However, near the surface, wind-driven turbulence transfers momentum into the ocean, generating currents that overlay the underlying geostrophic flow. The resulting force balance in these currents involves the Coriolis force and the frictional forces induced by the wind. This region, where these frictional effects are significant, is known as the Ekman layer, named after V. W. Ekman, who first developed a mathematical model, in 1905
Ekman (1905)
, to describe the behavior of wind-driven surface currents in order to explain the observations made by F. Nansen during the 1893-1896 Arctic expedition on board of the Fram vessel that sea ice drifts somewhat to the right of the prevailing wind direction (see
Jenkins and Bye (2006)
for a summary of Ekman’s work).

Ekman’s explicit solution is applicable to a uniform, steady wind blowing over a homogeneous ocean with a constant eddy viscosity. In this scenario: the surface flow is oriented at a
45^{\circ}
angle to the wind, with the direction being to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere, and as one dives deeper into the water, the current speed gradually decreases, and the direction of flow rotates further away from the wind, following a spiral pattern. On average, the wind-driven motion, known as Ekman transport, occurs at a right angle to the wind direction, to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere.
These conclusions hold for cases with depth-dependent eddy viscosity (see
Constantin (2020)
), though the first point only qualitatively matches observations, with deflection angles observed in the range of
10^{\circ}
to
75^{\circ}
(see
Roberti (2022)
and the references therein). The discrepancy between the predicted and observed deflection angles can be mostly attributed to the assumption of constant vertical eddy viscosity in the original model (see
Roberti (2022)
for a three-valued piecewise-constant eddy viscosity, or
Shrira and Almelah (2020)
for depth and time dependent eddy viscosity).

As eddy viscosity is just a simple model to “close” the turbulence problem, based on the hypothesis that Reynolds stresses are proportional to the mean velocity gradient and representing no physical characteristic of the fluid (but being function of flow conditions), there were numerous attempts to improve the Ekman solution by choosing a better turbulence model (see
Soloviev and Lukas (2014)
). We work with the assumption of constant eddy viscosity. The exploration of more intricate models will be the object of subsequent investigations.

Extending
Constantin (2022a)
and
Constantin (2022b)
, we provide an exact description of the nonlinear flow, which consists of oscillations superimposed on an Ekman mean spiraling current. However, in our case, the stress on the ocean surface is not directly generated by the wind but rather by the ice motion, whereas in
Constantin (2022a)
and
Constantin (2022b)
, the stress is induced by the wind, ice, or a combination of both. Differently from
Constantin (2022a)
and
Constantin (2022b)
our solution also features a background geostrophic flow, due to the presence of the Transpolar Drift Current.

The paper is structured as follows: in Section
2
, after recalling the set of rotated spherical coordinates developed in
Constantin and Johnson (2023)
suitable for the analysis of fluid flows in regions centered around the poles (where classical spherical coordinates fail) we derive the governing equations of our interest, starting from the Navier-Stokes and continuity equations via a thin-shell approximation coupled with a tangent plane approximation. We also describe the boundary conditions associated with the presence of ice and its interaction with the water beneath it.

In Section
3
we present the solution for the ice-drift nonlinear flow, obtained via a Lagrangian formulation of the flow variables. Such a solution features oscillations superimposed on an Ekman-type mean spiraling current and on the background geostrophic component of the Transpolar Drift Current. Moreover, it will be shown that the boundary conditions uniquely determine the surface
current, therefore explaining how, given accurate field data about the ice motion, the mean surface current is connected to the ice-sea stress.

Finally, we conclude with a discussion of the results in Section
4
.

In Appendix
5.1
we review the basics of differential geometry, with a focus on orthogonal curvilinear coordinate systems, in order to provide explicit expressions of the differential operators used in the subsequent derivation of the Navier-Stokes and continuity equations in their most general form in classical spherical coordinates (Appendix
5.2
) and rotated spherical coordinates (Appendix
5.3
), suitable for the study of the geophysical fluid dynamics of oceans and of the atmosphere. Only two assumptions will be made: the fluid is Newtonian and the viscosity varies only with depth.

Typically, the motion of a fluid is studied by adopting the so-called Eulerian approach, in which the motion of a fluid is described by observing it from a fixed reference frame in space. and
\mathcal{D}_{2}=d_{2}(0)+m\sin(\alpha)
represent the non-dimensional ice-drift non-geostrophic surface current. Writing

Re^{i\theta}=(u_{i}-\mathfrak{u}_{g}-\mathcal{D}_{1})+i(v_{i}-\mathfrak{v}_{g}-\mathcal{D}_{2}),

(62)

(
61
) can be rewritten as

\beta\left[(\mathcal{D}_{1}-\mathcal{D}_{2})+i(\mathcal{D}_{1}+\mathcal{D}_{2})\right]=R^{2}e^{i\theta},

(63)

or equivalently, as

R^{2}e^{i\theta}+\beta(1+i)Re^{i\theta}=\zeta,

(64)

with
\beta=\frac{\lambda A_{V}}{C_{i}}>0
, for the unknowns
R\geq 0
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, where

\zeta=\beta[(u_{i}-\mathfrak{u}_{g}-v_{i}+\mathfrak{v}_{g})+i(u_{i}-\mathfrak{u}_{g}+v_{i}-\mathfrak{u}_{g})]\in\mathbb{C}.

(65)

As the ice moves faster than the Trasnpolar Drift Current (see
Constantin and Johnson (2024)
),
\zeta\not=0
. We therefore infer from (
64
) that
R\not=0
and

e^{i\theta}=\frac{\zeta}{R[R+\beta(1+i)]}.

(66)

Multiplying (
66

\mathcal{P}(R)=R^{4}+2\beta R^{3}+2\beta^{2}R^{2}-|\zeta|^{2}=0,

(67)

and, since
\mathcal{P}(0)<0

\lim_{R\rightarrow\pm\infty}\mathcal{P}(R)=+\infty
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}(R)>0

\mathcal{P}
is strictly convex, ensuring that there is one solution
R>0
of (
67

We remark that our analysis shows that the boundary condition (
31

d(0)
knowing the sea-ice velocity and the TDC velocity
\mathfrak{U}_{TDC}
at the surface.

Writing
(u_{i}-\mathfrak{u}_{g})+i(v_{i}-\mathfrak{v}_{g})=\mathcal{U}e^{i\phi}
with
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
and
\mathcal{U}>0
, and
\mathcal{D}_{1}+i\mathcal{D}_{2}=\mathcal{D}e^{i\psi}
with
\psi\in[0,2\pi)
and
\mathcal{D}>0
,
R=|\mathcal{U}e^{i\phi}-\mathcal{D}e^{i\psi}|>0
and (
61
) can be rewritten as

\mathcal{U}e^{i(\phi-\psi)}=\mathcal{D}+\beta\frac{(1+i)\mathcal{D}}{|\mathcal{U}e^{i\phi}-\mathcal{D}e^{i\psi}|}.

(68)

As the right-hand side of (
68
) is a complex number with real part bigger than the (positive) imaginary part, it follows that
0<\phi-\psi<\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{4}}
.

Writing the ice velocity
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{ice}}=u_{i}+i\,v_{i}
as

\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{ice}}=\mathfrak{U}_{TDC}+\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{rel}}=\mathfrak{U}_{g}+(m\cos\alpha+im\sin\alpha)+\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{rel}},

(69)

where
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{rel}}
is the vector of the ice velocity relative to the TDC, we have that

\mathcal{U}e^{i\phi}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{ice}}-\mathfrak{U}_{g}=(m\cos\alpha+im\sin\alpha)+\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{rel}}.

(70)

As we have defined
\mathcal{D}e^{i\psi}
as

\mathcal{D}e^{i\psi}=\left(d_{1}(0)+id_{2}(0)\right)+\left(m\cos(\alpha)+im\sin(\alpha)\right),

(71)

the fact that
0<\phi-\psi<\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{4}}
shows that the surface current vector
\left(d_{1}(0)+id_{2}(0)\right)
is directed to the right of the ice moving relatively to the TDC, forming an angle of less than
45^{\circ}
with its path.

Finally, observe that, for any given
k>0
, the horizontal particle path given by (
35
) coupled with (
58
) is

\displaystyle x(t;a,b,z)+iy(t;a,b,z)=(a+ib)+\bigg[(d(0)+me^{i\alpha})e^{(1+i)\lambda z}+

(72)

\displaystyle+\mathfrak{U}_{g}\bigg]t+\frac{1}{k}e^{k(b+z)}e^{i\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+k(a-z)-(f+2A_{V}k^{2})t\right)},

representing trochoids, and is a solution of (
19
) and (
21
).

The mean-drift current, over a period
T=\frac{2\pi}{ck}=\frac{2\pi}{f+2A_{V}k^{2}}
,

\displaystyle\langle u+iv\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left(u(t;a,b,z)+iv(t;a,b,z)\right)dt=

(73)

\displaystyle=(d(0)+me^{i\alpha})e^{(1+i)\lambda z}+\mathfrak{U}_{g}

is the superposition of a classical Ekman spiral and a geostrophic current, while the oscillatory perturbation of the current

\frac{1}{k}e^{k(b+z)}e^{i\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+k(a-z)-(f+2A_{V}k^{2})t\right)}

(74)

is essentially inertial, as
A_{V}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
k<<1
gives

f+2A_{V}k^{2}\approx f

(75)

for the frequency. Averaging over the depth the Ekman component of (

) gives the depth-averaged mean-drift Ekman of the solution (

), also known as (time-averaged) Ekman trasport,

\mathcal{I}_{Ek}:=\int_{-\infty}^{0}(d(0)+me^{i\alpha})e^{(1+i)\lambda z}\,dz=\frac{(d(0)+me^{i\alpha})}{\lambda\sqrt{2}}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}},

(76)

which is at
45^{\circ}
to the right of the Ekman surface current
d(0)+me^{i\alpha}
. Given that
d(0)+me^{i\alpha}
is directed to the right of
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{ice}}-\mathfrak{U}_{g}
(due to (

)), as a consequence we have that
\mathcal{I}_{Ek}
is directed to the right of
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{ice}}-\mathfrak{U}_{g}
with an angle between
45^{\circ}
and
90^{\circ}
.

Observe that, for every fixed value of
z
, the particle path (
72
) represents a trochoid, the only known solution for the
2D
incompressible Euler equations with free boundary discovered by Gerstner (see
Milne-Thomson (1938)
and
Abrashkin and
Pelinovsky (2022)
). Therefore, the solution (

) of the governing equation for arctic ice-drift flow represents a superposition of an Ekman spiral, a Gerstner-type solution and a geostrophic current.

4
Discussion

Our analysis provided an explicit solution to the nonlinear governing equations describing the leading-order dynamics of ice-drift flows in a region centered around the North Pole. Such governing equations have been obtained from the Navier-Stokes and continuity equations adopting a thin-shell approximation coupled with a tangent plane approximation. The solution, in the Lagrangian formalism, highlights the superposition of near-inertial oscillations on a mean flow, the latter being the sum of an Ekman spiral (partially driven by the Transpolar Drift Current’s Ekman-type spiral and partially driven by ice stress) and of the geostrophic component of the TDC. This solution extends the one found in
Constantin (2022a)
and
Constantin (2022b)
for regions outside the Amundsen Basin, where the Transpolar Drift Current is not present:

\tilde{u}+i\tilde{v}=(\tilde{d}_{1}(0)+i\tilde{d}_{2}(0))e^{(1+i)\lambda z}+ce^{k(b+z)}e^{ik(a-z-ct)}.

(77)

In (
77
), the entirety of the “forcing” velocity (namely the velocity of ice or that of the wind, or a combination of them, as regions partially covered by ice where considered in
Constantin (2022a)
and
Constantin (2022b)
) induced the Ekman spiral
(\tilde{d}_{1}(0)+i\tilde{d}_{2}(0))e^{(1+i)\lambda z}
. Instead in our analysis, it is shown that only a fraction of the ice-velocity (namely the velocity of ice with respect of the TDC) acts as a forcing for the ice-drift component of the Ekman spiral
({d}_{1}(0)+i{d}_{2}(0))e^{(1+i)\lambda z}
in

\displaystyle u+iv=(d_{1}(0)+m\cos(\alpha))e^{(1+i)\lambda z}+i(d_{2}(0)+m\sin(\alpha))e^{(1+i)\lambda z}+

(78)

\displaystyle+ce^{k(b+z)}e^{ik(a-z-ct)}+(\mathfrak{u}_{g}+i\mathfrak{v}_{g}),

thus implying that the amplitude of the nonlinear ice-drift current at the North Pole is smaller that in other arctic regions (for the same surface stress), due to the presence of the Transpolar Drift Current.

Moreover, note that the coordinates used in
Constantin (2022a)
and
Constantin (2022b)
are the standard Cartesian coordinates for the tangent plane approximation, based on the classical spherical coordinates (
x
pointing to East and
y
pointing to North), while our analysis is built upon the use of the “new” rotated spherical coordinates developed in
Constantin and Johnson (2023)
, and here reviewed in Appendix
5.3
. In spite of this difference, the qualitative properties of the solution we found in our analysis and the ones in
Constantin (2022a)
match.

Finally, our solution (
36
) shows an interesting feature: in (
34
) we described the Ekman-type part of the Transpolar Drift Current as

me^{i\alpha}e^{(1+i)sz}

(79)

with
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, but in the nonlinear solution (
36
) and subsequently in (
58
) instead of
s
, the exponent features the presence of
\lambda
(namely the inverse of the Ekman depth), as a result of the pressure gradient being balanced by the geostrophic current in (
19
), leading to the system of ODEs (
50
). Consequently, if
s\not=\lambda
we can conclude that only the value of the Ekman-type current of the TDC at the surface influenced the Ekman spiral of the nonlinear ice-drift flow. On the other hand, if the Ekman-type current of the TDC is a “pure” Ekman spiral, i.e.
s=\lambda
, the entirety of it is present in the solution. This result is a consequence of the fact that
\left(me^{\lambda z}\cos(\lambda z+\alpha),\ me^{\lambda z}\sin(\lambda z+\alpha)\right)
is a solution of the “classical” system of differential equations for the Ekman spiral (see e.g.
Kundu (1990)
or
Pedlosky (1987)
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -f\mathsf{v}=A_{V}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathsf{u}}{\partial z^{2}},\\
f\mathsf{u}=A_{V}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathsf{v}}{\partial z^{2}},\end{aligned}\right.

(80)

recalling that
\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{f}{2A_{V}}}
. This, applied to (
48
), leads (
50
) to became

\left\{\begin{aligned} 0&=fd_{2}(z)+A_{V}d_{1}^{\prime\prime}(z)+\left(fc+2A_{V}k^{2}c-kc^{2}\right)e^{k(b+z)}\sin(\xi),\\
0&=-fd_{1}(z)+A_{V}d_{2}^{\prime\prime}(z)-\left(fc+2A_{V}k^{2}c-kc^{2}\right)e^{k(b+z)}\cos(\xi),\end{aligned}\right.

(81)

whose solution is
d(z)=(d_{1}(0)e^{(1+i)\lambda z},d_{2}(0)e^{(1+i)\lambda z})
, provided the dispersion relation (
52
). Inserting such a solution into (
35
) gives exactly the same expression for the solution to (
19
) and (
21
), namely

\left\{\begin{aligned} x(t;a,b,z)&=a+\left[\left(d_{1}(0)+m\cos(\alpha)\right)e^{(1+i)\lambda z}+\mathfrak{u}_{g}\right]t-\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt-\frac{1}{k}e^{k(b+z)}\sin(k(a-z-ct)),\\
y(t;a,b,z)&=b+\left[\left(d_{2}(0)+m\sin(\alpha)\right)e^{(1+i)\lambda z}+\mathfrak{v}_{g}\right]t+\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt+\frac{1}{k}e^{k(b+z)}\cos(k(a-z-ct)).\end{aligned}\right.

(82)

Therefore we can state that only if
s=\lambda
, the whole Ekman component of the TDC is present in the solution of the nonlinear ice-drift problem.

The structure of (
50
) also shows that, if a non-geostrophic constant current is present, it is forced to “transform” into an Ekman spiral, provided that the pressure perturbation
\hat{p}
or its gradient to be zero. More precisely, assuming

\left\{\begin{aligned} x(t;a,b,z)=a+d_{1}(z)t-\frac{1}{k}e^{k(b+z)}\sin(k(a-z-ct))+\overline{\mathfrak{u}}t\\
y(t;a,b,z)=b+d_{2}(z)t+\frac{1}{k}e^{k(b+z)}\cos(k(a-z-ct))+\overline{\mathfrak{v}}t\end{aligned}\right.

(83)

with
\overline{\mathfrak{u}},\overline{\mathfrak{v}}
representing a non-geostrophic constant current. We have omitted the TDC for simplicity. Given the dispersion relation (
52
) and repeating the same arguments as in the previous section and assuming again the pressure perturbation
\hat{p}
or its gradient to be zero, we get the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &A_{V}d_{1}^{\prime\prime}(z)+fd_{2}(z)=-f\overline{\mathfrak{v}},\\
&A_{V}d_{2}^{\prime\prime}(z)-fd_{1}(z)=f\overline{\mathfrak{u}},\end{aligned}\right.

(84)

which, in complex-variable notation,

d(z)=d_{1}(z)+id_{2}(z)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma:=-\frac{f}{A_{V}}\overline{\mathfrak{v}}+i\frac{f}{A_{V}}\overline{\mathfrak{u}}

(85)

is equivalent to

d^{\prime\prime}(z)-i\frac{f}{A_{V}}d(z)=\gamma.

(86)

The solution to (
86
), recalling that ice-drift currents are insignificant at great depth, is

d(z)=[d(0)+\overline{\mathfrak{u}}+i\overline{\mathfrak{v}}]e^{(1+i)\lambda z}-\overline{\mathfrak{u}}-i\overline{\mathfrak{v}},

(87)

where
d(0)\in\mathbb{C}
is the ice-drift current at the surface. Therefore (
83
) becomes

\left\{\begin{aligned} x(t;a,b,z)=a+[d_{1}(0)+\overline{\mathfrak{u}}]e^{(1+i)\lambda z}t-\frac{1}{k}e^{k(b+z)}\sin(k(a-z-ct)),\\
y(t;a,b,z)=b+[d_{2}(0)+\overline{\mathfrak{v}}]e^{(1+i)\lambda z}t+\frac{1}{k}e^{k(b+z)}\cos(k(a-z-ct)),\end{aligned}\right.

(88)

showing that the non-geostrophic current
\overline{\mathfrak{u}},\overline{\mathfrak{v}}
is forced to “transform” into an Ekman spiral.

5
Full derivation of the Navier-Stokes equations

This appendix is devoted to the derivation of the Navier-Stokes equation in spherical coordinates for geophysical flows (atmospheric or oceanic) in its most general setting, correcting the ones in the literature, e.g. in
Constantin and Johnson (2023)
or
Constantin and Johnson (2024)
, in order to provide the most complete formulation of the governing equations.

The only assumption will be that the vertical and horizontal eddy viscosities
\mu_{V}
and
\mu_{H}
vary with depth but not with respect to the horizontal coordinates, which is physically reasonable since, in most cases, the scales on which the fluid flow is analyzed are much smaller than those on which eddy viscosities vary horizontally. However, extending this to account for viscosities that vary with horizontal coordinates will be almost straightforward, at the price of making the equations more intricate.

Before proceeding with the derivation, we briefly recall the formulae for the differential operators in orthogonal curvilinear coordinates used in this paper, namely the gradient and the Laplacian of a scalar field, the divergence of a vector field and of a second order tensor, the gradient of a vector field and the advection operator
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla
. We refer to
Arfken et al. (1985)
,
Jog (2015)
, and
Spiegel et al. (2009)
for a more complete exposition.

5.1
Differential Operators in Orthogonal Curvilinear Coordinates

In this subsection, we will not make the distinction between physical variables (previously denoted with
′
) and non-dimensional variables. For simplicity, everything will be denoted without primes.

Let us start by setting
(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3})
a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and Cartesian coordinates
(x,y,z)
. A curvilinear coordinate system
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is given by the scalar functions

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{1}=u_{1}(x,y,z),\\
u_{2}=u_{2}(x,y,z),\\
u_{3}=u_{3}(x,y,z),\end{array}\right.\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x=x(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}),\\
y=y(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}),\\
z=z(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}),\\
\end{array}\right.

(89)

giving the so-called change of coordinates and the inverse change of coordinates. The functions in (
89
) are assumed to be single-valued and to have continuous derivatives so that there is a one-to-one correspondence between
(x,y,z)
and
(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
.

Let
\mathfrak{r}=x\,\mathbf{e}_{1}+y\,\mathbf{e}_{2}+z\,\mathbf{e}_{3}
be the position vector of a given point
P
, which, due to (
89
) can be written as
\mathfrak{r}=x(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\mathbf{e}_{1}+y(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\mathbf{e}_{2}+z(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\mathbf{e}_{3}
, namely
\mathfrak{r}=\mathfrak{r}(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
, and let

\mathsf{h}_{i}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial u_{i}}\right|,

(90)

denote the scale factors (also known as metric coefficients or Lamé coefficients). The associated unit tangent vectors are given by
\mathfrak{e}_{i}=\nicefrac{{\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial u_{i}}}}{{|\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial u_{i}}|}}=\nicefrac{{\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial u_{i}}}}{{\mathsf{h}_{i}}}
, for
i=1,2,3
.

The aforementioned formulae for the differential operators are summarized below.

•

Gradient and Laplacian of a scalar field: given a scalar field
f(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
, its gradient is given by

\nabla f=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{i}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{i}}{\mathfrak{e}_{i}}

(91)

and its Laplacian by

\Delta f=\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{1}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{h}_{2}\mathsf{h}_{3}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{1}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{2}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{3}}{\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{2}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{3}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{2}}{\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{3}}\right)\right].

(92)

•

Divergence and gradient of a vector field: given a vector field
V(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})=\left(V_{1}(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}),V_{2}(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}),V_{3}(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\right)
with respect to the base
\mathfrak{e}_{i}
the divergence of
V
is a scalar given by

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot V=\sum_{i=1,2,3}\frac{\mathsf{h}_{i}^{2}}{\mathsf{h}}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\mathsf{h}}{\mathsf{h}_{i}}V_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}}\sum_{i=1,2,3}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{h}}{\mathsf{h}_{i}}V_{i}\right),

(93)

while its gradient, which is a second-order tensor (see
Jog (2015)
), is given by the relation
(\nabla V)^{T}=\nabla\otimes V
where

\nabla\otimes V=\sum_{i=1,2,3}\frac{\mathfrak{e}_{i}}{\mathsf{h}_{i}}\frac{\partial\left(\sum_{j}V_{j}\mathfrak{e}_{j}\right)}{\partial u_{i}}.

(94)

•

Advection operator
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla
: in previous sections, we wrote the velocity vector field
\boldsymbol{u}
as
\boldsymbol{u}=(u,v,w)
. However, this notation in the following computations could be confusing, therefore let us write
\boldsymbol{u}=(\mathfrak{U}_{1},\mathfrak{U}_{2},\mathfrak{U}_{3})
. Given a vector field
V
, the components of the advection operator
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla
acting on a vector field
V
are given by

\displaystyle\left\{(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)V\right\}_{i}=\sum_{j=1,2,3}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{U_{j}}}{\mathsf{h}_{j}}\frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial u_{j}}-\frac{\mathfrak{U_{j}}V_{j}}{\mathsf{h}_{i}\mathsf{h}_{j}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{j}}{\partial u_{i}}+\frac{\mathfrak{U_{i}}V_{j}}{\mathsf{h}_{i}\mathsf{h}_{j}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{i}}{\partial u_{j}}\right).

(95)

•

Divergence of a second-order tensor: the formula for the divergence of a second-order tensor
\mathbf{A}=A^{ij}\mathfrak{e}_{i}\otimes\mathfrak{e}_{j}
is given by (see
Jog (2015)
)

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}=\sum_{i,j}\left(\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{j}}\frac{\partial A^{ij}}{\partial u_{j}}\mathfrak{e}_{j}+A^{ij}\left(\frac{\omega_{j}}{\mathsf{h}_{j}}\times\mathfrak{e}_{i}\right)+\sum_{k}A^{ij}\left[\frac{\omega_{k}}{\mathsf{h}_{k}}\cdot(\mathfrak{e}_{i}\times\mathfrak{e}_{k})\right]\mathfrak{e}_{i}\right),

(96)

which, written component by component, reads as

\displaystyle(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A})_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{1}}\frac{\partial A^{11}}{\partial u_{1}}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial A^{12}}{\partial u_{2}}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial A^{13}}{\partial u_{3}}+

(97)

\displaystyle+\frac{A^{13}+A^{31}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{1}}{\partial u_{3}}+\frac{A^{12}+A^{21}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{1}}{\partial u_{2}}+\frac{A^{13}}{\mathsf{h}_{2}\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{2}}{\partial u_{3}}

\displaystyle+\frac{A^{11}-A^{22}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{2}}{\partial u_{1}}+\frac{A^{12}}{\mathsf{h}_{3}\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{3}}{\partial u_{2}}-\frac{A^{33}-A^{11}}{\mathsf{h}_{3}\mathsf{h}_{1}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{3}}{\partial u_{1}}

\displaystyle(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A})_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{1}}\frac{\partial A^{21}}{\partial u_{1}}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial A^{22}}{\partial u_{2}}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial A^{23}}{\partial u_{3}}+

\displaystyle+\frac{A^{23}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{1}}{\partial u_{3}}+\frac{A^{22}-A^{11}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{1}}{\partial u_{2}}+\frac{A^{23}+A^{32}}{\mathsf{h}_{2}\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{2}}{\partial u_{3}}+

\displaystyle+\frac{A^{12}+A^{21}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{2}}{\partial u_{1}}+\frac{A^{22}-A^{33}}{\mathsf{h}_{3}\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{3}}{\partial u_{2}}+\frac{A^{21}}{\mathsf{h}_{3}\mathsf{h}_{1}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{3}}{\partial u_{1}}

\displaystyle(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A})_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{1}}\frac{\partial A^{31}}{\partial u_{1}}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial A^{32}}{\partial u_{2}}+\frac{1}{\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial A^{33}}{\partial u_{3}}+

\displaystyle+\frac{A^{33}-A^{11}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{1}}{\partial u_{3}}+\frac{A^{32}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{1}}{\partial u_{2}}+\frac{A^{33}-A^{22}}{\mathsf{h}_{2}\mathsf{h}_{3}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{2}}{\partial u_{3}}+

\displaystyle+\frac{A^{31}}{\mathsf{h}_{1}\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{2}}{\partial u_{1}}+\frac{A^{23}+A^{32}}{\mathsf{h}_{3}\mathsf{h}_{2}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{3}}{\partial u_{2}}+\frac{A^{13}+A^{31}}{\mathsf{h}_{3}\mathsf{h}_{1}}\frac{\partial\mathsf{h}_{3}}{\partial u_{1}}.

Now we turn to the derivation of the equations of motion.

Newton’s second law, relating the acceleration to the net force at a point, sometimes also called Cauchy’s equation of motion in the context of continuum mechanics, in vectorial (coordinate-free) form is given by
Kundu (1990)
:

\rho^{\prime}\frac{D\mathbf{u}^{\prime}}{Dt^{\prime}}=\rho^{\prime}\mathbf{g}^{\prime}+\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime},

(98)

while the continuity equation is

\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}\frac{D\rho^{\prime}}{Dt^{\prime}}+\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{u^{\prime}}=0,

(99)

where
t^{\prime}
is the time,
\rho^{\prime}
is the fluid density,
\frac{D}{Dt^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}+\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla^{\prime}
is the advective derivative,
\mathbf{g}^{\prime}
is the gravity acceleration (its modulus being approximately
9.81\,m\,s^{-2}
at the surface of the Earth) and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}
is the stress tensor (a second-order tensor with nine components that describes the internal forces within a solid body under external loads. It comprises three normal stresses and six shear stresses). Recall that we use primes to represent dimensional (physical) variables.

In the standard Cartesian coordinates system
(x,y,z)
(with respect to the basis
(\mathbf{e_{1}},\mathbf{e_{2}},\mathbf{e_{3}})
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
), the stress tensor’s components, for

5.2
Classical Spherical Coordinates

A coordinate system suitable for the Earth, with the poles
excluded, is the system of (right-handed) spherical coordinates
(\varphi,\theta,r^{\prime})
:
r^{\prime}
is the distance from the Earth’s center,
\theta\in[-\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}]
is the angle of latitude and
\varphi\in[0,2\pi]
is the angle of longitude. The unit vectors in this system
are
(\mathbf{e}_{\varphi},\mathbf{e}_{\theta},\mathbf{e}_{r})
and the corresponding velocity components are
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}=(u^{\prime},v^{\prime},w^{\prime})
:
\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}
points from West to East,
\mathbf{e}_{\theta}
from South to North and
\mathbf{e}_{r}
points upwards. See Figure
6
.
The
(\varphi,\theta,r^{\prime})
-system is associated with a point fixed on the sphere (other than at the
two poles where the unit vectors are not well-defined) which is rotating about its polar axis (with
constant angular speed
\Omega^{\prime}\approx 7.29\cdot 10^{-5}\,s^{-1}
).

The change of coordinates from spherical to Cartesian and vice-versa is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x^{\prime}=r^{\prime}\cos\theta\cos\varphi,\\
y^{\prime}=r^{\prime}\cos\theta\sin\varphi,\\
z^{\prime}=r^{\prime}\sin\theta,\end{array}\right.\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y^{\prime}}{x^{\prime}}\right),\\
\theta=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{z^{\prime}}{\sqrt{x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}}}\right),\\
r^{\prime}=\sqrt{x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}}.\end{array}\right.

(105)

Classical spherical coordinate system. Image from
Constantin and Johnson (2023)
. CC BY 4.0 (
http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0
)

and the unit tangent vectors are

\mathbf{e_{\varphi}}=\frac{\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial\varphi}}{\left|\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial\varphi}\right|},\qquad\mathbf{e_{\theta}}=\frac{\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial\theta}}{\left|\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial\theta}\right|},\qquad\mathbf{e}_{r}=\frac{\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial r}}{\left|\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial r}\right|},

(106)

where
\mathfrak{r}=x(\varphi,\theta,r)\mathbf{e}_{1}+y(\varphi,\theta,r)\mathbf{e}_{2}+z(\varphi,\theta,r)\mathbf{e}_{3}
,
leading to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{e_{\varphi}}=-\sin\varphi\mathbf{e_{1}}+\cos\varphi\mathbf{e_{2}},\\
\mathbf{e_{\theta}}=-\cos\varphi\sin\theta\mathbf{e_{1}}-\sin\varphi\sin\theta\mathbf{e_{2}}+\cos\theta\mathbf{e_{3}},\\
\mathbf{e}_{r}=\cos\varphi\cos\theta\mathbf{e_{1}}+\sin\varphi\cos\theta\mathbf{e_{2}}+\sin\theta\mathbf{e_{3}},\end{array}\right.

(107)

and

\displaystyle\mathsf{h}_{\varphi}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial\varphi}\right|=r^{\prime}\cos\theta,\qquad\mathsf{h}_{\theta}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial\theta}\right|=r^{\prime},\qquad\mathsf{h}_{r^{\prime}}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathfrak{r}}{\partial r}\right|=1,

(108)

\displaystyle\mathsf{h}:=\mathsf{h}_{\varphi}\mathsf{h}_{\theta}\mathsf{h}_{r^{\prime}}=r^{\prime 2}\cos\theta.

5.3
Rotated Spherical Coordinates

Classical spherical coordinates present two polar singularities, where longitude is undefined at the North and South Poles. This issue reflects the fundamental impossibility of defining global coordinates on a sphere: singularities, where the coordinate system either fails or degenerates, are inevitable. According to the Poincaré–Hopf index theorem, continuous vector fields free of singularities can exist only on manifolds with a zero Euler characteristic. Since the sphere has an Euler characteristic of 2, there cannot exist a continuous, nonvanishing tangent vector field on it. This result is commonly referred to as the “hairy ball theorem” (see
Burns and Gidea (2005)
).

The convergence of meridians at the poles illustrates this limitation in spherical coordinates. However, the problem can be resolved by rotating the coordinate system so that both singularities are relocated to the Equator. This adjustment enables a smooth parameterization for the entire region north (or south) of the Equator. In this section we review the construction of this rotated spherical coordinates developed in
Constantin and Johnson (2023)
. See also
Constantin and Johnson (2024)
.

(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3})
