Synthesis of adaptive 15N-nitrosyl-Co7 nanocluster for electrocatalytic C–H functionalization
Authors: Chao Wu, Xu Zhang, Chang Liu, Ying Zhang, Weigang Fan, Sheng Zhang, Man-Bo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal nanoclusters have been found to be highly active in catalysis, while balancing their structural stability and catalytic activity remains a key challenge. Herein, we integrate nitrosyl chemistry into nanoclusters to resolve this paradox. Leveraging the adaptive coordination modes of NO ligand, the stability and activity of a Co7 nanocluster are well balanced for electrocatalysis. Particularly, a 15N-labeling approach has been developed to facilitate the structural assignment of the nitrosyl-Co7 nanocluster and its catalytic mechanism investigation. The synthesis of 15N-nitrosyl-Co7 counterpart validates the unique role of cobalt nitrate as both nitrosyl ligand and metal sources, and reveals the synergy between the NO shell and the cobalt core in hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). We redirect the HER activity toward electrocatalytic allene C-H functionalization under high-current-density electrolysis, a transformation that is otherwise inaccessible to conventional metal complex catalysts.Subject terms: Catalyst synthesis, Organic-inorganic nanostructures, Electrocatalysis, Ligands

Introduction

Nitric oxide (NO), a gaseous free radical, has been revealed as a key physiological regulator in mammals1–3, and its discovery was awarded the 1998 Nobel prize in physiology or medicine. Its distinctive electronic structure allows it to bind to metals as NO+, NO•, or NO−, depending on the electronic property of metal centers (Fig. 1a). These diverse ligand structures lead to two geometrically distinct forms4,5, linear (NO+) and bent (NO•/NO−). The flexible configuration and variable structure enable NO to act as an adaptive electron reservoir or scavenger, effectively stabilizing metal centers. On the other hand, this often results in metal complexes that are thermodynamically or kinetically less reactive6, which has severely hindered the catalytic application of nitrosyl-metal complexes. Nevertheless, as Berke predicted6, “if these effects can be exploited in a rational design approach, they may serve and support homogeneous catalysis”.

Organic ligand-protected metal nanoclusters have attracted widespread attention as emerging catalysts. They bridge homogeneous metal complex catalysis and heterogeneous metal nanocrystal catalysis, demonstrating high catalytic activity and unique selectivity7–12. Moreover, their atomically precise structures provide ideal models for investigating the structure-property correlations in nano-catalysis. However, the ultrasmall size and abundant active sites of metal nanoclusters also make them extraordinarily reactive, which results in poor stability during catalytic processes. Therefore, balancing stability and reactivity is a central challenge in metal nanocluster catalysis. For example, the multinuclear metal core facilitates electronic storage and transportation, endowing metal nanoclusters with the potential to trigger challenging redox electrocatalytic transformations13–16. However, valence changes of the metal core during the redox process would lead to structural collapse of metal nanoclusters due to the Jahn−Teller distortion17 (Fig. 1b). As a result, the molecular electrocatalysis of nanoclusters still remains uncultivated.

Considering the less reactive nitrosyl-metal complexes and too reactive metal nanoclusters, we envisaged that metal nanoclusters stabilized by the adaptive nitrosyl ligands might resolve the stability-reactivity paradox, thereby verifying Berke’s prediction. Building on our long-term interest in designing functional and catalytically active metal nanoclusters18–24, we herein successfully synthesized a nitrosyl Co7 nanocluster using cobalt(II) nitrate as both the metal and nitrosyl source. Moreover, a novel 15N-labeling approach was devised to enable clear structural assignment and mechanistic study of the nitrosyl nanocluster. As expected, the adaptive nitrosyl-Co7 nanocluster exhibits both structural stability and high activity in the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER)25, a fundamental transformation in energy chemistry. This robust HER activity was further repurposed for the electrocatalytic C-H functionalization26–32 by converting inert C-H bonds into H2 and active carbanions. The adaptive NO ligand has been revealed to play three important roles in the HER catalysis (Fig. 1c): a) the rapid structural conversion of NO+/NO•/NO− maintains the stability of the Co7 during catalysis; b) in situ formed nitroxyl anion (NO−) serves as a proton relay33,34 to enhance the HER activity and promote the carbanion generation; c) the bent coordination mode of NO− releases vacant Co sites for aldehyde activation and facilitates the subsequent transformation of the active carbanions with aldehydes. The synergy between the nitrosyl ligand and the cobalt core in the Co₇ nanocluster molecular electrocatalyst enables highly efficient allene C–H functionalization under high-current-density electrolysis35,36 (up to 200 mA/cm2), which proved to be a formidable challenge for conventional metal-complex-catalysts.

Results

Nanocluster synthesis and characterization

Despite the fact that various metal nanoclusters protected by thiol37–40, phosphine41–43, alkyne44,45, amidinate46 and carbene47–49 ligands have been reported, synthesis of nitrosyl-protected metal nanoclusters has been largely overlooked50,51. Their synthesis faces two main challenges: a) the high toxicity and limited accessibility of NO gas; and b) requiring good π-back donation of the metal center, as NO is a typical π-Lewis ligand. Developing a mild approach for in situ formation of nitrosyl ligand and modulating the metal π-back donation ability are key to addressing these issues.

We commenced the nanocluster synthesis using various NO and cobalt sources via a sequential protocol involving phosphine/thiol complexation and NaBH4 reduction (Table 1). Triphenylphosphine and 2,6-dimethylbenzenethiol were evaluated as supporting ligands to modulate the π-donation ability of the cobalt center. Gratifyingly, when cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate was used as both the NO and cobalt source, we obtained the desired nitrosyl cobalt nanocluster Co7S6(PPh3)3(NO)4 (Co₇-I) in 52% yield (entry 1), along with an analogous nanocluster [Co7S6(PPh3)3(SR)3](OH)/(NO) (Co₇-II, R = 2,6-dimethylphenyl). These two Co7 nanoclusters were isolated using preparative thin-layer chromatography (PTLC, Fig. S1); alternatively, a flash column chromatograph was employed for gram-scale reactions (for details, see Fig. S2). Directly introducing NO gas to react with cobalt(II) acetate failed to give the same nitrosyl cobalt nanocluster, yielding only a cobalt complex instead (entry 2, see Fig. S5–S7 for its preparation and single-crystal structure). Although NOBF4 proved to be a feasible NO source, the corresponding cobalt nanoclusters were observed in significantly lower yields even under optimized cobalt(II) acetate conditions (entry 3). These results highlight the superiority of cobalt(II) nitrate for in situ generation of nitrosyl ligand while also serving as the cobalt source. Initial examination employing alternative metal salts showed that neither iron(III) nitrate nor nickel(II) nitrate afforded the corresponding nitrosyl iron or nickel nanoclusters (Figs. S8 and S9). Control experiment using other cobalt salts (entries 4–6), such as Co(OAc)2, CoCl2 and Co(ClO4)2•6H2O, further demonstrated the necessity of nitrate ion, as no desired nanocluster was detected by TLC (Fig. S10a–c) and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS, Fig. S10e). Additionally, the combination of NaNO3 and CoCl2 also failed to give the desired nanocluster, presumably due to inefficient ion exchange in THF solvent (entry 7, Fig. S10d).

To better understand the reaction mechanism, we trapped the in situ formed NO+ using potassium (4-methoxyphenyl)trifluoroborate (A), which reacts with NO+ in a few seconds52. As expected, the adduct 1-methoxy-4-nitrosobenzene B was readily isolated (entry 8) and identified by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (Fig. S11). Additionally, triphenylphosphine oxide (TPPO) was also detected (Fig. S12) during the triphenylphosphine complexation. Collectively, these observations suggest that the nitrosyl ligand is generated from cobalt(II) nitrate via reduction by excess triphenylphosphine.

The as-synthesized cobalt nanoclusters were characterized with single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD). As shown in Fig. 2a, both Co7 nanoclusters share the similar crown-like metal core (Co7S6) and are stabilized by different ligands, which contributes to their different ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption peaks (Fig. S13). The metal core of Co7-I is supported by three triphenylphosphine ligands and installed with four NO molecules, while Co7-II has three 2,6-xylenethiolates instead of three surrounding NO ligands, with a central OH or NO ligand. It should be noted that low steric hindrance among the four NO ligands led to flexible coordination modes of Co7-I, and four stereoisomers were observed in the single crystal structure (Fig. S3). The structure parameters (Table S1) collected from these isomers showed that the NO bond length ranges from 1.12 to 1.21 Å and Co–N–O angle varies from 163.9-178.1°, with the average values (1.16 Å, 171.3°) supporting a linear NO+ structure. In contrast, the rigid structure of three 2,6-xylenethiols fixes the linear configuration of NO in Co7-II with a typical 180° Co-N-O angle and a slightly shorter NO bond of 1.08 Å. The contrasting coordination environments of NO in the two Co7 nanoclusters were further verified by variable temperature electron paramagnetic resonance (VT-EPR) (Figs. S14 and S15). The flexible NO structure of Co7-I gave rises of variation of the EPR signal at high temperature, while Co7-II, with restricted NO, largely maintains its EPR signal.

To unambiguously confirm the nitrosyl nanocluster structure, 15N isotope-labeled counterpart was synthesized from specially prepared 15N-cobalt(II) nitrate (Fig. 2b). We obtained 15N-cobalt(II) nitrate through the metathesis reaction between commercially available Na15NO3 and CoCl2, taking advantage of the poor solubility of NaCl in acetone (for details, see p S17). Under standard conditions, 15N-cobalt(II) nitrate was successfully converted into the desired cobalt nanoclusters, which were named as Co7-I-15N and Co7-II-15N. Subsequently, we compared HRMS spectra of nitrosyl nanocluster Co7-I with the isotope-labeled Co7-I-15N (Fig. 2c). Both nanoclusters proved to be monodispersed, and there is a 4 Da difference (1511.6782 vs. 1515.7020 Da) between them (for details, see Fig. S16). This result, on the one hand, verifies the existence of NO in the nanocluster and, on the other hand, further validates the origin of NO. With 15N-nitrosyl nanocluster in hand, infrared spectroscopy (IR) was recorded (Fig. 2d), and the stretching vibration peaks of NO at 1746 cm-1 were distinguished based on the isotope effect (Co7-I-15N, 1732 cm−1). This result is consistent with the single-crystal structure of Co7-I, jointly advocating for a formal interpretation as a linear NO+ structure. Similarly, the characterization of Co7-II was also validated by 15N-labeling HRMS and IR spectra. (for details, see Figs. S17 and S18). X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) further confirmed the elemental composition of both nanoclusters (Figs. S19 and S20). Moreover, the high stability of Co7-I was confirmed by time-dependent UV-vis spectroscopy (Fig. S21), establishing it as an ideal platform for electrocatalysis.

Electrochemical property investigation

Different nitrosyl binding modes correspond to distinct formal oxidation states. Using the classical Enemark–Feltham notation4 {MNO}n (n = metal d + NO-π* electrons), the cobalt atom bound to NO+ was assigned as {Co(NO)}7, which aids in understanding its electrochemical behavior. Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) were then explored to investigate the redox property of Co7-I and Co7-II (Fig. 3a, b, for details, see Figs. S22–S28). As depicted in Fig. 3a, both nanoclusters exhibit a uniform reduction wave at −1.99 V that should arise from the reduction of the Co7S6 core. Apart from this peak of metal core, we noticed extra quasi-reversible waves at -0.87 V and −1.64 V in the CV of Co7-I, which are ascribed to the reversible redox process of nitrosyl-cobalt moiety. According to reports of nitrosyl complexes53–55, these two peaks correspond to the {CoNO}7/8 and {CoNO}8/9 redox couples. The high steric-hindrance of Co7-II blocks the conversion of linear NO+ to bent NO•/NO−, leading to the silence of corresponding redox peaks of nitrosyl-cobalt moiety. To further validate this assignment, a control CV experiment mixing NOBF4 with Co7-II was elaborated (Fig. 3b). The in-situ ligand exchange of Co7-II with NO should lead to analogous reversible couples with Co7-I, as they share the same cobalt core. As expected, treating NOBF4 with Co7-II gave rises of noticeable couples of {CoNO}7/8 and {CoNO}8/9, supporting the above peaks assignment.

Having identified the electrochemical property of both nanoclusters, we sought to explore their activity as HER catalysts (Fig. 3c, d). By using acetic acid (HOAc) as the proton source, obvious increments were observed for the peak of Co7S6 core (−1.99 V) in both cases, showcasing their HER activity. Comparison of the catalytic current increment reveals that Co7-I is more efficient than Co7-II (ic/ip ~ 2.7 vs 1.5). Moreover, an increase in the {CoNO}8/9 peak (−1.64 V) was also detected for Co7-I (Fig. 3c), suggesting a catalytic role of the surface NO− ligand. Hydrogen gas released from the reaction was verified by gas chromatography (GC) experiment (Fig. S30). Encouraged by the promising HER activity of Co7-I, we further examined its catalytic performance in facilitating the HER of allene 1a, whose site-selective C-H functionalization has long been known as a challenge in synthetic chemistry56,57. As shown in the CV titration experiment (Fig. 3e), both peaks arising from the cobalt core and NO shell increased significantly with the amount of 1a added. Meanwhile, the HER process of allene was also promoted as a sharp increase of the peak at -2.25 V. In situ UV-vis spectra further confirmed the allene anion species generated via the HER of 1a (Figs. S32 and S33). Taken together, the above results illustrate that Co7-I exhibits a promising HER catalytic activity for 1a.

Given the versatility of allenol products58,59, the site-selective C-H functionalization of allene 1a with benzaldehyde 2a was selected as a pilot reaction to test the catalytic performance of Co7 nanoclusters (Fig. 3f). We compared various HER catalysts under the established conditions (for detailed condition optimization, see Table S2), using DMF as a solvent, nBu4NBF4 as an electrolyte, graphite felt (GF) and stainless steel (SS) plate as an anode and a cathode, respectively. The results reveal that most of the reported HER catalysts27,28 (cat 1–cat 4) are sensitive to the electrolysis current density, as the reaction yields decreased by nearly half upon increasing the current density from 10 mA/cm2 to 100 mA/cm2. In sharp contrast, the nitrosyl nanocluster Co7-I showed an excellent tolerance for high current density even at a significantly lower catalyst loading (1 mol%) compared to the metal complexes (5 mol%), and the reaction yield was largely maintained (73% vs 69%). Co7-II showed diminished catalytic efficiency and poor tolerance for high current electrolysis, albeit with the same cobalt core. We presume that the synergy between the cobalt core and NO shell enables a rapid generation of allene carbanions, and the simultaneous activation of benzaldehyde by the cobalt core facilitates the subsequent nucleophilic addition with the sensitive allene carbanion.

Substrate scope examination

With the robust Co7-I nanocluster electrocatalyst in hand, we investigated the generality of the catalytic reaction. As shown in Fig. 4, although further improving the electrolysis current to 200 mA largely maintained the yield of 3a (60%), we decided to conduct the reaction under 100 mA/cm2 electrolysis. The aldehyde substrates were examined first. It is shown that the electronic property variations (3b–3j) are well-tolerated, and redox-sensitive groups, such as thioether (3k), amine (3l) and iodine (3m), are also amenable to afford the desired products. The electrochemical protocol exhibits an excellent tolerance for the substitution patterns (3n–3r), and the reaction performance was marginally affected. Fused rings and heterocycles are also suitable substrates to afford the desired products (3s–3v) in satisfactory yields. Replacing the aryl group with tert-butyl (3w) and cyclopropyl (3x–3y) groups is feasible in the electrochemical approach. Specifically, the canonical radical-clock testing group cyclopropyl (3x–3y) remained intact during the reaction, implying a non-radical pathway of the transformation. We next examined the scope of allenes. Electronic effect was tested with para-substituted aryl rings (3z–3ad), and electron-withdrawing groups are more efficient than electron-donating groups, presumably due to their enhanced acidity. Varying the aliphatic chain to functional groups such as ether and acetal (3ae–3ag) is feasible. To highlight the robustness and selectivity of the present protocol, trisubstituted allenes were synthesized and employed in the reaction. To our delight, fully substituted allenols are readily accessed in an exclusive site-selective manner and satisfactory yields (3ah–3ak). Notably, installing a cyclopropyl group into the allene substrates (3ak–3al) maintained the reaction efficiency without detecting any ring-expansion byproduct, further evidencing the ionic reaction mechanism. 1-Phenylallene, which would give a mixture of two isomers (3am vs 3am’), proves to be competent and selectively gives product 3am, albeit with a lower yield. This high regioselectivity may be attributed to the efficient carbanion interception enabled by the nanocluster catalyst, which suppresses the competing isomerization pathway.

The robustness of the nanocluster electrocatalyst was highlighted in the gram-scale reaction. Upon scaling up to 20 mmol, 3.4 g of the desired product 3a was delivered in 61% yield with only 0.5 mol% of Co7-I nanocluster. By comparison, the cobalt complex catalyst (cat 4) affords the product in 14% yield even with much higher catalyst loading (5 mol%). The hydrogen byproduct released from the large-scale reaction was readily collected with a balloon and verified by GC (Fig. S31).

Mechanism investigation

To gain insight into the catalytic mechanism of the nitrosyl nanocluster Co7-I, we successfully trapped the active catalytic intermediate during the reaction. Figure 5a–d presents the characterizations of an observed catalytic species, which forms after short-term cathodic electrolysis of Co7-I in divided cell (Fig. S36a). This species exhibits higher polarity than the starting nanocluster Co7-I (Fig. 5a). Notably, anodic oxidation of Co7-I fails to give any new species, suggesting its anodic inertness (Fig. S36b). We characterized the trapped intermediate using the developed ¹⁵N-labeling strategy combined with UV-vis, CV, IR, HRMS and ¹⁵N NMR. Its nearly identical UV-vis (Fig. 5b) and CV (Fig. S29) spectra compared to Co7-I suggest their similar geometric structure; we tentatively assigned its structure as a bent nitrosyl nanocluster (Fig. 5a), denoted Int-A. IR spectra reveal the structural differences between Co7-I and Int-A (Fig. 5c). Compared to Co7-I, the characteristic N ≡ O+ stretching vibration at 1746 cm−1 decreased significantly, along with a new peak rising at 1657 cm−1. This observation suggests the nitroxyl anion (N− = O) structure in Int-A54. The isotope labeled Int-A-15N was further synthesized from Co7-I-15N under cathodic reduction. Comparison of IR spectra of Int-A and Int-A-15N (Fig. 5c, inset, for details, see Fig. S37) further confirms the stretching frequency consistent with a nitroxyl anion. HRMS analysis on positive mode showed the peaks of Int-A and Int-A-15N at 1510.5575 and 1514.7191 Da (Fig. 5d, for details, see Fig. S39a, b), which are similar to those of Co7-I and Co7-I-15N. Comparatively, negative mode HRMS gave completely different results. None of the signals was detected for Co7-I-15N (Fig. S39c), while a cluster anion peak at 1514.6241 Da was clearly observed for Int-A-15N (Fig. S39d). We also compared the 15N NMR spectra of Co7-I-15N and Int-A-15N, and up to 36.3 ppm up-field shift is detected (Fig. S38). These observations, combined with a slightly higher yield (70%) of 3a using Int-A to replace Co7-I in the standard reaction (pS29), authenticate that NO ligands dynamically stabilize Co7-I via NO+ and NO− forms during HER catalysis.

The interaction between cobalt nanoclusters (Int-A, Co7-I) and the aldehyde substrate was also investigated to understand the high-current-density-electrolysis tolerance. HRMS spectra (Fig. 5e, for details, see Fig. S40) show a contrasting result that Int-A leads to a more obvious adduct peak than Co7-I, directly evidencing the strong interaction of the aldehyde with the intermediate Int-A. Treating aldehyde with Int-A-15N also delivers an obvious 15N-labeled adduct. This result can be explained by the bent nitrosyl structure of Int-A, which would release a vacant metal site to activate the aldehyde. This activation mode would enhance the local concentration of aldehyde and promote its subsequent reaction with sensitive allene carbanions, leading to the high-current-density-electrolysis tolerance. Subsequently, we designed a series of control experiments to gain deeper mechanistic insight (Fig. 5f, for details, see p S34–38). An ionic mechanism was supported by radical suppression and deuterium incorporation experiments. Radical scavengers, such as 1,1-diphenylethylene, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinooxy (TEMPO) and P(OEt)3 had negligible effects on the reaction efficiency (Eq a), while the introduction of deuterated water completely suppressed the reaction and led to the recovery of deuterium-incorporated allene substrate (Eq b). A control experiment in a divided cell further confirms that the allene C–H functionalization occurs under cathodic electrolysis (Eq c). Kinetic isotope effect (KIE) study reveals that the cleavage of the allene C(sp2)–H bond is the rate-determining step (Figs. S42 and S43).

On the basis of the above experimental observations and related mechanism26,60,61 reported, a plausible catalytic cycle is proposed. As depicted in Fig. 6, the NO+ nanocluster Co7-I ({CoNO}7) is readily reduced to a NO• counterpart Co7-I’ ({CoNO}8) to initiate the catalytic cycle. Under further cathodic electrolysis (−1.64 V vs. Fc0/+), a NO− nanocluster Int-A ({CoNO}9) is generated and serves as a base to shift the dissociation equilibrium of allene 1a, thus affording Int-B along with an allene anion Int-I. Subsequently, the reduction of Int-B delivers a low-valent cobalt species Int C, which favors a rapid proton transfer to form a Con+1 hydride species Int-D. Ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) of Int-D produces an alkalinous Con hydride that readily regenerates Co7-I’ via the deprotonation of allene 1a to release hydrogen and Int-I. Finally, the activated aldehyde on the surface of Int-A intercepts the allene anion Int-I to afford the allenol product 3a. The synergy between the cobalt core and adaptive NO ligand of the Co7 nanocluster is the key to the high efficiency of the electrochemical allene C–H functionalization under high current density. Notably, the oxidation of DMF is proposed to be the predominant reaction at the anode, owing to its reported lower oxidation potential27.

Discussion

In summary, we have synthesized an adaptive nitrosyl‑Co7 nanocluster that exhibits both stability and high reactivity as a molecular electrocatalyst. With the newly developed 15N-labeling strategy, synergistic HER catalysis through cooperation between the metal core and the NO shell has been clarified. The superiority of the nanocluster HER catalyst is highlighted in allene C-H functionalization with unmatched tolerance for high-current-density electrolysis. A broad range of allenes were readily functionalized with aldehydes, exhibiting excellent functional group tolerance (39 examples), high efficiency (<40 min) and good scalability (>3 g scale). The in situ released activation sites of a bent nitrosyl Co7 nanocluster intermediate, and the cobalt-NO synergy have been established as the key factors responsible for the high electrocatalytic performance. Further studies on nitrosyl metal nanoclusters and the 15N-labeled strategy may provide efficient solutions for other conventionally challenging transformations and the corresponding mechanism clarification.

Methods

Procedure for the Co7 nanocluster synthesis

Co(NO3)2·6H2O (65 mg, 0.22 mmol) and PPh3 (66 mg, 0.25 mmol) were dissolved in 10 mL of THF in a flask with continuous stirring for 30 min (the reaction setup is shown in Fig. S1a). To the above solution, 2,6-dimethylbenzenethiol (120 mg, 0.87 mmol) was added. Two minutes later, a cold solution of NaBH4 (130 mg, 3.44 mmol) in water (4 mL) was added in a whole. The resulting mixture was then stirred at room temperature for 3 h. After the reaction was complete, the black solution was dried and washed with water. The resulting crude product was purified by PTLC with DCM/PE (1/1, v/v) as eluent (Fig. S1b) to give the two nanoclusters (Co7-I: 25 mg, 52% yield based on Co atom; Co7-II: 18 mg, 31% yield based on Co atom). Black diamond-shaped crystals of Co7-I and Co7-II used for SCXRD analysis were obtained by slow evaporation of the purified product in DCM/n-hexane and DCM/n-pentane solutions, respectively.

General procedure for the electrochemical functionalization of allene

An undivided cell was equipped with a magnet stirrer, stainless steel plate (1.8*0.56 cm2), and graphite felt (1.8*0.56 cm2), as cathode and anode, respectively (the electrolysis setup is shown in Fig. S34). The substrate aldehyde (51 µL, 0.5 mmol), allene 1a (172 mg, 1.0 mmol), Co7-I (8 mg, 0.005 mmol), and nBu4NBF4 (329 mg, 1.0 mmol) were added to the solvent DMF (10 mL). The resulting mixture was allowed to stir and electrolyze under constant current conditions (100 mA, J = 100 mA•cm−2) at 0 °C for 36 min. The reaction mixture was subsequently poured into water (100 mL) and extracted with ethyl acetate (40 mL × 3). The combined organic phases were washed with saturated brine solution (100 mL). The volatile solvent was then removed with a rotary evaporator, and the residue was purified by column chromatography (PE/EA = 20/1-10/1, v/v) on silica gel to afford the desired product 3a (96 mg) in 69% yield.
