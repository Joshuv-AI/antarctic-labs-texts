Mixed Quantum-Classical Methods for Polaron Spectral Functions
Haimi Nguyen, Arkajit Mandal, Ankit Mahajan, David R. Reichman

Abstract
In this work, using two distinct semiclassical approaches, namely the mean field Ehrenfest (MFE) method and the mapping approach to surface hopping (MASH), we investigate the spectral function of a single charge interacting with phonons on a lattice. This quantity is relevant for the description of angle-resolved photoemission experiments. Focusing on the one-dimensional Holstein model, we compare the performance of these approaches across a range of hopping parameters, coupling strengths, and lattice sizes at low and high temperatures, exposing the relative strengths and weaknesses of each. We demonstrate that these approaches can be efficiently applied with reasonable accuracy to ab initio polaron models. Our work provides a route to the calculation of spectral properties in realistic electron-phonon-coupled systems in a computationally inexpensive manner with encouraging accuracy.



I
Introduction

Electron-phonon interactions (EPI) are of crucial importance in condensed matter physics, playing a critical role in phenomena such as superconductivity, charge transport, and the thermodynamic properties of solids.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
Prominent models of EPI are the Holstein, Fröhlich, and Su-Schrieffer-Heeger models.
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
The accurate computation of spectral functions in electron-phonon coupled systems provides essential information such as the density of states and the lifetimes of quasi-particles, and is thus crucial for the interpretation of experimental data obtained from techniques such as angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy.
[
6
]
Among the models listed above, the Holstein model stands out for its simplicity, making it an ideal testing ground for numerical methods while still capturing the essential lattice level features of small polaron formation in systems such as molecular crystals.
[
6
]
Calculating the dynamical behavior of the Holstein model is thus important both for uncovering the underlying physics of real materials and for evaluating the effectiveness of different numerical techniques.

Numerically exact spectral functions have been obtained for the Holstein model using a variety of methods. Bonča
et al.

[
9
]
have employed the finite-temperature Lanczos approach to compute the spectral function for the Holstein polaron on a one-dimensional ring.
Mitrić
et al.

[
10
]
demonstrated that dynamical mean field theory provides an excellent approximation for spectral functions across all parameter regimes, even in one-dimensional systems where it might be expected to perform poorly. The Hierarchical Equations of Motion (HEOM) technique has also been employed to study spectral properties in a highly accurate manner
[
11
]
despite challenges associated with numerical instabilities
[
12
]
.
Other controlled approaches, such as the density matrix renormalization group at zero
[
13
,
14
]
and finite
[
15
]
temperatures, and the generalized Green’s function cluster expansion
[
16
]
, have been successfully applied to this problem.
In addition, recent studies
[
17
,
18
]
have investigated in detail the validity of the second-order cumulant expansion for calculating spectral functions and quasiparticle properties within the Holstein model, further enriching the methodological toolkit for this and related problems.

Mixed quantum-classical (MQC) methods, where electrons are treated quantum mechanically and phonons are treated classically, offer a promising alternative to these methods.
[
19
,
20
]
Indeed, a recent study by Mitrić
et al.

[
21
]
shows a remarkable agreement between MQC and exact treatments of the frequency-dependent mobility in the one-dimensional Holstein model near adiabatic limit.
MQC approaches, albeit approximate, offer the flexibility of treating systems beyond simple examples such as the Holstein model, including those where the EPI is non-linear and where the phonons are anharmonic.
In addition, MQC methods can be used to efficiently simulate the behavior of
ab initio
models of EPI in solids where near-exact approaches become extremely expensive. For example, they have been applied to study charge transport in crystalline rubrene.
[
22
]

However, there are many variants of MQC techniques, and to date there has been no systematic comparison of MQC in models of EPI to assess their efficacy and accuracy for quantities such as the spectral function.

One of the most widely used MQC methods is the mean field Ehrenfest method (MFE), in which the classical bath dynamically evolves on an average potential surface weighted by electronic wavefunction coefficients. However, it is known to suffer from the issue of overcoherence and incorrect detailed balance, where heating to infinite temperature can occur in the long time limit.
[
23
,
24
]

Another popular MQC approach is a class of surface hopping methods inspired by Tully’s fewest switches surface hopping (FSSH)
[
25
,
26
]
developed in the 1990s. Unlike MFE, the interaction between the classical bath and the quantum system in FSSH is chosen stochastically among the many potential surfaces, allowing for wave-packet branching that is absent in MFE. This approach is very popular in nonadiabatic dynamics simulations due to its ease of use in
ab initio
settings. Recently, Runeson and Manolopoulos have developed the multi-state mapping approach to surface hopping (MASH)
[
1
,
2
]
based on a previous two-state MASH formulation by Mannouch and Richardson
[
29
,
30
]
. Unlike FSSH, MASH selects the potential surface from which to evolve the classical degrees of freedom deterministically. MASH has been shown to obey detailed balance and provide accurate populations in various models.
[
1
,
2
]

In this work, we employ MFE and MASH to calculate the spectral function of the Holstein model across various parameter regimes and compare the results to exact benchmark data. By applying these MQC methods, we can assess the ability of these distinct approaches to reproduce the detailed underlying features encoded in the spectral function.
Having determined the utility of MFE for the Holstein model, we turn to a more challenging goal where semiclassical methods may be of great utility, namely for the simulation of
ab intio
problems.
Specifically, we utilize MFE to compute the spectral function of the hole polaron in LiF, an archetypal strong-coupling polaron system.

This paper is organized as follows: In Sec.
II
, we describe the models studied in this work, namely the Holstein model and the
ab initio
LiF Hamiltonian. Sec.
II.3
and Sec.
II.4
provide a detailed overview of the MFE and MASH methods respectively and explain how spectral functions are computed within these frameworks. Specifically, Sec.
II.5
discusses analytical expressions for the exact, MFE, and MASH spectral functions in the limit of zero hopping amplitude and temperature of the Holstein model. In Sec.
III
, we present a comparative analysis, first evaluating the performance of MFE against MASH in various regimes, then comparing different variations of MFE and the cumulant expansion method, and finally applying MFE to LiF. We conclude in Sec.
IV
with a discussion on the utility of MQC methods for calculating spectral functions in the Holstein model and LiF.

II
Models and Methods

In this section, we begin by describing the model systems studied in this work: the one-dimensional periodic Holstein model and an
ab initio
model of a hole in the valence bands of solid LiF. We then proceed to define the main observable we calculate, namely the spectral function, and outline the MFE and MASH algorithms. Finally, we provide analytical expressions for the spectral function of the Holstein model in the analytically solvable limit of zero hopping as a means to gain intuition for our numerical results.

II.1
Model System

II.1.1
Holstein model

In this study, we focus on the one-dimensional periodic Holstein model. In real space, the Hamiltonian (
\hbar=1
) is expressed as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left[-J(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n+1}\hat{c}_{n}+h.c.)+g\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{c}_{n}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n}+\hat{b}_{n})+\omega_{0}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{b}_{n}\right].

(1)

Periodic boundary conditions are used such that site
N
is equivalent to site 0. Here, the index
n
runs over all
N
sites in the real space. The operators
b^{\dagger}_{n}
and
b_{n}
are the bosonic creation and annihilation operators, respectively, for the harmonic bath at site
n
. The parameter
J
represents the hopping amplitude between nearest-neighbor sites, while
c^{\dagger}_{n}
and
c_{n}
denote the fermionic creation and annihilation operators at site
n
. The coupling strength between the electronic carrier at site
n
and the harmonic mode at the same site is given by
g
. The harmonic baths are assumed to be dispersionless, with a constant frequency
\omega_{0}
.
Taking the lattice constant as 1, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) can be equivalently written in momentum space as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left[-2J(\cos{k})\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}_{k}+\sum_{q}\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k+q}\hat{c}_{k}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{-q}+\hat{b}_{q})+\omega_{0}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k}\right],

(2)

where
k
and
q
are momenta of the electrons and phonons.

In the limit of a single electronic particle excitation, the real-space Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}-J\left(\outerproduct{n+1}{n}+h.c.\right)+g\outerproduct{n}{n}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n}+\hat{b}_{n})+\omega_{0}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{b}_{n},

(3)

where
\ket{n}=\ket{\emptyset}_{0}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{1}_{n}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\emptyset}_{N-1}
means an occupation of one electron on site
n
while the other sites remain empty.
The thermodynamic limit may be approximated for large
N
. Empirically,
N\approx 16
is sufficient to limit all finite-size effects for the parameters used here.
[
17
]
The study of small
N
cases, namely “Holstein molecules,” is interesting in its own right, as finite-size effects could represent physical manifestations of spectral behavior in small molecules. In this work, both limits are discussed.

II.1.2
Ab Initio
Hamiltonian for LiF

LiF is a polar ionic crystal with a large band gap (14.2 eV experimental optical gap). We consider the case of a single hole in the valence bands close to the band maximum. It is known that holes form small, strongly coupled polarons in this setting. The LiF hole Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{nk}\epsilon_{nk}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{nk}\hat{c}_{nk}+\sum_{\nu k}\omega_{\nu k}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\nu k}\hat{b}_{\nu k}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mn\nu\\
kq\end{subarray}}g_{mn\nu kq}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{mk+q}\hat{c}_{nk}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\nu,-q}+\hat{b}_{\nu q}),

(4)

where
m,~n
label the hole bands,
\nu
labels the phonon bands, and
k,~q
denote the momenta of the hole and phonon degrees of freedom. We consider 3 hole bands and the coupling to longitudinal optical (LO) phonon modes in our MQC and variational Monte Carlo (VMC) implementations. Details of the VMC method are described in the supplementary materials (SM). The Hamiltonian parameters were obtained from density functional perturbation theory calculations using Quantum Espresso with the EPW package (see Ref.
31
for details where the same parameterization was used.). Due to the polar nature of this system, the hole coupled to the LO mode is the largest contributor to polaron formation, and this coupling is essentially of the Fröhlich type.

II.2
Spectral function

We define the correlation function
C_{k}(t)
with
\hbar=1
as

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-\beta H}e^{iHt}\hat{c}_{k}e^{-iHt}\hat{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}[e^{-\beta H}]}.

(5)

In the limit of a single particle in a band, this becomes

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}e^{iHt}\hat{c}_{k}e^{-iHt}\hat{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]},

(6)

where
H_{B}
is the purely bath term in the Hamiltonian
H
. The derivation of Eq. (
6
) from Eq. (
5
) can be found in the SM.
The correlation function is related to the finite temperature retarded one-particle Green’s function
G(k,t)
as

\displaystyle G(k,t)=-i\theta(t)C_{k}(t),

(7)

where
\theta(t)
is the Heaviside step function.
Upon Fourier transforming the Green’s function, we obtain the spectral function

\displaystyle A(k,\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\imaginary\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}e^{-\gamma t}G(k,t)\right].

(8)

Here,
\gamma
is a broadening parameter. Unless otherwise specified, we set
\gamma=0.05
in this work.
In this paper, we focus on calculating the spectral function
A(k,\omega)
for different models using various exact and mixed quantum-classical methods. These methods are described below.

II.3
Mean Field Ehrenfest Dynamics

We start with a generic Hamiltonian of a system interacting with multiple baths,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}H_{S,ij}(\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{j})+\sum_{ij}H_{B,ij}(\hat{Q}_{i},\hat{P}_{j})

\displaystyle+\sum_{ii^{\prime}jj^{\prime}}H_{SB,ii^{\prime}jj^{\prime}}(\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{i^{\prime}},\hat{Q}_{j},\hat{P}_{j^{\prime}}),

(9)

where
\hat{P}_{i}
and
\hat{p}_{i}
are the mass-normalized momentum operators of the
i^{\text{th}}
bath and system degree of freedom, respectively, while
\hat{Q}_{i}
and
\hat{q}_{i}
are their corresponding mass-normalized position operators.
We next replace the bath operators with bath variables, whose dynamics is classical, to define the mixed quantum-classical Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H_{\text{MQC}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}H_{S,ij}(\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{j})+\sum_{ij}H_{B,ij}(Q_{i},P_{j})

\displaystyle+\sum_{ii^{\prime}jj^{\prime}}H_{SB,ii^{\prime}jj^{\prime}}(\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{i^{\prime}},Q_{j},P_{j^{\prime}}).

(10)

Denoting

\displaystyle H_{S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}H_{S,ij}(\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{j}),

(11)

\displaystyle H_{SB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ii^{\prime}jj^{\prime}}H_{SB,ii^{\prime}jj^{\prime}}(\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{i^{\prime}},Q_{j},P_{j^{\prime}}),

(12)

\displaystyle H_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}H_{B,ij}(Q_{i},P_{j}),

(13)

the classical equations of motion for the
i^{\text{th}}
bath variables are

\displaystyle\dot{P_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\bra{\psi}\nabla_{Q_{i}}H_{SB}\ket{\psi}-\frac{\partial H_{B}}{\partial Q_{i}},

(14)

\displaystyle\dot{Q_{i}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}\nabla_{P_{i}}H_{SB}\ket{\psi}+\frac{\partial H_{B}}{\partial P_{i}},

(15)

and the system is propagated with a Schrodinger equation of the form

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=\left(H_{S}+H_{SB}(\vec{Q},\vec{P})\right)\ket{\psi(t)}.

(16)

The change in the system’s wavefunction directly influences the force acting on the bath positions and momenta, while the evolution of the bath variables affects the Hamiltonian that governs the propagation of the system’s wavefunction. The system and the bath are propagated alternately at each time step according to Eq. (
14
), Eq. (
15
), and Eq. (
16
).
In this work, the initial bath positions and momenta are sampled from the Wigner distribution
\rho_{W}(\vec{P},\vec{Q},\beta)
of a harmonic bath at an inverse temperature
\beta
.
For the approach we refer to in the following as the frozen bath method, the bath variables
\vec{P}
and
\vec{Q}
remain fixed at their initially sampled values throughout the propagation.
[
32
]
Meanwhile, in the MFE method without back-reaction,
\vec{P}
and
\vec{Q}
evolve with the system-bath coupling term
H_{SB}
set to zero.

To compute
C_{k}(t)
within MFE, we initialize the wavefunction in state
\ket{k}
with a well-defined wave vector
k
.
C_{k}(t)
is equivalent to the coefficient of the
\ket{k}
state at time
t
,

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)=\int d\vec{P}_{0}d\vec{Q}_{0}\rho_{W}(\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0},\beta)\langle k|e^{-i(H_{S}+H_{SB})t}|k\rangle.

(17)

The reader is referred to the SM for a derivation of Eq. (
17
).

II.4
The Multi-State Mapping Approach to Surface Hopping

In this section, we begin by reviewing the Multi-State Mapping Approach to Surface Hopping
[
29
,
1
,
2
,
30
]
(MASH), particularly the formalism developed by Runeson
et al.

[
1
,
2
]
We then introduce two distinct ways to employ MASH to compute
A(k,\omega)
. Although these methods approach the problem from different angles, we find that they provide identical spectral functions numerically.

II.4.1
Overview of the Algorithm

As is typical of mixed quantum-classical methods, MASH propagates the electronic wavefunction quantum mechanically, as in Eq. (
16
), while treating the bath variables classically. However, MASH differs from MFE in two important ways. First, the equations of motion in MASH for bath variables take the form of

\displaystyle\dot{P_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\bra{\alpha}\nabla_{Q_{i}}H_{SB}\ket{\alpha}-\frac{\partial H_{B}}{\partial Q_{i}},

(18)

\displaystyle\dot{Q_{i}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\alpha}\nabla_{P_{i}}H_{SB}\ket{\alpha}+\frac{\partial H_{B}}{\partial P_{i}},

(19)

where
\ket{\alpha}
is the current active adiabatic state. Unlike the average force in MFE, the force in MASH arises from one adiabatic state at a time.
Although MASH is derived from Tully’s Fewest Switches Surface Hopping
[
25
]
, where hops are stochastic, it is deterministic. The state
|\alpha\rangle
is selected based on the adiabatic surface with the highest population, except when energy conservation prohibits such a hop.
Hops are carried out by switching the active surface and adjusting the momentum to conserve the total energy. The element of the vector along which the momentum is rescaled is

\displaystyle v_{j}=\sum_{a^{\prime}}\real[c^{*}_{a^{\prime}}(d^{j}_{a^{\prime}a}c_{a}-d^{j}_{a^{\prime}b}c_{b})],

(20)

where
c_{i}
are the coefficients of the wavefunction in the adiabatic basis, and

\displaystyle d^{j}_{a^{\prime}a}=\langle a^{\prime}|\nabla_{j}|a\rangle.

(21)

If the kinetic energy is insufficient to overcome the potential difference between the old and new active surfaces, the momentum’s direction will be reversed in the opposite direction of the above vector without any rescaling of the magnitude, and the hop is canceled.

Second, in contrast to MFE, MASH involves the stochastic sampling of the initial bath variables as well as the initial coefficients of the system’s wavefunction. We refer to Ref.
2
for the specifics on the different sampling schemes for the wavefunction. In practice, we have found essentially no differences in the results between these schemes, and thus have utilized focused initial conditions to sample the wavefunction coefficients.
In particular, we sample a wavefunction focused on state
\ket{k}
from the following density distribution,

\displaystyle\rho_{k}(c)=\delta\left(|c_{k}|^{2}-\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}\right)\prod_{i\neq k}\delta\left(|c_{i}|^{2}-\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}\right),

(22)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{N-1}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n}-1},

(23)

\displaystyle\beta_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{N}-1}{N},

(24)

and
N
is the size of the system.
The phases of the coefficients are sampled uniformly over
[0,2\pi)
.

II.4.2
Estimator of the Electronic Wavefunction Coefficients

One way to compute the correlation function
C_{k}(t)
is to use an estimator for the electronic wavefunction coefficients. In this section, we give a general derivation of the estimator for the electronic wavefunction coefficients, while giving a specific example on how it is applied to calculate
C_{k}(t)
.
For a given initial arbitrary wavefunction
\ket{\psi_0}
, we consider a superposition, normalized state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(0)}=m_{\phi}\ket{\phi}+m_{\psi_{0}}\ket{\psi_0},

(25)

where
|m_{\phi}|^{2}+|m_{\psi_{0}}|^{2}=1
, and
\ket{\phi}
is an auxiliary state uncoupled to the original Hilbert space of the system in the Hamiltonian, meaning that the Hamiltonian does not act on the
\ket{\phi}
state. In the limit of
m_{\phi}\rightarrow 0
and
m_{\psi_{0}}\rightarrow 1
, we recover the original state
\ket{\psi_0}
. In our specific context of calculating
C_{k}(t)
according to Eq. (
17
), we set
\ket{\psi_0}=|k\rangle
, and sample the initial state as follows,

\displaystyle\rho(c)

\displaystyle=\delta\left(|c_{\tilde{k}}|^{2}-\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}\right)\delta\left(|c_{\tilde{\phi}}|^{2}-\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{i\neq k}\delta\left(|c_{i}|^{2}-\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}\right),

(26)

where

\displaystyle|\tilde{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi(0)}=m_{\phi}\ket{\phi}+m_{\psi_{0}}\ket{k},

(27)

\displaystyle|\tilde{\phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=m_{\psi_{0}}\ket{\phi}-m_{\phi}\ket{k},

(28)

The index
i
runs through all the states in the momentum basis that are orthonormal to state
|k\rangle
. It is important to note that the magnitudes of the coefficients
include
the auxiliary state. We take
m_{\phi}
and
m_{\psi_{0}}
to be real numbers, with
m_{\phi}^{2}+m_{\psi_{0}}^{2}=1
, so that the tilde basis is orthonormal.

Returning to our general formulation of the electronic coefficient estimator, from Ref.
1
, we have the following estimators for the diagonal and off-diagonal elements of a system observable,

\displaystyle\Phi_{nn}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N+1}+\alpha_{N+1}\left(|c_{n}|^{2}-\frac{1}{N+1}\right),

(29)

\displaystyle\Phi_{nm}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{N+1}c^{*}_{n}c_{m},

(30)

where
\Phi_{nn}
and
\Phi_{nm}
estimate the elements
\outerproduct{n}{n}
and
\outerproduct{n}{m}
respectively, and
c_{n}
is the coefficient of state
\ket{n}
. The use of
N+1
here instead of
N
in the original formulation in Ref.
1
explicitly emphasizes the inclusion of the auxiliary state in our formulation.
We consider an element of the density matrix constructed from the wavefunction in Eq. (
25
),

\displaystyle\rho_{i\phi}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle i|e^{-iHt}|\psi(0)\rangle\langle\psi(0)|e^{iHt}|\phi\rangle

(31)

\displaystyle=m_{\psi_{0}}m_{\phi}\bra{i}e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi_0}

(32)

\displaystyle=m_{\psi_{0}}m_{\phi}c_{i}(t).

(33)

\rho_{i\phi}(t)
is estimated by the population or coherence estimator
\Phi_{\phi i}(t)
as expressed in Eq.
29
and Eq.
30
.
We obtain

\displaystyle\langle c_{i}(t)\rangle=\frac{\left\langle\rho_{i\phi}(t)\right\rangle_{\text{MQC}}}{m_{\psi_{0}}m_{\phi}}=\frac{\left\langle\Phi_{\phi i}(t)\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}}{m_{\psi_{0}}m_{\phi}}=\left\langle\frac{c_{i}(t)}{c_{\psi_{0}}(0)}\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}.

(34)

The derivation of Eq. (
34
) can be found in the SM. We can now compute the estimator for
\langle i|e^{-iHt}|\psi_{0}\rangle
as

\displaystyle W_{i\psi_{0}}(t)=\frac{c_{i}(t)}{c_{\psi_{0}}(0)}.

(35)

Since this estimator is based on the population or coherence estimator
[
1
]
, it is basis-equivariant.

Although
m_{\phi}
and
m_{\psi_{0}}
do not explicitly appear in the final expression for the electronic coefficient estimator, they might influence it indirectly. For example, in the case of computing the correlation function
C_{k}(t)
, after the initial sampling by Eq. (
26
), we rotate this basis to the real space basis, and MASH propagates the system in this basis. Note that real space states
|n\rangle
are Fourier transforms of physical momentum states
|k\rangle
only, excluding the auxiliary state
|\phi\rangle
. The initial wavefunction now becomes

\displaystyle|\Psi(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{k}}}-m_{k}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{n}m_{k}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}N}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{k}}}e^{-ikn}|n\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{n}m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}N}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}e^{-ikn}|n\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{n}\sum_{q}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}N}}e^{i\theta_{q}}e^{-iqn}|n\rangle.

(36)

One can observe that due to the interactions of
m_{\phi}
,
m_{k}
and the initial sampled phases, such as
\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}
and
\theta_{\tilde{k}}
, the relative populations of the states
|n\rangle
might be different in each trajectory, influencing the selection of the adiabatic surface for the MASH force. This happens even though the system never interacts with the state
|\phi\rangle
in our MASH algorithm. We also note that in our MASH algorithm, the system never hops to this uncoupled surface even if it carries the highest population.
To remain as close as possible to the original physical system, one without the uncoupled state, we can initialize the system in a superposition with minimal mixing with the
\ket{\phi}
state, where
m_{\phi}\rightarrow 0
and
m_{\psi_{0}}\rightarrow 1
. However, in practice, the specific choice of
m_{\phi}
and
m_{\psi_{0}}
has little impact on the estimator for the electronic coefficient. In this work, we set
m_{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and have found no qualitative difference compared to the spectra obtained at
m_{\phi}=10^{-5}
. Representative examples are provided in the SM.

From the derivation of the correlation function in MFE (Eq. (
17
)), we see that the estimator for the correlation function in MASH is really the estimator for
\langle k|e^{-i(H_{S}+H_{SB})t}|k\rangle
. The expression for the correlation function in MASH is therefore

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\int d\vec{P}_{0}d\vec{Q}_{0}\rho_{W}(\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0},\beta)\int d\vec{c}\rho_{k}(\vec{c})W_{kk}(t)

\displaystyle=\int d\vec{P}_{0}d\vec{Q}_{0}\rho_{W}(\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0},\beta)\int d\vec{c}\rho_{k}(\vec{c})\frac{c_{k}(t)}{c_{k}(0)}.

(37)

II.4.3
Computation of the correlation function via the pure-state decomposition

Another way to compute the correlation function
C_{k}(t)
is via the pure-state decomposition. Our numerical calculations show that the spectra calculated via the pure-state decomposition and the wavefunction coefficient estimator method give indistinguishable results in both MFE and MASH (see SM).
The creation and annihilation operators
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}
and
\hat{c}_{k}
can be rewritten as
\outerproduct{k}{\emptyset}
and
\outerproduct{\emptyset}{k}
respectively in the single-particle limit.
Following work by Lieberherr
et al.

[
33
]
as well as previous studies
[
34
,
35
]
, we decompose these operators into linear combinations of pure-state density matrices as follows,

\displaystyle\outerproduct{a}{b}=\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}\outerproduct{j}{j},

(38)

where

\displaystyle\ket{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{\ket{a}}{\sqrt{\innerproduct{a}{a}}}+e^{ij\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\ket{b}}{\sqrt{\innerproduct{b}{b}}}\right),

(39)

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\innerproduct{a}{a}\innerproduct{b}{b}}}{2}e^{ij\frac{\pi}{2}}.

(40)

\displaystyle w_{0}=\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle\quad w_{1}=\frac{i}{2},

\displaystyle\quad w_{2}=\frac{-1}{2},

\displaystyle\quad w_{3}=\frac{-i}{2},

(41)

and

\displaystyle\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\emptyset}+\ket{k}),

\displaystyle\quad\ket{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\emptyset}+i\ket{k}),

(42)

\displaystyle\ket{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\emptyset}-\ket{k}),

\displaystyle\quad\ket{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\emptyset}-i\ket{k}).

(43)

The correlation function is computed as

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}e^{iHt}\outerproduct{\emptyset}{k}e^{-iHt}\outerproduct{k}{\emptyset}\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

(44)

\displaystyle-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}w_{0}\innerproduct{j}{2(t)}\innerproduct{2(t)}{j}\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

\displaystyle+\frac{\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}w_{0}i\innerproduct{j}{+i(t)}\innerproduct{+i(t)}{j}\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

(45)

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}w_{0}\alpha_{N+1}\left(|c^{0}_{j}|^{2}-|c^{2}_{j}|^{2}\right)\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

\displaystyle+\frac{\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}w_{0}\alpha_{N+1}i\left(|c^{+i}_{j}|^{2}-|c^{-i}_{j}|^{2}\right)\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}.

(46)

Here,

represents the coefficient of state
\ket{j}
when the initial state is
\ket{n}
, and
\ket{\pm i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}\pm i\ket{2})
.
To sample the initial states
|j(0)\rangle

|\phi\rangle
is excluded from the hopping manifold and does not interact with the remaining states during the propagation.

II.5
Exact Analytical Solutions in The Regime of Zero Hopping Amplitude and Zero Temperature

Analytical expressions for the spectral function in MFE, MASH, and in the exact quantum case can be derived for the one-dimensional Holstein model in the simplified limit where the hopping amplitude
J
is set to 0. For simplicity, we set
T=0
as well. In this regime, the sites are completely decoupled, resulting in a spectral function that is independent of variation in
k
. For the exact quantum case, it is simple to show that the spectral function is

\displaystyle A(\omega)=e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{2n}}{n!}\delta(\omega+g^{2}\omega_{0}-\omega_{0}n),

(47)

and is independent of
N
.
Despite the fact that the sites are completely decoupled for
J=0
, MFE and MASH produce spectral functions that are dependent on
N
in different ways. This dependency is an artifact of the methods themselves rather than a physically meaningful phenomenon and arises due to the way each method treats the initial superposition state,
\ket{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n}e^{ikn}\ket{n}
. In particular, MFE yields exact results when the number of sites is 2 (as shown in the upper two rows of Fig.
1
). For arbitrary
N
, the MFE spectral function can be calculated analytically,

\displaystyle A(\omega)=e^{-g^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{m}\left(g^{2}\right)J_{n-m}\left(-\frac{2}{N}g^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\delta\left(\omega+2\frac{g^{2}}{N}\omega_{0}+n\omega_{0}\right),

(48)

where
J_{n}
and
I_{n}
are the
n^{th}
Bessel function and modified Bessel function of the first kind, respectively. For
N=2
, Eq. (
48
) reduces to Eq. (
47
).

The modified MASH approach also fails to reproduce the exact results in general. In the scenario where
g^{2}
is an integer (as shown in the lower two rows of Fig.
1
), MASH produces peaks that align well with the exact ones. However, the weights of the peaks are inaccurate, and additional peaks emerge that are not present in the exact results. The spectral function produced by MASH can be analytically determined to be

\displaystyle A(\omega)=\frac{1}{N}

\displaystyle e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle I_{m}\left(g^{2}\right)J_{n-m}\left(-2g^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\delta\left[\omega+2g^{2}\omega_{0}+\omega_{0}n\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{N-1}{N}e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle I_{n}\left(g^{2}\right)\delta[\omega+\omega_{0}n].

(49)

Note that both MFE and MASH may produce regions of negative spectral weight, and this is reproduced analytically for the
J=0
case, a fact that implies the associated regions of negative spectral weight seen in Fig.
1
and Fig.
2
do not arise from numerical undersampling. Interestingly, these regions are more noticeable in MFE than in MASH in a few regimes.
For derivations of the spectral functions in Eq. (
47
), Eq. (
48
), and Eq. (
49
), the reader is referred to the SM.

Regarding peak positions, MFE is known to suffer from the overcoherence problem

IV
Summary and Conclusions

In this work, we have compared distinct semiclassical approaches, namely the standard mean field Ehrenfest theory (MFE) and the more recently formulated MASH approach, by considering the 1-particle Green’s function for electron-phonon systems such as the one-dimensional Holstein model.
We compare these approaches to exact results as determined by variational exact diagonalization and variational quantum Monte Carlo. Our comparisons are aided by analytical calculations in the simplified limit of zero hopping.

Focusing on the one-dimensional Holstein model, we find that both MFE and MASH produce similar spectra for
k
values at the band minimum and maximum when the number of sites is large. These results are largely consistent with exact diagonalization results.
In line with this, Runeson
et al.

[
22
]
report semi-quantitative, instead of quantitative, agreements between MFE and MASH in computing charge transport in the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model.
However, for small periodic lattices, we find that MFE is capable of reproducing sharp features in the spectral function more accurately than MASH, albeit at the expense of some small, unphysical spectral regions. This negative spectral weight appears to be suppressed in our formulation of MASH for standard model parameters.

Within MFE alone, we examined the role played by the treatment of the phonon dynamics, finding that unlike the case for transport problems in some regimes
[
8
]
, inclusion of the back-reaction force notably increases the accuracy of the method. We have also applied MFE to an
ab initio
model of the spectral function for a valence hole in LiF, and compare the results to near-exact real-frequency variational quantum Monte Carlo calculations. We find that MFE quantitatively agrees with previous cumulant calculations, and that both of these approximate methods produce spectra which appear to be notably broader than that found by Monte Carlo calculations performed on somewhat smaller grids.

Overall, our work illustrates that semiclassical methods offer an attractive and inexpensive means to estimate spectral properties of electron-phonon coupled systems. These methods are competitive with, and sometimes more accurate than, other approximate methods which have been used previously to calculate spectral functions in polaronic systems, and are easy to adapt for calculations in complex
ab initio
models of solids.

General Expressions for the Correlation Functions in the Mixed Quantum-Classical Methods in the Single Excitation Basis

In this section, we derive an expression for the correlation function in general mixed quantum-classical methods, where the phonon momenta and positions are classical variables, and electronic states are treated quantum mechanically.
We start from the Hamiltonian of the one-dimensional Holstein model in the single excitation basis,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{S}+H_{SB}+H_{B}

(S1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}-J\left(\outerproduct{n+1}{n}+\outerproduct{n}{n+1}\right)+g\outerproduct{n}{n}(b^{\dagger}_{n}+b_{n})+\omega_{0}b^{\dagger}_{n}b_{n}.

(S2)

The correlation function has the form of

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\operatorname{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta H}e^{iHt}\hat{c}_{k}e^{-iHt}\hat{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\right)

(S3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{n}\bra{n}_{\text{ph}}\otimes\bra{\emptyset}e^{-\beta H}e^{iHt}\hat{c}_{k}e^{-iHt}\hat{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\ket{\emptyset}\otimes\ket{n}_{\text{ph}}

(S4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{B}}\sum_{n}\bra{n}_{\text{ph}}e^{-\beta H_{B}}e^{iH_{B}t}\otimes\bra{\emptyset}\hat{c}_{k}e^{-iHt}\hat{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\ket{\emptyset}\otimes\ket{n}_{\text{ph}}

(S5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{B}}\sum_{n}\bra{n}_{\text{ph}}e^{-\beta H_{B}}e^{iH_{B}t}\otimes\bra{k}e^{-iHt}\ket{k}\otimes\ket{n}_{\text{ph}},

(S6)

where
\ket{\emptyset}=\otimes_{i=0}^{N-1}\ket{\emptyset}_{i}
is the vacuum state for
N
sites, while
\ket{n}_{\text{ph}}=\otimes_{i=0}^{N-1}\ket{n}_{\text{ph},i}
is the occupation number state for
N
local baths associated with
N
sites.
The above derivation is based on the fact that

\displaystyle e^{aH}\ket{\emptyset}\otimes\ket{n}_{\text{ph}}

\displaystyle=(1+a(H_{S}+H_{SB}+H_{B})+\frac{1}{2}\left[a(H_{S}+H_{SB}+H_{B})\right]^{2}+\ldots)\ket{\emptyset}\otimes\ket{n}_{\text{ph}}

(S7)

\displaystyle=(1+aH_{B}+\frac{1}{2}\left(aH_{B}\right)^{2}+\ldots)\ket{\emptyset}\otimes\ket{n}_{\text{ph}}

(S8)

\displaystyle=\ket{\emptyset}\otimes e^{aH_{B}}\ket{n}_{\text{ph}},

(S9)

since
H_{S}
,
H_{SB}
, and any products of these operators acting on the vacuum state of the system give zero.
In a mixed quantum-classical method, since
H_{B}
becomes a function of the bath momenta and positions,
P
and
Q
, respectively, Eq. (
S6
) is now

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{B}}\int d\vec{P}_{0}d\vec{Q}_{0}e^{-\beta E_{B}(\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0})}\prod_{i=0}^{M-1}e^{iE_{B}(\vec{P}_{i},\vec{Q}_{i})\Delta t}\bra{k}\prod_{i=0}^{M-1}e^{-i(H_{S}+H_{SB})\Delta t}e^{-iE_{B}(\vec{P}_{i},\vec{Q}_{i})\Delta t}\ket{k}

(S10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{B}}\int d\vec{P}_{0}d\vec{Q}_{0}e^{-\beta E_{B}(\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0})}\bra{k}\prod_{i=0}^{M-1}e^{-i(H_{S}+H_{SB})\Delta t}\ket{k}

(S11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{B}}\int d\vec{P}_{0}d\vec{Q}_{0}e^{-\beta E_{B}(\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0})}\bra{k}e^{-i(H_{S}+H_{SB})t}\ket{k}

(S12)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\bra{k}e^{-i(H_{S}+H_{SB})t}\ket{k}\right\rangle_{\text{MQC}},

(S13)

where
M=\frac{t}{\Delta t}
. Note that
H_{SB}
is dependent on
\vec{P}(t)
and
\vec{Q}(t)
as parameters.

In mean field Ehrenfest (MFE), the correlation function is the amplitude
c_{k}(t)
of state
\ket{k}
, calculated when the initial wavefunction is
\ket{k}
and averaged over the trajectories of the classical variables. In contrast, in MASH, the correlation function is represented by the electronic coefficient estimator
W_{kk}(t)
averaged over the trajectories of the classical phonon variables and initial wavefunction coefficients.

II
Estimator of electronic wavefunction coefficients in MASH

In this section, we derive an expression to estimate the coefficients of the electronic wavefunctions in MASH. To do so, we establish a connection between elements of the propagator in the original system,
\langle k|e^{-iHt}|i\rangle
, and elements of the density matrix of the expanded system by introducing a fictitious state
\ket{\phi}
, which is uncoupled from the original system by the Hamiltonian. Note that the state
\ket{i}
here corresponds to the state
\ket{\psi_0}
in Eq. (25) of the main text. Letting the initial wavefunction be

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{i}}=m_{\phi}\ket{\phi}+m_{i}\ket{i},

(S14)

where
m_{\phi}
and
m_{i}
are real numbers for simplicity, and
m_{\phi}^{2}+m_{i}^{2}=1
, we consider an element of the density matrix,

\displaystyle\rho_{k\phi}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle k|e^{-iHt}|\Psi(0)\rangle\langle\Psi(0)|e^{iHt}|\phi\rangle

(S15)

\displaystyle=\langle k|e^{-iHt}(m_{\phi}\ket{\phi}+m_{i}\ket{i})(\bra{\phi}m_{\phi}+\bra{i}m_{i})e^{iHt}\ket{\phi}

(S16)

\displaystyle=m_{i}m_{\phi}\langle k|e^{-iHt}|i\rangle.

(S17)

The derivation of Eq. (
S17
) from Eq. (
S16
) relies on the fact that the Hamiltonian does not act on the
\ket{\phi}
state in any way.
From Ref.
1
,

\langle O\rangle=\sum_{nm}O_{nm}\rho_{mn}\rightarrow\sum_{nm}O_{nm}\left[\alpha_{N}c^{*}_{n}c_{m}+\beta_{N}\delta_{nm}\right],

estimators for elements in electronic observables are complex conjugates to estimators for elements in density matrices.
\alpha_{N}
and
\beta_{N}
are constants that we later define at Eq. (
S25
) and Eq. (
S26
), and
c_{n}
is the amplitude coefficient for state
\ket{n}
.
From Eq. (
S17
), it is now clear that

\displaystyle\left\langle\langle k|e^{-iHt}|i\rangle\right\rangle_{\text{MQC}}=\frac{\left\langle\rho_{k\phi}(t)\right\rangle_{\text{MQC}}}{m_{i}m_{\phi}}=\frac{\left\langle\alpha_{N}c_{k}(t)c^{*}_{\phi}(t)\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}}{m_{i}m_{\phi}}=\left\langle W_{ki}(t)\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}.

(S18)

where

\displaystyle\langle O(t)\rangle_{\text{MQC}}=\int d\vec{P}_{0}d\vec{Q}_{0}\rho_{W}(\beta,\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0})O(\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0},t)

(S19)

and

\displaystyle\langle O(t)\rangle_{\text{MASH}}=\int d\vec{P}_{0}d\vec{Q}_{0}\int d\vec{c}_{0}\rho_{W}(\beta,\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0})\rho_{M}(\vec{c}_{0})O(\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0},\vec{c}_{0},t).

(S20)

\rho_{W}
and
\rho_{M}
are the Wigner and MASH initial density functions for the bath variables and electronic coefficients respectively.
W_{ki}(t)
is the estimator for
\langle k|e^{-iHt}|i\rangle
that we need to determine. The
N
in
\alpha_{N}

includes
the auxiliary state.

In order to sample from
\rho_{M}(\vec{c}_{0})
, one needs to establish an orthonormal basis, one of whose basis vectors is the state we want to start from. For our case, we want to start with state
\ket{\tilde{i}}
. With the original orthonormal basis set {
\ket{i}
}, our new orthonormal basis is now

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{i}}

\displaystyle=m_{i}\ket{i}+m_{\phi}\ket{\phi},

(S21)

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{\phi}}

\displaystyle=m_{\phi}\ket{i}-m_{i}\ket{\phi},

(S22)

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{j}}

\displaystyle=\ket{j},

(S23)

where states
|j\rangle
denote the basis vectors orthogonal to
|i\rangle
, and together they span the original Hilbert space.
We take
m_{i}
and
m_{\phi}
as real numbers. In the limit of
m_{i}\rightarrow 1
and
m_{\phi}\rightarrow 0
, we recover the original basis.
Here, we take
\rho_{M}(\vec{c}_{0})
to be the focused initial sampling
[
2
]
, where the magnitudes of the wavefunction coefficients are fixed to predetermined values, and only the phases are sampled. However, this derivation also applies to other sampling schemes.
The initial wavefunction focused on state
\ket{\tilde{i}}
(Eq. (
S21
)) is

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(0)}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}\ket{\tilde{i}}+\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\ket{\tilde{\phi}}+\sum_{j\neq i}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{j}}\ket{j},

(S24)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{N-1}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{k}-1},

(S25)

\displaystyle\beta_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{N}-1}{N}.

(S26)

N
represents the total system size,
including
the fictitious state. The wavefunction can be rewritten in the expanded basis as

\displaystyle|\psi(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}-m_{i}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)|\phi\rangle+\left(m_{i}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}+m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)\ket{i}+\sum_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{j}}|j\rangle.

(S27)

Since the state
\ket{\phi}
is completely uncoupled from the original system, propagating
\ket{\psi}
gives

\displaystyle|\psi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}-m_{i}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)|\phi\rangle+\left(m_{i}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}+m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)\sum_{k^{\prime}}\gamma_{k^{\prime}i}(t)\ket{k'}+\sum_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{j}}\sum_{k^{\prime}}\gamma_{k^{\prime}j}(t)|k^{\prime}\rangle,

(S28)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{mn}(t)=\langle m|e^{-iHt}|n\rangle.

(S29)

We can now expand Eq. (
S18
):

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\alpha_{N}c_{k}(t)c^{*}_{\phi}(t)\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}}{m_{i}m_{\phi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{N}\left\langle\left(\left(m_{i}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}+m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)\gamma_{ki}(t)+\sum_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{j}}\gamma_{kj}(t)\right)\left(m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{-i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}-m_{i}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{-i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}}{m_{i}m_{\phi}}

(S30)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{N}\left\langle\gamma_{ki}(t)\left(\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}-\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}+\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}\left(\frac{m_{\phi}}{m_{i}}e^{-i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}-\frac{m_{i}}{m_{\phi}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}e^{-i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)\right)\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}

(S31)

\displaystyle+\alpha_{N}\left\langle\sum_{j}\gamma_{kj}(t)\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{j}}\left(\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{-i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}-\frac{1}{m_{\phi}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{-i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\gamma_{ki}(t)\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}.

(S32)

The previous derivation works because any term that averages over random phases will equate zero. That leaves us with a simple final expression.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{c_{k}(t)}{c_{i}(0)}\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\left(m_{i}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}+m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)\gamma_{ki}(t)+\sum_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{j}}\gamma_{kj}(t)}{\left(m_{i}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{i}}}+m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N}}{\alpha_{N}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)}\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}

(S33)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\gamma_{ki}(t)\right\rangle_{\text{MASH}}.

(S34)

We can now establish the MASH estimator for
\langle k|e^{-iHt}|i\rangle
as

\displaystyle W_{ki}(t)=\frac{c_{k}(t)}{c_{i}(0)}.

(S35)

In our MASH algorithm, even when
\ket{\phi}
plays no part in the MASH dynamics, it is clear that the values of
m_{\phi}
and
m_{k}
affect the relative populations between states
|n\rangle
, leading to potentially different selections of the adiabatic surfaces acting on the MASH force throughout the propagation. One clear example for this is Eq. (
S94
), which we later show for the dynamics in the exact case of
J=0
. However, from our empirical observations, this effect is negligible on the resulting spectral functions. Fig.
(SM 1)
shows a few representative cases from the main paper to demonstrate the robustness of the spectral functions against the change in the value of
m_{\phi}
.

Figure (SM 1):
Spectral functions computed by MASH in different regimes for
m_{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
(blue curve) and
m_{\phi}=10^{-5}
(red dashed curve).

III
Expression for the correlation function in the pure-state decomposition method

In the single-particle projection basis, we can rewrite
c_{k}\rightarrow|\emptyset\rangle\langle k|
to get the following expressions for the correlation function,

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle c_{k}(t)c^{\dagger}_{k}(0)\rangle

(S36)

\displaystyle=\left\langle e^{iHt}|\emptyset\rangle\langle k|e^{-iHt}|k\rangle\langle\emptyset|\right\rangle

(S37)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\langle\emptyset|\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}e^{iHt}|j\rangle\langle j|e^{-iHt}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{3}w_{j^{\prime}}|\tilde{j^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\tilde{j^{\prime}}|\emptyset\rangle\right]}{\Tr_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

(S38)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\langle\emptyset|\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}|j(-t)\rangle\langle j(-t)|\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{3}w_{j^{\prime}}|\tilde{j^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\tilde{j^{\prime}}|\emptyset\rangle\right]}{\Tr_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

(S39)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Tr_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\right]}\Tr_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\langle 0|+\langle 2|)\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}|j(-t)\rangle\langle j(-t)|\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{3}w_{j^{\prime}}|\tilde{j^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\tilde{j^{\prime}}|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\tilde{0}\rangle-|\tilde{2}\rangle)\right].

(S40)

From the definition of the pure-state decomposition,

\displaystyle\outerproduct{a}{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}\outerproduct{j}{j},

(S41)

\displaystyle\ket{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{\ket{a}}{\sqrt{\innerproduct{a}{a}}}+e^{ij\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\ket{b}}{\sqrt{\innerproduct{b}{b}}}\right),

(S42)

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\innerproduct{a}{a}\innerproduct{b}{b}}}{2}e^{ij\frac{\pi}{2}},

(S43)

we know that

\displaystyle w_{0}=\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle\quad w_{1}=\frac{i}{2},

\displaystyle\quad w_{2}=\frac{-1}{2},

\displaystyle\quad w_{3}=\frac{-i}{2},

(S44)

\displaystyle\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\emptyset}+\ket{k}),

\displaystyle\quad\ket{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\emptyset}+i\ket{k}),

\displaystyle\quad\ket{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\emptyset}-\ket{k}),

\displaystyle\quad\ket{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\emptyset}-i\ket{k}),

(S45)

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{0}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{k}+\ket{\emptyset}),

\displaystyle\quad\ket{\tilde{1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{k}+i\ket{\emptyset}),

\displaystyle\quad\ket{\tilde{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{k}-\ket{\emptyset}),

\displaystyle\quad\ket{\tilde{3}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{k}-i\ket{\emptyset}).

(S46)

Now, with the relations

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{0}}=\ket{0},

\displaystyle\quad\ket{\tilde{1}}=i\ket{3},

\displaystyle\quad\ket{\tilde{2}}=-\ket{2},

\displaystyle\quad\ket{\tilde{3}}=-i\ket{1},

\displaystyle\quad w_{1}\ket{3}+w_{3}\ket{1}=w_{0}(\ket{0}-\ket{2}),

(S47)

Eq. (
S40
) becomes

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}w_{0}\left(\innerproduct{0}{j(-t)}\innerproduct{j(-t)}{0}+\innerproduct{2}{j(-t)}\innerproduct{j(-t)}{0}\right)\right]}{\Tr_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

\displaystyle+\frac{\Tr_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}w_{2}\left(\innerproduct{0}{j(-t)}\innerproduct{j(-t)}{2}+\innerproduct{2}{j(-t)}\innerproduct{j(-t)}{2}\right)\right]}{\Tr_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}w_{0}\left(\innerproduct{0(t)}{j}\innerproduct{j}{0(t)}+\innerproduct{2(t)}{j}\innerproduct{j}{0(t)}\right)\right]}{\Tr_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

\displaystyle+\frac{\Tr_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}w_{2}\left(\innerproduct{0(t)}{j}\innerproduct{j}{2(t)}+\innerproduct{2(t)}{j}\innerproduct{j}{2(t)}\right)\right]}{\Tr_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]}

(S48)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr_{B}\left[e^{-\beta H_{B}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}w_{j}w_{0}\left(\innerproduct{j}{0(t)}\innerproduct{0(t)}{j}-\innerproduct{j}{2(t)}\innerproduct{2(t)}{j}+i\innerproduct{j}{+i(t)}\innerproduct{+i(t)}{j}-i\innerproduct{j}{-i(t)}\innerproduct{-i(t)}{j}\right)\right]}{\Tr_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}]},

(S49)

where

\displaystyle\ket{+i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+i\ket{2}),

(S50)

\displaystyle\ket{-i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}-i\ket{2}).

(S51)

Eq. (
S49
) allows one to compute MASH spectral function from the standard population estimator established in Ref.
1
. We compare numerical evaluations of the MFE (Fig.
(SM 2)
) and MASH (Fig.
(SM 3)
) spectral functions by Eq. (17) (standard approach) and Eq. (45) (pure-state decomposition) of the main text. We conclude that our method and the pure-state decomposition method give indistinguishable numerical results.

IV
Analytical Expressions for Spectral Functions of the Holstein Model at
J=0
and Zero Temperature

IV.1
The Exact Solution

Given the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
S2
) with
J=0
,

\displaystyle H=\sum_{n}\,g\omega_{0}\outerproduct{n}{n}(b_{n}+b_{n}^{\dagger})+\omega_{0}b_{n}^{\dagger}b_{n},

(S52)

we define the unitary operator,

\displaystyle U=\exp\left[\sum_{n}g\outerproduct{n}{n}(b^{\dagger}_{n}-b_{n})\right].

(S53)

At zero temperature,

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{B}}\bra{\emptyset}_{\text{ph}}\otimes\bra{k}e^{-iHt}\ket{k}\otimes\ket{\emptyset}_{\text{ph}}

(S54)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{B}}\bra{\emptyset}_{\text{ph}}\otimes\sum_{n}\bra{n}\frac{e^{-ikn}}{\sqrt{N}}U^{\dagger}Ue^{-iHt}U^{\dagger}U\sum_{n^{\prime}}\frac{e^{ikn^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{N}}\ket{n'}\otimes\ket{\emptyset}_{\text{ph}},

(S55)

where
\ket{\emptyset}_{\text{ph}}=\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\ket{\emptyset}_{\text{ph},i}
is the vacuum state for
N
local baths associated with
N
sites, and
\ket{n}=\ket{\emptyset}_{0}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{1}_{n}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\emptyset}_{N-1}
means an occupation of one electron on site
n
while the other sites remain empty.
We know that

\displaystyle U\ket{n}\otimes\ket{\emptyset}_{\text{ph}}

\displaystyle=\ket{n}e^{g(b^{\dagger}_{n}-b_{n})}\ket{\emptyset}_{\text{ph}}

(S56)

\displaystyle=\ket{n}\otimes\ket{g}_{n,\text{ph}}\otimes_{m\neq n}\ket{\emptyset}_{m,\text{ph}}

(S57)

\displaystyle=\ket{n}\otimes e^{-g^{2}/2}\sum_{\tilde{n}=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{\tilde{n}}}{\sqrt{\tilde{n}!}}\ket{\tilde{n}}_{n,\text{ph}}\otimes_{m\neq n}\ket{\emptyset}_{m,\text{ph}},

(S58)

where
\ket{g}
is a coherent state, and the tilde emphasizes the bosonic bath’s number basis instead of the site occupation basis.
Given that

\displaystyle UHU^{\dagger}=\sum_{n}-g^{2}\omega_{0}\outerproduct{n}{n}+\omega_{0}b^{\dagger}_{n}b_{n},

(S59)

we have

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{B}}\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\frac{e^{-ik(n-n^{\prime})}}{N}\bra{\emptyset}_{m\neq n,\text{ph}}\bra{g}_{n,\text{ph}}e^{-it\sum_{n}\omega_{0}b^{\dagger}_{n}b_{n}}\ket{g}_{n^{\prime},\text{ph}}\ket{\emptyset}_{m^{\prime}\neq n^{\prime},\text{ph}}\bra{n}e^{-it\sum_{n}-g^{2}\omega_{0}\ket{n}\bra{n}}\ket{n'}

(S60)

\displaystyle=e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{2n}}{n!}e^{-it\omega_{0}n}e^{itg^{2}\omega_{0}},

(S61)

and therefore,

\displaystyle A(\omega)=e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{2n}}{n!}\delta\left[\omega+g^{2}\omega_{0}-\omega_{0}n\right].

(S62)

IV.2
Mean Field Ehrenfest (MFE)

Given that,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{0}}}(b^{\dagger}_{n}+b_{n})

\displaystyle\rightarrow Q_{n},

(S63)

\displaystyle i\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}}(b^{\dagger}_{n}-b_{n})

\displaystyle\rightarrow P_{n},

(S64)

the mixed quantum-classical Hamiltonian at
J=0
is given by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}g\sqrt{2\omega_{0}^{3}}Q_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}^{2}Q_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}P_{n}^{2}.

(S65)

In MFE, to compute
C_{k}(t)
, we start from the initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{k}
, propagate this wavefunction to time
t
by
H_{S}+H_{SB}
, and take the overlap
\langle k|\psi(t)\rangle
. Since the Hamiltonian is diagonal in the site occupation basis, it is convenient to rewrite the wavefunction in the site basis as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{e^{ikn}}{\sqrt{N}}e^{-ig\sqrt{2\omega_{0}^{3}}\int_{0}^{t}Q_{n}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\ket{n}.

(S66)

As a consequence, the equations of motion for the bath variables are

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{n}

\displaystyle=-\bra{\psi(t)}\frac{\partial V}{\partial Q_{n}}\ket{\psi(t)}-\frac{\partial H_{\text{ph}}}{\partial Q_{n}}

(S67)

\displaystyle=-\frac{g\sqrt{2\omega^{3}_{0}}}{N}-\omega^{2}_{0}Q_{n},

(S68)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{n}

\displaystyle=P_{n}.

(S69)

Solving Eq. (
S68
) and Eq. (
S69
) for given initial position and momentum
Q_{0}
and
P_{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle Q_{n}(t)=\left(Q_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}}\frac{g}{N}\right)\cos\omega_{0}t+\frac{P_{0}}{\omega_{0}}\sin\omega_{0}t-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}}\frac{g}{N}.

(S70)

Since
Q_{n}(t)
does not depend on
n
, we drop the
n
index. Integrating
Q
over
dt
gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}Q(t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\left(Q_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}}\frac{g}{N}\right)\sin\omega_{0}t-\frac{P_{0}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\cos\omega_{0}t+\frac{P_{0}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}}\frac{g}{N}t.

(S71)

At zero temperature,
P_{0}
and
Q_{0}
are sampled from the Wigner function
\rho_{W}(P_{0},Q_{0})=\frac{1}{\pi}\exp\left[-P_{0}^{2}/\omega_{0}-Q_{0}^{2}\omega_{0}\right]
. Therefore, the correlation function has the form of

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\innerproduct{k}{\psi(t)}

(S72)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{N\pi}\int dP_{0}dQ_{0}e^{-P_{0}^{2}/\omega_{0}-Q_{0}^{2}\omega_{0}}e^{-ig\sqrt{2\omega_{0}^{3}}\int_{0}^{t}Q(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=e^{-g^{2}}\exp\left(g^{2}\cos\omega_{0}t\right)\exp\left(-\frac{2ig^{2}}{N}\sin\omega_{0}t\right)\exp\left(\frac{2ig^{2}}{N}\omega_{0}t\right).

(S73)

Although the correlation function in the
J=0
Holstein model is expected to be independent of
N
, MFE produces an
N
-dependent correlation function.
This arises because the initial state
\ket{k}
is formed from a superposition of populations on all
N
sites.
For the special case of
N=2
, Eq. (
S73
) reduces to

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-g^{2}\right)\exp\left(g^{2}e^{-i\omega_{0}t}\right)\exp\left(ig^{2}\omega_{0}t\right),

(S74)

and the spectral function is

\displaystyle A(\omega)=e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{2n}}{n!}\delta\left[\omega+g^{2}\omega_{0}-\omega_{0}n\right].

(S75)

Thus, when
N=2
, MFE gives the exact results for the Holstein model in the limit of
J=0
.

For general
N
, by the Jacobi-Anger expansion,

\displaystyle e^{z\cos\theta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{n}(z)e^{in\theta},

(S76)

\displaystyle e^{iz\sin\theta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(z)e^{in\theta},

(S77)

where
J_{n}
and
I_{n}
are
n
-th Bessel function and modified Bessel function of the first kind, respectively. Eq. (
S73
) becomes

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-g^{2}+2i\frac{g^{2}}{N}\omega_{0}t\right]\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{n}\left(g^{2}\right)e^{in\omega_{0}t}\sum_{n^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n^{\prime}}\left(-\frac{2}{N}g^{2}\right)e^{in^{\prime}\omega_{0}t},

(S78)

and the spectral function can be expressed as

\displaystyle A(\omega)=e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{m}\left(g^{2}\right)J_{n-m}\left(-\frac{2}{N}g^{2}\right)\delta\left[\omega+2\frac{g^{2}}{N}\omega_{0}+n\omega_{0}\right].

(S79)

At
N=2
, Eq. (
S79
) reduces to the exact spectral function:

\displaystyle A(\omega)

\displaystyle=e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{m}\left(g^{2}\right)J_{n-m}\left(-g^{2}\right)\delta\left[\omega+g^{2}\omega_{0}+n\omega_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-g^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{m}\left(g^{2}\right)J_{n-m}\left(-g^{2}\right)e^{i\omega t}e^{ig^{2}\omega_{0}t}e^{in\omega_{0}t}dt

(S80)

\displaystyle=e^{-g^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{m}\left(g^{2}\right)e^{im\omega_{0}t}J_{n-m}\left(-g^{2}\right)e^{i(n-m)\omega_{0}t}e^{i\omega t}e^{ig^{2}\omega_{0}t}dt

(S81)

\displaystyle=e^{-g^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{g^{2}\cos(\omega_0 t)}e^{-ig^{2}\sin(\omega_0 t)}e^{i\omega t}e^{ig^{2}\omega_{0}t}dt

(S82)

\displaystyle=e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{2n}}{n!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-in\omega_{0}t}e^{i\omega t}e^{ig^{2}\omega_{0}t}dt

(S83)

\displaystyle=e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{2n}}{n!}\delta\left[\omega+g^{2}\omega_{0}-n\omega_{0}\right].

(S84)

As shown in Fig.
(SM 4)
, the spectral function computed by MFE is dependent on the number of sites
N
, with
N=2
always producing exact results. When value of
g^{2}
is such that
g^{2}(\frac{2}{N}-1)
is an integer (as seen in the lower right panel of Fig.
(SM 4)
), MFE for any
N
will generate peaks that align with the exact solution. However, for
N\neq 2
, MFE introduces additional peaks that are not present in the exact results.

J=0
at zero temperature. Each panel represents a unique set of coupling strength
g
and bath frequency
\omega_{0}
across different lattice sizes. The red dashed lines represent the exact solution.

IV.3
Multi-State Mapping Approach to Surface Hopping (MASH)

Given the mixed quantum-classical Hamiltonian at
J=0
in Eq. (
S65
), it is evident that the Hamiltonian is diagonal in the site occupation basis. As a result, the adiabatic surfaces correspond directly to the site occupation states. The active surface,
\ket{\alpha}
, represents one of them and does not change during the time evolution. We can derive the equations of motion for the bath variables associated with this active site as

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\alpha\left|\frac{\partial V}{\partial Q_{\alpha}}\right|\alpha\right\rangle-\frac{\partial H_{\text{ph}}}{\partial Q_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=-g\sqrt{2\omega_{0}^{3}}-\omega_{0}^{2}Q_{\alpha},

(S85)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=P_{\alpha}.

(S86)

For other sites not the active surface,
n\neq\alpha
,

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{n}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\alpha\left|\frac{\partial V}{\partial Q_{n}}\right|\alpha\right\rangle-\frac{\partial H_{\text{ph}}}{\partial Q_{n}}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{0}^{2}Q_{n},

(S87)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{n}

\displaystyle=P_{n}.

(S88)

In MASH, the correlation function is expressed as

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)=\int d\vec{P}_{0}d\vec{Q}_{0}\rho_{W}(\vec{P}_{0},\vec{Q}_{0},\beta)\int d\vec{c}\rho_{k}(\vec{c})W_{kk}(t).

(S89)

To compute the correlation function
C_{k}(t)
, we use focused sampling to sample the initial wavefunction as follows,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(0)}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{k}}}\ket{\tilde{k}}+\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\ket{\tilde{\phi}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\neq\tilde{k}\\
q\neq\tilde{\phi}\end{subarray}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{q}}\ket{q},

(S90)

where

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{k}}

\displaystyle=m_{k}\ket{k}+m_{\phi}\ket{\phi},

(S91)

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde{\phi}}

\displaystyle=m_{\phi}\ket{k}-m_{k}\ket{\phi},

(S92)

and
m_{k},~m_{\phi}
are real numbers. Note the use of
N+1
in the
\alpha
and
\beta
coefficients to emphasize that the auxiliary state must be included here, and
N
is the size of the original system
without
the auxiliary state.
The wavefunction can be rewritten in the momentum basis as

\displaystyle|\psi(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{k}}}-m_{k}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)|\phi\rangle+\left(m_{k}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{k}}}+m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)\ket{k}+\sum_{q}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{q}}|q\rangle.

(S93)

Since the Hamiltonian is diagonal in real space, we rewrite the wavefunction in the site occupation basis and propagate it in time to find

\displaystyle|\Psi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{k}}}-m_{k}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)|\phi\rangle+\sum_{n}e^{-ig\sqrt{2\omega_{0}^{3}}\int_{0}^{t}Q_{n}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\left(m_{k}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}N}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{k}}}e^{-ikn}+m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}N}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}e^{-ikn}+\sum_{q}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}N}}e^{i\theta_{q}}e^{-iqn}\right)|n\rangle.

(S94)

In the momentum basis, the wavefunction becomes

\displaystyle|\psi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{k}}}-m_{k}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}\right)|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{n}\frac{1}{N}e^{-ig\sqrt{2\omega_{0}^{3}}\int_{0}^{t}Q_{n}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\sum_{k}\left(m_{k}\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{k}}}+m_{\phi}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{\tilde{\phi}}}+\sum_{q}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{N+1}}{\alpha_{N+1}}}e^{i\theta_{q}}e^{i(k-q)n}\right)|k\rangle.

(S95)

Plugging in the coefficients
c_{k}(0)
from Eq. (
S93
) and
c_{k}(t)
from Eq. (
S95
), and integrating over the phases, we find

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int dP_{0}dQ_{0}e^{-P^{2}_{0}/\omega_{0}}e^{-Q^{2}_{0}\omega_{0}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\vec{\theta}\frac{c_{k}(\vec{\theta},P_{0},Q_{0},t)}{c_{k}(\vec{\theta},t=0)}

(S96)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\int dP_{0}dQ_{0}e^{-P_{0}^{2}/\omega_{0}}e^{-Q_{0}^{2}\omega_{0}}\frac{e^{-ig\sqrt{2\omega_{0}^{3}}\int_{0}^{t}Q_{n}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}}{\pi N}

(S97)

It is evident that
C_{k}(t)
and the corresponding spectral function are expressed as a sum of two components: one dependent on
Q_{\alpha}(t)
, and the other on
Q_{n}(t)
, where
n
refers to a site that is not the active surface which remains unchanged throughout the propagation.

IV.3.1
Spectral function for state
\ket{\alpha}

Solving Eq. (
S85
) and Eq. (
S86
) gives

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(Q_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}}g\right)\cos\omega_{0}t+\frac{P_{0}}{\omega_{0}}\sin\omega_{0}t-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}}g

(S98)

Therefore,

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\exp\left[-g^{2}\right]\exp\left[g^{2}\cos\omega_{0}t\right]\exp\left[-i2g^{2}\sin\omega_{0}t\right]\exp\left[i2g^{2}\omega_{0}t\right],

(S99)

and

\displaystyle A_{\alpha,k}(\omega)=\frac{1}{N}e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{m}\left(g^{2}\right)J_{n-m}\left(-2g^{2}\right)\delta\left[\omega+2g^{2}\omega_{0}+\omega_{0}n\right],

(S100)

where
J_{n}
and
I_{n}
are the
n^{th}
Bessel function and modified Bessel function of the first kind, respectively.

IV.3.2
Spectral function for state
|n\neq\alpha\rangle

Similarly, solving Eq. (
S87
) and Eq. (
S88
) gives

\displaystyle Q_{n}(t)=Q_{0}\cos\omega_{0}t+\frac{P_{0}}{\omega_{0}}\sin\omega_{0}t,

(S101)

which leads to

\displaystyle C_{n,k}(t)=\frac{N-1}{N}e^{-g^{2}}\exp\left[g^{2}\cos\omega t\right]

(S102)

and

\displaystyle A_{n,k}(\omega)=\frac{N-1}{N}e^{-g^{2}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{n}\left(g^{2}\right)\delta[\omega+\omega_{0}n].

(S103)

Fig.
(SM 5)
illustrates that MASH fails to produce accurate results across all parameter regimes. Although it generates peaks that coincide with the exact solution when
g^{2}
is an integer (as shown in the lower right panel of Fig.
(SM 5)
), it also introduces additional peaks that are absent in the exact results. When
g^{2}
is not an integer and
N>1
, two distinct series of peaks emerge: one originating from the active site, as described in Eq. (
S100
), and the other from the remaining sites, as described in Eq. (
S103
). As
N
increases, the peaks from the remaining sites grow in weight, leading to a broadening effect evident in the upper panels and lower left panel of Fig.
(SM 5)
.

g
and bath frequency
\omega_{0}
across different lattice sizes. The red dashed lines represent the exact solution.

V
Variational Monte Carlo with neural quantum states

Here we provide a brief description of the VMC method with a NN ansatz. We refer the reader to Ref.
[
3
]
for more details. The NN wave function is given by

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{\textbf{n}}\frac{\exp\left[f(\textbf{n})\right]}{\sqrt{\prod_{i}\nu_{i}!}}\ket{\mathbf{n}},.

(S104)

Here
\textbf{n}=(\left\{e_{i}\right\},\left\{\nu_{i}\right\})
is a configuration, where
\left\{e_{i}\right\}
and
\left\{\nu_{i}\right\}
are electron and phonon occupation numbers, respectively. The function
f
is a sum of two neural nets:

f(\textbf{n})=r(\textbf{n})+i\phi(\textbf{n}).

(S105)

We use multilayer perceptron NNs with real-valued weights and biases in this work. Properties of this wave function, such as the energy, are evaluated using Monte Carlo sampling. The wave function parameters are optimized using a generalized gradient descent method to minimize the energy.

We calculate spectral functions by working in the tangent space of the optimized ground state wave function (
\ket{\psi_0}
) with basis functions given by

\ket{\psi_{\mu}}=\frac{\partial\ket{\psi_0}}{\partial p_{\nu}}.

(S106)

where
p_{\mu}
are the optimized variational parameters. The resulting basis is nonorthogonal and has linear dependencies. Thus to obtain the excited states and spectral functions, we solve the generalized eigenvalue problem

\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}=E\mathbf{S}\mathbf{C},

(S107)

where the Hamiltonian and overlap matrices are given by

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{\mu\nu}&=\langle\psi_{\mu}|H|\psi_{\nu}\rangle,\\
\mathbf{S}_{\mu\nu}&=\langle\psi_{\mu}|\psi_{\nu}\rangle.\end{split}

(S108)

We use the eigenspectrum of this effective Hamiltonian
\left\{E_{i},\ket{i}\right\}
to calculate the one-particle spectral function at zero temperature as

\begin{split}A(k,\omega)&=-\frac{1}{\pi}\text{Im}\langle 0|c_{k}\frac{1}{\omega-H+i\eta}c^{\dagger}_{k}|0\rangle\\
&=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{i}|\langle 0|c_{k}|i\rangle|^{2}\frac{\eta}{(\omega-E_{i})^{2}+\eta^{2}},\\
\end{split}

(S109)

where
\eta
is a Lorentzian broadening parameter.
