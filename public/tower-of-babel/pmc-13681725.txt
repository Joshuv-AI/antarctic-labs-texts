Early catastrophic posterior migration of a cervical disc replacement polyethylene insert resulting in complete spinal cord injury: illustrative case
Authors: Pirooz Fereydouni, Arthur W. Cowman, Matthew J. Strok, Sultan Baz, Shahabeddin Yazdanpanah, Ryan Hoang, Michael S. Kim, Sohaib Z. Hashmi, Don Y. Park, Nitin N. Bhatia, Yu-Po Lee, Hao-Hua Wu

Cervical disc replacement (CDR) has emerged as a motion-­preserving alternative to anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) for select patients with cervical radiculopathy and cervical myelopathy.1 While CDR generally demonstrates favorable outcomes, failure rates of approximately 4% have been reported across prospective studies, and the mechanisms by which CDR fails remain incompletely characterized.2 Zavras et al. proposed a six-type classification of CDR failure, including recurrent stenosis, migration, mechanical loosening, device motion loss, implantation error, and wear, identifying migration as a potentially catastrophic failure mode.2

The Mobi-C (LDR Spine USA) is a three-component unconstrained prosthesis consisting of superior and inferior cobalt-chromium-­molybdenum endplates and an ultra-high-molecular-weight ­unattached mobile polyethylene insert. The insert articulates with a spherical superior surface and a flat inferior surface, while lateral stops on the inferior plate limit translation. Rows of teeth provide initial fixation, and titanium and hydroxyapatite coatings support longer-term bone integration.3 The surgical technique emphasizes preservation of the vertebral endplates and discourages burr use during endplate preparation to avoid weakening them. Release of the posterior longitudinal ligament may assist in achieving parallel distraction, although the extent of decompression is left to surgeon discretion.3 Proper sizing and implant alignment are important for achieving optimal surgical outcomes. Reported failures specific to the Mobi-C device have included symptomatic segmental kyphosis due to anterior endplate slippage and at least 1 case of nucleus migration causing spinal cord compression.4,5 However, early posterior displacement and vertical flipping of the Mobi-C polyethylene insert into the spinal canal resulting in irreversible complete spinal cord injury (SCI) after recent trauma and documented loosening has not, to our knowledge, been previously reported. We present this case to both highlight a catastrophic CDR failure pattern with potentially identifiable early radiographic warning signs and emphasize the clinical implications of posttraumatic radiographic instability, which may warrant urgent, rather than elective, surgical intervention.

Illustrative Case

Presentation and Initial Imaging

A 48-year-old female underwent two-level Mobi-C CDR at C5–6 and C6–7 at an outside hospital 3 weeks before presentation to the emergency department with a sudden onset of complete motor and sensory loss below the mid-chest after completing household chores involving lifting a laundry basket. Eight days prior to presentation to the emergency department, she had sustained a ground-level fall that resulted in radiographic imaging demonstrating loosening of the cervical implants (Table 1). Postfall anteroposterior (AP), lateral, and swimmer’s radiographs from the outside institution are shown in Fig. 1. Both prostheses were abnormal. On the lateral radiograph, the C6–7 endplates were nonparallel, and the endplates appeared posteriorly translated, concerning for partial migration into the spinal canal. At C5–6, the lateral radiograph showed endplates that were divergent in an anteriorly open, fish-mouth configuration. The AP radiograph demonstrated abnormal angulation and separation of the C6–7 endplates. The index surgeon suggested that an elective revision surgery should be performed, but an urgent/emergency surgery was not performed by the index surgeon. The patient’s medical history was notable for hypothyroidism, chronic pain disorder, and prior spinal surgeries, including L2–3 posterior spinal instrumented fusion and placement of a spinal cord stimulator.

On examination at our institution, she was alert but in mild distress with bradycardia (heart rate 44 bpm) and hypotension. Neurological examination revealed intact strength and sensation in the C5–7 distributions, with complete motor (0/5) and sensory loss below C7 bilaterally, absent rectal tone, and absent perianal sensation, consistent with American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade A complete SCI at approximately the C7 neurological level. CT of the cervical spine demonstrated catastrophic failure of both Mobi-C prostheses: the C5–6 implant showed an anterior fish-mouthed configuration, and at C6–7 the endplates were posteriorly displaced into the canal causing severe spinal canal stenosis, with the polyethylene insert not visualized (Figs. 2 and 3). Preoperative MRI was not obtained given the emergency need for immediate surgical decompression and the presence of the patient’s spinal cord stimulator, which would have rendered MRI unsafe or significantly delayed imaging acquisition. Surgical Intervention

The patient underwent emergency revision C5–7 ACDF via a standard anterior approach through the prior right-sided incision with removal of the failed arthroplasty devices and placement of structural allograft interbodies. Intraoperatively, both Mobi-C disc replacements at C5–6 and C6–7 were grossly loose with no evidence of osseointegration. Significant endplate destruction was noted bilaterally at both operative levels, with the appearance of over-burred endplates suggestive of aggressive endplate preparation at the time of the index procedure; underlying bone quality was otherwise reasonable. The explanted components were not sent for formal analysis; therefore, polyethylene wear, component deformation, or device fracture could not be assessed. Based on gross intraoperative inspection, this failure pattern is most consistent with a fixation failure due to loss of osseointegration rather than a primary component failure. The C6–7 polyethylene insert had rotated and vertically flipped posteriorly into the spinal canal and was gently removed. All hardware was removed, structural allograft interbodies were placed at both levels, and anterior cervical plating was performed from C5 to C7. Postoperative radiographs obtained at the approximately 5-week follow-up demonstrated stable-appearing, revised C5–7 ACDF instrumentation with maintained hardware integrity (Fig. 4). Postoperative Protocol and Follow-Up

Despite timely decompression and stabilization, the patient remained C7 ASIA grade A postoperatively. She completed standard SCI rehabilitation and required midodrine for neurogenic hypotension. At the 1-year follow-up, she demonstrated no neurological recovery.

Discussion

Observations

CDR has emerged as a preferred alternative to ACDF for patients requiring surgical intervention for cervical radiculopathy or myelopathy, offering motion preservation and reduced rates of adjacent segment degeneration.1 Zavras et al. identified migration (type II) and mechanical loosening (type IIIA) as distinct but interrelated failure modes, noting that loosening from host bone can progress to gross extrusion with continued cyclic loading.2 DiCesare et al. reported 4 cases of Mobi-C failure presenting as symptomatic segmental kyphosis with anterior endplate slippage, none involving posterior canal compromise or acute neurological injury.4 The present case is mechanically distinct: rather than anterior slippage, the defining feature is posterior displacement and vertical flipping of the polyethylene core directly into the spinal canal after recent trauma and documented loosening, resulting in irreversible complete SCI. This failure pattern has not been previously characterized in the cervical or lumbar disc replacement literature.6–9

The Mobi-C consists of three unlinked components: superior and inferior cobalt-chromium-molybdenum endplates and an ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene mobile insert, constrained by axial compressive forces and implant geometry. Unlike constrained two-piece CDR devices in which the core is fixed to an endplate, the Mobi-C mobile core depends on maintained osseointegration and axial loading to remain constrained. In this case, the most plausible sequence of failure is that the ground-level fall disrupted the bone-implant interface; subsequent endplate migration and loss of parallel alignment reduced axial constraint; and a later low-energy lifting event precipitated posterior dislocation and vertical flipping of the polyethylene insert. Once osseointegration failed and the endplates migrated, the absence of any mechanical linkage between components may have allowed the polyethylene core to escape posteriorly.

Limitations

One limitation of this case report is the lack of imaging prior to and immediately after CDR. The index C5–7 CDR was performed at an outside institution that declined to provide imaging or medical records because of active litigation. Postfall radiographs were provided by the patient. Preoperative MRI at our institution was not obtained because of the emergency presentation and the presence of a spinal cord stimulator, which would have made MRI unsafe or substantially delayed imaging acquisition. The explanted components were also not sent for formal analysis, limiting our ability to distinguish fixation failure from component-level failure definitively. Accordingly, the proposed failure mechanism remains a clinically grounded inference rather than a proven mechanical conclusion.

Lessons

The most important clinical lesson from this previously unreported Mobi-C CDR failure mode is that posttraumatic radiographic evidence of posterior endplate translation, loss of endplate parallelism, or change in polyethylene core position may represent early warning signs of catastrophic failure that can result in irreversible SCI and should prompt urgent surgical evaluation and likely revision. This patient presented to the index surgeon after the fall and received AP, lateral, and swimmer’s view radiographs that demonstrated loosening, loss of parallelism of endplates, and posterior migration of C6–7 endplates. The patient was scheduled for elective revision but sustained complete irreversible SCI while lifting a laundry basket 8 days after the fall. Unlike ACDF constructs, mobile-bearing CDR devices with posttraumatic migration may be at immediate risk for component dissociation and canal compromise. In cases such as this, the window for preventive intervention may be days rather than weeks, underscoring the potential importance of timely recognition. Accordingly, further characterization of this failure mode, such as through future studies and national device registry use, is needed to define associated risk factors and potential radiographic thresholds warranting such emergency intervention.
