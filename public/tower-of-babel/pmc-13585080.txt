Comparative Evaluation of Transverse Maxillary Arch Changes in Mixed Dentition with Clear Aligners vs Conventional Maxillary Expanders: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Sayali Athawale, Nikita Mohelay, Puneet Batra, Aditya Talwar, Ashish Kumar Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo systematically evaluate and compare the effects of clear aligner therapy (CAT) vs conventional maxillary expanders [rapid maxillary expansion (RME) and slow maxillary expansion (SME)] in treating maxillary arch constriction during mixed dentition with a focus on transverse arch parameters, including intercanine and intermolar widths, molar inclination, arch perimeter, and palatal depth.Materials and methodsElectronic searches of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Medline upto August 2025 identified 341 records. Studies included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective and retrospective cohorts assessing ME outcomes with clear aligners and conventional RME/SME expanders in mixed dentition patients. Outcomes included intercanine width, intermolar width, arch perimeter, arch width and depth, molar inclination, and palatal volume. Risk of bias was evaluated using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) for RCTs and the Methodological Index for Nonrandomized Studies (MINORS) for nonrandomized studies.ResultsEight studies met inclusion criteria, comprising a total of 412 patients aged 8.4 ± 1.3 years. Meta-analysis showed a significant trend favoring maxillary expanders over CAT for both intercanine width expansion [−0.54; 95% confidence interval (CI): −1.17, 0.09; p = 0.09] and intermolar width [−1.01 (95% CI: −1.59, −0.43; p = 0.0006)]. CAT demonstrated superior control over molar inclination with reduced buccal tipping. Heterogeneity varied across outcomes, largely owing to differences in study designs and protocols. Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) assessment revealed overall very low quality of evidence with moderate risk of bias.ConclusionRME demonstrated significantly greater increases in intermolar width compared to CAT, while intercanine width changes were comparable between treatments. Although both modalities effectively address transverse deficiencies in mixed dentition, they differ in expansion patterns and dental effects, with CAT offering more controlled dentoalveolar expansion and minimal molar tipping.How to cite this articleAthawale S, Mohelay N, Batra P, et al. Introduction

Transverse discrepancies represent a significant subset of malocclusions frequently encountered in clinical orthodontics, exhibiting prevalence rates ranging from 7.5% to 22% in mixed dentition and 10% to 14% in permanent dentition.1 These discrepancies typically manifest as unilateral or bilateral posterior crossbites and are associated with compromised occlusal function, aesthetic concerns, and potentially deleterious effects on dentofacial development.

The timely diagnosis and judicious management of transverse maxillary deficiencies during the mixed dentition period are paramount. Such interventions are crucial for mitigating posterior crossbites, facilitating the eruption of impacted or ectopically positioned teeth, enhancing nasal airway patency to support optimal respiratory function, aiding in the correction of class II malocclusions with mandibular locking, and, critically, potentially obviating or reducing the necessity for more invasive future interventions or surgical procedures.2,3 These objectives underscore the importance of early and appropriate therapeutic approaches to optimize occlusal development and facial harmony.4–6

Orthopedic expansion of the maxillary arch constitutes the primary approach for managing maxillary transverse deficiencies during the mixed dentition phase, typically implemented through either rapid maxillary expansion (RME) or slow maxillary expansion (SME) protocols. The selection between these two treatment modalities is dependent upon several factors, including the stage of midpalatal suture maturation, the magnitude and rate of force application, the specific expansion appliance utilized, the screw activation regimen, and the anticipated duration of therapy.5 Biomechanically and biologically, these approaches differ, with RME employing heavy, intermittent forces designed to induce midpalatal suture separation over a brief activation period, whereas SME utilizes continuous, low-force activation, fostering more gradual expansion over an extended duration, which promotes sutural and alveolar bone remodeling.

Seminal investigations, such as the Michigan Expansion Study, have yielded critical insights into the efficacy and stability of RME in mixed dentition, demonstrating a statistically significant increase in maxillary arch widths with sustained post-treatment stability.7 Conversely, SME, characterized by more gentle force application, is generally perceived to offer advantages in terms of patient comfort and tissue adaptation. Despite this, its long-term comparative effectiveness and stability relative to RME have been broadly reported as clinically equivalent.

Although both rapid and slow expansion methods are equally effective for correcting transverse maxillary deficiency, the literature suggests that differences in maxillary intercanine and interpremolar widths postexpansion and retention were not statistically significant between the two treatment protocols.3,8,9

Traditionally, both RME and SME have been administered using cumbersome palatal expansion devices, including banded, screw-activated expanders, and appliances such as the Haas or Hyrax types.10,11 While effective, these conventional appliances are frequently associated with various challenges that can negatively impact patient compliance and oral health. Common issues encompass discomfort, mucosal ulcerations, difficulties with speech and mastication, the stringent requirement for adherence to activation schedules, and complications in maintaining adequate oral hygiene, all of which can influence treatment outcomes and patient satisfaction. In recent years, however, the orthodontic paradigm has evolved with the advent of clear aligner therapy (CAT) systems, which are capable of effectively facilitating maxillary arch expansion. This innovation potentially offers a more comfortable and convenient alternative, thereby expanding the scope of interceptive treatment.3,12–15

In skeletally growing patients, maxillary arch expansion achieved with clear aligners primarily results in an increased interarch width between the deciduous canine and first molar in the primary dentition, alongside buccal tipping of the permanent molars, with an approximate mean predictability of 80%.16–18 Furthermore, clear aligners have demonstrated superior control over molar inclination when compared to slow palatal expanders.19

Despite these emerging benefits, the literature remains limited on the comparative dentoalveolar effects of CAT vs conventional RME and SME appliances in children during the mixed dentition. It is also imperative to consider specific parameters such as intercanine and intermolar widths, molar inclination, arch perimeter, arch depth, and palatal volume when assessing changes in transverse dimensions, as these factors collectively influence the success of orthodontic interventions and patient quality of life.17

The present systematic review aims to compare the dentoalveolar outcomes associated with CAT vs RME and SME in growing patients. By synthesizing studies evaluating changes in transverse dimension metrics—including intercanine width, interdeciduous first and second molar width, molar inclination, arch perimeter, arch depth, and palatal volume—we seek to establish an evidence-based foundation for refined clinical decision-making and patient-centered management during the mixed dentition phase.

Materials and Methods

Protocol Registration

The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024586786) https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024586786. The review was carried out according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) criteria.

Focused Research Question and Eligibility Criteria

This systematic review was designed to assess the following research question based on the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, and Study design (PICOS) framework: “Is there a change in transverse arch dimensions with clear aligner expansion and conventional maxillary palatal expanders (slow/rapid) in mixed dentition?”

Exclusion Criteria

Opinion pieces, systematic reviews, abstracts only, narrative reviews, scoping reviews, and descriptive research were excluded. Studies that utilized methods like surgical or implant-assisted maxillary arch expansion and other modalities that did not describe the arch width changes were excluded.

Study Selection

The initial search was applied by two researchers independently (Sayali Athawale and Nikita Mohelay). It revealed 341 articles, including 251 articles in PubMed, 70 articles in Scopus, and 120 in EBSCO [Details of the PRISMA are given in Figure 1]. After duplicate removal, all the titles and abstracts were screened for relevance based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and reviewed in detail. Any discordance in any of the data was discussed with a third researcher (Puneet Batra), and resolution was reached after reaching a consensus, which yielded the final eight articles.

PRISMA flow diagram for study selection and inclusion in analysis

Data Extraction

The characteristics and data of the selected studies were systematically extracted and entered in a predesigned participant characteristics form with the following items: study design, intervention, outcome, and conclusions by the two independent assessors (Sayali Athawale and Nikita Mohelay).

Risk of Bias Assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed through the Methodological Index for Nonrandomized Studies (MINORS) and the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) by two assessors (Sayali Athawale and Nikita Mohelay) independently and in duplicate, and any disagreement was resolved with additional discussion.

Quality of Evidence

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was applied independently by two reviewers (Sayali Athawale and Aditya Talwar) to evaluate the quality of evidence across the included studies. Each reviewer assessed each study across the different domains for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias for each main outcome. Discrepancies in ratings were resolved with a third reviewer (Nikita Mohelay).

Synthesis of Results

The original results data were subjected to meta-analysis using the RevMan 5.4 software (Cochrane IMS). For the aggregation of continuous data, total sample size was extracted, with the mean and standard deviation (SD) converted into standardized mean difference (SMD) and standard error (SE) using Hedges' method to account for variation in measurement scales. The inverse of variance method and a 95% confidence interval (CI) were reported. The results were also reported with a forest plot. Heterogeneity was statistically evaluated by means of a chi-square test, in which a p-value < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant heterogeneity. It was also quantified using the I2 index. Substantial heterogeneity was represented by values above 75%.

Furthermore, funnel plots were planned to explore the presence of any publication bias for the studies included in the meta-analysis, and leave-one-out analysis was carried out to investigate the influence of each study on the overall effect size estimate.

Study Characteristics

Table 1 shows the demographic characteristics of the included studies. The eight studies were published in the English language between 2021 and 2025. Among the eight studies included in the meta-analysis, seven studies were published in peer-reviewed journals, and one study was a dissertation thesis. Study samples ranged from 30 to 42 individuals, obtaining a total sample of n = 412, with an average age range of 8.4 ± 1.3 years.

Three studies (Lombardo et al.,20 Lombardo et al.,21 Bruni et al.22) out of all included studies were based in Italy, two studies (Torbaty et al.,23 and Zaverdinos et al.24) were based in the USA, a study by Wang et al.19 was based in Canada, one study (Lu et al.25) was based in China, one study (Pamukcu et al.26) was based in Turkey.

Authors of most studies used digital study models for assessment of transverse arch dimension parameters. With the data available, it was possible to analyze the following outcomes: 1) intercanine width, 2) intermolar width, 3) interdeciduous molar widths, 4) molar inclination, 5) arch perimeter, 6) arch depth, 7) palatal volume.

Quantitative Synthesis of ME Therapy

The results of the quantitative analysis of ME therapy vs CAT were considered as two subgroups for different parameters. For intercanine width (Fig. 2), the pooled SMD was −0.54 (95% CI: −1.17, 0.09; p = 0.09), indicating a statistically significant difference between CAT and maxillary expanders (SME/RME). Subgroup analysis revealed a significant difference between RME and SME (p = 0.05), though heterogeneity was high (I2 = 85%). For intermolar width (Fig. 3), the overall SMD was −1.01 (95% CI: −1.59, −0.43; p = 0.0006), favoring RME/SME over CAT, suggesting that conventional expansion methods produced a greater transverse increase in intermolar dimension. Both RME [SMD = −1.06 (−1.89, −0.23); p = 0.01] and SME [SMD = −0.95 (−1.95, 0.05); p = 0.06] groups exhibited similar trends. Similarly, for second deciduous intermolar width (Fig. 4), the meta-analysis demonstrated a statistically significant overall effect favoring RME over CAT, with an SMD of −0.59 (95% CI: −0.94, −0.25; p = 0.0008). While molar inclination (Fig. 5), a statistically significant difference was found between CAT and conventional maxillary expanders (RME/SME), with a pooled SMD of 1.42 (95% CI: −0.64, 3.47; p = 0.18). Subgroup analyses of RME [SMD = 4.46 (−3.02, 11.94); p = 0.24] and SME [SMD = −0.90 (−1.98, 0.18); p = 0.10] also demonstrated nonsignificant results, although heterogeneity remained very high (I2 = 96%).

Risk of Bias

The quality of the included studies was assessed through the MINORS and RoB 2.

Figure 6 enlists the included studies with their 12 assessment criteria, which were scored 0 (not reported), 1 (reported but inadequate), or 2 (reported and adequate) depending on the information contained in each article. The majority of the included literature had a high risk of bias, as adequate information with regard to loss of follow-up, sample size calculation, biased assessment of endpoint, and baseline equivalence of the control group was lacking. All the overall scores of the nonrandomized studies were evaluated based on the following cutoff points: <14 points (poor quality), 15–22 points (moderate quality), and 23–24 points (good quality), revealed an overall moderate risk of bias, with total scores ranging from 11 to 16 out of 24. The highest methodological quality was observed in the studies by Zaverdinos et al.,24 Wang et al.,19 and Torbaty et al.,23 each scoring 16, followed closely by Lu et al.,25 and Pamukçu et al.,26 which scored 15. Lombardo et al.21 demonstrated intermediate quality (13/24), whereas Lombardo et al.20 presented the lowest score (11/24), reflecting greater methodological limitations.

Risk of bias assessment using the MINORS tool

The ROB 2 risk of bias assessment for the study by Bruni et al.22 reveals an overall judgment of “low risk of bias” as depicted in Figure 7. Across the five domains evaluated, they exhibited a low risk of bias in three domains: bias arising from the randomization process (D1), bias due to deviations from intended interventions (D2), and bias due to missing outcome data (D3), indicating strong methodological rigor in these aspects. However, “some concerns” in the domains of bias in measurement of the outcome (D4) and bias in selection of the reported result (D5) were also noted. These concerns suggest potential issues related to outcome assessment procedures and selective reporting that may have influenced the interpretation of findings (Fig. 7).

Quality of Evidence

Using the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence for the meta-analyzed outcomes was rated very low, primarily because most of the included studies were nonrandomized in design (retrospective), which inherently provides lower-quality evidence. In addition, the presence of substantial heterogeneity across studies and indications of publication bias further downgraded the confidence in the pooled estimates, limiting the overall strength and generalizability of the findings (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis critically evaluates transverse maxillary arch changes attributable to CAT vs conventional SME and RME modalities in patients during the mixed dentition phase. Eight selected studies19–26 involving a total of 412 subjects with an average age of 8.4 ± 1.3 years were synthesized, providing nuances of transverse arch development achievable through these diverse treatment modalities. Various dentoalveolar parameters were analyzed, including intercanine width, intermolar width, arch perimeter, arch width and depth, molar inclination, and palatal volume. Additional measurements such as interdeciduous molar widths, transpalatal dimensions, and dentoalveolar widths were also considered to provide a comprehensive view of the transverse effects of both treatment modalities.

Overall, the evidence indicates that both CAT and conventional expanders are effective in producing transverse dentoalveolar changes. However, the quality of reported outcomes is heterogeneous and often graded as weak to moderate, thereby reinforcing the need for rigorously designed, high-quality trials and research in this area.

The biomechanical principles of RME and SME diverge significantly. RME applies high intermittent forces through protocols involving multiple daily activations (commonly ¼ turn or 0.25 mm once or twice per day) to achieve disarticulation of the midpalatal suture over a relatively short treatment duration averaging 6–8 months, combining active expansion and retention phases.

Conversely, SME employs gentler continuous forces activated less frequently (ranging from one quarter turn every 2–7 days), producing slower dental and alveolar adaptation typically extending treatment times to approximately 9–10 months. CAT adopts a sequential staging protocol with incremental expansions of 0.25 mm per stage combined with buccal root torque, an approach that transitions molars initially before targeting simultaneous posterior deciduous teeth and canines, usually completed within a 6-month treatment window.

While most studies compared the effect of RME/SME vs CAT, three studies reported the effect of maxillary arch transverse width with untreated controls as well.19,23,25 The study concluded with an increase in arch width being considerably higher in the intervention group than in the controls. However, the skeletal changes were not assessed in the present systematic review, as all of the included studies had measured the dentoalveolar changes on dental casts with no skeletal parameters.

Intercanine Width

All studies documented a statistically significant increase in intercanine width between T0 and T1 in both CAT and RME groups (p < 0.05). Bruni et al.22 reported a greater increase in the RME group, though the difference was not statistically significant when compared to CAT. In contrast, Lombardo et al.21 observed greater anterior width expansion in the CAT group, but without statistical reporting. Similarly, SME protocol in studies by Wang et al.,19 Zaverdinos et al.,24 and Pamukçu et al.26 confirmed significant increases in both groups with greater expansion with SME protocol, reinforcing the ability of both appliance systems to facilitate anterior transverse development. These findings further indicate that CAT, despite being primarily designed for tooth movement, may also contribute to transverse adaptation at the palatal level when used early and strategically.

Intermolar Width

Intermolar width, particularly at the first molar mesial and distal cusp tips, was assessed in all studies. Lu et al.,25 Bruni et al.,22 Lombardo et al.,20,21 and Torbaty et al.23 documented statistically significant increases (p < 0.05) between RME and CAT groups, with a slightly higher increase in the RME group. Lombardo et al.20,21 also observed an increase in both groups but did not report on statistical significance. In the SME vs CAT, Pamukcu et al.,26 Wang et al.,19 and Zaverdinos et al.24 also observed a statistically significant increase in the transverse maxillary dimensions.

Interdeciduous Molar Widths

The first and second interdeciduous molar widths, representing early posterior arch development, were assessed by Wang et al.,19 Zaverdinos et al.,24 and Pamukçu et al.26 with the SME protocol and Bruni et al.,22 Lombardo et al.,20,21 Lu et al.,25 and Torbaty et al.23 with the RME protocol and comparing with CAT. Although all groups show an increase in transverse arch form from T0 to T1. The changes suggest that aligners, when used in the mixed dentition phase, can guide transverse development in primary molar regions similar to RME/SME appliances.

Molar Inclination

Differences in molar inclination between groups were particularly noteworthy. Lu et al.25 observed a statistically significant decrease in inclination, with the RME group exhibiting greater changes (p = 0.001), likely reflecting buccal tipping of molars secondary to lateral force application. Pamukçu et al.26 and Wang et al.19 noted a nonsignificant increase in molar inclination in the CAT group and an increase in the SME group. Lombardo et al.21 also reported a decrease in molar inclination in the CAT group, suggesting that clear aligners may allow for bodily molar movement with reduced undesired tipping.

Arch Perimeter and Width

All studies reported an increase in arch perimeter following treatment. Lu et al.25 found a more substantial increase in the RME group (p = 0.001) than in the CAT group (p = 0.003). Bruni et al.22 supported these findings, showing greater perimeter gains in the RME group, whereas Lombardo et al.20,21 observed the opposite, with greater changes in the CAT group. Pamukçu et al.26 confirmed significant improvements in both groups (p < 0.005). This variability suggests that arch perimeter changes are influenced not only by appliance type but also by factors such as initial arch form, patient age, and the extent of crowding.

Arch width increased significantly in both groups in Bruni et al.,22 with RME demonstrating more prominent changes, while Lombardo et al.21 reported greater changes in the clear aligner group (CAG). These findings align with the general understanding that RME promotes skeletal expansion, while aligners primarily induce dentoalveolar widening.

Arch Depth

Arch depth, reflecting sagittal arch development, showed mixed results. Lu et al.25 reported a statistically significant increase in the CAT group (p = 0.041) and a nonsignificant increase in the RME group (p = 0.065), suggesting that aligners may offer better sagittal arch control. In contrast, Pamukçu et al.26 did not observe statistically significant changes in either group (p > 0.005), possibly due to limited sample sizes or measurement variability. Nevertheless, these findings imply that CAT may provide modest improvements in arch length and incisor positioning without aggressive skeletal modification.

Palatal Volume

Changes in palatal volume were evaluated in studies by Bruni et al.22 and Lu et al.25 These studies documented volume increases in both groups, with significantly greater changes observed in the RME group. Lu et al.25 reported that anterior palatal volume increased more prominently with RME than CAT. These findings are consistent with the skeletal mode of action of RME, which expands the midpalatal suture, whereas aligners primarily remodel the dentoalveolar complex.27

Limitations

The reviewed evidence does face some limitations. Most included studies assessed dentoalveolar parameters via dental casts or digital study models without direct evaluation of skeletal changes using three-dimensional imaging or cephalometry, limiting the ability to discriminate between skeletal and dental contributions to expansion. Heterogeneity in study designs, expansion protocols, measurement techniques, and small sample sizes impede the generalizability of findings and caution in extrapolation. Additionally, the moderate to high risk of bias noted in many studies reduces confidence in definitive conclusions. Crucially, there is a paucity of long-term follow-up data evaluating stability and relapse tendencies, a vital aspect given the ongoing craniofacial growth processes during mixed dentition and their influence on treatment outcomes.

Future Recommendations

Large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled studies with standardized 3D imaging and measurement protocols should be the primary focus for future research in order to delineate skeletal and dental expansion components. Furthermore, long-term efficacy validating the stability and relapse tendency, along with incorporating patient-reported outcomes on treatment comfort, psychological impact, and quality of life, will provide insight into therapeutic choices and exploring cost-effectiveness across different healthcare settings. Innovations integrating digital treatment planning, artificial intelligence–guided appliance design, and emerging biomaterials hold promise for optimizing ME strategies. Exploring combined skeletal-dentoalveolar treatment protocols and personalized timing according to growth patterns may enhance clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

The current systematic review concentrated on patients with mixed dentition, a crucial time for orthodontic and interceptive orthopedic treatments, where growth modulation and interceptive orthodontics can have long-term advantages.

Rapid maxillary expansion demonstrated a significantly greater increase in intermolar width compared to CAT, while intercanine width changes were comparable between the two treatment modalities.

Clear aligners showed superior control of molar inclination with reduced tipping. Both modalities effectively address transverse deficiencies in mixed dentition but differ in expansion patterns and dental effects.
