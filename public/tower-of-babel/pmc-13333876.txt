Coupling mechanisms between energy compensation and metabolic adaptation during high-intensity training in athletes: a longitudinal study based on multi-omics and individualized energy modeling
Authors: Yang Liu, Ziyu Wang, Lee Ji Young, Gang Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this prospective longitudinal study, the authors investigated the mechanisms of coupling between energy compensation behaviors and metabolic adaptation in the course of a 48-week training cycle in 120 elite athletes stratified by sport type (endurance versus power/strength). Using energy compensation rate, multi-omics profiling, endocrine biomarkers, and gut microbiota composition in six measurement points from baseline to recovery phases, they found that energy compensation follows a characteristic U-shaped pattern, with nadirs of 79.6% and 82.6% in endurance and power/strength athletes at peak training load, reflecting persistent energy deficits amounting to 624–840 kcal/day. This energy deficit was accompanied by a coordinated suppression of leptin (effect size: − 1.9/− 1.4), an increase in cortisol (+ 1.7/+1.4), upregulation of pathways for fatty acid oxidation, and decreased Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratios. Systematic correlation analyses point to hierarchical patterns of coupling, according to which endocrine markers most closely related to energy status showed the highest association with compensation rate (leptin: r = 0.55; cortisol: r = − 0.46), whereas downstream phenotypes only express weaker associations. In subgroup analyses, greater metabolic perturbations were observed in athletes experiencing severe energy deficits (effect size: − 1.68 vs. − 0.72). These findings support an integrated “energy behavior-metabolic state” approach for personalized nutritional monitoring and intervention in high-performance sport.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51352-x.

Introduction

Elite athletes face a chronic challenge in maintaining energy homeostasis in the face of the high metabolic demands imposed by intensive training. The condition of low energy availability (LEA), wherein inadequate caloric intake relative to exercise energy expenditure occurs, has been recognized as a common issue among athletes of different sports and competitive levels, affecting 15–80% of athletic populations1. When energy availability drops below critical levels, athletes become vulnerable to a multifaceted syndrome of physiological decrements collectively known as Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs), including disturbances in endocrine, metabolic, immunological, and mental systems2. This problem extends beyond individual health consequences, as recent research demonstrates that LEA severely impairs training adaptation and competitive performance3. Notably, the prevalence, manifestations, and consequences of LEA have been shown to differ between male and female athletes, underscoring the importance of sex-specific considerations in energy balance research4,5. Despite increasing attention, sex-stratified analyses remain relatively scarce in studies examining energy balance during intensified training4. Understanding how athletes of both sexes regulate energy balance during intensified training is therefore a key question in sport science, with direct implications for individualized nutritional intervention strategies.

Energy compensation reflects the behavioral and physiological responses to exercise-induced energy deficits; however, the mechanisms controlling these responses in athletic populations remain incompletely characterized. Traditionally, energy balance models have been described as linear, in which increases in physical activity lead to proportional increases in total energy expenditure. However, accumulating evidence suggests that total energy expenditure may be constrained by physiological regulation, leading to compensatory adjustments in other components of energy expenditure6–9. Compensatory mechanisms may therefore blunt the expected energy deficit through reductions in non-exercise activity thermogenesis or changes in resting metabolic rate5. Weight-sensitive sport athletes seem most susceptible to maladaptive patterns of compensation that may promote chronic states of energy deficiency10. The metabolic cost of exercise varies substantially with movement mode, gradient, and intensity, and does not scale proportionally with mechanical work output across varying locomotor demands, as elastic energy recovery and locomotor efficiency modulate the actual metabolic cost incurred11,12. Methodological complications in measuring energy availability during free-living conditions have traditionally introduced error and reduced the precision of these studies, due in part to dietary assessment errors and incomplete capture of exercise energy expenditure13. This challenge is compounded by inter-individual variability in locomotor mechanical efficiency and metabolic cost, which standard field-based measurement approaches can only partially account for14. Modern approaches using objective measurement tools have increased accuracy for the longitudinal monitoring of energy balance dynamics in athletes.

The biological response to sustained energy deficit involves coordinated adaptations across multiple regulatory systems that collectively function to preserve metabolic homeostasis. Hormonal perturbations represent a hallmark feature of LEA, with documented changes in circulating leptin and insulin-like growth factor-1 levels reflecting the hypothalamic sensing of energy status15. Thyroid hormone suppression and increased cortisol secretion further characterize the neuroendocrine response to inadequate fueling16. These endocrine changes are thought to contribute to downstream alterations in substrate metabolism, with shifts in fuel utilization detectable through metabolomic profiling of blood and tissue samples17. The application of multi-omics technologies to exercise science has revealed previously unrecognized complexity in the metabolic response to training, identifying pathway-level changes in amino acid catabolism and lipid oxidation that correlate with training load18.

Despite significant strides in the characterization of both behavioral energy compensation and metabolic adaptation as independent phenomena, a critical knowledge gap persists in the dynamic interplay between these domains. Most prior investigations have utilized cross-sectional designs focused solely on either nutritional behaviors or biological markers19. This methodological limitation precludes the examination of how changes in energy intake patterns temporally relate to metabolic adaptations across training phases20. The hypothesis that energy compensation behaviors and metabolic adaptations may represent coupled systems—whereby behavioural responses to training stress are temporally associated with subsequent metabolic trajectories—has not been systematically tested in elite athletic cohorts4. Further complicating this picture, the physiological responses to intensified training exist along a spectrum of severity. Underlying this spectrum, exercise at different intensity domains elicits qualitatively distinct physiological adaptations, with metabolic responses shifting substantially as training demands move across key intensity thresholds21. It is important to distinguish between overreaching, which exists on a continuum from short-term functional responses to non-functional states, and overtraining syndrome, a prolonged maladaptive condition characterized by persistent performance decrements and neuroendocrine dysregulation2. The substantial symptom overlap between overtraining syndrome and REDs—including fatigue, hormonal disruption, and impaired recovery—makes differential diagnosis particularly challenging and highlights the need for integrated, longitudinal assessment frameworks that can track the temporal evolution of these conditions2,22.

The current study will use an integrated multi-omics and behavioral assessment framework to elucidate the energy compensation-metabolic adaptation coupling mechanisms during high-intensity training periods among elite athletes. This investigation would, therefore, seek to quantify the temporal relationship between behavioral and biological responses to training stress through tracking individualized energy compensation rates with comprehensive metabolomic and hormonal profiling across a 24-week training cycle23. Advanced analytical approaches, such as linear mixed modeling and weighted correlation network analysis, enable identification of metabolite modules that characterize specific energy compensation trajectories24. This provides a new “energy behavior-metabolic state” assessment paradigm with broad potential implications for personalized nutritional programming in high-performance sport settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study used a prospective longitudinal cohort design to explore temporal changes in the energy compensation and metabolic adaptation process throughout one complete annual training cycle in elite athletes. The study was conducted within the residential training facilities and affiliated sports science laboratories of two national-level sports training centers, both fully equipped for broad physiological and biochemical assessments, in Eastern China. Data collection lasted 48 weeks, including a 24-week main observation period during the most intensive periods of training and competition, followed by a 24-week follow-up throughout the recovery period and into the following pre-season.

Measurements were placed at strategic time points to capture different phases of the annual training periodization for characterizing metabolic trajectories under different physiological demands. Assessment windows were defined at baseline, week 4, week 12, week 24, week 36, and week 48 corresponding to pre-season preparation, peak training load, mid-competition, end-of-season, recovery, and initiation of the new cycle, respectively. This temporal framework allowed for an assessment of both acute responses to training intensification and longer-term adaptive processes. Participants

Elite athletes were recruited from provincial and national sports training centers through purposive sampling to ensure representation across endurance and power-based disciplines. By sport type, athletes were stratified into either endurance (marathon, cycling, rowing) or power/strength (weightlifting, sprinting, throwing), with a balanced sex distribution within each stratum. Sample size estimates, based on previous longitudinal studies investigating energy availability fluctuations in athletic populations, suggested that 100 participants would provide adequate statistical power (β = 0.80, α = 0.05) to detect moderate effect sizes in mixed-effects models, with recruitment of 120 athletes accommodating an anticipated 15–20% attrition rate.

The inclusion criteria included the following: (1) aged 20–35 years; (2) more than five years of systematic training experience; (3) national second-level athlete certification or above, corresponding to athletes who have achieved podium finishes at provincial-level competitions or qualifying standards for national championships within China’s centralized sport classification system; (4) currently in regular training status; and (5) will complete the entire competitive season at their respective training center. Exclusion criteria consisted of the following: (1) musculoskeletal injuries within the previous three months; (2) diagnosed metabolic and endocrine disorders; (3) current use of medications known to affect energy metabolism or hormonal profiles; (4) medically prescribed diets; (5) pregnancy or lactation for female athletes. For female athletes, those who used hormonal contraceptives were allowed, provided their regimens did not change during the observation period. Of note, while exclusion criterion (3) addressed chronic medication use at enrollment, the intermittent use of antibiotics and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) during the 48-week observation period was not prospectively monitored. Given the known effects of these agents on gut microbiota composition and systemic inflammatory markers, this is acknowledged as a methodological consideration and is addressed further in the limitations section. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gangneung-Wonju National University with Approval No. GWNU-IRB-2024-217.Prior to enrollment, informed written consent was obtained from each participant.

Data collection timepoints

The 48-week observation period had six time points for data collection, at time points aligned with distinct phases of the annual training periodization. Baseline assessment (T0) fell within the pre-season preparation phase (Week 0), capturing physiological status prior to training intensification. The second assessment was conducted at Week 4 (T1), during the peak training load phase, where athletes experienced maximal training stress and thus the most considerable anticipated energy deficit. Mid-season evaluation at Week 12 (T2) represented the competitive phase when the metabolic responses could be examined during continued high-level performance demands.

The end-of-season assessment (T3) at Week 24 marked the completion of the main training and competition phase, reflecting cumulative adaptive responses to several months of high-intensity training. Two follow-up assessments during the recovery phase at T4, Week 36, and the new cycle initiation phase at T5, Week 48, were performed to characterize metabolic trajectory reversal and re-adaptation patterns following the reduction in training load. This temporal framework allowed the present investigation to capture both the acute responses to training intensification during the initial 24-week period and longer-term adaptive processes extending through the subsequent follow-up period of 24 weeks, in line with established approaches for tracking energy availability fluctuations in athletic cohorts.

Measurements

The current study utilized an integrated assessment battery consisting of five measurement domains: energy balance, body composition, multi-omics profiling, endocrine and inflammatory biomarkers, and training load monitoring (Table 1). Energy intake was calculated using 7-day weighed food records analyzed using standardized nutritional software, while total energy expenditure was estimated using triaxial accelerometry (ActiGraph GT9X) worn continuously for seven days at each assessment window25,26. Resting metabolic rate was measured using indirect calorimetry under standardized fasting conditions27. Body composition, including fat mass, fat-free mass, and regional distribution, was measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry according to established protocols for athletic cohorts28.

Fasting venous blood samples were taken between 07:00 and 09:00 h for multi-omics and biomarker analyses. Untargeted metabolomics and targeted lipidomics profiling were conducted using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry29. Gut microbiota composition was assessed by 16 S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing of fecal samples at each time point. No new sequencing was performed in the present study. All raw 16 S rRNA gene sequencing FASTQ files were obtained from the publicly accessible NCBI SRA BioProject PRJNA1376234. The study conducted only downstream bioinformatics processing and statistical analysis of these publicly archived sequencing data.

Endocrine markers, including leptin, insulin, insulin-like growth factor-1, cortisol, and testosterone, were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, while thyroid hormones were determined by chemiluminescence immunoassay. Inflammatory markers, including interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, were measured to describe systemic inflammation status. Training load was quantified daily using the session rating of perceived exertion (session-RPE) method based on Foster’s modified CR-10 scale (0–10), with training load calculated as the product of session RPE and session duration in minutes, expressed in arbitrary units (AU). Heart rate was continuously recorded during all training sessions using chest-strap monitors (Polar H10), with mean exercise heart rate and percentage of individual maximal heart rate (%HRmax) used as objective indices of internal training intensity. Heart rate data were also combined with activity logs to estimate daily exercise energy expenditure30. Other measures that were collected as covariates included maximal oxygen uptake from graded exercise testing of endurance athletes, one-repetition maximum for power/ strength athletes, and sleep by ActiGraph accelerometry. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables at each time point; that is, means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges were presented for continuous data depending on distribution characteristics. Linear mixed-effects models with participant as a random effect and time, sport type, and sex as fixed effects examined temporal trajectories of energy compensation and metabolic parameters31. This analytical approach accommodates repeated measures data while accounting for individual variability and handling missing observations under the missing-at-random assumption.

Multi-omics data were subjected to quality control steps that included normalization, batch correction, and filtering of low-abundance features before analysis. Principal component analysis and partial least squares discriminant analysis were applied to identify global metabolic patterns across training phases. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis was used to identify modules of coregulated metabolites associated with energy compensation trajectories32. For correlations between energy compensation rate and metabolic biomarkers, Spearman rank correlation was used including correction for multiple comparisons by the false discovery rate. Subgroup analyses stratified by sport type and sex were conducted to investigate differential adaptation patterns. Statistical significance was predefined at α = 0.05, and all analyses were performed using R statistical software v4.3.0.

Gut microbiota processing and bioinformatics workflow

Gut microbiota profiling in this study was conducted using publicly available 16 S rRNA gene sequencing data targeting the V3–V4 region from the NCBI SRA BioProject PRJNA1376234. No new sequencing was performed.

Raw FASTQ files were processed using QIIME2 (version 2023.5). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated using the DADA2 plugin and taxonomically assigned against the SILVA 138.1 reference database. Low-abundance features (< 0.01% across all samples) and singleton ASVs were removed prior to downstream analyses.

To normalize sequencing depth, the ASV table was rarefied to 15,000 reads per sample, corresponding to the plateau of rarefaction curves. Alpha diversity (Shannon index, Chao1 richness) and beta diversity (Bray–Curtis, weighted UniFrac) were calculated using QIIME2 and the vegan R package. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was used to visualize microbial community structure. The Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio was derived from centered log-ratio–transformed phylum-level abundances.

All processed microbiome tables and taxonomic assignments used for statistical modeling are available in the Figshare dataset (DOI: 10.6084/m9.figshare.30811814). The original raw FASTQ files remain accessible under the NCBI SRA BioProject PRJNA1376234.

Results

Participant characteristics

Initially, 135 elite athletes entered the screening process for eligibility and a total of 120 were enrolled since they met the inclusion criteria. Participants were stratified into two groups: endurance sports (n = 60; marathon, cycling, rowing) and power/strength sports (n = 60; weightlifting, sprinting, throwing), with equal distribution of both sexes in both groups (Table 2). During the observation period of 48 weeks, a total of 108 participants (90.0%) completed all six time points of assessment, while 12 athletes dropped out (10.0%) due to injury (n = 5), transfer to other training centers (n = 4), or personal reasons (n = 3). The overall rate of dropouts did not vary significantly between categories of sport type or sex.

The baseline characteristics showed that the physiological profiles of the two sport groups differed. Power/strength athletes had significantly higher body mass (77.0 ± 11.3 vs. 65.8 ± 8.6 kg, p < 0.001), fat-free mass (64.0 ± 11.2 vs. 57.2 ± 9.4 kg, p < 0.001), and resting metabolic rate (1796 ± 278 vs. 1652 ± 242 kcal/day, p = 0.002) compared with endurance athletes. Endurance athletes, however, had higher weekly training volume (12.4 ± 2.6 vs. 8.8 ± 2.4 h/week, p < 0.001), total energy expenditure (3684 ± 548 vs. 3220 ± 598 kcal/day, p < 0.001), and energy intake (3124 ± 486 vs. 2848 ± 532 kcal/day, p = 0.003). Notably, the baseline energy availability for both groups was below the established threshold of 30 kcal/kg FFM/day, but it was lower in endurance athletes than in power/strength athletes (19.1 ± 5.8 vs. 22.3 ± 7.2 kcal/kg FFM/day, p = 0.012), indicating prevalent low energy availability at the onset of the intensified training period.

Longitudinal changes in energy balance and body composition

Training load and energy expenditure

Training load demonstrated a typical periodization pattern over the 48-week observation period (Fig. 2a). Both groups exhibited significant increases from baseline (T0) to peak training (T1), with endurance athletes increasing 33.6% (4250 to 5680 AU/week) and power/strength athletes 42.1% (3180 to 4520 AU/week). Training load remained high over the competition phase (T2–T3) before significantly decreasing during the recovery period (T4), to values 22.8% and 23.0% below baseline for endurance and power/strength groups, respectively. The significant time × group interaction (p = 0.038) reflected larger absolute changes in the endurance athletes over the training cycle.

Total energy expenditure closely followed the dynamics of training load (Fig. 2b). Peak values were observed at T1, amounting to 4125 ± 71 kcal/day in endurance athletes versus 3586 ± 95 kcal/day in power/strength athletes. The lowest energy expenditures were reached during the recovery phase (T4), decreasing to 3285 ± 62 kcal/day and 2985 ± 65 kcal/day in the endurance and power/strength groups, respectively. Energy expenditure was higher throughout in endurance athletes compared with power/strength athletes (group effect p < 0.001); the difference between the two groups ranged from 300 to 539 kcal/day. The time × group interaction was significant (p = 0.042), indicating that training-induced changes in energy expenditure were more marked among endurance athletes.

Energy compensation rate

Energy compensation rate had a marked U-shaped pattern of change during the observation period, reflecting the dynamic balance between energy intake and energy expenditure in relation to training load variations (Fig. 3). During the training and competition phase (T0–T3), both groups were in a state of energy deficit (ECR < 100%). Compensation was lowest at T1 when training load was highest. Among endurance athletes, it reached a nadir of 79.6 ± 3.6% at T1, equivalent to a calculated energy deficit of approximately 840 kcal/day, whereas the power/strength group reached a minimum of 82.6 ± 3.8%, corresponding to a calculated energy deficit of approximately 624 kcal/day. The extent of under-compensation decreased progressively from T1 to T3, suggesting a gradual behavioral adjustment to the increased energy requirements.

Near energy balance was only achieved during the recovery phase (T4), where both groups approached but did not exceed 100% (endurance: 98.8 ± 4.2%; power/strength: 98.0 ± 4.5%). This convergence toward energy balance coincided with the substantial reduction in training load during off-season. However, the initiation of a new training cycle (T5) was associated with an immediate reduction in ECR to approximately 91% in both groups, indicating rapid re-emergence of energy deficit upon training resumption. Linear mixed-effects analysis showed a significant main effect of time (p < 0.001), whereas the group effect (p = 0.089) and the time × group interaction (p = 0.624) did not achieve significance, demonstrating that endurance and power/strength athletes exhibited a similar pattern of energy compensation despite differences in absolute energy flux.

Body composition changes

Body composition showed a differential response over the observation period, with significant changes in adiposity but relative preservation of lean mass (Table 3). Fat mass progressively decreased during training and competition to reach its nadir at T3 in both groups. Thus, endurance athletes reduced fat mass from 8.6 ± 3.1 kg at baseline to 7.3 ± 2.7 kg at end-season (− 15.1%), while corresponding estimates for power/strength athletes revealed a parallel reduction from 13.0 ± 4.3 kg to 11.6 ± 3.9 kg (− 10.8%). This was associated with a corresponding decrease in body fat percentage in endurance athletes from 13.1 ± 4.8% to 11.4 ± 4.3% and power/strength athletes from 16.9 ± 5.5% to 15.4 ± 5.1%. Both variables showed a significant time effect (p < 0.001) but no time × group interaction, indicating similar patterns of fat loss between sport types.

In contrast, fat-free mass remained relatively stable throughout the period of study. Despite the persistent energy deficit observed during the training phase (ECR: 80–92%), endurance athletes maintained FFM within a narrow range of 57.2 to 56.8 kg and power/strength athletes did similarly at 64.0 to 63.5 kg. The time effect for FFM did not reach significance (p = 0.124), indicating that lean tissue mass was largely maintained despite the sustained energy deficit observed during the training phase. During the recovery phase (T4–T5), fat mass and percent body fat gradually returned toward baseline levels, while FFM remained unchanged. These findings indicate that body mass reductions during the intensified training period were primarily attributable to fat loss rather than lean tissue catabolism. Sex-stratified analyses of body composition changes are presented in the subgroup analyses section ("Subgroup analyses" section).

Metabolic and physiological adaptation profiles

Endocrine and inflammatory responses

Endocrine and inflammatory markers showed concerted changes across the training cycle that corresponded temporally to periods of intensified training and negative energy balance (Fig. 4). Energy-sensing and anabolic hormones were progressively suppressed throughout the training period. Leptin was most profoundly suppressed at T1, with very large effect sizes of − 1.9 and − 1.4 in endurance and power/strength athletes, respectively, which suggested reduced adiposity and energy availability. Similarly, insulin (− 0.9/−0.7), IGF-1 (− 1.1/−0.9), and triiodothyronine (− 0.8/−0.6) were suppressed at peak training load, indicating downregulation of metabolic and anabolic signaling. Testosterone was moderately suppressed (− 1.2/−0.9), while cortisol was markedly elevated (+ 1.7/+1.4), resulting in a profoundly reduced testosterone-to-cortisol (T/C) ratio (− 1.9/−1.5) that is indicative of a catabolic hormonal environment.

The pattern of inflammatory markers was opposite, with elevations during the intensified training phase. The increase in interleukin-6 was most marked at T1 (+ 1.5/+1.2), reflecting the exercise-induced myokine nature of this cytokine. Tumor necrosis factor-α (+ 0.6/+0.5) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (+ 0.8/+0.7) were increased more modestly, indicating low-grade systemic inflammation associated with training stress. During recovery (T4), partial normalisation was observed for most markers, with positive rebounds being evident for testosterone and T/C ratio (+ 0.2/+0.3 and + 0.4/+0.4, respectively) while IL-6 returned to near-baseline levels. Mixed-effects models showed significant time effects for all markers (all p < 0.05), with the greatest effects for leptin, cortisol, T/C ratio and IL-6 (p < 0.001). Time × group interactions were significant for leptin (p = 0.032) and T/C ratio (p = 0.041), indicating greater perturbations in endurance athletes.

Metabolomics and lipidomics

Untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics profiling revealed coordinated changes in metabolic pathway activity according to training load and energy deficit (Fig. 5). Lipid catabolism and pathways involved in utilizing alternative energy sources were found to be upregulated consistently throughout the entire intensified training period. Fatty acid oxidation showed the largest number of changes, with 18 and 15 significantly changed metabolites at T1 in endurance and power/strength athletes, respectively. Similarly, acylcarnitine metabolism, indicative of increased mitochondrial transport of fatty acids (16/13 metabolites), and ketone body metabolism (8/6 metabolites) were increased, reflecting a metabolic shift toward relying on lipid-derived fuel sources. Flux through the tricarboxylic acid cycle was elevated, consistent with an increase in oxidative metabolism, and branched-chain amino acid catabolism was elevated (12/10 metabolites), suggesting amino acid mobilization for energy production during periods of carbohydrate insufficiency.

On the other hand, carbohydrate metabolism and cellular maintenance-related pathways were downregulated. Glycolysis/gluconeogenesis presented lower activity (14/12 metabolites at T1), which reflected a reduced availability of carbohydrates. Membrane lipid metabolism, such as sphingolipid (9/7 metabolites) and glycerophospholipid (11/9 metabolites) pathways, was suppressed, which may indicate membrane remodeling under energy stress. Remarkably, glutathione metabolism (8/7 metabolites) and tryptophan metabolism (7/6 metabolites) presented downregulation, suggesting impaired antioxidant capacity and altered serotonin precursor availability. During the recovery phase (T4), metabolite counts across all pathways substantially decreased (range: 2–8), with color intensity indicating attenuated effect sizes. Re-initiation of training at T5 was associated with the partial re-emergence of these metabolic signatures, further highlighting the cyclical nature of metabolic adaptation to periodized training.

Gut microbiota composition

Gut microbiota composition exhibited notable changes over the observation period, characterized by shifts in the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio (Fig. 6). Alpha diversity, assessed by the Shannon index and Chao1 richness, remained relatively stable across all six time points in both groups, with no significant time effect (Shannon: p = 0.218; Chao1: p = 0.342) or time × group interaction (Shannon: p = 0.485; Chao1: p = 0.612), indicating that overall microbial richness and evenness were maintained throughout the training cycle. Beta diversity analysis using weighted UniFrac distances showed a modest but significant separation between endurance and power/strength athletes (PERMANOVA: R2 = 0.08, p = 0.036), while a weak temporal shift was also detected (R2 = 0.03, p = 0.042), driven primarily by samples collected during the peak training phase (T1–T3). PCoA ordination revealed that inter-individual variability remained the dominant source of variation in community structure, with sport-type and temporal effects accounting for a small proportion of overall compositional differences. These results indicate that the global microbial community architecture was largely preserved despite the phylum-level compositional shifts detailed below. The F/B ratio at baseline was within the normal range reported for trained athletes in both groups (endurance: 2.07 ± 0.25; power/strength: 2.25 ± 0.20). During the intensified training period, there was a progressive reduction of Firmicutes and an increase of Bacteroidetes, reducing the F/B ratios in both groups. However, the temporal dynamics were different: power/strength athletes reached their nadir at T2 (1.43 ± 0.35), whereas endurance athletes continued to decline until T3 (0.95 ± 0.28)-a reduction of 54% compared with baseline. Proteobacteria, a phylum commonly associated with physiological stress, demonstrated transient increased relative abundance during peak training load, especially in endurance athletes (5.2% to 8.5% at T2).

Recovery trajectories diverged considerably between groups. Power/strength athletes experienced rapid microbiota recovery, where the F/B ratio returned to 97% of baseline values at T5 (2.19 ± 0.22). In contrast, endurance athletes experienced incomplete and delayed recovery, reaching only 68% of baseline F/B ratio at T5 (1.41 ± 0.35). Mixed-effects models indicated a significant time effect in Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes abundance (p < 0.001), while there was also a significant time × group interaction for the F/B ratio (p = 0.018), confirming differential patterns in response. Minor phyla like Actinobacteria and Verrucomicrobia presented small fluctuations with no clear pattern. These findings suggest that both the magnitude and duration of energy deficit may be associated with gut microbiota resilience, with the greater cumulative metabolic stress experienced by endurance athletes potentially contributing to the incomplete recovery of microbial community structure.

Coupling between energy compensation and metabolic adaptation

To explore the relationship between energy availability and metabolic responses, we conducted correlation analyses between energy compensation rate (ECR) and key adaptation markers (Fig. 7). ECR was positively related to leptin change (r = 0.55, p < 0.001), with greater energy compensation associated with less suppression of this adipokine. ECR was also negatively related to cortisol elevation (r = − 0.46, p < 0.001), indicative of an attenuated hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis stress response in the face of reduced energy deficits. These relationships were consistent in both athlete groups, although endurance athletes generally clustered in the lower ECR range with more pronounced hormonal perturbations.

The relationship between ECR and changes in body composition was very weak and not significant (r = 0.06, p = 0.646), suggesting that changes in fat mass are not directly related to the magnitude of energy compensation. This is probably due to a multifactorial regulation of body composition, such as baseline adiposity, training modality, macronutrient distribution, and interindividual metabolic variability, which might mask any direct relationships between ECR and fat mass changes. Conversely, ECR was positively related to the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio, with a moderate and statistically significant correlation coefficient (r = 0.28, p = 0.041), indicating that larger energy deficits were associated with more severe gut microbiota dysbiosis. Altogether, correlational analyses identified a hierarchical pattern of coupling: endocrine markers most closely related to energy status (leptin, cortisol) showed the strongest associations with ECR, whereas downstream phenotypes (body composition, gut microbiota) presented weaker or non-significant relationships, which agrees with the indirect and multifactorial nature of their regulation.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses by athlete type, sex, and energy deficit severity were conducted to explore whether leptin response to training-induced energy deficit was different (Fig. 8). The overall effect size for leptin suppression was − 1.25 (95% CI −1.52 to − 0.98), with a large negative effect of energy deficit on circulating leptin concentrations.

Athlete type significantly moderated the leptin response, with greater suppression evident in endurance athletes (ES = − 1.52, 95% CI −1.95 to − 1.09) than for power/strength athletes (ES = − 0.95, 95% CI −1.48 to − 0.42). This finding is in agreement with the greater energy deficits observed within endurance athletes during intensified training. Sex-based differences were minor, with a slightly greater suppression of leptin in females (ES = − 1.35, 95% CI −2.02 to − 0.68) compared with males (ES = − 1.18, 95% CI −1.55 to − 0.81), although confidence intervals overlapped substantially, suggesting a limited statistical difference. Sex-stratified analyses were also performed for body composition and gut microbiota outcomes. Longitudinal body composition data stratified by sex are presented in Supplementary Table S1. As expected from known sexual dimorphism, male athletes exhibited greater fat-free mass while female athletes had higher fat mass and body fat percentage across all time points. However, the temporal patterns of change did not differ significantly between sexes for fat mass (time × sex interaction: p = 0.182), fat-free mass (p = 0.836), or body fat percentage (p = 0.418). Fat-free mass was preserved in both sexes throughout the observation period. For gut microbiota, the decline in Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio followed a similar temporal pattern in male and female athletes, with no significant sex-based difference detected (time × sex interaction: p = 0.386). These results suggest that, within this cohort of elite athletes, the longitudinal patterns of body composition change and gut microbiota response to intensified training were broadly comparable between sexes.

The largest subgroup difference occurred according to the severity of the imposed energy deficit, where athletes creating a severe deficit (ECR ≤ 90%) exhibited markedly greater suppression of leptin (ES = − 1.68, 95% CI −2.25 to − 1.11) when compared to those creating mild deficit conditions (ECR > 90%; ES = − 0.72, 95% CI −1.22 to − 0.22). This dose-response pattern suggests that the magnitude of energy deficit may be closely linked to the extent of metabolic adaptation, with an approximate 2.3-fold greater effect size when comparing severely vs. mildly energy-deficient athletes.

Discussion

The present study offers new insights into the dynamic interaction between energy compensation behaviors and metabolic adaptation in elite athletes during intensified training. The U-shaped trajectory of energy compensation rate observed over the 48-week training cycle in the present study aligns with predictions of the constrained energy expenditure model from recent theoretical frameworks33. Within both sport categories, athletes developed large energy deficits during peak periods of training, with compensation rates falling to below 83% at the point of maximal training load. These results support previous observations that behavioral compensation of energy intake is consistently incomplete during increased exercise energy expenditure, particularly during periods of rapid escalation in training intensity34. The level of energy deficit recorded in the present cohort exceeds levels normally considered compatible with optimal training adaptation, indicating that current nutritional practice may be inadequate to meet the metabolic demands of elite sport.

The endocrine perturbations observed during periods of energy deficit are consistent with a coordinated hypothalamic response to perceived energy insufficiency. The suppression in leptin emerged as the most sensitive marker of energy status, consistent with its established role as an adiposity signal that integrates information about energy stores and acute energy flux35. The corresponding rise in cortisol concentrations is consistent with activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, a well-characterized stress response associated with substrate mobilization during periods of negative energy balance36. These hormonal changes replicate patterns previously observed in athletes developing relative energy deficiency and support the clinical relevance of monitoring these biomarkers in high-performance sport settings37. The differential magnitude of the response between endurance and power/strength athletes may be partly attributable to the greater cumulative energy deficit incurred by the former group, although differences in neuroendocrine sensitivity between sport types cannot be excluded.

Metabolomic profiling demonstrated systematic shifts in fuel utilization pathways that parallel the hormonal adaptations. The upregulation of fatty acid oxidation and ketone body metabolism represents a classical substrate shift toward lipid-derived fuels during carbohydrate insufficiency38. Such metabolic signatures are consistent with enhanced mitochondrial fat oxidation capacity, an adaptation that may confer performance advantages in endurance events while potentially compromising high-intensity power output39. The concurrent suppression of glycolytic pathway activity and amino acid catabolism suggests a protein-sparing effect that may explain the relative preservation of fat-free mass despite persistent energy deficit. Such metabolic flexibility has been characterized as an evolutionarily conserved response to energy scarcity, thought to enable continued physical function during periods of limited food availability40.

The changes in gut microbiota described here extend earlier findings on the relationship between exercise stress and the restructuring of microbial communities. In this context, the decreased Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio during intensified training periods suggests that energy deficit and physical stress may jointly contribute to shifts in gut ecosystem dynamics41. An incomplete recovery of the microbiota composition poststudy in endurance athletes may be indicative of long-term consequences for gut barrier function and systemic inflammation42. These data underscore the gut as an underappreciated target organ in the physiological response to training stress and energy restriction. Notably, while overall microbial diversity remained stable throughout the training cycle, the phylum-level compositional shifts observed in the present study suggest that specific taxonomic groups may be more sensitive to energy deficit than the broader community structure.

The hierarchical coupling pattern identified between energy compensation and downstream physiological markers has significant implications for athlete monitoring and intervention strategies. The endocrine markers most closely related to energy status demonstrated the strongest associations with compensation rate, supporting their potential utility as early warning indicators of inadequate energy availability43. The assessment of body composition is useful for longitudinal tracking but appears less sensitive to acute changes in energy balance, in a manner consistent with the multifactorial regulation of adiposity, which includes training modality and distribution of macronutrient intake44. These observations support previous recommendations for integrating multiple assessment modalities during the evaluation of the energy status of athletes rather than relying on single biomarkers45. The sex-stratified analyses revealed that the temporal patterns of body composition change, gut microbiota response, and leptin suppression were broadly comparable between male and female athletes in this cohort. While baseline differences in absolute values were expected and observed, the similar longitudinal trajectories suggest that, for the variables examined, male and female elite athletes exhibited comparable responses to training-induced energy deficit. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively modest sample size for sex-stratified comparisons, and larger studies with greater statistical power for detecting sex-specific effects are warranted.

To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the raw 16 S rRNA FASTQ files used in this study were obtained from the publicly available NCBI SRA BioProject PRJNA1376234. The present study did not generate any new sequencing data; therefore, no new SRA or BioSample accession numbers were created. All processed microbiome tables (including ASV abundance matrices and taxonomic assignments), together with metabolomics, lipidomics, endocrine, and energy balance data, are deposited in Figshare (DOI: 10.6084/m9.figshare.30811814).

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. The prospective longitudinal design spanning an entire competitive season with six assessment time points enabled characterization of temporal trajectories rather than reliance on cross-sectional snapshots. The inclusion of elite athletes from both endurance and power/strength disciplines, with balanced sex distribution, enabled stratified analyses by sport type, sex, and energy deficit severity, enhancing the specificity of the findings. The multi-omics approach integrating metabolomics, lipidomics, gut microbiota profiling, and endocrine biomarkers provided a comprehensive view of the physiological response to training-induced energy deficit. Additionally, the use of objective measurement tools, including accelerometry, DXA, and indirect calorimetry, enhanced the reliability of energy balance estimates.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Reliance on accelerometry and dietary records introduces measurement error that may attenuate observed associations, although standardized protocols were employed to minimize systematic bias46. As noted in the methods, intermittent antibiotic and NSAID use was not prospectively tracked, which may have introduced uncontrolled variability in microbiota and inflammatory outcomes. Furthermore, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing provides taxonomic classification but does not yield direct functional information; shotgun metagenomics would offer greater resolution of microbial metabolic capacity, though it carries its own limitations in cost and analytical complexity. Fecal sample collection and storage protocols, while standardized, were managed within the training center environment and may have introduced variability compared with laboratory-controlled conditions. The sample was restricted to elite athletes from specific sport disciplines, possibly limiting generalizability to recreational athletes or other sport categories47. The observational design precludes causal inference; the associations reported should be interpreted as temporal co-occurrences rather than established causal pathways. Future investigations should incorporate doubly labeled water methodology for more precise energy expenditure quantification and extend observations across multiple competitive seasons to characterize long-term adaptation trajectories48.

Conclusions

This longitudinal study provides evidence that energy compensation and metabolic adaptation are dynamically linked processes during intensified training in elite athletes. The typical U-shaped response of the rate of energy compensation, with troughs of 79.6% and 82.6% for endurance and power/strength athletes, respectively, shows that behavioral energy intake adaptations do not match exercise-induced energy expenditure increments throughout. The energy deficits that arise are accompanied by coordinated physiological responses including endocrine suppression, metabolic pathway reconfiguration, and gut microbiota remodeling, whose magnitudes are associated with the degree of energy deficiency.

The hierarchical organization of energy-metabolism coupling revealed in this study carries direct implications for athlete monitoring practices. Leptin and cortisol emerge as sensitive proximal indicators of energy status, with strong correlations with compensation rate, whereas the downstream phenotypes of body composition and microbiota show weaker associations reflecting their multifactorial regulation. The differential recovery trajectories across sport groups, where power/strength athletes reached near complete restoration of metabolic parameters, while endurance athletes still showed persistent perturbations at 48 weeks, underscore the importance of sport-specific nutritional interventions calibrated to the individual’s energy requirements. The broadly comparable responses observed between male and female athletes for the variables examined suggest that the monitoring and intervention principles outlined above may be applicable across sexes, although further investigation with larger sex-stratified samples is needed.

The integrated assessment approach employed in this study provides an initial foundation for individualized nutritional programming in high-performance sport settings. Practitioners should focus on early identification of energy deficit through combined monitoring of behavioral and biological markers rather than reliance on single assessment modalities. Future studies should extend these observations across multiple competitive cycles and include more accurate energy expenditure methodologies, examining whether targeted nutritional interventions can mitigate the metabolic consequences of training-induced energy deficit without compromising desired training adaptations. Such work will continue to serve the dual purposes of optimizing athletic performance while protecting long-term athlete health.
