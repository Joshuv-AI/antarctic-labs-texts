Long-Term Exposure to Arsenic in Community Water Supplies and Risk of Cardiovascular Disease among Women in the California Teachers Study
Authors: Danielle N Medgyesi, Komal Bangia, Emma S Spielfogel, Jared A Fisher, Jessica M Madrigal, Rena R Jones, Mary H Ward, James V Lacey, Tiffany R Sanchez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Inorganic arsenic in drinking water (wAs) is linked to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease. However, risk is uncertain at lower levels present in US community water supplies (CWS), currently regulated at the federal maximum contaminant level of 10μg/L.Objectives:We evaluated the relationship between long-term wAs exposure from CWS and cardiovascular disease in the California Teachers Study cohort.Methods:Using statewide health care administrative records from enrollment through follow-up (1995–2018), we identified fatal and nonfatal cases of ischemic heart disease (IHD) and cardiovascular disease (CVD). Participants’ residential addresses were linked to a network of CWS boundaries and annual wAs concentrations (1990–2020). Most participants resided in areas served by a CWS (92%). Exposure was calculated as a time-varying, 10-year moving average up to a participant’s event, death, or end of follow-up. Using Cox models, we estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the risk of IHD or CVD. We evaluated wAs exposure categorized by concentration thresholds relevant to regulation standards (<1.00, 1.00–2.99, 3.00–4.99, 5.00–9.99, ≥10μg/L) and continuously using a log2-transformation (i.e., per doubling). Models were adjusted for baseline age, neighborhood socioeconomic status, race/ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status. We also stratified analyses by age, BMI, and smoking status.Results:Our analysis included 98,250 participants, 6,119 IHD cases, and 9,936 CVD cases. The HRs for IHD at concentration thresholds (reference, <1μg/L) were 1.06 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.12), 1.05 (95% CI: 0.94, 1.17), 1.20 (95% CI: 1.02, 1.41), and 1.42 (95% CI: 1.10, 1.84) for 1.00–2.99μg/L, 3.00–4.99μg/L, 5.00–9.99μg/L, and ≥10μg/L, respectively. HRs for every doubling of wAs exposure were 1.04 (95% CI: 1.02, 1.06) for IHD and 1.02 (95% CI: 1.01, 1.04) for CVD. We observed statistically stronger risk among those ≤55 vs. >55 years of age at enrollment (pinteraction=0.006 and 0.012 for IHD and CVD, respectively).Discussion:Long-term wAs exposure from CWS, at and below the regulatory limit, may increase cardiovascular disease risk, particularly IHD. https://doi.org/10.1289/EHP14410

Introduction

Inorganic arsenic in drinking water (wAs) remains an environmental exposure of concern, potentially linked to several prevalent chronic diseases in the US.1,2 According to the American Heart Association and other leading health agencies, there is substantial evidence that arsenic exposure increases the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD).3–6 This includes evidence of risk at high arsenic levels (>100μg/L) in drinking water.7–12 The United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) reduced the maximum contaminant level (MCL) for arsenic in community water supplies (CWS) from 50μg/L to 10μg/L beginning in 2006. Even so, drinking water remains an important source of arsenic exposure among CWS users.13,14 Prolonged exposure to relatively lower wAs levels experienced in the US may increase CVD risk, but few studies have evaluated risk at such levels.7,15–20

Inorganic arsenic has been linked to oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, which contribute to the development of atherosclerosis and hypertension.5,21–23 These pathophysiological changes can increase CVD risk, including ischemic heart disease (IHD) (also called coronary heart disease) and stroke.6 Epidemiologic studies conducted in Bangladesh, Taiwan, and Chile have demonstrated that exposure to high wAs levels increases CVD risk.7–12 In the US where wAs is generally <50μg/L, a prospective study of American Indians living in the Great Plains and Southwest found that higher urinary arsenic levels were associated with the risk of IHD, CVD, and stroke.19 A case–cohort study in Colorado reported a 38% greater risk of IHD with a 15-μg/L increase in long-term wAs exposure.20 However, the former study used a urinary biomarker of arsenic exposure not directly translatable to drinking water levels, while the latter study did not evaluate risk at levels below the MCL (10μg/L), providing little insight into the safety of current regulatory standards. Two meta-analyses suggest that wAs levels ranging from 3 to 20μg/L may increase CVD risk, particularly IHD, but associations at these levels are described by few studies.15–17

The natural occurrence of arsenic in groundwater is observed in regions of New England, the upper Midwest, and the Southwest, including California.24 In the San Joaquin Valley, 16% of CWS contained average arsenic concentrations of ≥10μg/L between 2005 and 2007.25 Arsenic from CWS has been associated with greater urinary arsenic levels in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) (2003–2014).13,14 This was especially evident among participants living in the western and southern US.13

Our study seeks to evaluate the association between long-term wAs exposure from CWS and CVD risk in the California Teachers Study (CTS), a large prospective cohort of women living in California. In collaboration with the California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA), we linked a network of CWS boundaries and corresponding wAs measurements from several decades to participants’ residential addresses.26,27 Our study is one of the first detailed analyses to evaluate wAs exposure from CWS in relation to IHD and CVD risk to better elucidate effects at concentrations near the current US regulatory limit. We describe in detail the novel data and methods developed for this analysis, presenting an approach that utilizes geospatial and environmental data. Such methods can be extended to future studies and may be particularly useful to studies that lack biomarkers of arsenic exposure.

Methods

Study Population

The CTS is a large ongoing prospective cohort of 133,477 women, 22–104 (median=53) years of age at enrollment in 1995–1996.28,29 Participants were recruited from members of the California State Teachers Retirement System and enrolled by completing the first mailed questionnaire (Q1) about health and lifestyle. The study continues to maintain contact with participants, including the administration of an additional five follow-up questionnaires.29 Our study used information on demographics, lifestyle, diet, and reproductive and general health history from Q1. Race and ethnicity were self-reported in response to the following question “to what race/ethnic group do you and your biological parents belong?” and by selecting from the following categories: black or African American, Chinese, Filipino, Hawaiian, Japanese, Korean, Mexican or other Hispanic/Latino, Native American, Vietnamese, white or Caucasian, or other. Original categories have been coded in compliance with the National Institutes of Health reporting requirements (Table S1). Due to limited numbers, we have consolidated American Indian or Alaskan Native (0.9%), Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander (0.7%), more than one race (1.2%), or not reported (0.9%), while recognizing the unique identity of each group. Neighborhood socioeconomic status (SES) at enrollment addresses was estimated as a composite score of occupation, education, and income at the census-block level in 1990 and categorized into quartiles across California. The California Teachers Study has been approved by the institutional review board at City of Hope, and participants provided informed consent at baseline. This project was approved by the institutional review boards of the City of Hope and Columbia University.

Outcome Ascertainment

Hospitalization data has been collected annually from the California Department of Health Care Access and Information.29,30 This includes statewide records of inpatient hospitalizations since 1991 and records of emergency department visits and ambulatory surgeries since 2005. Mortality records detailing the primary underlying cause of death have been collected from the State of California mortality files, Social Security Administration Death Master File, and National Death Index.29

Nonfatal and fatal IHD and CVD cases were ascertained from hospitalization and mortality records up to 31 December 2018, the end of analytic follow-up for this analysis. Cases were defined using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding system, ninth or (since 1 October 2015) tenth revision. IHD cases include those with a principal discharge diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction or unstable angina,31,32 those who underwent a surgical procedure for coronary revascularization (e.g., coronary artery bypass graft or percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty),33 or whose death was due to IHD. See Table S2 for full definitions. CVD cases include IHD plus those with a principal discharge diagnosis of stroke or death due to cerebrovascular disease. Incident stroke was defined using ICD codes previously validated in the CTS.32 In secondary analyses, we separately evaluated stroke, and further restricted to ischemic stroke (86% all stroke cases). Participant follow-up concludes at the earliest of the following: date of event, date of death, initial relocation out of California (as outcome ascertainment mainly relies on statewide data), or the end of the analytic follow-up period.

Drinking Water Exposure Assessment

Community water supply boundaries.

The California Environmental Health Tracking Program developed the “Water Boundary Tool” project in an effort to map the customer service boundaries of public drinking water supplies in California.34 From 2012 to 2020, water system operators and local and state regulatory agencies collaboratively uploaded and revised service boundaries on a secure portal, successfully mapping supplies that serve over 90% of California’s population.34 The project obtained active boundaries during the time of data collection; therefore, historical boundary changes are not available. OEHHA has further revised and cleaned these boundaries for their “CalEnviroScreen” project; details documented elsewhere.26,35,36 Briefly, this involved estimating boundaries for other small systems, rectifying overlapping boundaries, and consolidating multiservice areas belonging to a single system. The dataset used in this analysis includes boundaries for 2,933 community and state small water systems (hereafter, CWS). Linkage to residential addresses.

Residential addresses were collected at enrollment, and subsequent address changes were tracked via linkages with the US Postal Service National Change of Address system, Experian, LexisNexis, and self-reported during follow-up questionnaires.28,39 The construction of residential histories for CTS participants through the follow-up period (1995–2018) is described in detail elsewhere.27 Briefly, this effort involved consolidating duplicate address records, prioritizing addresses confirmed by participants through follow-up contact, and estimating the move-in and move-out dates for each address in California up to a participant’s end of follow-up. Our initial dataset included 118,692 participants living in California at the time of enrollment, with addresses geocoded to at least the ZIP code centroid (Figure S1). Enrollment and any subsequent residential addresses in California were linked to CWS boundaries. It was assumed that addresses within CWS boundaries were served by a public utility, while those outside the boundaries relied on private wells. The majority of participants (n=109,251; 92%) lived within CWS boundaries for the duration of the study. There were 9,441 (8%) participants that lived outside these boundaries who were excluded from our analysis (Figure S1).

Accuracy of CWS linkage.

We assessed the accuracy of CWS assignment for a subset of participants (n=30,402; ∼25% of the initial cohort) who reported their primary drinking water source in questionnaire 6 (2017–2019), selecting one of the following responses: municipal, private well, bottled water, other, or do not know (Table S3). Of participants who reported using either municipal water or a private well (80% of respondents), 92% relied on municipal water and 8% relied on a private well, consistent with the percentage of women found to live or not live within CWS boundaries. Almost all (97%) of self-reported municipal water users lived at an address at the time of questionnaire completion that fell within CWS boundaries (Table S3). Agreement was 70% among self-reported private well users who were correctly found to live outside CWS boundaries. While self-reported information was used to assess the accuracy of the CWS boundary linkage, we did not apply further exclusions based on responses, given this information was collected for a small proportion of women decades after enrollment.

Annual arsenic concentrations.

In partnership with OEHHA, we collected data on arsenic measurements from CWS reported to the Safe Drinking Water Information System.40 These measurements were used to calculate average annual wAs concentrations in CWS from 1990 to 2020, following similar methods as CalEnviroScreen and described elsewhere.26,35,36,41 Data for years prior to 1990 were limited. We included only treated/delivered samples, with the exception of a small fraction of CWS with only raw/untreated arsenic samples (∼3%).35 While some sampling points are no longer active, we incorporated historical samples taken during active periods before decommission. For CWS that purchase water, often large systems serving ≥100,000 people, a volume-weighted average was applied to account for contributions from wholesale systems. CWS are required to report arsenic concentrations exceeding 2μg/L, the detection limit for purposes of reporting (DLR). About 38% of samples in our dataset were marked as “below the DLR.” In cases where samples had a value of zero or were marked as “below the DLR” with values unlikely to reflect true concentrations (i.e., equal to standard values: DLR, one-half DLR, one-half MCL, or MCL), we estimated values from a log-normal distribution using Tobit regression-based imputation.42 The upper bound for imputation was derived from the median of nonstandard (i.e., likely true) concentrations below the DLR. We found the median decreased around the time the current MCL was introduced; therefore, we employed an imputation range of 0 to 1μg/L for years before 2008 and 0 to 0.5μg/L from 2008 onwards.

Interpolation of arsenic concentrations following the regulatory schedule.

In compliance with the Safe Drinking Water Act, CWS routinely measure regulated drinking water contaminants, including arsenic, following the standardized monitoring framework (Figure S2). Three 9-year compliance cycles overlap with our study (first cycle: 1993–2001, second cycle: 2002–2010, and third cycle: 2011–2019), with each cycle further divided into 3-year periods.43 Most CWS sample annually or at least once per 3-year period. State waivers may be granted to reduce sampling frequency to once every 9-year cycle under certain circumstances.43 Among CWS linked to CTS participant addresses (n=1,389), 60%–92% of CWS had at least one reported annual wAs concentration per 3-year period (Table 1). We interpolated wAs concentrations for missing years using available data in accordance with the compliance schedule. Years without data were replaced with the average of annual concentrations within the same 3-year period when available. Otherwise, the average from either the preceding or subsequent (whatever came first) 3-year period within that 9-year cycle was assigned. For preregulation years without data (1990–1992), the average from the first available period of the first cycle was assigned. After interpolation, 79% of CWS had complete data for the first cycle, 96% for the second cycle, and 99% for the third cycle. Annual concentrations from CWS were linked to participant addresses, corresponding to the years lived at each residence. We excluded 1,838 participants from our analysis due to the unavailability of arsenic data for one or more of the compliance cycles (Figure S1).

Baseline exposure assessment.

We first estimated wAs exposure around the time of enrollment using a straightforward, time-invariant approach. To do so, we calculated average wAs exposure during the first compliance cycle (1993–2001) at participants’ enrollment addresses. We also restricted our analysis to participants who remained at their enrollment address during follow-up (i.e., “nonmovers”; n=52,237).27 For nonmovers, baseline exposure better reflects continued exposure from the same CWS during follow-up, as opposed to movers who may have different exposures from other CWS after enrollment.

Time-varying exposure assessment.

We then employed a dynamic approach, estimating time-varying wAs exposure over the course of each participant’s follow-up. This approach enabled us to account for secular trends in wAs concentratio'ns in response to regulatory standards, account for residential mobility over follow-up, and compare various exposure windows to investigate periods most relevant to the onset of IHD/CVD. Our primary measure of long-term wAs exposure was calculated as a 10-year moving average, recalculated for each year of follow-up until the participant was no longer at risk (i.e., upon the year of the event or censor). This exposure window was informed by a study in Chile that found mortality rates for acute myocardial infarction were highest during and immediately after a period of high arsenic exposure (1958–1970), with peak mortality occurring around 10-years after exposure began.12

In this time-varying framework, risk is estimated by comparing the 10-year average exposure leading up to an index case’s IHD/CVD event to the same exposure window for those who still remained at risk. As an example, for follow-up years 2005 and 2010, the corresponding wAs exposure averages ranged from 1996 to 2005 and 2001 to 2010, respectively. For the initial 4 years of follow-up (1995–1998), we extrapolated the first years of wAs exposure (1986–1989) using the earliest period of data available. In a sensitivity analysis, we restricted to those with follow-up <15 years (22,910; 23%) or ≥15 years (75,340; 77%) to assess for potential exposure misclassification in the earlier years.

For comparison with our primary exposure, we calculated 3- and 5-year moving averages, representing more acute exposures, as well as a cumulative average since 1990, representing more chronic exposure. In secondary analyses, we explored latency effects by lagging the 10-year moving average up to 5 years, beginning analyses in 2000 to accommodate lagged exposure.

Statistical Analysis

There were 107,413 participants with estimates of wAs exposure, as described above. We further excluded participants who were 85 years of age or older at enrollment (n=2,044) and those with a history of CVD before enrollment (n=779).33 An additional 6,340 participants were excluded due to missing covariates. Our final analytic dataset includes 98,250 participants, 6,119 IHD cases, and 9,935 CVD cases (Figure S1).

We used Cox proportional hazard models to evaluate the association between wAs exposure and IHD or CVD risk, presenting hazard ratios (HRs) with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). When modeling wAs exposure as a time-dependent variable, we included a cluster term to account for repeat measurements within a participant. We used person-years as the underlying time metric. We categorized wAs exposure by concentration thresholds relevant to current regulatory standards: <1.00, 1.00–2.99, 3.00–4.99, 5.00 (one-half MCL)–9.99, ≥10.00 (MCL) μg/L, and calculated ptrends by deriving the median of each exposure category. Exposure was also modeled continuously using a logarithm base 2 transformation (i.e., risk per doubling) and using penalized splines. In supplementary analyses, we present wAs exposure categorized into percentiles (<50th, 50th–74th, 75th–94th, and ≥95th) to depict the exposure distribution in the study population.

We identified potential confounders in the relationship between wAs exposure and IHD/CVD risk, informed by previous analyses33,44 and conceptualized with a directed acyclic graph (DAG).45 For our main model, we included continuous age as a time-dependent variable, continuous body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2) at enrollment, smoking status at enrollment (never smoker, never smoker passively exposed in the household, former smoker, current smoker),46 as well as race/ethnicity and neighborhood SES quartiles (see DAG, Figure S3). These factors are important in capturing underlying social and health disparities that can influence an individual’s wAs exposure and risk of IHD/CVD.47,48 In our second model, we additionally adjusted for other potential confounders including self-reported menopause and menopausal hormone therapy (MHT), diabetes, hypertension, and exercise (hours/week). In sensitivity analyses, we additionally accounted for smoking pack-years, to capture residual confounding, and log-transformed fine particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5μm (PM2.5) exposure, a well-established risk factor for IHD/CVD that can widely vary geographically.49 Exposure to ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) at enrollment addresses was estimated using the 1999 average from the nearest EPA Air Quality System monitor.50

We evaluated the consistency of the associations across participant subgroups in stratified analyses and by adding an interaction term between wAs exposure and risk factors, including age (≤55 vs. >55 years), BMI (<25 vs. ≥25 kg/m2), and smoking status (never vs. ever smokers) at enrollment. Our ability to discern differences by race/ethnicity and neighborhood SES was limited by exposure prevalence and the limited number of participants in the non-white and lowest SES subgroups. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.0.2 in the CTS Researcher Platform.38

Results

About 48% of participants were exposed to average wAs <1μg/L, 39% to 1.00–2.99μg/L, 9% to 3.00–4.99μg/L, 3% to 5.00–9.99μg/L, and 1% to ≥10μg/L during the first compliance cycle (1993–2001) (Table 2). Participants in the highest vs. lowest exposure categories tended to be younger, had slightly higher BMI, and were less likely to smoke. Participants living in the lowest SES neighborhoods were disproportionally exposed to higher wAs concentrations. For example, 3% of those exposed to <1μg/L vs. 16% of those exposed to ≥10μg/L lived in neighborhoods with the lowest SES (quartile 1). Exposure was also disproportionally higher among Hispanic or Latina individuals. A slightly higher proportion of those in the top exposure categories reported having diabetes, high blood pressure, and exercised less.

Baseline Exposure

Compared to an average wAs exposure at a baseline of <1μg/L, IHD risk increased across exposures ranging from 1.00–2.99μg/L (HR=1.10; 95% CI: 1.04, 1.16), 3.00–4.99μg/L (HR=1.11; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.22), 5.00–9.99μg/L (HR=1.19; 95% CI: 1.03, 1.37), and ≥10μg/L (HR=1.20; 95% CI: 0.97, 1.48) (Table 3). The effects were stronger in the highest exposure group when we included only those who did not move during follow-up (≥10μg/L : HR=1.34; 95% CI: 1.04, 1.71). The risk of CVD across exposure categories was weaker, as indicated by confidence intervals that included an HR of 1.00. However, exposure–response relationships were evident when restricted to nonmovers, with a significant trend of 0.035 (ptrend) across exposure categories, and a 1.03-fold risk (95% CI: 1.01, 1.05) per doubling of wAs exposure.

Secondary Analyses of Exposure Windows

Associations with time-varying cumulative average wAs were relatively weaker and less consistent across exposure categories compared to 10-year moving average exposure (Table S7). In lagged analyses of 10-year moving averages, we found some evidence of diminished effects with increased lag time at the highest two exposure categories (Figure S4). For example, IHD risk at ≥10μg/L, relative to <1μg/L, was 1.56 times greater (95% CI: 1.17, 2.07) when exposure was not lagged compared to 1.35 times greater (95% CI: 1.04, 1.75) when lagged by 5 years.

Discussion

In a large prospective cohort of women living in California, we found long-term exposure to arsenic from community water supplies, even at average concentrations below the current regulatory limit (10μg/L), was associated with increased relative risk of cardiovascular disease, particularly ischemic heart disease. We found an 18–20% greater risk of IHD among those exposed to a 10-year average of 5.00–9.99μg/L relative to <1μg/L. This relative risk more than doubled (42%) among those exposed to ≥10μg/L. Our study is one of the first detailed analyses to evaluate long-term wAs exposure from residential CWS and cardiovascular disease risk in the US to better understand effects below the current regulatory level.

Cardiovascular disease risk associated with relatively low As concentrations near the current regulatory limit has been described by few studies. A case–cohort study in Colorado found a 15μg/L higher lifetime average wAs exposure was associated with IHD risk (HR=1.38; 95% CI: 1.09, 1.78).20 An ecologic study in Michigan found that those living in six counties with a population-weighted average wAs concentration of 11μg/L (compared to the rest of Michigan with an average wAs=2.98μg/L) had an increased risk of circulatory diseases, including atherosclerosis among females [standardized mortality ratio (SMR)=1.11; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.21].51 Another ecologic study in Spain found those living in municipalities with CWS concentrations >10μg/L vs. <1μg/L had higher rates of cardiovascular disease (SMR=1.10; 95% CI: 1.08, 1.12) and coronary disease (SMR=1.18; 95% CI: 1.15, 1.22).18 Our study similarly found a 10-year average exposure to ≥10μg/L vs. <1μg/L was associated with IHD risk (HR=1.42; 95% CI: 1.10, 1.84) and CVD risk (HR=1.21; 95% CI: 0.96, 1.51). We consistently found that the relationship between wAs exposure was stronger for IHD compared to CVD, and we found no evidence of risk for stroke. Inorganic arsenic is widely recognized for its role in the development of atherosclerosis, which is a direct precursor of IHD.5,21–23 Consistent with our findings, numerous studies evaluating both outcomes have reported that arsenic exposure increased the risk of coronary diseases but found little to no evidence of an increased risk for cerebrovascular diseases.8,12,18,52–54 There are a few notable studies that have reported an increase in stroke risk associated with arsenic exposure.9,19,55 However, considering all the evidence together, several meta-analyses have concluded that the risk from arsenic is stronger for coronary disease than for stroke.15,17 Our findings align with the collective body of literature, which supports the methodological strengths of our study.

The availability of extensive arsenic data over the course of our study provided an opportunity to evaluate relevant windows of exposure. We observed similar findings in risk when assessing exposure at baseline with 9 years of arsenic data, particularly among nonmovers during follow-up, compared to our dynamic approach of 10-year moving averages. When comparing 3-, 5-, and 10-year moving averages, we found associations were strongest for the 10-year exposure. More prolonged exposure, calculated as a cumulative average, showed somewhat weaker associations. A study in Chile that found mortality rates for acute myocardial infarction were highest during and immediately after a period of high arsenic exposure (1958–1970), with peak mortality occurring around a decade after exposure began.12 A longer latency period was observed for lung and bladder cancer.12 To explore this further, we lagged the 10-year moving average up to 5 years, noting that effects at the highest exposure categories slightly diminished with increased lag time. Our study provides evidence that wAs exposure from the previous decade plays a key role in the onset of cardiovascular disease, and suggests that recent years of exposure also contribute to risk.

Our study is one of the first to describe exposure–response relationships at exposures below the current regulatory limit. When categorizing 10-year averages at policy-relevant concentration thresholds in reference to <1μg/L, we found modest elevations in IHD risk at 1.00–2.99 and 3.00–4.99μg/L, with HRs of 1.06 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.12) and 1.05 (95% CI: 0.94, 1.17), respectively. At higher concentrations of 5.00–9.99 and ≥10μg/L, the risk of IHD increased, with HRs of 1.20 (95% CI: 1.02, 1.41) and 1.42 (95% CI: 1.10, 1.84), respectively. Our findings of risk ≥1μg/L closely align with the California OEHHA noncancer Public Health Goal (PHG) of 0.9μg/L, primarily based on studies of cardiovascular effects.56 Unlike federal standards, PHGs are health objectives based solely on scientific evidence and do not consider other factors like cost and feasibility.57 Our study highlights the need for additional research evaluating health effects at exposure levels CWS users experience today to better understand risk below current regulatory standards.

We explored differential effects of wAs exposure on cardiovascular disease by known risk factors including age, BMI, and smoking status. Risk from wAs exposure was statistically greater among younger vs. older participants. Other cohorts have similarly found stronger associations among younger subgroups when evaluating arsenic exposure in relation to cardiovascular disease and lung cancer risk.58,59 One plausible explanation is that CVD risk among older women may be largely attributed to other age-related factors.60 We observed somewhat greater risk among those not overweight or obese at enrollment, compared to the those who were. Some studies have reported women with lower BMI tend to exhibit lower arsenic methylation efficiency, which can lead to greater systemic effects from arsenic in its more toxic forms.61–63 The synergistic effect between arsenic exposure and smoking in relation to cardiovascular disease has been described in previous studies.8,64,65 We observed nominal difference in the risk from wAs exposure by smoking status, although most CTS participants were never smokers or former smokers who had quit smoking by enrollment.

The CTS cohort was well-suited for this environmental epidemiologic study given the large sample size, geographic diversity across California, over 20 years of follow-up, comprehensive linkages to hospitalization and mortality records, availability of residential histories, and covariate data to assess for potential confounding and effect modification. Our study included over 98,000 women with 1.8 million person-years of follow-up and over 6,000 IHD and 9,000 CVD cases. This was instrumental to our ability to detect risks at relatively low wAs concentrations. However, relying on hospitalization and mortality data presents limitations (e.g., women must seek medical attention to be diagnosed, reliance on diagnostic codes). Studies that measure subclinical end points for all participants have the advantage of higher sensitivity but are more time-intensive and costly. Although women were recruited from the teacher’s retirement system, the study did not collect information about participant’s place of work. Therefore, our study is limited to estimating water arsenic exposure at the home and did not account for exposures at work or other locations. Our cohort includes only women who were mostly non-Hispanic white and exclusively teachers or school administrators. This limits the generalizability of our study, as it does not include men and may be subject to selection bias, notably the healthy worker effect.66 Most CTS women are well-educated and white and are likely healthier relative to the general population. The relatively homogenous makeup of our study population could in part explain why our results remained similar after adjustment for several factors related to health and social inequities. This also limited our ability to evaluate differences in risk by race/ethnicity and SES, especially given low wAs exposure prevalence. Despite this, we still observed disproportionately greater wAs exposure among those who identified as Hispanic or Latina or resided in lower SES neighborhoods. This coincides with previous studies describing disparities in water arsenic exposure across the US in more diverse populations.47,48

By collaborating with experts at the California EPA (OEHHA), our study leveraged a comprehensive drinking water quality dataset that included several decades of arsenic measurements from CWS.35,36 One strength of our study was the availability of CWS service boundaries that were linked to participant residences.34 We found 97% agreement between self-reported municipal water users and those residing within CWS boundaries. Boundaries lacked sufficient granularity to discern some private well users whose households were within service areas (30% misclassification). However, this potential source of misclassification affects a small proportion of our cohort who reported using a private well (6%). About 15% of participants reported using bottled water as their primary drinking water source, but this data was available for a small proportion of women who completed Q6 (2017–2019), and bottled water use has likely increased over time.67 Our exposure assessment accuracy is limited by not accounting for individual habits like tap water consumption, daily intake, and filtration use, although several popular tabletop water pitcher filters like Brita are not effective in removing arsenic.68 We also lacked information about historical changes in CWS boundaries prior to data collection in 2012.34 Future work to evaluate boundary changes, and whether this varies by system size and population demographics, would provide insight into this uncertainty.

We note several limitations to our exposure assessment approach including potential for measurement error, imputation of water samples below the DLR, and sparseness of arsenic data in the years prior to enrollment. In our sensitivity analysis, effects were stronger for those with ≥15 years relative to <15 years of follow-up, suggesting that there is likely more exposure misclassification in the earlier years. By recalculating exposure for each year of follow-up, our time-varying approach inherently accounts for secular trends in wAs from CWS, shown to decline in the early 2000s following the implementation of the reduced MCL.69,70 Future research using a causal framework to evaluate the impact of systematic wAs declines on cardiovascular disease incidence would significantly contribute to policy evaluation. Finally, we lack exposure biomarkers (e.g., urinary metabolites of inorganic arsenic) to validate our estimates using CWS measurements. However, two recent US studies showed a positive correlation between wAs exposure and urinary arsenic concentrations among CWS users.14,24 These studies were conducted across multiple states, including participants and arsenic measurements from CWS in California.

This study paves the way for several future research directions and opportunities. The drinking water quality data, now linked to the CTS cohort, provides numerous opportunities to study the health effects of arsenic, as well as other regulated contaminants. Beyond the CTS cohort, the data and methods used in this study can extend to other cohorts in California to assess the replicability and generalizability of our study results, as well as disparities among more diverse populations. Comparison of our exposure assessment approach against urinary biomarkers would provide insight into the validity of our study. This could include leveraging biospecimens in the CTS cohort that were collected in later years.29 Our study focused on risk posed to CWS users, but private well users may be exposed to higher arsenic concentrations in groundwater and do not receive routine monitoring.71 Future work is warranted to investigate risk among private well users, which could include leveraging groundwater measurements collected by the California Water Boards (https://www.waterboards.ca.gov/gama/).

In a large cohort of women in California, we found evidence that long-term arsenic exposure from community water supplies at levels below the current regulatory limit of 10μg/L may increase the risk of cardiovascular disease, particularly ischemic heart disease. We found IHD risk was significantly elevated among women with 10-year average exposures at and above 5μg/L, half the regulatory limit. This study is one of the first to comprehensively describe risks at exposure levels below the current regulatory limit. Our results are novel and encourage a renewed discussion of current policy and regulatory standards. However, this also implies that much more research is needed to understand the risks associated with arsenic levels that CWS users currently experience. The data and methods developed in this study can be used to bolster and inform future studies and can be extended to evaluate other drinking water exposures and health outcomes. Expanded research efforts in this field will better inform comprehensive reviews and future policies that aim to achieve safe drinking water quality standards nationwide.
