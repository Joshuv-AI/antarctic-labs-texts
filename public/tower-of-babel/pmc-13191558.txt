Experimental Study on the Purification of High-Hardness Arsenic-Containing Water Using Sintered-Clay Waste Materials
Authors: Qinghao Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sintered-clay waste (arising from the crushing of red and blue bricks), as the main constituent of urban construction waste, has a porous structure and water purification components: the fixed adsorbents (SiO2, MgCa­(CO3)2) and the effective purification substances (sodium aluminum silicate, potassium aluminum silicate), which provide a new adsorbent for the purification of high-hardness arsenic-containing water. Analysis of the components of sintered clay and filtered sintered clay showed that adsorption, complexation, and chemical reactions were the main purification mechanisms of high-hardness arsenic-containing water. The pore-specific surface area of the large-particle sintered-clay filter media was smaller than that of their small-particle counterparts. The experimental results showed that the process of total hardness softening included three stages: the accelerated stage, the decelerated stage, and the stable stage; the arsenic removal process consisted of three stages: the decelerated stage, the accelerated stage, and the stable stage. Both the effective purification duration of the total hardness and arsenic was 40–60 > 20–40 ≈ 10–20 mesh; the removal rates were 40–60 > 20–40 > 10–20 mesh at each purification stage, and the optimal particle size of the sintered-clay filter media was 20–40 mesh. After filtration, the amounts of components SiO2 and MgCa­(CO3)2 increased in the sintered-clay media; SiAs2 and Pb28As12S46 also appeared. The precipitates and the strong-adsorbent flocculation substances Al­(OH)3 were produced during the reaction between Ca2+, Mg2+, and silicates and aluminates in the sintered clay. The ions Ca2+, Mg2+, and arsenic were further removed during Al­(OH)3 redissolution and surface group M–OH complexation with Ca2+ and Mg2+. Both the confidence ranges of the total hardness and arsenic were narrow, and the confidence range of total hardness softening showed a greater change in the early stage than in the later stage; the trend for arsenic ran contrary. The average correlation coefficients of the total hardness and arsenic adsorption models were, respectively, significant at 0.9410 and 0.9625, and both consistencies of experimental adsorption capacities with their theoretical counterparts were greater in the middle stages than in the end stages. The hardness removal rate was 69.36%, and that of arsenic was 53.45%, both higher than those achieved by existing adsorption filters for purifying high-hardness arsenic-containing water.

Introduction

The utilization of groundwater and industrial wastewater has come into human view with the gradual exploitation of high-quality surface water resources. However, it is difficult to directly utilize groundwater and industrial wastewater because of high hardness and the presence of small amounts of heavy metal ions. Therefore, the softening of high-hardness water and the removal of heavy metal ions are of great significance for improving water utilization efficiency and alleviating pressure on water resources . However, the large-scale use of existing adsorption materials is difficult due to limitations in treatment effectiveness and regeneration costs. It is of practical significance to explore adsorption materials that are low-cost, highly efficient , and capable of strong-sustainable purification to improve the efficiency of water softening and the removal of heavy metal ions.

At present, the methods for removing Ca2+, Mg2+, and heavy metal ions include chemical precipitation, ion exchange, membrane separation, electrochemistry, and adsorption. Adsorption is a common method for water softening, and the main adsorption materials include lime, zeolite, alumina, bauxite, and fly ash. − In recent years, studies on the removal of Ca2+ and Mg2+ by exchange resins, polymer materials, weathered coal, bamboo charcoal, and nut shells have gradually emerged, but these methods were not widely utilized due to secondary pollution. The mixed zeolite A and X cocrystallized functionalized adsorption material promoted the synergistic effect of cation exchange and alkaline precipitation. The new sepiolite-polyacrylonitrile polymer composite resin could also effectively soften water. However, the above research could not meet the requirements of large-scale water treatment and was not easy to widely promote or apply . The permeable reaction membranes were relatively expensive, and the purification capacity decreased significantly with prolonged usage. − The application of electrochemical softening technology was severely restricted by the lack of environmentally friendly liquid–solid separation methods and numerous issues such as equipment, performance, and economy. − The methods for arsenic removal from water included commonly membrane technology, electrocoagulation, ion exchange, and adsorption. Membrane separation technology could not be used on a large scale due to high cost. The removal rate of electrocoagulation was significantly affected by factors such as metal electrodes, pH, current density, treatment time, and ion concentration. − Iron-containing substances had a good enrichment effect on arsenic; however, pure iron-containing substances were prone to agglomeration and hardening. − Adsorption was widely used due to advantages such as simplicity, high efficiency, and recyclability. At present, arsenic removal adsorption materials include carbon-based, inorganic, and waste-based ones. Arsenic adsorption by carbon-based and inorganic materials was greatly affected by pH, functional active groups, temperature, etc., and the leachate was unstable and even toxic. − The arsenic treatment using waste-based adsorption materials has been emerging, including electrolytic manganese slag materials, pyrolysis waste from discarded tires, and stainless steel slag. The adsorption materials of electrolytic manganese slag and pyrolysis waste had a relatively low arsenic removal rate. , Stainless steel slag permeable reactive barriers (PRB) had certain effects in remediating arsenic-contaminated groundwater, while the large-scale removal of arsenic-containing water was limited, and the sources were few. Obviously, research on the treatment of arsenic-containing water is a long and arduous task.

The accumulation of demolished and abandoned construction waste occupies a large amount of land with the development of cities. Conventional covering and spraying have solved the problem of air dust, and filling and mine underground filling could reduce the land occupation of construction waste. However, existing methods do not consider the high-performance utilization of urban construction waste. The research on construction waste components is of great significance for enhancing its utilization value and conserving resources. Construction waste contains multiple components such as waste concrete, red and blue bricks, soil, etc. Red and blue bricks (sintered-clay waste) account for a large proportion in construction waste, which have well-developed micropore structures and good adsorption properties, and contain a small amount of arsenic-removing components such as aluminum and iron. Based on the component determination and analysis, the adsorption, complexation, and chemical reactions of sintered-clay waste were explored in high-hardness arsenic-containing water purification. The characteristics of the component migration and the mechanisms of the ion adsorption, complexation, and chemical reaction were clarified, and the optimal particle size was determined. Application tests verified the feasibility of scale treatment on hardness softening and arsenic removal of high-hardness arsenic-containing water by sintered-clay waste. This paper used sintered-clay waste for the first time to simultaneously achieve hardness softening and arsenic removal of high-hardness arsenic-containing wastewater, which saved treatment costs and alleviated environmental pressure. The research results achieved a win–win situation of efficient utilization of construction waste and low-cost, long-period-scale treatment of high-hardness arsenic-containing wastewater.

Methods

Clay Components and Adsorption Modification

The sintered-clay waste was taken from an urban construction dump (Figure a), which was made from clay at high temperature. The main clay components (containing kaolinite) are hydrated aluminum silicate, calcium oxide, magnesium oxide, etc. The molecular formula of hydrated aluminum silicate is Al2O3·2SiO2·2H2O with a 1:1 type dihedral layered structure, and the crystalline water was present in the form of hydroxyl groups (Table ). The structural functional groups on the surface of kaolin included −Si­(Al)–OH, −Si–O–Al–, and −Si­(Al)–O (Figure b). Subjecting the clay to high-temperature sintering, calcination, dissolution, and crystallization, multidimensional pore materials similar to molecular sieves were formed, which possessed ion-exchange adsorption properties. Silicoaluminate (Na/KAlSiO4) produced in sintered clay could remove Ca2+ and Mg2+ through chemical reactions, and generate the flocculating constituent Al­(OH)3, which could adsorb Ca2+ and Mg2+ again in the treatment of high-hardness arsenic-containing water. During this period, some flocculating constituent Al­(OH)3 disintegrated to produce the ion Al3+, which reacted with H3AsO4 to form the precipitate of aluminum arsenate (AlAsO4).

The pores of granular sintered-clay waste included internal micropores and particle voids (Figure a). The abundant micropores in the sintered-clay waste were the main sites for Ca2+/Mg2+ adsorption, presented as linear or curved shapes with good connectivity, which was conducive to permeation. The sintered-clay waste mainly consisted of hydrated aluminosilicate, calcium oxide, and magnesium oxide.

Experimental Scheme

Based on the flow velocity and the crushing cost, the sintered-clay waste was mechanically crushed and sieved into granules with particle sizes 10–20 mesh (2–1 mm), 20–40 mesh (1–0.4 mm), and 40–60 mesh (0.4–0.2 mm). Each filter medium was then respectively filled into a transparent column of φ45 mm × 300 mm in three layers. The water sample was taken from the Quaternary aquifer at a buried depth of 60 m in the south of Jiaozuo, with BOD5 ≤ 2 mg/L, CODMn ≤ 5 mg/L, 7.2 ≤ pH ≤ 8.2, and 600 ≤ EC ≤ 1800 μS/cm. The contents of arsenic, Ca2+, Mg2+, and total hardness were respectively 0.15, 98.17, 113.91, and 709.89 mg/L. The water sample was placed into a Bartensioner flask and fed into the column through a hose. 1000 mL water sample was taken once every 8 h at a flow rate of 0.0004m3/min, which ensured both the bed contact time and the replacement of the water sample in the column. The process was monitored using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer until the contents of the ions Ca2+, Mg2+, and arsenic stabilized. Sampling was conducted over a period of 520 h. The experimental design is shown in Figure .

Results

Analysis of Total Hardness Softening

The curve of the Ca2+ content in the filtered water sample is shown in Figure a. In the initial filtration flushing stage, the Ca2+ content in the filtered water sample was higher than that in the raw water because the calcium components in the fine-grained filter media were dissolved. The washing times for 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media were, respectively, 22, 22, and 30 h. Subsequently, the Ca2+ content rapidly dropped below that of the raw water. The accelerated purification stages for 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media lasted for 144, 136, and 112 h, respectively. Over time, the adsorption effect of the sintered-clay filter media weakened; meanwhile, the strong-adsorption flocs Al­(OH)3 could be formed from the Ca2+ and Mg2+ with aluminate. Therefore, the decelerated purification time was prolonged, and the purification effect stabilized (representing the decelerated purification stage). The stable purification stage followed: the Ca2+ removal of sintered-clay filter media presented three stages after the initial filtration washing: the accelerated purification stage, the decelerated purification stage, and the stable purification stage. The curve of the Ca2+ removal rate is shown in Figure b. The smaller the particle size, the more Ca2+ dissolution occurred in the sintered-clay filter media during the filtration washing stage. In the accelerated purification stage, the smaller the particle size, the greater the Ca2+ adsorption and exchange, and the higher the removal rate. The maximum removal rates of the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media reached, respectively, 56.88%, 61.99%, and 67.27% at 144, 136, and 112 h, when the adsorption and ion exchange were sufficient. In the decelerated purification stage, the adsorption and exchange effects of small-particle filter media started to weaken, and the purification capacity of small-particle filter media rapidly decreased, as did the removal rate. The decelerated removal of Ca2+ continued, respectively, to 376, 376, and 400 h with removal rates of 54.07–56.88%, 57.80–61.99%, and 60.14–67.27% for 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media. The stable purification stage then ensued. The Ca2+ removal rates of the three particle-size filter media were 40–60 > 20–40 > 10–20 mesh.

The accelerated purification stages of 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media ranged from 8 to 32 h, during which the Mg2+ removal rates reached, respectively, 68.44%, 73.62%, and 82.25%, and Mg2+ contents were purified, respectively, to 35.95, 30.04, and 20.22 mg/L. This was followed by the decelerated purification stage (32–152 h), wherein Mg2+ adsorption was apparent in the early part thereof. The removal rate of 40–60 mesh filter media gradually approached that of the 20–40 mesh counterpart in the later stage due to the sufficient release of the ion Mg2+ in CaMg­(CO3)2. The Mg2+ removal rates of 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media decreased to 52.17%, 55.69%, and 56.05%, with the maximum contents thereof being 54.48, 50.47, and 50.06 mg/L, respectively. Due to the complexation of the flocculating constituent Al­(OH)3 and the Al–OH functional group, the Mg2+ decelerated removal of 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media was more stable and prolonged. The smaller the particle size of the filter media, the higher the Mg2+ removal rate was. The Mg2+ removal rate of the 40–60 and 20–40 mesh filter media was significantly better than that of their 10–20 mesh counterparts. In the later decelerated purification stage and the stable purification stage, the ion Mg2+ released from the CaMg­(CO3)2 significantly influenced the removal rate; the smaller the particle size, the greater the influence was. The Mg2+ removal rate of the 20–40 mesh filter media gradually closed in on that of the 40–60 mesh one.

The curve of total hardness softening is shown in Figure e. The washing stage of the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media was respectively 16, 16, and 19 h, during which the total hardness was higher than that of the raw water due to the dissolution of cations in the sintered-clay media. The total hardness entered the accelerated purification stage after the washing stage. The accelerated purification stages of the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media were, respectively, 16–48, 16–56, and 19–56 h, with the minimum content 279.78, 255.83, and 217.48 mg/L. In the stable deceleration purification stage, the total hardness was, respectively, 327.03–301.69, 398.58–297.74, and 285.60–291.24 mg/L. The curve of the total hardness removal rate is shown in Figure f. In the accelerated purification stage, the total hardness removal rates of the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media increased rapidly in 16–48, 16–56, and 19–56 h, with the removal rates being 60.59%, 63.96%, and 69.36%, respectively, and gradually decreased in 48–152, 56–152, and 56–152 h, with the maximum removal rates 53.93%, 57.93%, and 59.82% in the deceleration purification stage. The stable purification then ensued and continued respectively to 376, 376, and 400 h. Obviously, the total hardness of the filtered water from the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media all decreased rapidly to less than that of the raw water, and entered the effective purification periods of 32–520 h, with the removal rates being 57.94–60.59%, 57.36–63.96%, and 57.74–69.36%, respectively, including the partial accelerated purification stage, the decelerated purification stage, and the stable purification stage. The total hardness removal rates of the three particle-size filter media were in the order: 40–60 mesh >20–40 mesh >10–20 mesh.

Analysis of Arsenic Removal

The curve of arsenic removal is shown in Figure . In the deceleration purification stage, arsenic adsorption by sintered-clay waste gradually increased. The arsenic content in the filtered water from the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh sintered-clay media decreased, respectively, to 0.0930, 0.0943, and 0.0903 mg/L at 16 h, and gradually increased, respectively, to 0.1066, 0.1050, and 0.1034 mg/L within 48, 48, and 40 h. Subsequently, the long accelerated purification stage occurred until the arsenic content decreased to a stable level, at which point the complexation and adsorption capacity decreased significantly, and the stable purification stage ensued. The boundary points were, respectively, 184, 208, and 208 h between the accelerated purification stage and the stable stage for the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh sintered-clay media. When the sintered-clay filter media were washed for 16 h, the arsenic removal rate increased due to the strong adsorption on the pore surface, but the adsorption capacity of the filter media was subsequently weakened. The arsenic removal capacity of the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media decreased, respectively, to 27.19, 28.29, and 29.34% at 48, 48, and 40 h, and the arsenic adsorption capacity gradually increased with decreasing particle size of the filter media. Subsequently, complexation, flocculation adsorption, and chemical reactions gradually intensified, and the accelerated purification stages for the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media lasted 48–184, 48–208, and 40–208 h, respectively, followed by a stable purification stage. The removal rates of arsenic during the entire purification stage for the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media were, respectively, 27.19–48.01, 28.29–50.63, and 29.34–53.45%.

Analysis of Filtration Purification

According to the XRD results of sintered-clay waste, quartz (SiO2) was 42.3–51.6%, silicoaluminates (NaAlSi3O8, KAlSi3O8, CaAl2Si2O8) 32.6–49.0%, CaMg­(CO3)2 7.8–2.1%. Quartz (SiO2) was stable in sintered-clay waste. Silicoaluminate formed a framework structure with three-dimensional silicon–aluminum–oxygen tetrahedral units, which contained balancing electrical cations such as Na+ and K+ (Figure e). The surface of the silicoaluminate structure underwent hydration and dissolution during sintered-clay waste purifying high-hardness arsenic-containing water, as shown in eq . 2 2NaAlSi3O8⁡+⁡2CO2+11H2O→2Na++Al2Si2O5(OH)4+3H4SiO4+2HCO3−

Meanwhile, the sintered-clay waste possessed significant ion exchange/adsorption capability because of the pore structure, which was the main way to remove Ca2+ and Mg2+, as shown in eq . 3 2KAlSi3O8⁡+⁡Ca2+→2K++CaAl2Si2O8+4SiO2

The sintered-clay waste contained a large amount of silicates and aluminates, which could react with Ca2+ and Mg2+ in water. The main reaction equations were, 4 Ca2+/Mg2++SiO32‐→Ca/MgSiO3↓ 5 Ca2+/Mg2++⁡2AlO2−⁡+⁡4H2O→Ca/Mg(OH)2⁡+⁡2Al(OH)3↓ 6 Ca2+/Mg2++⁡AlO2−⁡+⁡2H2O→Ca/Mg(OH)2⁡+⁡Al(OH)3↓

The strong adsorption flocculating constituent Al­(OH)3 is produced in the reaction of Ca2+ and Mg2+ with aluminate, which can physically adsorb Ca2+ and Mg2+. When the concentrations of Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions are sufficient , these ions react chemically with Al­(OH)3 to form the coprecipitates Mg2Al­(OH)7 and Ca2Al­(OH)7. The functional group OH– in Al­(OH)3 surface complexes with Ca2+ and Mg2+. CaMg­(CO3)2 in the sintered-clay waste could release Ca2+ and Mg2+ in sufficient CO2 water. The reaction formula was, 9 CaMg(CO3)2⁡+⁡CO2⁡+⁡H2O→Ca2++Mg2++⁡(OH)2+2HCO3−

The forms of arsenic in water included H2AsO4 – (pH between 4 and 9), HAsO4 2– (pH between 4 and 9), H3AsO3 (pH between 4 and 10), and H2AsO3 – (pH between 4 and 10). Arsenic could be reduced by adsorption, complexation, and the chemical action of the sintered-clay waste. Arsenic in water usually exists in the form of AsO3 –, AsO4 –, and their salts. In acidic water, the aluminum oxides or hydroxides containing Al3+ partially dissolved, and the removal effect of As­(III) was poor in the case of aluminum-containing sintered materials. In neutral or alkaline water, As­(III) mainly existed in the form of neutral molecules (H3AsO3), some complex anions would appear, and physical adsorption (Coulomb force) would be generated between the surfaces of the sintered-clay media and the adsorbate, which improved the removal capacity of As­(III). pH had a small impact on As­(V) removal; the oxidized or dissolved Al3+ could react with H3AsO4 to form the precipitate AlAsO4 (reaction eq ). Arsenic removal of sintered-clay waste was divided into three stages: the deceleration purification stage, the accelerated purification stage, and the stable purification stage (Figure h). As­(III) existed as H3AsO3 in raw water. The first stage, known as the deceleration purification stage (AC section), included the arsenic descending segment (AB) and the arsenic rising segment (BC). In the descending segment (AB), arsenic was removed by physical adsorption and complexation. Physical adsorption included pore adsorption of the sintered-clay waste and flocculation adsorption. The large pore-specific surface area of the the sintered-clay waste guaranteed a stable adsorption effect on As­(III), and the adsorption capacity of the flocculating constituent Al­(OH)3 for As­(III) was greater than that for As­(V). In the arsenic descending segment, less Al­(OH)3 was generated and participated in flocculation, so the KSP of Al3+ did not reach the reaction level with H3AsO4 to form the precipitate AlAsO4, and no chemical reaction occurred for arsenic removal. During the early deceleration purification stage, Al3+ and As­(V) did not meet the KSP conditions; thus, no precipitation occurred during pore adsorption and complexation reactions. In the later stage, the arsenic removal rate was lower than the arsenic release rate, causing the arsenic content in water (Figure a). The second stage, known as the accelerated purification stage (CD section), was the main stage for arsenic removal , during which both As­(III) and As­(V) underwent their maximum removal rates. Adsorption, complexation, and chemical reactions all occurred during this stage The third stage was the stable purification stage (DE section), in which arsenic removal capacity reached a relatively stable state and then entered the attenuation period (after E): the purification capacity of the sintered-clay waste weakened, and the arsenic content in the filtered water upturned.

Influence of Particle Gradation on Purification

Due to the differences of particle voids, porosity, and pore-specific surface area, the adsorption effect of filter media with different particle gradations was significantly different. The removal rate of Ca2+ and Mg2+ was related to the pore-specific surface area, silicate, and aluminate components in the filter media, while that of arsenic was closely related to the pore-specific surface area and the Al­(OH)3 generated during the removal of Ca2+ and Mg2+. The pore-specific surface area of the large-sized filter media was smaller than that of its smaller counterparts; therefore, the removal rates of Ca2+, Mg2+, and arsenic in the small-sized filter media were better than those in the larger ones. However, the small particle-size filter media had a greater effect on water flow than the large particle-size ones because the particle voids of the large-sized filter media were more voluminous than those of the smaller ones (Figure b and c). The smaller the particle size of the filter media, the larger the specific surface area, and the stronger the adsorption purification capacity. When water flowed in the pores, water molecules were attracted to the pore walls by molecular force. The smaller the particle size of the filter media, the smaller the pores, the larger the molecular force, and the greater the resistance to the flow of water; therefore, a moderately sized particle medium was preferred when trying to ensure the removal effect of Ca2+, Mg2+, and arsenic.

Discussion

It has become a research hotspot to develop adsorbents from waste materials for treating high-hardness arsenic-containing water due to their environmental and economic benefits. Common methods for treating calcium, magnesium, and arsenic mainly include adsorption, electrocoagulation, ion exchange, and membrane technology, among which adsorption is widely used due to its simplicity and low cost. Research on adsorption methods mainly focused on the modification of filter materials in recent times. At present, among filter materials made from waste, electrolytic manganese slag, pyrolyzed waste tires, stainless steel tailings, and fly ash are used for arsenic removal, while lime, alumina, zeolite, fly ash, and lime + alumina are used for water softening. Among the various waste-utilizing adsorbents, fly ash had a higher arsenic removal rate than that observed in this study but could not be used to soften hardness; zeolite had a slightly higher hardness softening capacity than this study, while its arsenic removal capacity was not significant. In this study, the sintered-clay waste could simultaneously achieve water softening and arsenic removal of high-hardness arsenic-containing wastewater, with the removal rates of hardness and arsenic content reaching 69.36% and 53.45%, respectively. Obviously, the sintered-clay waste was a widely available, low-cost, environmentally friendly adsorbent that possessed significant advantages in both water softening and arsenic removal. For arsenic treatment, sintered-clay waste media (derived from the demolition of red and blue bricks from construction) was adopted for the treatment of wastewater, groundwater, or nonpotable use. Post-treatment or material modification would be required to meet stricter standards (e.g., the WHO guideline of 10 μg L–1). After being used, the sintered-clay filters could also be reused as filling materials.

Conclusion

The main clay components were hydrated aluminum silicate, CaO and MgO, and the surface structural functional groups formed the basis for high-temperature modification. The pores increased in both number and volume, and the pore-specific surface area expanded after the clay was sintered, which made the sintered-clay waste an ideal material for purifying high-hardness arsenic water. The main sintered-clay components were the fixed adsorbents (SiO2, MgCa­(CO3)2) and the effective purification substances (sodium aluminum silicate, potassium aluminum silicate salts, etc.).

The total hardness softening of the filtered water from sintered-clay media showed three stages: accelerated, decelerated, and stable, while the arsenic removal process consisted of three stages: the decelerated stage, the accelerated stage, and the stable stage. During the purification periods, the total hardness removal rates of the 10–20, 20–40, and 40–60 mesh filter media were, respectively, 57.94–60.59%, 57.36–63.96%, and 57.74–69.36%; those for arsenic were, respectively, 27.19–48.01%, 28.29–50.63%, and 29.34–53.45%.

The pore-specific surface area of the large-particle filter media was less than that of their smaller counterparts. The effective purification periods of the sintered-clay media for total hardness and arsenic were such that 40–60 > 20–40 ≈ 10–20 mesh. The purification rate at each purification stage was such that 40–60 > 20–40 > 10–20 mesh. Considering the purification cost and the purification rate for total hardness and arsenic, the optimal particle size of the sintered-clay media was 20 ∼ 40 mesh.

The components SiO2 and MgCa­(CO3)2 increased and SiAs2 and Pb28As12S46 appeared in the filtered sintered-clay media, which showed that precipitates formed and strong adsorbent flocculation substances Al­(OH)3 were produced during the reaction between the ions Ca2+, Mg2+, silicates and aluminates in the sintered-clay media. Ca2+, Mg2+, and arsenic were further removed due to coprecipitation and complexation during Al­(OH)3 redissolution and the M–OH surface groups’ reaction with Ca2+, Mg2+, and arsenic.

Both of the confidence ranges for total hardness and arsenic were narrow. The confidence range for total hardness softening changed more in the early stage than in the later stage, while the situation for arsenic was the opposite. Both the adsorption rate constants for total hardness and arsenic changed little, and the total hardness adsorption rate constant was greater than that pertaining to arsenic. The average correlation coefficients for the total hardness and arsenic adsorption models were, respectively, significant at 0.9410 and 0.9625.

The use of sintered-clay waste was proposed for purifying high-hardness arsenic-containing water. In such an application, the total hardness removal rate and that for arsenic reached, respectively, 69.36% and 53.45%, both of which were superior to those of existing adsorbents, thereby realizing improved waste utilization. In the future, the research focus could usefully be on the modification technology for the sintered-clay filter media to further optimize the purification effect of high-hardness arsenic-containing water.
