Genetic diversity analysis of sorghum grain traits and development of KASP markers for grain width genes SbTGW3 and SbGS5
Authors: Huiyan Wang, Jinbiao Li, Meijia He, Ruochen Zhang, Yujie Wu, Juanying Zhao, Yizhong Zhang, Qingshan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA thorough assessment of phenotypic and genetic diversity in sorghum germplasm is essential for uncovering key genes and advancing crop breeding. In this study, 340 sorghum accessions were evaluated over two years in field trials, with six grain yield-related traits systematically measured. Analyses of genetic parameters, correlations, and principal components indicated that grain width (GW) exhibits high heritability and is strongly positively correlated with 1000-grain weight (TGW), while the grain length-width ratio (LWR) negatively correlates with both traits. These results highlight the importance of GW in yield enhancement and imply that increasing grain length (GL) may not benefit yield performance. Using principal component and cluster analyses, we selected 11 germplasm accessions showing high yield potential. Through haplotype-based association analysis of eight key grain size regulatory genes, we identified ten superior haplotypes significantly associated with GW and TGW. Notably, two particularly promising haplotypes, SbTGW3-hap5 and SbGS5-hap6, which have been rarely utilized in breeding, increased TGW by 27.69% and 17.85%, and GW by 8.50% and 5.70%, respectively, compared to the population mean. We developed highly efficient kompetitive allele-specific PCR (KASP) markers for these two haplotypes, which achieved 100% target genotype selection accuracy in a test of 178 materials. This study not only deepens the theoretical understanding of the genetic architecture of grain traits in sorghum but also provides critical phenotypic targets, clearly defined molecular markers, and effective KASP genotyping tools for high-yield breeding programs. Introduction

Sorghum [Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench] belongs to the genus Sorghum in the family Gramineae and is the fifth most widely grown cereal crop globally [1]. It is widely used for animal feed and bioenergy production [2]. Its high drought tolerance and adaptability make it an important food crop in arid regions. Through long-term domestication and breeding, abundant germplasm resources have accumulated, providing a basis for studying sorghum agronomic traits and genetic mechanisms [3, 4]. Analyzing the phenotypic variation and genetic diversity of breeding materials, combined with evaluation methods to screen for high-quality germplasm, can help in breeding new varieties. Genetic diversity research is widely used for crops such as rice [5], maize [6], and wheat [7]. Sorghum-related research [8] has also promoted gene mining and breeding innovation to support improved traits and optimized breeding strategies.

Grain yield is a core target trait in crop production and is mainly affected by three key agronomic traits, namely, the number of spikes, number of grains per spike, and TGW [9]. An important component of TGW, GW has long been regarded as a key target for crop domestication and genetic improvement because of its significant effects on grain morphology and yield [10, 11]. In a Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) of sorghum, gene loci associated with GW have been identified, including SbGW2, SbTGW3, SbGS3, SbGS2, SbGS5, SbGW8, SbGL7, and SbGW6 [12]. Several genes involved in the regulation of rice grain size have been validated to regulate rice grain size and enhance yield. OsGW2 negatively regulates rice GW by inhibiting cell expansion [13]. OsTGW3 carries the encoded protein OsSK41 that negatively regulates grain size and weight, and OsSK41 interacts with and phosphorylates OsARF4. Loss-of-function mutations in either OsSK41 or OsARF4 result in larger rice grains [14]. OsGS3 is a major quantitative trait locus that negatively regulates grain size [15]. Increased expression of OsGS2 leads to cell enlargement and an increase in cell number, which improves grain size and yield [16]. OsGS5 controls the grain size by regulating the GW, fullness, and weight [17]. OsGW8 is homologous to OsSPL16, which encodes a protein that is a positive regulator of cell proliferation. Increased expression of this gene promotes cell division and fills up grains, which positively affects GW and yield in rice [18]. The presence of OsGL7, a protein homologous to the Arabidopsis LONGIFOLIA protein, which regulates longitudinal cell elongation, the upregulation of OsGL7 and the downregulation of its nearby negative regulators resulted in the elongation of the TGW and improvement in the appearance quality of the grains [19]. OsGW6 positively controls grain size and weight by promoting spikelet hull cell expansion and grain filling rate, thereby increasing grain yield, and knockdown of OsGW6 results in reduced grain size and weight [20]. Studies on the functions of these genes have revealed the mechanisms underlying genetic regulation of grain size, thereby providing important theoretical foundations for breeding sorghum and other crops. An in-depth analysis of rice gene function not only reveals conserved regulatory pathways for grain development but also provides molecular modules that can be used for crops such as sorghum.

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are the most abundant form of genetic variation in plant genomes and are widely used in gene localization, molecular marker breeding, and functional genomics studies [21, 22]. This typing technique constructs gene haplotypes by resolving nucleotide polymorphisms in conserved regions [10, 23]. It provides precise targets for analyzing the genetic mechanisms of complex traits and for molecular design breeding [24, 25]. Although the selection of superior haplotypes in traditional breeding mostly relies on unconscious selection, genome resequencing can trace the genetic imprints left by artificial selection and systematically excavate superior haplotypes [26, 27]. Parental selection strategies can be optimized by assembling ideal haplotype combinations [28]. Functional markers have become central tools in marker-assisted breeding as they associate allelic variations with phenotypic traits [29, 30]. Molecular markers based on single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci are widely used [31]. KASP markers developed based on SNPs have been widely used in crop improvement because of their advantages, such as high throughput and high accuracy [32–34]. For example, KASP labeling has been successfully applied to the allele typing of brown trait genes (bmr6 and bmr12) in sorghum, enabling efficient screening of target traits [35]. This technique has become a representative method for SNP genotyping because of its rapid analysis and visual detection [36].

In this study, 340 sorghum varieties were systematically analyzed for the following yield-related grain traits, namely, GL, GW, TGW, grain area (GA), grain circumference (GC), and LWR in three environments at two points over two years. Genetic diversity analysis showed that yield-related GW, GA, and GC were relatively genetically stable. In sorghum breeding, an increase in GW can significantly increase the TGW, thereby achieving a high yield. Through an association analysis of haplotypes and phenotypic traits, high-quality haplotypes of seven key genes, SbGS2, SbGS3, SbGS5, SbTGW3, SbGW8, SbGW6, and SbGW2, which are important for increasing the GW and TGW of sorghum, were identified. By analyzing different superior haplotypes for breeding applications, two superior genotypes, SbTGW3-hap5 and SbGS5-hap6, which have not yet been selected for sorghum breeding, were identified, and the corresponding KASP markers were developed. This provides an important reference for breeding selection and molecular research on sorghum germplasms with large grains and high TGW.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The 340 sorghum germplasm samples selected for this study were provided by the Forage Sorghum Research Laboratory of the Sorghum Research Institute of Shanxi Agricultural University. The population includes local varieties formed through long-term natural and artificial selection with regional characteristics and covers many modern breeding varieties, including restoration and maintenance lines. This includes parental lines that have made important contributions to the history of sorghum breeding in China, hybrid parents with a large area of popularization and breeding materials independently selected by our team. This population has a wide range of sources and rich genetic diversity, and more details on the plant material are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Plant growth

These plants were cultivated in two consecutive seasons in 2022 and 2023 at the breeding base of the Sorghum Research Institute of Shanxi Agricultural University, Jinzhong City, Shanxi Province, China (Dongbai Breeding Base, 37°41′12.0″N, 112°44′18.0″E) and the experimental site of the Sorghum Genetics and Germplasm Innovation Team of the Sorghum Research Institute of Shanxi Agricultural University, Yuncheng City, Shanxi Province (Yuncheng Breeding Base, 35°1′39.5 ″N, 111°0′7.7″E) in three growing environments, namely, E1 (2022 Dongbai), E2 (2023 Yuncheng), and E3 (2023 Dongbai).

Before sowing, sorghum seeds of uniform size, color, and shape were selected. Seeds that were unsuitable for planting were removed to ensure seed germination. The sowing row spacing was 55 cm, the plant spacing was 20 cm, and each material was planted in three rows. Each row was 5 m, three times replicated, in a randomized district group test. The seeding of identification materials was completed on the same day, the planting density of the materials was the same, the depth of the seeding was 3–5 cm, the seeds were sown in strips, and pressure was applied to the seeds to cover them with growing medium after sowing. Seedlings had 3–4 leaves for inter-seedling, 5–6 leaves for seedling fixing, as far as possible equally spaced seedling fixing, retention of seedlings, and strong and positive seedlings left. Double-planted seedlings were also removed. Each planting site was set up within two days, followed by consistent watering and fertilization, and plants of uniform height were then selected for bagging.

Characterization of agronomic traits

Nine plants were selected from each variety for agronomic traits in the experimental field. Six agronomic traits were evaluated: GL, GW, TGW, GA, GC, and LWR. Approximately 300–400 seeds were measured using automatic seed analysis and a thousand-seed weighing instrument, and the data of three replicated groups were averaged. The precision of GW and GL was 0.01 mm, and the precision of TGW was 0.01 g (Table S2).

Statistical analysis of phenotypic data

The mean, standard deviation, coefficient of variation, and principal component analysis of each trait were calculated using SPSS 27.0.1. Origin2024 software was used to analyze the frequency distribution of yield traits, significant difference analysis, cluster value refinement analysis, principal component vector analysis using R package “ggplot2,” correlation analysis using R package “PerformanceAnalytics,” correlation analysis using R package “ggtree,” correlation analysis using R package “ggtree,” and correlation analysis using R package “ggtree.” R package “ggplot2” was used for principal component vector analysis, R package “PerformanceAnalytics” was used for correlation analysis, and R package “ggtree” was used for cluster analysis. Haplotype analysis

In this study, a total of 340 sorghum germplasm accessions were resequenced. The sequences were aligned to the sorghum reference genome BTx623, yielding an initial set of 8,151,131 SNP markers. After stringent quality control, which involved removing markers with a missing rate > 20% and a minor allele frequency < 5%, a final set of 3,084,386 high-quality SNPs was retained for subsequent analysis.

Based on this dataset, SNP information within the target gene regions (including promoter regions) was extracted. The genotype information was obtained by reading the VCF file using the R package “vcfR”. Subsequently, haplotype analysis was performed for the target genes based on these data. To ensure statistical power in the analysis, rare haplotypes occurring in fewer than five accessions were excluded [37]. Finally, using Origin 2024 software, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) coupled with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test was employed to assess the significance of differences in TGW and GW among the different haplotypes of the eight target genes.

DNA extraction and KASP marker development

Genomic DNA was extracted from 178 sorghum seedling leaves using the CTAB method. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were detected using a microspectrophotometer, and the DNA concentration was greater than 25 ng/µl. This was loaded into 96-well PCR plates according to the number and used for high-throughput amplification detection. Sequences 150 bp above and below the SNP site were selected, and specific KASP primers were designed using the Primer 3 online tool (Primer3 Input [version 0.4.0]) (Table S3). The PCR system contained four components: KASP 2× Mix, SNP Primer Mix, DNA samples, and sterile water. The 5.0 µL system consisted of 2.5 µL of KASP 2× Mix, 0.07 µL of SNP Primer Mix, 25–100 ng of DNA samples, and sterile water supplemented to 5 µL. The reaction conditions were 95 °C pre-denaturation for 10 min, 1 cycle; 95 °C denaturation for 20 S; 61–55 °C annealing for 45 S, with a decrease of 0.6 °C per cycle; 95 °C denaturation for 20 S, 55 °C annealing for 45 S, 34 °C annealing for 45 S, and 0.6 °C decrease per cycle. 55 °C annealing for 45 S, 34 cycles; end of amplification. After the PCR amplification cycle, the fluorescence values were read using a fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument at 35 °C. The KASP genotyping system uses the fluorophores FAM and HEX to differentiate between two allelic loci. ROX was used to correct for signal differences between wells due to reaction volume errors.

Results

Genetic diversity analysis based phenotypic characters in sorghum germplasm

A total of 340 core germplasm resources were cultivated at the experimental stations (Yuci and Yuncheng, Shanxi). Six phenotypic data points for yield were measured over two consecutive seasons in 2022 and 2023 (Fig. 1). The distribution of six yield traits – GL, GW, TGW, GA, GC, and LWR – was analyzed in 340 sorghum germplasm resources. GL ranged from 3.11 to 5.48 mm, GW from 2.04 to 4.58 mm, TGW from 9.15 to 42.26 g, GA from 4.59 to 17.34 mm², GC from 8.33 to 16.01 mm, and LWR from 1.07 to 1.72 mm (Fig. 1, Table S4). Statistical analysis of variance across environments revealed that all six grain traits were influenced by environmental factors to varying degrees (Fig. 1). These findings illustrate the role of environmental factors in shaping crop phenotypic expression and underscore the need for further genetic characterization of these traits. Furthermore, analysis of the coefficient of variation (CV) for each trait in the sorghum germplasm resources can reflect the degree of phenotypic variation and improvement potential. The CVs for the six grain traits across three environments ranged from 5.89% to 21.53%. Among them, TGW showed the highest CV, with an average of 17.79%, indicating the richest variation for this trait in the tested germplasm; while LWR had the lowest CV, averaging 6.03%. The CVs for the traits, in ascending order, were as follows: LWR, GC, GW, GL, GA, and TGW (Table S4). Further analysis revealed that the broad-sense heritability of these six traits ranged from 88.34% to 97.09%, and the differences in CVs across different years and locations were all less than 2.00%. This indicates that these traits are primarily controlled by genetic factors, demonstrate high genetic stability and breeding value, and are highly suitable for further optimization through genetic improvement and molecular marker-assisted breeding.

Correlation analysis of grain yield traits in sorghum

Correlation analysis of the six traits showed that 340 materials were closely correlated in the three environments (Fig. S1). This was consistent with the results of the previous genetic diversity analysis. The correlations among the six traits showed a high degree of complexity (Fig. 2). The highest correlation with TGW was for GW, with a correlation coefficient of 0.85, followed by GA (0.84) and GC (0.81). Meanwhile, GL had the weakest correlation with TGW (0.67), which suggests that an increase in GW may be the key factor leading to an increase in TGW in sorghum during the process of breeding improvement (Fig. 2). The LWR was negatively correlated with TGW (− 0.14), indicating that increasing the GL and LWR is not conducive to increasing grain weight in sorghum breeding (Fig. 2).

Correlation analysis and principal component analysis of six traits. a Correlation analysis of GL, GW, TGW, GA, GC, and LWR. b Principal component analysis of GL, GW, TGW, GA, GC, and LWR. c Illustration of measurement method for GL, GW, GA, and GC. LWR = GL/GW

Principal component analysis based on the six phenotypic traits showed that the contributions of the first two principal components were dominant, with a cumulative contribution of 95.89%. The first five principal components together accounted for 99.94% (Table S5), indicating that the first two principal components adequately reflected most of the variance in sorghum grain traits. Principal component 1 (PC1) explained 73.26% of the total variation and was the main source of variation. The eigenvectors of PC1 showed that all traits except LWR had high positive contributions to PC1, with GA (0.991) and GC (0.981) contributing the most. This suggests that PC1 mainly reflected the combined information related to grain size (Fig. 2, Table S5). Principal component 2 (PC2) explained 22.63% of the total variance, and its eigenvectors showed that LWR (0.995) contributed the most to PC2. This indicated that PC2 mainly reflected the information of LWR (Fig. 2, Table S5). These results provide an important basis for the screening of germplasm resources related to grain size and shape. While GW (− 0.346), TGW (− 0.216), and GA (− 0.022) contributed to PC2 in opposite directions, this further suggests that research on GW traits should be emphasized in breeding sorghum for high yield, which is in line with the results of correlation studies. By focusing on PC1 and PC2, the genetic structure of grain traits can be resolved more efficiently and can guide the selection of breeding targets.

Clustering and exploration of sorghum germplasm resources based on grain traits

Based on the data of six-grain traits in sorghum, 340 test materials were cluster analyzed, and based on the genetic distance between different clusters, we clustered the germplasm into four primary clusters (Fig. 3). Cluster I comprises 11 samples, accounting for 3% of the total test materials. Cluster I varieties had significantly higher values for six traits, namely TGW (mean 33.06 g), GL (mean 5.09 mm), GW (mean 3.96 mm), GA (mean 14.37 mm2), GC (mean 14.60 mm), and LWR (mean 1.29), than the other three clusters. Cluster II contained 113 samples, accounting for 33% of the total test material, and the data for the six yield traits were fewer than those for Cluster I. The data for the six yield traits were significantly higher than those for the other three clusters. Cluster III contained 19 samples, accounting for 5% of the total experimental material, and all six yield trait arrays were the smallest. Cluster IV contained 167 samples, representing 49% of the total test material. Six yield traits were greater than those in Cluster III. Among the four taxa, the performance size of yield traits was Cluster Ⅰ>Ⅱ>Ⅳ>Ⅲ. Cluster Ⅰ showed the highest values of agronomic trait values related to grain yield, with an average TGW of 33.06 g, which had a significantly high yield potential (Fig. 3, Table S6). Cluster I represents a class of high quality germplasm resources characterized by large grain size and high TGW, which was significantly higher than that of the other clusters. Cluster II is an alternative material for larger grain germplasm resources. The six yield traits of Cluster III were the smallest and belonged to germplasm resources with poor performance in yield traits. Cluster IV was located between Clusters II and III, with medium performance.

Haplotype analysis of genes related to grain width in sorghum

To resolve the genetic variation associated with sorghum grain size, we resequenced the genomes of 340 sorghum samples and correlated the genotypic data with the grain size phenotypic traits. Through selective breeding, we aimed to identify the potential genes associated with increased grain size in sorghum. We selected eight genes SbGS2, SbTGW3, SbGW8, SbGS5, SbGW6, SbGW2, SbGS3, SbGL7 (Table S8). These genes were identified by Tao et al. through a GWAS that integrated two genetically diverse populations (n = 2258), identifying multiple genetic loci significantly associated with GW. Notably, these loci are enriched with orthologs of known grain shape-related genes in rice and maize, and show significant overlap with previously reported grain width-associated SNPs in these two species. This finding strongly supports common and conserved genetic regulatory mechanisms for GW in cereals [12]. Resequencing data were used to extract SNP information from the target gene regions. By comparing the sorghum reference genome (Sorghum bicolor v3.1.1), we screened SNP sites located in the coding region of the target genes and the 2 kb upstream promoter region. haplotype combinations and frequency distributions for each target gene were obtained by haplotype construction (Fig. S2, Table S9).

Haplotype analysis revealed significant associations between specific haplotypes and both TGW and GW for each gene. By correlating genotypic data with phenotypic traits (TGW and GW), this study identified ten superior haplotypes associated with enhanced yield potential: SbGS2-hap2, SbTGW3-hap3, SbTGW3-hap5, SbGW8-hap2, SbGW8-hap4, SbGS5-hap6, SbGW6-hap2, SbGW2-hap6, SbGS3-hap5, and SbGS3-hap6 (Fig. 4). Compared to other haplotypes within their respective genes, these superior haplotypes exhibited significantly larger TGW and GW. In contrast, the different haplotypes of SbGL7 exhibited no significant differences in either TGW or GW.

Analysis of superior haplotypes for use in sorghum breeding

The distribution of dominant haplotypes in different categories of materials was analyzed to assess their application value in breeding. In this study, large-grain materials (60 samples) with a TGW greater than 30 g were selected from 340 participating germplasms. The breeding values of SbGS3-hap5, SbGS3-hap6, SbGS5-hap6, SbGW2-hap6, SbTGW3-hap3, SbTGW3-hap5, SbGW8-hap2, SbGW8-hap4, SbGS2-hap2, and SbGW6-hap2 were evaluated based on the percentages of superior haplotypes. SbGS2-hap2 superior haplotypes accounted for 41 (68.3%), SbGW8-hap2 superior haplotypes accounted for 44 (73.3%), and SbGW6-hap2 superior haplotypes accounted for 31 (51.7%) large-grain material, respectively. This indicated that these superior haplotypes may have been selected by breeders during sorghum breeding (Table S10).

The superior haplotypes SbGS3-hap5, SbGS3-hap6, SbGS5-hap6, SbGW2-hap6, SbTGW3-hap3, SbTGW3-hap5, and SbGW8-hap4 appeared less frequently in the experimental materials. The percentages of 60 large-grain materials were 5.0%, 3.3%, 6.7%, 6.7%, 26.7%, 10%, and 8.3%. These results suggest that these superior haplotypes have potential for use in sorghum breeding (Table S10). We identified two significantly superior genotypes. The superior haplotype SbTGW3-hap5 had an average TGW of 32.81 g and an average GW of 3.88 mm (Fig. 5, Table S11). This increased its TGW and GW by 27.69% and 8.50%, respectively, compared with the population mean (Table S12). The high-quality haplotype SbGS5-hap6 had an average TGW of 30.28 g and an average GW of 3.78 mm (Fig. 5, Table S11). Its TGW and GW increased by 17.85% and 5.70%, respectively, compared to the population mean (Table S12). These two high-quality haplotypes have important reference values in selecting high-yielding germplasms with large grains and are of guiding importance in the study of the molecular mechanisms involved in the regulation of GW in sorghum.

SbTGW3 and SbGS5 molecular marker development

Based on the resequencing results and haplotype analysis, two exceptional genotypes, SbTGW3-hap5 and SbGS5-hap6, which are significantly superior and have not been selected for breeding, were identified. These two exceptional genotypes provide options for the subsequent polymerization of exceptional genotypic germplasm materials. The corresponding genotypes were distinguished by SNP site combinations, with the SbTGW3 gene located on chromosome 1 at position 1,529,622 bp (TGW3-SNP1) for G-to-A changes and 1,526,093 bp (TGW3-SNP2) for T-to-G changes. The SbTGW3 gene is divided into three genotypes: SbTGW3-GGTT, SbTGW3-GGGG, and SbTGW3-AAGG, with the SbTGW3-GGGG genotype being the target genotype SbTGW3-hap5. The SbGS5 gene is located on chromosome 9 at position 5,404,386 bp (GS5-SNP1) in the T-to-C variant and at position 5,403,747 bp (GS5-SNP2) in the A-to-T variant. The SbGS5 gene is divided into three genotypes: SbGS5-TTAA, SbGS5-CCAA, and SbGS5-CCTT, and the SbGS5-CCAA genotype is the target genotype SbGS5-hap6. KASP markers were developed at SNP1 locus 1 and SNP2 locus 2 of the SbTGW3 gene, and SNP1 locus 1 and SNP2 locus 2 of the SbGS5 gene, respectively (Table S13). The identification of 178 sorghum samples using this KASP molecular marker separated the six genotypes (Fig. 6). Among the 178 materials selected, there were seven materials of the SbTGW3-hap5 genotype (GGGG) and four materials of the SbGS5-hap6 genotype (CCAA). The selection rate of the marker results for the two genotypes was 100% (Table S14), which could accurately screen out the target genotypes. The results of the KASP markers for the 178 samples were highly compatible with the sequencing results, with 99.43% agreement for the SbTGW3 gene and 96.02% agreement for the SbGS5 gene (Table S15). By further analyzing the typing results of the KASP marker, a significant difference analysis of the TGW and GW showed that the target genotypes were significantly different from the other genotypes (Fig. 7). These results indicate that this molecular marker can effectively detect genotypes and can be used in molecular marker-assisted selection breeding for high-yield sorghum to improve efficiency and selection accuracy.

Discussion

Sorghum has become a globally important crop due to its strong drought tolerance; however, its average yield has remained stagnant over the past three decades and is significantly lower than that of maize and rice [38]. This yield gap underscores the urgent need for enhancing production potential through genetic improvement. A key strategy to achieve this goal lies in the systematic phenotyping and genetic characterization of germplasm resources, which is fundamental for uncovering key functional genes and providing support for molecular breeding [39–41]. Studies targeting genes and their allelic variants associated with yield traits can provide important theoretical support and genetic resources for sorghum breeding [42]. Rich sorghum germplasm resources are conserved globally. However, systematic evaluation of their genetic diversity remains inadequate, limiting the efficient use of these resources in breeding [3, 4]. This study was based on 340 sorghum germplasm resources, a population that contains both local varieties formed through long-term natural and artificial selection with regional characteristics and covers many modern breeding varieties. This includes restoration and maintenance lines, including parental lines that have made important contributions to the history of sorghum breeding in China, hybrid progeny with a large area of popularization, and breeding materials selected independently by our team [43]. This population is characterized by a wide range of sources and rich genetic diversity, making it an ideal material population for studying domestication selection patterns and mining key genes for important traits in sorghum.

Previous sorghum phenotyping studies have focused on major agronomic traits, such as plant height, spike weight, and number of tillers, which visually reflect crop growth characteristics, but do not fully reveal the mechanisms of yield formation [8, 44]. In contrast, there is a relative paucity of studies on grain phenotypes, such as single GL, GW, GC, and GA. The research often lacks precision and systematicity in their measurements limits in-depth analysis of the genetic basis of sorghum yield. To fill this research gap, in this study, we investigated the grain phenotypes of 340 core sorghum germplasm resources, focusing on six grain traits directly related to yield, with the aim of revealing the genetic basis of sorghum yield traits on a fine scale. The coefficients of variation for the six traits ranged from 5.89 to 21.53%, indicating that sorghum grain phenotypes exhibited abundant genetic variation in the core germplasm. The mean values of the coefficients of variation for GL and GW were 7.71% and 7.05%, respectively. The sorghum grain size had a stable phenotype with low variation. As the agronomic traits of cereal crops have a similar physiological basis, further cross-species comparisons showed that the pattern of variation in sorghum grain size was similar to that of cereals and rice, suggesting that these crops may control grain size through the same genetic mechanism [45].

Thousand-grain weight is one of the elements constituting crop yield, and increasing the GW is an important way to improve crop yield [46]. In the present study, correlation and principal component analyses of six agronomic traits related to grain size were conducted to investigate the relationships among yield-related traits. A larger sorghum grain size was mainly associated with an increase in GW, whereas the LWR was negatively correlated with both TGW and GW. This indicates that an increase in GW leads to an increase in yield, whereas an increase in GL may not be favorable. The TGW, GW, and LWR contributed in opposite directions, further supporting previous results, while revealing a negative correlation between the LWR and TGW improvement. This insight led us to conclude that slender grains, which were gradually eliminated during domestication and breeding, and contemporary sorghum breeding to enlarge grains primarily involve increasing the GW. The 340 sorghum varieties could be categorized into four distinct clusters that were closely related to GW and TGW. Xu et al. studied the population structure of 320 local Chinese-bred varieties of sorghum and categorized them into three clusters [47]. These findings suggest that the germplasm set employed in this study likely encompasses greater diversity in both origin and type, which may account for the observed differentiation in genetic structure. Notably, Cluster I displayed prominent agronomic characteristics, with its average GW and TGW significantly exceeding the overall mean of the population. The 11 accessions within Cluster I harbor a wealth of favorable genetic information, rendering them valuable parental sources for mining genes related to large-grain and high-yield traits, as well as for developing new high-yield sorghum varieties. Moreover, the high heritability and stable performance of their grain-related traits further enhance their potential utility in breeding programs.

Compared with the limited explanatory power of individual SNPs for phenotypic variation (typically < 10%), haplotype analysis enables a more precise resolution of genotype-phenotype associations by integrating genetic units (haplotype blocks) formed by multiple interlocking SNPs [24, 48, 49]. This haplotype-based selection strategy could significantly improve the efficiency of molecular marker-assisted breeding. In rice, haplotype hap2 of the LOC_Os01g68500 gene significantly enhanced seedling vigor by regulating the elongation of the embryo sheath [44]. In contrast, haplotype modules such as AET1-H2 and SNAC1-H1 are closely associated with an increase in TGW and optimization of grain morphology [50]. haplotype mapping allows the systematic mining of key haplotype modules controlling complex traits, which in turn guides breeding for the aggregation of dominant alleles [51]. This strategy has resulted in a standardized process for major crops, including haplotype effect assessment, functional marker development, and field phenotypic validation, ultimately closing the loop from genetic variation to breeding applications [52]. Eight genes involved in the regulation of sorghum grain size were selected. haplotype analysis identified seven genes that corresponded to superior haplotypes: SbGS2-hap2, SbGW8-hap2, SbGW8-hap4, SbGW6-hap2, SbGS3-hap5, SbGS3-hap6, SbGS5-hap6, SbGW2-hap6, and SbTGW3-hap3, SbTGW3-hap5. These superior haplotypes provide precise genetic strategies for breaking through yield bottlenecks in sorghum as well as theoretical references for complex trait improvement in other crops. Clustering analysis showed that the material of Cluster I carried both SbGS2-hap2 and SbGW8-hap2 haplotypes, which not only verified the accuracy of haplotype identification in this study but also suggested the potential for yield enhancement through haplotype aggregation. Further analysis revealed that SbGS2-hap2, SbGW8-hap2, and SbGW6-hap2 appeared more frequently (68.3%, 73.3%, and 51.7%, respectively) in this study population. This suggests that these haplotypes may have been unconsciously selected during traditional breeding. In contrast, SbGS3-hap5, SbGS3-hap6, SbGS5-hap6, SbGW2-hap6, SbTGW3-hap3, SbTGW3-hap5, and SbGW8-hap4 haplotypes had a lower frequency of distribution but carried material with significantly higher GW and TGW than the population mean. The SbTGW3-hap5 and SbGS5-hap6 haplotypes, whose TGW increased by 27.69% and 17.85%, respectively, compared to the population mean, were of high value for breeding use.

The molecular marker method is a genetic marker method based on variation in nucleotide synthesis sequences within the genetic material between individuals, and the development of molecular markers can provide an important basis for the analysis of species genetic diversity [32, 36]. In this study, we analyzed the haplotype distribution patterns of sorghum grain size-regulated genes to provide precise targets for molecular design breeding. The superior haplotypes, SbTGW3-hap5 and SbGS5-hap6. Polymerization of materials carrying superior genes, we can provide a selection method for breeding new large-grain sorghum breeding materials based on retaining the superior traits of chassis materials and improving the breeding efficiency of key sorghum materials. KASP molecular markers were developed for the key genes, SbTGW3-hap5 and SbGS5-hap6, which regulate sorghum grain size, based on their dominant haplotypes, SbTGW3-hap5 and SbGS5-hap6. The KASP markers characterized 178 samples with 100% selection accuracy for the target genotypes SbTGW3-hap5 and SbGS5-hap6. The results of the KASP marker on 178 materials matched 99.43% and 96.02% of the sequencing results of the SbTGW3 gene and SbGS5 gene, respectively, illustrating the high accuracy and effectiveness of the marker.

Our study systematically investigated the genetic basis of sorghum grain traits, indicating that GW could be an important trait for improving yield. Several superior haplotypes associated with increased yield were identified, which may be directly applicable in breeding programs. We also observed natural aggregation of favorable haplotypes in subpopulation cluster I, possibly resulting from unintentional phenotypic selection during long-term breeding. This finding provides useful insights for future efforts in targeted haplotype pyramiding to develop high-yielding sorghum varieties. To enhance selection efficiency in molecular breeding, we developed markers for haplotypes with high breeding potential. While this study outlines a comprehensive process from phenotypic selection to the utilization of natural variation for yield improvement, the biological functions of these haplotypes require further investigation. Moreover, the practical application of these haplotypes through pyramiding will need to be achieved via crossbreeding.

Conclusion

In this study, a genetic diversity analysis of 340 sorghum accessions was conducted, revealing a significant contribution of GW to yield potential. Based on principal component and cluster analyses, 11 sorghum germplasms suitable for high-yield breeding were selected. Evaluation of eight GW-related genes led to the identification of 10 favorable haplotypes that were significantly associated with increased GW and TGW. Among these, SbTGW3-hap5 and SbGS5-hap6 were found to be rare yet markedly superior in the natural population, demonstrating high breeding potential. KASP markers were developed for these two haplotypes to enable marker-assisted selection. In summary, this work lays a solid genetic and technical foundation for future development of sorghum varieties with breakthrough yield potential through genetic resource evaluation, molecular design breeding, and pyramiding breeding exploration.
