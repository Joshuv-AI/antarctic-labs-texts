Time trends in cluster profiles of psychosomatic problems, self-rated poor health, and low life satisfaction from 2002 to 2022 among adolescents in five Nordic countries
Authors: Einar Thorsteinsson, Håkan Stattin, Charli Eriksson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim was to perform person-oriented analyses among adolescents in five Nordic countries to estimate changes from 2002 to 2022 based on three key indicators of poor health status: psychosomatic complaints, self-rated poor health, and poor life satisfaction.MethodsData were employed from the HBSC survey of 15-year-olds (2002 to 2022) from five Nordic countries (N = 48,320): Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, and Sweden. Cluster analyses were performed.ResultsSeven cluster profiles were identified. There were small differences between countries based on these profiles. Substantially more boys than girls were found in the two profiles indicating good health. Among the clusters indicating poor health status, girls were overrepresented in profiles that had high levels of psychosomatic complaints. Increasing time trends over the last 20 years appear to be associated with an increase in psychosomatic problems rather than other indicators of poor health status. Decreases were found in profiles involving high levels of self-rated poor health only, low life satisfaction only, or a combination of the two.ConclusionsThe cluster profiles reveal different trends of poor health status among adolescents over time, increases and decreases, with sex being a significant factor. The increase in poor health status over the last two decades appears concentrated in psychosomatic symptoms, possibly reflecting an increase in everyday stressors during the survey period or increased sensitivity to these stressors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24673-1.Keywords: Adolescents, Mental health, Health profiles, Psychosomatic complaints, Self-rated health, Life satisfaction, Time trends, Sex differences, Cluster analysis

Mental health problems among adolescents have increased in many high-income countries over the past two decades [1, 2]. The Nordic countries—despite their strong welfare systems and comprehensive youth support policies—have not been immune to this trend [3]. This conclusion is largely drawn from analyses of temporal trends in adolescents’ self-reported psychosomatic complaints, as recorded in the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) database. These psychosomatic complaints have commonly been used as a proxy for broader mental health problems among adolescents. However, it is essential to broaden the scope of inquiry beyond this measure. Key indicators of poor health status in HBSC data include psychosomatic complaints, self-rated poor health, and low life satisfaction. Research suggests these three health indicators capture adolescents' subjective well-being more broadly [4, 5]. We will use the term “poor health status” in the present study to capture these three indicators , acknowledging both the term's subjective nature and the term's conceptual opposition to subjective well-being. Longitudinal studies in the Nordic countries have demonstrated links between psychosomatic symptoms among adolescents and future anxiety and depression [6, 7]. Poor self-rated poor health has been associated with future morbidity and mortality [8, 9], but also anxiety disorders [10]. Both these indicators are associated with future medication use and the use of medical services [9, 11]. Large-scale longitudinal studies investigating the long-term impact of poor life satisfaction on mental health outcomes are rare. Most often, life satisfaction was not the main predictor but rather embedded within broader well-being constructs. Studies tend to be cross-sectional or short-term longitudinal [12]. The need to broaden the array of health predictors employed to predict future health complications gives rise to the question of how these predictors can be integrated at the individual level to differentiate between health profiles of homogeneous groups of adolescents.

A recent study demonstrated a substantial interrelation among these three health indicators. They collectively constituted a common factor and were found to be fairly equally associated with various adverse psychosocial conditions [3]. As they measure the same underlying concept – poor health status – it is reasonable to assume that they would show similar trends over the last couple of decades. The question is: have they? Analyses of the time trends in these three indicators reveal divergent patterns. Most Nordic studies show an increase in psychosomatic complaints over the past two decades [13–15]. However, self-rated health and life satisfaction have remained fairly stable during this period [13, 16, 17] for both sexes.

Prior studies have mainly relied on correlational approaches. Here, the differentiation between variable- and person-oriented analytical approaches needs to be examined [18, 19]. Variable-oriented analyses examine an individual’s position relative to others in a group and draw conclusions about the entire sample. Problems are formulated in terms of variables, and the results are interpreted in such terms. In contrast, person-oriented analyses examine patterns of characteristics within individuals and draw conclusions about subgroups within the sample. Cluster analysis, a common person-oriented technique, was employed in the present study to group individuals based on three mental health indicators. Thus, rather than examining the three indicators in isolation from each other, we examined how individual combinations of the health indicators are grouped into distinct subgroups (i.e., cluster profiles) within the sample. Much is known about time trends in individual indicators of poor health status among adolescents in the Nordic countries—psychosomatic complaints, self-rated poor health, and low life satisfaction. However, little is known about how these indicators evolve over time for groups of adolescents who share similar health profiles but differ from other groups. Thus, how have groups of people with different combinations of these three indicators of poor health status changed in size and proportions of boys and girls over the last couple of decades?

Two recent studies have used person-oriented strategies to examine cluster profiles of adolescent health status using HBSC data with samples from Nordic countries. They used psychosomatic complaints and self-rated poor health as clustering variables. A cluster analysis of Swedish 15-year-olds for psychosomatic complaints and self-rated poor health, combining all five HBSC survey years between 2002 and 2018, revealed four cluster groups: self-rated good health, high psychosomatic complaints, self-rated poor health, and poor mental health. The last group included both high psychosomatic complaints and self-rated poor health [20]. The proportion of adolescents in the high psychosomatic complaints cluster increased for both sexes, especially for girls. The poor mental health cluster was stable for both sexes. The proportion of adolescents in the self-rated poor health cluster decreased over the survey years, especially among girls.

To investigate whether the findings from the Swedish study of 15-year-olds were applicable to adolescents in other Nordic countries, Eriksson and Stattin [13] conducted a cluster analysis of psychosomatic complaints and self-rated poor health for five Nordic countries for the survey years 2002–2022. They identified five clusters with similar content in the five countries: adequate health, self-rated good health, self-rated poor health, high psychosomatic complaints, and a dual health problem cluster characterized by both high psychosomatic complaints and self-rated poor health. Girls were overrepresented in the clusters high psychosomatic complaints and dual health problems. The number of participants in these two clusters more than doubled between 2002 and 2022, whereas the proportion of participants in the cluster self-rated poor health was half as high in 2022 as it was in 2002. Apparently, both increases and decreases characterize individual clusters of psychosomatic complaints and self-rated poor health over time.

So far, analyses of long-term trends in cluster profiles of poor health status among adolescents in the Nordic countries are limited to one study of Swedish adolescents and another study covering five Nordic countries, using psychosomatic complaints and self-rated poor health as clustering variables. There is a knowledge gap regarding time trends in health profiles for the three key indicators of poor health status. In theory, an individual may experience any combination of the three health indicators. In practice, however, these three indicators are closely related and represent a common factor [3]. Therefore, a smaller number of health profiles can be expected in a national sample. This study aims to identify health profiles that are similar across different groups of adolescents as well as between countries. The study examines time trends in the three HBSC health indicators analyzed as individual cluster profiles for 15-year-old Nordic adolescents from 2002 to 2022. Our approach to the concept of subjective well-being [4, 5] is original in that we examine how each of the three health indicator integrates with the others at an individual level, giving rise to different mental health profiles for different groups of adolescents, that can be similar across countries. Given that most time trend studies in the Nordic countries using HBSC data have highlighted increasing psychosomatic complaints as evidence of deteriorated mental health over the last two decades, a critical question is whether this increase reflects increases specific to profiles with elevated levels of psychosomatic complaints or shows similar trends in other cluster profiles.

Methods

Participants

The present study includes data from 15-year-olds who participated in the HBSC surveys conducted in 2002, 2006, 2010, 2014, 2018, and 2022, in Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, and Sweden, see Table 1. Participants were selected by random cluster sampling within strata defined by geographical area to ensure nationally representative datasets. School classes served as sampling units and stratification was proportional. In Iceland, which began participating in the HBSC after 2002, all schools were invited to participate. Participation rates of eligible students in the 2022 survey are as follows: Denmark: 70%, Finland: 74% (estimated), Iceland: 83%, Norway: 81%, and Sweden: 80%. Participation rates have been somewhat higher among students during previous data collections. The proportion of schools that accepted the invitation have varied by country over the years due to differences in acceptance rate at the school level. To achieve the target number of participants, non-participating schools have been replaced by other schools belonging to the same stratum, which is defined by school size or other relevant aspects. Further information on the participants can be found in a previous publication [13]. Students completed the internationally standardized questionnaire under the guidance of their teacher. They were informed verbally and in writing about the confidentiality of their answers, and participation was voluntary and anonymous. The HBSC Data Management Centre at the University of Bergen, Norway, monitored data quality, performed necessary data cleaning and merged the national data sets into a Nordic file. The data collection methodology is described in the HBSC protocol [21], which ensures consistency in sampling methods, survey instruments, and data collection procedures.

Measures

To measure psychosomatic complaints (HBSC-SCL), adolescents were asked, “In the past 6 months, how often have you had the following.” followed by eight items: “headache,” “stomachache,” “backache,” “feeling low,” “irritability or bad temper,” “feeling nervous,” “difficulty falling asleep,” and “feeling dizzy.” Responses were scored on a scale from 1 (rarely or never) to 5 (almost every day). The Cronbach's alpha for this scale was 0.86. The HBSC-SCL has acceptable test-retest reliability and internal consistency [22–24] and has cross-cultural validity and good psychometric properties [25–27].

Self-rated poor health was assessed with a single item asking, “Would you say your health is…?” with responses ranging from 1 (excellent) to 4 (poor). This measure was adapted from Kaplan and Camacho's [28] measure of self-rated health. It provides researchers with a simple and convenient way of including information in a health survey and has been shown to work well in large epidemiological surveys [29, 30]. Studies have established good test–retest reliability of self-rated health [31]. Self-rated health has been shown to have good validity in many HBSC publications [21]. The possibility of semantic bias has been estimated to be low when comparing Nordic countries [32]. The scale has been analysed using excellent high self-rated health [17] or low self-rated health [33]. In the present analyses the continuous scale is used in the assessment.

Low life satisfaction was measured using the Cantril Ladder, where participants rated their life satisfaction on a scale from 0 (worst possible life) to 10 (best possible life), reverse coded. The single item life satisfaction has good reliability and validity among adolescents [34] and similar validity to that of multi-item assessments of life satisfaction among adults [35] and adolescents [36].

Statistical analyses

To determine the number of clusters, a hierarchical cluster analysis using Ward’s method for the three health indicators was conducted. They were standardized prior to the cluster analysis. The lower limit for the number of clusters was set to 67% of the error sums of squares [18]. As recommended by Kinder, Curtiss and Kalichman [37], with knowledge of the number of clusters, a non-hierarchical cluster analyses, K-means clustering, was used to arrive at the final cluster solution. The clusters were cross-tabulated by sex, country, and survey year. Adjusted standardized residuals were used to examine differences in the proportions of each cluster. These residuals in a contingency table follow a standard normal distribution. Values greater than or equal to 3.29 or less than or equal to −3.29 indicate a significant deviation from the null hypothesis at the 0.001 level. To avoid clusters with extreme values, we recorded the three standardized cluster variables so that values equal to and above 2.5 were recoded to 2.5. The effect size was measured by Cramer’s V where .02 is a small effect; .20 to .60 is a medium effect; and > .60 is a large effect.

Results

Psychosomatic complaints correlated with self-rated poor health at .37 and with low life satisfaction at .49. Self-rated poor health was correlated with low life satisfaction at .42.

Cluster analysis combining data from all included Nordic countries and survey years identified seven clusters. As shown in Table 2, two clusters reflected good health: good overall health and adequate health. The remaining five clusters represented different forms of poor health status: psychosomatic complaints only, low life satisfaction only, low life satisfaction and self-rated poor health, self-rated poor health only, and poor overall health status (with high levels for all three indicators). These seven health profiles accounted for 71.2% of the error sums of squares in the cluster analysis.

Country differences

First, we examined the reproducibility of the overall cluster solution for each country. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the cluster analyses conducted on the data from each of the five Nordic countries mostly produced the same cluster groups as the analysis combining all the countries’ data. One exception was low life satisfaction and self-rated poor health. This cluster was only reproduced in Norway. The psychosomatic complaints only cluster was not reproduced in Norway, while it was present in the other countries. Overall, we conclude that cluster analysis combining all countries yields similar results to those conducted for individual countries, with a few exceptions.

Table 3 shows the differences between countries for each cluster, organised by sex, with the same cluster centroids for all countries. The differences between countries had a small effect size both for boys and girls (Cramer’s V is .10 for boys and .12 for girls). The psychosomatic complaints only cluster was more frequent in Finland, Iceland, and Sweden than in Denmark and Norway for both sexes. The poor overall health status cluster was more common in Iceland and Sweden than in the other countries, especially among girls. When the seven clusters were dichotomized into ‘good’ and ‘poor’ health categories, girls in Finland scored lower and girls in Sweden scored higher in the ‘bad’ health clusters than girls in the other countries.

Time trends

An overall analysis was performed, cross-tabulating these profiles with the six survey years (see Table 4). We would expect to find significantly low values of the adjusted standardized residuals in the early years of the survey if there is a general increase in a mental health profile over time, followed by significantly high residuals in the final years of the survey. Conversely, the opposite would be true for declines in mental health over time. The clearest example of a gradual increase from 2002 to 2022 in Table 4 is the health profile psychosomatic complaints only which affects both sexes. A gradual decrease is evident in the adequate health cluster for girls, as well as in the 'perceived health problems' cluster self-rated poor health, also for girls. Despite these time trends, a small effect size was revealed for both boys, χ²(df = 30, N = 23,699) = 330.90, p <.001, Cramer’s V = .05 and girls, χ²(df = 30, N = 24,351) = 1,212.05, p <.001, Cramer’s V = .10. It appears that only minor changes occurred from one survey year to the next.

Although differences between individual survey years were small, changes over time may be more substantial when comparing the period’s end points—2002 and 2022. The seven health profiles presented in Table 4 can be grouped into broader categories of health profiles (see Table 5). We conducted an overall analysis of time trends, comparing the survey years of 2002 and 2022 separately for boys and girls. We consolidated the seven clusters into three types of health profiles: (a) the first two clusters involving good health; (b) the two clusters involving high levels of psychosomatic complaints (either psychosomatic complaints only, or together with high levels of self-rated poor health and low life satisfaction); and (c) the remaining three clusters (high self-rated poor health only; low life satisfaction only; or high self-rated poor health in combination with low life satisfaction). The changes that took place between these three profiles are reported in Table 5. The increases or decreases that occurred for these three clusters were substantial, particularly for girls. This was most evident in the two clusters involving high levels of psychosomatic complaints. The proportion of boys in these clusters increased by over 100%, and by over 150% among girls. A slight decrease was found for the two good health clusters among boys but belonging to a good health cluster decreased by almost 50% among girls. Finally, the proportion of boys and girls belonging to the three clusters involving high levels of either self-rated poor health alone, low life satisfaction alone, or a combination of the two decreased by 24 and 38%, respectively. Overall, according to Cramer’s V (.15 for boys and .33 for girls), the effect size was low for boys but medium for girls. Hence, more substantial changes took place for these three types of health profiles for girls than for boys between 2002 and 2022.

Discussion

Drawing on the conceptualization of adolescents’ subjective well-being as a construct encompassing psychosocial issues, self-reported poor health and low life satisfaction [4, 5], this study integrated these health indicators at an individual level in an attempt to identify distinct health profiles among groups of adolescents. We conducted a cluster analysis of three indicators of poor health status using HBSC survey data from 15-year-olds from 2002 to 2022. Cluster analyses across survey years revealed two clusters characterized by good health and five clusters characterized by poor health status. Boys were overrepresented in the “good health” clusters while girls were overrepresented among the “poor health” clusters with high levels of psychosomatic complaints. The seven clusters were largely similar to the clusters extracted from each of the five Nordic countries separately. This demonstrates that the key findings are not specific to one country but rather apply more broadly to the Nordic countries as a whole. Furthermore, the percentage of boys and girls in each of the seven clusters was similar in each country.

An overall analysis that cross-tabulated these profiles with the six survey years revealed a small effect size. This suggests that the changes that occurred over the survey years were not substantially different from one year to the next. However, considerable changes occurred in some of the cluster profiles over the total 20-year timeframe. A key finding is that increases in health problems over the two decades were primarily driven by an increase in psychosomatic issues.

These findings are consistent with previous research examining health profiles based on psychosomatic problems and self-reported poor health among adolescents in the five Nordic countries during the same time period [13]. In that study, clusters with high levels of psychosomatic complaints doubled over the 20 years. This increase in psychosomatic complaints is also consistent with cluster studies of Swedish adolescents of different ages. Eriksson and Stattin found that among the two clusters characterized by high levels of psychosomatic complaints, the increase with age was double among 15-year-olds compared with 11-year-olds (21% versus 41%) [20].

Trends in poor health status are not only characterized by increases. There have also been declines in some clusters of poor health status over time. Declines were observed in health profiles involving high levels of self-reported poor health, either alone or in conjunction with low life satisfaction.

Sex differences

There is a clear difference between boys and girls in the proportion belonging to the two “good” health clusters versus the five “bad” health clusters. In 2002, two out of three boys were in “good” health clusters, and in 2022, six out of ten boys were still in these two clusters. Among girls, 50% were in these clusters in 2002, compared to only 28% in 2022. This change in sex trends over time is notable.

Girls were overrepresented in the two health profiles that included high psychosomatic complaints, and the increase in these profiles from 2002 to 2022 was higher in girls (157%) than in boys (107%). The overrepresentation of girls in health profiles with high levels of psychosomatic complaints, and the stronger increase over time for girls, has much support in the health literature. Many studies have examined changes in psychosomatic complaints over time, and national public health agencies have noted this deterioration in psychosomatic complaints in the Nordic countries, leading to conclusions about a general decline in adolescent mental health, especially among girls [14, 15, 38–42]. These studies rarely acknowledge that temporal stability has been observed for self-rated health [13, 17] and low life satisfaction [16], with this stability evident among both boys and girls. The present study shows that profiles involving high levels of self-rated poor health, either alone or in combination with low life satisfaction, actually decrease over time.

Higher prevalence of girls in profiles involving high psychosomatic problems and lack of sex differences in other profiles involving high levels of self-rated poor health appear to have analogous sex differences in the predictive impact for health and mental health in the literature. Existing longitudinal studies show that sex appears to be important in linking psychosomatic symptoms to future anxiety and depression [6, 9]. In contrast, stability in self-rated health over time was not found to be associated with sex [43], sex was not important in the relationship between self-rated health and a variety of measures covering medical, social, and personal factors [44], and no significant sex interactions were found in a longitudinal study examining the association between self-rated health and future mortality [45].

Potential explanations for increases and decreases in “poor” health clusters

Increases and decreases in “poor” health clusters, raise the question of what macro-, meso-, and micro-level conditions are driving these opposite trends over time. Psychosomatic complaints have been conceptualized as responses to psychosocial stressors over which individuals have little control [46]. A study by Corell et al. [47] confirmed that psychosomatic complaints in early adolescence are highly indicative of stress. The researchers demonstrated a high correlation of 0.71 among 13-year-old adolescents between psychosomatic complaints and Cohen’s Perceived Stress Scale, a well-known instrument for measuring perceived general stress, thus suggesting that these two instruments may be measuring about the same underlying construct. This study underscores the central role of psychosocial stress in shaping the prevalence and intensity of psychosomatic complaints during this period. In this context, psychosomatic complaints can be conceptualized as adolescents’ responses to unpredictable and uncontrollable experiences, functioning as a sensitive indicator of stress exposure. The rise in health profiles marked by elevated levels of psychosomatic complaints over the span of two decades in the present study can be interpreted as indicative of an escalation in prevalent stressors during this period or an increase in adolescents’ sensitivity to these stressors [48]. These stressors may include academic pressure, interpersonal conflict with peers, problematic social media use, familial challenges, or a perceived lack of autonomy when it comes to different aspects of daily life.

Relevance for policy and practice

When information on time trends is combined with information on psychosocial problems in these profiles, the main message is that increases in recent decades have taken place mainly in the health profile with high levels of psychosomatic complaints only. But this health profile is not characterized by adolescents with many psychosocial problems. Two previous studies of health profiles have come to this conclusion. In a cluster analysis of psychosomatic complaints and self-rated poor health among Swedish 15-year-olds, Eriksson and Stattin [49] found that, compared with the average person, adolescents in the high psychosomatic complaints cluster did not differ much in terms of support from parents and friends, and had only slightly lower perceptions of a positive self and less positive school experiences. Using the same cluster variables for 15-year-olds in five Nordic countries, Eriksson and Stattin [13] found significantly higher but not substantially higher levels compared to the average adolescent for feeling stressed, loneliness, irregular health habits, poor communication with parents, not liking school, and taking medicine. In short, even if the prevalence of young people in the cluster profile with high levels of psychosomatic complaints only has increased considerably between 2002 and 2022, these adolescents do not seem to report much higher levels of psychosocial problems than the average young person. It is when their high levels of psychosomatic problems are combined with self-rated poor health that these adolescents differ markedly from other young people. This means that an increase in psychosomatic complaints over time is not necessarily accompanied by an increase in problematic psychosocial functioning over the same period. This is important information for public health practitioners. The results indicate when psychosomatic complaints can be considered a risk factor and when they are not.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of the present study is its examination of three key indicators of poor health status (psychosomatic complaints, self-rated poor health, and low life satisfaction) in HBSC data and how combinations of these indicators in different subgroups help us understand the observed trends in poor health status among adolescents in the Nordic countries. The use of a large dataset from the internationally recognized HBSC study, which follows a standardized research protocol across 50 countries, is another strength. This protocol covers all stages of data collection, ensuring consistency in sampling, instrument design, and data cleaning. However, the study’s primary limitation lies in its inability to provide insight into the causal relationships between the three health indicators. A limitation is also that the psychosomatic complaints scale had eight items, whereas self-rated poor health and low life satisfaction were single-item measures. Single items give less precision and increase measurement error. Further, the study used non-clinical measures of subjective health. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to clinical settings. One final limitation is our lack of information on how adolescents evaluate and cope with stress in their lives. Our study focuses on the end results, namely adolescents’ different perceptions of health indicators.

Conclusions

Across survey years, adolescents in the Nordic countries have consistently rated themselves positively on self-rated health and life satisfaction, and these two indicators have remained fairly stable over recent decades. In contrast, reports of psychosomatic complaints have increased. To move beyond variable-oriented approaches that rely on mean levels across entire samples—and to acknowledge that individuals differ in their patterns across the three indicators—the present study examined how the prevalence of adolescent subgroups with distinct health profiles has changed over the past 20 years. Three trends in patterns could be observed over time. First, the prevalence of adolescents in the two “good” health clusters was significantly lower today than it was 20 years ago for girls. Second, the proportion of adolescents in the clusters with high levels of psychosomatic complaints increased considerably over time, especially for girls. Third, the period from 2002 to 2022 was not only marked by an increase in mental health issues among adolescents. Over time, there has been a decline in clusters characterized by high levels of self-rated poor health, low life satisfaction, or both. The common assumption that adolescent health in the Nordic countries has worsened in recent decades may have overlooked the role of differing health status patterns across adolescent groups. We speculate that the observed rise in health profiles marked by high levels of psychosomatic complaints over the past two decades reflects an increase in stressors during the study period, as perceived by the adolescents, or heightened sensitivity to those stressors.
