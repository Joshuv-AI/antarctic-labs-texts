Beliefs underlying weight control behaviors among adolescents and emerging adults living with obesity: an elicitation qualitative study
Authors: Yu-Cheng Lin, Pei-Ching Liu, Fang-Fei Chou, I-Ching Hou, Cheng-Chen Chou, Chi-Wen Chen, Sophia H Hu, Shu-Ping Chen, Hui-Ju Lo, Fen-Fen Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWeight control behaviors are commonly observed among adolescents and emerging adults. However, the high prevalence of overweight and obesity in both these age groups remains an imperative health concern. Adolescents and emerging adults represent the most vulnerable groups facing the health burden of obesity.MethodsThis study was designed to articulate the underlying beliefs concerning weight control behaviors among adolescents and emerging adults living with obesity by an elicitation qualitative study based on the integrated behavioral model (IBM). Semi-structured and individual interviewing was utilized. Eligible participants were recruited during September and November 2020. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using a hybrid inductive and deductive content analysis approach.ResultsTwenty-seven participants, comprising 12 adolescents and 15 emerging adults living with obesity, were interviewed to explore their beliefs and strategies regarding weight control behaviors. Emerging adults were more likely to engage in extreme weight control methods. Three overarching themes emerged across both age groups: eating-centered concerns, focus on physical appearance, and social interference. Significant differences were noted while adolescents and emerging adults shared some common beliefs. Emerging adults expressed more economic concerns about weight control, whereas adolescents identified food restriction as the primary barrier to their weight management efforts. These findings highlight the nuanced differences in beliefs about the weight control practice of these two developmental groups.ConclusionMore beliefs-tailored and theoretically driven interventions are needed for these two unique age groups. Healthcare providers should address dietary and economic concerns while engaging adolescents and emerging adults in weight management. Future obesity interventions should incorporate shared and unique beliefs identified in each group to promote healthy weight management effectively.Keywords: Adolescents, Emerging adults, Beliefs, Weight control behaviors

Introduction

Weight control behaviors are commonly observed among adolescents and emerging adults [1, 2]. Adolescents and emerging adults represent the most vulnerable groups facing the health burden of obesity [3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) identifies the 10–24 age group as a distinct population with unique health needs that require specific and targeted attention [4]. However, the high prevalence of overweight and obesity in both these age groups remains an imperative health concern [5, 6]. The prevalence of obesity is 22.2% among adolescents aged 12 to 19 years and 39.8% among young adults aged 20 to 39 years in the United States [7]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the prevalence of overweight and obesity has risen dramatically from 8% in 1990 to 20% in 2022 [8]. The prevalence of obesity is higher among adolescents than among other age groups of children, and the rate of weight gain is higher among emerging adults than among other age groups [3, 6]. Although adolescents and emerging adults living with obesity may engage in more weight control behaviors, these behaviors do not necessarily translate to effective weight management [9, 10].

Weight control behaviors comprise healthy and unhealthy weight loss attempts [11, 12]. Healthy weight control strategies include eating more fruits and vegetables, consuming less high-fat or high-calorie foods, and exercising more [13]. Unhealthy weight control behaviors involved in practice may cause adverse mental and physical effects on the well-being of adolescents and emerging adults [14, 15]. Meanwhile, unhealthy weight control may refer to a more extreme level of exercise and a stricter diet—over exercise (vigorous exercise lasting more than two hours), skipping meals or intermittent fasting, consuming mono diet or limited foods less than 800 calories per day, taking pills, smoking and even purging [16, 17]. Since the difference may be subtle, adolescents and young adults may engage in a mixture of healthy and unhealthy weight control practices [18]. The discrepancy between healthy and unhealthy weight control behaviors sometimes only lies in the extent of which strategy they are utilizing—elevated intensity or frequency of regular exercise vs. extreme exercise lasting more than two hours or reduced food portions vs. little foods less than 800 calories per day. Therefore, adolescents or emerging adults may not be aware of engaging in unhealthy weight control behaviors while they are expecting to gain a healthier weight. Still, instead, they are harming their well-being [19].

While previous studies have shown that adolescents and emerging adults who frequently engage in weight control behaviors are at a higher risk of psychological distress, less attention has been given to the cognitive mechanisms that drive their intentions, particularly among those living with obesity [2, 20]. Understanding the beliefs or norms that shape these intentions can enable healthcare providers to develop targeted interventions that promote healthy weight control strategies in overweight adolescents and young adults [21, 22]. For example, a study on circumcision among adult males in Zimbabwe identified 14 essential beliefs strongly linked to the intention to undergo the procedure. The researchers recommended using these insights to design targeted interventions with persuasive messaging to increase motivation and uptake [23]. Similarly, identifying underlying beliefs about weight control can help clarify the relationship between healthy and unhealthy practices, as overweight adolescents may engage in both simultaneously [24]. A deeper understanding of these beliefs can aid in developing effective interventions for promoting healthy weight control behaviors while discouraging unhealthy weight control behaviors among this population.

Adolescence and emerging adulthood represent two consecutive and critical transitional stages in life [25, 26]. Although adolescence, typically from 12 to 18 years, is widely recognized as the period of development between childhood and adulthood, emerging adulthood has gained recognition as a distinct developmental stage in recent decades [26]. Adolescence is the challenging process of transitioning from childhood to adulthood, encompassing various physical and psychological changes [25]. By contrast, emerging adulthood is an unprecedented life stage marked by being caught in between, grappling with uncertainty and instability while embarking on a journey of self-identity and exploring new possibilities [26]. Although many interventions aim to alleviate the health burden of obesity among adolescents, more validated and tailored weight control interventions targeting these two underrepresented yet unique and distinct developmental ages are warranted [3, 27].

To develop effective interventions for these age groups, it is crucial to understand the cognitive factors influencing their health-related behaviors, including weight control behaviors. The theory of planned behavior (TPB) offers a practical framework for elucidating the cognitive mechanisms underlying the intention to engage in a specific behavior [28]. TPB theorists have proposed that three principal components (attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control) precede the formation of intention [21]. The integrated behavioral model (IBM), as an extension of TPB, incorporates constructs from the health belief model (HBM) and social cognitive theory (SCT) to illustrate better the underlying beliefs affecting intention and behavior (Fig. 1) [21, 29]. As presented in Fig. 1, the IBM model has three constructs (attitude, perceived norm, and personal agency) that precede the formation of intention before it leads to the enactment of a behavior. Attitude is composed of experiential and instrumental attitude. Experiential attitude is related to emotional reactions, whereas instrumental attitude is associated with the cognitive evaluation of a specific behavior. As originally specified in the TPB, instrumental attitude is influenced by behavioral beliefs related to attributes or outcomes associated with the behavior. Perceived norm encompasses injunctive and descriptive norms, representing the social support or pressure one perceives from influential individuals. Both injunctive and descriptive norms are shaped by normative beliefs, which refer to one’s beliefs about whether specific individuals or groups would approve or disapprove of a specific behavior and whether they would engage in that behavior. Personal agency represents one’s ability to perform the target behavior and comprises perceived control and self-efficacy. Perceived control is preceded by control beliefs, indicating one’s perceptions of the likelihood of facilitating or constraining conditions. Self-efficacy is determined by efficacy beliefs, which refer to an individual’s perception of their ability to overcome any facilitating or constraining condition [21].

Integrated behavioral model

Adolescents and Emerging adults represent the most vulnerable groups facing the health burden of obesity [3, 6]. Adolescents and emerging adults may also have different beliefs about the intention behind weight control behaviors. Moreover, various belief systems may underlie each construct of intention that drive them to engage in weight control behaviors actively [30]. As IBM theorists recommended, conducting an elicitation study is crucial to identify the beliefs underlying the intention of adolescents and emerging adults to engage in a specific behavior [21]. This method involves interviews with open-ended questions to explore behavioral, normative, and control beliefs that precede the formation of intentions related to a specific behavior in a target population [31]. Understanding these beliefs can inform interventions designed to modify the underlying factors, ultimately shaping the intention toward the desired behavior [29]. Although many TPB-based intervention studies have been conducted, an elicitation study is warranted. Based on identified pivotal beliefs underlying the intention of weight control behaviors among adolescents and emerging adults, effective interventions to promote healthy weight control practices can be developed [21]. Therefore, by conducting an elicitation study following the conceptual framework of IBM, the beliefs underlying the intention to engage in weight control behaviors among adolescents and emerging adults living with obesity were qualitatively explored. The study findings can provide insights for developing theoretically driven interventions tailored to beliefs for weight control. These insights will contribute to future research efforts to consolidate effective interventions in this domain. This study aimed to articulate the underlying beliefs concerning weight control behaviors among adolescents and emerging adults living with obesity by an elicitation qualitative study.

Methods

Design

This qualitative elicitation study was conducted following the conceptual framework of IBM, which extends the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) framework [21]. An elicitation study uses interviews with open-ended questions to explore behavioral, normative, and control beliefs that precede the formation of intentions related to a specific behavior in a target population [31]. In this case, semi-structured, individual interviews were conducted with adolescents and young adults living with obesity to elicit their beliefs underlying the intention of weight control behaviors.

Participants

Using convenience sampling, we recruited eligible participants. The inclusion criteria were being aged between 12 and 25 years, having a BMI ≥ 85th (adolescents) or ≥ 24 (emerging adults), and being able to sign a consent form. The exclusion criteria were having a history of psychiatric disorders, being unable to read or communicate in Mandarin, or failing to provide the consent form. Though the definition of the age range for emerging adults varies, we recruited participants up to 25 years old, following the WHO’s definition and supported by relevant prior research [4, 15]. Participants were recruited from a junior high school and a college from the beginning of September to the end of November 2020. The recruitment poster for participants was displayed on the school’s bulletin board and at the health center, enabling interested individuals to reach out to the principal investigator using the contact information provided on the poster. Given that the recommended number of participants for an elicitation study is 25, we recruited 27 participants in our study [32]. This recommendation has been similarly followed in other qualitative elicitation studies [33, 34].

A total of 27 participants were recruited, with 12 adolescents and 15 emerging adults. The mean age of all the participants was 18.5 ± 4.1 years. Furthermore, 11 (40.7%) were male participants, and 16 (59.3%) were female. The adolescent population comprised an equal number of male and female participants (N = 6 for each). However, in the emerging adults, the number of female participants was higher than that of male participants (N = 10 and 6 for female and male participants, respectively). All adolescents were full-time students, one preadolescent in elementary school and others attending junior or high schools. Among emerging adults, 6 (40%) were students, whereas the remaining 15 (60%) were employed full-time. The mean BMI for all participants was 29.3 ± 3.8, and the average BMI values for adolescents and emerging adults were 28.1 ± 3.3 and 30.3 ± 4.4, respectively. Three adolescents had BMI percentiles at or above the 85th percentile, categorizing them as overweight, while nine had BMI percentiles at or above the 95th percentile, classifying them as obese. These categorizations were made according to Taiwan’s Health Promotion Administration (HPA) criteria for determining overweight and obesity in adolescents [35, 36]. Overall, 74.1% of all participants were living with obesity (defined as BMI percentiles at or above the 95th percentile for adolescents and BMI ≥ 27 for emerging adults), with 75% of adolescents and 73.3% of emerging adults falling into this category. Notably, the percentage of female emerging adults living with obesity (72.7%) was higher compared to that of adolescents living with obesity (44.4%) (Table 1).

Procedure and interviews

Eligible individuals interested in participating in the study were invited to join. Since only adolescents and emerging adults with overweight or obesity were recruited, participants were made aware of their weight status before being asked for permission to participate. Due to the COVID-19 outbreak in 2020, most interviews were conducted online. Proponents of the IBM framework suggest that online interviews serve as a practical alternative for elicitation studies [28].

A series of open-ended questions, structured according to the IBM framework, were developed specifically for this study to guide the interviews, as shown in full in Table 2 [21]. Each interview, lasting 20 to 30 min, was conducted by the corresponding author (PCL), who had no prior interactions with the participants. Before addressing the elicitation questions concerning weight control behaviors, participants were first asked about their previous attempts to control or lose weight with the question: “Have you ever tried any method(s) to control or lose your weight over the past year?” (Table 2). In this study, weight control refers explicitly to strategies aimed at weight loss.

Basic demographic information, including age, occupation, height, weight, and previous weight control attempts, was then collected. The interviews continued by exploring participants’ underlying beliefs regarding their intentions to engage in weight control behaviors. Throughout the interview process, notes were taken and later cross-referenced with verbatim transcripts to ensure accuracy and validation.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using a hybrid inductive and deductive content analysis approach [37]. This method applies to both qualitative and quantitative data, accommodating an inductive approach (where patterns and themes emerge from the data) or a deductive approach (where predefined categories or theories guide the analysis) [38]. A predefined matrix based on the IBM framework was developed to guide the analysis. All participant responses were categorized alongside their corresponding elicitation questions, as shown in Table 2. Responses to the same question were coded and combined to create distinct belief items, illustrating the inductive process. These belief items were later mapped back onto the predefined IBM-based matrix to complete the deductive phase of the content analysis.

For example, when participants were asked about the advantages or disadvantages of weight control, a female participant (No. 3) stated, “I could feel my body more after losing some weight,” which was coded as “feel my own body.” Similarly, a male participant (No. 5) expressed, “I felt more confident after losing some weight,” coded as “self-confidence,” while a female participant (No. 17) mentioned, “I felt better after going outside and exercising,” coded as “feel happier.” These three subthemes were grouped to form a distinct belief item labeled “intrinsic rewards.” This belief item was then deductively categorized under behavioral beliefs and incorporated into Table 5 [38]. Two research team members were involved in the content analysis process: the corresponding author (PCL), who conducted the interviews and is a PhD and trained pediatric nurse practitioner with over 20 years of clinical experience in pediatrics, and a PhD candidate (FFC) with more than 10 years of experience in clinical teaching and adolescent health. The coding and classification generated from the content analysis were discussed iteratively until a 100% agreement was reached between the two researchers. The belief items generated were incorporated into the IBM-based matrix to organize the frequency tables from Tables 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9. Belief items in each table were ranked according to the frequency of responses provided by the participants [30].

Weight control strategies

First, we explored adolescents’ and young adults’ previous weight control attempts. As illustrated in Table 3, the most adopted weight control strategy was “adjust diet combination” among all participants. “Exercise” was the second most utilized method among the participants. Though seeking medical treatments or food replacements was ranked third among all participants, it was less frequently adopted among adolescents, except one male adolescent (No.19) mentioned that he was given some digestive enzymes to facilitate weight control by his mother. On the other hand, “diet restriction” was ranked fourth among all participants, the third among adolescents, and the fifth among young adults. However, a significant portion of participants (N = 12, 44.4%) adopted unhealthy strategies (skipping meals and vomiting) to achieve weight control. Though it was ranked fifth and fourth among adolescents and young adults, respectively, it represented that nearly 25% of adolescents and 40% of young adults in our study adopted unhealthy weight control strategies to lose weight. The remaining strategies were “special diet” and “others’ support or accompany.” The previous one was utilized only by young adults, and the latter was exclusively mentioned by adolescents in our study.

Attitude

Feelings about weight control behaviors

Within the construct of attitude, we observed two concepts: experiential and instrumental attitude (Fig. 1). Because experiential attitude is associated with one’s feeling toward weight control behaviors, we asked the following questions to our participants: “How do you feel about the idea of weight control behaviors?” and “What do you like or dislike about weight control behaviors?” (Table 2). The responses are listed in Table 4, with the top five “likes” and the three most mentioned “dislikes” identified. “Benefits from weight loss” was the most favorable item reported by our participants (N = 5, 18.5%). The second most mentioned item was “rewards from exercise” (N = 4, 14.8%), although it was reported by three emerging adults (20% among adults) and only one adolescent (8.3% among adolescents). The remaining two items (likes, unspecified, and weight loss without suffering) were mentioned by two participants (7.4%). Although they did not meet the cutoff of being mentioned by three participants, the items were retained for further comparison. The number of adolescents who mentioned “Likes, unspecified” (N = 2, 16.7%) was the same as that who mentioned “benefits from weight loss.”

The most common item described with “dislikes” was “cannot eat whatever I want;” this item was reported exclusively by emerging adults (N = 3, 18.5% overall and 33.3% of emerging adults). Therefore, it was not present in the list of “dislikes” among adolescents. The second most described item with “dislikes” was “trade-offs for weight control,” which was mentioned by five participants (18.5% overall), including three adolescents (25%) and two emerging adults (13.3%). One teenage girl mentioned the need to endure the taste of vegetables while engaging in weight control (No. 25). Another adult expressed that she enjoyed the result of weight control behaviors, but not the process (No. 7). An adult described that the process was painful because he was undergoing alternative treatment for weight loss (No. 16). The third most cited item with “dislikes” was “exercise,” which was reported by two participants from each group (N = 2, 14.8% overall). It ranked second among adolescents (16.7%) and third among emerging adults (13.3%).

Concerning their feelings toward weight control behaviors, other miscellaneous responses obtained from our participants were “lacking motivation,” “neutral,” and “desensitized.” Three participants (11.1%), comprising one adolescent and two emerging adults, expressed a lack of motivation to engage in weight control behaviors. Two adolescents had a “neutral” feeling toward weight control, with one female teenager describing that “it was okay” (No. 9) and the other male adolescent stating that “it was not that unpleasant” (No. 27). Two other emerging adults exhibited a “desensitized” attitude toward weight control behaviors. Among them, one young man mentioned that “It is not a matter of likes or dislikes. You will feel ok as long as you get used to it” (No. 6). Another young woman expressed that she felt “nothing” toward weight control behaviors, even though she used to vomit after meals if she felt she was overeating (No. 10).

Behavioral beliefs

The other concept embedded within the construct of attitude is instrumental attitude, which is influenced by behavioral beliefs. We examined behavioral beliefs by asking, “What are some advantages/benefits of weight control behaviors?” and “What are some disadvantages/negative consequences of weight control behaviors?” (Table 2). The six most frequently mentioned advantages by adolescents and emerging adults are listed in Table 5. “Physically appealing” was the most reported item by 16 participants (59.3% overall), including five adolescents (41.7%) and 11 emerging adults (73.3%). This item was also the most mentioned advantage regarding weight control behaviors in both groups. “Becoming healthier” was the second most cited advantage, which was reported by 9 participants (33.3% overall), comprising four adolescents (33.3%) and 5 emerging adults (33.3%). “Less physical burden” was the third-ranked advantage, as mentioned by six individuals (22.2% overall), including more emerging adults (N = 5, 33.3%) than adolescents (N = 1, 8.3%). Thus, it was ranked third among emerging adults but fourth among adolescents. “Feeling more energetic” was the fourth most quoted advantage by all participants (N = 5, 18.5% overall). In contrast to the third-ranked advantage of “less physical burden,” more adolescents (N = 3, 25%) than emerging adults (N = 2, 13.3%) reported “feeling more energetic” as one of the advantages of weight control behaviors. Therefore, it ranked third among adolescents, but not among emerging adults. “Saving money” was the fifth-ranked advantage, as mentioned by four participants (14.8% overall). Although more emerging adults than adolescents reported it as an advantage, its ranking was relatively low among these two age groups. “Intrinsic rewards” was the least quoted advantage, and it was mentioned by three participants (11.1% overall). This item was exclusively reported by emerging adults (20%). For instance, a male participant shared, “I felt more confident after losing some weight” (No. 5), while a young female participant (No. 3) stated, “I could feel my body more after losing some weight.”

We also explored the disadvantages of weight control behaviors, and six frequently mentioned items emerged (Table 5). “Can’t eat whatever I want” was the most frequently mentioned disadvantage, as reported by eight participants (29.6% overall). It was the top-ranked disadvantage among emerging adults (N = 5, 33.3%) and the second among adolescents (N = 3, 25.0%). This disadvantage was also reported as the most common item with “dislike” among emerging adults (Table 4). “No disadvantage” was the second-ranked item, as mentioned by six participants (22.2% overall); a higher proportion of adolescents (N = 4, 33.3%) and a lower proportion of Emerging adults (N = 2, 13.3%) reported this disadvantage. This item had a different ranking: it was the first among adolescents and the fifth among emerging adults. “Exercise” was also reported by six participants (22.2% overall), with more emerging adults (N = 4, 26.7%) than adolescents (N = 2, 16.7%) considering it as a disadvantage. It was even ranked second among emerging adults. Similar to the top disadvantage, “can’t eat whatever I want,” “exercise” was also included in the “dislikes” list regarding participants’ feelings about weight control behaviors (Table 4). “Economic concern” was the next-ranked disadvantage, which was reported exclusively by emerging adults (N = 4, 14.8% overall). Therefore, it was among the top three disadvantages among emerging adults (26.7%). “Trade-offs for weight control behaviors” was reported as a disadvantage by four participants (14.8% overall), with two from each age group (16.7% of adolescents and 13.3% of emerging adults). Similar to the two aforementioned disadvantages of “can’t eat whatever I want” and “exercise,” “trade-offs for weight control behaviors” was also among the “dislikes” items listed by our participants (Table 5). “Emotional strain” was listed as a disadvantage by two participants (7.4% overall) and was exclusively mentioned by emerging adults (13.3%). One young woman reported that controlling weight was stressful for her, and she felt guilty after consuming high-calorie foods (No. 22). Another young man admitted that he felt distressed when following food restrictions while attempting to achieve ideal weight control (No. 16).

Perceived norm

Normative beliefs: others’ expectation

Within the construct of perceived norm, two concepts are embedded: injunctive norm and descriptive norm (Fig. 1). Although both injunctive and descriptive norms are a function of normative beliefs, they are hypothesized to capture different aspects of normative influence. Injunctive norm is associated with normative beliefs concerning others’ expectations, whereas descriptive norm is related to normative beliefs about others’ behaviors (Montano & Kasprzyk, 2015). In this study, we explored normative beliefs regarding others’ expectations toward weight control behaviors by asking, “Who would support you to engage in weight control behaviors?” and “Who would not support you to engage in weight control behaviors?” (Table 2).

“Parents” were the most frequently mentioned supporters of weight control behaviors by all participants (N = 14, 51.9%; Table 6). This ranking was consistent among adolescents (N = 7, 58.3%), and this factor ranked second among emerging adults (N = 7, 46.7%). In addition to parents, “family” was the second most mentioned among all participants (N = 13, 48.1%). Although it ranked second among adolescents (N = 5, 41.7%), it had the highest ranking among emerging adults (N = 8, 53.3%). “Friends” ranked third among all participants (N = 9, 33.3%). More emerging adults (N = 6, 40%) than adolescents (N = 3, 25%) mentioned that “friends” would support their weight control behaviors. “Classmates” was ranked fourth among all participants (N = 7, 25.9%). More adolescents (N = 4, 33.3%) than emerging adults (N = 3, 20%) mentioned that “classmates” would encourage them to engage in weight control behaviors. Four participants indicated that their “siblings” would support their weight control behaviors (14.8% for all), including two adolescents (16.7%) and two emerging adults (13.3%).

When participants were asked who would not support their engagement in weight control behaviors, “can’t think of anyone” was the most commonly cited response among all participants (N = 17, 63%). It also ranked the top response among both adolescents and emerging adults (N = 8, 66.7% of adolescents; N = 9, 60% of adults). Although only three participants (one adolescent and two Emerging adults) mentioned that “parents” would not support their weight control efforts, “parents” were also the most cited figures who would support their engagement in weight control behaviors. “Family” was the third most cited figure that would not support participants’ weight control behaviors (N = 2, 7.4%). However, only emerging adults expressed that “family” would discourage them from engaging in weight control behaviors (N = 2, 13.3%). Two adolescents responded “no one knows” when asked about who would not support their weight control efforts, and this response was provided by 16.7% of adolescents and ranked second.

Normative beliefs: others’ behaviors

Within the construct of perceived norm, the descriptive norm is another embedded concept that is a function of normative beliefs. However, unlike injunctive norm preceded by normative beliefs concerning others’ expectations, the descriptive norm is a function of normative beliefs regarding others’ behaviors. We asked our participants the following questions: “Who can you think of that would engage in weight control behaviors?” and “Who can you think of that would not engage in weight control behaviors?” (Table 2).

As listed in Table 7, most participants reported that “classmates” were likely to engage in weight control behaviors (N = 8, 29.6%). Although it had the top ranking among emerging adults (N = 5, 33.3%), it was ranked second among adolescents (N = 3, 25%). The second most mentioned item was “can’t think of anyone” (N = 6, 22.2%). More adolescents (N = 5, 41.7%) than emerging adults (N = 1, 6.7%) stated that they “can’t think of anyone.” Therefore, its ranking was the highest among adolescents and the lowest among emerging adults. “Parents” was reported as being the third most quoted figure to likely engage in weight control behaviors (N = 5, 18.5%). Its ranking (third) was the same among adolescents and emerging adults. Both “siblings” and “friends” were mentioned by three participants (N = 3, 11.1%) as being likely to engage in weight control behaviors; however, only emerging adults (N = 3, 20%) indicated that their “friends” would also engage in weight control behaviors.

When participants were asked whom they believed would not engage in weight control behaviors, “can’t think of anyone” was the most reported response (N = 12, 44.4%). It also ranked the top among both adolescents (N = 4, 33.3%) and emerging adults (N = 8, 53.3%). “Classmates,” which was the most mentioned figure as likely to engage in weight control behaviors, also appeared in the list of those who would not engage in weight control behaviors (Table 7). It ranked second among both the overall study population (N = 6, 22.2%) and adolescents (N = 4, 33.3%) but third among emerging adults (N = 2, 13.3% emerging adults). Both “siblings” and “friends” were mentioned by three participants (11.1%) as being unlikely to engage in weight control behaviors. “Friends” was mentioned only by emerging adults (N = 3, 20%) and ranked second among them. This aligns with the finding that “friends” were exclusively mentioned by emerging adults when considering whom they believed would not engage in weight control behaviors.

Personal agency

Control beliefs

The construct of personal agency includes two concepts: perceived control and self-efficacy (Fig. 1). The underlying beliefs precedent to perceived control are control beliefs. Our participants’ control beliefs regarding weight control behaviors were assessed by asking the questions: “What things make it easy for you to conduct weight control behaviors?” and “What things make it hard for you to conduct weight control behaviors?” (Table 2). Regarding factors that encourage our participants to engage in weight control behaviors, several “facilitators” were identified, as listed in Table 8. “Social support” was the most frequently reported by our participants (N = 15, 55.6%). It also ranked first among adolescents (N = 7, 58.3%) and emerging adults (N = 8, 53.3%), with more than half of adolescents and emerging adults reporting it. “Physically appealing” was the second most mentioned facilitator for weight control behaviors, as reported by 25.9% of all participants. Significantly more emerging adults (N = 6, 40%) than adolescents (N = 1, 8.3% of adolescents) reported it. This discrepancy was also reflected in their rankings, with “physically appealing” ranking second among Emerging adults and the last among adolescents. “Rewards” was the third most reported facilitator that made weight control behaviors easier among all participants (N = 4, 14.8%). However, only one adolescent mentioned it (8.3%), with the lowest ranking among adolescents, whereas it ranked third among emerging adults (N = 3, 20%). The remaining three factors facilitating weight control behaviors were all quoted by three participants (11.1%). Both “becoming healthier” and “adopting a healthy lifestyle” were reported by two adolescents (16.7%) and one adult (6.7%). Three exclusive adolescents (25%) stated they “can’t think of any” factors that would make their weight control behaviors easier. Therefore, it still ranked second among adolescents.

Regarding barriers discouraging adolescents and emerging adults from engaging in weight control behaviors, several “obstacles” were identified. “Social eating” was the most cited obstacle preventing them from engaging in weight control behaviors (N = 14, 51.9%). The number of emerging adults who reported it outnumbered that of adolescents by 2.5 times. Therefore, “social eating” was the most cited obstacle among emerging adults, and it ranked second among adolescents. The second most mentioned was “lack of time” (N = 12, 44.4%). However, the number of emerging adults outnumbered that of adolescents by three times (emerging adults vs. adolescents: N = 9 vs. N = 3; 60% of emerging adults vs. 25% of adolescents). Its ranking was second among emerging adults and third among adolescents. Ten participants (37%) expressed that “weight control is burdensome,” comprising eight emerging adults (53.3%) and only two adolescents (16.7%). The number of emerging adults was four times of that of adolescents. Thus, it ranked third among all participants and emerging adults but fourth among adolescents. The least reported obstacle was “food as a source of pleasure,” which was mentioned by nine participants (33.3%). However, it ranked first among adolescents because it was the most mentioned obstacle by adolescents (N = 5, 41.7%).

Efficacy beliefs

Within the construct of personal agency, self-efficacy is a key concept preceded by efficacy beliefs (Fig. 1). We inquired about adolescents’ and emerging adults’ efficacy beliefs by using two questions: “If you want to control your weight, how confident are you that you will succeed?” and “What other factors may affect your ability to execute weight control behaviors?” (Table 2). Not many of our participants had full confidence regarding achieving successful weight loss. As presented in Table 9, almost one-third of our participants expressed that they only possessed half of the confidence for achieving successful weight management (N = 8, 29.6%). Moreover, 14.8% of all participants (N = 4), with an equal number of adolescents (N = 2, 16.7%) and emerging adults (N = 2, 13.3%), reported that they had 70% confidence in achieving successful weight loss. Other responses included “being positive,” “80% confidence level,” and “not sure,” each reported by three participants (11.1%). “Being positive” was exclusively mentioned by emerging adults (20%), and it ranked second among them. The other two responses, “80% confidence level” and “not sure,” were quoted by two adolescents (16.7%) and one adult (6.7%).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic that explores the beliefs underlying weight control behaviors among overweight adolescents and emerging adults, using IBM as a guiding framework. Previous research has shown that adolescents and emerging adults with obesity are more likely to engage in weight control behaviors compared to their non-obese peers [39]. Therefore, this study specifically recruited participants with higher-than-average BMI values to focus on this population.

The findings revealed shared and distinct strategies and beliefs related to weight control behaviors between adolescents and emerging adults. Both groups predominantly reported “adjusting diet combination” and “exercise” as their primary strategy. However, emerging adults were more likely to use medical treatments and food replacements, while adolescents relied more on other’s support. A concerning portion of both groups engaged in unhealthy strategies like skipping meals, but emerging adults were more prone to adopting extreme methods, including vomiting.

Three overarching themes concerning underlying beliefs were eating-centered concerns, physical appearance, and social interference. Despite shared themes, emerging adults emphasized economic concerns related to weight control, whereas adolescents identified food restriction as the main barrier to weight management. These distinctions highlight the need for developing tailored, age-appropriate interventions targeting the unique concerns of participants from these two developmental stages.

Weight control behaviors among adolescents and emerging adults

Our participants, including adolescents and emerging adults, could recall some weight control strategies they had adopted during the past year, as previous findings suggest that weight loss attempts are commonly seen among overweight and obese young populations [40, 41]. Both adolescents and emerging adults in this study agreed that adjusting diet combination and exercise were the two most utilized strategies for weight management. Individuals participating in our research addressed that “eating more vegetables,” “consuming less meat,” or “eating more balanced foods” were applicable strategies to control body weight. Though there was a common regarding the two most mentioned weight control strategies adopted by overweight adolescents and emerging adults in this survey, significantly more emerging adults than adolescents reported that seeking medical consultations or embracing food replacements were helping their weight control. It is reasonable to speculate that emerging adults with increased economic independence and autonomy would be more likely than adolescents to gather medical advice or resources for controlling their weight [26, 42]. Instead, adolescents may have limited chances to access professional guidance if their parents do not give them support. Our study further supports this in that only one adolescent (No.19) indicated that his parent gave him digestive enzymes to attempt weight loss. To be noticed that though “unhealthy strategies” were only ranked fourth among all weight control methods, nearly half of our participants (44.4%) had involved in unhealthy weight control strategies, with 25% of adolescents and 60% of emerging adults proclaiming that they ever adopted unhealthy strategies for weight managing. Notably, all overweight adolescents in this study who engaged in unhealthy weight control predominantly used meal skipping as their primary strategy. It is of concern that it is not only a precursor for disorder eating, but it may compromise the adolescent’s developmental health since they are still growing [43]. Previous findings suggested a close relationship between skipping meals and psychological disturbances [44]. Either adolescents or emerging adults engaging in meal skipping are more prone to develop psychological problems and predict further weight gain [17]. Though there was only one female adult utilizing vomiting as a weight control strategy, it is still an unneglectable warning since it may reflect underlying mental-health issues among emerging adults living with obesity [45].

Feelings and behavioral beliefs underlying the attitude toward weight control behaviors

The responses provided by our participants regarding their feelings toward weight control behaviors revealed that they exhibited an ambivalent feeling toward weight control practice (Table 4). They expressed enjoyment of the benefits and rewards resulting from the adoption of weight control behaviors. However, they experienced distress from having to refrain from eating whatever they wanted or from the trade-offs associated with weight control. The mixed feelings toward weight control behaviors may be supported by the equivalent number of items assigned as advantages and disadvantages by our participants (Table 5) when considering the pros and cons of weight control behaviors.

Under the same attitude construct, it is unsurprising that the underlying beliefs listed in Tables 3 and 4 are intercorrelated and overlapping. By linking the item with the most “likes” with the most quoted advantage of “physically appealing,” we can infer that the most favorable trait associated with weight control behaviors was the desire to become physically attractive. Physical attractiveness is a primary focus among adolescents and Emerging adults [5, 46]. After puberty, adolescents consider others’ opinions toward their physical appearance, especially those of peers [46]. Moreover, for emerging adults, seeking a romantic relationship with a stable partner often requires considerable effort to maintain a glamorous appearance [26]. In a systematic review of motivations for weight loss among adolescents, Silva et al. (2018) revealed that physical appearance was the primary motivation for adolescents to engage in weight loss attempts in half of the reviewed articles. LaRose et al. (2013) also reported that young adults expressed more concerns regarding physical appeal than older adults when asked about their motivation to control weight.

In the present study, three identical items listed in Tables 4 and 5 as items with “dislikes” and that were “disadvantages” were concurrently associated with weight control behaviors (“cannot eat whatever I want,” “trade-offs for weight control,” and “exercise”). Among these, “cannot eat whatever I want” ranked first in Tables 4 and 5 as the most disliked item and the highest-ranked disadvantage resulting from weight control behaviors. Although only emerging adults indicated “cannot eat whatever I want” as a “dislikes” item (Table 4), three adolescents reported it as a disadvantage related to weight control behaviors (Table 5). Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to energy-dense and palatable foods [47]. Their immature top-down regulatory brain system and amplified sensitivity to rewarding stimuli associated with limbic systems may drive adolescents to pursue more appetitive and palatable foods to satisfy immediate cravings [47]. In addition, we observed that “food as a source of pleasure” was listed as the top obstacle when adolescents were inquired about their control beliefs related to weight control behaviors (Table 8). This finding suggests that gaining an immediate reward or satisfaction from consuming desirable foods is crucial when adolescents contemplate their underlying beliefs toward weight control behaviors.

Different considerations may apply to emerging adults because they are in a developmental stage distinct from adolescence. Similar to our findings for adolescents, the highest-ranked obstacle perceived by young adults was “social eating” related to weight control behaviors (Table 8). Because “cannot eat whatever I want” was ranked the first among emerging adults (Tables 4 and 5) within the construct of attitude, we postulate that the distress or inconvenience perceived as “dislikes” or “disadvantages” among emerging adults was correlated with the peer pressure they encountered in social occasions involving obligatory social eating. Scientists have indicated that young adults expressed that although they were occasionally reluctant to eat more or consume unhealthy foods, they inevitably overeat and consume extra calories in certain social situations and under peer pressure [48, 49].

Two opposite behavioral beliefs related to economic concerns were identified (Table 5). However, three emerging adults and one adolescent agreed that “saving money” was an advantage of weight control behaviors, and four other emerging adults considered “economic concerns” as a disadvantage associated with weight control behaviors. With emerging adults having greater independence than adolescents, they may be more focused on earning a living independently [26]. Although they may have different opinions about the costs of weight control behaviors, the economic burden of weight control behaviors is a substantial concern [50]. A study examining the correlates of weight change among more than 600 young women reported that cost was one of the major obstacles to weight management. They further mentioned that the lack of affordable exercise facilities and inexpensive, healthy foods hindered their engagement in weight control behaviors [51]. Sand et al. (2017) explored the motivations and barriers to weight reduction among young women. They reported that the monetary burden associated with accessing sports activities and buying healthy foods was a significant barrier for these women as they attempted to manage their body weight. In addition, Larson et al. (2018) identified a gender difference; young men under financial strain were more likely to maintain a healthy weight, whereas young women were more likely to gain excessive weight. However, three emerging adults in our study reported “saving money” as an advantage associated with weight control behaviors. The disparity between our findings and previous studies necessitates investigations into young adults’ behavioral beliefs related to weight control endeavors. In our research, excessive concerns related to the economic burden associated with weight control behaviors were only observed among emerging adults but not among adolescents (Table 5). Therefore, economic concerns related to weight control behaviors should be considered when developing weight control interventions targeting this population, especially emerging adults.

Normative beliefs under perceived norm toward weight control behaviors

Parents were the most mentioned referents supporting our adolescents and emerging adults in weight control behaviors. Although peer influence was pivotal, parental support played a more critical role in encouraging adolescents and young adults to engage in weight control behaviors. This finding aligns with the latest guidelines from the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) in 2023, emphasizing the vital role of families in a child’s weight status, primarily through parental feeding practices [52]. Consistent with our findings, a systematic review concluded that parental support was the primary reason adolescents engaged in more frequent physical activities [53]. A longitudinal study also indicated that parental support significantly influenced physical activity engagement in a group of emerging adults as they transitioned from adolescence to emerging adulthood [54]. However, Mendonça et al. (2014) suggested that family and friends played vital roles in determining adolescents’ physical activity levels in addition to parental influence. This finding aligns with our finding that “parents,” “family,” and “friends” were the top three significant sources of support for our participants for engaging in weight control behaviors.

As depicted in Table 6, we observed more commonalities than differences between the lists generated separately by adolescents and emerging adults regarding the individuals that support their weight control behaviors. Although a slight difference was observed, in that “family” instead of “parents” ranked at the top among emerging adults and “classmates” instead of “friends” ranked third among adolescents, the importance of parents and family members perceived by participants was superior to that of peers (friends and classmates) when considering their impact on our participants’ engagement in weight control behaviors. The elevated ranking of “classmates” compared with “friends” regarding their influence on adolescents’ weight control behaviors is because all adolescents in our study were students. However, only emerging adults mentioned that “family” would discourage them from engaging in weight control behaviors. This could be associated with the higher proportion of emerging adults in our study who reported engaging in unhealthy weight control practices.

In addition to the results in Tables 6 and 7 reveals the ranking of individuals designated by our participants as those who would also engage in weight control behaviors. Although the lists in both Tables 6 and 7 were associated with normative beliefs underlying the construct of “perceived norm,” different aspects of normative beliefs (others’ expectations vs. others’ behaviors) were scrutinized and captured [55]. Compared with the list in Table 6, the ranking of “classmates” was elevated to the top, whereas “parents” was ranked third because our participants nominated referents who would also adopt weight control behaviors. However, “cannot think of anyone” was the most quoted by adolescents, with more than 40% indicating that they could not think of anyone who would engage in weight control behaviors. Despite parents and family ranking at the top as the most nominated referents supporting our participants in their engagement in weight control behaviors, they were not the most quoted as individuals who would also engage in weight control behaviors. This raises concerns that a lack of significant others around adolescents who would also engage in weight control behaviors may hinder them from engaging in such behaviors through modeling and vicarious learning, as proposed by social cognitive theory [56]. In addition, although “classmates” ranked first in Table 7, they only received eight nominations, which was less than that received by the top-ranking figure (“parents” with 14 nominations) in Table 6. The overall number of nominations in Table 7 was lower than that of nominations in Table 6. This observation supports a paucity of recognizable referents surrounding our participants who serve as models for them to emulate regarding weight control behaviors, as proposed by social cognitive theory [56]. This observation aligns with the assertion made in a previous study that an increasing number of social contacts who are also trying to lose weight positively contributes to the frequency of weight control attempts among overweight emerging adults [57].

Control beliefs and efficacy beliefs under personal agency toward weight control behaviors

“Social support” emerged as the top facilitator for weight control behaviors among all participants, including adolescents and emerging adults (Table 8). This finding aligns with that of Mendonça et al. (2014), who indicated that social support was consistently and positively associated with the physical activity level among adolescents in both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies. Young people are more likely to adopt healthy eating habits, engage in physical activities, and avoid unhealthy weight control practices when they receive support from significant others or peers who serve as positive role models [1]. Although “social support” was the most significant facilitator for encouraging emerging adults to engage in weight control behaviors, “social eating” was considered the most prominent obstacle preventing emerging adults from engaging in weight control behaviors in this study (Table 8). This suggests that the influence or pressure exerted by the social network surrounding young people may either encourage or discourage their successful weight control efforts [48].

In contrast to the beliefs within other constructs, more disparities than commonalities were observed in the ranking of the control beliefs of adolescents and emerging adults (Table 8). Although “social support” ranked at the top for both adolescents and emerging adults, the ranking of other facilitators differed between these groups. For instance, “physically appealing” ranked second among emerging adults but was at the bottom for adolescents, and only one adolescent mentioned it. In the list of behavioral beliefs (Table 5), “physically appealing” was the most commonly cited advantage associated with weight control behaviors across all participants and all age groups. This suggests that although overweight adolescents considered “physically appealing” to be a significant advantage related to weight control behaviors following emerging adults, they did not necessarily view it as a significant facilitator causing them to engage in weight control behaviors.

Although previous studies have indicated that self-efficacy is essential for successful weight control, our participants did not possess sufficient self-efficacy toward engaging in weight control behaviors [58, 59]. Instead, most reported that they were only half confident of achieving successful weight control. Self-efficacy refers to one’s capacity or capability to perform certain behaviors under restrictive or adverse situations [60]. Young adults with a higher level of self-efficacy are more likely to adopt healthy eating habits, consume less sugary beverages, and engage in more physical activity [58, 60]. Therefore, a self-efficacy-tailored strategy is considered one of the most effective methods for promoting health behavior changes among children and adolescents, mainly targeting their dietary and physical activity habits [61]. Moreover, when applying a multicomponent intervention to improve adolescent health behaviors, including diet and physical activity, enhancing self-efficacy was associated with improved healthy behaviors, especially among obese adolescent girls [62].

Regarding other influential factors affecting weight control behaviors, our participants mentioned the prevailing idea related to eating. “Eating for relief or pleasure” and “food environment” were the top two influential factors regarding weight control behaviors. Our participants’ consistency in expressing eating-related beliefs across different constructs underscores their primary concern regarding weight control behaviors centered on “eating.” Therefore, future measures or interventions targeting weight control behaviors among this population should focus on addressing dietary modification or guidance to alleviate their concerns and encourage them to take steps toward healthy weight management.

Overarching themes across different beliefs

Three major overarching themes across different constructs were identified following the beliefs-related items aggregated and described above. The first theme was “eating-centered concern.” As illustrated in Fig. 2, this theme collected ideas from three different beliefs (behavioral, control, and efficacy beliefs). The consistency of eating-related items found across three different beliefs stressed its significance when addressing the weight control issue among this population. Future measures or interventions targeting weight control behaviors among the young population should address dietary modification or guidance to encourage them to manage their weight healthily.

Common themes generated from beliefs for weight control behaviors

The second common theme was “physical appearance” (Fig. 2). “Physically appealing” was the most quoted advantage under behavioral beliefs and the second most nominated facilitator under control beliefs. Though only one adolescent mentioned it as the facilitator concerning weight control behaviors, the overall predominated idea of connecting weight control behaviors with “physically appealing” among adolescents and emerging adults informs healthcare providers to incorporate this aspect when engaging them in effective weight management.

The third common theme was “social interference” (Fig. 2). Although both “social support” and “social eating” were under the group of control beliefs, they were in the opposite site referring to a facilitator (social support) or an obstacle (social eating) owing to weight control behaviors. Another relevant item titled “social pressure” was under efficacy beliefs. In addition, the three most important sources of social interference, including parents, family, and peers, were under normative beliefs.

Organizing the study’s findings through distinct constructs and beliefs within the IBM framework (Fig. 1) provides a clearer understanding of the underlying beliefs shaping weight control behaviors in adolescents and emerging adults. Healthcare providers should address shared concerns regarding weight control behaviors while recognizing the differences in beliefs between these two age groups. For instance, emerging adults highlighted economic concerns as a critical challenge in weight control, whereas adolescents identified food restriction as the principal barrier to weight management. Although both groups expressed concerns about eating, emerging adults emphasized the social dimensions of dietary practices, identifying “social eating” as a significant obstacle, unlike adolescents, who viewed food restriction as a deprivation of pleasure. These findings underscore the necessity for targeted, age-appropriate interventions that account for each developmental group’s unique social and psychological challenges.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study is the recruitment of participants living with obesity, which enables a more focused exploration of the beliefs underlying weight control behaviors. Because individuals living with obesity are more likely to engage in weight control behaviors, this targeted approach can provide valuable insights into their specific beliefs and attitudes. In addition, including participants from adolescents to emerging adults can offer a comprehensive understanding of the commonalities and differences in beliefs between these two developmental stages, providing insights into the trajectories that drive them to engage in weight control behaviors. However, this study has some limitations that should be addressed. First, our study had more female than male participants, which is a common limitation in obesity-focused research that female participants often outnumber males [3]. Second, convenience sampling may have undermined the representativeness and generalization of the findings. Relatedly, limiting the study to participants who are overweight or obese may restrict the generalizability of the findings across the broader weight spectrum, particularly for underweight individuals, who may also represent a high-risk group engaging in unhealthy weight control behaviors. Third, although BMI is a widely used metric in research on weight control practices among adolescents and young adults [17, 63], its application may introduce bias in defining weight status. Specifically, BMI may misclassify individuals as overweight or obese when elevated BMI values are attributable to higher muscle mass rather than excess body fat [64]. Despite these limitations, the merit of this elicitation study lies in its potential to inform and guide forthcoming large-scale quantitative and intervention studies. The beliefs identified in this study can serve as a foundation for further research, and their impact on modifying and predicting the intention and enactment of weight control behaviors among overweight young people will be further consolidated in prospective large-scale studies [21, 29]. Building on the qualitative findings, the research team has conducted a cross-sectional study that evaluates the strength of top-ranked beliefs about the intention to engage in weight control behaviors among a group of adolescent participants (currently under review).

Conclusion

Adolescence and emerging adulthood are separate but interconnected developmental stages. Both adolescents and emerging adults are highly vulnerable to obesity. Identifying the beliefs underlying weight control behaviors in this study is a valuable foundation for future research. These findings can inform the development of measures and interventions to promote healthier weight control strategies among adolescents and emerging adults living with obesity.

Implications for future studies

This study provides valuable insights into the distinct and shared beliefs underlying weight control behaviors among adolescents and emerging adults living with obesity. Tailoring interventions to address these groups’ developmental and cognitive differences is essential. Adolescents often see food restriction as a barrier due to their association of food with pleasure, while emerging adults emphasize social eating and economic concerns more. These distinctions underscore the necessity for age-specific strategies in promoting effective weight management.

Given that young people may unknowingly engage in healthy and unhealthy behaviors, future research should focus on identifying the specific beliefs that lead to either outcome. Distinguishing between the beliefs that drive healthy versus unhealthy practices will enable the development of interventions to promote safer and more sustainable weight control strategies. This understanding is critical for clinicians and healthcare providers in guiding young people toward healthier behaviors and mitigating the risks of harmful weight control practices.

It is also recommended that future studies include participants with psychiatric histories, as they are at higher risk for unhealthy weight control behaviors [20]. Investigating how distinct belief systems shape their approach to weight control behaviors could provide a foundation for interventions designed to prevent harmful behaviors while promoting healthier weight management practices.

This study calls for beliefs-tailored, theoretically driven interventions addressing specific social, psychological, and economic concerns for adolescents and emerging adults. Such interventions will help clinicians and healthcare providers engage adolescents and EAs more effectively in weight control behaviors, fostering healthier outcomes and reducing the risk of engaging in harmful practices.
