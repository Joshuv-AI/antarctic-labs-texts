Isatin derivatives as potent cholinesterase inhibitors: recent developments and future challenges
Authors: Muhammad Shahzad, Alia Mushtaq, Christophe Rochais, Muhammad Moazzam Naseer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cognitive decline, memory impairment, and neuronal loss, primarily associated with β-amyloid plaque deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, and cholinergic dysfunction. Since the disruption of cholinergic neurotransmission is a key pathological feature of AD, the cholinesterase (ChE) inhibitors targeting acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) remain the mainstay of symptomatic treatment by enhancing synaptic acetylcholine levels. However, the currently approved small molecules, including tacrine, donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine, are limited by their modest efficacy, poor selectivity, and adverse effects, necessitating the development of improved therapeutic agents. Among emerging scaffolds, isatin (1H-indole-2,3-dione) has attracted considerable research attention owing to its structural versatility, synthetic accessibility, and favorable pharmacological properties. Recent studies have reported diverse isatin-based derivatives, including hydrazones, Schiff bases, thiosemicarbazones, spirooxindoles, and molecular hybrids, exhibiting potent AChE and BChE inhibitory activities, often in the submicromolar or nanomolar ranges. Structure–activity relationship studies have revealed the critical influence of electronic effects, linker optimization, N-substitution, and molecular hybridization on inhibitory potency and selectivity. This review summarizes the most promising isatin-based cholinesterase inhibitors reported from 2020 to the present, highlighting their biological activities, structure–activity relationships, and potential for the development of next-generation anti-Alzheimer therapeutics.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a chronic and irreversible neurodegenerative disease1 characterized clinically by the progressive deterioration of cognitive function, intellectual decline, memory loss, and behavioral changes.2 A central pathological feature of AD is the deterioration of cholinergic neurons within the basal forebrain, specifically those neurons projecting from the nucleus basalis of Meynert, and this leads to diffuse cortical acetylcholine (ACh) deficiency.3,4 This reduction is accompanied by decreased choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) activity and disrupted ACh synthesis and release, and these changes are parallel to the development of cognitive impairment.5 The cholinergic hypothesis suggests that the symptoms of AD, such as memory impairment and executive dysfunction, are characterized by the loss of neurotransmitters.6 Within the synaptic cleft, acetylcholine (ACh) undergoes rapid hydrolysis catalyzed by cholinesterases (ChEs), a family of enzymes that regulate cholinergic signaling. The two principal ChEs are AChE and BChE.7 AChE is predominantly found in cholinergic and adrenergic nerve endings, muscle fibers, erythrocytes, and placental tissue, where it plays a central role in terminating synaptic transmission. In contrast, BChE (EC 3.1.1.8) is mainly produced in the liver and secreted into the circulatory system, serving a compensatory function8 as AChE activity diminishes in the course of the disease. A characteristic peculiarity of AChE is its substrate inhibition at high concentrations of the substrate due to its special catalytic process and substrate binding kinetics, a feature that is not commonly found with BChE.9 One of the major strategies to control AD is the suppression of the hydrolysis of ACh in order to increase its concentration in the synaptic cleft, which can be achieved by the inhibition of AChE and BChE.10–12

Three ChE inhibitors—donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine—are clinically approved for the symptomatic management of AD.13 These agents exert their therapeutic effects by enhancing cholinergic neurotransmission; however, their efficacy is largely limited to symptomatic relief. In addition, their clinical use is frequently associated with adverse effects, including gastrointestinal disturbances, anorexia, vomiting, and bronchoconstriction.14 Given the established significance of cholinesterase inhibition in AD therapy, substantial research efforts are currently directed toward the discovery and development of novel inhibitors with improved therapeutic efficacy and reduced side-effect profiles.

Isatin (1H-indole-2,3-dione) is a natural bioactive heterocyclic framework consisting of a five-membered non-aromatic ring fused with a six-membered aromatic ring, and it possesses two carbonyl groups at positions 2 and 3 and a secondary nitrogen at position 1.15–19 It is a highly versatile pharmacophore for the development of a wide array of biologically active compounds with anticonvulsant,20 anticancer,21–23 antidepressant,24 antidiabetic,25,26 antiulcer,27 anti-HIV,28 antimalarial,29 antibacterial,30 and antioxidant31 activities. The C-3 keto functionality is reactive towards nucleophilic addition, which facilitates the preparation of pharmacologically interesting derivatives, such as hydrazones, imines, oxindoles, and thiosemicarbazones.32–34 For the treatment of AD, isatin-derived AChE inhibitors have the ability to interact with the aromatic residues of the peripheral anionic site (PAS) of AChE though π–π interactions, thereby improving their therapeutic value.35 Therefore, it would be worthwhile to highlight the recently reported promising ChE inhibitors based on the isatin moiety. This may facilitate the search for novel drug candidates having improved safety and pharmacological profiles by catching the attention of the drug development community.

Cholinesterase inhibition mechanism

Cognitive deterioration in AD is attributed to β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition, dysfunctional cholinergic neurotransmission, and oxidative injury, which all result in neuronal dysfunction and structural impairment.36 Reactive oxygen species (ROS) contribute significantly by mediating oxidative damage to neuronal lipids and proteins, which compromise cellular integrity. Of these pathological factors, cholinergic deficiency is the most enduring biochemical signature, evidenced by the diminished levels of ACh and AChE in brain tissues obtained post-mortem. AChE, a major serine hydrolase, accelerates the hydrolysis of ACh at an extremely high turnover rate of approximately 25 000 molecules per second, resulting in the swift synaptic loss of ACh, which is engaged in learning and memory.37–39 The active site of the enzyme, consisting of anionic and esteratic subsites, has been structurally elucidated, providing insights into its catalytic activity. Based on the cholinergic hypothesis, cholinergic neuron degeneration plays a key role in age-related cognitive impairment. Therefore, AChE inhibitors have been designed to enhance cholinergic transmission by inhibiting ACh breakdown (Fig. 1). These compounds prolong the duration of ACh availability in synapses, thereby yielding relief from cognitive symptoms. Various pharmacophoric models have been utilized to design effective AChE inhibitors, and as a result, the FDA has approved drugs, such as physostigmine, rivastigmine, tacrine, galantamine and donepezil, for the symptomatic treatment of AD and other neurodegenerative disorders.40

Isatin derivatives as cholinesterase inhibitors

N-substituted isatin-based derivatives

Triazole, a nitrogenous heterocyclic moiety, possesses a unique electronic structure and a rigid framework that make it a privileged scaffold in medicinal chemistry, exhibiting an impressive spectrum of pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antiproliferative, anticancer, and antimicrobial activities.41 Coumarin derivatives are also known to inhibit ChE enzymes predominantly by binding to the peripheral anionic site (PAS).42 In this regard, a series of coumarin-triazole isatin hybrids (8) was synthesized (Scheme 1) and evaluated for their ChE inhibitory activity.43 The target hybrids (8) were synthesized through a multistep synthetic approach. Initially, isatin (1) was treated with propargyl bromide (2) to afford the corresponding N-propargyl isatin derivatives (3). In the parallel pathway, 4-hydroxycoumarin (4) reacted with dibromoalkane (5) under basic conditions to yield a bromoalkyl coumarin intermediate (6), which was subsequently converted into an azide derivative (7) using NaN3 in DMF. Finally, the key intermediates (7 and 3) were coupled via a copper(i)-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) using CuSO4/sodium ascorbate under mild conditions, affording the desired coumarin-triazole-isatin hybrid (8) (yield: 93%).

Synthesis of the coumarin-triazole isatin hybrid (8). Reagents and conditions: (i) K2CO3, DMF, 4 h, and rt, (ii) K2CO3, DMF, 6 h, and rt, (iii) DMF, 4 h, and rt, and (iv) CuSO4, sodium ascorbate, DMF, distilled water, 0.5–2 h, and rt.

In vitro evaluation demonstrated that most of the derivatives (8) exhibited good to excellent inhibition for BChE, with IC50 values ranging from 1.74 ± 0.29 µM to 14.84 ± 1.44 µM, while showing comparatively weak activity for AChE. Structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies revealed that minimal or no substitution at the C-5 position of isatin improved enzyme binding by reducing steric hindrance. Substitution with fluorine yielded moderate activity, whereas relatively bulky halogens, nitro, and methyl groups lowered potency. Short linkers restricted flexibility, hindering AChE interactions. Furthermore, Bhagat and coworkers44 reported the synthesis and evaluation of isatin triazole hybrids (13) as potent AChE inhibitors. The target molecule (13) was synthesized (Scheme 2) by first preparing N-propargylated isatin (10) by the reaction of 5-fluoroisatin (1) with propargyl bromide (9) in DMF, using potassium carbonate as a base. Meanwhile, phenacyl azide (12) was formed from 4-chlorophenacyl bromide (11) and NaN3 in DMF. Subsequently, the coupling reaction of N-propargylated isatin (10) and phenacyl azide (12) was conducted through the CuAAC click reaction using DMF with catalytic CuSO4 and sodium ascorbate, affording the desired product (13) (yield: 82%).

Synthesis of the isatin triazole hybrid (13). Reagents and conditions: (i) K2CO3, DMF, and rt, (ii) DMF, NaN3, and rt, and (iii) DMF, sodium ascorbate, CuSO4, and rt.

Results revealed that most of the compounds exhibited comparable inhibitory activities, with IC50 values ranging between 155 ± 1.65 nM and 852 ± 7.17 nM (Table 1). SAR analysis also identified that the electronegative substituents (–F and –Cl) on both the isatin and benzoyl phenyl rings showed the highest AChE inhibitory activity, while –CH3 and –OCH3 were least favorable. Overall, isatin ring substitutions followed this activity trend: F > Cl > H > CH3 > OCH3. Compound 13, having (RF and R1Cl) substituents, demonstrated the best AChE inhibitory activity, with an IC50 value of 155 ± 1.65 nM, as compared to the reference drug, donepezil (IC50 = 42 ± 0.78 nM).

Afterward, the synthesis and biological evaluation of a series of isatin-linked thiazole hybrids (19) were reported. The synthesis (Scheme 3) was initiated with the N-alkylation of isatin (14) using 1,3-dibromopropane (15), affording the corresponding N-alkylated isatin intermediate (16). Meanwhile, the thiazolidine-2-thione heterocycle (18) was synthesized from 1,1-dimethylpropargylamine (17) and CS2 in the presence of a Pd catalyst. Subsequently, the N-alkylated isatin intermediate (16) was coupled with the prepared thiazolidine-2-thione derivative (18) in the presence of K2CO3, furnishing the desired hybrid compound (19) (yield: 42%).

Synthesis of the isatin-linked thiazole hybrid (19). Reagents and conditions: (i) K2CO3 and DMF, (ii) Pd (PPh3)4 and toluene and (iii) K2CO3 and MeCN.

All the synthesized compounds demonstrated excellent to moderate AChE inhibitory activity.45 Out of them, compound 19 (n = 3) was a hit derivative, exhibiting superior AChE inhibition (IC50 = 18.2 ± 1.2 µM) compared to the reference drug, galantamine (IC50 = 275 ± 25 µM) (Table 1). SAR studies have shown that inhibitory activity is influenced by the chain length of the linker between the isatin and thiazole moieties. Optimal activity was observed in derivatives bearing a linker of three or four carbon atoms, while deviation from it (either shorter or longer) resulted in a considerable reduction in inhibitory activity.

Similarly, considering the pivotal role of Isatin derivatives in the development of bioactive compounds, Mehreen et al. synthesized and explored the in vitro AChE inhibitory activity of a library of phenoxy pendant isatin (23).46 The synthetic strategy was initiated by treating 4-fluorophenol (20) with a large excess of 1,2-dibromoethane (21) at 50 °C in a CH3CN : DMF (9 : 1) mixture, affording the desired intermediate (22). The intermediate (22) was then reacted with isatin (14) in DMF in the presence of K2CO3 as a base at 60 °C to furnish a phenoxy pendant isatin (23) (yield: 83%) (Scheme 4).

Synthesis of the phenoxy pendant isatin derivative (23). Reagents and conditions: (i) K2CO3/CH3CN : DMF (9 : 1)/50 °C–90 °C and (ii) K2CO3/DMF/60 °C.

Biological evaluation demonstrated that most of the synthesized compounds exhibited superior inhibitory activities against AChE, with IC50 values ranging from 0.52 ± 0.073 µg mL−1 to 1.31 ± 0.083 µg mL−1 in comparison to donepezil. The SAR analysis of the phenoxy pendant isatin revealed that halogen substitution at position 5 of the isatin core and on the phenoxy pendant tended to decrease the AChE inhibitory activity. Among halogens, fluorine resulted in the smallest loss of activity, probably because of its small size and high electronegativity, which influenced electronic properties but had little steric effect. Among them, the substituted compound (23), with R1H and R2H, emerged as a lead candidate, showcasing excellent AChE inhibition, with an IC50 of 0.52 ± 0.073 µg mL−1 compared to donepezil (IC50 = 8.234 ± 0.628 µg mL−1) (Table 1).

A later study by Macha and coworkers47 established the potential derivatives of cycloalkyl-fused quinoline–isatin Schiff base hybrids (31) and investigated their biological properties against ChE. As shown in Scheme 5, the synthesis of the hybrid molecule (31) was carried out via a four-step process. First, 2-aminobenzonitrile (24) was heated under reflux in dry toluene in the presence of BF3·Et2O and cycloheptanone (25) for 24 hours to obtain the cycloalkanoquinoline amine (26). This amine (26) was then reacted with 3-chloropropanoyl chloride (27) in dry dichloromethane, along with triethylamine, from 0 °C to room temperature to give rise to the cycloalkanoquinolinyl-N-chloroacetamide compound (28). In a parallel step, isatin (14) was reacted with aniline (29) by refluxing for 3–5 hours in the presence of absolute ethanol containing glacial acetic acid to give the isatin Schiff base compound (30). Lastly, these isatin Schiff bases (30) were reacted with a cycloalkanoquinolinyl-N-chloroacetamide compound (28) in dry dimethylformamide (DMF), along with K2CO3, yielding the desired derivative (31) (yield: 49%).

Synthesis of the cycloalkyl-fused quinoline–isatin Schiff base hybrid (31). Reagents and conditions: (i) BF3, reflux, and 24 h, (ii) DCM, 0 °C, and 4 h, and (iii) acetic acid, EtOH, reflux, and 6 h.

Biological evaluation demonstrated that all the synthesized compounds exhibited comparable inhibitory activities, with IC50 values ranging from 0.71 ± 0.04 µM to 3.05 ± 0.03 µM for AChE and from 1.08 ± 0.02 to 4.21 ± 0.14 µM (Table 1) for BChE. Out of them, compound 31, possessing (n = 3, n′ = 2, and RH) substituents, showed the best activity, with IC50 values of 0.71 ± 0.04 µM (AChE) and 1.08 ± 0.02 µM (BChE), in comparison to the reference drug, tacrine. SAR studies showed that increasing the cycloalkane ring size from five to seven members and extending the linker length enhanced AChE inhibition. Additionally, substitution at the para-position of the phenyl ring also influenced activity, with fluorine generally reducing BChE inhibition, while chlorine improved AChE potency in certain analogs.

On the other hand, oximes, being hydroxy-imine derivatives, exhibit a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, including antifungal, antibacterial, antioxidant, and anticancer effects. Remarkably, these compounds serve as effective antidotes to nerve agents by reactivating inhibited AChE.48,49 In light of their therapeutic relevance, a series of isatin–pyridine oxime hybrids (35) has been explored for their AChE inhibition potential.50 Isatin (14) was reacted with 1,5-dibromopentane (32) in dry DMF at 80 °C in the presence of K2CO3 as a base to afford the N-alkylated isatin intermediate (33). This intermediate (33) was subsequently treated with pyridine-4-aldoxime (34) in dry acetonitrile with a catalytic amount of KI. The reaction was carried out at 80 °C, yielding the corresponding isatin–oxime hybrid (35) (Scheme 6). In vitro analysis showed that compound 35 (n = 5 and oxime position = 4) (Table 1) behaved as a competitor inhibitor of AChE, exhibiting a promising inhibitory activity, with IC50 = 24.7 µM, as compared to the reference drug, pyridostigmine bromide (IC50 = 21.7 µM). SAR studies revealed the critical effect of the linker length between the isatin and pyridine-oxime moieties, with the most potent inhibition observed for a linker encompassing five methylene units.

Synthesis of the isatin–pyridine oxime hybrid (35). Reagents and conditions: (i) K2CO3, DMF, and 80 °C and (ii) CH3CN, KI cat. and 80 °C.

Moreover, building on the excellent inhibitory activity of previously reported N-alkyl-isatin hybrids, a library of N-alkyl isatin derivatives (37) was recently prepared and evaluated for their AChE and BChE inhibitory activities.51 The target compound (37) was synthesized by the reaction of isatin (14) with 1-bromononane (36), using K2CO3 as a base in DMF at room temperature, affording the target compound (37) (yield: 42%) (Scheme 7).

Synthesis of the N-alkyl isatin derivative (37). Reagents and conditions: (i) K2CO3, DMF, and rt.

Results showed that all the compounds proved to exhibit moderate to high AChE inhibition (IC50 = 62.2 ± 8.5 to 647 ± 57 µM), along with BChE inhibition (IC50 = 3.77 ± 0.45 µM to >1000 µM) (Table 1). SAR analysis indicated no definite relationship between AChE inhibition and the length of the N-alkyl chain, although compounds with more elongated chains had increased activities. Conversely, BChE inhibition was consistently enhanced with chain length, likely due to indirect hydrophobic and steric interactions suggested by molecular docking. Out of them, compound 37, bearing an alkyl chain of n = 8, was a potent one, showing inhibitory activity for AChE (IC50 = 82.1 ± 8.1 µM) and BChE (IC50 = 3.77 ± 0.45 µM), as compared to the reference drug, rivastigmine (AChE: IC50 = 275 ± 25 µM and BChE: IC50 = 1.14 ± 0.13 µM).

In a recent report, a set of 3-indolyl-3-hydroxy-2-oxindole dimers (41) was synthesized and tested for their cholinesterase inhibitory activity.52 Target 41 was synthesized through straightforward one- and two-step procedures. The synthesis of an isatin dimer (39) was achieved through the N-alkylation of isatin (14), with 1,5-dibromopentane (38) as an alkylating agent in DMF, using K2CO3. The further reaction of the isatin dimer (39) with indole (40) in KOH resulted in the formation of the desired product (41) (Scheme 8).

Synthesis of the 3-indolyl-3-hydroxy-2-oxindole dimer (41). Reagents and conditions: (i) KOH and MeOH, (ii) K2CO3 and DMF, and (iii) KOH and MeOH.

Although none of the compounds exhibited marked AChE inhibition, several of them had moderate to effective BChE activity, with IC50 values ranging from 3.20 ± 0.20 µM to 357 ± 6 µM (Table 1). SAR analysis showed that isatin dimerization improved BChE inhibition compared to the parent isatin and that the length of the linkers was critical, with the intermediate-sized linkers in most cases showing the highest activity. Compound 41 was the most effective in both series, exhibiting similar BChE inhibition, with IC50 values of 4.49 ± 0.43 µM, as compared to the standard drug, rivastigmine (IC50 = 1.14 ± 0.13 µM).

Isatin-derived Schiff bases (imines)

Hydrazone derivatives, possessing a biologically active azomethine moiety, have gained significant interest in medicinal and pharmaceutical chemistry.53 In this context, Arshad and colleagues developed a series of hydrazone conjugates by coupling heteroaromatic aldehydes with N-alkyl-5-chloroisatins (47)54 and screened for in vitro ChE inhibitory activity. The synthesis involved carrying out the N-alkylation reaction on 5-chloroisatin (42) with 2-bromoethylbenzene (43), using K2CO3 as the base in DMF at 45 °C–50 °C for 3 h, giving compound N-(2-phenethyl)-5-chloroisatin (44). On the other hand, the reaction of tetrahydrothiophene-2-carbaldehyde (45) with hydrazine hydrate (NH2NH2·H2O) in a MeOH solvent, with Et3N as the catalyst at reflux temperature for 2 h, produced compound 46. Finally, the condensation of 44 with 46 in isopropanol (i-PrOH) and acetic acid (AcOH) under reflux for 3 h furnished the target thioether-linked isatin hydrazone derivative (47) (yield: 87%) (Scheme 9).

Synthesis of the phenoxy pendant isatin (47). Reagents and conditions: (i) K2CO3, DMF, 45 °C-50 °C, and 3 h, (ii) NH2NH2·H2O, MeOH, Et3N, reflux, and 2 h, and (iii) i-PrOH, AcOH, reflux, and 3 h.

Notably, results demonstrated promising inhibitory activity for all synthesized compounds against AChE, with IC50 values ranging between 16.22 ± 0.18 and 20.21 ± 0.06 µM, and BChE, with IC50 values ranging from 20.11 ± 0.12 to 28.01 ± 0.01 µM, as compared to the reference drug, galantamine. SAR studies through the docking analysis of compounds exhibited strong BChE binding due to the hydrogen and arene-H interactions of the thiophene ring with Phe278 and Phe295, along with electrostatic interactions involving the carbonyl group. Compound 47, with RCH2–C6H5 (Table 2), exhibited significant dose-dependent inhibition of AChE, with IC50 values of 16.22 ± 0.18 µM, and BChE, with IC50 values of 20.11 ± 0.12 µM, in comparison to the reference drug, galantamine (AChE: IC50 = 10.13 ± 0.01 µM; and BChE: IC50 = 22.00 ± 0.01 µM).

Building upon this concept, a novel study was undertaken to synthesize a set of isatin-linked piperazine/morpholine derivatives (50) and evaluate their cholinesterase inhibition potential.55 The target compound (50) was synthesized in two steps. First, isatin hydrazone (48) was prepared by refluxing 5-chloroisatin (42) with hydrazine hydrate in methanol in the presence of glacial acetic acid for 3 h. Subsequently, the obtained isatin hydrazone (48) was condensed with morpholine benzaldehyde (49) in methanol, using glacial acetic acid as a catalyst under reflux at 160 °C for 72 h to afford the final product (50) (Scheme 10).

Synthesis of the phenoxy pendant isatin (50). Reagents and conditions: (i) EtOH, glacial acetic acid, reflux and 3 h and (ii) EtOH, glacial acetic acid, reflux, 160 °C and 72 h.

Interestingly, most of the compounds selectively displayed moderate to significant AChE inhibition, with an IC50 value ranging between 1.60 ± 0.51 µM and 5.73 ± 0.97 µM. SAR studies showed that the derivatives of the morpholine series bearing electron-withdrawing groups on the C-5 position of the isatin moiety exhibited more potent human cholinesterase (hAChE) inhibition, following the order Cl > Br > F > CH3. In contrast, the use of electron-donating substituents, such as methyl or methoxy, led to diminished inhibition. In addition, morpholine-based halogenated compounds generally surpassed their piperazine counterparts. Notably, compound 50, having (RCl and R14-(4-morpholinyl) (Table 2), showed notable hAChE inhibition, with an IC50 value of 1.60 ± 0.51 µM, in comparison to the reference drug, donepezil (IC50 = 0.016 ± 0.002 µM).

Similarly, the 1,3,5-triazine scaffold is seen in several bioactive compounds showing anticancer, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and AD activities.56 Following these observations, Abibigloo et al. designed and synthesized a series of isatin 1,3,5-triazine benzylamine derivatives (57) as multi-target-directed ligands (MTDLs) for AD.57 The 2,4,6-trichloro-1,3,5-triazine compound (51) was initially methoxylated through the reaction of methanol in the presence of NaHCO3 in an ice bath to give rise to 4,6-dichloro-2-methoxy-1,3,5-triazine (52). Then, the obtained intermediate (52) was subjected to reaction with benzylamine (53) in the presence of 1,4-dioxane/acetone, with NaHCO3 as the base, at temperatures ranging between 0 °C and 5 °C to obtain the benzylamino triazine intermediate (54). The obtained intermediate (54) was reacted with hydrazine hydrate in 1,4-dioxane, yielding an intermediate (55). Finally, the obtained intermediate (55) was subjected to a condensation reaction with an appropriate isatin derivative (56) in ethanol under reflux, yielding the desired derivative (57) (yield: 65%) (Scheme 11).

Synthesis of the isatin 1,3,5-triazine benzylamine derivatives (57). Reagents and conditions: (i) CH3OH, NaHCO3, and 0 °C, (ii) NaHCO3, 0 °C, and 1,4-dioxane, (iii) NH2NH2·H2O, rt, and 1,4-dioxane and (iv) glacial acetic acid, ethanol, and reflux.

Remarkably, these compounds were found to possess strong in vitro inhibitory activities towards AChE, with IC50 values in the range of 0.86–36.30 µM, and BChE, with IC50 values ranging between 0.66 µM and 3.88 µM, in comparison with the reference drug, donepezil (eeAChE: IC50 = 22.4 ± 2.1 µM and eqBChE: IC50 = 0.21 ± 0.04 µM) (Table 2). SAR studies indicated that N-benzyl isatin hybrids improved AChE inhibition via hydrophobic interactions. Furthermore, replacing aniline with benzylamine improved activity in isatin and N-benzyl analogs but reduced potency in N-methyl derivatives. Additionally, 4-OCH3 groups lowered AChE activity, and benzylamine substitution decreased BChE inhibition. Among them, hybrid 57, having (R1R2R3H and R4CH2Ph) substituents, exhibited marvelous inhibitory activity against both electrophorus electricus acetylcholinesterase (eeAChE) (IC50 = 0.86 ± 0.03 µM) and equine butyrylcholinesterase (eqBChE) (IC50 = 0.66 ± 0.05 µM), outperforming the standard reference drug, donepezil.

In the same way, a library of compounds of s-triazine, isatin, and aniline derivatives (64) was synthesized and assessed as anti-ChE. The target compound (64) was prepared in multiple steps from aniline (58), which was first treated with chloral hydrate and hydroxylamine hydrochloride in the presence of Na2SO4 and H2SO4 to produce isonitrosoacetanilide (59). Thereafter, cyclization was carried out under concentrated H2SO4, affording isatin (14). The isatin (14) was then reacted with benzyl bromide (43) in the presence of K2CO3 in DMF to form an intermediate (60). On the other hand, cyanuric chloride (51) was subjected to methoxylation with methanol at 0 °C to produce an intermediate (52), which was then reacted with o-anisidine (61) in a NaHCO3 aqueous solution to yield an intermediate (62). The intermediate (62) was further treated with hydrazine hydrate in acetone at room temperature to produce a hydrazinyl-s-triazine intermediate (63). The condensation of intermediates 59 and 63 in ethanol containing acetic acid gave the desired product (64) (yield: 74%) (Scheme 12).

Results showed that all the synthesized compounds exhibited excellent inhibitory activity, with IC50 values ranging from 0.2 µM to 734.5 µM for AChE and 0.02 µM to 1.92 µM for BChE58 (Table 2). The results showed that many of the synthesized compounds exhibited good inhibitory activities against AChE, with a range of IC50 values from 0.023 ± 0.003 µM to 19.85 ± 0.002 µM, as compared to the reference drug, donepezil.75 SAR investigations indicated that the unsubstituted quinoxaline derivative exhibited moderate AChE inhibitory activity, whereas the phenyl-substituted compounds had the lowest activity, which might be attributed to steric hindrance. Structure–activity relationships and pharmacophoric features

Collectively, the preceding studies highlight several structural features that govern ChE inhibition, with the effects of substitution, N-functionalization, linker length, hydrophobicity, and molecular conformation varying across isatin-based scaffolds. In the coumarin–triazole-isatin series (8), the unsubstituted isatin derivative (RH) showed the strongest BChE inhibition. However, fluorine provided moderate activity, and relatively bulky halogen, nitro, and methyl substituents were less favorable, suggesting that steric compatibility within the binding site can outweigh the intrinsic electronic effects of substituents.43 In contrast, the isatin-triazole series (13) showed a different trend, with C-5 fluoro and chloro substitution enhancing AChE inhibition relative to methyl and methoxy analogues, indicating a favorable contribution from electron-withdrawing substituents in this scaffold.44 Similarly, hydrazone derivatives (19), bearing 5-chloroisatin, displayed potent AChE inhibition, supporting the beneficial role of C-5 halogenation in that series.45 However, this relationship is not universal: in the phenoxy–isatin derivatives (23), halogen substitution reduced AChE inhibition, demonstrating that the effect of C-5 substitution depends on substituent identity, position, steric demand, and the surrounding pharmacophore.46 Additional evidence from compounds 50 and 78 supports the contribution of electron-withdrawing groups, particularly chloro, bromo, and nitro substituents, although the magnitude and direction of these effects vary among chemical series.55,62 Thus, rather than defining a universal electronic preference, the available data indicate that C-5 substitution should be optimized in conjunction with the complete molecular framework (Scheme 25).

N-functionalization and linker architecture provide additional determinants of activity. N-substitution can modulate lipophilicity, molecular shape, and hydrophobic recognition within the ChE active site. In the N-substituted series (37), increasing the N-alkyl chain length improved BChE inhibition, although a comparable systematic trend was not observed for AChE, highlighting the different steric environments of the two enzymes.51N-benzyl substitution was also favorable in compounds 57 and 64, where the appended hydrophobic group contributed to improved enzyme recognition.57 Linker length is particularly important in molecular hybrids because it determines the relative orientation and conformational freedom of the interacting pharmacophores. For the coumarin-triazole-isatin hybrids (8), the three-carbon linker afforded the most favorable BChE inhibition, whereas short linkers were less effective.43 Similarly, the isatin–thiazole derivatives (19) showed improved AChE inhibition with optimized three- to four-carbon linkers,45 while the isatin-pyridine oxime system (35) displayed optimal activity, with an intermediate five-methylene spacer.50 The isatin dimer (41) further demonstrated that intermediate linker lengths can provide a favorable balance between conformational flexibility and appropriate positioning of the two pharmacophoric units.52 Heterocyclic incorporation, including triazole, thiazole, quinoline, pyridine and triazine-containing systems, can additionally introduce π-interactions, hydrogen bonds and electrostatic contacts. Collectively, these findings indicate that N-substitution and linker optimization should be considered together rather than as independent structural variables.

Molecular hybridization, hydrophobicity and conformational rigidity further influence AChE/BChE selectivity and potency. The generally larger and more permissive BChE active site can accommodate bulky hydrophobic substituents, as illustrated by 37, 41, and the dispiropyrrolidine (87), which showed preferential BChE inhibition and extensive hydrophobic contacts within the enzyme active site.67 In contrast, the narrow AChE active site may favor more compact architectures, although appropriately oriented aromatic and heterocyclic groups can engage the peripheral anionic site through π-interactions. Spiro and fused systems can restrict conformational freedom and thereby preorganize pharmacophores, but the available data do not support a universal relationship between increased rigidity and potency. For example, compound 87 benefited from hydrophobic interactions involving its phenyl groups and brominated oxindole moieties, whereas the spirooxindole series (92) did not show a consistent SAR trend.67,68 The broad set of derivatives, including 100, 105, and 110a-b, further demonstrates that substituent effects are dependent on the specific molecular framework and enzyme isoform.70–72 Overall, the emerging pharmacophore comprises a planar aromatic surface derived from the isatin and/or appended aryl/heteroaryl groups; hydrogen-bonding functionalities associated with carbonyl, imine, hydrazone, oxime or related groups; and an appropriately tuned linker or hydrophobic domain. Therefore, the combined evidence favors the balanced optimization of electronic properties, steric complementarity, hydrophobic interactions and conformational flexibility, rather than maximizing any single structural feature. Given the substantial differences in chemical series and assay conditions, these relationships should be regarded as guiding SAR principles rather than universal rules.

Conclusions and future outlook

The isatin scaffold has emerged as a versatile and privileged platform for the development of ChE inhibitors, offering significant promise for the treatment of AD. A wide range of structurally diverse isatin derivatives have demonstrated potent inhibitory activity against AChE and BChE, with several compounds exhibiting superior in vitro efficacy compared with clinically used agents, such as donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine. Structure–activity relationship studies have revealed the pivotal roles of substituent type and position, linker design, N-substitution, and molecular hybridization in modulating enzyme inhibition, selectivity, and overall biological performance.

Collectively, the evidence summarized in this review highlights the isatin scaffold as an attractive framework for the rational design of next-generation ChE inhibitors. The remarkable chemical diversity and favorable pharmacological properties of isatin-based derivatives provide valuable opportunities for the identification of novel lead compounds with enhanced therapeutic potential. Given the projected rise in the global prevalence of AD over the coming decades, the development of more effective and safer therapeutic agents remains an urgent priority. Therefore, future research should focus on improving cholinesterase isoform selectivity, minimizing off-target toxicity, enhancing blood–brain barrier permeability, and optimizing pharmacokinetic profiles. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for translating promising isatin-based ChE inhibitors into clinically viable candidates and advancing the development of effective therapies for AD and related neurodegenerative disorders.
