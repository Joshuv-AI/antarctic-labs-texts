Design and Evaluation of Selective Positive Allosteric Modulators Targeting Kainate Receptors
Authors: Thomas Colson, Yasmin Bay, Alexandra Manos-Turvey, Giacomo Baudino, Bastiaan Poldermans, Eric Goffin, Marine Piette, Cindy Lesenfants, Marie Leclercq, Jette S. Kastrup, Bernard Pirotte, Anders S. Kristensen, Pierre Francotte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kainate-type ionotropic glutamate receptors (KARs) are emerging therapeutic targets for the modulation of excitatory neurotransmission in epilepsy, psychiatric disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases, yet selective positive allosteric modulators (PAMs) remain scarce. Starting from the benzothiadiazine AMPA receptor (AMPAR) PAM BPAM344 (1), we pursued a structure-based scaffold repurposing strategy to identify KAR-directed analogues. A focused library of benzothiadiazine 1,1-dioxides (7a–7z) was designed through systematic diversification at the N4 position and modulation of the C7 substituent (H, F, and Cl). Compounds were synthesized via a convergent route involving benzothiadiazine core assembly, N4 alkylation with aliphatic (including nitrile-terminated), trifluorinated, benzyl, or phenethyl substituents, followed by reduction to the corresponding 3,4-dihydro derivatives. All compounds were fully characterized and evaluated for modulatory activity at GluA2 and GluK1–3 receptors using Ca2+ fluorescence assays in HEK cells. Clear structure–activity relationships (SAR) were established. Short nitrile-terminated aliphatic substituents (7a–7g) retained GluA2 potentiation but lacked KAR activity, indicating that flexible polar chains favor AMPAR selectivity. In contrast, the N4-benzyl series revealed a more complex structure–activity relationship. The para-nitro benzyl analogue 7y was inactive across the receptor subtypes tested, whereas the 3-fluoro-4-nitrobenzyl derivative 7q showed preferential potentiation of GluK1 and GluK3, with minimal GluA2 modulation. Notably, the meta-fluorinated benzyl analogue 7u preferentially potentiated GluK3 while remaining inactive at GluK1, GluK2, and GluA2, whereas the other fluorinated derivatives were devoid of detectable activity across the receptor subtypes tested. These findings reveal a distinct subtype-dependent SAR and support the existence of a spatially constrained polar microenvironment within the GluK1/3 allosteric sites, which appears to be optimally engaged by rigid para-polar benzyl substituents. In line with our docking observations, acidic substituents may further exploit this polar region while the core scaffold remains favorably accommodated in the surrounding hydrophobic pocket. On the basis of the pharmacological testing, meta-fluorination appears to further refine subtype selectivity toward GluK3 and may represent a promising starting point for further exploratory work. Collectively, these results establish 1,2,4-benzothiadiazine 1,1-dioxides as a tractable chemotype for the development of subtype-selective KAR PAMs and provide a medicinal chemistry framework for second-generation optimization, while the docking-derived contacts should be regarded as plausible structural hypotheses rather than experimentally confirmed interactions, given that they are based on SwissDock-predicted poses rather than a crystallographic ligand–receptor complex.

Introduction

Most of the excitatory signals in the central nervous system (CNS) are mediated by the amino acid L-glutamate through the activation of ionotropic glutamate receptors (iGluRs) and metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs). iGluRs are classified into three main categories according to their interaction with specific agonists. The first class includes receptors that respond to N-methyl-D-aspartic acid (NMDA), known as NMDA receptors (NMDARs). The second class consists of receptors sensitive to 2-amino-3-(5-methyl-3-hydroxy-1,2-oxazol-4-yl)­propanoic acid (AMPA), known as AMPA receptors (AMPARs). The third class includes receptors that respond to kainic acid (2-carboxy-3-carboxymethyl-4-isopropenylpyrrolidine), known as kainate receptors (KARs). Additionally, there is a lesser-known type of iGluRs, the δ (GluD) receptors. ,,

iGluRs are composed of different subunits: for the NMDARs, GluN1, GluN2A-D, and GluN3A/B; for the AMPARs, GluA1–4; and for the KARs, GluK1–5. ,,, AMPARs are tetrameric assemblies of GluA1–4 subunits in which the amino-terminal domain (ATD) and ligand-binding domain (LBD) form an intertwined dimer-of-dimers arrangement with domain-swapped subunit pairs, whereas the transmembrane domain (TMD) forms a 4-fold symmetric tetrameric ion channel. ,, KAR assemblies can be composed of the five different subunits, GluK1–5. , The GluK1, GluK2, and GluK3 subunits can form heterotetrameric as well as homotetrameric receptors. The GluK4 and GluK5 subunits necessarily form heterotetramers by association with GluK1–3 subunits. ,, In contrast to NMDARs, which are obligatory heterotetramers, AMPARs and KARs can thus exist both as heterotetramers and homotetramers.

AMPARs and KARs comprise a large extracellular region containing two distinct domains: the amino-terminal domain (ATD) and the ligand-binding domain (LBD), which harbors the glutamate-binding site. There is also a transmembrane domain (TMD), consisting of three hydrophobic membrane-spanning helices (M1, M3, and M4) and an intramembrane reentrant loop (M2) that forms part of the ion channel pore. , Finally, an intracellular carboxyl-terminal domain (CTD) is present, with motifs for binding and phosphorylation of transmembrane regulatory proteins.

Alternative splicing in AMPARs ,, gives rise to two variants, flip (which is dominant during early development) and flop (the most abundant variant in adults).

AMPARs are believed to regulate the rapid excitation required to remove the magnesium block of nearby NMDARs, thereby allowing information transmission. The physiology of KARs is less understood than that of AMPARs. KARs seem to show similar properties to AMPARs, allowing ion flow immediately after glutamate binding, but they are generally less permeable to calcium ions than GluA2-lacking AMPARs and more permeable than GluA2-containing AMPARs. AMPARs are primarily located on the postsynaptic membrane, while research indicates that KARs can be found on the presynaptic , as well as the postsynaptic side of the neuronal synapse. ,, Postsynaptic KARs, like AMPARs, can depolarize the membrane sufficiently to relieve the Mg2+ block of NMDARs, although KAR-mediated excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) exhibit slower kinetics than those mediated by AMPARs.

In addition to their importance for rapid excitatory communication in the brain, iGluRs also play a role in synaptic plasticity, which is critical for learning and memory. , Also, iGluRs have vital functions in neural development and neurodegeneration, and their abnormal activity is often associated with a wide range of neurological disorders, such as epilepsy, ischemic stroke, and ADHD, and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. ,, Dysregulation may also be associated with psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia, obsessive-compulsive disorder, major depressive disorder, and bipolar disorder. Because of these multiple implications, iGluRs have attracted considerable interest in drug development. , Several studies indicate that KAR subtypes, defined by their subunit composition, differ in function and may be differentially implicated in disease. In particular, heterotetrameric receptors composed of low-affinity subunits (GluK1–3) together with high-affinity subunits (GluK4 or GluK5) have been associated with neurological disorders such as epilepsy, autism, schizophrenia, and depression. ,,, These observations highlight KARs as potential therapeutic targets and have motivated efforts to develop pharmacological tools to modulate their function.

Initial studies in the field of iGluR modulators focused on the synthesis of positive allosteric modulators of AMPARs (AMPAR PAMs), and some of them were also tested on KARs. Among such modulators, BPAM344 (1) showed marked activity as a potentiator of KARs. This project aimed to produce a set of new positive allosteric modulators of KARs (KAR PAMs) based on the structure of BPAM344 (1). Our main scaffold (A) illustrated in Figure was inspired by Frydenvang et al., who suggested targeting a region in the receptors where differences are observed between AMPARs (GluA2 Asp781) and KARs (GluK1 His792) to achieve selectivity by extending PAMs that bind to the conventional site with substituents that reach this region. The new compounds contain a hydrogen atom or a small halogen atom, such as fluorine or chlorine, at the 7-position of the benzothiadiazine ring. The presence of a H-bond donor group at the 2-position appears to be important and therefore has been retained.

Structure of BPAM344 (1) and the primary scaffold of the new KAR PAMs (A).

The 4-position of the benzothiadiazine ring allows the introduction of a broad range of substituents. , Here, we address the effect of introducing apolar substituents (R4) on the modulatory efficacy of the compounds on AMPARs (GluA2) and KARs (GluK1–3) using Ca2+ fluorescence-based assays. Initially, short aliphatic chains with steric and hydrophobic properties comparable to the cyclopropyl group of BPAM344 were explored (Figure ), including alkene and alkyne analogues designed to mimic its compact apolar volume. A second series, including nitrile-terminated aliphatic chains, was conceived as “polar probing” substituents to explore the potential formation of additional interactions within the GluK1–3 binding pocket. Similarly, the 4,4,4-trifluorobutyl moiety was introduced with the same exploratory rationale, aiming to evaluate whether polarizable or partially electron-withdrawing substituents could favor new protein–ligand contacts.

Structures of benzothiadiazine derivatives with aliphatic and polar side chains.

Indeed, it has been investigated that the cyclopropyl has the ability to interact with neighboring π-electron systems and π-electron centers. The goal of this second pool was to generate molecules capable of establishing π interactions with their surroundings, similar to those observed with the cyclopropyl chain.

To complete this approach, substituted benzyl and phenethyl groups were also fixed at the 4-position of the benzothiadiazine ring (Figure ). Tolerated apolar R4 substituents may be further explored by the introduction of charged groups.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis

The target compounds 7a–z were synthesized starting from aniline (2a; X = H), 4-fluoroaniline (2b; X = F), or 4-chloroaniline (2c; X = Cl) (Scheme ). Initially, the reaction of anilines with chlorosulfonyl isocyanate in the presence of aluminum chloride (AlCl3) led to the formation of the ring-closed “3-oxo” intermediates 3a–c. Subsequent hydrolysis under strongly acidic conditions of these intermediates yielded the corresponding aminobenzenesulfonamides 4a–c. Next, the latter underwent a ring closure reaction with triethyl orthoformate to generate the 1,2,4-benzothiadiazine 1,1-dioxides 5a–c. These key intermediates were then selectively alkylated at the 4-position using the appropriate aliphatic/benzylic/phenethylic alkylating agent and potassium carbonate (K2CO3), afforded an aliphatic/benzylic/phenethylic chain at the 4-position of the 1,2,4-benzothiadiazine 1,1-dioxides 6a–z. Finally, reduction of the CN bond of this ring by means of sodium borohydride (NaBH4) provided the target structures 7a–z.

Biological Testing of KAR Modulators

The potentiating activity of the compounds was evaluated in HEK293 cells expressing recombinant homomeric GluA2 AMPA receptors or KARs GluK1–3 using a Ca2+-sensitive fluorescence assay (Figure ). Cells were stimulated with saturating concentrations of agonist in the absence or presence of test compounds, and agonist-evoked responses were quantified as peak fluorescence increases. Potentiation was normalized to the response produced by the reference modulator BPAM344 (100%). Compounds were tested at 100 μM at GluA2, GluK1, and GluK2 receptors and at 300 μM at GluK3 receptors. Compounds in series 1 (Figure B), containing linear aliphatic R4 substituents, generally retained potentiating activity at GluA2 comparable to BPAM344. Within this series, several analogues also potentiated GluK3 responses, whereas activity at GluK1 and GluK2 was largely absent. In contrast, compounds in series 2 (Figure C), bearing aryl-based R4 substituents, showed no potentiation at GluA2 overall, but a subset showed activity at GluK1 and/or GluK3. Based on the primary screening, four compounds (7l, 7m, 7q, and 7u) that displayed significant GluK1, GluK2, or GluK3 potentiation were further evaluated in concentration–response experiments to assess their potency and receptor subtype selectivity (Figure D). Increasing concentrations of compounds 7l, 7m, 7q, and 7u produced concentration-dependent potentiation of agonist-evoked responses, with clear subtype-dependent differences in efficacy and apparent potency. As indicated by the screening, compounds 7l and 7m showed preferential potentiation at GluK3 and GluA2, and minimal activity at GluK1 and GluK2. Notably, compound 7q exhibited pronounced potentiation at GluK1 and GluK3 receptors at the maximum tested concentration of 300 μM, as well as significant potentiation at GluK2, but no potentiation of the GluA2 AMPA receptor, indicating a degree of KAR selectivity. Compound 7u displayed concentration-dependent potentiation primarily at GluK3 receptors, with comparatively weaker effects at GluA2 and no detectable activity at GluK1 or GluK2. For all compounds, full saturation of the concentration–response curves could not be achieved, as concentrations above 300 μM were not attainable due to limited solubility in the assay buffer. Consequently, maximal responses should be interpreted as apparent estimates, and EC50 values could not be reliably determined from these data.

Docking Exploration

To gain preliminary insight into the binding mode of compound 7q within the allosteric pocket, molecular docking was performed using the GluK1 crystal structure 5MFQ. This template was used after the removal of one of the two cocrystallized BPAM344 molecules present in the pocket. The top-ranked SwissDock pose was then selected for interaction analysis using PLIP to identify the types of contacts that could account for the marked interest in this compound within the acidic KAR PAM series.

Prior to selecting the pose retained for Protein–Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) analysis, we first verified that docked conformations of 7q could reproduce the binding mode of the cocrystallized PAM BPAM344 in 5MFQ. In particular, several of the highest-ranked poses showed a close overlap of the benzothiadiazine core of 7q with the corresponding bicyclic scaffold of BPAM344, while maintaining a comparable orientation of the 4-substituent toward the central region of the allosteric pocket (illustrated for poses 1–4 in Figure A–D).

In addition to these hydrophobic contacts, one direct hydrogen bond is predicted between the ligand and His792B, with the protein acting as a donor toward the ligand atom O3. Notably, this interaction pattern points to a polar microenvironment in the vicinity of the 4-substituted region of the benzothiadiazine core, in particular near a histidine side chain, while the rest of the scaffold remains anchored in a largely hydrophobic environment. This observation was a key element of our design rationale and motivated the development of a second series of 4-substituted acidic derivatives designed to probe this polar subpocket more effectively. In particular, these analogues were designed to favor the formation of a strong salt bridge with His792B, with the aim of enhancing the selectivity or potency of the newly synthesized KAR PAMs (Table ).

The PLIP output also reveals several water-mediated interactions involving mainly Ser761A and Gln786B, further supporting the presence of a structured polar network around the acidic region of the ligand. Taken together, these data suggest that the favorable behavior of 7q may not arise from a single dominant contact but rather from a cooperative interaction network. Discussion

The newly synthesized KPAM library was evaluated for its ability to potentiate AMPA and kainate receptor subtypes, using BPAM344 as the reference ligand. BPAM344 is known for its modulatory activity on KARs (GluK1b EC50 = 26 μM; GluK2a EC50 = 75 μM; GluK3a EC50 = 639 μM) but differs markedly in its activity at AMPARs (EC50 = 0.9 μM).

At first, the subset comprising 7a–g, characterized by a short aliphatic linker terminated with a nitrile group, yielded an unexpected pharmacological outcome. Although originally designed as polar chemical probes to explore new interactions within the GluK1–3 binding site, these compounds, tested at 100 μM at GluK1 and GluK2 and at 300 μM at GluK3, showed minimal activity on KARs while potentiating the GluA2 AMPA receptor, albeit less than that of BPAM344. These findings indicate that the nitrile-terminated chain favors AMPA receptor engagement and may represent a structural feature biasing selectivity toward AMPARs rather than KARs.

Attempts to mimic the electronic contribution of the cyclopropyl (7h–k, 7n, and 7o) substituent in BPAM344 resulted in inactive compounds across all tested kainate receptor subtypes. Taken together, these data do not allow us to attribute the effect of the cyclopropyl motif solely to electronic factors. Instead, the cyclopropyl group likely contributes to ligand–receptor fit through a combination of steric and conformational constraints and shape and electrostatic complementarity that are not fully reproduced by the corresponding π-extended analogues. In line with this view, the alkyne derivatives 7h and 7i, the allyl analogues 7j and 7k, and the benzyl derivatives 7o and 7n all show little or no activity at KARs, whereas the CF3-substituted compounds 7l and 7m display activity at GluK3, and 7m shows weak activity on GluK1 but they also gain GluA2 activity, indicating a different balance between potency and subtype selectivity. The aryl-substituted series (Figure C) reveals an interesting SAR governing KAR selectivity within the benzothiadiazine scaffold. The most pronounced activity was observed for derivatives bearing a 3-fluoro-4-nitrobenzyl substituent, exemplified by 7q, which display clear potentiation at GluK1 and GluK3 while remaining essentially inactive at GluA2. Importantly, however, the presence of a polar, electron-withdrawing substituent in the para position is not sufficient on its own to confer activity. Indeed, compounds 7y and 7z, which also bear a nitro substituent, are inactive across all receptor subtypes. This observation demonstrates that KAR engagement requires a precise combination of electronic, positional, and scaffold-dependent parameters rather than a single polar functionality.

Additional complexity emerges from the behavior of 7u, which bears a meta-fluoro substituent on the aromatic ring and selectively potentiates GluK3. Notably, its direct analogue 7t, which lacks the C7-fluorine substituent, is devoid of activity. This discontinuous SAR suggests a delicate interplay between (i) the electronic modulation imparted by meta-fluorination and (ii) the substitution at the C7 position of the benzothiadiazine core, highlighting cooperative effects that drive a finely tuned three-dimensional recognition process rather than additive substituent contributions.

Collectively, these findings strongly support the existence of a spatially restricted polar microenvironment within the GluK1 and GluK3 allosteric sites. Productive engagement appears to require (i) a benzyl-type vector capable of projecting a polar group to a defined depth, (ii) precise angular orientation of the substituent (para vs meta effects), and (iii) an appropriate electronic environment modulated by both ring substitution and core decoration. The inactivity of several closely related analogues underscores the narrow tolerance of the pocket.

Importantly, the p-polar benzyl derivatives remain inactive at GluA2, further supporting the view that structural differences between KAR and AMPAR allosteric sites are sufficient to enable selective modulation. This divergence provides a mechanistically grounded opportunity to drive subtype selectivity within an AMPAR-biased chemotype.

Altogether, the aryl series defines a nontrivial, cooperatively controlled selectivity motif rather than a simple substituent effect. An N4-benzyl appendage bearing a properly oriented polar functionality emerges as a necessary but not sufficient condition for KAR potentiation, with subtype selectivity further modulated by meta-fluorination and adapted C7 substitution. These insights establish a structurally coherent framework for second-generation optimization aimed at enhancing potency while preserving KAR selectivity.

Finally, it should be emphasized that the docking analysis provides only a qualitative hypothesis of the binding mode of 7q at the GluK1 allosteric site. Because the calculations were performed on a single, crystallographically derived receptor conformation, they do not account for the conformational changes that are expected to accompany positive allosteric modulator binding at the dimer interface. As a consequence, the docked poses and the corresponding interaction patterns should be regarded as plausible models that are consistent with the SAR rather than as experimentally validated structures. A more realistic description of the allosteric recognition process would require molecular dynamics simulations.

Experimental Section

General Procedures

Melting points were determined on a Buchi M-565 capillary apparatus and were uncorrected. Elemental analyses (C, H, N, S) were performed on a Thermo Scientific Flash EA 1112 elemental analyzer and were within ±0.4% of the theoretical values for carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen; a higher tolerance (±0.6%) was accepted for sulfur, considering its corresponding peak shape. This analytical method confirmed a purity of ≥95% for all final compounds submitted for biological evaluation. The 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker Avance instruments operating at 1H frequencies of 400, 500, and 700 MHz and 13C frequencies of 125 and 126 MHz using deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d 6) as the solvent and tetramethylsilane (TMS) as an internal standard. Chemical shifts were reported in δ values (ppm) relative to the internal TMS standard. The abbreviations s = singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, q = quadruplet, m = multiplet, and bs = broad singlet were used throughout. All reactions were routinely checked by TLC on silica gel 60 F254 (Merck).

Biological Evaluation

To assess positive allosteric modulation of AMPA and kainate receptors, compound activity was evaluated using a Ca2+-sensitive fluorescence-based assay adapted from previous studies. GripTite HEK293 cells transiently expressing homomeric recombinant rat GluA2, GluK1, GluK2, or GluK3 receptors (as described by Bay et al.) were seeded in poly-d-lysine–coated 96-well plates and loaded with the Ca2+-sensitive dye Fluo-8 AM (AAT Bioquest). Following dye loading and washing, fluorescence signals were recorded in a FlexStation I plate reader (Molecular Devices) at excitation/emission wavelengths of 485/538 nm. Agonist-evoked Ca2+ responses were measured in the absence and presence of test modulators added in an assay buffer containing 100 mM choline chloride, 10 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 20 mM CaCl2, and 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4). Compounds were dissolved in DMSO and diluted into buffer immediately before use (final DMSO concentration ≤1% v/v). Peak fluorescence changes following application of agonist (typically 100 μM l-glutamate or kainate) were quantified as relative fluorescence units (RFU) and normalized to the reference modulator BPAM344. Data were obtained from multiple independent experiments with three or four technical replicates per plate and analyzed using SoftMax Pro and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Inc.) to calculate mean ± SEM values and statistical significance.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking studies were carried out using SwissDock with the AutoDock Vina engine, employing the GluK1 crystal structure (PDB ID: 5MFQ) as the receptor template. Prior to docking, one of the two BPAM344 molecules cocrystallized in the allosteric pocket was removed, and the resulting protein structure (5MFQ_1lig.pdb) was used for the calculations. The docking search space was defined around the allosteric site, with a box centered at (−4, 40, 3) and dimensions of 13 × 14 × 13, and the sampling exhaustivity was set to 28. Compound 7q was docked into this binding pocket, and the generated poses were ranked according to their predicted binding free energies.

Among the solutions obtained, four representative poses displayed calculated affinities of −9.587, −9.570, −6.842, and −6.725 kcal/mol, respectively. The best-scoring pose (−9.587 kcal/mol), which also showed no steric clashes and a favorable overlap of the benzothiadiazine scaffold with the cocrystallized BPAM344 orientation, was selected for subsequent analysis. Protein–ligand contacts for the selected pose were characterized using the online version of PLIP (Protein–Ligand Interaction Profiler), which identified hydrophobic contacts with Pro532A, Thr535B, Gln786B, and Leu791B, together with one direct hydrogen bond to His792B and several water-mediated interactions involving Ser761A and Gln786B. Three-dimensional visualization and figure generation were performed using the commercial executable build of PyMOL on an Apple system (Apple M-series GPU, OpenGL 2.1/Metal backend).

Methods

7-Fluoro-2 H -benzo­[ e ]­[1,2,4]­thiadiazin-3­(4 H )-one 1,1-dioxide (3b). To a 100-mL flask containing 20 mL of dichloromethane, 2.1 mL of chlorosulfonyl isocyanate (24.36 mmol; 1.2 equiv) was added. The mixture was then sealed and immersed in an ice bath until a temperature of −5 °C was reached. In addition, 3.79 mL of 4-fluoroaniline (20.30 mmol; 1 equiv) was dissolved in 8 mL of dichloromethane. This solution was added dropwise to the chlorosulfonyl isocyanate solution over 5 min with stirring and was left stirring for 15 min after the end of the addition. 3.55 g of AlCl3 (26.39 mmol; 1.3 equiv) was then added to the reaction medium, and the mixture was brought to reflux for 1 h. The hot solution was poured onto 200 g of ice. Once the ice had melted, the precipitate was filtered under vacuum and washed with 2 × 25 mL of water. The solid was then dissolved in 100 mL of a 10% w/v NaHCO3 solution. After addition of 1 g of charcoal, the medium was boiled for 60 min. The charcoal was removed by filtration under vacuum. The filtrate was placed in an ice bath and acidified by adding 6 N HCl (pH = 1).
