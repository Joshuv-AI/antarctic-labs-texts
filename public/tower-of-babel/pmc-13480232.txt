Evolving Heart Failure Profiles in Quebec from 1998-2021: Insights from the Vision C+ Database
Authors: Shweta Todkar, Emmanuel Marier-Tétrault, Pierre Yves Turgeon, Félix Gélinas-Gascon, Gabriel Couture, Philippe Després, Hugo Lortie, Lauren Basmadjian, Charaf Eddine Ahnadi, Anique Ducharme, Serge Lepage, François Tournoux, Paul Poirier, Jacinthe Leclerc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) affects over 750,000 Canadians and remains a major cause of hospitalization. Although specialized HF clinics deliver structured multidisciplinary care, population-level data describing patients followed in these settings are limited. Vision C+, established in 1998, provides a comprehensive dataset to evaluate changes in HF characteristics across Quebec.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional analysis of adults followed in Quebec outpatient HF clinics between 1998 and 2021 using the Vision C+ electronic medical record. Baseline data at inscription were analyzed and compared across periods (1998-2009 and 2010-2021). Analyses were descriptive and were based on available data without imputation.ResultsData of 63,755 patients were analyzed (1998-2009: 14,908; 2010-2021: 48,847), with a median age of 77 years (quartile 1-quartile 3: 67-85), and 65% were male. HF with reduced ejection fraction (left ventricular ejection fraction ≤ 40%) remained predominant (70%), while HF with preserved ejection fraction (left ventricular ejection fraction > 40%) was higher in 2010-2021 (31% vs 26%). New York Heart Association functional class III-IV was higher in 1998-2009 (58% vs 39%), as was ischemic etiology (84% vs 56%). Comorbidities were highly prevalent overall, with small between-period differences observed (all P < 0.001). Cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator use was higher in 2010-2021, whereas coronary revascularization was higher in 1998-2009.ConclusionsThis first comprehensive description of the Vision C+ dataset shows outpatient HF populations with higher proportions of HF with preserved ejection fraction, substantial multimorbidity, and lower ischemic disease in the later period.

Heart failure (HF) is a chronic and progressive condition affecting over 64 million people globally and over 750,000 Canadians aged ≥ 40 years, with more than 50,000 new diagnoses annually.1,2 HF is the leading cause of hospitalization in individuals aged ≥ 65 years and contributes to healthcare costs exceeding $2.8 billion annually.2,3 In Quebec, approximately 160,000 adults aged ≥ 40 years are affected, with the number of new diagnoses rising annually, highlighting the growing provincial burden.4 The prevalence of HF increases with age, reaching up to 20% in adults aged ≥ 75 years, and it is projected to increase further with the aging of the population.5,6 HF is associated with high morbidity, recurrent hospitalizations, and substantial mortality; the 5-year survival after hospitalization incidence is 50%.6, 7, 8

Despite this high burden, HF management recommendations for both HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) emphasize early diagnosis, optimization of comorbidities, lifestyle counselling, patient self-management, and monitoring of congestion and volume overload, with regular assessment of symptoms, renal function, biomarkers, and signs of fluid overload. All of this is used to guide timely intervention.9 Over the past 2 decades, management of HFrEF has significantly advanced through optimization of guideline-directed medical therapies (GDMTs), including beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), as well as device-based therapies such as implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) and cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT).10,11 These interventions have been shown to improve survival and reduce HF-related hospitalizations in patients with HFrEF.8,12 However, implementation of these therapies into routine clinical practice remains suboptimal, even in specialized HF clinics, due to patient complexity, multimorbidity, and system-level barriers.8,13, 14, 15 Recent Canadian studies highlight persistent gaps in therapy optimization, adherence, and dose titration in patients with HFrEF.14,16,17 Concurrently, evolving demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex distribution, comorbidity burden, and underlying etiologies, further influence treatment decisions and outcomes.18,19 Characterizing these temporal trends is therefore essential to assess the evolution of HF management, identify gaps between evidence-based recommendations and real-world practice, and inform strategies to optimize care delivery.14,20

Large-scale clinical registries are crucial for capturing patient characteristics and HF management, provided that data are collected and used according to Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) principles.21 Although previous Canadian and international registries have offered valuable insights into HF management and guideline adherence,15,17,22,23 long-term, multicentre analyses over 2 decades, including the COVID-19 pandemic period, remain limited in Canada. Vision C+ software offers a unique platform to capture real-world HF data.24 Established in 1998 by the Quebec Heart Failure Society, Vision C+ is a Web-based electronic medical record (EMR) used across multiple outpatient HF clinics in Quebec to systematically capture patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and follow-up data.24 Using baseline data from the EMR, we conducted a cross-sectional retrospective analysis of HF patients from 1998 to 2021, to assess the feasibility of describing the outpatient HF population recorded in the Vision C+ software, as well as describing their characteristics.

Methods

Study design, population, and study sample

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional analysis of adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with a documented HF diagnosis followed in outpatient HF clinics across Quebec between January 1, 1998 and December 31, 2021. All patients recorded in Vision C+ during this period were considered eligible, irrespective of the ejection fraction phenotype (HFrEF or HFpEF). For the analytic/study sample, patients aged < 18 years, those with implausible age or enrollment year, and records with no available clinical information were excluded from analysis, as illustrated in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) flowchart (Fig. 1). Setting

The Heart Failure Clinics (HFCs) are part of the provincial network originally affiliated with the Quebec Heart Failure Society.24 A total of 40 hospital-based outpatient HFCs across Quebec use the Vision C+ EMR. These HFCs provide structured, multidisciplinary care generally delivered by HF cardiologists, nurses, pharmacists, and dietitians. Patients are typically referred following HF hospitalization or emergency consultation and are seen within 2 weeks of discharge. Care includes personalized management plans, on-site intravenous diuretics for decompensation if needed, collaborative up-titration of guideline-directed HF therapies (GDMT), and follow-up visits or telephone assessments based on clinical status. Details on this multidisciplinary HF clinic care model were previously published.15

Data source

Data were obtained from the Vision C+ software (https://sqic.org/vision-c/), a Web-based EMR system used in all participating HFCs with a centralized database hosted on secure servers at the CIUSSS de l’Estrie – CHUS (Sherbrooke University Hospital Centre) and managed by the IT platform at Collaboration in Research for Diagnostic Efficiency (CRED). The Vision C+ software has been previously described and used in Canadian studies.15,16 Additionally, data are entered by multidisciplinary care teams during routine clinical care. Access for research requires approval by a research ethics committee. The IT platform at CRED was responsible for maintaining the system throughout the study period.

Variables

The Vision C+ software captures granular clinical data through structured fields, dropdown menus, and free-text fields completed by a multidisciplinary team during patient consultations. Variables include demographics, clinical history, HF phenotypes (HFrEF, HFpEF), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class I-IV, COVID-19 diagnosis, medical history, HF etiology (ischemic, nonischemic, other), comorbidities, cardiac history, diagnosis (coronary and valvular diseases), electrophysiological devices, and cardiac interventions (surgical and percutaneous). A detailed list of variables and their description is provided in Supplemental Table S1. Even though the Vision C+ EMR contains some structured dropdown fields for variables such as comorbidities, medications, and other clinical characteristics, the exportation format contained aggregated or free-text entries. For example, multiple comorbidities or medication details were frequently listed in single fields in earlier versions of the software, sometimes with typographical errors, prior to the implementation of standardized dropdown menus in more recent versions. Natural language processing (NLP) was applied to disaggregate, normalize, and structure these entries into suitable data formats for statistical analysis. The following section provides a detailed explanation of the NLP application in data structuring.

Use of NLP in data structuring

A custom rule-based NLP algorithm was applied to extract reference terms from unstructured text. Text preprocessing, n-gram generation (1-4 words), and similarity matching based on Levenshtein distance and manual correction of typos was used to ensure accurate mapping. Post-matching steps prioritized the most specific and highest-similarity term–n-gram pairs to standardize entries for analysis. Detailed steps are provided in Supplemental Appendix S1.

Statistical analysis

Cross sectional analysis

After characterizing the full dataset, 2 cross-sectional analyses were performed based on patient inscription year (1998-2009 and 2010-2021) as a methodological choice to create broad, comparable intervals for describing baseline patient characteristics across time. Baseline LVEF was used to classify HF phenotypes. HF phenotypes were defined as HFrEF (LVEF ≤ 40%) and HFpEF (LVEF > 40%), with the HFpEF category including both mildly reduced ejection fraction (LVEF 41%-49%) and fully preserved ejection fraction (LVEF ≥ 50%).9,25

For period-specific analysis, only patient records with a valid inscription year were included, and those with a missing inscription year were excluded. All analyses were based on baseline data at inscription. For each variable, analyses were conducted using available variable data only (variable-specific complete-case analysis). No imputation was performed for missing values. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs; Q1 and Q3), depending on the distribution, and categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. A 2-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Normality and model assumptions were assessed for all analyses. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

The overall median age was 77 years (IQR, 67-85), and 65% were male. Patients included during 2010-2021 were younger than those included in 1998-2009 (76 [66-84] vs 79 [70-87] years, P < 0.001). Among 52,137 patients with available LVEF data, 36,426 (70%) had HFrEF (≤ 40%) and 15,711 (30%) had HFpEF (> 40%). The proportion of HFrEF was higher during 1998-2009, whereas HFpEF was more frequent during 2010-2021. Median LVEF was 32% (IQR, 25%-45%) overall and was lower in patients included during 1998-2009, compared with those included in 2010-2021. NYHA functional class was documented in 29,137 patients. Compared with 1998-2009, patients included during 2010-2021 were less likely to present with NYHA III–IV symptoms (58% vs 39%, P < 0.001), whereas NYHA II was more common in the 2010-2021 period.

Ischemic was the predominant HF etiology overall (62%). Prevalence was higher during 1998-2009, compared with 2010-2021 (84% vs 56%, P < 0.001), whereas nonischemic and other etiologies were more frequent during the later period. Comorbidities were prevalent in both periods. Hypertension (65%), dyslipidemia (55%), diabetes II (38%), and chronic kidney disease (CKD; 32%) were the most prevalent conditions. Hypertension and dyslipidemia were more frequently reported during 2010-2021, whereas CKD and diabetes were more prevalent during 1998-2009 (all P < 0.001). Sleep apnea demonstrated a marked increase over time for 1998-2009 vs 2010-2021 (2% vs 11%, P < 0.001). Arrhythmias were present in 31% of patients. Past myocardial infarction (MI) and unstable angina were more frequent during 1998-2009, whereas non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) was more common during 2010-2021 (all P < 0.001; Table 1). Valvular heart disease was documented in 56% of patients and was more prevalent during 2010-2021 (59% vs 47%, P < 0.001). Use of electrophysiological devices differed between periods, with greater use of cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT-D) during 2010-2021 (27% vs 3%, P < 0.001), whereas implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICD) use and pacemaker implantation were more common during 1998-2009 (both P < 0.001). Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) was more frequent during 1998-2009 vs 2010-2021 (19% vs 12%, P < 0.001), and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) differed modestly (27% vs 25%, P = 0.010; Table 1). Additionally, 53% of patients were followed at non-university HFCs and 47% at university HFCs. The distribution remained stable across inscription periods (Table 1). Results for overall patients’ data (n = 63,998) are provided in Supplemental Table S2.

Discussion

This study presents a cross-sectional analysis of baseline characteristics of HF patients over 2 decades. Key observations included the following: (i) the feasibility of using the Vision C+ data to describe a whole HF population; (ii) a greater HFpEF proportion, although HFrEF remained the predominant phenotype; (iii) higher prevalence of hypertension and dyslipidemia, lower prevalence of diabetes and chronic kidney disease, while arrhythmias remained common; (iv) a decline in ischemic etiology over time, although it remained predominant overall, and lower coronary revascularization; and (v) greater adoption of implantable electronic devices such as CRT-D/P, with ICDs remaining widely used but with their use declining over time, along with reduced pacemaker use. Collectively, these findings underscore the evolving epidemiology of HF, with HFpEF being now the most prevalent phenotype with its associated comorbidities and the technological developments in cardiovascular care.

A notable finding was the greater proportion of patients with HFpEF in 2010-2021, although HFrEF remained the predominant phenotype overall. This observation is consistent with studies reporting that HFpEF now accounts for nearly 50% of all HF cases, largely driven by population aging and the higher burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity.19,26,27 The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Heart Failure Long-Term Registry of ambulatory patients demonstrated that HFpEF represents a substantial proportion of the HF population and is associated with distinct clinical characteristics and lower 1-year mortality, compared with HFrEF, and recent data from Sweden show that although all-cause mortality declined for all HF phenotypes, the decline was slower for HFpEF than HFrEF, likely reflecting limited disease-modifying therapies and higher comorbidity burden.28,29 In our study, the greater proportion of HFrEF in 1998-2009 may partly reflect the diagnostic challenges in HFpEF at that time, given its heterogeneous pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and lack of clear diagnostic criteria.30,31 National survey data further show substantial knowledge gaps among primary care physicians regarding HFpEF diagnosis and management.32 Enhanced availability of outpatient echocardiography improved clinical awareness, and the structured HFC model in Quebec likely contributed to greater identification of HFpEF.30,31 The difference between periods is further supported by the observation that patients in 2010-2021 were younger, less symptomatic (more in NYHA class II and fewer in NYHA class III-IV), and had more preserved LVEF at baseline, suggesting earlier referral and greater optimization of GDMT.15

Cross-sectional differences in comorbidities were also evident. Hypertension (66%) and dyslipidemia (57%) were more prevalent in 2010-2021, whereas diabetes (40%) and CKD (36%) were more prevalent in the earlier period (1998-2009). These patterns are consistent with the Canadian Heart Failure (CAN-HF) registry, in which hypertension (68%), diabetes (40%), CKD (35%), and dyslipidemia (33%) were prevalent among inpatients with HF.20 The findings of our study may indicate improved outpatient risk-factor management, differences in cardiometabolic disease patterns in the population, frequent outpatient follow-up, and earlier HF diagnosis in more recent years, resulting in fewer patients presenting with advanced renal or metabolic complications.33 The higher prevalence of diabetes and CKD in earlier years likely reflects more advanced comorbidity at presentation, driven by shared cardiometabolic and cardiorenal pathways that worsen HF prognosis.34, 35, 36 Sleep apnea prevalence in our study was higher in 2010-2021 (11%) compared with 1998-2009 (2%; P < 0.001). Findings from the CAN-HF registry showed that sleep apnea was prevalent in 13% of patients with HFrEF (n = 171 of 1319) and 22% with HFpEF (n = 98 of 447) between 2017-2020.17 This finding likely reflects expanded diagnostic testing and the higher proportion of HFpEF, which is strongly associated with obstructive sleep apnea, obesity, and metabolic syndrome.37

Although ischemic etiology remained the predominant cause of HF overall (62%), its prevalence was lower in 2010-2021 (84% vs 56%). This difference is reflected by lower proportions of coronary artery disease (16% vs 7%), prior MI (10% vs 2%), unstable angina (4% vs 2%), and CABG procedures (19% vs 12%). These findings align with population-level reductions in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) reported in Canada and internationally. In Canada, major cardiovascular events declined by nearly 50% between 1994 and 2014,38 and data from the Framingham Study similarly demonstrated substantial reductions in lifetime ASCVD risk over 6 decades.39 These patterns likely reflect improvements in primary and secondary prevention, increased uptake of GDMT for coronary disease, and advances in acute coronary syndrome management.40,41 Moreover, valvular heart disease, a cause of HF, was more prevalent in 2010-2021 (59% vs 47%), likely reflecting population aging, and improved detection through advanced cardiac imaging.42

Device therapy also differed meaningfully between (2010-2021) vs (1998-2009). CRT-D implantation was higher in 2010-2021 (27% vs 3%), whereas ICD use was lower (47% vs 68%) and pacemaker implantation was lower (14% vs 28%); CRT-P use remained stable (12% vs 11%). These differences reflect broader adoption of resynchronization-based strategies with evolving guideline recommendations.43 However, real-world device implementation remains closely linked to GDMT optimization, and patient-specific physiological factors.15,44 In specialized Canadian HFCs, only 54% of ICD-eligible and 59% of CRT-eligible patients were referred within 6 months of GDMT initiation.44 Referral patterns did not align consistently with predicted benefit, and GDMT was not fully optimized in all cases, highlighting the need to optimize medical therapy and align device referral with individual patient risk–benefit profiles.44 The 2010 Registry to Improve the use of Evidence-Based Heart Failure Therapies in the Outpatient Setting (IMPROVE-HF) reported baseline device use of 50% for ICD/CRT-D and 37% for CRT-P/CRT-D, with structured interventions including GDMT increasing use to 78% and 66% over 24 months. In our study, combined device use was 73% for ICD/CRT-D and 33% for CRT-P/CRT-D, indicating higher ICD adoption but slightly lower CRT uptake compared with that in IMPROVE-HF. These findings highlight variability in baseline device use across settings and suggest that GDMT optimization and quality-improvement interventions may further enhance utilization.45

The main strength of this study is that, to our knowledge, it represents the first large-scale, province-wide multicentre analysis in Quebec using a standardized EMR (via Vision C+) to comprehensively characterize outpatients with HF over more than 2 decades, providing insights into baseline clinical characteristics and demonstrating its feasibility. Although previous studies have utilized Vision C+ data from a single HFC,15,16 the present study expands this work by integrating data from all participating HFCs across the provincial network.

In addition to its clinical findings, Vision C+ offers methodological benefits. The platform standardizes structured data capture across clinics and collects granular patient-level clinical information during routine outpatient care within a centralized database, enabling large-scale real-world research. However, challenges include variable data completeness, reliance on clinician documentation, and earlier free-text entries that may affect data standardization. In addition, major outcomes such as hospitalization and mortality are not uniformly captured, limiting direct assessment of longitudinal patient trajectories. Nevertheless, the availability of granular data presents important opportunities for future research, including longitudinal analyses of care trajectories, HF management patterns, and treatment optimization. Linkage with administrative databases could enable evaluation of clinical outcomes at the population level and long-term HF management strategies in Quebec.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, this cross-sectional retrospective analysis was limited to baseline characteristics at inscription and did not evaluate follow-up data, longitudinal outcomes, or temporal or annual trends. Second, comparisons between the 2 periods are descriptive and were not adjusted for key demographic factors such as age or sex. In addition, the use of only 2-decade-long time intervals limits inference regarding true temporal trends, and the observed differences may reflect changes in patient characteristics, population composition, or referral patterns, rather than true changes in disease epidemiology. Third, major outcome data are not uniformly available in Vison C+, limiting identification of outcome-related trends. Fourth, the study includes only patients managed in the Quebec HFC network, which may limit generalizability to centres without specialised HF services. Fifth, although multiple HFC sites across Quebec were included, findings may not be generalizable to other provinces or healthcare systems with differing HF care models. Sixth, some fields in Vision C+ are free-text, and earlier entries were less standardized; differences in documentation practices over time and across clinics may influence the consistency or interpretation of certain variables. Seventh, incomplete documentation for certain clinical variables may affect comparisons; however, patients with missing inscription years were excluded from period-specific analyses but are included in Supplemental Table S2. Finally, all analyses rely on accurate clinician documentation within the EMR, and the comprehensiveness of the dataset is limited by the information captured at each site.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive overview of HF care across Quebec, demonstrating the feasibility of describing a whole outpatient HF population with the Vision C+ dataset, significant important shifts in HF phenotype distribution, substantial comorbidity burden, and evolving device therapy over 2 decades. These findings highlight the increasing clinical heterogeneity of the HF population and reinforce current guideline recommendations that outpatient management should be individualized and guided by patient phenotype to support effective the GDMT implementation. By describing real-world patterns of HF presentation within a large provincial network, this study offers clinically relevant insights to strengthen diagnostic evaluation, improve coordination of outpatient care, and support provincial health-system planning. Given the central role of GDMT across HF phenotypes, future research should evaluate how treatment patterns align with these evolving phenotypic trends to guide strategies for optimizing outpatient HF management and improving patient outcomes.
