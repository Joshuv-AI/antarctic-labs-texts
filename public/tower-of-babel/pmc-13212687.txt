A bayesian approach to robust modeling of skewed biomedical data
Authors: Mehmet Ali Cengiz, Zeynep Öztürk, Emre Dünder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article proposes a robust Bayesian approach to regression for modelling continuous, strictly positive, and asymmetric biomedical data. The methodology is based on log-symmetric distributions, offering flexibility in capturing skewness and accommodating heavy-tailed behaviour. Robustness is achieved through the careful specification of prior distributions and full posterior inference is conducted using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques. This allows for accurate estimation, comprehensive uncertainty quantification, and resilience to outliers. Model performance is evaluated through both classical and fully Bayesian selection criteria, including AIC, BIC, DIC, ICOMP, WAIC, and PSIS-LOO, providing a thorough assessment of fit and predictive ability. The effectiveness and adaptability of the proposed approach are demonstrated through extensive simulation studies and applications to real biomedical datasets involving physiological and diagnostic measurements. These results highlight the practical value of robust Bayesian regression for modern biomedical research, especially when distributional asymmetry and inferential reliability are of primary concern.

Introduction

Log-symmetric distributions have gained increasing attention in regression modelling since they are able to offer accommodation for data asymmetry without the sacrifice of a flexible parametric form1. Unlike usual symmetric distributions, such as the normal distribution, Log-symmetric models allow for skewness through the transformation of symmetric random variables to the Log scale2. These distributions have been used in wide-ranging applications in economic and financial modelling in which income, asset returns, and insurance claims are among the variables that exhibit significant right-skewed behaviour3.

The family of Log-symmetric distributions is well-known for its popular members such as the Log-normal, Log-Student-t, Log-power-exponential, Log-hyperbolic, and Log-slash distributions4. The distributions have been applied well across various domains. For instance5, applied Log-symmetric models to econometric robust inference, while6 extended these models to accommodate censored data in survival analysis. Moreover, Log-symmetric models have also been applied in financial risk analysis to model stock returns and volatility, demonstrating their versatility in different research fields7.

Despite these advantages, classical estimation techniques of Log-symmetric regression models are mostly rooted in Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE), which is riddled with some pitfalls. MLE-based techniques are prone to being sensitive to outliers and model misspecification and, as such, lead to biased estimates of parameters8. Furthermore, model selection is conventionally performed using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which provide point estimates ignoring model uncertainty9. The Information Complexity Criterion (ICOMP) is used as an alternative to traditional criteria like AIC and BIC10. The Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) is a widely used Bayesian model selection tool that balances model fit and complexity. It is particularly useful in hierarchical and complex Bayesian models estimated via MCMC. DIC combines a measure of model deviance with a penalty for model complexity, offering a generalization of AIC within a Bayesian framework11.

Bayesian inference has emerged as a strong alternative to classical approaches, which provides a probabilistic framework for parameter estimation and model selection. Bayesian regression involves setting prior probabilities over the model parameters, and inference involves conditioning on the priors and data using Bayes’ theorem12. It provides several significant benefits. Prior distributions allow for the incorporation of expert opinion, leading to more reliable estimates in small-sample cases, and posterior distributions estimate uncertainty naturally, unlike non-Bayesian MLE approaches that provide only point estimates. Additionally, Bayesian model selection methods, such as Bayes factors, WAIC, and LOO-CV, also account for model uncertainty, and are hence less prone to model misspecification than more conventional measures such as AIC and BIC13–15.

Among the major computational challenges in Bayesian inference is sampling from posterior distributions, which are normally addressed using numerical approximation methods. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms, such as the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm, Gibbs sampling, and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), have widely been used to estimate posterior distributions in Bayesian regression models16. Most recently, Variational Inference (VI) has been proposed as a faster MCMC option, though it generally compromises on approximation quality for computational speed17.

Bayesian methods have previously been used with good results on asymmetric regression models like quantile regression18, skewed-t regression19 and heteroskedastic models20. However, application of Bayesian inference to Log-symmetric regression models has only been relatively less explored. Few studies have touched on Bayesian solutions for Log-normal and Log-Student-t regression models21, but a general Bayesian approach to Log-symmetric regression remains to be fully developed.

Model selection plays a crucial role in statistical modelling since it determines the most suitable distributional assumption and parameterization. Classic measures of model selection such as AIC, BIC, and Hannan-Quinn Information Criterion (HQIC) rest on likelihood-based penalization strategies that are posterior model probability free22. On the other hand, Bayesian model choice methods such as Bayes factors directly compare the relative evidence of models by computing the ratio of their posterior probabilities23.

Two other more recently established Bayesian model choice procedures, WAIC (Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion) and LOO-CV (Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation), have been promoted as more general alternatives to Bayes factors. WAIC and LOO-CV provide fully Bayesian measures of model fit, averaging over the posterior distribution rather than point estimates15. These methods have gained usage amongst Bayesian regression research, particularly hierarchical modelling and machine learning17.

Despite the advantages of Bayesian model selection, its application with Log-symmetric regression models is not highly developed. As the need for robust models capable of handling skewed data increases, a Bayesian treatment of Log-symmetric regression provides a useful line of future research. This contribution enriches the literature by developing a Bayesian framework for Log-symmetric regression models, featuring prior and posterior inference through Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. In contrast to conventional approaches based on maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and deterministic model selection criteria, the proposed methodology enables full uncertainty quantification and more robust parameter estimation, particularly in the presence of small sample sizes and asymmetric distributions. The research also examines a wide range of Bayesian model selection tools—including AIC, BIC, DIC, ICOMP, WAIC, and PSIS-LOO—to rigorously evaluate model adequacy and predictive performance.

Extensive Monte Carlo simulation studies are conducted to assess the robustness of the Bayesian procedures under varying levels of skewness and sample complexity. Beyond theoretical development and controlled experiments, this study places particular emphasis on real-world biomedical applications, where asymmetry, heavy tails, and outlier-prone structures are inherent in physiological and diagnostic data. The proposed framework is applied to two widely recognized medical datasets involving body composition and glucose-insulin dynamics, thereby demonstrating its practical utility and interdisciplinary relevance. By offering a distributionally flexible and inferentially resilient solution, this research contributes a valuable Bayesian alternative for modern biomedical data analysis and supports the broader integration of statistical modelling in health sciences.

This study makes several notable contributions to the literature on Bayesian regression modelling for asymmetric and heavy-tailed data. Most significantly, a prior specification strategy is proposed that is carefully tailored to the structure of Log-symmetric regression models. These priors are designed to enhance posterior stability, regularize estimation in small-sample settings, and flexibly accommodate varying degrees of skewness and tail behaviour. This innovation addresses key limitations in the current literature, where prior choices are often generic and disconnected from the properties of the underlying distributional family.

Another major contribution lies in the dual demonstration of the framework through comprehensive simulation studies and real medical data applications. By applying the proposed Methodology to two well-known biomedical datasets—one involving body fat percentage in athletes and another involving insulin levels in diabetes diagnostics—the study showcases the practical value of Bayesian Log-symmetric regression in handling real-world challenges such as outliers, right skewness, and covariate-driven variability. The results consistently highlight the superiority of heavy-tailed Log-symmetric models, especially under the prior structures, in delivering robust and interpretable inferences in medical research contexts. These contributions establish a solid foundation for future applications of Bayesian Log-symmetric regression in healthcare, diagnostics, and other interdisciplinary domains requiring reliable modelling of positive and asymmetric outcomes.

Bayesian log-symmetric regression framework

In the proposed Bayesian log-symmetric regression framework, the model is specified on the logarithmic scale of the response variable. Consequently, the location parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{i}=\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}\left({\eta\:}_{i}\right)={x}_{i}^{\mathrm{\:}}\beta\:$$\end{document}represents the conditional median of the response on the original scale rather than the conditional mean. This distinction is important, particularly in biomedical applications, where outcomes are often right-skewed and the median provides a more robust measure of central tendency.

The regression coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document}have a linear interpretation on the log scale. When transformed back to the natural scale, each coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{j}$$\end{document}corresponds to a multiplicative effect on the median of the response variable. Specifically, a one-unit increase in covariate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{ij}$$\end{document}is associated with a change in the median of the response by a factor of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}\left({\beta\:}_{j}\right)$$\end{document}, holding other covariates constant. Thus, the proposed model captures covariate effects in terms of relative (percentage) changes in the median outcome, rather than absolute changes in the mean.

Log-symmetric regression models provide a flexible method for modelling strictly positive and skewed data, both for median modelling and for estimation of skewness (or relative dispersion). In the Bayesian setting, we assign prior distributions to model parameters, and this allows for uncertainty quantification and robust inference.

Although the computational algorithms employed in this study—namely Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No-U-Turn Sampler as implemented in Stan—are well established in the Bayesian literature, the contribution of this work does not lie in proposing new sampling techniques. Rather, the novelty resides in the unified Bayesian formulation and systematic application of these algorithms to a broad class of log-symmetric regression models for strictly positive and heavy-tailed biomedical outcomes.

Specifically, our framework enables full posterior inference, principled uncertainty quantification, and coherent model comparison across multiple members of the log-symmetric family within a single computational pipeline. To our knowledge, such a comprehensive Bayesian treatment—combining robust likelihoods, modern MCMC, and predictive model assessment—has not been previously presented in the biomedical regression literature. This integration allows practitioners to move beyond ad hoc transformations and classical point-estimation approaches toward fully probabilistic and interpretable inference.

In the simulation study, some heavy-tailed log-symmetric models, particularly the log-contaminated-normal, log-slash, and log-hyperbolic specifications, yield posterior estimates for the intercept and dispersion parameters that appear substantially larger than the corresponding values used in the log-normal data-generating process. This behavior should not be interpreted as a numerical instability or estimation failure. Rather, it reflects well-known identifiability characteristics of heavy-tailed and contaminated distributions, especially in small to moderate sample sizes.

The dispersion parameter in these models does not coincide with the log-scale variance parameter of the log-normal distribution used to generate the data. Instead, scale and shape parameters jointly govern variability, tail thickness, and robustness to outliers, making direct numerical comparison with the data-generating log-normal scale inappropriate. In such settings, the posterior may allocate variability to the scale parameter as a compensatory mechanism for heavy tails, leading to apparently inflated estimates without affecting predictive performance or regression inference.

Convergence diagnostics, including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{R}$$\end{document}statistics, effective sample sizes, and trace plots, indicate satisfactory mixing and convergence across all models, suggesting that the observed behavior arises from model structure rather than MCMC artifacts. Similar phenomena have been reported in the literature for heavy-tailed Bayesian models, where scale and shape parameters exhibit weak identifiability under limited sample information.

The relatively large estimation errors observed for the scale parameter and certain additional parameters in Tables 4, 5, 6 and 7 are a known and expected feature of heavy-tailed and robust distributional models, particularly under small to moderate sample sizes. In log-symmetric models with heavy tails, information about variability is shared between scale and shape parameters, leading to increased posterior uncertainty for individual parameters without compromising overall model performance.

These parameters primarily serve to control tail behavior and robustness rather than being direct inferential targets. As a result, larger estimation errors for scale-related parameters do not imply unreliable regression inference or poor predictive performance. Similar behavior has been documented in the robust Bayesian modelling literature, where parameter-level uncertainty increases while predictive accuracy and median-based inference remain stable.

The Log-slash model appears to capture heavy tails but exhibits high variance, which may lead to challenges in parameter interpretability. Generally, the results highlight the importance of selecting a Bayesian regression model appropriate to the type of data, where Log-normal is suitable for nearly symmetric distributions, Log-CN and Log-HB are suitable for modelling skewness, and Log-student-t or Log-slash are suitable for robust estimation with outliers. Heavy-tailed models such as Log-student-t and Log-slash exhibit superior performance in heavy outlier datasets, demonstrating their heavy tail and skewness insensitivity. Log-CN and Log-HB models also provide competitive results when data has moderate skewness, showing their flexibility in dealing with asymmetry and kurtosis. Log PE models exhibit unstable behaviour on different datasets, which reveals sensitivity to tail forms.

In some simulation settings, the reported 95% credible intervals exhibit negative lower bounds for certain parameters. This behaviour arises from the fact that inference is conducted on the transformed (logarithmic) scale, where posterior distributions are approximately symmetric, while parameters are constrained to be positive on the natural scale. As a result, credible intervals constructed on the working scale may extend into negative values without violating parameter support after back-transformation.

While alternative reporting strategies such as presenting transformed credible intervals on the natural scale or evaluating frequentist coverage probabilities could provide additional insight into estimator behaviour, our primary focus is on regression effects and predictive performance rather than strict interval coverage properties. We therefore retain the current presentation for consistency, while acknowledging these alternatives as potential extensions.

Biomedical applications

Australian Institute of Sport dataset

To demonstrate the empirical applicability of Bayesian Log-symmetric regression models, we used the AIS (Australian Institute of Sport) dataset, freely accessible and widely used in statistical modelling. The data includes physioLogical and anthropometric measurements collected from 202 top-level athletes (102 females and 100 males) representing different sports disciplines. Prior to analysis, all the observations with missing data were removed, and continuous predictors were kept in their initial scale. The AIS dataset provides a real-world scenario with right-skewed and strictly positive response variables, thus making it particularly suited for the evaluation of the performance of Log-symmetric regression models under Bayesian estimation.

We trained six Bayesian Log-symmetric regression models with response body fat percentage (Bfat) and explanatory variables subscapular skinfold thickness (SSF) and height (Ht). These were selected due to their clinical significance and presumed nonlinear association with body composition.

To allow regularization and prevent overfitting, weakly informative priors were put on the model parameters. The regression coefficients were given a normal prior with mean 0 and standard deviation 2, allowing for moderate variation but not extreme values. Similarly, the intercept was given a normal prior with mean 0 and standard deviation 10, allowing for greater flexibility in capturing the central tendency of the response variable. The scale parameter was modelled under a Student-t prior with 3 degrees of freedom, shifted at 0 with a scale of 10, which offers robustness to outliers. These priors strike a balance between having some prior knowledge and still having sufficient flexibility when estimating parameters.

Although the simulation study allows the dispersion parameter to depend on covariates in order to demonstrate the full flexibility of the proposed framework, we also explored covariate-dependent dispersion models in the real-data applications. Since these extensions did not lead to substantively different conclusions or improved model performance, we opted for a constant dispersion specification in the applied analyses to preserve parsimony and interpretability.

Bayesian regression models were fitted using the brms package in R for flexible Bayesian modelling with the Stan framework. Models were estimated to the data with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) with four Markov chains of 8000 iterations, with 4000 warmup samples to check convergence. The adaptive step size was calibrated with adapt_delta = 0.99 to reduce divergences, and the tree depth was set to 15 to allow sufficient exploration of the posterior distribution. The priors for the parameters were made uniform across all models to allow for comparison. The calculations were parallelized across four CPU cores to allow for efficiency without loss of accuracy in posterior estimation.

The results in Table 9 also indicate distinctive discrepancies between the Bayesian Log-symmetric regression models’ parameter estimates and their respective uncertainties. One of the most considerable discrepancies is the intercept estimates, where the Log-normal model provides a significantly lower estimate (2.815) compared to the remaining models, whose estimates range from 14.736 to 14.793. This difference means that the Log-normal assumption imposes a restrictive shape on the baseline of the response variable, which can result in a conservative estimate compared to more flexible Log-symmetric models allowing heavier tails to the distribution.

An additional key result is in the effect of the Subscapular Skinfold Thickness (SSF) on the percentage of body fat. In every model, SSF is directly correlated with body fat, in that SSF would increase and percentage of body fat would also increase significantly. However, the magnitude of this effect varies by model. The lowest SSF coefficient is estimated by the Log-normal model (0.013), whereas Log-symmetric models such as Log-PE, Log-slash, and Log-student-t provide substantially higher estimates (0.183). This suggests that Lognormality may be too strong an assumption, which would lead to a fallacious underestimation of SSF’s actual effect. The Log-symmetric models, particularly those that accommodate heavier tails, seem to give a more realistic representation of this relationship.

The height (Ht) effect on percentage body fat is negative across all models, i.e., individuals who are taller have lower body fat percentages, after controlling for SSF. However, similar to the SSF effect, the Log-normal model provides a much lower effect size (−0.007), while the other models, in particular the Log-symmetric models, estimate the effect as being in the neighborhood of −0.077 to −0.078. The 95% credible intervals (CI) for height are entirely negative in all models, in favor of the strength of this relationship. Despite the range in effect size, all models are in agreement that height significantly negatively affects percentage body fat.

Uncertainty in parameter estimates, expressed as standard deviations (SD) and credible intervals (CIs), also varies across models. The Log-normal model is the one with the lowest SD values, expressing lower uncertainty in its parameter estimates. This could, however, be a sign of an over-confident model due to its strict normality assumption. Conversely, by contrast, the Log-symmetric models, viz., Log-CN, Log-HB, and Log-Slash, provide higher values of SD, indicating greater flexibility to model data variability. Broader credible intervals in these models signify more realistic quantification of uncertainty, which is especially true for skewed and heavy-tailed distributions.

In general, the findings indicate that Log-symmetric regression models, namely Log-PE, Log-slash, and Log-student-t, provide more satisfactory estimates compared to the Log-normal model, which appears to underestimate the intercept and the effects of the predictors. That Log-symmetric models have the ability to accommodate extreme values, and distributional skewness renders them a preferable option for body fat percentage modelling, where non-normality and skewness are the rule. Of these, the Log-student-t model emerges as a strong contender, as it accounts for both heavy tails and robustness to outliers, leading to more plausible parameter estimates with appropriate uncertainty quantification.

Though Log-Student-t has a slightly higher AIC and BIC than Log-HB, it is yet to demonstrate strong model performance, particularly with respect to DIC and ICOMP, meaning greater flexibility to model skewness and heavy tails. Log-CN, Log-PE, and Log-slash models are likewise found to be competitive with reasonable information criterion values and are hence viable depending on the purpose.

The Pima Indians diabetes dataset

To further illustrate the practical applicability of the Bayesian Log-symmetric regression framework, we employed the Pima Indians Diabetes dataset, a widely recognized medical dataset available in the UCI Machine Learning Repository. The dataset consists of diagnostic measurements for 768 female patients of Pima Indian heritage, aged 21 years or older. The primary objective of this dataset is to predict the onset of type 2 diabetes based on various biomedical indicators.

For our purposes, we modeled serum insulin concentration (measured in µU/mL) as the response variable of interest.

The insulin variable is known to exhibit substantial variability and pronounced right-skewed, heavy-tailed behavior, a characteristic that has been widely documented in studies using the Pima Indians Diabetes dataset. This variable is strictly positive and exhibits significant right-skewness, making it an appropriate candidate for Log-symmetric modelling. Previous studies have also indicated that insulin values are prone to high variability and heavy-tailed behaviour, which further supports the need for a flexible and robust modelling approach.

We selected Body Mass Index (BMI) and Glucose concentration as predictor variables based on their established physioLogical relationships with insulin sensitivity and beta-cell function. These variables were retained on their original scale, and rows with zero or missing insulin values were removed to ensure distributional integrity. The model was specified using a Bayesian Log-symmetric regression framework. Priors on regression coefficients were set as normal distributions centered at zero with a moderately informative standard deviation. The scale parameter was modelled using a student-t prior to account for potential outliers, and the shape parameter for the kernel function was drawn from a gamma prior distribution.

Posterior inference was performed using the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) implemented via the brms package in R, which leverages the Stan probabilistic programming language. We ran four MCMC chains with 8000 iterations each (4000 warm-up, 4000 post-warm-up), yielding a total of 16,000 posterior draws. Convergence diagnostics such as R̂ values, effective sample size (ESS), and trace plots indicated successful convergence across all parameters.

The results confirmed that both BMI and Glucose are significant predictors of insulin concentration, with credible intervals excluding zero in all models considered. Among the models compared, the Bayesian Log-Student-t and Log-slash regression exhibited the best fit in terms of predictive accuracy and uncertainty quantification. These models also provided wider credible intervals, appropriately reflecting the uncertainty inherent in biomedical data.

This analysis underscores the flexibility and robustness of Log-symmetric regression models for skewed and heavy-tailed data. It highlights the potential of Bayesian inference to improve parameter estimation in clinical research settings, especially when traditional models fail to account for distributional asymmetry and outlier-prone observations.

To verify the distributional suitability of the response variable, we conducted an exploratory analysis of the Insulin values. As shown in Fig. 3, the distribution of raw insulin measurements is highly right-skewed, which is characteristic of endocrine biomarkers in population-level data. Upon Logarithmic transformation, the distribution becomes approximately symmetric, confirming the appropriateness of Log-symmetric regression techniques. This transformation preserves the strictly positive domain of the response while facilitating more stable and interpretable model estimates. Therefore, we proceeded to fit various Bayesian Log-symmetric models to assess the robustness and accuracy of parameter estimates under asymmetric distributional assumptions.

Results and conclusions

The simulated dataset experiment suggests a broad overview of how Bayesian model selection performs across different Log-symmetric data-generating processes. The key point is that how well a model works depends heavily on the distribution of the data, emphasizing the importance of choosing a model that aligns well with the underlying data structure to draw accurate conclusions.

For instance, when the data follows a Log-normal distribution, the Bayesian Log-normal model consistently produces the lowest AIC, BIC, DIC, and ICOMP values. This suggests it’s well-suited for data that is nearly symmetric and light tailed. However, when the data has mild skewness or slightly heavier tails, models like Log-HB and Log-CN tend to outperform the Log-normal model. Thanks to their ability to handle asymmetry and moderate kurtosis, they are especially effective for financial, biomedical, and economic datasets. In cases of mild skewness, these models are usually the preferred choice.

The use of multiple model selection criteria in this study reflects the fact that different measures emphasize different aspects of model performance. Classical criteria such as AIC and BIC primarily penalize model complexity and are grounded in asymptotic approximations, whereas Bayesian criteria such as DIC, WAIC, and PSIS-LOO focus more directly on posterior predictive accuracy.

In some settings, these criteria may lead to slightly different rankings of competing models, particularly when differences are small. Such discrepancies should not be interpreted as contradictory conclusions, but rather as indications that several models provide comparable explanatory or predictive performance. In the present study, while minor differences across criteria were observed, they consistently identified a small subset of log-symmetric models as top-performing. For inference and interpretation, greater emphasis was placed on fully Bayesian predictive measures (WAIC and PSIS-LOO), which are more aligned with the probabilistic framework adopted in this work.

For data with heavy tails, such as that from Log-student-t or Log-slash distributions, the corresponding models show superior performance. The Log-slash model excels at identifying extreme outliers, while the Log-student-t model is particularly adept at managing long-tailed distributions with moderate outliers. These results underscore the value of using distribution-specific models like Log-student-t or Log-slash to improve accuracy and better capture uncertainty. The Log-PE model, on the other hand, shows inconsistent results. While it can accommodate heavy tails, it doesn’t always deliver the best fit. This points to the importance of selecting models carefully, especially when the data departs from normality.

Overall, the findings confirm that there’s no universal Bayesian Log-symmetric model that fits every type of data. Log-normal works best for symmetric distributions, Log-HB and Log-CN are better for skewed data, and Log-student-t or Log-slash are the top picks for handling outliers and heavy tails. This all reinforces the importance of having flexible model choices to ensure reliable and meaningful statistical inference.

In applying these models to the AIS dataset to analyse body fat percentage, the study shows how crucial it is to choose the right distribution. Log-symmetric models outperform the traditional Log-normal model, which tends to underestimate both intercept and predictor effects. Across all models, SSF is positively linked to body fat percentage, while height shows a negative relationship. Among the models, Log-PE, Log-slash, and Log-student-t provide stronger estimates, with larger SSF effects and wider credible intervals that more accurately reflect uncertainty. The Log-normal model, however, yields narrower intervals and smaller estimates—suggesting it may give an overly confident picture and struggle with non-normal data.

These results make a strong case for using Log-symmetric models, especially the Log-student-t model, in bioLogical research due to its robustness and ability to handle heavy tails. The study also highlights the importance of choosing the right distributional assumptions in Bayesian regression and encourages further research to broaden the use of these models. In particular, the Log-HB and Log-student-t models strike a good balance between model fit and simplicity, making them ideal for modeling body fat percentage in the AIS dataset.

It was demonstrated that Bayesian model selection criteria such as WAIC and DIC are effective in identifying the correct data-generating distribution when applied to simulated Log-symmetric data. Models perform best when the assumed distribution matches the underlying distribution of the data, reinforcing the value of flexible model selection frameworks in Bayesian inference. Table 11 highlights the impact of distributional assumptions on posterior estimates. While all models identified BMI and Glucose as significant predictors of insulin levels, heavy-tailed models like Log-Student-t and Log-Hyperbolic provided more robust estimates with wider credible intervals, suggesting better capacity to capture outliers and data asymmetry.

While the empirical results demonstrate strong performance of the proposed Bayesian log-symmetric regression framework for the AIS and Pima Indians Diabetes datasets, the underlying methodology is broadly applicable to biomedical outcomes that are strictly positive, right-skewed, and potentially heavy-tailed. Examples include biomarkers, hormone levels, survival-related measures, and metabolic indicators, where median-based inference and robustness to extreme values are often desirable.

From a computational perspective, heavy-tailed log-symmetric models are more demanding than their log-normal counterparts due to additional shape parameters and increased posterior complexity. However, with modern Hamiltonian Monte Carlo implementations such as Stan, the computational cost remains manageable for moderate sample sizes, and convergence can be reliably assessed using standard diagnostics.

Regarding sample size considerations, stable inference for regression effects is typically achievable with moderate sample sizes, whereas precise estimation of tail-related parameters may require larger datasets. In practice, the proposed framework is particularly well suited for small-to-moderate biomedical studies where robustness and interpretability are prioritized over precise tail parameter estimation. These considerations provide practical guidance for applying the method beyond the datasets analyzed in this study.

In conclusion, these findings confirm that Bayesian Log-symmetric regression models offer a robust and flexible framework for modelling strictly positive and skewed outcomes. Compared to conventional Log-normal models, the heavy-tailed alternatives—particularly Log-Hyperbolic and Log-Student-t—demonstrate superior predictive performance and more realistic uncertainty quantification. These results support the use of Bayesian approaches in biomedical and physioLogical studies where data asymmetry and extreme values are common. The proposed Methodology, through both simulation and real data analysis, contributes to the growing literature on robust Bayesian regression and opens further directions for distributionally adaptive modelling.
