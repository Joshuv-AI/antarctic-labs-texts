Abstract
The synergy of fluid antenna systems (FAS) and reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) promises robust vehicle-to-everything (V2X) links, yet most analyses invoke the central limit theorem (CLT) and thus fail to capture practical, finite-size deployments. This paper develops a realistic and tractable framework for FAS–RIS systems with a finite number of elements. We approximate the cascaded end-to-end gain via a Gamma distribution using moment matching, model spatial dependence across FAS ports with a block-correlation structure, and derive an accurate, closed-form approximation for the outage probability using Gauss–Chebyshev quadrature. Extensive simulations show that the proposed Gamma-based analysis markedly outperforms CLT-based baselines—especially for small numbers of RIS elements and ports—while converging to them as the array grows. The results provide actionable guidance for V2X design and dimensioning under practical constraints on RIS size, FAS aperture, and channel correlation.

Introduction

To meet the increasing demand for high data rates in vehicular networks, particularly in vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communications that enable vehicles to exchange information with surrounding infrastructure, pedestrians, and other vehicles, the fluid antenna system (FAS) has emerged as one of the crucial techniques for the next generation of wireless communications. To cope with this challenge, the reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) technique has been proposed. This idealized assumption creates a critical gap between theoretical predictions and the performance of realistic V2X systems, where physical and cost constraints often dictate the use of small-scale RIS. For instance, to ensure cost-effective, wide-scale coverage in V2X deployments, RISs are likely to be deployed on existing urban infrastructure like lampposts or building facades, where the number of possible elements is inherently finite. In these practical scenarios, CLT-based approximations can lead to significant errors, hindering effective system design. The primary goal of this paper is to bridge this gap, unlocking a more realistic and accurate framework for performance analysis. To this end, this paper makes the following key contributions:

•

Gamma-based Analytical Framework:
We propose a novel analytical framework based on the Gamma distribution to characterize the end-to-end channel of a downlink FAS-RIS aided vehicular network. This approach overcomes the limitations of the conventional CLT-based method, especially in practical scenarios with a limited number of RIS elements.

•

Closed-form Outage Probability Expression:
We derive a new closed-form approximate expression for the outage probability of the considered system. The derivation leverages the Gamma distribution approximation in conjunction with a block-correlation channel model, providing a tractable way to evaluate system performance.

•

Performance Validation and Insights:
We provide extensive numerical results to validate the accuracy of our proposed analysis. The results confirm that our Gamma-based approach significantly outperforms the CLT-based benchmark, particularly when the number of RIS elements is small (e.g., fewer than 30), offering valuable insights for the practical design of FAS-RIS systems in vehicular networks.

II-A

Channel and Signal Model

The end-to-end communication from the BS to the
\displaystyle k
-th port of the FAS comprises two hops: the BS-RIS link and the RIS-vehicle link.

II-A
1
Channel Model

The channel coefficient of the link between the BS and the
\displaystyle m
-th RIS element is denoted by
\displaystyle h_{m}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\epsilon_{1})
. The channel coefficient between the
\displaystyle m
-th RIS element and the
\displaystyle k
-th FAS port is denoted by
\displaystyle v_{m,k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\epsilon_{2})
. Consequently, the channel gains
\displaystyle|h_{m}|
and
\displaystyle|v_{m,k}|
follow a Rayleigh distribution. Due to the continuous nature of the FAS, the channels to its different ports are spatially correlated. We adopt the widely-used Jakes’ model to characterize this correlation. The correlation coefficient between the channels to ports
\displaystyle k
and
\displaystyle l
, for any RIS element
\displaystyle m
, is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{k,l}=J_{0}\bigg(\frac{2\pi|k-l|W}{K-1}\bigg),

(1)

where
\displaystyle J_{0}(\cdot)
is the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind. The channel vector from the
\displaystyle m
-th RIS element to all
\displaystyle K
FAS ports,
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{m}=[v_{m,1},\dots,v_{m,K}]^{T}
, is a complex Gaussian vector with zero mean and a covariance matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times K}
. Given the distance-dependent nature of Jakes’ model,
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}
is a Toeplitz matrix with
\displaystyle[\mathbf{\Sigma}]_{k,l}=g_{k,l}
.

II-A
2
Signal Model

Let
\displaystyle s
be the transmitted symbol from the BS with average power
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[|s|^{2}]=1
, and let
\displaystyle P_{S}
be the transmit power. The signal received at the
\displaystyle k
-th FAS port is the superposition of the signals reflected from all
\displaystyle M
RIS elements, which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\displaystyle y_{k}=\sqrt{P_{S}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}h_{m}v_{m,k}e^{i\theta_{m}}\right)s+n_{k},

(2)

where
\displaystyle n_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
is the additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) at the receiver, and
\displaystyle\theta_{m}
is the phase shift applied by the
\displaystyle m
-th RIS element. To maximize the received signal strength, the RIS is configured to perform optimal co-phasing by aligning the phases of all cascaded paths. This is achieved by setting the phase shift to
\displaystyle\theta_{m}=-\arg(h_{m}v_{m,k})
. Selecting
\displaystyle\theta_{m}=-\arg(h_{m})
aligns the phases of the scattered components, converting the complex-valued sum into a coherent accumulation of magnitudes and thus maximizing the post-RIS gain. With this optimal configuration, the effective channel gain for the
\displaystyle k
-th port becomes the coherent sum of the individual path gains:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi_{k}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|.

(3)

The FAS then selects the port with the maximum channel gain to decode the signal. The resulting channel gain is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi^{*}=\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\psi_{k}.

(4)

II-B

Channel Statistics and Correlation

To analyze the system’s outage performance, we first characterize the statistical properties of the effective channel gain
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
.

II-B
1
Mean and Variance

Since
\displaystyle|h_{m}|
and
\displaystyle|v_{m,k}|
are independent Rayleigh-distributed random variables (RVs), the expectation of their product is
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|]=\mathbf{E}[|h_{m}|]\mathbf{E}[|v_{m,k}|]=(\pi/4)\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}}
. The variance can be computed as
\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}(|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|)=\mathbf{E}[|h_{m}|^{2}|v_{m,k}|^{2}]-(\mathbf{E}[|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|])^{2}=\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}(1-\pi^{2}/16)
.
As
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
is the sum of
\displaystyle M
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) RVs
\displaystyle|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|
, its mean
\displaystyle E_{\psi}
and variance
\displaystyle V_{\psi}
are given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{\psi}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=M\mathbf{E}[|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|]=\frac{M\pi\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}}}{4},

(5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{\psi}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=M\mathbf{Var}(|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|)=M\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\left(1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}\right).

(6)

II-B
2
Correlation Coefficient

The effective channels
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
and
\displaystyle\psi_{l}
for different ports
\displaystyle k
and
\displaystyle l
are correlated due to the spatial correlation of the RIS-vehicle channels. The Pearson correlation coefficient between them, denoted by
\displaystyle\eta(g_{k,l})
, is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\eta(g_{k,l})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{E}[\psi_{k}\psi_{l}]-E_{\psi}^{2}}{V_{\psi}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{M\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{E}[|v_{m,k}||v_{m,l}|]-E_{\psi}^{2}/M}{V_{\psi}},

(7)

where the derivation relies on the independence of channels across different RIS elements. The term
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[|v_{m,k}||v_{m,l}|]
is the expected value of the product of two correlated Rayleigh RVs, given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[|v_{m,k}||v_{m,l}|]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}xyf_{|v_{m,k}|,|v_{m,l}|}(x,y)dxdy.

(8)

Here,
\displaystyle f_{|v_{m,k}|,|v_{m,l}|}(x,y)
is their joint probability density function (PDF), which is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{|v_{m,k}|,|v_{m,l}|}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{4xy}{\epsilon_{2}^{2}(1-|g_{k,l}|^{2})}e^{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{\epsilon_{2}(1-|g_{k,l}|^{2})}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times I_{0}\bigg(\frac{2|g_{k,l}|xy}{\epsilon_{2}(1-|g_{k,l}|^{2})}\bigg),

(9)

where
\displaystyle I_{0}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind. (
8
) integrates the element-wise correlation kernel over the joint distribution of the Rayleigh magnitudes; evaluating this integral with the PDF in (
II-B2
) yields the closed-form expectation
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[|v_{m,k}||v_{m,l}|]
, which is then substituted into (
II-B2
) to obtain the final correlation coefficient
\displaystyle\eta(g_{k,l})
. This integral is typically computed via numerical methods.
Based on the Pearson correlation coefficient, the correlation coefficient matrix of
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}=[\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{K}]
is given as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbf{topelitz}\big(\eta(g_{1,1}),\eta(g_{1,2}),\cdots,\eta(g_{1,K})\big).

(10)

The correlation matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}
is Toeplitz, constructed from the spatial correlation vector
\displaystyle[\eta(g_{1,1}),\eta(g_{1,2}),\ldots,\eta(g_{1,K})]
, so that
\displaystyle[\mathbf{\Omega}]_{i,j}=\eta(g_{|i-j|+1,1})
for
\displaystyle i,j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.

III
Outage Performance Analysis

The outage probability is a key metric for evaluating the reliability of the communication link. It is defined as the probability that the received signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) falls below a predefined threshold, which corresponds to a target data rate
\displaystyle R
. The instantaneous SNR at the output of the FAS is
\displaystyle\gamma=P_{S}(\psi^{*})^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}
. Thus, the outage probability is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{\rm{out}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\log_{2}(1+\gamma)\leq R\right)=\Pr\left(\psi^{*}\leq\Lambda_{\rm{th}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=F_{\psi^{*}}(\Lambda_{\rm{th}}),

(11)

where
\displaystyle F_{\psi^{*}}(\cdot)
is the CDF of the selected channel gain
\displaystyle\psi^{*}
, and
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm{th}}=\sqrt{(2^{R}-1)\sigma_{n}^{2}/P_{S}}
. Since
\displaystyle\gamma=P_{S}(\psi^{*})^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}
, this threshold enforces the SNR condition
\displaystyle\gamma\geq 2^{R/W}-1
, meaning the cascaded FAS–RIS channel must provide a gain of at least
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm th}
to sustain the target rate
\displaystyle R
over bandwidth
\displaystyle W
.

However, deriving a closed-form expression for
\displaystyle F_{\psi^{*}}(y)
is intractable. This difficulty stems from two main challenges: (1) the complicated distribution of each channel gain
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
, which is a sum of products of Rayleigh RVs, and (2) the complex correlation structure among the channel gains
\displaystyle\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. To overcome these challenges and unlock a tractable analysis, we propose a two-pronged approximation strategy. First, we approximate the marginal distribution of each
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
using a Gamma distribution. Second, we employ a block-correlation model to simplify the joint distribution of the channel vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}
.

III-A

Gamma Approximation for the Marginal Distribution

The channel gain
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
is a sum of
\displaystyle M
i.i.d. RVs. According to the CLT, its distribution approaches a Gaussian distribution as
\displaystyle M\to\infty
. However, as motivated in Section I, practical vehicular networks often feature a small or moderate number of RIS elements, where the CLT approximation becomes inaccurate.

To develop a more accurate model, we approximate the distribution of
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
with a Gamma distribution, which is well-suited for modeling sums of non-negative RVs, even for a small
\displaystyle M
. By applying the moment matching method, we match the first two moments of
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
(given in (
5
) and (
6
)) with those of a Gamma distribution
\displaystyle\mathbf{Ga}(a_{k},b_{k})
. Moment matching preserves the exact first- and second-order statistics of the aggregated gain while keeping the Gamma form tractable; empirical tests confirm that higher-order discrepancies remain negligible across the simulated FAS configurations. The shape parameter
\displaystyle a_{k}
and scale parameter
\displaystyle b_{k}
are found to be

\displaystyle\displaystyle a_{k}=\frac{E_{\psi}^{2}}{V_{\psi}}=\frac{M\pi^{2}}{16(1-\pi^{2}/16)},\quad b_{k}=\frac{V_{\psi}}{E_{\psi}}=\frac{4(1-\pi^{2}/16)\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}}}{\pi}.

(12)

Consequently, the CDF of
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
can be accurately approximated by

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\psi_{k}}(x)\approx\frac{1}{\Gamma(a_{k})}\gamma\left(a_{k},\frac{x}{b_{k}}\right),

(13)

where
\displaystyle\gamma(\cdot,\cdot)
is the lower incomplete gamma function, defined as
\displaystyle\gamma(a,x)=\int_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}e^{-t}dt
.

Remark 1

The effectiveness of the Gamma approximation for a finite number of RIS elements
\displaystyle M
can be understood by examining the shape parameter
\displaystyle a_{k}
. From (
12
), we see that
\displaystyle a_{k}
is directly proportional to
\displaystyle M
. For a Gamma distribution to resemble a Gaussian distribution (where the CLT holds), its shape parameter must be large. When
\displaystyle M
is small, as is common in practical vehicular deployments,
\displaystyle a_{k}
is also small. In this regime, the Gamma distribution exhibits significant skewness and is fundamentally different from a symmetric Gaussian distribution. For instance, with
\displaystyle M=5
,
\displaystyle a_{k}\approx 5.7
, resulting in a PDF that is far from bell-shaped. This inherent structural mismatch is why the CLT-based approach leads to significant errors for small
\displaystyle M
. Conversely, our Gamma-based framework, by construction, accurately captures the distribution’s shape even for small
\displaystyle M
, thus unlocking a realistic performance analysis and directly addressing the key contribution highlighted in Section I. In addition, approximating
\displaystyle\psi_{k}
with a Gaussian would allocate non-zero probability to negative channel gains and underestimate the outage tail, whereas the Gamma family preserves the support on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
, matches the first two cumulants via moment matching, and provides closed-form CDF/MGF expressions that we later leverage in the outage derivations. Compared with lognormal or Weibull alternatives, the Gamma model aligns with additive scattering physics (sums of Rayleigh products) and offers closed-form Laplace transforms, simplifying both outage and ergodic rate analysis. As
\displaystyle M
grows large,
\displaystyle a_{k}
also becomes large, and the Gamma distribution itself converges to a Gaussian distribution, explaining why both methods yield similar results in the asymptotic regime.

III-B

Block-Correlation Model for Tractable Dependencies

While the Gamma approximation accurately models the marginal distribution, the correlation among the
\displaystyle\{\psi_{k}\}
still makes the analysis of their maximum,
\displaystyle\psi^{*}
, intractable. To address this, we simplify the correlation structure using the block-correlation model proposed in
[
17
]
.

This model approximates the true, complex correlation matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}
with a structured matrix
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}
that is more amenable to analysis. Specifically, the set of
\displaystyle K
correlated RVs
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}
is partitioned into
\displaystyle D
groups. Within each group
\displaystyle d
, the
\displaystyle L_{d}
RVs are equi-correlated with coefficient
\displaystyle\rho_{d}=\eta(\mu_{d})
. Across any two different groups, the RVs are also equi-correlated, but with a different coefficient
\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\eta(0)
. This leads to a block-structured correlation matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{\Omega}}
for the approximated channel vector
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}=[\hat{\psi}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\psi}_{K}]^{T}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathbf{B}_{1}&\mathbf{C}&\cdots&\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{C}&\mathbf{B}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{C}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{C}&\mathbf{C}&\cdots&\mathbf{B}_{D}\\
\end{array}\right).

Here,
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d}\times L_{d}}
is a matrix with ones on the diagonal and
\displaystyle\rho_{d}
elsewhere, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d}\times L_{j}}
is a matrix with all entries equal to
\displaystyle\rho_{0}
. The parameters
\displaystyle L_{d}
and
\displaystyle\mu_{d}
are determined by minimizing the distance between the eigenvalues of the true and approximate covariance matrices, as detailed in
[
17
]
.

A key benefit of this structure is that the RVs
\displaystyle\{\hat{\psi}_{l,d}\}
within this model can be constructed from a set of common, independent Gamma RVs. Specifically, each
\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{l,d}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{l,d}\approx r_{l,d}+w_{d}+t,

(18)

where
\displaystyle r_{l,d}\sim\mathbf{Ga}((1-\rho_{d})a_{k},b_{k})
,
\displaystyle w_{d}\sim\mathbf{Ga}((\rho_{d}-\rho_{0})a_{k},b_{k})
, and
\displaystyle t\sim\mathbf{Ga}(\rho_{0}a_{k},b_{k})
are all mutually independent Gamma-distributed RVs. This decomposition is crucial for deriving the CDF of the maximum channel gain.

III-C

Derivation of the Approximate Outage Probability

With the Gamma approximation for the marginals and the block-correlation model for the dependencies, we can now derive a tractable expression for the outage probability. The CDF of the selected channel gain
\displaystyle\hat{\psi}^{*}=\max_{d,l}\hat{\psi}_{l,d}
can be expressed by conditioning on the common RV
\displaystyle t
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\hat{\psi}^{*}}(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{t}\bigg[\Pr\left(\max_{d,l}(r_{l,d}+w_{d})\leq y-t\ |\ t\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{t}\bigg[\prod_{d=1}^{D}F_{\hat{\psi}_{d}^{*}|t}(y)\bigg],

(19)

where
\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{d}^{*}=\max_{1\leq l\leq L_{d}}\hat{\psi}_{l,d}
, and the product form in the second step is due to the independence among blocks after conditioning on
\displaystyle t
.

The conditional CDF
\displaystyle F_{\hat{\psi}_{d}^{*}|t}(y)
can be found by further conditioning on
\displaystyle w_{d}
. For the case
\displaystyle\rho_{d}<1
, this leads to

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\hat{\psi}_{d}^{*}|t}^{2}(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{y-t}\left[\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha_{r})}\gamma\left(\alpha_{r},\frac{y-t-x}{b_{k}}\right)\right]^{L_{d}}f_{w_{d}}(x)dx,

(20)

where
\displaystyle\alpha_{r}=(1-\rho_{d})a_{k}
and
\displaystyle f_{w_{d}}(x)
is the PDF of the Gamma RV
\displaystyle w_{d}
. For the special case
\displaystyle\rho_{d}=1
, the expression simplifies to

\displaystyle F_{\hat{\psi}_{d}^{*}|t}^{1}(y)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha_{w})}\gamma(\alpha_{w},\frac{y-t}{b_{k}})
, with
\displaystyle\alpha_{w}=(1-\rho_{0})a_{k}
.

The integral in the
\displaystyle\rho_{d}<1
case does not have a simple closed form. To obtain a final expression, we resort to the Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature method for numerical integration. This allows us to approximate the integral as a weighted sum. Applying this method to both the inner integral over
\displaystyle w_{d}
and the outer expectation over
\displaystyle t
yields the final closed-form approximate expression for the outage probability:

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{\rm out}=F_{\hat{\psi}^{*}}(\Lambda_{\rm th})\approx\frac{\Lambda_{\rm th}\pi}{2(U_{j}+1)}\sum_{j=0}^{U_{j}}\sqrt{1-\chi_{j}^{2}}\prod_{d=1}^{D}F_{\hat{\psi}_{d}^{*}|t=\delta_{j}}(\Lambda_{\rm th}),

(21)

where
\displaystyle U_{j}
is a parameter controlling the accuracy of the quadrature, and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\chi_{j}=\cos\Big(\frac{(2j+1)\pi}{2U_{j}+2}\Big),\quad\delta_{j}=\frac{\Lambda_{\rm th}(\chi_{j}+1)}{2}.

(22)

The term
\displaystyle F_{\hat{\psi}_{d}^{*}|t=\delta_{j}}(\Lambda_{\rm th})
is also computed using a similar Gauss-Chebyshev approximation. This final expression, while seemingly complex, is easily computable and provides the accurate outage performance analysis that was previously elusive for FAS-RIS systems with a finite number of elements.

V
Conclusions

We developed a Gamma-based outage analysis for FAS–RIS systems with finite
\displaystyle M
and derived a tractable expression via block-correlation modeling and Gauss–Chebyshev quadrature. Numerical results show that the proposed analysis agrees closely with simulations and is markedly more accurate than CLT-based baselines, especially for small
\displaystyle K
and
\displaystyle M
(e.g.,
\displaystyle M<30
), which is the practically relevant regime for V2X deployments. Looking ahead, the framework can be extended to scenarios with line-of-sight components—including weak direct links and Rician fading—by incorporating the deterministic component via moment-matching techniques, yielding either a shifted-Gamma or a mixture model while preserving analytical tractability. Additionally, future work will incorporate imperfect CSI by propagating estimation error variances into the matched-Gamma parameters, allowing reliability margins to be quantified for practical pilot-limited V2X links.
