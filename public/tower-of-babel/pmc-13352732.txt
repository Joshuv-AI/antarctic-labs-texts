CB2R agonism protects intestinal epithelium through β-catenin/HoxA10 loop in radiation injury
Authors: Qianyi Yao, Ying Tian, Yi Yuan, Yongqing Cai, Qunfang Yang, Lanfang Zhang, Yunong Li, Tanjun Wei, Yi Wang, Qin Ouyang, Haigang Zhang, Xiaoli Li, Tao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) represents a significant dose-limiting complication of radiotherapy, characterized by substantial loss of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs). While the activation of cannabinoid receptor 2 (CB2R) is protective in immune‑mediated colitis, the intrinsic role of CB2R in IECs and its potential therapeutic relevance in RIII have not been defined.MethodsAn RIII mouse model was established in wild-type and CB2R−/− mice. Small-molecule CB2R agonists were screened for radioprotective efficacy, followed by pharmacological and siRNA-based interrogation of CB2R signaling in intestinal epithelial cell lines and primary mouse intestinal epithelial cells. RNA sequencing and bioinformatics were combined with permeability assays, immunofluorescence, electron microscopy, and molecular analyses of oxidative stress and ferroptosis to elucidate underlying mechanisms.ResultsIrradiation induced CB2R expression in the intestinal epithelium, and genetic ablation of CB2R markedly aggravated RIII. A focused pharmacological screen identified a recently synthesized dual‑target compound, CB2R/FAAH modulator‑2 (CF‑2), which combines CB2R agonistic activity with fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) inhibition and significantly mitigated radiation‑induced colonic injury while exerting protective effects on the small intestine and spleen. Mechanistically, CB2R activation attenuated irradiation‑induced ferroptosis and preserved intestinal epithelial integrity. Integrative transcriptomic analyses identified Homeobox A10 (HoxA10) as a critical epithelial‑enriched transcriptional amplifier that reinforced CB2R/β‑catenin signaling through a positive‑feedback loop, thereby enhancing CB2R‑mediated epithelial protection.ConclusionsThis study defines an epithelial‑intrinsic CB2R signaling axis in RIII, linking ferroptosis suppression to preservation of intestinal barrier integrity. CF‑2 is positioned as a promising mechanism‑based candidate for radioprotection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08268-x.

Introduction

Radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) represents a significant dose-limiting complication of radiotherapy, primarily resulting from direct damage to the intestinal epithelium [1]. Intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), which form the main barrier against the luminal environment, are particularly susceptible to ionizing radiation due to their rapid turnover. Damage to IECs from radiation undermines barrier integrity and critically shapes disease severity [2]. Current management strategies are predominantly supportive and do not adequately address the epithelial mechanisms crucial for tissue protection and repair [3–8]. Thus, identifying potential targets to directly protect IECs is vital for developing mechanism-based RIII interventions.

The endocannabinoid system (ECS) has emerged as an important regulator of gastrointestinal homeostasis, exerting protective effects on epithelial barrier integrity and tissue adaptation under stress conditions [9]. Extensive experimental and clinical evidence indicates that ECS signaling mitigates intestinal injury in a variety of pathological settings, particularly inflammatory bowel diseases [10]. Endocannabinoids involving anandamide and 2-arachidonoyl glycerol can bind to two G protein–coupled seven transmembrane cannabinoid receptors, CB1R and CB2R [11]. In contrast to CB1R, which is predominantly expressed in the central and peripheral nervous systems, CB2R has garnered significant attention due to its predominant expression on immune cells, such as macrophages, neutrophils, and various lymphocyte subsets, as well as its well-established role in immune regulation [12]. In numerous experimental models of colitis, pharmacological activation of CB2R has been shown to confer intestinal protection primarily by suppressing immune cell activation and mitigating inflammation-related tissue damage [13, 14].

However, a fundamental paradox arises from the apparent discrepancy between the pathophysiology of RIII and the established protective mechanisms of CB2R against immune‑driven colitis models. Due to the inability to discriminate between rapidly proliferating neoplastic cells and similarly fast-dividing basal epithelial compartments, irradiation causes a profound devastation of high radiosensitivity immune cells [15]. This necessitates the question: Within the immunosuppressive intestinal environment after irradiation, the canonical immunomodulatory effects of CB2R activation may be attenuated, raising the possibility that CB2R signaling exerts context‑dependent functions and engages alternative cellular targets in the irradiated intestine. Beyond immune cells, accumulating evidence indicates that CB2R is also expressed in epithelial cells across multiple organs, including the kidney [16], liver [17], lung [18] and gastrointestinal tract [19], particularly under pathological conditions. Nevertheless, the functional role of epithelial CB2R remains inconsistent and controversial. In kidney tubular epithelium, CB2R signaling has been reported to exert divergent effects, ranging from promotion of fibrosis to protection against acute injury [20, 21], while in the lung, CB2R activation has been implicated in the suppression of fibrotic remodeling [18, 22]. These context‑dependent and sometimes opposing observations highlight the complexity of CB2R signaling in epithelial compartments. Within the gastrointestinal tract, the expression of cannabinoid receptors in the intestinal epithelium is subject to variation based on species, anatomical location, and pathophysiological state. Under homeostatic conditions, the expression of the CB2 receptor in IECs is typically low. However, this expression is upregulated during acute inflammatory injury and subsequently decreases in cases of chronic inflammation, as evidenced in human Crohn’s disease [23]. Despite this emerging evidence in immune‑driven intestinal disorders, the expression dynamics, cellular specificity and functional contribution of CB2R within IECs during RIII remain poorly characterized. Whether activation of CB2R can directly protect irradiated IECs and preserve epithelial barrier integrity, and through which epithelial‑intrinsic mechanisms such protection may be mediated, therefore remains unclear. Addressing these unresolved questions is essential for defining the role of CB2R in RIII and for evaluating its potential as a mechanism‑based therapeutic target for radiation‑induced intestinal damage.

In this study, we set out to systematically investigate the role of CB2R in radiation‑induced intestinal injury using genetic and pharmacological approaches. We identify an epithelial‑intrinsic CB2R signaling programme that protects against radiation‑induced tissue damage by suppressing ferroptotic cell death and promoting intestinal barrier integrity. Mechanistically, the epithelial protective efficacy is mediated through a CB2R/β‑catenin axis reinforced by the homeobox transcription factor HoxA10 via a positive-feedback loop. Through the screening of a panel of CB2R agonists, we further establish the translational relevance of this pathway by identifying a novel small‑molecule agent with potent radioprotective efficacy in the intestine, without compromising the anti-tumor effects. Together, these findings define CB2R as a key epithelial protective regulator in RIII and highlight its potential as a mechanism‑based therapeutic target.

Results

Irradiation promotes CB2R upregulation in the intestinal epithelium

Initial mining of public databases revealed that expression of genes encoding the endocannabinoid system (ECS) receptors CB1R and CB2R was significantly elevated in patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (Fig. 1A). Further analysis demonstrated that the increase in CB2R gene expression was more pronounced than that of CB1R (Fig. 1A). Given the established protective role of CB2R in intestinal inflammation, the observed upregulation of CB2R was considered to be compensatory [24]. Subsequent investigation of rectal adenocarcinoma datasets in the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database indicated that a higher CNR2/CNR1 expression ratio was associated with improved patient prognosis (Fig. 1B left), suggesting that selective CB2R agonism may confer superior protective effects compared to CB1R in IBD. Consistent with this, analysis of the Kaplan–Meier Plotter database revealed that colon cancer patients in stages I and II with high CB2R expression exhibited better prognosis and higher survival rates over 120 months (Fig. 1B right). These findings collectively imply a potentially critical role for CB2R in the management of several intestinal diseases. However, due to distinct pathogenic mechanisms, its role in radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) remains unclear. To address this, a mouse model of RIII was established of ⁶⁰Co γ-irradiation to the abdomen, with other body regions shielded by lead bricks (Fig. S1A). Informed by earlier studies, a series of preliminary experiments were first performed using doses between 12 and 16 Gy. Survival analysis showed that doses of 15 Gy or higher led to excessive mortality within 84 h, whereas a dose of 12 Gy produced only mild histopathological changes in the intestinal tissue during the acute phase (Fig. S1B, C). Consequently, a dose of 13 Gy was chosen for the establishment of the acute RIII model in all subsequent experiments (Fig. 1C). RT-qPCR results demonstrated downregulation of CNR1 gene expression, whereas CNR2 gene was upregulated in mice colonic tissues (Fig. 1D). Concordantly, elevated CB2R protein levels were observed in the colonic tissues (Fig. 1E). To visualize the location and distribution of CB2R, immunofluorescence staining was performed on the mice colonic tissues. Epithelial Cell Adhesion Molecule (EpCAM), a marker of epithelial cells and a critical factor in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, is highly expressed at the crypt base [25]. Following irradiation, EpCAM exhibits translocalization with increased expression toward the villus tip, contributing to the repair of irradiation-induced villus injury. Another marked increase of CB2R signal intensity was also observed in the colonic tissue. Intriguingly, in control group, CB2R signaling was predominantly localized to the nuclei of crypt and lamina propria epithelial cells. In contrast, irradiated specimens exhibited redistribution of CB2R to the apical epithelial compartments of the villi (Fig. 1F). The redistribution of CB2R aligns with the changing pattern of peak EpCAM signaling, indicating that CB2R may shift its functional role in IECs from homeostatic regulation to orchestrating damage response, and still remains to be elucidated. Collectively, Irradiation-induced CB2R upregulation indicates its critical role in RIII.

CB2R deficiency exacerbates radiation-induced intestine injury

Having established that the development of RIII influences CB2R signaling, the role of CB2R deficiency in altering RIII progression was then investigated. CB2R knockout (CB2R−/−) mice and their wild-type (WT) littermates were subjected to total abdominal irradiation to establish the RIII model, as above-mentioned described (Fig. S2 and Fig. 2A). Compared to WT littermates, CB2R−/− mice exhibited more pronounced body weight loss, and greater colon shortening post-irradiation, indicating that CB2R knockout exacerbated prognostic phenotype (Fig. 2B, C). Histopathological evaluation revealed crypt distortion and focal epithelial erosion in the colon of irradiated WT mice. In contrast, CB2R−/− mice exhibited exacerbated colonic damage, characterized by crypt abscesses, submucosal edema, and extensive epithelial erosion, accompanied by a significant increase in the histological activity index score (Fig. 2D, E). These observations indicate that CB2R deficiency aggravates RIII. Given that irradiation rapidly disrupts the intracellular redox balance, thereby inducing oxidative stress, analysis of classic oxidative stress indicators were then proceeded. A more severe injury phenotype in CB2R−/− mice compared to WT controls following irradiation. Specifically, the levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and malondialdehyde (MDA) indicated exacerbated oxidative damage, suggesting that CB2R deficiency leads to impaired antioxidant capacity and heightened oxidative stress in colonic tissue (Fig. 2F, G). Notably, compared to the unirradiated group, irradiation significantly inhibited both the pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines levels. Consistent with a few previous studies [26, 27], the decreased cytokines levels are attributable to the immunosuppressive impact of our specific irradiation protocol on the intestine. Post-irradiation, CB2R−/− mice showed lower intestinal inflammatory cytokine levels compared to WT mice, which was accompanied by a worse overall phenotype, suggesting a shift toward a more severe, non-inflammatory-driven pathology (Fig. 2H). Moreover, evidenced by western blotting and immunofluorescence, CB2R−/− group exhibited a greater vulnerability to the irradiation-induced downregulation of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin in the colonic tissue, thereby suggesting the essential role of CB2R in preserving intestinal barrier integrity upon irradiation challenge (Fig. 2I-K). Collectively, genetic ablation of CB2R significantly exacerbated RIII, evidenced by severe deterioration in both pathological and molecular parameters, highlighting the essential protective role of CB2R in the intestinal epithelium.

CF-2 alleviates radiation-induced intestinal injury via activating CB2R

Based on the established aggravating effect of CB2R deficiency on RIII, it was hypothesized that CB2R activation confers protection. To address this, a panel of small-molecular CB2R agonists, encompassing the classic, validated agents AM1241, JWH-133, and GW842166X, alongside biologically unverified compound CB2R/FAAH modulator-2 (CF-2) [28] and the novel, internally developed compounds YX-2102 [22] and YX-2125, was screened (Fig. S3A). The protective efficacy against RIII-induced cell death was determined, and the specificity of the effect was confirmed by pre-treatment with the antagonist SR144528 (10 µM). Critically, agonist concentrations were normalized to their Ki values to ensure equal efficacious receptor engagement before the cell survival outcome was determined. The CCK-8 reads demonstrated that several agonists conferred protection against irradiation-induced cell death, confirming the beneficial role of CB2R activation in RIII. However, it was noted that upon pre-administration of the antagonist SR144528, the protection of CB2R activation against irradiation was only partially attenuated, in contrast to its complete abolition of the other agonists (Fig. 3A). Given the known dual function of CF-2 as both a CB2R agonist and a fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) antagonist (Fig. 3B), it was supposed that the sustained efficacy was attributed to FAAH inhibition, which potentiates local anandamide (AEA) accumulation, thus outcompeting the antagonist at CB2R. ELISA analysis demonstrated that GW842166X, YX-2125, and CF-2 both exerted a statistically significant upregulatory effect. Compared with the irradiation group, CF-2 produced the most significant increase in AEA levels within the cell-cultured supernatant, further supporting CF-2’s role in elevating local AEA via FAAH inhibition (Fig. 3C). The additional AEA accumulation by CF-2 enhanced the agonistic effect and protein expression level on CB2R, which was further supported by western blot analysis in HT-29 cells (Fig. S3B). Consequently, CF-2 was prioritized for further investigation in vivo.

CF-2 was administered via intraperitoneal injection according to the schedule outlined (Fig. 3D). Within the 3.5-day acute RIII model, CB2R protein expression was induced in mouse colonic tissue by irradiation and sustained at elevated levels following treatment with CF-2 (Fig. 3E). CF-2 treatment conferred a significant post-irradiation benefit, markedly improving the 30-day survival rate compared to the irradiated vehicle group (Fig. 3F). Monitoring of continuous body weight changes over 30 days post irradiation also revealed a trend toward mitigation of weight loss by CF-2 treatment, despite the lack of statistical significance (Fig. 3G). Similarly, no significant mitigation of body weight loss was observed with CF-2 treatment at the 3.5-day post-irradiation (Fig. 3H). As a comprehensive phenotype indicator modulated by multiple factors [29, 30], the lack of a statistically significant difference in body weight does not contradict the protective effect of CF-2. Furthermore, CF-2 administration dramatically attenuated the clinical symptoms of RIII, as evidenced by reduced peritoneal adhesions, decreased hematochezia, and lower diarrhea scores (Fig. 3J-K). The severe shortening of the colon induced by irradiation was significantly prevented by the CF-2 treatment (Fig. 3L). The protective effect of CF-2 was further confirmed via histopathological examination. H&E staining demonstrated the preservation of intestinal crypt architect, and the alleviation of mucosal injury, while AB-PAS staining revealed substantial restoration of goblet cells and the mucin layer (Fig. 3M). Moreover, histological assessment of colon sections, based on parameters including the disease activity index (DAI), pathological score, crypt number, and goblet cell count per crypt, collectively demonstrated the protective effect of CF-2 against RIII (Fig. 3N). The specific criteria for DAI scoring were included in the Tbl. S2. Another key cytoprotective mechanism involved suppression of apoptosis, as evidenced by a pronounced decrease in TUNEL-positive cells within the crypts of CF-2-treated mice (Fig. 3O). At the molecular level, CF-2 counteracted irradiation-induced oxidative stress, enhancing the bioactivities of the antioxidant enzymes SOD and CAT, elevating GSH levels, and reducing the lipid peroxidation product MDA (Fig. 3P). DNA damage, assessed by phosphor histone H2AX (γ-H2AX, a marker closely associated with DNA double-strand breaks) level, was significantly reduced by CF-2 in both mice colonic tissues and HT-29 cells after irradiation (Fig. 3Q and Fig. S3C), indicating a role in mitigating DNA damage. Together, these findings indicate that CF-2 alleviates irradiation-induced intestinal injury by maintaining intestinal integrity, suppressing apoptosis, attenuating oxidative stress, and promoting against DNA damage.

Surprisingly, however, JNJ42165279 against irradiation-induced decrease of cell viability was not statistically significant (Fig. S4A, B). Likewise, the irradiation-induced increase in γ-H2AX, a marker of DNA damage, was not significantly ameliorated by JNJ42165279 treatment (Fig. S4C, D). Flow cytometric analysis of intracellular ROS and lipid ROS levels in HT-29 cells indicated that JNJ42165279 exerted only a modest inhibitory effect on intracellular oxidative stress (Fig. S4E, F). These observations collectively suggest that highly potent or excessive inhibition of FAAH provides very limited protective efficacy against irradiation-induced cellular damage. Hence, we hypothesize that robust FAAH inhibition by JNJ42165279 may cause supraphysiological AEA accumulation, overactivating these deleterious CB1R and TRPV1 pathways. This could largely offset the theoretical benefits of FAAH inhibition, potentially explaining the limited efficacy of JNJ42165279 in restoring cell viability or mitigating oxidative stress in irradiated HT-29 models.

Moreover, safety concerns that CB2R activation by CF-2 might accelerate cancer cell growth were investigated in HT-29 cells. The effect of gradient concentrations of CF-2 on HT-29 cell proliferation was assessed in vitro via 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay. The results indicated that low concentrations of CF-2 had no significant impact on proliferation, with higher doses even exerting inhibitory effects (Fig. S5A, B). Crucially, while IR exposure significantly suppressed HT-29 proliferation, CF-2 treatment did not compromise this anti-tumor efficacy, as it failed to reverse the IR-induced inhibition of EdU incorporation (Fig. S5C). Consistent results were further confirmed by cell viability and proliferative capacity measurements using the CCK-8 assay and colony formation assay (Fig. S5D, E). Collectively, CF-2 did not attenuate the significant therapeutic effect of radiotherapy on inhibiting cancer cell proliferation, establishing a solid foundation for its translational potential in treating RIII.

Taken together, CF-2 confers substantial protective effects against irradiation-induced DNA damage, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and impairment of intestinal barrier integrity. Furthermore, its lack of stimulatory effect on tumor cell proliferation highlights the favorable therapeutic profile and translational potential.

CF-2 confers protective effects beyond the colon following irradiation

Given that irradiation-induced DNA damage and oxidative stress eliminate rapidly dividing lymphocytes and stromal cells, thereby causing severe immune dysregulation [31]. The effect of irradiation on spleen was next examined, along with the potential for CF-2 to reverse injury. We firstly demonstrated a compensatory upregulation of CB2R signaling following radiation-induced splenic injury, which established a mechanistic basis for the potential therapeutic efficacy of CF-2 (Fig. 4A). Next, the restorative effects of CF-2 on splenic morphology and architecture were evaluated. Irradiation resulted in marked splenic atrophy, characterized by a significant reduction in both size and weight, accompanied by notable discoloration (Fig. 4B, C). These morphological alterations were markedly attenuated by CF-2 administration. Compared to the irradiated group, less hemosiderin deposition was observed in the CF-2 treatment group as brownish pigmentation within the spleen sections, as indicated by green arrows. Histological analysis via H&E staining indicated that CF-2 treatment effectively reversed the irradiation-induced near-total loss of white pulp (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, representative Ki67 immunohistochemistry fields implied that CF-2 may facilitate recovery from radiation-induced damage by promoting cellular proliferation within the spleen (Fig. 4E). Notably, beyond the documented effects of CF-2 on spleen immunological function, it was investigated whether CF-2 could reverse irradiation-induced disruption of intestinal immunity. Enhanced CB2R signaling in the mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs) was observed by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 4F); however, no significant reversal of radiation-induced MLN atrophy was found following CF-2 administration (Fig. 4G). These results indicated that although irradiation induced spleen injury was mitigated by CF-2, its intestinal protective effect may not involve a major role for the modulation of the gut immune landscape.

The small intestine is highly sensitive to radiation due to its rapid cellular renewal [32]. Following our analysis of lymphoid tissues, we therefore extended our investigation to this tissue to determine whether CF-2 could preserve mucosal integrity. Upon gross examination of the entire intestinal tract in mice, segmental darkening and luminal swelling were observed. And a protective effect of CF-2 was also evident in the small intestine (Fig. 4H). Further assessment of small intestinal length, villus height, and histopathological scores revealed that intestinal damage was effectively ameliorated by CF-2 (Fig. 4I-K). Additionally, H&E staining demonstrated irradiation-induced villus shortening and irregular coiling. Treatment with CF-2 significantly promoted villus regrowth and maintained villus integrity, supporting its role in preserving epithelial structure (Fig. 4L). Consistent with these findings, transmission electron microscopy revealed that irradiation nearly abolished the microvillus brush border, whereas CF-2 treatment restored regular and intact microvillar morphology, thus indicating ultrastructural protection (Fig. 4M). Moreover, TUNEL staining further demonstrated that CF-2 significantly inhibited irradiation-induced epithelial cell death in the small intestine (Fig. 4N). Collectively, these findings suggested that the therapeutic efficacy of CF-2 extends beyond the colon, effectively preserving both splenic and small intestinal architecture against radiation injury.

CF-2 suppresses radiation-induced ferroptosis and epithelial barrier disruption

The repair of spleen and small intestinal morphology implied that irradiation injury was attenuated by CF-2. Since the intestinal barrier integrity is ultimately determined by the fate of epithelial cells and the stability of intercellular tight junctions [33], it was subsequently investigated whether CF-2 mitigated irradiation-induced barrier disruption via the inhibition of epithelial ferroptosis and the stabilization of intercellular tight junctions. Analysis of human ulcerative colitis datasets further revealed a positive correlation between CNR2 expression and glutathione peroxidase (GPX4), a central regulator of ferroptosis (Fig. 5A). Subsequent in vivo experiments were conducted to evaluate whether CF-2 treatment could alleviate irradiation-induced ferroptosis, following the previously described procedures. In irradiated mice, decreased colonic expression of the key ferroptosis defense proteins GPX4 and amino acid transport system Xc- (xCT) were observed via western blot, and these decreases were rescued by CF-2 treatment (Fig. 5B). However, CB2R activation showed no significant effect on the expression of acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 4 (ACSL4), suggesting that its protective mechanism distinct from phospholipid remodeling (Fig. 5B). To further investigate expression profile alterations of more ferroptosis-associated genes, mice colon tissues were subjected to RNA sequencing analysis. A different pattern between irradiation group and CF-2 treatment group was observed, indicating a significant regulatory effect of CF-2 on the xCT/GPX4 axis (Fig. 5C). The parallel protein expression analyses in cultured HT29 cells also revealed the reverse of CF-2 on irradiation-induced GPX4 and xCT expression decrease (Fig. 5D, E). Flow cytometry suggested that CF-2 treatment significantly mitigated the irradiation-induced increase in both intracellular ROS and lipid peroxidation levels in HT-29 cells. A significant decrease in MitoSOX fluorescence signaling upon CF-2 administration further suggested alleviation of irradiation-activated mitochondrial ROS production (Fig. 5F). Ultrastructural analysis confirmed that CF-2 treatment effectively promoted the maintenance of normal mitochondrial morphology in HT-29 cells (Fig. 5G), implying a protective role of CB2R activation and its downstream cascade within mitochondria. Lipid peroxidation triggered by ferroptosis disrupts not only the monolayer cell membrane but also weakens intestinal barrier, likely through the oxidation of intercellular tight junction-associated proteins or cytoskeletal components [34, 35]. Consequently, the distribution and function of key tight junction proteins were next determined. Accordingly, RT-qPCR analysis of mice colonic tissues revealed that irradiation significantly downregulated the expression of genes associated with intestinal barrier tight junctions. This downregulation was subsequently reversed to different extent after CF-2 treatment, indicating an important role for CF-2 in maintaining high expression levels of epithelial tight junction genes. Additionally, gene expression within the endocannabinoid system was assessed. While no significant alterations were observed in CNR1 (encoding CB1R), CNR2 (encoding CB2R), or FAAH after irradiation. However, after CF-2 was administered, CNR2 expression was markedly increased, confirming its specific modulation by CF-2. The increase in FAAH mRNA levels may be attributed to a compensatory upregulation due to the inhibition of FAAH enzyme bioactivity (Fig. 5H). Furthermore, serum levels of diamine oxidase (DAO), a biomarker released upon epithelial injury, were significantly elevated post irradiation, and this increase was attenuated by CF-2 (Fig. 5I). Similarly, the irradiation-induced elevation in serum FITC-dextran (MW 4000), administered orally before sacrifice to assess gut permeability, was reversed by CF-2 treatment (Fig. 5J), underscoring its key role in maintaining barrier integrity. Additionally, Western blot analyses of both mice colonic tissue and HT29 cells demonstrated that CF-2 treatment reversed the irradiation-induced downregulation of the key tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin (Fig. 5K, L). Representative immunofluorescence images corroborated the pivotal role of CB2R activation in promoting the expression of these proteins within the epithelium (Fig. 5M). In summary, CB2R activation was found to significantly inhibit irradiation-induced ferroptosis in IECs and promote epithelial barrier repair, effectively mitigating RIII.

Moreover, CCK‑8 assays revealed that both CF‑2 and Ferrostatin‑1 significantly attenuated irradiation induced reductions in cell viability, with CF‑2 showing markedly stronger protective effect than Ferrostatin‑1 (Fig. S6A, B). Given that irradiation typically triggers multiple forms of cell death (e.g., ferroptosis and apoptosis), this superior protection indicates that the effects of CF-2 are not limited to inhibiting ferroptosis, but likely involve additional protective pathways. To determine the key contribution of ferroptosis to its protective activity, we subsequently examined additional ferroptosis specific indicators. Measurements of ferrous ion levels, a hallmark of ferroptosis, showed that Ferrostatin‑1 suppressed the irradiation induced elevation of ferrous ions more effectively than CF‑2 (Fig. S6C-G). While less effective than Ferrostatin‑1, CF‑2 still significantly lowered ferrous ion concentration, ROS and lipid ROS levels in HT‑29 cells. At the same time, CF‑2 and Ferrostatin‑1 produced comparable modulation of GPX4 and xCT protein levels (Fig. S6H). MDA and GSH levels also suggested that CF‑2 acts on the ferroptosis pathway with an efficacy close to that of Ferrostatin‑1 (Fig. S6I, J).

In summary, multiple forms of cell death occur in radiation induced intestinal injury. The above experiments confirm that CF‑2 acts in part through ferroptosis modulation, yet they do not exclude the possibility that it also exerts therapeutic effects by inhibiting other cell death pathways. Moreover, given its favorable in vivo suitability and translational prospects, CF‑2 retains potential for further development.

CB2R is required for CF-2-mediated modulation of ferroptosis and the gut barrier in IECs

Inhibition of epithelial ferroptosis and stabilization of tight junctions by CF-2 supported the hypothesis of CB2R-dependent protection. To definitively establish CB2R as the key executor, further investigation was conducted using CB2R knockout (CB2R−/−) mice subjected to the same irradiation paradigm (Fig. 6A). Substantially, CF-2 conferred no significant benefit against irradiation-induced body weight loss in both WT and CB2R−/− mice (Fig. 6B). Also, the protective effect of CF-2 to prevent spleen atrophy, as seen in WT mice, was completely absent in CB2R−/− group, indicating that its protective efficacy was mediated specifically through CB2R activation (Fig. 6C, D). Macroscopic examination of the colon revealed apparent cecal swelling and liquid intestinal contents in all CB2R−/− mice (Fig. 6E). After irradiation, excreted stools were noted to be pale yellow and loose. In contrast to the normalized stool appearance in wild-type (WT) mice after CF-2 treatment, no improvement was observed in CF-2-treated CB2R−/− mice (Fig. 6F). H&E staining indicated that the therapeutic effect of CF-2 was attenuated in the absence of CB2R (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, the protective effect of CF-2 to reverse the irradiation-induced low-inflammatory cytokine state in the intestinal tract, as observed in WT mice, was completely absent in CB2R−/− group (Fig. 6H). The regulatory effects of CF-2 on SOD activity and MDA levels, observed in WT mice, were absent in CB2R−/− mice (Fig. 6I). Similarly, CF-2 failed to counteract the irradiation-induced downregulation of genes associated with intestinal tight junctions in CB2R−/− mice (Fig. 6J). These results collectively suggest that the therapeutic actions of CF-2 are dependent on the presence of CB2R. Furthermore, representative western blot images indicated that in WT mice, CF-2 significantly reversed the irradiation-induced increase in γ-H2AX, indicating a therapeutic effect. However, in CB2R-deficient mice, γ-H2AX levels remained elevated following CF-2 administration, demonstrating that its protective effect was abrogated (Fig. 6K). This finding was reiterated using primary IECs isolated from both WT and CB2R−/− mice (Fig. 6L). In the colon, CF-2-mediated regulation of tight junction proteins and ferroptosis-related proteins was abolished upon CB2R deletion (Fig. 6M). This loss of therapeutic effect was even more pronounced in isolated primary IECs (Fig. 6N). These findings indicate that CF-2 exerts protective effects through CB2R to ameliorate irradiation-induced ferroptosis, promote restoration of the epithelial barrier, and significantly alleviate RIII.

Transcriptomics implicates HoxA10 as a candidate downstream effector in CB2R signaling

Therefore, to investigate the hypothesis that CB2R activation promotes barrier damage by regulating ferroptosis in IECs, a transcriptomic profiling was conducted to delineate the key downstream factors of CB2R. As above-described, the same experimental procedure for CF-2 intervention in mouse acute radiation enteropathy was employed, and colonic tissues were collected for transcriptomic profiling. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated that both irradiation and subsequent CF-2 treatment were major drivers of global transcriptional variance (Fig. 7A). Comparative transcriptomic analysis of post-irradiated mouse colon tissue revealed 687 significantly upregulated and 718 downregulated genes (Fig. 7B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the downregulated genes indicated that the intestinal immune network pathway was among the most suppressed. Notably, the top 20 most inhibited pathways were predominantly related to intestinal immunity, with significant suppression observed in signaling pathways governing T cell, B cell, and NK cell functions, highlighting severe radiation-induced intestinal immune dysregulation (Fig. 7C). Subsequently, comparison between the CF-2 treatment group and the irradiation-only group identified 234 upregulated and 213 downregulated genes (Fig. 7D). Strikingly, its restorative effect was minimal on immune pathways (only modest upregulation of TNF-α/IL-17 signaling) but significant on pathways governing cell adhesion, cytoskeleton, proliferation, and survival (Fig. 7E). These findings further support the hypothesis that CB2R activation in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) confers protection primarily by mitigating irradiation-induced cell death through mechanisms related to cellular structure and survival. Subsequently, the most significantly upregulated genes post-irradiation were intersected with the set of genes downregulated after CF-2 treatment. This intersection produced a list of candidate genes most likely to be downstream of CB2R (Fig. 7F). Next, by taking the intersection and the protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis of the most significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified six mostly interconnected hub proteins from Homeobox family, which spatially and temporally regulating embryonic development, morphogenesis, and cell differentiation [36] (Fig. 7G). Following validation of their mRNA levels in intestinal tissue by RT-qPCR, we focused on HoxA10, which exhibited the most statistically significant alteration (Fig. 7H). HoxA10, a sequence-specific transcription factor, is part of a developmental regulatory system that provides cells with specific positional identities on the anterior-posterior axis [37]. HoxA10 typically functions to promote biological processes such as cell proliferation and embryo implantation [38]. Therefore, high expression of HoxA10 in IECs is postulated to enhance repair of the damaged intercellular tight junction. Subsequent immunofluorescence analysis showed distinct spatiotemporal expression patterns of HoxA10. Under unirradiated environment, HoxA10 was predominantly localized to the rapidly proliferating cells at the crypt base (indicated by yellow arrows), in contrast to less distribution at the villus tip (white arrows) (Fig. 7I). After irradiation, overall intestinal expression of HoxA10 was markedly diminished, including at the crypt base. Interestingly, a striking monolayer upregulation was specifically noted in the EpCAM positive epithelial cells at the villus tip (white arrows), suggesting a potential role of HoxA10 in promoting epithelial repair (Fig. 7J). CF-2 treatment resulted in elevated HoxA10 expression within both the crypt base and the epithelium, indicating a potential regulatory effect of CB2R activation on HoxA10 during RIII (Fig. 7K). Collectively, the specific localization of HoxA10 to EpCAM-positive cells underpins its promising biofunction in IECs.

HoxA10 amplifies CB2R-β-catenin signaling to enhance epithelial resilience against radiation

Guided by RNA-seq analysis that nominated HoxA10 as a downstream candidate of CB2R signaling transduction. Thus, role of HoxA10 in alleviating RIII was investigated after knockdown by transient transfection. Consistent with the RNA-seq results, Hoxa10 gene expression was found to be significantly reduced in rectal mucosal tissues from patients with ulcerative colitis, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target (Fig. S7A). To elucidate the underlying mechanism, Hoxa10 was knocked down in HT-29 cells (Fig. S7B). Western blot analysis revealed that CF-2 treatment significantly reversed the irradiation-induced decrease in HoxA10 expression in primary IECs isolated from both WT and CB2R−/− mice. This restorative effect persisted, though less pronounced, in CB2R-deficient group, indicating a potential influence of CB2R activation on the recovery of HoxA10 expression (Fig. 8A). Irradiation-induced DNA damage, marked by γ-H2AX, was effectively mitigated by CF-2 treatment in normal control group. However, the protective effect of CF-2 was partly attenuated in Hoxa10-deficient group (Fig. 8B). Similarly, the expression of key anti-ferroptosis proteins GPX4 and xCT, which were upregulated by CF-2 in normal control group, was moderately reversed after Hoxa10 knockdown (Fig. 8C). Flow cytometry demonstrated that CF-2 effectively reduced the overall cellular ROS levels and concurrently inhibited lipid peroxidation in the non-transfected group. However, same effect was significantly attenuated in Hoxa10 knockdown groups (Fig. 8D, E). Representative immunofluorescence images further revealed that while CF-2 substantially suppressed the accumulation of oxidized BODIPY probe signal (green) following irradiation in normal control cells. This inhibitory effect was partially diminished, though still present, in Hoxa10-knockdown cells (Fig. 8F). Together, these observations proposed HoxA10 as a partial mediator, rather than an absolute prerequisite, for the anti-ferroptosis effect elicited by CF-2. To investigate the role of HoxA10 in modulating gut barrier, subsequent analysis focused on the expression and localization of the tight junction protein ZO-1. The disruption of ZO-1 post-irradiation was largely prevented by CF-2 in normal control cells, but the preservation of gut barrier integrity was partly reduced in the absence of Hoxa10 (Fig. 8G). The therapeutic efficacy of CF-2 against RIII was consistently and partially reversed upon Hoxa10 knockdown across multiple cellular processes. The partial reversal of CF-2 effects upon Hoxa10 knockdown suggests that additional mechanisms might be also involved.

Notably, β-catenin has been identified as a principal downstream molecule mediating CB2R activation, playing a pivotal role in regulating cell proliferation and adhesive interactions [21]. Consistently, representative immunofluorescence images initially revealed a marked reduction in β-catenin expression within the colonic epithelium of WT mice post-irradiation, characterized by diminished apical expression (white arrows) and a near-complete loss in crypt regions (yellow arrows). Under the same irradiation dose, a similar, yet more pronounced, downregulation was observed in CB2R−/− group (Fig. S8). These observations preliminarily suggested a potential regulatory role of CB2R in modulating β-catenin expression. Consequently, CF-2 was subsequently included in further investigations of β-catenin. In WT mice colonic tissue, CF-2 treatment showed a non-significant trend toward reversing the irradiation-induced downregulation of β-catenin mRNA (Fig. 9A). Furthermore, analysis of the public dataset GSE109142 revealed a significant positive correlation between CNR2 and Ctnnb1 gene expression in patients with ulcerative colitis (Fig. S9). Next, β-catenin expression in primary IECs isolated from both WT and CB2R−/− mice was examined. CF-2 upregulated β-catenin expression in WT group but lost this regulatory effect in CB2R−/− group (Fig. 9B). Interestingly, the hierarchical relationship governing the transcriptional regulation between HoxA10 and β-catenin has been controversial [39, 40]. To explore the functional network, we examined the relationship between the downstream effector HoxA10 and β-catenin. Bioinformatics analysis (GEPIA2.0) confirmed their significant co-expression in the transverse colonic tissues of COAD patients (Fig. 9C), consistent with the theoretical synergy effect in cellular growth and adhesion programs. Dual-luciferase reporter assay demonstrates that HoxA10 positively regulates the transcription of Ctnnb1 by activating its promoter region (Fig. S10A, B). In Hoxa10-knockdown group, an attenuated upregulation of β-catenin levels after CB2R activation, comparable to that in WT group, was observed (Fig. 9D). In contrast, compared to untransfected controls, the upregulatory effect of CF-2 on HoxA10 was completely abolished in Ctnnb1-knockdown group (Fig. 9E). In summary, Hoxa10 knockdown was found to only partially modulate β-catenin expression level, whereas Ctnnb1 knockdown completely antagonized HoxA10 transactivation. This suggested a more central signaling transduction role for β-catenin than HoxA10. Subsequently, the effect of Ctnnb1 knockdown on the phenotypic rescue by CF-2 was validated. Compared to normal control cells, the protection of CF-2 was lost in Ctnnb1 knockdown cells, as indicated by persistently high levels of γ-H2AX (Fig. 9F). A parallel loss of function was observed in the ferroptosis pathway. The ability of CF-2 to upregulate the key antioxidant proteins GPX4 and xCT was entirely abolished in the absence of β-catenin (Fig. 9G). Further quantitative fluorescence analysis confirmed that the potent suppressive effect of CF-2 on the accumulation of both total ROS and lipid ROS was entirely eliminated upon Ctnnb1 knockdown (Fig. 9H, I). Representative immunofluorescence images further corroborated the maintaining of pervasive signal of lipid oxidation in Ctnnb1 knockdown cells despite CF-2 treatment (Fig. 9J). In normal control group, CF-2 potently preserved the continuous, membranous localization of the tight junction protein ZO-1. In stark contrast, this protective effect was nullified in Ctnnb1 knockdown group, which displayed a characteristic fragmented and disorganized ZO-1 staining pattern, indicating a compromised epithelial barrier biofunction (Fig. 9K). Collectively, β-catenin was established as an indispensable downstream effector of CB2R. The entire radioprotective profile of CF-2, including its roles in mitigating DNA damage, inhibiting ferroptosis, maintaining redox homeostasis, and preserving barrier integrity, is critically dependent on the presence of β-catenin. Taken together, β-catenin was identified as central to the downstream signaling of CB2R activation, while HoxA10 functioned in an accessory role.

Discussion

This study elucidates the role of CB2R as a radiation-responsive receptor in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), offering both mechanistic and translational evidence that supports the activation of CB2R as a viable therapeutic strategy for addressing radiation induced intestinal injury (RIII). Our findings demonstrate that CB2R signaling within IECs mitigates irradiation-induced ferroptosis while facilitating the preservation of the epithelial barrier. Mechanistically, the activation of CB2R exerts therapeutic effects through CB2R/β-catenin axis, with HoxA10 serving as a transcriptional amplifier that enhances signaling output. Furthermore, the screening of CB2R agonists has identified a promising small-molecule candidate, CB2R/FAAH modulator-2 (CF-2), which provides intestinal radioprotection without diminishing the antitumor efficacy of radiotherapy. Collectively, these findings expand the functional scope of CB2R beyond immune regulation and underscore the potential of epithelial-directed CB2R modulation in the management of RIII.

Previous research on CB2R has predominantly conceptualized its biological function within immune-centric mechanisms, where CB2R activation mitigates inflammatory processes and modulates immune responses [41]. However, in RIII, the pathophysiological context differs significantly: early epithelial cell damage and inadequate regenerative processes can swiftly lead to barrier failure, thereby enhancing microbial translocation and subsequent inflammation [42]. Our data reveal a stress-responsive role for CB2R within the intestinal epithelium that contributes to epithelial protection following irradiation. Irradiation upregulated epithelial CB2R expression, consistent with an adaptive injury response, and CB2R deficiency aggravated intestinal damage, supporting a protective contribution rather than a purely correlative change. This emphasis on epithelial cells is consistent with growing evidence that CB2R is detectable and functionally significant beyond immune compartments, including in parenchymal cells across various organs [43]. In these contexts, its expression and phenotypic outcomes seem to be influenced by the specific tissue environment and the nature of the injury. For instance, in renal disease model, CB2R signaling has been associated with responses to epithelial or stromal injury [44], and in other non-immune scenarios, CB2R bioactivity has been associated with epithelial barrier protection and repair mechanisms [22, 43]. Importantly, our findings are not sufficient to exclude concurrent contributions from CB2R within the immune system. Instead, they propose that, at a critical pathological stage in RIII, a pharmacologically accessible epithelial-intrinsic protective pathway can be engaged to maintain epithelial viability and gut barrier integrity. The cell-type-specific localization may explain the organ- and model-dependent variability in CB2R phenotypes, as distinct injuries involve various key effector cells and rate-limiting steps, thereby modulating CB2R signaling outcomes.

In RIII, ferroptosis of IECs is increasingly recognized as a pathogenic driver that amplifies oxidative lipid peroxidation-mediated epithelial loss, thereby precipitating breakdown of tight junction integrity and disrupting intestinal barriers. Previous studies have demonstrated that irradiation triggers ferroptosis in IECs, characterized by elevated levels of labile iron and lipid peroxidation, disruption of the GSH-GPX4 antioxidant system, and mitochondrial damage [45–47]. Based on these, in this work, it was observed that CB2R activation significantly mitigates irradiation-induced ferroptosis in IECs, a functional relationship that, to our knowledge, has not been previously established in the RIII model. Consistently, CF-2 ameliorates ferroptotic damage in irradiated epithelial cells and restores the irradiation-induced decreased levels of GPX4 and xCT both in vivo and in vitro. Notably, CF-2 limited irradiation-associated ferroptosis in IECs and facilitated barrier restoration, and these effects required CB2R, supporting receptor-dependent epithelial protection rather than a CB2R-independent cytoprotective effect of the compound. These findings support a model in which CB2R signaling strengthens the system Xc-/GSH/GPX4 axis to limit lipid peroxidation, thereby promoting epithelial cell survival and facilitating barrier recovery.

More broadly, the precise mechanisms by which CB2R signaling interacts with the regulation of ferroptosis remain inadequately elucidated. In our research, transcriptomic analysis identified HoxA10 as a candidate effector predominantly expressed in epithelial tissues and functionally characterized as a transcriptional enhancer that amplifies CB2R-associated β-catenin signaling. CB2R activation restored the irradiation-suppressed expression of HoxA10, a homeobox factor previously implicated in epithelial growth and tissue remodeling programme, including proliferation, adhesion and migration [37, 48, 49], and reported to associate with an intestinal mucin phenotype [50]. Notably, the knockdown of HoxA10 did not completely abolish the therapeutic phenotype, which aligns with its modulatory role and suggests the involvement of parallel pathways. As direct crosstalk between CB2R and HoxA10 has not been established to our knowledge, our findings are most consistent with an indirect relationship where HoxA10 enhances downstream effects rather than a direct target of CB2R. Concurrently, β-catenin has been identified as a pivotal node for the efficacy of CF-2: Depletion of β-catenin notably diminished the CF-2-mediated restoration of GPX4 and xCT, as well as its capacity to mitigate lipid ROS. This indicates that β-catenin-dependent transcription contributes to, but does not entirely explain, the anti-ferroptotic program triggered by CB2R activation. This interpretation aligns with previous findings indicating that β-catenin/TCF transcriptional activity can regulate GPX4, thereby influencing ferroptosis sensitivity [51, 52]. It also concurs with evidence demonstrating that CB2R can activate β-catenin signaling in a Wnt-independent manner in various contexts [20, 53]. A plausible synthesis of these observations suggests a feed-forward module in which β-catenin and HoxA10 mutually reinforce each other, as indicated by previous studies describing reciprocal regulation between HoxA10 and β-catenin signaling [39, 54]. Nonetheless, the existence of β-catenin-mediated transactivation of Hoxa10 in IECs, including the identification of relevant cis-regulatory elements, necessitates direct experimental validation. Finally, while our bioinformatic analyses of human colorectal datasets did not reveal a robust co-expression signal between Hoxa10 and CNR2, this does not preclude the possibility of context-specific interactions at the signaling or functional level, which merits further investigation. Overall, our findings support a model wherein CB2R activation initiates a β-catenin-centered transcriptional response that is enhanced by HoxA10 to maintain system Xc⁻-GPX4-mediated lipid peroxide detoxification under irradiation stress. This mechanism complements previously identified RIII protective pathways that operate upstream by modulating iron handling, lipid peroxidation substrates, or AMPK-associated metabolic regulation [45, 46, 55].

To further investigate the epithelial-protective function of CB2R within the RIII model, a CB2R-focused screening methodology was employed to identify potential agonists. Unexpectedly, CF-2 emerged as the most effective compound in alleviating irradiation-induced damage to primary IECs, despite the suboptimal CB2R binding affinity compared to the established specific agonists including AM1241, JWH-133, and so on. Consistent with previous reports implicating cannabinoid signaling in intestinal epithelial restitution [14], both genetic and pharmacological evidence in our work indicated that the protective effect of CF-2 is mediated predominantly through CB2R. The pronounced efficacy of CF-2 can be mechanistically rationalized by its recently identified dual biofunction as both a CB2R agonist and an inhibitor of fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH), a key enzyme responsible for the degradation of anandamide (AEA) [28]. The dual pharmacological profile of CF-2 suggests a distinct mechanism of action within the endocannabinoid system. Because AEA has inherently low affinity for CB2R, excessive FAAH blockade may lead to toxic AEA accumulation and off-target effects rather than effective CB2R activation. CF-2 operates independently of AEA by directly activating CB2R. This is supported by the marked loss of its protective efficacy in CB2R-deficient mice, which indicates that alternative pathways, such as CB1R or transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) [56], do not primarily drive the observed phenotype. Alongside direct receptor activation, the moderate FAAH inhibition of CF-2 likely prevents AEA from reaching toxic concentrations while providing secondary anti-inflammatory benefits. Given that FAAH inhibition is known to suppress the cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) / prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) cascade [57], we hypothesize that CF-2 restricts the production of pro-inflammatory arachidonic acid metabolites. A decrease in these downstream inflammatory mediators could, at least in part, account for the superior efficacy of CF-2 over selective CB2R agonists observed in our experiments, though this interpretation is necessarily limited by the absence of direct lipidomic measurements in the present study.

While CF-2 has been confirmed to alleviate RIII significantly, several improvements need to be addressed for future investigation. Firstly, the lack of validation using patient-derived materials restricts the direct clinical applicability of the findings. Future research should focus on examining CB2R expression in intestinal tissues from irradiated RIII patients to establish clinical relevance. Then, while a HoxA10-reinforced, positive-feedback loop involving the CB2R/β‑catenin axis is suggested in this work, direct evidence elucidating the molecular underpinnings is currently lacking. Moreover, although our study primarily focuses on epithelial CB2R, the potential contributions from non-epithelial compartments, particularly immune cells, cannot be excluded. Future investigations aimed at resolving these issues will be pivotal for therapeutic optimization and translational advancement.

Admittedly, this work is confined to preclinical evaluation of CF-2, underscoring several priorities for subsequent investigation. Firstly, while the mechanistical regulation of IECs was characterized, effects on other intestinal cell types and intercellular communication remain to be clarified. Moreover, given the critical role of the gut microbiota in maintaining intestinal homeostasis [58, 59], the impact of CF-2 on microbial composition and metabolites warrants further exploration. To facilitate clinical translation, the efficacy of CF-2 merits comprehensively evaluated in humanized animal models and ultimately in clinical patients. From a clinical pharmacology perspective, given the association of RIII with radiotherapy, the combination of CF-2 with anticancer agents represents a promising avenue to enhance patient outcomes. Therefore, as CF-2 has not yet been assessed clinically, its optimal route of administration and dosage remain to be determined. Meanwhile, chemical structural modification or advanced formulation strategies may be pursued to improve druggability. Collectively, a comprehensive assessment integrating basic medicine, clinical medicine, pharmacology, medicinal chemistry, and pharmaceutics represents the critical potential for future research on CF-2.

In summary, diverging from the canonical paradigm of CB2R-mediated therapy through immunomodulation, we demonstrate the efficacy of intestinal epithelial CB2R activation in mitigating RIII. Mechanistically, HoxA10 is identified as a critical amplifier within the CB2R/β-catenin signaling cascade, ensuring robust signal transduction that suppresses ferroptosis and promotes the maintenance of intercellular tight junctions. Based on this axis, the potent CB2R agonist CF-2 was sifted from series of small-molecular compounds. Its therapeutic efficacy was validated in vivo, establishing a key reference for the further clinical development. Therefore, this study provides a viable treatment strategy targeting CB2R for alleviating RIII, along with a fresh understanding of the mechanistic foundation.

Methods

Cell and animal model

Primary intestine epithelial cells (IECs) were isolated as described previously [60]. Then, primary IECs were cultured in Epithelial cell medium (EpiCM) kit (Cat#CM-ZY002, Procell) supplemented with 1× penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B solution (Cat#PB180121, Procell). HT-29 (Human colorectal carcinoma cell line, Cat#CL-0118, Procell) was cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Cat#G4541, Servicebio) with 10% FBS (Cat#BC-SE-FBS07, Bio-Channel). Caco-2 (Human colon epithelial cell line, Cat# CL-0050, Procell) was cultured in Caco-2 Cell Complete Medium (Cat#CM-0050, Procell). HEK293T (Human embryonic kidney cell line, Cat# CL-0005, Procell) was cultured in DMEM (High glucose) Medium (Cat#PM150210, Procell) with 10% FBS.

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) wild-type male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Chongqing Laibite Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The 6 or 8-week-old mice weighing 18–22 g were fed at 25 ℃ and 55% humidity with a 12 h daylight cycle. CB2R gene knockout mice (C57BL/6JCya-CNR2em1/Cya, strain number S-KO-01562) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc (Suzhou, China). Mice had free access to a standard laboratory rodent food and water.

Irradiation model

For cells in vitro, before being irradiation-exposed, the culture medium was replaced with new media without FBS overnight. An hour before IR exposure, 500nM CB2R/FAAH modulator-2 (Cat#T67745, TargetMol), 157.4nM AM1241 (Cat#T6755, TargetMol), 157.4nM JWH-133 (Cat#1343, Tocris Biotech), 2.92µM GW842166X (Cat#T6527, TargetMol), 5.09 µM YX-2102 and 175.9nM YX-2125 (Synthesized in-house, and their chemical formulas are provided in Fig. S3A), 10µM SR144528 (Cat#HY-13439, MedChemExpress), 1µM JNJ42165279 (Cat#1346528-50-4, TargetMol) and 1µM Ferrostatin-1 (Cat#347174-05-4, TargetMol) were added into medium respectively according to the experimental design. Drug concentrations were converted based on their Ki values for CB2R to match those of CF‑2. For irradiation, cells were received a single dose of total abdominal irradiation with 8 Gy using a 60Co γ-ray source (Irradiation Center, Third Military Medical University).

To determine the optimal irradiation doses for establishing the RIII mice model and for subsequent mortality assessment, preliminary dose titration experiments were conducted. Mice received abdominal irradiation (12 to 16 Gy, respectively) with the rest of the body shielded by lead bricks. The experimental setup is arranged as shown in Figure S1A. After anesthesia with Delivector™ Avertin (Tribromoethanol, Cat#DW3106, Dowobio), mice were received a single dose of irradiation with 13 Gy (or 15 Gy for survival rate) using a 60Co γ-ray source (Irradiation Center, Third Military Medical University).

Transient cell transfection

To downregulate Hoxa10 and Ctnnb1 (coding β-catenin) expression, we transiently transfected Hoxa10 and Ctnnb1 siRNA (Sangon Biotech) into HT-29 cells, respectively. In particular, when cells were grown to about 30% confluency, siRNA was added into the cell culture using Lipofectamine 3000 (Cat#L3000008, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Cells were collected about 36 h after transfection for subsequent functional experiments. The sequences of siRNA and plasmid are involved in Supplemental Table S1.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

To overexpress HoxA10, we constructed a HoxA10 overexpressing plasmid (Cat#pHBLP003658, HanBio). The sequences were included in the Supplemental Table S3. A luciferase reporter plasmid containing the promoter region of Ctnnb1 (the gene encoding β-catenin) and co-transfected it with a HoxA10 overexpression plasmid into HEK293T cells via Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (Cat#L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufactures’ protocol. The Renilla luciferase plasmid was used as an internal control. The specific sequences were included in Supplemental Table S3.

Intestinal permeability assay

FITC-dextran (MW: 4000) reagent (Cat#T18988L, TargetMol) was adopted to determine the intestinal permeability in RIII mice model. Briefly, all mice were fasted for 4 h, and then mice were oral gavaged with FITC-dextran solution (200µL, 80 mg/mL). After 4 h, mice were euthanized and sacrificed. The orbital serum was harvested and immediately read at 480 nm excitation and 520 nm emission in black fluorescence 96-well microplate to determine the concentration of FITC-dextran.

Western blot assay

Protein extraction was conducted using RIPA lysis buffer (Cat#P0013, Beyotime) containing 1mM Phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Cat#ST506, Beyotime). After the concentrations were determined using the NanoPhotometer (Cat#N30-Touch, Implen Tech), protein was separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. The proteins were transferred onto the PVDF membrane surface using the wet transfer, and then blocked with QuickBlock™ Western Blocking Buffer (Cat#P0252, Beyotime).Upon completion of blocking, PVDF membrane was incubated with corresponding primary antibody overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies against CB2R (1:1000, Cat#ab3561, Abcam), β-Tubulin (1:2000, Cat#ab179513, Abcam), ZO-1 (1:800, Cat#AF5145, Affinity), Occludin (1:800, Cat#DF7504, Affinity), GPX4 (1:800, Cat#R381958, ZenBio), xCT/SLC7A11 (1:1000, Cat#12691, Cell Signaling Technology), Phospho-Histone H2AX (Ser139) (γ-H2AX) (1:500, Cat#AF5836, Beyotime), Histone H2A (1:1000, Cat#PTM-7034, PTM.Bio), β-Catenin (1:1000, Cat#8480, Cell Signaling Technology), HoxA10 (1:1500, Cat#26497-1-AP, Proteintech). Then, the PVDF membranes were washed with TBST (Tris-buffered saline, 0.1% Tween-20) (Cat# BL1683A, Biosharp), further incubated with Anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (1:5000, Cat#7074, Cell Signaling Technology) or Anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (1:5000, Cat#7076, Cell Signaling Technology) secondary antibody. After 5 times of cleaning with Tris-buffered saline with tween-20 for 5 min each time, the visualizing images were captured by Tanon 5200 Multi Automatic Chemiluminescence Image Analysis System (5200 Multi, Shanghai Tanon Life Science).

RT-qPCR assay

Total RNA in intestine or cultured cells was used RNAiso Plus (Cat#9109, Takara) for extraction. The RNA concentration was determined using a NanoPhotometer (Cat#N30-Touch, Implen Tech). After quantification, about 1000 ng of total RNA from each sample, with A260/A280 ratio at the range of 1.8-2.0, was then reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using PrimerScript FAST RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Cat#RR092A, Takara). Quantitative PCR was performed via SYBR Prime qPCR Set (Cat#BG0014, BgBiotech) to determine the relative expression of mRNA in a Real-Time PCR Detection System (CFX connect, Bio-Rad Laboratories). The total volume of the reaction system was 10 µL, including 5 µL of SYBR qPCR Master Mix, 0.4 µL of forward or reverse primers respectively, 1 µL of cDNA of each sample and 3.2 µL of DEPC water (Cat#R0603, Sangon). The detail sequences of primers have been listed in Supplementary Table 1. The thermal cycle program was set as follows: 95 °C for 2 min (Initial denaturing step), followed by 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 20 s (39 cycles). All samples were run in duplicates. Mouse Actb gene served as the internal control. The relative mRNA levels were analyzed using the 2− Ct method.

Transcriptomic and bioinformatics analysis

RNA-seq was performed on mouse colon tissue (with fecal contents gently removed), using three biological replicates per condition. Total RNA was isolated from the colonic tissues utilizing TRIzol Reagent according the manufacturer’s guidelines (Magen). RNA integrity and purity were evaluated spectrophotometrically via A260/A280 ratios using a Nanodrop ND-2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA) and electrophoretically via RNA integrity number (RIN) determination on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 4150 (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Only qualified samples will be used for library construction. Strand-specific paired-end libraries were generated employing the ABclonal mRNA-seq Lib Prep Kit according to vendor protocols. Terminally, sequencing was executed on Illumina Novaseq 6000 or MGISEQ-T7 instrument. Bioinformatic processing was conducted through a proprietary analytical framework developed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology. Differential expression was analyzed with DESeq2, applying an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 as statistical thresholds. The RNA-seq data in this work have been deposited to the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GEO Series accession number GSE328344).

Functional annotation and pathway enrichment were assessed through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses via the clusterProfiler R package. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Unlike conventional hypergeometric tests that focus exclusively on significantly altered genes and potentially overlook biologically relevant but statistically subtle changes, our approach captures broader functional patterns. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were reconstructed based on the STRING database. For species represented in STRING, interaction networks were retrieved directly; for non-represented species, target gene sequences were aligned via BLASTX against STRING reference proteins, with interaction maps inferred from orthologous relationships.

For external validation, human transcriptome datasets were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus repository (GSE193677, GSE109142), and the expression of each gene was extracted using GEO2R. Functional enrichment utilized GO annotations from the R package org.Hs.eg.db (v3.1.1) as background reference, with candidate genes mapped against this dataset and analyzed through ClusterProfiler (v3.14.4). Updated KEGG pathway annotations were accessed through the KEGG API. The top 20 enriched terms in each category were analyzed for graphical representation. Survival and correlation analyses were performed using data queried from the GEPIA2.0 database.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Mouse colon specimens were precisely weighed and subjected to homogenization in 9 volumes (v/w) of 4℃ sterile PBS containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The tissue lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the clarified supernatants were collected for the cytokines’ levels determination. The ELISA kits used are as follows: mouse TNF-α uncoated ELISA Kit (Cat#88-7324-88, Invitrogen), mouse IL-6 uncoated ELISA Kit (Cat#88-7064-88, Invitrogen), mouse IL-1β uncoated ELISA Kit (Cat#88–7013 A-88, Invitrogen), and mouse IL-10 uncoated ELISA Kit (Cat#88-7105-88, Invitrogen).

Oxidative stress analysis

The oxidative-sensitive fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Cat#S0033, Beyotime) was adopted to determine the level of intracellular ROS. At the end of experimental procedures, cells were respectively washed with non-sterile PBS and incubated with DCFH-DA (5 µM) for 30 min at 37℃ in the dark. Equal amounts of cells were washed and resuspended in medium for flow cytometry analysis. The C11-BODIPY 581/591 kit (Cat#D3861, Invitrogen) was adopted to determine the lipid peroxidation concentration in cells following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Cells were photographed under inverted microscope solution DMi8S platform (Leica) and analyzed with Image J software.

The cells were harvested, washed with non-sterile PBS, and lysed in RIPA (Strong) lysis buffer. Then, the indicators were respectively analyzed by CAT assay kit (Cat#BC0205, Solarbio), SOD assay kit (Cat#TE0727, Leagene biotech), GSH assay kit (Cat#A006-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Biotech), and MDA assay kit (Cat#A003-4-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Biotech) following the manufacturers’ protocol.

At the end of experimental procedures, cells were respectively washed with non-sterile PBS. Then, ferrous ions level was determined by mitochondrial ferrous ion green fluorescence detection kit (Cat#S1072, Beyotime) according to the manufactures’ protocol. The ferrous ion concentration was determined by measuring fluorescence intensity using a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX) and a fluorescence microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) analysis

The mice tissues were collected and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4). After fixing overnight on a slow shaker at room temperature, the tissues were embedded in paraffin for sectioning. Subsequently, the tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated by xylene and alcohol, respectively. For histological visualization, the sections were stained by H&E solution (Cat#AFIHC007, AiFang Biological) to observe pathological morphology. For each sample, three non-overlapping visual field images were randomly collected.

Immunofluorescence analysis

The collected tissue sections were dewaxed by conventional xylene and hydrated with gradient alcohol. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining (mIF) of the tissue sections was conducted by a single label signal amplification immunofluorescence staining kit (Cat#AFIHC022, AiFang Biological) or a three-color multiplex immunofluorescence staining kit (AFIHC023, AiFang Biologcal). According to the manufacturer’s protocols, the antigens were exposed via microwave repair. Each section was cocultured with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution at room temperature for 15 min, and then blocked with 10% goat serum for 15 min at room temperature. The respective primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4℃, and then washed three times with TBST. Polymer-HRP anti-mouse or -rabbit universal secondary antibody IgG (Cat#AFIHC002 or Cat#AFIHC003, AiFang Biological) was added, and the sections were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were washed with TBST, and the TYR fluorescent dye was dropped and reacted for 8 min, followed by three washes with TBST. The primary antibody A was removed by microwave treatment, and the sections were washed three times with TBST. After dropping goat serum for blocking, the primary antibody B was added, and the operation was repeated until all the antigens, including CB2R (Cat#3561, Abcam), ZO-1 (AFRM0361, AiFang Biological), Occludin (AFRP0025, AiFang Biological), EpCAM (AFRM0040, AiFang Biological), HoxA10 (Cat#26497-1-AP, Affinity), β-Catenin (Cat#8480, Cell signaling technology), were completely visualized. DAPI staining solution was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min, followed by three washes with TBST. The sections were mounted with an anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium, and the mIHC slides were imaged using an eight-channel fluorescence digital slide scanner (Model AF-KL-20-8, AiFang Biologcal, China). During the image acquisition stage, an eight-channel fluorescence digital slide scanner (AF-KL-20-8, AiFang Biologcal, China) was adopted to collect images of mIHC slides. In the image data analysis stage, custom algorithms were developed in the VISIOPHARM software (VISIOPHARM, Hoersholm, Denmark) to conduct data analysis on the images. To ensure the consistency of the analysis results, fixed and uniform threshold parameters were set through the VISIOPHARM application to identify and quantitatively analyze respective fluorescence signaling. After the analysis was completed, the analysis results were exported.

The Caco-2 cells were washed by non-sterile PBS for three times, and then fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 30 min. Next, cells were washed with non-sterile PBS for three times. Subsequently, cells were incubated with QuickBlock Blocking Buffer for Immunol Staining (Cat#P0260, Beyotime) for 30 min at room temperature. Primary ZO-1 Rabbit pAb (1:100, Cat#AF5145, Affinity) dissolved in dilution buffer (Cat#P0262, Beyotime) was incubated overnight at 4℃. After washing with PBS, secondary AF488-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:500, Cat#A0423, Beyotime) dissolved in dilution buffer (Cat#P0265, Beyotime) was incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Then, nuclei were stained with DAPI (Cat#C1006, Beyotime) and then sealed with an anti-quencher. Images were captured by a laser confocal microscope in the dark.

Cell viability and proliferation

Cell viability was evaluated by the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8, Cat#K1018, APExBio) assay. Following experimental intervention, cells were exposed to diluted 1× CCK-8 reagent and maintained at 37 °C for 2 h prior to spectrophotometric analysis at 450 nm. Each experimental condition was assessed in triplicate wells across a minimum of three separate trials.

Cell proliferation was assessed by Edu Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Cat#C0071, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, cells were seeded in a 6-well plate. Each well was treated with 500 µL of 10 µM EdU reagent and incubated for approximately 2 h. After washing three times with non-sterile PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution (Beyotime, Cat# P0099) for 15 min. The fixed cells were then incubated with the click reaction reagent for 30 min protected from light at room temperature. Finally, cell nuclei were counterstained using the 1× Hoechst reagent provided in the kit. The staining results were observed using an inverted microscope (Leica DMi8 S platform), and the data were analyzed with Leica Application Suite X software.

For colony formation, HT-29 cells were plated in 12-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 2 weeks. Then, the supernatant medium was updated about every 3 days. After colony formatted, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Cat#P0099, Beyotime) for 30 min at room temperature and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Cat#C0121, Beyotime) for 30 min at room temperature, then cells were washed with deionized water and then air-dried, finally took pictures.

Electron microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging, samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight, then refixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, and then dehydrated with acetone in a gradient of 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 100% (100% concentration repeated 3 times). Subsequently, each sample was infiltrated with dehydration agent and Epon-812 embedding agent at ratios of 3:1, 1:1, and 1:3 respectively, and then embedded with pure Epon-812 embedding agent. Ultrathin sections of 60 nm from ultramicrotome were precisely placed on copper grids. Then, sections were stained with uranyl acetate for 15 min with lead citrate for 2 min at room temperature. Image acquisition of copper mesh using JEM-1400FLASH TEM produced by Japan Electronics Corporation (JEOL).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Randomization protocols were employed for both animal allocation to treatment cohorts and microscopic field selection. Intergroup comparisons were statistically evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post-hoc test. Experimental procedures were independently replicated multiple times, with specific replication numbers detailed in the respective figure legends. Statistical computations were performed using GraphPad Prism and Origin software. A threshold of p < 0.05 (corresponding to 95% confidence intervals) was established as the criterion for statistical significance, with exact p-values for most comparisons presented graphically within the figures.
