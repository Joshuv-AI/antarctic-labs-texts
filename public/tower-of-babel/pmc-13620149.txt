The Labrador Sea as a conduit for AMOC lower-limb transport anomalies on multiple timescales
Authors: Jia-Jia Chen, M. Susan Lozier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is an essential element of the global climate system, yet its variability on timescales longer than those observed remains unsettled. Recent studies show that transport variability in the lower AMOC limb is maximized on density surfaces well below the density of maximum overturning. To explore the source and impact of this feature, we analyze lower-limb transport variability using climate simulations and observations from the Overturning in the Subpolar North Atlantic Program. We find that enhanced variability at select densities reflects transport redistribution within the lower limb and is not strongly linked with local or downstream AMOC variability on monthly to multidecadal timescales. Furthermore, the strong transport variability in dense waters leaving the Labrador Sea is tightly linked to variability entering the basin, with the opposing inflow and outflow largely canceling in basin-integrated diagnostics. These results underscore the need to account for density-space compensation and cancellation of opposing flows when interpreting the climatic relevance of strong subpolar transport variability.

Introduction

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) plays a central role in the Earth’s climate system by redistributing vast amounts of heat, salt, and carbon globally. Its upper limb carries warm waters northward from the tropics to polar latitudes, releasing heat to the atmosphere along the way, thereby impacting the weather and climate of surrounding regions1,2. As these waters lose heat, they become denser and sink, forming North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW), which includes upper NADW formed in the subpolar North Atlantic and lower NADW comprised mainly of denser overflow waters originating from the Nordic Seas. These waters constitute the AMOC lower limb that flows equatorward throughout the Atlantic.

With AMOC changes historically linked to deep water formation on various timescales2,3, oceanographers have long been interested in the amount of deep water formed in the Labrador, Irminger and Nordic Seas4–8 to understand how variability in each region impacts the AMOC. Over the past two decades, modelling studies have indicated strong links between Labrador Sea deep convection, density and AMOC variability7–10. However, summer hydrographic surveys from the 1990s11 suggested that the Labrador Sea contributes minimally to NADW volume. Consistent with these earlier estimates, direct observations from the Overturning in the Subpolar North Atlantic Program (OSNAP)12 later revealed that overturning across the eastern OSNAP section (i.e., in the Iceland and Irminger basins) dominates both the mean and monthly variability of subpolar overturning, with minimal contribution from the Labrador Sea13.

One modelling study14 attempted to reconcile the discrepancy between OSNAP observations and earlier modelling studies linking AMOC variability to Labrador Sea processes. It suggested that subsurface density anomalies in the Labrador Sea are strongly influenced by signals originating in the Irminger Sea, implying that the Labrador Sea is unlikely to be the primary source of AMOC variability. Another eddy-resolving modelling study15 showed that low-frequency AMOC anomalies at 45°N are preceded by pronounced lower-limb transport anomalies associated with enhanced water mass transformation (WMT) within the Labrador Sea Water (LSW) density range. On this basis, the authors argued that although the Labrador Sea contributes only weakly to the mean and monthly variability of the AMOC, it may play a disproportionately important role in driving low-frequency variability. Recent observational analyses, however, complicate this interpretation. While surface-forced water mass transformation accounts for a substantial fraction of the mean lower-limb transport, its variability explains only part of the AMOC variability on seasonal to interannual timescales, with the remainder arising from basin storage, interior mixing and advective processes16. Together, these studies leave unresolved how transport variability within specific density classes, including the LSW density range, relates to AMOC variability across timescales, and which density-space transport anomalies more effectively capture AMOC variability, both at the OSNAP section and farther downstream.

Here, we show that pronounced lower-limb transport variability largely reflects redistribution among density classes and, on monthly to multidecadal timescales, is only weakly related to AMOC variability at OSNAP and farther downstream. In the Labrador Sea, both the dense-water inflow and outflow correlate with AMOC variability, yet their opposing contributions largely cancel upon basin integration, thus substantially weakening the basin-scale relationship with the AMOC.

Linkage between outgoing and incoming transports in the Labrador Sea

Having shown that strong variability near \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{SD}\max }$$\end{document}σSDmax primarily reflects a redistribution of transport across density classes, we next examine how this variability evolves along subpolar circulation pathways, and in particular whether the dense transport anomalies near \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{SD}\max }$$\end{document}σSDmax are transmitted coherently from O-East into and out of the Labrador Sea, or are instead substantially modified within O-West before reaching downstream sections. To do so, we analyze directional density-bin transport anomalies across three connected sections: southward transport across O-East (OE-Out), northward transport into O-West (OW-In), and southward transport exiting O-West (OW-Out). In the primary analysis, these quantities are obtained by integrating transport over the full O-East or O-West section in the specified flow direction within the two density bins immediately denser than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{SD}\max }$$\end{document}σSDmax specific to each branch, rather than by forming the cumulative density-space integral from the bottom, as used to define the overturning streamfunction.

We show results from MPI-ESM1-2-HR (Fig. 3a) because the correlation between OW-In and OW-Out for this model is closest to the multimodel mean. This analysis shows that low-frequency transport anomalies evolve coherently across the three pathway components. In particular, variability in OW-Out (at its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{SD}\max }$$\end{document}σSDmax) is strongly linked to variability in OW-In (at its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{SD}\max }$$\end{document}σSDmax), indicating that anomalies entering the Labrador Sea are clearly expressed in the outgoing flow. Together, the OSNAP observations support the key elements of the model-based picture. On monthly timescales, transport variability exhibits partial compensation across adjacent density classes, although this compensation is more broadly distributed in density space than in the low-frequency model results. The eastern subpolar basin contributes most to monthly OSNAP MOC variability, and transport exiting the Labrador Sea remains strongly linked to upstream variability though modified during transit through the basin. These results indicate that, although the relative importance of individual processes varies with timescale, the structural relationships among density-space variability, pathway coherence, and overturning variability are consistent between models and observations.

Discussion

A key outcome of this study is that, although transport variability in density space peaks at a density (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{SD}\max }$$\end{document}σSDmax) denser than the density of maximum overturning (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{moc}}$$\end{document}σmoc), this pronounced variability is only weakly related to fluctuations in local and downstream overturning strength. Similar separations between the depth or density of maximum overturning transport and that of maximum variability have been reported across a wide range of latitudes and models15,21. Here we show that this separation persists from monthly to multidecadal timescales in both climate models and OSNAP observations. Pronounced dense variability in the western subpolar North Atlantic should therefore not be interpreted as a direct proxy for AMOC strength.

We attribute this weak linkage to substantial transport compensation within the AMOC lower limb. Composite analyses show that enhanced transport anomalies near \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{SD}\max }$$\end{document}σSDmax are accompanied by anomalies of the opposite sign at adjacent densities, thus reducing their net contribution when transport is integrated across the densities that comprise the lower limb. Decomposition of the transport anomalies indicates that this compensation is driven primarily by changes in layer thickness rather than by compensating flow speed anomalies. This strong thickness compensation essentially reflects the redistribution of transport within the lower limb without producing proportional changes in the overturning strength. The thickness-driven compensation surrounding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{SD}\max }$$\end{document}σSDmax could be related to strong variability in the surface outcrop area of those densities.

Across the model ensemble, variability in the eastern subpolar basin exhibits stronger correlations with both the OSNAP MOC and the downstream MOC than variability in the western basin across most density classes and timescales. This result is consistent with previous studies highlighting the dominant role of the Irminger and Iceland basins in shaping the AMOC mean state and variability13,22.

Although basin-integrated overturning in the western subpolar basin exhibits only weak correlations with the downstream MOC in the multimodel mean, decomposing the western (Labrador) basin into its northward inflow and southward outflow components reveals a markedly stronger relationship. This contrast arises because inflow and outflow anomalies are strongly correlated yet opposite in sign, so their lateral integration across the basin leads to partial cancellation, contributing to the weak relationship between basin-integrated western overturning and overall MOC variability. At the same time, the broadly similar correlations of OE-Out, OW-In, and OW-Out with the downstream AMOC indicate that variability most relevant to downstream overturning is already present in the transport exiting OSNAP East. As shown in previous studies, the signal exiting OE-Out mainly reflects buoyancy-forced transformation in the Iceland and Irminger basins, as well as the broader eastern subpolar basin23,24. This variability is subsequently advected into the Labrador Sea, where it is modified, often amplified and shifted toward denser classes during transit25. Despite these modifications, its relationship with the downstream AMOC is largely unchanged from that exiting OSNAP-East. This interpretation is supported by the comparison between OW-In and Labrador Sea SFWMT: OW-In explains substantially more variance in OW-Out than SFWMT, and including both increases explained variance only modestly. Consistent with recent work suggesting that dense waters exported from the Labrador Sea reflect cumulative transformation across the broader subpolar gyre, rather than formation within a single localized region26–28, these results indicate that the Labrador Sea acts primarily as a conduit for transport anomalies linked to AMOC strength variability. This conduit interpretation refers specifically to the incoming and exported Labrador Sea transport anomalies, which are connected to transport variability exiting OSNAP-East and are significantly correlated with downstream and OSNAP-Full MOC strength. It does not imply that the local Labrador Sea overturning residual is inherited directly from upstream. OSNAP observations, despite their limited duration and restriction to monthly timescales, support this interpretation by revealing similar compensating structures and strong correlations between incoming and outgoing transports across OSNAP-West.

This framework helps reconcile our results with previous studies emphasizing the importance of Labrador Sea water mass transformation for downstream AMOC variability15. Our results do not argue against such a contribution. Rather, they suggest that across the broader model ensemble, Labrador Sea SFWMT explains only part of the downstream signal. That downstream signal, expressed as AMOC variability at 50°N, is more consistently reflected in the upstream transport anomalies (OE-Out, OW-In, and OW-Out) than in SFWMT. Thus, although Labrador Sea transformation can modify the dense signal, it does not emerge as the dominant or most robust control on downstream AMOC variability across models. This distinction is important for identifying indicators of the downstream MOC. When basin-wide section data are available, O-East and O-Full provide the clearest measures of downstream AMOC strength, whereas decomposing Labrador Sea overturning into inflow and outflow components helps recover the downstream-relevant variability that is obscured in the basin-integrated western overturning in the Labrador Sea.

These conclusions should be interpreted with awareness of the limitation of CMIP6 climate models in the subpolar North Atlantic. Models differ substantially in stratification, overflow representation, and the location and intensity of deep convection and water mass transformation, particularly in the Labrador Sea, leading to large inter-model spread in density structure and variability26,28–30. Our aim is therefore not to quantify absolute overturning or water mass transformation in any individual region, nor to attribute all model differences to a single mechanism. Instead, it focuses on the structural relationship between transport variability across density classes and integrated overturning that persist across models and are also evident in observations. Across the CMIP6 ensemble, key features, such as strong variability in dense waters, compensation across density classes, coherent pathway evolution, and the weak projection of dense variability onto overturning strength, emerge despite model spread. Similar structural signatures are also evident in OSNAP observations on monthly timescales, lending observational support to the mechanisms identified here. In that sense, the value of the multimodel framework here is not to provide a single deterministic picture, but to identify which features emerge robustly despite large differences in model mean state and variability.

An important implication of our results is that diagnosing AMOC variability based on transport at a single density or depth can be misleading. Variability below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{moc}}$$\end{document}σmoc may appear prominent, yet we show here that its relationship to local or downstream overturning strength can be tenuous when compensation across density classes is strong. This perspective clarifies why studies focusing on lower-limb variability or transformation often identify pronounced signals that show limited correspondence with overturning metrics defined by the maximum streamfunction. Extending sustained observations such as OSNAP will be essential for further constraining how these structural relationships evolve on longer timescales and for anchoring model performance in simulating AMOC variability.

Temporal filtering

For all model simulations, monthly output from the first 300 years of the preindustrial control (piControl) integrations is used. Annual means are computed from the monthly data prior to filtering. Low-frequency (LF) variability is isolated using a fourth-order Butterworth low-pass digital filter with a 20-year cutoff period, while high-frequency (HF) variability is obtained using a Butterworth high-pass filter with a 10-year cutoff. Sensitivity tests using alternative cutoff periods (e.g. 10 and 15 years for the low-pass filter) yield qualitatively similar results and do not affect the main conclusions. For OSNAP observations, the shorter record length limits the analysis to monthly timescales. No temporal filtering is applied unless explicitly stated.

For diagnostics that depend on variability amplitude in density space, including the identification of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{{SD}\max }$$\end{document}σSDmax, all calculations are performed on temporally filtered, mean-removed time series.

Surface-forced water mass transformation

Surface-forced water mass transformation (SFWMT) was computed offline in density space following the standard Walin framework and using the same formulation as in previous studies of subpolar overturning variability15,23. Briefly, monthly surface heat and freshwater fluxes were combined with surface temperature and salinity to obtain the surface density flux, which was then integrated over the outcrop area of each density class to yield transformation as a function of density. The calculation used the monthly model fields hfds, wfo, tos, and sos, and was performed on the same density grid as the overturning analysis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \sigma$$\end{document}Δσ = 0.02 kg m-3). Positive values indicate transformation toward denser classes. Because only seven models provide the full set of required surface variables, all comparisons between SFWMT and transport variability are based on this seven-model subset.

Observations and models

Observational estimates of the overturning circulation are based on data from the Overturning in the Subpolar North Atlantic Program (OSNAP). We use eight years of monthly OSNAP observations spanning August 2014 to July 2022, covering the full OSNAP section (O-Full) and its two coast-to-coast subsections, O-West and O-East (Fig. 1a). The OSNAP observing system provides gridded property and velocity fields, MOC and other time series across the section, derived from a combination of moored instruments, gliders, and ship-based measurements. The processing steps include quality control, interpolation to a common grid, and the calculation of transports in density coordinates, following established methodologies32. We use the gridded temperature, salinity, and velocity products to compute the overturning streamfunction and associated transport diagnostics in density space, consistent with previous OSNAP-based studies19.

We analyze simulations from nine coupled climate models participating in CMIP6 together with one high-resolution coupled model (CESM1.3-HR), using 300 years from each preindustrial control simulation to isolate internally generated variability. The full list of models and their key characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

The resulting ensemble spans six distinct ocean model formulations, with two model pairs differing primarily in horizontal resolution, providing a range of subpolar circulation representations without targeting specific processes. The nine CMIP6 models were drawn from the CMIP6 archive based on the availability of long preindustrial control simulations and the three-dimensional fields required for density-space diagnostics. We used Heuzé et al.29 as a broad reference to exclude models with extreme bottom-density biases in the Greenland–Iceland–Norwegian Seas and subpolar gyre, which could possibly dominate density-space structure and hinder inter-model comparison. Beyond this exclusion of clear outliers, no selection was made based on overturning behavior. Thus, the final ensemble represents a quasi-random subset spanning a range of ocean model formulations and horizontal resolutions. Accordingly, our analysis does not rely on the fidelity of any individual model, but instead emphasizes relationships that are consistent across models, timescales, and independent observations.

To ensure consistency with observational diagnostics, model output is first interpolated onto the OSNAP section grid, and velocity components are rotated to obtain the section-normal velocity. The overturning streamfunction and associated transport metrics are then computed using the same methodology as applied to OSNAP observations.

While our primary results are based on multimodel statistics, two individual models are used in selected figures for illustration. MPI-ESM1-2-HR is shown in Fig. 3a because the correlation between OW-Out and OW-In in this model is close to the multimodel mean, making it a representative example of the ensemble behavior. CESM2 is used in selected figures to illustrate the mean overturning structure and spatial patterns of variability. Neither model is chosen for superior performance; rather, the qualitative behaviors highlighted are representative of those found across the ensemble.
