Can the integration of new rules into a clinical decision support system reduce the incidence of acute kidney injury and hyperkalemia among hospitalized older adults: a protocol for a stepped-wedge, cluster-randomized trial (DETECT-IP)
Authors: Anaïs Payen, Nour Elhouda Tlili, Etienne Cousein, Laurie Ferret, Antoine Le Bozec, Aurélie Lenglet, Romaric Marcilly, Pierre Pilven, Arnaud Potier, Chloé Rousselière, Julien Soula, Laurine Robert, Jean-Baptiste Beuscart
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical decision support systems (CDSSs) enable the automated, real-time detection of situations associated with a risk of adverse drug events (ADEs). However, the effectiveness of CDSS in reducing ADEs has yet to be demonstrated. We have chosen to focus on the detection of ADE such as hyperkalemia and/or acute kidney injury (AKI), which are common among hospitalized older adults. The present study’s primary objective is to use a CDSS to reduce the number of ADEs (such as AKI and/or hyperkalemia) that occur in hospitalized older adults.MethodsThis is a multicenter, stepped-wedge, cluster-randomized study involving five hospitals. Each hospital will start with a control period (i.e., routine care, during which each center’s CDSS is deactivated) and then switch to an intervention period (during which the CDSS is activated). The intervention will be the use of a CDSS and a strategy for managing and transmitting alerts to clinical pharmacists. The rules concerning AKI and hyperkalemia have been drafted and reviewed by a multidisciplinary group. Each rule created in the CDSS is associated with a standardized procedure, based on a review of the literature. Older patients (aged 65 or over) admitted to a participating general medicine ward, a surgical ward, or obstetrics ward will be eligible for inclusion after the provision of verbal informed consent.DiscussionThis study will assess the effectiveness of the CDSS in reducing the incidence of AKI and hyperkalemia. The implementation of the CDSS can assist clinical pharmacists in their daily work and is expected to prevent ADEs.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05923983. Administrative information

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Adverse drug events (ADEs) are associated with multimorbidity and polymedication and are therefore frequent in hospitalized older adults [2]. In turn, ADEs increases the risks of morbidity, mortality and unscheduled hospitalizations and may lead to additional costs [3, 4]. Preventing ADEs in hospitalized older adults is therefore a priority. Several strategies have been developed to reduce the risk of ADE in older patients, such as medication review, deprescribing protocols or tools for identifying inappropriate prescriptions [5–7].

With the massive computerization of prescription systems and healthcare data, clinical decision support systems (CDSSs) are seen as a promising means of detecting situations linked to ADEs in real-time and suggesting corrective actions. However, CDSSs still need to be optimized on a practical level [8]. For example, two studies have demonstrated the lack of effectiveness of CDSSs, due mainly to the irrelevance of their rules [9, 10]. Several systematic reviews of the literature have found that CDSSs have a low impact on ADEs in routine practice, (including in the older adults) [8, 11]. Firstly, the iatrogenic risk is not reliably detected because the CDSS’s poorly specific rules leading to over-alerting [9, 10]. Secondly, the CDSS often interrupts the physician at the time of order entry, which can induce alert fatigue [12–17]. All these findings suggest that the effectiveness of CDSSs in preventing ADE could be increased by complying with two key aspects: the conception of ADE-specific rules and careful, appropriate integration of these rules into the hospital’s care processes [18]. These aspects steps require a variety of skills and a multi-disciplinary team comprising clinical pharmacists, pharmacologists, specialists physicians, human factors experts, and medical IT experts. The DETECT-IP project has been designed to address these challenges. For the last few years, our team has been developing methods and systems for detecting high-risk situations and evaluating the iatrogenic risk (e.g., in a European-Union-funded project entitled “Patient Safety Through Intelligent Procedures in Medication”) [19]. We are focusing on hyperkalemia and acute kidney injury (AKI) because these are frequent, serious events in hospitalized older adults [20–24]. In a hospital setting, respectively 80.5% and 58.8% of cases of hyperkalemia and AKI are induced by drugs [20]. Moreover, hyperkalemia and AKI are easily detected automatically because their definitions are standardized and based on laboratory variables. Furthermore, we suggest including the pharmacist in the CDSS management process. Indeed, it has been shown that the involvement of clinical pharmacists improves physicians’ acceptance of alerts triggered by the CDSS [25]. We hypothesize that the integration of appropriate rules into a CDSS and the analysis of alerts by a clinical pharmacist can reduce the frequency of hyperkalemia and AKIs.

Trial design {8}

DETECT-IP is a prospective, multicenter, stepped-wedge, cluster-randomized, controlled trial (Table 1). Every 6 weeks, an investigating center switches from the control period to the intervention period (Fig. 1).

This trial was designed in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for stepped-wedge, cluster-randomized trials [26] and the SPIRIT guidelines for clinical trials (Fig. 2) [1].

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

The trial involves five hospitals in the French cities/towns of Amiens, Le Krelim-Bicêtre, Lille, Luneville, and Valenciennes: Amiens University Hospital, Kremlin-Bicêtre University Hospital, Lille University Hospital, Luneville General Hospital, and Valenciennes General Hospital.

The randomized clinical trial was preceded by a preliminary phase in which the use of the CDSS in the five centers was standardized (see the “Intervention description {11a}” section). Furthermore, semidirective interviews were conducted with two senior physicians, two junior physicians, two nurses, a biologist, and a clinical pharmacist, in one department of each participating center. The aim is to understand how ADEs are prevented or managed during the control phase (i.e., before starting the intervention (SO-6).)

This stepped-wedge, cluster-randomized, controlled trial comprises a control group and an intervention group. The study centers were randomized at the start of the study, in order to determine when each will switch from the control period to the intervention period (Fig. 1). Every 6 weeks, a hospital center switches from the control period to the intervention period. The unit of randomization is the hospital (n = 5), and the unit of analysis is the patient, or the prescription, or the ADE.

At the start of the study, all the hospitals were in the control group, i.e. with the CDSS deactivated (period 1). At the start of period 2, the hospital that was randomly selected reactivate its CDSS and form the intervention group (ni = 1), while the other hospitals remain in the control group (nc= 4). At the start of period 3, another hospital joins the intervention group (ni = 2), while three hospitals remain in the control group. At the start of period 4, a new hospital joins the intervention group (ni = 3), and two hospitals remain in the control group. At the start of period 5, a new hospital joins the intervention group (ni = 4), and only one hospital remain in the control group. Lastly, at the start of period 6, all hospitals joins the intervention group (ni = 5).

Eligibility criteria {10}

The main inclusion criteria is age 65 or over, hospitalization for 3 days or more, and affiliation with the French national health insurance system. The patient (or their next of kin, for patients with neurocognitive disorders) must provide verbal informed consent. The exclusion criteria are age under 65, discharge or death after less than 3 days of hospitalization, receipt of palliative care or end-of-life care on admission to the ward, legal protection (curatorship), lack of affiliation with the national health insurance system, and the absence of verbal consent for participation in the study.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

A clinical research associate (CRA) and/or pharmacist is in charge of assessing patient eligibility and obtaining an informed consent. When the patient is affected by a major neurocognitive disorder and/or delirium (making it impossible to obtain informed consent), the patient’s next of kin decides whether or not the patient is included. Patients or next of kin receive written and verbal information on the study’s objectives and procedures, as required by the independent ethics committee/institutional review board (IEC/IRB). The patient’s consent is documented on an electronic case report form (e-CRF).

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

Data is collected in accordance with the French National Data Protection Commission (Commission nationale de l’informatique et des libertés : CNIL, no. 922237). As required by French legislation, the participants’ verbal informed consent is required for the collection of personal data from hospital records.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

During the control phase (i.e., usual care), the CDSS is deactivated. For each hospitalized patient, pharmacists review the prescription via the computerized physician order entry (CPOE) system. If a patient is considered to be at risk of AKI or hyperkalemia, the pharmacist assesses the situation and issues a PI to the medical team in charge of the patient. This intervention will probably be subject to both inter- and intrahospital variability. Data related to the hospital stay is collected and extracted from the CPOE by the CDSS’s developers and will be cross-referenced with the e-CRF so that only patients who have given their verbal, informed consent are selected.

Intervention description {11a}

The use of the CDSS is linked to each center’s clinical pharmacy activity [27–29]. The CDSS conducts a real-time data analysis and produces alerts for situations where there is a risk of an ADEor a proven ADE. The clinical pharmacist in charge examines the alert in three steps: (i) verification of technical validity (comparison of the rule’s appraisal elements with the patient’s record); (ii) evaluation of the alert’s pharmaceutical relevance, with the issuance of a PI if necessary; (iii) follow-up of whether the prescribing physician has accepted or not the PI. Before the start of the stepped-wedge trial, we carried out a preliminary phase of creating, integrating and testing the rules in the CDSS. Firstly, the rules were drafted in a seminatural language by an expert group, on the basis of the scientific literature and clinical expertise [19]. We have 12 rules for preventing ADEs (six for AKI and six for hyperkalemia) and 19 rules for monitoring the event (18 for AKI and one for hyperkalemia). All the centers reviewed and corrected the rules. The rules were then coded in computer language in collaboration with the industrial partners (Keenturtle and Quinten). Next, we tested the rules in real life in the five investigating centers, by using a CDSS mode in which alerts are visible only to the centers’ principal investigators. This approach enabled us to detect errors in how the rules were drafted.

Lastly, we created a set of standardized courses of action for each rule. These measures were based on a review of the literature and subsequently proofread by a committee of experts (comprising several pharmacists, an infectious disease specialist, a geriatrician, and a nephrologist) and then by all the centers for validation. The CDSS developers and the clinical pharmacists ensured that these standardized courses of action are integrated into the CDSS so that the latter are readily available to any clinical pharmacist analyzing the alerts.

The intervention is carried out in addition to the usual care. The intervention phase starts at the beginning of period 2 when the CDSS is activated (Fig. 1). During this phase, the CDSS is used to detect a risk of AKI or hyperkalemia or confirmed AKI or hyperkalemia. An alert is triggered when the patient’s data matches all the rule’s criteria. The clinical pharmacist analyzes the alert from a technical and pharmaceutical viewpoints. Depending on the standardized course of action, the pharmacist may issue a PI to the medical team in charge of the patient.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions were not possible to define, as the interventions are allocated by hospitals.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

To avoid the loss of information at-risk situations that have not been analyzed by other pharmacists, a pharmacist at each center checks for non-analyzed alerts on a weekly basis.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

No types of concomitant care is prohibited during the trial.

There are no provisions for post-trial care.

Outcomes {12}

For the primary objective, the outcome is the occurrence of one or both of the following events: AKI and/or hyperkalemia. Hyperkalemia is defined as a high blood potassium value; the cut-off is set by each hospitals’ lab but is usually > 5.0 mmol/L). The definition of AKI is based on the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes criteria: elevation in creatinine by a factor of 1.5 over a period of 7 days or less (AKI stage 1) [30]. The outcomes corresponding to the above-listed SOs are as follows.

SO-1: Safety of the intervention. The PI linked to the alert may lead to a change in the prescription that could induce a subsequent ADEs (e.g., hypokalemia induced by the discontinuation of potassium supplementation, or a report of a high blood pressure following the discontinuation of an anti-hypertensive treatment during an AKI). The pharmacist reports the occurrence of any ADE associated with a change in prescription.

SO-2: Indicator of the pharmaceutical relevance. When an alert is triggered, the pharmacist analyzes the patient’s record to determine whether or not a PI should be sent to the medical team. The number of alerts leading to a PI being sent on to the medical team is divided by the total number of alerts for the same rule triggered by the CDSS and analyzed by a pharmacist.

SO-3: Indicator of medical relevance. Upon receiving the PI, the medical team assesses the relevance of the intervention and decides whether or not to accept it. The number of the PIs accepted by the medical team is divided by the total number of PIs for the same rule sent by the pharmacists following a CDSS alert. Furthermore, the CLEO scale (developed by the French Society for Clinical Pharmacy (Société Française de Pharmacie Clinique) is applied to all the PIs sent to the medical team [31]. The CLEO scale provides a standardized assessment of the clinical, economic, and organizational impact of a given PI.

SO-4 Time period for prescription changes. The respective mean time intervals between the issuance of an alert by the CDSS, the analysis of the alert by the pharmacist, the transmission of the PI to the medical team, and the decision to accept (or not) the PI by the medical team.

SO-5: Health economic assessment. This evaluation is divided into three parts: (i) the intervention cost, corresponding to the mean time spent creating and integrating the rules into the CDSS; (ii) the usage cost, corresponding to the time spent analyzing alerts triggered during the study; and (iii) the intervention cost, corresponding to the cost of preventing and managing AKI or hyperkalemia during a hospital stay.

SO-6: Organizational impact of using a CDSS. In one department of each participating center, a team of human factors specialists investigates the detection and management of AKI and hyperkalemia events. The aim is to understand the role of each professional (senior physicians, junior physicians, nurses, clinical pharmacists and medical biologists), their work processes, and the technologies used in the management of medication-related iatrogenic events (hyperkalemia and AKI). This analysis is carried out before and after the activation of the CDSS in the centers, in order to identify and explain the impact of the introduction of the DETECT-IP rules on their workflows.

Sample size {14}

In this stepped-wedge, cluster-randomized, controlled trial, the unit of randomization is the center. The number of participants depends on the correlation between the participants in the same cluster, as estimated with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). No data are available for determining the ICC for the primary endpoint ADE in a population similar to that expected in our study. However, we have estimated the ICC to be 0.005 on the basis of on the ADAMS study [32], which provides ICC values for a large number of variables in primary care. The estimateADE rate is 10% in the control group (4.5% for hyperkalemia and 8.0% for AKI, with both events concomitantly in some cases) [20]. Based on these values, we estimated that 7% in the interventional group were at risk of hyperkalemia and/or AKI (70% of ADEs are considered to be preventable, and the system will correct about 40% of these). With the five-cluster, six-phase design, a total of 4920 patients will have to be recruited (164 per cluster and per phase, in a two-sided test with an alpha risk of 5% and a power of power = 80%). All cases of AKI and hyperkalemia are included as events, regardless of the causes; this has been taken into account in the sample size calculation.

Recruitment {15}

The recruitment process is assisted by the use of two documents: (i) a detailed study information sheet given to the patient upon arrival in the ward and (ii) a plain-language study summary flyer for the patients and their families. Patients are recruited in certain wards of each hospital, in which the pharmacists and physicians have agreed to participate in the study and are covered by the CDSS.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

The various sources of data are as follows: (i) the e-CRF, containing all information on compliance with the inclusion and the exclusion criteria, the hospital’s identification of the patients, the PI, and the pharmacist time associated with the PI during the control phase (with a deactivated CDSS), and (ii) the CDSS, which provides all information on the triggered alerts (type, date, time, technical validity, the PIs issued by the pharmacists, and the follow-up of the PIs) and the data in electronic medical records on the prescriptions, the laboratory results, the diagnostic codes related to the hospital stay, and the patient’s administrative data (age, sex, length of hospital stay, the hospital ward, etc.). The electronic medical records are extracted via the CDSS during both phases of the study. During the intervention phase, all the triggered alerts are recorded in a file via the CDSS. The data concerning these alerts are collected and extracted by the CDSS’s developers and cross-referenced with the e-CRF, so that only the patients who have given their verbal informed consent are selected.

All the extracted data are sent daily to a health data warehouse (HDW) approved by the French government (i.e., with “hébergeurs de données de santé” certification). Each day, a digital fingerprint is calculated for these files, which are then automatically transferred to the HDW. Following the reception of these files, a new digital fingerprint is calculated and compared with the initial digital fingerprint. If the two fingerprints are different, the files is transferred back to the HDW for a new analysis. The data will be sent to the HDW for 36 weeks. Hospital stay data will be cross-checked with the e-CRF data; so that only the patients who have given their verbal, informed consent are selected.

The primary outcome will then be evaluated on the resulting datasets. The SO-1 is recorded in the e-CRF by the investigators, who track ADEs linked to a PI. SO-2 and SO-3 will be calculated from the data provided by the CDSS. SO-4 will be determined using the e-CRF during the control phase and using data from the CDSS during the intervention phase. SO-5 will be assessed on the basis of the time spent by the pharmacist creating and integrating rules into the CDSS (time measured beforehand and recorded in the initial phase of the project), the time spent by the pharmacist managing alerts issued by the CDSS, and comparing the cost of hospitalization in the event of an AKI or hyperkalemia with that of hospitalization without the event (according to data from Medical Informatics Department at Lille University Hospital). SO-6 will be assessed using qualitative, semidirective interviews with the healthcare professionals involved in the detection and management of ADEs (such as AKI or hyperkalemia) in one department from each investigating center [33]. The data will be analyzed in a deductive approach based on a Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety 2.0 model [34]. This qualitative study will be carried out by human factors specialists.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

The DETECT-IP Working Group comprises all the investigating pharmacists, the staff actively involved in the trial from the five centers, the sponsor and the project manager. To promote the involvement of pharmacists in the study, monthly meetings are held to track the patient inclusion rate. A FAQ is set up to answer any questions encountered during the study. At the same time, the project manager checks on a weekly basis that the files are being transferred correctly to the HDW.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

All statistical analyses will be carried out independently in the Statistics, Evaluation, Economics and Data Management Unit at Lille University Hospital, using SAS software (version 9.4 or higher). All statistical tests will be two-sided, with a significance threshold of 0.05. The analyses will be performed on all randomized patients, by allocated group (intention-to-treat). A detailed statistical analysis plan will be drafted and validated before the database is frozen. The inclusion criteria will be described for the intervention group and for the control group. Quantitative variables will be described as the mean ± standard deviation (for variables with a Gaussian distribution) or the median [interquartile range] (for all other cases). The normality of the data distributions will be checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test and graphically with histograms. Qualitative variables (except variables from the semidirective interviews) will be described as the frequency (percentage) per modality. The trial will be reported in compliance with the 2010 extension of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials to cluster-randomized trials [26].

The primary outcome is as follows: the intervention and control groups will be compared with regard to the proportion of patients with a AKI or hyperkalemia, using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) (binomial distribution, log link function) including group and time as a fixed effect and center as a random effect [35].

The ratio of success rates (the relative risk) between the groups (intervention vs. control) and its 95% confidence interval (estimated from the GLMM) will be reported as a measure of effect size. The absolute difference in success rates will also be calculated with a GLMM (binomial distribution, identity link function).

SOs: The methodology used for the primary outcome will be applied to compare the proportion of inappropriate prescriptions. The other SOs will be analyzed quantitatively or qualitatively.

Interim analyses {21b}

No formal interim analyses are planned during the trial, since the study does not include any additional analyses (e.g laboratory tests). In fact, the study is integrated into each hospital’s care procedures, and the trial’s intervention is known to be feasible and safe for the intended population.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

No additional analyses are planned during the trial.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

We do not expect missing data for the primary endpoint. In fact, the primary endpoint is collected passively from the information system (with all the laboratory data obtained during the stay in question): the events potentially corresponding to the endpoint are automatically detected, and a review by two experts is then used to determine whether the event corresponds to the endpoint. This approach has been used in several of our previous studies [20, 36].

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant-level data, and statistical code {31c}

The full protocol in French (as approved by IEC/IRB) is available on request. Anonymized data will be made available on reasonable request and in compliance with the French legislation.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

A data monitoring committee was constituted to discuss any ADE (whether serious or not) that may occur after a PI.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

The expected risks are minimal. Indeed, during the intervention, what may happen to the patient in addition to usual care is to have an expert review of his/her prescription by a clinical pharmacist, the application of expert recommendations based on scientific literature, and/or a multidisciplinary discussion involving pharmacists, physicians, and/or pharmacologists. However, it cannot be excluded that some modifications of prescriptions will themselves have undesired effects (recurrence of a symptom or re-evolution of a pathology), even if the multiple expert and human validations (included in the process) strongly limit this risk. A study has shown that an increase in healthcare professional’s awareness of patients at risk of acute ADEs (detected automatically by a CDSS) was associated with a lower incidence of these events [37].

Responsibilities of the investigator: For each adverse event, the investigator assesses the severity and the possible causal link between the adverse event and the trial- or protocol-specific procedures. Only serious adverse events potentially related to trial- or protocol-specific procedures are reported to the sponsor. The investigator must report adverse events that are not related to the study to the relevant drug safety department. The investigator will follow-up any adverse events and must notify the sponsor of any new safety event without delay, by fax or by e-mail.

Data and safety monitoring board (DSMB): as recommended by the IEC/IRB, a DSMB is formed. It monitors the progress of the study and analyzes safety data in general and the occurrence of ADEs such as hyperkalemia and acute renal failure in particular.

The DSMB’s meeting schedule is set at the first DSMB meeting, which took place before the first inclusion in order to present the study to the members of the board.

The trial results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and other media. The results will be presented at national and international conferences corresponding to the various disciplines involved (geriatrics, clinical pharmacy, pharmacology, and medical informatics). Discussion

The study’s objective is to reduce the number of ADEs such as AKI or hyperkalemia using CDSS in hospitalized older adults. We are focusing on these two adverse events because they are frequent and often linked to treatment with one or more drugs. A systematic review of the literature has shown that electrolyte disorders are frequent in older adults [8]. In previous work, we showed that it is possible to detect these events automatically and reliably [20, 36]. In response to these challenges, new rules have been integrated into the CDSS. We have developed specific ADE prevention and detection rules, on the basis of our previous work, the scientific literature, and the expertise of the healthcare professionals involved in the development of the CDSS. Furthermore, we have drafted specific courses of action for each rule, in order to standardize patient management at all centers. We are planning a qualitative, human factors study to investigate the extent to which alerts and procedures linked to CDSS are embedded in the workflow. The study faces a number of challenges: (i) the specificity of the prevention and surveillance rules created in the CDSS, (ii) the variability in the analysis of patient records by pharmacists during the control phase, and (iii) the requirement for all centers to be fully operational at the same time. The main sources of bias of this study are likely to be variability in the use of the CDSS prior to the implementation of the study (by experienced or non-experienced users), the sensitivity of detection of the situations identified in the study, and the use of two different CDSSs.

Trial status

This report concerns version 3.1 of the trial protocol, dated March 14, 2024. During the study, we submitted various amendments concerning the addition of patients with neuro-cognitive disorders, a reduction in the time required to obtain patient consent, and the production of an information sheet for patient carers. The process of submitting, analyzing, and responding to these amendments delayed submission of the study protocol. In fact, the IEC/IRB’s positive agreement was mandatory to ensure the study’s viability and guarantee optimization of patient enrollment. Recruitment started on 02 October 2023, and the target recruitment completion date is June 10, 2024. At the time of writing, five hospitals have been recruited as investigating centers. Since October 2, 2023, 4600 patients have been enrolled.
