Multi‑omics identification of MSI2 as a super-enhancer‑driven vulnerability in MYCN‑amplified neuroblastoma
Authors: Xi Zhang, Yang Yang, Mengzhen Zhang, Kaisi Fan, Zhaorong Huang, Yang Zhang, Lingkai Kong, Qinming Chen, Rongying Tan, Shimeng Zhang, Daiyue Yu, Xiaozhe Xiong, Kai Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroblastoma (NB) is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children and is characterized by marked clinical heterogeneity and poor prognosis. MYCN amplification drives NB tumorigenesis through epigenetic reprogramming and is frequently accompanied by a copy-number gain of the long arm of chromosome 17 (17q). Epigenetic dysregulation of enhancer landscapes—particularly large regulatory elements termed super‑enhancers (SEs), which are enriched for H3K27ac and bound by lineage-specific master transcription factors (TFs)—establishes distinct NB cellular identities and states. These SE domains demarcate oncogenes that function as critical regulators of cell proliferation and apoptosis. Therefore, SE-driven genes represent tumor vulnerabilities, offering selective therapeutic opportunities.MethodsBy integrating ATAC-seq data from 22 NB cell lines, the intratumoral heterogeneity of MYCN-amplified NB was characterized at the level of chromatin accessibility. Subsequently, based on H3K27ac ChIP-seq data from 38 NB cell lines, the ROSE algorithm was employed to identify SE-driven oncogenes. The synergistic mechanism between MYCN amplification and the 17q SE-driven gene MSI2 was investigated through genome‑wide CRISPR/Cas9 loss‑of‑function screens. At single‑cell resolution, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the characteristics of heterogeneous tumor subpopulations and their immune microenvironment features. This analysis was performed using multiple bioinformatics workflows, including AUCell scoring, SCENIC analysis, copy‑number inference, cell differentiation‑state evaluation, neighborhood abundance tests, and separability tests. Finally, functional validation was performed using NB cell lines (MYCN‑amplified and non‑amplified) to assess gene perturbations.ResultsPronounced epigenetic heterogeneity was observed within MYCN-amplified NB. MSI2 is an SE‑driven oncogene and is highly expressed in MYCN‑amplified NB. MSI2 and MYCN are co-expressed, may be mutually dependent, and are both correlated with cell cycle-related pathways. At the single-cell level, we identified and redefined an NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype characterized by malignant transcriptional features, an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and poor patient prognosis. Building on this, combined targeting of MSI2 and MYCN markedly reduced proliferation and migration in MYCN‑amplified NB cells.ConclusionsThe NB‑MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype defines a clinically aggressive, therapy‑refractory state characterized by high proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and immunosuppression. For patients with MYCN-amplified NB, MSI2 is both a prognostic biomarker and a candidate therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08296-7.Keywords: Neuroblastoma, Heterogeneity, Super-enhancer, CRISPR screen, Synthetic lethality, MSI2, MYCN

Introduction

NB is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children, predominantly affecting those under 10 years of age. Patients with low- and intermediate-risk NB may experience spontaneous regression without intervention, whereas high-risk patients exhibit extensive tumor metastasis and poor prognosis. This pronounced clinical heterogeneity makes NB a key focus in pediatric oncology research [1–4].

Alterations in chromatin structure within the epigenetic landscape critically influence cellular identity and state transitions [5, 6]. SEs are clusters of enhancers spanning several kilobases in close genomic proximity that are characterized by dense occupancy of H3K27ac and H3K4me1 and strong transcriptional activation potential [7]. Across a wide range of cancers, including NB [8], small-cell lung cancer, medulloblastoma, and breast and gastric cancers, SEs are newly established during tumorigenesis. These cancer-specific SEs drive the expression of key oncogenes and are fundamental to maintaining the oncogenic state [9, 10].

Oncogenic signaling pathways converge at transcriptional SEs [11]. Core transcriptional regulatory circuitries (CRCs), composed of specific genes driven by SEs, establish NB’s adrenergic (ADRN) and mesenchymal (MES) phenotypes through self-regulatory mechanisms and feedforward loops. These phenotypic differences are closely associated with dysregulated epigenetic networks, which not only partially explain the origin of NB heterogeneity but also provide crucial therapeutic targets [12–15]. Notably, MYCN amplification on chromosome 2 remodels transcriptional programs through enhancer invasion [16], and synergizes with 17q gain to significantly reduce long-term patient survival rates [17]. Therefore, in MYCN-amplified NB, targeting the oncogenic storm mediated by SE-driven genes in the 17q region holds significant therapeutic potential.

MSI2, located on 17q, encodes an RNA-binding protein (RBP) involved in neural development and cell cycle regulation. Studies indicate that MSI2 is aberrantly expressed across multiple tumor types. It promotes malignant transformation in hematopoietic lineages and intestinal epithelial tissues, while maintaining tumor cell stemness and drug resistance [18–21]. In addition, increased MSI2 expression in cancer-associated fibroblasts promotes the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma, revealing complex multicellular co-regulatory interactions [22]. Recently, MSI2 was found to interact with the SE-driven long non-coding RNA PRKCQ-AS1 in non-MYCN-amplified NB, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation [23]. However, the functional role of MSI2 in MYCN-amplified NB remains undefined.

Here, we identified MSI2 (chr17:57,255,851–57,701,581) on 17q as an SE-driven oncogene that is significantly upregulated in MYCN-amplified NB. The regulation of MSI2 and MYCN expression is potentially interdependent. Subsequently, we defined a distinct NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype characterized by enhanced proliferative and migratory capacities and poor clinical outcomes. In vitro experiments showed that combined knockdown of both genes synergistically inhibited MYCN-amplified NB cell proliferation and migration and induced apoptosis. Collectively, these findings suggest that MSI2 could be a promising therapeutic target for MYCN-amplified NB.

Materials and methods

Raw data collection and processing

Raw data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and processed under standardized environmental conditions to minimize batch effects during data analysis. Single-cell raw FASTQ data were processed using Cell Ranger 7.1.0, with the refdata-gex-GRCh38-2020-A genome as the reference, to generate downstream expression matrices. The immune cell atlas of human NB, including cell subtype annotations and cell embeddings, was obtained from the GEO dataset GSE147766 [24]. ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq reads were aligned to the hg38 genome sequence after MACS2 peak calling (Bowtie2 2.5.3, Picard 2.27.5, Samtools 1.9, MACS2 2.2.9.1). ATAC-seq downstream matrices were merged from replicate BAM files, shifted by + 4/-5 bp, and normalized for GC content and gene length. BigWig files were generated from bedGraph files using deepTools. Finally, visualization was performed by selecting “scale-regions” mode, running ComputeMatrix, and plotting heatmaps.

Identification of SE-driven genes by ROSE analysis

For each sample, H3K27ac ChIP-seq BAM files were analyzed together with matched input controls. Peak summits overlapping ENCODE blacklist regions were excluded. SE-driven genes were identified using the ROSE algorithm (https://bitbucket.org/young_computation/rose) with the following parameters: genome = hg38, stitching distance = 12.5 kb, and default ranking settings. Duplicate SE-driven genes identified across the same cell lines or biological replicates were removed from the dataset. BigWig files were smoothed and visualized using R.

DepMap data processing

Gene expression, gene effect, gene dependency, and gene copy number data for cancer cell lines were extracted from the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap). Log2-transformed gene copy numbers were dichotomized using a cutoff of 1.58 (diploid baseline = 2; copy number ≥ 3 indicating amplification). After retaining 18,119 genes with complete information across 802 common cell lines, median differences in gene dependency scores between MYCN_AMP and WT groups were calculated for cancer cell lines. Cell lines were grouped according to tissue of origin. Tissue types retaining at least 3 mutant cell lines (Mut ≥ 3) and at least 5 wild-type cell lines (WT ≥ 5) were included. These groups were then directly compared, and the difference in mean gene dependency between mutant and wild-type cohorts was calculated. Differential analysis of gene dependency scores for SE-driven genes between NB and pan-cancer was performed using limma (v3.58.1), with results visualized in volcano plots.

Univariate Cox regression analysis and survival analysis

Hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using univariate Cox regression and visualized in forest plots for the top-ranked SE genes with significant differences in dependency. Using the median expression level of the MSI2 gene and MYCN status, we constructed a joint grouping variable. Death and disease recurrence were defined as endpoint events. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the survfit function, while the survdiff function was employed for log-rank tests and pairwise group comparisons. Survival curves were plotted using ggsurvplot, with the shaded area representing the 95% confidence interval (CI) and the risk table at the bottom displaying patient attrition during follow-up.

Relative correlation analysis

By calculating average gene-expression profiles across NB tumor cell subpopulations, we identified the top 2000 genes with the highest variance. Rank correlation coefficients were used to assess the similarity of expression patterns between subpopulations, and results were visualized using pheatmap. Applying the same analytical strategy, we generated scatter plots of correlations between gene expression and CRISPR screen phenotypic scores. The strength of correlation was quantified using the correlation coefficient (r) and its statistical significance (t-test), with the gray-shaded area around the regression curve representing the 95% CI.

Gene set enrichment analysis

The gene sets utilized in this study were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), encompassing Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), Hallmark gene sets, the Reactome Pathway Database, and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.10.0) based on the hypergeometric test. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on gene ranking was employed. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was applied to quantify pathway activity scores at the sample level. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Pseudobulk construction for single-cell samples

Cells from different individuals were distinguished by sample identifiers (orig.idents). The dgCMatrix was extracted, and gene expression values were summed across all cells. This process was iteratively applied to generate a whole-sample matrix, converting single-cell-level counts into pseudobulk expression values for each sample. To correct for sequencing depth variations and facilitate cross-sample comparisons, expression values for each gene were divided by their column sums and multiplied by 10^6 to achieve CPM normalization. Natural logarithms were then applied to every value in the normalized matrix, making the data more closely approximate a normal distribution. Finally, hierarchical clustering of samples was performed using Euclidean distance to achieve grouping.

Single-cell data processing

Single-cell RNA count matrices underwent standardization and normalization for preprocessing, with doublet identification performed by DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) and DecontX to calculate doublet rates and ambient RNA contamination rates [25, 26]. During the initial quality control step, cells with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 10% or erythrocyte gene expression exceeding 1% were removed, and cells with fewer than 500 or more than 9000 genes (nFeatures < 500 & > 9000) were filtered. Subsequently, we performed regression analysis on cell cycle genes and conducted principal component analysis (PCA) dimensionality reduction using 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs). The top 30 principal components were selected for batch effect correction across samples using the Harmony algorithm. Cell type annotation was based on canonical marker genes for known biological cell types, as reported in the literature and in the CellMarker database (http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker). DEGs for each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function (thresholds: |log2FC| ≥ 0.25, expression ratio ≥ 0.25, adj.p < 0.05) [27]. Visualization was performed using UMAP and t-SNE dimensionality-reduction techniques.

AUCell scoring identifies specialized subtypes

Genes not expressed in any cell were removed. The AUCell_buildRankings function was used to rank gene expression in the single-cell count matrix, producing a gene-expression ranking matrix for each cell. Feature gene sets overlapping with the single-cell data were retained. Based on these preprocessed gene sets and the ranking matrix, the area under the curve (AUC) for each cell across gene sets was calculated. Specific cells were identified by setting an AUC threshold based on the position of the bimodal distribution in activity scores.

SCENIC constructs gene regulatory networks

Meta cells were constructed based on sample grouping to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio. The gene regulatory network (GRN) was inferred using pySCENIC (v0.12.1) [28], first constructing a gene co-expression network, then incorporating the relevance rankings between transcription factor (TF) binding motifs and genomic regions (spanning 500 bp upstream to 100 bp downstream of the TSSs) obtained from the RcisTarget database, to determine the direction of transcriptional regulation. The inferred TFs and their downstream target genes were assembled into regulons, with TF activity quantified using AUCell.

Cell differentiation state inference and pseudotime analysis

Based on the core hypothesis that cells with higher differentiation states exhibit lower transcriptome complexity, CytoTRACE (v0.3.3) was employed to assess gene expression diversity in individual cells using gene expression entropy [29]. The index ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating lower differentiation states, whereas terminally differentiated cells approach 0. Pseudotime analysis was performed using Monocle 2 (v2.32.0). This involved constructing a CellDataSet object, filtering out low-expression genes, selecting HVGs, performing dimensionality reduction with the DDRTree algorithm, and finally performing pseudotemporal ordering with orderCells. Cell state transition trajectories and pseudotime visualizations were generated using the plot_cell_trajectory function.

Single-cell copy number variation and metabolic activity analysis

Employing infercnv (v1.14.2), genes expressed in at least 10 cells were retained. Genes were sorted by chromosomal position using a gene coordinate file (hg38_gencode_v27.txt), and data smoothing was performed using a sliding-window approach. Using immune cells as the reference, chromosomal copy number variations (CNVs) in other cells were inferred. Default parameters, including “denoising,” “Hidden Markov Model (HMM),” and a 0.1 “cutoff,” were selected to generate CNV values. In the generated heatmap, red indicates CNV amplification, while blue indicates deletions. Superscripts designate chromosomal positions, with left-side annotation bars labeling distinct tumor cell subtypes. Single-cell metabolic pathway activity was quantified using scMetabolism (v0.2.1) [30], via the AUCell algorithm, revealing metabolic reprogramming across tumor cell subtypes.

MiloR-based differential cellular abundance analysis and Augur-based intergroup separability assessment

MiloR (v1.8.1) was employed to construct a k-nearest neighbor graph, followed by representative cell anchor sampling [31]. Using sex as a covariate, negative binomial regression was used to detect intergroup abundance differences in cellular neighborhoods. Output p-values were spatially FDR-corrected, with results visualized using beeswarm plots and neighborhood differential abundance plots. Intergroup separability was computed using Augur (v1.0.3) [32]. Machine learning models were independently trained for each cell type, and cross-validation was used to evaluate classification performance across experimental groups. The response magnitude of each cell type to specific perturbations was quantified using the AUC.

Reference mapping of the NB immune cell atlas

Based on the count matrices, cell type annotations, and cell embeddings provided by the immune cell atlas of human NB [24], the reference atlas was constructed using ProjectSVR (v0.2.1) (https://github.com/JarningGau/scutilsR). First, meta cells were generated in UMAP space using density-based grid partitioning. Then, the FindOptimalK function determined the optimal hyperparameter K by extracting HVG features via non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) with 50 iterations. Finally, a support vector machine classifier was applied to complete atlas construction, and immune cells from different groups were projected onto the resulting atlas.

Immune infiltration analysis

Signature gene sets for 28 immune cell types were acquired [33], and GSVA (v1.50.0) with single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was applied to quantify immune cell enrichment levels in patients with NB. Estimate (v1.0.13) was used to calculate the stromal score, the immune score, and the tumor purity in patients with NB [34].

Cell culture and transient transfection

Human NB cell lines SH-SY5Y (CVCL_0019), SK-N-AS (CVCL_1700), IMR-32 (CVCL_0346), and SK-N-BE(2) (CVCL_0528) were obtained from the BDBIO Cell Bank, authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling, and cultured in MEM/F12, high-glucose DMEM, or DMEM/F12 medium (Procell, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Procell, China) and 5% penicillin-streptomycin (NCM Biotech, China). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cells were transiently transfected with siRNA and plasmids (HanYi Biosciences, China) using jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus, France). The siRNA sequences are listed in Table S1. Knockdown or overexpression efficiency was assessed 48 h post‑transfection by RT‑qPCR and immunoblotting.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure® Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, China), and the RNA concentration was measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed through reverse transcription using the Evo M-MLV RT Premix Kit (Accurate Biology, China). qPCR was performed using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (EZBioscience, USA) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The PCR reaction mixture contained: 5 ng cDNA template, 5 µL of 2× SYBR Green Mix (ROX Plus), 0.25 µL forward primer, and 0.25 µL reverse primer. The ΔΔCt method was used to quantify relative gene expression. Experimental data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 10. All primer pair sequences are provided in Table S2.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted on ice using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors at a 1:100 ratio. The lysate was centrifuged to collect the supernatant, and protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein quantification kit (Heruibio, China). Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE, and proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes using the wet transfer method. After membrane transfer, the membranes were blocked for 2 h at room temperature in TBST buffer (containing 0.1% Tween-20) with 5% skim milk. The membranes were incubated in primary antibody working solution (anti-MSI2 1:1000; anti-MYCN 1:1000) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, after washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with species-matched HRP-labeled secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Following thorough washing, signals were developed using an ECL chemiluminescent substrate.

Immunofluorescence

We seeded cells in 24-well plates and discarded the culture medium once the cells reached 70–80% confluence. We then fixed the cells with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 25 min. Afterward, we discarded the solution and permeabilized the membranes with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After three washes with PBS, cells were blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h, then incubated with primary antibodies (anti-MSI2, 1:300; anti-MYCN, 1:200) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the cells were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After DAPI nuclear staining, multi-channel fluorescence images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

After baking tissue sections at 60 °C for 1 h, deparaffinization was performed with xylene, followed by hydration using a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA (pH 9.0) with microwave heating for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 for 5 min, followed by a 30-minute block with 5% BSA, and then incubated with primary antibodies (anti-MSI2, 1:300; anti-MYCN, 1:200) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, HRP-labeled secondary antibodies were applied, with incubation at room temperature for 1 h. Sections were developed with diaminobenzidine (DAB), counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for microscopic examination.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in pre-chilled Co-IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 4 °C and 12,000 × g for 15 min, then incubated with the target protein antibody (or control IgG) with rotation at 4 °C overnight. Protein A/G agarose magnetic beads (Biolinkedin, China) were added, and the mixture was incubated for 2 h. After washing the complexes three times with lysis buffer, proteins were eluted by boiling in 1× SDS loading buffer at 95 °C for 5 min. Finally, interacting proteins were detected by Western blot analysis.

Cell proliferation assessment

Cell proliferation was assessed using CCK-8 and colony formation assays. In the CCK-8 assay, transfected cells were counted using a hemocytometer. A 100 µL cell suspension containing 4,000 cells per well was seeded into 96-well plates. At 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (NCM Biotech, China) was added to each well and thoroughly mixed. After incubating for 90 min in a CO2 incubator, the optical density at 450 nm was measured for each experimental group using a microplate reader (Biotek, USA). For the colony formation assay, cells plated at 1,000 or 2,000 cells per well in 6-well plates were transfected and subsequently cultured in a 37 °C/5% CO2 incubator with medium replacement every 2 days. After 14 days, the medium was discarded, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min, and then stained with 1% crystal violet. Excess dye was gently rinsed off with ddH2O, and plates were air-dried at room temperature for photographic recording. Colony counts were analyzed statistically using ImageJ.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration capacity was detected using the Transwell assay. Five hundred microliters (500 µL) of complete medium were added to the lower chamber of a 24-well plate, and then the Transwell insert (8 μm pore size) was placed into the well. After cells were digested and counted, they were resuspended in serum-free medium. One hundred microliters (100 µL) of cell suspension (1 × 105 cells) were transferred to the upper chamber of the Transwell insert. The plate was incubated in a humidified incubator for 48 h. The Transwell insert was removed, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min, and cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed using a cotton swab. Cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution. After rinsing with PBS, membranes were observed and photographed under a microscope.

Apoptosis detection by flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis kit (Elabscience, China). Experimental procedure: After 24 h of treatment, adherent cells were trypsinized, washed once with PBS, and resuspended in 1 × Annexin V Binding Buffer. Annexin V-FITC and PI reagents were added, and the mixture was incubated for 20 min in the dark. Samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman). Early apoptotic cells (FITC + PI-) and late apoptotic/necrotic cells (FITC + PI+) were distinguished using dual-parameter scatter plots of FITC versus PI. Quantitative analysis was performed with FlowJo.

Statistical analysis

Raw data (single-cell RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, ChIP-seq, and CRISPR screen data) underwent preprocessing on Linux servers. Statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R (v4.2.3), ImageJ, CaseViewer 2.3, and GraphPad Prism 10. Correlations between continuous variables were measured using Spearman correlation coefficients (r). Statistical significance for between-group comparisons was assessed using appropriate tests, including Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA, and Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The data were presented as mean ± SD, with error bars representing the standard deviation. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).

Results

Epigenetic heterogeneity exists within MYCN-amplified NB

Imbalances in chromatin epigenetic regulation can lead to abnormal epigenetic landscapes that drive tumor initiation and progression [5]. In NB, MYCN amplification enables distinct genomic occupancy, while MYC provides functional redundancy in non-amplified states [6]. To characterize chromatin accessibility in NB, this study integrated published ATAC-seq data from 22 NB cell lines [35]. Comparative analysis revealed that peaks associated with promoters and exons constituted a relatively small proportion; approximately 46% of peaks were in distal regulatory elements (Fig. 1A), suggesting a widespread activation of enhancers in NB [36]. Chromatin accessibility is more cell‑type specific than mRNA expression, and distal regulatory elements better reflect cellular identity than promoters [37, 38]. To identify intra-lineage heterogeneity, correlation analyses were performed on count matrices encompassing all peaks, promoter peaks, and distal element peaks across the NB cell lines. Clustering of replicate samples from the same cell line confirmed the validity of the data processing. None of the three approaches could clearly distinguish MYCN status in NB cell lines (Fig. 1C). A potential explanation is that certain cell lines, such as SK-N-FI, SK-N-AS, and NBL-S, do not exhibit MYCN amplification yet maintain high expression levels of MYCN or c-MYC [39, 40]. Moreover, regardless of MYCN status, cells exhibit fundamental dependencies on genes within the CRC module that regulate cellular growth and survival [15]. PCA on distal‑element counts revealed partial distinctions between MYCN-amplified and non-amplified cell lines, though complete segregation was not observed (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, significant heterogeneity exists even among MYCN-amplified cell lines, which are roughly classified into three major subgroups represented by SK-N-BE(2), IMR-32, and cell lines from the Children’s Oncology Group (COG; Fig. 1C). This heterogeneity may be associated with ALK/TP53 status, telomerase reverse transcriptase activity, treatment phase, sample type, and ectopic enhancer hijacking [16, 41]. Additionally, transcription start site (TSS)‑centered ATAC-seq signals indicate that MYCN-amplified cell lines exhibit globally increased chromatin accessibility (Fig. 1D), reflecting an active state of transcriptional regulation.

MSI2 is a dependency-upregulated, SE-driven oncogene on chromosome 17q in NB

The heterogeneity and plasticity of SEs can directly cause or amplify differences in transcriptional profiles among tumor cell populations, thereby serving as a critical source of tumor heterogeneity [10, 42]. SE-driven genes can be identified by quantifying the level of H3K27ac modification across genomic sequences. By integrating publicly available ChIP-seq datasets of a human neural crest cell line and 38 NB cell lines from the GEO database, SE‑driven genes were identified using the ROSE algorithm with default parameters, followed by ranking based on H3K27ac signal intensity (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Material 1). To identify SE-driven genes with increased functional dependency in NB, we integrated CRISPR screen data from the DepMap database across cancer cell lines [43]. Through a systematic comparison of CRISPR/Cas9 dependency scores between 25 NB cell lines and 980 other cancer cell lines for 872 SE-driven genes (|log2FC| > 0.1, P < 0.05), 132 genes with significant dependency differences were identified (Supplementary Material 2). GO enrichment analysis showed significant overrepresentation of terms related to nervous system development, stem cell maintenance, metabolic and hormone responses, cell proliferation, and transcriptional activators. The top-ranked dependency-upregulated genes were CRC TFs MYCN, HAND2, GATA3, PHOX2A, and PHOX2B, as well as other genes, including IRS2, ALK, and MSI2 [13]. We also identified genes functionally linked to the CRC, such as ASCL1, CCND1, and TBX2 (Fig. 1F) [14, 44, 45]. The univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that among dependency-attenuated SE genes, SP1 and SCD exhibited the highest HRs, while among dependency-upregulated SE genes, PPP1R15B, ALK, MSI2, and MYCN showed the most pronounced changes in HR (Fig. 1G; Supplementary Material 3). Given that chromosome 17q harbors numerous “essential genes” for MYCN [46], MSI2 emerged as a key component and exhibited an HR comparable to that of MYCN. Their co-upregulation patterns, including both HR elevation and dependency upregulation, suggest an intrinsic functional linkage. Pan-cancer analysis across 33 cancer types revealed that, compared with adjacent normal tissues, MSI2 expression was elevated in most primary tumors, including BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, and LUAD (Fig. S1A), suggesting a potential oncogenic role [47].

MYCN amplification promotes activation of the MSI2 epigenetic landscape

By integrating SE-driven gene predictions across 38 NB cell lines, we identified high-confidence SE-driven genes defined by H3K27ac signal values exceeding 10,000 in at least 10 cell lines (Supplementary Material 4). The MSI2 locus met these criteria and was selected. Given that enhancer invasion can remodel MYCN transcriptional regulation [16], we observed markedly elevated H3K27ac enrichment across a 620-kb genomic region surrounding the MSI2 locus in MYCN-amplified NB cell lines (Fig. 2A and B). When enhancer regions were delineated by H3K4me1 signals, abundant enhancer marks were observed across the MSI2 locus regardless of MYCN status (Fig. 2C). Its enhancer region resides in a poised state, providing the foundation for potential activation into a functional enhancer. Histone modification of H3K27ac distinguishes active enhancers from poised enhancers [48]. In the context of MYCN amplification, the high H3K27ac signal at the MSI2 enhancer region is associated with increased transcriptional activity. Active promoters are marked by RNA Pol II, H3K4me3, and H3K27ac, while transcription elongation regions are characterized by RNA Pol II Ser2P [49, 50]. In the MSI2 locus of IMR-32, ATAC signals indicate a high level of chromatin openness; RNA Pol II and abundant H3K4me3 at the promoter provide the fundamental conditions for transcription, and RNA Pol II Ser2P across the gene body indicates active elongation (Fig. 2D). A limited set of essential TFs—MYCN, HAND2, GATA3, PHOX2B, ISL1, and TBX2—serve as CRC members maintaining the cellular state of MYCN-amplified NB [13]. Among these, HAND2, GATA3, and PHOX2B drive NB lineage addiction, thereby defining their ADRN phenotypic identity [12]. We observed co-localization of MSI2 enhancer peaks with binding peaks for HAND2, GATA3, PHOX2B, and MYCN, accompanied by enhanced activation marker H3K27ac and diminished repressive marker H3K27me3. This suggests that the recruitment of CRC master TFs to the MSI2 enhancer contributes to MSI2 transcriptional activation and may reinforce ADRN lineage programs. Compared to the MES phenotype, the induced ADRN-phenotype cell lines showed higher H3K27ac signals at the MSI2 locus [51], further supporting this regulatory propensity.

MSI2/MYCN exhibits strong co-expression and functional dependency

MYCN exhibits a preference for genes on 17q, cooperatively promoting NB progression in high-risk patients [17]. Multi-omics data revealed a positive correlation between MSI2 and MYCN across both NB cell lines and tumor tissue. Pseudobulk transcriptomes constructed from tumor cell subpopulations in NB single-cell data demonstrated a high correlation coefficient (r = 0.83) between MSI2 and MYCN expression (Fig. 3A). Baseline transcriptional and protein levels in two MYCN-non-amplified (SK-N-AS and SH-SY5Y) and two MYCN-amplified NB cell lines (SK-N-BE(2) and IMR-32) further corroborated this trend (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence in SK-N-BE(2), IMR-32, and patient samples revealed that MSI2 is present in the cytoplasm, whereas MYCN is mainly present in the nucleus (Fig. 3C). Genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function screens across 802 cancer cell lines (including 46 NB cell lines) indicated that MYCN amplification shows increased dependency on ADRN-type TFs and on genes such as MSI2, ISL1, SOX11, IGF2BP1, CCND1, and ALK (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Material 5) [43]. The positive correlation between MSI2 and MYCN gene-effect scores suggests potential functional interdependence between these genes. Interaction analysis of expression and susceptibility revealed that NB’s dependency on MSI2 intensifies with elevated MYCN expression, and vice versa (Fig. 3E). Knockdown of MYCN in SK-N-BE(2) and IMR-32 resulted in approximately 40% reduction in both MSI2 mRNA and protein levels. Although MYCN mRNA levels remained unchanged upon MSI2 knockdown, its protein expression decreased by approximately 60% (Fig. 3F and G). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed no direct interaction between MSI2 and MYCN proteins (Fig. S1B). Functional experiments using SK-N-BE(2) and SK-N-AS showed that overexpression of either MSI2 or MYCN alone promoted proliferation, while MYCN knockdown on a background of MSI2 overexpression significantly inhibited growth (Fig. 3H). Additionally, among common genetic alterations in high-risk NB—mutations in NRAS, ATRX, ALK, TP53, and PHOX2B [1, 52], ALK-mutated peripheral nervous system cell lines exhibited heightened sensitivity to MSI2 knockout (Fig. S1C; Supplementary Material 6), and ALK was also highly expressed in MYCN-amplified NB (Fig. S1D).

Single-cell omics reveals malignant features and developmental origins of the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype

Given the unique intrinsic link between MSI2 and MYCN, NB single-cell transcriptomic data were used to determine the transcriptional characteristics of tumor cell subpopulations. After rigorous quality control, a total of 190,999 cells were obtained from 14 samples. Major cell subpopulations were annotated based on specific markers [53], including T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, monocytes, macrophages, Schwann cells, and tumor cells (Fig. 4A and B). Subsequently, cell cycle-related genes were regressed out to eliminate their potential interference with tumor heterogeneity assessment. Tumor cells were then reclustered into 18 clusters (Fig. S1E), and transcriptional similarity between clusters was evaluated (Fig. 4C). Using the combined expression score of MSI2 and MYCN (Fig. S1F), we defined the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype with a cutoff value of 0.13 in the bimodal AUC plot (Fig. 4D). Based on transcriptional similarity and DEGs among clusters (Fig. S2A), the remaining subpopulations were annotated as NB-NEUROD1, NB-MKI67, NB-STAT1, NB-SOX6, NB-CSMD1, and NB-HOXC8 (Fig. 4E). SCENIC analysis identified regulon modules (M1–M8) and highlighted differences in GRNs between MYCN‑amplified and non‑amplified groups (Fig. S2B). In the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype, the top five regulons ranked by the regulon specificity score were ZNF536, ETV1, FOXA2, HOXD10, and FOXC1 (Fig. 4F). Cellular differentiation potential was estimated based on gene-expression abundance and diversity [29]. Compared to other subtypes, the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype exhibited higher developmental potential (Fig. 4G). Pseudotime trajectory inference revealed that most of these cells clustered at the initiation end of low-differentiation branches (Cell State 1). With pseudotime progression, they diverged at Branch Point 1 toward NB-SOX6 and NB-HOXC8 differentiation trajectories (Fig. 4H). Using T/B cells and monocytes/macrophages as normal references, the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype exhibited an increased copy-number burden on chromosome 17q (Fig. 4I), consistent with aggressive NB genomics [52]. Considering that tumors can enhance the Warburg effect to maintain their malignant phenotype [54, 55], the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype showed significant activation of highly metabolically active pathways, including those involved in oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, and pyruvate metabolism (Fig. 4J). To explore the developmental origins and transcriptional features underlying this subtype, we compiled four NMF transcriptional modules, nine gene sets representative of embryonic neural crest development-related cell types (CHCs, SCPs, SNs, NCCs, EMT NCCs, Pre-EMT NCCs I, Pre-EMT NCCs II, NTCs, and Sympathoblasts), and canonical ADRN/MES lineage signature gene sets for evaluation (Supplementary Material 7) [15, 53]. Comparative analysis revealed that the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype exhibited substantial overlap with EMT NCC-like regions characterized by robust migration and invasion. Among the two SE-driven and mutually convertible lineage types, MES and ADRN [12, 15], due to MYCN’s inherent addiction to ADRN module TFs [56] and the aforementioned ChIP-seq results that indicate that HAND2, GATA3, and PHOX2B bind to the MSI2 SE regions, the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype is transcriptionally predisposed toward the ADRN lineage (Fig. 4K). Notably, within the benign NMF module Program4 (represented by HAND2, GATA3, and PHOX2B), this subtype exhibits an attenuated ADRN phenotype, which may be associated with enhanced stemness. This is further reflected in the downregulation of the benign differentiation module Program3 (marked by NPY, PRPH, and NTRK1). Concurrently, this subtype is highly enriched in malignant proliferation programs (Program1/6) characterized by the expression of MKI67, PCNA, TOP2A, and eukaryotic translation initiation factor family genes (Fig. 4L) [53].

MSI2/MYCN co-expression predicts poor prognosis in NB and correlates with the cell cycle pathway

To evaluate the clinical prognostic value of MSI2 and MYCN in patients with NB, gene expression profiles and clinical follow-up data from 498 patients (GEO: GSE49710) were systematically analyzed [57]. Expression levels of both genes peaked in International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS) stage 4 tumors and reached their nadir in stage 4 S, showing a progressive increase with advancing disease stage (Fig. 5A). Notably, MSI2 expression appeared to correlate more closely with INSS stage. Immunohistochemical staining of MSI2 was performed on tissue specimens from 14 patients across INSS stages. The results were concordant with transcriptomic data, with MSI2 demonstrating the highest expression levels in MYCN-amplified cases (Fig. 5B). Patients were stratified into three subgroups based on MSI2 expression and MYCN status (MSI2-MYCN-, MSI2 + MYCN-, and MSI2 + MYCN+). The MSI2-high group exhibited significantly poorer clinical outcomes. Moreover, when high MSI2 expression coexisted with MYCN amplification, patients showed further reductions in overall survival (OS) and event-free survival (EFS; Fig. 5C). In MSI2 + MYCN+ patients, DEGs were significantly enriched in GO terms related to DNA metabolism and energy-dependent nuclease activity, including single-stranded DNA helicase activity, ATP-dependent activity acting on DNA, and catalytic activity acting on DNA. Further Hallmark, Reactome, and KEGG analyses revealed enrichment of proliferation-related pathways, including cell cycle, G2/M checkpoint, DNA replication, homologous recombination, and E2F targets (Fig. 5D and E). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analyses identified hub cell‑cycle regulators—CDK2, PCNA, E2F1, PLK1, CHEK1, CCNA2, and CCNB1—which were upregulated in patients with high MSI2 or MYCN expression (Fig. 5F and G). Concordantly, knockdown of MSI2 and MYCN in SK-N-BE(2) cells led to decreased expression of these cell cycle genes at the mRNA level (Fig. 5H), supporting a functional link between MSI2/MYCN co‑expression and cell‑cycle activation.

Targeted dual intervention against MSI2/MYCN demonstrates potent anti-tumor efficacy

Previous analyses demonstrated elevated MSI2 expression in MYCN‑amplified NB, where cells exhibited heightened sensitivity to MSI2 knockout. We therefore knocked down MSI2 via siRNA using the same transfection conditions in four NB cell lines (SK‑N‑BE(2), IMR‑32, SK‑N‑AS, and SH‑SY5Y). Transfection efficiency was evaluated by detecting the uptake of fluorescently labeled siRNA, and MSI2 knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qPCR. SH-SY5Y proliferation was minimally affected by MSI2 depletion, consistent with its low MSI2 dependency observed in functional screens. Compared to SK-N-AS, both MYCN-amplified cell lines (SK-N-BE(2) and IMR-32) exhibited significantly greater proliferation inhibition following MSI2 knockdown (Fig. 6A). Given the potential synergistic effect between MSI2 and MYCN, we established four experimental groups (control, MSI2 single knockdown, MYCN single knockdown, and combined MSI2 + MYCN knockdown). CCK-8 assays in SK-N-BE(2) and IMR-32, along with colony formation assays in SK-N-BE(2), demonstrated that individual knockdown of either MSI2 or MYCN similarly inhibited cell proliferation, whereas combined knockdown produced the most pronounced inhibitory effect (Fig. 6B and C). Considering the transcriptional program shared between the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype and EMT NCCs, MSI2 and MYCN may confer strong metastatic capabilities on tumor cells. Transwell migration assays demonstrated that co-silencing of MSI2 and MYCN significantly reduced SK-N-BE(2) cell migration (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, flow cytometry (Annexin V-FITC/PI) revealed that combined knockdown significantly increased apoptotic cell fractions compared with single knockdowns and control (Fig. 6E).

MSI2 + MYCN+ reprograms the immunosuppressive microenvironment

Tumor heterogeneity arises not only from intrinsic factors but also from the surrounding microenvironment [4]. After extracting and annotating immune cell subsets (Fig. 7A and B), the constructed single-cell pseudobulk transcriptomes were used to stratify patients through hierarchical clustering and PCA dimensionality reduction (Fig. 7C). Patients with high MSI2 and MYCN expression were defined as the high-risk group, while those with low expression constituted the low-risk group (Fig. 7D). Quantitative analyses of cellular abundance revealed significant differences between the two groups of immune cells in both overall proportions and the number of local neighborhoods. Specifically, the high-risk group exhibited decreased abundance of T cells and B cells alongside increased myeloid cell infiltration (Fig. 7E). Beyond pronounced abundance changes, cell state alterations in Th cells, Tcyto cells (cytotoxic T cells, CTLs), monocytes, and macrophages were also significant (Fig. 7F). To explore specific functional changes underlying these subpopulations, we reconstructed the NB immune cell reference atlas for reference mapping [24]. Patients with high-risk NB exhibited significant heterogeneity in myeloid cell infiltration, characterized by an increased proportion of immunosuppressive M2-type Macro1 cells that demonstrate tumor-associated macrophage-specific gene expression patterns (e.g., TREM2, FABP5, and FABP4), which correlated with poor prognosis. No significant changes were observed in pro-inflammatory monocytes (Mono-1 and Mono-2). Myeloid dendritic cells associated with improved survival (mDC-CLEC9A + and mDC-CD1C+) and Macro-4 cells with high human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class II gene expression were reduced. Given that cytokines released by myeloid cells can influence the abundance and functionality of T cells [58], projection of T cells revealed that in the high-risk group, although proliferating T cells, CTLs, and naïve T cells (serving as a differentiation reservoir) showed no significant changes in relative proportions, their absolute cell counts decreased sharply. Among these, reduced numbers of CTL-1, CTL-3, CTL-4, and naïve T cells were significantly associated with decreased patient survival. Additionally, no significant changes were observed in tumor-promoting Treg cells [59]. In natural killer cells (NK) with potent tumor-killing capabilities [60], no significant differences were found in CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells between groups, whereas their activated counterparts (Active NK) showed decreases in both number and proportion. This reduction directly correlated with diminished long-term survival rates in patients. B cell projection revealed significant declines in the proportions and absolute numbers of active and germinal center B cells in the high-risk group, alongside slight increases in memory B cells and plasma cells (Fig. 7G). Using 28 immune cell-type-specific gene signatures [33], immune infiltration analysis revealed that the vast majority of patients with INSS stage 4 disease and the MSI2 + MYCN+ group fell into the low-immune-infiltration subgroup (Fig. 7H). This subgroup exhibited advanced tumor progression and shortened survival. Correlation analyses further demonstrated that upregulation of MSI2 and MYCN expression was significantly associated with increased tumor purity, whereas stromal and immune scores decreased, indicating the opposite trend (Fig. 7I).

Discussion

NB arises from dysregulated cell differentiation due to developmental abnormalities [1, 2] and is thus metaphorically described as “differentiation goes awry” [61]. Although the origin of NB remains debated [62], lineage tracing and single-cell sequencing studies have provided critical clues for decoding gene perturbations in disease states [53, 63]. Regardless of their origin, the determination of NB cell identity is governed by transcriptional networks disrupted through epigenetic dysregulation [13, 37].

During this regulatory process, cell type-specific master TFs occupy enhancer regions of target genes, thereby controlling key identity-determining genes and directing dominant transcriptional programs of cell differentiation [42]. Notably, SEs—large regulatory elements formed by clustered enhancers—serve as pivotal regulatory hubs due to their potent transcriptional activation capacity [64]. Based on SE characteristics, NB can be classified into four epigenetic clinical subtypes: MYCN-amplified, high-risk MYCN-non-amplified, low-risk MYCN-non-amplified, and a fourth subtype exhibiting mesenchymal features [14]. According to dependency on the CRC TFs, these can be classified into the ADRN phenotype—dominated by master TFs such as HAND2, GATA3, and PHOX2B through feedforward loops—and the MES phenotype driven by PRRX1 and characterized by the AP-1 TF (FOS/JUN) [12, 15]. Because distinct lineages exhibit interconvertible properties, such plasticity potentially underlies treatment resistance and tumor recurrence [15]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that intratumoral heterogeneity in NB is not stochastic but governed by specific epigenetic contexts, with MYCN amplification status playing a predominant regulatory role.

As a key TF in NB, MYCN amplification drives pluripotency programs and serves as a marker of high-risk clinical subtypes and poor prognosis [52, 65]. ATAC-seq data reveal highly accessible chromatin near TSSs in MYCN-amplified NB, with epigenetic heterogeneity primarily governed by distal regulatory elements. Additionally, MYCN promotes a highly dependent oncogenic transcriptional network and maintains its activity by hijacking SEs of relevant genes [16, 41]. By integrating H3K27ac ChIP-seq data from multiple NB cell lines, we identified a group of SE-driven genes and performed a differential comparison with pan-cancer dependency scores. These NB-specific SE-driven genes were enriched for the GO terms “nervous system development” and “cell proliferation.” Among these, some SE-driven genes were uniquely present in MYCN-amplified NB cell lines. Through univariate Cox regression modeling, MSI2 and MYCN were identified as high-risk oncogenes.

ChIP-seq signals revealed that MSI2 SE regions are occupied by master TFs of the ADRN phenotype. Although H3K4me1 is broadly enriched at MSI2 enhancer regions and maintains a primed state, only MYCN-amplified NB cell lines exhibit relatively high H3K27ac levels indicative of active enhancers. Multi-omics integration confirmed a positive correlation between MSI2 and MYCN expression, which is associated with a 17q gain [17, 46]. Immunofluorescence localized MSI2 predominantly to the cytoplasm, whereas MYCN was mainly present in the nucleus. Subsequently, the CRISPR/Cas9 library was screened to compare essential gene differences between oncogene-dependent cell lines and their non-dependent counterparts [66, 67], revealing MSI2 as a synthetic lethality partner of MYCN. The CRC members HAND2, GATA3, and PHOX2B identified concurrently reflect MYCN’s preference for the ADRN phenotype [13]. Interaction analysis demonstrated a mutual dependence between MSI2 and MYCN, with dependency scores positively correlated with expression levels of both genes. Mechanistic perturbations indicate that MYCN knockdown reduces MSI2 mRNA and protein levels by approximately 40%, yet basal expression persists, suggesting both MYCN-dependent and -independent regulatory inputs. Beyond direct MYCN regulation, TFs associated with the ADRN phenotype may sustain residual transcriptional activity by binding to the SE regions of MSI2. Notably, although MSI2 functions as an RBP, MSI2 knockdown did not alter MYCN mRNA levels but resulted in an approximately 60% reduction in MYCN protein. However, Co-IP failed to detect a stable direct MSI2–MYCN PPI. This indicates that MSI2 does not directly regulate MYCN at either the transcriptional or protein-stability level. Instead, MSI2 likely modulates MYCN protein abundance indirectly by influencing its translational efficiency. Functional validations demonstrated that individual overexpression of either MSI2 or MYCN promoted NB cell proliferation. Notably, MSI2 overexpression potentiated the inhibitory effect of MYCN knockdown, suggesting that increased MSI2 levels may increase cellular dependence on MYCN signaling. We propose a model in which MYCN, together with ADRN-type TFs (HAND2, GATA3, and PHOX2B), binds to the MSI2 SE region to transcriptionally activate MSI2 expression, while MSI2 in turn enhances MYCN protein levels post‑transcriptionally. This reciprocal interaction forms a positive feedback regulatory loop that reinforces survival pathways and promotes tumor progression (Fig. S3). Furthermore, the expression of ALK—a malignant proliferation marker in NB [68]—correlates positively with both MSI2 and MYCN, while ALK-mutant peripheral nervous system cell lines demonstrate heightened dependency on MSI2. This implies that ALK may participate in the MSI2/MYCN oncogenic circuit, and together they may drive malignant progression in NB.

Intratumoral transcriptomic comparisons frequently serve as the cellular and molecular foundation for establishing tumor heterogeneity [69]. At single-cell resolution, we defined the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype characterized by lower differentiation, aberrantly activated metabolic pathways, and concurrent 17q gain. Comparative analysis revealed that the transcriptional profile of the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype resembles the malignant proliferation program of NB and exhibits migratory potential associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Although this subtype showed an attenuated ADRN signature, it did not shift toward the MES phenotype, reflecting MYCN addiction to the ADRN lineage [56]. On the other hand, given that MSI2 can confer stemness to tumor cells and enhance drug resistance [18–20], the MSI2/MYCN co-expression state is conducive to tumor cells acquiring chemotherapy tolerance while maintaining rapid proliferation.

Prognostic analysis demonstrates extremely poor outcomes in the MSI2 + MYCN+ patient cohort. Both transcriptomic data and immunohistochemical results from patients with NB across different INSS stages collectively indicate that MSI2 outperforms MYCN in clinical staging, suggesting that MSI2 may serve as a novel molecular biomarker for prognostic evaluation in patients with NB. Pathway enrichment analysis in the MSI2 + MYCN+ patient group reveals significant activation of cell proliferation-related programs, including DNA replication, E2F targets, G2/M checkpoint, and cell cycle. Co-knockdown of MSI2 and MYCN resulted in comprehensive downregulation of the transcriptional levels of key cell-cycle regulators, including CDK2, PCNA, and E2F1, consistent with their role in sustaining high proliferative activity.

The dependency of tumors on SE-driven genes provides critical therapeutic targets [10]. Because MSI2 is SE-driven and exhibits synthetic lethality with MYCN, we propose that the MSI2/MYCN interaction may represent a potential therapeutic target. Our experimental results demonstrate that MYCN-amplified NB cell lines exhibit heightened sensitivity to MSI2 knockdown. Compared with single-target knockdown, dual targeting of MSI2 and MYCN more effectively suppressed NB cell proliferation and invasion while inducing apoptosis.

Although MSI2 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in MYCN‑amplified NB, RBPs remain difficult to drug because most lack conventional catalytic pockets for small‑molecule inhibition [70]. While no potent and selective MSI2 inhibitors are currently available, several small-molecule MSI2 inhibitors with partial activity have been reported, indicating that MSI2 is not entirely undruggable [21]. Given that MSI2 expression is driven by SEs and influences cell‑cycle progression, selectively eliminating cancer cells may be achievable by blocking SE‑driven oncogenic transcription using BRD4 or CDK7 inhibitors [8, 10]. In addition, combinatorial strategies, including co-treatment with MYCN inhibitors or retinoic acid, which induces neuronal differentiation and proliferation arrest, may offer additional therapeutic benefit and warrant further investigation.

It must be emphasized that alterations in the immune microenvironment surrounding cancer cells similarly impact long-term patient survival [4], manifested specifically through changes in tumor-suppressive and tumor-supportive immune cell phenotypes. In the MSI2 + MYCN+ patient cohort, T-cell abundance decreased significantly while myeloid-cell abundance markedly increased. Reference mapping of the NB immune cell atlas revealed diminished pro-inflammatory capacity in myeloid cells, which concurrently demonstrated tumor growth-promoting effects [24]. CTLs and highly cytotoxic activated NK cells primarily exhibited a marked reduction in population size [60]. Taken together, these shifts in immune composition and state suggest MSI2/MYCN likely facilitates immune evasion by remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment.

This study has limitations. Although we have validated the MSI2-MYCN association through multidimensional approaches, the precise molecular mechanism governing their reciprocal regulation remains incompletely understood. Regarding the tumor microenvironment, although patients in the MSI2 + MYCN+ group exhibit overall immunosuppressive features, the spatial heterogeneity of such tumor cells within tissues and their interactions with immune-infiltrating components warrant further investigation. Future research should integrate proteomics and spatial transcriptomics with more sophisticated in vivo models and molecular mechanism experiments to systematically elucidate the complex molecular networks that drive the development of the NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identified the SE-driven oncogene MSI2 on chromosome 17q in MYCN-amplified NB and untangled its underlying oncogenic circuitry from an epigenetic regulatory perspective. Through multi-omics analyses and functional validation, we demonstrated that MSI2 is co-expressed with MYCN and represents a vulnerable target in MYCN-amplified NB. On this basis, we defined an NB-MSI2 + MYCN+ subtype with distinct molecular characteristics. Tumors of this subtype display a malignant transcriptional program, an ADRN phenotype characterized by attenuated differentiation, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Finally, we proposed a combined therapeutic strategy targeting both MSI2 and MYCN, which holds clinical translational value for the treatment of MYCN-amplified NB.
