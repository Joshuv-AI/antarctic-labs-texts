Factors Associated With Uptake of Iron Supplement During Pregnancy Among Women of Reproductive Age in Somaliland: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Awo Mohamed Kahie, Mohamed Said Hassan, Suhaib Mohamed Kahie, Abdikadir Abdillahi Husein, Asma Abdirahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnemia in pregnancy is a severe public health problem in low‐ and middle‐income countries, yet the uptake of recommended iron supplementation remains critically low. In a post‐conflict and resource‐constrained setting like Somaliland, there is a significant evidence gap regarding the factors that determine whether pregnant women use these essential supplements. This study aimed to identify the individual and community‐level factors associated with the uptake of iron supplements during pregnancy in Somaliland.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of data from the 2020 Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS), including a nationally representative weighted sample of 2577 women aged 15–49 years who had a live birth in the 5 years preceding the survey. A multilevel binary logistic regression model was used to identify determinants of iron supplement uptake while accounting for the hierarchical data structure.ResultsThe uptake of iron supplements was alarmingly low, with only 37.08% of women reporting use during their most recent pregnancy. The strongest predictor was attending four or more antenatal care (ANC) visits (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 6.20; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 4.70–8.19). Other significant positive determinants included belonging to a rich household (AOR = 1.93; 95% CI: 1.43–2.60), having a husband in paid employment (AOR = 2.13; 95% CI: 1.71–2.66), and exposure to television. Conversely, women whose husbands had no formal education had significantly lower odds of uptake (AOR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.51–0.91). Significant regional disparities were observed, with markedly lower uptake in the Sool and Sanaag regions.ConclusionIron supplement uptake in Somaliland is critically deficient and driven by profound inequities in healthcare access and socioeconomic status. A multipronged strategy is urgently needed, focusing on strengthening ANC services, leveraging mass media and mHealth for targeted health promotion, addressing socioeconomic barriers, and engaging male partners to improve maternal and child health outcomes.Keywords: anemia, antenatal care, DHS, iron supplementation, maternal health, multilevel analysis, Somaliland

Introduction

Anemia during pregnancy remains a formidable global public health challenge, exerting a profound toll on maternal and infant health. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that nearly 40% of pregnant women worldwide are anemic, a figure that masks stark disparities, with the highest prevalence concentrated in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [1]. Iron deficiency is the predominant cause, fueling a cascade of adverse outcomes, including an elevated risk of maternal mortality, preterm birth, low birth weight, and long‐term impaired cognitive development in children [2, 3].

In Somaliland, the 2020 Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS) indicates that anemia remains a significant burden, with approximately 31% of women of reproductive age suffering from some degree of anemia. Despite its prevalence, the clinical outcomes of pregnancies in this region are often compromised by poor nutritional status and a lack of preventive care. The disproportionate burden in LMICs is driven by a convergence of factors, including inadequate dietary iron intake, high rates of infectious diseases, and fragile health systems, unable to deliver essential preventive care consistently.

In response, the WHO advocates for daily oral iron and folic acid supplementation (IFAS) as a core, cost‐effective intervention throughout pregnancy (World Health Organization, 2016) [4]. However, translating this global recommendation into effective local practice is fraught with challenges. Across sub‐Saharan Africa, uptake and adherence to IFAS are critically low, often failing to reach impactful coverage levels [5, 6, 7]. The barriers are complex and interwoven, spanning individual factors, like socioeconomic status and educational level, health system factors, such as the accessibility and quality of antenatal care (ANC), and sociocultural factors, including local beliefs and awareness of the supplement's benefits [8].

Somaliland, located in the Horn of Africa, represents a context of unique vulnerability. Decades of instability have resulted in a fragmented health system with limited public funding, high maternal mortality rates, and significant barriers to healthcare access, particularly for its large rural and nomadic populations [9]. The health system in Somaliland follows a tiered structure where the Essential Package of Health Services (EPHS) mandate the provision of IFAS free of charge at all public health facilities. However, supply‐side issues, such as frequent stock‐outs of supplements and the uneven distribution of health personnel, mean that an ANC visit does not always guarantee that a woman will physically receive the supplements. Furthermore, even when provided, systemic gaps in counseling and patient education often leave women unaware of the correct dosage or the importance of oral consumption [7]. Although IFAS is officially part of the essential health services package, a critical evidence gap exists regarding its actual implementation and use. Investigating these factors is essential for designing targeted, context‐specific interventions that can effectively improve maternal nutrition and health outcomes.

Methods and Materials

Study Area

The research was conducted in Somaliland, officially known as the Republic of Somaliland, a self‐declared sovereign entity in the Horn of Africa. This region comprises six geographical areas: Awdal, Marodijeh, Sahil, Togdheer, Sanaag, and Sool. Access to healthcare services poses significant challenges, particularly in rural communities. To address these issues, the Ministry of Health Development has implemented the EPHS, aligned with the WHO's foundational health system building blocks. Healthcare delivery in Somaliland is structured into five tiers, from community‐level preventive care to regional hospitals for complex cases [5].

Study Design and Study Period

This study used a cross‐sectional design, analyzing data from the SLDHS, which was carried out nationally in 2020. Its aim was to investigate the factors contributing to the uptake of iron supplements among pregnant women.

Sample Size and Sampling

A total of 2577 women aged 15–49 years were included in the study, drawn from the SLDHS dataset and weighted for analysis. The sampling strategy considered six geographic regions and various types of residences: urban, rural, and nomadic. A multistage stratified cluster sampling technique was employed to ensure a representative sample [5].

Study Variables

The outcome variable investigated in this study was iron supplementation during pregnancy, classified as “Yes” or “No.” Respondents in the SLDHS were prompted to indicate whether they had received any iron tablets or syrup during their most recent pregnancy. The response was coded as “Yes = 1” and “No = 0.”

The independent variables were classified into two levels as follows:

Community‐Level Variables: These included region and type of residence (rural and urban).

Community‐Level Variables: These included region and type of residence (rural and urban).

Individual‐Level Variables: These included: number of ANC visits (<4 visits vs. ≥4 visits), maternal age, maternal education, husband's education, household wealth index (poor, middle, and rich), ownership of a mobile telephone, internet usage, frequency of watching television, women's occupation (categorized as “engaged in paid employment” or “no paid employment” to acknowledge that women without formal jobs still perform significant domestic work), and husband's work status (engaged in paid employment vs. no paid employment).

Individual‐Level Variables: These included: number of ANC visits (<4 visits vs. ≥4 visits), maternal age, maternal education, husband's education, household wealth index (poor, middle, and rich), ownership of a mobile telephone, internet usage, frequency of watching television, women's occupation (categorized as “engaged in paid employment” or “no paid employment” to acknowledge that women without formal jobs still perform significant domestic work), and husband's work status (engaged in paid employment vs. no paid employment).

Data Management and Analysis

Data were managed and analyzed using STATA version 16.0. The svy command was used to account for the complex survey design (weighting, clustering, and stratification). Descriptive statistics (weighted frequencies and percentages) were used to summarize participant characteristics. At the bivariate level, the Pearson chi‐square test was used to assess associations between each independent variable and iron supplement uptake.

Given the hierarchical data structure (individuals nested within communities), a multilevel binary logistic regression model was used to identify determinants of iron supplement uptake. A three‐model building approach was utilized as follows:

Model I (Null Model): An empty model with no predictors to assess community‐level variance via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Model I (Null Model): An empty model with no predictors to assess community‐level variance via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Model II (Individual‐Level Model): Included only individual and household‐level variables.

Model II (Individual‐Level Model): Included only individual and household‐level variables.

Model III (Full Model): Included both individual‐ and community‐level variables. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Bivariate Analysis of Factors Associated With Iron Supplement Uptake

The bivariate analysis presented in Table 2 shows that several factors were significantly associated with iron supplement uptake. At the community level, a strong association was observed between geographical region and uptake (χ 2 = 227.51, p < 0.00001), with Sool and Sanaag showing substantially lower rates compared to Marodijeh and Togdheer. Conversely, no significant association was found with place of residence (χ 2 = 2.70, p = 0.4745). The number of antenatal visits showed a highly significant association (χ 2 = 361.47, p < 0.00001), with women attending ≥4 visits more likely to report uptake compared to those with <4 visits. At the individual level, maternal age did not show a significant association (χ 2 = 10.13, p = 0.1380), though mothers aged 40–49 years had a lower uptake rate. However, a significant association was found between maternal educational level and uptake (χ 2 = 153.12, p < 0.00001), with uptake rising from those with no education to secondary education. Similarly, the frequency of watching television (χ 2 = 160.40, p < 0.00001) and mobile telephone ownership (χ 2 = 57.32, p < 0.00001) were both highly significant, with those watching TV weekly and owning a mobile phone more likely to report uptake. Internet use also showed a strong association (χ 2 = 111.33, p < 0.00001), as did the husband's employment status (χ 2 = 290.02, p < 0.00001), with higher uptake reported when the husband worked. The wealth index indicated a significant relationship (χ 2 = 345.19, p < 0.00001), showing a clear dose–response correlation with uptake increasing from the poor to the rich. Additionally, a significant association was found regarding whether the husband ever attended school (χ 2 = 16.03, p = 0.0143), whereas women's occupation did not show a significant association (χ 2 = 1.91, p = 0.4918). Finally, the total number of children ever born showed a statistically nonsignificant association with iron supplement uptake (χ 2 = 19.63, p = 0.1113).

Random Effect Analysis and Model Comparison

The random effects analysis, presented in Table 4, employs a mixed‐effects logistic regression to explore how individual and community‐level factors impact the uptake of iron supplements while accounting for data clustering. Model I (the null model), featuring only a random intercept, reveals significant cluster variance (τ 2 = 2.02, p < 0.001) and an ICC of 0.38, indicating that 38% of the variance is attributable to differences between clusters, highlighting the necessity of a multilevel approach. In Model II (the individual‐level model), which incorporates individual‐level predictors, the cluster variance significantly decreases to 0.46 (p < 0.001), with an ICC dropping to 0.12, suggesting that individual characteristics explain a substantial amount of the cluster‐level variation. Finally, Model III (the full model), which includes both individual and community predictors, dramatically reduces cluster variance to 0.04 and results in a very low ICC of 0.01, with a nonsignificant p value for the LR test (0.190). This indicates that the inclusion of both levels of predictors sufficiently accounts for the clustering in the data. Model comparison through AIC, BIC, and −2Log‐likelihood demonstrates that Model III provides the best fit, confirming its effectiveness in capturing the outcome's complexity.

Discussion

This study provides critical insights into iron supplement uptake in Somaliland, revealing a critically low prevalence and highlighting the powerful roles of healthcare access, socioeconomic status, and media exposure. The finding that only 37.08% of pregnant women took any iron supplements is alarmingly deficient, falling far short of the WHO's 80% coverage target for routine interventions and signaling a major public health failure [1]. This rate is comparable to or lower than those in other challenging settings in sub‐Saharan Africa [7, 10, 11, 12]. Interestingly, although Somaliland struggles with an uptake of 37%, neighboring regions, such as the Ethiopian Somali Region, have reported even lower ANC 4+ attendance and IFAS adherence, suggesting a pervasive cross‐border challenge in providing maternal health services to nomadic and pastoralist populations [13, 14].

The most potent predictor of iron supplement uptake was attending four or more ANC visits. However, it is notable that even among those attending ANC, oral consumption is not guaranteed. Behavioral barriers, such as the unpleasant metallic taste of iron, constipation, and local myths, regarding the supplements making the baby “too large” for delivery, often lead women to receive the pills but not actually ingest them orally. Furthermore, even when women are motivated, supply‐side inconsistencies mean that facilities frequently run out of stock, leaving providers unable to dispense the medication despite the visit.

Socioeconomic factors emerged as powerful determinants. The strong, dose‐dependent association with household wealth and the significant influence of a husband's employment and education align with studies across LMICs [15, 16, 17]. In this study, the wealth index was used as a composite variable; however, the influence of its individual components—such as livestock ownership in nomadic areas versus cash income in urban areas—suggests that economic stability primarily facilitates uptake by reducing the “opportunity cost” of traveling to a clinic. The husband's role is particularly salient in a patriarchal context, like Somaliland. An employed and educated partner is more likely to understand, support, and enable his wife's access to healthcare. This finding, juxtaposed with the fact that 99% of women were not in paid employment, underscores that maternal health is inextricably linked to household economics and male engagement. The term “no paid employment” is used here to reflect that although these women are not earning a salary, they are often engaged in demanding household labor and childcare which limits their time for healthcare visits.

Although this study focused on uptake (whether a woman ever took the supplement), future research must address the varying levels of adherence (the duration and consistency of use), as many women who “take” the supplement do not complete the recommended 90‐day course.

Strengths and Limitations

The primary strength of this study is its use of a large, nationally representative dataset. However, there are limitations. The cross‐sectional design prevents causal claims. Furthermore, the survey did not capture supply‐side variables, such as facility‐level stock‐outs or the specific quality of counseling provided by midwives, both of which are critical to understanding why uptake remains low even among ANC attendees.

Conclusion and Recommendations

The uptake of iron supplementation during pregnancy in Somaliland is critically low. On the basis of these findings, the following recommendations are proposed as follows:

Strengthen and Promote ANC Services: Prioritize increasing the coverage and quality of ANC. This must include a robust supply chain management system to ensure that iron supplements are consistently in stock so that an ANC visit always results in the provision of the medication.
