A Dataset for Multiple Structural Representations of Urban Road Maps Across Chinese Cities
Authors: Hong Zhang, Yukan Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban road networks form the backbone of cities, shaping mobility, accessibility, spatial organization and urban form. However, widely used road-network data sources, such as OpenStreetMap, are primarily organized for general geographic representation and navigation-oriented applications, and they often require further processing before they can support systematic urban studies. In particular, existing datasets rarely provide standardized multi-representational versions of the same road networks, although geometric, topological, semantic, and cognitive perspectives are all essential for understanding different aspects of urban structure. Here, we present a dataset of multiple structural representations of urban road maps, covering 293 prefecture-level cities and four provincial-level municipalities in China. The dataset provides simplified and topologically reconstructed road networks in four complementary graph models: segment-based primal graphs, segment-based dual graphs, stroke-based dual graphs, and mixed dual graphs that integrate named streets with strokes. Together, these representations capture the geometric, topological, semantic, and cognitive properties of urban road networks within a standardized framework.Validation demonstrates high geometric fidelity (R2 = 0.991) and strong topological consistency, with 99% of original connectivity retained after processing. Provided in GIS-compatible Shapefile and GraphML formats, the dataset offers an open-access and reproducible foundation for research on urban morphology, spatial cognition, accessibility, space syntax, and complex network analysis. It is designed primarily for morphological and structural analysis of urban road networks, rather than detailed operational traffic modelling requiring lane capacity, turning restrictions, signalisation, travel speeds, real-time traffic conditions, or fine-grained directionality.

Background & Summary

Road networks serve as the fundamental physical infrastructure of urban systems, and embody the cumulative outcomes of planning decisions, socioeconomic interactions, and cultural influences embedded in urban space1–5. The topological structure of road networks organizes accessibility, mobility patterns, and spatial structure6,7, while also influencing urban growth, land-use evolution, and spatial transformation8–12. Understanding road-network structure has therefore become increasingly important in urban planning, transportation geography, and network science13–16.

A key step in studying road networks lies in choosing appropriate representations that emphasize characteristics relevant to the research question. Representation refers to the transformation of raw street maps into abstract forms, typically graph-based structures14,17. Different representations highlight different dimensions of road systems: for example, segments preserve geometry, junctions emphasize connectivity, names add semantic meaning, and strokes capture perceptual continuity18–21. These choices are not neutral, but directly influence structural analyses, such as the statistical distribution of graph metrics and their correlations with urban form and human activity22–25. Consequently, representation must be understood as a methodological foundation that shapes the outcomes of modeling, simulation, and optimization17,26.

Currently, most open road datasets are designed for visualization or navigation, represented by OpenStreetMap. To facilitate studies that focus more on the morphology of street networks, several open-source tools and workflows have been developed for road-network preprocessing and simplification, including OSMnx9, cityseer27, parenx28, neatnet29, and recent street-simplification methods. These studies provide important methodological foundations for cleaning, simplifying, and extracting centerline representations from road-network data. However, most existing datasets and workflows primarily remain confined to a single representation, commonly the primal graph, thereby overlooking the explicit connectivity, hierarchical organization, semantic structure, and cognitive continuity of road networks. Furthermore, there is still a lack of standardized datasets that provide multiple structural representations of the same road networks, making it difficult to evaluate how representation choices shape research outcomes.

To address these gaps, we present a dataset of multiple structural representations of urban road networks for 293 prefecture-level cities and 4 provincial-level municipalities in China, derived from OpenStreetMap(OSM). Specifically, we simplified the road network as necessary and then constructed four representations based on road segments, junctions, named streets, and strokes to generate the corresponding network datasets. Each representation highlights a distinct perspective for understanding road structure, drawing respectively from geometric, topological, semantic, and cognitive standpoints. Building on this design, the standardized dataset facilitates direct comparison among these representations, providing a basis to assess how alternative structural perspectives influence the understanding of urban road networks,

Our work aims to lower technical barriers, enhance reproducibility, and promote interdisciplinary research on the spatial organization and evolution of cities. As such, this dataset holds promise for applications in accessibility modeling, urban morphology studies, spatial cognition research, and comparative analyses of city form.

Methods

This research develops multiple graph representation approaches for urban road maps. The overall workflow (Fig. 1) consists of three main stages: simplification, topology reconstruction, and network representation. Data source

The raw road-network data used in this study were obtained from OpenStreetMap (OSM, https://www.openstreetmap.org), a publicly accessible, community-contributed geographic database. Road-network data for each city were retrieved using the graph_from_polygon() function in OSMnx v2.1.09. The extraction were based on the administrative polygon boundary of each target city. The OSM access date was fixed as 2025-01-01 to ensure temporal consistency across cities.

The selected road classes include motorway, trunk, primary, secondary, tertiary, unclassified, service, residential, and living_street. These classes were used to construct the fundamental urban road network (URN). To ensure topological consistency, only the largest connected component of each city’s road network was retained. The processed data were stored in a geographic database, and the three key OSM attribute fields, namely “highway”, “oneway”, and “name”, were retained for subsequent processing and analysis. The city administrative boundaries used for polygon-based extraction were obtained from https://cloudcenter.tianditu.gov.cn/administrativeDivision.

Simplification of an urban road map

Because OSM road data are primarily designed for traffic representation, they may introduce duplication issues when applied in urban spatial research27,28. Simplification is therefore a crucial step to transform OSM road data from a traffic-oriented to a spatial-oriented representation. Given the computational constraints and the objectives of this study, we adopted an efficient simplification strategy focused on aggregating aggregating multi-line features that represent different carriageways of the same road.

Given that these features are typically approximately parallel road segments and are tagged as one-way roads, we implemented a buffer-based method to identify candidate roads to be aggregated. Specifically, we first filter the dataset using the “oneway” attribute, retaining only roads marked as “yes”. Then, a fixed-width buffer polygon (50 meter) was generated around each one-way road. If two parallel one-way roads had similar azimuths (≤30°) and a sufficient portion (80%) of one segment’s length fell within the buffer of another, they were treated as candidate duplicates for aggregation (Fig. 2b).

Simplifcation of Roads in OSM data. (a) Original road network from OSM. (b) Blue buffers are generated around one-way roads, and roads identified for aggregation are highlighted in yellow. (c) Buffers of roads marked for aggregation are merged into unified zones. (d) Centerlines of the aggregated roads are extracted, shown in red.

After candidate roads were identified, all 50-meter buffer polygons of these roads, were aggregated into unified spatial zones through the Dissolve operation in ArcGIS32 (Fig. 2c). To preserve topological continuity, buffer polygons of connector roads whose two endpoints intersected candidate roads were also incorporated and dissolved into the unified zones.

Finally, road centerlines were extracted from the dissolved buffer polygons33, resulting in a simplified and topologically consistent representation of the road network (Fig. 2d). The centerline extraction procedure was implemented using the Polygon to Centerline tool developed by Dilts34.The method is based on Voronoi skeletonization. Specifically, the input polygons are first densified to increase boundary sampling density and improve the stability of subsequent geometric operations. The boundary vertices are then used as input points to construct Thiessen (Voronoi) polygons, from which the Voronoi edges located within the original polygon are extracted to form an initial skeleton network. Finally, a pruning procedure is applied to iteratively remove dangling segments that are not connected to other line segments, thereby refining the skeleton and producing a centerline representation of the polygon.

After simplification, duplicate roads were converted into single-line representations. However, the process of centerline extraction may sometimes compromise the original topological structure. To address this, we applied following corrective measures to restore integrity:

correction of displaced hanging nodesSome hanging nodes of the extracted centerlines were originally connected to roads that did not require simplification. During centerline extraction, these nodes could become spatially displaced, producing erroneous new nodes or artificial dead ends. To identify and correct such cases, we first obtained the original connection points between simplified and non-simplified roads through intersection analysis. For each hanging node in the extracted centerlines, a search was performed within a 50 m radius, corresponding to the buffer width used in the simplification process. If an original connection point was found within this distance, the hanging node was regarded as an artifact of spatial displacement rather than a true dead end. The terminal 50 m segment of the centerline was then trimmed and reconnected to the original connection point, thereby restoring the correct network topology. True dead ends were retained when no original connection point existed within the search radius.

In some cases, roads connected to one side of neighboring or parallel road segments representing the same road corridor appeared disconnected after simplification (Fig. 3a). To resolve this issue, we extended road segments whose endpoints lay within a predefined search range from the new centerline, enabling them to intersect correctly with the simplified centerline. Based on the buffer width and the possible displacement introduced during simplification, the search range was set to 100 meters. This operation re-established junctions and ensured that road connections were properly represented. For junctions formed by extended lines, overly close nodes were further merged to avoid redundancy and maintain a clean network topology.

In addition to topological simplification, this study also applied the Wang–Müller algorithm35 to reduce road geometry complexity. During simplification, key inflection points of road segments that reflect the bending characteristics of roads were preserved, as they directly influence directional changes and human perception of spatial morphology. Intermediate points that did not significantly alter geometric form were removed.

The Wang–Müller algorithm effectively streamlines geometric data while avoiding distortion caused by excessive simplification. This reduces the storage burden of curve geometry in the Linestring format, improves computational efficiency in large-scale urban analysis, and alleviates constraints imposed by limited system resources.

Derivation of the basic modeling units of an urban road map

Graph-based representations of urban road networks enable researchers to identify structural regularities underlying the apparent diversity of road morphology. The construction of a topological connectivity graph depends critically on the definition of the basic modeling units and the way their junction relationships are represented. Depending on the analytical focus, these units may be defined as road segments, junctions, strokes, or named streets. The concepts and derivation procedures for these units are outlined below.

road segments and junctionsSegment-based and junction-based representations are widely used in GIS models, as they provide an intuitive way of capturing connectivity through adjacent nodes and links. Most existing methods for road segments generation rely solely on geometric characteristics, treating each continuous stretch of road between two junctions as an independent segment. Together, these segments and their associated junctions form the basic segment-junction representation of the road network.

strokesAlthough segment-junction representations are straightforward and widely adopted, they have limitations in capturing the emergent properties of complex networks and traffic flow distribution. To address this, the concept of the stroke or natural street was introduced to reflect cognitive aspects of human perception and mobility18. A stroke is a higher-level unit formed by concatenating adjacent segments according to the principle of good continuity.Three main strategies are commonly used for stroke generation: self-fit, self-best-fit, and every-best-fit36. In this study, we adopt the every-best-fit strategy, as it has been shown to yield more consistent results and reduce the randomness associated with other methods37. The principle of the every-best-fit strategy is to evaluate all pairs of road segments incident at a junction and iteratively concatenate those with the smallest deflection angle. For example, as shown in Fig. 4, if segment b forms a smaller deflection angle with segment c than with segment a, then b will be concatenated with c, while a will instead be concatenated with d—even if the deflection angle between a and b is smaller than that between a and d.The implementation procedure is as follows:Step 1: For each junction, calculate the pairwise deflection angles between all incident segments.Step 2: Identify the two segments with the smallest deflection angle and assign them to the same stroke. The deflection angle between segments considered to be part of the same stroke must satisfy a continuity threshold, which is set to 60° based on conventions in the existing literature.Step 3: Repeat this pairing process until only one or no unassigned segments remain, or until no further pairs satisfy the continuity threshold.Step 4: After processing all junctions, merge all segments assigned to the same stroke into continuous stroke lines.

The implementation procedure is as follows:

Step 1: For each junction, calculate the pairwise deflection angles between all incident segments.

Step 2: Identify the two segments with the smallest deflection angle and assign them to the same stroke. The deflection angle between segments considered to be part of the same stroke must satisfy a continuity threshold, which is set to 60° based on conventions in the existing literature.

Step 3: Repeat this pairing process until only one or no unassigned segments remain, or until no further pairs satisfy the continuity threshold.

Step 4: After processing all junctions, merge all segments assigned to the same stroke into continuous stroke lines.

Segments with identical names that are spatially connected are then merged into continuous streets.

For unnamed segments, the stroke-generation method described above is applied, ensuring that all parts of the network are represented as meaningful structural units. Finally, the named streets and stroke-based units are integrated to produce a combined map that captures both semantic (name-based) and geometric–cognitive (stroke-based) properties of the urban road network.

Construction of the topological connectivity graph of an urban road map

After deriving the basic modelling units of an urban road map, including road segments, junctions, named streets, and strokes, the topological connectivity graph can be constructed by defining the intersection relationships among these units. In general, two main types of graph representations are employed: the primal graph and the dual graph. The topological connectivity graphs constructed in this study are designed for morphological and structural representations of urban road networks rather than operational transport-network modelling. Accordingly, intersections are defined primarily according to planimetric geometric relationships. Relevant OSM attributes, such as layer, bridge, tunnel, access restrictions, road hierarchy, and ramp-related classifications, are retained where available, but they are not used in the current version to reconstruct a fully operational three-dimensional transport topology.

primal graphThe primal graph captures the fundamental connectivity of the road network. In this representation, nodes correspond to junctions where roads converge, and edges represent the continuous segments connecting these junctions. This provides a direct and intuitive visualization of the physical layout of the road network, making it particularly suitable for analyzing overall road network topology.

primal graph

The primal graph captures the fundamental connectivity of the road network. In this representation, nodes correspond to junctions where roads converge, and edges represent the continuous segments connecting these junctions. This provides a direct and intuitive visualization of the physical layout of the road network, making it particularly suitable for analyzing overall road network topology.

dual graphIn contrast, the dual graph defines the basic modeling units as nodes, while edges capture the intersection relationships between these units in the original road map. Rather than focusing on geometric continuity, the dual graph emphasizes the relational structure among road units, highlighting the hierarchical organization of the network and the patterns of connectivity among streets. This approach is particularly useful for studying structural hierarchies, network resilience, and patterns of traffic flow. The dual graph in this study was constructed by identifying intersection relationships among road units—whether defined as segments, strokes, or named streets.

Together, the primal and dual graphs provide complementary perspectives on urban road networks. From these representations, a wide range of network properties can be computed, including centrality measures, clustering coefficients, hierarchy, small-world characteristics, and community structures. Comparative analyses can then be carried out across road networks of different sizes, shapes, and patterns.

Technical Validation

Because the dataset integrates geometric simplification with topological reconstruction, rigorous validation procedures were undertaken to ensure its accuracy and reliability. The validation focused on two key aspects: (i) Geometric fidelity: whether the simplified road networks preserve the spatial form and geometry of the original maps; (ii) Topological soundness: whether the connectivity structures of the networks remain consistent after simplification. To this end, the simplified road network dataset was compared against several well-established datasets describing the morphology and structure of urban roads. Validation combined both visual inspection and statistical analysis to comprehensively assess the effects of simplification.

Geometric examination of road networks

The geometric accuracy of the simplified road networks was examined through visual comparisons and quantitative measurements. As shown in Fig. 6, visually, the simplified networks closely reproduce the orientation and spatial relationships of the original road segments, with strong visual congruence between the two. Importantly, arterial roads that were represented by multiple parallel lines in the original OSM data are simplified into single-line representations, thereby reducing redundancy while maintaining geometric consistency.

Examples of original and corresponding simplified urban road maps.

To quantitatively assess geometric fidelity, we compared road segment lengths before and after simplification. For Chinese cities, a linear regression analysis was performed between the lengths of original and simplified road segments (Fig. 7). The results show a coefficient of determination (R² = 0.991) and a regression slope of 0.887, which is very close to the ideal value of 1. These findings indicate that the simplification introduces minimal geometric distortion and that the simplified road networks remain highly consistent with their original counterparts.

Regression analysis of road segment lengths before and after simplification.

Topological examination of road networks

While geometry was simplified, our workflow was designed to preserve the fundamental connectivity and navigable paths of the road network. Duplicate roads were aggregated and segments merged into higher-level units, but the overall connectivity relationships were maintained. Topological verification was conducted at two levels: (i) global level: connectivity integrity was assessed using the largest connected component of the network; (ii) local level: The logic of road connectivity was validated using a combination of spatial grid sampling and shortest path comparisons39.

Since the reconstruction of higher-level topological units does not alter the underlying road connectivity, validation was performed against the original graph. At the global level, the connected component rate of most cities reached 99% in the simplified networks. The average difference compared with the fully connected graphs before simplification was only 0.07%, primarily due to some terminal roads failing to connect correctly under the distance threshold. At the local level, Pearson correlation coefficients were used to quantify differences in path lengths between original and simplified networks. Across all cities, the average correlation coefficient was 0.943, indicating a strong positive relationship.

These results confirm that the simplified networks preserve both the geometric structure and topological connectivity of the original road maps, thereby demonstrating that the simplification process is both efficient and reliable.

Usage Notes

Our dataset provide formats that support both spatial and network analysis, allowing researchers to apply the data flexibly according to their research objectives. For studies requiring geometric information, the spatial datasets can be directly accessed in Geographic Information System (GIS) software such as QGIS and ArcGIS, or processed programmatically using Python libraries such as GeoPandas. These formats preserve the detailed geometry of road networks and are suitable for analyses of spatial patterns, accessibility, and morphology.

For network-based studies, the datasets can be imported into graph visualization and analysis tools including Gephi, Pajek, and NetworkX. Different graph representations can be selected depending on the analytical focus. The primal graph, in which nodes represent junctions and edges represent road segments, is particularly useful for space syntax analysis and shortest-path modeling. The dual graph, in which nodes represent reconstructed road units such as strokes or named streets, is better suited for evaluating network robustness, hierarchy, and community structure.

A key feature of the dataset is the consistency of node identifiers across spatial and network files. In the primal graph dataset, node identifiers correspond to the endpoints of road segments in the spatial data, while in the dual graph dataset, node identifiers correspond to reconstructed road units. This design ensures seamless interoperability between geometric and network-based analyses, enabling researchers to combine spatial and topological perspectives in an integrated framework.

While our workflow has been applied to a single time slice of road network data for Chinese cities, we believe its applicability extends well beyond this specific context. The proposed processing framework, centered on generating standardized multi-representational networks, is not inherently dependent on regional mapping conventions or street-naming practices. Rather, it operates on fundamental topological and geometric properties of road networks that are universally present. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that variations in road morphology, hierarchical classification, and informal street structures across world regions may influence certain preprocessing steps. We therefore encourage researchers to apply our workflow to cities in other geographical and cultural contexts, as well as to the same cities across different time points, to further test its generalizability and to explore dynamic urban evolution. Such applications would not only validate but also potentially enrich the framework through region-specific adaptations.

Limitations

Our processing framework relies on OpenStreetMap (OSM) data and therefore inherits the inherent variability in data completeness, positional accuracy, and mapping practices. Although OSM coverage in Chinese cities is generally sufficient for large-scale urban road-network analysis, differences in contribution density, digitization practices, road-class tagging, and the representation of complex junctions across cities may introduce heterogeneity into the resulting network representations. Such variation may also affect the identification and merging of parallel carriageways, thereby influencing the stability of the simplification process in certain contexts.

A second limitation concerns the simplified treatment of complex road structures. Because the dataset is designed to support standardized morphological and structural comparisons across cities, complex configurations such as grade-separated junctions, elevated roads, tunnels, flyovers, motorway ramps, and interchange systems are represented within a planimetric connectivity framework. This choice improves cross-city consistency and comparability, but it may introduce inaccuracies when the research objective is to model operational transport processes that depend on vertical separation, access restrictions, turning rules, lane capacity, or lane-level details.

Future extensions could address this limitation by developing attribute-enriched network representations. For example, intersection types, including at-grade intersections, grade-separated intersections, T-junctions, cross intersections, and trumpet interchanges, could be encoded as node attributes in the primal graph or as edge attributes in the dual graph. Similarly, road-segment types such as bridges, tunnels, flyovers, ramps, and elevated roads could be represented as edge attributes in the primal graph or as node attributes in the dual graph. Such extensions would allow the dataset to support more refined modelling of infrastructural and functional heterogeneity while preserving its core value as a comparable morphological and structural road-network dataset.

Finally, although the aggregation of parallel carriageways improves the clarity and consistency of the resulting spatial representation, it inevitably leads to a loss of fine-grained geometric and functional information. Features such as lane-level distinctions, directional carriageways, and complex multi-branch configurations are simplified into unified structures, which may not fully preserve all infrastructural details of the original road system. Consequently, the dataset is well suited for research on urban form, spatial cognition, space syntax, accessibility, and structural network analysis, but is less appropriate for detailed operational traffic modelling that requires lane capacity, turning restrictions, signalisation, travel speeds, real-time traffic conditions, or fine-grained directionality.
