Integrative multimodal graph convolutional models for predictive short-form video recommendations
Authors: Xi Zhang, Jun Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVideo clips became extremely popular when viewers began to shift away from television and to mobile. However, their ability to report real world distribution will be limited by their ability to obtain data or evaluation criteria accurately and efficiently. This paper describes a new recommendation system based on Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), a self-attention mechanism, and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) to capture multimodal user-item interactions as well as user preferences and item attributes. The use of modality-specific graphs allowed us to express user preferences and item attributes correctly, while the separate visual, audio, and textual modality contributed to our comprehensive representation of multimodal features. The multi-head attention mechanism assigns adaptive weights to neighbors during aggregation, while dynamic negative sampling selects hard negative items that are similar to positive interactions but not engaged by the user. The integrated model consistently outperformed baseline models and partially upgraded configurations in experimental evaluations. The results show that incorporating multimodal data and reinforcement learning improves recommendation performance, particularly in terms of Precision@K, Recall@K, and NDCG@K. DRL allowed the model to adapt to changing user preferences by optimizing recommendation policies based on cumulative reward signals from sequential user interactions.Keywords: Short-form video, Recommendations, Graph Convolutional Model, PredictionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Driven by the popularization of services such as TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, short form videos have quickly established themselves as a cornerstone of mobile platform content consumption. Unlike long-form videos, short-form videos focus on brief story telling, fast visual stimulation, and high interaction with users, giving rise to extremely dynamic consumption styles and rapidly changing user interests. These attributes create significant challenges for recommendation systems because the system must model multimodal content features while continuously updating user preference representations.

The conventional recommendation systems, such as collaborative filtering, matrix factorization, etc. have been well studied and proved to be successful in static or mildly dynamic scenarios1. But these approaches rely on the past user item interaction and rarely take into account the vast semantic information of short video content2,3. Compared to traditional media, short videos naturally involve multiple heterogeneous modalities including visual appearance, audio signals and textual descriptions, all of which provide complementary information in terms of content semantics and potential user interests. Disregarding these modalities or handling them in a homogenized way may severely reduce the expressiveness and performance of recommendation models4.

Promising developments in multimodal recommender systems have been made to incorporate external content features to enhance recommendation quality5. However, a number of existing methods either concentrate on a single leading modality (e.g., visual features) or apply early fusion or late fusion methods, which cannot explicitly capture the interactions between modalities6,7. Besides, the majority of multimodal recommendation approaches focus on optimizing short term goals (i.e. immediate clicks or views), and they do not explicitly be built to model long term user engagement or preference drift, which is an essential feature for short-form video platform as user’s interest can volatilize very fast in the flow of time8,9.

Graph-based recommendation models are an effective approach for modeling complex user–item relationships, as they represent interactions as graphs and learn structured representations through message passing between nodes10,11.Particularly, graph convolutional networks (GCN) are able to effectively diffuse the collaborative signals on the user–item interaction graph12. However, current graph based approaches usually consider content features as uniform node attribute or single interaction graph, resulting in the loss of Modality specific information and failing to leverage fine-grained multi modal dependencies in short form videos13,14.

Meanwhile, reinforcement learning (RL) has attracted more and more attention in the recommender systems community for its capacity to model sequential decision making and optimize long term effectiveness. Different from supervised learning methods, which are designed to predict instant interactions, RL-based recommenders have the potential to explicitly take the delayed feedback into account and to tradeoff between exploration and exploitation. Even so, However, the integration of multimodal representation learning, graph neural networks, and deep reinforcement learning remains limited, particularly in short-form video recommendation tasks where both multimodal content and sequential user behavior must be modeled15–17. To capture cross-modal relations, we propose a multi-head attention based fusion mechanism that projects Modality specific representations to a common latent space, facilitating cross mode information sharing. In addition, we add a dynamic negative sampling as well as a contrastive learning to further improve the discrimination of representations and the robustness on noisy or insufficient interactions.

In addition to representation learning, we model the recommendation as a sequential decision making problem and leverage deep reinforcement learning to maximize long term user engagement. The deep reinforcement learning (DRL) agent learns a recommendation policy to maximize the expected cumulative rewards from user interactions, and the model captures the changes of preferences over time. Then, the prediction layer refines the outputs of DRL to generate a ranked candidate list, which is a value-aware ranking refinement instead of another recommendation module.

Related work

There has been a significant amount of progress in short-form video recommendation systems, and yet many models are still confronted by challenges that do not allow them to appropriately understand the complexities of multimodal content and fast-evolving user preferences. A slotted discussion of some of the relevant research identify is presented below, so that each paper can be directly compared to the proposed model.

Collaborative filtering is one of the most popular techniques employed in the design of the recommendation system. He and McAuley18 proposed scalable factorization techniques that consider visual features learned from pre-trained deep networks to solve the cold start problem in item recommendation. However, the proposed method is based on the use of visual features and fails to consider the multimodal nature of short-form video content that may contain various types of features, including visual, acoustic, and textual features. Similarly, the Attentive Collaborative Filtering (ACF) model proposed in19 that uses attention mechanisms to design the item recommendation system for multimedia content. The proposed method considers item and component-level implicit feedback to design the item recommendation system. The experimental analysis of the proposed method proves the effectiveness of the attention mechanism in improving the accuracy of the item recommendation system. However, the proposed method fails to consider the evolution of user preferences that is an essential characteristic of short-form video content.

Furthermore, graph-based recommendation models have received significant attention due to their ability to capture user-item interactions as a graph. GraphSAGE20 is a prominent inductive framework that generates low-dimensional node embeddings within a large graph by aggregating neighborhood information. However, it mainly focuses on textual features without considering other features like visual and acoustic features, which are important for short-form video recommendation. In addition, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF)21 models user-item interactions by utilizing graph convolution operations to learn collaborative representations. Although it enhances user-item interactions, it fails to consider multimodal features of short-form videos. Therefore, it has limited capabilities to capture heterogeneous features for recommendation models.

Multimodal features are important for short-form video recommendation models as they consider heterogeneous features like visual, acoustic, and textual features. In this regard, self-organizing framework is proposed in22 for predicting user behavior for short-term user-generated videos (UGVs). The proposed approach mainly focuses on optimizing neural network architectures for dealing with cold-start problems. However, it mainly focuses on visual features without considering other features like acoustic features. In23, the authors proposed an econometric modeling approach along with deep learning for predicting consumer responses to e-commerce short videos. The proposed approach mainly considers features like product descriptions, visual aesthetics, and user enjoyment. Although it enhances prediction accuracy for recommendation models, it fails to consider interactions between heterogeneous features like visual, acoustic, and textual features. However, it is clear that content features are important for short-form video recommendation models; thus, most of the existing models have limited capabilities to capture heterogeneous features.

User engagement is seen to be an important aspect in ensuring the success of short-form video recommendation systems, especially on social media platforms such as TikTok, where user interests are constantly changing. For instance, the study24 sought to understand consumer behavior on Taobao.com by analyzing how short videos influence the product consumption experience. Some of the factors considered in this research include short videos’ subjectivity and product prices. This research provided insight into user engagement in short-term periods but did not consider long-term user engagement behavior. The study25 examined how short videos influence consumers’ engagement behavior by analyzing short video advertisements. From this research, it is evident that entertainment value, performance expectancy, and sales-related factors are some of the factors that impact user engagement behavior. However, this research mainly focused on short-term user engagement behavior and did not consider long-term user interaction behavior. The study26 analyzed 2,578 TikTok videos collected in 2022 to examine how perceived trustworthiness, expertise, and attractiveness influence consumers’ purchasing behavior. From this research, it is evident that these factors positively impact consumers’ purchasing behavior, whereas authenticity and brand heritage are seen to be related to consumers’ behavior in a U-shape. Although these research studies provided insight into user engagement behavior in short-term periods, they did not explicitly consider long-term user interaction behavior. On the other hand, reinforcement learning-based recommendation systems aim to improve user engagement behavior through optimization of recommendations to fit changing user interests.

Some studies regarding short-form video recommendation systems are focused on a narrower topic area, such as predicting mental health status or video addiction (often within the context of broader mental health topics) and do not address multi-mode short-form video recommendations. For example, the study27 estimated the effect of short-form videos on suicidal ideation and developed a Knowledge-Guided Neural Topic Model (NTM), highlighting the importance of topic modeling for video metrics. This study provides helpful information on the societal effects of short videos but the focus is limited and specific, which makes the results even less beneficial for a recommendation task generally. Another study28 prepared data for the early detection of video addiction from social media, using large language models to organize multimodal data and identify video addiction. Although the research provides significant advances in addiction detection, it has not explicitly attempted to optimize recommendations for sustained user engagement. In contrast, our contribution provides a generalizable, scalable solution for multimodal recommendation settings. It addresses the multimodal nature of short-form video content, as well as ensures sustained user engagement by optimizing for long term engagement through DRL, therefore being applicable to more short-form video platforms.

Multimodal recommendation systems have received increasing attention in recent years, with many studies focusing on graph-based modeling and cross-modal representation learning. Recent surveys on multimodal recommender systems emphasize that effectively exploiting heterogeneous content features—such as visual, acoustic, and textual modalities—and modeling interactions among these modalities remain major challenges in recommendation research29,30.

Several multimodal graph-based approaches have been proposed to address these challenges. For example, multimodal graph convolutional network (MMGCN) constructs modality-aware graphs to incorporate heterogeneous content features for micro-video recommendation31. Other studies have explored transformer-based architectures with multi-level fusion to model cross-modal interactions in structured recommendation tasks32. The study33 proposed a dual-perception framework (DP-GRGNN) to capture multimodal content features and evolving interaction patterns through graph neural networks. Similarly, the study34 introduced MVideoRec, which decomposes video content into modality-specific factors and applies contrastive learning to align multimodal representations for micro-video recommendation.

Although these approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of modeling modality-aware representations and cross-modal interactions, most existing methods primarily focus on representation learning and do not explicitly address long-term user engagement dynamics in recommendation systems.

Proposed method

The proposed method is made up of three parts. prediction, combination, and aggregation layers. We embed the information exchange between users and items into the representation learning in each modality by stacking numerous aggregate and combination layers. Finally, in the prediction layer, we use multi modal representations to forecast how each user will engage with each short video. In the following, we go over each element in depth. Finally, the model is trained using reinforcement learning to optimize recommendations, which are outputted through the prediction layer based on the learned user-item representations.

Positive pairs are defined as user-item pairs where an explicit interaction occurred, such as clicks, views, or ratings. In these cases, an interaction demonstrates the user prefers that item, and the intention is to pull representations of positive pairs closer together in embedding space to help the model learn meaningful relationships between users, and items a user has interacted with. In particular, the positive pair for each user consists of the user’s profile and items that user has interacted with.

Negative pairs have been sampled to serve as a backup for the positive pairs, helping the model learn to distinguish between relevant and irrelevant objects. In our work, negative pairs are generated dynamically during the training process. For each positive pair, we sample items that are similar in properties (e.g., features of content, attributes of items, user behavior characteristics) that the user has not engaged with. This is not random sampling, but rather creates dynamic negative pairs that are relevant to the user’s context and likely not as interesting, which makes them harder for the model to classify. The intended outcome is for the model to learn not only what a user likes, but equally as important, what they will likely not engage with, even though they are similar in other ways; learning from the negative pairs is critical.

The dynamic negative sampling method guarantees that negative pairs are useful and promote a more effective contrastive learning process. By including similar, cold items, the model learns to identify items more fiercely in their predictive likelihood of engaging or not engaging based on their relevance or not relevant. By sampling positive pairs based on user engagement and relevant but unengaged pairs, we are training a model to learn and optimize for a contrastive learning objective. The Contrastive Loss function penalizes the model when positive and negative embedding pairs have a similar embedding which acts as a driving force for the model to differentiate between items a user interacted with from other items. This consistently allows the model to represent user preferences supported by user preferences, resulting in improved recommendation performance overall.

Enhanced optimization framework with DRL

In the proposed optimization framework, we extend pairwise ranking methods, particularly Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR), by incorporating a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) approach to model user–item interactions. The recommendation task is formulated as a reinforcement learning problem in which the agent (the recommendation system) interacts with the environment (the user) to maximize long-term user engagement. At each time step \:t, the agent observes the user state , which represents the user profile and may include demographic attributes, historical interactions, and preference features. Based on this state, the agent selects an action , corresponding to recommending a set of candidate items. The agent then receives a reward that reflects user feedback, such as clicks, views, or purchases. Positive interactions produce higher rewards, while lack of engagement results in lower or negative rewards. To guard against overfitting, sophisticated regularization methods, such as dropout and layer normalization strategies are invoked to bolster model stability. Dropout helps to prevent overfitting and develops improved generalizability through randomly handling deactivation of a subset of the neurons in the model during training. Layer normalization facilitates each layer in the architecture to have consistent scaling across its features, as it is standardized through normalization that would speed up the progression of optimization and stabilize training of complex architectures operating on sparse data distributions. Dynamic negative sampling is a significant improvement over previous negative sampling methodologies. Dynamic indicates that the selection of hard negatives, for example, the negatives that are similar in some way to the positive items that the model needs to learn the finer distinctions that differentiate a positive item from a negative item. By concentrating on the more challenging negatives, the entire learning experience and resultant representations is improved.

An adjustable learning rate method is additionally implemented to make the training procedure even more efficient. The learning rate is adjusted according to how steep the gradient is, allowing for more precise fine tuning near convergence, and faster convergence when needed farther from the optimal solution. This adjustment allows the gradient to avoid excessive conservativeness when it approaches the optimal solution, or else converge too quickly. Similarly, regularizing methods such as dropout, or layer normalizing, help keep the model stable, especially when training on sparse data or modeling complex deep architectures. These regularizing methods prevent overfitting and keep a good balance between the complexity of the model and the accuracy of predictions, further enabling the model to generalize well to new data.

Dynamic negative sampling significantly boosts the model’s ability to discriminate between positive and negative items by focusing on more difficult, hard to learn instances38. This not only improves the model’s ability for effective item ranking, but more importantly, creates more discriminative user and item embeddings. We further transition from conventional pairwise ranking optimization to maximizing for long term user engagement and personalization through reinforcement learning (RL), specifically through deep Q-network (DQN) RL. By optimizing for improved user experience through balancing exploration and exploitation, this model can adapt over time with user interaction and improve the overall recommendation process.

Scalability

Scalability is an important consideration for recommendation systems operating on large-scale datasets. As the number of users and items increases, efficient graph processing becomes necessary to maintain manageable computational and memory costs. To address this challenge, we incorporate graph sampling and parallel computation techniques. Specifically, we adopt Cluster-GCN39, a sampling-based graph convolution method that partitions the full interaction graph into smaller subgraphs (clusters). Instead of processing the entire graph at once, the model performs convolution operations on sampled subgraphs during training. This strategy significantly reduces memory consumption and computational complexity while preserving the structural information of the original graph. In addition, the framework supports parallel computation through GPU acceleration using graph learning libraries such as PyTorch Geometric and Deep Graph Library (DGL). These libraries provide optimized implementations for graph convolution operations and enable distributed training across multiple processors or GPUs. By combining graph sampling with parallel computation, the proposed framework can efficiently scale to large user–item interaction graphs while maintaining reasonable training time and computational efficiency.

Handling noisy and delayed rewards in DRL

User interactions (like clicks, views, and purchases) with recommendation systems are often noisy, sparse, and delayed, creating challenges to using deterministic rewards. Below are some strategies to effectively deal with noisy and delayed rewards. Temporal Discounting allow actions to contribute different values to the reward signal based on when it is received. Temporal discounting works as a mechanism to reward delaying feedback appropriately while still assisting the agent in balancing immediate and delayed consequences when interactions occur much later. Reward Smoothing establishes a more stable reward signal through accounting for the level of user engagement, such as longer watching of a video (variability here could be > 0 rev for shorter view and 1.5 revs for longer view). Smoothing reduces the impact of random feedback and allows learning to focus on the stable patterns of user behavior over time. The Exploration-Exploitation Balance handles sparse rewards that can be addressed through forced exploration so that the model can acquire new items and exploit higher-performing items to enable feedback across a wider array of items. Lastly, Bootstrapping allows the agent to move back in time and add the reward signal from delayed feedback through the actions directly leading up to the reward signal, enabling the agent to adjust its policy to the delayed nature of the reward feedback and improve learning with intangibly sparse reward signals. Temporal-Difference Learning (TD) is a method that reduces the susceptibility of noisy rewards by quelling the recomputation of the policy and moves the policy forward, thus may improve stability if there is a large fluctuation in feedback. In conclusion, these strategies, including temporal discounting, reward smoothing, exploration–exploitation balancing, bootstrapping, and temporal-difference (TD) learning, enable the model to better handle noisy and delayed reward signals, resulting in more stable policy updates and improved ranking performance in recommendation tasks.

DRL agent and prediction layer interaction

In the proposed recommendation framework, Steps 7 and 8 play complementary roles in generating personalized recommendations. In Step 7, the deep reinforcement learning (DRL) agent produces candidate recommendations based on the learned user–item embeddings and the current state of the environment. The agent learns a recommendation policy that optimizes long-term user engagement by considering historical user interactions, such as clicks or views, and balancing exploration and exploitation during item selection. The recommendations generated by the DRL agent represent candidate items selected according to the learned policy. Because the agent must explore potentially useful items while exploiting known user preferences, the candidate set may include both highly relevant and exploratory recommendations. In Step 8, the prediction layer refines these candidate items by ranking them using the learned user–item representations. This layer evaluates the relevance of each candidate item based on the user’s historical behavior and overall preference profile, producing the final ranked recommendation list. The interaction between the DRL agent and the prediction layer forms a two-stage recommendation process. The DRL agent focuses on policy-driven candidate generation under a long-term engagement objective, while the prediction layer performs value-aware ranking refinement. Together, these components produce recommendations that balance immediate relevance with long-term user engagement.

Experimental validation

Dataset

One of the biggest and most varied datasets accessible for short-form videos recommendation tasks is the MicroLens one. It comprises one million short-form videos, 34 million users, and one billion user-item interaction behaviors. MicroLens distinguishes itself by including a vast range of raw modality data for videos, including titles, cover pictures, soundtrack, and full-length video material. Given that many conventional recommendation datasets rely on pre-extracted characteristics from films, this rich multimodal data provides a notable benefit.

MicroLens was created especially to enable study on content-driven short-form videos recommendation, therefore enabling researchers to use several modalities of video data for more accurate and customized suggestions. MicroLens enables more complex suggestions based on a larger variety of data, including textual, visual, and aural elements than many conventional datasets like MovieLens, which concentrates on movie ratings. Over a year, from June 2022 to June 2023, the dataset was gathered from a social entertainment venue. Videos with significant user engagement (i.e., more than 10,000 likes) were collected during this period, and user preferences were inferred from user–video interaction patterns such as comments, which were treated as indicators of positive feedback. This method guarantees that the dataset captures actual user behavior instead of depending just on synthetic ratings or comments. To extract feature embeddings, modality-specific models are used. For instance, visual features are extracted by using a convolutional neural network, while textual information is generated by using a transformer model. Similarly, acoustic features are extracted by using specialized models for audio information processing. These extracted features are incorporated into modality-specific user-item graphs.

To propagate information through modality-specific user-item graphs, graph convolutional networks are used. To further enhance the proposed system’s ability to represent users and items, an attention mechanism is proposed, which dynamically evaluates the importance of neighboring nodes during graph convolution.

To further enhance the proposed system’s ability to represent users and items, a cross-modal attention mechanism is proposed, which enables information from one modality to influence another modality.

The recommendation policy is optimized through a Deep Q-Network (DQN), which is used to address the recommendation problem as a sequential decision-making problem. By learning the Q-values that represent the estimation of long-term rewards, the recommendation system is able to maximize long-term user engagement. Furthermore, multitask learning is used to improve the optimization of the recommendation task and auxiliary tasks such as user attribute or item feature prediction.

The learning rate is an important parameter used in optimization. In our work, a learning rate of 0.001 is used for the DQN and GCN-based recommendation optimization. The Adam optimizer is used to train the model, which can dynamically adjust the learning rate during training to achieve better optimization performance.

The code provided in the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for Long-Term User Engagement in Video Recommendation Systems (Zhang & Yin, 2026) was used to perform the analysis42.

Results

In this section, the proposed method is evaluated on microlens data to answer the following case studies:

• Case 1

• How does the proposed method perform compared to state-of-the-art multi modal recommendation systems and other graph based models?

Our results show that the proposed method, consistently outperforms both traditional multi modal recommendation systems and graph based models. Specifically, the proposed method shows improvements in recall by 14.07% MicroLens dataset, compared to the best baseline models. These results indicate that modality-specific graph representations capture user preferences more accurately than traditional feature fusion approaches.

• Case 2

• How do various architectural choices (e.g., number of modalities and number of attention layers) influence the performance of the proposed method?

Through extensive ablation experiments, we find that incorporating multiple modalities significantly improves the performance of the proposed method. Among the modalities, visual features proved to have the most significant impact, followed by acoustic and textual modalities. Our results also show that increasing the number of layers improves performance up to three layers, after which the model starts to experience diminishing returns, especially for sparse datasets. The element-wise combination layer also outperforms the concatenation approach, as it better integrates Modality specific features.

Performance comparison (case 1)

We assess the efficacy of our method by comparing it with the state-of-the-art methods. Study18 incorporates the content characteristics and ID embeddings of each item as its representation, using the Matrix Factorization (MF) framework to recreate past interactions between people and objects. In the studies, we use a mixture of multi modal features as the content information to forecast the interactions between users and short videos. Study19 is the first approach that investigates multimedia recommendation feedback. It presents two modules based on attention mechanism to investigate feedback in item and component level. To investigate Modality specific user and short video features, we consider each modality as a component of the short video. Study GraphSAGE20 on a generic inductive framework that utilizes node information to enhance node representations for unobserved data. Specifically, it examines the structural information and the distribution of node attributes within the vicinity. The study NGCF21 proposed an innovative recommendation architecture that incorporates interactions between users and items into the embedding space. By using these interactions, the model encodes the collaborative signal to the representation. To provide an equitable comparison, we consider the multi modal aspects of short videos as ancillary information and include them in the framework to forecast the interactions between users and goods.

VBPR and ACF are content-aware collaborative filtering methods that take content information or multi-modal feedback information into account. However, these methods fail to fully leverage content information or multi-modal feedback information due to shallow feature fusion and poor modeling of high-order user-item relationships. Therefore, these methods perform less well than graph-based methods. On the other hand, graph-based methods such as NGCF21 and GraphSAGE20 achieve better performance than other methods by modeling neighborhood structures and high-order collaborative relationships through graph propagation. However, these methods usually fail to fully leverage multi-modal features as node features. This is because multi-modal features are important in short-form video recommendation tasks.

Recently, some multimodal graph-based methods, like MMGCN31, HTMF32, DP-GRGNN33, and MVideoRec34, have further improved the performance of recommendations. For example, in MMGCN, modality-specific graphs are constructed to capture heterogeneous information from contents. HTMF adopts transformer-based fusion mechanisms, while DP-GRGNN models dynamic interaction patterns with gated recurrent networks. MVideoRec further improves representation learning with modality decomposition and contrastive learning. Compared with the state-of-the-art method, MVideoRec, the proposed method achieves improved Precision from 0.1554 to 0.1589, with a relative improvement of 2.25%, and Recall from 0.3615 to 0.3849, with a relative improvement of 6.46%. This verifies that our method, which combines modality-specific graph convolution and deep reinforcement learning, can effectively capture user preference and thus improve the performance of recommendations. Although existing methods have made significant achievements in representation learning and modeling user behavior in the short term, our proposed method adopts deep reinforcement learning, modeling user behavior in the long term, and thus achieves the state-of-the-art results in all evaluation metrics.

The Proposed Model, which includes GCN, Attention, and DRL, needs the most training time, which is about 48 h, due to the combination of complexity from the GCNs, Attention, and Deep Reinforcement Learning model features. The use of GCNs, DRL, and Attention leads to a higher computational burden, especially due to the nature of DRL agents, who find items to recommend through exploration-exploitation over many steps during training.

The baseline models require less training time than the proposed framework. VBPR, which incorporates visual features into a collaborative filtering model, required approximately 20 h of training. ACF, which introduces attention mechanisms into collaborative filtering, required about 18 h. Although attention mechanisms improve representation learning, ACF remains computationally less intensive than models that integrate reinforcement learning.

Graph-based methods required moderately longer training times. GraphSAGE, which focuses on graph-based representation learning, required approximately 24 h of training. NGCF required about 22 h, as it models user–item interactions through graph convolution operations. These models are less complex than the proposed framework because they do not incorporate multimodal modeling, attention-based fusion, or reinforcement learning components.

In contrast, the proposed model—which combines graph convolutional networks, attention mechanisms, and deep reinforcement learning—required approximately 48 h of training. This increased training time is expected due to the additional components used to model multimodal features and optimize long-term user engagement. Despite the higher computational cost, the proposed framework achieves improved recommendation performance. Future work will investigate strategies to reduce training time while maintaining or improving model performance.

Inference latency analysis

In addition to training complexity, inference latency is an important consideration for practical recommendation systems deployed in online services. During inference, the model does not perform reinforcement learning updates or graph training; instead, it uses the pretrained embeddings and the learned policy to generate recommendations. Candidate items are first retrieved using the learned user–item embeddings, and the DRL agent then evaluates these candidates using the trained Q-network. Since the graph convolution and multimodal embedding computations are performed offline during training, the online inference stage mainly involves embedding lookup and lightweight neural network computations. This design significantly reduces inference latency and allows the model to generate recommendations in real time. Furthermore, parallel processing using GPU acceleration and efficient graph libraries such as PyTorch Geometric enables scalable inference even for large user–item graphs. In practice, the recommendation ranking step can be executed within milliseconds, making the proposed framework suitable for online recommendation environments.

Conclusion

This research presented a multimodal recommendation framework based on graph convolutional networks (GCNs), attention networks, and deep reinforcement learning (DRL) to improve short-form video recommendation. The proposed framework built modality graphs for visual content, acoustic content, and textual content to allow the recommendation system to process heterogeneous information from multiple sources. The framework is able to process multiple types of content features and user behavior through graph-based representation learning and attention networks. The experimental results showed that the proposed framework outperformed existing methods in Precision, Recall, and NDCG. This indicates that the proposed framework is effective in short-form video recommendation tasks.

Although this research has achieved good results in short-form video recommendation tasks, there are some limitations to be addressed. For example, the proposed framework is based on graph convolutional networks, attention networks, and deep reinforcement learning. These techniques increase the computational cost of training the recommendation framework. Additionally, the experiments were performed on a single dataset. The evaluation on other datasets is required to verify the generality of the proposed framework on different recommendation tasks. Besides, the reward function used in the reinforcement learning component is based on user interaction. However, user behavior is not only determined by interaction.

For future work, we will improve the efficiency of the proposed framework to reduce training costs without degrading recommendation accuracy. Additionally, we will test our framework on other datasets to verify its generality on different recommendation tasks. Furthermore, we will explore more advanced reward functions to improve the accuracy of recommendation systems.
