Energy-based devices for enhanced transdermal drug delivery in pathological scars: A review
Authors: Yinhua Zhao, Jiaxing Ma, Bo Li, Huang Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although laser-assisted drug delivery (LADD) technology is well established, its clinical application has now expanded to include a broader range of energy-based devices (EBDs). This review aims to evaluate the use of energy-based device-assisted drug delivery (EADD) in the treatment of hypertrophic scars (HTS) and keloids. Following a comprehensive search of English and Chinese databases, 30 relevant studies were included. These studies evaluated the combined application of various EBDs—including ablative and non-ablative fractional lasers, micro-plasma radiofrequency (MPR), ultrasound, and thermomechanical fractional injury—with pharmacological agents such as corticosteroids, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), verapamil, botulinum toxin type A (BTXA), and photosensitizers (5-aminolevulinic acid). The results indicate that for corticosteroids, EADD achieves efficacy comparable to that of intralesional injection while significantly reducing pain and lowering the risk of skin atrophy and telangiectasia, offering particular benefits for pediatric patients. The combination of EADD with 5-FU effectively improves scar thickness, whereas CO2 AFL-assisted photosensitizers delivery shows an exceptionally low recurrence rate in the treatment of acne-induced HTS. In contrast, for keloids, intralesional BTXA injections remain superior to CO2 AFL-assisted BTXA delivery. Furthermore, emerging strategies such as the triple-modality “light–light–sound” therapy, which combines intense pulsed light, CO₂ ablative fractional laser, and ultrasound, provide promising prospects for recalcitrant cases. In summary, EBDs enhance drug delivery through micropore formation or acoustic effects, establishing it as a key therapeutic strategy that balances efficacy with improved safety and patient compliance compared to monotherapy. However, standardized treatment protocols still require further validation through future research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10103-026-04965-7.

Introduction

Laser-assisted drug delivery (LADD) is a promising administration strategy that facilitates the deep penetration of drug molecules, devices, or cells by altering the structure of the stratum corneum, epidermis, or dermis. It was first successfully applied to the delivery of local anesthetics in 2002 [1] and has since gained widespread application in the field of dermatology [2]. However, with the rapid advancements in biomedical engineering, clinical exploration has extended beyond lasers alone. In recent years, various other energy-based devices (EBDs), including not only traditional ablative and non-ablative lasers but also micro-plasma radiofrequency (MPR) [3], ultrasound [4], and thermomechanical fractional injury (TMFI) technology [5]—have been successively applied to assist transdermal drug delivery. Of note, these technologies have demonstrated efficacy and safety profiles comparable to, or even superior to, lasers in treating pathological scars. Given this progress, a focus solely on LADD is no longer sufficient to comprehensively encompass the latest therapeutic advancements for pathological scars. Therefore, this review introduces the broader concept of “energy-based device-assisted drug delivery (EADD).” We aim to provide a comprehensive and systematic discussion on the clinical applications, mechanisms of action, and comparative efficacy of various EBDs (both laser and non-laser) in treating pathological scars, specifically hypertrophic scars (HTS) and keloids.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, and the VIP Database using the following key terms and their combinations: “energy-based device,” “laser,” “radiofrequency,” “plasma,” “electroporation,” “triamcinolone acetonide,” “corticosteroid,” “5-fluorouracil,” “bleomycin,” “verapamil,” “drug delivery,” “microchannel,” “combined,” “topical,” “keloid,” “hypertrophic,” and “scar.” Studies were included if they investigated EADD for treating HTS or keloids. To ensure the quality and relevance of the evidence, the following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) animal or in vitro studies; (2) incomplete data; (3) single case reports or case series with fewer than 10 patients; (4) non-primary literature (e.g., commentaries, reviews); (5) studies not published in English or Chinese; (6) studies published prior to 1998; and (7) articles with unavailable full text.

The initial search yielded 787 records. Two investigators (Yinhua Zhao and Jiaxing Ma) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by full-text assessment. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus after consultation with a third reviewer (Bo Li). Data extracted from the included studies encompassed the following: (1) study population; (2) primary outcomes (objective scar assessment); (3) secondary outcomes (patient satisfaction, subjective scar evaluation, and adverse events); (4) types of EBDs employed; (5) medication details (transdermal or other routes of administration); (6) treatment course; and (7) type of lesion treated.

This review primarily focuses on the enhancement of EADD through the formation of micropores or microscopic treatment zones (MTZs). The underlying mechanism of action is characterized by three key aspects. Firstly, micropores or MTZs transiently create channels within the dense stratum corneum and scar tissue, establishing direct physical pathways for drug molecules to access the dermis. Secondly, controlled thermal energy induces “thermal effects and permeability alteration” by temporarily disrupting the intercellular lipid structure of the epidermis, thereby enhancing the capacity for intercellular drug diffusion. Finally, these modifications collectively facilitate “targeting and accumulation,” promoting the precise enrichment of therapeutic agents within the scar tissue and prolonging their local retention time. This significantly improves drug bioavailability, achieving a synergistic enhancement of physical permeation with pharmacotherapy.

Below is a brief description of the EBD types most frequently described in the included EADD studies:

Ablative lasers

The primary ablative fractional (AFLs) lasers discussed in this review are the carbon dioxide (CO2) (10,600 nm) and erbium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Er: YAG) (2,940 nm) lasers. Both modalities generate a central zone of microscopic ablation (effectively a vaporized “cavity” or channel), which is surrounded by a zone of thermal coagulation. This zone presents as a rim of coagulated tissue of varying thickness encircling each microchannel [6]. On one hand, this process facilitates the breakdown of disordered extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins and promotes re-epithelialization and neocollagenesis, thereby inducing rapid repair and regeneration of the surrounding skin. Concurrently, these channels serve as conduits for transdermal drug delivery, enabling hydrophilic and macromolecular agents to bypass the skin barrier and penetrate deep into the dermis [7, 8].

Non-ablative fractional laser

This review also includes the 1,564 nm non-ablative fractional laser (NAFL) [9]. Similar to ablative lasers, NAFL generates MTZs; however, these zones result from thermal injury while preserving the structural integrity of the overlying stratum corneum. Although not vaporized, the stratum corneum undergoes significant barrier disruption, thereby enhancing transdermal drug delivery and offering the advantage of a shorter recovery period.

Micro-plasma radiofrequency

MPR employs radiofrequency energy to ionize nitrogen in the air gap between its tip and the skin, producing micro-plasma discharges. This leads to epidermal ablation and thermally induced injury reaching the superficial dermis, forming precisely controlled micro-channels. By disrupting the skin barrier, these micro-channels enhance transdermal drug delivery while also promoting collagen remodeling and tissue regeneration [3].

Ultrasound

Ultrasound enhances transdermal drug delivery by increasing skin permeability through mechanisms such as sonoporation and transient barrier disruption [4]. Moreover, the mechanical effects of ultrasound can promote the partitioning and penetration of therapeutic agents into the dermis [10]. In the combined approach examined in this review, laser and ultrasound are used sequentially to optimize delivery. First, a CO₂ AFL creates MTZs in the stratum corneum, compromising the skin barrier. Ultrasound is then applied, utilizing acoustic cavitation and radiation pressure to actively drive drug molecules into deeper dermal layers, thereby significantly improving delivery efficiency.

Thermomechanical fractional injury

TMFI generates micropores approximately 200 μm deep in the stratum corneum via transient contact (6–18 ms) with a microneedle array heated to 400 ℃ (81 pyramid-shaped micro-pins/cm²) [5]. Its distinctive mechanism relies on the thermally induced humidity gradient that forms between the drier epidermis and the more hydrated dermis. This gradient establishes an effective alternative pathway for transdermal penetration of hydrophilic drugs, significantly enhancing their absorption.

EADD for keloids in adult population

In this review, the literature on the treatment of keloids in adults mainly encompasses three types of EADD therapies: (1) Er: YAG AFL-assisted delivery of corticosteroids; (2) CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of corticosteroids; and (3) CO₂ AFL combined with ultrasound-assisted delivery of corticosteroids (Table 1).

In 2017, Park et al. conducted a split-scar self-controlled study on BCG vaccine-induced refractory keloids on the shoulder [11]. One side received Er: YAG AFL-assisted delivery of desoxymethasone (DOM), and the other side received Er: YAG AFL plus intralesional triamcinolone acetonide (TAC) injection. After four sessions, similar reductions in Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) scores were observed in both sides; however, pain was significantly lower on the Er: YAG AFL-assisted drug delivery side, and injection-site complications (e.g., telangiectasia, 20%) were absent. Subsequently, a 2020 split-scar self-controlled study found that, compared with intralesional betamethasone (BM) injection, Er: YAG AFL-assisted BM delivery also achieved a significant reduction in VSS scores, along with lower pain levels [8]. In parallel with the above findings, a 2022 randomized controlled trial (RCT) compared the.

treatment effects of CO₂ AFL monotherapy, CO₂ AFL-assisted TAC delivery, and CO₂ AFL-assisted trichloroacetic acid (TCA) delivery [12]. After four sessions, VSS scores had decreased significantly from baseline in all three groups. Of note, the CO₂ AFL-assisted TAC group showed the greatest improvement, whereas the TCA-assisted group showed the least. Furthermore, a 2025 retrospective study evaluated a triple-modality therapy combining intense pulsed light (IPL), CO₂ AFL, and ultrasound to assist TAC delivery for refractory keloids [13]. Using ultrasound-assisted TAC delivery as a control, the study added IPL for vascular targeting. After nine monthly sessions, the triple-therapy group demonstrated superior improvement in pruritus, color, vascularity, thickness, and pliability, with a significantly higher response rate (93% vs. 76.9%). Crucially, its 2-year recurrence rate was only 7%, compared with 23.1% in the control group, thereby offering a novel comprehensive strategy for keloid management.

EADD for HTS in adult population

This review included the largest number of EADD therapies for treating adult HTS, encompassing not only the three modalities mentioned above for keloids but also: (1) CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of 5-FU, (2) CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of photosensitizers, (3) NAFL-assisted drug delivery, and (4) TMFI-assisted drug delivery (Table 2).

The proliferative stage of HTS generally refers to the first six months post-injury, during which scars grow rapidly; therefore, treatment during this stage is critical for controlling scar hyperplasia. In line with this, a 2021 prospective study showed that CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of TAC was superior to microneedling-assisted TAC and CO₂ AFL monotherapy, achieving a clinical efficacy rate of 93.3% (vs. 70%) with higher patient satisfaction [14]. Consistently, two 2024 studies further confirmed that EADD treatment relieves scar pruritus, improves quality of life (with a 6-month recurrence rate of 2.33% vs. 16.28%), and reduces serum transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), without increasing the incidence of adverse events [15, 16]. Furthermore, a 2022 prospective study utilized a 1565 nm NAFL combined with low-dose topical BM to treat proliferative-stage HTS [9]. After six sessions, the EADD group showed significant reductions in total and component VSS scores and achieved a patient satisfaction rate of 89.2%. The primary adverse events were transient mild discomfort (burning, erythema, swelling) and a low incidence of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (10.7%).

The following results have been reported for long-standing HTS. In a 2018 RCT that first used CO₂ AFL combined with ultrasound-assisted TAC delivery, results showed that after the second and third sessions, this combination group achieved significantly greater modified Vancouver Scar Scale (mVSS) score reductions than CO₂ AFL monotherapy, with no increased risk of adverse events [17]. Subsequently, a 2019 RCT compared CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) versus TAC, and found similar efficacy: after three monthly sessions, scar thickness was reduced by 27% in the 5-FU group and 23% in the TAC group, with no statistically significant difference [18]. However, safety profiles differed – TAC caused dermal atrophy and telangiectasia, whereas 5-FU did not. A 2021 RCT evaluated Er: YAG AFL-assisted delivery of clobetasol propionate (CP) cream versus Er: YAG AFL with petrolatum (control) [19]. At three months post-treatment, scar thickness was significantly lower in the CP group. Although no severe adverse events occurred in either group at six months, the CP group showed only a slightly greater reduction in scar thickness than the petrolatum group (0.45 mm vs. 0.35 mm). A 2024 prospective study evaluated CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of a combined TAC/5-FU solution (1:9 volume ratio) for HTS of 5–12 months [20]. Compared with CO₂ AFL monotherapy, the EADD group showed rapid onset – significant VSS and Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale (POSAS) reductions after just two sessions and improved Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) after four – with a slightly higher but not statistically significant blistering rate (12.5% vs. 7.5%). Similarly, a 2024 self-controlled RCT for post-thyroidectomy HTS found that CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of TAC solution and CP cream were comparably effective, both significantly improving all scar metrics [21]. More recently, a 2025 retrospective study (uniform penetration depth 1–2 mm) reported that CO₂ AFL-assisted TAC delivery achieved a significantly higher response rate (91.67% vs. 75.56%) than Er: YAG AFL-assisted TAC delivery, with similar safety profiles [22]. In addition to laser-based approaches, other energy devices have also been investigated. In a 2022 randomized split-body trial for HTS, TMFI-assisted delivery of TAC was compared with intralesional TAC injection [23]. After five sessions, both significantly reduced scar volume and VSS scores at six-month follow-up. Injection was slightly superior in thickness reduction (74.2% vs. 64.3%), but the EADD group had higher patient satisfaction (> 75% improvement: 47.6% vs. 42.9%). More importantly, EADD caused significantly less treatment pain and no skin atrophy or telangiectasia (injection group atrophy rate: 47.6%).

Although photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a first-line treatment for active moderate-to-severe acne, it has also been investigated for the treatment of acne-induced HTS. A 2018 prospective study found that CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of 5-aminolevulinic acid(ALA)-PDT had a good therapeutic effect on mandibular acne-induced HTS [24]. That study compared ALA-PDT alone, CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of ALA-PDT, and intralesional compound betamethasone (CB) injection. After three sessions, the EADD group showed the best improvement in VSS parameters, with a 100% response rate (vs. 85.71% in both control groups), the lowest recurrence rate (3% vs. 6% and 37.1%), and short-term adverse events (e.g., pain, erythema) that resolved within approximately three weeks. Unlike the above study, a 2021 RCT used microneedling combined with ALA-PDT as a control [25]. All groups had high response rates (> 90%) and progressive VSS reduction, but the EADD and microneedling groups improved pigmentation and vascularity faster than the CB injection group. At six-month follow-up, the EADD group had the lowest recurrence rate (0%), outperforming microneedling (6.25%) and injection (25%).

EADD for HTS in pediatric population

Pediatric scars are characterized by high incidence, prolonged erythema, and severe hypertrophy. Compared with adults, there is relatively little literature on EADD therapies for pediatric HTS. The main approaches include CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of corticosteroids and CO₂ AFL combined with ultrasound-assisted drug delivery (Table 3).

Several studies have investigated these EADD approaches. For example, a large 2020 retrospective study of 349 pediatric patients with HTS found that following a single session of EADD treatment (CO₂ AFL combined with ultrasound-assisted transdermal TAC delivery), both EADD and laser monotherapy significantly improved VSS scores and parental satisfaction [26]. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups in the overall cohort. In contrast, within the low-risk zone subgroup (e.g., face, trunk, and non-joint limb areas), the EADD group demonstrated significantly greater improvement than laser monotherapy. Subsequently, in 2023, Cai et al. retrospectively analyzed 120 pediatric patients and reported an efficacy rate of 92.5% for CO₂ AFL-assisted TAC delivery, surpassing laser monotherapy (77.5%) and CO₂ AFL combined with intralesional TAC injection (90%) [27]. Transdermal delivery also caused significantly less pain than injection, with no significant difference in adverse events among groups. Similarly, a 2024 study on early facial HTS showed that CO₂ AFL-assisted TAC delivery achieved an overall clinical response rate of 98.11%, significantly higher than CO₂ AFL monotherapy (87.04%) and microneedling-assisted TAC (84.31%) [28]. This treatment was accompanied by downregulation of serum transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), bone morphogenetic protein-7 (BMP-7), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), without a notable increase in adverse events.

EADD for HTS in unspecified populations (Including One Keloid Study)

Of the nine studies conducted in unspecified populations (i.e., without age distinction between pediatric and adult patients), eight examined HTS and one examined keloid. The EADD therapies reported for HTS were: (1) Er: YAG AFL-assisted drug delivery, (2)CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of corticosteroids; (3) CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of 5-FU; (4) CO₂ AFL combined with ultrasound-assisted drug delivery; and (5) MPR-assisted drug delivery. The only keloid study used CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of corticosteroids (Table 4).

The earliest study, a 2017 retrospective study, compared CO₂ AFL monotherapy versus its combination with ultrasound-assisted delivery of 5-FU and TAC for HTS [29]. Following an average of three treatment sessions administered every 2–3 months, the EADD group demonstrated higher clinical response rates and superior overall efficacy. Subsequently, a 2022 retrospective study found that after three sessions, CO₂ AFL-assisted TAC delivery showed superiority over laser monotherapy only in scar thickness reduction [30]. More recently, a 2024 large-scale retrospective study provided further clinical evidence, demonstrating that compared with Er: YAG AFL monotherapy, Er: YAG AFL-assisted CB delivery offered significant advantages in improving scar thickness, pigmentation, and vascularity [31].

In addition to comparisons with monotherapy, one study investigated the effect of different energy settings. A study compared CO₂ AFL-assisted TAC delivery at energy levels of 400, 600, and 800 mJ/pixel. Ex vivo analysis of human HTS tissue demonstrated progressively greater ablation depth, width, and coagulation zone thickness with increasing energy (reaching maximum depths of 582.18 μm, 724.50 μm, and 919.01 μm, respectively). Clinically, however, the 600 mJ/pixel group showed the most significant improvement in VSS, Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale (POSAS), and FIIQ scores at the 1-year follow-up, whereas cases of wound infection (0.6%) were reported in the 800 mJ/pixel group [32]. Beyond energy settings, another study investigated the effect of increasing the number of treatment sessions. A 2025 RCT that increased the treatment regimen to six sessions yielded more favorable outcomes: compared with laser monotherapy, CO₂ AFL-assisted TAC delivery significantly improved scar thickness, pliability, and dyschromia [33]. Nevertheless, the incidence of adverse events—such as burning sensation (10%), telangiectasia (20%), and skin atrophy (10%)—increased with the number of sessions.

Several studies specifically focused on proliferative-stage or severe HTS. In 2019, a randomized, split-scar, self-controlled trial in patients with severe HTS found that after eight sessions, both Er: YAG AFL-assisted 5-FU delivery and topical 5-FU alone significantly reduced scar thickness [34]. However, the EADD-treated side showed superior improvements in scar thickness and texture, achieving a “moderate to excellent” grade, whereas the monotherapy side only reached “mild to moderate” improvement. Adverse effects were more common on the EADD side (erythema 75%, ulceration 25%) than on the monotherapy side (50% and 8.33%, respectively). In a 2024 RCT on early-stage HTS, CO₂ AFL monotherapy (six monthly sessions) did not significantly improve VSS scores [33]. In contrast, CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of either 5-FU or TAC yielded differential benefits: 5-FU improved thickness, pliability, and vascularity, whereas TAC favored thickness, pliability, and dyschromia. However, adverse events were more frequent with EADD; the 5-FU group had 60% burning pain and 40% erosions/ulcerations, while the TAC group had 20% telangiectasia and 10% skin atrophy.

The clinical value of MPR in assisted drug delivery is emerging. A 2024 retrospective study of 208 HTS patients found that after ≥ 3 sessions, MPR-assisted CB delivery was superior to MPR monotherapy in improving scar thickness, pigmentation, and vascularity, achieving a total effective rate of 94.8% [31]. However, this rate was lower than that of Er: YAG AFL-assisted CB (100%). The study also lacked long-term data on adverse events and recurrence rates.

Regarding keloids, a 2022 study found that in patients over 12 years of age, CO₂ AFL-assisted BM delivery was more effective than cryotherapy combined with intralesional TAC injection in improving keloid scars and relieving pain [35]. Furthermore, the EADD group exhibited 20% dyspigmentation, whereas the cryotherapy-plus-injection group showed higher rates of skin atrophy (48.2%) and dyspigmentation (25.8%).

EADD for both HTS and keloids in unspecified populations

In this review, all EADD therapies for treating both HTS and keloids in unspecified populations were CO₂ AFL-assisted drug delivery (Table 5).

The first study, a 2018 RCT, demonstrated that for improving dyspigmentation and texture in both keloids and HTS, CO₂ AFL-assisted TAC delivery was superior to conventional intralesional TAC injection [36]. However, injection therapy retained an advantage in improving overall cosmesis and reducing scar bulk. Subsequently, a 2019 RCT compared CO₂ AFL monotherapy versus CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of either 5-FU or verapamil [37]. After four sessions, all groups showed improvement in pruritus, vascularity, pliability, and TGF-β1 expression; importantly, both EADD groups were superior to laser monotherapy in reducing scar thickness and POSAS scores. Between the two EADD groups, 5-FU was slightly more effective for overall scar characteristics and significantly better for pain relief, without increasing the risk of adverse events. Furthermore, a 2022 prospective study found that for HTS, CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery of botulinum toxin type A (BTXA) showed superior improvement in total and component VSS scores (vascularity, pigmentation, pliability, and height) compared with intralesional BTXA injection [38]. In contrast, for keloids, intralesional injection was significantly more effective than EADD in improving scar vascularity, pliability, and POSAS scores.

Discussion

In this review of EADD for HTS and keloids, we synthesize the literature to evaluate its efficacy compared to monotherapy (laser or pharmacotherapy) and conventional combination therapies (e.g., laser plus injection). We further compare outcomes between EADD protocols that use different drugs, as well as between different EBDs employed to deliver the same drug.

Multiple studies—including non-controlled prospective and retrospective analyses as well as RCTs—demonstrate that EADD outperforms monotherapy for HTS and keloids across specific metrics. It is noteworthy that while the majority of studies employed AFL, technologies such as MPR, NAFL, and TMFI are gradually being applied to assist drug delivery, offering a broader range of therapeutic options for pathological scars [9, 23, 31].

Regarding glucocorticoids (e.g., TAC), both Er: YAG AFL and CO₂ AFL-assisted delivery have shown significant efficacy [11, 14–16, 19, 30, 31, 33]. Notably, while some studies indicate no statistically significant difference in VSS score improvement between EADD and traditional intralesional injection, patients in the EADD groups experience significantly less pain and avoid the frequent adverse effects associated with injections, such as skin atrophy and telangiectasia [8, 11, 35]. Moreover, since lower pain scores and reduced adverse event rates are directly correlated with improved patient compliance, EADD is particularly advantageous in pediatric populations, where it has demonstrated excellent tolerability [27, 28].

Regarding pharmacological options, the combination of EADD with 5-FU is highly effective at reducing scar thickness and avoids the tissue atrophy associated with corticosteroids [18, 34]. Its use in early-stage HTS, however, may carry a higher risk of ulceration [33], necessitating a balance between efficacy and safety based on the scar’s stage of development. For BTXA, EADD delivers excellent outcomes in HTS. In keloids, however, traditional intralesional BTXA injection remains superior for improving vascularity and pliability [38]. EADD-assisted ALA-PDT, moreover, has shown exceptionally low recurrence rates in acne-induced HTS, performing significantly better than monotherapy [24, 25]. Finally, the EADD strategy of combining CO₂ AFL with ultrasound—particularly the “light-light-sound” triple therapy—holds considerable promise for reducing recurrence in refractory keloids [13].

One limitation of this review lies in its inclusion criteria: to ensure broad coverage, we incorporated studies that utilized various combinations of EBDs and drugs, which consequently introduced heterogeneity in study design and quality. A further limitation is that, despite consistent trends in outcomes, direct cross-study comparisons remain challenging due to variations in assessment metrics, energy parameters, drug concentrations, treatment intervals, and follow-up periods across the literature. These factors hinder the establishment of definitive, standardized EADD protocols for different pathological scar types.

Pathological scars are notoriously difficult to treat and prone to recurrence, typically requiring multimodal strategies to achieve meaningful outcomes. Despite the methodological limitations noted earlier, the body of evidence synthesized in this review supports the consideration of EADD as a key therapeutic strategy for HTS and keloids, with certain drug–device combinations demonstrating particular promise. Although current findings are clinically encouraging, future large-scale RCTs with long-term follow-up are needed to fully evaluate the sustained benefits of EADD and to establish standardized treatment protocols for pathological scars.
