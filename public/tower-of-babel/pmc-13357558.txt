Impact of artificial intelligence on cardiovascular workflow, engagement, and outcomes: a systematic review
Authors: Yi-En Lin, Shu-Mei Yang, Chi-Jung Huang, Yi-Wen Tsai, Hao-Min Cheng, Wui-Chiang Lee, Shuu-Jiun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) is progressively utilized in cardiology; nonetheless, the overarching advantages across various care domains remain ambiguous. We conducted a search of PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, and trial registries for randomized controlled trials up to January 16, 2026, assessing prospectively applied interventions based on machine/deep-learning algorithms while excluding rule-based systems. Endpoints were categorized according to NICE evidence tiers: workflow efficiency (Tier A), patient engagement/health promotion (Tier B), and clinical outcomes (Tier C). The risk of bias was evaluated using RoB 2.0. In 32 randomized controlled trials (27 of which were meta-analyzed), artificial intelligence improved all levels. Tier A: workflow time reduced (SMD − 0.71; 95% CI − 1.04 to −0.39), corresponding to a diagnostic time that is 30–120 s shorter and a decrease of 1.0–4.2 hospital days in trials reporting length of stay. Tier B: Behavioral nudging enhanced medication adherence (RR 1.59; 95% CI 1.01–2.50; NNT = 12). Tier C: decision-support implementations decreased all-cause mortality (RR 0.84; 95% CI 0.75–0.94; I² = 8%; NNT = 32). Limitations encompassed restricted blinding and insufficient sham-AI controls. Data-driven clinical AI yields quantifiable efficiency improvements, enhances engagement, and reduces adverse outcomes when integrated with actionable decision support, hence informing a structured framework for governance and implementation.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have evolved from traditional information systems into dynamic, integrative platforms capable of processing both human- and machine-generated data. In cardiology, AI technologies have been applied to a broad range of diagnostic and prognostic tasks, including electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation, arrhythmia detection, prediction of heart failure exacerbations, risk stratification for coronary artery disease (CAD), automated image analysis in echocardiography and cardiac computed tomography (CT), and the integration of electronic health record data to identify high-risk patients and optimize care pathways1,2. For example, Armoundas et al. summarized AI applications that enhance diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision-making, while Lin et al. demonstrated that AI-based risk stratification for CAD outperformed traditional methods3,4. In parallel, the number of FDA-cleared AI-enabled devices increased from six in 2015 to 223 in 2023, reflecting measurable uptake and growing trust3,5–10.

Despite this momentum, the true magnitude, consistency, and durability of the impact of AI on real-world cardiovascular outcomes and its cost-effectiveness remain difficult to determine. Previous syntheses largely reflected the historical predominance of rule-based systems. As cardiovascular AI evolves toward adaptive machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), a distinct evaluation is required to isolate the impact of algorithmic intelligence from simple digitization. Furthermore, recent implementation frameworks and scientific statements have called for outcome-focused trials, governance, and economic evaluation rather than reliance on model-centric metrics alone. However, existing randomized controlled trials (RCTs) often feature heterogeneous designs, short follow-up periods, limited power for hard endpoints, inconsistent outcome definitions, and insufficient external validation or transparency, thereby constraining clinical generalization and value appraisal1–3,5,7–10.

To address this gap, a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of data-driven AI technologies, specifically machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), was conducted. This study focuses on their deployment at the point of care in cardiology. Unlike previous reviews, we strictly excluded rule-based expert systems to isolate the clinical value of contemporary AI. The aims were to (i) consolidate the clinical evidence, (ii) quantify benefits across care pathways and systems, and (iii) identify trial-design blind spots to guide more rigorous clinical implementation and research. System-level clinical impact was assessed using the NICE three-tier framework, comprising process efficiency (Tier A), patient engagement (Tier B), and direct clinical outcomes (Tier C), as summarized in Table 111. Given accelerating regulatory approvals and sustained clinical interest, distinguishing rigorously validated clinical benefits from inflated or speculative claims becomes increasingly important. Ultimately, this study seeks to inform clinicians, policymakers, and developers by providing robust, evidence-based insights into the value and limitations of AI technologies in cardiovascular healthcare, to the best of our current knowledge. Structured according to the NICE evidence tiers, this meta-analysis is among the first studies to systematically evaluate the clinical impact of modern AI and provide decision-ready evidence for safe deployment, oversight, and value assessment in cardiovascular care.

Results

Literature search and study characteristics

The expanded search yielded 4643 records (821 from PubMed, 3169 from Embase, 386 from CINAHL, 153 from ClinicalTrials.gov, and 114 from WHO ICTRP). After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 223 unique articles remained for full-text evaluation. Ultimately, 32 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) met the predefined eligibility criteria for the systematic review10,12–42, with 27 included in the quantitative meta-analysis10,12–15,18,21,23–42. The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Supplementary Fig. S1). Detailed qualitative synthesis and management of population overlaps (e.g., prioritization of Kumar et al.35 and Huang et al.26) are provided in Supplementary Text S1.

The included studies were categorized into three tiers according to the NICE framework: Tier A (Process Efficiency, n = 19)10,12–22,32–38, Tier B (Patient Engagement, n = 11)10,23–28,39–42, and Tier C (Clinical Outcomes, n = 14)29–42. A comprehensive summary of the study characteristics, including PICO elements and quality assessment scores, is provided in Supplementary Tables S4–S9.

Functional synthesis of clinical impact

To elucidate the drivers of clinical benefit, we performed a stratified meta-analysis based on AI functions (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Workflow Automation emerged as the most consistent driver of efficiency, yielding a large reduction in process time (Tier A: SMD −1.01, 95% CI −1.68 to −0.34). Crucially, this function also demonstrated a significant reduction in clinical events (Tier C: RR 0.31, 95% CI 0.12–0.83; NNT ≈ 3), acting as a procedural safeguard in high-stakes settings. Diagnostic Support tools significantly accelerated workflows (Tier A: SMD −0.97, 95% CI −1.49 to −0.45) and promoted health (Tier B: RR 1.23, 95% CI 1.01–1.49; NNT ≈ 33). However, this diagnostic precision showed a “translation gap,” as it did not independently reduce hard clinical outcomes (Tier C: RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.79–1.05; P = 0.20) without coupled interventions. Decision-Support systems showed a trend toward improved efficiency that approached marginal significance (Tier A: SMD −0.24, 95% CI −0.49 to 0.00; P = 0.05).

Quantitative synthesis for other functional domains was precluded by limited study counts.

Tier A: process efficiency

Tier A outcomes primarily contributed to cost savings, workforce optimization, and reduced clinical process time. Most of the studies focused on reducing clinical process time, while a smaller proportion examined reductions in radiation dose or contrast agent exposure time. Specifically, reductions in diagnosis time were reported in 9 studies10,12–15,18,21,35,38, (SMD −0.56, P = 0.006), translating to savings of 30–120 s per case. Time to treatment in 2 studies10,36, and treatment duration in 3 studies33,34,37, showed no significant difference. Notably, patient admission time in 3 studies demonstrated the most profound benefit (Pooled SMD −2.69, P = 0.02), reducing hospital length of stay by approximately 1.0–4.2 days32,36,37. Detailed meta-analysis results for each outcome category are presented in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Figure 1 shows that AI interventions significantly reduced clinical workflows (SMD = –0.71; 95% CI, –1.04 to –0.39; P < 0.0001), representing a medium-to-large effect size. This substantial reduction underscores a clinically meaningful gain in high-volume settings. Substantial heterogeneity reflected variations in study populations, AI implementation strategies, outcome definitions, and the inclusion of three provider-level randomized trials. This was further compounded by inherently large differences in time requirements across procedures. Given the procedural diversity and provider-level designs contributing to variance, Tier A estimates should be interpreted as system-level efficiency indicators rather than as task-specific time guarantees. Tier B: patient engagement

Tier B outcomes highlight AI’s role in enhancing individual health management and promoting wellness-related behaviors. Across 11 studies, 5 reported improvements in medication adherence facilitated by AI interventions23,26,39–41, 4 studies focused on the use of AI-generated notifications to encourage timely medical visits10,25,28,42, and 6 examined how AI tools supported the identification of new clinical cases23,24,26,27,40,42.

To evaluate the overall impact of AI on cardiovascular health promotion, the meta-analysis pooled RRs for Tier B outcomes and found that AI-assisted interventions significantly increased the likelihood of health promotion events (pooled RR = 1.36; 95% CI, 1.13–1.63; P = 0.001; NNT ≈ 22) (Fig. 2), with individual outcome results detailed in the Supplementary Fig. S3. Subgroup analysis revealed that this benefit was primarily driven by improvements in medication adherence (RR = 1.59; 95% CI, 1.01–2.50; P = 0.05; NNT ≈ 12). Tier C: clinical outcomes

Tier C outcomes underscore the potential of AI-driven approaches to exert a direct and measurable impact on patient prognosis and clinical safety. Among the 14 studies analyzed, 12 addressed all-cause mortality29–34,36–40,42, 5 evaluated the occurrence of MACE29,30,34,38,39, 5 studies reported stroke-related outcomes31,36,37,39,40, 3 studies assessed the influence of AI on rehospitalisation rates29,32,38, and procedure failure was examined in 2 studies33,35.

As shown in Fig. 3, the aggregate effect derived from studies focusing on patient-centered clinical outcomes demonstrated a pooled RR of 0.80 (95% CI 0.69–0.93; P = 0.004; NNT ≈ 36 in high-risk cohorts). This benefit was primarily driven by significant reductions in hard endpoints. Notably, all-cause mortality, analyzed in 12 studies (N = 33698), showed a pooled RR of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.75–0.94; P = 0.002; NNT ≈ 32 in high-risk vs. 143 in general settings) with low heterogeneity (I2 = 8%). Similarly, MACE also demonstrated a significant reduction (RR = 0.89; 95% CI: 0.82–0.98; P = 0.01; NNT ≈ 122). Furthermore, AI guidance drastically reduced the risk of procedural failure (RR = 0.12; 95% CI: 0.02–0.64; P = 0.01; NNT ≈ 3). Effects of study designs

No significant subgroup differences were observed between provider-/cluster-level and case-level trials. Adjusting for design effects in clustered trials or excluding such trials did not significantly affect the pooled effects or conclusions (Supplement Text 1).

Quality assessment and publication bias

The results of ROB 2.0 assessment are summarized in Supplementary Table S9. In Domain 2 (deviations from intended interventions), 5 studies (16%) were rated as high-risk, and 11 (34%) were rated as having some concerns, mainly due to inadequate blinding. This issue is particularly evident in behavioral AI interventions, where awareness of group allocation may introduce placebo effects. Consequently, performance bias may have favored the AI arm. Blinding of outcome assessment was a concern in 7 studies (22%). In the randomization domain, 16 studies (50%) had some concerns owing to incomplete reporting. In contrast, missing outcome data and selective reporting were judged to be low risk in over 80% of studies.

Assessment of publication bias revealed distinct patterns across tiers (Supplementary Fig. S6). For Tier A and Tier C, Egger’s tests indicated no significant asymmetry (P = 0.117 and P = 0.229, respectively), supporting the robustness of these findings. In contrast, Tier B showed significant asymmetry (P = 0.002). However, given the substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 80%) within Tier B, this asymmetry is likely attributable to clinical heterogeneity—such as varying intensities of patient engagement—and small-study effects, rather than solely reflecting publication bias. All meta-analyses utilized random-effects models to rigorously account for this variability.

Discussion

AI technologies have demonstrated clinically meaningful benefits in cardiology across NICE tiers by improving workflow efficiency, strengthening patient engagement, and reducing all-cause mortality and MACE. This review specifically evaluates data-driven ML and DL interventions to isolate the impact of adaptive intelligence from simple digitization. By synthesizing evidence across workflow, engagement, and prognosis, our findings illustrate that modern AI has matured from experimental validation to delivering system-level value at the point of care, providing a scalable blueprint for its future integration into clinical cardiovascular implementation.

Our findings are directionally consistent with studies supporting the clinical utility of AI. Computed tomography–derived fractional flow reserve reduced invasive testing while maintaining safety, and AI models have reliably predicted outcomes in HF and post–PCI populations43–45. Other studies reported benefits in operational decision-making, educational interventions, early detection, and remote monitoring46–52. Beyond cardiology, oncology trials reveal broader potential to optimize research through improved enrolment, screening, and tailored therapies53. However, concerns persist regarding placebo bias, algorithmic bias, limited external validation, and lack of transparency50,54, as well as challenges of generalizability and workflow integration45,51,52, with dataset diversity and interpretability limitations underscoring the need for rigorous validation and responsible deployment53.

We structured the synthesis according to the NICE evidence-tier framework to illustrate these system-level benefits (Fig. 4). Across 32 randomized trials, validated AI tools embedded into care pathways enhanced operational efficiency, strengthened patient participation, and improved outcomes. Across multiple care stages, AI consistently reduced time, although some pooled estimates were not statistically significant, reflecting variation in tasks, settings, and implementation maturity. Examples include shorter length of stay with AI-assisted echocardiography32, lower radiation and contrast with deep-learning reconstruction while maintaining diagnostic integrity17, and fewer complications and lower radiation with AI-guided preprocedural simulation for left atrial appendage closure. Two studies reported increased time, highlighting implementation challenges10,37. Provider-level randomized trials were retained because service-level time savings were relevant to system efficiency. These were analyzed as a predefined provider-/cluster-level subgroup with patient-level trials; subgroup differences were not significant (Supplementary Text 1).

The consistent direction of effect suggests that Workflow Automation demonstrates the most reliable clinical benefit. The success of this function stems from its “frictionless” integration: unlike complex decision-support systems that demand clinicians to alter their reasoning, these algorithms simply remove repetitive computational burdens. Specifically, reductions in diagnosis time (30–120 s per case) and patient admission time (1.0–4.2 days saved). This suggests that automation acts as an ‘efficiency multiplier’ by removing computational friction without requiring behavior change from clinicians. However, technical strengths may not translate into clinical benefit without behavioral adaptation10, and adoption remains limited by mistrust and workflow misalignment6,15,17,30.

The observed high statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 97%) likely reflects varying degrees of overfitting and implementation maturity across different health systems55–57. Addressing system-level barriers remains essential to realize clinical value58–60. Tier A effects should be interpreted as service-level efficiency gains, with magnitudes varying by case mix and implementation maturity.

AI interventions in this tier, particularly in signal processing and wearable monitoring, expanded healthcare reach, though the notable statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 91%) underscores that clinical utility depends on the precise alignment between the AI’s functional design and the target outcome, particularly to ensure equitable care delivery.

Regarding Medication Adherence, our analysis identifies this as the primary driver of Tier B benefit (NNT ≈ 12). However, the specific type of intervention matters. Liu et al.40 and Berman et al.23, which incorporated Behavioral Nudging as a key secondary function alongside clinical support, achieved significant improvements. In contrast, Persell et al.41, classified primarily as Behavioral Nudging but targeting patients in isolation without robust downstream clinical integration, failed to improve adherence. Similarly, Huang et al.26, which functioned primarily as Diagnostic Support without a nudging component, also showed no benefit. This suggests that “closing the loop” requires more than just patient-facing alerts; it demands active nudging coupled with clinical oversight.

Conversely, outcomes related to Notification and Identification did not reach statistical significance. While Tsai et al.42 and Huang et al.26 (both Diagnostic Support) successfully identified cases, this detection did not consistently translate into broader patient engagement compared to adherence-focused interventions.

Tier C analyses revealed prognostic benefit across endpoints, with consistent reductions in all-cause mortality and MACE. The all-cause mortality analysis showed low statistical heterogeneity (I² = 8%), reinforcing the robustness of these hard endpoints (NNT ≈ 32 in high-risk cohorts). Whereas prior studies emphasized event prediction, our findings suggest that clinical benefit arises only when prediction is coupled with a predefined protocol for intervention—distinguishing a ‘passive risk score’ from an ‘active clinical decision support system’.

A critical ‘Translation Gap’ was observed for Diagnostic and Decision-Support tools. For instance, while Yang et al.38 successfully used AI-derived CT-FFR to optimize workflow (Tier A), this diagnostic precision did not translate into a significant reduction in MACE (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.62–1.29). Similarly, Cikes et al.29 included Diagnostic and Decision-Support functions but failed to demonstrate significant reductions in mortality (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.57–1.03). In contrast, Lin et al.30 coupled detection with active behavioral nudging and successfully reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.98). This underscores that prediction alone is not prevention.

Collectively, AI-supported interventions reduced adverse events, particularly all-cause mortality. Reinforcing the shift from prediction to action, these findings support prevention, likely mediated by personalized coordination and earlier escalation beyond risk scoring. As mortality events were concentrated in a few large trials (e.g., Lin et al., Tsai et al.), broader, adequately powered studies with longer follow-up and more diverse populations are needed to refine precision and generalizability.

Beyond efficacy, AI requires lifecycle governance distinct from drugs and devices. Traditional therapies are static after approval, whereas clinical AI evolves with data, workflows, and hardware. Quantifiable indicators (accuracy, calibration, drift, fairness, and explainability) with predefined thresholds and periodic monitoring are essential. When performance declines, models should be version-locked, retrained or recalibrated, and revalidated before redeployment under clinical AI committee oversight. This monitor–detect–intervene–revalidate–redeploy cycle keeps AI explainable, fair, and safe, ensuring external validity and accountability61–65.

RoB 2.0 identified performance and detection biases primarily stemming from limited blinding (Domain 2), particularly in behavioral AI interventions where group awareness may introduce placebo effects. 5 studies (16%) were rated as high-risk, and 11 (34%) raised concerns in this domain. Additionally, blinding of outcome assessment raised concerns in 7 studies (22%), and randomization issues regarding incomplete reporting were noted in 16 studies (50%), although missing outcome data and selective reporting were judged to be low risk in over 80% of studies. These patterns suggest placebo or expectancy effects where sham or attention-matched controls were lacking. Provider/cluster-level randomization can cause intra-cluster correlation; cluster trials were analyzed accordingly, and subgroup plus design-effect adjustments yielded consistent results (Supplement Text 1). Heterogeneity was mitigated through tier-based organization, one salient endpoint per tier, random-effects models, and pre-specified subgroup analyses (Supplementary Figs. S2–S4).

Assessment of publication bias revealed distinct patterns. For Tier A and Tier C, Egger’s tests indicated no significant asymmetry (P = 0.117 and P = 0.229, respectively), supporting the robustness of these findings. In contrast, Tier B showed significant asymmetry (P = 0.002). However, given the substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 80%) within Tier B, this asymmetry is likely not only reflecting publication bias but also attributable to clinical heterogeneity—such as varying intensities of patient engagement interventions—and small-study effects. All meta-analyses utilized random-effects models to rigorously account for this variability. Future trials should strengthen blinding, use sham comparators, apply cluster-aware designs, and predefine engagement endpoints66,67.

Limitations include heterogeneity in AI types and settings. Quantitative synthesis for certain functional domains (e.g., risk prediction, behavioral nudging) was precluded by limited study counts, necessitating a broader categorization. Additionally, cross-sectional metrics (RR) may overlook temporal dynamics like the durability of behavioral nudging. Statistical heterogeneity in Tiers A and B further reflects this functional diversity. Notably, while Tier C showed prognostic benefit, the “translation gap” in diagnostic tools confirms that precision alone does not guarantee prevention.

Conversely, strengths include a comprehensive tier-based synthesis spanning workflow (Tier A), engagement (Tier B), and prognosis (Tier C). Key contributions are the cross-tier quantification of clinical impact—specifically the calculation of Number Needed to Treat (NNT) for safety and mortality—and a practical blueprint emphasizing blinding, sham controls, and cluster-aware analysis to address gaps highlighted by RoB 2.0.

To bridge the gap between algorithmic performance and clinical benefit, we propose three strategic pivots based on our findings. First, from passive prediction to active management: predictive models must move beyond identifying risk to suggesting action. Future RCTs should evaluate Clinical Decision Support that pairs alerts with guideline-directed recommendations to optimize therapy. Second, focus on “human-in-the-loop” efficiency: imaging AI must prioritize ergonomics. Tools should be evaluated on reducing “click burdens” and screen time to address clinician burnout. Third, closed-loop wearable care: passive monitoring is insufficient. Success requires active behavioral nudging to drive adherence, with algorithms designed to filter false positives and facilitate timely follow-up.

Clinical AI in cardiology enhances workflow efficiency (Tier A), patient engagement (Tier B), and outcomes like mortality (Tier C), serving as a scalable adjunct rather than a replacement for clinical expertise. This review shifts the focus from isolated algorithmic efficacy to clinically validated system-level impact. To realize this potential, clinicians should prioritize adoption where workflow burdens are high or safety risks are elevated, while policymakers must foster responsible ecosystems through equitable regulations and reimbursement. Future research demands large-scale pragmatic trials with rigorous safeguards—specifically sham controls, blinding, external validation, and transparency—to ensure safe, effective, and equitable human–AI collaboration.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

We executed a comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Embase, and CINAHL from their inception through January 16, 2026. To capture the rapidly evolving landscape of modern AI, the search strategy was significantly expanded. We utilized a combination of controlled vocabulary, specifically MeSH for PubMed and Emtree for Embase, and free-text terms relevant to two key domains. These domains included AI Technologies, with terms such as “Artificial Intelligence,” “Machine Learning,” “Deep Learning,” “Neural Networks,” “Convolutional Neural Networks” (CNN), “Large Language Models” (LLM), “Transformers,” “Generative AI,” “Computer Vision,” and “Decision Support Systems,” and Cardiovascular Conditions, with terms such as “Cardiovascular Diseases,” “Heart Failure,” “Arrhythmias” including Atrial Fibrillation, and “Myocardial Ischemia”. The search syntax was adapted for each database to account for differences in indexing. In addition to bibliographic databases, we searched ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) to identify unpublished trials and gray literature. The search was limited to randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and the database-specific Boolean search strings are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

The systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 6.5, and reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Table S3)68,69. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the National Institute for Health Research (NIHR) PROSPERO database (Registration ID: CRD42024610972)70.

Eligibility criteria

We established specific eligibility criteria based on the PICOS framework to ensure a rigorous evaluation of modern, data-driven AI technologies in cardiology. To isolate the clinical impact of adaptive algorithmic intelligence from static digitization, we applied a strict operational definition for the intervention, focusing exclusively on machine learning and deep learning while excluding rule-based systems. Furthermore, to capture the multifaceted impact of AI on healthcare systems, outcomes were pre-specified according to the NICE evidence standards framework.

This systematic review evaluated randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating AI-driven technologies across various domains of cardiology. To ensure a comprehensive evaluation of cardiovascular health, the population was operationally defined to encompass cardiac, cerebrovascular, and peripheral vascular diseases. Provider or cluster-randomized trials were eligible when interventions directly modified clinical workflows, as workflow time and utilization outcomes are inherently provider-mediated.

“AI interventions” were defined as prospectively deployed tools that (i) used data-driven machine learning (ML) or deep learning (DL) embedded in imaging or signal processing, risk stratification, triage or alerts, or decision support, and (ii) modified clinical processes or patient behaviors in real time. We strictly excluded static rule-based expert systems, such as simple if-then algorithms, to isolate the clinical impact of adaptive algorithmic intelligence. Representative examples include AI-guided echocardiography acquisition, ML-driven alerts for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, AI-enhanced ECG risk notifications, and deep-learning–based image reconstruction for coronary CT angiography (CCTA) or aneurysm triage.

Eligible trials compared AI-assisted approaches with standard clinical care or conventional non-AI workflows.

A tiered framework was pre-specified to capture the system-level effects of AI in cardiovascular care. In addition to analyzing individual endpoints, results were pooled according to the NICE evidence tiers to reflect workflow efficiency (Tier A), patient engagement or health promotion (Tier B), and clinical outcomes (Tier C). All time-based endpoints were harmonized to seconds before synthesis to ensure consistency across studies reporting heterogeneous time units (e.g., seconds, minutes, or hours), whereas radiation and contrast exposure were qualitatively synthesized owing to heterogeneous designs. Tier-specific operational definitions and representative endpoints are summarized in Table 1.

Studies were excluded if they were not RCTs, lacked clinical implementation or measurable clinical impact, or were limited to purely prognostic modeling without deployment. Non-AI telemonitoring interventions and publications categorized as letters, correspondence, reviews, meta-analyses, or editorials were also excluded. Clinical trials unrelated to cardiovascular disease were not considered.

After initial screening of titles and abstracts, two authors independently reviewed the full texts of 223 articles using the predefined criteria. Any discrepancies in eligibility were resolved by consensus. 32 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis10,12–42.

As this study is a systematic review of published data, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

Outcome definitions

We pre-specified outcomes within a three-tier framework aligned with Table 1.

Process efficiency (Tier A) consisted of diagnosis time, time to treatment (e.g., door-to-needle), treatment duration, admission metrics (index episode length of stay, LOS), and radiation and contrast exposure.

Patient engagement or health promotion (Tier B) comprised medication adherence/self-monitoring and ‘new cases’ outcomes. Adherence was quantified as the proportion of days covered (PDC) or the medication possession ratio (MPR). Within the ‘new cases’ construct, we distinguished between notification (AI-prompted outreach triggering contact) and identification (confirmed incident diagnoses such as new atrial fibrillation [AF] or heart failure [HF]).

Direct clinical outcomes (Tier C) included all-cause mortality, stroke, unplanned cardiovascular readmission, procedure failure, and other clinically important adverse events. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were analyzed as defined by each study—typically including myocardial infarction, coronary revascularisation (including coronary artery bypass grafting [CABG]), and heart failure hospitalization—though death and stroke were assessed separately to avoid double-counting.

Outcome measures were harmonized so that improvements reflected shorter times, lower exposures, higher adherence, and fewer events.

Data extraction

Two authors independently extracted key information from each included study, specifically characteristics encompassing all elements of the PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes) and randomization methods (Supplementary Tables S4). All extracted data were systematically recorded in a standardized spreadsheet.

We stratified included studies into three categories based on input data modality: Computer Vision and Imaging Analysis (utilizing deep learning to interpret medical imaging), Signal Processing and Wearable Monitoring (analyzing continuous physiological waveforms), and Risk Prediction and EHR Analytics (processing structured records to predict events). Furthermore, we mapped each intervention to its clinical functions. The Primary Function was defined as the dominant mechanism of action directly targeted by the study’s primary endpoint (e.g., an intervention measuring “time to diagnosis” was classified primarily as Workflow Automation). Secondary Functions were defined as ancillary capabilities inherent to the AI system that supported the primary goal but were not the sole focus of the primary outcome.

Specific functional domains included: (1) Workflow Automation, defined as tools designed to accelerate procedural speed or streamline reporting; (2) Diagnostic Support, providing interpretation assistance to improve accuracy; (3) Early Detection, identifying previously undiagnosed conditions in asymptomatic populations; (4) Risk Stratification, calculating the probability of future clinical events; (5) Behavioral Nudging, delivering alerts to promote adherence; and (6) Decision Support, providing actionable management recommendations beyond pure diagnosis. The detailed functional classification is provided in Supplementary Table S5.

In cases where the change in standard deviation (SD) from baseline was not explicitly reported, we adopted estimation methods from the Cochrane Handbook68. Values were derived from standard errors (SE), 95% confidence intervals (CI), or baseline/final SDs, assuming a correlation coefficient of 0.5. Data presented only in graphical format were extracted using the online tool WebPlotDigitizer; in situations where overlapping lines obscured details, the highest visible SD was conservatively adopted. Studies completely lacking variance information were excluded from quantitative synthesis.

For studies presenting medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), we estimated means and SDs following Cochrane Handbook recommendations, provided sample sizes were moderate (n ≥ 25) and the distribution was not substantially skewed. The mean was inferred by averaging the first quartile, median, and third quartile, while the SD was approximated from the interquartile spread. For studies reporting multiple follow-up time points, data were aggregated to estimate the overall average change using the online tool StatsToDo. When only baseline and post-treatment measurements were available, changes were calculated by applying a correlation coefficient of 0.512.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan), version 5.471. For continuous variables, including Tier A time outcomes, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to account for heterogeneous measurement scales. To enhance clinical interpretability, pooled SMDs were back-transformed into natural units using median standard deviations from representative control groups. For Tier B and Tier C outcomes, we calculated relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). RR was selected as the primary effect measure because most included RCTs reported binary event counts at fixed administrative time points rather than survival curves suitable for hazard ratio extraction. This approach maximized evidence inclusion, avoided selection bias associated with restricting analysis to the minority of studies reporting hazard ratios, and facilitated the calculation of the Number Needed to Treat (NNT) to inform policy decision-making. We calculated NNT for significant dichotomous outcomes using the formula: NNT = 1/ | ACR x (1 - RR)|. The Assumed Control Risk (ACR) was derived from the median event rate of the control groups. Specifically for all-cause mortality, NNT calculations were stratified into “general screening” and “high-risk disease management” populations. This distinction was necessary to address the bimodal distribution of baseline risks observed across trials and to prevent the dilution of survival benefits in high-risk cohorts.

To avoid analyzing the same population twice, the single endpoint with the greatest clinical salience was selected when a study reported multiple related endpoints within the same tier. Random-effects models were used to obtain pooled estimates to account for clinical diversity, and statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic. To elucidate the specific drivers of clinical impact and address heterogeneity, we performed a pre-specified functional subgroup analysis. Studies were stratified and quantitatively evaluated based on their Primary Clinical Function as detailed in Section 2.4. Subgroup analyses were conducted across relevant Tiers (A, B, C). Consistent with our statistical power considerations, functional subgroups containing only a single study were excluded from the primary quantitative synthesis to ensure generalizability. Within each functional subgroup, outcomes were further stratified by NICE Tier rather than aggregated globally, as the clinical endpoints across tiers (e.g., diagnosis time vs. mortality) were fundamentally heterogeneous and clinically distinct. All trial endpoints were extracted into Supplementary Table S6–S8, and the analyses were organized by tier.

We conducted pre-specified subgroup analyses to differentiate provider or cluster-level trials from patient-level trials. To address potential unit-of-analysis errors inherent in cluster-randomized designs, we performed sensitivity analyses by adjusting the effective sample size according to the design effect (DE). The effective sample size was recalculated using the formula DE = 1 + (m - 1) x rho, where m represents the average cluster size, and rho represents the assumed intraclass correlation. We applied a baseline assumption where m equals 20 and rho equals 0.05, yielding a DE of 1.95, which is consistent with typical values in health services research. We further explored the robustness of findings across a plausible range, with m varying from 10 to 50 and rho varying from 0.01 to 0.10. The results of these sensitivity analyses are detailed in Supplementary Text S1. For continuous outcomes, group means and standard deviations were retained, whereas sample sizes were replaced by effective sample sizes. These were calculated as the total sample size divided by DE for patient-level cluster trials or derived from cluster counts for provider-level trials. For binary outcomes, both event counts and total sample sizes were scaled by the DE. Study-level variances were recomputed, tier-level effects were re-pooled under the same random-effects model, and the χ2 test for subgroup differences was re-estimated. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test for outcomes with ten or more studies, as presented in Supplementary Fig. S6. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias was independently assessed by two authors (YEL and SMY) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool, in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook68,72. We evaluated five specific domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result (Supplementary Table S9). Disagreements were resolved by consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer (HMC). Publication bias was evaluated for outcomes encompassing ten or more studies (specifically Tier A, Tier B, and Tier C) using visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression asymmetry test via Review Manager71. In line with methodological recommendations, bias assessment was not performed for outcomes with fewer than 10 studies to avoid misleading interpretations due to limited statistical power (Supplementary Fig. S6).
