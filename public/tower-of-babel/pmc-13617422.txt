Agreement of Multimodal Large Language Models and Novice Readers With Expert‐Based Radiographic Scoring of Presumptive Septic Carpal Arthritis in Calves
Authors: Ayşe Gölgeli Bedir, Esra Modoğlu, Tuğçe Kartal, Büşra Kibar, Mümin Gökhan Şenocak, Ömer Tarık Orhun, Ferda Turgut, Uğur Ersöz, Yakup Kocaman, Yasemin Akçora, Sıtkıcan Okur, Latif Emrah Yanmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeptic arthritis is an important cause of morbidity in calves, and radiographic interpretation may vary between observers, especially when structured scoring systems are used by less experienced readers. This single‐center retrospective reader study evaluated whether general‐purpose multimodal large language models (LLMs) could support rubric‐based radiographic scoring of presumptive septic carpal arthritis in calves. Fifty calves aged 0–3 months with clinical findings consistent with presumptive septic carpal arthritis were included, and one carpal radiograph per case was assessed. Two novice veterinary surgeons and three multimodal LLMs, ChatGPT‐5, Gemini‐2.5 Pro, and Claude Sonnet‐4, independently scored each case using a Constant‐based ordinal radiographic scoring framework. Expert consensus served as the operational reference standard. Agreement with the reference was assessed using exact agreement, agreement within one score category, and quadratic weighted kappa. Novice 1 showed the highest concordance, with a mean exact agreement of 55.6%, mean ±1 agreement of 89.8%, and substantial agreement (mean κ w = 0.68; 95% CI, 0.56–0.80). ChatGPT‐5 was the best‐performing model, achieving moderate agreement (mean exact agreement, 53.0%; mean ±1 agreement, 82.6%; mean κ w = 0.58; 95% CI, 0.41–0.72). Claude Sonnet‐4 and Gemini‐2.5 Pro showed slight agreement overall. The best‐performing novice reader remained more reliable than the evaluated models, although ChatGPT‐5 performed comparably to, or better than, one novice evaluator in selected parameters. Selected LLMs may have limited adjunctive value for structured review or training, but not as replacements for human interpretation.Keywords: artificial intelligence, bovine imaging, musculoskeletal radiology, observer agreement, ordinal scoring

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being integrated into human and veterinary medicine to support workflow efficiency, standardization, and diagnostic confidence. In human radiology, AI has been shown to improve the detection of anatomical and pathological abnormalities, in some settings approaching or exceeding novice‐level performance [1]. In veterinary medicine, its use is also expanding, particularly in digital pathology and imaging interpretation [2, 3] Within this broader context, large language models (LLMs) have attracted growing interest because of their ability to combine textual and visual information and generate structured outputs [1].

Although the LLMs evaluated in the present study were not developed specifically for veterinary radiographic interpretation, they are increasingly used in clinical and educational settings as general‐purpose support tools. This raises important questions about their reliability when applied outside their intended domain, particularly in unsupervised real‐world use [1, 3, 4]. In veterinary practice, such systems are not intended to replace expert interpretation, but they may still be used by trainees or early‐career clinicians for preliminary assessment, structured learning, or review. Their performance therefore needs to be evaluated within clearly defined use cases [1, 4].

Septic arthritis is an important cause of morbidity in calves and commonly presents with joint swelling, lameness, fever, and reduced mobility. Although reported prevalence varies according to herd type, case definition, and study population, joint ill/septic arthritis has been reported in approximately 2.47% of crossbred dairy calves in a farm‐based study, and a recent clinical case series described 248 calves treated for septic arthritis, highlighting its continued clinical relevance in calf practice [5, 6]. Infection may occur via hematogenous spread or secondary to umbilical and respiratory disease [7].

The radiographic features of septic arthritis in calves have been described in detail. Constant et al. [8] reported joint‐space widening, articular surface irregularity, and subchondral lysis as common findings, whereas Yurdakul [9] showed that acute cases are more often associated with swelling and joint‐space enlargement, whereas chronic cases may progress to narrowing, periosteal proliferation, and ankylosis. Radiography is therefore important for suspicion and severity assessment, although its sensitivity may be limited in very early disease [8, 9]. Because interpretation may vary between observers, structured scoring systems, such as the Constant et al. [8] scale, may help improve reproducibility, especially outside expert settings.

In the present study, we evaluated three LLMs for rubric‐based radiographic scoring of presumptive septic carpal arthritis in calves and compared their performance with that of novice veterinary surgeons in a standardized multimodal framework using single‐view JPEG radiographs, a Constant‐based scoring rubric, and a brief clinical prompt. To our knowledge, no previous study has compared LLMs with novice readers for single‐view mediolateral (ML) or dorsopalmar (DP) calf carpal radiographs within a defined ordinal scoring system. Our aim was therefore to assess agreement, reproducibility, and concordance with expert‐derived reference scores in order to define the potential role and limitations of LLMs as adjunct readers in bovine musculoskeletal imaging.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This single‐center, retrospective reader study was conducted at the Atatürk University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine Teaching Hospital, Erzurum. The case accrual spanned January 2022–April 2025. The protocol was approved by the Atatürk University Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments (Decision No.: 213; Date: 2025/08), in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the use of animals in research. Given the retrospective analysis of de‐identified clinical data and images, owner consent was waived by the committee.

Calves aged 0–3 months, regardless of breed or sex, with presumptive septic carpal arthritis were included if ≥2 of the following were documented: joint swelling, lameness, fever, anorexia, leukocytosis, or elevated acute‐phase reactants. Exclusion criteria were non‐carpal joint disease, traumatic carpal fracture/dislocation, motion or processing artifact precluding scoring, or incomplete clinical data/radiographs. The final dataset comprised 50 unique cases (one radiograph per case), selected from the eligible clinical archive on the basis of complete clinical records and diagnostically usable radiographs. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because this was an exploratory retrospective reader study. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible cases available during the predefined study period. When both ML and DP projections were available in the clinical archive, one representative projection was selected for standardized scoring on the basis of image availability and diagnostic usability. Paired ML and DP projections from the same calf were not analyzed; therefore, projection‐stratified analyses were performed as exploratory between‐case comparisons rather than direct within‐subject comparisons.

Image Acquisition and File Handling

Radiographs were acquired using a POSKOM beam‐limiting device (Model PCMAX‐100, maximum tube voltage 100 kV, input 240 V/AC, 1500 W). Exposure parameters followed routine calf protocols: 60–70 kV, 5–8 mAs, and source‐to‐image distance (SID) 80–100 cm. Positioning targeted standard ML or DP views with tight collimation centered on the carpus. Animals were physically restrained without image‐guided adjustments; no post‐acquisition enhancement was performed. Images were exported directly as JPEG files via USB from the radiography system without post‐processing, resizing, or contrast manipulation. Original DICOM metadata were not available after export; all files were de‐identified and renamed using Okur et al. [10] study IDs. Projection labels (ML/DP) were withheld from the raters. However, because radiographic anatomy could have allowed implicit recognition of the imaging view, the procedure was regarded as label‐blinding rather than complete projection blinding. Accordingly, blinding was described as label‐blinding rather than true projection blinding.

Case Presentation

Each case packet contained three standardized components: (i) the original‐resolution JPEG radiograph, (ii) a Word document embedding the Constant et al. [8] scoring framework for septic arthritis in calves (10 predefined soft‐tissue and osseous parameters on an ordinal scale), and (iii) a standardized clinical prompt summarizing signalment and key presenting signs (swollen/painful joint, lameness, fever, anorexia) consistent with presumptive septic arthritis. Thus, scoring was performed in a standardized multimodal assessment setting that combined radiographic appearance with structured written criteria and limited clinical context, rather than as unrestricted image‐only interpretation.

Evaluators and AI Models

Scoring was performed by two novice veterinary surgeons, each with 2 years of postgraduate surgical training, and three LLMs: ChatGPT‐5 (OpenAI, August 2025), Gemini‐2.5 Pro (Google, June 2025), and Claude Sonnet‐4 (Anthropic, April 2025). The number of novice readers was determined by the availability of early‐career veterinary surgeons with comparable postgraduate surgical training who could complete the full standardized scoring workflow. All models were evaluated zero‐shot, without additional training or veterinary radiograph exposure, reflecting general reasoning and vision capabilities. The reference scores were established by a two‐member expert panel consisting of a veterinary surgeon and a radiologist with 4 and 10 years of experience, respectively, to combine clinical surgical experience with specialist imaging expertise. Both experts first scored all radiographs independently using the same Constant‐based rubric. Discrepant items were then reviewed jointly, and final reference scores were assigned by consensus. This consensus‐based score set was used as an operational reference standard for agreement analysis.

Each evaluator was allowed up to 2 min per case to standardize case review time across raters and to simulate a constrained first‐pass assessment environment. The purpose of this limit was to ensure comparability of exposure across human and LLM evaluations, rather than to reproduce the workflow of a full specialist radiographic interpretation. The 50 radiographs, comprising 25 ML and 25 DP views, were equally distributed and randomized before presentation, and projection labels were withheld from evaluators.

For each LLM, all cases were evaluated through the commercially available user‐facing chat interface rather than through API or sandbox access. This approach was selected to reflect a structured real‐world use scenario in which veterinary trainees or early‐career clinicians may interact with general‐purpose multimodal LLMs. Each case was opened in a separate cold‐start conversation window, and no feedback was provided during scoring. Where applicable, system memory was disabled, and no prior case information was available to the model. For each model, all 50 case packets were uploaded and scored during a single evaluation session on the same day. The same standardized prompt, radiographic image, clinical summary, and Constant‐based scoring rubric were provided for each case. Model outputs were recorded immediately and transcribed verbatim into the study database. The following standardized prompt was used for every case:

You are a veterinary radiologist. Provided are a carpal radiograph of a calf and the Constant et al. scale designed to systematically score articular radiographs in calves. The calf presents with swollen and painful joints, lameness, fever, and anorexia, findings consistent with a presumptive diagnosis of septic arthritis. Using only the image and these clinical details, assign the radiographic scores according to the scale.

Scoring and Radiographic Evaluation

The Constant et al. [8] framework comprises 10 parameters assessing soft‐tissue and osseous changes, including swelling, joint‐space height, articular surface irregularity, bone proliferation, perichondral erosion, subchondral lysis/sclerosis, and an “others” category (joint‐space reduction, osteophytes, ankylosis, fracture, luxation, and intra‐articular gas). Two composite indices were calculated: Osseous (0–24) and Overall (0–27).

Blinding and Response Collection

Raters were blinded to the reference and to each other's assessments. Projection labels (ML/DP) were withheld, although the projection itself may have been inferable from the radiographic appearance. Case packets were pre‐assembled and randomized (fixed seed), and data were double‐checked for completeness before analysis. No feedback was given during scoring. Human raters entered scores in Excel, and LLM outputs were transcribed verbatim by an independent researcher. The final dataset comprised 50 cases scored by five evaluators (2 novices, 3 LLMs) plus the expert reference. Because microbiological confirmation was not available for all cases, the consensus scores were treated as an expert‐derived operational reference for rubric‐based radiographic evaluation rather than as a definitive ground truth.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The primary endpoint was exact agreement between each rater and the expert‐consensus reference for each parameter; secondary endpoints were ±1 agreement and quadratic weighted Cohen's kappa (κ_w), interpreted according to Landis and Koch [11]. Agreement patterns across the 10 scoring parameters were summarized using violin plots (Figure 1). Pairwise inter‐rater reliability was assessed using κ_w, exact agreement, and ±1 agreement, whereas overall multi‐rater agreement was described with Fleiss’ κ. Misclassification patterns were examined using confusion matrices (Figure 2; detailed parameter‐ and composite‐score confusion matrices are presented in Figures S1–S10), and case‐level dispersion and scoring deviations across raters were assessed using the range and variance of scores (Figure 3) and visualized with a case‐level deviation heatmap (Figure 4).

Projection‐specific performance was evaluated separately for ML (n = 25) and DP (n = 25) views using the overall and osseous scores. For each rater and projection, exact agreement, ±1 agreement, and κ_w were calculated against the reference. Differences between ML and DP were tested with two‐sided Fisher's exact test, and absolute differences were reported with Newcombe–Wilson 95% confidence intervals. Confidence intervals for κ_w were estimated by nonparametric bootstrap (5000 replicates, fixed seed). All tests were two‐sided, p values were considered descriptive, and no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons. Results

Novice 1 showed consistently strong performance across parameters. Exact agreement values were 42% (swelling), 40% (joint space), 74% (surface irregularity), 76% (bone proliferation), 58% (perichondral erosion), 62% (subchondral lysis), 78% (sclerosis), 62% (others), 42% (osseous), and 22% (overall). Agreement was predominantly substantial to almost perfect, with moderate agreement observed for swelling and joint space. Overall, Novice 1 showed the highest concordance with the reference. Novice 2 showed more variable performance, with exact agreement values of 50% (swelling), 42% (joint space), 26% (surface irregularity), 10% (bone proliferation), 18% (perichondral erosion), 22% (subchondral lysis), 4% (sclerosis), 18% (others), 2% (osseous), and 2% (overall), demonstrating moderate agreement for selected parameters but fair agreement overall. Direct comparison between the two novice evaluators also showed notable inter‐reader variability. Direct novice‐to‐novice comparison showed a mean exact agreement of 16.4%, mean ±1 agreement of 56.8%, and mean quadratic weighted κ of 0.33 across the 10 scoring parameters, confirming notable inter‐reader variability despite comparable postgraduate experience. Although Novice 1 demonstrated the highest overall concordance with the expert‐consensus reference, Novice 2 showed lower and more variable agreement across several parameters.

ChatGPT‐5 was the strongest performing LLM and, in selected parameters, approached novice‐level performance. Exact agreement values were 46% (swelling), 30% (joint space), 52% (surface irregularity), 80% (bone proliferation), 68% (perichondral erosion), 70% (subchondral lysis), 74% (sclerosis), 64% (others), 38% (osseous), and 8% (overall). Agreement was generally moderate to substantial, with near‐perfect agreement for bone proliferation and comparatively strong performance for osseous.

Claude Sonnet‐4 showed inconsistent performance. Exact agreement values were 44% (swelling), 46% (joint space), 20% (surface irregularity), 52% (bone proliferation), 18% (subchondral lysis), 54% (sclerosis), with comparatively lower exact agreement for the “others,” osseous, and overall scores. Agreement was slight in most parameters, reflecting low overall reliability. Gemini‐2.5 Pro showed the weakest results overall. Exact agreement values were 28% (swelling), 20% (joint space), 16% (surface irregularity), 18% (bone proliferation), and low across the remaining parameters, with mostly slight agreement, including osseous and overall (all detailed analysis tables are available in Supporting Information 2).

Across all parameters, Novice 1 showed the highest overall concordance with the expert‐consensus reference and remained the best‐performing evaluator overall, whereas ChatGPT‐5 followed with moderate agreement. Novice 2 showed fair agreement, whereas Claude Sonnet‐4 and Gemini‐2.5 Pro showed the weakest overall agreement (Table 1). In projection‐stratified analysis, Novice 1 achieved higher exact and ±1 agreement on DP than ML overall, with κ_w increasing from the substantial to the almost perfect range. ChatGPT‐5 also showed higher performance on DP for osseous, with ±1 agreement increasing from 56% to 84% and stronger agreement metrics overall. Other raters showed minimal, nonsignificant differences. Projection‐specific agreement metrics for the overall and osseous scores are presented in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. Selected projection‐specific differences were observed, particularly for Novice 1 and ChatGPT‐5, with DP views showing numerically higher agreement metrics in some analyses. Case‐level deviation heatmaps illustrated the magnitude and direction of scoring deviations from the expert‐consensus reference across individual cases for each rater (Figure 4).

Discussion

In the present study, the best‐performing novice reader showed closer agreement with the expert‐consensus reference than the evaluated general‐purpose LLMs. However, novice‐reader performance was not uniform. Novice 2 showed lower and more variable agreement, and ChatGPT‐5 performed comparably to, or better than, this novice evaluator in selected parameters. Overall performance was strongest for Novice 1, whereas ChatGPT‐5 was the best‐performing model among the LLMs; however, no model matched the consistency of the best‐performing novice reader across all parameters. These findings indicate that, within this structured radiographic scoring task, general‐purpose LLMs may provide partial support but do not yet perform at a level that would justify their use as stand‐alone readers.

Among the evaluated models, ChatGPT‐5 showed the most favorable agreement profile and, for some conspicuous osseous features, approached novice‐level performance. This pattern is important because it suggests that LLM performance was not uniformly poor, but rather depended on the type of lesion being scored. In our dataset, agreement was higher for more overt structural abnormalities such as bone proliferation, subchondral lysis, sclerosis, and the osseous composite score, whereas softer or potentially earlier radiographic changes, including swelling and joint‐space abnormalities, were assessed less consistently. Importantly, the comparison with novice readers should be interpreted at the individual‐rater level. Although Novice 1 remained the strongest overall evaluator, ChatGPT‐5 performed comparably to, or better than, Novice 2 in selected parameters. This finding is consistent with the broader radiographic literature showing that observer agreement may vary across readers, lesion types, and scoring tasks, particularly when ordinal or structured radiographic assessments are used [12, 13]. Under structured rubric‐based conditions, selected LLMs may, therefore, provide performance similar to some novice interpreters for conspicuous osseous abnormalities, while still remaining unreliable as stand‐alone readers. This interpretation is in line with the radiographic literature indicating that septic arthritis in calves is associated with changes such as joint‐space alteration, articular irregularity, and subchondral lysis, but that observer reproducibility is not uniform across lesion types and disease stages [8, 9]. It is also consistent with broader imaging literature showing that reader and model agreement tends to be higher for visually conspicuous abnormalities than for subtle or early‐stage findings [14, 15].

Exact agreement should also be interpreted with caution in this setting. To our knowledge, there is no universally accepted clinically meaningful threshold for adequate exact agreement between novice and expert readers for Constant‐based radiographic scoring of septic carpal arthritis in calves. Exact agreement is a stringent metric, because even a one‐category difference on an ordinal scale is counted as disagreement. Therefore, in the present study, exact agreement was interpreted together with ±1 agreement and quadratic weighted κ, which provide additional information on near‐category agreement and the magnitude of ordinal disagreement. This combined interpretation is particularly important for structured radiographic scoring tasks, in which adjacent‐category discrepancies may not carry the same practical meaning as larger scoring errors.

The difference between ChatGPT‐5 and the other evaluated LLMs should, therefore, be interpreted cautiously. The better performance of ChatGPT‐5 does not imply diagnostic equivalence to trained readers; rather, it likely reflects better consistency in mapping conspicuous radiographic features onto a predefined ordinal rubric. This distinction is important because the present study assessed agreement with an expert‐derived scoring framework, not unrestricted diagnostic interpretation. In that sense, the observed performance may relate as much to rubric adherence and structured multimodal reasoning as to raw image interpretation itself. This is compatible with recent reviews emphasizing that multimodal LLM outputs in medical imaging are highly task‐dependent and influenced by prompt structure, image quality, and the specificity of the requested output format [1, 4].

Our findings are also broadly consistent with previous veterinary AI literature suggesting that AI performance in radiographic interpretation remains variable, particularly in heterogeneous clinical material and outside task‐specific training conditions [2, 13, 15]. At the same time, our results appear more conservative than reports describing stronger AI or multimodal model performance in other medical imaging contexts [16, 17]. This difference is defensible and likely reflects major differences in study design rather than a contradiction. In the present study, the evaluated systems were general‐purpose, zero‐shot LLMs applied to single‐view JPEG calf carpal radiographs and required to assign ordinal scores within a veterinary‐specific framework. This represents a narrow and demanding task, and performance in such a setting should not be expected to mirror results obtained in other species, imaging modalities, anatomical regions, or task‐specific AI pipelines.

Projection‐stratified analyses showed numerically higher agreement for DP views in selected comparisons, particularly for Novice 1 in overall scoring and for ChatGPT‐5 in osseous scoring. However, these differences were inconsistent across raters and endpoints, and the corresponding confidence intervals were wide. These findings should, therefore, be considered exploratory and interpreted in light of the projection‐selection limitations described below.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the study included only 50 cases from a single center. Second, the reference standard was based on expert consensus rather than an independent biological gold standard; therefore, the results reflect agreement with expert‐defined rubric scores rather than absolute diagnostic truth. Because the reference standard was generated by two experts, this may limit the stability and generalizability of the reference classification, although discrepant items were resolved through consensus review. Accordingly, the reference standard represents the outcome of the consensus process rather than independent expert ratings. Third, not all cases had microbiological confirmation, and the analysis therefore concerns presumptive septic arthritis rather than uniformly confirmed infection. Fourth, the human comparator group consisted of only two novice surgeons, which limits generalization both across different experience levels and within the same novice experience level, given the observed variability between the two novice evaluators. Fifth, only one radiographic projection was analyzed per case, and paired ML and DP radiographs from the same calf were not evaluated. Projection selection was based on clinical availability and diagnostic usability rather than random or paired sampling. Consequently, the projection‐stratified analyses represent between‐case comparisons, may have been affected by selection bias, and cannot establish that either projection provides superior diagnostic or model performance. Sixth, the evaluated LLMs were not trained specifically on veterinary radiographs, and the analysis was performed using single‐view JPEG images within a structured prompt‐and‐rubric environment. In addition, LLM evaluation was performed through user‐facing chat interfaces rather than through API‐ or sandbox‐based access. Therefore, model parameters could not be fully controlled, and exact reproducibility of model behavior cannot be guaranteed. Repeated evaluations for intraobserver variability were not performed, and control assessments using text‐only or image‐only inputs were not included. Consequently, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory results from a structured real‐world chat‐interface workflow rather than as validation of a fully controlled diagnostic AI system.

In conclusion, the present results show that the best‐performing novice reader showed the highest overall concordance with the expert‐consensus reference for rubric‐based radiographic scoring of presumptive septic carpal arthritis in calves. ChatGPT‐5 showed the most promising performance among the tested models, particularly for conspicuous osseous changes, and performed comparably to, or better than, one novice evaluator in selected parameters. However, ChatGPT‐5 remained less consistent than the best‐performing novice reader and less dependable for subtle findings and composite scores. These findings support a cautious view in which selected LLMs may have value as adjunctive tools for training or structured review, but not as replacements for human interpretation in this setting.
