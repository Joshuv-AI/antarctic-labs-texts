Age and livability in a hotter climate
Authors: W Larry Kenney, Rachel M Cottle, Daniel J Vecellio, Kat G Fisher, Olivia K Leach, Qinqin Kong, S Tony Wolf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMen and women over the age of 65 years comprise the most vulnerable population to increasing heat events associated with climate change. One measure of risk involves the physiological determination of the boundary conditions between compensable (thermal balance possible) and uncompensable (continually rising core temperatures) heat strain. The PSU H.E.A.T. (Human Environmental Age Thresholds) Project conducted 273 human subject-based environmental chamber experiments designed to establish critical environmental limits for a cohort of men and women ranging in age from 65 to 92 yrs, both at rest and at a metabolic rate reflecting activities of daily living (i.e., “livability”). Each critical environmental limit comprises a combination of ambient temperature and relative humidity that reflects the upper extremes of livability for older adults. This review documents and provides an overview of the procedures and seminal findings from the project specific to adults over the age of 65 yrs. Predicted changes in the over-65-year-old population in the United States and the consequent impact of climate change projections on future livability are also presented.Keywords: Elderly, Core temperature, Heat stress, Uncompensable heat, Climate change, Heat waves

Background

The purpose of review is to synthesise and provide an overview of the PSU H.E.A.T Project procedures and seminal findings. This review contains previously published data with the exception of the Future Population and Climate Projections analysis. The PSU H.E.A.T Project aimed to determine the environmental conditions beyond which heat balance can no longer occur. In addition to this primary aim, cardiovascular and sweating responses to heat stress and sex differences in critical environmental limits were investigated. This review provides a synthesis of the seminal findings from the project.

Age-heat vulnerability

Epidemiological data demonstrate the significant public health impact of heat waves1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 in the context of excess morbidity and mortality.7, 8, 9, 10 Accordingly, only a brief overview is presented here. Importantly, epidemiological reports consistently illustrate that adults over the age of 65 yrs are disproportionately affected by extreme heat events compared to all other age groups.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6,11,12 Reflecting our changing climate, heat-related mortality for people over 65 years of age increased by approximately 85% between the first 5 years of the 21st century and 2017–2021.13 A meta-analysis of temperature impacts on mortality among the elderly suggested that all-cause mortality increases by 2–5% for each 1 °C increment in ambient temperature during hot weather periods (above region-specific temperature thresholds).14 The health burden of climate change on elderly men and women is undeniable.

Although some excess deaths during environmental heat events are directly attributable to heat illnesses (including heat stroke), the majority of heat-related deaths in older adults report elevated body temperature as a precipitating, exacerbating, or contributing factor, with the primary cause of death recorded as a co-morbidity, such as cardiovascular disease.11,12,15,16 Heat-related mortality data from New York City in 2024 illustrate the impact of chronic underlying health conditions, with 89% of those decedents (with detailed medical examiner records available) having at least one documented chronic health condition; 62% showed evidence of cardiovascular disease and/or hypertension.12

Notably, although ambient outdoor environmental conditions are recognised as dangerous during heatwaves, the majority of heat-related deaths among older men and women occur indoors, primarily in the home.1,12 For example, in the 2021 heat dome over British Columbia, Canada, 90% of heat deaths occurred in adults over the age of 60 years, with 98% occurring in indoor environments. More older women than older men died, and 56% of decedents lived alone.17 Similar results were reported in a 2021 New York City Heat-Related Mortality Report,18 with 71% of deaths occurring in the home. In homes without air conditioning, indoor temperatures can become higher than those outdoors, and the thermal inertia of building interiors causes a time lag in the temperature change relative to outdoors.19 This lag can result in prolonged high indoor temperatures and higher nighttime temperatures compared to outdoors, meaning that residents are often exposed to excessive heat that lasts for several days with no respite.

In summary, although primary ageing increases the risk of heat-related morbidity and mortality, risk is greatest in older adults with concomitant comorbidities. Furthermore, livability for free-living older adults during extreme heat events is largely dictated by the indoor environment in which they reside (i.e., whether there is access to air conditioning or other cooling strategies). Therefore, understanding upper environmental (temperature and humidity) limits of livability – defined here as the ability to conduct activities of daily living -- for older adults is critical to protect those most vulnerable during heat waves.

Survivability vs. livability

In 2010, Sherwood & Huber20 introduced an “adaptability limit” for human heat stress equal to 35 °C wet-bulb temperature (Twb; i.e., 35 °C and 100% rh), an environmental threshold that would result in death after 6 h of exposure. That theoretical limit treats the human body as an adiabatic system in which no heat transfer takes place between the skin and the environment, and thus no cooling occurs. However, humans are dynamic systems with myriad homeostatic adjustments aimed at survival and the underlying rationale behind the theoretical and actual critical environmental limits have been outlined and discussed.20,21

Vanos and colleagues22 introduced the term “livability,” defined as “the maximum safe internal heat production/physical activity that a person can generate without a sustained rate of positive heat storage in the prevailing environment, thus allowing people to safely sustain work or play for an extended period.” In the context of the present review, this definition specifically refers to the environmental conditions that will allow individuals to sustain very low metabolic heat production associated with activities of daily living, i.e., approximately 1.8 METS, for an extended period of time.

With that backdrop, the main purpose of the PSU H.E.A.T. Project was to determine, from a physiological perspective, how hot and humid is too hot and humid for livability among the vulnerable aged population. While critical environmental limits were derived for young and middle-aged men and women as well for comparative purposes,23,24 the scope of this review centres on adults over the age of 65 years.

Approach and methodology

Critical environmental limits are defined as those temperature/humidity combinations above which heat balance cannot be maintained for a given metabolic heat production. In environments exceeding these threshold environments (termed “uncompensable” thermal environments), core temperature cannot achieve steady state for prolonged exposures, but rather continues to rise. The PSU H.E.A.T. (Human Environmental Age Thresholds) Project used a combination physiological/biophysical approach pioneered by Kamon and Belding25 testing human participants in a programmable, controllable environmental chamber. In brief, the participant's core temperature (Tc) is monitored as heat stress is progressively and systematically increased. Over a wide range of increasingly more stressful environments, Tc is able to equilibrate at a temperature proportional to metabolic heat production, yet fully independent of the environmental conditions.26,27 These compensable environments were termed the “prescriptive zone” by Lind.28,29 In thermal environments above these limits,28, 29, 30 heat production by the body plus heat gain from the environment exceed the ability to lose heat, resulting in a continuous rise in Tc (uncompensable heat stress).

These protocols require the ability to systematically change ambient water vapour pressure at a fixed ambient dry-bulb temperature (or vice versa). An example of the time course of environmental conditions for a typical experiment is shown in Fig. 1.

Time course of core temperature (Tc; measured here as gastrointestinal temperature) and ambient water vapour pressure (Pa) for a critical vapour pressure test with increasing Pa and a constant dry bulb temperature (Tdb) of 34 °C. Lines are drawn on the Tc responses showing the change in the rate of rise in core temperature during the equilibration period where core temperature is stable and after the inflection point. The intersection of these lines occurs at the Tc inflection point, which represents the combination of environmental conditions above which heat stress becomes uncompensable and a stable core temperature can no longer be maintained. In this case, the Tc inflection point (i.e., critical water vapour pressure, Pcrit) occurs at Pa = 27 mmHg. Uncompensable environments in this example are to the right of the dashed line.

Our lab31, 32, 33 and others34 have validated that the critical environmental limit does delineate between compensable and uncompensable thermal environments. Above the critical environmental limit it is project that the core temperature associated with heat stroke (40.2 °C) can occur within 4–10 h of sustained exposure while immediately below the threshold, 35–36 h exposure can be sustained before this dangerously elevated Tc is reached.31,34 Cottle also showed that during a 2-h uncompensable heat stress exposure (i.e., above the critical environmental limit) Tc rose continuously.33 Altogether this supports such progressive heat stress protocols as a reliable and valid method for determining the limits of compensability in hot environments.

In warm-to-hot environmental conditions, Tc begins to plateau by ∼30–40 min and remains at an elevated steady state (equilibration phase) through a wide range of increasingly hot and humid conditions. The Tc reached is dictated solely by metabolic heat production and independent of air temperature and humidity. After equilibration, we increase ambient water vapour pressure (Pa) by 1 mmHg every 5 min while keeping dry-bulb temperature (Tdb) constant, systematically increasing the heat stress on the participant. At some “critical” combination of Tdb and Pa, an inflection threshold occurs and Tc starts to rise progressively. The Pa at the fixed chosen dry-bulb temperature (Tdb) is termed the Pcrit and is defined by the conditions immediately preceding the upward inflection of Tc. The inflection point is selected graphically as previously described and validated.7,35 Similarly, in hotter, dry conditions (high Tdb and low Pa environments), Pa is held constant and Tdb is increased 1 °C every 5 min to determine Tcrit at a given Pa. The experimental protocol and inflection in Tc are depicted in Fig. 1.

Additional physiological data, including mean skin temperature (Tsk), heart rate (HR), and steady state oxygen consumption (VO2) are collected to allow for further biophysical modeling. The validity and reliability of this methodology have been well characterised.32,34

The data presented in this review represent the results of 273 such experiments conducted on 49 men and women aged 65–92 years. The subject sample was designed to be inclusive and representative of this age cohort; thus, the only exclusion criterion was based on safety of participation as designated by a cardiologist. Critical environmental limits

The critical environmental limits for aged adults can be represented across a range of ambient conditions from warm, humid to very hot and dry as shown in Fig. 2. Fig. 2A presents upper limits of heat compensability for older men and women seated at rest, while 2B presents limits associated with livability (light activity). Even light metabolic heat production associated with activities of daily living shifts the limit downward and leftward on the relative humidity vs. ambient temperature spectrum. For example, an environment of 42 °C and 30% rh would be compensable for older adults at rest, but uncompensable during light activity, leading to a continual rise in Tc if the activity is prolonged. The shape and locus of critical environmental limit lines vary with metabolic heat production,37 heat acclimation,38 and – germane to this paper – age.

The thicker black line illustrates the mean and gray dashed lines outline the 95% confidence interval. Blue lines depict wet-bulb temperature. Data were redrawn from Wolf et al., 2023.36

For humid environments, e.g., over 50% rh, the critical environmental limit is constrained by the capacity of the environment for sweat evaporation. The critical environmental limit in such humid environments tracks wet-bulb temperature closely; thus a critical Twb is a useful approximation of the compensability limit.

For older adults in hot environments over 50% rh, this value is approximately 28.5 °C Twb. Obviously, this isothermic Twb is substantially lower that the 35 °C adaptability limit of Sherwood and Huber and a significantly lower livability threshold than the 31–32 °C Twb limit previously published for young adults.21 This clearly illustrates the increasing heat vulnerability as humans age.

The combinations of air temperature and humidity depicted in Fig. 2 are physiologically determined and rely on principles of biophysical heat exchange associated with human homeostatic control. In the United States, the Heat Index (HI) is a well-known number developed by the National Weather Service based on the work of Steadman39 and promulgated by meteorologists. The HI is a perceptual index -- what the ambient temperature feels like when relative humidity is combined with the air temperature. The HI has important implications for the thermal comfort but is not physiologically based. In young individuals, the empirically derived livability limit follows the “Extreme Danger” category of the Heat Index as defined by the National Weather Service.40 However, in older adults, this relation falls into the lower range of the more conservative “Danger” zone, despite their increased vulnerability. Therefore, reliance on the HI as a heat stress warning is unsupported and potentially hazardous. While thermal indices are valuable tools for assessing and mitigating heat related adverse health outcomes, most indices do not perform well across a wide variety of populations and activities. Specifically in humid heat stress conditions, Twb for indoor environments and WBGT for outdoor environments are better predictors of heat stress compensability and best align with our critical environmental limits.

Sweating and Skin wettedness

During heat stress, the evaporation of sweat from the skin surface is the predominant cooling mechanism for the maintenance of thermal balance. In the hot, dry regions of Fig. 2, free evaporation can occur and the critical limit reflects both dry heat gain through radiation and the ability to continue to produce, and therefore evaporate, sweat. Evaporative heat loss is strongly influenced by the proportion of the body covered in sweat at a given time, termed skin wettedness (ω).

Partitional calorimetry, using the data collected in the PSU H.E.A.T. protocols, can be used to determine ω, which in turn can be used by researchers to model a variety of human responses, including how long extreme heat can be tolerated. In our protocols, maximal skin wettedness (ωmax) was determined at each critical environmental limit for older adults. Current heat exposure duration limits established by the ISO are based on seminal work by Candas et al.41 that assumes a ωmax of 0.85 for unacclimated individuals. However, ωmax is not a constant, but rather depends on both the physiological capacity for sweat production and the environmental capacity for evaporation and is therefore influenced by age, metabolic work rate, heat acclimation, and environmental conditions.

Unsurprisingly, ωmax declines with advancing age and is lower during even light activity in older compared to young adults in both warm-humid and hot-dry environments.42 Within the older adult cohort, as seen in Table 2, ωmax was lower during seated rest compared to the light activity defining the Livability condition in the humid environment only. In the livability condition, ωmax was lower in the dry environment compared to the humid environment, likely due to the higher evaporative potential of the dry environment. The lack of difference in ωmax between livability and resting conditions likely reflects a basement effect of the already very low ωmax in older adults at both metabolic rates.

ωmax determined as part of the PSU H.E.A.T project are substantially lower than the currently assumed ωmax of 0.85 for unacclimated adults. Accurate age-appropriate values of ωmax are critical when modeling responses to heat stress to estimate heat balance – and thus the risk of heat stress -- in older adults. Findings from the PSU H.E.A.T Project suggest that the commonly used ωmax of 0.85 is not achievable for most older adults under heat wave conditions and that ωmax is variable depending on the environmental conditions and metabolic work being performed. Therefore, the ISO standards may overestimate what environmental conditions are safe for older adults.

Heart rate drift as a Precursor to thermal Imbalance

During extreme heat events, cardiovascular episodes and complications secondary to elevated core temperatures are the leading cause of increased morbidity and mortality among older adults.43,44 For example, 94% of excess deaths during the 1995 Chicago heat wave had a cardiovascular sequelae listed as the underlying cause of death.15 In Beijing, extreme high temperatures resulted in a greater proportion of cardiovascular deaths among older adults compared to other age cohorts.45 Further, a heat wave in Philadelphia resulted in a 98% increase in cardiovascular mortality and significantly increased the risk of death from cardiovascular disease for people aged 65 yrs and older.46 Importantly, as the frequency, duration, and severity of heat events become more extreme, projective models indicate that future environmental heat events could cause tens of thousands of excess deaths due to cardiovascular complications among older adults.47

Heat stress produces cardiac strain, disproportionately so in older men and women. Cardiac output must increase to maintain blood pressure and support substantial increases in skin blood flow despite decreasing blood volume consequent to sweat losses. In young individuals, this increase in cardiac output is primarily driven by an increase in HR as stroke volume is fairly well maintained.48 In older individuals during supine passive heating, the increase in cardiac output was lower by half and stroke volume was slightly reduced.48 Even in studies in which older men and women were able to maintain a relatively stable stroke volume, elevated left ventricular systolic strain was evident.49 As such, older adults rely on a greater percent of their maximal heart rate (chronotropic reserve) to meet the necessary cardiovascular adjustments, increasing myocardial strain.48 Increases in cardiac output in heat stressed older individuals are primarily driven by larger (relative to maximum) increases in HR coupled with enhanced contractility, increasing the work of the left ventricle. The increased myocardial perfusion requirement may exceed coronary blood flow reserve, resulting in ischemia.50 For example, Foster et al. recently assessed cardiac responses to dry (47 °C, 15% relative humidity) and humid (41 °C and 40% relative humidity) heat stress in young and older adults at a metabolic rate similar to activities of daily living. Their findings illustrate that while cardiac output in maintained in both groups, older adults rely on a greater percentage of their maximum heart rate and systolic mechanisms to maintain stroke volume.51

Like the determination of critical environmental limits for core temperature, critical HR thresholds (that when surpassed result in a continuous rise in HR), can be determined using the same progressive heat stress protocols. HR follows a similar temporal pattern to Tc, reaching an elevated steady state ∼30–40 min after initiation of the experiment but being forced out of equilibrium as heat stress increases. The critical HR threshold, i.e., the onset of cardiovascular drift, is defined as the environmental conditions immediately preceding the upward inflection in HR. As shown in Fig. 3 and further described by Cottle et al.,24 the HR inflection consistently precedes that of Tc. For example, in a 36 °C environment, the onset of cardiovascular drift occurred at a relative humidity of 53% (CI 47, 59) with Tc inflecting at 57% rh (CI 50, 64). In a hot, dry environment (12 mmHg Pa), HR began to rise at 41 °C (CI 38, 43) and Tc at 43 °C (CI 41, 45).

We propose that this onset of cardiovascular drift represents an early adjustment to the onset of uncompensable heat stress, aimed at the maintenance of heat balance.52 The increase in blood flow directed to the skin during heat stress is supported by an increase in cardiac output; however, in older adults the increase is substantially less.48 In response, the HR threshold occurs at lower temperatures and humidities for older adults compared with their young counterparts.24

Sex differences

In young men and women aged 18–35 yrs, there are no sex differences in the critical. environmental core temperature limits at low metabolic rates.23 On the other hand, older women have lower critical environmental limits than age-matched men in both humid and dry extreme environments, that is, a narrower range of compensable environmental conditions (Fig. 4).54 In fact, women aged 40–60 have critical environmental limits that are comparable to men over the age of 65 yrs. This finding is congruent with epidemiological evidence demonstrating greater rates of morbidity and mortality in older women compared to older men during environmental heat waves.55, 56, 57 This age-specific sex difference emerges starting at midlife (around age 50 yrs) and persist throughout the later adult lifespan, suggesting that midlife is a crucial timepoint in the decline of body temperature regulation in women. The age at which these sex differences emerge correspond with the age at which most women go through the menopause transition. Though speculative, the physiological adjustments associated with the menopause transition could contribute to the impaired body temperature regulation in women when compared to age matched men.

Future population and climate projections

Anthropogenic climate change has increased the average global temperature by 1.1 °C since the latter part of the 19th century.58 Average temperatures have risen across the contiguous 48 United States since 1901, with an exaggerated rate of warming since the late 1970s. Nine of the top 10 warmest years on record have occurred since 1998 and heat waves are occurring 3 times more often than they did in the 1960s.59

At the same time, the world's population is rapidly ageing, as is the U.S. population. The number of people ≥65 years old in the U.S. projected to increase to nearly 80 million by the year 2040.60 This group experienced the largest percentage increase between 2010 and 2022, with its population increasing by 43%. By 2034, older adults are expected to outnumber children under 18 for the first time in U.S. history, a trend that will continue for decades. By 2060 there will be 95 million older adults in the U.S.61 A rapidly growing aged population coupled with an increased frequency and severity of heat waves combines to exponentially increase the number of older men and women at risk during environmental extremes.

Synopsis

Older men and women represent the most vulnerable group with respect to tolerance to environmental extremes, as evidenced by morbidity and mortality data and physiological studies. The PSU H.E.A.T. Project was aimed at determining one parametre of risk: what are the critical limits of ambient temperature and humidity beyond which heat production and gain overwhelm the capacity for heat dissipation? As such, the critical environmental limits can be used to develop evidence-based heat safety guidelines to protect vulnerable population, specifically adults over 65 yrs, during extreme heat events. Because heat stress is accompanied by cardiovascular strain, the critical environments at which cardiovascular drift begins represents an additional, more conservative, threshold. Unlike their young counterparts middle-aged and older women are at greater risk by these metrics than age-matched men. Finally, future disproportionate increases in the over-65-year-old population in the United States coupled with climate change projections paint an ominous picture for future livability in this age cohort. The critical environmental limit thresholds established by the PSU H.E.A.T Project may be used to predict and warn of possible adverse heat related health outcomes so that heat safety guidelines can be developed and heat stress prevention can occur for adults over the age of 65 yrs.

Outstanding Questions

The studies reported here were conducted in relatively temperate areas of the world with four seasons. They may not reflect responses of populations who live year-round in tropical climates. The concurrence of the decline in thermoregulatory function in women with the typical age of menopause also deserves further study. Finally, the impact of a wide variety of age associated co-morbidities on critical environmental limits has not been investigated.
