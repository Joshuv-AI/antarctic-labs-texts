Caregiver burden, attachment and cognitive emotion among the family caregivers of severe mental illness patients
Authors: Zahra Bagheriamiri, Zahra Mirsepassi, Leila Sayadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSerious mental illness (SMI) is a debilitating medical condition that causes stress and challenges for the family caregivers (FCs) of affected patients, increasing their caregiver burden (CB). This situation can activate attachment styles (AS) and trigger negative emotions, further contributing to CB. Given that AS and cognitive-emotional regulation (CER) can affect the CB of FCs of patients with SMI, the aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between CB with AS, and CER among the FCs.MethodsThis cross-sectional descriptive-correlational study was carried out in May–November 2022. Participants were 278 FCs of patients with SMI consecutively recruited from Roozbeh leading psychiatric hospital, Tehran, Iran. Data were collected using a patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics questionnaire, an FCs’ demographic characteristics questionnaire, the Attachment Style Questionnaire, the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, and the Burden Scale for Family Caregivers, and were analyzed using the SPSS software (v. 16.0).ResultsCB had significant inverse relationship with secure AS (r = − 0.262) and significant positive relationship with fearful AS (r = 0.194) and dismissive AS (r = 0.242) (P < 0.01). Moreover, CB had significant inverse relationship with adaptive CER strategies and significant positive relationship with maladaptive CER strategies (P < 0.001). Regression analysis also showed that CB had significant relationship with secure AS and catastrophizing, rumination, self-blame, and positive refocusing CER strategies (P < 0.05).ConclusionThis study concludes that AS and CER can impact CB. There is a negative relationship between secure AS and CB, as well as between adaptive CER strategies and CB. Conversely, there is a positive relationship between avoidant AS and increased CB, as well as between maladaptive CER strategies and CB. It is recommended to adopt strategies that promote the use of secure AS and adaptive CER among the FCs of patients with SMI.

Background

Serious mental illness (SMI) refers to a wide range of psychiatric diagnoses such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depression [1]. SMI has high global prevalence [2]. Given the chronic course of SMI [1], deinstitutionalization policies since 1960, and improvement in the treatment and the life expectancy of patients with SMI, most afflicted patients live in community-based and home-based care settings [3]. Studies show that more than 60% of clients discharged from mental care settings return to their homes [4]. In Asian countries, like Iran, almost 70% of patients with schizophrenia receive care mainly from their family members [5]. Therefore, family caregivers (FCs) are the main source of caregiving to patients with SMI and have critical role in maintaining the continuity of care provision to them [6].

Caregiving to patients with SMI seriously affects FCs [7] and causes them different problems and challenges [8–10] such as stress, anxiety, boredom, depression, and burnout [8]. These problems and challenges are associated with heavy caregiver burden (CB) [9, 10]. CB refers to physical, mental, emotional, and financial problems for FCs due to the responsibility of caregiving to a family member with chronic illness. A study reported that 73.6% of the FCs of individuals with mental disorders suffered from moderate to high levels of mental strain [7]. Heavy CB has significant negative effects on different aspects of FCs’ lives, such as their quality of life, life expectancy, roles, and social interactions [11].

Attachment style (AS) is a factor with potential impact on CB [12]. Attachment is an independent behavioral system formed based on interpersonal relationships between caregiver and care receiver [13]. Its development starts early in the childhood and evolves throughout the life. Although attachment-related behaviors are present throughout the life, they are most prominent in emergencies and threats [13]. AS ensures the development of relationships between caregivers and care receivers [12]. Based on individuals’ self-image and image of others, Bartholomew and Horowitz determined four types of AS, namely secure, preoccupied, dismissive, and fearful. Individuals with secure AS have positive image of self and others, can trust others, and are more resistant to stressful events. Individuals with preoccupied AS have negative self-image and positive image of others and seek others’ acceptance and approval. This AS is due to maladaptive care. Individuals with dismissive AS have positive self-image and negative image of others and consider that they do not need others’ help and support. Individuals with fearful AS have negative image of self and others and are reluctant to communicate with others due to their fear over others’ rejection [14]. Attachment influences caregivers’ coping and care quality [15]. Secure AS is a mental protective factor for FCs [16] and is associated with more empathetic and sensitive care, while insecure AS is associated with mental distress and maladaptive caregiving-related outcomes [15, 17].

Cognitive emotion regulation (CER) is another factor with potential impact on CB [18]. Emotion regulation through thinking or cognition has inseparable relationship with human life and helps individuals manage their emotions and stressful conditions and circumstances [19]. There are two main types of CER strategies, namely positive or adaptive and negative or maladaptive. Adaptive CER strategies include positive refocusing, acceptance, refocusing on planning, and putting into perspective, while maladaptive CER strategies include self-blame, other-blame, rumination, and catastrophizing [20]. Effective emotion regulation facilitates successful functioning in social interactions, reduces negative emotions, and improves resilience [21]. Expressed emotion is a significant predictor of symptom and illness recurrence so that a study showed that the recurrence rate of bipolar disorder in families with high levels of expressed emotion was 90% in nine months [22]. A study in China also revealed that 42.1% of the FCs of patients with schizophrenia had high levels of emotion expression which was associated with increased subjective CB [23]. High levels of emotion expression increase mental strain among all family members and increase the risk of affliction by physical and mental problems by three times [4]. Therefore, positive CER strategies can help FCs more effectively regulate their cognition and reduce their emotional distress and CB [20].

A study in Qatar reported that compared with the mothers of children without neurodevelopmental disorders, the mothers of children with these disorders had higher levels of depression and anxiety and less frequently used adaptive CER strategies [24]. Another study on the parents of children with autism spectrum disorders in China also revealed that parents’ problem in emotion regulation was associated with higher levels of parenting stress and lower levels of adaptive behaviors [25]. Moreover, a study on the FCs of individuals with Alzheimer’s disease indicated that FCs with avoidant and anxious/ambivalent ASs had high levels of anxiety [26]. Another study on the FCs of patients with cancer in China also found that FCs with anxious AS had higher levels of depression and lower levels of self-confidence [16].

Studies have been conducted on As, the CER, and the CB experienced by caregivers of patients with mental disorders or chronic illnesses in various countries. However, the relationship between these factors has not been thoroughly investigated in caregivers of other diseases. A study in England aimed at investigating the AS and anxiety levels in FCs of Alzheimer’s patients found that those with avoidant and ambivalent (anxious) AS had higher anxiety scores than FCs with secure attachment styles [26]. In another study exploring the relationship between insecure attachment, social support, and psychological experiences in caregivers of cancer patients, researchers found that caregivers with an anxious AS experienced higher levels of depression and lower self-confidence. They perceived the challenges of caring for patients as exaggerated and uncontrollable [16]. Other studies have also examined insecure attachment and the CB of parents with children [27], AS and filial commitment in caregivers of dementia patients [18, 26], caregivers of cancer patients [16], and caregivers of patients with consciousness disorders [28]. In another study, the quality of life, emotional regulation problems, and CB in caregivers of patients with personality disorders have been investigated [29]. Furthermore, studies examining this issue among caregivers of patients with SMI have not yielded significant findings. Although most previous studies reported the effects of AS and CER on FCs’ CB [30, 31], there are limited data about their effects among the FCs of individuals with SMI, particularly in Iran. The objective of the present study was to evaluate the relationship between CB with CER and AS among the FCs of patients with SMI. It was hypothesized that there is a relationship between CER strategies and CB in the FC of patients with SMI. Additionally, there is a relationship between AS and CER strategies, as well as between AS and CB in the FC of patients with SMI.

Methods

Design

This cross-sectional descriptive-correlational study was carried out in May–November 2022.

Setting and participants

The study setting was the clinics and the day care centers of Roozbeh psychiatric hospital, Tehran, Iran. Participants included 278 FCs of patients with SMI, who were recruited for the study through consecutive sampling, a form of non-probability sampling. Inclusion criteria were age of 18–60 years, first-degree kinship with the patient, being the main FCs of a patient with SMI, patient’s age of 18–60 years, caregiving experience of at least twelve months [32], ability to read and write in Persian, possession of a smartphone and ability to use it, access to the internet, consent for participation, no self-report affliction by debilitating physical conditions, apparent mental disorder, or substance abuse, no significant life event during the past six months, no intake of sedative agents, caring for a patient who was diagnosed only with SMI and had no other diseases, not taking care of a family member with a mental or physical illness simultaneously and no psychosis diagnosis for the patient due to substance abuse. Participants who did not answer to study questionnaires after three online requests were excluded.

Data collection

Data were collected using a patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics questionnaire, an FCs’ demographic characteristics questionnaire, the Attachment Style Questionnaire, the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, and the Burden Scale for Family Caregivers. The items of the patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics questionnaire were on age, gender, educational level, comorbid conditions, duration of affliction by SMI, and type of SMI. The FCs’ demographic characteristics questionnaire had items on age, gender, marital status, number of children, educational level, employment status, kinship with the patient, living arrangement, financial status based on self-declaration, length of caregiving, and length of daily caregiving.

The Attachment Style Questionnaire was developed by Van Oudenhoven et al. in 2003 [34]. It has 24 items in four subscales, namely secure, fearful, preoccupied, and dismissive ASs. Items are scored on a five-point scale from 1 (“Completely disagree”) to 5 (“Completely agree”). The AS with the highest score is the dominant AS of the respondent. Van Oudenhoven et al. reported the acceptable reliability of all subscales of this questionnaire [34]. A study in Iran culturally-adapted the questionnaire and evaluated its psychometric properties. The Persian Attachment Style Questionnaire has 23 items in the secure AS (seven items), fearful AS (five items), preoccupied AS (five items), and dismissive AS (six items) subscales. The score of each subscale is determined through summing the scores of its items. The total possible scores of the subscales are 7–35 for secure AS, 5–25 for fearful AS, 5–25 for preoccupied AS, and 6–30 for dismissive AS. The Cronbach’s alpha values of the Persian Attachment Style Questionnaire and its secure, fearful, preoccupied, and dismissive AS subscales were 0.70, 0.63, 0.72, 0.76, and 0.69, respectively [35]. The questionnaire had acceptable validity and reliability in the present study and its Cronbach’s alpha was 0.76.

The Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, developed by Garnefski and Kraaij in 2006 [36], was used for CER strategies assessment. This questionnaire has eighteen items in nine two-item subscales, namely self-blame, other-blame, rumination, catastrophizing, putting into perspective, positive refocusing, positive reappraisal, acceptance, and refocusing on planning. Putting into perspective, positive refocusing, positive reappraisal, acceptance, and refocusing on planning are adaptive CER strategies, while self-blame, other-blame, rumination, and catastrophizing are maladaptive CER strategies. Items are scored on a five-point scale from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Always”). In total, eight items are on maladaptive CER strategies and ten items are on adaptive CER strategies. The possible total score of each subscale is 2–10, with higher scores showing greater use of that strategy. The total score of adaptive strategies is calculated through dividing the sum score of adaptive subscales by ten, while the total score of maladaptive strategies is calculated through dividing the sum score of maladaptive subscales by eight [37]. Previous studies reported that the Cronbach’s alpha of the questionnaire was more than 0.80 [36, 38]. The Cronbach’s alpha values of the adaptive and maladaptive strategies of this questionnaire in a study in Iran were 0.77 and 0.86, respectively [38]. The questionnaire had acceptable validity and reliability in the present study and its Cronbach’s alpha was 0.71.

The Burden Scale for Family Caregivers, developed by Graessel et al. in 1993 [39], was used for CB assessment. This scale has ten items scored on a four-point scale from zero (“Completely disagree”) to 3 (“Completely agree”) and hence, the total possible score of the scale is 0–30, with higher scores showing greater CB. Graessel et al. reported that the Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was 0.92 [39]. A study in Iran also evaluated the psychometric properties of the scale and reported its acceptable reliability [40]. The validity and reliability of the scale in the present study were also acceptable and its Cronbach’s alpha was 0.83.

Data analysis

Data were summarized and reported using absolute and relative frequencies for categorical variables and mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. The relationship of demographic and clinical characteristics with CB was assessed using the independent-sample t and the Chi-square test and the relationship of AS and CER strategies with CB was assessed using correlation analysis. Linear modeling was also employed to evaluate the relationship of CB with other study variables. Data analysis was performed using the SPSS software (v. 16.0) at a significance level of less than 0.05.

Results

The means of patients’ age, length of affliction by SMI, FCs’ age, and length of daily caregiving were 37.089 ± 13.89 years, 10.39 ± 9.53 years, 43.43 ± 10.89 years, and 13.44 ± 9.41 h, respectively. Most patients were male (56.8%, n = 158) and suffered from bipolar disorder (59.7%, n = 166), and most FCs were female (84.2%, n = 234). Moreover, 43.9% of FCs were parents (n = 122) and 39.9% of them had a caregiving history of more than ten years (n = 111) (Table 1).

Regression analysis with the adjustment of the effects of potential confounders revealed that CB had significant relationship with secure AS (P = 0.017) as well as catastrophizing (P < 0.001), rumination (P < 0.001), self-blame (P = 0.003), and positive refocusing (P = 0.007) CER strategies. Each one point increase in the mean scores of secure AS and positive refocusing CER strategy reduced the mean score of CB by 1.204 and 0.501 points, respectively. On the other hand, each one point increase in the mean scores of rumination and self-blame CER strategies increased the mean score of CB by 0.75 and 0.577, respectively. Among demographic characteristics, gender, educational level, living arrangement, financial status, division of work, and length of caregiving had significant relationship with CB (P < 0.05). The mean score of CB among female FCs was more than male FCs by 2.536 points (P = 0.007) and the mean score of CB among FCs with bachelor’s degree and higher was significantly more than other FCs by 2.1 points (P = 0.001). Moreover, the mean score of CB among FCs who lived with their patients was significantly more than those who did not live with their patients by 3.419 points (P = 0.001) and the mean score of CB among FCs with poor financial status was significantly more than those with moderate and good financial status (P < 0.05). The mean score of CB among FCs with division of work was also significantly less than those without division of work by 2.353 points (P = 0.002) and each one point increase in the mean of the length of caregiving significantly increased CB by 0.298 points (P = 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship of CB with CER and AS among the FCs of individuals with SMI. Study findings showed that secure AS with a mean score of 3.90 ± 0.70 and dismissive AS with a mean score of 3.07 ± 0.77 were the dominant ASs among the FCs of patients with SMI. Similarly, a study in Turkey showed that secure and dismissive ASs were the dominant ASs among informal FCs [17]. Another study also showed that secure and anxious ASs were respectively the most and the least prevalent ASs around the world [41]. However, a study in Iran found anxious AS as the dominant AS among the mothers of patients with schizophrenia [42]. This contradiction may be due to the small sample size and lack of inclusion criteria for caregivers in that study [42]. Besides genetic factors, environmental, sociocultural, ecological, and ethnic factors can influence the formation of norms, values, beliefs, and ASs.

We also found that the mean score of adaptive CER strategies was more than the mean score of maladaptive CER strategies (3.27 ± 0.67 vs. 2.59 ± 0.65). This is in agreement with the findings of a study on patients with dementia in Switzerland [20] and a study on the parents of children with cancer in Iran [43]. Culture is a known influential factor on emotion regulation because it forms social norms, values, and beliefs and encourages individuals to use certain types of emotion regulation strategies [44].

The mean score of CB in the present study was 16.079 ± 7.184, denoting moderate CB. This is in agreement with the findings of studies on the FCs of patients with mental disorders and schizophrenia [45–47]. CB is determined by many different factors such as family support, social support, support by healthcare system, and financial status of the family and the society. The low proportion of healthcare providers to patients in Iran requires family members to take the responsibility of caregiving to their patients and this imposes heavy physical, mental, emotional, financial, and social burden on them [48].

Our findings also indicated that CB had significant negative relationship with secure AS and significant positive relationship with preoccupied, fearful, and dismissive ASs. This is consistent with the findings of a study on the FCs of individuals with Alzheimer’s disease in Korea [18] and the FCs of patients with altered consciousness in Italy [28]. Secure AS is a protective factor for health and well-being among FCs [24] and can reduce their mental distress [15, 17].

Another finding of the present study was that CB had significant negative relationship with adaptive CER strategies and significant positive relationship with maladaptive CER strategies. This is in agreement with the findings of a study on the FCs of patients with dementia in Switzerland and the findings of a study on the FCs of elderly patients with stroke in Iran [49]. Of course, cultural differences respecting value orientations can lead to differences in the mean score of CER strategies. Culture forms norms, values, and beliefs, and thereby, influences individuals’ emotion regulation strategies [44].

We also found that after adjusting the effects of potential confounders, FCs’ CB had significant relationship with secure AS as well as catastrophizing, rumination, self-blame, and positive refocusing CER strategies. A study on the FCs of patients with cancer in China also reported the same finding [16]. Moreover, our findings indicated that compared with their male counterparts, female FCs had significantly greater CB, which is consistent with the findings of two previous studies [32, 50]. Women are the main FCs in most caregiving situations. In Middle East countries, men usually do not openly complain about their problems due to their cultural norms [2, 3]. On the other hand, women usually experience greater CB and hence, experience more fatigue, isolation, and despair [51]. In contradiction to our findings, a study in Egypt reported that men had greater CB than women [46].

Participants with bachelor’s degree or higher had greater CB than those with diploma or below-diploma educational level. FCs with higher educational level may have various occupational and social roles and hence, may experience role conflicts, which can increase their CB. Moreover, FCs with higher educational level may better know and predict their patients’ needs and hence, feel necessary to make greater effort to fulfill their needs. Contrary to our findings, several previous studies reported greater CB among FCs with lower educational level [5, 32, 46]. Moreover, we found that FCs who lived with their patients as well as those with poorer financial status had greater CB, which is in line with the findings of two previous studies [6, 45]. Besides, FCs with division of work and shorter length of caregiving had lower CB. This is also in agreement with the findings of two previous studies [32, 52]. FCs who give care to their patients for long hours are more involved in caregiving and have less time for participation in social and recreational activities and hence, experience greater CB [8].

Strengths

This study was conducted on a large enough sample and in a leading referral center of psychiatric care in Tehran, Iran. Study instruments had forced-choice items and participants completed them online; thus, the study had no missing data.

Limitations

Given that the relationship between AS and CER strategies among FCs of patients with SMI has not been extensively researched in Iran, this study was conducted utilizing a cross-sectional design. Based on these results, it may be possible to implement programs aimed at enhancing CER and transforming insecure AS into secure ones among FCs of patients with SMI. The effects of these interventions on the perceived burden of FCs can then be assessed. Another limitation of this study was participant selection through consecutive sampling. Given that the study showed FCs exhibit secure, dismissive, fearful and preoccupied AS, we did not assess the reasons for these AS in this study. Further research is needed to identify their underlying causes. Since AS and CER strategies can change over time and can be learned through interventions, we did not measure them longitudinally.

Conclusion

This study concludes that the FCs of patients with SMI have moderate CB, use adaptive CER strategies more frequently than maladaptive CER strategies, and their dominant ASs are secure and dismissive ASs. Moreover, their CB has significant relationship with secure AS, catastrophizing, rumination, self-blame, and positive refocusing CER strategies, gender, educational level, living arrangement, financial status, division of work, and length of caregiving. As ASs develop over time and throughout life, psychiatric nurses and psychiatrists can use strategies such as attachment-based education, cognitive-behavioral counseling, schema therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, stress management training, and resilience-based training to modify insecure ASs of FCs and promote their secure AS. Moreover, as the FCs of patients with SMI use maladaptive CER strategies in addition to adaptive CER strategies and given the significant effects of maladaptive CER strategies on CB, strategies such as problem solving-based therapies and cognitive-behavioral therapies are necessary to improve their use of maladaptive CER strategies. The significant relationship of FCs’ financial status with their CB also highlights the necessity of strong financial and social support for them.
