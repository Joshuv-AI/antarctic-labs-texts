Target-volume-based prescription selection for specified adverse-effect levels in automated stereotactic radiosurgery
Authors: Joel A Pogue, Rex Cardan, Farnaz Rahim Li, Rodney Sullivan, Natalie Viscariello, Luke Moradi, John Fiveash, Richard Popple
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomated stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) planning produced consistent dose falloff, enabling adverse-effect-relevant isodose volumes (IDV) to be estimated before plan generation. We derived a direct isoeffect prescription dose (DRX) model from previously validated target-volume (Vtarget) power laws for single-isocenter automated SRS. IDV coefficients and exponents were modelled as continuous functions of relative isodose level (RIL), allowing DRX to be solved from Vtarget, desired IDV, and HyTEC brain adverse-effect dose thresholds. Across held-out levels, target-level IDV residuals had a median of 0.2%, an interquartile range of [−5.2%, +7.0%], and a 95% interval of [−12.8%, +27.0%].Keywords: Stereotactic radiosurgery, Isodose volume, Isoeffect prescription, Brain metastases, Dose–volume relationship

Introduction

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is widely used for brain metastases, providing high local control while limiting irradiation of normal brain tissue [1], [2]. Treatment selection requires balancing tumor control against the risk of radiation-induced adverse effects, particularly radionecrosis, which depends on the volume of normal brain receiving intermediate dose levels [3]. Dose–volume relationships from quantitative analysis of normal tissue effects in the clinic (QUANTEC) and high dose per fraction, hypofractionated treatment effects in the clinic (HyTEC) provide clinical adverse-effect benchmarks, including the volume of brain receiving 12 and 14 Gy in a single fraction and biologically equivalent dose-volume limits for multi-fraction SRS [4], [5]. In particular, HyTEC summarized adverse-effect risk estimates for 5, 10, and 20 cm3 isodose volumes (IDV) receiving adverse-effect-relevant dose levels (Dadverse), allowing dose-volume metrics to serve as practical surrogates for treatment safety [4].

Despite their clinical importance, HyTEC/QUANTEC dose-volume guidance is typically evaluated after a candidate treatment plan has already been generated. As a result, prescription and fractionation selection may require iterative planning, with candidate prescriptions reviewed and modified until normal brain dose guidance is satisfied. This process can be inefficient, planner-dependent, and difficult to apply consistently before planning. The challenge is particularly relevant for linear accelerator-based SRS, where manually generated plan quality can vary across institutions, planners, and treatment techniques [6], [7], [8]. Automated accelerator-based SRS planning has reduced this variability by standardizing optimization objectives and delivery geometry, resulting in more consistent relationships between target geometry and dose falloff, largely due to black-box normal tissue objectives which negate the need for ad-hoc nested tuning structures [8], [9], [10].

In prior work, automated SRS IDVs were accurately predicted from target volume (Vtarget) using power-law relationships at discrete relative isodose levels (RIL) [11], resulting in the creation of an online calculator which predicts IDV from 1-, 2-, and 3-D target dimensions. These models enabled evaluation of whether commonly used prescription dose (DRX) and fractionation schemes were likely to result in IDVs exceeding HyTEC Dadverse. However, those models were limited to discrete RILs; thus, IDVs had to be queried and modelled separately for each prescription-adverse-effect dose combination. Therefore, while the previous framework supported fractionation selection among predefined clinical options, it did not solve continuously for the DRX corresponding to a specified HyTEC-based adverse-effect level. Related work has explored SRS adverse-effect modelling and geometric predictors of intermediate dose spill; however, these studies focused on gamma knife-based SRS [12], modelled adverse effect using target surface area [13], [14], or derived prescriptions or feasibility criteria from selected adverse-effect-related IDVs [15], [16].

Our work addressed this gap by converting our prior discrete IDV models into a continuous prescription-selection framework. The aims of this study were to 1) model isodose volume as a continuous function of Vtarget and relative isodose level, and 2) solve the resulting equation for DRX corresponding to combinations of isodose volume-based adverse-effect estimates and fractionation schemes.

Materials and methods

Cohort and treatment planning

This retrospective study, which was conducted under an institutional review board protocol (IRB-080613002), used a previously described cohort of 3361 marginless GTV targets from 1495 treatment-approved HyperArc plans (Eclipse treatment planning system, Siemens Healthineers) in 1178 patients [11]. Detailed cohort selection, imaging, treatment planning, and data extraction are provided in Supplementary material A.

Modelling

RILs were defined using quotients of HyTEC brain Dadverse and commonly used SRS DRX, linking dose falloff directly to clinically meaningful adverse-effect metrics.

Dadversevalues were defined as 12 Gy for single-fraction, 19.6 Gy for 3-fraction, and 24.4 Gy for 5-fraction SRS [4]; these were combined with commonly used DRX (single fraction: 15, 16, 18, 20, and 24 Gy; 3 fractions: 24 and 27 Gy; 5 fractions: 25 and 30 Gy). Thus, IDVs for eight unique RILs were calculated for each target. For example, 60%=100·12Gy20Gy. RIL = 81.3% was used to model both 24.4 Gy /30 Gy and 19.6 Gy /24 Gy, and 100% IDVs were also calculated. Thus, 9 IDVs were calculated per Vtarget (50.0, 60.0, 66.7, 72.6, 75.0, 80.0, 81.3, 97.6, and 100.0%). The final analysis included 30,249 IDVs from 3361 clinically treated target volumes.

Power-law modelling was supported by the scale-dependent geometry of highly conformal SRS dose falloff, in which intermediate-dose volumes varied systematically with target size and surface-area-related dose spill [13], [14]. Consistent with this geometric basis, our prior automated SRS analysis demonstrated that IDV was precisely predicted by Vtarget using power-law relationships at discrete RILs, with Vtarget outperforming surface area and largest axial dimension as an IDV predictor [11]. Here, this framework was extended by treating the power-law coefficient (α) and exponent (β) as continuous functions of RIL.

Results

IDV was strongly predicted by Vtarget across the six training RILs, with power-law fit R2 values ranging from 0.98 to 0.99 (Fig. 1a), although modelling quality deteriorated for Vtarget ≤0.2 cm3. Among these 1561 targets (46.4%), predicted median/maximum 50% IDV were 0.37 cm3 / 1.16 cm3; the maximum observed value was 2.75 cm3. The fitted power-law coefficients decreased with increasing RIL and were well described by an exponential decay function, where a=20 and b=0.030 (R2 = 0.99). In contrast, the power-law exponents increased approximately linearly with RIL, where c=0.0030 and d=0.70 (R2 = 0.99). The predicted α and β values for the held-out RIL models are shown in Fig. 1b (closed circles). Predicted power-law models closely matched directly fitted models for the held-out data, with R2 ≥ 0.99 for 60.0, 72.6, and 81.3% RIL (Fig. 1c). Residual uncertainty was evaluated separately for each combination of held-out RIL (60.0, 72.6, and 81.3%) and model-predicted IDV (5, 10, and 20 cm3) using the 100 targets with predicted IDVs closest to the specified value. Across these nine evaluations, the median residual averaged 0.2%, with 25th–75th and 2.5th–97.5th percentile intervals of [−5.2,+7.0] % and [−12.8,+27.0] %, respectively. Discussion

This study developed a continuous closed-form framework for isoeffect DRX selection using 3361 Vtarget and 30,249 IDV from automated single-isocenter SRS plans. Vtarget strongly predicted IDV across the six training RIL, with R2 ≥ 0.98. Predicted models closely matched direct fits at held-out RIL, with R2 ≥ 0.98. Across held-out RILs, target-level IDV residuals had a median of 0.2%; the IQR and 95% interval were − 5.2% to +7.0% and − 12.8% to +27.0%, respectively.

Unlike prior discrete models [11], this framework directly estimated prescription dose for specified adverse-effect levels. Results reflect a single automated platform and institutional planning strategy; detailed planning procedures and prior sensitivity analyses are provided in Supplementary material B for reproducibility. Prior institutional data showed that expert manual VMAT produced median Paddick gradient indices 26.9% and 27.2% higher than HyperArc for two- and four-table-angle configurations, respectively [10], suggesting that manually generated plans may observe a 25–30% IDV increase at the 50% RIL. Clinics using different planning platforms or recipes should develop and validate local models before applying these prescription equations clinically.

The three held-out RILs represented internal interpolation using the same targets, institution, and planning approach rather than independent or prospective validation. Positive residuals indicatedIDVunderprediction; thus, the upper bound of the 95% residual interval could produce an over-escalated initial prescription, warranting post-optimization verification. Future multi-institutional work should investigate analogous models across manually planned volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) and other SRS delivery platforms to assess external validity and platform-specific differences. Although IDVwas systematically underpredicted for targets ≤0.2 cm3, the observed maximum remained below the lowest 5 cm3 HyTEC threshold, limiting the clinical impact of reduced accuracy in this subgroup. This framework explicitly modelled per-lesion adverse effects by attributing IDVs to individual targets without bridging at the 50% RIL. It therefore did not account for cumulative normal-brain dose from multiple lesions or targets with 50% RIL bridging, which may limit applicability in high-count multi-metastasis plans where aggregate low- and intermediate-dose exposure may contribute to adverse-effect risk not considered in HyTEC. The model did not distinguish between equal-volume targets with different shapes/surface areas, which may introduce uncertainty for highly irregular lesions. Although prior studies used surface area to model intermediate-dose spill or normal-brain dose [13], [14], [16], our previous analysis found that target volume alone predicted IDV more precisely than surface area alone in this cohort, indicating that Vtarget drives IDV more than target shape [11]; future models may determine whether combining both metrics further improves IDV prediction.

Several assumptions were inherent to this framework. First, IDVs were treated as total target-specific isodose volumes, including GTV volume and any non-brain tissue within the isodose surface. Second, adverse-effect was assumed to be attributable to individual targets when neighboring targets did not bridge at the 50% isodose level. These assumptions are consistent with HyTEC recommendations, which support analysis of total IDV (including target/non-brain) and summarize adverse-effects primarily using per-lesion dose-volume metrics [4]. Third, our cohort included clinically treated brain SRS plans across many disease histologies, consistent with the heterogeneous datasets underlying QUANTEC and HyTEC guidance. Furthermore, HyTEC dose-volume values were treated as discrete adverse-effects, and uncertainty in the published adverse-effect estimates was not considered in our analysis. The framework may have overestimated adverse-effects for targets near the skull, where the IDV includes non-brain tissue, and may have underestimated adverse-effects in patients with prior cranial irradiation, particularly prior whole-brain radiotherapy. Accordingly, these equations should support, not replace, physician judgment and patient-specific review.

This work provided a direct empirical framework for estimating isoeffect SRS DRX from Vtarget and HyTEC-based IDV limits. By converting discrete IDV prediction into continuous DRX estimation, the model enabled a priori dose escalation and fractionation selection for automated SRS plans generated with similar planning methods.
