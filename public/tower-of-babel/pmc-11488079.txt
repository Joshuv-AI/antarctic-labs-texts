Gene expression profile analysis of severe influenza-based modulation of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Jianping Kong, Liang Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is known severe influenza infections and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) disease might stimulate each other. Till now, no associated mechanism has been reported.MethodWe collected the genetic pattern of expression of severe influenza (GSE111368) and IPF (GSE70866) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Common differentially expressed genes (C-DEGs) were identified from the two datasets, and using this data, we conducted three forms of analyses, functional annotation, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and module construction, and hub gene identification and co-expression analysis.ResultsIn all, 174 C-DEGs were selected for additional analyses. Based on our functional analysis, these C-DEGs mediated inflammatory response and cell differentiation. Furthermore, using cytoHubba, we identified 15 genes, namely, MELK, HJURP, BIRC5, TPX2, TK1, CDT1, UBE2C, UHRF1, CCNA2, TYMS, CDCA5, CDCA8, RAD54L, CCNB2, and ITGAM, which served as hub genes to possibly contribute to severe influenza patients with IPF disease as comorbidity. The hub gene expressions were further confirmed using two stand-alone datasets (GSE101702 for severe influenza and GSE10667 for IPF).ConclusionHerein, we demonstrated the significance of common pathways and critical genes in severe influenza and IPF etiologies. The identified pathways and genes may be employed as possible therapeutic targets for future therapy against severe influenza patients with IPF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02107-9.

Background

Influenza is a highly contagious disease that affects the respiratory system. It is brought on by the influenza virus, which exhibits seasonal upticks and an occasional global pandemic [1]. Despite remarkable advancements in medicine, influenza is responsible for 3–5 million severe incidences and 290,000–650,000 deaths worldwide [2]. Among the critically ill patients, the most common presentations are respiratory failure (e.g., inspiration oxygen oxygen/fraction arterial pressure ≦ 100 mmHg) requiring invasive mechanic ventilation (IMV), and a mortality rate between 50 and 80% [3, 4].

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic and progressive lung disorder that severely impairs lung function [5]. The annual IPF incidence rate is between 3 and 20 per 100,000 people among inhabitants from Europe, America, and Asia [6]. IPF causes severe respiratory failure, and carries a survival range of 3–5 years from diagnosis [6]. The specifics of mechanism of fibrosis pathogenesis remains unknown. However, it is suspected that considerable lung tissue inflammation and injury may contribute to IPF [5, 7].

Previous research found that people with IPF are more likely to develop severe influenza following the infection. These patients are susceptible to respiratory failure and might experience fatal outcomes following the infection [8, 9]. Moreover, accelerated progression of lung fibrosis was noted in some IPF patients after recovering from severe influenza [10]. The relationship between viral infection and pulmonary fibrosis, usually influenced by tissue injury and lung inflammation, has been widely discussed in recent years [11, 12]. Unfortunately, there have been few reports on the detailed mechanism underlying the pathology of severe influenza interaction with IPF.

Analysis of common gene expression profiles (C-DEGs) between severe influenza and IPF may offer certain insight into their etiologies. Herein, we assessed two gene profile datasets from the GEO database. Using extensive bioinformatics and enrichment analyses, we established C-DEGs and their pathophysiological roles. Additionally, we generated a PPI network to evaluate gene modules and screen 15 hub genes using the STRING database and Cytoscape software. Furthermore, we identified transcription factors (TFs) associated with these genes, and further confirmed their expression. Our study design is provided in Fig. 1. Methods

Data collection

We retrieved the gene expression profiles of severe influenza and IPF patients from the NCBI-GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) website. The following patients were recruited for analysis: (i) datasets included information about patients with severe influenza or IPF, and healthy controls; (ii) influenza infection, identified via RT-PCR of respiratory tract-based RNA samples; while IPF was assessed via pathological identification of a typical interstitial pneumonia (UIP), not secondary to any other known disease (namely, autoimmune disease and sarcoidosis, and so on); (iii) patient age ≧ 16 years; (iv) severe influenza (defined as patients requiring invasive ventilation). In this investigation, we obtained data from two largest population datasets for severe influenza (GSE111368 and GSE101702) and IPF (GSE70866 and GSE10667), respectively. The GSE111368 whole-blood microarray data comprised 69 samples from severe influenza patients, and 130 samples from healthy volunteers. The GSE101702 whole-blood microarray data comprised 44 samples from severe influenza patients and 52 samples from healthy volunteers. The GSE70866 bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) cell microarray data comprised 112 samples from IPF patients and 20 samples from healthy volunteers. The GSE101706 lung tissue microarray data comprised 31 samples from IPF patients and 15 samples from health volunteers. The GSE111368 and GSE70866 datasets were employed for hub gene identification, and the GSE101702 and GSE10667 datasets were employed for hub gene expression verification.

Identification of DEGs

Training dataset normalization and probe annotation was conducted using the R (v. 4.2.3) ‘GEOquery’ and ‘limma’ packages (v. 3.52.4). DEGs identification employed criteria as follows: adjusted P < 0.05 and logFC > 0.5. A volcano plot was constructed, and the leading 50 DEGs were used to generate a heatmap. The C-DEGs from the GSE101702 and GSE24206 datasets were identified using the ‘VennDiagram’ package (v. 1.7.3).

Enrichment analyses of DEGs

The R ‘clusterprofiler’ package (v. 4.4.4) was employed for GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. FDR < 0.05 and an adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significant. GO terms were stratified as biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). The 10 leading enrichment terms are displayed.

PPI network generation and modular analysis

A PPI network with clear demonstration of complex modulatory associations was generated using Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING, version 11.5). An association was considered significant if the combined score was > 0.4. The network visualization was done using Cytoscape (version 3.9.1). Cytoscape’s plug-in molecular complex detection technology (MCODE) was employed for the major functional module analysis. The selection criteria were as follows: K-core = 2, degree threshold = 2, max depth = 100, and node score threshold = 0.2.

Identification and analysis of hub genes

The cytoHubba plug-in (Cytoscape) was used for hub gene identification. Subsequently, degree algorithms were employed for hub gene evaluation and selection. Next, a co-expression network was generated with these hub genes using GeneMANIA (http://www.genemania.org/).

Verification of hub gene contents in test datasets

To verify the transcript profile of recognized hub genes in the GSE111368 and GSE70866 datasets, we employed the t- or Wilcox test after evaluating distribution normality. P-value < 0.05 was set as the significance threshold.

Estimation of TFs

TF networks can be accurately predicted using the Transcriptional Regulatory Relationships Unraveled by Sentence-based Text mining (TRRUST) database (https://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/Network_search_form.php). It not only consists of TF-associated target genes, but also modulatory associations among TFs. TFs that control hub genes were identified using the aforementioned database, and adjusted P-value < 0.05 was set as the significance threshold.

Recognition of candidate drugs

Drug molecule identification is critical to disease research. Using the 15 hub genes for severe influenza and IPF, we designed a targeted drug molecule using the Drug Signatures database (DSigDB, https://amp.pharm.mssm.edu/Enrichr/) platform. Subsequently, we utilized Enrichr, an enrichment analysis platform with wide-ranging visualization details to assess the collective functions of the identified genes.

Discussion

Severe influenza is marked by massive inflammation and extensive tissue damage [3]. At present, there are increasing evidences that inflammation heavily contributes to disease genesis and IPF progression [5–8]. However, the mechanism(s) of involvement remains elusive. Based on our literature screening, this study is the first to explore the impact of severe influenza on IPF using gene expression profiles and bioinformatic analysis.

IPF is the result of varying degrees of lung injury, and it is closely correlated with inflammatory responses [13]. Alveolar inflammation is crucial for lung host defense amplification, and numerous forms of inflammatory cells modulate this response. Repeated injury of the alveolar epithelium triggers the early development of fibrosis. Hence, common pathophysiological process can serve as the basis of severe influenza complicated with IPF. Lung damage can occur from influenza virus direct infection or immune-based injury [5–8]. Following injury, lung undergoes inflammatory infiltration, whereby immune cells, such as, macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, and T cells accumulate at the injury site, and secrete excess pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic cytokines/factors, namely, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), interleukin (IL)-1, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-13, IL-17, and so on [13, 14]. The simultaneous attack from the virus and the aforementioned inflammatory factors produce a sustained and substantial lung damage, followed by repair and remodeling, which also accelerates pulmonary fibrosis. Herein, 174 C-DEGs were screened between severe influenza and IPF. Based on our functional enrichment analysis, these genes primarily modulated networks related to the inflammation response and cells differentiation in promoting the progression of lung fibrosis. However, the role of inflammation as a significant factor contributing to IPF etiology is controversial and it is sometimes seen as an epiphenomenon of fibrosis, which partly explains the findings of several clinical trials that anti-inflammatory medications were ineffective [7, 15].

Furthermore, we identified 15 common hub genes, namely, MELK, HJURP, BIRC5, TPX2, TK1, CDT1, UBE2C, UHRF1, CCNA2, TYMS, CDCA5, CDCA8, RAD54L, CCNB2, and ITGAM. Based on the GO/KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, these genes showed significant enrichment in the cell cycle and viral infection-related networks (e.g., TGF-β, JAK–STAT, MAPK networks, and so on). As previously reported, certain chemokines and cytokines possess the ability to not only directly repair and remodel the lung, but also produce and activate profibrotic proteins like TGF-β [15, 16]. Moreover, H1N1 and H5N1 infections markedly enhances TGF-β expression by macrophages and alveolar cells [17, 18]. Active TGF-β is a multifunctional cytokine that is synthesized within, and is associated with animal models of lung fibrosis. TGF-β displays elevated contents in the fibrotic lung diseases, such as, IPF [13–18]. Furthermore, TGF-β, in concert with connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), augments and sustains lung fibrosis via induction of the tissue fibroblast-based collagen expression and secretion [19]. In addition, TGF-β 1 induces pulmonary fibrosis by accelerating the differentiation of human lung fibroblasts (HLF) to myofibroblast, or via decreasing the vascular endothelial growth factor-D (VEGF-D) content in HLF via a MAPK-based network [20]. The JAK/STAT axis is activated by a myriad of profibrotic/pro-inflammatory cytokines, namely, IL-6, IL-11, IL-13, and so on, which are typically enhanced during severe influenza-based viral infection [21]. Emerging evidences suggest that the JAK/STAT axis modulates cellular fibrosis, namely, the actions of proliferation, senescence, autophagy, endoplasmic reticulum stress, or epithelial/fibroblast to mesenchymal transition [22].

CCNA2/CCNBN2 belong to the cyclin family. They serve as major cell cycle modulators, and they are regulated by TP53 [23]. During severe infections or sepsis, the CCNA2/CCNB2 levels are strongly enhanced, and this is directly associated with an enhanced mortality rate [24]. Prior investigations reported that CCNA2 is ubiquitous in the alveolar epithelium, but not the fibroblasts or myofibroblastic foci of IPF patients, particularly, during the acute exacerbations period [25]. This represents further injury, on top of the typical epithelial injury and dysregulation that marks IPF. CCNA2-expressing cells are also positive for the proliferation marker Ki-67, which suggests a proliferative response of the epithelium [25]. CCNA2 over-expression is also associated with liver cirrhosis pathogenesis [26]. In an in vitro investigation, TGF-β treatment was shown to strongly enhance CCNB2 activity. CCNB2 also interacts with the transforming growth factor beta R2 (TGF-β R2) [27]. Hence, it is likely that cyclin B2 plays a major role in TGF-β-mediated cell cycle regulation, as well as the modulation of progressive glomerular sclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis of diabetic nephropathic mice [28]. Ubiquitin-like containing PHD and RING finger domains 1 (UHRF1) are otherwise called ICB90 in humans [29]. Cheng et al. [30] reported that TGF-β1 upregulates UHRF1 and represses beclin 1, which ultimately activates fibroblast and lung fibrosis both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, UHRF1 depletion severely impairs fibroblast proliferation and reactivates beclin 1 in lung fibroblasts. Additionally, intravenous UHRF1 siRNA-loaded liposome administration provides excellent protection of mice against experimental pulmonary fibrosis. Similarly, CDCA8, BIRC5, and UBE2C are known to correlate with the endometrial fibrotic disease (intrauterine adhesion) development [31]. ITGAM, also called CD11b, is activated by TGF-β1 in vitro [32]. This leukocyte-specific integrin modulates cell activation as well as neutrophil and monocyte adhesion. In influenza virus-infected mice models, ITGAM is strongly elevated, and the CD11b+ macrophages and monocytes recruitment to the lung is intricately linked to aberrant repair and fibrosis [33]. In contrast, enhancing CD11b+ macrophage death effectively attenuates fibrosis development. The aforementioned cyclin transcriptional modulation is a result of an interplay between cell cycle-based periodic relief of transcriptional repression and TGF-β- and MARK-based signals, which are associated with IPF progression [34].

Furthermore, using the DSigDB database, we screened for potential drug compounds that target severe influenza and IPF. In mouse models, testosterone treatment protects against severe influenza by diminishing response to the pro-inflammatory cytokine (IL-1β) in the female murine lung, while reduced testosterone content negatively influences outcome of male mouse with influenza virus [35]. In male IPF patients, the testosterone levels are diminished, which inversely correlates with lung function and hypoxemia [36]. Given these evidences, testosterone can be a potential therapeutic target for severe influenza patients with IPF comorbidity. Prior investigation demonstrated that vitamin D (VitD) pre-treatment reduces responsiveness to the murine lung fibroblasts to pro-fibrosis stimuli, TGF-β1 [37]. IPF patients display serum VitD deficiency. VitD deficiency is associated with the predicted baseline forced vital capacity (FVC)%, predicted lung diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO)%, and all-cause mortality among IPF patients. When administered with calcitriol, both before and after administration, there are marked alleviation of acute lung injury among mice infected with the influenza A H5N1 virus [38]. Taken together, these data indicate that calcitriol is another potential agent for severe influenza with IPF.

This study encountered certain limitations. Firstly, being a retrospective examination, our findings require external verification. Secondly, despite selection of the largest datasets meeting our inclusion criteria, the sample population was still relatively small, which may have limited the accuracy of our data. Lastly, the hub gene functions require further validation in an in vitro model, which is the direction of our future investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, herein, we demonstrated that severe influenza and IPF have a certain degree of shared pathogenesis, modulated by specific hub genes and signaling networks associated with inflammation and the cell cycle. The genes identified in this study provide novel insights into the mechanism(s) underlying both severe influenza and IPF, and may potentially aid in the development of new targets for the treatment and prevention of severe influenza in patients with IPF disease comorbidity.
