Retrofitted conversion of a Maruti Alto gasoline engine to compressed natural gas for sustainable mobility
Authors: Rajesh Kumar Pandey, Shankar Kumar, Y C Sharma, Rajiv Varshney, Ashok Kumar Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetrofitting existing internal combustion engine vehicles to operate on cleaner fuels offers a practical pathway toward reducing urban emissions during the transition to sustainable mobility. This study experimentally investigates the feasibility of converting a 796 cc Maruti Alto multi-point fuel injection (MPFI) spark-ignition engine to operate exclusively on compressed natural gas (CNG) without petrol as a pilot fuel. The influence of iridium spark plug electrode gaps (0.5, 0.6, and 0.8 mm) on ignition stability, engine performance, and exhaust emissions was evaluated using a computerized test bench equipped with an eddy current dynamometer. Experimental results indicate that the 0.6 mm electrode gap provides the most stable combustion and minimum misfire tendency under lean CNG operation. Compared with conventional petrol operation, carbon monoxide (CO) and hydrocarbon (HC) emissions decreased by approximately 42% and 38%, respectively, while brake power declined by about 4–5% due to the lower energy density of gaseous fuel. ANOVA analysis confirmed the statistical significance of these variations (p < 0.05). The results demonstrate that optimized ignition parameters enable stable CNG-only operation in compact MPFI engines, supporting engine retrofitting as a cost-effective strategy for cleaner urban transportation.Keywords: CNG retrofitting, Maruti Alto engine, Sustainable mobility, IC engine conversion, Optimized spark, Emission reductionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Research on gaseous-fuel spark-ignition (SI) engines has been conducted for several decades. Early studies demonstrated that blending gaseous fuels such as hydrogen or producer gas with gasoline could improve thermal efficiency and significantly reduce carbon monoxide (CO) emissions1. Subsequent thermodynamic modeling studies investigated the effects of mixture preparation and ignition timing on dual-fuel combustion characteristics2. Experimental investigations on SI engines operating with liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) and CNG blends reported reductions in hydrocarbon (HC) emissions, although challenges related to knocking tendency, power reduction, and mixture stability were also observed3. More recent research has explored advanced ignition strategies and combustion control techniques to improve combustion stability and performance in gaseous-fuel engines4. Investigations involving compression ratio and spark timing adjustments further confirmed that accurate ignition calibration is essential for stable combustion and improved engine efficiency in gaseous-fuel SI engines5.

The environmental advantages of CNG compared with petrol have been widely reported. Because methane is the primary component of CNG and contains a higher hydrogen-to-carbon ratio, its combustion produces lower CO and HC emissions6,7. However, petrol engines typically generate slightly higher brake power because liquid fuels possess greater volumetric energy density and improved mixture formation characteristics8. Studies conducted on Indian passenger vehicles have shown that CNG operation can significantly reduce operating costs and improve urban air quality, although a slight reduction in engine performance may occur without proper calibration9. Advanced combustion visualization studies have demonstrated that ignition system modifications are necessary to compensate for the slower flame propagation characteristics of methane-based fuels7. Even in modern multi-point fuel injection (MPFI) engines, minor reductions in brake power are sometimes observed despite the emission advantages of CNG operation10–12.

Ignition system parameters play a critical role in optimizing combustion performance in gaseous-fuel engines. Several experimental investigations have shown that spark plug gap variation significantly influences flame kernel development, combustion stability, and emission formation in lean CNG mixtures13. In addition, advancing ignition timing can compensate for slower flame propagation and help maintain combustion near optimal crank angle positions8,14. Experimental studies using stroboscopic timing adjustments have demonstrated that optimized ignition strategies can reduce misfire occurrence and improve brake thermal efficiency in CNG engines15. Furthermore, investigations examining the combined effects of compression ratio and spark timing highlight the importance of accurate ignition calibration for achieving stable combustion and improved efficiency in gaseous-fuel engines5,16.

Although several studies have investigated the broader adoption of CNG vehicles in India17, most research has focused on policy, market trends, or large-capacity engines rather than experimental validation of ignition calibration, injector pulse width adjustments, and emission trade-offs in small passenger vehicles9,15,18,19. Comparative studies between petrol and CNG operation in MPFI engines demonstrate the potential for emission reductions20; however, systematic experimental investigations on compact vehicles such as the Maruti Alto remain limited. In addition, recent research has emphasized the importance of spark plug optimization and combustion stability when operating engines exclusively on gaseous fuels21, while broader mobility and energy transition analyses highlight the role of alternative fuels such as CNG in achieving sustainable transportation systems22,23. Despite extensive research on gaseous-fuel engines, limited experimental studies have examined ignition system optimization for dedicated CNG retrofits in compact MPFI passenger vehicles commonly used in India, such as the Maruti Alto.

Therefore, this study experimentally investigates the performance and emission characteristics of a Maruti Alto MPFI engine retrofitted for exclusive CNG operation, with particular emphasis on spark plug gap optimization and ignition calibration. The findings provide experimental insight that may support the development of cleaner and more efficient retrofit strategies for small passenger vehicles in the Indian automotive sector.

Methodology: vehicle and test setup

A test was conducted on a 796 cc, three-cylinder, spark-ignition Maruti Alto MPFI petrol engine, which is prevalent in the small passenger motor car market of India. This was selected because it is popular, does not take a lot of space, and it can be converted into alternative fuel systems.In this work, the base petrol engine under analysis was turned into a dedicated compressed natural gas (CNG) one. The conversion entailed the fitting of special CNG kit fitted with altered injectors, precise regulators and strengthened fuel lines. All these modifications guaranteed safe fuel delivery, stable pressure control, and the compliance of these systems with the combustion features of CNG, eradicating the necessity to use petrol as a pilot fuel.

Figure 1. shows the experimental test setup. The vehicle was connected to the Apex computerized test bench in order to assess the performance of the converted engine and also to assess emissions produced. Such an arrangement was able to measure performance and emissions in a controlled manner. The system was given automated data acquisition and accurate monitoring of the engine parameters. The SAJ eddy current dynamometer used variable loads and allowed the accurate measurement of brake power, torque and fuel consumption under varying working conditions. These tools were combined to make sure that the performance of the engine was in the conditions of the CNG-only operation was assessed thoroughly.

Experimental test bench.

Performance and emission measurements were conducted under steady-state conditions across an engine speed range of approximately 1500–5000 rpm using an eddy current dynamometer with incremental load application. Each operating point was maintained for approximately 5 min to allow stabilization of engine parameters before data recording. To ensure repeatability and minimize experimental uncertainty, each test condition was repeated three times, and the average values were used for analysis. The total duration of a complete experimental cycle, including stabilization and measurement across all load conditions, was approximately 60–75 min. These details have now been incorporated into the methodology section to improve clarity and reproducibility of the experimental procedure.

Ignition system modifications

The redesign of the Maruti Alto MPFI engine so that it could operate on CNG only proved to be sensitive to changes at the ignition system to incorporate the special combustion characteristics of methane. At a distance, unlike petrol, CNG spreads slower and its auto-ignition temperature is high and can affect the stability of combustion without countermeasures.

In order to eliminate these problems, iridium spark plugs and optimized spark plug gaps were chosen to assure reliable ignition during lean-burn conditions. A smaller gap was selected to enhance the intensity of the sparks, which boosts the growth of the flame kernel at a young age of combustion. Also, the ignition timing was brought forward compared to the original petrol settings. This change compensated the lower flame speed of CNG and enabled the optimal peak cylinder pressure to be achieved nearer to the desirable crank where the engine is most efficient. Dynamometer testing ensured that the engine was able to run at different loads and speeds under the modified ignition plan. This solution played a crucial role in ensuring steady combustion and reduction of misfires and also facilitated the objective of CNG operation without petrol dependence.

CNG conversion practical implication

For petrol-powered engines such as the Maruti Alto MPFI, the use of iridium spark plugs provides significant advantages when retrofitted for dedicated CNG operation. Their ability to maintain strong and stable spark discharge at smaller electrode gaps improves ignition reliability without the need for petrol as a pilot fuel. Compared with conventional copper spark plugs, iridium plugs offer higher ignition stability, longer service life, improved cold-start performance, and reduced emissions, making them more suitable for CNG combustion, which typically requires higher ignition energy. In contrast, copper plugs are less durable and may not consistently meet the ignition requirements of gaseous fuels. To eliminate the influence of electrode wear or prior usage on ignition performance, all experiments were conducted using new iridium spark plugs. Fuel delivery calibration

The fuel delivery system was recalibrated to accommodate the gaseous nature and lower calorific value of CNG compared with petrol. Because CNG requires a higher volumetric flow rate to achieve equivalent energy output, the injector pulse width was increased by approximately 12–18% relative to petrol operation. A pressure regulator maintained a constant fuel supply pressure of approximately 2 bar to ensure stable fuel delivery and prevent lean misfire or excessive enrichment.

The air–fuel ratio was maintained near stoichiometric conditions (λ = 1) using real-time feedback from the dynamometer test bench and exhaust gas analyzer. Ignition timing was advanced by 6°–8° crank angle relative to petrol operation to compensate for the slower flame propagation characteristics of methane. This timing was kept constant during spark plug gap experiments in order to isolate the influence of electrode gap on combustion behaviour.

The Apex computerized test bench enabled continuous monitoring of injector duty cycle, air–fuel ratio, and other engine parameters during calibration. These adjustments ensured accurate fuel metering and stable combustion during CNG operation.

Performance and emission testing

A systematic experimental protocol was developed to evaluate the performance and emission characteristics of the engine operating exclusively on CNG. Engine performance was assessed through measurements of brake power, torque, brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC), and exhaust gas temperature, while emission measurements included carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), and nitrogen oxides (NOx). Testing was conducted using an eddy current dynamometer capable of applying controlled loads over an engine speed range of 1500–5000 rpm. Each test condition was maintained under steady-state operation for approximately five minutes before recording measurements to ensure stable engine performance. To improve reliability, each operating condition was repeated three times, and average values were used for analysis.

Emission measurements were obtained using a five-gas AVL exhaust analyzer installed in the exhaust line. Conventional hydrocarbon analyzers measure total hydrocarbons and do not distinguish methane from other hydrocarbon species. Consequently, methane slip may be included within the measured HC values, representing a limitation of the present emission analysis. Statistical analysis revealed a positive correlation between BSFC and NOx emissions, reflecting the typical thermodynamic trade-off between combustion efficiency and emission formation. Higher combustion efficiency leads to increased peak combustion temperatures, which promote thermal NOx formation through the Zeldovich mechanism. Although NOx increased with improved combustion efficiency, knocking risk remained low because methane possesses a high octane number (> 120), providing strong resistance to auto-ignition.

Spark plug gap variation significantly influenced combustion stability. Although smaller spark gaps increase spark intensity, excessively small gaps reduce the exposed spark kernel volume and may limit early flame development. Under the tested conditions, a 0.6 mm spark plug gap provided the most stable combustion, while the 0.8 mm gap improved flame kernel propagation compared with the 0.5 mm gap, resulting in lower CO and HC emissions. During long-term operation, electrode erosion may gradually increase the spark gap. Based on typical wear characteristics of iridium spark plugs, the gap may increase by approximately 0.02–0.03 mm per 10,000 km. Therefore, periodic inspection of spark plugs at intervals of 15,000–20,000 km is recommended to maintain optimal ignition performance in dedicated CNG engines.

The integration of the eddy current dynamometer with the Apex computerized test bench enabled precise control of engine load and accurate measurement of performance and emission parameters. This experimental framework provided reliable data for evaluating the feasibility of dedicated CNG operation in compact passenger vehicles.

Results and discussion

The test on the Maruti Alto MPFI engine when driven on pure CNG gave a comprehensive record of the performance and emissions. The findings are tabulated into two parts (i) performance analysis, and (ii) emission analysis. Each finding is talked about in terms of the reference petrol operation, giving both benefits and disadvantages of the conversion.

Performance characteristics

The dynamometer tests indicated that there was a slight decrease in the brake power usually within the range of 5–7% as compared to petrol mode. This decrease is correlated with the decreased energy content of CNG which is less energy per unit volume. However, the engine was not crippled, meaning that operating exclusively on CNG does not have any adverse effect on the usability of small passenger cars. Emission characteristics

Emissions of carbon monoxide (CO) and unburned hydrocarbons (HC) were reduced by approximately 42% and 38%, respectively, under optimized CNG operation with a 0.6 mm iridium spark plug gap. These reductions reflect the cleaner combustion characteristics of methane compared with petrol.

Comparative insights

These findings have validated earlier research on CNG retrofits, and what is special about this piece of work is that it has obtained the results of exclusively operating on CNG without the need of petrol being used as a pilot fuel. This brings out the possibility of small passenger vehicles operating fully on CNG, thus minimizing the use of petrol and contributing to the quality of air in the urban areas.

Statistical analysis

Performance and emission parameters were studied using statistical analysis to determine the relationships between parameters.

(i)ANOVA results

ANOVA results

ANOVA was used to compare petrol and CNG functioning on the performance and emission parameters in the one-way test.

Brake Power: Significant difference (F = 18.72, p < 0.01).

BSFC: Significant difference (F = 12.45, p < 0.05).

CO Emissions: Highly significant reduction with CNG (F = 45.63, p < 0.001).

HC Emissions: Significant reduction with CNG (F = 28.14, p < 0.001).

NOx Emissions: Slight but significant increase (F = 6.92, p < 0.05).

(ii)Tukey Post-Hoc test

Tukey Post-Hoc test

Pairwise comparisons confirmed that:

Brake power and BSFC differences between petrol and CNG are significant at all tested speeds.

CO and HC emissions are consistently lower with CNG (p < 0.001).

NOx emissions show significant increases only at higher speeds (3500–4500 rpm).

(iii)Correlation matrix

Correlation matrix

The correlation matrix (Fig. 4) of CNG operation interprets that the Brake power has a negative relation with CO and HC, exhibiting a cleaner combustion at greater loads. There is a positive correlation of BSFC with CO and HC, which implies efficiency-emission trade-offs. NOx has a positive correlation with brake power, which is in line with increased temperature of combustion with increased loads.

The experimental findings are supported by the statistical analysis: the use of CNG exclusively reduces the amount of CO and HC emissions by a significant margin, but with trade-offs in the brake power and BSFC, which can be managed. The correlation table indicates how the performance and the emission parameters are interdependent. These results justify the feasibility of CNG-only operation in small passenger vehicles and support the claim in favor of its contribution to the green mobility.

Additionally, the findings demonstrate that close optimization of the spark plug gaps and ignition timing would help to limit the growth of NOx, and the emission reductions would not be at the expense of air quality. The performance-emission trade-offs gives the point that small units can retain driving and performance even switching to cleaner fuels, which has given the association to be used in wider application in the urban fleets. Such insights also form the basis of future studies on the durability testing, adaptive control measures and combination with hybrid systems to achieve the best environmental and performance benefits.

Optimizing the spark plug gap and ignition timing significantly enhances combustion stability and fuel efficiency while reducing CO and HC emissions in CNG-retrofitted vehicles. The use of iridium spark plugs with optimized gaps offers a scalable and cost-effective retrofitting solution that can be implemented without major engine modifications. These findings provide practical guidance for automotive workshops, engineers, and policymakers to improve the performance and environmental benefits of CNG conversion systems, thereby supporting cleaner urban transportation and contributing to India’s sustainable mobility goals.

In contrast, a 0.6 mm gap provides the best compromise between combustion stability, spark intensity, emission reduction, and durability.
