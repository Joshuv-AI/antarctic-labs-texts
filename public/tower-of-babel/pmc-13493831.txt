AdaptiveFrequencyNet: a long-term time series forecasting approach based on learnable frequency decomposition and cross-scale attention fusion
Authors: JiaoJiao Cui, Wei Li, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long-term time series forecasting depends on modeling variation across time scales together with persistent temporal dependence. Existing frequency decomposition methods partially address multi-scale modeling but suffer from fixed frequency decomposition, simplistic feature fusion, and insufficient cross-scale interaction. Fixed splits can also shift the predictive distribution away from the input scale, which hurts long-horizon consistency. We describe AdaptiveFrequencyNet (AFNet), an architecture that combines learnable multi-scale filter banks with cross-scale attention fusion. WAD applies depthwise convolutions with branch-wise softmax mixing (a learnable time-domain bank, not a discrete wavelet transform (DWT) or hand-crafted Fourier subband split). AMSFF couples self-attention, multi-scale convolutions, and gating. CSAF uses cross-attention for progressive mixing across scales. On six standard benchmarks under a fixed multivariate protocol (shared lookback), AFNet is compared with nine strong baselines, including recent Transformer-style models iTransformer and Pathformer under the same evaluation procedure. AFNet attains competitive test errors at a moderate parameter budget (Table 4). Outcomes are dataset- and horizon-dependent: among tabulated dataset averages, iTransformer reports the lowest MSE on Electricity (0.170) with AFNet at 0.174, whereas on ETTh1 average MSE for AFNet (0.443) is close to Pathformer (0.439). On Weather, Pathformer and FreTS average lower MSE than AFNet in the same table. The reported numbers should be read only under this protocol. AMSFF and CSAF use standard multi-head attention; each attention block scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(T^2)$$\end{document} in the processed sequence length T and is therefore not linear in T. Ablation experiments under the same protocol support the contribution of each module.

Introduction

Research background

Time series forecasting underpins applications in finance1,2, meteorology3,4, energy5,6, and transportation7,8. Neural forecasting models9 have improved markedly, yet long-horizon prediction remains difficult when short transients, slow drifts, and nonlinear couplings must be handled jointly.

Traditional Recurrent Neural Networks10 and Long Short-Term Memory networks11 perform well in short-term forecasting but suffer from issues such as gradient vanishing and information loss in long-term forecasting. The Transformer architecture12 partially addresses the long-range dependency problem through self-attention mechanisms, but directly applying Transformers to time series forecasting still has limitations such as high computational complexity and insufficient capture of local patterns.

Development of frequency decomposition methods

Frequency decomposition treats a series as superposed components at different rates. Autoformer13 and FEDformer14 exemplify long-horizon models that rely on fixed FFT splits and relatively shallow fusion, which makes it hard to adapt band boundaries to the dataset or to mix scales nonlinearly (Tables 1, 2, 3).

Research motivation and contributions

The main limitations of existing frequency decomposition methods are: (1) Fixed frequency decomposition: using fixed FFT cannot adaptively select important frequency components based on data characteristics; (2) Simple feature fusion: single convolutions and simple residual connections are insufficient to effectively capture complex temporal dependencies; (3) Limited cross-scale interaction: fixed interpolation and simple addition cannot achieve effective interaction between different frequency components.

Related Work

Time series forecasting methods

Time series forecasting methods have evolved from statistical methods to deep learning approaches. Early methods were mainly based on statistical models such as autoregressive models15, moving average models16, and autoregressive integrated moving average models17. While these methods have solid theoretical foundations, they have limitations in handling nonlinear relationships and long-term dependencies.

The rise of deep learning has brought considerable advances to time series forecasting. Recurrent Neural Networks and their variants, LSTM11 and GRU18, model sequential dependencies through recurrent structures and perform well in short-term forecasting. However, RNN-based methods suffer from issues such as gradient vanishing, training difficulties, and low computational efficiency.

The Transformer architecture12 enables parallel training and long-range modeling via self-attention and is now standard in NLP. Informer19 and Autoformer13 adapt Transformers to time series with sparse or frequency-aware variants. FEDformer14 adds explicit frequency attention. PatchTST20 tokenizes patches for long histories. Later designs include iTransformer21, Pathformer22, TimesNet23, and TimeMixer24. Section 4.2 links these lines of work to Table 2, where iTransformer and Pathformer appear as extra baselines under the AFNet protocol.

Frequency decomposition methods

Frequency decomposition is a classical tool in signal processing and has been reused extensively in deep forecasting25,26. Forecast-oriented variants map the series to components that emphasize different temporal scales; wavelets, empirical mode decompositions, and Fourier transforms are common building blocks.

In deep learning, frequency decomposition methods typically decompose a time series into multiple frequency components and then model each component separately. Autoformer13 proposes a series decomposition mechanism that decomposes a time series into trend and seasonal components. FEDformer14 introduces a frequency-domain attention mechanism to model time series in the frequency domain. FreTS25 transforms time series to the frequency domain and designs dual-scale frequency-domain MLPs (inter-series and intra-series) to explore the global view and energy compression characteristics of frequency components. FiLM26 combines the historical information retention capability of Legendre polynomials with the high-frequency noise filtering characteristics of Fourier transform, introduces low-rank approximation to reduce computational overhead, and achieves high-precision long-term time series forecasting in noisy scenarios.

Although frequency decomposition methods have achieved good results in long-term forecasting, they still have limitations: (1) Fixed FFT decomposition cannot be adaptively adjusted based on data characteristics; (2) Simple feature fusion struggles to capture complex temporal dependencies; (3) Fixed cross-scale fusion strategies limit the flexibility of information interaction.

Attention mechanisms in time series

Attention layers score pairwise time positions and are now standard in neural forecasting27,9. Self-attention improves long-range modeling relative to purely local operators but costs quadratic time in the sequence length, unlike fixed-depth convolutional stacks whose per-layer cost grows linearly with T. Multi-head variants run several attention maps in parallel.

In time series work, attention is used along time, in frequency bins, and across scales. CSAF belongs to the third group: cross-attention between components with learned fusion weights and a fixed low-to-high schedule instead of a fully symmetric attention graph.

Multi-scale feature learning

Multi-scale feature learning is standard in deep networks for encoding patterns at several temporal spans22. Convolutions, pooling pyramids, and multi-branch designs are common implementations.

Different time scales in time series correspond to different patterns: short-term scales capture local fluctuations and detailed information, while long-term scales capture trends and periodic patterns. The goal of multi-scale learning is to simultaneously model features at these different scales. Existing methods usually use fixed-scale convolution kernels or pooling windows, which may limit adaptability to heterogeneous data. The AMSFF module uses learnable multi-scale convolutions and weighted fusion to adjust emphasis across scales in a data-driven manner.

Weighted adaptive decomposition (WAD)

Terminology. WAD stands for Weighted Adaptive Decomposition. In practice it is a learnable multi-scale depthwise filter bank: K parallel convolutions per scale (shared kernel width at that scale) followed by softmax weights over branches, i.e. branch-wise reweighting of time-domain filter outputs. The module is not a discrete wavelet transform, not tied to a fixed orthonormal wavelet basis, and not a rigid Fourier subband partition, although the learned responses can emphasize complementary temporal structure similarly to adaptive filtering.

The WAD module builds multi-scale representations and propagates residual information from coarse to fine scales. It replaces a fixed FFT split with learnable temporal filtering. Adaptive multi-scale feature fusion

The Adaptive Multi-Scale Feature Fusion (AMSFF) block processes one frequency component at a time, mixing a self-attention path with parallel depthwise convolutions and a gate. Figure 7 gives the layout.Fig. 7Adaptive multi-scale feature fusion module architecture.

Adaptive multi-scale feature fusion module architecture.

First, we apply a two-layer MLP for channel mixing (with a residual connection and layer normalization), then capture long-range dependencies via multi-head self-attention. For each attention head h, we compute:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{E}_{head,h} = \text {Softmax}\left( \frac{\textbf{Q}_h \textbf{K}_h^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) \textbf{V}_h \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Q}_h$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{K}_h$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{V}_h$$\end{document} are projections of the channel-mixed features. The outputs of all heads are concatenated and projected to obtain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{E}_{attn}$$\end{document}.

To capture multi-scale local patterns, we use three depthwise temporal convolutions with kernel sizes (3, 5, 7) in parallel, corresponding to short-term, medium-term, and long-term local patterns, respectively.

Kernel set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{3,5,7\}$$\end{document} . Odd widths \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{3,5,7\}$$\end{document} give symmetric receptive fields at short, medium, and longer spans without a single very wide kernel. Softmax-normalized depthwise weights allocate mass across the three paths while keeping the convolutional branch light relative to self-attention. Other tuples may work on some benchmarks; we leave a broader search aside and record a small ETTh1 pilot in Appendix B.

Learnable weights are normalized with softmax for weighted fusion:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{E}_{temporal} = \sum _{k \in \{3,5,7\}} w_k \cdot \text {Conv1D}_k\left( \textbf{E}_{attn}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{w} = \text {Softmax}\left( \boldsymbol{\theta }_{scale}\right)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\theta }_{scale}$$\end{document} are learnable scale weight parameters.

Finally, we fuse the attention features and temporal convolution features using a gating mechanism:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{E}_{out} = \textbf{g} \odot \textbf{E}_{attn} + (1 - \textbf{g}) \odot \textbf{E}_{temporal} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{g} = \sigma \left( \text {MLP}_{gate}\left( \bar{\textbf{E}}_{combined}\right) \right)$$\end{document} is computed from the concatenated features.

Computational complexity

AMSFF uses the same multi-head self-attention operator as in Transformers12. With sequence length T, dot-product attention costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(T^2)$$\end{document} time and memory in standard implementations, so AMSFF is not linear in T. CSAF applies multi-head cross-attention between aligned scales and inherits the same \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(T^2)$$\end{document} scaling in the attended length. We therefore report both asymptotic order in T and empirical counts (parameters, MACs, and optional wall-clock under the profiler settings of Table 4). Total runtime also depends on the number of pyramid levels, FDFL layers, and heads; the full AFNet stack has no closed-form linear-time guarantee.

Across the table, AFNet matches or beats many baselines on individual dataset–horizon cells.

Aggregate error

AFNet achieves competitive MSE and MAE on many horizons (96–720) across the six datasets. On Electricity, its tabulated dataset-average MSE (0.174) is slightly above iTransformer (0.170) but below most other listed methods; on Weather it remains usable across horizons yet does not attain the lowest dataset-average MSE (Pathformer 0.239, FreTS 0.245 versus AFNet 0.257 in Table 2).

Long horizons

As the forecast horizon extends (e.g., to 720 steps), AFNet often degrades less than unstable baselines. On ETTh2, MSE rises from 0.292 to 0.437 for AFNet, whereas MICN moves from 0.372 to 0.824 under the same table.

Per-dataset averages

Dataset-average MSE across the four horizons reads as follows: ETTh1: AFNet achieves an average MSE of 0.443, competitive with several baselines in Table 2 (Pathformer 0.439; iTransformer 0.454) and lower than Dlinear (0.461) among the listed classical baselines. ETTh2: AFNet obtains an average MSE of 0.387; several baselines report lower averages (e.g., Pathformer at 0.344, iTransformer at 0.383), while AFNet remains competitive with PatchTST (0.391) and clearly stronger than unstable large-error baselines such as MICN on this split. Weather: AFNet records an average MSE of 0.257, which is competitive with several baselines but is not the best in Table 2; Pathformer and FreTS achieve lower average MSE values of 0.239 and 0.245, respectively. Electricity: AFNet averages 0.174, second only to iTransformer (0.170) among the methods in Table 2.

Cross-domain behaviour

Errors vary by domain, as expected for a single fixed architecture; we do not extrapolate beyond the six benchmarks.

Lookback fairness and comparison scope

Fixing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T{=}96$$\end{document} for all models in Table 2 gives every method the same history length on each split, which is the usual reporting convention for these benchmarks. Architectures differ in how much they gain from longer contexts; PatchTST, for instance, is often tuned with histories of 336 or 512 steps, where attention over patches behaves differently.

Scope. The table is one controlled snapshot, not a search over each competitor’s preferred lookback. Readers who need other histories should rerun the public code with matched splits. Appendix B mentions optional sensitivity work (including other T) for future releases.

We compare AFNet with PatchTST20, FEDFormer14, AutoFormer13, and MICN29 in parameter count and multiply-accumulate operations (MACs) for identical (T,F)=(96,96) and batch size 32. The goal is relative cost, not a claim of Pareto optimality.

Table 4 shows AFNet uses far fewer parameters and smaller MAC tallies than the listed Transformer and CNN baselines while occupying the same accuracy band as Table 2. On the six datasets the AFNet parameter count stays near \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.4\times 10^5$$\end{document}, roughly 6.9× smaller than the smallest PatchTST row (3.75M) and about 46.5× smaller than MICN (25.20M) on the ETT-style rows.

MACs are 4.419G on the four ETT datasets in the table, below the PatchTST/FEDformer/Autoformer/MICN entries on those rows. Weather and Electricity read 13.26G and 20.26G and should be read next to their higher input widths.

Relative to the same profiling setup, AFNet lowers MACs by roughly 25.5–93.8% depending on the dataset and competitor; the percentages are empirical estimates for the stated (T, F) and batch and will move if implementations or hardware change.

Conclusion

AFNet stacks learnable depthwise filter banks with cross-scale attention so that rigid FFT splits are not required. The three trainable blocks are WAD with branch-wise mixing, AMSFF with self-attention and gated multi-scale convolutions, and CSAF with a fixed low-to-high cross-attention pass.

Table 2 compares AFNet with nine baselines, including iTransformer and Pathformer rerun under the same protocol as AFNet (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T{=}96$$\end{document}, horizons \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{96,192,336,720\}$$\end{document}; official implementations22,21). On Electricity, iTransformer attains the lowest tabulated dataset-average MSE (0.170), with AFNet at 0.174 and a much smaller parameter footprint (Table 4), so the design trades a small average gap for compactness on this benchmark. On ETTh1, dataset-average MSE for AFNet (0.443) is close to Pathformer (0.439) and better than iTransformer (0.454) and DLinear (0.461). On ETTh2, Pathformer leads on the dataset average (0.344); AFNet (0.387) tracks iTransformer (0.383) and PatchTST (0.391) and degrades less toward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F{=}720$$\end{document} than unstable baselines such as MICN. On Weather, Pathformer and FreTS average lower MSE than AFNet (0.239 and 0.245 versus 0.257). Ablations in Table 3 show that removing WAD, AMSFF, or CSAF hurts accuracy under the same training recipe.
