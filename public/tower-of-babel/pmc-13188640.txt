Development and validation of the Health Belief Scale for Sarcopenia Prevention and Management (HBSPM): a pilot study among older adults in Guangdong, China
Authors: Jiamin Fang, Hao Liang, Jintao Zhong, Zhixia Jiang, Shilong Lu, Zhuyun Liu, Rui Chen, Ruotong Liao, Tao Zou, Qingyuan Su, Lin Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesThe prevalence of sarcopenia among older adults is rising annually, highlighting the importance of enhancing awareness and self-management in sarcopenia prevention as a critical public health priority. This study aimed to develop and validate the Health Belief Scale for Sarcopenia Prevention and Management (HBSPM) in older adults and to assess its reliability and validity.MethodsThis study was conducted in two phases. Guided by the Health Belief Model (HBM), the HBSPM was developed through a comprehensive literature review, expert consultation, and cognitive interviews. In May 2024, 701 older adults were recruited through stratified convenience sampling across from cities classified into three tiers according to their 2023 GDP levels in Guangdong Province. The validity of the HBSPM was assessed using item analysis, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).ResultsThe scale consists of 30 items across six dimensions: Susceptibility (5 items), Seriousness (6 items), Benefits (4 items), Barriers (5 items), Cues for Action (5 items), and Self-Efficacy (5 items). EFA revealed that these six factors accounted for 60.42% of the total variance. Factor loadings for the 30 items ranged from 0.615 to 0.759. The scale demonstrated good validity (χ²/df = 1.466, RMSEA = 0.045, TLI = 0.965, IFI = 0.961, NFI = 0.897, CFI = 0.965, GFI = 0.852, I-CVI > 0.86, S-CVI = 0.989) and reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.935).ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the HBSPM is an effective tool for measuring health belief regarding sarcopenia prevention and management among older adults and provides a robust foundation for targeted health education and behavioral interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02537-5.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Health belief, Older adults, Prevention, Management

Sarcopenia is an age-related geriatric syndrome characterized by the gradual loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and physical function [1]. Currently, an estimated 50 million people worldwide are affected by sarcopenia, and this number is projected to exceed 200 million by 2050 [2]. In China, the prevalence of sarcopenia among older adults is as high as 38.8% and increases with age, making China one of the countries with the highest prevalence worldwide [3]. Sarcopenia is associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes, such as falls, fractures, disability and even mortality in older adults [4–6]. Additionally, it contributes to the rising healthcare burden on healthcare systems and governments [7]. Consequently, sarcopenia has emerged as a significant public health challenge in aging societies [8, 9].

The development of sarcopenia is influenced by various factors, including physiological, disease-related, lifestyle, and psychosocial elements [10, 11]. Both the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) [12] and expert consensus [13, 14] emphasize the importance of raising health awareness among older adults to prevent sarcopenia progression. However, older adults currently pay insufficient attention to sarcopenia, showing low motivation for prevention and inadequate self-management capabilities [15, 16]. Research indicates that public awareness of sarcopenia is generally low to moderate [17, 18]. Additionally, a study conducted in the Netherlands found that even after being informed about treatment options, 29.7% of older patients were still unwilling to change their behavior to prevent sarcopenia [19]. These findings suggest that, while knowledge dissemination is essential, it alone may not suffice to facilitate behavioral change; deeper psychological motivations also play a critical role in influencing older adults’ willingness to engage in preventive practices.

In response to this issue, some scholars have attempted to develop assessment tools to enhance older adults’ understanding of sarcopenia-related knowledge and behaviors. For example, the Sarcopenia Knowledge Questionnaire developed by Lee et al. focuses on evaluating older adults’ understanding of sarcopenia-related concepts and risk factors [20]. Liu et al. designed the Sarcopenia Cognitive Behavior Questionnaire, which emphasizes cognitive and behavioral responses toward sarcopenia prevention in community-dwelling older adults [21]. Similarly, Huang et al. introduced a Knowledge-Attitude-Practice scale targeting sarcopenia prevention and control, primarily measuring factual knowledge, attitudes, and behavioral practices [22]. While existing instruments have contributed to identifying knowledge gaps and behavioral patterns related to sarcopenia, they lack systematic exploration of the motivational mechanisms underlying behavior change, particularly why individuals choose to adopt or forgo preventive measures.

Health belief, as core psychological determinants of health behavior, encompass individuals’ perceptions of disease susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits and barriers of action, self-efficacy, and cues to action [23]. The Health Belief Model (HBM), which integrates these constructs, has been widely employed to explain and predict the adoption of preventive health behaviors. It has demonstrated strong explanatory power and practical utility, especially in the contexts of aging populations and chronic disease management [24, 25]. The HBM framework not only offers a comprehensive understanding of the motivational factors but also provides a robust theoretical basis for designing targeted behavioral interventions.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate the Health Belief Scale for Sarcopenia Prevention and Management (HBSPM) based on the HBM framework. The goal is to systematically assess the health belief structures of older adults in China regarding sarcopenia prevention and management, thereby providing theoretical and empirical support for the design of precision-based intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a mixed-methods pilot study that employed both quantitative and qualitative approaches. We developed and validated a Chinese-language scale to measure the health belief of older adults regarding sarcopenia prevention and management. The development and validation of the HBSPM proceeded in two phases. In the first phase, a literature review, Delphi expert consultation, and cognitive interviews were performed to identify the attributes of health belief for sarcopenia prevention and management, and then generated a set of items. In the second phase, the psychometric properties of the scale were evaluated.

First phase: scale development

Formation of initial scale items

The scale was developed based on the HBM revised by Rosenstock in 1988, which includes six concepts: susceptibility, seriousness, benefits, barriers, cues for action, and self-efficacy [26]. From October to November 2023, we conducted a systematic literature review in five databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, CNKI, and WanFang. Search terms included “Health Belief Model”/“HBM,” “sarcopenia,” “prevention,” “management,” “elderly”/“older adults,” “scale,” and “guidelines.” The time frame was limited to 2016–2023, aligned with the year sarcopenia was officially classified as an independent disease in ICD-10-CM (M62.84) [27]. The search was performed by two nursing researchers with master’s degrees and formal training in evidence-based literature retrieval and psychometric instrument development. Three experts in sarcopenia (one PhD and two with master’s degrees, all actively involved in national or provincial research projects) reviewed the extracted items to ensure their clinical relevance and scientific rigor. A nursing graduate student with relevant training assisted in data organization and documentation. Based on the literature synthesis, the research team reviewed existing sarcopenia-related scales for older adults [21, 22], national and international guidelines [13, 14, 28–31], qualitative studies on perceptions and behaviors of older adults [16], and quantitative research on clinical outcomes of older adults with sarcopenia [4, 5, 6, 9, 32]. Health belief-based instruments for other chronic diseases were also consulted to guide item phrasing and structural design. The item development process involved several rounds of in-person and online expert discussions, integrating clinical insights and psychometric principles. This process yielded a preliminary item pool comprising six dimensions and 38 items.

Delphi expert consultation

Criteria for Expert Inclusion: (1) Having at least 10 years of professional experience in geriatric medicine, chronic disease management, or nursing education. (2) Demonstrating strong academic achievements in geriatrics and sarcopenia, evidenced by publishing at least two high-quality peer-reviewed articles or leading government-funded research projects. (3) Holding at least the rank of associate professor (or equivalent) or a doctoral degree. (4) Willingness to participate. In this study, informed consent was obtained from all experts, and their confidentiality was assured.

The consultation questionnaire consists of four parts: (1) Explanation of the study purpose, required tasks, and instructions. (2) General Information of the experts, included gender, age, education, title, research area, and work experience. (3) Each item was evaluated using two types of expert ratings: ① Item importance ratings, assessed using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = most unimportant to 5 = very important). Item retention was determined based on a mean score greater than 3.5 and a coefficient of variation (CV) less than 0.25, where CV was calculated as the standard deviation divided by the mean [33]. Additionally, Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) was calculated based on importance ratings to assess the degree of agreement among experts. ② Item relevance ratings, assessed using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant). These ratings were used to calculate the content validity indices, including the Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and the Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI). (4) Assessment of the expert’s familiarity with the content and the basis for their judgments. The familiarity coefficient (Cs) was determined through expert self-rating on a scale ranging from 0 (most unfamiliar) to 1 (very familiar). The judgment basis coefficient (Ca) was determined based on the sources upon which the expert’s evaluation was grounded, including theoretical knowledge, practical experience, peer-informed knowledge, and intuition, with a final score ranging from 0.1 to 0.5. Detailed scoring criteria were provided in the Supplementary Material Table S1-S4.

After each round, all the item importance ratings were compiled and analyzed. The scale dimensions and items were revised according to expert feedback. Questionnaires were distributed and collected by email, with each round of consultation lasting two weeks and a one-week interval between rounds.

Cognitive interviews

Through cognitive interviews, unclear items were identified and revised to enhance the clarity and accuracy of the scale. The interviews were conducted at the Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, located in Yuexiu District, Guangzhou—a nationally recognized clinical research center with a high outpatient volume and a patient population drawn from diverse regions and cultural backgrounds. Purposive sampling was employed to ensure heterogeneity among older adults in terms of gender, age, and educational background. This approach aimed to capture a broad range of cognitive perspectives, enhancing the cultural adaptability and applicability of the scale. Cognitive interviews required 5 to 15 participants per round, with a minimum of three rounds. Interviews were discontinued once data saturation was achieved [34].

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Age between 60 and 85 years, regardless of gender. (2) Ability to communicate effectively. (3) Provision of signed informed consent and voluntary agreement to participate in the study. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Presence of severe primary diseases. (2) Severe cognitive or mental disorders, or inability to cooperate with the survey for other reasons.

This study followed the recommendations of Willis by adopting the think-aloud method for interviews and developing the following interview outline: [34] (1) What did you think of the overall design and length of the scale? (2) Why didn’t you respond to this item? / Why did you change the answer to this question? / Why did you hesitate to answer this question? (3) Please describe the meaning of the item in your own words. (4) What exactly did you say when you answered this question? (5) Did you feel uncomfortable about any of the questions in the filling process? (6) Do you have any other suggestions for improving the scale? Based on the interview findings, the items were revised and adjusted to form the first draft of the test version of the scale. (Table 1)

Second phase: scale testing

Participants and samples

From May to July 2024, the 21 cities in Guangdong Province were categorized into three tiers based on their Gross Domestic Product (GDP) levels in 2023 [35]. Using a convenience sampling method, 1–2 cities were selected from each tier, and one tertiary hospital was chosen for data collection in each selected city. The first tier represented high-GDP cities, including Guangzhou and Shenzhen; the second tier included medium-GDP cities, including Zhongshan; and the third tier comprised low-GDP cities, including Shaoguan and Yunfu. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were consistent with those used in the cognitive interviews. The sample size was calculated as 5 to 10 times the number of items in the scale, accounting for a 10% invalid response rate; therefore, a minimum of 237 valid responses was required. To ensure the reliability of both EFA and CFA results, the sample size for each analysis was required to exceed 200 participants [36]. Accordingly, a sample size of 200 participants per tier was deemed sufficient to meet the requirements.

Measures

The questionnaire consisted of three parts: (1) General Information: It was developed by researchers through a comprehensive review of relevant literature and team discussions. It encompassed various factors, including city (stratified by GDP level), sex, age, education level, pre-retirement occupation, personal income, marital status, and living situation. (2) Sarcopenia Screening Tool (SARC-CalF): Developed by Barbosa-Silva et al. in 2016, the SARC-CalF combines the SARC-F scale with calf circumference, which is correlated with muscle mass, to create a sarcopenia screening tool [37, 38]. The first five items were identical to those on the SARC-F scale. For calf circumference, a measurement greater than 34 cm in men and greater than 33 cm in women scores 0 points, while measurements less than these thresholds score 10 points. The total score ranges from 0 to 20, with a score greater than 11 indicating a positive screening result for sarcopenia. (3) HBSPM: Uses a Likert 5-point scale ranging from “Not Important” to “Very Important,” with scores from 1 to 5. In the barriers dimension, reverse scoring is applied. A higher score indicates a better level of health belief.

All questionnaires were collected face-to-face by two trained postgraduate nursing students, Jiamin Fang and Zhixia Jiang. Standardized instructions were provided to participants to ensure consistency. If a participant was unable to complete the questionnaire independently, the researchers read each question and response option aloud and recorded the participant’s verbal answers. After collection, the completed questionnaires were immediately reviewed for missing or erroneous entries and clarified when necessary. Two other team members, Ruotong Liao and Qingyuan Su, independently reviewed all responses and excluded incomplete, duplicate, or logically inconsistent questionnaires to ensure data quality. Data were entered using EpiData 3.1 software by Jintao Zhong and Shilong Lu, with double-entry verification to ensure accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 24.0, NVivo 11.0, and AMOS 26.0. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical data are expressed as frequency (percentage). Group differences among the three subsamples were assessed using the Chi-square test for categorical variables.

A total of 725 questionnaires were distributed, with 701 valid responses received, resulting in an effective response rate of 96.69%. Missing data were examined using Little’s MCAR test based on the EM algorithm in SPSS. The results indicated that the missing data were consistent with the assumption of Missing Completely at Random (χ² = 735.413, df = 920, p = 1.000). Given the low proportion of missing data (3.31%), listwise deletion was applied in the final analysis. To further assess potential selection bias, baseline characteristics were compared between participants included in the final analysis (n = 701) and those excluded due to incomplete or logically inconsistent responses (n = 24). No statistically significant differences were observed across demographic or health-related variables (p > 0.05). See Supplementary Material Table S5.

Delphi expert consultation

The authority of the experts was evaluated using the expert authority coefficient (Cr), which was calculated as the mean of the familiarity coefficient (Cs) and the judgment basis coefficient (Ca): Cr = (Cs + Ca)/2 [39]. The degree of consensus among experts was assessed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [40]. To assess content validity, the I-CVI and the S-CVI were calculated based on expert relevance ratings. The I-CVI was defined as the proportion of experts who rated each item as relevant, with a score of 3 or 4 on a 4-point Likert scale. The S-CVI was calculated as the average of the I-CVI values across all items in the scale. The scale was considered to have good content validity if I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI ≥ 0.9 [41].

Item analysis

Items were screened using four methods: dispersion trend analysis, critical ratio analysis, correlation coefficient analysis, and internal consistency testing. An item was considered for deletion if it failed to meet the criteria in at least two of these methods. The specific criteria were as follows: (1) Discrete Trend Analysis: Items with a standard deviation (SD) less than 0.75 were considered to have low variability and were removed [42]. (2) Critical Ratio (CR) Analysis: Based on total scores, the top and bottom 27% of participants were compared using independent-samples t-tests. Items with CR < 3 or p > 0.05 were deleted due to poor discriminatory ability [43]. (3) Correlation Coefficient Analysis: Pearson correlation coefficients between each item and the total score were calculated. Items with r ≤ 0.30 or p > 0.05 were considered weakly correlated and removed [44]. (4) Internal Consistency Testing: If the Cronbach’s α of a subscale increased significantly after an item was removed, the item was considered to reduce internal consistency and was deleted.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

It used principal axis factoring and varimax rotation. Common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, with the requirement that each factor includes at least three items. Item loadings on their respective factors had to be greater than 0.4. Items with factor loadings less than 0.4 or with substantial cross-loadings were considered for removal. Cross-loading was defined as having loadings ≥ 0.4 on two or more factors with a difference between loadings less than 0.2 [45].

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

The following fit indices were calculated using AMOS26.0, including chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), goodness of fit index (GFI), incremental fit index (IFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI). A model is considered to have a good fit if χ²/df < 3, RMSEA < 0.08, and GFI, NFI, TLI, IFI and CFI > 0.80 [46]. In addition, convergent validity and discriminant validity were calculated.

Reliability testing

Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess internal consistency for the scale and its dimensions. A Cronbach’s alpha value > 0.7 indicates good reliability of the scale (the minimum should not fall below 0.6) [47].

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup comparisons of total HBM score were conducted across selected demographic variables, including sex, city tier (based on GDP classification), educational level, personal income, living situation, and sarcopenia status. Normality assumptions were assessed prior to analysis. For variables meeting normality, independent samples t-tests or one-way ANOVA were applied as appropriate. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significance.

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (ZE2024-092-01). All experts and participants provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in this study.

Result

First phase: scale development

Results of delphi expert consultation

From November 2023 to January 2024, three rounds of Delphi expert consultations were conducted with a panel of 15 experts from Beijing, Guangdong, Shanxi, Shandong, Henan, Hebei, Guizhou, Xinjiang, and the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region. The panel included one chief physician of geriatrics, two experts in chronic disease management, six geriatric nursing specialists, two nursing education experts, and four clinical nursing professionals. The experts had an average age of 49.8 ± 8.04 years and an average of 26.53 ± 9.07 years of professional experience. In terms of academic qualifications, three held doctoral degrees, nine held master’s degrees, and three held bachelor’s degrees. Ten experts held senior professional titles, while five held associate senior titles. The Ca, Cs and Cr values all exceeded 0.8, indicating a high level of expert authority [48]. The Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) was 0.142, and the coefficient of variation (CV) ranged from 0 to 0.22, reflecting good consistency and consensus among expert opinions for each item [33]. The I-CVI for each item exceeded 0.86, and the S-CVI was 0.989, both higher than the reference values of 0.78 and 0.9, respectively, indicating good content validity [41]. After three rounds of expert consultations, the revised scale comprised 6 dimensions and 42 items.

Result of cognitive interviews

From April to May 2024, 25 older community members from Yuexiu District, Guangzhou, Guangdong Province, were selected for three rounds of cognitive interviews using purposive sampling. Among the 25 participants, 13 were male and 12 were female, with an average age of 70.88 ± 7.13 years. In the first round of interviews, 9 items with issues were revised; in the second round, 1 item was revised; and in the third round, all participants had no objections to the scale items, so the interviews were concluded. After discussions by the research team, the final scale was developed, consisting of 6 dimensions and 43 items. Specific revisions and their reasons can be found in Supplementary Material Table S6, S7.

Item analysis with subsample 1

A total of 233 participants were subjected to item analysis. (1) Dispersion Trend Method: Item B1 had SD < 0.75. (2) Critical Ratio Method: Items D1, D2, and D7 had CR < 3 and P > 0.05. (3) Correlation Coefficient Method: Items D1, D2, D7, D8, and D9 had correlation coefficients ≤ 0.3. (4) Internal Consistency Testing: The deletion of items D1, D2, D7, and D9 resulted in increased Cronbach’s α values for the scale. Items that met the deletion criteria in at least two of the four methods were removed. As a result, D1, D2, D7, and D9 were deleted, yielding a final scale of 39 items.

Construct validity

A total of 468 patients were evenly and randomly divided into two groups, A and B, based on three tiers of cities. Each group included 39 older adults from Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Shaoguan, and Yunfu, and 78 older adults from Zhongshan. Group A (N = 234) was used for EFA, and Group B (N = 234) for CFA.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with subsample 2

Principal component analysis with oblique rotation was conducted in three rounds. In the first round, the Bartlett’s test of sphericity showed χ² = 3979.748 (P < 0.001), and the KMO value was 0.924, indicating that the scale was suitable for factor analysis [49]. A total of six factors with eigenvalues > 1 were extracted, and the cumulative variance contribution rate was 60.42% (Table 3). Items A4、C6、E4 and E7, which had loadings < 0.4 for all factors, and items C5, which exhibited cross-loadings below 0.2, were removed. In the second round, items B2 and C7, with loadings < 0.4, and items B1 and C2, which were single-item factors with insufficient reliability and interpretability, were also removed. The remaining 30 items were subjected to a third round of factor analysis, resulting in six factors with a cumulative variance contribution rate of 60.42%. Bartlett’s test showed χ²=3979.748 (P < 0.001), and the KMO value was 0.924. The scree plot also indicated that six factors were appropriate (Fig. 1), with factor loadings ranging from 0.615 to 0.759. The final categorization was tested in CFA to verify its validity. The final items were categorized according to the HBM into six dimensions: susceptibility (5 items), seriousness (6 items), benefits (4 items), barriers (5 items), cues for action (5 items), and self-efficacy (5 items) (Table 4).

Model fit was assessed through CFA, showing χ²/df = 1.466, RMSEA = 0.045, TLI = 0.965, IFI = 0.961, NFI = 0.897, CFI = 0.965 and GFI = 0.852, indicating a good fit for the six-factor model (Fig. 2). The convergent validity analysis revealed that the square root of the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for the six factors ranged from 0.782 to 0.825, while the Composite Reliability (CR) values ranged from 0.881 to 0.918. Typically, AVE values above 0.36 and CR values above 0.70 are considered sufficient [50]. In terms of discriminant validity, the correlation coefficients among the six dimensions were all below the standard threshold of 0.85, indicating no multicollinearity within the scale [51]. Additionally, the square root of the AVE for each dimension exceeded the correlation coefficients between that dimension and others, demonstrating that the factors are related yet distinct from one another (Table 5).

Final version of the HBSPM

The finalized Health Belief Scale for the Prevention and Management of Sarcopenia in the Elderly (HBSPM) consists of 30 items across six dimensions: susceptibility (5 items), seriousness (6 items), benefits (4 items), barriers (5 items), cues to action (5 items), and self-efficacy (5 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Not Important”) to 5 (“Very Important”). For the perceived barriers dimension, reverse scoring is applied to ensure that higher scores consistently reflect stronger or more positive health belief across all subscales. The total scale score is calculated by summing the item scores, with a higher total score indicating a more favorable overall health belief profile regarding sarcopenia prevention and management. Subscale scores can also be calculated independently to reflect specific belief domains. For practical application in clinical and research settings, the total score of the scale is recommended to be interpreted using quartile-based categories: scores ranging from 30 to 60 indicate a low level of health belief, 60 to 90 indicate a low-to-medium level, 90 to 120 indicate a moderate level, and 120 to 150 indicate a high level. The scale may be used to identify individuals with low health belief, inform the design of targeted health education interventions, and evaluate behavioral change readiness.

Discussion

The current study developed and preliminarily validated the HBSPM scale based on the HBM theoretical framework. To our knowledge, this is the first instrument specifically designed to assess health belief regarding sarcopenia prevention and management among older adults. The initial questionnaire comprised 43 items and required 5–8 min to complete. All 701 participants completed the questionnaire, demonstrating strong feasibility. Following psychometric evaluations, including EFA and CFA conducted on two randomly split subsamples from different cities, the scale showed good reliability, content validity, and structural validity. The final version of the scale consists of 30 items across six dimensions, comparable in length to other health belief scales [44, 52]. This instrument can identify key factors that influence health behaviors among older adults, providing a foundation for targeted health education and intervention strategies.

The HBSPM scale encompasses six dimensions, offering a comprehensive framework for assessing cognitive aspects of health belief. It evaluates not only knowledge of sarcopenia but also cognition related to preventive health behaviors. While previous research developed the Sarcopenia Knowledge Questionnaire to assess older adults’ understanding of sarcopenia diagnosis, outcomes, and causes [20], the HBSPM extends this approach by incorporating dimensions that address preventive behaviors. In particular, the benefits dimension reflects awareness of health behaviors that can prevent sarcopenia, whereas the barriers dimension captures perceived obstacles to adopting these behaviors. Assessing cognition related to preventive actions provides insight into older adults’ understanding of effective preventive measures and the factors that may impede adherence, forming a basis for tailored intervention strategies.

In addition to cognitive assessment, the HBSPM scale includes dimensions of cues for action and self-efficacy. Self-efficacy reflects older adults’ confidence in their ability to prevent and manage sarcopenia, while cues for action identify key factors that prompt older adults to engage in health behaviors [53]. Both are essential motivational factors for adopting healthy behaviors [54]. Compared to previous studies, such as Liu’s development of the Sarcopenia Cognitive Behavior Questionnaire for older adults in the community [21], and Huang’s construction of the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice Scale for Sarcopenia Prevention and Control for Older Adults in the Community [22], the HBSPM provides a more comprehensive assessment of the motivational factors influencing preventive behaviors, even though it does not directly measure health behaviors. By thoroughly evaluating older adults’ beliefs and cognition related to sarcopenia, the HBSPM can indirectly predict their health behaviors. Furthermore, to more accurately assess health behaviors related to sarcopenia prevention, our research team is developing a scale specifically designed to measure these behaviors among older adults.

The Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia, along with various expert consensus statements [12–14], has stressed the importance of promoting proactive health management among older adults, urging them to actively engage in the prevention and management of sarcopenia. Despite numerous intervention studies aimed at improving sarcopenia-related health outcomes and encouraging healthier behaviors among older adults, it is evident that their knowledge and attitudes toward sarcopenia remain insufficient. This gap in awareness directly affects adherence to preventive and therapeutic measures. Therefore, investigating health belief as a key underlying factor and identifying the barriers that hinder older adults from adopting preventive and management behaviors for sarcopenia have become a pressing challenge. The HBSPM scale has important clinical and public health implications. By quantifying older individuals’ beliefs about sarcopenia prevention and management across multiple dimensions, it identifies factors that contribute to poor awareness and attitudes. This tool allows for the identification of individuals with lower scores, enabling targeted health education and interventions focused on key behavioral factors. Consequently, it enhances the effectiveness of preventive and management strategies for sarcopenia, potentially improving overall health outcomes and quality of life for older adults.

Subgroup analyses revealed that city tier, education level, and personal income significantly influenced total health belief score among older adults. Higher education was associated with stronger health belief, in line with findings by Wang et al. [18], likely due to improved access to and comprehension of health information, which enhances disease awareness and intention to act [55]. Similarly, individuals living in more developed cities or with higher incomes may benefit from better healthcare resources and health literacy, leading to stronger preventive attitudes [56]. The absence of a significant association between living arrangement and health belief score may be attributable to the small number of participants living alone, which could have reduced the statistical power to detect meaningful differences. Considering their heightened risk of social isolation and poorer access to health information, older adults living alone warrant further attention in both future research and targeted health interventions [57]. Individuals with sarcopenia had slightly higher scores than those without, though the difference was not statistically significant. This suggests that a diagnosis of sarcopenia alone does not significantly enhance health motivation. The underlying cause may be the low level of awareness among older adults regarding sarcopenia as a latent condition [17, 18]. Most individuals focus on age-related muscle loss and decreased strength, but are unaware that sarcopenia can be prevented and managed. Therefore, targeted health education and intervention strategies are essential to enhance health motivation and facilitate early prevention and management of sarcopenia in older adults.

This study has several strengths. First, stratified convenient sampling was employed, considering the varying GDP levels across cities in Guangdong Province, thereby enhancing the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings. Second, cognitive interviews were conducted during the preliminary phase to minimize differences in item interpretation and measurement errors among older adults. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample was drawn exclusively from older adults in Guangdong Province. Although this region is economically and culturally diverse, the findings may not be fully generalizable to populations from other regions or cultural backgrounds. Therefore, future research should undertake rigorous cross-cultural adaptation and linguistic validation of the HBSPM to ensure its applicability across different sociocultural contexts and international settings. Second, although a preliminary score interpretation based on quartile ranges was proposed to facilitate practical use, clinically validated cutoff values have not yet been established. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples are needed to further determine meaningful threshold values and evaluate their utility in screening and intervention. Third, while the HBSPM includes the basic dimensions of health belief, it does not account for other factors that may affect sarcopenia prevention behaviors, such as environmental factors or individual capabilities. Future studies could explore the integration of additional models to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of sarcopenia prevention and management.

Conclusion

This study is the first to develop a scale for assessing health belief related to sarcopenia prevention and management in older adults based on the HBM. The HBSPM scale consists of six dimensions and 30 items, including susceptibility, seriousness, benefits, barriers, cues for action, and self-efficacy. It comprehensively assesses the motivational factors influencing older individuals’ preventive behaviors related to sarcopenia. The findings demonstrate acceptable reliability, content validity, and structural validity, Additionally, the brevity of this new scale enhances its practicality for use with older adults. Nevertheless, the scale requires further refinement and enhancement.
