Selective subperineurial dissection in vestibular schwannoma surgery: how I do it
Authors: Taichi Sayanagi, Ryota Tamura, Takayuki Ohira, Masahiro Toda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe dissection plane in vestibular schwannoma surgery significantly influences facial and cochlear nerve outcomes. Although histological studies demonstrate that the "tumor capsule" represents vestibular nerve perineurium, epicapsular dissection along its outer surface remains prevalent in clinical practice.MethodWe describe subperineurial dissection, removing tumor from beneath the perineurium while preserving this protective layer on the facial and cochlear nerves. Key steps include early internal auditory canal drilling and identification of the true perineurial plane.ConclusionSubperineurial dissection prioritizes nerve function over radical resection, achieving high rates of facial nerve preservation with acceptable tumor control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00701-026-06919-y.

Relevant surgical anatomy

The "tumor capsule" of vestibular schwannoma is histologically the vestibular nerve perineurium containing residual nerve fibers, not a true reactive fibrous capsule [4]. There is often no distinct interface between tumor parenchyma and these nerve fibers. The perineurium serves as a protective barrier that maintains the endoneurial environment and supports axonal function. Attempting extracapsular dissection risks stripping nerve elements or compromising their vascular supply, potentially leading to permanent functional deficits. Nevertheless, epicapsular dissection along the outer surface of the capsule remains a widely practiced approach in clinical settings [1, 3].

Three distinct layers exist in the surgical field: (1) Arachnoid membrane (outermost), which is avascular and can be safely opened; (2) Tumor capsule/perineurium (middle layer), which contains the vasa nervorum and should be preserved; (3) Tumor parenchyma (innermost), which represents the actual neoplastic tissue to be removed.

The "subperineurial" plane lies between tumor parenchyma and perineurium. Establishing this plane allows tumor removal while leaving the nerve-invested capsule attached to the facial and cochlear nerves, protecting functional fascicles from direct mechanical trauma and preserving their blood supply.

Description of the technique

Step 1: Exposure and membrane identification

Step 2: Meatal dissection (early)

Early drilling of the internal auditory canal is advocated, as this region offers an optimal site to identify the true perineurial plane. The posterior IAC wall is drilled using a diamond burr or ultrasonic bone curette (Sonopet) with constant irrigation to prevent thermal injury. At the porus acusticus, the tumor capsule (superficial perineurium) is incised longitudinally, and tumor debulking is initiated from within (Fig. 2, a-c). Step 3: Developing the subperineurial plane

A dissection plane is developed deep to the capsule using fine dissectors and gentle suction (Fig. 2d, e). Unlike extracapsular dissection, the tumor bulk is peeled out from within the capsule, leaving it attached to the facial and cochlear nerves [2, 6]. Step 4: Internal debulking and nerve dissection

Internal debulking with ultrasonic aspirator or tumor forceps collapses the tumor inward, reducing tension on neurovascular structures (Fig. 2e, f). Working circumferentially from the decompressed center, the surgeon separates the tumor shell in the subperineurial plane. When encountering the facial nerve, typically at the anterior–superior aspect of the tumor, dissection remains strictly subperineurial, leaving the capsule on the nerve. Continuous facial nerve monitoring with electromyography provides real-time feedback.

If tumor is densely adherent to neural structures, it is safer to leave a thicker remnant (near-total resection) rather than risking traction injury. The decision to leave residual tumor should be documented with intraoperative photographs. Intraoperative monitoring of facial nerve compound muscle action potentials, along with adherence of the tumor to the facial nerve, guides the decision-making process (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Flowchart depicting the key decision points and surgical steps for selective subperineurial dissection. Step 5: Closure

Meticulous hemostasis is achieved with sparing bipolar coagulation at low settings, carefully avoiding the vasa nervorum on preserved neural structures. All opened mastoid air cells are sealed with bone wax, muscle plugs, and fibrin glue to prevent CSF rhinorrhea. Watertight dural closure is essential, supplemented with dural sealant if needed. The bone flap is replaced and secured.

Indications

Severe tumor-nerve adherence where traditional extracapsular dissection carries high risk of nerve injury.

Priority on facial nerve function preservation (House-Brackmann grade I-II preoperatively).

Hearing preservation surgery in small-medium tumors less than 30 mm in maximum diameter.

NF2-related schwannomatosis: Given the difficulty of long-term hearing preservation in bilateral tumors, the primary goal shifts to facial nerve protection while maintaining cochlear nerve integrity for potential cochlear implantation [5].

Elderly patients or those with significant medical comorbidities: planned subtotal resection with possible adjuvant stereotactic radiosurgery for residual tumor.

Limitations

Giant tumors exceeding 30 mm: The subperineurial plane may be significantly distorted or completely obliterated by tumor expansion and chronic compression. In such cases, the traditional extracapsular approach may be necessary.

Oncological considerations: If true perineurium is preserved without any adherent tumor cells, recurrence risk is equivalent to gross total resection. However, if a thin tumor film remains on the preserved capsule, regrowth risk increases and requires careful long-term surveillance with serial MRI imaging.

Calcified or cystic tumors: Heterogeneous tumor consistency with calcification or multiple cystic components can obscure the natural cleavage plane and make subperineurial dissection technically challenging.

Hearing preservation: While theoretically advantageous for cochlear nerve function, hearing preservation is not guaranteed and depends on multiple factors including preoperative hearing status, tumor size, and cochlear nerve integrity.

How to avoid complications

Facial nerve injury: Use careful blunt dissection and sharp micro-dissection techniques in combination. If tumor is densely adherent to the facial nerve, intentionally leave the capsule attached. The principle is: "Better to leave tumor than to injure nerve."

Vascular preservation: Identify the labyrinthine artery early in the dissection as it courses through the IAC. Preserve all vessels visible on the capsule surface, as these represent the vasa nervorum essential for nerve survival.

Brainstem protection: Only dissect the medial tumor pole after sufficient internal debulking has been achieved. The subperineurial technique inherently leaves protective tissue layers between the dissection and brainstem.

CSF leak prevention: Systematically seal all mastoid air cells with bone wax, reinforce with muscle plugs, and apply fibrin glue. Early mobilization on postoperative day one is generally safe with proper closure technique.

Postoperative surveillance: Histological studies have demonstrated that the "tumor capsule" is vestibular nerve perineurium [4]. When true perineurium is preserved without residual tumor, recurrence risk is minimal (Fig. 3b). Annual MRI surveillance is recommended.

Specific information for the patient

We prioritize quality of life, specifically facial movement and hearing preservation, over achieving complete tumor removal.

A small capsule remnant may be intentionally left attached to the facial nerve to prevent facial paralysis—this is a deliberate safety measure, not an incomplete operation.

Even with successful nerve preservation, hearing function can decline over time due to changes in cochlear blood flow or delayed effects of the surgery.

Annual MRI scans are necessary for long-term surveillance; if any tumor regrowth is detected, it can typically be managed effectively with stereotactic radiosurgery without requiring repeat surgery.
