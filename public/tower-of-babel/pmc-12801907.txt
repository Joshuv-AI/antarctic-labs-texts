Construction of a survival prognosis model for epithelial-mesenchymal transition-related genes in gastric cancer
Authors: Ju Wu, Yanan Huang, Xinyue Wang, Shuanghshuang Hou, Yaoyuan Chang, Xi Chen, He Li, Jian Xu, Zhequn Nie, Jiajun Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) is a prevalent malignancy with high mortality rate. The process of Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) significantly contributes to its metastasis and resistance to therapy. This research is designed to develop a survival prediction model for EMT-related genes in GC.MethodsThis study used GC data from public databases and screened core module genes via weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Subsequently, we combined univariate Cox regression and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to identify key feature genes and establish a prognostic model for GC patients. The survival package was used for survival analysis, and the pROC package was employed to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for evaluating model performance. The CIBERSORT and ESTIMATE algorithms were applied to assess immune cell infiltration in patients with different risk levels. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted for pathway enrichment analysis. Finally, in vitro cell experiments were performed to verify the expression levels and potential biological functions of the key genes.ResultsWe obtained three key genes, NPR3, OLFML2B and GREB1, and established a prognostic RiskScore model consisting of these three key genes, and the area under ROC curve (AUC) > 0.6 confirmed the predictive efficacy of this model. GSEA confirmed that the tumor progression pathways, such as EMT, angiogenesis, and so on, were notably activated in high-risk group. Moreover, patients in high-risk group exhibited a stronger tendency to immune escape and were significantly less sensitive to Afatinib, Gefitinib, and Lapatinib. Finally, in vitro tests displayed that NPR3 knockdown markedly decreased the viability, migration and invasion of GC cells.ConclusionOur study provides a prognostic assessment tool for GC based on EMT-related genes and offers novel insights into understanding the roles of EMT in GC progression and treatment resistance. These findings may aid in the development of precision therapy strategies for GC.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Epithelial-mesenchymal transition, Prognostic model, Immune infiltration, Drug sensitivity

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a common malignancy that brings a severe threat to human health [1, 2]. According to the reports of International Agency for research on Cancer (IARC), there will be about 18.1 million new cancer cases worldwide in 2020, accounting for 5.6% of all malignant tumours worldwide, after breast (11.7%), lung (11.4%), colorectal (10.0%) and prostate cancers (7.3%) [3, 4]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1/PD-L1 have revolutionized GC therapy with durable responses. Pembrolizumab/nivolumab monotherapy shows 10–26% response rates in advanced GC without selective biomarkers, highlighting the urgent need to identify patients most likely to benefit from immunotherapy [5, 6]. A variety of biomarkers, such as tumour mutation burden (TMB), expression of PD-L1, microsatellite instability (MSI), and the infection status of Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), have been suggested to help determine vulnerability to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Nonetheless, findings from multiple clinical trials assessing these biomarkers individually show inconsistency, with some results even conflicting [7–9]. As a result, suitable biomarkers for effective patient stratification are still lacking, possibly because of the intricate nature of the immune response to cancer.

In recent years, the process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) has emerged as a significant focus within the realm of cancer research [66]. EMT denotes the transformation of epithelial cells into mesenchymal-like cells through a specific set of changes [10], a process that confers on tumor cells the ability to metastasize and invade. Multiple studies have shown that EMT acts a key role in invasive metastasis, treatment resistance and poor prognosis of GC [11, 12]. EMT promotes tumour cell invasion into the mesenchyme and is associated with increased cancer stem cell traits and tumourigenicity [13]. Numerous evidences demonstrate that many genes, like TSP50, CD44 and FOXA1, can regulate EMT in GC [14–16]. The inhibitory effects of Celastrus orbiculatus extract (COE) on the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of GC cells were explored by Wang et al. who found that COE suppresses the EMT process and impacts the TGF-β signaling pathway [12]. However, systematic studies on EMT-related genes in the prognostic assessment of GC are still relatively insufficient, especially the construction of EMT-related prognostic models based on large-scale genomic data still needs to be explored in depth.

This study innovatively adopts an integrated perspective to systematically correlate EMT-related gene signatures with prognosis, immune microenvironment, and treatment sensitivity in GC patients. The research first identified core EMT genes closely associated with GC progression, thereby establishing a novel prognostic assessment model that effectively distinguishes patient cohorts with differing survival outcomes. Further analysis revealed unique immunosuppressive features in patients with different risk profiles and their potential sensitivity to specific targeted therapies. Functional experiments ultimately validated the oncogenic roles of key genes. This study not only provides a novel molecular tool for GC prognosis prediction but also deepens our understanding of the EMT-driven mechanisms of tumor progression, laying a crucial theoretical foundation for developing personalized treatment strategies in the future.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data: the GC dataset was derived from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) database as the training set for this study and Class A samples with retained survival days greater than or equal to 30 days were screened, and 238 cancer samples as well as 25 paracancerous control samples were finally obtained [17].

The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) data: the GSE15459 dataset was selected as the validation set. Sourced from the GEO database, 192 GC patients were extracted from it [18].

Additionally, A total of 1184 EMT-related genes were retrieved from the dbEMT database (https://bioinfo-minzhao.org/dbemt/) to serve as the background gene set for this study.

Calculation of EMT scores and screening of key gene modules

EMT-related genes were used as background genes, and the enrichment scores for each sample in the background gene set were calculated employing the R language ssGSEA [19]. Next, the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was utilized to delineate key modular genes implicated in the EMT process. The training set of samples was subjected to analysis using the R package WGCNA to identify an appropriate soft threshold (β value) via the application of the pickSoftThreshold function. This process facilitated the establishment of a standardized weighted co-expression network. The soft threshold of 0.90 was chosen for the construction of the co-expression network, with a minimum module size set at 200 genes and a merging height threshold of 0.2 for module identification. Subsequently, a module-trait correlation was analyzed between each module and the EMT score using the Spearman correlation method. Modules with an absolute correlation value of ≥ 0.5 and a p-value < 0.01 were identified as significant modules [20, 21].

Differential expression analysis

The samples were differentiated according to the tumour group and control group, and the differences of gene expression levels between cancer and control groups were calculated by DESeq2 package [22], and significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined using |log2foldchange (FC) ≥ 1 and padj < 0.01 as criteria.

Construction and validation of prognostic models

The intersection of WGCNA and DEGs was taken, and a univariate COX analysis was conducted through the R language survival package [23], and genes with p-value < 0.05 were imported into the glmnet package for LASSO analysis, and the model with the smallest Lambda was selected, and a prognostic RiskScore model was constructed by multivariate COX analysis for genes within the model [23]. Subsequently, patients were classified into high- and low-risk groups based on the RiskScore, and patient survival was assessed by Kaplan–Meier (K–M) curves. ROC curves were employed to analyze the prognostic predictive value of the model, and ROC analysis to predict classification efficiency was conducted on the RiskScore by the R software package PROC [24]. In addition, the robustness of RiskScore model was confirmed in the validation set.

Immune infiltration analysis

CIBERSORT R package was utilized to calculate the level of immune cell infiltration in the training set samples, 22 common immune infiltrating cell expression data LM22 from the CIBERSORT official website (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) [25]. Immune infiltration analysis was also conducted on the training set with the ESTIMATE R package [26]. Immune infiltration analyses performed by ESTIMATE calculated the StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore.

GSEA

GC samples from training set were categorized into high- and low-risk groups based on RiskScore, and enrichment analysis was carried out in the background gene set using GSEA v4.4.0 software, and the gene sets were selected as c2.cp.kegg vs. c5.go as well as the HALLMARK gene set [27].

Analysis of immunotherapy response and drug sensitivity

Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) database (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) was applied to evaluate the potential clinical effectiveness of immunotherapy in high- and low-risk groups. The samples in training set were analyzed employing the R package oncoPredict [28], and the samples were trained using the data of 198 drugs from Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC2) database, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of the drugs were calculated, and the drugs with significant correlation with biomarker expression were screened by Spearman’s correlation coefficient (|cor|≥ 0.5, p < 0.01).

Cell lines and transfection

The human gastric mucosal epithelial cells (GES-1, C5068) and the human gastric adenocarcinoma cells (AGS, C5025) were acquired from BDBio Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China), and the human GC cells (HGC-27, BNCC338546) were purchased from Bei Na Biotech. GES-1 cell was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (C5068-500, BDBio, China) contained 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). AGS cell was cultured in F-12 K medium (C5025-500, BDBio, China) encompassed 10% FBS. HGC-27 cell was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium contained 20% FBS. These cells were stored at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. The cells underwent short tandem repeat (STR) identification, and the results of mycoplasma detection for these cells were found to be negative.

Further, the small interfering (si) RNAs of NPR3 (si-NPR3#1: 5ʹ-CGCGAATCAATGAGATCAAATGC-3ʹ; si-NPR3#2: 5ʹ-CACCGTCAATTACAAACACTTGG-3ʹ) and negative control (si-NC) were provided by Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd for knockdown the expression of NPR3 in GC cells. Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent kit (11668027, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was employed to transfect the si-NPR3 into GC cells AGS and HGC-27.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Initially, total RNA was collected from GES-1, AGS, and HGC-27 cells utilizing TriZol RNA Extraction Kit (15596026CN, Invitrogen, CA, USA). The concentration and purity of RNA were measured. Then, total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA by the SuperRT cDNA Synthesis Kit (S665657-100 T, Aladdin, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Mix (D7260, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The amplification program was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Melt curve analysis was performed at the end of the amplification cycles to verify the specificity of the PCR products. Finally, data analysis was performed using the 2−ΔΔCT method with GAPDH serving as the internal control. The specific primer pairs for the genes are presented in Table 1.

Cell viability test

To evaluate the influence of transfection with si-NC and si-NPR3 on the proliferation of AGS and HGC-27 cells, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) colorimetric assay was performed. Specifically, cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were cultured in a 96-well microplate for 24, 48, and 72 h. Next, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was supplemented to each well and placed at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance in each well at 450 nm, indicative of cell proliferation, was detected applying a microplate reader (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, US) to construct the CCK-8 growth curves.

Cell migration assay

For the migration assay, cells post-transfection were seeded into six-well plates (5 × 105 cells per well). A 2 mL cell suspension was plated into each well and incubated in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Once the cells had adhered, a uniform wound was formed by scraping the monolayer with 10 µL plastic pipette tip. To remove any detached cell debris, cells were washed twice using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (P1010, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Imaging of the wound edges was performed at 0- and 48-h post-wounding to assess cell migration.

Cell invasion assay

For the invasion assay, cell suspensions of HGC-27 and AGS (3 × 104 cells/well) were prepared in serum-free medium. 100 μL of cell suspension was injected to the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (Corning, Inc., Corning, NY, USA), which had been pre-coated with Matrigel (30 μg/well; BD, San Jose, CA, USA). Whereas, the lower Transwell chamber was supplemented with complete medium containing serum. After incubation, the plates were removed, and cells were gently rinsed with PBS (P1010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) to remove dead cells and residual medium. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained by crystal violet. The invading or migrating cells were counted with a microscope using five randomly selected fields of view.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out utilizing R software (version 4.1.0) and Graphpad Prism (version 8.0). The normality of distribution for all continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups of normally distributed data, the unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups of normally distributed data, one-way or two-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by appropriate post-hoc tests (e.g., Tukey’s test) for multiple comparisons. For data that violated the assumption of normality, the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test (also known as the Mann–Whitney U test) was used for two-group comparisons, and the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test was employed for multiple group comparisons. p < 0.05 was defined to be significant difference.

Prognostic modelling and validation

The 138 genes identified earlier were analyzed using univariate Cox regression, revealing that 65 of these genes showed a significant impact on survival outcomes (Fig. 3A). LASSO regression method was applied to the 65 candidate genes to select the most critical ones, including NPR3, OLFML2B, and GREB1. The final RiskScore model constructed based on multivariate Cox regression was RiskScore = 0.172×NPR3 + 0.027×OLFML2B + 0.124×GREB1.

Immune infiltration analysis

CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to analyze the proportional differences of distinct immune cell types between high-risk and low-risk groups. The results demonstrated significant disparities in the infiltration levels of all five immune cell types examined. Specifically, the reduced-risk cohort exhibited notably higher infiltration of CD4 T cell memory resting and dendritic cells activated compared with the elevated-risk cohort. Conversely, the elevated-risk cohort showed significantly greater infiltration of macrophages M2 and mast cells resting relative to the reduced-risk cohort (Fig. 4A). OLFML2B exhibits a positive association with macrophages M2 and an inverse relationship with dendritic cells activated. NPR3 was positively correlated with macrophage M2, quiescent mast cells. GREB1 positively correlates with resting mast cells (Fig. 4B). Estimate’s analysis displayed remarkable differences in stromal infiltration scores, immunization scores, and ESTIMATE scores in both groups, with higher scores in high-risk group for all three (Fig. 4C), and significant correlations with biomarker expression for all three (Fig. 4D). These results elucidate the biological basis of EMT-related RiskScore from the perspective of the immune microenvironment and provide an important basis for understanding the immune escape mechanism of GC and developing combined immunotherapy strategies.

RiskScore model-based correlation analysis of drug sensitivity and response to immunotherapy

This study evaluated the predictive values of EMT-related RiskScore for immunotherapy response and drug sensitivity by systematic analysis (Fig. 6). Utilization of the TIDE algorithm demonstrated that individuals in high-risk group had markedly elevated TIDE scores compared to low-risk group, indicating a higher capacity for immune evasion among high-risk patients (Fig. 6A). Examination of 198 pharmaceutical datasets sourced from the GDSC2 database indicated a significant correlation between the IC50 values for three medications and the risk scores. The boxplot analysis indicates that the low-risk group typically had lower IC50 values compared with high-risk group, implying that samples in the low-risk category might exhibit greater sensitivity to Afatinib, Gefitinib, and Lapatinib (Fig. 6B–D).

Downregulation of NPR3 impairs migration and invasion of GC cells

Initially, qPCR was performed to detect the mRNA expression profiles of NPR3, OLFML2B, and GREB1 in HGC-27, AGS and GES-1 cells. The qPCR data showed that the expression levels of the 3 genes were markedly heightened in AGS and HGC-27 cells in comparison to GES-1 cells (Fig. 7A). Here, NPR3 contributed the highest weight to the prognostic model (with a coefficient of 0.172), making it the candidate gene with the greatest functional and translational potential for prioritized validation. Two NPR3-targeting knockdown plasmids were constructed and introduced into AGS and HGC-27 cells, with the most efficiently silenced cell lines (AGS, si-NPR3#1 and HGC-27, si-NPR3#1) ultimately chosen for subsequent experimental procedures (Fig. 7B and C). The outcomes revealed that NPR3 silencing significantly reduced the proliferation of AGS and HGC-27 cells (Fig. 7D and E). Following this, wound healing and Transwell experiments were conducted to evaluate the role of NPR3 in regulating the invasive and metastatic capabilities of AGS and HGC-27 cells. The results demonstrated that NPR3 silencing notably reduced the migratory and invasive potentials of AGS and HGC-27 cells (Fig. 7F, I). These findings confirm that NPR3 might serve as a critical determinant in facilitating tumor cell migration and invasion during the development of GC.

Discussion

GC represents a prevalent malignancy within the gastrointestinal tract, distinguished by its elevated incidence and mortality rates. Despite continuous advancements in GC treatment, tumor recurrence due to metastasis and drug resistance remains a major clinical challenge [29, 30], and the prognosis for advanced-stage patients remains poor [31]. EMT represents a pivotal biological process in GC progression and metastasis, conferring malignant phenotypes including enhanced invasiveness, metastatic potential, and immune evasion capabilities [32, 33]. While previous high-throughput studies have explored the role of EMT-related genes in GC prognosis using databases like TCGA and GEO [34, 35], the novelty of our work lies in several aspects. First, our analytical strategy, which integrated ssGSEA with WGCNA, allowed us to pinpoint a core gene module most critically associated with the EMT phenotype, moving beyond simple screening of pre-defined EMT gene lists. Second, the resulting prognostic model, comprising only three genes (NPR3, OLFML2B, and GREB1), offers a more concise and potentially clinically translatable signature compared to models with larger gene sets. Most importantly, we extended beyond prognostic prediction to systematically elucidate the model’s connection with an immunosuppressive microenvironment, a heightened potential for immune escape, and sensitivity to targeted therapeutics. Furthermore, we experimentally validated the pivotal role of the core gene, NPR3, in driving GC cell migration and invasion in vitro. This multi-faceted approach, from bioinformatic discovery to functional validation, provides novel and profound insights into the role of EMT in GC progression and treatment resistance.

This study screened three key genes (NPR3, OLFML2B and GREB1) associated with EMT in GC. NPR3 has been shown to be associated with the development of various malignant tumors [36]. Inhibition of NPR3 expression promotes the spread of renal clear cell carcinoma [37]. NPR3 inhibited the development of osteosarcoma by suppressing the phosphoinositide 3-kinase-Akt (PI3K/AKT) pathway [38]. Upregulation of NPR3 promotes the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells [39]. Studies have verified that NPR3 could remarkably facilitate the proliferation and migration of GC cells. Functioning as an accelerator of GC progression, NPR3 shows higher expression of stromal markers. This suggests that NPR3 might boost the invasiveness and metastatic potential of GC cells by spurring EMT [36]. Therefore, NPR3 holds significant potential as a therapeutic and prognostic biomarker for GC. OLFML2B belongs to a family characterized by olfactory-mediated protein motifs, is implicated in the selective binding of chondroitin sulfate E and heparin within the extracellular matrix [40]. Studies have established a correlation between OLFML2B expression and the development as well as the prognostic outcome of bladder cancer [41]. Additionally, OLFML2B has been posited as a promising candidate for prognostic biomarker status in osteosarcoma [42]. Notably, elevated levels of OLFML2B expression have been linked to a diminished overall survival in patients with GC [43], suggesting its potential utility as both a diagnostic and prognostic indicator for this malignancy. Furthermore, OLFML2B participates in multiple biological functions like promoting cell growth and sustenance, regulating cell cycle, controlling apoptosis, and facilitating cell communication. OLFML2B participates in various oncogenic pathways, such as angiogenesis, and is associated with tumor invasiveness [43, 44]. OLFML2B may also participate in the construction of immune microenvironment of GC by affecting the infiltration and activity of immune cells [44]. GREB1 is an early estrogen response gene whose expression is linked to estrogen levels in breast cancer sufferers [45]. GREB1 plays a significant role in various hormone-dependent cancers through the regulation by sex hormones, including estrogen, progesterone, and androgens [46]. Investigations have revealed that the expression of GREB1 is elevated in ovarian epithelial carcinoma, contributing to tumor expansion and the spread of cancer [47]. GREB1 is regulated by the androgen receptor (AR) and promotes proliferation in prostate cancer cells [48, 49]. GREB1 participates in estrogen and progesterone signaling pathways that affect the proliferation and differentiation of endometrial cells [45]. Its overexpression increases the proliferation rate of ovarian cancer cells, while GREB1 knockout inhibits proliferation [47]. GREB1 facilitates the advancement of tumors in cancers that rely on hormones, including breast cancer, through its role in modulating hormone signaling, enhancing cell division, and altering epigenetic landscapes [46]. Nonetheless, the precise association between GREB1 and GC remains to be thoroughly investigated. Collectively, these three genes may modulate the onset and progression of GC through multifaceted mechanisms, thereby exhibiting considerable potential as biomarkers for guiding GC therapy and predicting patient prognosis.

Our analysis delineates a distinct immunologic landscape between the two risk groups, which provides a compelling mechanistic basis for their divergent clinical outcomes. The high-risk group was characterized by an immunosuppressive microenvironment, predominantly shaped by the significant enrichment of M2 macrophages and resting mast cells, alongside a concomitant reduction in activated dendritic cells and resting memory CD4 + T cells. This specific cellular constellation is functionally coherent: M2 macrophages, a classic tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) phenotype, foster tumor progression by releasing anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive cytokines [50], and their abundance is an established marker of unfavorable prognosis [51]. Conversely, the paucity of activated dendritic cells in the high-risk group likely impairs the initiation of antitumor immunity, as these cells are pivotal for activating tumor-specific T cells [52, 53]. Thus, the TME in high-risk patients appears permissive for tumor growth, facilitating immune evasion and potentially enabling the EMT process, which collectively drives poorer survival. In contrast, the immune contexture of the low-risk group, marked by higher levels of activated dendritic cells [52, 53] and resting memory CD4 + T cells, suggests a more competent immune surveillance system poised for effective anti-tumor responses. The role of neutrophils in this setting remains complex [54, 55], but their presence in the low-risk group may contribute to a more balanced inflammatory state conducive to better patient outcomes [55, 56]. In summary, the immune infiltration patterns we identified are not merely correlative but are functionally integrated, explaining, at least in part, the aggressive biology and unfavorable prognosis associated with the high-risk group in GC.

The IC50 for Afatinib, Gefitinib, and Lapatinib drugs was higher in high-risk group than in low-risk group. Afatinib is an irreversible pan-HER family tyrosine kinase inhibitor [57]. This drug forms a covalent bond with the kinase domains of receptors including Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR), Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2), and Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 4 (HER4). It exerts its antitumor effect by inhibiting the activity of these receptors and blocking downstream signaling pathways [58]. Afatinib achieves its anticancer activity by suppressing the mTORC1 pathway, thereby triggering apoptosis in malignant cells [59]. Afatinib has demonstrated antitumor activity against HER2-positive GC in both in-vitro and in-vivo models. It curbs the proliferation of tumor cells by impeding the PI3K/AKT and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascades, leading to cell cycle halt and programmed cell death [58, 60]. Gefitinib is a first-generation oral active EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor that selectively inhibits EGFR activity, thereby blocking the growth of EGFR-dependent tumor cells [61]. In addition, in Gefitinib-resistant lung cancer cell lines, overexpression of Baculoviral IAP Repeat Containing 5 (BIRC5) may confer Gefitinib resistance by regulating apoptosis [61]. Lapatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor of small molecular size, is crucial for regulating cell growth and viability [62]. As the inaugural dual inhibitor targeting both the EGFR and HER2 tyrosine kinases, it reversibly attaches to the Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-binding site within the kinase domain, thereby hindering the signal transduction pathways of these receptors [63]. This interaction leads to reduced growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis by affecting the Ras/Raf MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, contributing to increased apoptosis and genetic instability [64]. Chen et al. showed that the activation of receptor tyrosine kinases leads to lapatinib resistance in patients with HER2-positive gastrointestinal cancer [65]. In summary, these three drugs have shown potential to inhibit GC tumor growth and induce cancer cell apoptosis to a certain extent, but their effectiveness in treating clinical treatment resistance still needs to be further verified.

Although this research validated the reliability of the prognostic model in multiple dimensions, certain limitations persist. First, the prognostic model constructed in this study is based entirely on retrospective data from public databases and has not yet been validated in our own prospectively collected clinical cohort. This may introduce selection bias and affect the model’s immediate applicability in real-world clinical settings. Moving forward, we will initiate the collection of a multicenter, prospective cohort of gastric cancer patients featuring fresh-frozen tumor tissue and comprehensive follow-up information. This cohort will validate our RiskScore model and further integrate it with traditional clinical-pathological staging systems to develop a more comprehensive and robust prognostic assessment nomogram, thereby advancing its translation into clinical practice. Additionally, although we validated our model on an independent GEO dataset, all data originated from chip-based high-throughput sequencing platforms. To address this limitation, we will further acquire GC datasets derived from diverse detection technologies and geographically distinct populations to comprehensively evaluate the model’s generalization capabilities. Concurrently, we will explore developing a simplified detection protocol suitable for routine clinical pathology samples to lower the technical barriers for future clinical translation. Finally, although we predicted the immune characteristics and pathway activation in the high-risk group through bioinformatics and validated the function of NPR3 via in vitro experiments, the specific molecular mechanisms by which these three genes synergistically interact and precisely regulate EMT and the tumor immune microenvironment remain poorly understood. Subsequent studies will integrate in vivo animal models (e.g., establishing GC mouse models with gene knockdown), advanced molecular biology techniques (e.g., chromatin immunoprecipitation, reporter gene assays), and single-cell sequencing. This comprehensive approach aims to elucidate the precise regulatory networks and upstream/downstream signaling pathways involving NPR3, OLFML2B, and GREB1 within GC cells and their microenvironments. Such insights will provide a robust theoretical foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies against these genes.

Conclusion

Collectively, this study constructed and validated a GC prognostic model based on EMT core genes (NPR3, OLFML2B, GREB1). The model not only accurately predicts patient survival, but more importantly, reveals the unique immunosuppressive microenvironment and potential sensitivity to specific targeted drugs in high-risk patients, providing a molecular typing basis for risk-adapted treatmen. Further experiments confirmed that the key gene NPR3 is a critical factor driving the migration and invasion of GC cells, suggesting it could serve as a potential therapeutic target. This study not only provides a novel and practical tool for GC prognosis evaluation, but also deepens the understanding of the EMT mechanism from the perspectives of immune microenvironment and treatment response, laying a theoretical foundation for advancing precision treatment strategies for GC.
