Integrating multi-omics to reveal the molecular mechanism of lipid metabolism regulated by a splice variant of Lysyl-oxidase like-2, LOXL2Δ13
Authors: Yang Chen, Liu Peng, Jian-Zhong He, Xiao-Dong Wu, Xing Xu, Geng Dong, En-Min Li, Li-Yan Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLOXL2Δ13, a splice variant of Lysyl-oxidase like-2 (LOXL2), enhances glucose metabolism and induces adipose depletion in mice. The precise role of LOXL2Δ13 in lipid metabolism is unclear. Through multi-omics integration, we analyzed the transcriptome, metabolome, proteome, and protein modifications profiles in LOXL2Δ13 overexpression mice. Molecular dynamics simulation and interaction mass spectrometry confirmed key biomarkers influenced by LOXL2Δ13. Here, LOXL2Δ13 was found to cause diglyceride and glycerophospholipid accumulation and alter kinase activities in pathways. Differentially expressed genes, proteins, and metabolites were enriched in lipid metabolism pathways, indicating LOXL2Δ13 influences metabolic enzyme activity by regulating protein expression and modifications. Further immunoprecipitation assay demonstrated that four proteins (Itpr1, Acat1, Canx, and Pdia3) directly interact with LOXL2∆13. This suggests that LOXL2∆13 can regulate lipid metabolic pathways through direct interactions affecting the activity of these key proteins, which in turn induce alterations in their phosphorylation or acetylation modifications, thereby affecting the lipid metabolic pathways. Subject terms: Data integration, Obesity

Introduction

Technological advancements in recent decades have facilitated a deeper understanding of the intricate biological mechanisms underlying metabolic diseases, including obesity. The integration of multi-omics associated biomarkers offers a promising avenue for gaining novel insights into the etiology of metabolic diseases. Furthermore, omics biomarkers enable researchers to accurately identify and characterize metabolic diseases, thus providing a foundation for precise preventive and therapeutic interventions1. A large part of multi-omics research involves genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. Tailored analytical platforms and bioinformatic tools have been developed to explore the biological significance embedded within the data generated by these diverse omics approaches2. Although single-omics studies can provide molecular profiles of diseases or cancers, they often fall short of capturing the broader biological context. Comparatively, multi-omics analyzes possess the potential to uncover disease mechanisms, offer new biological insights, and systematically reveal various health and disease states. The integration of multi-omics methodologies proves advantageous in deciphering the molecular characterization of various complex diseases, such as cancer and obesity3. Recent developments in mathematics and computational science have led to innovative methods for data integration. Appling multi-omics integration analysis facilitates the exploration of relationships between biomarkers and their corresponding phenotypes while also aiding in interpreting data within the intricate framework of biological networks and molecular interactions. Despite being in its early stages, the practice of multi-omics integrative analysis has gained traction in various fields, including brain diseases4, diabetes5, and cancer6.

In 2014, our laboratory made a pioneering discovery by identifying a novel splice variant of LOXL2, named LOXL2Δ13, present in various tissues and organs. Notably, this variant mRNA lacks the 13th exon, which truncates 45 amino acid residues at the C-terminus of the LOXL2Δ13 protein. Consequently, a distinct amino acid sequence, comprising 18 residues, emerges in this protein. Furthermore, the enzymatic activity center of LOXL2Δ13 is also different from LOXL27. Furthermore, LOXL2Δ13 also differs from LOXL2 in its cytoplasmic and cytosolic localization, which is mainly localized in the cytoplasm around the cell membrane7. Interestingly, despite the partially impaired deamidation activity of LOXL2Δ13, LOXL2Δ13 is more oncogenic than LOXL2 in tumor cells such as ESCC, indicating that LOXL2∆13 may play an important role in cancer7. Based on these, we theorized that LOXL2∆13 could promote esophageal cancer cell invasion and metastasis, leading to poor prognosis in esophageal cancer patients8. Moreover, our investigations showed that both LOXL2 and LOXL2∆13 can stimulate cancer cell division by increasing cellular lipid synthesis8,9. Building on our prior laboratory findings, we collaborated with Shanghai Southern Model Biotechnology to establish a transgenic mouse model (C57BL/6J) expressing LOXL2∆13 to unravel the functional implications of LOXL2∆13. Notably, our investigations during the maturation of LOXL2∆13 mice revealed a significant reduction in their body weight compared to their wild-type (WT) counterparts. Subsequent studies showed that LOXL2Δ13 mice exhibited a notable decrease in adipocyte size and downregulated expression of adipogenesis-related genes10. In vitro experiments demonstrated the inhibitory effect of LOXL2∆13 on the lipogenic differentiation of 3T3-L1 preadipocytes10. In addition, the number of metabolites associated with adipocyte differentiation, such as lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) and phosphatidic acid (PA), was significantly increased in the adipose of LOXL2Δ13 mice and was significantly and negatively correlated with body weight10. Overall, these results suggest that LOXL2Δ13 can regulate lipid metabolism, inhibit adipose tissue differentiation and suppress obesity in mice. Furthermore, mass spectrometry analyzes conducted in our laboratory also revealed that LOXL2∆13 interacts with important glycolytic-related enzymes such as aldolase A. LOXL2∆13 can also regulate glycolysis by influencing the acetylation status of aldolase A and inhibiting its aldolase activity and is involved in the metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells9, suggesting that LOXL2∆13 may have functions beyond its traditional enzymatic actions. More importantly, our previous study revealed the potential regulatory role of LOXL2∆13 in maintaining intestinal microbiota equilibrium, modulating their metabolism, and influencing homeostasis in plasma lipid metabolism through its impact on hepatic lipid metabolism. However, the precise molecular mode of action remains unclear. Consequently, this present study aims to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which LOXL2∆13 regulates lipid metabolism through comprehensive high-throughput sequencing using transcriptome, metabolome, histone modification profile, proteome, and protein modification group in liver tissues from the LOXL2∆13 overexpression mouse model and synergistically integrate these multi-omics data.

Results

LOXL2∆13 affects phospholipid metabolism and regulates the expression of genes related to lipid metabolism

To elucidate the regulatory mechanism of LOXL2Δ13 on lipid metabolism in mice, we first performed untargeted metabolomic analysis in both LOXL2Δ13 mice and their WT counterparts. The results revealed distinct metabolic profiles between the two groups, as evident from the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) results (Fig. 1A, B). Differential analysis identified 530 significantly altered metabolites (SAMs) meeting the following criteria: adjusted P_value < 0.05, |log2fold-change| ≥ 1, and VIP ≥ 1 (Fig. 1C). In the liver tissues of LOXL2Δ13 mice, we observed an abnormal increase in lipids such as phosphoglycerol (PG), phosphatidylinositol (PI), monoglyceride (MG), diglyceride (DG), pernol lipids, sphingolipids, and steroids. In contrast, the levels of triglycerides (TG) and carbohydrates (CA) were significantly reduced (Fig. 1D), suggesting an altered glycolipid metabolism in LOXL2Δ13 mice. Notably, Fig. 1D summarizes lipid class-level changes, however, representative individual lipid species within these classes (e.g., PC(14:0_16:0), DG(16:0_16:1_0:0), and PE(15:0_20:0)) were also significantly elevated in LOXL2Δ13 mice, supporting the observed lipid remodeling. Meanwhile, phospholipids play an essential role in physiological functions such as cell membrane composition, stability and signaling, revealing a potential mechanism of LOXL2Δ13.

Additionally, we performed PCA on various datasets derived from liver tissues, including transcriptomic, proteomic, protein phosphorylation, and protein acetylation data. The results, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. S1, highlight that the overexpression of LOXL2∆13 distinctly segregates the RNA expression levels, protein expression levels, and protein phosphorylation statuses in LOXL2Δ13 mice from those in WT mice. Accordingly, LOXL2Δ13 can affect transcriptional regulation, protein expression, and phosphorylation modifications.

To further elucidate the function of LOXL2Δ13, we analyzed the transcriptomes of both LOXL2Δ13 mice and WT mice. We identified 1604 Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) (Fig. 1E), which were subsequently annotated within metabolic pathways. The upregulated genes exhibited significant enrichment in Glutathione metabolism, Steroid biosynthesis, MAPK signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway and Cholesterol metabolism, while the downregulated genes were primarily associated with lipid metabolism pathways, including PPAR signaling and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 1F). Interestingly, genes within the PPAR signaling pathway displayed both upregulation and downregulation. This dual regulation likely reflects the modulation of distinct pathway components by LOXL2Δ13. For example, upregulation of genes such as Acaca and Scd1 may enhance lipid biosynthesis, while downregulation of genes like Cyp4a10 and Cpt1a may suppress lipid catabolism. This suggests a context-dependent regulatory effect of LOXL2Δ13 on PPAR signaling. These findings are consistent with our metabolomic results, reinforcing the pivotal role of LOXL2Δ13 in influencing lipid metabolism.

Subsequently, we examined the potential metabolic impact of LOXL2Δ13 by integrating DEGs and altered metabolites (Supplementary Fig. S2). The LOXL2Δ13-influenced metabolic flow begins with Acyl-CoA carboxylation, catalyzed by acetyl coenzyme A carboxylase (Acaca), resulting in malonyl coenzyme A. Sequential condensation of malonyl coenzyme A by Fatty Acid Synthase (Fasn) leads to the formation of saturated fatty acids, such as palmitate. Further transformation occurs as palmitate undergoes desaturation by stearoyl coenzyme A desaturase (Scd1), yielding monounsaturated fatty acids. Subsequent elongation and desaturation steps involving fatty acid elongases (Elovls) and fatty acid desaturases (Fads) yield polyunsaturated fatty acids like oleate (FA18:1) (Supplementary Fig. S2). These fatty acids are then synthesized into more complex lipids through various enzymatic processes. For instance, glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase 1 (Gpat1) and acyl-coenzyme A phosphatidic acid acyltransferase (Lpat) facilitate the direct conversion of oleate into phosphatidic acid (PA)11. PA, as a phospholipid class, serves structural and signaling functions and serves as a substrate for synthesizing diglycerides (DG) via enzymes like phosphatidic acid phosphatases (Lipin1-3) and LPA acyltransferases (Agpat), in addition to complex glycerolipids11. Through CDP-DAG synthase (Cds), PA is further converted into CDP-DAG, a pivotal precursor for the de novo synthesis of complex glycerolipids including phosphatidylinositol (PI), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), and phosphatidylcholine (PC) (Supplementary Fig. S2). Notably, the metabolic profile in LOXL2Δ13 mice reveals elevated levels of numerous glycolipids and glycerophospholipids. Interestingly, the transcript levels of the corresponding upstream or downstream metabolic enzymes (e.g., Gpats, Agpats) remain largely unchanged, suggesting that LOXL2Δ13 does not regulate these pathways at the transcriptional level. Instead, our proteomic and post-translational modification (PTM) analyzes reveal that many of these enzymes undergo altered acetylation or phosphorylation, which are known to influence enzymatic activity, substrate affinity, or cellular localization. This post-translational regulation likely contributes to the observed increase in lipid metabolite levels, providing a mechanistic explanation for the lipid remodeling and associated phenotypes such as reduced adipocyte size and body weight. Thus, LOXL2Δ13 may reshape lipid metabolism via modulation of protein function rather than gene expression.

LOXL2∆13 affects lipid metabolism-related protein expression and causes acetylation modifications of metabolism-related enzymes

The PCA analysis underscored the potential significance of LOXL2Δ13 in protein expression and post-translational modifications. Given the predominantly cytoplasmic localization of LOXL2Δ13, we examined whether it indirectly affects nuclear histone modifications. However, most histone methylation and acetylation marks showed no statistically or biologically significant changes (P-values > 0.05; fold change < 1.5), with only a small subset (e.g., H3K27me3, H3K9ac) showing modest differences (Supplementary Fig. S3). These findings suggest that LOXL2Δ13 has minimal impact on histone modifications, and its primary mode of action likely involves cytosolic and organelle-localized protein regulation via post-translational modifications.

Subsequently, we conducted proteomic analyzes on liver tissues from both mouse groups. Our screening revealed 203 significantly Differentially Expressed Proteins (DEPs) within the liver tissues of LOXL2Δ13 mice. Among these, 69 proteins were significantly downregulated, while 134 were significantly upregulated (FDR < 0.15, |log2fold-change| ≥ 0.263, Fig. 2A). These 203 DEPs further analyzed using Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) to assess their associations with mouse phenotypes, following which three co-expression modules were identified, all demonstrating positive or negative correlations with mouse body weight (Fig. 2B). Subsequent functional analysis revealed distinct pathways enriched within these modules: Protein.ME1 exhibited a notable enrichment in the PPAR signaling pathway, Protein.ME2 showed effects on steroid biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism, PPAR signaling, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and Protein.ME3 was intricately linked to the IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 2C). Further, we pinpointed pivotal sub-networks within each module through sub-network analysis of co-expression networks. Notably, the Protein.ME1 co-expression network encompassed proteins tied to fatty acid oxidation, including Cyp4a10 and Cyp4a14. The Protein.ME2 network predominantly featured molecules associated with ketogenic processes, lipid synthesis, and transport, such as Scd1, Scd4, Fasn, and Hmgcs1. On the other hand, the ME3 network prominently comprised inflammation-related molecules like S100a8 and S100a9. Collectively, these findings provide strong evidence that LOXL2Δ13 significantly influences lipid metabolism at the protein level.

To explore the potential impact of LOXL2Δ13 at the protein post-translational modifications level, we comprehensively analyzed protein acetylation in these mice, based on which we identified 225 differentially expressed acetylation modification sites comprising 168 proteins (P_value < 0.05). Among the 168 significantly altered acetylated proteins in LOXL2Δ13 vs. WT mice, only 3 proteins were also identified among the 203 DEPs (Supplementary Fig. S4). This partial overlap is consistent with the notion that acetylation often regulates protein activity, localization, or stability independently of expression levels. The majority of acetylated proteins showed no significant change in total abundance, highlighting post-translational modification as a key regulatory mechanism through which LOXL2Δ13 may influence lipid metabolism. Leveraging the acetylation modification sites, we performed WGCNA, discovering six co-expression modules based on these sites. Significantly, the Acety.ME5 module exhibited a correlation with the body weight index, while Acety.ME3 and Acety.ME6 modules demonstrated associations with blood glucose levels (Fig. 2F). All modules exhibited enrichments in pathways tied to Carbon metabolism, Pyruvate metabolism, and Biosynthesis of amino acids. Notably, Acety.ME2 was annotated to the Sulfur metabolism pathway, Acety.ME3 showed connections to Glycerolipid metabolism, Pantothenate, and CoA biosynthesis. Acety.ME4 displayed specific enrichment in bile secretion and primary bile acid biosynthesis. Acety.ME5 was primarily associated with Retinol metabolism, Steroid hormone biosynthesis and Fatty acid biosynthesis, while Acety.ME6 exhibited associations with metabolic pathways such as steroid hormone biosynthesis and biosynthesis of amino acids (Fig. 2G). A deeper analysis of sub-networks within each module revealed a substantial portion of proteins undergoing acetylation modifications are intricately associated with lipid metabolism (Fig. 2H), strongly suggesting that LOXL2Δ13 potentially influences acetylation modifications of proteins pertinent to lipid metabolism, thereby participating in the intricate realm of metabolic regulation. While protein acetylation in lipid metabolic pathways is a common observation in the field, our data reveal novel site-specific acetylation events on key lipid metabolic enzymes (e.g., Acadvl, Acat1, Canx, and Pdia3) that have not been previously described, underscoring a targeted regulatory role for LOXL2Δ13 in lipid metabolism.

LOXL2∆13 alters the phosphorylation modification of metabolic enzymes to regulate the activity of pathway-related kinases

We compared protein phosphorylation levels between LOXL2Δ13 mice and WT mice, uncovering 6648 differentially phosphorylated sites across 2412 proteins (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we employed WGCNA analysis, which grouped them into five co-expression modules. Modules Phos.ME1 and Phos.ME2 were significantly associated with body weight, while Modules Phos.ME4 and Phos.ME5 were associated with blood glucose (Fig. 3B). Enrichment analysis revealed that Phos.ME1 was enriched in protein export, endoplasmic reticulum protein processing, and phosphatidylinositol signaling. Phos.ME2 was specific to Glutathione metabolism, Carbon metabolism, and Pyruvate metabolism. Phos.ME3 was linked to primary bile acid biosynthesis and retinol metabolism pathways. Phos.ME4 was related to Fatty acid elongation, PPAR signaling pathway, and FoxO signaling pathway, whereas Phos.ME5 was enriched in MAPK signaling, Fatty acid degradation, and other metabolic pathways (Fig. 3C). Similarly, we identified key sub-networks within each module. Interestingly, many phosphorylated proteins, like Map3k5, Prakk2, Creb1, Fasn and Acaa2, were closely tied to lipid metabolism and signal transduction (Fig. 3D). These findings suggest that LOXL2Δ13 could regulate lipid metabolism by influencing phosphorylation modifications in proteins crucial for signal transduction and lipid metabolism pathways.

Subsequently, we assessed whether LOXL2Δ13 could trigger kinase activation in related signaling pathways. Based on protein phosphorylation data, we used the Kinase-substrate enrichment analysis (KSEA) method to predict kinase activities, which showed that LOXL2Δ13 impacted 11 protein kinase activities (Map2k3, Prkcd, Nek2, Camkk1, Raf1, Prkci, Pak1, Camk2a, Prkd1, Mapkapk2, and Mapk14) (Fig. 3E). For instance, Map2k3 activates Mapk14, a MAP kinase involved in cell cycle regulation and stress responses. Mapkapk2 encodes a Ser/Thr protein kinase involved in stress and inflammation responses. Prkcd, Prkci, Prkd1, Nek2, Camkk1 and Camk2a are serine/threonine protein kinases affecting the cell cycle, migration, differentiation, MAPK8/JNK1 and Ras signaling. Intracellular Raf1 activates MEK1 and MEK2, which subsequently activate ERK1 and ERK2 protein kinases, which are critical for cell division, apoptosis, differentiation and migration. Subsequent enrichment analysis identified pathways such as MAPK signaling, VEGF signaling, and Rap1 signaling (Fig. 3F), potentially suggesting a role for LOXL2Δ13. Lastly, we integrated findings from the proteome, protein acetylation, and protein phosphorylation groups into the phospholipid metabolic flow outlined earlier. These results revealed alterations in the phosphatidic acid phosphatase family (Lipin1-3), which converts phosphatidic acid into diglycerides (DG) and key transferases involved in synthesizing complex glycerolipids like phosphatidylinositol (PI), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), and phosphatidylcholine (PC). These alterations manifested at the protein level due to LOXL2Δ13 influence or via acetylation and phosphorylation modifications (Supplementary Fig. S5). Taken together, these findings suggest that LOXL2Δ13 indeed impacts glyceride and phospholipid metabolism at the protein level, influencing cell membrane composition and participating in signal transduction.

This comparison revealed only 38 overlapping molecules showing consistent changes at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4B).

This limited overlap underscores the importance of post-transcriptional mechanisms in LOXL2Δ13-mediated regulation. Notably, enrichment analysis of these 38 overlapping genes/proteins still identified key metabolic pathways such as the PPAR signaling pathway, steroid biosynthesis, and retinol metabolism (Fig. 4C), suggesting that a subset of LOXL2Δ13-regulated targets is controlled at both the transcriptional and proteomic levels.

In recent years, it has been recognized that variations in protein quantity might yield false positives when assessed alongside post-translational modifications. To address this, we conducted additional analyzes of DEPs and DEP post-translational modifications influenced by LOXL2∆13. As shown in Fig. 4D, we identified proteins whose protein expression levels remained unchanged while their protein phosphorylation levels differed (colored dark red and gray, N = 2346). Enrichment analysis of these proteins indicated their predominant involvement in metabolic pathways like MAPK signaling, Fatty acid elongation, Pyruvate metabolism, Fatty acid degradation, and Glycolysis and Gluconeogenesis (Fig. 4E). Similarly, we identified proteins not differentially expressed at the protein level but with varying protein acetylation levels (colored dark red and gray, N = 163, Fig. 4F), which were mainly enriched in metabolic pathways such as Carbon metabolism, Fatty acid metabolism, Pyruvate metabolism, Biosynthesis of amino acids and the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 4G). 2154 These findings align with our earlier results from individual omics data and suggest that post-translational modifications of LOXL2∆13-regulated proteins significantly influence lipid metabolism.

We employed the Order statistic algorithm to integrate data from the five dimensions, which comprised three levels of analysis. At the first level, we applied the Order Statistic algorithm to DEGs (N = 1604), DEPs (N = 203), differentially phosphorylated modified proteins (N = 2412), differentially acetylated modified proteins (N = 168) and metabolically enriched proteins (N = 277, as defined in the Methods, these proteins were identified by enzyme genes in SAMs enriched metabolic pathways) (Fig. 5B). In the second level, we assessed the influence of DEPs on post-translational modifications. This involved DEGs (N = 1604), DEPs (N = 203), proteins not differentially expressed but differentially phosphorylated (N = 2346), proteins not differentially expressed but differentially acetylated (N = 163), and metabolically enriched proteins (N = 277) (Fig. 5C). To address the inherent variability in transcriptomic and proteomic data and minimize false positives, our third level analysis prioritized DEPs (N = 203), proteins not differentially expressed but differentially phosphorylated (N = 2346), proteins not differentially expressed but differentially acetylated (N = 163), and metabolically enriched proteins (N = 277) (Fig. 5D). Among the top 100 ranked proteins across these integrated levels, pathway enrichment consistently underscored lipid metabolism pathways such as carbon metabolism, PPAR signaling, and fatty acid metabolism. Further accounting for the transcriptomic aspect revealed that these top 100 crucial proteins were also associated with pathways like starch and sucrose metabolism, thyroid hormone synthesis, primary bile acid biosynthesis and fatty acid elongation (Supplementary Fig. S6A). These findings reaffirm the role of LOXL2∆13 in lipid metabolism.

The RankAggregate algorithm was used to integrate the data from these five dimensions at the same three levels to ensure their robustness. As shown in Fig. 5E–G, most of the proteins in the top 100 of the Order statistic algorithm also rank well in the RankAggregate algorithm, including Gstp2, Cyp4a10, Sec61b, and Fabp1. Furthermore, we conducted enrichment analyzes for the top 100 proteins determined by the RankAggregate algorithm across the three integration levels. Our findings revealed consistent enrichment in lipid metabolism pathways such as the PPAR and Adipocytokine signaling pathways. Remarkably, upon excluding transcriptomic influence, the top 100 proteins retained annotations associated with the same metabolic pathways as identified through the Order statistics algorithm, including pathways associated with starch and sucrose metabolism, bile acid biosynthesis and fatty acid elongation (Supplementary Fig. S6B).

Subsequently, we focused on the top 100 essential proteins, prioritized through the third integration level. Initially, we assessed the consistency among these proteins, revealing that 92 proteins were overlapped (Fig. 6A). Then, we investigated the subcellular localization of these 92 proteins to gain deeper insights into the mechanisms influenced by LOXL2∆13 (Fig. 6B). Our findings indicated that these proteins were predominantly distributed across six distinct regions: (1) cell membrane (including Adh1, Fasn, Erbb3, and others); (2) cytoplasm (including S100a9, Gstp1, Hmgcs1, Pklr, and others); (3) vesicles (such as Cyp17a1 and Itpr1); (4) mitochondria (including Acat1, Acadvl, Idh2, and others); (5) endoplasmic reticulum (encompassing Canx, Pdia3, Sec61b, Scd1, Lcn2, and others); and (6) nucleus (including Cebpe, Rif1, Scp2, and others). These findings strongly suggest that LOXL2∆13 could play a vital role within each of these six organelles.

Identification of LOXL2∆13-interacting proteins by co-IP

To screen out proteins with direct interactions with LOXL2∆13 from the pool of 92 filtered proteins, we conducted mass spectrometry analysis, our findings highlighted 382 proteins interacted with LOXL2∆13 (Fig. 7A, B). Subsequently, we merged these interacting proteins with the initial set of 92 crucial proteins, which revealed 8 proteins that were influenced by LOXL2∆13 across various dimensions and had established interactions with LOXL2∆13, namely Pklr, Itpr1, Acat1, Acadvl, Canx, Pdia3, Sec61b and Tat (Fig. 7C). These 8 proteins demonstrated distinct subcellular localizations: mitochondria (Acadvl, Acat1, and Tat), vesicles (Itpr1), endoplasmic reticulum (Canx, Pdia3, Itpr1, and Sec61b), and cytosol (Pklr). All 8 proteins exhibited alterations in phosphorylation status, while Acadvl, Acat1, Canx, and Pdia3 simultaneously displayed changes in acetylation modifications (Table 1). Importantly, the functional roles of these 8 proteins were notably associated with lipid metabolic pathways, including processes like protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, pyruvate metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 7A). These findings collectively suggest that LOXL2∆13 potentially interacts with these 8 proteins to influence lipid metabolism.

Identification of LOXL2∆13 and key protein binding sites

Molecular docking was used to assess how LOXL2∆13 interacts with these 8 proteins and affects their downstream lipid metabolism. Initially, we generated a homology model of LOXL2∆13 based on the known structure of LOXL2. In Fig. 7D, the blue region of the LOXL2∆13 homology model represents the segment aligning with the corresponding structure in LOXL2, while the purple section signifies the distinct structure unique to LOXL2∆13. Interestingly, despite exon 13 being the sole variance between LOXL2 and LOXL2∆13, the spatial structure of LOXL2∆13 undergoes notable alterations (Fig. 7D). This structural divergence offers insights into the functional disparities observed between LOXL2 and LOXL2∆13, further enhancing our understanding of their distinct roles.

Subsequently, we conducted docking simulations to analyze the interaction of LOXL2∆13 with the 8 proteins, generating 8 distinct LOXL2∆13-protein complexes and also revealing specific interaction sites for each complex. For instance, LOXL2∆13 and Tat engage in hydrogen bond interactions at three sites (Fig. 7E). LOXL2∆13 and Pklr interact at eight sites, involving salt bridges, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 7F). LOXL2∆13 and Sec61b form interactions at six sites, primarily through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 7G). LOXL2∆13 and Acadvl interact through seven sites, forming salt bridges and hydrogen bonds (Fig. 7H). Similarly, LOXL2∆13 and Itpr1 interact at three sites, involving hydrogen bonds formed by G439, G440 and R436 of LOXL2∆13 with K720, E721, and T668 of Itpr1 (Fig. 7I). LOXL2∆13 and Canx primarily form interactions through a salt bridge (R627 of LOXL2∆13 and D448 of Canx), hydrogen bond (F414 of LOXL2∆13 and T591 of Canx) and hydrophobic interactions (Y593, F569, Y562, and H691 of LOXL2∆13 to F204, P145, and L200 of Canx) at six interaction sites (Fig. 7J). LOXL2∆13 and Pdia3 exhibit five interaction sites, predominantly involving hydrogen bonds (R617, H618, and D678 of LOXL2∆13 to S478, D475, and R482 of Pdia3), salt bridges (D678 of LOXL2∆13 to R482 of Pdia3), and hydrophobic interactions (H496 of LOXL2∆13 to C406 of Pdia3) (Fig. 7K). In the LOXL2∆13-Acat1 interaction, a hydrogen bond forms between R98 of LOXL2∆13 and T130 of Acat1 (Fig. 7L).

Further co-IP assay showed that four proteins (Itpr1, Canx, Pdia3, and Acat1) of these eight proteins directly interact with LOXL2∆13 (Fig. 7M). There has been very strong evidence indicating that these four genes are closely related to lipid metabolism. Acat1, on the other hand, catalyzes the condensation of two acetyl-CoAs to generate acetoacetyl-CoA and also facilitates its reverse reaction12. Acat1 plays a vital catalytic role in isoleucine degradation, ketosis, ketogenesis, and fatty acid oxidation12. Although the modification sites of Acat1 identified in this study have not been previously reported, they show new perspectives for further exploration into the functions of Acat1 and LOXL2Δ13 in lipid metabolism. The phosphorylation of Canx has been acknowledged for its regulatory function in protein folding and transport processes13. Here, we have identified several modification sites of Canx, and although these sites have not previously documented in existing studies, their significance cannot be underestimated. Itpr1, located predominantly in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum (ER) cell membrane, functions as a calcium channel. It is associated with the second messenger IP3, which, upon activation, triggers the binding of Itpr1 to IP3, facilitating the intracellular mobilization of calcium ions from the endoplasmic reticulum to the cytoplasm14. Furthermore, specialized channels in the nuclear membrane enable Ca2+ storage and controlled release into the nucleoplasm via a Itpr1. Phosphorylation modifications on Itpr1 enhance Ca2+ release15,16. site S1755 on Itpr1, as identified in our study, is phosphorylated by PKG, which intensifies the capacity of Itpr1 to mediate Ca2+ mobilization17, suggesting a critical role for the interaction between Itpr1 and LOXL2Δ13 in shaping Ca2+ signaling dynamics and activating downstream signaling pathways. Pdia3, with its redox and protein disulfide isomerase functions, is a prototypical resident protein of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). It operates in tandem with the molecular chaperone calcium ligand protein (Canx) to facilitate protein folding through its protein disulfide isomerase activity18. At the ER membrane, Pdia3 plays a role in modulating ER calcium ion flux19. Notably, Pdia3 has been recognized as a membrane-associated receptor for the biologically active form of vitamin D3, 1α, 25(OH)2D3 (also known as 1α, 25-dihydroxy vitamin D3), which regulates gene transcription related to bone health18. Our study identifies the site K214 on Pdia3 as significant for its role in ER chaperone function and its association with 1α,25(OH)2D3 binding20.

In conclusion, our study reveals that LOXL2∆13 primarily exerts its influence within the cell membrane, endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, and cytoplasm. Through direct interactions and modifications at various sites, particularly phosphorylation sites, LOXL2∆13 exerts its regulatory effects on 4 key proteins. Consequently, this intricate interplay impacts lipid metabolism and downstream signaling pathways (Fig. 8).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated how LOXL2Δ13 influences lipid metabolism using various high-throughput techniques. Our main findings are as follows: Overexpression of LOXL2Δ13 resulted in glycolipid and phospholipid accumulation in the liver, along with altered protein levels and modifications of enzymes like Cds2, Lpin1, and Pcyt2. This suggests LOXL2Δ13 affects lipid metabolism-related enzymes at both the protein and modification levels. Our network analysis of LOXL2Δ13-affected proteins revealed its impact on multiple metabolic pathways and kinases, suggesting multi-layered regulation. By employing two multi-omics integration methods, we identified eight key proteins (Pklr, Itpr1, Acat1, Acadvl, Canx, Pdia3, Sec61b, and Tat) directly interacting with LOXL2Δ13. Protein interaction mass spectrometry and molecular docking confirmed four proteins (Itpr1, Canx, Pdia3, and Acat1) interactions. Notably, Acadvl, Acat1, Canx, and Pdia3 exhibited altered acetylation, and all eight proteins showed changes in phosphorylation, underscoring LOXL2Δ13’s role in modulating lipid metabolism through post-translational modifications of these crucial proteins.

In recent years, proteomics has gained significant attention for its role in discovering new disease biomarkers and uncovering disease mechanisms21. Proteomics studies aim to identify and characterize proteins, peptides, and their modifications, shedding light on changes in their profiles. This approach provides valuable insights into the molecular underpinnings of metabolic disorders and aids in identifying early biomarkers22. Importantly, proteomic analyzes can uncover post-translational modifications that hold functional significance22,23. Leveraging unbiased, high-throughput, and quantitative techniques, proteomics offers reproducible results across various proteins, making it well-suited for biomarker discovery24. The proteomic analysis in this study revealed that LOXL2Δ13 impacted several proteins closely associated with lipid metabolism, which corroborates its essential role in metabolic processes. In our previous study, our immunoprecipitation experiments on LOXL2 and LOXL2Δ13, their N-terminal truncates containing the SRCR structural domain and their C-terminal truncates containing the enzyme active center. Both the N- and C-terminal ends of LOXL2 and LOXL2Δ13 interact with the glycolytic enzyme aldolase A and cause deacetylation of their K230 and K13 sites, enhancing aldolase A9. By increasing the enzyme activity of aldolase A, activating cellular metabolic reprogramming, and increasing cellular glycolysis, ATP and lipids accumulate, promoting malignant transformation and cell division and proliferation9. Similarly, LOXL3, another LOX family member, functions as a nucleus-associated deacetylase, utilizing its SRCR structural domain, to inhibit STAT3 transcription25. These findings collectively underscore the substantial role of LOXL2Δ13 in protein acetylation modification. Consistent with this perspective, our study demonstrated that LOXL2Δ13 impacted the acetylation status of multiple proteins linked to glycolysis, TCA cycles, and lipid metabolism. Notably, LOXL2Δ13’s interaction with the cytosolic microfilament backbone protein F-actin and its association with binding proteins like Ezrin and Fascin indicates its role in cytoskeletal dynamics8. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed that LOXL2 and LOXL2Δ13 can activate Ezrin by recruiting PKCα to phosphorylate the T567 site of Ezrin protein, which in turn regulates cytoskeletal rearrangement and promotes invasion and metastasis of esophageal cancer cells9. Here, in addition to changed phosphorylation modification levels, kinase prediction results also showed changes in related kinase activities, which were closely related to lipid metabolism. This indicated that LOXL2Δ13 affects protein kinase or phosphatase activities, thus regulating lipid metabolic pathways and metabolic flow (Supplementary Fig. 7B).

While our integrated omics analysis and protein interaction mapping provide strong evidence for the involvement of LOXL2Δ13 in modulating lipid metabolism through key post-translational modifications, we recognize that many of these findings are associative in nature. Molecular docking simulations suggest potential structural interfaces but do not confirm functional regulatory roles. Future work involving site-directed mutagenesis, enzymatic activity assays, and cellular rescue models will be critical to validate the biological consequences of the identified phosphorylation and acetylation events. These experiments will strengthen the mechanistic understanding of how LOXL2Δ13 modulates lipid flux and metabolic phenotypes.

In conclusion, we have identified four proteins related to lipid metabolism affected by LOXL2Δ13 based on integrated multi-omics analysis, transgenic mouse modeling, and co-IP validation. However, we acknowledge that the current study lacks mechanistic in vitro experiments such as LOXL2Δ13 knockdown/knockout, rescue assays, and metabolic flux analyzes. Additionally, our LC-MS/MS analysis employed high-resolution mass spectrometry and rigorous spectral library matching to annotate metabolites, we did not systematically confirm these annotations using authentic chemical standards. Future targeted metabolomics experiments using reference standards will be essential to validate these key metabolic features with Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) Level1 confidence. Despite the lack of systematic standard confirmation, the observed metabolic changes are consistent with known pathophysiological alterations in lipid metabolism in LOXL2Δ13 mice. These will be crucial next steps to validate causality and dissect the precise pathways through which LOXL2Δ13 regulates lipid metabolism. The evidence presented here lays a strong foundation for such future investigations and highlights the biological significance of LOXL2Δ13 in metabolic regulation.

Methods

Ethics statement

Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Review of the Animal Management Center of Shantou University (Ethics Review Number: SUMC2019-404). The Guangdong Provincial Animal Test Certificate was applied to all animal experiments (No.00258438), and all the tests were performed referring to the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for Animal Research (Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, NIH Publication No. 8023, revised 1996).

Animals

The LOXL2∆13 overexpression mice used in this study have been described previously9. Herein, two cohorts of mice were analyzed. The first cohort consisted of 12 male SPF-grade C57BL/J mice (NWT = 6, NLOXL2∆13 = 6). All mice were fed the same regular diet (RD). Histone modification profiling, transcriptome analysis, and untargeted metabolome sequencing were performed on their liver tissues at week 16 (Supplementary Table 1). The second cohort of mice was used to explore whether LOXL2∆13 affects changes in protein levels in 16 mice (NWT = 8, NLOXL2∆13 = 8) which were also fed the same regular diet. We then collected their liver tissues at week 16 for proteomic (NWT = 8, NLOXL2∆13 = 8), protein post-translational modification analysis (NWT = 8, NLOXL2∆13 = 8), and immunoprecipitation analysis (NWT = 4, NLOXL2∆13 = 4) (Supplementary Table 2).

Transcriptome

Sample extraction and pre-processing were performed as previously described26. Quality control and pre-processing of the raw data were performed using FastQC and TrimGalore software, and the expression counts for each gene were computed utilizing the STAR software. Differential analysis of these genes was conducted using the EdgeR R package. Furthermore, based on the count number, we also calculated the RPKM expression value of each gene, which in total encompassed the expression levels of 13,740 genes.

Metabolome

The liver sample extraction and processing procedures were performed as previously described27. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was used for untargeted metabolomic analysis and data were acquired using a high-resolution mass spectrometer Xevo G2 XS QTOF (Waters, UK) in positive and negative ion mode, respectively. Metabolite extraction of the samples by organic reagent protein precipitation method and simultaneous preparation of quality control (QC) samples (mixing of equal quantities of the prepared samples), the extracted samples were tested on the machine, and 10 QC samples were used to equilibrate the instrument (to monitor the status of the instrument during liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) detection). All samples were acquired by the LC-MS system followed machine orders. Firstly, all chromatographic separations were performed using an ultraperformance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Waters, UK). An ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters, UK) was used for the reversed-phase separation. The column oven was maintained at 50 °C. The flow rate was 0.4 ml/min and the mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water + 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid). Gradient elution conditions were set as follows: 0~2 min, 100% phase A; 2~11 min, 0% to 100% B; 11~13 min, 100% B; 13~15 min, 0% to 100%A. The injection volume for each sample was 10 μl. A high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer Xevo G2 XS QTOF (Waters, UK) was used to detect metabolites eluted form the column. The Q-TOF was operated in both positive and negative ion modes. For positive ion mode, the capillary and sampling cone voltages were set at 3.0 kV and 40.0 V, respectively. For negative ion mode, the capillary and sampling cone voltages were set at 2.0 kV and 40.0 V, respectively. The mass spectrometry data were acquired in Centroid MSE mode. The TOF mass range was from 50 to 1200 Da and the scan time was 0.2 s. For the MS/MS detection, all precursors were fragmented using 20–40 eV, and the scan time was 0.2 s. During the acquisition, the LE signal was acquired every 3 s to calibrate the mass accuracy. Furthermore, in order to evaluate the stability of the LC-MS during the whole acquisition, a quality control sample (Pool of all samples) was acquired after every 10 samples

Subsequently, data preprocessing was accomplished using metaX software, including the use of the KNN method on the extracted data for missing value filling, removing low-quality separators (separators with more than 50% missing in the QC samples or more than 80% missing in the actual samples), and using QC-RSC (Quality control-based robust LOESS signal correction) method to correction. The corrected data were filtered by removing ions with relative standard deviation > 30% from all QC samples. PCA plots including QC samples were used to verify clustering and reproducibility (Supplementary Fig. S8). Compound Discoverer 2.1 software was used to analyze the obtained LC-MS/MS data, including peak extraction, metabolite identification, and peak comparison. Metabolite identification and quantification were performed using Compound Discoverer 2.1 software integrated with KEGG and HMDB databases. MS data were acquired at a mass accuracy of <10 ppm, and MS/MS spectra were matched with library entries using a similarity score ≥0.85. Here, the total number of MS ions identified was 5748 (MS data refers to precursor ion information obtained after adduct formation (e.g., H+, NH4+, or COOH–) at the ion source, and the MS identification count (n = 5748) indicates the number of features annotated based on database matching of these precursor ions). The number of MS2 ions identified was 3151 (MS2 refers to fragment ion information generated by fragmentation of precursor ions in the mass spectrometer, and the count (n = 3151; details in Supplementary Table 3) represents the number of features for which both precursor ion and fragmentation spectra matched entries in reference libraries. According to MSI criteria, these features correspond to Level 2). The ions were subsequently merged and compared against public databases (KEGG and HMDB), resulting in the tentative annotation of 22,882 metabolic features. All lipid identifications follow the LSI guidelines. Fatty acyl sn-positions and double bond locations were not determined with our analytical setup. Accordingly, lipids are reported at the class, molecular species, or sum composition level. An underscore “_” is used to indicate that sn-positions are unresolved (e.g., PC(16:0_18:1)). All annotations are assigned MSI/LSI Levels 1–4 as listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Further analyzes were conducted using the MetabolAnalystR R package. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test, fold-change analysis, PCA, and PLS-DA were used to select abnormal metabolites. Metabolites with an adjusted P < 0.05, absolute log2fold-change > 0.585, and VIP ≥ 1 were considered as SAMs (N = 530). SAMs were then mapped to their corresponding enzyme‑encoding genes (N = 277) via KEGG annotations.

Histone modifications

The detailed steps of histone extraction from liver tissues were performed as previously described28. The process involved subsequent propionic anhydride derivatization and Trypsin enzyme digestion after histone extraction from mouse liver tissue, followed by mass spectrometry in DIA mode, data retrieval and quantification.

TMT (tandem mass tags) quantitative proteomics

Sample preparation

The mouse liver tissue samples were processed according to the Filter Aided Sample Preparation method as described previously29,30. Briefly, the tryptic peptides were desalted using StageTips and then lyophilized. Subsequently, they were labeled with TMTpro 16plex (Pierce) reagents according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Afterward, the labeled peptides were off-line fractionated by bRP using a Waters XBridge BEH C18 5 μm 4.6 × 250 mm column on an Ultimate 3000 high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Dionex) operating at 1 mL/min. Peptides were separated by a linear gradient from 5 to 40% B (5 mM ammonium formate, 90% ACN) in 90 min followed by a linear increase to 70% B in 6 min. A total of 96 fractions were collected. The 5% of 96 fractions were concatenated to 32 fractions for proteome analysis, and the 95% of 96 fractions concatenated to 8 fractions for phosphopeptide enrichment by using Fe-NTA kit (Thermo Scientific, A32992) according to the manufacturer’s instruction, all the peptides were lyophilized for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Proteomic and phosphoproteomic LC-MS/MS analysis

MS experiments were performed on Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid (FA) and separated on an analytical column (75 μm × 25 cm) packed with 2 μm C18 beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a linear gradient ranging from 9% to 40% B (80% ACN and 0.1% FA) in 100 mins and followed by a linear increase to 50% B in 20 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The MS was operated in data-dependent acquisition mode. The spray voltage was set at 2.2 kV and the temperature of ion transfer capillary was 300 °C. The MS spectra (350–1500 m/z) were collected with 60,000 resolution, AGC of 4 × 105 and 50 ms maximal injection time. Selected ions were sequentially fragmented by HCD with 38% normalized collision energy in a 3 s cycle, specified isolated windows 0.7 m/z, 50,000 resolutions. AGC of 1 × 105 and 105 ms maximal injection time were used. Dynamic exclusion was set to 30 s.

MS data analysis

The data were collected using Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, version 3.0). Raw data was processed using Proteome Discoverer (PD, version 2.4), and MS/MS spectra were searched against the Uniprot human proteome database. Search parameters were as follows: 20 ppm tolerance of precursor mass error, 0.02 Da tolerance of fragment mass error, variable modification for oxidation (Met) (+15.9949 Da), TMTpro (Lys) (304.207 Da), phosphorylation (Ser, Thr, Tyr) (+79.966 Da) and acetylation (protein N-terminus) (+42.0106 Da), carbamidomethylation (Cys) (+57.0215 Da), TMTpro (N-terminal) (304.207 Da) as fixed modification. The peptide and protein identifications were filtered by PD to control the false discovery rate (FDR) < 1%. At least one unique peptide was required for protein identification.

To mitigate systematic, sample-specific bias in the quantification of protein levels, the protein expression ratios were log2-transformed and normalized using the mean centering method across all proteins. In the normalized samples, the proteins should have a log2-transformed expression ratio centered at zero.

Acetylome

Sample preparation

50 mg of each mouse liver tissue was lysed with ten volumes of modified SDT buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCL, pH 7.6, 0.1 M DTT, 3% SDS, 1% SDC) and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min.The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 20 °C. The supernatant was transferred to ultrafiltration units (Millipore, Amicon Ultra 15 Ultracel 10 KD) and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 40 min. After centrifugation, the concentrates were mixed with 2 mL of 50 mM iodoacetamide in UA solution (8 M urea, 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5) and incubated in darkness at room temperature (RT) for 30 min followed by centrifugation for 30 min. After alkylation, the filter units were washed four times with 10 ml UA buffer and two times 10 mL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate by centrifugation at 4000 × g. Proteins were then digested with Lys-C (1:100, w/w, Wako) for 6 h at 37 °C and trypsin (1:50, w/w, Promega) overnight at 37 °C. The resulting peptide mixture was acidified with formic acid, loaded onto SepPak tC18 cartridges (Waters), desalted and eluted with 50% acetonitrile. The eluted peptides were lyophilized. After lyophilization, the peptides were dissolved by 600 μl 1×PTMScan IAP Buffer (CST). PTMScan Acetyl-Lysine Motif immunoaffinity beads (CST) were incubated with dissolved peptides for 6 h at 4 °C, then the immunoprecipitates were washed three times in ice-cold immunoprecipitation buffer followed by three washes in water, and modified peptides were eluted with 2 × 50 μl of 0.2% trifluoroacetic acid in Milli-Q water. Peptide eluates were desalted on reversed-phase C18 StageTips and dried by speed vacuum at 45 °C.

LC-MS/MS analysis

MS experiments were performed on a nanoscale EASYnLC 1200UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) connected to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos equipped with a nanoelectrospray source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mobile phase A contained 0.1% formic acid (v/v) in water; mobile phase B contained 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile (ACN). The peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid (FA) with 2% acetonitrile and separated on a RP-HPLC analytical column (75 μm × 25 cm) packed with 2 μm C18 beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a linear gradient ranging from 8% to 30% ACN in 90 min and followed by a linear increase to 44% B in 20 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The Orbitrap Fusion Lumos acquired data in a data-dependent manner alternating between full-scan MS and MS2 scans. The spray voltage was set at 2.2 kV and the temperature of ion transfer capillary was 300 °C. The MS spectra (350–1500 m/z) were collected with 120,000 resolutions, AGC of 4 × 105, and 50 ms maximal injection time. Selected ions were sequentially fragmented in a 3 seconds (s) cycle by HCD with 30% normalized collision energy, specified isolated windows 1.6 m/z, and 30,000 resolutions. AGC of 5 × 104 and 150 ms maximal injection time were used. Dynamic exclusion was set to 15 s. Unassigned ions or those with a charge of 1+ and >7+ were rejected for MS/MS.

MS data analysis

The data were collected using Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, version 3.0). Raw data was processed using Proteome Discoverer (PD, version 2.4), and MS/MS spectra were searched against the uniport human proteome database. Search parameters were as follows: 20 ppm tolerance of precursor mass error, 0.02 Da tolerance of fragment mass error, variable modification for oxidation (Met) (+15.9949 Da), acetylation (Lys) (42.0106 Da) and acetylation (protein N-terminus) (+42.0106 Da), carbamidomethylation (Cys) (+57.0215 Da) as fixed modification. The peptide and protein identifications were filtered by PD to control the FDR < 1%. At least one unique peptide was required for protein identification.

Protein post-translational modifications abundance

The abundance of phosphorylated modifications for each protein was calculated by combining the three classes of amino acid sites from the eluted phosphopeptides (S: serine; T: threonine; Y: tyrosine). The abundance of phosphorylation at a specific site was determined by calculating the median abundance across all phosphopeptides corresponding that site. To normalize phosphorylated protein expression, we utilized the limma R package (version 3.42.2)31. Missing values for each phosphosite were imputed by assigning the global minimum non-zero intensity observed across all quantified phosphopeptides, thereby modeling values below the detection limit32.

Similarly, acetylation modifications were represented by the cumulative abundance of the two amino acid sites (K: lysine; N: N-terminal) within the eluted acetylated peptides. The median abundance of acetylated peptides associated with a particular site was used to quantify the acetylation at that site. Data normalization and management of missing values for acetylation followed the same approach as phosphorylation.

This study included measurements of 17,577 phosphorylation modification sites and 2154 acetylation modification sites.

Immunoprecipitation

We performed Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) experiments on liver tissues from WT mice and LOXL2∆13-overexpressing mice (with Flag tags) to isolate proteins interacting specifically with LOXL2∆13 and identify critical target proteins. The pre-processing procedures, encompassing proteomic sample extraction and subsequent on-board processing, were performed as described in a previous study29,30. Briefly, 50 mg wild-type (WT) and LOXL2Δ13-Flag knock in mice liver tissues were dissected for each Immunoprecipitation-Mass Spectrometry (IP-MS) assay. Mouse liver tissue was washed three times with pre-cooled PBS, 1 mL EBC buffer (50.0 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 120.0 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1×protease inhibitors) was added, and chopped with scissors on ice. Transfer the tissue suspension to a Dounce Homogenizer and grind thoroughly on ice until there are no tissue fragments. Transfer the solution to a centrifuge tube and centrifuge at 1300 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was taken and added to the Pierce Anti-DYKDDDDK Magnetic Agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then incubated at 4 °C overnight, the next day IP samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and then the gel slice containing proteins was cut off. Decolorization and dehydration with ACN were used to treat the gel.

For immunoprecipitation assay, the liver tissue was pre-treated as described above. Briefly, 20 mg wild-type (WT) and LOXL2Δ13-Flag knock in mice liver tissues was ground and centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was added to the beads and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The eluted IP samples were used to detect target proteins by Western blot.

Bioinformatics analysis

Difference analysis

Wilcoxon tests were conducted on the proteome, protein phosphorylation, and acetylation profiles to assess aberrantly altered proteins and protein modification sites in LOXL2∆13 mice. All p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. DEPs were defined by a FDR threshold below 0.15 and an absolute logFC value greater than 0.263 (1.2-fold). The FDR threshold of less than 0.15 was applied to identify differential protein modification sites.

Weighted co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The WGCNA is a computational approach to investigate associations between molecules (including coding genes, non-coding genes, proteins, etc.) and clinical or phenotypic attributes. Using the WGCNA algorithm, molecules can be organized into co-expression modules, enabling the computation of connections between phenotypic traits and molecules. Initially, co-expression modules are constructed for proteins exhibiting differential expression and sites displaying differential modifications, employing the WGCNA R package. Metrics such as Molecular Significance (GS) and Module Membership (MM) are computed to establish correlations between molecular modules and mouse phenotypic characteristics. The pivotal nodes within these modules are visually represented and subjected to enrichment for network analysis purposes.

Protein kinase activity prediction

The regulation of kinases is essential for understanding numerous biological signaling processes as well as the molecular pathogenesis of various diseases. The increasing interest in bioinformatic tools for calculating changes in kinase activity from complex datasets has arisen due to the development of mass spectrometry-based high-throughput platforms for phosphorylated proteomics. These tools can provide researchers with insights into the mechanisms of action of molecules. Several algorithms offer such analyzes, including IKAP33, KinasePA34, CLUE35, KEA36, and KSEA37. The IKAP platform currently relies on MATLAB, necessitating programming skills from users. KinasePA and CLUE are limited to multi-conditioning studies, while KEA focuses on substrate over-expression rather than kinase scoring. KSEA scores each kinase based on the relative hyper- or dephosphorylation of most of its substrates. This study used the KSEA R package to predict LOXL2∆13-affected kinases based on phosphorylation histology data.

Enrichment analysis

Using the clusterProfile R package and the Metascape enrichment tool, we conducted enrichment and analysis of differential genes, differential proteins, and proteins undergoing differential modifications. The background functional datasets encompassed GO and KEGG pathways. Enrichment results were considered significant if the FDR was less than 0.05, serving as the threshold for determining enriched pathways and terms.

Sorting algorithm: order statistic

To comprehensively integrate multiple dimensions of high-throughput datasets and effectively rank genes/proteins affected by LOXL2∆13, we formulated a gene/protein ranking framework based on the Order statistic. This innovative framework aggregates N distinct sets of gene/protein rankings, resulting in a cohesive and integrated gene/protein ranking38,39. For this analysis, five dimensions of protein/gene ranking datasets were considered.

Subcellular localization analysis

Proteins and genes are distributed across various cellular compartments, including the membrane, plasma and nucleus, as well as organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. The specific subcellular location of a protein often corresponds to its distinct functions, making subcellular localization analysis a valuable aspect for gaining initial functional insights. To further determine the underlying mechanism of LOXL2∆13, we conducted a comprehensive subcellular localization analysis on its critical proteins to provide preliminary understanding of the functions associated with these proteins using. The Metascape tool, a powerful resource for assessing protein localization, which employs a combination of experimentally validated and algorithmically predicted subcellular localization data from the UniProt database.

Interaction mass spectrometry analysis

We performed Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) experiments on liver tissues from WT mice and LOXL2∆13-overexpressing mice (with Flag tags) to isolate proteins interacting specifically with LOXL2∆13 and identify critical target proteins. Further details of these experiments can be found in our prior study30.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

Through molecular simulation docking, we predicted interactions between LOXL2∆13 and key proteins, building upon insights from mass spectrometry outcomes. As LOXL2∆13 lacks an established molecular structure, we initiated the process by homology modeling based on the structure of LOXL2. The precursor structure of LOXL2 in the PDB database (PDB ID: 5ZE341) served as a template. Additionally, we leveraged both PDB database structures and AlphaFold2 predictions, a recognized resource for molecular structures. These inputs facilitated the homology modeling of LOXL2∆13’s distinct protein structure, accomplished using the Modeler software. Simultaneously, we accessed structures of eight vital proteins (Canx, Pdia3, Pklr, Sec61b, Acadvl, Acat1, Itpr1, and Tat) from the PDB database and AlphaFold2. We refined these structures using Discovery Studio software to exclude non-protein elements, such as small-molecule drug structures. To obtain LOXL2∆13 complexes bound to these 8 proteins, we performed docking calculations using HDCOK docking software42. In HDOCK, we used the automatic search mode and default parameters for all docking calculations. In this mode, the receptor is held fixed while the ligand is globally sampled using a translational grid spacing of 1.2 Å and a rotational sampling interval of 15° (4392 total Euler orientations), with fast Fourier transform–accelerated scoring. For each orientation, the top 10 translations are rescored and locally optimized. The best docking model was selected from 10 docking results for each protein.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data analysis was performed using the R (4.3.2) and GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) software. Unpaired t-test was used to assess the differences between groups, and the results were marked. For all omics datasets, multiple hypothesis testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the FDR. Unless otherwise stated, an FDR < 0.05 was used to define statistical significance (metabolomics, transcriptome, multi-omics integration). Another threshold (FDR < 0.15) was consistently applied across proteomics, phosphorylation, and acetylation datasets. In the context of multi-omics integration, all candidate features included in the Orderstatistic and RankAggregate algorithms were pre-filtered using their corresponding FDR values to reduce the risk of false positives.
