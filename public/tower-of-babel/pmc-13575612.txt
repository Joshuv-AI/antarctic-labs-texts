Body Mass Index Outperforms Triglyceride-Glucose Index in Predicting MAFLD in Type 2 Diabetes: A Cross-Sectional Mediation and Interaction Analysis
Authors: Zhenzhen Sun, Li Yang, Nan Wang, Jie Wang, Xiaozhen Zhu, Xiqiao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe interplay between body mass index (BMI) and triglyceride-glucose (TyG) has yet to be systematically investigated on metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This knowledge gap regarding the specific nature of their combined effects limits our ability to develop precise risk stratification algorithms and targeted intervention strategies.PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the independent and synergistic roles of BMI and TyG in MAFLD development among T2DM patients.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 816 T2DM patients recruited from the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. MAFLD was diagnosed via Fibroscan. Dose-response relationships were assessed using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Multiplicative and additive interactions were evaluated using logistic regression models. Causal mediation analysis quantified the proportion of the BMI-MAFLD association mediated by TyG. Predictive performance was compared using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsAmong 816 participants, 528 (64.7%) had MAFLD. RCS confirmed linear positive associations of BMI and TyG with MAFLD. Compared to those with BMI <24 kg/m2, patients with BMI ≥28 kg/m2 had an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 6.94 (95% CI: 2.59–18.6). The highest TyG quartile had an aOR of 3.23 (95% CI: 1.08–9.64) vs the lowest. Mediation analysis suggested that TyG explained 7.5% of the observed association between BMI and MAFLD. Significant multiplicative interaction was observed (P < 0.05), whereas additive interaction was not significant. ROC analysis demonstrated that TyG had inferior discriminative ability (AUC: 0.679) compared to BMI (AUC: 0.784), with combined models (AUC: 0.796) providing minimal incremental value over BMI alone in identifying MAFLD.ConclusionIn T2DM, BMI exhibited a stronger association with MAFLD risk than TyG, with TyG accounting for a portion of this association but providing limited incremental improvement in discriminative ability.Keywords: metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, type 2 diabetes, body mass index, triglyceride glucose index, interaction, mediation

Background

Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), wherein patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and imaging-confirmed hepatic steatosis are considered to have MAFLD per the 2020 international consensus,1 is a critical comorbidity affecting up to 65% of T2DM patients2 and is projected to be the leading cause of liver cirrhosis.3 Beyond hepatic injury, this ectopic lipid deposition drives systemic metabolic dysfunction, exacerbating insulin resistance, hastening β-cell failure, and increasing cardiovascular mortality through a bidirectional pathophysiological loop,4–6 posing a significant challenge to diabetes management. Critically, MAFLD is typically asymptomatic until the advanced stages,7 underscoring the urgent need for precise, non-invasive risk stratification tools tailored to this high-risk population.8

Two readily available clinical metrics, the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) and body mass index (BMI), are central to MAFLD risk assessment.9–11 Biologically, the TyG index reflects the status of insulin resistance (IR) driven by dysfunctional adipose tissue.12 Impaired suppression of lipolysis in hypertrophic adipocytes floods the portal system with free fatty acids (FFAs); subsequent hepatic FFA influx promotes diacylglycerol synthesis and protein kinase C isoform activation, ultimately inhibiting insulin receptor signaling cascades and promoting steatosis.13,14 Given this mechanistic interplay between adiposity and IR, translating these biological insights into precise clinical risk stratification remains a challenge.

Although composite indices like TyG-BMI have been proposed,15,16 it remains unknown whether BMI and TyG exert additive effects (simple sum) or multiplicative synergy (biological interaction), nor has the proportion of the BMI–MAFLD association mediated by TyG been quantified in T2DM populations. This uncertainty is particularly relevant for non-obese MAFLD (lean MAFLD), a distinct phenotype characterized by the absence of overt obesity, potentially involving metabolic drivers beyond adiposity, such as genetic variants including PNPLA3-I148M.17 Addressing these gaps is essential for refining risk stratification and targeted interventions, particularly in patients with non-obese MAFLD where metabolic drivers may differ from those in obese individuals.

To address these gaps, this study systematically investigates the multifaceted roles of BMI and TyG in MAFLD among T2DM patients. Specifically, we aim to: (1) evaluate independent and interactive effects of BMI and TyG on MAFLD risk, distinguishing multiplicative interaction (statistical effect modification) from additive interaction (true biological synergy and public health impact, measured by RERI, AP, and SI); (2) quantify the proportion of the BMI–MAFLD association mediated by TyG; (3) compare the predictive performance of BMI, TyG, and their combinations for MAFLD screening. By integrating interaction, mediation, and discrimination analyses, our findings are intended to inform precise risk stratification and targeted intervention strategies for MAFLD in diabetes care.

Methods

Study Population

This was a cross-sectional study carried out in the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, and participants were selected among T2DM who visited the endocrinology departments from November 2021 to August 2024. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age<18 years; (2) a history of excessive alcohol intake (defined as >30 g/day for men and >20 g/day for women) to avoid confounding by alcoholic liver disease and ensure that the observed interactions between BMI and TyG were attributable solely to metabolic dysfunction; (3) any history of liver diseases (such as hepatitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, hepatic cysts, or masses) or other specific etiologies known to cause fatty liver; (4) combined with malignant tumors; (5) pregnancy or lactation; (6) recent use of hepatotoxic or nephrotoxic medications; or (7) insufficient data for the calculation of required study indicators. Finally, 816 participants were included in this study. The current study was carried out under Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (approval number: 2025 NL-080-02).

Data Collection and Definition

Data on demographic information (age, sex, height, weight, smoking and drinking status) and health-related variables (clinical history and medication use) were obtained from medical records. Smoking status was dichotomized as a current smoker or never/former smoker. Drinking status was recorded as the daily amount of alcohol consumed. Body mass index (BMI) was computed as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).

After fasting blood collection, the levels of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), uric acid (UA) and platelets were measured by the automatic biochemical analyzer of the Laboratory Department of Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. TyG=Ln [TG (mg/dL) * FPG (mg/dL)/2], TyG -BMI=TyG * BMI.

Evaluation of Hepatic Steatosis

Fibroscan (Echosens, Paris, France) was used to assess the extent of liver steatosis by measuring the CAP values. Trained and manufacturer-certified nurses performed all examinations. Participants underwent a minimum 3-hour fast beforehand. For the procedure, subjects were positioned supine with the right arm elevated above the head to maximize exposure of the liver region. Measurements were taken from the 7th to 9th intercostal spaces along the anterior to midaxillary lines. Probe selection was performed manually by the operator based on the skin-to-liver capsule distance (SCD). The XL probe was preferentially used for patients with an SCD > 25 mm to ensure adequate ultrasound penetration and avoid the underestimation of CAP values in individuals with a thick subcutaneous fat layer. The reported result represents the median of 10 valid measurements. The principle of controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) measurement has been described elsewhere.18 Patients were considered to have steatosis based on Fibroscan if CAP ≥274 dB/m.19–21

Statistical Analysis

The descriptive statistics reported as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables and group comparisons were performed using independent t-test. Non-normally distributed variables described as median (IQR), and group comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and were assessed using the Fisher exact test for expected frequencies <5; otherwise, we used the Chi-squared test.

Firstly, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to model the dose-response relationships. Three knots were placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the exposure distributions (BMI and TyG) to capture potential non-linearities. We used binary logistic regression analysis to evaluate the association of MAFLD associated with BMI (categorized as <24.0 kg/m2, 24.0–27.9 kg/m2, and ≥28.0 kg/m2) and TyG (categorized as quartiles). To avoid biased estimates due to multicollinearity, a preliminary collinearity diagnosis was conducted for all candidate covariates using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). A VIF threshold of 5 was applied as the criterion for acceptable collinearity (VIF < 5 indicates no severe collinearity). Variables with VIF > 5 were either excluded or theoretically justified for retention. Three models were estimated: in model 1, no variables were adjusted; in model 2, age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes duration, medication use (including antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, SGLT2 inhibitors, thiazolidinediones, GLP-1 receptor agonists, insulin, and other oral antidiabetic drugs), and history of hypertension, dyslipidemia were adjusted; and in model 3, the variables in model 2 plus HbA1c, HDL-C, LDL-C, ALT, AST and GGT were adjusted. As shown in Table S1, all VIF values were below the threshold of 5 (tolerance > 0.2), indicating no severe multicollinearity issues in the regression models.

The interaction effects were assessed using three measures: relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), proportion attributable to interaction (AP), and the synergy index (SI), computed based on established methods.22 A significant interaction between BMI and TyG was determined if the 95% confidence intervals for RERI and AP did not include 0, and that for SI did not include 1. Specifically, values of RERI > 0, AP > 0, and SI > 1 indicate a synergistic effect, meaning the combined influence of BMI and TyG on MAFLD incidence exceeds the sum of their individual effects.23

Meanwhile, a mediation analysis was carried out to examine the mediating effect of TyG in the association of BMI with MAFLD, using the “mediation” package in R version 4.2.2. The analysis included 1000 simulations to estimate the total, direct, and indirect effects, along with the proportion mediated. Bootstrapping was used for significance testing for mediation analysis.

Finally, we assessed the performance of BMI, TyG, TyG-BMI index, and TyG+BMI by evaluating their discriminative capabilities using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and calculating the area under the ROC curve (AUC). When AUC is less than 0.5, it indicates that the discrimination of the model is extremely low, which does not conform to the real situation.

All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.2), at a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of 816 T2DM stratified by hepatic steatosis status. The cohort comprised 70.1% males and 29.9% females, and there was no significant diabetes duration between the two groups. T2DM with MAFLD (n=528) were significantly younger, had higher BMI, and greater prevalence of hyperlipidemia compared to those without MAFLD (n=288). Significant metabolic differences included higher TyG index, elevated liver enzymes (ALT: 33 vs 21 U/L; AST: 24 vs 18 U/L; GGT: 41 vs 23 U/L, all p<0.001), and unfavorable lipid profiles (higher TG: 1.88 vs 1.26 mmol/L; lower HDL-C: 1.20 vs 1.35 mmol/L, both p<0.001) in the MAFLD group. Besides, liver stiffness measurements were significantly higher in participants with MAFLD, and glycemic parameters showed modest but significant elevations in the steatosis group (all P <0.05).

Association of BMI and TyG with MAFLD

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis evaluated dose-response relationships between BMI, TyG, and MAFLD risk (Figure 1). For BMI, a significant positive association was observed (P-overall < 0.001), with a steady, linear increase in risk across the range (P-nonlinear = 0.962). For TyG, the overall association was also significant (P-overall = 0.015). The curve showed a monotonic upward trend with a changing slope: steeper at lower TyG levels, flattening in the mid-range, and ascending gently at higher values. However, this non-linear pattern was not statistically significant (P-nonlinear = 0.225).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis of the association between (A) BMI and (B) TyG index with MAFLD. Knots were placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the exposure distributions. The vertical dashed line indicates the reference value (median: BMI = 25.6 kg/m2, TyG = 7.5). Adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were derived from Model 3.

Subsequently, we stratified TyG and BMI, and used binary logistic regression to separately explore the relationship between these indicators and MAFLD under different levels of TyG and BMI. The results show that after adjusting for potential confounders (model 3), by comparing quartile 4 of TyG with quartile 1, the adjusted OR was 3.23 (95% CI: 1.08–9.64) for MAFLD; by comparing BMI≥28.0 kg/m2 with BMI<24.0 kg/m2, the adjusted OR was 6.94 (95% CI: 2.59–18.6) for MAFLD (Figure 2).

The TyG and BMI as Predictive Indicators for MAFLD

ROC curve analysis was performed with MAFLD as the state variable and BMI, TyG, TyG+BMI, and TyG-BMI as test variables. The AUCs of the four indicators were 0.784, 0.679, 0.796 and 0.792 (Figure 3). Subgroup Analysis

Stratified analysis revealed that the association between TyG and MAFLD was modified by adiposity (Figure 4). In the obesity group (BMI ≥28.0 kg/m2), higher TyG quartiles were significantly associated with elevated odds of MAFLD (all P < 0.05). In the overweight group (BMI 24.0–27.9 kg/m2), a significant association was observed only in the highest quartile (P = 0.001). No significant associations were found among participants with normal weight (BMI < 24.0 kg/m2).

Stratified analysis of the TyG-MAFLD association by BMI categories. All models were adjusted for the variables included in Model 3.

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of the main findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding participants with extreme obesity (BMI > 35 kg/m2), resulting in a final analytic cohort of 767 patients with T2DM. In fully adjusted Model 3, higher BMI remained strongly associated with MAFLD (≥28 kg/m2 vs <24 kg/m2: OR = 7.29, 95% CI: 2.66–19.95), whereas the TyG index showed a weaker and less consistent association (Q4 vs Q1: OR = 2.28, 95% CI: 0.89–5.88) (Table S2). Multiplicative interaction between BMI and TyG remained statistically significant, whereas additive interaction was not observed (AP = 0.300, 95% CI: −0.239 to 0.838; RERI = 1.501, 95% CI: −2.124 to 5.171; Table S3). Furthermore, mediation analysis showed that TyG accounted for 8.2% (95% CI: 1.5–20.9%) of the total effect of BMI on MAFLD (Table S4).These findings indicate that the main results are stable and not unduly influenced by individuals with extreme BMI values.

Discussion

This multi-dimensional analysis characterized the distinct yet correlated associations of BMI and TyG with MAFLD in T2DM, integrating findings across dose-response relationships, statistical mediation, interaction effects, and discriminative performance. Restricted cubic spline models demonstrated positive linear associations between both BMI and TyG with MAFLD. Notably, participants with severe obesity (BMI ≥28 kg/m2) exhibited a significantly higher odds of MAFLD (aOR=6.94) compared to those with lower BMI, a magnitude greater than the maximal association observed for TyG (aOR=3.23 for Q4 vs Q1). This gradient underscores the strong cross-sectional association between elevated BMI and MAFLD, which may be related to pathways involving adipose-derived lipotoxic metabolites24,25 and altered hepatokine secretion.26

The finding that TyG mediated only 7.5% of the association between BMI and MAFLD might seem counterintuitive, yet it aligns with the complex, multi-hit pathophysiology of MAFLD. Firstly, BMI serves as a macroscopic indicator of total adiposity, capturing pathogenic pathways that extend beyond glucolipotoxicity. When adipose tissue exceeds its capacity for adaptive expansion, a massive flux of free fatty acids occurs directly through the portal system.27,28 This “lipid overflow” phenomenon overwhelms hepatic beta-oxidation capacity, leading to de novo lipogenesis and macrovacuolar steatosis due to physical overload,29,30 effectively bypassing the systemic insulin resistance reflected by the TyG index. Secondly, excess adiposity induces a chronic systemic pro-inflammatory state characterized by altered gut microbiota and increased intestinal permeability.31 This allows the translocation of bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS) into the portal circulation.32 LPS activates Kupffer cells via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR-4), triggering a cascade of inflammatory cytokines (eg, TNF-α, IL-6).33 This immunometabolic axis promotes the progression from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis and mitochondrial dysfunction—processes that are largely independent of systemic insulin resistance and thus undetectable by the TyG index.

Intriguingly, while multiplicative interaction analysis identified significant effect modification between BMI and TyG, no significant additive interaction was observed. This discordance between scales offers critical insights for risk stratification and intervention. The multiplicative interaction helps identify individuals with concurrent elevations in TyG and BMI as a high-risk subgroup warranting prioritized screening and intensified management (eg, integrated management addressing both glycemic control and weight reduction). Conversely, the lack of additive interaction implies that the absolute risk reduction achieved by simultaneously addressing both factors may align with the sum of benefits derived from targeting each factor independently at a population level. Thus, while clinical precision strategies should prioritize this high-risk subgroup, resource allocation for broader public health initiatives can effectively treat TyG and BMI as independent targets for absolute risk reduction.

The ROC analysis revealed that BMI is a more robust indicator than TyG for discriminating MAFLD risk in T2DM patients. From a clinical utility perspective, this reinforces BMI as a fundamental, low-cost, and universally available tool for initial risk stratification. The present findings align with observations by Zou et al, who systematically assess the diagnostic ability of 12 noninvasive scores for MAFLD, and reported that the TyG index demonstrated the lowest discriminative ability among them.34 Similarly, a cross-sectional survey conducted on the Chinese population also revealed that TyG’s predictive performance for MAFLD was inferior to that of BMI.35 The modest discriminative ability of TyG may be partly explained by its specific focus on glucose-lipid metabolism, which does not capture the broader spectrum of pathophysiological factors implicated in MAFLD, such as inflammatory cytokines or hepatocyte stress responses. Specifically, BMI serves as a macroscopic indicator of adiposity and reflects a wider range of obesity-related metabolic disturbances, such as intestinal barrier dysfunction,36 adipocyte-macrophage crosstalk,37 or mitochondrial inefficiency38—that contribute to hepatic lipid deposition through pathways distinct from those represented by the TyG index. Consequently, combining TyG with BMI improved the model’s performance. While this improvement was statistically significant, the absolute increase in AUC was modest (from 0.784 to 0.796), suggesting limited incremental clinical value. Therefore, in routine clinical practice, measuring TyG may not be necessary for all T2DM patients if BMI is already being monitored. Nevertheless, TyG remains valuable for identifying high-risk subgroups—particularly obese patients with elevated TyG levels—who may benefit from more intensive lifestyle interventions or metabolic profiling, as suggested by the significant multiplicative interaction observed in our study.

Strengths of this study include the rigorous evaluation of additive/multiplicative interactions and mediation effects, providing a holistic view of TyG-BMI interplay. The use of RCS reduced categorization bias in exposure modeling. However, several limitations warrant mention. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; prospective cohorts are needed to validate temporality. Second, hepatic steatosis was diagnosed via non-invasive biomarkers rather than histology, potentially misclassifying early-stage MAFLD. Third, unmeasured confounders (eg, dietary patterns, genetic predisposition) might influence residual bias. Finally, the single-center cohort limits generalizability to diverse ethnic populations.

Conclusion

In this T2DM cohort, BMI exhibited a stronger association with MAFLD than the TyG index, and combining TyG with BMI offered limited incremental improvement in discriminative ability. TyG accounted for 7.5% of the cross-sectional association between BMI and MAFLD. The significant multiplicative interaction suggests that TyG may be useful for risk stratification among obese patients. Although both factors were associated with MAFLD risk, the lack of additive synergism suggests that their combined statistical effect aligns with a multiplicative scale. Future research should validate these findings in prospective cohorts using imaging-based steatosis quantification and explore BMI-integrated prediction algorithms for clinical deployment.
