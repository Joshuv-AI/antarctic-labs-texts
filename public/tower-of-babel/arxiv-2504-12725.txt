The S S -resolvent estimates for the Dirac operator on hyperbolic and spherical spaces
Ivan Beschastnyi, Fabrizio Colombo, Simão Andrade Lucas, Irene Sabadini

Abstract
. This seminal paper marks the beginning of our investigation into on the spectral theory based on S S -spectrum applied to the Dirac operator on manifolds. Specifically, we examine in detail the cases of the Dirac operator 𝒟 H \mathcal{D}_{H} on hyperbolic space and the Dirac operator 𝒟 S \mathcal{D}_{S} on the spherical space, where these operators, and their squares 𝒟 H 2 \mathcal{D}_{H}^{2} and 𝒟 S 2 \mathcal{D}_{S}^{2} , can be written in a very explicit form. This fact is very important for the application of the spectral theory on the S S -spectrum. In fact, let T T denote a (right) linear Clifford operator, the S S -spectrum is associated with a second-order polynomial in the operator T T , specifically the operator defined as Q s ​ ( T ) := T 2 − 2 ​ s 0 ​ T + | s | 2 . Q_{s}(T):=T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2}. This allows us to associate to the Dirac operator boundary conditions that can be of Dirichlet type but also of Robin-like type. Moreover, our theory is not limited to Hilbert modules; it is applicable to Banach modules as well. The spectral theory based on the S S -spectrum has gained increasing attention in recent years, particularly as it aims to provide quaternionic quantum mechanics with a solid mathematical foundation from the perspective of spectral theory. This theory was extended to Clifford operators, and more recently, the spectral theorem has been adapted to this broader context. The S S -spectrum is crucial for defining the so-called S S -functional calculus for quaternionic and Clifford operators in various forms. This includes bounded as well as unbounded operators, where suitable estimates of sectorial and bi-sectorial type for the S S -resolvent operator are essential for the convergence of the Dunford integrals in this setting.

Introduction

This paper represents the onset of our investigation into the spectral theory based on the
S
-spectrum as applied to the Dirac operator on manifolds. In particular, we focus on the Dirac operator in hyperbolic space and spherical space. In these contexts, the Dirac operator
\mathcal{D}_{H}
on hyperbolic space and the Dirac operator
\mathcal{D}_{S}
on spherical space, along with their squares
\mathcal{D}_{H}^{2}
and
\mathcal{D}_{S}^{2}
, can be expressed in a very explicit form. For these specific cases, we provide explicit estimates that indicate the regions where the
S
-spectrum of Dirac operators, under various boundary conditions, is contained, along with suitable estimates for the
S
-resolvent operator.
More in general in this paper we illustrates the applicability of the new spectral theory based on the
S
-spectrum to the Dirac operator on manifolds, establishing a link between spectral theory and differential geometry, thereby giving rise to the
S
-spectral theory in Riemannian geometry.
Our theory on the
S
-spectrum associated with Dirac operators on manifolds works even when these operators are not assumed to be self-adjoint.

As an example we mention that in the context of Clifford algebras, the theory has been recently applied to investigate the spectral properties of the gradient operator with nonconstant coefficients in

Unlike in classical complex spectral theory, the definition of the

This fact is a significant distinction from classical operator theory.

Let

be a Clifford module and by

the denote the space of all bounded right-linear operators

S
-spectrum is grounded in the sum of an analogue of the resolvent series expansion, where the bounded operator

Specifically, we have:

(1.3a)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T^{n}s^{-1-n}=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1}(\overline{s}-T),\qquad|s|>\|T\|,

(1.3b)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{-1-n}T^{n}=(\overline{s}-T)(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1},\qquad|s|>\|T\|,

for

of the form
s=s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}
.
That is, for every

S
-spectrum
and the
S
-resolvent set
of

\rho_{S}(T):=\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\ \ |\ \ Q_{s}(T)^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{S}(T):=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\rho_{S}(T).

For every

, the inverse

is called the

-resolvent operator

A second main difference between complex and
S
-spectral theory lies in the fact that

is not a (operator valued) slice hyperholomorphic function. The operator

is used for the definition of the

and the

These resolvent operators now preserve the slice hyperholomorphicity and are used in the definition of the

Observe that for unbounded operators

Clifford operators that are sectorial, bi-sectorial or strip-operators, can be defined in analogy to complex operators, but in this case the estimates associated with the

\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{C}{|s|}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{C}{|s|},

2.
Functional setting in Clifford modules over
\mathbb{R}_{n}

Clifford algebras are a class of algebras that generalize the concept of complex numbers and quaternions. They are formed from a vector space equipped with a quadratic form, where the key feature is the relation between the basis vectors, typically expressed through anticommutation relations. In the following we denote by
\mathbb{R}_{n}
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 2
the
Clifford algebra generated by
n

imaginary units

e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
which satisfy the relations

e_{i}^{2}=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},\qquad i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

More precisely,
\mathbb{R}_{n}
is given by

(2.1)

\mathbb{R}_{n}:=\left\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}x_{A}e_{A}\ \ |\ \ x_{A}\in\mathbb{R},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\right\},

using the index set

\mathcal{A}:=\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\ |\ r\in\{0,\dots,n\},\ \ 1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq n\},

and the
basis vectors

e_{A}:=e_{i_{1}}\dots e_{i_{r}}
. Note, for
A=\emptyset
the empty product of imaginary units is the real number
e_{\emptyset}:=1
. Moreover, we will consider the set of all
paravectors

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:=\left\{x_{0}+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}e_{i}\ \ |\ \ x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

For any Clifford number
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we define

\displaystyle{\rm Sc}(x)

\displaystyle:=x_{\emptyset}=x_{0},

(scalar part)

\displaystyle\overline{x}

\displaystyle:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}x_{A}\overline{e_{A}},

(conjugate)

\displaystyle|x|

\displaystyle:=\Big(\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}|x_{A}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=({\rm Sc}(x\overline{x}))^{\frac{1}{2}}=({\rm Sc}(\overline{x}x))^{\frac{1}{2}},

(norm)

where
\overline{e_{A}}=\overline{e_{i_{r}}}\dots\overline{e_{i_{1}}}
and
\overline{e_{i}}=-e_{i}
. It is now obvious that for any Clifford number
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
one can calculate its coefficients
x_{A}
by

(2.2)

{\rm Sc}(x\overline{e_{A}})={\rm Sc}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}x_{B}e_{B}\overline{e_{A}}\Big)=\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}x_{B}{\rm Sc}(e_{B}\overline{e_{A}})=x_{A},

where in the last equation we used that

(2.3)

{\rm Sc}(e_{B}\overline{e_{A}})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }B=A,\\
0,&\text{if }B\neq A.\end{cases}

2.1.
Clifford Hilbert modules

Clifford (Hilbert) modules arise in the context of Clifford algebras. Specifically, a Clifford module is a vector space equipped with a linear action of a Clifford algebra.
This action is typically defined such that the elements of the Clifford algebra act on the module in a way that respects the algebra’s multiplication rules.
Clifford modules are particularly important to provide a framework for studying the representations of the Dirac operator.
For any real Hilbert space
V_{\mathbb{R}}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the
Clifford module

V:=\left\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}F_{A}\otimes e_{A}\ \ |\ \ F_{A}\in V_{\mathbb{R}}\right\}.

For any vector
F=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}F_{A}\otimes e_{A}\in V
and any Clifford number
x=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}x_{A}e_{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we equip this space with a left and a right scalar multiplication

(2.4a)

\displaystyle xF:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(x_{B}F_{A})\otimes(e_{B}e_{A}),

(left-multiplication)

(2.4b)

\displaystyle Fx:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(F_{A}x_{B})\otimes(e_{A}e_{B}).

(right-multiplication)

Moreover, we define the
inner product

(2.5)

\langle F,G\rangle:=\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}\langle F_{A},G_{B}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\,\overline{e_{A}}e_{B},\qquad F,G\in V,

and the
norm

(2.6)

\|F\|:=\Big(\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|F_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\big({\rm Sc}\langle F,F\rangle\big)^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad F\in V.

The sesquilinear form (
2.5
) is clearly right-linear in the second, and right-antilinear in the first argument, i.e. for every
F,G,H\in V
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, there holds

\displaystyle\langle F,G+H\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle F,G\rangle+\langle F,H\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle G,Hx\rangle=\langle G,H\rangle x,

\displaystyle\langle F+H,G\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle F,G\rangle+\langle H,G\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle Gx,H\rangle=\overline{x}\langle G,H\rangle.

Moreover, there also holds

(2.7)

\langle G,xH\rangle=\langle\overline{x}G,H\rangle.

Next we recall some more basic properties of the inner product (
2.5
) and the norm (
2.6
).

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
F,G\in V
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we have:

i)

\|Fx\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|x|\|F\|

and
\|xF\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|x|\|F\|
,

ii)

\|Fx\|=\|xF\|=|x|\|F\|

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,

iii)

|\langle F,G\rangle|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}\|F\|\,\|G\|
,

iv)

|{\rm Sc}\langle F,G\rangle|\leq\|F\|\,\|G\|
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the vector space
V
, we consider two inner products that serve different purposes.
The first inner product,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
is the one in (
2.5
), while the second is its scalar part, namely
\mathrm{Sc}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
.
The inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is used in the
Riesz’s representation theorem, that is,
every linear and continuous functional
\varphi:V\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
is
represented by an element
F_{\varphi}\in V
such that

\varphi(G)=\langle F_{\varphi},G\rangle
for every
G\in V
.
The second inner product is important since, unlike the first one, it gives rise to a norm in the classical sense.

We now recall the Lax-Milgram lemma for Clifford modules. This lemma is well known in complex functional analysis while its Clifford algebra version can be found in
[
24
]
.
We will need this result in the later sections to prove the unique solvability of the spectral problem for the
S
-spectrum and, in particular, to detect regions of
S
-resolvent set and related estimates of the
S
-resolvent operator.

Lemma 2.3

(Lax-Milgram lemma)
.

Let
q:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
be right-linear in the second and right-antilinear in the first argument. Moreover, we assume that there exist constants
C\geq 0
and
\kappa>0
, such that

{\rm Sc}(q(F,F))\geq\kappa\|F\|^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|q(F,G)|\leq C\|F\|\|G\|,\qquad F,G\in V.

Then, for every right-linear bounded functional
\varphi:V\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
, there exists a unique vector
F_{\varphi}\in V
, satisfying

\|F_{\varphi}\|\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\|{\rm Sc}(\varphi)\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad q(F_{\varphi},G)=\varphi(G),\qquad\text{for all }G\in V.

2.2.
Clifford-Sobolev spaces

The spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum is designed to work in Banach modules; however, the simpler setting to investigate boundary value problems is the
L^{2}
spaces.
In this setting the
L^{2}
space is composed of square-integrable functions which means that the integral of
Sc(\overline{F(x)}F(x))
, over a specified domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, converges to a finite value.
Precisely, let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set with smooth boundary.
We define

L^{2}(\Omega)\coloneqq L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})\coloneqq\Bigl\{F\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{n}\ \ |\ \ \int_{\Omega}Sc(\overline{F(x)}F(x))\,dx<+\infty\Bigr\}

with the sesquilinear form

\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{2}\coloneqq\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\overline{F(x)}G(x)\,dx,

where
dx
is the Lebesgue measure and
F=\sum_{A}F_{A}e_{A}
and
G=\sum_{A}G_{A}e_{A}
, with
F_{A},G_{A}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
.
Clearly

L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})=L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathbb{R}_{n}

and it is a Clifford module.
Moreover, we introduce the Sobolev space:

H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})=H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathbb{R}_{n}

equipped with the sesquilinear form:

\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})}\coloneqq\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})}\coloneqq\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\langle\partial_{x_{j}}F,\partial_{x_{j}}G\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

where
\partial_{x_{j}}u
denotes the
j
-th weak derivative of
u
and so

H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})=H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathbb{R}_{n}
is a Clifford module.

We define
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\coloneqq H^{1}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
to be the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
in
H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
. As in the scalar case,
see
[
13
, Theorem 9.17]
, if we suppose that
\partial\Omega
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and we assume that

F\in H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}_{n})

then the condition
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
is equivalent to
F\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
.
In the general case, for arbitrary functions
F\in H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
, we have to consider the trace operator
u\mapsto F|_{\partial\Omega}
,
and the space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
turns out to be the kernel of the trace operator, i.e.

H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n}):=\left\{F\in H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n}):F|_{\partial\Omega}=0\right\},

where
F|_{\partial\Omega}:=\mathrm{tr}\,F
is the Dirichlet trace, also denoted by
\tau_{D}
,
has to be understood in the sense of the trace operator, see
[
13
, p. 315]
.
Finally,
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathbb{R}_{n}
is a subspace of
H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
that is a Clifford Hilbert module itself.
For the sake of brevity we will also use the notation
L^{2}
for
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
and similarly
H^{1}
and
H_{0}^{1}
for
H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
and
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})
, respectively.
We finally state two basic lemmas, which will be crucial throughout the paper, namely the Sobolev and the Poincaré inequality, for more details see
[
24
]
.
In order to state these inequalities we will need for every function
F\in H^{1}(\Omega)
the
Sobolev-seminorm

\|F\|_{D}:=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\|\partial_{x_{i}}F\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{n})}^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Lemma 2.4

(Poincaré inequality)
.

[
13
, Corollary 9.19 & Remark 21]

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and either bounded in one direction or of finite measure. Then there exists a constant
C_{P}(\Omega)>0
, such that

(2.8)

\|F\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{P}(\Omega)\|F\|_{D},\qquad F\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

3.
Basic results on the Dirac operator on manifolds

In this section we collect the necessary concepts of the description of Dirac operators on manifolds, for more details on this topic see e.g.
[
51
]
, and for the proofs of the theorems stated in this section see the book
[
40
]
.
To give a precise definition of the
S
-spectrum of the Dirac operator on
M
, we need to the following steps:

•

Definition of the Dirac operator
\mathcal{D}
on
M
,

•

Identification of
\mathcal{D}^{2}
as the sum of a second-order Laplacian and a curvature operator (Bochner-Weitzenböck theorem).

The precise expression of the operator
\mathcal{D}^{2}
is of crucial importance in order to define the
S
-spectrum because it is associated with the operator

Q_{s}(\mathcal{D}):=\mathcal{D}^{2}-2s_{0}\mathcal{D}+|s|^{2}.

A way to treat functions on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
that take values on a Clifford module (see Definition
3.3
) is through the theory of fiber bundles. One uses the fact that the space of such functions is in bijection with the sections of a vector bundle with such Clifford module as fiber. Moreover, to define a Dirac operator on
(M,g)
, so that when
(M,g)
is
(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{\text{eucl}})
we get the standard Dirac operator in Clifford analysis, one needs several things:

(1)

A bundle of Clifford algebras
\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow M
such that
\mathcal{C}_{x}\cong\mathbb{R}_{n}
,
\forall x\in M
;

(2)

A fiberwise injective morphism of vector bundles
\iota:\mathcal{T}^{\ast}M\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}
such that,
\forall x\in M

\left\{\iota(u),\iota(v)\right\}_{\mathcal{C}}=-2g(u,v),\quad\forall u,v\in\mathcal{T}_{x}^{\ast}M,

where
g
is the co-metric on the cotangent bundle;

(3)

A bundle of Clifford modules, i.e. a vector bundle
\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow M
, together with a morphism
\mathbf{c}:\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\textup{End}(\mathcal{E})
whose restrictions to the fibers are morphism of algebras;

(4)

A connection on
\mathcal{E}
.

One way to define a connection exploits the fact that the spin group
\textup{Spin}(n)
, seen as a subgroup of the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
, is a
2
-fold cover of the special orthogonal group
SO(n)
with the covering map
\sigma:\textup{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)
. This can be exploited by introducing additional structure on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
.

Definition 3.1
.

Consider a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
and denote by
\pi:SO(\mathcal{T}M)\to M
the principal bundle of oriented orthonormal frames of
M
. A
spin structure
on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
is a principle
\textup{Spin}(n)
-bundle
\rho:P_{\textup{Spin}}\to M
together with a double-covering map
\mu:P_{\textup{Spin}}\to SO(\mathcal{T}M)
such that
\rho=\pi\circ\mu
and

\mu(g\cdot p)=\sigma(g)\cdot\mu(p),\qquad\forall p\in P_{\textup{Spin}},\,\forall g\in\textup{Spin}(n).

We recall that neither existence nor uniqueness is guaranteed for existence of spin structures. Nevertheless, existence is completely determined by a topological invariant (the vanishing of the second Stiefel-Whitney class of
M

[
46
]
). A manifold that allows a spin structure on its tangent bundle
\mathcal{T}M
is then called a
spin manifold
. We shall, in this article, restrict our studies to the case when
(M,g)
is a spin manifold. Additionally, as for the moment we are not concerned with the dependence of our studies on the spin structure, we shall consider that our manifold
(M,g)
is simply connected.

In order to define the necessary ingredients recall, that given a
G
-principle bundle
P
with a principle connection and a representation
\tau:G\to GL(V)
, one can construct the associated vector bundle
\mathcal{E}:=P\times_{\tau}V
, which will have a connection induced from
P
. In our example, a principal connection on
SO(\mathcal{T}M)
can be lifted to a principal connection on the Spin bundle
P_{\textup{Spin}}
. Given a representation
\tau:\textup{Spin}(n)\to GL(V)
, we obtain the associated bundle and the induced connection. We can take the
V
to be the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
and the
\textup{Spin}(n)\subset\mathbb{R}_{n}
representation on
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. Thus we can define the Dirac operator in an invariant way as follows

Definition 3.2
.

The Dirac operator
\mathcal{D}
is the first-order differential operator defined by the composition of the Clifford multiplication with the covariant derivative
\nabla^{\tau}
,

\mathcal{D}:=\mathbf{c}\circ\nabla^{\tau}:C^{\infty}(\mathcal{E})\overset{\nabla^{\tau}}{\longrightarrow}C^{\infty}(T^{*}M\otimes\mathcal{E})\cong C^{\infty}(TM\otimes\mathcal{E})\overset{\mathbf{c}}{\longrightarrow}C^{\infty}(\mathcal{E}).

For simplicity of exposition, we introduce all the tools locally on a simply connected subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
g:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where
g(x)=[g_{ij}(x)]

is a smooth matrix-valued function defined on the open set
U
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and that
g(x)
will always be taken to be positive-definite and symmetric.
Then

(3.1)

g_{x}(u,v)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g_{ij}(x)u_{i}v_{j},\ \ u,\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a positive-definite inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

(3.2)

g_{x}(X,Y)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g_{ij}(x)a_{i}b_{j},\ \ {\rm where}\ \ X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(x)\partial_{x_{i}},\ \ \ Y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}(x)\partial_{x_{j}},

defines a positive-definite inner product space on the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{x}(U)
to
U
at
x
.
So
U
can be seen as a coordinate neighborhood for the Riemannian manifold
M
taking
x=(x_{1},...,x_{n})
as coordinates and (
3.2
) as inner product, making it possible to study Dirac operators on
M
, by introducing it as a nonconstant coefficients nonhomogeneous first-order systems of differential operators on
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
. Here
\mathcal{H}
should be considered as a Clifford module in the following sense:

Definition 3.3
.

A finite-dimensional real or complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is said to be a
Clifford module
if there exist skew-adjoint operators
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
in
\textup{Aut}(\mathcal{H})
such that

(3.3)

e_{j}e_{k}+e_{k}e_{j}=-2\delta_{jk}e_{0}\qquad(1\leq j,k\leq n),

where
e_{0}\ (=I)
is the identity operator.

Let now

(3.4)

g^{-1}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\ \ g^{-1}(x)=[g^{ij}(x)]

be the inverse matrix-valued function inverse to
g
and let

\gamma(x)=[\gamma_{ij}(x)],\ \ \gamma^{-1}(x)=[\gamma^{ij}(x)]:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

be the unique square roots of
g
and
g^{-1}
, respectively.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be the skew-adjoint operators on
\mathcal{H}

satisfying the Clifford relation (
3.3
), and set

\iota(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma^{ij}(x)\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113ptx_{j}):=e_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma^{ij}(x)e_{j},\quad x\in U,\quad\text{for every }i=1,...,n,

where
\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113ptx_{j}
are just the duals to
\partial_{x_{j}}
.

By definition we have that

(3.5)

e_{j}(x)e_{k}(x)+e_{k}(x)e_{j}(x)=-2g^{jk}(x),\ \ \ x\in U.

With respect to
g
one has an orthonormal frame, i.e., a set of vector fields that form an orthonormal basis at each point
x\in U
. These are given by

E_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma^{ij}(x)\partial_{x_{j}},\ \ \ x\in U\ \ i=1,...,n,

where
\partial_{x_{i}}
are the basis with respect to the local coordinates
x=\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\right)
.

On a manifold one differentiates vector fields with respect to another vector field through the notion of
connection
. Let
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))
be the space of smooth vector fields
X=\sum_{i}a_{i}(x)\partial_{x_{i}}
on
U
.

Definition 3.5
.

A connection on
(U,g)
is any bilinear mapping
\nabla:(X,Y)\to\nabla_{X}(Y)
from
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))\times\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))
which satisfy the conditions

(i)

\nabla_{fX+gY}(Z)=f\nabla_{X}+g\nabla_{Y}(Z)
,

(ii)

\nabla_{X}(fY)=(Xf)Y+f\nabla_{X}(Y)
,

for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))
and for all
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U)
.

Of particular interest is the Levi-Civita connection, which is the unique connection that is torsion-free and compatible with the metric. This is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6
.

There exists a unique connection on
(U,g)
having the further properties

(i)

\nabla_{X}(Y)-\nabla_{Y}(X)=[X,Y],

(ii)

Xg(Y,Z)=g(\nabla_{X}(Y),Z)+g(Y,\nabla_{X}(Z))

for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))
.

A connection when applied to two vector fields is again a vector field, i.e.,
\nabla_{X}(Y)\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,T(U))
. This makes it possible to write the connection as the linear combination of the elements of the frame.

Definition 3.7
.

The functions
\nu_{ij}^{k}\in C^{\infty}(U)
given
by

(3.6)

\nabla_{E_{i}}(E_{j}(x))=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{ij}^{k}(x)E_{k}(x)

are called
connection coefficients
.

There is a rather straightforward way of computing such functions.

Theorem 3.8

( )
.

The connection coefficients associated to an orthonormal frame
E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
are given by

\nu_{ij}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(c_{ij}^{k}-c_{jk}^{i}+c_{ki}^{j}\right),\qquad i,j,k=1,\dots,n,

where
c_{ij}^{k}
are structure functions defined by the identities
[E_{i},E_{j}]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{ij}^{k}E_{k}
, for
i,j=1,\dots,n
.

We now wish to be able not only to differentiate vector fields but also
\mathcal{H}
-valued functions. For that we need to extend the Levi-Civita connection on
(U,g)
to a connection
(X,F)\longrightarrow\nabla_{X}(F)

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))\times\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
. For that, we define connection coefficients that are not
\mathbb{R}
but
\mathfrak{spin}(n)
-valued, the Lie algebra of the Lie group
\textup{Spin}(n)
, in terms of the Levi-Civita connection coeffiecients.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\nu_{ij}^{k}
be the functions defined above. The function
\nu_{i}(x)
given by

(3.7)

\nu_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j<k}\nu_{ij}^{k}(x)e_{j}e_{k}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\nu_{ij}^{k}(x)e_{j}e_{k}

is a smooth
\mathfrak{spin}(n)
-valued function on
U
.

Let
d\tau:\mathfrak{spin}(n)\to\textup{Aut}(\mathcal{H})
be the representation of the Lie algebra of
\textup{Spin}(n)
derived from the representation
\tau:\textup{Spin}(n)\to\textup{Aut}(\mathcal{H})
.

Definition 3.10
.

The map
\nabla^{\tau}:(X,F)\to\nabla_{X}(F)
from
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))\times\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
defines a connection, which is expressed as a covariant derivative
\nabla_{X}^{\tau}
by

\nabla_{X}^{\tau}=X(x)+d\tau(\nu_{i}(x))

acting on
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
.

In analogy with Definition
3.5
it has the properties
the conditions

(i)

\nabla^{\tau}_{fX+gY}(F)=f\nabla^{\tau}_{X}(F)+g\nabla^{\tau}_{Y}(F)
,

(ii)

\nabla^{\tau}_{X}(fF)=(Xf)F+f\nabla^{\tau}_{X}(F)
,

for all
F,G\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
and for all
X\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))
and
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U)
.

Remark 3.11
.

Since
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U)
-module
condition (i) in Theorem
3.6
does not make sense but (ii) in Theorem
3.6
becomes

(3.8)

Xg_{x}(F(x),G(x))=(\nabla_{X}^{\tau}F(x),G(x))+(F(x),\nabla_{X}^{\tau}G(x)),

where
(\cdot,\cdot)

denotes the inner product on
\mathcal{H}
.

The following theorem shows how to write the Dirac operator locally in terms of the covariant derivative
\nabla_{E_{i}}^{\tau}
with respect to a local orthonormal basis

Theorem 3.12
.

As a differential operator on
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
the Dirac operator on
(U,g)
becomes

(3.9)

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}}

and, more explicitly, we can write

(3.10)

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\Big(E_{i}(x)+d\tau(\nu_{i}(x))\Big),

where
\nu_{i}(x)
are given by (
3.7
).

Remark 3.13
.

Since the covariant derivative
\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}}
becomes simply the vector field
\partial_{x_{i}}
on

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
when

is an open set of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the usual Euclidean structure, it is clear that the operator
\mathcal{D}
in Theorem

In order to define the
S
-spectrum it is necessary to write an explicit
form for the operator
\mathcal{D}^{2}
.

Theorem 3.14
.

As a differential operator on
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
the square of the Dirac operator is given by

(3.11)

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}^{2}&=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{j}}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{j}}-\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{jj}^{k}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{k}}\Big)\Big)+\sum_{i<j}e_{i}e_{j}\big(\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{j}}-\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{j}}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}}-\nabla^{\tau}_{[E_{i},E_{j}]}\big).\end{split}

Remark 3.15
.

The formula for
\mathcal{D}^{2}
is at the heart of the property of
\mathcal{D}
on manifold and in the case the operator
\mathcal{D}
is not self-adjoint this formula is
of crucial importance for the definition of the
S
-spectrum.
We point out that the first term generalizes the Laplace operator while
the second term plays the role of a curvature operator.

The definition of the second order Laplacian
\Delta_{\tau}

follows the classical
\Delta={\rm div}({\rm grad})
form of the classical Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

where in the present context the gradient operator is represented by the total derivative

(3.12)

\nabla_{\tau}:F\to\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla_{E_{j}}^{\tau}F(x)\otimes e_{j}\ \ {\rm for}\ \ \ F\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})

mapping

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
into
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
As
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
\mathcal{U}_{n}
-module on which
\tau\otimes\sigma
defined a representation of
Spin(n)

a linear connection
(X,G)\to\nabla_{X}^{\tau\otimes\sigma}
on

\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued functions is obtained by replacing
\tau
by

\tau\otimes\sigma
throughout the covariant derivative.

Definition 3.16
.

The definition of the divergence operator from

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
is given by

(3.13)

G\to\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{j}}(G_{j})-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{jj}^{k}(x)G_{k}\Big),\ \ \ \ {\rm where}\ \ \ G=\sum_{j=1}^{n}G_{j}\otimes e_{j}.

This operator is the adjoint
\nabla_{\tau}^{*}
of the divergence operator defined in (

Definition 3.17
.

The Laplace operator
\Delta_{\tau}

is defined by

\Delta_{\tau}F=\nabla_{\tau}^{*}\nabla_{\tau}F=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{j}}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{j}}(F)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{jj}^{k}(x)\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{k}}F\Big),

for

.

Clearly

is given by

\Delta_{\tau}=\sum_{ij=1}^{n}g^{ij}(x)\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}+{\rm\ lowerorder\ terms},

so every
\Delta_{\tau}
has the same principal symbol, in particular
\Delta_{\tau}
is elliptic. We now write the part of the operator related to the curvature.
Heuristically speaking the curvature is a measure of the failure of
\nabla_{X}
,
\nabla_{Y}
, to commute as operators on

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))
, while the curvature on
\mathcal{H}

measures of the failure of
\nabla_{X}^{\tau}
,
\nabla_{Y}^{\tau}
, to commute as operators on
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
.

Definition 3.18

(Curvature operators)
.

The curvature operators are defined as

R(X,Y)=\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}-\nabla_{[X,Y]},\ \ \ \ {\rm for\ all}\ \ \ X,Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{T}(U))

and

R^{\tau}(X,Y)=\nabla_{X}^{\tau}\nabla_{Y}^{\tau}-\nabla_{Y}^{\tau}\nabla_{X}^{\tau}-\nabla^{\tau}_{[X,Y]},\ \ \ \ {\rm for\ all}\ \ \ X,Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})

So we have the important theorem on the structure of the operator
\mathcal{D}^{2}
.

Theorem 3.19
.

On
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
we have

(3.15)

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}^{2}&=-\Delta_{\tau}+\sum_{i<j}e_{i}e_{j}R^{\tau}(E_{i},E_{j})\\
&=-\Delta_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i<j}e_{i}e_{j}\Big(\sum_{k<\ell}R_{ijk\ell}(x)\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\tau(e_{k}e_{\ell})\Big),\end{split}

where
\Delta_{\tau}=\nabla_{\tau}^{*}\nabla_{\tau}
is the Laplace operator on
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
and

are the components of the Riemann curvature tensor with respect to the orthonormal frame
E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
.

In some special case the curvature term simplifies.
We recall that the scalar curvature of
(U,g)
is defined by

(3.16)

\kappa(x)=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}R_{ijij}(x).

When we assume
\mathcal{H}=\Theta_{n}
(see
[
40
]
) and
\tau
is the Spin representation of
\textup{Spin}(n)
on

,

We conclude this section by presenting results that are essential for explicitly formulating the Dirac operator on hyperbolic and spherical spaces, which will serve our purposes in the subsequent sections. Precisely, we need to select a representation of
\tau

appearing in the Dirac operator, see (

) below.
To this end, as representation
\tau
of
\textup{Spin}(n)
on
\mathbb{R}_{n}
we choose the left multiplication on
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. More precisely, the map

(3.17)

\tau:\textup{Spin}(n)\longrightarrow\text{End}(\mathbb{R}_{n}),

is defined as
\tau(a)(u):=au
for any
a\in\textup{Spin}(n)
and
u\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
. In the next lemma we determine
d\tau
for a given representation
\tau
.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
\tau
be the representation of
\textup{Spin}(n)
on
\mathbb{R}_{n}
given by left multiplication. Then
\tau
induces a representation
\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\tau

\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\tau:\mathfrak{spin}(n)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{end}(\mathbb{R}_{n})

of
\mathfrak{spin}(n)
on
\mathbb{R}_{n}
given by
\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\tau\equiv e_{i}e_{j}u
, for
u\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
.

Proof.

There is a canonical embedding
\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{R}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}_{n}
. Using Proposition 6.1 in
[
46
]
, we know that the Lie algebra of
(\mathbb{R}_{n},[\cdot,\cdot])
corresponding to the subgroup
\textup{Spin}(n)\subset\mathbb{R}_{n}^{\times}
is

\mathfrak{spin}(n)=\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This implies that any element
X\in\mathfrak{spin}(n)
is a linear combination of
e_{i}e_{j}
. In particular, we can consider the basis elements
X_{ij}=e_{i}e_{j}

and compute the represention of Lie algebra
\mathfrak{spin}(n)
on
\mathbb{R}_{n}
as follows,

\displaystyle\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\tau(X_{ij})(u)=\frac{\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt}{\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113ptt}\tau(\exp tX_{ij})(u)\Big|_{t=0}=\frac{\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt}{\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113ptt}(\cos t+e_{i}e_{j}\sin t)u\Big|_{t=0}=e_{i}e_{j}u.

This then induces as representation of the whole Lie algebra
\mathfrak{spin}(n)
as the left multiplication by
e_{i}e_{j}
.
∎

To understand what are the natural Robin-like boundary conditions we mentioned, in Remark

,
that the integration by part formula associated with the operator

The type of Robin boundary conditions turn out to be

, given in

, for

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\int_{\Omega}y^{2}\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}\overline{F}G\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113ptx
