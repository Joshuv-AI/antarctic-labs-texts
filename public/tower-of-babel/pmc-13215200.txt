Distinct properties of plastic-derived submicrometer particles from smoldering burning
Authors: Lin Kong, Hongru Shen, Xing Wang, Michael A. R. Tawadrous, Emily R. Halpern, Qiaorong Xie, Alexander Laskin, Kin Fai Ho, Bonnie M. Hamilton, Alex K. Y. Lee, Man Nin Chan, Arthur W. H. Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Widespread plastic use and mismanaged disposal release substantial amounts of particles into the atmosphere during waste burning. We define these thermally generated particles as plastic-derived submicrometer particles (PDSPs), a poorly characterized class of contaminants. We provide direct evidence that PDSPs are distinct from conventional micro- and nanoplastic particles that are formed mainly by mechanical processes. We show that PDSPs contain mixtures of altered polymeric substances and oxygenated compounds produced by extensive thermal decomposition and chemical transformations during smoldering. Volatility distributions suggest that PDSPs exist in semisolid phase states with lower viscosities than their original polymers. Upon exposure to hydroxyl radicals, the principal oxidant in the atmosphere, PDSPs exhibit higher reactivity than the original polymer particles, forming highly oxidized aerosol species at reaction rates comparable to typical atmospheric organic aerosols. These findings demonstrate that plastic smoldering introduces a previously unrecognized class of atmospherically active particles, with implications for their contribution to global plastic budgets, persistence in the atmosphere, and potential effects on human health and climate-relevant processes.

INTRODUCTION

Plastics have become indispensable materials across the world, and the degradation of plastics are primary sources of micro- and nanoplastic particles (MNPPs) (1, 2). Owing to their small sizes, MNPPs can be readily inhaled, ingested, or absorbed by living organisms, leading to potential bioaccumulation and toxicological effects (3). Recent studies further highlight that MNPPs are ubiquitous in the atmosphere, which serves as a critical pathway for their long-range transport and environmental transformation (4).

Airborne plastic particles have largely been classified into three main sources according to their generation mechanisms: biogeochemical weathering of plastic debris (5), direct industrial emissions of purpose-made particles (6, 7), and thermal degradation (8). The latter includes high-temperature processes, such as plastic incineration (9, 10), uncontrolled open burning (11), industrial operations [e.g., the “cured-in-place-pipe” method for sewer repairs (12)] and extreme wildfire events at the wildland-urban interface (13). Among these pathways, thermal degradation remains a substantial but insufficiently understood source of atmospheric plastic particles. Open burning is widespread and represents a major waste management pathway in many regions. Approximately 57% [29.9 million tonnes (Mt)/year] of global plastic waste undergoes open burning, underscoring its substantial contribution to the uncontrolled release of plastic-related pollutants into the atmosphere (14). Beyond this large global flux, open burning and other plastic-rich fire scenarios can drive disproportionate local impacts. For instance, emissions from burned structures have been shown to be more spatially concentrated than vegetation-fire emissions, and the release of specific hazardous air pollutants in these events can rival or exceed those from all other anthropogenic sources combined in fire-affected regions (15). These lines of evidence underscore that thermal degradation is not only a potential source of airborne plastics but a direct atmospheric entry pathway that transforms plastic mass into aerosol emissions. Open burning is frequently dominated by smoldering combustion, which occurs under low-temperature, oxygen-limited conditions and is especially prevalent in rural and remote regions lacking adequate waste-management infrastructure (16).

Currently, MNPPs in the environment primarily refer to particles mechanically generated from plastic debris (17, 18), which retain original polymer structures even after environmental aging (19). While some previous studies hypothesized that plastic burning may produce airborne MNPP-like aerosols (13, 20), the formation, composition, and properties of these plastic-derived aerosols, and how they differ from conventional MNPPs remains poorly understood. Recent studies have started to elucidate the characteristics of particles emitted from smoldering. Plastic smoldering releases an estimated 3.4 Mt of particles per year, which exhibit complex chemical composition, incorporating both degraded polymer and oxidative byproducts (21). Burning plastics can also generate persistent organic pollutants (22) and other hazardous chemicals. For instance, burnt plastic recovered from ship fires contained polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) at concentrations as high as 199 μg/g of plastic, approximately two orders of magnitude higher than those measured in unburned plastic pellets, indicating substantial fire-induced chemical transformation (23).

We define the particle mixture formed through thermal and oxidative transformation of plastics as plastic-derived submicrometer particles (PDSPs), representing a distinct class of environmental contaminants. PDSPs are expected to have distinct chemical compositions and physicochemical properties compared to their original plastics, placing them outside existing MNPPs classification frameworks and complicating efforts to understand their environmental fate and impacts. In this study, we characterize PDSPs emitted during the smoldering of common plastics, focusing on their chemical composition, polymeric restructuring, physiochemical properties, and oxidative reactivity under atmospherically relevant conditions. The results provide critical insights into the atmospheric behavior and environmental fate of PDSPs, linking plastic disposal to atmospheric aerosol formation and transformation, advancing our knowledge of the role of plastic burning in global plastic pollution.

RESULTS

Restructured polymers and oxidation products drive PDSP chemical complexity

Plastic smoldering yields thermal decomposition products containing chemical species substantially modified from the original polymer (21). To characterize their transformation, we analyzed PDSPs produced from the smoldering burns of four commonly produced and used polymers (polypropylene (PP), low-density polyethylene (LDPE), polystyrene (PS), and polyethylene terephthalate (PET)], alongside reference standard pellets and commercially synthesized polystyrene latex (PSL) nanoplastics, using pyrolysis (Py)–gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (Py–GC-MS) under thermal desorption (TD) and Py modes (figs. S1 and S2).

As an illustration, LDPE pellets contain saturated hydrocarbon polymers, and they have high thermal stability with no detectable organic species released during TD analysis (100° to 250°C), as indicated by a flat chromatographic baseline (Fig. 1A). In contrast, LDPE-PDSPs release a broad range of organic compounds, including oxygenated species such as oleic and erucic acids, indicating oxidative fragmentation of polymers during smoldering. Under Py analysis (600°C), LDPE pellets produce a regular series of sharp, evenly spaced peaks (Fig. 1B), corresponding to short-chain alkanes and alkenes produced via β-scission (24). These products are consistent with the well-defined molecular structure of the unaltered polymer (25). In contrast, LDPE-PDSPs display a markedly broadened chromatographic pattern, with an elevated baseline and diminished peak resolution, indicative of molecular heterogeneity arising from chain branching and cross-linking. These indicate partial preservation of polymeric macromolecules alongside thermally driven isomerization, degradation, and oxidative transformation (26).

To examine the effect of thermal degradation across polymer types, we calculated the weighted mean retention time (WMRT) of chromatographic peaks (fig. S3). PDSPs generated from four different polymers consistently exhibited shorter WMRTs than their corresponding pellets, suggesting the generation of lower–molecular weight and higher-volatility species that elute earlier from the column. Mass spectral analysis (Fig. 1C and fig. S4) further revealed a clear compositional shift with distinct fragmentation patterns. For example, LDPE-PDSPs are enriched in unsaturated, aromatic, and oxygenated ions, including mass/charge ratio (m/z) 44 (CO2+), m/z 39 (dominant in C3H3+), 67 (dominant in C5H7+), 91 (dominant in C7H7+), and 105 (C8H9+, C7H5O+), in contrast to the dominance of alkyl fragments (e.g., CnH2n-1+, m/z 41, 55, 69, 83, 97) in their original pellets.

To further probe the polymeric characteristics of PDSPs and differentiate between labile organics and more stable polymeric residues, we applied hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) digestion to the samples. This oxidative treatment is widely used to remove low–molecular weight and partially oxidized organic matter, whereas the more persistent, polymer-derived fractions retains for subsequent chemical analysis (27). Across all four PDSP types, TD mode revealed substantial signal reductions after digestion (table S1 and fig. S5A). For example, PS-, LDPE-, and PP-PDSPs showed TD mode signal reductions of 98.9, 84.4, and 81.7%, respectively, confirming the high proportion of oxidized and thermally labile material generated during smoldering. In contrast, PP- and LDPE-PDSPs retained a substantial portion of their Py mode signals after digestion, with reduction of 43.3 and 18.2%, respectively. Consistently, the corresponding Py chromatograms (fig. S5B) preserve prominent polymer-characteristic features after digestion, suggesting that these particles contain measurable fractions of polymer-like residues that are relatively resistant to chemical digestion. Conversely, PS- and PET-PDSPs showed Py mode signal reductions exceeding 50% (60.4% for PS and 87.9% for PET). This suggests a higher fraction of polymeric residues in PS and PET that are susceptible to oxidation. These findings show that PDSPs formed from smoldering are complex mixtures of partially oxidized polymer components and chemically transformed residues, rather than simple, uniformly degraded polymers and their fragments.

Thermal degradation transforms plastics into semisolid PDSPs

The phase properties of PDSPs critically shape their atmospheric behavior, chemical reactivity, and persistence (28, 29). Using temperature programmed desorption combined with direct analysis in real-time ionization and high-resolution MS (TPD-DART-HRMS) (30, 31) and volatility basis set (VBS) modeling (32, 33), we assessed the volatility, gas-particle partitioning, glass transition temperatures, and viscosity of PDSPs (34). VBS-based analysis showed that most organic mass in the burnt plastic emission consists of compounds with saturation mass concentrations (C298K∗) below 10−2 μg m−3, corresponding to low- to extremely-low volatility organic compounds (35). These saturation concentrations translate to particle-phase fractions of 75.0% for PET-PDSPs, 71.3% for PP-PDSPs, 68.9% for PE-PDSPs, and 69.7% for PS-PDSPs at room temperature (table S2) under typical organic mass loadings (5 to 10 μg m−3) in the atmosphere. These findings indicate that particle-phase PDSP compounds are more likely to remain airborne and undergo long-range transport on regional to global scales, as evaporation to the gas phase is not a major sink (36, 37).

On the basis of the volatility distribution, the glass transition temperatures (Tg) of PDSPs are modeled to be 237 to 272 K at relative humidity (RH) 35% (table S3), which are lower than those of original plastics, which typically exceed 293 K (38). Given that these Tg values are lower than ambient atmospheric temperatures in summer, PDSPs likely exist in semisolid states but may become more rigid under colder winter conditions or at higher altitudes. The semisolid character of PDSPs allows reversible transitions between more viscous (rubbery) and glassy (rigid) states, depending on temperature, humidity, and hygroscopic properties of PDSPs (39, 40). In turn, these behaviors influence hygroscopic growth, reactive uptake, and cloud processing, thereby modulating the atmospheric lifetime and transport pathways of PDSPs (41).

Furthermore, PDSPs fall within an intermediate viscosity regime (104 to 107 Pa·s at 35% RH, T = 296 K, Fig. 2), between highly diffusive secondary organic aerosols (SOA) and solid-phase polymers (34). PS-PDSPs exhibit the lowest viscosity (~104 Pa·s), with corresponding mixing timescales of seconds to minutes for 200-nm particles. In contrast, PET-PDSPs exhibit viscosities exceeding 107 Pa·s, resulting in limited molecular mobility and diffusion timescales of several days. This higher viscosity in PET-PDSPs is associated with a greater abundance of extremely low-volatility, highly oxygenated organics, as indicated by elevated oxygen-to-carbon (O:C) ratios (0.4 to 0.6). These species could promote intermolecular cohesion, reducing molecular mobility more effectively than in less oxidized PDSPs. Notably, these values are several orders of magnitude lower than those of original plastic polymers (>1010 Pa·s) (12, 28), reflecting substantial thermal degradation that disrupts the long-range order and polymer entanglement of the original macromolecules (42, 43). Such changes facilitate molecular mobility and internal diffusion within PDSPs, allowing their properties to diverge from those of MNPPs (44).

Higher oxidation reactivity of PDSPs compared to MNPPs

Given their propensity to remain in the condensed phase, PDSPs are subject to atmospheric transformations. These reactions could occur through oxidative processing driven by reactive gas-phase species, particularly hydroxyl (OH) radicals that initiate heterogeneous aging typical of those in ambient particles (45, 46). Here, the chemical evolution of PDSPs during aging was assessed using a compact time-of-flight aerosol MS (C-ToF-AMS). The bulk composition of four PDSP types and commercially synthesized PSL (as a proxy for MNPPs) was measured before and after OH exposure, which simulated photochemical aging up to 2.9 days (mass spectra shown in fig. S6).

A pronounced decline in the mass fractions of fragment ions originating from PDSPs was observed (fig. S7), indicating substantial oxidation during simulated aging. This transformation reflects a continued shift toward smaller oligomeric and fragmented species following atmospheric release. These oxidative reactions are likely facilitated by structural features in PDSPs such as unsaturated and short-chain fragments introduced during smoldering, as discussed earlier. These fragments likely contain more secondary and tertiary carbon atoms that are preferential targets for OH-initiated hydrogen abstraction (47, 48).

PS-PDSPs show substantially faster oxidative loss of the main PS ions than PSL particles (representative of the original plastic) within relatively short timescales of OH exposure (<3 days), despite having a similar styrene-based backbone. The ion at m/z 104 (C8H8+, styrene monomer), commonly used as a PS marker (49), decreases by 91% in PS-PDSPs compared to only 17% in PSL after oxidation (Fig. 3A). Across a broader range of fragments, PS-PDSPs exhibit reductions of 77 to 95% in mass concentration, while PSL shows more modest declines of 17 to 29%, consistent with previous studies reporting the slow oxidative decay of PSL over comparable aging timescales (50). These results highlight the role of structural disorders introduced during smoldering in enhancing the oxidative reactivity of PDSPs.

The emergence of oxygenated ions, including m/z 29 (CHO+), and 44 (CO2+), indicates extensive incorporation of oxygen into the particle phase. Despite these transformations, total mass concentrations of oxidized PDSPs remain largely stable across OH exposures up to 2.8 × 1011 molecules cm−3 s (fig. S8). This indicates that most oxidation products are likely non- or less volatile, remaining in the particle phase rather than partitioning to the gas phase. This observation is further supported by the mass size distribution data (fig. S9) that shows less than 20% shift in particle size modes after oxidation.

The oxidative trajectory of PDSPs is also evident in the evolution of normalized organic signals at m/z 43 (𝑓43, associated with C2H3O+ and C3H7+) and m/z 44 (𝑓44, CO2+, a proxy of O:C) (Fig. 3B). Compared to PSL, which exhibits minimal compositional change (𝑓44 increase by 0.02), PDSPs show a clear progression toward the upper left of the plot, representing a transformation from less-oxidized oxygenated organic aerosols (LO-OOA) to more-oxidized OOA (MO-OOA) (51). These changes indicate substantial oxidation processes, in a good agreement with the typical oxidation pathway of atmospheric organic aerosols, which evolve from hydrocarbon-like precursors to more oxygenated products (52).

These oxidation processes are further quantified by the linear increase in 𝑓44 with OH exposure, which varies by polymer type and reflects distinct oxidative reactivities. Slopes of f44 versus OH exposure (Fig. 3C) are 4 to 10 times steeper for PDSPs than for PSL, indicating substantially faster formation of oxidized products. This rapid chemical transformation is further supported by the estimated O:C ratios (fig. S10), which increased markedly with OH exposure for PDSPs (ΔO:C > 0.5), whereas PSL exhibits only a slight increase (ΔO:C < 0.1), in line with prior observations of PSL oxidation (50). Notably, the oxidation rates of PDSPs exhibit an inverse relationship with their calculated viscosities. PS-PDSPs, with the lowest viscosity, undergo the fastest oxidation, whereas the more viscous PET-PDSPs show the slowest rate of oxidation. This trend suggests that decreased viscosity increases molecular diffusivity within the particle phase, thereby accelerating reactive uptake and internal mixing during oxidation (53). The oxidation rates observed for PDSPs are comparable to those observed for biomass burning organic aerosol (BBOA) (54), cooking, and vehicle exhaust emissions (55), positioning PDSPs within the broader framework of reactive atmospheric particle types.

The elevated oxidation rates observed for PDSPs compared to original plastics point to underlying differences in oxygen uptake efficiency and particle-phase reactivity. To assess the efficiency of oxygen uptake during oxidation, we estimated the ΔO/OH collision ratio, defined as the change in oxygen mass per OH collision with the particle surface (56, 57), and is detailed in the Supplementary Text. It confirms higher oxygen uptake across all PDSP types compared to PSL, particularly at early stages of OH exposure (fig. S11), indicating a more rapid and extensive incorporation of oxygen. PDSPs consist primarily of thermally degraded, lower–molecular weight compounds rather than intact polymer matrices, enhancing their reactivity and promoting oxygen uptake. Their high oxygen incorporation efficiency likely reflects the abundance of labile hydrogen atoms and polar functional groups, which enable radical-driven mechanisms such as autooxidation and chain propagation (58). Given that 4 to 8 oxygen atoms are added per OH collision, this would suggest that radical propagation pathways, particularly autoxidation (intramolecular H-shift) and intermolecular H-abstraction (e.g., ROO·+ RH → ROOH + R·) are prominent, creating at least one additional carbon-centered radical site for further O2 addition, as outlined in fig. S12. These chain-propagation mechanisms compete with chain-termination and scission pathways, such as alkoxy radical (RO·) fragmentation and bimolecular peroxy-peroxy reactions (RO2· + RO2·), collectively facilitating particle-phase oxidation and enables more extensive chemical transformation than would be expected from pristine polymer particles.

DISCUSSION

The global plastic cycle includes production, use, environmental accumulation, chemical transformation, and spatial redistribution (59). Within this cycle, burning is not merely a terminal process but a transformative pathway that converts solid plastics into airborne material and generates PDSPs with chemical and physical properties distinct from mechanically fragmented MNPPs. PDSPs therefore represent previously unidentified and atmospherically active materials that further influence global environmental systems through atmospheric transport and deposition. With climate change expected to increase the frequency and intensity of wildfires, particularly in wildland-urban interfaces where plastic materials are abundant (60), this study provides previously unknown insights into air quality impacts and particle formation from smoldering plastics in regions affected by open burning of plastic waste.

The physical phase of PDSPs is one of the key properties that influences their atmospheric fate and behavior. PDSPs can be in a semisolid state under ambient conditions, exhibiting low to intermediate viscosities that facilitate molecular diffusion and water uptake, enhancing their potential for wet deposition (61). In contrast, typical MNPPs have higher viscosities and rigid, glassy phases that constrain molecular mobility (62). Accurately representing their phase behavior and reactivity is therefore essential for predicting their atmospheric lifetime and removal processes, which ultimately dictate their potential for long-range transport and deposition patterns in the environment.

Laboratory oxidation experiments indicate that PDSPs undergo rapid transformation within days, primarily driven by hydroxyl radicals. This fast oxidative aging parallels the behavior of organic aerosols but differs substantially from the chemical stability of most MNPPs, which degrades over much longer timescales (63). Consequently, PDSPs should be treated as dynamic, reactive trace components in the atmospheric system that are likely to be substantially aged before deposition. Future studies should therefore prioritize similar analyses to better assess the distinct atmospheric fates of these emerging contaminants.

The high reactivity of PDSPs also raises important questions regarding their potential health implications through inhalation. Wildland-urban interface fires can impose disproportionately large health impacts relative to their share of emissions, suggesting elevated per-mass toxicity of smoke from structural fuels that often contain plastics (64). PDSPs enriched in oxidized surface functionalities may enhance the generation of reactive oxygen species, key mediators of oxidative stress and cellular damage, in the respiratory tract (65). In addition, the lower viscosity of PDSPs compared to MNPPs may enhance their bioavailability, facilitating uptake into lung tissue and potential translocation across epithelial barriers (66). Their ability to carry and release additional thermal degradation byproducts, including toxic organics such as PAHs, dioxins, and benzofurans, underscores the urgent need for toxicological assessments.

This study focuses on low-temperature smoldering, a condition commonly encountered during plastic burning (67). However, burning plastic occurs across a wide range of temperatures and burning conditions. Higher temperature smoldering or flaming conditions, such as those found in industrial incineration, structural fires, and increasingly frequent wildland-urban interface fires, are likely to generate particles with greater molecular fragmentation, distinct physiochemical characteristics, and reactivity (68, 69). Incorporating such burning regimes into future research is essential to capture the full diversity of plastic aerosol emissions under real-world conditions.

This study identifies PDSPs as previously unrecognized yet important trace components of the atmosphere. Unlike inert polymer fragments, PDSPs have a mixed organic composition enriched in oxygenated functional groups and undergo rapid oxidative aging, aligning them more closely with organic aerosol than with conventional MNPPs. Standard MNPP detection methods, such as polymer fingerprinting using stable polymer standards like PSL, may not be an ideal approach for identifying these chemically transformed particles in the atmospheric environment. Recognizing their distinct nature will improve air quality models by moving beyond treating plastic particles as inert polymer fragments. Representing PDSPs within established organic aerosol frameworks enables their composition, hygroscopicity, and removal to evolve during transport, thereby improving predictions of lifetime, spatial distribution, and population exposure. Moving forward, PDSPs should be monitored, modeled, and managed not as solid plastic fragments, but as dynamic trace components to atmospheric composition and processes, representing a distinct class of reactive organic aerosols.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Nanoplastic generation

Four thermoplastics—PP, LDPE, PS, and PET (Sigma-Aldrich)—in standard pellet form were used for PDSP generation. A schematic overview of the smoldering-derived NP sample preparation process is provided in fig. S13. A total of 0.5 g of each plastic type was individually placed in a crucible and inserted into a quartz tube within a tube furnace (Across International, STF1200). The furnace temperature was set to 300°C, above the melting points of the plastics (table S4), to generate PDSPs under a controlled, smoldering-like thermal-degradation regime that promotes release of condensable decomposition products and their subsequent particle formation upon cooling. Zero air flowed through the furnace at 1 liter min−1 with no added oxygen. Under these conditions, particle formation likely occurred through homogeneous nucleation as vapors traveled to cooler regions of the tube (70). The generated aerosol was subsequently passed through a Liebig-type condenser to reduce gas-phase semivolatile compounds, and volatile organic compounds were further removed using an inline charcoal denuder. The generated particles were collected on poly(tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE) membrane filters (0.2-μm pore size, 47-mm diameter, Whatman, Sigma-Aldrich) downstream of a PM1 (particulate matter less than 1 μm) cyclone.

PDSPs were obtained from the filters by suspending them in Milli-Q water, followed by vortex mixing and ultrasonication to disaggregate particles and achieve a homogeneous suspension. For comparative analysis, commercially sourced PSL (200-nm diameter, Sigma-Aldrich) served as a standard surrogate for MNPPs. Both PDSPs from smoldering burns and PSL solutions were individually atomized using a constant output atomizer (TSI model 3076) to generate particles, which subsequently passed through a diffusion dryer. The mass size distribution and composition of the PDSPs was measured using a C-ToF-AMS with a modal vacuum aerodynamic diameter centered at around 200 nm, as shown in fig. S14.

Oxidation experiments

The oxidation experimental setup shown in fig. S15 involves exposing PDSPs and PSL to OH radicals in the custom-built 10-liter flow tube reactor (10.2-cm inner diameter, 120-cm length) to simulate atmospheric heterogeneous aging processes (71). Inside the reactor, OH radicals were produced from the photolysis of O3 in the presence of water vapor under the irradiation of 254-nm ultraviolet (UV) lamp. O3 was generated by passing dry air through an ozone generator (Oxidation Technologies, OZX-300), and its concentrations were continuously monitored by an ozone analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., model 49i). The concentration of OH radicals was varied by changing the amount of O3 and determined by measuring the decay of cyclohexane using GC–flame ionization detector (model 8610C, SRI Instruments Inc.) equipped with a Tenax TA trap. The rate constant for cyclohexane-OH reactions is reported to be 6.97 × 10−12 cm3 molecule−1 s−1 at 298 K (72). The aerosol residence time in the reactor was approximately 100 s. OH exposure ranged from 1.9 × 1011 to 3.8 × 1011 molecules cm−3 s, equivalent to a photochemical age of approximately 1.5 to 2.9 days, based on an average ambient OH radicals concentration of 1.5 × 106 molecules cm−3 over 24 hours, following the methodology outlined in literature (73). Reactor temperature (23° ± 2°C) and RH (35 ± 5%) were monitored using an Omega HX94C RH/T transmitter.

Control experiments were conducted to assess the impact of factors, including UV lamp only and O3 only on heterogeneous oxidative evolution of NPs. The changes in the AMS mass spectra of NPs under these conditions were minor (within 10%) compared to those observed with OH radicals (fig. S16), confirming that the observed transformations were primarily driven by OH-induced reactions.

Chemical analysis

Particles exiting the flow reactor were analyzed by a C-ToF-AMS (Aerodyne Research Inc., Billerica, USA). The C-ToF-AMS (hereafter referred to as AMS) provided insights into the bulk chemical composition and oxidation evolution of the NPs. In this study, the sampling flow rate of AMS was 1.4 cm3 s−1. After the sampled particles pass through the particle time-of-flight sizing chamber, the particles were vaporized on a heated porous tungsten surface (600°C) and immediately ionized by electron impact at 70 eV. Data were processed using “SQUIRREL 1.60P” software in Igor Pro7 (Wavemetrics, Oregon, USA). Mass spectral data from AMS were in unit mass resolution, and therefore, molecular formulas were proposed on the basis of unit m/z and knowledge of plastic material studied. PDSPs were collected on quartz fiber filters in parallel with PTFE filter sampling, PSL particles were deposited from solution onto quartz fiber filters and dried before analysis, and standard plastic pellets were analyzed directly by Py–GC-MS (7890A GC/5975C MSD, Agilent) with a multishot pyrolyzer (Frontier Laboratories). Samples in stainless steel cups (4 mm o.d. by 5 mm H, 50 μl) underwent double-shot analysis: TD (100° to 250°C, 7.5 min) followed by Py (600°C, 12 s). Details of Py–GC-MS analysis have been given elsewhere (74).

H2O2 digestion

For each polymer type, 1 ml of PDSP suspension was mixed with 4 ml of 30% H2O2 solution in a glass vial. The mixture was incubated at 60° to 70°C for 24 hours to initiate oxidation and then maintained at room temperature for 72 hours until gas evolution ceased. A control treatment was conducted by replacing the H2O2 with an equivalent volume of Milli-Q water under identical conditions. Quartz filters were prebaked at 500°C for 24 hours to eliminate any residual organic contaminants. The treated solutions were then dropped onto the baked filters and dried under ambient conditions. The loaded filters were analyzed by Py–GC-MS using both TD and Py modes. The resulting chromatograms were evaluated by comparing peak areas between TD and Py modes to quantify the extent of digestion.

TPD-DART-HRMS analysis

Samples of PP, LDPE, PS, and PET plastics were thermally decomposed in a copper sample pot mounted on an Ion-Rocket heating stage (BioChromato Inc. Fujisawa, Kanagawa, Japan) interfaced with a DART ionization source (JumpShot, IonSense Inc., Saugus, MA) and an Orbitrap Q Exactive HF-X high-resolution mass analyzer (Thermo Scientific Inc.). Samples were heated at a rate of 100°C min−1 to a final temperature of 600°C to induce thermal decomposition. Positive ion mode was used for the analysis of PP, LDPE, and PS fragments (75) as it provided higher ionization efficiency and signal intensity than negative mode for these polymers (34). The high oxygen content of PET favors the detection of its thermal decomposition products in negative ion mode (76), which was therefore used for their characterization. High-resolution mass spectral features were integrated over the full heating range (50° to 600°C) to determine the elemental composition and relative abundances of the thermal degradation products (34). Molecular formula assignments were constrained to C ≤ 100, H ≤ 200, N ≤ 1, O ≤ 50, with a mass accuracy window of ±2 parts per million. The analysis was limited to common ion species, including [M + H]+, [M + NH4]+, and [M•]+ in positive mode and [M-H]−, [M + NO2]−, [M•]− in negative mode, where M represents neutral species.

Saturation mass concentrations and enthalpies of vaporization for individual plastic thermal decomposition products were calculated using parameterization methods described in a previously described work (34), enabling the construction of VBS distributions. These data were used to assess gas-particle partitioning behavior and estimate particle-phase viscosity across atmospherically relevant temperatures and representative total organic aerosol mass loadings (34).
