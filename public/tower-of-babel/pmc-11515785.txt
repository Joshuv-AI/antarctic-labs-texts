The psychometric properties of Chinese version of the Gilles de la Tourette syndrome-quality of life scale (GTS-QOL) for children and adolescents
Authors: Kai Yang, Xianbin Wang, Shujin Hu, Yanlin Li, Tianyuan Lei, Ying Li, Yonghua Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGilles de la Tourette syndrome (GTS) is a persistent neurological disorder that profoundly affects the quality of life for afflicted individuals, however, tailored health-related quality of life (QOL) measures for Chinese pediatric GTS patients are lacking. This pioneering study aims to develop a QOL scale for Chinese children with GTS.MethodsA cohort of 1,121 children (aged 6–16 years) diagnosed with GTS participated in scale development. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were performed to assess the factor structure of the GTS-QOL-Chinese, examining goodness-of-fit indices, factor loadings of individual items, and covariances between factors. Validity was determined through interscale correlations and comparisons with external measures to establish convergent and discriminant validity.ResultsFollowing reliability and validity analyses, a Chinese version of the 24-item GTS-specific quality of life scale was conclusively established, encompassing five subscales (psychological, compulsive, cognitive, physical, social). The scale demonstrated high internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.93, and validity was substantiated through interscale correlations ranging from 0.46 to 0.68. EFA identified five factors, explaining 61.26% of the total variation. CFA further confirmed the scale’s robustness, with all goodness-of-fit indicators meeting acceptable criteria (AGFI = 0.86, PCFI = 0.78, GFI = 0.89, CFI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.06).ConclusionsThe Chinese version of the GTS-QOL scale has exhibits robust reliability and validity and is valuable for assessing the quality of life of Chinese children and adolescents with GTS. This scale may assist in tailoring interventions and improving patient care.Keywords: Gilles de la Tourette Syndrome, Quality of life, Reliability and validity, Tic disorder, Sinicization

Background

Gilles de la Tourette syndrome (GTS) is a neurological disorder characterized by the presence of multiple tics and vocal expressions, typically manifesting in childhood and often accompanied by behavioural symptoms [1]. The global prevalence of GTS is estimated to range from 0.3 to 0.9% [2]. In Chinese children, the overall prevalence rate of GTS is approximately 2.5%, with some individuals experiencing concurrent psychological and behavioural disorders [3]. Research indicates that tic disorders, including GTS, are linked to a diminished quality of life among children and adolescents. The health-related quality of life of children with GTS is notably lower than that of their counterparts without GTS [4]. This discrepancy is particularly pronounced in cases involving psychological abnormalities, affecting various aspects such as learning, social interactions, and emotional well-being [5, 6]. For example, adolescents with GTS face an elevated risk of peer attachment insecurity, which may serve as a significant variable influencing their overall quality of life.

Since its initial introduction in 2008, the Gilles de la Tourette Syndrome-Quality of Life Scale (GTS-QOL) has undergone validation in Europe for its original English, French, and Italian versions [7–10]. These validated versions have proven instrumental in assisting individuals with GTS in assessing their quality of life. The GTS-QOL, a self-administered questionnaire consisting of 27 questions, has emerged as one of the most widely utilized assessment tools for the GTS in clinical settings [7]. Each version of the GTS-QOL has demonstrated robust internal consistency, supported by compelling data. The content validity, calibration validity, and structural validity of each scale also exhibited commendable performance on the basis of their respective datasets. Notably, only in the past two years has the health-related quality of life (HR-QOL) indicator specific to GTS been introduced in the Asian population [11]. This indicator targets various aspects of the disorder that significantly impact a patient’s QOL.

Nevertheless, relevant studies on the validation of the GTS-QOL have been conducted predominantly in Western populations, with very few studies specifically addressing Asian populations, especially Chinese patients [11]. Notably, there is a substantial population base in China, with studies suggesting that tens of millions of children in the country are currently affected by tic disorders [12]. While tools for evaluating the severity of multiple GTS symptoms in Chinese children and adolescents are available [13], there is still a notable absence of disease-specific HR-QOL indicators for children with GTS in China. The evaluation of relevant scales in this context remains largely unexplored. Some general HR-QOL instruments used in GTS patients may not adequately capture specific distress-causing characteristics of GTS, such as motor and vocal tics and repetitive behaviours [7]. Hence, there is a need for specific tools such as the GTS-QOL. In China, the application of the GTS-QOL-Chinese is highly warranted and spans basic scientific research on the aetiology, mechanism, treatment, and prognosis of GTS, as well as its clinical management. However, a dedicated assessment tool is lacking, and the GTS-QOL-Chinese effectively addresses the primary shortcomings identified. When used in conjunction with other general quality-of-life scales capable of assessing a child’s social psychology, physiological psychology, and living environment [14], it can provide a more comprehensive evaluation of various disease characteristics in children with GTS.

Our primary objective is to develop and assess the reliability and validity of GTS-QOL-Chinese for children and adolescents. This initiative aims to serve as a valuable reference for studies focusing on the quality of life and health of children and adolescents with GTS. The ultimate goal is to establish this scale as an effective tool for clinicians and researchers, enabling a more nuanced characterization of each individual patient with GTS and facilitating monitoring of the clinical course or treatment outcomes. Moreover, it will assist psychiatric professionals in conducting more accurate prognostic assessments for children with GTS. This research represents an inaugural attempt to validate and apply the GTS-QOL in Chinese children, marking a significant step forwards in enhancing our understanding and evaluation of the impact of the GTS on the lives of affected individuals in this population.

Methods

Participants

Data for the scale were collected from children who visited the psychiatric outpatient department of Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of GTS by two attending child psychiatrists simultaneously, in accordance with the criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition (DSM-5); (2) aged 6–16 years old; (3) no comorbidity with other mental disorders based on clinician diagnosis (excluding obsessive-compulsive disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder); (4) no diagnosis of epilepsy or other neurological disorders; (5) exclusion of duplicate items, clearly random responses, and missing answers. Notably, we found that children with GTS had a lot of anxiety symptoms in clinical observation, but did not reach the diagnosis of anxiety, so we did not include children with other comorbidities such as anxiety and mood disorders. After applying these criteria, the effective sample size was ultimately determined to be 1,121 cases. Following the recommended sample size calculation method, which suggests a range of 5 to 10 times the number of scale items [15], and considering that the initial version of the scale comprised 27 items, the calculated sample size ranged from 135 to 270 cases, which made the sample size included in this study sufficiently large. Additionally, 33 patients returned to the hospital within one month and underwent retesting for consistency (Fig. 1). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Medical Ethics Committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital Affiliated to Capital Medical University (Ethics Approval Number IEC-C-006-A04-V.07) and with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and their parents or legal guardians included in the study.

Questionnaire translation

The GTS Quality of Life scale, developed by Cavanna and colleagues in 2008, comprises 27 items [7]. It uses a Likert 5-point scoring system, where subjects are required to select a response ranging from 1 (no trouble) to 5 (extremely troubled). A higher score indicates a greater negative impact on the quality of life of individuals affected by GTS.

For the sinicization scale, professional researchers and disease experts in China translated the original scale with authorization from Cavanna, the first author of the original scale. The translation process involved a forwards-backwards approach. Initially, the questionnaire was translated into Chinese by a professional translator, and the Chinese version was then back-translated into English by two independent, blinded professional translators. The language validity of the provisional questionnaire in Chinese was confirmed through discussions with translation specialists and clinicians. This rigorous process culminated in the development of a new Chinese version of the GTS-QOL scale.

Measures

The Gilles De La Tourette syndrome–quality of life scale (GTS-QOL)

The GTS-QOL is a self-rating scale consisting of 27 questions covering mental and psychological aspects of daily life, intense thoughts and behaviours, and cognitive functions [7]. Each question offers five distinct options, corresponding to varying levels of severity. The participants selected their perceived severity levels on the basis of their experiences over the preceding four weeks.

The Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS)

The YGTSS is an examiner-rating scale, structured to evaluate the overall severity of tic symptoms across various dimensions, including number, frequency, intensity, complexity, and interference [16]. The effective utilization of YGTSS necessitates clinical expertise in dealing with multiple Tourette cases. The conclusive evaluation is derived from a comprehensive consideration of all available data, embodying the clinician’s holistic impression of each assessed item [17].

Children and adolescents’ quality of Life Scale

The Children and Adolescents’ QOL Scale is a comprehensive, multidimensional self-assessment tool designed to evaluate the quality of life of children and adolescents [14]. It centers around aspects related to education and daily life, encompassing areas such as physiology, psychology, social functioning, and living environment. Tailored for primary and middle school students, three dimensions—social psychology, physiological psychology, and living environment—comprising a total of 49 items. The scoring system employs a Likert scale with 4-point options [14].

Statistical analyses

Scaling assumptions were scrutinized via various statistical measures, including the range of item mean scores, range of item standard deviations, and range of corrected item-total correlations. Additionally, principal component factor analysis was conducted to provide insights into scaling assumptions. Acceptability was assessed through the mean and standard deviation, median, range of scores, standard errors, and skewness. For reliability evaluation, internal consistency was measured via Cronbach’s alpha, ensuring the consistency of the responses within the scale. Test-retest reliability was determined through intraclass correlation coefficients, which provide an assessment of stability over time. Validity was assessed through inter-scale correlation, examining how well the scale correlates with related measures. Intergroup differential validity was also considered to gauge the extent to which the scale discriminates between different groups, establishing its validity across diverse populations. Initially, we employed the principal component extraction method along with the oblique proximal rotation method for factor analysis, aiming to establish correlations among all factors. This approach facilitated the exploration of the dimensions and factor distribution within each subscale [18]. Given the potential influence of social culture on the understanding and definition of QOL, we opted for an exploratory analysis of the project structure [19, 20]. To validate the appropriateness of the factor analysis, we utilized Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) statistics and the Bartlett sphericity test. A KMO value exceeding 0.5 is considered acceptable [21], and Bartlett’s test results in significant results (p < 0.05). In the analysis, a project load greater than 0.40, with minimal or no cross-loading across projects (i.e., projects retained with a load of 0.40 or higher on two or more factors), was considered [18]. During the factor analysis, items with low factor loads or those with cross-loads above 0.40 in different dimensions were excluded to enhance the reliability and interpretability of the extracted factors [22, 23].

Second, the internal consistency of each subscale was assessed via Cronbach’s α coefficient. For all scales, we aimed for corrected item-total correlations to surpass the recommended threshold of 0.4 [24]. All reliability estimates, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, exceeded 0.7 [25]. To evaluate test-retest reliability, the same group of subjects completed the same scale at two different times—before and after. The correlation coefficient of the two measurement results was used to express test-retest reliability. The intraclass coefficient (ICC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated via a two-way mixed effects model for a single measurement. Test-retest reliability was categorized as poor, moderate, good, or excellent on the basis of the 95% CI of the ICC: less than 0.5, between 0.5 and 0.75, between 0.75 and 0.9, or greater than 0.90, respectively [26, 27].

Third, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis using identified models to assess factor load sufficiency and goodness of fit. We employed several fit indices, including root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.1 [28], adjusted goodness of fit index (AGFI) > 0.8 [29], comparative fit index (CFI) > 0.8 [30], parsimony comparative fit index (PCFI) > 0.5, goodness-of-fit index (GFI) > 0.8 [31] and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) < 0.08 [32]. Additionally, we examined the standardized covariance between the factors and assessed the inter-scale correlation of the subscales to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the model fit and relationships between different dimensions.

All statistical analyses were conducted via SPSS 26.0. Descriptive statistical analysis, reliability, and validity analyses were performed for the general information and scale scores of the subjects after data collection. The five-dimensional factor model was constructed via Amos Graphics 21.0.

Results

The sample dataset included a total of 1,121 children, with 913 (81.4%) being male and 208 (18.6%) being female. The mean age of the participants was 8.7 years, with a standard deviation of 2.2. The age range of the children included in the study ranged from 6 to 16 years. The YGTSS was administered to 1,121 patients, while 37 patients completed the Children and Adolescent’ QOL Scale for the validity questionnaire. Exploratory factor analysis

We conducted an extensive evaluation encompassing scaling assumptions, acceptability, reliability, and validity. A principal component factor analysis of the 27 GTS-QOL-Chinese items was performed. The KMO measure validated the sampling adequacy of the analysis, with a value of 0.95, surpassing the recommended threshold of 0.6. This indicated that the input variables were deemed sufficient for conducting factor analysis. The Bartlett sphericity test (chi-square = 12991.30, p < 0.001) further confirmed that the correlation between items was substantial enough to justify proceeding with a principal component factor analysis.

Scaling assumptions and acceptability

We assessed the scaling assumptions and acceptability for the GTS-QOL-Chinese and its five factors. The assessment revealed a total skewness of 1.91, with individual factor skewness ranging from 1.32 to 4.23. The ceiling effect for the total GTS-QOL-Chinese was 0.05, while the floor effect was 0.08. Ceiling effects for the five factors ranged from 0.03 to 0.05, and floor effects varied between 0.18 and 0.78. Notably, Factor 2, which pertains to obsessive-compulsive symptoms, exhibited a higher floor effect. Item-total correlations were greater than 0.40 for all items.

Reliability

The Cronbach’s alpha for the GTS-QOL-Chinese scale is 0.93, indicating high internal consistency. All reliability estimates, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, surpassed the recommended threshold of 0.7. Regarding test-retest reliability in 33 patients, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for the overall scale was 0.91. The ICC values for the psychological, obsessive-compulsive, cognitive, social, and physical factors were 0.83, 0.88, 0.90, 0.65, and 0.48, respectively (Table 2).

Validity

For each scale, all the items’ response options were endorsed, except for three items (Item 6, 18, and 24: ‘Embarrassing gesture,’ ‘Lack of self-confidence,’ and ‘Need more supports’). CFA demonstrated sufficient factor loadings and a good fit (Fig. 2). The final version of the GTS-QOL-Chinese comprises 24 items distributed across five dimensions: psychological, physical, obsessive-compulsive, social, and cognitive factors (Table 3). All goodness-of-fit indicators affirmed the appropriateness of the 5-factor model (RMSEA = 0.07, AGFI = 0.86, CFI = 0.89, PCFI = 0.78, GFI = 0.89, SRMR = 0.06).

Discussion

The GTS-QOL represents a novel standardized self-rating scale within the Asian context. The existing literature on GTS-QOL validation in Asian populations is limited, with the relevant scale only being studied in the Japanese population in 2022 [11]. In its original language and cultural context, stable psychometric characteristics have been confirmed, establishing it as an effective and reliable tool for measuring psychological resources [7]. This study represents the first exploration of the reliability and validity of the GTS-QOL in Chinese children and adolescents aged 6–16 years. The application of 24 items, guided by both exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis, led to the successful adaptation of the scale to the Chinese population. The results indicate that the Chinese version of the GTS-QOL has robust validity and reliability, positioning it as an effective measurement tool for evaluating the quality of life among Chinese children with GTS. This achievement enables a more direct observation of the specific quality of life experienced by Chinese children with GTS.

The results clearly show that among Chinese children and adolescents, social psychology, physiological psychology, and living environment are highly correlated with the GTS-QOL. Notably, physiological and psychological factors display the highest correlation with the GTS-QOL. The ICCs for the social and physical subscales are 0.65 and 0.48, respectively. According to relevant studies, the retest reliability of the social subscale is deemed moderate, whereas that of the physical subscale is considered poor. Although this discrepancy might be related to the time lapse between the two scale tests before and after, both subscales were statistically significant (ps < 0.01) [26]. Additionally, different age groups may warrant distinct treatment plans on the basis of varying quality of life considerations.

Recent research, utilizing quantitative and qualitative methods, has demonstrated that individuals with GTS experience significantly lower subjective quality of life than the general population does. Children with TS reported lower subjective quality of life across various dimensions, including family life, school life, peer relationships, cognitive components, the environment, self-awareness, and total score of depressive experiences, than did the control group [33]. The Children and Adolescents’ QOL Scale is capable of quantifying these aspects. However, these tools face limitations in terms of feasibility and acceptability in patients with neuropsychiatric disorders and may fail to track the impact of interventions on tic-related symptoms. Furthermore, these tools often lack sensitivity to specific characteristics crucial to individuals with GTS, such as motor and vocal tics, repetitive behaviours, and other tic-associated symptoms. The specific tool developed in this study is strongly correlated with the psychological and physical factors mentioned in previous research. These factors are incorporated into the GTS-QOL-Chinese subscales, suggesting potential psychological factors influencing the disease in GTS patients. Consequently, while QOL measures have been employed to assess QOL in GTS patients, they may underestimate or distort subjective perceptions of health problems in these individuals. Therefore, the introduction of a disease-specific measure of quality of life, such as the GTS-QOL-Chinese, can enhance the evaluation of GTS patients’ conditions, particularly in terms of vocal tic and motor tic severity. Given the limited studies on the quality of life of children and adolescents with GTS in recent years [34], this study holds significant value for GTS patients.

Compared with previous related research, the GTS-QOL-Chinese adopts five dimensions—psychological, obsessive-compulsive, cognitive, social, and physical—similar to the French version of the scale [10]. Additionally, it introduces social factors, distinguishing it from the original four-dimensional scale and the Japanese version of the scale [7, 11]. The English version of the scale includes six dimensions: cognitive, coprophenomena, psychological, physical, obsessive-compulsive, and activities of daily living (ADL) [9]. In terms of social factors, numerous factors, including parental factors, teacher education level, family economic factors, and traditional culture, significantly influence on Chinese children with GTS, many of whom are left-behind children. Research indicates that maternal education level and democratic parenting patterns play crucial roles in the social function of children with GTS [35].

When the Chinese study sample was compared with the British sample in the English version of the GTS-QOL study [9] and the Italian sample in the Italian GTS-QOL study [8], the average GTS-QOL score in the Chinese study was lower than that in the British sample but higher than that of the Italian sample (36.4 in the present study compared with 45.0 in the English version and 26.7 in the Italian version). The mean YGTSS total tic severity score in our study was lower than that in the Italian sample, with no recorded data from the UK version (28.1 in this study compared with 44.3 in the study by Cavanna and colleagues). Although Chinese children with GTS report less severe tic symptoms, they appear to be more distressed by their tic symptoms than their Italian counterparts. While the GTS-QOL may not necessarily be culturally equivalent, the cultural context should be considered when assessing the overall score of the GTS-QOL [11].

We acknowledge several limitations in our study that warrant clarification. First, our study focused exclusively on Chinese children and adolescents aged 6 to 16 years, excluding data from adults and younger children. In the future, we aim to enhance the applicability of our scale across a broader age spectrum. Second, this study is a single-center clinical research conducted at a comprehensive children’s hospital. Patients with GTS who seek treatment here may predominantly present with mild to moderate cases, as those with more severe conditions are more likely to attend specialized hospitals. As a result, our study population may exhibit fewer comorbidities. Future work should expand to multicenter and larger populations in hospital, community and school settings to validate the scale’s reliability and validity. Finally, although numerous studies have demonstrated that GTS comorbidities often result in more clinical impairment than GTS itself [5], our study did not delve into the impact of comorbidities on the quality of life of GTS patients.

Conclusion

The Chinese version of the GTS-QOL for children and adolescents demonstrates strong reliability and validity, making it a valuable tool for assessing the quality of life in the Chinese children and adolescents affected by GTS.
