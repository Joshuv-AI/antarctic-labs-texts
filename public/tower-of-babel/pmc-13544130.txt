Spatially Contextualized Integrative Genomics Highlights Neuronal and Glial Regulatory Programs in Low Back Pain
Authors: Guangyi Tao, Linzi Wang, Hanzhe Du, Zhonghe Zeng, Longming Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLow back pain (LBP) is a heterogeneous pain condition with a measurable genetic contribution, but the genes, brain cell types, and spatial tissue contexts through which inherited risk is expressed remain unclear. We aimed to define cell-type-specific and spatially contextualized genetic mechanisms underlying LBP.MethodsFinnGen R12 LBP GWAS summary statistics (42,521 cases and 353,224 controls) were integrated with brain single-nuclei eQTL data across eight major brain cell classes. We evaluated genome-wide polygenic signal using LDSC, prioritized genes using MAGMA and PoPS, and performed brain cell-type-specific eQTL-anchored Mendelian randomization, primarily based on single-instrument Wald ratio estimates, followed by Bayesian colocalization. Spatial genetic mapping was conducted using gsMap in an E16.5 murine embryonic atlas and two adult human lumbar spinal cord Visium sections. Selected candidates were assessed by RT-qPCR in neuronal-like and astroglial-like inflammatory cell models.ResultsLDSC supported interpretable polygenic signal for LBP. MAGMA and PoPS showed partial gene-level convergence, with TCF4 and TMEFF2 supported by both approaches. Across 1641 tested gene-cell type exposures, significant eQTL-anchored MR associations were concentrated in excitatory neurons, oligodendrocytes, inhibitory neurons, and astrocytes. Integrated eQTL-anchored MR, colocalization, and gene-prioritization evidence highlighted CLEC18A, QPRT, and GMPPB as higher-priority non-MHC candidates with moderate, but not strong, colocalization support. gsMap localized LBP-associated enrichment to neuroaxis-related embryonic regions, including brain, spinal cord, sympathetic nerve, and dorsal root ganglion, and to neuronal-like niches in adult lumbar spinal cord. RT-qPCR showed model-dependent expression changes, with QPRT and LGI4 preferentially responsive in neuronal-like SH-SY5Y cells and GMPPB and DPYSL5 responsive in astroglial-like U251 cells.ConclusionThese findings support neuronal and glial regulatory programs as plausible contributors to LBP genetic susceptibility and highlight CLEC18A, QPRT, and GMPPB as higher-priority non-MHC candidates with moderate colocalization support. The results provide a spatially contextualized framework for candidate prioritization in LBP, while emphasizing the need for larger cell-type-specific eQTL resources and functional validation before therapeutic or mechanistic conclusions can be drawn.Keywords: low back pain, pain genetics, integrative genomics, Mendelian randomization, colocalization, spatial transcriptomics, gsMap, single-nuclei eQTL, neuroaxis, RT-qPCR

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide and represents a major clinical and socioeconomic burden.1 Importantly, LBP is increasingly understood as a biologically heterogeneous condition rather than a purely local structural disorder, and contemporary pain frameworks emphasize that persistent LBP may reflect varying contributions from nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic mechanisms.2 This conceptual shift has increased interest in identifying the molecular and neurobiological pathways that shape individual susceptibility to chronic or persistent back pain.

Evidence from family- and population-based studies suggests that LBP has a substantial heritable component.3 Consistent with this, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have progressively expanded the catalog of back pain-associated loci, from early reports identifying a limited number of chronic back pain loci4 to more recent studies implicating broader sets of genes and pathways related not only to cartilage and bone biology but also to neurological and inflammatory processes.5 Most recently, a large multi-ancestry meta-analysis identified 67 new loci associated with chronic back pain and reported enrichment in brain- and pituitary-related biology, further reinforcing the view that the genetic architecture of back pain extends beyond local spinal structures alone.6

Despite these advances, a major translational bottleneck remains. As with many complex traits, GWAS signals alone do not readily resolve the relevant effector genes, disease-relevant cell types, or anatomical niches through which genetic risk is implemented, particularly because many associated variants act through regulatory rather than protein-coding mechanisms. This gap is especially important in back pain research, where chronic back pain heritability appears to be enriched for brain-expressed genes,7 and where converging experimental evidence implicates spinal inhibitory interneurons,8 astrocytes,9 and oligodendrocyte-lineage cells in pain amplification, persistence, and circuit-level modulation.10 At the same time, brain- or neuroaxis-related genetic signals in a musculoskeletal pain condition must be interpreted cautiously. They may reflect CNS-mediated pain susceptibility, central sensitization, or shared neuropsychological components of chronic pain, and they do not exclude peripheral nociceptive, inflammatory, or sensitization mechanisms arising from spinal tissues, paraspinal structures, or immune–neural interactions. Thus, the aim is not to replace peripheral models of LBP, but to define the central neuroaxis-related regulatory layer through which inherited risk may be partly expressed.

Recent methodological advances now make this question more approachable. In particular, genetically informed spatial mapping of cells for complex traits (gsMap) provides a framework for integrating GWAS summary statistics with spatial transcriptomics to localize trait-associated cellular signals in anatomically resolved tissue contexts.11 In this study, the E16.5 murine embryonic atlas was used as a cross-species developmental discovery reference rather than as a direct adult disease validation dataset. Its value lies in providing a broad spatial map of conserved neurodevelopmental and neuroaxis-related transcriptional programs, whereas the adult human lumbar spinal cord Visium sections were used to provide limited but disease-relevant anatomical contextualization in adult spinal tissue. Building on these complementary resources, we designed a stepwise integrative genomics strategy to characterize the genetic architecture of LBP, prioritize candidate genes, test brain cell-type-specific eQTL-anchored regulatory associations, and define the spatial contexts in which LBP-associated genetic liability may be expressed. Specifically, we combined LDSC, MAGMA, PoPS, brain cell-type-specific eQTL-anchored MR, Bayesian colocalization, cross-species and adult human spinal gsMap, and supportive RT-qPCR assessment. We hypothesized that inherited susceptibility to LBP would preferentially converge on neuronal and glial regulatory programs and that these signals would localize to neuroaxis-related spatial contexts rather than being uniformly distributed across tissues. Materials and Methods

Study Design and Analytical Framework

This study was based entirely on the analysis of publicly available, summary-level genetic and transcriptomic datasets. All original studies had obtained the necessary ethical approvals and informed consent from participants. We implemented a stepwise integrative genomics framework to characterize the genetic architecture of low back pain (LBP), identify brain cell-type-specific candidate genes associated with disease susceptibility, and define the spatial contexts in which genetic risk may be expressed. Specifically, the analytical workflow comprised six major components: (1) acquisition and harmonization of LBP GWAS summary statistics and brain cell-type-specific eQTL datasets; (2) evaluation of genome-wide polygenic signal using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC); (3) gene-level prioritization using Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA) and Polygenic Priority Score (PoPS); (4) brain cell-type-specific Mendelian randomization (MR) followed by Bayesian colocalization analysis to refine candidate genes; (5) spatial genetic mapping using gsMap in a cross-species embryonic atlas and adult human spinal cord sections; and (6) supportive cell-based RT-qPCR expression assessment of selected candidates. This design was intended to proceed from global genetic signal to gene-level prioritization, then to cell-type-resolved inference, spatial localization, and supportive experimental assessment.

GWAS Summary Statistics for Low Back Pain

Summary statistics for LBP were obtained from FinnGen release R12. The primary outcome used in this study was M13_LOWBACKPAIN, which included 42,521 cases and 353,224 controls. To ensure compatibility across downstream analyses, the outcome dataset was standardized into two complementary formats: a full-variant table indexed by chr:pos:ref:alt, and an rsID-ready table for LDSC, MAGMA, MR, and colocalization analyses. When multiple rsIDs were listed for the same variant, the first valid rsID was retained. For each variant, the effect allele, non-effect allele, beta, standard error, P value, effect allele frequency, and sample-size information were preserved during harmonization. The resulting standardized outcome resource was used consistently across all downstream analyses.

Brain Cell-Type-Specific eQTL Datasets and Exposure Preprocessing

To investigate cell-type-specific regulatory effects, we used brain cis-eQTL summary statistics derived from single-nuclei RNA-seq of post-mortem human brains across eight major brain cell classes: astrocytes, endothelial cells, excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, microglia, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), and pericytes. Because eQTL-anchored Mendelian randomization is best performed in molecular datasets minimally confounded by disease status, the primary MR analyses in this study used the controls-only subset (n = 183) from the full resource of 391 individuals, as provided in the publicly available summary-statistics dataset reported by Haglund et al.12

To derive candidate exposure instruments, we first retained genome-wide significant cis-eQTLs at P < 5×10−8 within each cell type. These significant eQTLs were then reformatted into exposure-ready files by restricting analyses to autosomal variants, excluding variants with MAF < 0.01, deriving the standard error as |beta/t|, and calculating the F statistic as t2. The cleaned exposure files were subsequently used for LD clumping and two-sample MR analyses.

Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression

To determine whether the LBP GWAS dataset carried sufficient polygenic signal for downstream integrative analyses, we performed LDSC using the standardized FinnGen M13_LOWBACKPAIN summary statistics.13 The GWAS summary statistics were first reformatted into an LDSC-compatible file containing SNP ID, effect allele, non-effect allele, association P value, total sample size, effect estimate, standard error, and allele frequency. These formatted statistics were processed with munge_sumstats.py, with allele harmonization against the HapMap3 reference SNP list (w_hm3.snplist) to restrict the analysis to well-imputed, high-confidence common variants. SNP heritability was then estimated using the baselineLD annotation model together with the European regression-weight LD score files (eur_w_ld_chr).14 The primary outputs of interest were the observed-scale SNP heritability, the LDSC intercept, and the attenuation ratio, which were used to evaluate the overall genetic signal and the relative contribution of confounding versus true polygenicity.

Gene-Level Prioritization Using MAGMA and PoPS

To prioritize LBP-associated genes beyond single-variant association signals, we combined gene-based association testing with genome-wide functional prioritization. First, MAGMA was applied to evaluate gene-level association signals using SNP-level summary statistics from the standardized LBP GWAS.15 SNP-level association statistics were converted into MAGMA input files containing SNP identifiers, genomic coordinates, and P values. Positional gene annotation was performed using an Ensembl-based gene location file derived from the PoPS-compatible annotation resource, and linkage disequilibrium was modeled using the 1000 Genomes European reference panel (g1000_eur).16 Gene-level testing was then performed using the SNP-wise mean model with the full LBP sample size. This ENSG-based MAGMA analysis generated the final.genes.out and.genes.raw files used for downstream prioritization.

We next applied PoPS to derive a genome-wide functional prioritization score for each gene. PoPS was run using the curated Ensembl gene annotation table (pops_native_for_pops.tsv), the preprocessed multi-feature matrix (pops_features), and the ENSG-harmonized MAGMA outputs. The feature matrix comprised 116 chunks. PoPS was run with MAGMA covariates and MAGMA error covariance enabled, HLA-region removal during feature projection and model training, a feature selection threshold of P < 0.05, and ridge regression as the prediction model. In the integrated interpretation framework, genes supported by both MAGMA and PoPS were considered convergent candidates, whereas genes highlighted predominantly by one method were retained as supportive but lower-priority candidates.

Brain Cell-Type-Specific eQTL-Anchored Mendelian Randomization Analysis

To evaluate directional associations between genetically proxied gene expression in major brain cell classes and low back pain risk, we performed two-sample MR using cis-eQTLs derived from the single-nucleus brain eQTL resource. For each cell type, genome-wide significant cis-eQTLs were retained at P < 5×10−8. Exposure files were restricted to autosomal variants, variants with MAF < 0.01 were excluded, and the exposure standard error was derived as |beta/t| when required. Instrument strength was quantified using the F statistic, calculated as t2 when the eQTL t statistic was available or as beta2/SE2 when beta and standard error were used.

To obtain independent instruments, LD clumping was performed within each gene and cell type using PLINK v1.9017 and the 1000 Genomes European reference panel, with clumping parameters set to r2 < 0.001 and a 10 Mb window. Because nearly all gene–cell-type exposures retained only one independent cis-eQTL after stringent clumping, the primary MR estimates were derived mainly using the Wald ratio method.18 For exposures with more than one independent instrument, inverse-variance weighted analysis was applied where applicable. Given the predominance of single-instrument Wald ratio estimates, pleiotropy-oriented MR sensitivity analyses such as MR-Egger regression, weighted median analysis, and MR-PRESSO could not be applied systematically. Therefore, the MR layer was interpreted as eQTL-anchored directional association evidence rather than definitive causal inference. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate across all tested gene–cell-type combinations, and FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Instrument-level details and F-statistics are reported in Supplementary Table S4, with the FDR-significant associations summarized in Table 1.

Bayesian Colocalization Analysis

To distinguish potentially shared local genetic signals from associations driven by regional linkage structure, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis for the FDR-significant non-MHC MR candidates using the coloc package (version 5.2.2). For each shortlisted gene–cell-type pair, the full gene-specific eQTL summary statistics were re-extracted from the original single-cell brain eQTL archives rather than using only the clumped lead SNPs. Outcome associations for the same set of SNPs were then extracted from the standardized LBP outcome summary statistics. Variants were aligned between exposure and outcome using SNP ID and allele orientation, and SNPs with irreconcilable allele configurations were excluded.19

For the eQTL dataset, colocalization was performed under a quantitative-trait model (type = “quant”), with the variance of beta calculated from the derived standard errors and the expression-trait standard deviation estimated from the eQTL summary statistics. For the LBP GWAS, a case-control model (type = “cc”) was used, with the observed case fraction defined from the FinnGen sample counts. Default prior probabilities were used throughout (p1 = 1 × 10−4, p2 = 1 × 10−4, and p12 = 1×10−5). Posterior probabilities were calculated for the five standard coloc hypotheses (H0–H4), with particular emphasis on PP.H4, the posterior probability that both traits share a single causal variant. Following the interpretation framework used in related brain cell-type MR studies, PP.H4 > 0.80 was considered strong evidence of colocalization, whereas PP.H4 between 0.50 and 0.80 was interpreted as moderate support. Candidates with PP.H4 in the moderate range were therefore not designated as strongly colocalized or core candidates. In the integrated prioritization framework, candidates with the highest PP.H4 values within the moderate range were interpreted as higher-priority moderate-support candidates.

Spatial Genetic Mapping Using gsMap

To localize the spatial distribution of LBP-associated genetic signals, we performed genetically informed spatial mapping of cells for complex traits (gsMap) using both a cross-species embryonic reference and adult human spinal cord spatial transcriptomic data. For cross-species spatial mapping, we used the E16.5 murine embryonic spatial transcriptomic atlas (E16.5_E1S1_MOSTA), which provides a multi-organ developmental reference encompassing both neuroaxis-related and non-neural embryonic tissues.11 For adult human validation, we analyzed two processed lumbar spinal cord 10x Visium sections, GSM6919906_V160-CGND-HRA-02636-D and GSM6919909_V225-CGND-HRC-00022-A, each containing precomputed spatial clusters.

The gsMap analysis was conducted in a stepwise manner. First, latent spatial representations were learned using run_find_latent_representations. Second, gene-level marker scores were derived from the latent embeddings using run_latent_to_gene. For the embryonic atlas, homolog mapping was performed between mouse and human genes during this step to enable cross-species projection. Third, chromosome-specific LD scores were generated using run_generate_ldscore with the 1000 Genomes European reference panel, HapMap3 SNP filters, and the GENCODE v46lift37 basic gene annotation. Fourth, trait-relevant spatial enrichment was evaluated by run_spatial_ldsc using the standardized FinnGen LBP summary statistics. Fifth, annotation-level evidence was summarized by Cauchy combination testing. Finally, report files and diagnostic plots were generated using run_report.

For the adult human spinal sections, the processed h5ad files were reformatted into gsMap-ready inputs by retaining in-tissue spots, preserving spatial coordinates, and using the existing clusters field as the annotation variable. A count layer was constructed from the raw count matrix for gsMap input. Enriched adult spinal clusters were subsequently interpreted biologically by examining their top marker genes from the ranked marker outputs stored in the processed h5ad objects.

Supportive Cell-Based RT-qPCR Expression Assessment

To provide supportive cell-based expression evidence for selected candidates emerging from the integrative genomic analyses, we examined four genes by RT-qPCR: GMPPB, QPRT, DPYSL5, and LGI4. Gene selection was based on combined evidence from eQTL-anchored MR, Bayesian colocalization, MAGMA/PoPS prioritization, and spatial compatibility with the gsMap findings. GMPPB and QPRT were selected as higher-priority moderate-support candidates, whereas DPYSL5 and LGI4 were included as supportive candidates representing glial-lineage and neuronal/axon–glia-related programs.

Two complementary cell models were used to approximate the principal cellular contexts suggested by the genomic and spatial analyses: RA-differentiated SH-SY5Y cells as a neuronal-like model and U251 cells as an astroglial-like model. Both cell lines were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China; catalogue nos. CL-0208 for SH-SY5Y and CL-0237 for U251). SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and differentiated with 10 μM all-trans retinoic acid in medium containing 1% fetal bovine serum for 5 days, with medium replacement every 48 h. U251 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin under standard humidified conditions (37 C, 5% CO2).

To generate an acute inflammatory stimulation context, both cell models were divided into control and stimulated groups. The stimulated group was treated with TNF-α (10 ng/mL) plus IL-1β (10 ng/mL) for 24 h, whereas control cells received vehicle treatment. This experiment was designed to assess whether selected candidates showed cell-context-dependent transcriptional responsiveness under inflammatory stimulation, rather than to directly model the chronic neuroinflammatory milieu of persistent LBP or to validate the constitutive genetic regulation captured by eQTL instruments. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and 1 μg RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. RT-qPCR was performed using a SYBR Green-based detection system with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. Each reaction included three technical replicates, and each condition included three independent biological replicates. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and statistical comparisons were performed on ΔCt values using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Genome-Wide Polygenic Signal and Convergent Gene Prioritization for Low Back Pain

We first evaluated whether the LBP GWAS carried sufficient genome-wide signal to support downstream integrative analyses. LDSC demonstrated a measurable polygenic architecture, with an observed SNP-based heritability of 0.037 (SE = 0.003), an intercept of 1.120 (SE = 0.011), an attenuation ratio of 0.300 (SE = 0.027), a lambda GC of 1.355, and a mean chi-square of 1.401 (Figure 2A). Taken together, these metrics indicate that the FinnGen LBP GWAS contains interpretable polygenic signal and is suitable for subsequent gene-level and cell-context analyses. Detailed LDSC heritability estimates and model-fit statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

We next asked whether gene prioritization would converge across complementary analytical frameworks. MAGMA highlighted a concentrated set of leading genes, including TCF4, SOX5, MICB, TRIM40, TMEFF2, and VARS2, whereas PoPS prioritized TCF4, UBC, NCAM1, MACROD2, MYRF, and TMEFF2 among the highest-ranking candidates (Figure 2B and C). Notably, TCF4 and TMEFF2 were supported by both methods, whereas several other genes showed clear method-preferential ranking. The integrated MAGMA-PoPS landscape therefore suggested partial convergence rather than complete overlap, consistent with a model in which LBP susceptibility is concentrated in a limited number of loci while still reflecting broader polygenic functional similarity across genes (Figure 2D). The complete MAGMA gene-level association results and genome-wide PoPS prioritization scores are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

Brain Cell-Type-Specific eQTL-Anchored MR Highlights Neuronal and Glial Regulatory Associations with Low Back Pain

To evaluate whether genetically proxied expression in specific brain cell classes showed directional associations with LBP susceptibility, we performed two-sample MR across eight major brain cell types. In total, 1641 gene-cell type exposures were evaluated, including excitatory neurons (n = 596), oligodendrocytes (n = 386), inhibitory neurons (n = 220), astrocytes (n = 179), OPCs (n = 121), microglia (n = 79), endothelial cells (n = 44), and pericytes (n = 16) (Figure 3A and Table 1). After global FDR correction, significant associations were concentrated in excitatory neurons and oligodendrocytes (five hits each), followed by inhibitory neurons and astrocytes (four hits each), OPCs (two hits), and single hits in microglia and endothelial cells, whereas no FDR-significant association was retained in pericytes (Figure 3B). The complete MR results for all tested gene-cell type exposures, including MHC-region signals and non-significant associations, are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Instrument-strength assessment showed that all retained MR instruments had F-statistics >10, and no exposure had missing instrument-strength information. Among the 22 FDR-significant gene–cell-type associations summarized in Table 1, F-statistics ranged from 33.03 to 123.57, indicating that weak-instrument bias was unlikely to explain the prioritized MR signals. Nevertheless, because all FDR-significant associations were based on single-SNP Wald ratio estimates, these findings were interpreted as eQTL-anchored directional association evidence rather than definitive causal effects.

Inspection of the leading MR signals revealed a biologically heterogeneous pattern. Negative effect estimates were observed for VARS2 in excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, and oligodendrocytes, as well as for SFTA2 in oligodendrocytes, suggesting that higher genetically predicted expression of these genes was associated with lower LBP risk. In contrast, positive associations were detected for astrocytic GMPPB, DPYSL5, and FBXO7, inhibitory-neuronal IBSP, OPC-specific FBXO7 and DPYSL5, excitatory-neuronal LGI4, and oligodendrocytic PPP6R2, indicating potential risk-increasing effects (Figure 3C and Table 1). The recurrent appearance of VARS2, DPYSL5, and FBXO7 across multiple cell types further suggested that some LBP-associated regulatory signals may be shared across brain lineages while remaining strongly cell-context dependent (Figure 3D).

It is also noteworthy that several of the strongest initial MR signals mapped to the MHC region, including BTNL2, HLA-F, HLA-DRB1, and ZSCAN31. Given the complex LD structure of this locus, we did not interpret these signals as final mechanistic candidates at this stage. Instead, we carried forward the non-MHC signals for colocalization-based refinement, with the aim of distinguishing recurrent MR associations from loci more likely to reflect genuinely shared causal variants between brain eQTLs and the LBP GWAS signal.

Colocalization-Based Refinement Identifies Moderate-Support Non-MHC Candidates

To refine the non-MHC eQTL-anchored MR findings, we integrated Bayesian colocalization with MAGMA and PoPS. This step substantially narrowed the candidate list and showed that only a subset of MR-significant gene–cell-type pairs retained evidence compatible with a shared local genetic signal. According to the pre-specified interpretation thresholds, PP.H4 > 0.80 was considered strong evidence of colocalization, whereas PP.H4 between 0.50 and 0.80 was interpreted as moderate support. None of the evaluated non-MHC candidates reached the strong-evidence threshold. Therefore, we revised the prioritization terminology and did not designate any candidate as a core colocalized gene.

Among the evaluated non-MHC candidates, oligodendrocytic CLEC18A, inhibitory-neuronal QPRT, and astrocytic GMPPB showed the highest PP.H4 values, but all three remained within the moderate-support range rather than the strong-support range. These candidates were therefore classified as higher-priority candidates with moderate colocalization support (Figure 4A). Spatial Genetic Mapping Localizes LBP-Associated Signals to Neuroaxis-Related Embryonic Regions and Neuronal-Like Adult Spinal Niches

We next asked whether the LBP genetic signal could be localized in spatial transcriptomic atlases. In the E16.5 murine embryonic spatial atlas, annotation-level Cauchy combination analysis showed the strongest enrichment in the brain (Pcauchy = 2.21×10−5), followed by the spinal cord (1.29 × 10−4), sympathetic nerve (7.21 × 10−4), and dorsal root ganglion (0.00234), with additional support in the meninges. By contrast, several non-neural tissues, including the GI tract, epidermis, liver, and bone, showed no meaningful enrichment. At spot-level resolution, the strongest hotspots were concentrated in brain regions, indicating that the cross-species spatial projection of LBP-associated genetic liability was preferentially aligned with the embryonic neuroaxis rather than diffusely distributed across peripheral organs (Figure 5).

To provide supportive cell-based expression evidence for selected candidates, we examined the expression of GMPPB, QPRT, DPYSL5, and LGI4 in two complementary cellular models: RA-differentiated SH-SY5Y cells as a neuronal-like model and U251 cells as an astroglial-like model, under control and acute inflammatory stimulation conditions (Figure 7).

In the neuronal-like SH-SY5Y model, QPRT showed a significant increase after inflammatory stimulation, whereas LGI4 showed a significant decrease. By contrast, GMPPB and DPYSL5 did not show significant expression changes in this model. These findings suggest that the neuronal-like stimulation context preferentially captured the responsiveness of QPRT and LGI4 rather than producing uniform changes across all selected candidates. In the astroglial-like U251 model, inflammatory stimulation resulted in significant upregulation of GMPPB and DPYSL5, whereas QPRT and LGI4 showed no significant changes. This pattern is consistent with model-dependent transcriptional responsiveness, with GMPPB showing responsiveness in an astroglial-like context and DPYSL5 showing supportive evidence in a glial-lineage-related model.

Taken together, the RT-qPCR results provide supportive, context-dependent expression evidence rather than definitive mechanistic validation. The selected genes did not exhibit uniform responses across both models; instead, QPRT and LGI4 were more responsive in the neuronal-like model, whereas GMPPB and DPYSL5 were more responsive in the astroglial-like model. This pattern is broadly compatible with the cell-type-resolved and spatially contextualized framework established by the eQTL-anchored MR, colocalization, and gsMap analyses. However, because the assay measured stimulus-induced transcriptional responses after 24 h TNF-α/IL-1β treatment, it should not be interpreted as direct validation of the constitutive genetic regulation captured by eQTL instruments or as a chronic LBP disease model. Discussion

Low back pain (LBP) is now widely regarded as a heterogeneous pain condition rather than a purely local structural disorder, and this shift has important implications for how its genetic architecture should be interpreted.1,2 In the present study, we combined genome-wide signal evaluation, gene prioritization, brain cell-type-specific eQTL-anchored Mendelian randomization, Bayesian colocalization, spatial genetic mapping, and supportive RT-qPCR assessment to move from broad polygenic association toward a more interpretable biological framework. Taken together, our results suggest that inherited susceptibility to LBP is not best understood as a diffuse, tissue-nonspecific signal. Instead, it appears to preferentially align with neuronal and glial regulatory programs and to localize, at the spatial level, to neuroaxis-related embryonic regions and neuronal-like niches in the adult lumbar spinal cord.

This central interpretation should be understood within a neuroaxis-continuum framework rather than as a claim that LBP is purely a brain disorder. Persistent LBP, particularly when neuropathic or nociplastic components are present, can involve distributed changes spanning peripheral nociceptive input, spinal dorsal horn processing, ascending sensory pathways, and supraspinal pain-modulatory networks. In this context, the use of brain single-nucleus eQTLs was intended to capture shared central nervous system regulatory programs across major neuronal and glial classes, not to imply that the brain is the exclusive site of LBP pathogenesis. Because adequately powered adult human spinal cord cell-type-specific eQTL resources remain scarce, brain cell-type eQTLs provide a practical proxy for central neuroaxis regulatory programs that may be partially conserved between brain and spinal neuronal and glial lineages.

A strength of this study is that the main biological interpretation does not depend on any single analytical layer. LDSC first showed that the FinnGen LBP GWAS carried measurable and interpretable polygenic signal, supporting downstream integrative analyses. MAGMA and PoPS then provided a gene-level prioritization framework, but neither method alone was sufficient to define mechanistic candidates. Brain cell-type-specific eQTL-anchored MR moved the analysis from global prioritization to directional regulatory association, whereas Bayesian colocalization helped distinguish recurrent MR-associated signals from loci more compatible with shared local genetic regulation. This layered design is important in LBP, where association signals are unlikely to map cleanly to one locus, one cell type, or one biological pathway. In that sense, the contribution of the present work lies less in the mere nomination of several genes than in the progressive refinement of LBP-associated genetic signal into cell-type-resolved and spatially contextualized biology.

The distribution of the eQTL-anchored MR signals across brain cell classes is biologically informative. Significant associations were concentrated in excitatory neurons and oligodendrocytes, followed by inhibitory neurons and astrocytes, whereas no FDR-significant signal was retained in pericytes. This pattern argues against a nonspecific brain-wide effect and instead points to selective involvement of cellular programs relevant to synaptic transmission, circuit inhibition, glial support, and myelin-related plasticity. Such an interpretation is consistent with current understanding of pain neurobiology. Disinhibition within spinal inhibitory interneuron circuits is a recognized mechanism of pain amplification,8 astrocytes actively contribute to chronic pain through cytokine signaling, glutamate regulation, and neuron–glia interactions,9 and oligodendrocyte-lineage cells are increasingly implicated in pain states through myelin remodeling and metabolic support of neural networks.10 Viewed together, these findings support the idea that part of LBP genetic susceptibility may be implemented through altered regulation of neuronal excitability together with glial modulation of pain-processing circuits. A related caveat is that brain eQTL associations in a musculoskeletal pain phenotype may reflect several overlapping biological and behavioral processes. The observed enrichment in neuronal and glial regulatory programs could represent inherited susceptibility to central sensitization, altered pain modulation, psychological comorbidity, or pain-related cognitive–affective traits, rather than primary neurobiological causation specific to LBP. This possibility is particularly relevant for chronic pain phenotypes, where the clinical label may aggregate individuals with different combinations of nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic mechanisms. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence that central neuroaxis-related regulatory programs contribute to the genetic architecture of LBP, while peripheral sensitization and local spinal-tissue mechanisms remain important and complementary components of disease biology.

At the candidate-gene level, our results support a cautious hierarchy rather than a definitive core-gene model. In the revised framework, CLEC18A, QPRT, and GMPPB were not designated as “core” or strongly colocalized candidates, because their PP.H4 values did not exceed the pre-specified threshold for strong colocalization evidence (PP.H4 > 0.80). Instead, they are best interpreted as higher-priority non-MHC candidates with moderate colocalization support after integrated consideration of eQTL-anchored MR, Bayesian colocalization, MAGMA, and PoPS evidence. This distinction is important because it aligns the biological interpretation with the formal colocalization thresholds and avoids overstating the evidence for shared local genetic regulation. Within this moderate-support tier, QPRT remains biologically interesting because it encodes quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase, a downstream enzyme in the kynurenine pathway,20 and kynurenine-pathway metabolites have repeatedly been linked to neuroinflammatory and chronic pain states.21 GMPPB is also biologically plausible, given its central role in GDP-mannose synthesis and glycosylation-related pathways with established neuromuscular relevance.22 CLEC18A showed the highest colocalization support among the evaluated non-MHC candidates, although its mechanistic relevance to LBP remains less well established and requires further investigation. Among the supportive candidates, DPYSL family proteins are associated with synaptic physiology and neurodevelopment,23 whereas LGI4 is involved in axon–glia signaling and myelination through ADAM22-dependent mechanisms.24 Importantly, VARS2 illustrates why colocalization-based refinement was necessary. Although VARS2 showed the strongest and most recurrent MR-associated signal across excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, and oligodendrocytes, it showed near-zero colocalization support in the present analysis. This discordance suggests that the VARS2 locus may reflect local linkage disequilibrium tagging, distinct causal variants, or complex regional architecture rather than a shared eQTL–LBP local genetic signal. Similarly, IBSP reached FDR significance in the inhibitory-neuron MR layer, but its established biological annotation is more closely aligned with mineralized tissue biology than with inhibitory neuronal regulation. Given the single-instrument MR setting and the absence of strong colocalization support, we did not prioritize IBSP as a mechanistic neuronal candidate. Therefore, the revised hierarchy is intended not as a definitive target list, but as a cautious prioritization framework that separates higher-priority moderate-support candidates from recurrent or biologically unexpected MR-associated signals lacking convincing colocalization support. The initial MR results also included several MHC-region signals, including BTNL2, HLA-F, HLA-DRB1, and ZSCAN31. These loci may be biologically relevant in the broader context of neuro-immune and nociplastic pain mechanisms, but the dense linkage disequilibrium and extensive immune-related haplotype structure of the MHC region make gene-level mechanistic interpretation difficult. We therefore retained these signals in the complete MR results while excluding them from downstream non-MHC colocalization-based prioritization.

A major feature distinguishing this study from many routine cell-type MR analyses is the addition of spatial genetic mapping. gsMap was developed to integrate GWAS summary statistics with spatial transcriptomics in order to localize trait-associated cells and tissue regions in anatomically resolved contexts.11 In our analysis, the E16.5 murine embryonic atlas showed strongest enrichment in the brain, spinal cord, sympathetic nerve, and dorsal root ganglion, with little comparable signal in several non-neural tissues. The adult human lumbar spinal cord data sharpened this observation further, because the dominant enrichment across two independent Visium sections localized to neuronal-like spinal clusters, with weaker astroglial-enriched or mitochondrial-high secondary signals. These findings provide anatomical coherence for the upstream genetic and cell-type results, but they should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The E16.5 murine atlas provides broad anatomical coverage and highlights neuroaxis-related developmental transcriptional programs, but it is not an adult human LBP tissue reference. Accordingly, the embryonic gsMap findings are best viewed as hypothesis-generating evidence that LBP genetic liability preferentially aligns with neuroaxis-related spatial programs across a developmental atlas. The adult lumbar spinal cord Visium sections add a more disease-relevant anatomical context by localizing enrichment to restricted neuronal-like spinal clusters, but the number of available sections remains limited. Thus, the spatial component strengthens the anatomical coherence of the genetic and cell-type findings, while not proving that developmental programs directly drive adult chronic LBP pathology.

The RT-qPCR findings are best interpreted as supportive, context-dependent expression evidence rather than as definitive mechanistic or causal evidence. In inflammatory stimulation models, QPRT and LGI4 were more responsive in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells, whereas GMPPB and DPYSL5 were more responsive in U251 cells. This pattern is directionally consistent with the cell-type specificity inferred from the genomic analyses and suggests that the prioritized candidates do not respond uniformly across cellular contexts. However, an important mechanistic gap remains between genetically regulated baseline expression captured by eQTL instruments and stimulus-induced transcriptional responses measured after 24-hour TNF-α/IL-1β treatment. The in vitro models therefore support cellular responsiveness of selected candidates in neuronal-like and astroglial-like inflammatory contexts, but they do not establish that these genes mediate LBP susceptibility or reproduce the chronic neuroinflammatory milieu of persistent LBP. Several additional limitations should also be acknowledged. Most MR estimates relied on single-instrument Wald ratio models, the source single-nucleus brain eQTL dataset was modest in size, and the adult spinal spatial validation was limited to two Visium sections. Although instrument-strength assessment argued against weak-instrument bias among the prioritized MR signals, pleiotropy-oriented MR sensitivity analyses could not be systematically applied. Even with these caveats, the overall pattern is coherent: LBP-associated genetic liability appears to be at least partly shaped by neuronal and glial regulatory programs within the broader neuroaxis. This framework should be regarded as a spatially contextualized candidate-prioritization model that requires further validation in larger spinal eQTL datasets, adult disease-relevant spatial references, and functional perturbation experiments.

Conclusion

In conclusion, by integrating LBP GWAS with brain cell-type-specific eQTL data, gene prioritization, eQTL-anchored Mendelian randomization, Bayesian colocalization, spatial genetic mapping, and supportive RT-qPCR, we provide convergent evidence that inherited susceptibility to low back pain is partly aligned with neuronal and glial regulatory programs. These findings do not exclude peripheral nociceptive, inflammatory, or sensitization mechanisms, but support a central neuroaxis-related regulatory layer within the broader genetic architecture of LBP. The spatial analyses further suggest that LBP-associated genetic signals preferentially localize to neuroaxis-related embryonic regions and neuronal-like niches in adult lumbar spinal cord, although the embryonic results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and the adult spinal validation remains limited by section number. CLEC18A, QPRT, and GMPPB were highlighted as higher-priority non-MHC candidates with moderate, but not strong, colocalization support. Overall, this study provides a spatially contextualized framework for prioritizing LBP-associated regulatory candidates, while emphasizing the need for larger cell-type-specific spinal eQTL resources and functional validation before mechanistic or therapeutic conclusions can be drawn.
