From transaction to transformation: a binational perspective on integrating community pharmacy into the digital health ecosystem of Southern European health systems
Authors: Diamantis Klimentidis, Zaheer Ud-Din Babar, Michael Petrides, Aliki Peletidi
Published: 2026

Within the evolving framework of the European Health Data Space (EHDS), integrating community pharmacy into national digital infrastructures represents a strategic opportunity for both nations to transition from transactional to transformative practice. Background

Globally, community pharmacists are recognised as among the most accessible healthcare professionals, serving as a vital frontline for public health (Muscat et al., 2024). This accessibility is particularly pronounced across Europe, which maintains a high density of pharmacists compared to other world regions (International Pharmaceutical Federation – FIP, 2021). In Greek-speaking countries (Greece and Cyprus), this accessibility is a key strategic asset; Greece has the highest pharmacy density in the EU, and Cyprus ranks second (Peletidi, Petrides, et al., 2025). Yet, these professionals operate with a profound informational handicap. Despite its promise of delivering the right information to the right professional at the right time, digital health has yet to fully translate into practice at the pharmacy counter. This creates a daily paradox where the most frequently visited healthcare professionals are the most informationally isolated, exposing patients to the risks of fragmented care and preventable medication-related harm.

The evolution of pharmacy practice has moved far beyond the simple act of dispensing. International bodies and national health strategies increasingly envision pharmacists as integrated primary care providers who deliver a range of advanced clinical services, from medication therapy management to chronic disease monitoring and preventive care (Babar et al., 2018; Hasan et al., 2019; Scahill & Babar, 2010). Concurrently, the global transformations introduced by the Fourth Industrial Revolution are redefining pharmacy practice through automation, artificial intelligence, and data-driven healthcare systems, requiring pharmacists to develop new digital competencies and adapt to rapidly evolving care models (Baines et al., 2020). However, this clinical role is fundamentally predicated on access to comprehensive, real-time patient data. A prescription, even an electronic one, offers only a fragment of a patient's clinical history, lacking the critical context of recent diagnoses, laboratory results, hospital discharge summaries, and allergies. Operating without this information is not only inefficient but inherently unsafe, contributing to the staggering global cost of medication errors, estimated at $42 billion USD annually (WHO, 2024). This global shift toward patient-centred and system-oriented pharmacy practice has been empirically documented in a longitudinal review of FIP World Congress programmes (2003–2019), which showed sustained growth in roles emphasising professional performance, public-health engagement, and interprofessional collaboration (Kusynová et al., 2022).

This Editorial argues that granting community pharmacists full, role-appropriate, read-and-write access to patients’ Electronic Health Records (EHRs) is no longer a matter of professional aspiration but a strategic necessity for Greece and Cyprus. This imperative is driven by a powerful convergence of factors: an increasingly consistent international evidence base, exemplified by recent pioneering pilots in the United Kingdom, that demonstrates clear benefits in safety and efficiency (Fynn et al., 2025); the pressing need to optimise healthcare delivery for aging populations; and the legal and political momentum of the European Health Data Space (EHDS) regulation, which provides a robust framework for secure, patient-centred data exchange across the Union (European Commission, 2025; Li & Quinn, 2024). The time has come to transform the community pharmacy from a transactional endpoint into a fully integrated clinical node in the digital health ecosystem.

Evidence appraisal and approach

This Editorial is based on a narrative review of international evidence, policy documents, and academic literature published primarily between 2021 and 2025. The aim was to synthesise high-impact exemplars and recent policy developments to inform a targeted argument for the Greek and Cypriot contexts. Sources were identified through targeted searches of PubMed, Google Scholar, and institutional websites of health authorities and professional bodies. Priority was given to peer-reviewed studies evaluating real-world implementations of pharmacist EHR access, national digital health strategies, and legal analyses of the EHDS. Consistent with recent recommendations from the International Pharmaceutical Federation’s Pharmacy Practice Research Special Interest Group, future policy and implementation studies should remain grounded in robust, theory-informed, practitioner-led methodologies that can translate evidence into actionable practice (Akhtar et al., 2022; Dawoud et al., 2021). The selection was guided by relevance to the core themes of interoperability, patient safety, workflow integration, and governance.

The case for pharmacist EHR access is not theoretical but it is supported by a growing body of international evidence demonstrating tangible improvements in patient safety, clinical effectiveness, and system efficiency. The UK read-write access pilot

Recent evidence, such as the mixed-methods evaluation of the SystmOne pilot in the UK (Fynn et al., 2025) confirms that passive ‘read-only’ access is insufficient. In this pilot, pharmacists granted write access documented over 16,000 clinical entries, transitioning from passive viewers to active collaborators. The critical finding was that the ability to ‘write back’ closes the loop; interventions like stopping nephrotoxic drugs or identifying non-adherence become immediately visible to the prescribing physician, replacing untracked phone calls with auditable electronic tasking.

Similar findings have been reported beyond the UK, where qualitative evaluations from England and Australia identified general practice pharmacists as key professionals bridging gaps in multidisciplinary teams, while underscoring the need for clearer role descriptions and structured training pathways (Akhtar et al., 2022).

National integration models

While comprehensive EHR access is the goal, several European nations have demonstrated the value of integrated national systems.

The Estonian model utilizes a decentralised data exchange layer that links all pharmacies and doctors (Tuula et al., 2022). Crucially, this system supports cross-border e-dispensing, serving as a functional prototype for the EHDS. For Greece's fragmented vendor ecosystem, this demonstrates that a central database is not strictly necessary if a secure exchange layer is enforced.

Denmark’s Shared Medication Record (SMR) and Switzerland’s shared eMedication plan are leading examples of systems that create a single, cross-setting ‘source of truth’ for a patient's medications (Aharaz et al., 2023; Bugnon et al., 2021). These systems address a fundamental flaw of simple e-prescribing platforms which is no other than the lack of clinical context.

A simple list of prescriptions does not explain why a medication was started, stopped, or changed (Schiff et al., 2018). The Danish and Swiss models are designed to capture this vital context, including prescriber intent and pharmacist annotations. This creates a dynamic, living medication record that is essential for complex clinical tasks like medication reconciliation after hospital discharge and deprescribing in patients with polypharmacy. Without access to diagnoses, lab values, and the prescriber's rationale, a pharmacist cannot perform these high-stakes functions safely. This directly addresses the gaps identified by healthcare professionals in Greece, who highlighted the need for better access to clinical information like allergies, adverse drug reactions, and drug-to-drug interactions to support their decision-making at the point of care (Grammatikopoulou et al., 2024). A shared, read-write medication record embedded within the EHR is the logical solution to this critical information gap.

However, direct transferability of these models to Southern Europe is constrained by three ‘local enablers’ currently maturing in Greece and Cyprus:

Legal Definitions: Unlike the UK, where pharmacists have a ‘duty to access,’ Greek Law has historically framed pharmacists primarily as dispensers.

Reimbursement: The UK compensates for clinical services (e.g. Pharmacy First), whereas Cypriot and Greek models are still predominantly fee-for-service, though evolving via 2025/2026 agreements.

Interoperability: Northern European systems rely on HL7 FHIR standards, while Greece's ecosystem remains fragmented with low interoperability maturity.

Lessons from the frontline: usability, workflow, and risks

International experience also provides crucial cautionary tales. A recent survey of community pharmacists in Australia (Hareem et al., 2024) that was conducted from September 2022 to June 2023 and included participants mostly from Tasmania, Victoria, and Western Australia, found that while e-prescribing was beneficial, its clinical utility was often impaired by incomplete data, sluggish software, and poor user-centred design. This finding is powerfully reinforced by the UK pilot mentioned above (Fynn et al., 2025), where the single greatest barrier to engagement was the burden of ‘duplicated documentation’ – requiring pharmacists to enter clinical data into the shared EHR for care coordination and then re-enter similar data into their separate pharmacy management system (PMS) for reimbursement.

Additionally, a systematic review published by Li et al. (2022) in the Journal of Medical Internet Research shows that patient safety and quality improvements are linked to genuine data liquidity, not standalone viewer portals. Furthermore, increased data access brings risks that should be managed. A key concern is alert fatigue, where an excessive number of low-specificity electronic alerts desensitises doctors, causing them to ignore both irrelevant and critical warnings. Another fact is the increased documentation burden, which can consume a significant portion of a doctor's workday if systems are not designed for efficient, workflow-aligned data entry. Any implementation must therefore include robust alert governance and prioritise a ‘document once, use many times’ architecture to avoid these predictable failure points.

The local context: digital progress and persistent silos in Greece and Cyprus

While international evidence provides the blueprint, successful implementation requires a strategy tailored to the unique digital health landscapes of Greece and Cyprus.

Greece's ‘E-prescription paradox’

Greece’s national e-prescription platform is a landmark achievement in digital health, with near-universal adoption by doctors and pharmacies (Grammatikopoulou et al., 2024). This success, however, has created an ‘e-prescription paradox.’ The system's efficiency in streamlining the transaction of prescribing and dispensing has inadvertently reinforced an outdated model of pharmacy practice that is exclusively medication centric. For Greek pharmacists, this system is their primary digital link to the healthcare ecosystem, cementing a relationship with prescribers that is transactional rather than collaborative.

The survey of 430 Greek healthcare professionals, including 137 pharmacists, by Grammatikopoulou et al. (2024), captured this paradox in a perfect way. While respondents praised the system for preventing errors and automating processes, they strongly highlighted the absence of crucial clinical features, such as history of allergies, adverse drug reactions, and interaction alerts, needed for robust clinical decision-making. The recent launch of the national ‘MyHealth’ platform presents a historic opportunity to resolve this contradiction (Tovima.com, 2025). Functioning as the country's official national EHR, MyHealth is a patient-facing system accessible via a web portal and mobile app that centralises a citizen's key medical data, including prescriptions, referrals, laboratory results, and hospital discharge summaries. The strategic imperative is not to build a parallel system for pharmacists, but to enrich the existing, universally adopted e-prescription workflow with read-write access to the broader clinical data now being centralised in the MyHealth record. This would transform the system from a transactional tool into a powerful platform for integrated clinical care.

The ‘Cyprus precedent’: A warning against partial implementation

Cyprus has pursued a more integrated approach, implementing a nationwide EHR as a core component of its General Health System (GHS) in 2019 (Peletidi, Petrides, et al., 2025; Petrou, 2021). However, the Cypriot experience offers a critical cautionary tale. A 2022 nationwide study of 429 doctor users (Rompolas et al., 2025) revealed only ‘moderate’ levels of user efficiency and, most tellingly, found that less than a third of doctors felt adequately supported by the national Health Insurance Organisation.

This ‘Cyprus Precedent’ is a stark warning that system availability does not guarantee effective use. The root causes of physician underutilization, identified as issues of usability, training, and institutional support, will inevitably be replicated if access is simply extended to pharmacists without addressing these fundamental problems. Therefore, the call to action for Cyprus must be twofold. First, grant community pharmacists role-appropriate access to the GHS EHR. Second, and concurrently, launch a system-wide initiative to improve EHR usability, training, and support for all healthcare professionals, leveraging the lessons learned from doctor feedback to ensure a successful and impactful rollout for the pharmacy sector.

Concerns about data privacy and compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) are often cited as barriers to expanding EHR access. However, the European legal architecture, particularly the new EHDS regulation, reframes privacy not as a barrier, but as a core design requirement (European Commission, 2025; Li & Quinn, 2024). The legal basis for processing sensitive health data for the provision of healthcare is already well-established under GDPR Article 9(2)(h), provided it is done by or under the responsibility of a professional subject to an obligation of secrecy. The EHDS establishes a concrete, EU-wide framework for lawful data governance, incorporating safeguards such as role-based access, transparent patient-facing audit logs, and the right of patients to restrict access to specific parts of their record.

To address accountability concerns within the current realities of community pharmacy in Greece and Cyprus, access privileges should focus on collaborative practice and medication safety:

Full Medication History: Pharmacists must have the ability to document Over-the-Counter (OTC) medicines and supplements alongside the prescribed list to generate a ‘Best Possible Medication History’ (BPMH).

Allergies and Intolerances: Read-write access to the allergy field is critical to prevent anaphylaxis and severe adverse reactions often missed in transactional dispensing.

Prescriber Communication: The system must facilitate secure, auditable communication between pharmacists and doctors to clarify orders without disrupting workflow.

Out-of-Scope: To maintain professional boundaries, pharmacists should be technically restricted from entering new medical diagnoses (ICD-10 codes) or ordering diagnostics.

Once the foundational layer of medication safety is established, the digital framework should expand to support advanced services:

Adherence & Safety Monitoring: Dedicated fields for pharmacists to document adherence barriers (e.g. side effects, cost) and clinical outcomes of chronic therapy.

Public Health Screening: Integration of structured templates for recording screenings (e.g. colorectal cancer), immunizations, and smoking cessation interventions directly into the national health record.

The policy recommendation is therefore not to invent a new privacy framework, but to faithfully implement the EHDS framework, formally recognizing community pharmacists as direct-care professionals within it.

Modern EHR platforms can readily enforce ‘least-privilege’ access, ensuring pharmacists see only the information relevant to their clinical duties (e.g. medications, allergies, relevant lab results, diagnoses) (Shojaei et al., 2024). By building a system with transparent, patient-visible audit trails showing ‘who looked, when, and why,’ Greece and Cyprus can build the public and professional trust upon which the entire system depends.

The technical challenge lies in breaking down the data silos between national EHR platforms and the proprietary PMS used in community pharmacies. As the UK and Australian experiences show, forcing pharmacists to work across multiple, disconnected systems creates unsustainable workflow friction and guarantees poor adoption (Fynn et al., 2025; Hareem et al., 2024). The solution is to mandate a ‘document once, use many times’ architecture.

This requires a policy-driven push for interoperability, using the EHDS's mandate for common standards as a lever. National health authorities should require that all PMS vendors adopt modern interoperability standards, such as HL7 Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR), to enable secure, bidirectional data exchange with the national EHR (Ross et al., 2025; Shojaei et al., 2024; Vorisek et al., 2022). Crucially, clinical documentation must be directly linked to reimbursement. A single, structured note entered by a pharmacist for a clinical service must simultaneously populate the patient's national EHR, become visible to the GP, update the shared medication list, and automatically generate a claim for payment. Eliminating workflow duplication by mandating this level of integration is the central technical requirement for success.

Providing access to a wealth of new clinical data creates a profound shift in the professional standard of care for community pharmacy. With access comes the professional and legal ‘duty to access’ and use that information to ensure patient safety (Ross et al., 2025). This increased accountability means that providing access without ensuring competence would be professionally irresponsible.

Therefore, a national strategy for EHR access must be paralleled by a fully funded national strategy for workforce development. This must go beyond basic technical training. National pharmaceutical associations, in collaboration with universities, must develop and implement mandatory, accredited Continuing Professional Development (CPD) programs for all practicing pharmacists (Peletidi, Petrides, et al., 2025). These programs should focus on core competencies in clinical data interpretation, health informatics, evidence-based practice, and digital privacy. Concurrently, undergraduate pharmacy curricula must be modernised to ensure that the next generation of pharmacists graduate with these skills embedded in their training. Investing in this ‘human infrastructure’ is as vital as the investment in the digital infrastructure itself.

Linking evaluation to European digital health policy

Embedding pharmacoepidemiological and adherence-related indicators within national pilot evaluations aligns directly with the European Health Data Space (EHDS) vision of data-driven, patient-centred healthcare (Li & Quinn, 2024). The EHDS explicitly promotes secondary use of anonymised health data for improving quality, safety, and efficiency (European Commission, 2025), providing a regulatory pathway for transforming pilot-generated metrics into policy evidence. By systematically tracking outcomes such as reductions in preventable adverse drug reactions, improved persistence with chronic therapies, and lower readmission rates, Greece and Cyprus could position themselves as regional exemplars in leveraging EHR interoperability for measurable health gains. Moreover, linking these indicators to reimbursement and workforce development would ensure that pharmacist integration is both clinically accountable and economically sustainable. In this way, EHR access becomes not only a technological reform but a policy instrument for realising the EHDS’s broader objectives-safer care, empowered professionals, and equitable access across European health systems.

Limitations

This Editorial presents a policy argument based on a narrative synthesis of existing evidence and is subject to certain limitations. The selection of international exemplars may be subject to selection bias, and we have not conducted a systematic review of all available literature. The transferability of findings from one national health system to another is complex and depends on local context, funding, and political will. Furthermore, this analysis does not include a formal cost–benefit analysis, which would be a necessary step for policymakers. Finally, the perspectives of patients and other healthcare professionals (e.g. GPs) on pharmacist data access, while acknowledged, require deeper local investigation.

Conclusion

The evidence base, while still evolving, is increasingly consistent: in health systems that grant pharmacists full, read-write, interoperable EHR access, patients can receive safer and more coherent care. The clinical, technical, and legal arguments have aligned to create a unique window of opportunity for Greece and Cyprus. The UK pilot proves feasibility and value at the clinical frontline (Fynn et al., 2025) while the Nordic, Swiss and Estonian models demonstrate the stabilizing power of national integration (Aharaz et al., 2023; Bugnon et al., 2021; Tuula et al., 2022). On the other hand, Australia’s experience provides a clear lesson on the importance of user-centred design (Hareem et al., 2024). Finally, the European Health Data Space provides the legal engine to make this transformation lawful, portable, and patient-centred (European Commission, 2025; Li & Quinn, 2024).

The path forward is clear. The Ministries of Health and Digital Governance in Greece and Cyprus must act decisively to formally recognise community pharmacists as integral primary care providers within the national digital health strategy. This requires granting them full, role-appropriate EHR access, mandating the technical interoperability needed to create a seamless workflow, and investing in the training required to ensure the workforce is competent and accountable. This is not merely an IT project; it is a fundamental reform of primary care. By breaking down the digital silos that have long isolated their most accessible clinicians, Greece and Cyprus can build a more resilient, efficient, and patient-centric healthcare system.
