Association between the metabolic score for visceral fat and mortality outcomes in stroke patients
Authors: Bing Kuang, Yu Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke survivors face an elevated risk of subsequent mortality. While visceral adiposity is linked to adverse cardiometabolic outcomes, traditional anthropometric indices fail to adequately capture its metabolic burden. The metabolic score for visceral fat (METS-VF) is a novel indicator of visceral fat-related metabolic activity; however, its prognostic value in stroke patients remains unclear. This national cohort study included 1557 adults with a history of stroke from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 1999 to 2018. METS-VF was calculated and stratified into tertiles. All-cause and cardiovascular mortality data were obtained via linkage to the National Death Index. Associations between METS-VF and mortality outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, and restricted cubic splines (RCS). During a median follow-up of 80 months, 665 all-cause deaths and 262 cardiovascular deaths occurred. In fully adjusted models, METS-VF served as an independent predictor for both all-cause (hazard ratio [HR], 1.62; 95% CI, 1.05–2.50) and cardiovascular mortality (HR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.09–3.67). Participants in the highest METS-VF tertile exhibited the greatest mortality risk. Furthermore, RCS analyses confirmed significant linear dose-response relationships with no evidence of nonlinearity. These associations remained robust across various demographic and clinical subgroups. Higher METS-VF is independently and linearly associated with increased subsequent all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with a history of stroke. As a highly accessible metabolic indicator, METS-VF can enhance risk stratification and guide targeted secondary prevention strategies in this high-risk population.

1. Introduction

Stroke remains a major cause of mortality and disability worldwide.[1,2] Despite advances in acute management and secondary prevention, individuals with a history of stroke remain at increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.[3,4] Identifying accessible markers associated with subsequent mortality may therefore facilitate risk assessment in this population.

Metabolic disorders and obesity, particularly visceral fat accumulation, are recognized as important contributors to stroke occurrence and adverse outcomes.[5,6] Compared with traditional anthropometric indicators such as body mass index (BMI) or waist circumference (WC), visceral fat exhibits stronger inflammatory and metabolic activity and may increase cardiocerebral vascular risk by promoting insulin resistance, activating inflammatory pathways, and accelerating atherosclerosis.[7–9] However, these conventional obesity indicators fail to accurately capture the metabolic burden of visceral fat, limiting their utility in poststroke risk assessment.[10] In recent years, the metabolic score for visceral fat (METS-VF), a novel metabolic indicator integrating multiple clinical and biochemical variables, has gained increasing attention.[11,12] METS-VF effectively reflects the metabolic activity of visceral adiposity and has been associated with insulin resistance, hypertension, diabetes, and various cardiometabolic events.[13,14] Existing studies have shown that METS-VF is positively associated with the risk of stroke onset.[15] However, evidence regarding the association between METS-VF and subsequent all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with a history of stroke remains limited.

Based on this background, the present study utilized data from NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999 to 2018 to evaluate the association of METS-VF with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among adults with a history of stroke, as well as its dose-response relationship and consistency across population subgroups. This study aimed to extend the existing evidence on METS-VF and stroke by examining its association with subsequent mortality in individuals with established stroke.

2. Methods

2.1. Study data sources and participants

This study was based on data from ten cycles of the NHANES conducted from 1999 to 2018. NHANES employs a stratified, multistage probability sampling design and is representative of the noninstitutionalized adult population in the United States. All NHANES protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The present study adhered to the STROBE reporting guidelines.

The inclusion criteria were: age ≥18 years and self-reported physician diagnosis of “stroke” in the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria were: missing data required for the calculation of METS-VF and missing key covariates or follow-up information. Because the proportion of missing values for key variables was low (<10%), complete-case analysis was applied without multiple imputation. This approach is widely used in NHANES-based cohort studies and ensures the robustness of analytical models. A total of 1557 stroke patients were included in the final analysis. 2.2. Calculation of METS-VF

The METS-VF was calculated using a previously validated formula,[16] as follows:

WHtR = waist circumference (cm)/height (cm)

Based on the sample distribution, METS-VF was divided into tertiles: Q1 (lowest), Q2, and Q3 (highest).

2.3. Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcome was cardiovascular mortality. Mortality status was obtained from the National Death Index linkage. Cause of death was determined using ICD-10 codes:All-cause mortality: all recorded deaths; Cardiovascular mortality: I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69. Follow-up time was calculated from the baseline interview date until death or December 31, 2019, whichever occurred first.

2.4. Covariates

Covariates were selected based on previous literature and clinical relevance. All variables were obtained from baseline questionnaires, physical examinations, or laboratory measurements, including: Demographics: age, sex, race, marital status, education level, poverty-income ratio; Lifestyle factors: smoking (≥100 cigarettes in lifetime), alcohol consumption (≥12 drinks in the past year); Clinical comorbidities: hypertension, cardiovascular diseases (CVD) (including coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, and heart failure, CVDs), diabetes, cancer; Anthropometric and laboratory indicators: BMI, WC, height, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, aspartate aminotransferase, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid (UA), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, calculated using the CKD-EPI equation). All variable definitions and measurement protocols adhered strictly to NHANES standardized procedures.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians (interquartile ranges), and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Differences between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or chi-square test. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to compare survival probabilities across METS-VF tertiles, and differences were evaluated using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to examine the association between METS-VF and mortality, with progressive adjustments as follows: Model 1: Unadjusted; Model 2: Adjusted for race, marital status, education, smoking, drinking, age, and poverty-income ratio; Model 3: Adjusted for Model 2 + hypertension, CVDs, diabetes, cancer, aspartate aminotransferase, BUN, UA, eGFR. Proportional hazards assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals (P > .05). Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 4 knots were applied to evaluate potential nonlinear associations between METS-VF and mortality risks. Subgroup analyses were stratified by age, smoking, drinking, hypertension, CVDs, cancer, and diabetes to assess robustness. Interaction terms were used to test effect modification (P for interaction). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3), with two-sided P < .05 considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 1557 stroke patients were included in this study, with a median follow-up duration of 80 months. During follow-up, 665 all-cause deaths (42.7%) and 262 cardiovascular deaths (16.8%) were recorded. As shown in Table 1, compared with survivors, individuals who died from any cause were older, had a higher proportion of males, and exhibited significant differences in race, marital status, and educational level (all P < .05). The mortality group also had higher prevalences of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and cancer, as well as a higher proportion of alcohol consumption. Regarding metabolic indicators, the mortality group had lower BMI, higher levels of BUN and UA, and significantly lower eGFR (all P < .001). In addition, METS-VF levels were notably higher among decedents (6.96 vs 6.83, P < .001). A similar pattern was observed for cardiovascular mortality, with decedents being older, more likely to be male, more likely to have cardiovascular disease, and exhibiting poorer renal function, along with higher METS-VF levels (6.98 vs 6.87, P < .001).

3.2. Survival differences across METS-VF tertiles

Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed significant differences in survival probabilities across METS-VF tertiles (all Log-rank P < .001). For both all-cause mortality (Fig. 2A) and cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 2B), patients in the highest METS-VF tertile (Q3) had the lowest survival rates. This separation emerged early during follow-up and persisted throughout the observation period.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for mortality across METS-VF tertiles (A) All-cause mortality, (B) Cardiovascular mortality. METS-VF = metabolic score for visceral fat.

3.3. Associations between METS-VF and mortality risks in cox regression models

After full adjustment for covariates, continuous METS-VF remained positively associated with all-cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.62, 95%CI: 1.05–2.50, P = .029). Compared with Q1, patients in the highest METS-VF tertile (Q3) had increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.29, 95%CI: 1.01–1.66, P = .014), with a significant linear trend (P for trend < .001). For cardiovascular mortality, fully adjusted models similarly showed a significant association between continuous METS-VF and risk of cardiovascular death (HR = 1.81, 95%CI: 1.09–3.67, P = .012). Patients in Q3 had the highest risk (HR = 1.43, 95%CI: 1.07–2.12, P = .027), with significant trend results (P for trend < .001) (Table 2).

3.4. Dose-response relationship between METS-VF and mortality

RCS curves demonstrated a linear increasing relationship between METS-VF and all-cause mortality (P for overall < .001), with no evidence of nonlinearity (P for nonlinear = .280) (Fig. 3A). A similar linear relationship was observed for cardiovascular mortality (P for overall < .001; P for nonlinear = .489) (Fig. 3B).

Restricted cubic spline curves showing the dose-response association between METS-VF and mortality (A) All-cause mortality, (B) Cardiovascular mortality. CI = confidence interval, METS-VF = metabolic score for visceral fat.

3.5. Subgroup analyses and interaction testing

Stratified analyses indicated that the association between METS-VF and all-cause mortality was directionally consistent across most subgroups. Stronger associations were observed among smokers (HR = 2.22, 95%CI: 1.29–3.83), nondrinkers (HR = 2.06, 95%CI: 1.05–4.05), and individuals with diabetes (HR = 2.19, 95%CI: 1.02–4.68), with no significant interactions detected (P for interaction > .05) (Fig. 4A). For cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 4B), elevated METS-VF was associated with more pronounced risk increases in certain subgroups, including individuals aged <65 years (HR = 10.64, 95%CI: 1.79–63.38), smokers (HR = 3.36, 95%CI: 1.33–8.53), drinkers (HR = 3.68, 95%CI: 1.32–10.25), and those without CVDs (HR = 2.94, 95%CI: 1.05–8.22). Similarly, no significant interactions were observed (P for interaction > .05).

4. Discussion

In this NHANES analysis of adults with a history of stroke, higher METS-VF was associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality after adjustment for potential confounders. The associations were approximately linear across the observed range of METS-VF, and no significant effect modification was identified in the subgroup analyses.

Previous studies have linked visceral adiposity to cardiometabolic disease through disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism, chronic inflammation, and vascular dysfunction.[17,18] Unlike BMI or WC alone, METS-VF incorporates both anthropometric and metabolic information and may therefore reflect several interrelated components of metabolic risk.[19,20] Several pathways may contribute to the observed association. Visceral adipose tissue can promote systemic inflammation through the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby contributing to atherosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction.[21,22] Visceral adiposity is also closely related to insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and metabolic abnormalities that may adversely affect vascular health.[23,24] Renal dysfunction and hyperuricemia, which frequently coexist with metabolic disorders, may further contribute to mortality risk in individuals with stroke.[25] Moreover, lipotoxicity associated with visceral adiposity may alter myocardial structure and function, contributing to cardiac remodeling and worsening cardiovascular mortality risk.[26,27] These mechanisms provide biological plausibility for the association between higher METS-VF and mortality, although mechanistic relationships cannot be established from the present observational analysis.

The RCS analysis further reinforced this interpretation by demonstrating a continuous increase in mortality risk with rising METS-VF, without a clear threshold effect. This suggests that higher metabolic visceral fat burden may be associated with lower survival during the observed follow-up period in stroke patients. In contrast, traditional obesity measures often lose predictive ability in the lower range, highlighting the sensitivity and prognostic advantage of METS-VF.[28,29] In subgroup analyses, stronger associations were observed among smokers, individuals with diabetes, and alcohol drinkers: groups characterized by heightened metabolic stress. This pattern implies that METS-VF may function as a marker reflecting the combined influence of metabolic and inflammatory stress, with amplified risk identification in susceptible subpopulations.

This study carries important clinical and public health implications. Stroke patients commonly present with elevated metabolic risk, yet clinical practice currently lacks simple and sensitive tools to identify individuals at high-risk for poor prognosis.[30] METS-VF requires no imaging examinations and can be calculated using routinely available anthropometric and biochemical measurements, making it highly accessible, low-cost, and practical for large-scale screening. Incorporating METS-VF into secondary prevention strategies may enhance early identification of high-risk patients and support individualized interventions targeting lifestyle modification, weight management, and metabolic control.

Despite the robustness and consistency of the findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as an observational study, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out, and causal relationships cannot be established. Second, stroke diagnosis in NHANES is based on self-report, which may introduce recall bias and does not allow differentiation between stroke subtypes. Third, NHANES lacks key variables related to mortality risk after stroke, such as stroke recurrence, treatment adherence, and functional status, which may lead to residual confounding or incomplete risk adjustment. In addition, the median follow-up duration was 80 months; therefore, the observed associations reflect mortality risk within this follow-up period and should not be extrapolated to outcomes over substantially longer periods. Future prospective cohort studies or multimodal biomarker-based assessments are needed to further validate the clinical utility of METS-VF in poststroke risk stratification.

5. Conclusion

In summary, higher METS-VF was associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among adults with a history of stroke, with approximately linear associations across the observed METS-VF range. Further studies are needed to determine whether METS-VF provides additional prognostic information beyond established risk factors and whether it can contribute to clinical risk assessment.
