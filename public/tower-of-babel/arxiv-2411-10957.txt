STAMP: Symmetric Temporal Averaging Message Passing for Domain Adaptation in Chronologically Split Graphs
Joo Young Park, Sejun Park, Hyunwoo Park

Abstract
This paper addresses domain adaptation in graph structured data under chronological splits. We consider a transductive graph learning setting in which each node is associated with a timestamp, and focus on Semi-Supervised Node Classification (SSNC), where labels of historical nodes are used to predict labels of more recent nodes. In such settings, temporal dependencies in graph connectivity induce distribution shifts across time, often causing substantial performance degradation when models trained on past nodes are deployed on future nodes. Despite its practical relevance, domain adaptation under chronological graph splits remains relatively underexplored. We propose Symmetric Temporal Averaging Message Passing ( STAMP ), a timestamp-balanced message passing operator that replaces standard neighborhood aggregation with a temporally aligned propagation scheme. We refer to the specialized variant for the heterogeneous ogbn-mag dataset as STAMP-mag, while reserving STAMP for the general formulation applicable to arbitrary graph datasets. By balancing messages across timestamps, STAMP enforces first-moment invariance across temporal domains while remaining scalable and easy to implement. Unlike many existing domain adaptation methods, whose assumptions are difficult to verify in graph settings, STAMP relies on transparent and practically checkable conditions, and is further supported by theoretical analysis. Empirically, STAMP and STAMP-mag improve accuracy across all evaluated temporal graph benchmarks. STAMP achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on ogbn-arxiv, while STAMP-mag does so on ogbn-mag. For temporal-split Pokec, where no established benchmark exists, applying STAMP to the state-of-the-art model for the original dataset consistently improves performance under our chronological split. These results demonstrate that timestamp-balanced propagation is an effective and practical approach for mitigating temporal distribution shift in graph learning.

Introduction

Semi-supervised node classification (SSNC) on graphs frequently arises in settings where nodes are associated with temporal information. For example, in an academic citation network, each node representing a paper may contain information including the year of its publication. Beyond citation networks, chronological graph structures arise in a wide range of practical applications, including fraud detection in temporal financial transaction networks
Xiang et al. (2023)
;
Duan et al. (2024)
, evolving social and communication networks
Longa et al. (2023)
;
Yang et al. (2024)
;
Zheng et al. (2025)
, and dynamic recommendation systems
Bai et al. (2020)
;
Gao et al. (2023)
;
Kim et al. (2024)
. In these settings, the ability to accurately classify recently added nodes using models trained on historical data is of paramount importance. This work focuses on such temporally annotated graph data, particularly on datasets where the train and test splits are arranged in chronological order. More specifically, as illustrated in Figure
1
, models are required to infer labels for the most recent nodes while trained solely from historical observations.

Figure 1:
Illustrative explanation of a chronological split dataset. Nodes are organized by their timestamps along the horizontal axis. The training set consists of nodes from earlier time periods, while the test set contains the most recent nodes. Edges may connect nodes across different timestamps, and the neighborhood composition of a node can change over time, leading to distribution shift between the training and test domains.

Although training graph neural networks (GNNs) using past data to classify recent data is a common scenario in both industrial applications and research practice
Liu et al. (2016)
;
Bai et al. (2020)
;
Pareja et al. (2020)
, systematic research on the effective use of temporal information within chronologically split graphs remains limited. Failure to adequately utilize temporal information can lead to significant performance degradation when the model attempts to classify the labels of recent data. This is because in temporal graphs, the neighborhood distribution of a target node is not stationary: both the composition of its neighboring nodes and the frequency of their interactions may evolve over time.

In this work, we propose Symmetric Temporal Averaging Message Passing (
STAMP
), a timestamp-balanced message passing operator to mitigate the temporal distribution shift in chronologically split graphs.
STAMP
performs aggregation in a balanced manner across timestamps, which enforces first-moment invariance across temporal domains at the initial message-passing level. As a result,
STAMP
directly targets the distributional discrepancy induced by chronological splits, while remaining scalable and easy to incorporate into existing GNN architectures by replacing a linear feature propagation layer. We further provide theoretical analysis showing that
STAMP
removes the first-moment component of the train-test representation shift and improves the Wasserstein-based distribution-shift term between train and test domains, and also achieves the minimax optimality. Finally,
STAMP
improves accuracy on ogbn-arxiv and temporal-split Pokec, achieving SOTA on ogbn-arxiv and outperforming the original Pokec SOTA baseline under the chronological split. We further introduce STAMP-mag, a specialized variant designed for the heterogeneous ogbn-mag dataset, which achieves SOTA performance on ogbn-mag.

Related Work

2.1
Graph Neural Networks

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have gained significant attention across a wide range of domains, including recommendation systems
Ying et al. (2018)
;
Gao et al. (2022)
, biology
Barabasi and Oltvai (2004)
, and chemistry
Wu et al. (2018)
. Spatial GNN architectures, such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN)
Kipf and Welling (2017)
, Graph Sample and Aggregate (GraphSAGE)
Hamilton et al. (2017)
, Graph Attention Networks (GAT)
Velickovic et al. (2017)
, and Hypergraph Neural Networks (HGNN)
Feng et al. (2019)
, learn node representations by iteratively aggregating information from neighboring nodes through message passing. This general mechanism can be written as

\displaystyle M_{v}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\operatorname{AGG}\left(\left\{X_{u}^{(k)}:u\in\mathcal{N}_{v}\right\}\right),\qquad\forall v\in\mathbf{V},\ 0\leq k<K,

(1)

\displaystyle X_{v}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\operatorname{COMBINE}\left(X_{v}^{(k)},M_{v}^{(k+1)}\right),\qquad\forall v\in\mathbf{V},\ 0\leq k<K.

(2)

Here,
\mathbf{V}
denotes the set of nodes,
K
is the number of GNN layers, and
X_{v}^{(k)}
is the representation of node
v
at the
k
-th layer, with
X_{v}^{(0)}=X_{v}
corresponding to the initial node feature. The aggregation function
\operatorname{AGG}
collects information from neighboring nodes, while
\operatorname{COMBINE}
updates the node representation by combining the aggregated message with the previous representation. The final representation
X_{v}^{(K)}
is then used for downstream prediction, such as node classification.

When GNNs are applied to large-scale graphs, scalability becomes a central challenge. The receptive field of a node, often referred to as its ego graph, grows rapidly with the number of message-passing layers. Therefore, scalable GNN architectures must be designed to efficiently control the cost of neighborhood expansion
Hamilton et al. (2017)
;
Shi et al. (2022)
;
Zeng et al. (2019)
. Decoupled GNN frameworks, including Simple Graph Convolution (SGC)
Wu et al. (2019)
, Scalable Inception GNN (SIGN)
Rossi et al. (2020)
, and Graph Attention Multi-Layer Perceptron (GAMLP)
Zhang et al. (2022)
, address this issue by separating feature propagation from model training. These methods precompute neighborhood aggregation in a parameter-free manner, significantly improving efficiency while maintaining strong predictive performance on large-scale graphs. For heterogeneous graphs, Simple and Efficient Heterogeneous Graph Neural Network (SeHGNN)
Yang et al. (2023)
, Random Projection Heterogeneous GNN (RpHGNN)
Hu et al. (2023)
, and more recent high-performing heterogeneous graph methods such as PSHGCN
He et al. (2023)
and HGAMLP-HOPE
Zhou et al. (2026)
further demonstrate the effectiveness of scalable propagation-based architectures.

Recent empirical studies also suggest that carefully tuned classical GNNs remain highly competitive. In particular, Luo et al.
Luo et al. (2024)
reassessed GCN, GAT, and GraphSAGE against recent graph transformer models and showed that, with proper hyperparameter tuning, classical message-passing GNNs can match or outperform more complex architectures on many node-classification benchmarks, including Pokec and ogbn-arxiv.

2.2
Temporal Graph Learning and Graph Distribution Shift

Our setting is graph learning under chronological splits, where nodes contain temporal information rather than edges. Such splits induce distribution shifts, and benchmarks such as GOOD
Gui et al. (2022)
and recent surveys
Liu and Ding (2024)
;
Zhang et al. (2024)
show that GNNs can suffer substantial performance degradation. Recent graph domain adaptation methods, such as SOGA
Mao et al. (2024)
, GraphCTA
Zhang et al. (2024)
, and DREAM
Yin et al. (2024)
, aim to learn transferable representations across domains. More recently, DGSDA
Yang et al. (2025)
addressed differences in node features and graph structures between domains, while GCAL
Qiao et al. (2025)
focused on adapting to new graph domains without forgetting previously learned information. Lei et al.
Lei et al. (2026)
proposed gradually adapting between different graph domains using intermediate graphs, while Lin et al.
Lin et al. (2024)
studied pretraining GNNs on multiple graphs for transfer to new domains. PromptDyG
Ai et al. (2026)
further addressed changes in graph structure over time by adapting pretrained models to newly observed graphs without requiring additional labels.

Domain adaptation has also been studied in other fields. In computer vision, Zhu et al.
Zhu et al. (2025)
studied how to adapt image classification models to new datasets without access to the original training data. In multi-agent collaborative perception, CoPEFT
Wei et al. (2025)
proposed adapting pretrained models to new environments by updating only a small fraction of their parameters. Unlike these approaches, which rely on representation alignment or model adaptation,
STAMP
and STAMP-mag directly modify the linear message-passing operator to balance neighborhood aggregation across timestamps, without requiring additional adaptation objectives or fine-tuning. This allows
STAMP
and STAMP-mag to be incorporated into strong contemporary baselines such as tuned GCNs on Pokec
Luo et al. (2024)
, SimTeG+TAPE+RevGAT on ogbn-arxiv
Li et al. (2021)
;
Duan et al. (2023)
, and HGAMLP-HOPE on ogbn-mag
Zhou et al. (2026)
.

2.3
Theoretical Analysis for Domain Adaptation

During a deep learning process, models are trained on data drawn from a training domain and evaluated on a test domain. Domain adaptation addresses scenarios in which training and test data are generated from different distributions, and therefore strong performance on the training domain does not necessarily guarantee comparable performance on the test domain
Pan and Yang (2009)
;
Ben-David et al. (2010)
. The performance on the test domain is known to depend both on the performance on the training domain and on the discrepancy between the two domains
Ben-David et al. (2006)
;
Ben-David et al. (2010)
;
Germain et al. (2013)
;
Germain et al. (2016)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the feature space and
\mathcal{Y}
the label space. The learning objective is to identify a predictor
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
that minimizes the training risk
R_{tr}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P_{tr}}\left[L(f(X),Y)\right]
where
P_{tr}
denotes the joint distribution of training feature-label pairs and
L:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a loss function. However, the ultimate goal is to minimize the test risk
R_{te}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P_{te}}\left[L(f(X),Y)\right]
where
P_{te}
represents the joint distribution of the test feature-label pairs. Recently, theories and applications adapted the Wasserstein-1 distance (
W_{1}
) for the metric between the test and train domain distributions
Lee et al. (2019)
;
Shen et al. (2018)
;
Arjovsky et al. (2017)
;
Redko et al. (2017)
. Under appropriate regularity conditions (see
Redko et al. (2017)
), the test risk admits the following upper bound:

\displaystyle R_{te}(f)\leq R_{tr}(f)+W_{1}(Q_{tr},Q_{te})+\lambda,

(3)

where
Q_{tr}
and
Q_{te}
denote the marginal feature distributions of
P_{tr}
and
P_{te}
, respectively, and
\lambda
accounts for the joint error of the ideal hypothesis across the two domains.

3
Method

3.1
STAMP
: Timestamp-Balanced Message Passing

Denote the graph dataset as
\mathbf{G}=(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{E})
, and denote the set of possible timestamps by
\mathbf{T}=\{1,2,...,t_{max}\}
and the set of labels by
\mathbf{Y}
. The training data consist of historical nodes, namely those with timestamps strictly smaller than
t_{max}
, while the test data consist of nodes with timestamp
t_{max}
. Consequently, the labels of timestamp
t_{max}
nodes are unknown. Throughout the paper,
y
and
t
denote the label and timestamp of a target node, while
\tilde{y}
and
\tilde{t}
denote the label and timestamp of one of its neighboring nodes, unless otherwise specified.

Message passing refers to the procedure of aggregating representations from neighboring nodes, typically applied across multiple layers. We begin by introducing the widely used averaging message passing scheme
Hamilton et al. (2017)
;
Kipf and Welling (2017)
. For an arbitrary target node
v\in\mathbf{V}
with label
y
and time
t
,

M_{v}^{(k+1)}=\frac{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})}X_{w}^{(k)}}{{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})|}},

(4)

where
\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t}):=\{w\in\mathbf{V}\mid w\;\text{is a neighbor of}\;v\;\text{with label}\;\tilde{y}\;\text{and time}\;\tilde{t}\}
,
X_{w}^{(k)}
is the
k
-th representation considered as a random variable, and
M_{v}^{(k+1)}
is the aggregated message at node
v
in the
(k+1)
-th layer. Let
\mathcal{N}_{v}
denote the full neighborhood of
v
. Then,
|\mathcal{N}_{v}|=\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})|
.

Suppose the initial features
X_{w}^{(0)}
have means which depends on time, i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{w}^{(0)}]=m_{1}(\tilde{t})
for
\forall w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}\left(\tilde{y},\tilde{t}\right)
, where
m_{1}:\mathbf{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{f}
and
f
is the dimension of the representation space.

We first examine the first moment of the aggregated message in the initial layer:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{M_{v}^{(1)}}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})}X_{w}^{(0)}}{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})|}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})}X_{w}^{(0)}}{|\mathcal{N}_{v}|}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{v}|}\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})}m_{1}(\tilde{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{v}|}\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})|m_{1}(\tilde{t}),

(5)

which depends on label and time of the target node
v
since
|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})|/|\mathcal{N}_{v}|
is dependent on
y
and
t
. The
STAMP
is defined in order to align the first moment of the initial aggregated messages to be invariant across time and labels as follows :

\displaystyle M_{v}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})}\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})\right|}X_{w}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})}\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})\right|}X_{w}^{(0)},

(6)

where
\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t}):=\{w\in\mathbf{V}\mid w\;\text{is a neighbor of}\;v\;\text{with time}\;\tilde{t}\}
. We additionally assume that each target node has at least one neighbor at every timestamp, i.e.,

|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|>0,\qquad\forall v\in\mathbf{V},\ \forall\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}.

(7)

This assumption ensures that the
STAMP
aggregation operator is well-defined. In large-scale graphs with sufficiently dense cross-time connectivity, this condition is often mild and can be empirically verified. Now, we can observe that

{\mathbb{E}}[{M_{v}^{(1)}}]=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}{\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}}m_{1}(\tilde{t}),

(8)

and therefore the mean of
M_{v}^{(1)}
is invariant with time
t
and label y.

STAMP
, and a pseudocode of
STAMP
. We further provide empirical validations of the assumption
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{w}^{(0)}]=m_{1}(\tilde{t})
for
\forall w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}\left(\tilde{y},\tilde{t}\right)
for the datasets ogbn-arxiv and temporal-split Pokec, in Appendix

.

3.2
STAMP-mag: A Specialized Variant for ogbn-mag

We propose STAMP-mag as an approach to achieve first moment invariance specifically for the dataset ogbn-mag. In this case we set the possible timestamps by
\mathbf{T}:=\{\dots,t_{max}-2,t_{max}-1,t_{max}\}
. Denote
{P}_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})
as the probability distribution over the label–timestamp pairs of neighboring nodes. By definition, it satisfies
\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}{P}_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})=1
. Now, the following assumption gives a unique property which arises the use of STAMP-mag.

\displaystyle\textit{Assumption 1}:{P}_{yt}\left(\tilde{y},\tilde{t}\right)=f(y,t)g\left(y,\tilde{y},|\tilde{t}-t|\right),\ \forall y,\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y},\forall t,\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}.

(9)

\displaystyle\textit{Assumption 2}:\mathbb{E}[X_{w}^{(0)}]=\mu_{1}(\tilde{y}),\ \forall w\ \text{with label}\ \tilde{y}.

(10)

Figure 3:
Graphical representation of functions
f
and
g
, which jointly govern the distribution of neighboring node label–timestamp pairs under Assumption 3. The shaded bars denote relative connectivity for a target node with label
y
; only neighboring nodes with label
\tilde{y}
are shown. The function
g(y,\tilde{y},|\tilde{t}-t|)
captures how relative connectivity decays with temporal distance, while the normalization factor
f(y,t)
adjusts the overall scale so that
{P}_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})
forms a valid probability distribution.

Assumption 1 states a separability condition on the distribution of neighboring nodes, motivated by the empirical observation that the proportion of neighboring nodes with timestamp
\tilde{t}
for a target node at time
t
decreases as the temporal distance
|\tilde{t}-t|
increases in the ogbn-mag dataset. We modeled this behavior using a function
g\big(y,\tilde{y},|\tilde{t}-t|\big)
, which characterizes the proportion of neighboring nodes as a function of temporal distance. In addition, a normalization function
f(y,t)
is introduced to ensure that
{P}_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})
forms a valid probability distribution. Figure
3
provides a graphical illustration of these properties. Assumption 2 states that the mean of the initial features are time-invariant, and only dependent to the label. These assumptions are motivated by structural patterns observed in the real-worl citation graph ogbn-mag, and we provide empirical validations in Appendix
7
.

Now, consider a target node
v
with timestamp
t
, and let
\tilde{t}
denote the timestamp of a neighboring node. Define
\Delta=|\tilde{t}-t|
. If
0<\Delta\leq|t_{max}-t|
, then
\tilde{t}
admits two symmetric candidates, namely
t+\Delta
and
t-\Delta
. In contrast, if
\Delta>|t_{\max}-t|
or
\Delta=0
, the timestamp
\tilde{t}
corresponds only to the single value
t-\Delta
. This asymmetry in temporal offsets induces unequal contributions during aggregation. To formalize this distinction, we partition the time index set as
\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}=\{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}\mid 0<|\tilde{t}-t|\leq|t_{max}-t|\}
and
\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}=\{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}\mid|\tilde{t}-t|>|t_{max}-t|\;\text{or}\;\tilde{t}=t\}
. Therefore, under conventional averaging message passing, nodes with timestamps in
\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}
contribute twice than the nodes with timestamps in
\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}
. The key idea of STAMP-mag is to correct this imbalance by assigning doubled weights to neighbor nodes whose timestamps belong to
\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}
, via graph modification in the preprocessing stage . From now on, we call the nodes with timestamp belonging to
\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}
and
\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}
as
double nodes
and
single nodes
, respectively.

Figure 4:
Graphical illustration of STAMP-mag. For a target node at timestamp
t=2018
, neighbors at timestamps
2017
and
2019
form a symmetric pair (
\Delta=1
), contributing equally during aggregation. Neighbors at timestamps outside this symmetric range (e.g.,
2016
,
2015
, …) are single nodes whose weights are doubled under STAMP-mag to restore balance.

To illustrate, consider Figure
4
, where the target node has timestamp
t=2018
. In this case,
\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}=\{2019,2017\}
and
\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}=\{2018,2016,2015,2014,...\}
. Neighbor nodes with timestamps
2017
and
2019
correspond to
\Delta=1
and form a symmetric pair around
t
. By assumption, these nodes contribute equally during message passing. However, single nodes lack such symmetric counterparts. Consequently, double nodes collectively contribute twice as much as single nodes do under conventional aggregation. We modify the initial graph during the preprocessing stage, in order to double the weight of single nodes. Accordingly, STAMP-mag adjusts the balance of double and single nodes to contribute equally when message passing, and therefore achieves first moment alignment for the initial message layers.

The STAMP-mag from the
k
-th layer to the
(k+1)
-th layer of target node
v
is defined by

M_{v}^{(k+1)}:=\frac{{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})}2X_{w}^{(k)}+\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})}X_{w}^{(k)}}}{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}}2|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})|+\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}}|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})|}.

(11)

Suppose the initial features
X_{w}^{(0)}
have means which depends on time, i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{w}^{(0)}]=m_{1}(\tilde{t})
for
\forall w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}\left(\tilde{y},\tilde{t}\right)
, where
m_{1}:\mathbf{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{f}
and
f
is the dimension of the representation space. Then,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{M_{v}^{(1)}}\right]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}}2P_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})m_{1}(\tilde{t})+\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}}P_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})m_{1}(\tilde{t})}{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}}2P_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})+\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}}P_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})},

(12)

using the approximation
{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})|/|\mathcal{N}_{v}|}\simeq P_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})
. The key property is that the first moment of the initial messages obtained by STAMP-mag does not depend on the target node timestamp
t
since by assumption as follows.

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}}2P_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})+\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}}P_{yt}(\tilde{y},\tilde{t})

\displaystyle=f(y,t)\big(\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{single}}}2g(y,\tilde{y},|\tilde{t}-t|)+\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\text{double}}}g(y,\tilde{y},|t-t|)\big)

\displaystyle=f(y,t)\big(2g(y,\tilde{y},0)+2\sum_{\tau>|t_{max}-t|}g(y,\tilde{y},\tau)+2\sum_{0<\tau\leq|t_{max}-t|}g(y,\tilde{y},\tau)\big)

\displaystyle=2f(y,t)\sum_{\tau\geq 0}g(y,\tilde{y},\tau),

(13)

and plugging this into
12
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{M_{v}^{(1)}}\right]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}f(y,t)\sum_{\tau\geq 0}g(y,\tilde{y},\tau)\mu_{X}^{(0)}(\tilde{y})}{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}f(y,t)\sum_{\tau\geq 0}g(y,\tilde{y},\tau)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tau\geq 0}g(y,\tilde{y},\tau)\mu_{X}^{(k)}(\tilde{y})}{\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{\tau\geq 0}g(y,\tilde{y},\tau)},

(14)

which does not depend on
t
. Therefore, the first moment of the initial layer message is invariant with time. Below, we provide the pseudocode of STAMP-mag. As discussed, STAMP-mag modifies the graph structure in the preprocessing stage, by duplicating single nodes, thus balancing their influence with double nodes.

3.3

STAMP
and STAMP-mag

Although both methods reduce temporal first-moment shift, they correct different sources of aggregation bias.
STAMP
directly balances the total contribution of source-timestamp buckets, whereas STAMP-mag corrects the unequal multiplicity of symmetric temporal offsets near the boundary of a finite chronological graph. Table
1
summarizes their differences.

and STAMP-mag.

4
Theoretical Analysis of
STAMP

We analyze how
STAMP
affects the representation-level discrepancy between the training and test domains. Throughout this section, we assume the full timestamp-neighborhood coverage condition

|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|>0,\qquad\forall v\in\mathbf{V},\ \forall\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}.

(15)

This ensures that the
STAMP
aggregation operator is well-defined. Note that the full timestamp-neighborhood coverage assumption is required for the theoretical analysis but not for practical implementation. When a node has no neighbors from a particular timestamp, the corresponding timestamp group is excluded from aggregation, and the weights are computed using the observed neighbors. Timestamp groups with few neighbors are handled in the same manner, although their contributions
may have higher variance.

Now, for a random variable
Z
, let
\mathsf{Law}(Z)
denote its probability distribution. Denote
{tr},{te}
as the train, test domains, respectively, and let

\mu_{tr}^{\mathrm{STAMP}}:=\mathsf{Law}(M_{{tr},\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}),\qquad\mu_{te}^{\mathrm{STAMP}}:=\mathsf{Law}(M_{{te},\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}).

(16)

Assume that the initial node representations admit the first-order temporal decomposition

X_{w}^{(0)}=m_{1}(\tilde{t})+\xi_{w},\qquad\forall w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t}),

(17)

where
m_{1}:\mathbf{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{f}
is the timestamp-dependent mean and

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{w}\mid time(w)=\tilde{t}]=0.

(18)

STAMP
defines the first-layer message by

M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}:=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})}X_{w}^{(0)}.

(19)

Under (
17
), its first moment is

\mathbb{E}[M_{{tr},\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}]=\mathbb{E}[M_{{te},\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}]=m_{\mathrm{STAMP}},\qquad m_{\mathrm{STAMP}}:=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}m_{1}(\tilde{t}).

(20)

Thus,
STAMP
removes the first-moment train–test discrepancy caused by different neighborhood timestamp mixtures.

Theorem 4.1

(Neighbor-size upper bound for
STAMP
)
.

Suppose that the residuals in (
17
) are pairwise uncorrelated in the sense that

\mathbb{E}\langle\xi_{w},\xi_{w^{\prime}}\rangle=0\qquad\text{for }w\neq w^{\prime},

and satisfy

\mathbb{E}\|\xi_{w}\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{\tilde{t}}^{2}\qquad\text{whenever }time(w)=\tilde{t}.

(21)

If

|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|\geq n_{\min},\qquad\forall v\in\mathbf{V},\ \forall\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T},

(22)

and
\sigma_{\tilde{t}}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
for every
\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}
, then

W_{1}(\mu_{tr}^{\mathrm{STAMP}},\mu_{te}^{\mathrm{STAMP}})\leq\frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}}.

(23)

Proof.

For a target node
v
, (
19
) and
(
17
) imply that the centered
STAMP
residual is

\varepsilon_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}:=M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}-m_{\mathrm{STAMP}}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})}\xi_{w}.

Using pairwise uncorrelatedness and

\mathbb{E}\|\xi_{w}\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{\tilde{t}}^{2}
, we get

\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|^{2}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\frac{\sigma_{\tilde{t}}^{2}}{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle W_{1}\left(\mu_{tr}^{\mathrm{STAMP}},\mu_{te}^{\mathrm{STAMP}}\right)

\displaystyle=W_{1}\left(\mathsf{Law}(\varepsilon_{{tr},{\mathrm{STAMP}}}),\mathsf{Law}(\varepsilon_{{te},{\mathrm{STAMP}}})\right)

(24)

\displaystyle\leq W_{1}\left(\mathsf{Law}(\varepsilon_{{tr},{\mathrm{STAMP}}}),\delta_{0}\right)+W_{1}\left(\delta_{0},\mathsf{Law}(\varepsilon_{{te},{\mathrm{STAMP}}})\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_{{tr},{\mathrm{STAMP}}}\|+\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_{{te},{\mathrm{STAMP}}}\|

(26)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_{{tr},{\mathrm{STAMP}}}\|^{2}}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_{{te},{\mathrm{STAMP}}}\|^{2}}

(27)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}}.

(28)

∎

Corollary 4.2

(Sufficient condition for
STAMP
to reduce Wasserstein discrepancy)
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
4.1
. Let

\mu_{tr}^{\mathrm{agg}}:=\mathsf{Law}(M_{{tr},\mathrm{agg}}^{(1)}),\qquad\mu_{te}^{\mathrm{agg}}:=\mathsf{Law}(M_{{te},\mathrm{agg}}^{(1)}).

(29)

and let

\Delta_{\mathrm{agg}}:=\mathbb{E}[|M_{{tr},\mathrm{agg}}^{(1)}-M_{{tr},\mathrm{agg}}^{(1)}|].

(30)

If

|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}>\left(\frac{2\sigma}{\Delta_{\mathrm{agg}}}\right)^{2},

(31)

then

W_{1}(\mu_{tr}^{\mathrm{STAMP}},\mu_{te}^{\mathrm{STAMP}})<W_{1}(\mu_{tr}^{\mathrm{avg}},\mu_{te}^{\mathrm{avg}}).

(32)

Proof.

Since
\Delta_{\mathrm{agg}}\leq W_{1}(\mu_{tr}^{\mathrm{avg}},\mu_{te}^{\mathrm{avg}})
,
Theorem
4.1
gives

W_{1}(\mu_{tr}^{\mathrm{STAMP}},\mu_{te}^{\mathrm{STAMP}})<W_{1}(\mu_{tr}^{\mathrm{avg}},\mu_{te}^{\mathrm{avg}}).

∎

4.1
Class and temporal decomposition

For a target node
v
, define the average representation of its neighbors at timestamp
\tilde{t}
by

\overline{X}_{v}^{(0)}(\tilde{t}):=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})}X_{w}^{(0)}.

(33)

Let
d\in\{tr,te\}
denote the domain of the target node. We assume that the timestamp-specific neighborhood average admits the decomposition

\overline{X}_{v}^{(0)}(\tilde{t})=a_{y}+b(\tilde{t})+r_{d,y}(\tilde{t})+\xi_{v,\tilde{t}},

(34)

whenever the target node
v
has label
y
and belongs to domain
d
. Here,
a_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{f}
denotes the class-dependent component,
b(\tilde{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{f}
denotes the timestamp-dependent component,
r_{d,y}(\tilde{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{f}
denotes the approximation error, and
\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{f}
denotes the centered residual.

We assume that

\|r_{d,y}(\tilde{t})\|\leq\beta,\qquad\forall d\in\{tr,te\},\ \forall y\in\mathbf{Y},\ \forall\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}.

(35)

We additionally assume that

\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}\mid y,d,\left\{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(s)|\right\}_{s\in\mathbf{T}}\right]=0.

(36)

Suppose that the residuals corresponding to different timestamps are pairwise uncorrelated in the sense that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\xi_{v,\tilde{t}},\xi_{v,\tilde{s}}\right\rangle\mid y,d,\left\{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(r)|\right\}_{r\in\mathbf{T}}\right]=0,\qquad\forall\tilde{t}\neq\tilde{s}.

(37)

We further assume that the node-level fluctuations within each timestamp-specific neighborhood are sufficiently weakly dependent so that the second moment of their average decreases inversely with the neighborhood size:

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}\|^{2}\mid y,d,\left\{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(s)|\right\}_{s\in\mathbf{T}}\right]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|}.

(38)

The decomposition in (
34
) separates the class-dependent component
a_{y}
, which should be preserved for classification, from the timestamp-dependent component
b(\tilde{t})
, which may induce a distribution discrepancy between the training and test domains.

By (
33
), the
STAMP
message can be written as

M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\overline{X}_{v}^{(0)}(\tilde{t}).

(39)

Substituting (
34
) into (
39
), we obtain

\displaystyle M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}={}

\displaystyle a_{y}+\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}b(\tilde{t})+\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}r_{d,y}(\tilde{t})+\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}.

(40)

When
\beta=0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}\mid y,d\right]=a_{y}+\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}b(\tilde{t}).

(41)

The right-hand side of (
41
) does not depend on the domain
d
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}\mid y,tr\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}\mid y,te\right].

(42)

Moreover, for arbitrary labels
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Y}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}\mid y,d\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}\mid y^{\prime},d\right]=a_{y}-a_{y^{\prime}}.

(43)

Therefore, under the decomposition (
34
),
STAMP
removes the temporal component which may differ between the training and test domains while preserving the first-order difference between different classes.

4.2
Classification error bound

Let
h=(h_{y})_{y\in\mathbf{Y}}
be a score-based classifier, where
h_{y}:\mathbb{R}^{f}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
L
-Lipschitz for every
y\in\mathbf{Y}
. Define the classification margin of
h
at representation
x
with label
y
by

\rho_{h}(x,y):=h_{y}(x)-\max_{y^{\prime}\neq y}h_{y^{\prime}}(x).

(44)

Since each
h_{y}
is
L
-Lipschitz, the function
x\mapsto\rho_{h}(x,y)
is
2L
-Lipschitz. For
\gamma>0
, define the margin loss by

\ell_{\gamma}(z):=\begin{cases}1,&z\leq 0,\\
1-\dfrac{z}{\gamma},&0<z<\gamma,\\
0,&z\geq\gamma.\end{cases}

(45)

For
d\in\{tr,te\}
, define the margin risk of
STAMP
by

R_{d}^{\gamma}(h):=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{\gamma}\left(\rho_{h}\left(M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)},y\right)\right)\mid d\right].

(46)

Define the classification error by

R_{d}(h):=\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{y^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Y}}{\arg\max}\ h_{y^{\prime}}\left(M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}\right)\neq y\mid d\right).

(47)

For
d\in\{tr,te\}
, define the label distribution by

\pi_{d}(y):=\mathbb{P}(y\mid d).

(48)

Define the discrepancy between the training and test label distributions by

\Delta_{\mathbf{Y}}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in\mathbf{Y}}\left|\pi_{tr}(y)-\pi_{te}(y)\right|.

(49)

Theorem 4.3

(Classification error bound for
STAMP
)
.

Assume (
15
) and
(
34
)–(
38
). Suppose that

|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|\geq n_{\min},\qquad\forall v\in\mathbf{V},\ \forall\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}.

(50)

Then, for every
\gamma>0
,

R_{te}(h)\leq R_{tr}^{\gamma}(h)+\Delta_{\mathbf{Y}}+\frac{4L}{\gamma}\left(\beta+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}}\right).

(51)

Proof.

For each
y\in\mathbf{Y}
, define

c_{y}:=a_{y}+\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}b(\tilde{t}).

(52)

It follows from (
40
) that

\displaystyle M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}-c_{y}={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}r_{d,y}(\tilde{t})+\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}.

(53)

By (
35
),

\left\|\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}r_{d,y}(\tilde{t})\right\|\leq\beta.

(54)

By (
37
),
(
38
), and the law of total expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}\right\|^{2}\mid y,d\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|^{2}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}\|^{2}\mid y,d\right]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|\mathbf{T}|^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|}\mid y,d\right]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}.

(55)

By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}\right\|\mid y,d\right]\leq\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}}.

(56)

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)}-c_{y}\right\|\mid y,d\right]\leq\beta+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}}.

(57)

Since the function
x\mapsto\rho_{h}(x,y)
is
2L
-Lipschitz and the function
\ell_{\gamma}
is
1/\gamma
-Lipschitz, the function

x\mapsto\ell_{\gamma}\left(\rho_{h}(x,y)\right)

(58)

is
2L/\gamma
-Lipschitz.

For
d\in\{tr,te\}
and
y\in\mathbf{Y}
, define

L_{d,y}^{\gamma}(h):=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{\gamma}\left(\rho_{h}\left(M_{v,\mathrm{STAMP}}^{(1)},y\right)\right)\mid y,d\right].

(59)

It follows from (
57
) that

\displaystyle\left|L_{d,y}^{\gamma}(h)-\ell_{\gamma}\left(\rho_{h}(c_{y},y)\right)\right|\leq\frac{2L}{\gamma}\left(\beta+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}}\right).

(60)

Applying (
60
) to both the training and test domains gives

L_{te,y}^{\gamma}(h)\leq L_{tr,y}^{\gamma}(h)+\frac{4L}{\gamma}\left(\beta+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}}\right).

(61)

By the law of total expectation,

R_{d}^{\gamma}(h)=\sum_{y\in\mathbf{Y}}\pi_{d}(y)L_{d,y}^{\gamma}(h).

(62)

Since
0\leq L_{tr,y}^{\gamma}(h)\leq 1
, the definition of total variation distance gives

\sum_{y\in\mathbf{Y}}\pi_{te}(y)L_{tr,y}^{\gamma}(h)\leq\sum_{y\in\mathbf{Y}}\pi_{tr}(y)L_{tr,y}^{\gamma}(h)+\Delta_{\mathbf{Y}}.

(63)

Combining (
61
),
(
62
), and
(
63
), we obtain

R_{te}^{\gamma}(h)\leq R_{tr}^{\gamma}(h)+\Delta_{\mathbf{Y}}+\frac{4L}{\gamma}\left(\beta+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}}\right).

(64)

Finally, every misclassified node has a nonpositive classification margin. Therefore,

R_{te}(h)\leq R_{te}^{\gamma}(h).

(65)

Combining this inequality with (
64
) proves
(
51
).
∎

Theorem
4.3
provides a direct connection between
STAMP
and the test classification error. In particular, the additional classification error caused by the temporal domain discrepancy is controlled by

\frac{4L}{\gamma}\left(\beta+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{T}|n_{\min}}}\right).

(66)

Therefore, the bound becomes smaller when the decomposition in (
34
) is more accurate, when the timestamp-specific neighborhoods are larger, and when more timestamp buckets are aggregated.

4.3
Optimality of
STAMP
among first-order matching methods

We next consider a general timestamp-weighted aggregation method of the form

M_{v,q}^{(1)}:=\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}\overline{X}_{v}^{(0)}(\tilde{t}),

(67)

where

q_{\tilde{t}}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}=1.

(68)

When
\beta=0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[M_{v,q}^{(1)}\mid y,d\right]=a_{y}+\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}b(\tilde{t}).

(69)

Therefore, every fixed weight vector satisfying
(
68
) aligns the training and test class-conditional first moments. However, different choices of
q_{\tilde{t}}
result in different residual variances.

Theorem 4.4

(Minimax optimality of
STAMP
)
.

Assume (
34
)–(
38
) with
\beta=0
, and suppose that

|\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|\geq n_{\min},\qquad\forall v\in\mathbf{V},\ \forall\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}.

(70)

Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|M_{v,q}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[M_{v,q}^{(1)}\mid y,d\right]\right\|^{2}\mid y,d\right]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n_{\min}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}^{2}.

(71)

Among all weights satisfying (
68
), the right-hand side of (
71
) is uniquely minimized by

q_{\tilde{t}}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|},\qquad\forall\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}.

(72)

Therefore,
STAMP
uniquely minimizes the worst-case residual variance among all fixed first-order matching aggregation methods when only the common variance upper bound in (
38
) is known.

Proof.

It follows from (
67
) and
(
34
) that

M_{v,q}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[M_{v,q}^{(1)}\mid y,d\right]=\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}.

(73)

By the pairwise uncorrelatedness of the residuals,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}\right\|^{2}\mid y,d\right]=\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\xi_{v,\tilde{t}}\|^{2}\mid y,d\right]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n_{\min}}\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}^{2}.

(74)

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\left(\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}\right)^{2}\leq|\mathbf{T}|\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}^{2}.

(75)

Since

\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}=1,

(76)

we obtain

\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|}.

(77)

Equality in (
77
) holds if and only if all weights are identical. Therefore, equality holds if and only if

q_{\tilde{t}}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{T}|},\qquad\forall\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}.

(78)

This proves that the uniform weights used by
STAMP
uniquely minimize the upper bound in
(
71
).
∎

Theorem
4.4
explains why
STAMP
is preferable among the numerous methods which align the first moment. Although every fixed timestamp weighting method aligns the training and test first moments under (
34
),
STAMP
minimizes the worst-case stochastic error without requiring the estimation of timestamp-specific residual variances.

In particular, the classification error bound corresponding to the general aggregation method (
67
) contains the stochastic term

\frac{4L\sigma}{\gamma\sqrt{n_{\min}}}\left(\sum_{\tilde{t}\in\mathbf{T}}q_{\tilde{t}}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

(79)

and this term is uniquely minimized by the
STAMP
weights. Hence,
STAMP
is the minimax choice when no timestamp is privileged and only the common upper bound in (
38
) is available.

5.1
Synthetic Graph Experiments

We evaluate
STAMP
and STAMP-mag on two synthetic benchmarks designed to isolate distinct forms of temporal aggregation bias.

Temporal aggregation skew.

The first benchmark is generated using a Temporal Stochastic Block Model (TSBM), in which the probability of a directed edge from source node
\tilde{i}
to target node
i
is

P(\tilde{i}\to i)=P_{0}[y_{i},y_{\tilde{i}}]\gamma[y_{i},y_{\tilde{i}}]^{|t_{i}-t_{\tilde{i}}|}.

(80)

The graph contains 3,000 nodes, five classes, and twelve balanced timestamp bins, each containing 250 nodes. Labels are sampled independently and uniformly, and 32-dimensional features are generated from weak class-dependent Gaussian distributions. For same-label pairs, we set
P_{0}=0.018
and
\gamma=0.94
; for cross-label pairs, we set
P_{0}=0.22
and
\gamma=0.025
. Cross-label edges are therefore abundant at the same timestamp, whereas same-label edges are sparse but persist across time. The resulting graph contains 163,276 directed edges, of which
82.15\%
are same-time edges and
15.75\%
are same-label edges.

We use timestamps
\{0,\ldots,6\}
for training,
\{7,8\}
for validation, and
\{9,10,11\}
for testing, so the test domain comprises the most recent timestamp block. By giving each observed source-timestamp bucket equal total mass while preserving the ordinary mean-aggregation scale,
STAMP
prevents the dominant same-time heterophilous bucket from overwhelming the persistent cross-time signal.

Temporal boundary asymmetry.

The second benchmark is constructed to isolate the boundary structure targeted by STAMP-mag. It contains eight timestamps, three classes, and 100 nodes per timestamp–class pair, giving 2,400 nodes. Node features are generated as

x_{v}=\mu_{y_{v}}+\epsilon_{v},\qquad\epsilon_{v}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1.75^{2}I),

(81)

where the class centers have scale
1.0
and the feature dimension is 16. For a target node at timestamp
t
, define

r_{t}=\min(t-t_{\min},t_{\max}-t).

(82)

Its incoming neighborhood contains 60 class-balanced same-time neighbors, six class-balanced neighbors from each of
t-\Delta
and
t+\Delta
for every
1\leq\Delta\leq r_{t}
, and 20 same-label neighbors in total, distributed over the unpaired timestamps satisfying

|t_{u}-t_{v}|>r_{t}.

(83)

The paired temporal counts are exactly symmetric, while the one-sided boundary neighbors carry the label-informative signal.

The graph contains 235,200 directed edges with average in-degree 98.0. Same-time edges account for
61.22\%
of all edges, while one-sided edges account for
20.41\%
and are exclusively same-label. We use timestamps
\{0,\ldots,5\}
for training, timestamp
{6}
for validation, and timestamp
{7}
for testing. Thus, the test set consists exactly of the most recent timestamp. STAMP-mag doubles the one-sided temporal contributions and normalizes the weights to preserve the ordinary mean-aggregation scale.

5.3
Pokec

We conducted an additional experiment on the Pokec dataset to evaluate generalizability. The Pokec dataset is a large-scale directed online social network collected from Pokec, a popular social networking service in Slovakia. Each node represents a user, and each directed edge represents a friendship relation. The publicly available SNAP version contains
1{,}632{,}803
users and
30{,}622{,}564
directed friendship edges. In addition to the graph structure, Pokec provides anonymized user-profile attributes such as gender, age, hobbies, interests, and education, written in Slovak
Rossi and Ahmed (2015)
. Unlike ogbn-mag and ogbn-arxiv, Pokec is not originally provided with a standard chronological train/validation/test split. Therefore, to evaluate temporal out-of-distribution generalization in the same spirit as the OGB chronological splits, we constructed a chronological node split using the user registration year as the node timestamp.

Specifically, let
t_{v}
denote the registration year of user node
v
. We first removed nodes whose registration year or target label was missing. Let

t_{\max}:=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{labeled}}}t_{v},

(84)

be the most recent registration year among labeled users after preprocessing. We then assigned nodes with
t_{v}\leq t_{\max}-2
to the training set, nodes with
t_{v}=t_{\max}-1
to the validation set, and nodes with
t_{v}=t_{\max}
to the test set. Thus, the model is trained only on historical users, validated on users from the year immediately before the test period, and evaluated on the most recent users. This follows the same chronological principle used for ogbn-mag and ogbn-arxiv, where recent nodes are held out to test robustness under temporal distribution shift.

Since Pokec does not provide edge timestamps, we used the full observed friendship graph in a transductive setting, while restricting supervision according to the chronological node split. That is, labels from validation and test nodes were not used during training, but their nodes and edges remained available for message passing, consistent with the transductive evaluation protocol of OGB node-classification benchmarks. The prediction task was formulated as user-profile classification. In our experiments, the target label was chosen from a profile attribute with sufficient coverage after preprocessing, and the same chronological split was used for all compared methods.

5.5
Computational Complexity

Both
STAMP
and STAMP-mag are aggregation operators applied to a pre-existing graph learning method rather than standalone models. We therefore analyze their additional cost relative to the original method. Let
N=|\mathbf{V}|
,
E=|\mathbf{E}|
, and let
f
denote the dimension of the initial node features. For
STAMP
, let

B:=\left|\{(v,\tilde{t})\middle||\mathcal{N}_{v}(\tilde{t})|>0\}\right|,

(85)

denote the number of nonempty target–timestamp buckets. Since every observed bucket contains at least one edge,
B\leq E
.

Additional cost of
STAMP
.

To construct the
STAMP
coefficients, each edge is grouped according to its target node and source timestamp. Computing the
B
observed bucket sizes and assigning the edge weights require
\mathcal{O}(E)
time. The resulting weighted feature propagation requires
\mathcal{O}(Ef)
time, which is the same asymptotic cost as the ordinary first mean-aggregation step that it replaces. Hence, adapting an existing method with
STAMP
introduces only an additional one-time preprocessing cost of
\mathcal{O}(E)
. The bucket counts require
\mathcal{O}(B)
memory, while explicitly storing all edge weights requires an additional
\mathcal{O}(E)
memory.

Additional cost of STAMP-mag.

STAMP-mag determines whether each edge corresponds to a paired or one-sided temporal contribution using the timestamps of its source and target nodes. A single edge scan assigns the unnormalized weights, and a second scan computes the target-wise normalization. The weight-construction cost is therefore
\mathcal{O}(E)
. As with
STAMP
, the weighted first propagation requires
\mathcal{O}(Ef)
time and replaces the ordinary propagation already present in the underlying method. Thus, the additional time introduced by STAMP-mag is
\mathcal{O}(E)
. The method requires
\mathcal{O}(N)
memory for the target-wise normalization factors and
\mathcal{O}(E)
memory if the edge weights are stored explicitly.

Consequently, neither method changes the asymptotic training complexity of the underlying architecture. Both introduce a one-time linear preprocessing cost, while retaining the
\mathcal{O}(Ef)
complexity of the original first feature-propagation operation.
STAMP
additionally stores the observed target–timestamp buckets, whereas STAMP-mag requires only target-wise normalization values.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we studied graph domain adaptation under chronological node splits. We proposed
STAMP
, a timestamp-balanced feature propagation operator that aligns the first moment of initial messages across temporal domains, together with STAMP-mag, a specialized boundary-correction variant for ogbn-mag. Both methods can be integrated into existing architectures by modifying only the initial aggregation step, introducing little additional computational cost. Experiments on citation, social, and synthetic graphs demonstrate their scalability and effectiveness. In particular,
STAMP
achieves SOTA performance on ogbn-arxiv, while STAMP-mag establishes SOTA performance on ogbn-mag. We further provide theoretical analyses characterizing their first-moment alignment properties under the stated assumptions.

Several limitations remain. The improvements over strong baselines are sometimes modest, and evaluation across a broader range of architectures and datasets would provide stronger evidence of generalizability. Moreover, the current analysis focuses on first-moment alignment but does not fully characterize alternative message passing methods which achieve first-moment alignment. Future work may consider higher-order distribution alignment, adaptive temporal weighting, and extensions to broader temporal graph learning settings.
