Gradient boosting machine for survival prediction in studies with general failure-time outcome-dependent sampling design
Authors: Haolin Li, Haibo Zhou, Jianwen Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Outcome-dependent sampling (ODS) designs are widely used in epidemiological and biomedical research as cost-effective alternatives to full cohort studies. While existing research has focused on parameter estimation and inference under specific models, survival prediction in ODS settings remains underexplored. In this paper, we propose a gradient boosting machine tailored for survival prediction in studies conducted using general failure-time ODS designs. Our approach integrates a weighting scheme to correct for sampling bias and improves predictive accuracy. We introduce a weighted empirical risk based on the integrated Brier score for hyperparameter tuning and establish its convergence to the corresponding integrated Brier-score risk. The proposed method is evaluated through extensive simulations and applied to the Cancer Incidence and Mortality of Uranium Miners Study, and is shown to have improved effectiveness in survival prediction. This work advances machine learning methods for ODS studies, offering valuable insights for personalized medicine and clinical decision-making in failure-time ODS settings.

Introduction

His work has shaped how researchers model event processes, recurrent events, and multistate data, and his books remain standard references in these areas (e.g. Lawless 2011; Cook and Lawless 2007, 2018). A consistent theme in his research is the development of effective methods when data are limited or collected under practical constraints. This includes his influential work on outcome-dependent and two-phase sampling strategies for failure-time data, where covariates may be expensive to obtain (e.g., Cook and Lawless 2018; Che et al. 2023). These contributions are closely related to the outcome-dependent sampling framework considered in this paper. Our goal of developing prediction methods that properly account for biased sampling builds on ideas that have been central to Jerry’s work. We are pleased to contribute this paper in recognition of his influential career and many contributions to lifetime data analysis.

In many epidemiological studies, collecting comprehensive covariate information for a large cohort can be prohibitively expensive, particularly when the event of interest is rare or when obtaining certain measurements requires costly or limited resources. Traditional study designs that rely on exhaustive data collection may lead to inefficiencies, especially in settings where only a subset of participants provides the most informative data. These challenges underscore the need for more efficient study designs. Outcome-dependent sampling (ODS) offers a cost-effective alternative by selectively collecting covariate information based on observed outcomes (e.g., Zhou et al. 2002; Weaver and Zhou 2005; Zhou et al. 2007; Song et al. 2009; Wang and Zhou 2010; Zhou et al. 2011). The rationale behind ODS is to focus on more informative subjects, thereby reducing study costs while maintaining statistical power.

For survival data, the case-cohort design (e.g., Prentice 1986; Cai and Zeng 2004, Ding et al., 2017) is a commonly used ODS approach, where a random subset of the original cohort (the “subcohort") is sampled alongside all event cases. This design improves efficiency, particularly in studies with low event rates. However, in studies with higher event rates, sampling all failures for exposure assessment can be both unnecessary and resource-intensive, especially when biological samples or other raw materials are limited. Collecting covariate information for every failure may not be the most efficient use of these valuable resources. To address this, more general ODS approaches sample only a subset of failures based on predefined criteria, achieving further cost reductions without sacrificing efficiency. Ding et al. (2014) introduced a general failure-time ODS design in which covariate information is collected for the subcohort, and subsequently, failure times are partitioned into mutually exclusive strata, and additional “supplemental cases" are sampled from each stratum, enriching the dataset by oversampling failures in regions of specific interest.

Statistical inference methods for general failure-time ODS designs are well developed. Ding et al. (2014) proposed a semiparametric empirical likelihood approach for the proportional hazards model. Yu et al. (2015) introduced a weighted pseudo-score method for the semiparametric additive hazards model. Yu et al. (2016) extended ODS inference for the Cox model using a weighted partial likelihood estimator. More recently, Yu et al. (2021) developed an efficient estimator for the accelerated failure time model under an ODS framework. However, limited attention has been given to survival prediction within these designs. Survival prediction is crucial in biomedical research, as it aids clinicians in making informed decisions about treatment strategies, resource allocation, and personalized interventions (Reddy 2015). Developing prediction methods that leverage ODS data has the potential to significantly improve patient outcomes and healthcare efficiency.

Developing survival prediction methods under general failure–time ODS designs presents unique challenges. Standard prediction models are typically developed under simple random sampling assumptions and may not account for the outcome-dependent sampling structure used in ODS studies. Because subjects are sampled with probabilities that depend on their observed outcomes, the resulting analysis dataset no longer represents a simple random sample from the target cohort. This selective sampling can introduce biases into the empirical distribution, and prediction models that ignore the design may in turn target a data distribution that differs from that of the underlying population. Consequently, methods that incorporate the ODS sampling mechanism are desirable for producing predictions that are calibrated to and generalizable to the target cohort. At the same time, flexible machine learning approaches offer substantial advantages in capturing complex covariate effects. Therefore, effective survival prediction under ODS designs benefits from algorithms that combine flexible modeling with principled use of design information to yield robust and reliable predictions.

The gradient boosting machine is an ensemble learning technique that constructs a predictive model by sequentially combining multiple weak learners (e.g.,Freund and Schapire 1996; Friedman 2001; Hastie et al. 2009). Each weak learner reduces the model error of the previous one by optimizing a loss function suited for censored survival data, such as partial likelihood in Cox models (Ridgeway 1999; Binder and Schumacher 2008), discrimination metrics (Chen et al. 2013; Mayr and Schmid 2014), or measures from accelerated failure time models (Schmid and Hothorn 2008a; Johnson and Long 2011). Gradient boosting machines have demonstrated strong performance in modeling complex relationships, handling censoring, and accommodating non-linear effects, making them highly effective for survival prediction.

In this paper, we propose a new gradient boosting machine tailored for survival prediction in general failure-time ODS designs. By adjusting for the biased and informative sampling inherent in ODS, the method offers a robust and generalizable approach to survival prediction. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the proposed gradient boosting machine algorithm and the hyperparameter-tuning procedure, and establishes the convergence of the proposed weighted empirical integrated Brier-score-based risk. Section 3 presents simulation studies that evaluate the method’s finite sample performance. Section 4 demonstrates its application through the Cancer Incidence and Mortality of Uranium Miners Study, which used a general failure-time ODS design. Section 5 concludes with a discussion of the method’s significance, practical implications, and future research directions.

We conduct extensive simulations to evaluate the finite-sample performance of the proposed method under various scenarios. Analysis of the cancer incidence and mortality of uranium miners study

In this section, we apply the proposed method to data from the Cancer Incidence and Mortality of Uranium Miners Study. Uranium miners experience chronic exposure to alpha particles emitted by radon and its progeny, which can cause random damage to chromosomes and DNA molecules in the cell nucleus, leading to carcinogenic effects. The study, conducted from January 1, 1977, to December 31, 1996, aimed to investigate risk factors for radiation-related cancer incidence.

The full cohort consists of 16,434 miners, among whom 2,330 developed cancer, with 1,444 cases of the cancer type of interest (Sandler et al. 1998). We incorporate the general failure-time outcome-dependent sampling (ODS) design described in Yu et al. (2015) and use the study data to predict the incidence of radiation-related cancer. The subcohort includes 1,839 miners. Under such conditions, the proposed gradient boosting machine performs comparably to the Cox model, while still retaining the capacity to capture nonlinear effects when they are present.

Discussion

In this paper, we proposed a gradient boosting machine algorithm for survival prediction under general failure-time ODS designs. Hyperparameter tuning is conducted via cross-validation using a weighted empirical risk based on the integrated Brier Score (equation (2)). Our method fills in a gap in the existing ODS literature by providing a machine learning approach for survival prediction. Additionally, we demonstrate that a Brier Score weighted by the sampling design is effective for hyperparameter tuning, making it a useful tool for other machine learning methods in similar settings.

Simulation studies show that the proposed gradient boosting machine performs comparably to the Cox model in low-dimensional settings (p=10) with linear covariate effects (equation (3)) but significantly outperforms it in nonlinear (equation (4)) and high-dimensional settings (p=200, 400). This advantage arises from two key factors. First, the gradient boosting machine adopts a coordinate-wise approach, selecting one covariate per iteration. As a result, irrelevant covariates are often excluded, reducing susceptibility to the curse of dimensionality. Second, unlike the Cox model, which estimates all coefficients simultaneously, the gradient boosting machine sequentially corrects model errors, improving predictive performance in situations where the Cox model struggles.

From a computational perspective, hyperparameter tuning is critical. Our experience suggests that the learning rate (ν in equation (1)) has minimal impact on predictive performance as long as it remains small (e.g., ν = 0.1). A more important parameter is the number of boosting iterations (M), as excessive iterations can lead to overfitting, degrading prediction accuracy. This aligns with previous findings in the gradient boosting literature (e.g., Schmid and Hothorn 2008b; Bühlmann and Hothorn 2007). We recommend setting ν = 0.1 and tuning M via grid search over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{50,100,500,1000,1500,2000\}$$\end{document}. These values are chosen to balance computational cost and predictive performance, and represent a grid that we have found sufficient to achieve satisfactory performance without requiring extensive tuning.

We also note that in our simulation studies, both the subcohort and the supplemental cases were selected using a Bernoulli sampling scheme. This choice is consistent with the assumptions used in establishing the asymptotic properties of the proposed weighted empirical integrated Brier-score-based risk. In practice, however, general failure-time ODS designs often employ fixed sample sizes for the subcohort and for the supplemental cases. To assess the robustness of our proposed method, we conducted additional simulations comparing Bernoulli sampling with fixed-size sampling. The resulting prediction performance was nearly indistinguishable across the two settings, indicating that the proposed method remains reliable and practically applicable in general failure-time ODS designs commonly encountered in real-world data analyses.

From a design perspective, the choice of strata in general failure-time ODS studies reflects the goal of improving efficiency by focusing on the most informative observations. In particular, failures occurring at the early and late portions of the failure-time distribution tend to be more informative about the relationship between covariates and the outcome, whereas failures in the middle range are often more densely observed in the cohort. As a result, ODS designs typically define wider intervals in the middle region and allocate additional sampling effort to the tails of the distribution, where observations are relatively sparse. This strategy is consistent with the general principle of ODS, where supplemental cases are selected from regions of the outcome space that provide the greatest information gain for a given sampling cost (e.g., Ding et al. 2014; Yu et al. 2015).

The significance of our method lies in its ability to leverage data from failure-time ODS designs for accurate survival prediction, expanding beyond traditional association analyses. This can enhance disease risk prediction, such as for cancers, leading to improved prevention strategies and individualized treatment plans. In precision medicine, our approach enables tailored interventions based on individual risk profiles, enhancing treatment efficacy. More broadly, it supports global health initiatives by optimizing resource allocation and public health strategies.

Although the proposed gradient boosting machine and the Cox model produced similar predictions in our application, this outcome is expected because the analysis involved a relatively low-dimensional set of covariates and the Cox model’s linear covariate–effect assumption appeared adequate. However, many contemporary studies involve high-dimensional data and nonlinear covariate effects, such as genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic measurements, electronic health record data, sensor and wearable data, and other multimodal sources. In these situations, more robust prediction procedures that remain accurate under outcome-dependent sampling are particularly valuable. The proposed gradient boosting machine offers a practical alternative for survival prediction under ODS designs and may provide advantages when data structure is richer or model assumptions are more likely to be violated.

Future research directions include expanding machine learning models to accommodate time-varying covariates in failure-time ODS studies. Additionally, reinforcement learning for survival data (e.g., Cho et al. 2023) presents opportunities to tackle complex epidemiological questions, such as policy evaluation and optimal intervention strategies, making its adaptation to failure-time ODS designs an important avenue for exploration. Moreover, ODS designs have been widely applied beyond survival outcomes (e.g., Zhou et al. 2002; Weaver and Zhou 2005; Zhou et al. 2007; Song et al. 2009; Wang and Zhou 2010; Zhou et al. 2011), highlighting the need for machine learning methods tailored to other ODS settings. Lastly, developing gradient boosting machine methods for additional failure-time ODS designs, including case-cohort (e.g., Prentice 1986; Cai and Zeng 2004, Ding et al., 2017), generalized case-cohort (e.g., Cai and Zeng 2007, Ding et al., 2016), and stratified case-cohort studies (e.g., Hu et al. 2014, Ding et al., 2017), remains an open challenge and a promising direction for future work.
