Prevalence estimates of nonordinary experiences depend on validity and measurement choices
Authors: Ronald Fischer, Giovanna Bortolini, Tiago Bortolini, Everton O. Maraldi, Larissa Hartle, Maria Vitória de Lima Varejão, Maria Clara Laport, Maria Eduarda Oliveira, Elliott Ihm, Ann Taves, Jorge Moll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nonordinary experiences (NOEs) that are subjectively marked as unusual or special have important implications for our understanding of consciousness but are challenging to quantify. Estimates of their prevalence vary widely across disciplines and research traditions, and the reasons for this variation remain unclear. We address this discrepancy in a series of six studies. In study 1, we validated 31 items using descriptive, non-judgmental phrasing of experiences (N = 1,284). In studies 2 to 4, we identified factors contributing to different prevalence rates (total N = 11,628). Study 2 showed that a mental health framing results in lower prevalence rates. Studies 3 to 4 demonstrated that dichotomous response options result in lower prevalence rates. Study 5 (N = 12,912) tested the comparative importance of these factors and showed that validity information (clarity and understandability of items), mental health framing of the study and response formats of items affect absolute point estimates, but relative lifetime prevalence rates can be estimated consistently. Study 6 (N = 11,628) reported the overall relative lifetime prevalence rates indicating that a sense of déjà vu, and unusually intense experiences of compassion and love ( > 70%) were relatively most prevalent; whereas perceptions of animated objects, devotion to objects or individuals, out-of-body experiences and seeing lights were rarer ( > 13%). Nonordinary experiences are common in general population samples, but study design choices obscure accurate prevalence estimates. Our approach enables the systematic identification of these experiences for studying consciousness in real-world contexts.

Introduction

Researchers have long pondered how we experience and reflect on our subjective states1–3. Yet, despite decades of empirical and theoretical advances, lived experience as the core substrate of consciousness remains methodologically elusive. By their very nature, the subjective experiences that constitute our lived experience are not objective recordings of internal or external events; they are interpreted, remembered, and reported through cognitive and social filters4,5. These characteristics make the measurement of nonordinary experiences particularly challenging. While such experiences promise to offer insights into consciousness at its experiential boundaries, researchers who study experiences that diverge sharply from ordinary daily life, such as hearing voices, seeing visions, sensing unseen presences, or experiencing unexpectedly overwhelming emotions6–8, report widely varying estimates of their prevalence7,9–15. Why these estimates vary so widely across disciplines that ostensibly study similar phenomena is unclear. To overcome the fragmented research landscape in which isolated or highly selective sets of experiences are framed via disciplinary lenses (psychiatric, spiritual, cultural) that yield conflicting interpretations and widely varying prevalence estimates, we present a series of pre-registered and exploratory studies designed to disentangle experiential frequency from item validity, framing effects, and stigma-sensitive reporting. Using the Inventory of Nonordinary Experiences (INOE)16 as a broad, participant-centered instrument spanning emotional, cognitive, sensory, and self-related phenomena, we identify factors that help explain the disparate absolute prevalence estimates and offer relative estimates of the prevalence ranges across a wide-range of human experiences that offer insights into the fundamental workings of the human mind and consciousness6,17. In doing so, we point out a prevalence paradox at the heart of the puzzle: Whether an experience is counted depends not only on whether it occurred, but on whether respondents recognize their past experience as matching a question, their interpretation of the question and whether they are willing to disclose the experience given normative cues that are part of any research study. This paradox indicates that measurement is not a neutral conduit but an active component of the phenomenon under investigation.

Nonordinary experiences have been of interest across disciplinary boundaries. This broad interest has led to fragmentation and a noncumulative science, in which selected experiences of interest for a specific academic audience have been variably framed as religious, indicative of extraordinary gifts or abilities, symptoms of clinical or pathological problems, or evidence of paranormal forces7,15. For example, the experience of an extraordinary presence have been framed as religious (e.g., “I feel God’s presence”) within the Daily Spiritual Scale18, as evidence of the paranormal (“I have seen a ghost or apparition”) in the Anomalous Experience Inventory19 or as indicator of schizotypy within the Oxford-Liverpool Inventory of Feelings and Experiences (O-LIFE)20,21 (e.g., “evil presence”). Furthermore, studies have been framed as being about religious or spiritual experiences12, mental health problems9 or paranormal events22, which is likely to influence participants’ willingness to acknowledge and report such experiences7,15.

A number of recent reviews have summarized the shortcomings of existing studies and inventories that used narrowly defined questions which tend to impose cultural or disciplinary interpretations on participants (be it medical, spiritual, or paranormal)7,8,15. The central challenge is to capture the core phenomenological aspects of experiences and express them in a clear, neutral, and non-judgmental form. Our study adopts Taves and Barlev’s participant-centered approach, shifting the focus from the researcher’s disciplinary perspective to the participant’s, enabling a bottom-up, participant-driven reporting process on the phenomenological features. They defined nonordinary experiences as “experiences that stand out to people or are marked by them as special… relative to what they consider ordinary or everyday, without assuming that the ordinary-nonordinary distinction is cross-culturally stable”7. It is arguably the inventory with the broadest list of both subjectively positive and negative experiences of interest to scholars in medicine, neuroscience, psychology, sociology, religious studies, management studies, and anthropology (see Supplementary Table 1). Using a broader and more inclusive definition that focuses on the phenomenological features, it becomes possible to examine variations of human conscious experiences more holistically and point towards features of lived experience that can be more easily queried in future studies.

In order to advance on the main goals of identifying (a) relative prevalence rates of nonordinary experiences and (b) factors that explain the prevalence paradox in previous research, we applied methods to first probe and improve item phrasings that facilitated participants’ understanding of survey content16,23–25. We then systematically varied a number of study features based on previous research in this area, such as (a) the context in which survey questions were presented, b) the implied normativity of the experiences, and (c) the inclusion or exclusion of hedges signaling uncertainty26,27. Comparing the impact of item validity information and these methodological features, we contribute to a discussion of factors that drive highly diverging prevalence rates across different literatures and help explain the prevalence paradox. Finally, summarizing prevalence rates across our studies via a random-effects meta-analysis, we identify more robust and nuanced relative estimates of the lifetime prevalence of these experiences. Ultimately, our approach can be used for probing the boundaries of consciousness in general population samples.

Elaborating on these points, the first challenge for estimating the lifetime prevalence of nonordinary experiences is achieving a neutral and non-judgmental way of measuring the phenotypical core characteristics of the experiences, in a way that is understandable by general population samples. Comprehension and recall of relevant experiences may be facilitated or hindered by the phrasing of survey items to match the local meaning of the experience. For example, an experience being phrased using clinical terminology may not be mapped to relevant experiences if they occur in non-clinical, culturally-salient contexts. Once retrieved, willingness to report can be hindered by perceived (self-)stigma28,29 or methodological biases26,30,31. Previous work has relied on academic or clinical experts to judge item content and appropriateness or used small pilot samples from the target population12,18,21,22, which limits insights into how lay-people may understand and respond to these items that are phenomenologically challenging and prone to misunderstanding.

In Study 1 we validate a Brazilian Portuguese version of a broad inventory of nonordinary experiences16 encompassing a spectrum of emotional, cognitive, sensory, and self-related phenomena that have been of interest across the social, behavioral, and medical sciences7,8,32–34. To do so, we applied the Response Process Evaluation Method24, a mixed-methods approach that systematically assesses how respondents interpret survey items, enabling both verification of intended item meaning and iterative refinement to improve clarity and validity. Engaging participants from the target population highlights the importance of community involvement and lived experience in mental health research35,36, addressing a key limitation of prior work that relied primarily on expert judgment or small pilot samples.

This approach provides both qualitative insights and quantitative estimates (Validity Scores) on the understandability of individual items. Our preregistered protocol specifies that items are considered provisionally validated if at least 80% of participants interpret them as intended. However, even among validated items, residual variation in interpretability remains. We leverage this variation to examine whether differences in item clarity systematically influence prevalence estimates. This constitutes a key contribution of our approach, as little is known about how item-level validity affects response behavior, despite its central role in measurement. Accordingly, our first research question asks whether marginal differences in item validity are associated with systematic shifts in reported prevalence.

Once a suitable validated instrument with clear item meanings is available, a second challenge for estimating prevalence rates is the framing of the study. Contextual framing may heighten perceptions of stigma or self-stigma (i.e., internalized negative evaluations of oneself when having specific experiences), thereby reducing participants’ willingness to report such experiences28,37. In study 2, we test whether mental health framing affects reporting behavior. When survey items are presented alongside content that clearly indicates clinical interpretations, this may increase stigma and reduce prevalence rates. Accordingly, we predict that mental health framing will reduce the prevalence rate overall (Hypothesis 1, see Supplementary Note 1 for complete wording of the pre-registered hypothesis).

In addition to the research framing, a further challenge in surveys on culturally-laden topics is that participants may use information embedded in the question or response formats, which influence response behavior26,38. One response option that may influence response behavior is the presence of hedging options, such as ‘I don’t know’ responses. Hedging might occur for several reasons. Affirming that one has had the experience may result in (self-)stigmatizing responses28. Hedging may also be a result of subjective uncertainty about the nature of the experience or uncertain memories about events, which may increase doubts about whether a personal experience qualifies as relevant for providing an affirmative answer26. Therefore, we predicted that the availability of hedging options would decrease the prevalence rates (Hypothesis 2, not preregistered).

Prevalence rates may also be affected by the number of response options. The original inventory has a yes vs no response format, which is more typical in the clinical literature26,38 When confronted with a binary response choice, there is little information available to individuals to detect whether such experiences are frequent. Using a frequency response scale communicates expectations about their prevalence and the implied normativity of having such experiences9,21,39. This is of great importance, because clinical studies reporting lower prevalence rates often use binary yes-no response options, whereas social or spiritual studies that tend to report higher prevalence rates tend to use Likert-type scales focusing on frequency (rather than presence or absence)27,40. We therefore experimentally tested in Study 3 and Study 4 how different response formats influence prevalence estimates. We expected that frequency information communicated via the response scale increases prevalence rates (Hypothesis 3, not preregistered). Building on those insights from Study 3, we then tested whether individuals use information about the implied frequency strategically26,31 when responding, which may be detected via the implied frequency of the response scales. For example, higher frequency rates available via the response scale may indicate greater normativity, therefore shifting prevalences upwards overall. Or, relatively rare experiences may be too uncommon to be influenced by frequency information, whereas moderately common experiences may be more sensitive to frequency information. People may have first- or secondhand information about more common experiences, which may make response behavior more susceptible to the manipulated frequency response scale. We experimentally manipulated the relative frequency to study the sensitivity of norm effects. We predict that higher implied frequency communicated via the response scale shifts prevalence rates upwards (Hypothesis 4, see Supplementary Note 1 for the full wording of this hypothesis). Furthermore, we expect that these effects are nonlinear, with effects of the manipulated frequency of the response scale more pronounced for experiences that are more prevalent (Hypothesis 5, see Supplementary Note 1 for a full wording of this hypothesis).

To bring these different variables together and to explore which of the variables included in our studies are particularly important for explaining the prevalence paradox, in study 5 we explicitly test the relative importance of validity information at item level, study framing and response format options, while controlling for all other methodological factors (research question 2 in study 5). Finally, in study 6 our aim is to identify the relative ordering of nonordinary experiences across all studies and to obtain insights into their relative prevalence rates in a general population sample.

In summary, in Study 1, we present validation of descriptive, non-judgmental items to capture nonordinary experiences. To address widely varying prevalence rate estimates, in Study 2, we test framing effects, predicting that mental health framing reduces prevalence rates (Hypothesis 1). In studies 2 to 4, we test methodological choices affecting response behavior. We predict that options allowing response hedging (e.g., ‘I don’t know’ responses) decrease affirmative responses (Hypothesis 2) and that frequency responses scales compared to binary response options increase affirmative responses (Hypothesis 3). Specifically, we also predict that participants use information strategically, with greater implied normativity increasing affirmative responses (Hypothesis 4), and that such effects may be nonlinear depending on relative prevalence rates in the general population (Hypothesis 5). In study 5, we explore whether validity information at the item level influences affirmative responses (research question 1) and comparatively test effects of item validity, research framing, and methodological choices on affirmative responses (research question 2). In the final study, we examine the relative prevalence rates across studies 2 to 4 to provide an overall estimate of nonordinary experiences across a wider range of sensory and cognitive features, and offer insights into the relative frequency of alterations or perturbations of consciousness1,41,42.

Methods

Samples

We recruited participants from the general population via an online panel provider (Netquest; www.netquest.com). For the first study43, 4,936 participants were recruited, of whom 1,284 were retained for analysis after excluding 3,652 cases because of nonsensical responses (for example, “fhasdjfasd”), responses indicating protocol non-compliance (for example, copied material from websites or irrelevant text), or empty responses. Among the retained participants, sex information was available for 1270 individuals: 722 (57%) self-identified as female and 548 (43%) as male. The mean age was 42 years (SD ± 13). Data collection occurred from December 2023 to January 2025. For Study 243, we first recruited 2311 participants from the general Brazilian population, of whom 1,652 were retained for analysis. We excluded 108 participants who failed the embedded attention check, 522 who discontinued the study, and 26 who did not provide consent. Of the retained participants, 493 (29.8%) identified as female and 1,159 (70.2%) as male. The mean age was 47 years (SD ± 16). We then collected an additional subsample of 542 participants. We excluded 9 participants because they did not consent, 83 because they discontinued the study, and 24 because they failed the attention check, resulting in 425 included participants. Combining this additional subsample with a subset of the initial sample, the sample analyzed in the preregistered part of the study included 1242 participants, with a mean age of 49 (SD age ± 15); 450 participants (36%) self-reported female and 792 (64%) self-reported male. In study 343, we recruited 19,016 participants from the general population. Of these, a total of 4,909 discontinued the questionnaire, 6,625 failed the embedded attention check, and 329 did not consent to participate, resulting in a final analytic sample of 7,516 individuals. The mean age was 44 years (SD age ± 15); 3,664 (49%) self-reported as female and 3,852 (51%) self-reported as male. In study 443, we recruited 7,808 participants from the general population. Of these, 2,340 discontinued the questionnaire, 3,237 failed the attention check, and 183 did not consent to participate, resulting in a final analytic sample of 2,035 participants. The mean age was 42 (SD ± 15); 1,135 (56%) self-reported as female and 900 (44%) self-reported as male. Study 5 used the combined sample from studies 1 to 4 (total N = 12,912), and study 6 the combined sample from studies 2 to 4 (total N = 11,628). We did not collect data on race or ethnicity.

Preregistrations

The study and research protocol for study 1 were preregistered on 10 January 2023 on Open Science Framework (OSF; 10.17605/OSF.IO/ZE6CB, with an updated version posted on 30 June 2023 in which we updated and clarified changes in the classification of item responses as well as updates to the appraisal section (which is not relevant for the current analyses). Study 2 had a preregistered component focusing on the comparison of the mental health vs personality framing. This part was preregistered on 24 June 2024 on the Open Science Framework (OSF; 10.17605/OSF.IO/PA3UT). We report the most complex multilevel analyses specified in the preregistration, because it tests the research question in the most comprehensive format while accounting for the nesting of responses within participants. The study protocol for study 3 was not preregistered. Building on the results from study 3, study 4 was preregistered on 6 December 2024 on the Open Science Framework (OSF; 10.17605/OSF.IO/UKVAP). Studies 5 and 6 were exploratory, we did not preregister the research question or protocol.

Ethical approval

The research project was approved by the D’Or Institute for Research and Education Research Ethics Committee in Brazil (CAAE: 65573322.6.0000.5249 for study 1, CAAE: 79141724.9.0000.5249 for studies 2 to 6). Participants were not paid for participation. All participants gave online informed consent for participation.

Instrument

We used the 38 item version of the Inventory for Nonordinary Experiences16 (INOE). The experience items were translated using a parallel committee approach44,45, with two independent groups of translators. We used the intended item interpretations16 as a reference to resolve translation problems. Any translation discrepancies were discussed until a consensus was reached.

Validation study procedure, validity criteria and item modification procedures (Study 1)

We used the Response Process Evaluation (RPE)24, which involves a series of probing questions to query whether participants had understood an item as intended. Using iterative online meta-surveys, participants were presented with two INOE experience items and asked for item understanding via a set of probing questions. In the supplement, we report information on the adaptation of the probing process to our study context and the full list of probing questions (see Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Methods).

One researcher (LH) assessed data quality. We removed: a) nonsensical responses (e.g., “fhasdjfasd”), b) responses indicating protocol non-compliance (e.g., copying text from websites, providing irrelevant information), and c) empty responses.

Validity criteria

Responses were scored on a 5-point scale (1 = Understood to 5 = Not Understood). The intended interpretations16 were used as guidance for classifications. We created validity criteria to enhance transparency and efficiency in the classification process (see Fig. 1). We first collapsed the five-point codes into tripartite classification (Understood vs Not Understood vs Unclear). We then used three rules for classification. First, if there was a 75% majority decision (3 out of 4 coders agree on the classification of a response), the majority choice prevails (majority rule). Second, in case of an even split between the coders team and no majority for either understood or not understood, the response was classified as unclear (not enough information available, 50:50 rule). To judge an item as preliminary validated, the overall percentage for the VS had to be 80% or higher in a sample of at least 20 individuals who provided valid responses.

Item modification criteria

If an item did not meet the 80% criterion, we developed a guide to the adaptation process (Fig. 1). If an item reached a VS score of at least 60% in a sample of at least 10 individuals, the item was not modified and re-evaluated in a new batch of 5 to 10 respondents. If the level of understanding was below 60% in a sample of at least 10 respondents, the item wording was re-evaluated and adapted to improve clarity by taking into consideration the available item responses. Responses by participants were used to revise the items to improve clarity and understandability, which was then tested again. Due to time and financial constraints, we decided to test a maximum of 5 different wordings (or less, if there were no clear alternatives for improved item wording).

Experimental Procedures (Studies 2 to 4)

Study Framing Effects (Study 2)

In study 2, participants were randomly assigned to either one of two conditions: (a) mental health context vs. neutral experience context and (b) response options with hedging (I don’t know option) available or not. The mental health context was manipulated by presenting a screening tool for Generalized Anxiety47 and Depression severity48,49 as well as mental health questions (life satisfaction, loneliness) before experience items. In the neutral control condition, the experience items were presented first. For the manipulation of hedging options, participants were randomly assigned to either a yes vs no response option condition or to a condition which included a hedging option (“I don’t know”). The data was analyzed with a multilevel logistic regression on the yes-responses for each of the experiences with a random intercept for participants using the lme4 (version 1.1-35.5)50 and lmerTest (version 3.1-3)51 packages in R (version 4.4.1)52. We added a further condition in which participants were first presented with a short version of a non-clinical personality measure53 before responding to the experience items. In this condition, individuals were given the yes vs no response scale only and no randomization was used (participants who participated in the first part of the study could not respond again). The hypotheses and analyses of this analysis were preregistered. We report the most comprehensive multilevel logistic regression with random intercepts for participants. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Response Scale (Implied Normativity and Response Hedging) Effects (Study 3 & 4)

In Study 3, participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: (1) a binary yes-no (have had the experience or not), (2) a binary yes vs no response with the presence of a hedging (I don’t know) option or 3) a six point frequency response scale, that varied from ‘Never’, ‘Once’, ‘2–3 times’, ‘4–5 times’, ‘6–10 times’ or ‘More than 10 times’. We coded the lifetime prevalence rates as the number of yes responses for the first two conditions and any response indicating that the individual has had the experience once or more in the third condition. We performed a multilevel ordinal regression analysis on the yes-response with a random effect for participants using the lme4 (version 1.1-35.5)50 and lmerTest (version 3.1-3)51 packages in R (version 4.4.1)52. Condition and experience items were treated as categorical variables, and we included interaction effects between condition and experience item to explore possible experience-specific effects. The intercept was set post-hoc to the highest prevalence rate. Omnibus effects were evaluated using a significance threshold of p < 0.001, and Bonferroni correction was applied to control family-wise error for the post-hoc tests. Follow-up tests on the relative and absolute stability of lifetime prevalence rates were conducted at the population level. We examined the degree to which the rank order of item lifetime prevalence rates was preserved across conditions by a) computing pairwise Spearman rank-order correlations and b) assessing the consistency of individual items ordinal positions. Confidence intervals for correlations were obtained via bootstrapping and for the ranks via calculation of standard deviations of ordinal ranks for each item. This study was exploratory and not preregistered.

In Study 4, we randomly assigned participants to one of three conditions: (1) a binary response condition with yes vs no responses, (2) a low-frequency response condition with responses varying in six steps between ‘never’ and up to ‘10 times or more’ in one’s life time, or (3) a high-frequency response condition varying in six steps from ‘never’ to up to ‘100 times or more’ in one’s life time. We coded the lifetime prevalence for each experience as the response to yes or any response that indicated that the individual has had the experience at least once. Lifetime prevalence was coded as 1 and responses indicating that an individual never had the experience as 0. To test the effects of response format on reported prevalence, we fitted a multilevel logistic regression model with lifetime prevalence as the dependent variable, response format as a fixed effect (with the binary condition as the reference category), and random intercepts for both participants and experience items using the lme4 (version 1.1-35.5)50 and lmerTest (version 3.1-3)51 packages in R (version 4.4.1)52.

To explore linear and nonlinear base rate effects, we rescaled the prevalence rates in the yes vs no binary response condition so that the minimum lifetime prevalence for the lowest prevalence experience was set to zero. Both the linear and quadratic effects of this rescaled base rate were included as a predictor in the multilevel logistic regression model. The experimental condition was entered as a categorical predictor and the interaction between response condition and the linear and quadratic base rates were included. Random intercepts were included for both participants and items. Statistical significance for the omnibus tests was defined as p < 0.001. We controlled family-wise error rates for post-hoc effects with Bonferroni correction. To further assess whether the absence of interaction effects masked condition-specific shifts in item-level prevalence, we calculated rank order correlations between the three response conditions using item-level prevalence rates. We computed Spearman rank-order correlations for each pair of conditions and derived the mean correlation across comparisons. These analyses were pre-registered.

Additionally, to evaluate potential deviations in rank consistency at the item level (this analysis was not preregistered), we calculated the standard deviation of each item’s rank across conditions. To assess the distributional properties of these deviations, we conducted Shapiro Wilk tests for normality. Because the data was normally distributed, we used a parametric cutoff, defined as two standard deviations above the mean rank standard deviation, to flag potential outlier items.

Comparative assessment of validity information (Study 5)

First, we meta-analytically summarized the associations between the different validity scores and prevalence rates across samples. To estimate the association between (a) the overall validations core, (b) the positive proportion understood (PPU), (c) negative proportion understood (NPU) and (d) proportion of unclear participant responses during the validation stage with the relative prevalence rates in each study and condition, we computed Spearman correlations at the study level for each experimental condition. These correlations were then r-to-z transformed and we computed a random effects meta-analysis model with sample size as weights with the metafor package (version 4.8-0)54 in R (version 4.4.1)52.

Second, to compare the relative importance of the methodological parameters identified in our studies, we computed a multilevel logistic regression model using the lme4 (version 1.1-35.5)50 and lmerTest (version 3.1-3)51 packages in R (version 4.4.1)52. We included the significant parameters from the meta-analysis as well as the main effects of mental health vs personality framing, availability of hedging and general effects of normative effects (combining the low and high frequency conditions) using the total sample size from all studies (total N = 11,628). Statistical significance for the omnibus tests was defined as p < 0.001. We included a random effect for participants and adjusted effects via Bonferroni corrections. This analysis was exploratory and not pre-registered.

Overall prevalence rate estimation

We conducted a random effects meta-analysis using REML for each experience within the metafor package (version 4.8-0)54 in R (version 4.4.1)52. We pooled the percentage of yes responses (e.g., individuals report having had the experience at least once in their life) for each study weighted by sample size. We computed average effect sizes as well as heterogeneity effects. To estimate the overall prevalence of these nonordinary experiences, we pooled the estimates across our reported studies here using sample-size weighted effects. This analysis was exploratory and not pre-registered.

Results

Study 1 - Validation of Experience Items

Using a criterion of 80% of responses being classified as understood in a sample of at least 20 responses16, we found sufficient evidence of validity for 31 items from the original list of 38 items in our population. Ten items had to be revised to be understood by participants. Among the non-validated items, experiences asking about events that explicitly involve meaning making processes (e.g., receiving messages, deep insights) were more difficult to validate (Supplementary Table 3). We elaborated on the cultural and linguistic context that may have contributed to problems in understandability in the supplementary material (see Supplementary Note 2). These analyses suggested that attention to items that show validity problems can provide insights into both cultural characteristics and local meaning-making processes driven by universal cognitive dynamics25. Furthermore, seven items (déjà vu, love, places (special), pleasure, animated places, meaning in life, faces) passed the VS but not the NPU (Fig. 2), which suggests13 that individuals who did not report the experience during validation might have misunderstood the items. This could lead to underestimating such experiences in the general population samples. Study 3– Response Scale (Implied Normativity) Effects and Response Hedging

Compared to the binary format, participants in the frequency response condition reported substantially more affirmative responses (OR = 4.68, SE = 0.44, 95% CI [3.88–5.64], p < .001), indicating higher estimated lifetime prevalence. This supports our third hypothesis. The comparison of the binary response with hedging option did not show any significant result after Bonferroni correction (OR = 1.24, SE ± .10, 95% CI = 1.045–1.468, puncorrected = .013, pbonferroni = 1.00), which again does not support our second hypothesis. Overall, the frequency effect compared to the binary response option with the availability of a hedging option was substantively larger z = 18.418, 95% CI [0.598, 0.772], p < .001.

We also observed 29 significant interactions between specific items and response conditions (see Supplementary Table 7). These interactions suggest subtle shifts across experiences, which point towards differences in absolute vs relative lifetime frequency effects. Absolute prevalence rates indicate the absolute number of individuals reporting a given experience, whereas relative prevalence rates focus on the ordering of prevalences, that is, whether certain experiences are relatively more common than others in a sample. The two estimates may not coincide. For example, while absolute rates may vary dramatically, the relative rank order might be well preserved, suggesting an overall response shift to respond affirmatively (or negatively) across all experiences. Alternatively, variability in absolute rates may be relatively minor, but relative rates may vary more widely due to factors such as differential stigma associated with specific experiences only. Such differences will be important for both research (e.g., prevalence of nonordinary experiences for studying features of consciousness) as well as practical purposes (planning of mental health service needs, etc.).

The relative ordering of the lifetime prevalence rates was well preserved when comparing the rank order correlations, see Supplementary Table 8. To ensure that this correlation was not masking differences in rank positions for individual items, we computed the variability of the ordinal positions across the conditions (see Supplementary Table 9). The average mean rank varied by 1.74 points (SD ± 1.31; range = 0.00–5.77), suggesting that most items maintained a consistent position across formats. We identified one outlier (Misfortune item; SD ± 5.77).

At the same time, the relative ordering of experiences in the population is preserved. Given the importance of absolute prevalence rates for clinical purposes of determining diagnosis rates and estimating unmet needs, our findings suggest that binary scales may lead to lower absolute estimates. In contrast, researchers interested in the mechanisms of nonordinary experiences as windows into consciousness are more likely to be interested in the relative prevalence rates: Items that elicit higher endorsement in one format show similar rank positions in the other formats, despite differences in absolute response rates.

Study 5 – The Relative Importance of Item Validity, Framing and Response Options

First, we explored the covariation between validity information from study 1 with prevalence rates in studies 2 to 4. We observed reliable associations between validity information and prevalence rates across all studies (see Supplementary Fig. 14). Specifically, items with a higher VS score (r = 0.10, 95%CI −0.01; 0.21, p = 0.0621), higher PPU (r = 0.15, 95%CI 0.04; 0.25, p < 0.001), lower NPU (r = -0.31, 95% CI −0.41, −0.21, p < 0.001) and lower number of unclassified ambivalent responses (r = −0.39, 95% CI −0.47, −0.29, p < 0.001) showed higher endorsement rates. This answers our first research question: higher validity is associated with greater endorsement, or inversely, if an item is phrased in unclear or ambiguous terms, the prevalence rate is lower.

To answer our second research question on the relative impact or contribution of the significant validity factors vis-a-vis measurement effects, the multilevel logistic regression analysis showed that all predictors were significant (p < 0.05), except for the availability of hedging options (see Supplementary Table 15A, Supplementary Table 15B reports results with VS instead of PPU and NPU). Importantly, higher NPU values (OR = 0.402, SE ± 0.009, 95% CI 0.385–0.419, p < 0.001) and higher proportions of unclear responses during the validation process (OR = 0.011, SE ± 0.000, 95% CI 0.010–0.011, p < 0.001) were associated with lower prevalence rates, whereas higher PPU values were associated with increased prevalence rates (OR = 20.399, SE ± 1.062, 95% CI 18.419–22.591, p < 0.001). Returning to our first research question, variability in validity parameters is systematically associated with prevalence estimates, even when controlling other design features. More clearly phrased items show higher prevalence rates overall, especially if those experiences seem to be more frequent (PPU effect, avoiding false negatives). At the same time, the NPU effect also suggested that more clearly formulated and understandable items help protect against false positives. Vague items on rare events increase prevalence rates, whereas vague items about more frequent events diminish prevalence rates. Concerning our second research question, validity information followed by mental health framing showed the strongest effects. Frequency effects were significant but showed smaller odds ratios.

Roughly a third of the experiences are reported by more than half of the population, with the lower confidence intervals for these experiences above the 50% mark. In this group, emotional and cognitively driven experiences predominate. In contrast, experiences that are relatively less frequent overall tend to include altered sensory states. Notably, even the rarest experience relative to all other experiences (“experiencing animated objects”) occurred in approximately 18% of participants (range 13–24%). Some altered states of consciousness (several sensory hallucinations, changes in the perception of self, etc.) have been included as symptoms of clinical conditions (e.g., psychosis, schizophrenia, and dissociative disorders). Our findings suggest that such experiences, despite appearing unusual, are relatively frequent in the general population. We observed relatively high rates of feeling touched (range: 30–48%), feeling guided by a force (range: 27–55%), feeling the presence of a force (range: 30–57%), or extrasensory perception (range: 41–55%). Some of these experiences have been included as symptoms in clinical manuals. Our results highlight that without additional information on an individual’s health and well-being, pathological explanations of extraordinary experiences are not justified. Further underscoring this point, between 97.57% (study 2) and 99.5% (study 3) of participants report at least one experience within their lifetime. Nonordinary experiences stand out not because they are infrequent, but rather because they escape people’s usual meaning-making frameworks.

Discussion

Our study shows that it is possible to obtain consistent relative lifetime prevalence estimates using locally validated items focusing on phenomenological features16 of experiences. These experiences have fascinated scholars for millennia but have remained difficult to estimate due to methodological challenges. Advancing on the ‘prevalence paradox’, we identified validity information and mental health framing as core features that influence prevalence rates. Focusing on the relative frequencies of these effects, it is noteworthy that (a) most individuals have experienced at least one nonordinary experience and (b) a substantive number of seemingly unusual experiences are common to very common on average in this population. We highlight three key take-home messages for estimating lifetime prevalence rates in future studies, before addressing substantive questions about these experiences.

First, it is important to carefully test item understanding in target populations24. Researcher-designed questions may not align with participants’ interpretations, leading to inaccurate estimates. Our results demonstrate that validity information was associated with lifetime prevalence estimates across more than 10,000 participants, which can have wide-ranging implications. For example, feeling extremely devoted to other people has been primarily studied in ritualistic and religious settings, but this experience is also relevant for politics (strong attachment to political leaders or groups57,58), management59,60 or clinical work (strong attachment in relationships61). Our NPU data suggests misinterpretations of such items could significantly underestimate true prevalence rates.

Second, the utilized response scale substantially influences prevalence estimates. Clinical research has favored binary lifetime prevalence rates, whereas social science prefers a frequency response scale. The two formats lead to diverging estimates on lifetime prevalence. Consistent with cognitive theories, participants likely interpret response formats as implicit cues about researchers’ assumptions26,27,31,38. The discrepancy between binary vs frequency scales might account for a substantive portion of the gap observed between previous studies9,11 (see Supplementary Note 3 for relevant calculations).

Third, framing and study context affect prevalence rates. We used a very subtle framing by presenting both positively and negatively framed mental health questions first, which led to a substantive reduction in prevalence rates. More explicit framing via consent forms or instructions may explain the lower prevalence rates observed in clinical studies9,62–65. Considering that clinically focused surveys are framed in a mental health context, often use binary response scales, and may use pathological framing of experiences, this could lead to unexamined interactive effects which may further suppress responses. Estimates of lifetime prevalence are certainly not independent of the research context, and this needs more attention23,66.

The preservation of relative prevalence, on the other hand, offers insights into the underlying dynamics of mind-culture interactions. Experiences that are more common in a population show higher prevalence rates than rarer experiences, relatively independently of the research context. Researchers often narrowly focus on specific sets of experiences, be it for clinical, spiritual or paranormal research questions. Our results suggest that it is beneficial to include a broader set of experiences to obtain insights into the relative prevalence rates within a population. Point estimates of single or conceptually narrow sets of experiences remain ambiguous.

Focusing on specific prevalence rates, positive emotional experiences, relatively innocuous cognitive lapses (e.g., experiences of déjà vu and absorption), and lucid dreaming were found to be more highly prevalent. Déjà vu was the most commonly reported experience, supporting previous work implying that familiarity-based recognition errors may be frequent and relatively harmless67,68. Given its lower NPU score, actual prevalence rates of dejà vu might be even higher. Lucid dreams were equally common, in line with some previous estimates69. Absorption, defined here cognitively as losing track of time during task engagement, was also relatively prevalent. Though interpretations of absorption are diverse, ranging from conditions of flow or creativity to fantasy proneness and disposition to experience altered states of consciousness70,71, our measure focused on its cognitive aspect, without implying altered states of consciousness. These three most commonly reported experiences centrally involve cognitive functions (attention and memory in particular), suggesting that objectively faulty cognitive processes are salient features of human cognition. Given their high prevalence, such experiences constitute important elements of people’s experiential repertoire and imply that alterations of awareness are a central feature of human consciousness. This observation of common states of changed awareness has implications for theoretical proposition that consciousness and awareness are intertwined1.

Other more frequently reported experiences (relative prevalence around 70%) included emotional experiences that ‘stand out’, such as compassion, love, joy, and pleasure. This raises questions about individuals who do not report these emotions. What is it like not having had an outstanding or highly remarkable experience of love, joy, or pleasure that clearly stands out? The prevalence of experiences such as compassion also aligns with arguments that positive emotions that connect individuals are likely a fundamental feature of human sociality, and without widespread other-focused emotional experiences, human societies would not function72.

The high prevalence of hopelessness is equally noteworthy, given its status as a depression marker73,74. These high levels point to unmet needs in the community, given the overall rates of depression75–77, but may also imply the importance of resilience and post-traumatic growth78, which allows individuals to overcome and learn from these experiences. All humans will suffer major setbacks at some point, which shifts the focus to identifying effective mechanisms to overcome challenges.

Focusing on relatively rare experiences, even the least frequent experiences were reported by about one in five individuals. Some experiences involving alterations of sensory perception or self-awareness (e.g., touch, guidance, presence, extrasensory perceptions, hearing voices) were even more frequent, reported by at least 30% or more of the population.

These findings raise interesting questions about the interaction between human perception, the environment, and human development. Frequent misalignments between the sensory system and the physical world require explanation, and supernatural or spiritual explanatory frameworks might offer intuitive and easier interpretations. In other words, the high prevalence of perceptual anomalies could provide the fertile ground for the ‘kindling’ and spread of nonphysical, supernatural, and spiritual explanations, potentially contributing to the universal presence of religious systems across human cultures17,79.

Limitations

Our study advances on previous research by using non-judgmental and descriptive items that have been validated in the same population prior to further estimates of prevalence rates. This mitigates the risks of researcher-imposed meanings to some extent. However, our sample was derived from an online panel, and the first study was more highly educated than the general population, which may lead to misclassifications of item responses. Similarly, our coders were extensively trained and were familiar with the cultural context of participants, but may have missed important cultural information embedded in answers that may challenge the classifications as understood or not understood. These issues are common in this area of research. Our validation process, including the RPE method, advances replicability and transparency of the validation process. The anonymized responses are available and can be independently classified by researchers to help identify any hidden researcher-driven biases. We strongly encourage replications and further cross-cultural validations to improve validity of items in this sensitive area of research. We also strongly recommend analyses of the comprehensiveness of nonordinary experiences. The current list is by no means exhaustive or comprehensive, and more research is needed to systematically explore experiences that fit into the definition of nonordinary experiences within and across cultural boundaries. Such a research program would be most important for studying consciousness on a global scale. At the same time, more systematic research is needed on what differentiates clinically relevant nonordinary experiences from relatively innocuous experiences. Our study is mute on the mental health implications of having had specific experiences, and this is a major area for further exploration, given the reported mental health impacts7,9–15.

Conclusions

In summary, we propose that extracting common phenomenological features of subjective experiences and estimating their relative prevalence in a general population is possible. We identified factors that help resolve the ‘prevalence paradox’ across different disciplines and research studies. To overcome the prevalence paradox, greater attention must be paid to measurement, beginning with the careful validation of items to ensure understanding in the target population. In the supplement, we discuss how further insights into the intersection between cognition and culture can be gained by qualitatively analyzing items that are confusing for participants. By utilizing this mixed-methods approach on subjective first-person accounts, we take a significant step toward establishing a rigorous science of human experiences that can be further queried using neuroscientific methods. To understand human consciousness, especially in the context of unique and nonordinary subjective experiences, we need to be able to question personal experiences in a scientific way, moving from subjective and idiographic noncomparable impressions to sample-level perspectives that allow generalizations about shared features. Our report offers one pathway in this larger scientific quest.
