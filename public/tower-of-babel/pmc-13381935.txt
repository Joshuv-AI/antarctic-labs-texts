Comparative genomic and physiological characterization of a lichen-associated Streptacidiphilus strain from Antarctica
Authors: Yun Ho Roh, Jehyun Jeon, Woohyun Kim, Se Jong Han, Seung Chul Shin, Bo Young Byun, Jun Hyuck Lee, Yung Mi Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Streptacidiphilus sp. PAMC 29251 represents the first member of its genus isolated from an Antarctic fruticose lichen Sphaerophorus globosus. To elucidate its ecological, metabolic, and biosynthetic features, we performed comparative genomic analyses in combination with physiological assays. PAMC 29251 exhibited distinct ecophysiological features compared with previously described Streptacidiphilus species, including the ability to grow at 4°C and across a broad alkaline range (pH 6.5–10.0). Genome sequencing revealed a 10.11 Mb linear chromosome containing 9,547 coding sequences, including 31 biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs)—one of the richest repertoires within the genus. Several BGCs, including those putatively encoding lomofungin, maduralactomycin, and thiolactomycin, were detected only in PAMC 29251 among 14 Streptacidiphilus genomes investigated in this study. Consistent with these predictions, both cell and supernatant extracts showed antimicrobial activity against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Candida albicans. Although PAMC 29251 lacked nitrogen fixation and phosphate solubilization, it possessed the capacity to hydrolyze multiple macromolecules and encoded diverse proteases and carbohydrate-degrading enzymes, suggesting a potential role in organic matter turnover within the lichen thallus. These results indicate that PAMC 29251 maintains the core genomic architecture of the genus Streptacidiphilus while exhibiting differentiated physiological traits and biosynthetic features, which may be relevant to its functional characteristics in lichen-associated environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52595-4.

Introduction

The genus Streptacidiphilus comprises Gram-positive, aerobic chemoorganotrophs that share many morphological and phenotypic characteristics with members of the genus Streptomyces and form a phylogenetically close sister clade within the phylum Actinomycetota. Since the first description of Streptacidiphilus albus from acidic forest soil1, the genus has expanded to include 20 validly named species (https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/streptacidiphilus), with genome sequences available for 14 type strains. Members of this genus have predominantly been isolated from low-pH terrestrial environments, including acidic coal mine waste, rhizosphere soils, pine grove or forest soils, or have been obtained under acidic cultivation conditions from pumpkin roots, and spruce forest soils1–7. Most species exhibit optimal growth under moderately acidic conditions, reflecting the ecological specialization of the genus toward acidophilic niches. However, several species—including Streptacidiphilus alkalitolerans, S. anmyonensis, S. fuscans, S. monticola, and S. jeojiensis— can tolerate alkaline conditions up to pH 9–10, indicating that the ecological and physiological range of the genus is broader than previously recognized2,6–9.

Although Streptacidiphilus is phylogenetically close to Streptomyces, one of the most major producers of antibiotics and other bioactive secondary metabolites, its chemical diversity remains underexplored compared with that of Streptomyces. To date, most chemical and genomic studies on Actinomycetota have primarily focused on Streptomyces, whereas research on Streptacidiphilus has largely centered on taxonomic descriptions10. Only a limited number of secondary metabolites, including spirotetronate-type polyketides, autophagy-inhibitory acidiphilamides, cyclic dipeptides, and indole derivatives, have been reported from this genus11–13. Nevertheless, genome-level comparisons of 11 Streptacidiphilus species revealed their genomes harbor diverse biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) encoding secondary metabolites with a broad range of predicted bioactivities, including antibacterial, antifungal, antimalarial, and antitumor activities14. Notably, some of these BGCs were found to be exclusive to Streptacidiphilus, highlighting a significant gap between genomic potential and experimentally characterized metabolites14.

Lichens are long-lived and highly structured symbiotic systems composed primarily of fungi and photosynthetic partners and are distributed across a wide range of terrestrial environments, from tropical to polar regions15. In extreme habitats, particularly in polar ecosystems, lichens experience strong fluctuations in temperature, hydration, nutrient availability, and ultraviolet radiation16–19. Bacterial communities inhabiting lichen thalli are considered to play significant roles in lichen survival and adaptation to extreme conditions, and lichens are therefore increasingly being revisited as ‘holobionts’ with diverse microbiomes, including members of the phylum Actinomycetota19–22. Culture-dependent approaches have further demonstrated that lichens harbor a high diversity of cultivable Actinomycetota spanning diverse genera and families, including a substantial proportion of putatively novel taxa18,19,22–29. In particular, lichen-derived Streptomyces strains have been reported to produce structurally unique and biologically active secondary metabolites, such as uncialamycin, cladoniamides, and a 1,1-dichlorocyclopropane-containing angucycline, highlighting lichens as promising reservoirs for metabolically versatile Actinomycetota19,25,30–33. Expanding the taxonomic scope of lichens as sources of bioactive actinomycetes beyond Streptomyces, strain PAMC 29251, previously identified as Streptacidiphilus carbonis based on 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity, was isolated from the Antarctic fruticose lichen Sphaerophorus globosus, representing the first lichen-associated member of the genus19. Previous chemical investigations revealed that PAMC 29251 produces multiple secondary metabolites, indicating that several BGCs are functionally active13. The strain exhibited psychrotolerant growth and diverse enzymatic activities, including protease and nucleobase hydrolysis19. Despite its unique ecological position, comprehensive investigations addressing the genomic features, biosynthetic capacity, and potential ecological roles of PAMC 29251 in lichen remain limited.

In this study, we first clarified the taxonomic position of strain PAMC 29251 based on genome-scale analyses. We then performed a comprehensive comparative genomic analysis with type strains within the same genus to identify metabolic and biosynthetic features specific to strain PAMC 29251. In addition, physiological characteristics, including growth conditions, macromolecule-degrading activities, and antimicrobial activity, were investigated to functionally validate genomic predictions and to gain insights into the potential roles within lichens.

Material and methods

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

Strain PAMC 29251 of the genus Streptacidiphilus was isolated from the Antarctic lichen Sphaerophorus globosus (Huds.) collected at Barton Peninsula, King George Island, Antarctica, as previously described by Noh et al.19. The strain was obtained from the Polar and Alpine Microbial Collection (PAMC), Korea Polar Research Institute34. The strain was routinely cultivated on R2A agar medium and incubated at 10°C.

Identification

For confirmation of the taxonomic affiliation of strain PAMC 29251 based on 16S rRNA gene sequences, the 16S rRNA gene sequence (MW507713) was compared with type strains available in the EzBioCloud database35 to find closely related species, and to choose reference sequences for the phylogenetic analyses. Phylogenetic trees of the 16S rRNA gene sequences were reconstructed by the maximum likelihood (ML), maximum parsimony (MP), and neighbor-joining (NJ) using the MEGA 6.06 program36.

To determine the taxonomic position of strain PAMC 29251 based on the genomes, genomic DNA was extracted using MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced using the PacBio RS II (Pacific BioSciences) sequencing platform with MagBead OneCellPerWell v1 Protocol (Insert Sizes 20 kb, movie time 1 x 240 min). De novo assembly was performed using the NextDenovo assembler (v.2.5.2)37, including polishing with NextPolish. Contig structures were examined using MUMmer 3.538, and overlapping terminal regions were identified and trimmed to ensure manual genome closure. To assess the linear structure, read coverage was examined across the contig, including both terminal regions (1–1,000 and 10,095,912–10,096,911) and a central region (5,047,956–5,048,955). Pairwise genome relatedness was assessed using average nucleotide identity (ANI), calculated with OrthoANI (v.140)39, and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH), estimated using the Genome-to-Genome Distance calculator (GGDC) (version 3.0, DSMZ; https://ggdc.dsmz.de/ggdc.php#)40.

For phylogenomic analysis, a multiple sequence alignment of the concatenated 120 ubiquitous single-copy proteins for the available genomes in the genus Streptacidiphilus was performed using GTDB-Tk41. A total of 13 representative genomes, Streptacidiphilus albus JL83T (GCF_000744705.1), Streptacidiphilus alkalitolerans N1-1T (GCF_042511045.1), Streptacidiphilus anmyonensis NBRC 103185T (GCF_000787855.1), Streptacidiphilus carbonis NBRC 100919T (GCF_000787775.1), Streptacidiphilus cavernicola N8-3T (GCF_042510955.1), Streptacidiphilus fuscans NEAU-YB345T (GCF_015690355.1), Streptacidiphilus jeojiensis N1-10T (GCF_042510965.1), Streptacidiphilus jiangxiensis CGMCC 4.1857T (GCF_900109465.1), Streptacidiphilus melanogenes NBRC 103184T (GCF_000787835.1), Streptacidiphilus monticola JCM4816T (GCF_042658505.1), Streptacidiphilus neutrinimicus NBRC 100921T (GCF_000787815.1), Streptacidiphilus pinicola MMS16-CNU450T (GCF_003258295.1), and Streptacidiphilus rugosus AM-16T (GCF_000744655.1) were downloaded from the NCBI repository. A phylogenomic tree was reconstructed using the ML, MP, and NJ algorithms by MEGA 6.0636. The robustness of the tree topologies was assessed by bootstrap analyses based on 1,000 replications of the sequences.

Determination of temperature and pH range for growth

The temperature range and optimal temperature for growth were determined by observation of the growth on R2A solid medium at different temperatures (0, 4, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30°C) for 3 weeks. The optimal pH for the growth of PAMC 29251 was determined in R2A liquid medium. The pH was adjusted using the following 0.05 M buffering systems: Na2HPO4–citric acid buffer (pH 3.0–5.0), MES (pH 5.5–6.0), MOPS (pH 6.5–7.0), AMPD (pH 8.0–9.5), and CAPS (pH 10.0). The pH was further adjusted using HCl or NaOH as needed. Growth was assessed at pH 3.0–10.0 (in 0.5 pH unit increments) at 20 °C for 10 days by measuring optical density at 600 nm (EnVision plate reader, PerkinElmer). All measurements were performed in duplicate in two independent experiments and mean optical density values were calculated for each condition.

Genome-based comparative analysis

The annotation of the whole genome of PAMC 29251 was performed by the Rapid Annotations using Subsystems Technology (RAST) server42. To predict possible biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), the antiSMASH 8.0 was used43. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was calculated based on presence–absence profiles of BGCs, and a dendrogram was generated to assess BGC-based relatedness among genomes using the vegan package (v2.64) in R. Comparative genomic analysis was performed using protein-coding sequences from the 13 Streptacidiphilus genomes included in the phylogenomic analysis. Sequence clustering was performed using CD-HIT (v4.8.1)44 with a 70% sequence identity threshold to define core, accessory, and unique genes. Upset plots were generated using the UpsetR package (v1.4.0) in R45.

Antimicrobial activity and hydrolytic enzyme assays

PAMC 29251 was cultured in R2A medium at 10 °C for 10 days in a shaking incubator at 130 rpm. After incubation, the culture was centrifuged (9,000 x g, relative centrifugal force) for 20 min at 4 ℃ to separate the cell pellet from the culture supernatant, and the cell pellet was lyophilized using a freeze dryer (HyperCool-HC3110, Hanil Scientific Inc., Korea). Harvested cells were extracted with methanol, while the culture supernatant was extracted with ethyl acetate. Lyophilized cells (2 g) were sonicated in 100 mL of 99.8% methanol for 1 h, filtered through a 0.2 μm membrane, and concentrated under reduced pressure with a rotary evaporator (EYELA N-1200A, Tokyo Rikakikai Co., Ltd., Japan). The remaining solvent was removed by freeze-drying. For supernatant extraction, an equal volume of ethyl acetate was added, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature, followed by liquid–liquid separation using a separatory funnel. The organic phase was collected and concentrated under reduced pressure to obtain the ethyl acetate extract.

The antimicrobial activities of PAMC 29251 extracts were qualitatively assessed against three pathogenic strains, Escherichia coli DH5α (ATCC 8739), Staphylococcus aureus (KCTC 3881), and Candida albicans (KCTC 27242). E. coli DH5α and S. aureus were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium at 37ºC for 24 h to a final density of 1 x 108 CFU/ml. C. albicans was grown in glucose-peptone-yeast (GPY) medium (pH 6.0; 40.0 glucose, 5.0 yeast extract, and 5.0 peptone) at 30°C for 24 h to 8 x 108 CFU/ml. The cultures were spread on agar plates prior to the inhibition assay. Cell extracts were dissolved in DMSO to final concentrations of 10, 50, and 100 mg/mL, and 5 µL of each was spotted onto agar plates to evaluate inhibition zones. The presence of a clear zone around the spot was interpreted as positive antimicrobial activity, whereas its absence was considered negative. Due to limited sample availability, the supernatant extract was tested only at 100 mg/mL. DMSO was used as the negative control. Kanamycin (20 µg/mL) and nystatin (2 µg/mL) were used as positive controls for bacterial and yeast strains, respectively. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Hydrolysis of macromolecules, including chitin, starch, and Tweens 20, 40, 60, and 80, was assessed qualitatively on R2A agar supplemented with 1% of each substrate by incubation at 10 °C for 3 weeks34,46. Clear zones surrounding the colonies were interpreted as positive for chitin hydrolysis. Starch hydrolysis was assessed by flooding the plates with Lugol’s solution; the formation of a clear zone surrounding the colony was considered positive, whereas its absence was regarded as negative. For the Tweens 20, 40, 60, and 80 hydrolysis assays, the formation of a white precipitate around the colonies was scored as positive and its absence as negative. All experiments were performed in duplicate in two independent replicates to ensure reproducibility.

Results and discussion

Identification and growth range of temperature and pH

16S rRNA gene sequence of strain PAMC 29251 (1,355 bp) showed high similarity with Streptacidiphilus alkalitolerans N1-1ᵀ (99.27%), S. carbonis JL451ᵀ (99.26%), and S. jeojiensis N1-10ᵀ (99.19%). Phylogenetic analysis based on the 16S rRNA gene sequence revealed that strain PAMC 29251 formed a monophyletic clade with S. alkalitolerans N1-1ᵀ (Fig. S1), which was isolated from soil samples inside a natural cave on Jeju Island, Republic of Korea8. A consistent topology was recovered from the phylogenomic trees, in which strain PAMC 29251 clustered with Streptacidiphilus alkalitolerans N1-1ᵀ with robust bootstrap support (100%) across all three inference algorithms (Fig. 1). Comparative genomic analysis showed the highest ANI value with S. alkalitolerans (88%) and the dDDH value of 40% (Fig. 1), both of which are below the species delineation thresholds (ANI ≥ 98.65%, dDDH ≥ 70%)47. These genome-based analyses demonstrate that strain PAMC 29251 is distinct from currently described Streptacidiphilus species and that its phylogenetic affiliation differs from previous assignment to S. carbonis, which was based solely on 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity and did not include the recently described S. alkalitolerans N1-1ᵀ19. While genomic evidence suggests that PAMC 29251 may represent a novel species within the genus Streptacidiphilus, a clear taxonomic assignment based on a comprehensive polyphasic approach would be important not only to accurately define its phylogenetic placement, but also to enable its formal recognition as a distinct species and to place its ecological and biosynthetic traits within a clearer taxonomic framework. Accordingly, this strain is referred to as Streptacidiphilus sp. Strain PAMC 29251 was able to grow at temperatures ranging from 4 °C to 25 °C, whereas the other species of the genus Streptacidiphilus showed detectable growth only at 10 °C or higher (Table S1). Most strains, except for Streptacidiphilus albus and Streptacidiphilus neutrinimicus (which grow up to 25 °C), were able to grow up to 30 °C, and Streptacidiphilus fuscans and Streptacidiphilus monticola grew even at 40 °C (Table S1). Growth of PAMC 29251 at 4 °C, together with its isolation from Antarctic lichen, indicated that this strain is more cold-adapted than the other described members of the genus, which did not exhibit detectable growth at 4°C. PAMC 29251 also exhibited a distinct pH growth range, from pH 6.5 to 10.0, with optimal growth under alkaline conditions (pH 7.0–9.0) (Table S1). In contrast, most strains in the genus Streptacidiphilus were able to grow under acidic conditions. Among them, S. albus (pH 3.5–6.0), S. carbonis (pH 3.5–6.0), S. durhamensis (pH 4.5–6.0), S. hamsterleyensis (pH 4.5–6.0), S. jiangxiensis (pH 3.5–6.5), S. neutrinimicus (pH 3.5–5.5), S. torunensis (pH 4.5–6.5) grew only under acidic conditions while several strains, S. alkalitolerans (pH 3.5–10.0), S. fuscans (pH 4.0–9.0), S. monticola (pH 4.0–9.0), and S. jeojiensis (pH 3.5–10.0) were capable of growing across a broader pH range, extending into alkaline conditions. Although Antarctic soils in many ice-free regions near King Sejong Station are slightly acidic to near neutral48,49, the lichen sample used for the cultivation of strain PAMC 29251 was collected from a locally alkaline area, as reported in a previous study48. Thus, the ability of strain PAMC 29251 to tolerate alkaline conditions may reflect adaptation to localized microhabitats within the broad range of soil chemistries present in Antarctic terrestrial ecosystems. Moreover, soil pH has been shown to influence the occurrence of specific lichen secondary metabolites, suggesting that chemically distinct lichen-associated niches may shape the physiological traits of associated bacterial communities, including alkali-tolerant Actinomycetota.

Genomic features and comparative analysis of genome functions

A total of 161,571 reads were generated, yielding 1,211,947,097 bp of sequence data, corresponding to an estimated sequencing depth of approximately 120x based on the genome size. The read length averaged 7,501 bp (median 6,924 bp) and ranged from 35 to 43,133 bp, with an N50 of 10,061 bp. Read coverage analysis showed high and uniform coverage in the central region (~97.7x), with lower but continuous coverage at the termini (~21.1x and ~4.9x), without any zero-coverage positions, supporting the continuity and completeness of the assembled contig and its linear genome structure. The genome contained 9,547 coding sequences (CDSs), 24 rRNA genes, and 79 tRNA genes (Table S2). All available genomes of Streptacidiphilus type strains are linear, and PAMC 29251 shows the highest assembly quality, consisting of a single linear contig with completeness 98.55% and contamination 0.79% (Table S2). Their genome sizes range from 6,885,252 (S. monticola) to 10,096,911 (PAMC 29251), and GC contents fall within 71.0 to 72.5%. A total of 1,377 genes were conserved across the Streptacidiphilus genomes (Fig. S2a). Each genome harbored unique genes between 886 and 4,075, with S. albus possessing the largest number of unique genes (4,075 genes; genome size: 9,677,773 bp). Although strain PAMC 29251 had the largest genome among the analyzed strains, it contained 3,054 unique genes, the majority of which (2,493 genes) were annotated as hypothetical proteins, suggesting the presence of a substantial number of lineage-specific or functionally uncharacterized genes. PAMC 29251 shared 79% of its gene repertoire with S. alkalitolerans N1-1ᵀ, its closest relative in the phylogenomic tree. In contrast, the proportion of shared genes with the remaining Streptacidiphilus genomes was below 70% (Fig. S2b).

To investigate the functional characteristics of PAMC 29251 in comparison with other Streptacidiphilus strains, genome-wide functional annotations were analyzed using RAST. Across all fourteen genomes, core metabolic functions, including amino acid (285–440 genes), carbohydrate (273–404 genes), and protein metabolism (208–250 genes), were broadly conserved across all genomes (Table 1). Despite this overall conservation, notable differences were observed in the distribution of genes related to stress response and secondary metabolism. In particular, genes associated with stress response varied substantially among strains, with PAMC 29251 harboring a relatively high number of stress response-related genes (n=64), comparable to or exceeding those of most other strains (30–58 genes) (Table 1) Among the Streptacidiphilus strains, S. albus contained the highest number of genes assigned to the secondary metabolism (n=19), followed by PAMC 29251 (n=15), S. alkalitolerans (n=13), S. melanogenes (n=11), and S. neutrinimicus (n=11), whereas the remaining strains harbored only 0–8 genes in this category (Table 1). In addition, several genes encoding various transposase families were detected in all Streptacidiphilus genomes (Table S3), in agreement with a previous report49. Notably, strain PAMC 29251 harbored the highest number of transposase-related genes (n=118), whereas other Streptacidiphilus genomes contained only 3–30 genes. Transposases are key mobile genetic elements that contribute to genome plasticity and horizontal gene transfer. Recent large-scale analyses of Actinomycetota have shown that transposases and other mobile elements are frequently associated with hypervariable genomic regions and play important roles in shaping lineage-specific adaptations and secondary metabolic repertoires50. In this context, the higher number of transposase -related genes in PAMC 29251 compared with other strains may reflect an increased level of genome plasticity. Such features have been associated with adaptation to extreme and fluctuating conditions in other systems. In PAMC 29251, they may contribute to genomic diversification, although their functional significance remains to be fully understood.

To assess whether strain PAMC 29251 harbors genomic features indicative of lichen-associated functional specialization, the presence of metabolic pathways that have been proposed to support nutrient provisioning in lichen symbioses were investigated. Several lichen-associated bacteria have been suggested to potentially contribute fixed carbon or bioavailable nitrogen to the lichen holobiont16,17,51. However, genes encoding ribulose-bisphosphate carboxylases (cbbS and cbbL), phosphoribulokinase (prk) involved in Calvin cycle-mediated carbon fixation, as well as nitrogenase genes (nifDHK) required for nitrogen fixation, were absent in PAMC 29251 and all other analyzed Streptacidiphilus genomes (Table S4). The potential for phototrophic or photoheterotrophic metabolism, given that photosynthetic or light-responsive bacteria have been identified as auxiliary members of certain lichen microbiomes52. Genes encoding the core components of the type II photosynthetic reaction center (pufML), light-harvesting complex (pufAB), and bacteriochlorophyll biosynthesis pathways were absent in PAMC 29251. In addition, since phosphorus is an essential macronutrient, and its bioavailability in nutrient-poor lichen microhabitats can depend on microbial processes17,53, genes involved in inorganic phosphate solubilization and organic phosphate mineralization were examined. Although all Streptacidiphilus genomes harbored ppx and ppa genes encoding inorganic pyrophosphatases, quinoprotein glucose dehydrogenase (gcd), acid phosphatase (phoN), and carbon–phosphorus (C–P) lyase were largely absent. Among the analyzed genomes, phoN was detected only in S. anmyonensis NBRC 103185T (Table S4). Taken together, the absence of genomic features associated with carbon and nitrogen fixation, phototrophy, and phosphate solubilization in PAMC 29251 suggests that this strain is not associated with direct nutrient provisioning in lichen symbioses and may instead be associated with heterotrophic or auxiliary functions.

The degradation of complex carbohydrates and proteins is a key process in nutrient recycling within natural ecosystems. In lichens, the turnover of older thallus tissues is thought to contribute to nutrient supply for actively growing regions16. To assess the potential contribution of lichen-associated Streptacidiphilus to this process, we investigated the genomic repertoire of carbohydrate- and protein-degrading enzymes. Genome analysis revealed that all Streptacidiphilus genomes harbored genes encoding chitinases and a diverse set of proteases, including Lon-like proteases, serine proteases, and metalloproteases (Table S5). In addition, genes involved in starch degradation, such as glgP and malQ, which encode glycogen phosphorylase and 4-alpha-glucanotransferase, respectively, were consistently detected across all analyzed genomes. These genomic features indicate that members of the genus Streptacidiphilus possess the metabolic potential to degrade complex polysaccharides, which may contribute to the breakdown of complex substrates within the lichen thallus. This is supported by previous cultivation-based and metagenomic studies showing that lichen-associated bacteria can degrade old lichen parts through multiple hydrolytic enzymes including chitinase, glucanase, lipase, and proteases16,18,19.

Secondary metabolites gene clusters and antimicrobial activity

To evaluate the biosynthetic potential of PAMC 29251, the distribution and diversity of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) across Streptacidiphilus genomes were compared. While the number of BGCs varied among strains, PAMC 29251 was characterized by a diverse set of BGC types, including several strain-specific clusters. Genomes of the Streptacidiphilus strains analyzed in this study contained between 20 and 52 BGCs (Fig. 3 and Table S6). Among them, S. albus harbored the highest number of BGCs (n=52), followed by S. neutrinimicus (n=44), S. melanogenes (n=42), and S. anmyonensis (n=40). In Streptacidiphilus sp. PAMC 29251, a total of 31 BGCs were predicted, corresponding to 38 putative secondary metabolites (Fig. 3 and Table S6). Several BGC types were conserved across all analyzed Streptacidiphilus genomes, including clusters encoding terpenes, non-α-poly-amino acids (NAPAA), and non-ribosomal peptides synthase-independent (NI)-siderophores (Fig. 3 and Table S6). These BGCs are commonly associated with primary ecological functions, such as terpene-derived pigment production54, antimicrobial activity-mediated by poly-amino acid compounds55, and iron acquisition via siderophore biosynthesis56. In contrast, multiple BGCs showed a species-specific distribution among the analyzed genomes. Arylpolyene BGCs were detected only in S. neutrinimicus, ranthipeptide BGCs in S. albus, tripeptide BGCs in S. melanogenes, furan BGCs in S. monticola, nucleoside BGCs in S. neutrinimicus, and aminocoumarin BGCs in S. pinicola. Lomofungin, originally discovered in Streptomyces lomondensis UG-5022, is a phenazine-derived compound known to inhibit RNA synthesis and to exhibit antifungal activity against various yeasts and filamentous fungi57,58. In PAMC 29251, a phenazine biosynthetic gene cluster containing the core gene phzB was identified, showing similarity to the known lomofungin biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC0001302.5 in Minimum Information about a Biosynthetic Gene Cluster, MIBiG) from Streptomyces lomondensis (similarity score: 0.47), as well as to known phenazine BGCs (BGC0002561.3 and BGC0002439.3) from Streptomyces sp. (0.55) and Streptomyces diastaticus (0.52), respectively (Fig. 4a). These results suggest that PAMC 29251 harbors a unique phenazine-like biosynthetic potential within the genus Streptacidiphilus. The presence of a phenazine-like biosynthetic gene cluster in PAMC 29251 suggests a potential antifungal strategy distinct from conventional antifungal mechanisms, potentially enabling inhibition of fungal growth via transcriptional interference. In addition, thiolactomycin, first isolated from Nocardia sp. No. 2–20059 is a well-characterized inhibitor of type II fatty acid synthase systems in plants and bacteria and has been considered a promising lead compound for antibiotic development60. The presence of a thiolactomycin-like biosynthetic gene cluster in PAMC 29251 suggests a potential capacity to interfere with essential lipid biosynthesis pathways in competing microorganisms (Fig. 4b). Maduralactomycin, originally characterized from a biosynthetic gene cluster in Actinomadura sp. RB2961 is a halogenated polyketide with activity against multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii62. The detection of a maduralactomycin-like BGC in PAMC 29251 further expands its predicted secondary metabolic repertoire toward structurally complex and pharmacologically relevant polyketides (Fig. 4c). These strain-specific biosynthetic gene clusters, including phenazine derivatives, fatty acid synthase inhibitors, and halogenated polyketides, distinguish it from currently described members of the genus Streptacidiphilus.

In line with the genome-based predictions, strain PAMC 29251 exhibited broad antimicrobial activities. The cell extract inhibited the growth of Escherichia coli DH5α, Staphylococcus aureus, and Candida albicans at concentrations of 100 and 50 mg/mL, while the culture supernatant also showed antimicrobial activities at 100 mg/mL (Fig. S2). Although the specific compounds responsible for the antimicrobial activity remain unknown, a previous chemical study demonstrated that PAMC 29251 produces multiple secondary metabolites under laboratory conditions13. The isolated compounds included four cyclic dipeptides—cyclo(L-Pro-L-OMet), cyclo(Pro-Ile), cyclo(Phe-Pro), and cyclo(Pro-Tyr)—and four alkaloids—N-(2-phenylethyl)acetamide, indole-3-carboxaldehyde, 3-acetylindole and 2,6-pyridinedicarboxylic acid. Although these metabolites are structurally simpler than the secondary metabolites predicted from the BGC analysis, including non-ribosomal peptide synthetase, polyketide- and ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptide (RiPP)-associated compounds, their isolation demonstrates that PAMC 29251 expresses multiple secondary-metabolite pathways under laboratory conditions. These observations suggest the presence of additional biosynthetic potential, possibly including yet undiscovered biosynthetic capacities, that warrant further investigation.

Hydrolysis of macromolecules

Strain PAMC 29251 exhibited the ability to hydrolyze a range of macromolecules, including starch and Tweens 20, 40, 60, and 80, indicating the production of extracellular enzymes involved in the degradation of complex carbohydrates and lipid-associated substrates. Notably, all type strains belonging to the genus Streptacidiphilus were capable of starch hydrolysis, except for S. fuscans (Table S7); however, the ability to degrade the full range of Tween substrates (20, 40, 60, and 80) was observed exclusively in PAMC 29251, highlighting its enhanced capacity for lipid-associated substrate utilization. Previous studies further reported its capacity to degrade purine compounds such as xanthine and hypoxanthine, as well as notably strong extracellular protease activity compared to other lichen-associated bacterial strains19. Although chitinase-encoding genes were identified in the genome, PAMC 29251 showed no detectable chitin hydrolysis and lacked other physiological traits commonly associated with nutrient acquisition, such as phosphate solubilization and atmospheric nitrogen fixation19. Together, these observations suggest that PAMC 29251 is functionally specialized toward the hydrolysis of specific macromolecular substrates rather than broad-spectrum nutrient acquisition.

Lichens are increasingly recognized as complex multispecies consortia characterized by high bacterial diversity and abundance, challenging the traditional view of lichens as a simple bipartite symbiosis between a mycobiont and a photobiont. Among the bacterial partners, Actinomycetota are one of the dominant groups, particularly at the older basal parts of lichen thalli63–65. This enrichment has been attributed to both long-term associations with lichen thalli and environmental inputs from surrounding soils, where Actinomycetota are major bacterial components, especially in cold and oligotrophic environments such as Antarctic and alpine ecosystems. Notably, soil-associated Actinomycetota are known to increase in relative abundance under stable physicochemical conditions, such as snow-covered soils with reduced temperature fluctuations, conditions that may resemble the microenvironment at the basal regions of lichen thalli influenced by the underlying substrate66. In this context, the macromolecule-hydrolyzing capacity of PAMC 29251 indicates the potential to degrade complex organic substrates. Such activities may be involved in the breakdown of organic compounds derived from senescent tissues or environmental inputs within the lichen thallus. Although the ecological significance of these processes in lichen symbiosis remains unclear, the observed enzymatic activities of PAMC 29251, together with the widespread occurrence of Actinomycetota in lichens, suggest a potential involvement of these bacteria in macromolecule degradation and nutrient turnover.

Conclusion

The genomic and physiological characterization of Streptacidiphilus sp. PAMC 29251 indicates that this strain possesses a diverse repertoire of secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters, some of which are associated with measurable antimicrobial activity and the capacity to degrade macromolecules. Compared with other members of the genus Streptacidiphilus, PAMC 29251 is distinguished by its strain-specific genomic features, including unique biosynthetic gene clusters and substrate utilization capabilities. These findings highlight the potential of this strain as a source of bioactive compounds. While these features may be relevant to processes occurring in lichen-associated environments, their ecological significance remains to be further elucidated. Overall, this study characterizes the genomic and physiological features of a lichen-associated Streptacidiphilus strain and provides a foundation for future investigations into its functional roles and biosynthetic potential.
