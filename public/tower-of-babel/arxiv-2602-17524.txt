Inspiral tests of general relativity and waveform geometry
Brian C. Seymour, Jacob Golomb, Yanbei Chen

Abstract
The phase evolution of gravitational waves encodes critical information about the orbital dynamics of binary systems. In this work, we test the robustness of parameterized tests against unmodeled deviations from general relativity. We demonstrate that these parameterized tests are flexible and sensitive in detecting generic deviations in the waveform using the Cutler-Vallisneri bias formalism. This universality arises from examining the inherent geometry of the waveform signal and understanding how biases manifest. We show how Bayes factors are governed by the intrinsic geometry of the waveform signal manifold when parameterized tests are used to approximate generic violations of GR. We use the singular value decomposition to propose templates that are orthogonal to parameterized tests, identifying degeneracies and enhancing the detection of potential deviations. More broadly, the geometric framework developed here clarifies—at a fundamental level—how subtle waveform effects (including orbital eccentricity, spin precession, waveform systematics, and instrumental glitches) can mimic one another in data, and when they are intrinsically distinguishable.

II.1
Review of biased parameter estimation

Suppose we have a true waveform that is
s_{t}\equiv h_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})
and we are attempting to recover it with a model
h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. The likelihood in GR is just the true waveform plus Gaussian noise

d=s_{t}+n\,,

(1)

while our template is
h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. For stationary and Gaussian noise the likelihood for a waveform is equal to

\log L(\boldsymbol{\theta})\propto-\frac{1}{2}\left(d-h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|d-h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)\,,

(2)

where the noise-weighted inner product is defined as

\left(a|b\right)\equiv 4\,\text{Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{*}(f)b(f)}{S_{n}(f)}df\,,

(3)

where
S_{n}(f)
is the power spectral density (PSD) of the detector.

We wish to find the Fisher information about the point to characterize detectability
38
;
47
;
39
;
110
;
93
. The maximum likelihood estimator is found where the derivative of the likelihood is zero

\left.\partial_{i}\log L\right|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{ML}}}=\left.\left(\partial_{i}h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|d-h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)\right|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{ML}}}=0\,.

(4)

In general, this is a nonlinear equation to solve for
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ML}}
, but we make the standard assumption that it is sharply peaked in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
so that
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ML}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}}\ll 1
. Let us define
\Delta h
as

\Delta h=h_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}})-h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}})\,.

(5)

We also assume that
\Delta h
is small
1
1

1

Note that stability of this equation is poor for actual numerical implementation, especially when
\Delta h_{\parallel}\gg\Delta h_{\perp}
. Appendix
A
discusses how this is rectified by modifying the form of
\Delta h
used in (

).
. Therefore, we can use
d-h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=n+\Delta h+\Delta\theta^{i}\partial_{i}h_{\mathrm{m}}
. The maximum likelihood estimate is thus equal to

\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}^{i}=\theta_{\mathrm{t}}^{i}+\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{i}+\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{stat}}^{i}\,,

(6)

where the bias and statistical errors are equal to

\displaystyle\Delta\theta^{i}_{\mathrm{stat}}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{ij}\left(\partial_{j}h_{\mathrm{m}}|n\right)\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{i}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{ij}\left(\partial_{j}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta h\right)\,,

(8)

where
\Sigma^{ij}
is the matrix inverse of the Fisher matrix
\Gamma_{ij}\equiv\left(\partial_{i}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\partial_{j}h_{\mathrm{m}}\right)
. One can show that the covariance of the
\Delta\theta^{i}_{\mathrm{stat}}
random variable is equal to the inverse of the Fisher matrix
E\left[\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{stat}}^{i}\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{stat}}^{j}\right]\equiv\Sigma^{ij}=\left(\Gamma^{-1}\right)^{ij}

. We emphasize that the
\boldsymbol{\Delta\theta}_{\mathrm{bias}}
comes from the mismodeling of the waveform and is independent of SNR.

Suppose that we are not in the nested case as described above, where our modeled waveform actually depends on some other parameters which are not in the signal. In this case, we model it as
h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\mu})
where
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are some small parameters which we want to measure in the waveform. In this case, we can perform the same analysis as above. If we define
\Theta^{I}=(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\mu})
as the full parameter set, the maximum likelihood estimate is

\Theta^{I}_{\mathrm{ML}}=\Theta^{I}_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta\Theta_{\mathrm{stat}}^{I}+\Delta\Theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{I}\,,

(9)

where
\Theta^{I}_{\mathrm{t}}=\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{0}\right)
so we are assuming the true value of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{t}}\sim 0
. The statistical error is the same
\Delta\Theta^{I}=\Sigma^{IJ}\left(\partial_{J}h_{\mathrm{m}}|n\right)
while the bias is

\Delta\Theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{I}=\Sigma^{IJ}\left(\partial_{J}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta h\right)\,.

(10)

We emphasize that at high SNR, our small model parameters will scale like

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{ML}}=\Delta\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{bias}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}})+\Delta\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{stat}}\,,

(11)

where the bias term is linearly proportional to the
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}}
parameter
\Delta\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{bias}}\propto\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}}
.
This key fact will be used to explain the
unreasonable effectiveness of ppE templates for testing GR

\Delta\Psi_{\mathrm{t}}
, this will be accessible if we measure any
\delta\varphi_{k}
since it is responsive to any modification to the waveform.

II.2
Geometric interpretation of bias equation

In the previous section, we reviewed how an injection can bias the parameter estimation in a mismodeled waveform. Now, we will look at Eq. (
10
) and discover how it can be understood more deeply by thinking about the geometry of the waveform. We define the parallel and perpendicular components as

\displaystyle\Delta h_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\Delta\Theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{J}\partial_{J}h_{\mathrm{m}}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta h_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\Delta h-\Delta\Theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{J}\partial_{J}h_{\mathrm{m}}\,.

(12)

Using these identities, one can see that Eq. (
10
) becomes

\Delta\Theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{J}=\Sigma^{IJ}\left(\partial_{J}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta h_{\parallel}\right)\,.

(13)

This demonstrates that
only deviations parallel to waveform manifold
bias the parameter estimation.

Let us now investigate the case that we have a waveform with a bias that is due to a small parameter
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and our model has a small parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. For simplicity assume that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are both one dimensional parameters. Then, one can show that the estimate for
\mu
is

\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{bias}}=\frac{\left(\left(\partial_{\mu}h_{\mathrm{m}}\right)^{\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\left(\Delta h\right)^{\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}{\|\left(\partial_{\mu}h_{\mathrm{m}}\right)^{\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}}\,,

(14)

where the
\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}
denotes removing the part of the signal which is parallel to the main
\boldsymbol{\theta}
parameters. Thus, we see evidence for
\boldsymbol{\mu}
when both the waveform
h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\mu})
and the residual
\Delta h(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
cannot eliminate the effect by a redefinition of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

In Eq. (
14
), we performed this calculation for the simplest case that you are trying to measure a single parameter
\mu
. If
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mu^{a}
is an array of beyond GR parameters, the equation above can be generalized using Schur decomposition which yields

\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{bias}}^{a}=\left(\Gamma_{\mathrm{red}}^{-1}\right)_{ab}\left(\left(\partial_{\mu^{b}}h_{\mathrm{m}}\right)^{\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\left(\Delta h\right)^{\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\,,

(15)

where the reduced Fisher matrix is equal to

\Gamma^{\mathrm{red}}_{ab}=\left(\left(\partial_{\mu^{a}}h_{\mathrm{m}}\right)^{\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\left(\partial_{\mu^{b}}h_{\mathrm{m}}\right)^{\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\,.

(16)

One can see in the single
\mu
case the equation reduces to Eq. (
14
) since
\Gamma^{\mathrm{red}}_{\mu\mu}=\|\left(\partial_{\mu}h_{\mathrm{m}}\right)^{\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}
. In App.
B
, we derive Eq. (
15
) using the Schur decomposition identities for a matrix consisting of blocks.

II.3
Multidetector geometry

The manifold language introduced in this chapter straightforwardly generalizes to the case of multiple detectors observing one GW event. The likelihood for parameters is

p(d|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\mu})\propto\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{A}\|d_{A}-h_{\mathrm{m}}^{A}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\mu})\|^{2}\right]\,.

(17)

It is useful to introduce the summed network inner product and use square brackets to represent this

\left[a|b\right]=\sum_{A}\left(a_{A}|b_{A}\right)_{A}\,,

(18)

then the maximum likelihood point is the solution to

\Gamma_{IJ}\Delta\Theta^{J}=\left[\partial_{I}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta h\right]+\left[\partial_{I}h_{\mathrm{m}}|n\right]\,,

(19)

where the network Fisher information matrix is
\Gamma_{IJ}\equiv\left[\partial_{I}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\partial_{J}h_{\mathrm{m}}\right]
. The equations for the statistical error and bias straightforwardly generalize from Eq. (
7
) and are equal to

\displaystyle\Delta\Theta^{I}_{\mathrm{stat}}

\displaystyle=\left(\Gamma^{-1}\right)^{IJ}\left[\partial_{I}h_{\mathrm{m}}|n\right]\,,

(20)

\displaystyle\Delta\Theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{I}

\displaystyle=\left(\Gamma^{-1}\right)^{IJ}\left[\partial_{I}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta h\right]\,.

(21)

Henceforth, we will continue to do calculations as if they were a single detector and use the normal inner product
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
notation, but the geometric description of testing GR in multiple detectors follows the same paradigm.

II.4
Bayes factors for tests of GR

We wish to compare whether there is support for beyond GR (bGR) signals in the data. Vallisneri
111
;
109
showed how the Bayes factor provides support for the bGR signal and identified the geometric meaning. If we have two hypotheses (a) GR
\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)
and (b) bGR
\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)
, the Bayes factor is equal to

\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{bGR}}_{\mathrm{GR}}\equiv\frac{p(d|\mathrm{bGR})}{p(d|\mathrm{GR})}\,,

(22)

where the evidence of data
d
in model
\mathcal{M}
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is

p(d|\mathcal{M})=\int d\boldsymbol{\Theta}p(d|\boldsymbol{\Theta},\mathcal{M})p(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\,,

(23)

where
p(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
is the prior and
p(d|\boldsymbol{\Theta},\mathcal{M})
is the likelihood. Vallisneri computed that the Bayes factor is

\left.\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{bGR}}_{\mathrm{GR}}\right|_{s_{\mathrm{bGR}}}=\frac{(2\pi)^{1/2}\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{stat}}}{\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}}e^{\rho_{\perp}^{2}/2+x\rho_{\perp}+x^{2}/2}\,,

(24)

where
x\equiv\left(\Delta h_{\perp}|n\right)/\|\Delta h_{\perp}\|\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a standard normal random variable,
\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{stat}}
is the statistical error on
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, and we assumed a flat prior with width
\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}
. The residual SNR is

\rho_{\perp}\equiv\|\Delta h_{\perp}\|

(25)

and
\Delta h_{\perp}
is the
true
perpendicular signal to the GR waveform as defined in Eq. (
5
) and Eq. (
II.2
) due to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
. One can see that the residual SNR is what provides evidence for a GR violation. If the true signal is a GR signal, the Bayes factor is just

\left.\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{bGR}}_{\mathrm{GR}}\right|_{s_{\mathrm{GR}}}=\frac{(2\pi)^{1/2}\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{stat}}}{\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}}e^{x^{2}/2}\,,

(26)

which has a randomly distributed exponent
x^{2}/2
and an
Occam factor
prefactor
\propto\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{stat}}/\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}
that will cause it to favor GR on average.

Suppose we searched for deviations from GR with post-Newtonian (PN) deviations from GR in the formalism of the parameterized post-Einsteinian/TIGER/FTI tests of GR

;

which mismodeled the bGR waveform. We will now generalize the Bayes factor formula in this case. We assume that the injected signal is
s_{\mathrm{bGR}}^{t}=h(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}})
. We will compare two hypotheses (a) GR
\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)
and (b) ppE
\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)
. Careful computation reveals that the Bayes factor is

\left.\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{ppE}}_{\mathrm{GR}}\right|_{s_{\mathrm{bGR}}}=\frac{(2\pi)^{1/2}\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{stat}}}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{prior}}}e^{\left(\rho_{\perp}^{\mathrm{ppE}}\right)^{2}/2+y\rho_{\perp}^{\mathrm{ppE}}+y^{2}/2}\,,

where
y\equiv\left(\Delta h_{\mathrm{ppE}}^{\perp}|n\right)/\|\Delta h_{\mathrm{ppE}}^{\perp}\|
is a unit normal Gaussian random variable and the captured residual SNR
\rho_{\perp}^{\mathrm{ppE}}
is defined via

\rho_{\perp}^{\mathrm{ppE}}=\mathcal{O}\rho_{\perp}\,,

(28)

which depends on the overlap of the bGR and ppE waveform deviations. The overlap is equal to

\mathcal{O}(\Delta h_{\mathrm{ppE}}^{\perp},\Delta h_{\mathrm{bGR}}^{\perp})=\frac{\left(\Delta h_{\mathrm{ppE}}^{\perp}|\Delta h_{\mathrm{bGR}}^{\perp}\right)}{\|\Delta h_{\mathrm{ppE}}^{\perp}\|\|\Delta h_{\mathrm{bGR}}^{\perp}\|}\,.

(29)

Note that this overlap is between 0 and 1, and quantifies how similar two signals are. We use notation
\Delta h_{\mathrm{ppE}}^{\perp}\equiv\left(\Delta h_{\mathrm{ppE}}^{\mathrm{ML}}\right)^{\perp}=\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{bias}}(\partial_{\mu}h_{\mathrm{m}})^{\perp}
where
\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{bias}}
is the best fit for the ppE test as a function of
\Delta h_{\mathrm{bGR}}^{\perp}
. The lost residual SNR is then equal to

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{diff}}_{\perp}

\displaystyle\equiv\|\Delta h^{\perp}_{\mathrm{bGR}}-\Delta h_{\mathrm{ppE}}^{\perp}\|\,,

\displaystyle=\rho_{\perp}\sqrt{1-\mathcal{O}^{2}}\,.

(30)

, we rederive the optimal Bayes factor in Eq. (

) using methods from

). One can see that the geometric structure comes from features related to how Fisher matrices invert when you have blocks using the Schur decomposition.

Finally, if we were to detect deviations from GR using e.g. one of the parameterized tests, it is important to see how well we could distinguish a bGR model from a ppE model. This would be important in the hypothetical case that we need to identify which particular bGR models are consistent with the data and favored. We show that the Bayes factor between bGR
\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)
and ppE
\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)
is equal to

\left.\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{bGR}}_{\mathrm{ppE}}\right|_{s_{\mathrm{bGR}}}=\frac{\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{stat}}\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{prior}}}{\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{stat}}}e^{(x+\rho_{\perp})^{2}/2-(y+\mathcal{O}\rho_{\perp})^{2}/2}\,,

where the

Additionally, we note that the fidelity at which we can distinguish between bGR and ppE is determined by the quantity
\rho^{\mathrm{diff}}_{\perp}

In the case of high residual SNR, we can express Eq. (

\rho_{\perp}\gg 1

\log\left.\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{bGR}}_{\mathrm{ppE}}\right|_{s_{\mathrm{bGR}}}\approx\mathcal{C}+\frac{\left(\rho^{\mathrm{diff}}_{\perp}\right)^{2}}{2}+\sqrt{1+\mathcal{O}^{2}-2\mathcal{O}\sqrt{1-\mathcal{O}^{2}}}\rho_{\perp}z\,,

where the normal approximation was used for the chi-squared variables and

is the constant coming from prior terms of Eq. (

Z=\frac{1-\mathcal{O}^{2}}{2\sqrt{1+\mathcal{O}^{2}-2\mathcal{O}\sqrt{1-\mathcal{O}^{2}}}}\rho_{\perp}\,,

(33)

which characterizes the degree of preference for bGR vs ppE in the presence of noise. This example demonstrates that the smaller the overlap is, the easier it is to discern two signals while the significance scales linearly with the residual SNR.

As we have seen in the previous sections, the ppE waveform deviations look relatively similar after removing the effects of GR biases. One can see that the right side of Fig.

In this work, we want to find the common features of
\Delta h_{k}^{\perp}
where each
k

107

C(v_{\alpha})=\sum_{k}\left\|\Delta h_{k}^{\perp}-\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\Delta h_{k}^{\perp}\right\|^{2}\,,

(48)

where we assume that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is an orthogonal projector onto the SVD basis and
\|\cdot\|
is a norm. The orthogonal projector is equal to

\mathcal{P}_{n}h_{a}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(v_{\alpha}|h_{a}\right)v_{\alpha}\,,

(49)

and
v_{\alpha}
are the SVD basis elements. These elements are orthonormal for the inner product

\left(v_{\alpha}|v_{\beta}\right)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,,

(50)

We will demonstrate that the SVD described by Pai et al

48

the SVD corresponds to finding an orthogonal basis that best projects all input waveforms

Let us lay the groundwork for the SVD by noting properties of the noise-weighted inner product. In this section, we describe the general procedure to describe how to
pack
a frequency domain signal which has the symmetry
h(-f)=-h^{*}(f)
corresponding to a real time domain signal. Note that the noise-weighted inner product is equal to

\left(h_{1}|h_{2}\right)=4\operatorname{Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}df\frac{h_{1}^{*}(f)h_{2}(f)}{S(f)}\,,

(51)

If we break it up into finite differences, the noise-weighted inner product is

\left(h_{1}|h_{2}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{f}-1}\frac{h_{1}^{*}(f_{i})h_{2}(f_{i})+h_{1}(f_{i})h_{2}^{*}(f_{i})}{2}w^{2}(f_{i})\,,

(52)

where the weight is defined via

w(f_{i})=\frac{2\sqrt{\Delta f_{i}}}{\sqrt{S(f_{i})}}\,,

(53)

where the frequency spacing is
\Delta f_{i}=f_{i+1}-f_{i}
and
S(f_{i})
is the value of the PSD at frequency bin
i
. Note that the discrete inner product in Eq. (
52
) does not have the form of the Hermitian inner product that we know and expect. This is just due to the fact that we simplified the original inner product integral in Eq. (
51
) using the fact that the time domain signals are real. Therefore, we need to construct an inner product where it is properly packed so that it is of the form that the SVD theorem applies
4
4

4

Note that we could have done this by including negative frequencies and doing the SVD on this case which does have a Hermitian inner product
(a|b)\sim a^{\dagger}b
, but this would need extra transformations to read out the real form of the singular vectors.
. Let us introduce the packing operator
\mathcal{P}
as

\mathcal{P}\left[\Delta h\right]=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Re}\,\Delta h(f_{0})\,w(f_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\operatorname{Re}\,\Delta h(f_{N_{f}-1})\,w(f_{N_{f}-1})\\[6.0pt]
\operatorname{Im}\,\Delta h(f_{0})\,w(f_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\operatorname{Im}\,\Delta h(f_{N_{f}-1})\,w(f_{N_{f}-1})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{f}}

(54)

which is just a vector of length
2N_{f}
which consists of the real and imaginary parts of
\Delta h_{k}
times the weight. This works because if we choose
h_{1,2}=a_{1,2}+ib_{1,2}
, then

\displaystyle\left(h_{1}|h_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}[h_{1}]^{T}\mathcal{P}[h_{2}]\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{f}-1}\left(a_{1}^{i}a_{2}^{i}+b_{1}^{i}b_{2}^{i}\right)w^{2}(f_{i})\,,

(55)

so the packing operator makes the inner product have the form of the Euclidean inner product. Thus, if we define the data matrix

(56)

where

Let us also give some comments about how to best use the SVD with modified gravity waveforms. In our work, we have been generically assuming that there are only phase deviations. If there are amplitude and phase deviations (e.g. in a true bGR waveform), the deviations are of the form

\Delta h^{\perp}(f_{i})=\left(\Delta A^{\perp}+i\Delta\Psi^{\perp}(f_{i})A(f_{i})\right)e^{i\Psi(f_{i})}\,.

(h_{1}|h_{2})=(h_{1}(f)e^{-i\Psi(f)}|h_{2}(f)e^{-i\Psi(f)})

\mathcal{P}\left[\Delta h^{\perp}\right]=\begin{bmatrix}\vdots\\
\Delta A^{\perp}(f_{i})w(f_{i})\\

\vdots\end{bmatrix}\,,

\sum_{\mathrm{det}\,A}\left(a|b\right)_{A}=\sum_{\mathrm{det}\,A}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{f}-1}a_{A}^{*}(f_{i})b_{A}(f_{i})w_{A}^{2}(f_{i})\,,

(63)

\vdots\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{f}\,N_{\mathrm{det}}}\,,

(64)

where this formalism now directly generalizes to this case. This also generalizes trivially to detectors of different bandwidths (e.g. LISA + LIGO) where the frequency bins are not the same in each detector.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have shown that there is a relationship between searches for beyond GR morphologies and the geometric description of noise in parameter estimation. We showed that the ppE parameters are able to capture generic beyond GR deviations due to the bias mechanism described above. We also showed that these parameterized tests should do a good job in real data analysis and there is not too big of a loss by having systematic issues in the beyond GR template. We demonstrated that the techniques for multiparameter tests of GR suffer from degeneracies, so this means measuring multiple ones simultaneously is difficult. Finally, we proposed a new template for searching for deviations from GR using the SVD. The SVD introduced in this paper generalizes previous approaches by allowing for amplitude + phase modulations and multiple detectors.

In this work, we have provided a geometric explanation for why parameterized tests are unexpectedly effective at capturing smooth, monotonic dephasing away from GR. It remains to be seen whether there are some dephasing terms that could not be captured by the parameterized tests. The approaches that are developed in this paper for geometrically understanding how similar small effects such as GR violation are to one another can be applied to other situations that GW astronomy is in. For example, this could help understand when it is possible to disentangle the observational signatures of spin precession, orbital eccentricity, waveform-model systematics, non-Gaussian instrumental glitches, and genuine deviations from GR in a more categorical manner. Many of the most informative measurements in GW astronomy hinge on resolving such subtle effects, and the geometric framework developed here provides a principled way to assess their detectability.

Appendix A
Comment on numerical stability

We stated in Eq. (
5
) that the best way to parameterize the bias equation is in terms of

\Delta h_{\mathrm{raw}}\equiv h_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}})-h_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}})\,,

(66)

and then “raw” bias to the GR parameters is equal to

\left(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{i}\right)_{\mathrm{raw}}=\Sigma^{ij}\left(\partial_{j}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta h_{\mathrm{raw}}\right)\,,

(67)

In fact, as first derived by Cutler-Vallisneri, the expression for Eq. (
67
) has a limited range of validity. In fact, it is more numerically stable to use the following expression for
\Delta h
as

\Delta h_{\mathrm{stab}}\equiv\left[\Delta A+i\Delta\Psi A_{\mathrm{s}}\right]e^{i\Psi_{\mathrm{s}}}

(68)

then the “stabilized” bias equation is

\left(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{i}\right)_{\mathrm{stab}}=\Sigma^{ij}\left(\partial_{j}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta h_{\mathrm{stab}}\right)\,,

(69)

where
h_{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}})\equiv A_{\mathrm{s}}e^{i\Psi_{\mathrm{s}}}
,

\displaystyle\Delta A

\displaystyle\equiv A_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}})-A_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}})\,,

(70)

\displaystyle\Delta\Psi

\displaystyle\equiv\Psi_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}})-\Psi_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}})\,.

(71)

This is derived in their paper, but one can show that the error for these expressions is

\text{error of }\left(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{\mathrm{raw}}\right)_{\mathrm{error}}\sim\Sigma^{ij}\left(\partial_{j}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta\Psi^{2}h_{\mathrm{s}}\right)

(72)

Let us define alternative expressions for amplitude and phase deviation which are the difference between true and ML point

\displaystyle\delta A

\displaystyle\equiv A_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}})-A_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ML}})\,,

(73)

\displaystyle\delta\Psi

\displaystyle\equiv\Psi_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{t}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathrm{t}})-\Psi_{\mathrm{m}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ML}})\,.

(74)

In fact, note that
\delta\Psi
is small compared to
\Delta\Psi
so expanding
in these has a much greater regime of validity
. One can show that

\text{error of }\left(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{i}\right)_{\mathrm{stab}}\sim\Sigma^{ij}\left(\partial_{j}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\delta\Psi^{2}h_{\mathrm{s}}\right)\,,

(75)

which is valid over a much larger part of the parameter space.

Note that the discussion above admits a simple geometric interpretation. Using the “raw” expression for the bias equation in Eq. (
67
) effectively assumes that the model waveform never departs from the true waveform by more than
\sim 1
radian in phase,
\Delta\Psi\lesssim 1
, which corresponds schematically to requiring
\|\Delta h\|\lesssim 1
. However, what actually controls the validity of the expansion is not the total model error, but the
residual
error after refitting the GR parameters. In other words, it is sufficient that the ML phase only deviates from the true phase by less than a radian,
\delta\Psi\lesssim 1
, which corresponds to
\|\Delta h_{\perp}\|\lesssim 1
, where
\Delta h_{\perp}
is the component of the model error orthogonal to the GR tangent space
\{\partial_{i}h_{\mathrm{m}}\}
.

Therefore, the regime of validity of the expressions in this paper is set by the smallness of the residual mismatch,
\|\Delta h_{\perp}\|
, rather than the total mismatch,
\|\Delta h\|
. By working with the “stabilized” expression in Eq. (
69
), the bias formula remains accurate even in situations with
\|\Delta h\|\gtrsim 1
, i.e. in the presence of substantial stealth bias
109
, as long as the residual component
\Delta h_{\perp}
remains small.

Bias formula for multiple
\mu^{a}
parameters

This can generically be proven in the following way. Assume
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mu^{a}

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\lambda^{A}

\Gamma_{IJ}\mu_{\mathrm{bias}}^{J}=\left(\partial_{I}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta h\right)\,.

Let us now write this equation as a matrix equation in block form

\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{ij}&\Gamma_{ib}\\
\Gamma_{ai}&\Gamma_{ab}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\theta^{i}_{\mathrm{bias}}\\

Using properties of the Schur complement, we can solve for
\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{bias}}^{a}
where the degeneracy with
\boldsymbol{\theta}

\mu^{a}

\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

\displaystyle\Delta\Theta^{I}_{\mathrm{stat}}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{IJ}\left[\partial_{I}h_{\mathrm{m}}|n\right]\,,

(115)

\displaystyle\Delta\Theta_{\mathrm{bias}}^{I}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{IJ}\left[\partial_{I}h_{\mathrm{m}}|\Delta h\right]\,.

(116)

(117)

where

is the reduced network covariance matrix and the sum is understood.
