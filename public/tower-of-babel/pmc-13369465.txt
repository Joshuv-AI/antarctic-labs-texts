Phenotypic and genotypic antimicrobial resistance profiles of gram-positive cocci and non-fermenting gram-negative rods
Authors: Carolina Peconick Silva, Cassia Lopes Silva, Ihtisham Ul Haq, Paula Prazeres Magalhães, Luiz de Macêdo Farias, Vera Lúcia dos Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance poses a significant global threat, particularly in healthcare settings, due to the spread of gram-positive cocci (GPCs) such as Staphylococcus spp. and Enterococcus spp. and non-fermenting gram-negative rods (NF-GNRs). This study investigates the phenotypic and genotypic resistance profiles of these pathogens. A total of 235 clinical isolates of GPCs and 108 NF-GNRs from a tertiary-care hospital were analyzed for antimicrobial susceptibility and resistance-associated genes. Chi-square tests were applied to assess genotype–phenotype associations, and multivariate analyses (principal component analysis, PCA) were used to explore clustering of isolates and antibiotics according to resistance profiles in each genus. Among GPCs, 69% of Enterococcus spp. and 71% of Staphylococcus spp. isolates were resistant to several first-line antimicrobials. Erythromycin, β-lactams, and tetracyclines showed the highest resistance rates, whereas nitrofurantoin, linezolid and fosfomycin remained largely effective against most isolates. In Enterococcus, PCA separated E. faecalis and E. faecium into two main resistance clusters driven mainly by ampicillin, vancomycin, teicoplanin, and erythromycin loadings, whereas for Staphylococcus no clear clustering by species was observed, and the variance was dominated by fluoroquinolones and selected β‑lactams. Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa grouped according to resistance, intermediate and susceptible profiles; however no robust grouping pattern could be inferred for Stenotrophomonas maltophilia due to the small sample size. Among NF‑GNRs, 89% of isolates were multidrug-resistant (MDR). Carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii reached 85% and was mainly associated with blaOXA‑23 and blaOXA‑143, while in P. aeruginosa ESBL genes (blaCTX‑M, blaSHV, blaGES, blaBEL‑1) and aac(6’)‑Ib‑cr were detected at lower frequencies, and. maltophilia primarily carried sul2 (± sul1). This study highlights the high prevalence of antimicrobial resistance among both gram-positive and gram-negative clinical isolates, with multidrug-resistant phenotypes common in Staphylococcus spp., Enterococcus spp., A. baumannii, and P. aeruginosa. The identified phenotypic and genotypic resistance patterns underscore significant therapeutic challenges and importance for strengthening antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51366-5.

Introduction

The widespread incidence of bacterial pathogens, particularly in healthcare-associated infections, has significantly threatened public safety and security1. Among the most frequently isolated pathogens in hospital settings are Gram-positive cocci (GPCs), including Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, and various coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CoNS) species2,3. The global prevalence of GPC infections ranges from approximately 5% to 35%, depending on population, specimen type, and healthcare setting4–6.

A comprehensive multicenter surveillance study reported that GPCs accounted for 21–27% of clinical bacterial isolates, with S. aureus and CoNS being the most frequent pathogens among both inpatients and outpatients, showing an almost similar incidence between these groups. The most common species identified, Enterococcus faecalis, S. aureus, S. saprophyticus, and S. agalactiae, have also been reported in other contexts3,7.

Enterococci have gained increasing importance as major nosocomial pathogens due to their multidrug resistance, including resistance to β-lactams, glycopeptides, and aminoglycosides, as well as their intrinsic resistance to cephalosporins and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. A study reported 34.29% ampicillin resistance among GPCs, with 23 isolates of E. faecalis and 9 of E. faecium. The emergence of vancomycin-resistant CoNS has escalated clinical challenge, resembling the resistance severity of S. aureus (references). Enterococcus spp. also exhibit reduced natural susceptibility to penicillins due to the expression of low-affinity penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)8,9.

Additionally, acquired resistance to aminoglycosides, penicillins, and the glycopeptide vancomycin significantly complicates treatment10. Methicillin-resistant strains of S. aureus (MRSA) rapidly emerged due to the acquisition of the mecA gene, which alters β-lactam receptor function11. S. aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, E. faecalis, and E. faecium utilize efflux pumps plasma membrane proteins that expel antibiotics from the intracellular environment, as a key resistance mechanism12,13. For example, resistance to sulfonamides and trimethoprim is linked to dfr and sulI genes14. Overexpression of efflux pumps confers resistance to chloramphenicol (cmlA1)15 and tetracycline (tetR and tetA) resistance16, while genes mediate extended spectrum-lactamases (ESBLs) resistance17,18. In turn, NF-GNRs infections have gained significant importance as hospital-associated pathogens since the 1970s.

These organisms accounted for approximately 16.4% of nosocomial infections, with P. aeruginosa and Acinetobacter spp. Stenotrophomonas maltophilia being the most frequently isolated species19. Among them, S. maltophilia is often reported as the predominant non-fermenter in hospital-acquired infections. Rangel et al. reported that 68% of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) relapse cases were caused by NF-GNRs such as Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, and Stenotrophomonas species20. These organisms are linked to diseases related to health care, mainly in intensive care units (ICUs) and burn units21,22. More than 120 NF-GNRs species have been classified as highly virulent pathogens; among them, P. aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, S. maltophilia, and Burkholderia cepacia are the most clinically relevant23,24.

A. baumannii is a major opportunistic pathogen linked to VAP, urinary tract infection25,26, and infections in immunocompromised patients27,28. P. aeruginosa causes serious nosocomial infections, infections29,30, while S. maltophilia is associated with several clinical syndromes, including bacteremia, meningitis, endocarditis, and urinary and respiratory tract infections, notably pneumonia in cystic fibrosis and immunocompromised patients31,32. Risk factors for S. maltophilia infections include organ transplantation, prolonged hospitalization, ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, and use of indwelling catheters33.

Herein, we evaluated the antimicrobial resistance profiles of A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and S. maltophilia from the NF-GNRs group, as well as Enterococcus spp. and Staphylococcus spp. from the GPCs group, isolated from clinical samples in a high-complexity hospital, a tertiary-care teaching institution that provides specialized and highly complex medical care, including intensive care, organ transplantation, oncology, and advanced surgical procedures. We also investigated the presence of antibiotic resistance genes and analysed clustering patterns using multivariate statistical methods to understand phenotypic and genotypic variations.

Methodology

Ethics statement

The research was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Santa Casa de Misericórdia de Belo Horizonte (Opinion Number: 3,209,929) and the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Minas Gerais (Opinion Number: 3,586,852). All the methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All experimental protocols were approved by the respective institutional committees. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s).

Bacterial samples

This study included samples isolated from clinical specimens of patients treated at the hospital from November to December 10, 2019. The selection of the bacteria included in the investigation was based on previous analysis of data from the Hospital to determine the species most frequently recovered in the collection of current samples. A total of 720 samples were collected, of which 235 were GPCs and 108 clinical samples of NF-GNRs (Table S1). Among the GPC isolates, eleven species were identified: Enterococcus faecalis (11%), E. faecium (4%), Staphylococcus aureus (19.5%), S. epidermidis (34%), S. capitis (11.5%), S. haemolyticus (11%), S. hominis (5.5%), S. intermedius (0.5%), S. cohnii (0.5%), and S. equorum (0.5%). Among the 108 NF-GNR isolates, 50% were Acinetobacter baumannii, 46.3% P. aeruginosa, and 3.7% S. maltophilia.

Samples previously identified by Hospital’s Microbiology Laboratory were inoculated onto Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA; Difco, Sparks, MD, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h at the Oral Microbiology and Anaerobic Laboratory (ICB/UFMG). Gram staining confirmed pure cultures, and the samples were stored in Brucella Broth (BBL, Sparks, MD, USA) with 10% glycerol at -80 °C freezer (Thermo Forma 700 Series; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For samples with presumptive identification, biochemical and physiological tests recommended by the National Health Surveillance Agency (ANVISA, 2004) were performed. Samples indicative of P. aeruginosa were grown on Cetrimide Agar (Difco) and tested for oxidase activity and growth at 42 °C on TSA. Samples of Gram-negative rods suggestive of A. baumannii or S. maltophilia were cultured on MacConkey Agar (Difco) and evaluated for glucose fermentation. Non-fermenting samples underwent oxidase testing, growth at 42 °C, motility, and DNase production assays when indicated.

Determination of the resistance profile

The antimicrobial susceptibility profile of the samples was evaluated using the disk diffusion method, according to the specifications of the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2021). The inoculum was adjusted to 0.5 of the McFarland scale in sterilized saline solution (NaCl 0.85%), and suspensions were seeded on Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA) using a sterile swab. After approximately 5 min, the antimicrobial discs were placed in plates. The plates were incubated at 36 ± 1 °C for 18 h, except for Enterococcus spp., for which vancomycin readings were taken at 24 h. For Staphylococcus, the use of vancomycin discs is not recommended. Zones of inhibition were measured and interpreted according BrCAST (2025) (https://brcast.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Tabela-pontos-de-corte-clinico-BrCAST-01-02-2025.pdf) clinical breakpoints as Susceptible, increased exposure (I), susceptible, standard dose (S), and resistant (R).

The selection of antimicrobial agents (AMA) varied according to bacterial species and followed the latest BRCAST recommendations, reflecting clinically relevant antimicrobials used in Brazil. For A. baumannii, the antibiotics tested included ampicillin–sulbactam, piperacillin–tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, imipenem, meropenem, gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, doxycycline, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim. For P. aeruginosa, the antibiotics tested were piperacillin–tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime, aztreonam, imipenem, meropenem, gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin. For S. maltophilia, the antibiotics tested were levofloxacin and sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim.

For E. faecalis and E. faecium, the tested antibiotics included ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, erythromycin, gentamicin, linezolid, teicoplanin, and vancomycin. Finally, S. aureus isolates were tested against oxacillin (cefoxitin), ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, erythromycin, gentamicin, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, and doxycycline.

Antibiotic resistance genes

DNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Bacterial DNA was extracted using the boiling method, as described by34, with some modifications. Colonies from TSA plates were transferred to 400µL of sterile Milli-Q water. The material was boiled for 12 min and, after cooling, centrifuged for 12 min at 17,562 x g at 4 °C (Eppendorf 5424; Hamburg, Germany). The supernatant was then transferred to another tube and stored at -20 °C. The DNA concentration was estimated using a spectrophotometer (Nanodrop ND-1000; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm to calculate the DNA/protein ratio. DNA samples were diluted in sterile Milli-Q water to a final concentration of 20ng/mL and stored at -20 °C until further use. Genes encoding resistance were detected by PCR. The PCR mix contained 1X buffer (Ludwig Biotechnology), 1.5 mM MgCl2 (Phoneutria), 0.8mM dNTPs (Ludwig Biotechnology, Alvorada, RS, Brazil), forward and reverse primers 0.5pmol/µL, Taq polymerase 1.0U (Phoneutria, Belo Horizonte, MG, Brazil), milli-Q water and DNA 2.0ng/ µL. After amplification, a volume of 5µL of PCR products was added to 1µL of sample buffer for DNA (loading buffer 6x; bromophenol blue 0.25%, xylene cyanol FF 0.25%, glycerol 30%) and subjected to electrophoresis in 1% agarose gel. The gels were stained for approximately 30 min in 1x TBE (Tris Base, Boric Acid, EDTA. 2H2O) plus GelRed 10,000X (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The gels were examined using Gel Doc EZ System (Bio-Rad, Santo Amaro, SP, Brazil) and the result was photodocumented.

The panel of resistance genes tested for primers, PCR conditions, and positive controls for resistance genes are mentioned in supplementary tables (Table S2 to S5). The genes investigated in this study are vanA, ermB, mefA/E, ant(4’)-Ia, tet(M), tet(L), mecA, ermA, ermC, blaOXA-23, blaOXA-143, blaKPC, blaVIM, blaIMP, blaSIM, mcr-1, aac(6’)-Ib-cr, blaCTX-M, blaSHV, blaGES, blaBEL-1, blaGIM, blaNDM, blaKPC-2, sul1, sul2, sul3, oqxA, oqxB, qnrB, qnrA, qnrS, qepA.

Statistical analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the results of disk diffusion tests to summarize multidimensional susceptibility patterns, as indicated by inhibition‑zone diameters for the tested antimicrobials, and to explore clustering of antimicrobials and isolates according to their susceptibility profiles. PCA was performed on the correlation matrix to account for differences in scale among antibiotics. Analyses were carried out using PAST (Paleontological Statistics Software Package), version 4.14. Additionally, Chi‑square tests were used to evaluate the association between the presence of specific resistance genes and the corresponding antimicrobial resistance phenotype.

Results

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of GPCs

Antimicrobial resistance in Enterococcus samples

Figure 1A-B shows the percentage distribution of resistance (R), intermediate resistance (I), and susceptibility (S) for 35 Enterococcus spp. isolates tested against 14 first-line antimicrobial agents, stratified by species and clinical specimen origin. E. faecalis isolates (n = 26) predominated in secretion (n = 16) and urine (n = 14) specimens, but were absent from blood, where all isolates (n = 5) were E. faecium. A high number of E. faecalis isolates from secretions exhibited resistance to multiple AMAs, except ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, doxycycline, and tetracycline, which exhibited higher resistance in urine cultures. In contrast, E. faecium blood isolates showed consistently high resistance across antimicrobial classes (Fig. 1B). Within the Enterococcus genus, vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) phenotype was detected in 10 samples (29%).

Supplementary Figure S1A presents clustering the antibiotics based on their overall resistance pattern using principal component analysis (PCA) within the Enterococcus genus, regardless of species or sample origin. AMAs located in the upper-left quadrant of the graph exhibited the highest resistance ratings, including erythromycin (74%), tetracycline, rifampin (57%), and penicillin (54%). Ciprofloxacin (37%), norfloxacin (31%), and doxycycline (23%) exhibited prominent intermediate resistance (Figure S1A). Conversely, fosfomycin (94%), nitrofurantoin (94%), linezolid (92%), teicoplanin (74%), vancomycin (69%), and ampicillin (66%) clustered in the high-susceptibility region.

In addition, E. faecium regardless of sample origin exhibited greater resistance than E. faecalis across multiple AMAs (Fig. 1). All E. faecium isolates exhibited resistance to the ampicillin, penicillin, erythromycin, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, and rifampin, while remaining susceptible to nitrofurantoin (Fig. 1B). Conversely, a high percentage of E. faecium isolates showed susceptibility to nitrofurantoin, linezolid, and fosfomycin and resistance to tetracycline, erythromycin, and rifampin (Fig. 1B). Multidrug resistance (MDR), defined as non-susceptibility to ≥ 1 agent in ≥ 3 antimicrobial classes, affected 69% of Enterococcus spp (Table S6).

The PCA supports the distinct resistance patterns to AMAs within the Enterococcus genus (Figure S1A), which clusters the isolates into 2 groups. The analysis explained 70.1% of the total variance, with 52.1% attributed to PC1 and 18% to PC2. The antibiotics contributing most to the separation along PC1, according to the loading values, were ampicillin (0.46), followed by levofloxacin (0.32), ciprofloxacin (0.32), penicillin (0.27), norfloxacin (0.29), erythromycin (0.22), vancomycin (0.22), and teicoplanin (0.17), which contributed positively to PC1. In contrast, tetracycline (-0.36) and doxycycline (-0.28) contributed negatively to this component (Figure S1B and C). This confirms the resistance pattern of E. faecalis isolates, higher resistance to erythromycin, penicillin, tetracycline, and doxycycline and greater susceptibility to ampicillin, vancomycin, teicoplanin, nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, and linezolid. In contrast, E. faecium showed a similar resistance profile to ampicillin, erythromycin, penicillin, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, and rifampin while remaining sensitive to linezolid and nitrofurantoin.

Antimicrobial susceptibility of Staphylococcus samples

Figure 2A-B presents the complete R/I/S percentages distribution for 200 Staphylococcus isolates tested against 17 AMAs, stratified by coagulase status (CoNS, n = 154; 2 A) and coagulase-positive (S. aureus, n = 46; 2B) and specimen origin (secretions n = 75, blood n = 87, urine n = 5). The resistance patterns varied substantially by coagulase status and specimen origin. CoNS exhibited systematically higher resistance than coagulase-positive isolates across β-lactams, macrolides, and sulphonamides.

Figure 2A shows oxacillin resistance in coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS), predominated in blood cultures (91.0% of 87 isolates) and secretions (83.1% of 54), with no cases among urine isolates (n = 5), resulting in 83.1% overall. Erythromycin resistance followed identical specimen patterns: blood 76.7%, secretions 66.7%, overall 77.3%, alongside trimethoprim (66.2%), penicillin (64.9%), cefoxitin (64.2%), and clindamycin (54.6%). Susceptibility exceeded 85% to nitrofurantoin (96.1%), linezolid (95.5%), doxycycline (94.8%), tetracycline (85.7%), chloramphenicol (86.4%), and rifampin (76.6%). Notably, fluoroquinolones demonstrated the highest intermediate resistance rates across CoNS isolates: moxifloxacin (16.5%), norfloxacin (9.0%), and levofloxacin (8.5%).

Regarding S. aureus, a high level of resistance to Oxacillin (90–100%), erythromycin (80–100%), tetracycline (70–100%), doxycycline (60–90%), and cefotaxime (80–100%) was observed (Fig. 2B), particularly in blood and secretion samples (Fig. 2B). Depending on the sample type, gentamicin and penicillin exhibit moderate resistance, ranging from 30% urine to 70% secretions. Levofloxacin shows around 30–50% resistance, while norfloxacin and moxifloxacin display resistance levels between 10% and 40%, with some isolates showing intermediate resistance. In contrast, nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim, and linezolid showed lower resistance levels, with less than 20% resistance, indicating better effectiveness against the tested bacteria. Rifampicin and clindamycin show varying resistance levels (10–40%), with some intermediate resistance. Regarding MDR phenotype, it was detected in 72% of secretion isolates (54/75), 72.5% of blood cultures, and 20% of urine specimens (1/5). CoNS demonstrated higher MDR prevalence (77.9%; 120/154) than S. aureus (47.8%; 22/46), primarily driven by S. epidermidis (61 isolates), S. haemolyticus (24 isolates), and S. capitis (22 isolates) (Table S6).

Antimicrobial resistance in NF-GNRs

Overall, NF-GNRs showed high levels of resistance across multiple AMAs classes. For 12 of the 16 antibiotics evaluated, resistance rates exceeded 50%, particularly for ciprofloxacin, meropenem, cephapirin, imipenem, piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, ceftazidime, and levofloxacin, for which resistance percentages ranged from 75% to 90%. It was observed that 89% of samples were MDR, as they were resistant to one or more antimicrobials from at least three different classes, including β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, and tetracyclines.

Among A. baumannii samples, resistance to carbapenems was frequent, with 85% of samples showing resistance to both imipenem and meropenem (Fig. 3A). Resistance to β-lactams overall was very high, exceeding 80% for imipenem, meropenem, cefepime, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, and piperacillin–tazobactam, while 33% of isolates were resistant to ampicillin–sulbactam. Resistance to fluoroquinolones reached 89%, and resistance to tetracyclines was 35%, with 13% of isolates resistant to both tetracycline and doxycycline. For aminoglycosides, 80% of isolates were resistant to at least one agent in the class. Consequently, 89% of A. baumannii isolates were classified as MDR. To summarize the antibiotic susceptibility patterns, we performed PCA (Figure S3A). The first two components explained 89.7% of the total variance, with PC1 accounting for 79.4% and PC2 for 10.3%. β-lactams (ceftazidime, cefepime, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, imipenem, meropenem, piperacillin–tazobactam), together with sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim clustered in the same quadrant of the plot, indicating similarly high frequencies of resistance across isolates. In contrast, tetracyclines (doxycycline, tetracycline) and aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin) were projected towards a different quadrant, reflecting a distinct susceptibility pattern characterized by higher proportions of intermediate responses.

The PCA based on isolate scores (Figure S3B) reduced the susceptibility data to two components, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 45.2% and 17.5% of the variance, respectively. PC1 represents the main resistance gradient and separates isolates with low resistance to ceftazidime, cefepime, sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim and piperacillin–tazobactam from those resistant to these drugs. PC2 reflects differences mainly related to responses tetracycline (0.36) and doxycycline (0.31), which contributed positively, and tobramycin (-0.60) and gentamicin (-0.55), which contributed negatively as shown in Figure S3C. Three clusters are evident: predominantly susceptible isolates (yellow circle), isolates with intermediate susceptible (blue circle), and MDR isolates showing concomitant resistance to β-lactams, sulfonamides, aminoglycosides and tetracyclines. The ATCC reference strain grouped with the susceptible cluster and remains clearly apart from the MDR group along PC1.

Regarding P. aeruginosa, resistance rates were lower. 34% of samples showed resistance to meropenem, 28% to cefepime, 26% to imipenem, 24% to ceftazidime, 22% to aztreonam and 16% to piperacillin-tazobactam. Additionally, 22% of samples showed resistance to fluoroquinolones, and 16% and 10% were resistant to amikacin and gentamicin, respectively, as represented by Fig. 3B.

In the PCA biplot of antimicrobials (Figure S4A), PC1 and PC2 explained 84.7% and 15.3% of the total variance, respectively. Aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin, and tobramycin) clustered together along PC1, while β-lactam antibiotics, including ceftazidime, cefepime, piperacillin–tazobactam, aztreonam, and carbapenems, were more dispersed across the axes associated with susceptible, intermediate and resistant categories, reflecting a wider range of resistance patterns within this class (Figure S4A). Clustering of P. aeruginosa isolates based on antimicrobial susceptibility resulted in three groups: multidrug-resistant, intermediate, and predominantly susceptible clusters (Figure S4B). PC1 explained 67.8% of the variance and was driven mainly by β-lactam resistance, (CAZ, CPM, ATM, IMP, MER), while PC2 accounted for 10.7% and was associated with aminoglycoside susceptibility (GEN, TOB, AMI). All the antibiotics contributed positively to the PC1, with values ranging from 0.262 to 0.338. The highest contributions to PC1 were observed for CPM (0.338), LVX (0.332), and CIP (0.320), while IMP (0.262) showed the lowest contribution (Figure S4C). Both negative and positive loadings were observed for the PC2. Negative loadings were associated with PPT (− 0.344), CAZ (− 0.356), CPM (− 0.237), ATM (− 0.421), IMP (− 0.012), and MER (− 0.065), with ATM (− 0.421) showing the strongest negative contribution. In contrast, positive loadings were observed for GEN (0.428), TOB (0.476), AMI (0.233), CIP (0.171), and LVX (0.154), with TOB (0.476) and GEN (0.428) (Figure S4C).

Among the S. maltophilia samples, one (25%) expressed resistance to levofloxacin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, whereas the remaining three isolates were susceptible to both agents. Because of the small sample size, multivariate analyses were not performed. Regarding genotypic resistance, 72.2% of the A. baumannii isolates harboured at least one antibiotic resistant gene. Table S11 illustrates the total number of positive samples for A. baumannii. 76% of A. baumannii samples harbored the blaOXA-23 and blaOXA-143 genes. Only 9% of carbapenem-resistant bacteria samples did not harbor any antibiotics resistant gene, while 4% samples harbored blaVIM, blaIMP or blaSIM. Additionally, 15% of A. baumannii samples harbored the blaKPC gene. 30% P. aeruginosa harbored aac(6’)-Ib-cr. The genes associated with ESβL synthesis, blaCTX-M, blaSHV, blaGES, and blaBEL-1, were detected in P. aeruginosa; however, at low frequencies, with blaBEL-1 detected in 16% of isolates and blaCTX-M, blaSHV and blaGES in 6% (Table S11).

The blaKPC-2 gene was detected in 18% of P. aeruginosa samples, while blaVIM was detected in 6% of P. aeruginosa samples, which harbored more than one antibiotic resistance genes, including the gene that confers resistance to aminoglycosides. With regard to S. maltophilia, three of the four samples harbored sul-2, one of which also carried sul-1 (Table S11). The sul-3 gene was absent in all the samples. Because S. maltophilia had a very small sample size, it is difficult to infer any grouping pattern in this type of statistical analysis. Samples 456 and 458, which were collected on the same date and isolated from the same site of infection, presented more similar profiles than the others.

Discussion

Antimicrobial resistance in both GPCs and NF-GNRs poses an escalating threat to public health worldwide. These bacterial groups are frequently involved in healthcare-associated infections and often present multidrug-resistant phenotypes that complicate treatment35,36. The increasing prevalence of AMR is influenced by multiple factors, including the selective pressure exerted by widespread antimicrobial use, horizontal gene transfer, and hospital-associated environmental reservoirs37. This study comprehensively analyzed phenotypic and genotypic resistance patterns of clinically relevant GPC and NF-GNR isolates from a tertiary care hospital, providing insights into resistance mechanisms and the epidemiological landscape of these pathogens.

The concept of One Health is increasingly recognized as essential in the fight against AMR in Brazil. National policies such as the Brazilian National Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Antimicrobial Resistance (PAN-BR, 2023–2028) integrate human, animal, and environmental health perspectives, encouraging coordinated efforts to understand and control the spread of resistant pathogens38. Hospitals represent not only endpoints of infection treatment but also hotspots for the emergence and dissemination of resistance determinants into the environment through wastewater systems and improper disposal of medical residues38. From a One Health perspective, our findings highlight how resistance genes initially selected in clinical settings can propagate into aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems, where they may be acquired by commensal or opportunistic bacteria, amplifying the environmental resistome. This environmental spread creates a feedback loop whereby resistance genes can re-enter clinical settings through contaminated water, food, or animal carriers 39,40.

A high proportion of the MDR isolates was detected, defined as non-susceptible to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories according to Magiorakos et al.41, reaching 69% among Enterococcus spp. and 71% among Staphylococcus spp. This predominance of MDR phenotypes underscores the widespread dissemination of resistance determinants within the hospital environment, reinforcing the urgent need for enhanced molecular surveillance and strengthened infection control measures42.

Regarding gram-positive cocci, 34.3% of Enterococcus spp. isolates were resistant to ampicillin and 54.3% to penicillin G. Among the ampicillin-resistant isolates, 23 were E. faecalis and nine were E. faecium. For instance, Abamecha et al. (2015) observed resistance rates of 35.96% for ampicillin and 74.56% for penicillin G, close to those found in this study43. In contrast, Anderson et al. (2016) reported complete susceptibility to ampicillin among clinical isolates, underscoring that local epidemiological pressures and antimicrobial usage patterns strongly influence Enterococcus resistance profiles44.

Our data also indicate a high prevalence of resistance to tetracycline-class antimicrobials, with 57.14% and 37.1% of Enterococcus isolates resistant to tetracycline and doxycycline, respectively. This resistance correlated with the detection of the tet(M) (54%) and tet(L) (25%) genes, and the absence of tet(O). This genotype–phenotype concordance has been consistently reported in Brazilian hospital isolates, where the presence of tet(M), tet(L), erm(A), mecA, and vanA genes is highly predictive of MDR phenotypes45. Our findings corroborate those of Santos et al. (2017), who described tet(M) and tet(L) prevalence of 35.3% and 10.3%, respectively, but differ from Jurado-Rabadán et al., who reported tet(M) in only 13.1% of Enterococcus spp.

Erythromycin resistance was also high (74.29%), comparable to the 63.2% reported by Abamecha et al.43. Regarding glycopeptides, vancomycin resistance was detected in 28.57% of isolates, with teicoplanin resistance at 25.71%. These rates are consistent with Andrade et al. (2018), who observed 23.3% among hospital isolates from Minas Gerais46, but markedly higher than the 1.75% found by Abamecha et al.43 and the absence of vancomycin-resistant strains in Anderson et al. (2016)44.

The vanA gene, conferring glycopeptide resistance, was found in 20% of Enterococcus spp. isolates, highlighting the circulation of vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) in hospital environments, representing a critical concern under the World Health Organization (WHO) priority pathogens list47. Molecular studies demonstrate that mobile genetic elements, such as plasmids and staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec), mediate the horizontal transfer of resistance genes mecA and vanA between species 48,49, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed gene exchange. Multivariate statistical analysis indicated distinct resistance patterns among Enterococcus isolates, suggesting clustering related to antimicrobial susceptibility profiles and resistance genes.

In Staphylococcus species, out of 200 isolates analyzed, only nine showed full susceptibility to all antimicrobials tested. The remaining isolates exhibited resistance to at least one agent, and 71% were classified as MDR. Resistance to penicillin was widespread affecting 54.4% of S. aureus and 65% of CoNS. Among CoNS, S. epidermidis (61%), S. capitis (77%), and S. haemolyticus (88%) exhibited the highest rates. Cefoxitin resistance was detected in 41.3% of S. aureus and 64.95% of CoNS isolates. Oxacillin resistance was also high (80%), with 34.5% of isolates carrying the mecA gene, confirming its key role in methicillin resistance among staphylococci. This correlation between phenotypic oxacillin resistance and mecA detection is consistent with previous work (Kuroda et al., 2001; Lowy, 2003), where mecA-encoded PBP2a expression conferred low β-lactam affinity, leading to methicillin resistance. National studies confirm that SCCmec type III predominates among MDR Staphylococcus isolates in Brazilian hospitals36.

Macrolide and fluoroquinolone resistance were also significant. Erythromycin resistance (72%) affected 41.3% of S. aureus and 22% of CoNS, with higher frequencies observed in S. capitis and S. epidermidis. Among these, 34.5% of isolates carried the erm(A) gene and 12.5% the erm(C) gene, consistent with study reporting erm(A), erm(B), and erm(C) in 34%, 32.5%, and 29% of S. aureus samples, respectively50. Fluoroquinolone resistance affected 36% of isolates for levofloxacin, 40.5% for norfloxacin, and 25.5% for moxifloxacin, higher in CoNS compared with S. aureus. Aminoglycoside resistance was high (60%), with 38.95% of CoNS resistant to gentamicin compared to 11% of S. aureus. Despite these trends, linezolid remained highly effective, with 94% susceptibility. Overall, the strong genotypic–phenotypic concordance observed, particularly for mecA, erm, van, and tet genes, confirms their reliability as molecular markers of resistance. Studies show that such concordance typically exceeds 80%, and divergence cases can often be explained by gene regulation or differential expression patterns under selective pressure50–52. PCA revealed diverse antimicrobial susceptibility patterns among the isolates. The first two components accounted for 53.7% of the total variance, indicating moderate explanatory power.

Among NF-GNRs, A. baumannii showed extensive resistance to β-lactams, with 80% of isolates resistant to most tested agents, except ampicillin-sulbactam, which retained activity in 33% of cases. Resistance to fluoroquinolones was high (89%), consistent with studies reporting mutations in gyrA and parC53. The resistance to tetracyclines was 35%, and all samples were resistant to doxycycline, although it is one of the most effective drugs of this class54. Carbapenem resistance was highly prevalent in this study, affecting 85% of isolates. Most of these isolates harbored blaOXA-23 and blaOXA-143 genes. Oxacillinases encoded by blaOXA genes are common in A. baumannii and belong to the family of class D β-lactamases.

It is important to clarify that carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii is not primarily driven by overexpression of the intrinsic chromosomal blaOXA-51-like gene, as previous statements in the literature; rather, the acquisition of plasmid-borne carbapenemases, such as blaOXA-23, represents the predominant mechanism reported in clinical isolates55.

In addition to class D oxacillinases, other carbapenemases may contribute to resistance. In the present study, genes encoding the class B metallo-β-lactamases, including blaVIM, blaIMP, blaSIM, and blaNDM, were detected at low frequency. These enzymes can hydrolyze a broad spectrum of β-lactams and are frequently associated with mobile genetic elements that facilitate horizontal gene transfer. Similar observations have been reported in clinical isolates, where metallo-β-lactamases occur less frequently than OXA-type enzymes but still represent an important mechanism contributing to carbapenem resistance. For example, Moulana and colleagues (2020) reported one blaVIM A. baumannii sample56. The blaNDM-1 gene confers resistance to almost all β-lactams except monobactams, and is typically located on plasmids, facilitating its horizontal dissemination among bacterial species57.

Interestingly, a small proportion of carbapenem-resistant isolates did not harbour the investigated carbapenemase genes, suggesting the involvement of alternative resistance mechanisms such as efflux pump overexpression, reduced membrane permeability due to porin loss, or alterations in penicillin-binding proteins57. However, the absence of this gene does not exclude the possibility of colistin resistance mediated by other mechanisms, such as lipid A modificationof lipopolysaccharide structure58.

In this study, P. aeruginosa displayed resistance to carbapenems and aminoglycosides, partially linked to the aac(6’)-Ib-cr gene59, detected in 30% of isolates, which correlated with phenotypic resistance patterns. PCA of antimicrobial susceptibility profiles separated isolates into distinct resistance groups. PC1 was driven primarily by β-lactam resistance, distinguishing multidrug-resistant from susceptible isolates, whereas PC2 captured variation in aminoglycoside resistance and further differentiated resistant subgroups.

In the antibiogram, nine samples were resistant to at least one of the aminoglycosides, among which six were positive for that aac(6’)-Ib-cr. Those bacteria that resisted two or three aminoglycosides were positive for aac(6’)-Ib-cr. Resistance to this class of antimicrobials correlated with the presence of other genes that encode aminoglycoside modifying enzymes, such as ant(3“)-Ia, aac(6’)-Ib, aph(3“)-1a, aac(3)-Ia and aph(3’)60. Extended-spectrum β-lactamase genes, including blaCTX-M, blaSHV, blaGES, and blaBEL-1, were detected at low frequencies (6 to 16%), with a single isolate carrying both blaCTX-M and blaSHV, corresponding to resistance to penicillins and cephalosporins59. 18% of P. aeruginosa isolates carried blaKPC-2, which conferred resistance to carbapenems in some isolates. In this subgroup, four expressed resistance to both imipenem and meropenem and only one expressed resistance to meropenem. S. maltophilia showed resistance to levofloxacin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole in 25% of isolates, with sul-1, sul-2, and sul-3 genes detected, despite phenotypic susceptibility variation. However, the small sample size limits broader epidemiological interpretation.

Overall, high levels of multidrug resistance were observed among GPCs and NF-GNRs. Enterococcus isolates showed frequent resistance to erythromycin, tetracycline, and penicillin, with 29% classified as vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) carrying the vanA gene. In Staphylococcus spp., 71% were MDR, mainly associated with mecA, erm, and tet genes. Among NF-GNRs, A. baumannii exhibited particularly high resistance to carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, and aminoglycosides, largely associated with blaOXA-23 and blaOXA-143. These findings highlight the widespread dissemination of resistance determinants and reinforce the urgent need for ongoing molecular surveillance.

In conclusion, the integrated phenotypic, genotypic, and multivariate analyses highlight that resistance among Gram-positive cocci in hospital settings is dominated by MDR phenotypes supported by pivotal resistance genes (mecA, erm, tet, van). The observed genotype–phenotype correlations validate the molecular data and suggest complex genetic backgrounds modulating expression levels. The detection of shared resistance determinants between genera emphasizes the potential for interspecies gene transfer, reinforcing the importance of genomic surveillance in infection control and antimicrobial stewardship.

Limitations

Although PCR amplification generated single bands with expected molecular sizes, sequencing confirmation was not performed for all amplicons. Consequently, the identification of certain resistance genes should be interpreted as presumptive, pending future sequence verification. Additionally, the study design was cross-sectional, precluding causal inferences between gene presence and phenotypic resistance. Future studies integrating whole-genome sequencing, transcriptomics, and proteomics could enhance our understanding of the complex dynamics of antimicrobial resistance in clinical pathogens and provide a more comprehensive framework to inform precision medicine and targeted infection control interventions.

Conclusion and future remarks

This study highlights the widespread presence of multidrug resistance among clinically relevant Gram-positive cocci (Enterococcus spp., Staphylococcus spp.) and non-fermenting Gram-negative rods (A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and S. maltophilia) in a tertiary hospital setting. For Enterococcus and staphylococcal isolates, MDR phenotypes were frequent, with strong concordance between key resistance genes and their corresponding phenotypes, notably for mecA, erm, tet, and van genes. This confirms that genotypic markers can reliably predict resistance in most cases, while also identifying instances where resistance genes were present in phenotypically susceptible strains, underscoring the complexity of resistance expression.

Among NF-GNRs, carbapenem resistance was widespread in A. baumannii, and aminoglycoside resistance in P. aeruginosa was associated with the presence of aac(6’)-Ib-cr and other modifying enzymes. Clustering analyses further revealed that phenotypically similar isolates often share genotypic determinants, suggesting circulation of related resistant lineages within the hospital environment. These findings underscore the critical role of continuous molecular surveillance and the integration of genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic approaches to better understand resistance dynamics, guide infection control, and inform precision antimicrobial therapy.
