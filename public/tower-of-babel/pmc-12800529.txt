Autologous transfusion of hemothoraces in resuscitation after thoracic trauma: a narrative review
Authors: Nicholas George Chapman, Devorah Leah Wineberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Traumatic hemothoraces represent a readily available, normothermic, and ABO-compatible source of blood. As a resuscitation fluid, pleural blood presents a reduced risk of transmissible disease and hemolytic transfusion reactions, and it minimizes patient exposure to the storage lesion that affects allogeneic blood products. Pleural blood therefore retains more physiological concentrations of electrolytes and 2,3-diphosphoglycerate when compared to packed red blood cells. However, pleural blood also has a lower oxygen-carrying capacity than packed red blood cells and is largely depleted of coagulation factors. Yet, due to the presence of tissue factor and other proinflammatory mediators, it may paradoxically increase clot formation once transfused. Uncertainty remains regarding the clinical relevance of the supranormal levels of proinflammatory mediators and the effects of autotransfusion on coagulation in vivo. There is now a body of evidence suggesting that autotransfusion reduces the requirement for donor blood products, and small studies have not identified any signals of harm; however, any positive or negative effects on patient outcomes are yet to be conclusively demonstrated. Centers with access to a robust supply of allogeneic donor blood should continue with standard care until more comprehensive research is conducted to clarify both the clinical benefits and risks of autotransfusion. Nonetheless, autotransfusion retains a role in cases where there is a contraindication to allogeneic transfusion, and in low-resource centers where safe and reliable access to donor blood products is limited.

INTRODUCTION

Since its first description in 1818, the autologous transfusion—or autotransfusion—of a patient’s own blood has remained an appealing concept: a readily available, normothermic, and ABO-compatible source of blood, without the associated risks of transmissible disease. Over time, as the reliability and accessibility of crossmatched donor blood have increased, the practice of autotransfusion has largely been relegated to low-resource settings [1], the prehospital environment in select cases [2,3], and the operating room. In the latter, autotransfusion is typically performed using cell salvage devices, which enable cells to be isolated via centrifugation and subsequently washed before reintroduction into the circulation. In this context, autotransfusion has been shown to reduce the need for additional blood products in emergency trauma surgery, potentially lowering transfusion-associated costs [4], but it has not demonstrated a mortality benefit over allogeneic transfusion [5,6]. These devices are, however, costly, requiring significant capital investment and ongoing operational expenses, as well as specialized training—factors that preclude their relatively infrequent use in the resuscitation room. Consequently, autotransfusion outside the operating room environment is generally conducted without such devices. In trauma, the principal reservoirs for internal hemorrhage available for autotransfusion are the thoracic and abdominal cavities; however, in the resuscitation room, access is typically limited to the thoracic cavity. Each adult hemithorax can accommodate approximately 40% of the circulating blood volume [7], equating to around 2 L—roughly equivalent in volume to 8 units of packed red blood cells (PRBCs).

We aim to review the existing evidence for direct autotransfusion of hemothoraces, to identify areas where further research could enhance our understanding of this treatment modality in the resuscitation of thoracic trauma, and to describe current practices.

RISK AND BENEFITS

While pleural blood is readily accessible and available for use in situations where obstacles or contraindications to allogeneic transfusion exist—and may potentially reduce transfusion costs—its composition differs significantly from that of allogeneic blood products. These differences have led to the proposal of several theoretical benefits, as well as the identification of distinct potential risks.

Oxygen-carrying capacity

Salhanick et al. [8] examined the characteristics of pleural blood sampled within the first 4 hours after intercostal drainage, comparing these with venous blood collected from the same patients. Pleural blood exhibited a lower mean hemoglobin (Hb; 93 g/L vs. 118 g/L, P<0.05) and hematocrit (26.4% vs. 33.9%, P<0.05) than the corresponding venous samples. Although this is markedly superior to the oxygen-carrying capacity of crystalloids, it remains inferior to PRBCs, which, due to their elevated hematocrit, have an Hb content of approximately 200 g/L [9]. When considered in terms of oxygen-carrying capacity, it becomes apparent that once transfused into a normoxic patient with an oxygen saturation (SaO2) of 100% and an arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) of 100 mmHg, the theoretical oxygen content (CaO2) for a given volume of pleural blood will be just 47.3% of that in a unit of PRBC (133 mLO2/L compared to 281 mLO2/L). This calculation is based on the following formula, using the theoretical value of 1.39 mLO2 per 1 g of Hb for Hüfner’s constant, and a value of 0.03mL/L/mmHg for the solubility constant of oxygen:

CaO2=(1.39×Hb×SaO2100)+(0.03×PaO2)

The formula assumes the same proportion of Hb molecules remain functional and does not account for further red cell lysis during retransfusion.

These studies of hemothorax composition suggest that, while autotransfusion represents an improvement over crystalloids, it may be a suboptimal alternative to allogeneic blood transfusion with respect to oxygen-carrying capacity.

Effects of storage

A potentially detrimental aspect of PRBC transfusion is the decline in quality, or “storage lesion,” induced by prolonged ex vivo refrigeration. Notably, PRBC units are depleted of 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG), with the majority lost after 2 weeks of storage [10], which, given its influence on oxyhemoglobin dissociation, may impair the offloading of oxygen in peripheral tissues, despite the higher oxygen content described above. Adenosine triphosphate is also depleted, resulting in failure of energy-dependent cell membrane pumps, which alters RBC morphology and impairs their rheological properties as they traverse the microvasculature, further impeding oxygen delivery [11,12]. In contrast, studies of salvaged blood used for autotransfusion have not shown such a marked reduction in 2,3-DPG [13,14], nor any changes to oxyhemoglobin P50 [13]. While quantifying the clinical effects of these biochemical differences is more complex, they are noteworthy: some evidence suggests that transfusing blood products stored for longer periods is associated with a significantly increased mortality risk compared to those stored for shorter durations [15]. However, subsequent large randomized controlled trials have found no difference within the range of storage times typical in modern clinical practice [16]. While the potential negative effects of allogeneic transfusion remain under debate, autotransfusion may avoid these, as the transfused blood is not affected by storage lesions.

Effects on electrolytes

Pleural blood has been shown to have a mean K+ concentration between 3.6 and 4.9 mmol/L [2,8], which is similar to normal serum levels. In contrast, PRBC units have a mean K+ concentration of 19.0±7.8 mmol/L in the first week of storage, 31.5±14.1 mmol/L in the second week, and 39.9±10.3 mmol/L in the third week [17], with older PRBCs often being dispensed first to manage blood bank inventory. This may be of clinical significance during rapid transfusion of patients with underlying renal impairment, crush syndrome, compartment syndrome, or concomitant burns. Case series of hyperkalemic cardiac arrests following PRBC transfusion have shown a mean prearrest K+ of just 5.1 mmol/L [17], and transfusion-related hyperkalemia has been shown to occur as a function of rate rather than volume [18]. The use of citrate in preservative solutions for PRBC anticoagulation also chelates the patient’s circulating ionized calcium, contributing to iatrogenic hypocalcemia [19]. This further impairs hemostasis in trauma patients [20] and is not a feature of autotransfused blood.

Immune compatibility

By its nature, autotransfusion does not expose patients to the risks of immune incompatibility, thereby eliminating the risk of immediate or delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions (HTR) that may arise from exposure to A or B agglutinins and other red cell antigens. Estimates for the incidence of HTR vary, but are approximately 1 in 2,500 to 11,000 for delayed HTR and 1 in 76,000 for acute HTR [21]. Although the incidence is low, the consequences can be severe. These figures also encompass nonurgent transfusions and would likely be higher if assessed exclusively in the resuscitation environment, where the risk of error is increased and only limited crossmatching is often employed.

Potential for contamination

Systematic reviews of autotransfusion using cell salvage devices in trauma surgery have not demonstrated any increased risk of sepsis [4]. However, fewer data are available regarding direct autotransfusion. A case-control study [22] and a number of smaller case series [2,14] have shown no indication that infective complications arise as a result of direct autotransfusion from drained hemothoraces. In a study with 272 patients by Rhee et al. [22], no statistically significant difference in the rate of sepsis was found between the group receiving autotransfusion and those who received only allogeneic products (2.9% vs 2.2%, P=0.71). Conventional wisdom holds that particular caution should be exercised in cases of penetrating thoracoabdominal wounds, where diaphragmatic violation—and therefore gastric or enteric contamination—is possible [23]. Nevertheless, several small studies have demonstrated that autotransfusion of salvaged hemoperitoneum in penetrating abdominal trauma does not increase mortality or sepsis rates, even when hollow viscus injury is present [5,24–26], and even when peritoneal blood is culture positive [26]. In all these studies, however, the peritoneal blood was washed through cell salvage devices before autotransfusion.

Effects on coagulation

Samples of pleural blood from drained hemothoraces have been shown to be profoundly incoagulable, with a mean platelet count of 53,000/μL and significant reductions in fibrinogen as well as factors V and VIII. These samples also exhibit elevated levels of fibrin-degradation products [8], which impair both platelet function and fibrin polymerization [27]. Multiple studies have highlighted the coagulopathic nature of drained pleural blood, consistently demonstrating an international normalized ratio (INR) of >9 and an activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) of >180 seconds [8,28,29]. These findings are observed regardless of whether the hemothorax was autotransfused at 1 or 4 hours after thoracostomy [19].

However, mixing studies have shown that, despite being factor-deficient, when pleural blood is combined with venous blood from the same patient, the admixture becomes hypercoagulable. This results in INR and APTT values lower than those of the venous samples, suggesting the presence of an accelerant in the pleural blood [28]. This effect may be attributable to elevated proinflammatory cytokine levels within the pleural blood, as discussed below, or to microparticles expressing prothrombotic compounds such as tissue factor [30]. Whether this phenomenon is beneficial remains unclear, as it occurs in an abnormal and unregulated fashion, with thrombus formation not necessarily taking place at the site of injury, and potentially exacerbating a consumptive coagulopathy alongside a dilutional one. Nonetheless, in their case-control study, Rhee et al. [22] observed no statistically significant difference in the rate of disseminated intravascular coagulation between patients receiving autotransfusion and those who did not (2.9% vs. 2.9%, P=0.9).

It remains uncertain what functional effects autotransfusion exerts on hemostasis in vivo, whether there is any increased risk of vascular occlusive events, or to what extent pleural blood requires anticoagulation prior to autotransfusion, if it requires any at all, as there are insufficient in vivo data at present. Further studies using pre- and post-autotransfusion thromboelastography may be instrumental in elucidating the functional impact of autotransfusion on coagulation in vivo, thereby clarifying its role in resuscitation.

Effects on renal function

Another potential risk highlighted in the literature is the presence of free Hb (fHb) in solution, resulting from RBC lysis. fHb may induce renal tubular injury, both directly and indirectly, via its scavenging of nitric oxide and subsequent impairment of microcirculatory perfusion. Serum fHb levels have been found to be elevated in patients following receipt of autotransfused pleural blood, with Barriot et al. [2] reporting a mean fHb of 21 mg/dL (13 μmol/L). PRBC units have been shown to have a mean fHb of 25.9 μmol/L [31], with levels increasing with prolonged storage duration. The transfusion of 2 units has been demonstrated to generate plasma fHb concentrations of 5.0 μmol/L, peaking 60 minutes after transfusion [31]. Although this concentration is less than half that observed in autotransfusion, the patients in the study by Barriot et al. [2] received substantially larger volumes of autotransfused blood (mean, 4.1±0.6 L), suggesting that this simply represents a greater total fHb load, rather than a risk unique to autotransfusion. In large volumes, the fHb burden may exceed the binding capacity of plasma haptoglobin, potentially increasing risk in the presence of concomitant rhabdomyolysis due to crush injury, compartment syndrome, or burns. However, this risk does not appear to be greater for autotransfusion, given the fHb levels detected in the single study available and the lack of evidence demonstrating any association with renal dysfunction; Rhee et al. [22] found no difference in the incidence of renal insufficiency following autotransfusion (2.2% vs. 2.2%, P=0.89).

Cytokine composition

There is ongoing debate regarding the clinical significance of proinflammatory mediators such as interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, interferon γ, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) present in autotransfused blood as a result of tissue injury. These cytokines have been shown to be present at concentrations 10- to 100-fold higher in pleural blood than in venous samples from the same patient, and their concentration increases in blood drained from hemothoraces over time; similarly, levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-10 also rise [32]. Other studies in elective surgical patients receiving autotransfusion via cell salvage devices have found comparable patterns, with some hypothesizing that the simultaneous presence of both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory mediators may attenuate the harmful effects of the former [33]. Some have interpreted these alterations as potentially beneficial, concluding that they might enhance the immune response to infection [34,35]. The presence of these cytokines may increase the risk of febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reactions (FNHTR), since the leukodepletion of stored allogeneic blood products reduces IL-6 and TNF-α concentrations and is associated with a lower risk of FNHTR than nonleukodepleted units [36,37]. PRBC units, even when leukodepleted, nevertheless present a non-negligible risk, being capable of provoking an acute-phase response via other mechanisms, such as passive transfer of human leukocyte antigens or human neutrophil antigens, which can activate the complement and coagulation cascades, resulting in a spectrum of inflammatory pathologies, including transfusion-related lung injury (TRALI) [38]. Notably, the passive transfer of such foreign antigens does not occur with autotransfusion. Rhee at al. [22] found no significant difference in rates of either TRALI (2.2% vs. 1.5%, P=0.67) or in-hospital mortality (18.3% vs. 15.4%, P=0.51) in patients receiving autotransfusion compared to those who did not. Nevertheless, the effect of these differences in cytokine composition on clinical outcomes remains unclear, given that this is one small retrospective study and there is otherwise a paucity of in vivo data on this subject.

Proposed technique

The indications for autotransfusion of drained hemothoraces will depend on the relative availability of resources at the treating clinician’s disposal. A pragmatic approach would be to consider autotransfusion of intercostal drain output in hemodynamically unstable patients once a clinically significant volume has been drained (the precise threshold may vary), primarily in situations where allogeneic blood products are unobtainable, where there is an anticipated delay to receiving allogeneic blood that would be clinically significant, or where there is a contraindication to allogeneic transfusion—such as the presence of multiple alloantibodies or religious beliefs precluding donor blood. Autotransfusion of pleural blood is generally contraindicated in cases where malignant cells are likely to be present in the pleural space, there is evidence of active infection, the patient has disseminated intravascular coagulation or another clinically significant coagulopathy, or contamination with gastrointestinal tract contents is suspected. It is also generally advised that once drained, blood from hemothoraces should not be autotransfused beyond the first 4 to 6 hours [8,39].

Other scoping reviews have found considerable variability in how direct autotransfusion is performed [39]. The limited availability of cell salvage devices and blood products in austere settings has led the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) to recommend improvised methods for the safe collection and transfusion of pleural blood in low-resource environments [1]. Multiple commercially available autotransfusion collection systems also exist, including the Sinapi XL1000 (Sinapi Biomedical), Pleur-Evac ATS bag A-1500-08LF (Teleflex), Ocean 2050 and Oasis 3650 ATS (Atrium Medical Corp), and the ConstaVac CBCII (3T Medical Systems). These are designed to provide a direct conduit for transfusion via a port that allows connection to dedicated transfusion giving sets or collection bags. Such closed systems reduce the risk of contamination inherent in improvised techniques like those recommended by the ICRC, but do not require expensive cell salvage devices. Here, we describe a simple technique, including variations, utilized by several South African centers [40], to enable autotransfusion of drained pleural blood using a direct collection system such as the Sinapi XL1000, currently in use within our trauma service:

(1) A drainage bottle is prefilled with 5,000 units of unfractionated heparin via the needleless sampling port, though other centers have used 3 U/mL [41], and others no anticoagulation at all [2,23]. Insufficient evidence exists to recommend a single anticoagulation strategy at this time.

(2) An intercostal catheter is inserted in the standard manner, under aseptic conditions.

(3) Pleural blood is then allowed to drain. No strict guidelines mandate the minimum volume required prior to transfusion, though >500 mL is a common threshold. This will be guided by the urgency of transfusion.

(4) Both the intercostal catheter and the drainage tubing should be temporarily clamped, and the two then disconnected.

(5) The drainage tubing entering the bottle should then be permanently occluded to prevent contamination, either by leaving the clamp in place or by tying the tubing in a knot.

(6) A new drainage bottle should then be connected to the intercostal catheter, after which the clamp on the intercostal catheter is released.

(7) Communication with the intrapleural space is confirmed by depressing the drain’s bulb and observing whether a swing occurs with the phases of the respiratory cycle.

(8) The contents of the full drainage bottle are visually inspected for contaminants, and the bottle is then hung above the patient to allow gravity-assisted drainage.

(9) The dust cover on the autotransfusion port is removed, and it is spiked with a giving set that includes an in-line filter (Fig. 1).

CONCLUSIONS

There is now a body of evidence suggesting that autotransfusion of pleural blood minimizes the requirement for donor blood products [22], which may reduce cost. However, at present, no high-quality prospective evidence examining patient-oriented outcomes exists to suggest greater risk or benefit for autotransfusion compared to allogeneic blood. Its utility as a resuscitation fluid may be limited to its role as a volume expander with some oxygen-carrying capacity, akin to diluted units of PRBC. Current in vitro data suggest that drained pleural blood is unlikely to be an effective substitute for whole blood, and in the acutely hemorrhaging or coagulopathic patient, transfusion of donor plasma and platelets is still likely necessary. Uncertainty remains regarding the clinical significance of the supranormal concentrations of proinflammatory mediators within pleural blood and the effects of autotransfusion on coagulation in vivo. Further noninferiority trials are needed to better investigate the safety profile of autotransfusion. The development of collection systems specifically designed for autotransfusion further improves the ability to return pleural blood in an aseptic manner. In this way, autotransfusion may be more applicable in the resuscitation of unstable thoracic trauma patients in settings where cell salvage devices are not available. Major trauma centers with robust access to allogeneic donor blood should continue standard care until more extensive research clarifies both the potential benefits and risks of autotransfusion. Nevertheless, autotransfusion remains indicated for patients with a contraindication to allogeneic transfusion, and in low-resource centers where safe and reliable access to donor blood products is limited.
