Digital Twin Calibration with Model-Based Reinforcement Learning
Hua Zheng, Wei Xie, Ilya O. Ryzhov, Keilung Choy

Abstract
This paper presents a novel methodological framework, called the Actor-Simulator, that incorporates the calibration of digital twins into model-based reinforcement learning for more effective control of stochastic systems with complex nonlinear dynamics. Traditional model-based control often relies on restrictive structural assumptions (such as linear state transitions) and fails to account for parameter uncertainty in the model. These issues become particularly critical in industries such as biopharmaceutical manufacturing, where process dynamics are complex and not fully known, and only a limited amount of data is available. Our approach jointly calibrates the digital twin and searches for an optimal control policy, thus accounting for and reducing model error. We balance exploration and exploitation by using policy performance as a guide for data collection. This dual-component approach provably converges to the optimal policy, and outperforms existing methods in extensive numerical experiments based on the biopharmaceutical manufacturing domain.



Keywords
Model-based reinforcement learning, digital twin calibration, model uncertainty, infinite-horizon Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), biomanufacturing

1
Introduction

This study is motivated by optimal control applications that exhibit
high complexity, high uncertainty, and very limited data

(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Plotkin et al. 2017
;
Mirasol 2017
)
. In particular, all of these challenges are present in the domain of biopharmaceutical manufacturing, used for production of essential life-saving treatments for severe and chronic diseases, including cancers, autoimmune disorders, metabolic diseases, genetic disorders, and infectious diseases such as COVID-19
(
Zahavi and Weiner 2020
;
Teo 2022
)
. Using cells as factories, biomanufacturing involves hundreds of biological, physical, and chemical
factors dynamically interacting with each other at molecular, cellular, and macroscopic levels and impacting production outcomes. Due to the complexity of these mechanisms, it is quite difficult to control production safely and effectively, especially in the presence of very limited data.

Digital twins have proven very useful in guiding the control of complex physical systems
(
Tao et al. 2018
)
. However, there are many situations where the system dynamics are not fully understood and only crudely approximated by digital twin models
(
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Nguyen-Tuong and Peters 2011
)
. For example, in biopharmaceutical manufacturing and cell culture processes, a digital twin, built only on limited knowledge of the critical metabolic mechanisms and kinetics of cells, can lead to suboptimal control strategies, such as oxygen and nutrient feeding, that eventually impact the production outputs (e.g., yield and product quality).
Therefore, the digital twin should be
calibrated
to reflect the physical system as accurately as possible, and it should be
optimized
to identify an effective control policy for the physical system.

This paper considers a situation where the digital twin is specified by a finite number of
calibration parameters
. The parameters are set based on experimental data from the physical system, which are expensive to collect and therefore very limited; often sample sizes are no more than 10-20
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
. However, under a particular parameter setting, the control policy obtained by simulating the digital twin can be used to guide the acquisition of new data
(
Powell and Ryzhov 2012
)
. Thus, there is a synergy between the physical and digital systems, with the overall goal being to optimize the control policy in the real world. Both calibration and data acquisition must be linked back to policy performance in the physical system.
Therefore, digital twin calibration and informative data collection must be tailored to specific decision making of the real system:
(i) the prediction of the mean response for system design; and (ii) the dynamics characterized by a state-action transition model for system control.

Most existing studies on computer model calibration (see
Sung and Tuo 2024
for a recent review) focus on statistical accuracy and uncertainty quantification for system design rather than control. Many of these studies build on the KOH framework, introduced by
Kennedy and O’Hagan 2001
, which uses Gaussian processes (GPs) to model the simulation output as well as the discrepancy from the physical system.
Tuo and Wu 2015a
refined the KOH framework to handle identifiability issues between calibration parameters and model discrepancy. Further improvements and extensions were developed by
Plumlee et al. 2016
and
Plumlee 2017
. None of these papers considered the problem of control or modeled any policy optimization problem.

In marked contrast, we model both the physical and digital systems as infinite-horizon Markov decision processes (MDPs) with continuous state spaces. The goal in such problems is to learn an optimal policy function mapping a state to an action (or to a distribution over actions). The transition dynamics may have some known structure, but depend on parameters that are unknown for the physical system. The digital twin can be simulated with any choice of parameters, but since we do not know the “true” values, the simulation output may be an inaccurate representation of the real process
(
Luo et al. 2020
)
. Our proposed approach integrates parameter calibration with optimal control, strategically prioritizing experiments on the physical system to acquire information that yields the greatest benefit for policy optimization.

The problem of learning a state transition model characterizing the dynamics of a physical system, and then using this model to guide the search for a control policy, has been considered in the literature on model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL). See
Moerland et al. 2023
for a survey describing various parametric and nonparametric state transition models. One widely used approach is based on GPs
(
Deisenroth and Rasmussen 2011
;
Kurutach et al. 2018
)
, which allow for a form of uncertainty quantification that can be explicitly considered when searching for a policy. However, it is quite difficult for GP-based MBRL to incorporate known structural information (for example, physics- and chemistry-based cell growth models). A different approach by
Wagenmaker et al. 2021
imposes linear structure on the transition dynamics, but linearity is unable to capture the full complexity of many real-world systems. This reference paper also did not extensively study the connection between the data acquisition strategy, the model error, and the search for the optimal policy.

Our proposed “Actor-Simulator” framework uses a general nonlinear state transition model, which incorporates existing structural information (e.g., physics-based models of bioprocess dynamics) as well as uncertainty in the form of unknown parameters. Our approach alternates between calibrating the digital twin, identifying information to be acquired in the next experiment, and learning an reinforcement learning (RL) policy to optimize the physical system. A crucial component of this framework is a novel uncertainty function quantifying the discrepancy between the two systems with respect to the RL objective (i.e., the cumulative discounted reward). We use this criterion in two ways: during calibration, we select the next experiment to maximize the uncertainty function, and during policy optimization, we augment the reward function of the MDP by penalizing high-uncertainty actions. This approach leads to more effective targeted exploration of the state-action space, identifying those parts of it that yield the greatest benefit for policy optimization.

We present both theoretical and empirical evidence in favor of the new approach. First, on the theoretical side, we show that a consistent estimator of the model parameters leads to consistency of the policy. In other words, when calibration and optimization are linked through the Actor-Simulator, we eventually arrive at an optimal policy for the
physical
system. We also discuss convergence rates. On the empirical side, we conduct extensive experiments on a problem motivated by biopharmaceutical manufacturing, with complex nonlinear dynamics and up to 40 unknown calibration parameters. The proposed approach significantly outperforms a state-of-the-art GP-based method as well as a baseline random policy.

In sum, this paper makes the following contributions.

1.

We propose a novel methodological framework linking a physical system to a digital twin model, in which calibration and optimization are conducted jointly.

2.

We create a novel uncertainty function quantifying the influence of model estimation uncertainty on the MBRL objective under general
nonlinear
dynamics.

3.

We develop a novel calibration policy for information acquisition, targeting parts of the state-action space that are the most relevant for learning an optimal policy.

4.

We prove the convergence of the policy obtained from this approach. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first theoretically justified MBRL method that does not rely on restrictive assumptions such as linear transitions.

5.

We present extensive experimental results demonstrating that the proposed approach outperforms benchmarks, with respect to both parameter estimation and policy optimization, in a difficult and realistic application context.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We review the most related studies in Section
2
and present the problem description and outline the overall algorithmic framework for theoretical analysis in Section
3
. Then, we state the Actor-Simulator Algorithm in Section
4
, prove its asymptotic optimality in Section
5
, and present numerical experiments in Section
6
.
Finally, we draw conclusions in Section
7
.

Related Work

In this section, we review several related streams of literature from reinforcement learning, computer model calibration, and machine learning.

Model Based Reinforcement Learning.
In reinforcement learning (RL), model-based approaches are widely considered to be more sample-efficient than model-free ones
(
Moerland et al. 2023
;
Nguyen-Tuong and Peters 2011
)
. We may categorize this literature according to different types of process dynamics and state transition models used, for example, MBRL with linear MDPs
(
Jin et al. 2023
)
, MBRL with linear mixture models
(
Ayoub et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2021
)
, MBRL with Gaussian processes
(
Deisenroth and Rasmussen 2011
)
, RL based on linear-quadratic regulators
(
Bradtke 1992
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Wagenmaker et al. 2023
)
, RL with neural networks
(
Levine and Abbeel 2014
;
Chua et al. 2018
)
, and ensemble-based RL
(
Kurutach et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2020
;
Kidambi et al. 2020
)
. These approaches tend to impose too much or too little structure on the dynamics. In many situations, particularly in biobiopharmaceutical manufacturing, physics-based models provide more information than can be reflected in a GP or neural network, but such models cannot be forced into restrictive linear or linear-quadratic forms.

In the realm of empirical MBRL, a variety of model uncertainty-aware techniques have been developed, including approaches like uncertainty-aware RL
(
Chua et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2020
)

as well as active exploration strategies outlined by
Nakka et al. 2020
. While these empirical methods have achieved notable success in practice, they often lack theoretical performance guarantees. The majority of theoretical research in this field has largely focused on more constrained settings, such as tabular models, Linear Quadratic Regulators (LQR), Gaussian Processes (GP), and linear MDPs. In addition, as
Song and Sun 2021
pointed out, many existing MBRL algorithms lack the ability to perform
exploration and thus cannot automatically adapt to exploration-challenging tasks, especially dynamic decision making for complex stochastic systems such as biopharmaceutical manufacturing. Our work mitigates these limitations by proposing a theoretically grounded calibration policy for systems with nonlinear dynamics, and by linking the exploration strategy to the policy optimization problem.

Computer Model Calibration.

Calibration methods have been widely used in biomanufacturing
(
Treloar et al. 2022
;
Helleckes et al. 2022
)
, climate prediction
(
Higdon et al. 2013
)
, cosmology
(
Murphy et al. 2007
)
, hydrology
(
Goh et al. 2013
)
, cylinder implosion
(
Higdon et al. 2008
)
, fluidized-bed coating
(
Wang et al. 2009
)
, composite fuselages
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
and biomechanical engineering applications
(
Han et al. 2009
)
.
Much of this literature uses the KOH framework of
Kennedy and O’Hagan 2001
as a foundation. Recent work has started to combine calibration with optimization
(
do Amaral et al. 2022
)
.
Li et al. 2019
introduce a metamodel-based approach, employing radial basis function metamodels to provide a smooth prediction surface for the objective value over the space of both design variables and preference parameters.
Similarly,
Chen et al. 2017
advances a surrogate-based calibration methodology by seamlessly incorporating it into a simulation-optimization framework.
However, these papers generally consider static decision-making problems or system design in which the goal is to estimate a single response surface. Thus far, the literature on computer model calibration has not explored sequential decision-making problems with probabilistic state transitions.

Physics-Informed Machine Learning (ML) and Calibration.

Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) is an emerging paradigm that integrates prior physical knowledge with data-driven machine learning models
(
Karniadakis et al. 2021
)
. PIML is commonly used in biomanufaturing
(
Cui et al. 2024
)
, fluid dynamics
(
Cai et al. 2021
)
, materials science
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, power systems
(
Misyris et al. 2020
)
, and biomechanical engineering applications
(
Cavanagh et al. 2021
)
. Calibration in PIML is enhanced by embedding physical laws into modeling, which guides mechanism learning, reduces the design space, and improves interpretability and sample-efficiency.
For example, to model the dynamics of Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cell culture by using process data,
Cui et al. 2024
integrate physical laws, such as mass balances and kinetic expressions for metabolic fluxes, into digital twin modeling. However, this literature generally does not explicitly model an optimization problem, much less one where the decision variable is a control policy.

Overview and Problem Description

Section
3.1
presents the mathematical formalism used to model both the physical and digital systems. Section
3.2
presents a high-level overview of our algorithmic framework and explains the role of each type of system in the procedure.

3.1
Markov Decision Process Formulation

Throughout this paper, we model two related, but distinct stochastic systems: the
physical system
and the
digital twin
. Both systems involve sequential decisions made in an uncertain environment and they can be modeled using the language of Markov decision processes (MDPs). In this paper, we consider an infinite-horizon MDP represented by
\mathcal{M}=\left(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\mu,\mu_{0}\right)
that consists of a state space
\mathcal{S}
, an action space
\mathcal{A}
, a reward function
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a transition probability
\mu
modeling the probability (or likelihood)
\mu\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\mid\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}\right)
of being in state
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
after taking action
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}
out of state
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}
, and a probability density
\mu_{0}
representing the distribution of the initial state of the system. For notational simplicity, we use
\mu(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})
and
\mu_{0}(\cdot)
to represent the corresponding probability measure.

Let
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}
be compact subsets of Euclidean space, with
\sup_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\|\boldsymbol{s}\|_{2}\leq U^{S}
and
\sup_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\boldsymbol{a}\|_{2}\leq U^{A}
for some
U^{S},U^{A}<\infty
. In biomanufacturing applications, state and action variables are continuous, but bounded, as biological systems have inherent physiological and engineering limits, such as temperature and pH, constrained by the capabilities and tolerances of biological systems (e.g., RNAs, cells, and proteins). We further suppose that
{0\leq}r\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}\right)\leq r^{\max}
for some
r^{\max}<\infty
, as infinite rewards are not meaningful in practical applications. We use the notation
\mathcal{M}^{p}=\left(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\mu^{p},\mu_{0}\right)
and
\mathcal{M}^{d}=\left(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\mu^{d},\mu_{0}\right)
to refer to the physical system and digital twin, respectively. These MDPs use the same state and action spaces, reward function, and initial state distribution. They differ only in the transition probability kernel, reflecting the fact that the digital twin uses an imperfect model of the dynamics of the real-world system.

In biomanufacturing processes, the immediate effect of action
\boldsymbol{a}
(such as feeding strategy) on the state is often known. Then, by leveraging existing structural information on bioprocessing mechanisms,
we impose a parametric structure on the state-transition model so that
\mu^{p}\left(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}\right)
can be written as
\mu\left(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}\right)
, where the calibration parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}
in the biomanufacturing systems characterize the underlying reaction network regulation mechanisms, such as how cell growth, nutrient uptake, and metabolic waste generation rates depend on environmental conditions.
The digital twin will use the same parametric function
\mu
, but with an estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
that is built on the historical data denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Thus, the practitioner has some structural knowledge of the dynamics, represented by the function
\mu
. In the field of biochemistry, this knowledge typically comes from “kinetic” or “mechanistic” physics-based models
(
Mandenius et al. 2013
)
. However, the true values
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}
of the uncertain parameters are difficult to estimate, and the dynamics may be highly sensitive to their values. An important distinguishing feature of our work is that, aside from the presence of parameters, we do not impose additional structure on the dynamics. For example, we do not require transitions to be linear in the state variable
(
Simchowitz et al. 2018
)
or some transformation thereof
(
Wagenmaker et al. 2023
)
.

A generic MDP
\mathcal{M}
is controlled through a policy that takes in a state
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}
and chooses an action probabilistically. We denote by
\pi\left(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{s}\right)
the probability of choosing action
\boldsymbol{a}
out of state
\boldsymbol{s}
under policy
\pi
. The expected infinite-horizon discounted reward earned by a policy
\pi
is denoted by

(1)

where
\gamma

is taken over the distribution
\mu_{0}
of the initial state
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
and over the conditional distributions
\mu\left(\boldsymbol{s}_{n+1}\mid\boldsymbol{s}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\right)
of each state
\boldsymbol{s}_{n+1}
given the previous state
\boldsymbol{s}_{n}
, and under the assumption that
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}
is chosen with probability
\pi\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\right)
. It is well-known
(

)
that, if
\mathcal{M}
is stationary and ergodic,
J\left(\pi;\mathcal{M}\right)
admits the equivalent representation

\boldsymbol{s}_{0}

. Similarly, we define the value function, i.e.,

\displaystyle V^{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{s};\mathcal{M}\right)\vcentcolon={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[\left.\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\gamma^{n}r\left(\boldsymbol{s}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\right)\right|\boldsymbol{s}_{0}=\boldsymbol{s}\right],

to be the value earned by policy
\pi
when starting at a particular state
\boldsymbol{s}
.

Since each experiment is expensive, our
objective
in this paper is to calibrate the digital twin model parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
to accelerate the search for the optimal policy

\pi^{\star}\vcentcolon=\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi}J\left(\pi;\mathcal{M}^{p}\right),

within some class
\Pi
, for the physical system
\mathcal{M}^{p}
.

3.2
General Framework for Joint Calibration and Optimization

While the physical system and digital twin are closely related, the decision-maker interacts with them in very different ways. Interactions with the physical system take the form of experiments performed at selected state-action pairs
\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}\right)
, resulting in observations of the next state
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\sim\mu\left(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}\right)
.
Therefore, the collection of experiments and their observed results is denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1})\}_{i=0}^{n}
, where
n
is typically small because each experiment is typically expensive. Note that, in the context of MDPs, these data are neither independent nor identically distributed.

On the other hand, given some estimate of model parameter
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, the digital twin can simulate
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\sim\mu(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
, which is much less expensive compared with the physical system. In fact, we can simulate an entire trajectory of states in a fraction of the time or cost that it would take to collect a single observation from the physical system. Given the wide array of reinforcement learning algorithms available in the literature, we may assume that it is relatively inexpensive to
optimize
the MDP
\mathcal{M}^{d}
under any particular estimated
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, or even with other modifications, e.g., to the reward function. In this way, we can obtain a policy that is optimal for this MDP, but not, of course, for the physical system. We therefore require a
calibration
mechanism that uses the historical data
\mathcal{D}_{n}
obtained from the physical system to configure the digital twin with some
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
so that we can efficiently reduce the digital twin prediction error measured by the uncertainty function
u
(see more details in Section
4
).

To configure the digital twin
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
with some
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
, letting
\hat{\pi}_{n}
be the optimal policy of
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
(again, assuming that this is relatively easy to obtain), our overall goal is to ensure and accelerate the convergence of
\hat{\pi}_{n}
to the optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
. An important aspect of our work is that each
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
will be used to guide the acquisition of new data.

The proposed Actor-Simulator approach works by iteratively calibrating the digital twin and is outlined in Algorithm
1
that iteratively calibrates the digital twin and searches the optimal policy for the real system. Basically, in the
n
-th iteration, the algorithm performs three steps:

(i)

Digital Twin Calibration:
Given the state
\boldsymbol{s}_{n-1}
and the candidate policy
\hat{\pi}_{n-1}
from the previous iteration, we select an action
\boldsymbol{a}_{n-1}
to maximize information gain, execute it in the physical system, and update
\mathcal{D}_{n}

by adding
\left(\boldsymbol{s}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{a}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\right)
.

(ii)

Model Estimation:
We use the updated dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
to build an estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
.

(iii)

Policy Optimization:
To account for the risk from model discrepancy, we construct the uncertainty-penalized reward
\tilde{r}
and the modified MDP
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{d}
with the estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
and
\hat{\pi}_{n-1}
, and then solve it for the updated policy estimate
\hat{\pi}_{n}
.

Note that the proposed Actor-Simulator algorithm closely integrates digital twin calibration and policy optimization.
The policy
\hat{\pi}_{n}
obtained in the policy optimization step is our estimate of
\pi^{\star}
, but it is also used in the digital twin calibration step in the selection of next action
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}
, though the precise mechanism is more complicated than simply choosing the action prescribed by the policy. We see, however, that the two systems work together: data from the physical system are used to calibrate the digital twin, and the updated policy from the digital twin is used to guide the collection of new data.
We call this framework “Actor-Simulator,” inspired by actor-critic methods from the reinforcement learning literature
(
Konda and Tsitsiklis 2003
)
. The key difference is that those methods worked only with a physical system, and updated a policy iteratively after each action. In our setting, the presence of a digital twin allows us to learn an optimal policy for an MDP very easily; the difficulty is that this policy is optimal only for an approximation of the physical system.

A more formal statement of the Actor-Simulator procedure is given in Algorithm
1
. It can be seen that both the calibration and optimization steps depend on an uncertainty function
u
. The formal definition of this criterion is deferred to Section
4
,
but in brief, it is an upper bound on the discrepancy between the predicted value of
\hat{\pi}_{n}
under the true and approximate dynamics, respectively. In other words, it quantifies the error made by the digital twin in computing the value of the current policy. As
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
becomes more accurate, this discrepancy is reduced.

Calibration and optimization use the uncertainty function in very different ways. When we perform calibration, we interact directly with the physical system, giving us an opportunity to collect new data and improve the accuracy of the digital twin. We therefore look for actions with high uncertainty, because learning about these will tend to yield the greatest information gain and reduction in
u
. Thus, step 1 in Algorithm
1
is risk-seeking, preferring higher uncertainty.

On the other hand, when we perform policy optimization, we are working only with the digital twin, and do not interact with the physical system at all. In this step, we are not able to collect any real data to improve our estimates. In such a situation, maximizing
u
will have the effect of amplifying the inaccuracies in the digital twin model, leading to erratic and unstable results. Instead, in steps 5-6 of Algorithm
1
, we use
u
to
penalize
the reward of
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
, leading to risk-averse policies. The distinction between risk-seeking calibration and risk-averse optimization is intentional and important for practical performance.

:
K
maximum number of iterations, uncertainty penalization parameter
\lambda
, reward function
r(\cdot,\cdot)
, and initial measure
\mu_{0}

Output
: calibrated dynamics
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{K}
and the optimal policy
\hat{\pi}_{K}

parameters of digital twin
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}
, initial state
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}(\cdot)
, and
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\varnothing

for

n=1,2,\ldots,K

do

(i) Digital Twin Calibration:

1. Given state
\boldsymbol{s}_{n-1}\in\mathcal{S}
and parameter
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n-1}
, compute the action

\boldsymbol{a}_{n-1}=\argmax\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}}u(\boldsymbol{s}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n-1},\hat{\pi}_{n-1})
;

2. Observe the next state
\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\sim{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n}|\boldsymbol{s}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{a}_{n-1};\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star})
(physical system);

3. Update the historical dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\mathcal{D}_{n-1}\cup\{(\boldsymbol{s}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{a}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{s}_{n})\}
;

(ii) Model Estimation:

4. Use the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
to update the model parameter estimate
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n}
;

(iii) Policy Optimization:

5. Compute the uncertainty-penalized reward

\tilde{r}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\vcentcolon=r(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})-\lambda u(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n-1})
and the modified MDP

\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{d}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\tilde{r},{\mu}(\cdot;\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}),\mu_{0})
;

Use a RL algorithm to solve
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{d}
and return the updated policy
\hat{\pi}_{n}
.

4
The Actor-Simulator: Algorithmic Overview

As described in Section
3.2
, the proposed Actor-Simulator framework consists of three key steps. Sections
4.1
-
4.3
give precise specifications of model estimation, calibration, and actor update for policy optimization.
We also present theoretical properties of each step that will eventually be relevant with the convergence analysis in Section
5
.

4.1
Model Estimation

Suppose that we are given the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
collected from the physical system.
We use maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), which minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence or, equivalently, maximizes the log-likelihood denoted by
\ell(\cdot)
, i.e.,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}D_{KL}\left(\mu(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}\right)\|\mu(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}))=\argmax_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})].

In practice, one uses the empirical expectation, giving rise to the problem

\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n}\vcentcolon=\argmax_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\ell(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

(2)

where
\ell(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\vcentcolon=\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1}|\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is the log-likelihood.
It is worth noting that the
L_{2}
distance used by
Tuo and Wu 2015b
is a special case of (
2
) under Gaussian assumptions on the underlying distribution.

The consistency of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
is not a foregone conclusion, because the data in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
do not obey standard independent assumptions. For dependent observations, strong consistency and asymptotic normality have been investigated under various regularity conditions and assumptions on the nature of the dependence
(
Crowder 1976
;
Heijmans and Magnus 1986
;
Tinkl 2013
)
. In this paper, the model estimation builds on the results of
Tinkl 2013
, that establishes the asymptotic normality of the MLE under the assumption that the sample path is a strictly stationary and ergodic
\alpha
-mixing process (Definition
1
).

Definition 1

(
Bradley 2005
)
.

Suppose
\{Y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}=\{(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1})\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a strictly stationary sequence of random vectors and ergodic for any
policy
\pi\in\Pi
. The
\alpha
-mixing coefficient
\alpha(T)
at time
T
is defined as:

\displaystyle{\alpha(T)}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-\infty},B\in\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{T}}\left|P(A\cap B)-P(A)P(B)\right|

where
\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-\infty}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{T}
represent the sigma-algebra generated by
\{Y_{i}:i\leq 0\}
and
\{Y_{i}:i\geq T\}
respectively.

We first present the necessary regularity conditions in Assumption
1
, followed by the main asymptotic result in Lemma
1
.

Assumption 1

(Regularity Conditions)
.

The following conditions hold:

(a)

The
\alpha
-mixing process
\{(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1})\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is strictly stationary and ergodic for any policy
\pi\in\Pi
.

(b)

The log-likelihood function
\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is continuous in model parameters
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{B}
for all
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})
and
\mathbb{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact.

(c)

The expected log-likelihood is bounded,
{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})]<\infty
for all
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})
.

(d)

For every compact set
G
containing
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}
, it holds
\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})>\mathop{\sup}\limits_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\notin G}\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
and also

\mathop{\lim\sup}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathop{\sup}_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{B}}\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\ell(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\leq\mathop{\sup}_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{B}}\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})

almost surely (a.s.), where the expected log-likelihood function is defined as,

\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\vcentcolon={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\sim{\mu}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})d^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}^{p}}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})}[\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})].

(3)

(e)

\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is continuously differentable w.r.t.
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
almost everywhere. For the derivative of log-likelihood function
\nabla\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
, there exists an integrable function
L(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})
such that
\|\nabla\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\|\leq L(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})
in a neighborhood of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}
.

(f)

The derivative
\nabla\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is measureable for all
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and differentiable w.r.t.
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
almost everywhere. For the second-order derivative
\nabla^{2}\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
, there exists an integrable function
U(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})
such that
\|\nabla^{2}\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\|\leq U(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})
in a neighborhood of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}
.

(g)

{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla^{2}\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]
is negative definite for any
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

These regularity conditions are collected from
Tinkl 2013
, Corollary 4.3.12 and
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
, Lemma 3.2.1. For the asymptotic convergence analysis in Section
5
, we only require mild conditions (Assumption
1
(
a
-
d
)).
In contrast, the additional conditions (e-g) are required for establishing the asymptotic normality in Lemma
1
and used in approximating the uncertainty function in Section
4.2
.

Lemma 1

(
Tinkl 2013
, Corollary 4.3.12)
.

Let the trajectory
\{(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1})\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies Assumption
1
(a-d). Then for the estimator
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n}
defined
by (
2
), we have

\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n}\xrightarrow{a.s.}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}.

In addition, if Assumption
1
(e-g) are met, we further have

\sqrt{n}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}))

where
\Sigma({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\vcentcolon=-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla^{2}\ell(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})^{\top}]^{-1}
is the inverse of Fisher information matrix.

We note that, in general, the asymptotic behavior of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
is challenging to characterize. Virtually all results of this type assume some particular structure on the sample dependence. Lemma
1
is not the only possible result. For example, by assuming
\phi
-mixing,

Crowder 1976
presented a different rate of convergence based on the sequence of smallest eigenvalues of Fisher information matrices.

The
\alpha
-mixing condition (Definition
1
) is less restrictive than what has been assumed in the recent RL literature. Notably, recent theoretical analyses of RL algorithms often rely on a stronger geometric ergodicity condition
(
Bhandari et al. 2018
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2020
;
Zheng et al. 2021
)
, which requires the transition probability distribution to converge to a stationary distribution at an exponentially fast rate. Under the
\alpha
-mixing assumption, we can obtain additional results on the tail behavior of the conditional expected log-likelihood,

\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\ell\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\right)\right|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right].

The following result presented in Lemma
2
is obtained by applying the delta method to the asymptotic normality in Lemma
1

and invoking
Theorem 2.4 (Prohorov’s theorem) in
Van der Vaart 2000
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
X_{n}\vcentcolon=\sqrt{n}({\mathcal{L}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n})-\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}))
. If Assumption
1
holds, then
\{X_{n}\}
is uniformly bounded in probability, i.e., for every
\delta>0
, there exists a large deviation
M_{n,\delta}>0
such that

\sup_{n\geq 1}P(\|X_{n}\|>M_{n,\delta})<\delta,

where
M_{n,\delta}
depends on both sample size
n
and tail probability
\delta
.

Using Lemma
2
, we can also show a convergence rate as a function of

for the expected loss in Theorem

. The proof is given in Appendix

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that we are in the situation of Lemma

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}\right)-\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}\right)\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\frac{M_{n,\delta}}{\sqrt{n}},\delta\right\}\right).

4.2
Digital Twin Calibration

Given any candidate policy
\pi
, when the digital twin is used to predict the performance of the physical system

J\left(\pi;\mathcal{M}^{p}\right)
,
the main issue is to relate the calibration error, i.e., the error in the estimated parameters
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, to the error in the estimate of the RL objective function made by the digital twin. The following result from
Yu et al. 2020
makes this relationship explicit.

Lemma 3

(
Yu et al. 2020
, Theorem 4.4)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{p}
and
\mathcal{M}^{d}
be two MDPs with the same reward function
r
, but different dynamics
\mu^{p}
and
\mu^{d}
, respectively. For any policy
\pi\in\Pi
, let
G^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\vcentcolon={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\sim\mu^{d}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})}\left[V^{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};\mathcal{M}^{p}\right)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\sim{{\mu^{p}}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})}\left[V^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};\mathcal{M}^{p})\right]
. Then,

J\left(\pi;\mathcal{M}^{d}\right)-J\left(\pi;\mathcal{M}^{p}\right)=\gamma{\mathbb{E}}_{(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\sim d_{\mathcal{M}^{d}}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s})\pi(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}\left[G^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\right].

Lemma
3
establishes the calibration discrepancy between the digital twin and the physical system through the quantity
\mathop{{\mathbb{E}}}_{(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\sim d_{\mathcal{M}^{d}}^{\pi}(s)\pi(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}\left[G^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\right]
. However, this quantity depends on the value function of the physical system and is difficult to compute. To address this issue, we apply Theorem 2.1 from
Bolley and Villani 2005
to propose a more tractable upper bound on
G^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})
in Theorem
2
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose we are given a policy
\pi\in\Pi
and an estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
. Define the uncertainty function of the state-action pair
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
as

\displaystyle u(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\pi)\vcentcolon=\sqrt{2}\left(1+\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)}\left[e^{V^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};\mathcal{M}^{p})^{2}}\right]\right)^{{1/2}}\sqrt{D_{KL}\left(\mu(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\|\mu(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right)}.

(4)

Then, we have

G^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\leq u(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\pi)

(5)

where
G^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})
is as in Lemma
3
.

The uncertainty function
u
incorporates the estimation error of the state transition model, represented by
D_{KL}\left(\mu(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\|\mu(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right)
, and a weight
1+\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)}\left[e^{V^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};\mathcal{M}^{p})^{2}}\right]
. The first term measures the pointwise discrepancy between the state transition models of the digital twin and the physical system. The second term relates the uncertainty quantification to the objective value of the policy by including the value function. Combining Lemma
3
and Theorem
2
yields

|J(\pi;\mathcal{M}^{d})-J(\pi;\mathcal{M}^{p})|\leq{\gamma}{\mathbb{E}}_{(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\sim d_{\mathcal{M}^{d}}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{s})\pi(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}\left[u(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\pi)\right].

(6)

Eq. (
6
) shows how the uncertainty function
u(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\pi)
connects the physical and digital systems. Recall from Algorithm
1
that
u
is used in both digital twin calibration and policy optimization. We will not repeat the details here; instead, in the remainder of this subsection, we will focus on the computation of
u
.

From (
4
), we see that
u
depends on
\mathcal{M}^{p}
, and therefore cannot be computed exactly. Thus, we develop an approximation as follows. Given
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n})
, we denote

{w(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n})=2\left(1+\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}}\right)}\left[e^{V^{\hat{\pi}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};\mathcal{M}^{p})^{2}}\right]\right)}

(7)

for convenience. Observe that, by the definition of the KL divergence,

\displaystyle u^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n}\right)=w\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n}\right){\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{p}}\left[\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})-\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n})\right].

(8)

We then take a second-order Taylor expansion of
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{p}}[\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n})]
at
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}
. Since

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{p}}[\nabla\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})]=\int\nabla{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=\nabla\int{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=0,

we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{p}}[\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n})]\approx{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{p}}\left[\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\right]+\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})^{\top}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{p}}\left[\nabla^{2}\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\right](\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}).

(9)

By substituting
{\mathbb{E}}[\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n})]
in (
8
) with its approximation (
9
), we have

u^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n}\right)\approx w\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n}\right)\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})},

(10)

where the
conditional
Fisher information matrix, denoted by

\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\vcentcolon=-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{p}}\left[\nabla^{2}\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\right],

is used to design the calibration criteria
u
that can guide the selection of next action
\boldsymbol{a}
for given current state
\boldsymbol{s}
, maximizing information gain. The term
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})}
is a Fisher information matrix weighted Euclidean distance that measures the discrepancy between the estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
and the true value

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}
, taking into account the variability and correlation of the estimates as captured by the information matrix. This essentially scales each component of the difference
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}
according to the information gain. We assume that
\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})
is nonsingular unless specified otherwise; such assumptions are common in the literature on experimental design for nonlinear models
(
Yang et al. 2013
)
.

Due to the asymptotic normality of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
as shown in Lemma
1
, we may apply the continuous mapping theorem to obtain

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})}\xrightarrow{d}G(v)

where
G(v)
is a weighted chi-square distribution
(
Solomon and Stephens 1977
)
with
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{d})
denoting the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\Sigma({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})
, where
\Sigma({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})
is the asymptotic covariance matrix obtained from Lemma
1
. From the properties of this distribution,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[G\left(v\right)\right]=\sum^{d}_{i=1}v_{i}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\Sigma({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\star})\right).

(11)

We now simply use plug-in estimators to write

u^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n}\right)\approx\hat{w}\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n}\right)\mathrm{Tr}({\mathcal{I}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})}\Sigma(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}))

(12)

where

{\hat{w}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n})=2\left(1+\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n})}\left[e^{V^{\hat{\pi}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime};\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n})^{2}}\right]\right)}

uses the digital twin
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
from the previous iteration instead of
\mathcal{M}^{p}
.

In practice, the asymptotic covariance can be estimated by using the inverse of the Hessian matrix,
\Sigma(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n})^{-1}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\nabla^{2}\ell(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n})
.
The estimation of the conditional Fisher information matrix, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}({\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})=-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{p}}\left[\nabla^{2}\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}})\right]
, for Gaussian state transition model is detailed in Appendix
F
.

4.3
Policy Optimization

As explained in Section
3.2
, this step produces an updated optimal policy candidate for the real system by using the digital twin
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
constructed in the
n
-th iteration. The process of actually computing this policy is not studied in this paper; we simply assume that, owing to the ease of simulating state transitions for
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
, any modern local search RL algorithm such as policy gradient can be applied to learn the policy
(
Ding et al. 2024
;
Cayci et al. 2024
)
. Our focus here is rather on the
design
of
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
to guide policy optimization accounting for model discrepancy.

Based on the digital twin with the updated state transition model
\mu(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n})
, the modified MDP, denoted by
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{d}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\tilde{r},{\mu}(\cdot;\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}),\mu_{0})
in Algorithm
1
, differs from
\mathcal{M}^{p}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},{r},{\mu}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}),\mu_{0})
of the physical system in two ways. First, the true parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}
are replaced by the estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
computed in the model estimation step (Section
4.1
). Second, the reward function of the digital twin is augmented as

\tilde{r}\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}\right)=r\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}\right)-\lambda u\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n},\hat{\pi}_{n-1}\right),

(13)

where
\lambda=c\gamma
with a tunable parameter
c\in[1,\infty)
and
u
is the uncertainty function accounting for the impact of model discrepancy. This function is the key to maintaining the connection between the physical and digital systems.
It is important to note that
u
depends on both
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
and
\hat{\pi}_{n-1}
, that is, both the statistical problem of parameter estimation, and the optimization problem of finding an optimal policy for the physical system, contribute to the uncertainty.

5
Convergence Analysis

The goal of this section is to show the convergence result, i.e.,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}J\left(\hat{\pi}_{n};\mathcal{M}^{p}\right)=J\left(\pi^{\star};\mathcal{M}^{p}\right)
, a form of asymptotic optimality, in the
physical
system, of the sequence
\left\{\hat{\pi}_{n}\right\}
of policies obtained from the respective digital systems
\left\{\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}\right\}
.

In our framework, the key link between the physical and digital systems is the uncertainty function
u
. Recall from (
6
) that the uncertainty function bounds the discrepancy between the value of a given policy in the physical and digital systems. The following result presented in Theorem
3
shows that, with the proposed Actor-Simulator framework outlined in Algorithm
1
,
this bound vanishes to zero almost surely (a.s.) as
n\rightarrow\infty
;
the proof is provided in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that the sample trajectory
\{(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{i+1})\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of the physical system satisfies Assumption
1
. Let
\pi
be a fixed policy, and suppose that
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n}
is computed according to (
2
). Then, for any
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we have

u\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{n},\pi\right)\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

(14)

We now connect this result to the asymptotic convergence of the policy
\{\hat{\pi}_{n}\}
produced by our framework. Recall that the policy is obtained by optimally solving the MDP
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
whose reward function
\tilde{r}
is penalized by the uncertainty function as in (
13
). Lemma
4
shows that this design produces a conservative estimate of the reward earned under the physical system
\mathcal{M}^{p}
, regardless of the policy.

Lemma 4

(
Yu et al. 2020
)
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Fix a policy
\pi
and an estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, and let
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
be an MDP with transition probability model
\mu(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
and reward function

\tilde{r}\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}\right)=r\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}\right)-\lambda u\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\pi\right).

where
\lambda=c\gamma
with
c\in[1,\infty)
. Then,
J(\pi;\tilde{\mathcal{M}})\leq J\left(\pi;\mathcal{M}^{p}\right)
.

Building on Theorem
3
and Lemma
4
, we proceed to establish the asymptotic convergence of policy optimization, as stated in Theorem
4
. To prove this result, we first derive an upper bound for the performance gap,
J(\pi^{\star};\mathcal{M}^{p})-J(\hat{\pi}_{n};\mathcal{M}^{p})
, using Lemma
4
. Since this bound is controlled by the uncertainty function
u
, we demonstrate that it converges almost surely to zero by leveraging Theorem
3

6.1
iPSC Culture Example

The design and control of iPSC cultures consists of adjusting cultivation conditions in a way that enhances cell growth, viability, and production consistency.
The performance of iPSCs is known to be very sensitive to small changes in the environmental conditions such as nutrient starvation and too much metabolic waste accumulation leading to reduced yields, compromised cell quality, and potential risks to patients. These challenges cannot be easily overcome with existing cell culture control strategies, which are largely ad hoc in nature
(
Dressel 2011
)
.

This problem provides a good example of a situation where practitioners have some structural knowledge about the dynamics of cellular metabolism, but with significant uncertainty remaining about key parameters characterizing the underlying bioprocessing regulation mechanisms. We use the metabolic reaction network model proposed by
Wang et al. 2023
, which is composed of differential equations modeling

30
reactions of central metabolic pathways,
34
state dimensions, and
73
parameters.
Figure

The control variable is a feeding strategy, i.e., the percentage of medium exchange (addition of fresh medium to the bioreactor and removal of spent medium).

We consider dynamic changes in state variables
\boldsymbol{s}(t)=\left(X(t),\boldsymbol{u}(t)^{\top}\right)^{\top}
, where
X(t)
is cell density and
\boldsymbol{u}\left(t\right)
is a vector representing concentrations of extracellular metabolites at any time

\Delta t=4

n

In biopharmaceutical manufacturing, the immediate impact of action on the state change is often given. The action depends on the ratio of the spent medium, denoted by a continuous variable
0\leq b\leq 1

The feeding decision

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}X(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=(\mu-\mu_{d})X(t)

(17)

\displaystyle\mu_{d}

\displaystyle=k_{d}\cdot\frac{\mbox{[ELAC]}}{\mbox{[ELAC]}+K_{Dlac}}

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mu_{max}\frac{\mbox{[GLC]}}{K_{glc}+\mbox{[GLC]}}\frac{\mbox{[EGLN]}}{K_{glc}+\mbox{[EGLN]}}\frac{K_{Ilac}}{K_{Ilac}+\mbox{[ELAC]}}

In the context of iPSC cultures, the metabolic regulation model calibration parameters

7
Conclusions

This paper introduced the Actor-Simulator, a novel framework for joint digital twin model calibration and policy optimization of complex stochastic systems.
The advantage of a digital twin is that it can easily be run and optimized. However, a policy that is optimal for the digital twin will be suboptimal for the physical system due to the presence of model calibration error. Our approach seeks to collect the most informative data, reduce the calibration error, and accelerate finding an optimal policy for the physical system.
We proved asymptotic optimality of the policy learned by the Actor-Simulator, and empirically demonstrated its effectiveness in finite sample performance
by using a real-case driven bioharmaceutical manufacturing example.

As the demand for efficient and scalable solutions in biomanufacturing continues to grow, tools like the Actor-Simulator algorithm will play a pivotal role in enhancing production capabilities
and reducing the costs of essential life-saving biopharmaceutical products.
The proposed framework is general and it can be useful for applications in other fields such as
epidemiological forecasting and control, and large-scale supply chain and manufacturing systems automation,
where the accuracy and cost of digital twin model parameter calibration can significantly influence outcomes.

It is worth emphasizing that the framework considered in this paper is much more general than previous work on calibration
(
Li et al. 2019
;
Treloar et al. 2022
)
, which focused on simpler dynamical systems with fewer than 10 parameters and state space dimensions. We also work with general nonlinear dynamics, a setting where parameter estimation poses considerable challenges. In our implementation, we tested multiple fitting methods, including least squares, Nelder-Mead, L-BFGS-B and others, but none of them consistently achieved reliable results.
Therefore, in the proposed framework, we developed the digital twin in PyTorch and used the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma and Ba 2015
)
and PyTorch’s “autograd” feature to compute Hessians and information matrices in digital twin model maximum likelihood estimation.

Appendix F
Estimation of Information Matrix

Suppose the state transition model follows the general form of
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}=f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, where
f(\cdot,\cdot;\boldsymbol{\beta})
is the deterministic mean function,
\boldsymbol{s}
is the current state,
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
is the next state, and
\boldsymbol{a}
is the action.
The term
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}

follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution with
zero mean and covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Thus the log-likelihood becomes
\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta}))^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta}))-\log(2\pi\Sigma)
.

We consider the conditional information matrix,

\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})=-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla^{2}\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right].

By taking second-order derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, we have

\displaystyle-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\log{\mu}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}}-(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta}))\Sigma^{-1}\frac{\partial^{2}f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right].

Given the expectation that
{\mathbb{E}}[\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})]=0
, the second term involving
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})
averages to zero. By simplifying the expression, the conditional information matrix
[\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta}|\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})]=\left(\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.
The gradient of
the model output with respect to parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
can be computed through the model
f(\cdot,\cdot;\boldsymbol{\beta})
using the automatic differentiation library.

For the general exponential family, the information matrix can be estimated by using Lemma 3.1 of
Overstall 2022
.
The Fisher information matrix considers the structural information in the dynamic model, then elucidates how uncertainties in parameter estimation propagate, ultimately influencing predictions and inferences.

Appendix G
Experiment Setup

We conducted the empirical experiments using a metabolic reaction network simulator designed to replicate the dynamic behavior of cell cultures under various enzymatic and
environmental conditions.
The initial metabolic state was selected based on the iPSC culture experimental data derived from
Odenwelder et al. 2021
, Additionally, a 20% perturbation was added to sample the initial state.

Simulation Configuration.
The metabolic network was configured to test various max enzymatic reaction rates (e.g.,
v_{max,PDH}
,
v_{max,CS}
) and substrate affinities (e.g.,
K_{m,GLC}
,
K_{m,GLU}
); see the detailed model in Appendix
H
and
Wang et al. 2023
. The number of calibration parameters varied in three case study settings with
20
,
30
, and
40

parameters.
For each case study, we initialized the digital twin by running five experiments with random medium exchange ratios and each experiment trajectory comprising 12 timesteps. We then conducted 100 sequential physical experiments to collect additional data.
At every
n
-th iteration, the historical dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
is used to update the model parameter estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n}
. The conditional Fisher Information matrix
{\mathcal{I}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n};\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})}
and Hessian matrix
\Sigma(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{n})
are further computed for estimating the uncertainty function (
11
) that is used in the calibration policy. During preliminary exploration, we observed the training was vulnerable to overfitting due to high sample dependence. To mitigate this, we employed an early-stopping strategy. We performed 30 macro-replications to ensure robust performance metrics. Finally, we evaluated the test error (e.g., the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)) by running the the estimated optimal policy on the physical system for 2,000 episodes.
The reward function parameters are set
c_{r}=30
$/g,
c_{m}=120
$/100%,
c_{l}=84
$/g.

Policy Optimization and Agent Configuration.
Given state
\boldsymbol{s}
, to learn an optimal action
\boldsymbol{a}
, a Deep Q-Network (DQN)
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
agent consists of a feed-forward neural network with two fully connected hidden layers, each containing 64 neurons, followed by ReLU activation functions. The input layer dimension corresponds to the size of the state space
input_dims
, and the output layer produces a Q-value, denoted by
Q(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\theta})
, for each possible action (
n_actions
). That means for a given state
\boldsymbol{s}
, the network computes
Q(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\theta})
for each possible
\boldsymbol{a}
in
\mathcal{A}
.

The agent uses the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma and Ba 2015
)
with a learning rate
0.001
and a small L2 regularization constant of
10^{-10}
. The loss function is the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted Q-values and the TD target. To stabilize training, the agent maintains a replay buffer with a fixed maximum size (e.g.,
100{,}000
transitions). At each timestep, the agent stores
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a},r,\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})
in the memory. For training, a mini-batch of size
\texttt{batch\_size}=64
is sampled uniformly at random. The discount factor is set as
\gamma=0.99
.

Epsilon-Greedy Exploration.
To balance exploration and exploitation, the agent employs an
\epsilon
-greedy policy. Formally, the agent’s action selection is given by:

\boldsymbol{a}=\begin{cases}\arg\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime};\boldsymbol{\theta})&\text{with probability }(1-\epsilon),\\
\mbox{Uniform Sampling}&\text{with probability }\epsilon.\end{cases}

With probability
\epsilon
, we select an action
\boldsymbol{a}=\pi(\boldsymbol{s};b)
with
b\sim\mbox{Uniform}(0,1)
; otherwise, the action with the highest predicted Q-value is chosen. Initially,
\epsilon
is set to 0.6, decaying linearly by
\Delta\epsilon=5\times 10^{-4}
after each training step until it reaches a minimum, i.e.,
\epsilon_{\min}=0.01
. This gradual decay allows sufficient exploration in the early stages of learning while converging to a more exploitative policy once the environment has been well-sampled.

The agent was trained using a batch size of 64, iterating through the environmental states and adjusting actions based on
the corresponding rewards
r(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})
, and the uncertainty function, i.e.,
u\left(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta},\pi\right)
.
Model training was guided by maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). Action selection is directed by maximizing information gain, specifically selecting the action with the highest
u(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a};\boldsymbol{\beta},\pi)
.
In addition, each experiment was seeded to ensure reproducibility, with results collected for actions, states, rewards, and optimized parameters over the course of the simulations. These results were stored systematically for further analysis and model parameter calibration.
