Uric Acid–to–HDL cholesterol ratio and probable MASLD across adiposity strata in postmenopausal women
Authors: Ifigeneia Avgousti, Iliana Karagkouni, Dimitrios S. Karagiannakis, Theodoros Kalampokas, Aikaterini Stefanaki, Stavroula Paschou, Panagiotis Alexandrou, Peter Chedraui, Irene Lambrinoudaki, Areti Augoulea, Eleni Armeni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo examine the association between serum uric acid–to–HDL cholesterol ratio (UHR) and probable metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) in postmenopausal women, and to assess whether this association varies across body mass index (BMI) strata.MethodsThis cross-sectional study evaluated 2647 postmenopausal women. Among first-visit participants with complete covariate data (n = 375), 67.7% met criteria for probable MASLD. Among participants with elastography data, 37/77 (48.1%) met CAP-defined MASLD criteria. Steatotic liver disease was defined according to contemporary MASLD consensus criteria. Alcohol intake was uniformly low, with no participants meeting criteria for moderate or high consumption. UHR was ln-transformed and analysed using multivariable, sensitivity, and BMI-stratified logistic regression models with standardised effect estimates.ResultsIn primary multivariable analyses, each 1-SD increase in ln-UHR was associated with higher odds of probable MASLD (OR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.15–3.29; p = 0.014), while BMI remained the dominant predictor. ROC analyses demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.70), although incremental discrimination beyond established metabolic markers was limited. While broader metabolic adjustment attenuated effect estimates, ln-UHR remained directionally consistent across sensitivity analyses. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested possible stronger associations among women with BMI <30 kg/m2.ConclusionUHR was associated with probable MASLD and may reflect broader metabolic vulnerability alongside anthropometric measures in postmenopausal women. Findings are associative and hypothesis-generating; prospective external validation is required before clinical translation.

Introduction

Metabolic steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously termed NAFLD, affects approximately 30% of the global population and represents a major driver of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and cardiometabolic morbidity [1,2]. Its prevalence is strongly associated with obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes mellitus, with particularly high rates among metabolically vulnerable populations [3].

The uric acid–to–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) has emerged as a composite marker of metabolic dysfunction, integrating the pro-inflammatory and insulin resistance–related effects of uric acid with the anti-inflammatory properties of HDL cholesterol. UHR has demonstrated superior performance compared with uric acid or HDL cholesterol alone in identifying insulin resistance, cardiovascular disease, and hepatic steatosis [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. However, evidence regarding its relevance in postmenopausal women remains limited.

Postmenopausal estrogen deficiency is associated with adverse metabolic remodelling, including increased visceral adiposity, dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance, and heightened susceptibility to MASLD [12,13]. Although MASLD is increasingly recognized in individuals without overt obesity, biomarkers that capture hepatic metabolic vulnerability beyond anthropometric measures remain insufficiently defined in postmenopausal women. To date, no study has specifically evaluated the uric acid–to–HDL cholesterol ratio (UHR) in relation to probable MASLD within an exclusively postmenopausal cohort or explored whether this association differs across adiposity strata, including lean and nonobese phenotypes. Accordingly, this study examined the association between UHR and probable MASLD in postmenopausal women using contemporary consensus criteria, while also assessing whether UHR may identify metabolically vulnerable phenotypes beyond conventional obesity thresholds.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional analysis of postmenopausal women attending a University Menopause Clinic. Women were evaluated for menopause management, bone health, or cardiometabolic risk as part of routine clinical care. Participants were recruited consecutively between June 2023 and October 2025 at their first clinic visit. The study protocol was revised and approved by the Ethics Committee of Aretaieio Hospital, National and Kapodistrian University of Athens, Greece (approval number: 518/30-05-2023). All women were informed of the study aims, procedures and provided written consent to participate. Consenting participants underwent transient elastography for liver health assessment.

Postmenopause was defined as the absence of menstruation for at least 12 consecutive months. Exclusion criteria included known diabetes mellitus, chronic liver disease other than metabolic steatotic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, renal or thyroid disease, malignancy, acute or chronic inflammatory conditions, pregnancy, recent antiviral therapy, and excessive alcohol consumption (>20 g/day).

Clinical, biochemical, and lifestyle assessment

Standardised questionnaires were used to collect demographic data, smoking status, menopausal hormone therapy use, medication history, alcohol intake, and physical activity. Anthropometric measurements were obtained using calibrated equipment, and BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared.

Fasting blood samples were collected and analysed for lipid profile, uric acid, liver enzymes, insulin, glucose, and SHBG using validated automated assays. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ).

Alcohol intake assessment

Alcohol intake was assessed by self-report. All participants reported low consumption (median 0.07 units/day; maximum 1.33 units/day), and none met criteria for moderate or high intake. Accordingly, participants were classified within the MASLD framework without MetALD or alcohol-associated liver disease subgroups.

Definition of steatotic liver disease

MASLD was defined according to the 2023 international consensus as hepatic steatosis [14], identified by controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and/or Hepatic Steatosis Index, in the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor. Because transient elastography was not uniformly available across all participants, a hierarchical steatosis ascertainment framework was prespecified, using CAP-based classification where available and validated HSI criteria otherwise. Given exclusion of participants with known diabetes mellitus, cardiometabolic dysfunction in this cohort was defined through overweight/obesity or other non-diabetic metabolic abnormalities, including dysglycaemia, dyslipidaemia, blood pressure abnormalities, or central adiposity. Participants with significant alcohol intake or other known causes of chronic liver disease were excluded.

Assessment of hepatic steatosis

Hepatic steatosis was assessed using transient elastography (FibroScan® 502 Touch, Echosens, Paris, France), with CAP used to quantify hepatic fat content. CAP thresholds were applied to define steatosis severity as S0 (<248 dB/m), S1 (248–268 dB/m), S2 (268–280 dB/m), and S3 (>280 dB/m), according to established cut-offs [15]. Measurements were performed by trained operators blinded to participants’ clinical and laboratory data.

In women without available CAP measurements, hepatic steatosis was evaluated using the Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI), calculated according to the validated original formula, HSI = 8 × (ALT/AST ratio) + BMI (+2 if female; +2 if diabetes present) [16]. HSI values < 30 were considered to exclude steatosis, while values > 36 were considered diagnostic.

Physical activity

Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), validated in the Greek population [17,18]. Participants reported the frequency and duration of walking, moderate, and vigorous activities during the previous week. Total physical activity was expressed as metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes per week and categorised as low, moderate, or high according to standard IPAQ scoring guidelines [19].

Biochemical measurements

Fasting plasma glucose and serum total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), HDL-C, triglycerides, and UA levels were measured using enzymatic colorimetric methods on automated analysers (i.e. Roche Cobas® 8000, Abbott Architect® c8000). The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) for these biochemical parameters were below 5% according to manufacturer specifications.

Serum gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) concentrations were measured using enzymatic colorimetric methods on an automated clinical chemistry analyser (Abbott Architect® c8000, Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA). AST and ALT assays were based on IFCC-recommended methods without pyridoxal phosphate activation, employing coupled enzymatic reactions with NADH consumption monitored photometrically at 340 nm. ALP activity was measured using a colorimetric method based on the hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) to p-nitrophenol at alkaline pH, with absorbance read at 405 nm. GGT was assessed using the γ-glutamyl-p-nitroanilide substrate method, in which enzymatic activity generates p-nitroaniline, measured at 410 nm. The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation for all liver enzyme measurements were below 5%, in accordance with manufacturer specifications.

Hormonal measurements

Fasting serum insulin was determined using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) on the Roche Elecsys® platform (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). The assay sensitivity was 0.2 μIU/mL, with intra- and inter-assay CVs below 5%. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentrations were measured using a chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) (DiaSorin LIAISON® 25 OH Vitamin D Total Assay, DiaSorin, Saluggia, Italy), with a detection limit of 4 ng/mL and inter-assay CV less than 8%. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index was used as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance and was calculated using fasting triglyceride and fasting glucose concentrations according to established methodology. For regression and comparative analyses, TyG values were standardized (z-scores) to improve interpretability across models.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Baseline clinical and biochemical characteristics were compared between women with and without steatotic liver disease using descriptive statistics, with continuous variables presented as mean ± SD and categorical variables as percentages. Continuous indices were standardized where appropriate. Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student's t-test or the χ2 test, as appropriate. Steatotic liver disease was defined using contemporary MASLD criteria. Primary analyses were conducted using the overall probable MASLD definition, while CAP-defined MASLD was examined separately in sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of findings using a stricter imaging-based definition. Univariate associations were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis.

To avoid intra-individual correlation and ensure independence of observations, analyses were restricted to the first available visit for each participant. Each individual, therefore, contributed a single observation, and longitudinal variability across visits was not modelled. The number of visits per participant varied, but only baseline data were included in the present analyses.

Standard logistic regression models were used, and analyses were conducted using complete cases for the variables included in each model; therefore, denominators vary across analyses. UHR was calculated as the log-transformed ratio of serum uric acid to HDL cholesterol x 100, and log-transformed (ln-UHR) for regression analyses.

Associations between UHR and MASLD were examined using logistic regression models. Multivariable models included clinically relevant covariates selected a priori. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, biological plausibility, and minimization of overadjustment or collinearity. Primary models, therefore, included age, BMI, TyG index, smoking, and years since menopause. Additional sensitivity analyses incorporating individual metabolic parameters (including fasting glucose, insulin, and triglycerides) were performed to assess robustness. BMI-stratified analyses were conducted to explore effect modification by adiposity. Given that HSI incorporates BMI and aminotransferase components, HSI-based classifications may partially share variance with predictors included in multivariable models; therefore, CAP-restricted and BMI-minimized sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate directional robustness. Given prior evidence that metabolic risk manifests differently across the menopausal transition, additional stratified analyses were performed by years since menopause (years since menopause <10 vs ≥ 10 years) to assess whether menopausal timing modified the association between UHR and MASLD.

Interaction terms (UHR × BMI and UHR × years since menopause category) were formally tested using likelihood ratio tests.

Subgroup analyses, including lean/nonobese strata and years-since-menopause categories, were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating given smaller subgroup sample sizes and reduced statistical power. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Study population and classification of steatotic liver disease

Across the consecutively recruited cohort of 2647 postmenopausal women, 376 participants met criteria for probable MASLD using combined CAP- and/or HSI-based contemporary consensus definitions. Primary inferential analyses were restricted to first-visit complete-case observations to ensure independence of observations. Among probable MASLD cases, 254 (67.6%) were identified using HSI criteria and 122 (32.4%) using CAP criteria, with partial overlap between ascertainment methods (n = 26).

As shown in Table 1, women with MASLD exhibited a significantly more adverse metabolic profile, with higher BMI, ALT, ALP, GGT, triglycerides, and UHR, and lower HDL-cholesterol and SHBG compared with those without MASLD. Age was modestly higher in the MASLD group, whereas AST, total cholesterol, and LDL-cholesterol did not differ meaningfully between groups. Current smoking prevalence did not differ significantly between groups, and was not associated with probable MASLD after multivariable adjustment. This non-significant pattern should be interpreted cautiously given potential residual confounding and limited statistical stability. In contrast, physical inactivity was significantly more common among women with probable MASLD. Multivariable logistic regression analyses

Predictors of MASLD

In the primary standardized multivariable logistic regression model (Table 3), each 1-SD increase in ln-UHR was associated with higher odds of probable MASLD (OR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.15–3.29; p = 0.014). BMI was the strongest predictor (OR = 2.85, p < 0.001), while older age was inversely associated with MASLD (OR = 0.93, p = 0.006). TyG index and smoking were not significantly associated. Given the incorporation of BMI within aspects of probable MASLD classification, BMI-adjusted findings should be interpreted as exploratory assessments of whether ln-UHR retains associative relevance beyond adiposity, rather than as definitive evidence of BMI-independent effects.

To assess the added predictive value of UHR, we compared nested models with and without ln-UHR. The base model (age, BMI, TyG index, smoking) demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.958; 95% CI, 0.940–0.977). Addition of ln-UHR resulted in only a minimal increase in discrimination (AUC = 0.961; 95% CI, 0.944–0.978; ΔAUC = 0.003). However, inclusion of ln-UHR significantly improved model fit (likelihood ratio test: Δχ2 = 11.19, df = 1, p < 0.001). Although ln-UHR improved overall model fit, its incremental discriminatory contribution beyond established metabolic markers was limited, supporting greater utility as an integrated metabolic risk-enrichment biomarker than as a stand-alone biomarker.

In multivariable analyses including physical activity, standardized ln-UHR remained associated with probable MASLD (OR = 1.88, 95% CI 1.07–3.30; p = 0.029), while BMI remained the dominant marker (OR = 2.92, 95% CI 2.23–3.84; p < 0.001). Higher physical activity was inversely associated with probable MASLD (OR = 0.45, 95% CI 0.24–0.85; p = 0.014), suggesting a potentially protective association independent of adiposity and major metabolic covariates. Model calibration remained adequate (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.333).

Exploratory interaction and sensitivity analyses

In the subset with complete data (n = 240; 127 MASLD cases), analyses using continuous ln-UHR demonstrated an association with probable MASLD after adjustment for age, BMI, TyG index, smoking, and years since menopause (OR = 4.15, 95% CI 1.34–12.82; p = 0.014), whereas years since menopause itself was not associated (p = 0.528). There was no evidence of significant interaction between ln-UHR and years since menopause when modelled either continuously (p for interaction = 0.394) or using a 10-year cutoff (<10 vs ≥ 10 years; MASLD prevalence 53.8% vs 53.7%; p for interaction = 0.132), suggesting that menopausal timing did not materially modify the ln-UHR–MASLD association in this cohort. These analyses were exploratory and may have been underpowered to detect modest effect modification. Model calibration remained adequate across multivariable models incorporating ln-UHR with either age at visit or years since menopause (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ2 = 5.97, p = 0.650 and χ2 = 8.63, p = 0.374, respectively).

Because probable MASLD definitions incorporating HSI include BMI and liver enzymes, additional sensitivity analyses were performed to address potential circularity. In the total sample with complete data (n = 343; 229 MASLD cases), ln-UHR was not associated with probable MASLD in models excluding BMI, although the direction of association remained positive (OR = 2.02, 95% CI 0.94–4.34; p = 0.070), suggesting attenuation but broad consistency when BMI-related circularity was minimized.

-CAP-defined MASLD

CAP-defined MASLD

CAP-defined MASLD (steatosis plus metabolic dysfunction) was available in 77 participants with elastography data, of whom 37 (48.1%) met MASLD criteria.

In CAP-defined sensitivity analyses, standardised ln-UHR demonstrated a positive but non-significant association with MASLD in unadjusted models (OR = 1.60, p = 0.089). In adjusted analyses including age, BMI, TyG index, and smoking, ln-UHR was not associated with CAP-defined MASLD, although the direction of association remained positive. Model fit was borderline (χ2 = 3.09, p = 0.079), with modest explanatory power (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.052), while calibration remained adequate (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ2 = 5.86, p = 0.663). Overall, these findings suggest that the observed ln-UHR–MASLD association remained directionally consistent when restricted to the smaller CAP-defined subset, but reduced statistical precision and limited power precluded definitive inference.-Additional metabolic confounder sensitivity analyses

-Additional metabolic confounder sensitivity analyses

Additional metabolic confounder sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analyses replacing composite metabolic indices with individual metabolic parameters (including fasting glucose, insulin, and triglycerides; n = 344), the association between ln-UHR and probable MASLD was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (OR = 1.56, 95% CI 0.90–2.71; p = 0.112). In this model, BMI (OR = 2.96, p < 0.001) and fasting glucose (OR = 1.08, p = 0.002) remained associated with MASLD, whereas insulin, triglycerides, and smoking were not. Model fit remained strong (χ2 = 289.42, p < 0.001), with high explanatory power (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.792) and good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ2 = 7.70, p = 0.463). Given the biological overlap between UHR and these metabolic variables, attenuation of association may reflect collinearity or overadjustment rather than the absence of metabolic relevance.-Exploratory BMI stratified and lean phenotype analyses

Exploratory BMI stratified and lean phenotype analyses

In BMI-stratified analyses, UHR remained associated with probable MASLD among women with BMI <30 kg/m2. In women with BMI <30 kg/m2 at first visit, addition of ln-UHR to a model including age, BMI, TyG index, and smoking significantly improved model fit (Δχ2 = 6.70, p = 0.010). ln-UHR was associated with probable MASLD (OR = 4.16, 95% CI 1.32–13.12, p = 0.013). Consistent findings were observed when ln-UHR was standardised, with each 1-SD increase associated with higher odds of probable MASLD (OR = 1.97, 95% CI 1.10–3.53, p = 0.022).

However, the improvement in discrimination was minimal (AUC 0.926 vs 0.930; ΔAUC = 0.004), mirroring findings in the overall cohort.

In exploratory analyses including an interaction term between BMI and ln-UHR, neither the interaction term (p = 0.372) nor the main effect of ln-UHR remained statistically significant. Discussion

In this cohort of postmenopausal women, UHR was associated with probable MASLD, with exploratory findings suggesting potentially stronger associations in selected nonobese phenotypes. Although BMI remained the dominant determinant, UHR may provide complementary metabolic risk-enrichment beyond anthropometric measures alone.

Support for the relevance of UHR in postmenopausal women comes from a recent NHANES analysis of over 7800 postmenopausal participants, which demonstrated strong dose-dependent associations between UHR and multiple indices of general and central adiposity [20]. These findings reinforce UHR as a marker of metabolically driven adiposity and provide important biological context for the present results, in which UHR identified women with metabolically adverse profiles associated with steatotic liver disease, including those without overt obesity.

Previous studies have reported associations between UHR and hepatic steatosis in general and nonobese populations [10,11,21,22], but evidence specific to postmenopausal women has been lacking. Our findings extend existing literature by demonstrating that UHR is associated with probable MASLD within an exclusively postmenopausal cohort and suggest that this relationship may remain relevant across adiposity strata, including selected lean or nonobese phenotypes.

The unexpected inverse association observed for smoking should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect residual confounding, selection bias, smoking-related weight differences, healthcare utilization patterns, or unmeasured lifestyle factors rather than any biologically protective effect. Given the observational design, no causal inference can be drawn.

Several biological mechanisms may underlie the observed association: uric acid may promote hepatic lipid accumulation through oxidative stress, inflammatory signalling, and impaired insulin signalling pathways [23,24]. whereas HDL cholesterol exerts anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective effects that may mitigate triglyceride accumulation within hepatocytes [25]. Accordingly, UHR may function as an integrated marker of metabolic dysfunction, potentially capturing adverse metabolic pathways more effectively than either biomarker alone.

This relationship may be particularly relevant in postmenopausal women, in whom estrogen deficiency contributes to rapid metabolic remodelling characterized by increased visceral adiposity, altered uric acid metabolism, reduced HDL functionality, and progressive insulin resistance [21]. These changes may precede overt obesity and could partly explain why exploratory associations between UHR and MASLD were observed even in selected lean or non-obese phenotypes.

The association between metabolic steatotic liver disease (historically termed NAFLD) and postmenopausal status is well established [26]. Estrogen exerts protective metabolic effects by enhancing insulin sensitivity, improving lipid profiles, and limiting adiposity; loss of ovarian function therefore predisposes to hepatic steatosis and fibrosis progression [13,27]. Yang et al. reported a 1.5-fold higher risk of advanced fibrosis in postmenopausal compared with premenopausal women with NAFLD, while also highlighting a potential protective role of menopausal hormone therapy [28,29]. Similarly, analyses from the NASH Clinical Research Network demonstrated greater fibrosis risk among postmenopausal women with longer duration of estrogen deficiency [28].

Although exploratory analyses considered menopausal timing, years since menopause did not significantly modify the UHR–MASLD association. These findings support further investigation into whether earlier metabolic risk stratification during the postmenopausal transition may improve recognition of steatosis. Even though ultrasonography is widely used for screening, its sensitivity is limited in individuals with obesity, renal disease, or mild steatosis (<20%) [30]. Liver biopsy and elastography, while informative, remain costly and not universally accessible in primary care settings, contributing to underdiagnosis [31]. UHR alone demonstrated modest discrimination; yet its principal value may lie in metabolic risk enrichment and biological phenotyping rather than substantial incremental discrimination beyond dominant adiposity-related markers. Notably, the association between UHR and MASLD in lean women without central obesity suggests that uric acid–HDL imbalance may capture early hepatic metabolic stress not reflected by conventional anthropometric thresholds.

Strengths include contemporary MASLD consensus definitions, incorporation of imaging-based CAP sensitivity analyses, and dedicated evaluation of lean/nonobese phenotypes in a postmenopausal cohort. This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design, single-centre recruitment, and reliance on non-invasive steatosis assessment may limit causal inference and broader generalizability. Although CAP represented the preferred imaging-based method for steatosis assessment, HSI was used when elastography data were unavailable. Because HSI incorporates BMI and liver enzymes, circularity and shared variance may partially inflate some associations despite CAP-based sensitivity analyses. Accordingly, BMI-adjusted and BMI-stratified analyses should be interpreted primarily as exploratory sensitivity analyses assessing whether ln-UHR retains associative relevance beyond adiposity, rather than as definitive evidence of BMI-independent association. Variability in probable MASLD classification across CAP- and HSI-based definitions further suggests that steatosis ascertainment method may substantially influence case identification in postmenopausal women.

Second, while CAP-restricted analyses reduced HSI-related circularity, the substantially smaller imaging-defined subset limited statistical power and precision. Similarly, subgroup analyses, including lean/nonobese and menopausal timing strata, involved relatively small sample sizes, and yielded wide confidence intervals. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Third, residual confounding remains possible, as important determinants of uric acid metabolism and MASLD risk, including dietary factors (such as purine, fructose, and broader nutritional patterns), renal function, socioeconomic characteristics, incompletely measured lifestyle variables, and broader metabolic syndrome components, were not fully captured. Although primary models adjusted for major metabolic covariates, sensitivity analyses incorporating fasting glucose, insulin, and triglycerides attenuated the association between ln-UHR and probable MASLD, suggesting potential collinearity or overadjustment rather than absence of metabolic relevance. Together, these findings support interpreting UHR as an integrated metabolic dysfunction biomarker rather than a fully independent determinant. Finally, the absence of validation in an independent external cohort limits generalizability, and prospective external validation is required before broader clinical applicability.

In postmenopausal women, UHR was associated with probable MASLD and may reflect integrated metabolic vulnerability, particularly in selected nonobese phenotypes. While adiposity remained the dominant determinant, UHR may offer complementary metabolic insight. Prospective validation using standardized imaging-based definitions is required before clinical implementation.
