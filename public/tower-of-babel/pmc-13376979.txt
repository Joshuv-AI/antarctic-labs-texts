Btg2 inhibits Fmo1 UFMylation thus exacerbating ferroptosis and apoptosis in hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury
Authors: Dadi Peng, Yihua Wang, Dengliang Lei, Kezhen Zong, Shanshan Li, Yunhai Luo, Qiuying Li, Liqing Jiang, Weifeng Huang, Yulin Ou, Huarong Yu, Jiayin Yang, Zuotian Huang, Zhongjun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatic ischemia‒reperfusion injury (HIRI) represents a frequently occurring pathological condition during liver surgery, yet the mechanisms governing HIRI remain inadequately comprehended. Here, we investigate the role of B-cell translocation gene 2 (Btg2) in HIRI. Btg2 is upregulated following HIRI. A 70% HIRI model using hepatocyte-specific Btg2 transgenic and systemic Btg2 knockout mice reveals that Btg2 deteriorates hepatic inflammation and apoptosis. In primary hepatocytes, Btg2 knockdown reduces hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R)-induced inflammation and mitochondrial stress. Metabolomics indicates that taurine metabolism is significantly affected in the livers of Btg2-/- mice. Mechanistically, Btg2 suppresses UFMylation of flavin-containing monooxygenase 1 (Fmo1), a key taurine synthesis enzyme, promoting its K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation. Virus-mediated Fmo1 overexpression inhibits ferroptosis, apoptosis, and HIRI significantly both in vivo and in vitro. Virtual screening of natural compounds indicates that Daturataturin A (DTA) inhibits Btg2, thereby attenuating ferroptosis and HIRI. These results suggest that Btg2 may constitute a promising therapeutic target for HIRI.

Introduction

Hepatic ischemia‒reperfusion injury (HIRI) is a common yet currently inevitable pathological event following hepatectomy and liver transplantation that induces inflammatory responses and impairs liver function, increasing the likelihood of complications and diminishing long-term graft success1–4. As a biphasic injury, HIRI occurs when the blood supply to the liver is temporarily halted and subsequently restored, triggering a cascade of detrimental events, including excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), inflammatory processes, and eventually cellular death5,6. Effective preventative and therapeutic strategies for HIRI have yet to be adequately developed. Consequently, developing a deeper grasp of the molecular mechanisms fundamental to HIRI is essential.

B-cell translocation gene 2 (Btg2), also referred to as PC3 or TIS21, belongs to the BTG/TOB family of anti-proliferative genes7. Btg2 was initially recognized as an immediate gene activated by growth inhibitory signals and has since attracted considerable interest for its tumor-suppressive characteristics. Its expression is generally downregulated in various cancers, often due to promoter hypermethylation or loss of p53 function8,9. In addition to its role in tumorigenesis, Btg2 has emerged as a multifunctional mediator of various biological processes, notably the regulation of oxidative stress, by modulating ROS levels, highlighting its involvement in maintaining cellular homeostasis10. However, the specific involvement of Btg2 in HIRI remains elusive.

Ferroptosis, a regulated form of iron-dependent cell death, features a build-up of lipid peroxidation (LPO) and ROS11. Initiated by a redox imbalance, this process involves the peroxidation of membrane polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), leading to membrane rupture and cellular death. Additionally, iron exacerbates oxidative stress by facilitating the Fenton reaction12,13. Ferroptosis has been implicated in a variety of pathological processes, including ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury14–16, and countering this process depends on antioxidant defense. Therefore, the regulatory mechanisms underlying ferroptosis in HIRI require further in-depth investigation.

Taurine, a semi-essential β-amino acid, serves as a vital endogenous cytoprotectant. In addition to its roles in osmoregulation and bile acid conjugation, taurine exhibits potent antioxidant properties, making it a therapeutic candidate for mitigating the damaging effects of HIRI17,18. Taurine’s ability to mitigate ferroptosis stems from its capacity to counteract oxidative stress, suppress lipid peroxidation, and regulate ferroptosis-associated factors including Gpx4, highlighting its potential role as a ferroptosis modulator19–21. However, the mechanisms that regulate endogenous taurine synthesis remain unclarified.

In this study, we observed that Btg2 expression was upregulated following HIRI. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed the aggravating effects of Btg2 in HIRI. Mechanistically, Btg2 limited the UFMylation of flavin-containing monooxygenase 1 (Fmo1) by the E3 ligase UFM1-specific ligase 1 (Ufl1), thereby restricting the synthesis of taurine, exacerbating ferroptosis. Moreover, the targeted inhibition of Btg2 by Daturataturin A (DTA) attenuated HIRI. Overall, the present findings elucidate the role of Btg2 in HIRI and highlight its therapeutic potential.

The publicly available RNA-Seq datasets GSE117066 (mouse 70% warm HIRI) and GSE228781 (human non-malignant liver samples that underwent warm HIRI after hepatectomy), along with our RNA-Seq dataset facilitated the identification of HIRI-pertinent genes. A Venn diagram was generated to confirm the differentially expressed genes with the same trend of change across all the three RNA-Seq datasets (Fig. 1C). Among these 13 genes, Btg2 expression was significantly upregulated in the HIRI group (Fig. 1D). Liver tissues were obtained from patients prior to and after HIRI. Western blot analysis indicated that BTG2 was elevated in the liver samples subjected to HIRI (Fig. 1E). The relationship between BTG2 expression and postoperative liver function was subsequently validated, indicating a positive connection with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels (Fig. 1F). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining confirmed BTG2/Btg2 upregulation in human/mouse HIRI specimens (Fig. 1G, H). The expression level of Btg2 exhibited a time-dependent increase in the mouse HIRI model, and peaked after 1 h of ischemia and 6 h of reperfusion (Fig. 1I, J). Thereafter, the time point of I/R (1 h/6 h) was selected as the fixed standard for in vivo modeling in the subsequent experiments. Furthermore, immunofluorescence revealed a substantial upregulation of Btg2 after HIRI in vivo modeling and H/R (6 h/6 h) in vitro modeling, which was mostly localized in primary hepatocytes (Fig. S1C, D). Consistently, H/R-exposed primary hepatocytes exhibited robust induction of Btg2 protein and mRNA (Fig. S1E, F). These data demonstrated an increase of Btg2 in hepatocytes following HIRI, indicating that Btg2 may yield potential involvement.

Btg2 deficiency attenuated the inflammatory response, liver mitochondrial injury, and apoptosis during HIRI

Generation of Btg2 knockout mice enabled functional studies (Fig. 2A). The efficiency of the Btg2 knockout in the liver was validated (Fig. S2A, B). Organ morphology in Btg2-/- mice remained comparable to that of Btg2+/+ wild-type mice (Fig. S2C). Following HIRI, serum ALT, AST, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels were markedly elevated in wild-type mice; however, these increases were attenuated in Btg2-/- mice (Fig. 2B, C). H&E staining demonstrated a substantial reduction in necrotic lesions after HIRI in Btg2-/- mice (Fig. 2D, I). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) indicated that after HIRI, the hepatocytes of Btg2 knockout mice presented restoration of mitochondrial cristae and membranes (Fig. 2E). TUNEL staining indicated a reduced occurrence of hepatocellular apoptosis in Btg2-/- mice post-HIRI (Fig. 2F, J). Btg2-/- mice presented a diminished inflammatory response, as confirmed by ELISAs of TNF-α and IL-6 (Fig. 2M). Furthermore, the expression of Bcl2 was substantially increased, whereas the levels of Bax and c-CAS-3 were decreased after Btg2 knockout (Fig. 2N). The RT-qPCR showed that mRNA abundance of inflammatory cytokines was elevated following HIRI, while Btg2-/- mice presented lower mRNA levels of inflammatory factors (Fig. 2O).

Further analysis on Btg2’s function in the context of H/R challenge in vitro was carried out (Fig. S3A). Btg2 expression was suppressed in primary hepatocytes following adenoviral-shBtg2 transfection (Fig. S3B). Btg2 knockdown inversely modulated apoptosis-related proteins, downregulating Bax and cleaved CAS-3 and upregulating Bcl2 (Fig. S3C). ELISA indicated that Btg2 knockdown effectively counteracted the H/R-induced elevation of inflammatory cytokines (Fig. S3D). The CCK-8 assay indicated that Btg2 knockdown mitigated the cellular death (Fig. S3E). JC-1 staining demonstrated that Btg2 knockdown in primary hepatocytes significantly increased the mitochondrial membrane potential under H/R stress (Fig. S3F).

Hepatocyte-specific Btg2 overexpression aggravated the inflammatory response, mitochondrial injury, and apoptosis during HIRI

For further verification of Btg2’s role in HIRI, mice carrying the CAG-LSL-Btg2 knockin allele at the junction of exons 1 and 2 of the Gt (ROSA) 26Sor locus by CRISPR/Cas technology were crossed with Alb-Cre mice to obtain hepatocyte-specific Btg2 transgenic (Btg2HTG) mice (Fig. 3A). Efficiency of Btg2 knockin was validated (Fig. S4A, B). Compared to the Btg2 non-transgenic control (Btg2NTG), the organs of Btg2HTG mice exhibited comparable morphology (Fig. S4C). Liver function impairment following HIRI was notably exacerbated in Btg2HTG mice (Fig. 3B, C). Necrotic regions were further amplified after HIRI in Btg2HTG mice (Fig. 3D, I). The hepatocytes of Btg2HTG mice displayed greater mitochondrial membrane rupture and fewer mitochondrial cristae (Fig. 3E). The hepatic apoptosis was more pronounced after HIRI (Fig. 3F, J). The results of ELISA indicated that, compared to Btg2NTG mice, the levels of inflammatory cytokines were significantly upregulated in the hepatocyte-specific Btg2 overexpression group (Fig. 3M). Additionally, Btg2HTG mice exhibited higher levels of Bax and c-CAS-3 than Btg2NTG mice, whereas the level of the Bcl2 was lower after HIRI (Fig. 3N). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed increase in the mRNA levels of inflammatory cytokines resulting from hepatocyte-specific Btg2 overexpression (Fig. 3O).

Verification in primary hepatocytes was conducted using adenoviral transfection to overexpress Btg2 (Fig. S5A). A considerable increase in Btg2 expression was validated following AdBtg2 transfection (Fig. S5B). Compared to the group transfected with AdVector, Btg2 overexpression increased the levels of Bax and c-CAS-3 while reducing the expression of Bcl2 under H/R challenge (Fig. S5C). Btg2 overexpression aggravated H/R-induced elevation of inflammatory cytokines (Fig. S5D). Btg2 overexpression intensified H/R-mediated cell death (Fig. S5E). Overexpression of Btg2 considerably promoted a further decrease in the mitochondrial membrane potential during H/R (Fig. S5F). Overall, the findings from the in vivo and in vitro experiments suggest that Btg2 in hepatocytes leads to the amplification of apoptosis, inflammation, and mitochondrial damage during HIRI.

Btg2 downregulates Fmo1 and thus decreases taurine levels in HIRI

With the upregulation of Btg2 following HIRI, enrichment of multiple metabolic pathway disturbances was found in liver tissue compared to sham control (Fig. S1A, B). Given the established critical role of metabolic imbalance in HIRI22–24, it was hypothesized that Btg2 may be involved in metabolic modulation. For the purpose of testing this, metabolomics in comparisons between Btg2-/- and Btg2+/+ mice livers was employed to identify potential metabolic alterations caused by Btg2 knockout during HIRI.

Based on the result of metabolomics, metabolism of taurine and hypotaurine was significantly impacted in Btg2-/- mice (Fig. 4A). Meanwhile, elevated levels of taurine and taurodeoxycholic acid, as well as reduced levels of hypotaurine in Btg2-/- mice livers were observed (Fig. S6), indicating an accelerated flux through the final step of taurine synthesis for expanding the bioavailability of taurine under Btg2-/- condition. For further validation, an increase in taurine levels in Btg2-/- mouse livers under HIRI stress compared to those in Btg2+/+ mice was observed, and conditional knockin of hepatocyte Btg2 limited the production of taurine (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, immunoprecipitation (IP) of Btg2 in primary hepatocytes under H/R stress was conducted to identify proteins that could interact with Btg2 and convert hypotaurine into taurine. The immunoprecipitated Btg2-3xFlag protein bands were visualized using silver staining, and the immunoprecipitated products were subjected to mass spectrometry (Fig. 4C, D). Eventually, Fmo1 was found to be the most likely reciprocal protein of Btg2 (Fig. 4E). Fmo1, a member of the Fmo family that is crucial for the metabolism of endogenous and xenobiotic substances, was identified as the critical enzyme responsible for the conversion of hypotaurine to taurine25 (Fig. 4F). In co-IP analysis, Btg2 and Fmo1 were individually immunoprecipitated from AML12 cells transfected with Btg2−3xFlag or Fmo1-His plasmids, and the corresponding proteins were identified by immunoblotting. Btg2 precipitated Fmo1, but the control IgG did not. Reverse co-IP indicated that Fmo1 precipitated Btg2 (Fig. 4G, S7). To identify the precise Btg2 domain that is responsible for their interaction, serially-truncated Btg2 constructs were constructed and co-IP was conducted. The result shows the Box-C domain of Btg2 mediated its interaction with Fmo1 (Fig. 4J). Molecular docking analysis was conducted to determine the binding interaction26,27. Btg2 and Fmo1 interacted primarily through hydrogen bonding, yielding a docking score of −256.69, a confidence score of 0.8941, and a ligand root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 56.00 (Fig. 4K). Furthermore, Btg2 overexpression decreased Fmo1 protein expression without affecting mRNA expression in primary hepatocytes following H/R intervention (Fig. 4L, M). Overall, Btg2 negatively regulated Fmo1 protein levels to restrict taurine production under HIRI stress; however, the detailed mechanism requires further investigation.

Btg2 exacerbated ferroptosis by downregulation of Fmo1, thus restricting taurine synthesis

Given that taurine is a crucial regulator of ferroptosis19–21, we investigated the involvement of taurine in ferroptosis in the context of HIRI. H&E staining, TUNEL assays, IHC analysis of 4-HNE, TEM analysis, immunofluorescence detection, and biochemical measurements (ALT, AST, and LDH) demonstrated that taurine intervention significantly decreased hepatocyte apoptosis, 4-HNE expression, mitochondrial damage, the extent of necrotic lesions, infiltration by CD11b+/Ly6G+ immune cells, and improved hepatic functional parameters (Fig. S8A–K, S9). Meanwhile, taurine significantly reduced MDA release and stimulated GSH production (Fig. S8L, M). Taurine also ameliorated ferroptosis signaling in HIRI, as evidenced by the increased expression of Gpx4 and Slc7a11 (Fig. S8N).

Primary hepatocytes were harvested from Btg2HTG mice in vitro and were transfected with AdFmo1 for overexpression (Fig. 5A). Transfection of AdFmo1 resulted in reductions in TNF-α and IL-6 secretion in the supernatant under H/R stress (Fig. 5B). Meanwhile, taurine synthesis was restored with AdFmo1 under H/R stress (Fig. 5C). H/R led to the accumulation of Fe2+ and LPO in primary hepatocytes, which was exacerbated by Btg2 overexpression, whereas transfection of AdFmo1 reduced the Fe2+ and LPO levels (Fig. 5D, E). Meanwhile, MDA release was decreased and GSH levels were elevated by AdFmo1 under H/R stress (Fig. 5F, G). As Btg2 lacks E3 ligase activity, the IP–MS data were interrogated to identify probable E3 ligases that might modify Fmo1 stability and interact with Btg2. Ufl1, a distinct E3 protein ligase, was chosen for further investigation because it exhibited the highest intensity and the greatest number of unique peptides (Fig. 6E, S12A). Ufl1 is unique as it facilitates UFMylation, which is the covalent binding of ubiquitin-fold modifier 1 (Ufm1) to target proteins28. Fmo1 levels were restored following the overexpression of Ufl1, but were further decreased after the knockdown of Ufl1 in H/R-stimulated primary hepatocytes (Fig. 6F, G). Ufl1 stabilized Fmo1 protein expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6H). Co-IP tests demonstrated that the quantity of Ufl1 bound to an equivalent amount of Fmo1 decreased following the introduction of the Btg2-Flag plasmid (Fig. 6I).

Since the Ufmylation system primarily functions as E1 (Uba5)/E2 (Ufc1)/E3 (Ufl1)/Ddrgk1, we transfected HA-tagged E1/E2/E3/Ddrgk1 into HEK293T cells to simulate a mouce-homologous Ufmylation system in the subsequent experiments. Analysis of Fmo1 ubiquitination patterns indicated that the K48 site of ubiquitin was both essential and adequate for the formation of the polyubiquitin chain on Fmo1 following Ufl1 vacancy (Fig. 6J). Ufl1-HA transfection facilitated UFMylation and decreased the K48-ubiquitination of Fmo1 in HEK293T cells (Fig. 6K). Furthermore, the transfection of truncated Btg2 (ΔBoxC region) promoted UFMylation and suppressed the K48-ubiquitination of Fmo1 (Fig. 6L). To identify the region of Fmo1 UFMylation, three deletion constructs were used in HEK293T cells together with the corresponding plasmids (Fig. S12B). As a result, UFMylation was observed in the Fmo1 Δ200-399AA construct (Fig. S12C).

Discussion

Despite the participation of Btg2 in various diseases, its specific involvement in HIRI remains undefined. The current study demonstrated that primary hepatocytes and livers of mice with HIRI presented increased expression of Btg2. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments indicated that Btg2 exacerbated HIRI. Mechanistically, Btg2 promotes the degradation of Fmo1, resulting in the suppression of taurine synthesis and the intensification of ferroptosis and apoptosis. In detail, Btg2 interacted with Fmo1 and hence limited the UFMylation of Fmo1 by Ufl1. These findings indicated that Btg2 might represent a viable target for the mitigation of HIRI.

Btg2 works primarily by interacting with other proteins to influence essential physiological processes, such as cell cycle progression, DNA repair, and transcriptional regulation. For instance, Btg2 interacts with the CCR4-NOT complex, enhancing its mRNA deadenylation activity. This interaction leads to the selective degradation of transcripts linked to cell proliferation, underscoring the role of Btg2 as a negative regulator of tumor development29. In addition, Btg2 was recognized as a binding partner of Smad3, thereby stabilizing this TGF-β-associated protein and enhancing its transcriptional activation of downstream targets in focal segmental glomerulosclerosis30. These investigations indicated that the interaction partners of Btg2 may vary across different cellular environments, thus clarifying its multiple physiological roles. Furthermore, Btg2 is implicated in myocardial I/R injury and plays a regulatory role in lipid and glucose metabolism31–33. Work in the current research indicated that Btg2 expression was increased in HIRI, and that HIRI was substantially alleviated when Btg2 was downregulated, confirming the detrimental effect of Btg2 in response to HIRI.

The metabolomics results indicated that, compared with wild-type mice, Btg2-/- mouse livers presented a significant restoration of taurine synthesis in the context of HIRI, providing insight into the relationship between Btg2 and taurine metabolic equilibrium. The established anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory characteristics of taurine make it an appealing therapeutic agent for maintaining redox balance, countering aging, and minimizing cellular damage34–36. Taurine is obtained from food sources and is also produced internally37,38. Although it has never been isolated or characterized, an enzyme that transforms hypotaurine into taurine was previously identified as an NAD-dependent hypotaurine dehydrogenase. In 2020, Veeravalli et al. identified Fmo1, a member of the Fmo family that plays a non-canonical but critical role in oxidizing hypotaurine to taurine, acting as the terminal enzyme in this pathway within the liver. Fmo1 is also essential for the metabolism of certain xenobiotics and endogenous chemicals25,39,40.

Taurine supplementation may increase liver graft viability, reduce inflammation, and improve overall outcomes in both experimental and clinical settings41,42. Taurine has recently been reported to suppress ferroptosis. Specifically, taurine prevents ferroptosis by enhancing the O-GlcNAcylation of Gpx4, thus preventing its degradation in osteoarthritis43. Additionally, the liver X receptor alpha/stearoyl-coenzyme A desaturase 1 pathway is stimulated by taurine, which results in the inhibition of ferroptosis in prostate cancer19. Taurine also acted as a regulator of Nrf2, thereby modulating the expression of its downstream genes including Gpx444,45. The current findings indicate that the knockout of Btg2 reinstates Fmo1 expression, thereby increasing taurine synthesis, which protects hepatocytes against ferroptosis, and ultimately, HIRI across various dimensions, including iron overload, excessive LPO production, and GSH imbalance, underscoring the exceptional anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative properties of taurine as a vital metabolite in HIRI.

Emerging as a lately identified post-translational modification, UFMylation is characterized by the covalent binding of Ufm1 to targeted proteins. This process is facilitated by a specialized enzymatic Uba5 (E1)/Ufc1 (E2)/Ufl1 (E3) cascade46,47. In contrast to ubiquitination, UFMylation involves distinct substrates and functions, particularly in the preservation of cellular homeostasis under stress conditions. UFMylation fulfills a vital regulatory function in maintaining endoplasmic reticulum (ER) homeostasis, aided by the partner protein, DDRGK1, by regulating ER-associated protein degradation and stress responses48–50. For example, the upregulation of Ufl1 enhances PD-1 UFMylation, counteracting proteasome degradation in T cells and leading to ineffective tumor suppression51. Tao et al. have suggested that Ufl1 maintains immunological tolerance to viral DNA infections by blocking the interaction between TRIM29 and STING, which decreases TRIM29 ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation52. Nevertheless, the role of UFMylation in HIRI is not yet fully understood. Here, our results indicated that UFMylation in hepatocytes was critical for the protein stability of Fmo1. In the context of HIRI, upregulated Btg2 inhibits Fmo1 UFMylation due to its distinctive protein sequence structure, leading to increased K48 ubiquitination-mediated degradation of Fmo1. Our research highlights a link between UFMylation and taurine metabolism, expanding the understanding of UFMylation beyond its conventional functions. The observed restoration of taurine levels following Btg2 inhibition underscored the therapeutic potential of targeting Btg2 to modulate UFMylation-dependent metabolic pathways.

DTI and DTA are bioactive substances extracted from the seeds of Datura metel L., a plant that has long been employed in herbal medicine. They are both members of the withanolide class of steroids and are well known for their diverse range of pharmacological activities53,54. DTA possesses strong anti-inflammatory properties. In immortalized human keratinocytes, DTA negatively regulated inflammation and proliferation, indicating its potential therapeutic use in skin-related disorders55. Furthermore, by controlling the PPAR pathway, DTA increases lipid metabolism in psoriasis and exhibits potential anti-psoriatic effects56. Nevertheless, the role of DTA in HIRI remains to be elucidated. Our study suggests that DTA may represent a prospective agent for HIRI, specifically targeting Btg2. Moreover, the specificity of this mechanism may translate into a more favorable safety profile than nonspecific anti-inflammatory medicine. Combination therapy involving DTA may provide synergistic protection by simultaneously addressing inflammation during HIRI.

In conclusion, our study indicated that the previously unrecognized Btg2 serves as a vital intracellular mediator in the progression of HIRI by regulating the UFMylation of Fmo1, taurine generation, ferroptosis, and apoptosis. The mechanism by which Btg2 mediates HIRI relies on its interaction with Fmo1, facilitating the inhibition of the UFMylation of Fmo1. Targeting Btg2 may be a viable strategy for the treatment of HIRI.

Methods

Ethics approval

This study involved human participants and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (2025-LS-No.178). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants as well. Sex and/or gender was determined based on self-reporting. All the experimental protocols of animal study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (IACUC-CQMU-2025-01069).

Patients and clinical study

The blood samples and liver samples utilized in this study were obtained from patients who had undergone partial hepatectomy for benign liver diseases at the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (Supplementary Data 1). The liver tissues were obtained before ischemia (Pre I/R group) and after portal reperfusion (Post I/R group). All samples were exclusively employed for experimental purposes.

Sex as a biological variable

Clinical samples of human liver were analyzed from both male and female subjects. Animal models have provided evidence that estrogen regulates HIRI57–59. To minimize variability linked to the female estrogen cycle, the study exclusively used male mice.

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice, weighing between 25 and 28 grams and aged between 6 and 8 weeks, were provided by the Laboratory Animal Centre at Chongqing Medical University. Mice were kept in a pathogen-free condition with a cycle of 12 h of light and darkness, a temperature of 23 °C, free access to water and food (1016706476803973120, Beijing Keao Xieli Feed Co., Ltd., China), and relative humidity that was maintained between 60% and 65%.

Btg2 knockout (Btg2-/-) mice (C57BL/6J background) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). Btg2+/- mice were intercrossed to generate the homozygous Btg2-/- mice, homozygous Btg2-/- mice were confirmed by PCR analysis of genomic tail DNA. Primer sequences used are shown in Supplementary data 2.

The targeting vector’s design included a polyA signal, a woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element, a loxP-flanked STOP cassette, a Kozak-Mouse Btg2 CDS sequence and a CAG promoter (from 5′ to 3′). Using CRISPR/Cas technology, the entire construct was inserted at the junction of exons 1 and 2 of the Gt (ROSA) 26Sor locus. Genetically targeted mice were created by co-injecting Cas9 and gRNA into fertilized eggs alongside the targeting vector. Hepatocyte-specific Btg2 transgenic (Btg2HTG) mice (C57BL/6J background) were created by mating ROSA26-LSL-Btg2 mice with Alb-Cre transgenic mice. PCR analysis of the genomic tail DNA was used to genotype the mice. Supplementary Data 2 lists the primer sequences that were employed. In detail, WT mice result in generation of a 453 bp fragment under the amplification of Btg2-CKI primer pair 2, with a 656 bp fragment under the amplification of Btg2-CKI primer pair 1 corresponding to the conditional knockin mice. The production of a 390 bp fragment under the amplification of Btg2-CKI primer pair 3 stands for the Alb-Cre positivity.

Mouse HIRI model

A stable and non-lethal mouse model was established following partial hepatic warm I/R injury in 70% of the mice. After being given anesthesia, mice livers were exposed via midline laparotomy. The portal vein branches supplying the liver’s left and center lobes were cut off using an atraumatic microvascular clamp. The clamp was removed after an hour of ischemia, and the liver suffered reperfusion injury for the given duration of time (1 h/3 h/6 h/12 h). After screening, a fixed time point of 1 h of ischemia followed by 6 h of reperfusion was selected as the fixed time node for in vivo modeling in the subsequent experiments. Samples of serum and liver tissues were gathered after HIRI for further examination.

Isolation and culture of primary cells

Male mice were initially perfused with D-Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution for 5 min, after which an in-situ type IV collagenase (Solarbio Lifesciences, China) procedure was employed to isolate primary hepatocytes. Primary hepatocytes were isolated using centrifugation at 50 × g for 5 min, followed with resuspension in DMEM medium. After adding 90% Percoll (Biosharp, China), primary hepatocytes were centrifugated at 600 × g for 10 min. The cells were cultivated at 37 °C in a water-saturated environment with 5% CO2 after being seeded in 6-well culture dishes using high glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (ExCell Bio, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, China).

In the case of Kupffer cells, we administered D-Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution buffer into the portal vein of male mice, followed by an injection of 0.5 mg/mL type IV collagenase. The liver was then excised, minced and digested in a solution of D-Hanks containing 0.1 mg/ml type IV collagenase for 30 min. Thereafter, the perfused liver was meticulously dissociated with a cell scraper, subsequently being filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to yield a single-cell suspension. This solution was then subjected to further separation via low-density gradient centrifugation (100 × g, 4 °C, 5 min, repeated three times) before being cultivated in 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment.

Cell lines

HEK293T (CL-0005) cells and AML12 cells (CL-0602) were obtained from Procell Life Science&Technology Co., Ltd. HEK293T cells were grown in 1640 medium, which was supplemented with 10% FBS. AML12 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 5 μg/ml insulin, 5 μg/ml transferrin, 5 ng/ml selenium, 40 ng/ml dexamethasone, and 10% FBS under standard cell culture conditions.

Cell hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model

A tri-gas incubator (Thermo Scientific, USA) filled with 94% N2, 1% O2, and 5% CO2 was used for establishing the hypoxic environment. Cells were cultivated under normoxic condition for 6 h after being hypoxic for 6 h in glucose-free DMEM to establish H/R model.

RNA-Sequencing and enrichment analysis

Libraries were created using the mRNA-Seq Library Preparation Kit (ABclonal, China) following the isolation of mRNA from total RNA extraction of mice liver tissues using oligo-dT magnetic beads. An Illumina Novaseq 6000 (USA) was used to sequence the library preparations, producing 150 bp paired-end reads (Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai). Differentially expressed genes (DEG) were defined as genes with |log2(fold change)| > 1.5 together with P < 0.05.

For KEGG and GO function enrichment, we utilized the “clusterProfiler” R software package. Basically, if the P value is less than 0.05, then the GO or KEGG function is deemed to be significantly enriched.

Construction and transfection of adenoviral vectors

The adenoviruses for overexpression and knockdown, along with their corresponding negative controls, were conceived, verified, and produced by Genepharma (Shanghai, China). The chosen interference vector for Btg2, Fmo1 and Ufl1 knockdown was ADV7 (U6), whereas the ADV6 (CMV) vector was employed for the overexpression of Btg2, Btg2−3xFlag, Fmo1 and Ufl1.

After being harvested and counted, primary hepatocytes were transferred to appropriate plates for cell transfection. After 8 h, regular medium was placed to the 6-well plates in place of the adenovirus-laden media (MOI = 50). The targeted sequences are shown as Supplementary data 3.

Construction and transfection of adeno-associated virus 8 (AAV8)

The over-expressing AAV8-TBG-Fmo1 and knockdown AAV8-TBG-shFmo1 was designed, validated, and synthesized by Genepharma (Shanghai, China). A dose of 1.5 × 1011 vg of AAV8 was injected into mice through the tail vein to achieve in vivo hepatocytes overexpression and knockdown of Fmo1. After stable expression for 3 weeks, the mice underwent further study. The sequences of AAV8s are shown as Supplementary data 3.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The total RNA that had been extracted from primary hepatocytes or liver tissues using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) was reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). A CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) was used to carry out RT-qPCR. TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Japan) was used for determining the relative mRNA levels, which were subsequently adjusted for the β-actin level. The 2−ΔΔCt approach was used for analyzing the results. Supplementary data 2 contains a list of primer sequences.

Western blotting assays

In conjunction with a protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChemExpress, USA), RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) was used to extract total protein from primary hepatocytes or liver tissues. After being separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide (SDS-PAGE) gel electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. PVDF membranes are blocked with 5% skim milk and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Supplementary data 4 lists the antibodies used.

Cell viability

At a density of 1500 cells/well, primary hepatocytes were seeded into 96-well plates, with the following treatments has been specified in the figures or figure captions. The assessment of cell viability was carried out using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Beyotime, China).

Liver function assessment

The extent of hepatic injury in mice was evaluated by measuring serum concentrations of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and activity of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). These were measured using commercially available kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Hepatic Malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione (GSH) assays

Diagnostic kits were applied to measure the levels of hepatic MDA (Beyotime, China) and GSH (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) as the manufacturer’s protocols.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining

After being de-paraffinized and rehydrated, paraffin liver slices (5 µm thick) were subjected to EDTA treatment for antigen retrieval (15 min, 96 °C). Liver slices were stained using an H&E Kit (Solarbio Lifesciences, China) for histopathology. Images were captured using an optical microscope.

In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, paraffin liver slices were stained with TUNEL utilizing a commercially accessible in situ apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, China). Using a fluorescence microscope, images were viewed and recorded.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence staining

For IHC and immunofluorescence of tissues, paraffin-embedded liver slices underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and EDTA-mediated antigen retrieval. Liver slices were blocked with PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin after being permeabilized in PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature. After an hour, the liver slices were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The sections were then incubated with the relevant secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, a light microscope or a fluorescence microscope was used to capture images, respectively.

For immunofluorescence of cells, the cells were fixed for 10 min with 4% paraformaldehyde. Goat serum was utilized for a 1-h blockage. Cellular permeabilisation was performed for 30 min using 0.3% Triton X-100 in goat serum. After an overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C, the coverslips were carefully washed three times with TBST and subsequently re-incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with secondary antibodies. The nucleus was then stained with DAPI for 8 min. A laser confocal microscopy was used to capture images. The principal antibodies utilized for immunofluorescence staining are presented in Supplementary Data 4.

Measurement of hepatic taurine

The preparation of liver tissue and cell samples involved sonicating them in cold PBS and then centrifuging them at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Following that, samples were subjected to colorimetric analysis using the commercially available Taurine Assay Kit (Cell Biolabs, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) measurement

The mitochondrial membrane potential test kit with JC-1 (Beyotime, China) was utilized to assess the MMP alterations of primary hepatocytes. In brief, treated cells were resuspended in a 500 μl culture medium and 500 μl JC-1 staining fluid combination and kept at 37 °C for 20 min avoiding exposure to light. Before being observed using a laser confocal microscope, the cells were then rinsed three times with cold staining solution.

Lipid peroxides (LPO) assay

LPO analysis was conducted by employing Liperfluo (Dojindo, Japan). Briefly, a working concentration of 10 µM Liperfluo probes was employed to label LPOs in cells, followed by a 30-min incubation. Laser confocal microscopy was then used to analyze and photograph the cells.

Intracellular Fe2+ detection

After being washed 3 times using cold PBS, a collection of 1 × 106 primary hepatocytes were collected, suspended, and subjected to 1 μM FerroOrange probes (Dojindo, Japan) for 30 min at 37 °C. A laser confocal microscope was then used to observe and take images of the cells at Ex 561 nm/Em 570–620 nm.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The livers of the mice were removed and placed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for more than 2 h at 4 °C. Following three rounds of washing in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (3 min each), the samples were postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide. After that, samples were dehydrated for 15 min each in a succession of ethanol concentrations. Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated in a 1:1 ethanol-acetone solution for 15 min, followed by two washes with pure acetone, each lasting 15 min. Samples for plastic embedding were incubated in 3:1 acetone to resin, 1:1 acetone to resin, and 1:3 acetone to resin for 1 h each, followed by an overnight immersion in 100% resin. Samples were immersed in new resin and subsequently penetrated for 3 h at room temperature. Subsequent to being inserted in a flat rubber mold, the samples underwent polymerization at 37 °C for 12 h and at 65 °C for 24 to 48 h. Post-polymerization, resin blocks were sectioned using an ultra-microtome (Leica, Germany). Serial slices with a thickness of 70 nm were gathered on 150 mesh formvar-coated copper grids and analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Mass spectrometry analysis

Mice primary hepatocytes were transfected with Btg2−3xFlag adenovirus, and immunoprecipitation was conducted using anti-Flag antibody (Meck, Germany) or IgG (Abmart, China) in conjunction with protein A/G beads (MedChemExpress, USA). Immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed and visualized using silver staining with the rapid silver stain kit (Beyotime, China). Subsequently, mass spectrometry was employed to identify all proteins bind to Btg2-3xFlag (Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai). In detail, gel pieces were destained with 200–400 μl of destaining solution (30% acetonitrile/100 mM NH₄HCO₃) until transparent, then dehydrated in 100% acetonitrile. Proteins were reduced with 10 mM DTT at 37 °C for 60 min, alkylated with 60 mM IAA in the dark at room temperature for 60 min, and washed twice with 100 mM NH₄HCO₃ for 15 min each. After dehydration with 100% acetonitrile for 5 min, the gel pieces were lyophilized for 10 min and digested with 2.5-10.0 ng/μl trypsin at 37 °C for 20 h. The resulting peptides were collected, and the remaining peptides in the gel were extracted twice by submersion in extraction solution (60% acetonitrile/0.1% TFA) with sonication for 15 min. All extracts were pooled, lyophilized, and reconstituted in 200 μl of 2% formic acid. Peptides from each sample were separated using a nanoElute system (Bruker, USA). Solvent A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in 99.9% water, while Solvent B was 0.1% formic acid in 99.9% acetonitrile. Peptides were loaded in 95% Solvent A and separated on a C18 column (Thermo Scientific EASY column, 15 cm, ID 150 μm, 3 μm) over 30 min at a flow rate of 300 nl/min with the following gradient: 5%–35% Solvent B for 18 min, 35%–80% Solvent B for 2 min, and held at 80% Solvent B for 10 min. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker, USA) operated in positive ion mode with an electrospray voltage of 1.5 kV. Precursors and fragments were detected across an m/z range of 100–1700 using the TOF detector. Data acquisition was carried out in parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode with settings of 1 MS scan followed by 8 MS/MS PASEF scans per cycle (cycle time: 0.95 s), and ion charge range of 0–5. Active exclusion was enabled with a release time of 24 s. Data were processed using MaxQuant (version 1.6.14) against the UniProt mouse proteome database. Trypsin was specified as the digestion enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine and acetylation of protein N-termini were set as variable modifications. The precursor mass tolerance was 20 ppm, and fragment ion mass tolerance was 0.1 Da. Peptide and protein identifications were filtered at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1%, with a minimum peptide length of seven amino acids. Protein identification required at least one unique peptide. Proteins interacting with Btg2-3×Flag are listed in Supplementary data 5.

CHX assay

Primary hepatocytes were transfected with AdBtg2 or AdshBtg2 adenovirus and grown with cycloheximide (CHX) (20 μM). Then, the cells were collected at the given time points and processed for Western blotting examination.

Immunoprecipitation

The indicated plasmids were used to transfect HEK293T or AML12 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) when performing co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP). Flag-tagged plasmids (Btg2 and its truncated fragments), HA-tagged plasmids (Uba5, Ufc1, Ufl1, Ddrgk1), Myc-tagged plasmid (Ub and its K48/K63 mutants), His-tagged plasmids (Fmo1 and its truncated fragments), V5-tagged plasmid (Ufm1) were purchased from Tsingke Biotech (Beijing) Co., Ltd. Ice-cold IP buffer (Beyotime, China) containing PMSF was employed to lyse cells. The incubation of Protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, USA) for a period of 4 h at room temperature was followed by the addition of 200 µg of total lysates and the application of 1 µg of corresponding primary anti-tag antibodies or IgG with moderate shaking at 4 °C overnight. Immunoblotting was then performed after the co-IP complexes were boiled for 10 min at 95 °C with 2 × SDS loading buffer. The employed primary antibodies are presented in Supplementary data 4.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Sample preparation was performed by adding pre-cooled methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:2:1, v/v/v) to each sample, followed by vortexing, ultrasonication at 4 °C for 30 min, and incubation at −20 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, supernatants were vacuum-dried and reconstituted in 100 μl acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) for LC-MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was conducted using a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with a HILIC column (25 °C) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min with 2 μl injection volume. The mobile phase consisted of A (water containing 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide) and B (acetonitrile). The gradient program was: 0–1.5 min, 98% B; 1.5–12 min, 2% B; 12–14 min, 2% B; 14–14.1 min, 98% B; 14.1–17 min, 98% B. Samples were maintained at 4 °C in the autosampler and analyzed in random order with quality control samples inserted throughout the sequence to monitor system stability. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Q Exactive Orbitrap MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in both positive and negative ion modes. Source parameters were: Gas1 and Gas2 at 60, curtain gas at 30 psi, ion source temperature at 600 °C, and spray voltage at ±5500 V. Full scan data were acquired over m/z 80–1200 at 60,000 resolution, followed by data-dependent MS/MS acquisition at 30,000 resolution over m/z 70–1200 with dynamic exclusion of 4 s. For metabolite identification, retention time, theoretical monoisotopic mass, detected m/z for [M + H]⁺ and/or [M − H]⁻ ions with mass error (ppm), and MS/MS fragmentation patterns were recorded. Known compounds were annotated using public databases including HMDB and KEGG. Representative chromatograms are provided to support identification confidence. The “ropls” R package, which enabled multivariate data analysis, including Pareto-scaled principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), was used to analyze the processed data after sum-normalization. The robustness of the model was evaluated using response permutation testing and seven-fold cross-validation. Each variable’s impact on classification in the OPLS-DA model was evaluated by computing its variable importance in projection (VIP) score. Metabolites that had changed significantly were identified using a P value of less than 0.05 and a VIP greater than 160,61.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

As per the manufacturer’s instructions, the serum and cell culture medium levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were measured with ELISA kits (Neobioscience, China).

Molecular docking analysis

The utilization of the HDOCK online platform (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) was for the purpose of molecular docking analysis. Two proteins were analyzed for interaction amino acid residues using the PyMOL program.

Virtual screening and molecular docking

3191 natural compounds from commercially accessible sources were selected to create a screening library for the purpose of virtual screening of Btg2 inhibitors. Batch docking was performed using the AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 software in combination with the SailVina Python script program. Receiver-based virtual screening (RBS) uses a cavity detection method to automatically search for active binding pockets. By defaulting, all flexible bonds were rotated, atomic types were allocated, and atomic charges were applied. Based on pre-docked ligand-protein complexes, AutoDockTools was used to calculate the center and size of the docking box. The obtained parameters of the Btg2 protein docking box are 60 Å × 60 Å × 60 Å, with a grid spacing of 1.00 Å, and the coordinates are: X: Y: Z: −10.41: 1.577: −3.9. Having determined the docking parameters, RBS uses the SailVina docking program to minimize the energy of each drug when docking with the receptor and ranks them based on predicted affinity.

Molecular docking between Btg2, DTA and DTI was performed using Autodock Vina 1.1.2, and the outcomes were computed. PyMOL 2.4 combined with Discovery Studio 2025 was then used to visualize the results.

Molecular dynamics simulation

We conducted molecular dynamics simulations using Amber24 software with the ff19SB force field and OPC water model. The system maintained a 1.0 nm cutoff for both electrostatic and van der Waals interactions, with a 2 fs integration time step. Electrostatic interactions were treated via the PME method for long-range corrections. System conditions were controlled at 300 K and 1 bar using the V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat. After constructing the composite system, we performed energy minimization, followed by sequential equilibration: 200 ps under NVE ensemble and 100 ps under NPT ensemble. Production sampling lasted 100 ns. Trajectory analysis utilized Amber modules, Cpptraj, and Python scripts to calculate RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, and hydrogen bond counts.

Reagents intervention

DTA powder (Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology, China) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution. This stock solution was then sterilized by filtration and diluted in the cell culture medium to achieve the final working concentration of 25 µM for in vitro experiments. Primary hepatocytes were switched to fresh medium containing DTA after a 6-h attachment period post-isolation. This pre-treatment phase lasted for 12 h under normal culture condition.

For the in vivo model, DTA was suspended in saline containing 1% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose, and administered by oral gavage at a dose of 25 mg/kg once daily for 7 days prior to the induction of HIRI. Mice were sacrificed at right after the HIRI modeling to collect blood and liver tissues for analysis.

Taurine powder (MedChemExpress, USA) was dissolved in saline to prepare a solution of 25 μg/μl. For in vivo experiments, taurine solution was injected into mice (200 μg/g weight) via tain vein 1 h before HIRI.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For parametric data, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc test was employed to assess significant differences across groups. Two‑sided Student’s t-test or two‑sided Welch’s t test was utilized to compare two groups, two‑sided Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare two groups as the data were not normally distributed. Significance was established as a P value below 0.05. The results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.0; USA). Specific statistical methods are outlined in the respective figure legends where applicable.
