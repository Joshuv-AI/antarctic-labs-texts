Anti-self-dual blowups II
Vsevolod Shevchishin, Gleb Smirnov

Abstract
. Let X X be a closed, oriented four–manifold with b 2 + ⩽ 3 b_{2}^{+}\leqslant 3 , and suppose X X contains a collection of pairwise disjoint embedded ( − 2 ) (-2) –spheres. We prove that there is a Riemannian metric on X X such that the Poincare dual of each of these spheres is represented by an anti–self–dual harmonic 2 2 –form. This extends our earlier result for ( − 1 ) (-1) –spheres. The main new ingredient is an application of Eliashberg’s h h –principle for overtwisted contact structures, which we use to construct self–dual harmonic forms on four–orbifolds with prescribed local behaviour near the orbifold singular set.



1. Main result

Let
(X,g)
be a closed, oriented Riemannian
4
-manifold, and let

\Lambda^{2}=\Lambda^{2}T^{*}_{X}
denote the bundle of
2
-forms. The Hodge operator
\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\small$\displaystyle{*}$}}{*}{*}{*}}\colon\Lambda^{2}\to\Lambda^{2}
has eigenvalues
\pm 1
and induces
the splitting:

\Lambda^{2}=\Lambda^{+}\oplus\Lambda^{-},

where
\Lambda^{\pm}
are the

\pm 1
-eigenbundles. A
2
-form
\varphi
is self-dual (SD) if
\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\small$\displaystyle{*}$}}{*}{*}{*}}\varphi=\varphi
and anti-self-dual (ASD) if
\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\small$\displaystyle{*}$}}{*}{*}{*}}\varphi=-\varphi
. Let

\mathcal{H}^{2}_{g}=\{\varphi\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{2}):\Delta\varphi=0\}

be the space of harmonic
2
-forms.
By Hodge theory, every harmonic
2
-form is closed, and the natural map

\mathcal{H}^{2}_{g}\to H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
is an
isomorphism.
Since
\Delta
commutes with
\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\small$\displaystyle{*}$}}{*}{*}{*}}
, we obtain the splitting:

\mathcal{H}^{2}_{g}=\mathcal{H}_{+,g}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-,g},

where
\mathcal{H}_{+,g}
and
\mathcal{H}_{-,g}
are the spaces of harmonic SD and ASD forms.
\mathcal{H}_{+,g},\mathcal{H}_{-,g}\subset H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
are positive- and negative-definite with respect to the cup product, and they are orthogonal.
Their dimensions
b_{2}^{\pm}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{\pm,g}
are independent of
g
; they are the maximal dimensions of positive- and negative-definite subspaces of
H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
, hence topological invariants of
X
.

For each metric
g
, consider the positive-definite subspace:

P(g)=\mathcal{H}_{+,g}\subset H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})\quad\text{of dimension $b_{2}^{+}$}.

g\to P(g)
is the period map of
X
, introduced in
[
9
]
.
P(g)
depends only on the conformal class of
g
, and the map
P
is open in the
C^{\infty}
-topology on metrics.
It remains an open problem whether a given positive-definite subspace
\pi\subset H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
arises as
P(g)
for some metric
g
.

Let us now state our main result:

Theorem 1
.

Let
X
be a closed,
oriented
4
-manifold with
b^{+}_{2}\leqslant 3
.
Suppose
X
contains a collection of pairwise disjoint embedded spheres
S_{i}
, each with self-intersection
(-2)
.
Then there exists a Riemannian metric
g
on
X
such that every
g
-self-dual harmonic form
u
satisfies:

\int_{S_{i}}u=0\quad\text{for all }S_{i}.

Equivalently, if
s_{i}\in H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})
denotes the Poincaré dual of
S_{i}
, then each
s_{i}
is represented by an anti-self-dual form.

Scaduto
[
13
]
recently proved that the period map
P
has dense image for every
4
-manifold, and is
surjective when
b_{2}^{+}=1
. This does not imply Theorem
1
. Indeed, the conditions
s_{i}\perp P(g)
define a closed, positive-codimension subset of
the Grassmannian of positive
b_{2}^{+}
-planes in
H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
, and a dense open subset may avoid such a set.

Remark
.

Consider the following example. Let
X
be the underlying manifold of a smooth complex K3 surface. The cup product on
H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
has signature
(3,19)
. Let
Gr^{+}_{X}
be the Grassmannian of
3
-dimensional positive-definite planes in
H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
. For any class
\delta\in H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})
with
\delta^{2}=-2
,
define:

\delta^{\perp}=\{\pi\in Gr^{+}_{X}:\pi\perp\delta\}.

Set:

D=Gr^{+}_{X}-\bigcup_{\delta^{2}=-2}\delta^{\perp}.

The global Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces states that
D
is precisely the points in
Gr^{+}_{X}
realized as the period of a Kähler metric on
X
; see
[
15
,
21
]
. Each
\delta^{\perp}
has codimension
2
in
Gr^{+}_{X}
, so
D
is open and dense. Although no point of
\delta^{\perp}
can be the period of a Kähler metric, it may still occur as the period of a more general Riemannian metric. This indeed occurs: Kummer surfaces, which are K3, contain
16
disjoint embedded rational curves. Thus, Theorem
1
produces metrics for which up to
16
pairwise disjoint integral
(-2)
-classes are simultaneously represented by ASD forms.

In our previous work
[
14
]
, we proved
the analogue of Theorem
1
for collections of pairwise disjoint embedded
(-1)
-spheres.
Both proofs follow the same overall strategy,
but the
(-2)
-case presents an additional difficulty, which we now describe.

The first step is to construct a Riemannian orbifold
(M,g)
together with a closed SD form that vanishes at a prescribed point
p\in M
. Blowing up
M
at
p
then produces a manifold
X
with an embedded exceptional sphere. In the
(-1)
-case, the point
p
may be chosen arbitrarily, so producing a metric
g
with a closed SD form vanishing somewhere is relatively straightforward. In contrast, in the
(-2)
-case, the point
p
must be the orbifold
point of
M
; otherwise the exceptional sphere created
in the resolution has self-intersection
(-1)

rather than
(-2)
. Thus, the metric
g
on
M
has to be chosen much more carefully.

The new geometric input
is the following existence theorem for closed SD forms with prescribed zeros. Its proof occupies §
5
, §
6
, and §
7
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
M
be a compact, oriented
4
-orbifold
with isolated singular points
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
, each of type
\mathbb{R}^{4}/\{\pm 1\}
, and assume
b_{2}^{+}(M)\geqslant 1
. Then there exist a Riemannian metric
g
on
M
, flat in neighborhoods of all
p_{i}
, and a closed,
g
-self-dual orbifold form
\psi
such that
\psi(p_{i})=0
for all
i
.

For each
p_{i}
, fix an orbifold chart:

\pi_{i}:(B^{4},g_{\mathrm{euc}})\longrightarrow(U_{i},g)\cong B^{4}/\{\pm 1\}\ni p_{i},

which is an isometry. An orbifold form
means that on
U_{i}-\{p_{i}\}
, the
pullback
\pi_{i}^{*}\psi
is antipodal-invariant and extends smoothly over
0\in B^{4}
;
\psi(p_{i})=0
means that
\pi_{i}^{*}\psi
vanishes at
0\in B^{4}
.

The proof of
Proposition
1
proceeds as follows.
To begin with, we reduce the problem to constructing a near-symplectic form on
M
with prescribed behavior near the points
p_{i}
. This is done via a theorem of Auroux-Donaldson-Katzarkov (Proposition 1 in
[
2
]
). Then, following Taubes
[
20
]
, we construct such a near-symplectic form via Eliashberg’s classification of overtwisted contact structures in dimension
3

[
3
]
.

With Proposition
1
in hand,
our argument proceeds as follows.
In §
2
, we introduce an iterative neck-stretching scheme for closed SD forms on manifolds with a long cylindrical neck. In §
3
, we apply
this scheme to the blowup of
M
at
p
.
This shows that, after inserting a sufficiently long neck, one can find a metric
g
for which
P(g)
is
arbitrarily close to being orthogonal to a prescribed collection of
(-2)
-classes. In §
4
, we then use Proposition
1
to deform such almost-orthogonal metrics so that those
(-2)
-classes become genuinely ASD, thereby proving Theorem
1
. Although these arguments closely parallel our earlier work
[
14
]
, we reprove certain steps here for completeness.

Combining Theorem
1

with the main result of
[
14
]
, one obtains the following mixed statement.

Theorem
.

Let
X
be a closed,
oriented
4
-manifold with
b^{+}_{2}\leqslant 3
.
Suppose
X
contains a collection of pairwise disjoint embedded spheres
S_{i}
, each with
self-intersection
(-1)
or
(-2)
.
Then there exists a Riemannian metric
g
on
X
such that every
g
-self-dual harmonic form
u
satisfies:

\int_{S_{i}}u=0\quad\text{for all }S_{i}.

This statement does not require any new ideas beyond those used in the pure
(-1)
– and
(-2)
–cases. Our neck–stretching scheme treats all spheres simultaneously. We
therefore omit a separate proof.

Remark
.

A closed ASD
2
-form whose cohomology class is integral is the curvature of a line bundle with an ASD connection. Thus, our neck-stretching construction is formally similar to Taubes’ existence proofs for ASD connections

[
18
,
16
,
17
]
on higher-dimensional
bundles. However, unlike Taubes’ positive-index problems, the condition that a given set of
(-2)
-classes be represented by ASD forms is not open in the
C^{\infty}
-topology on metrics; the index of our problem is negative.

2. A neck-stretching argument

The gluing argument in this section is inspired by the existing literature (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
11
,
18
]
), but we present it in a form tailored to our later applications.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be compact, oriented
4
–manifolds with common boundary

\partial X_{1}=\partial X_{2}=Y.

Fix
\delta>0
small. On each
X_{i}
choose a Riemannian metric
g_{i}
which, on a collar neighborhood

(-\delta,1]\times Y\subset X_{i},

splits as a product:

(2.1)

g_{i}=dt\otimes dt+g_{Y}.

Throughout let us take
Y=\mathbb{RP}^{3}
equipped with the metric
g_{Y}
induced from the unit round metric

g_{S^{3}}
on
S^{3}
via the double cover
S^{3}\to\mathbb{RP}^{3}
. All results remain valid if
H^{1}(Y;\mathbb{R})=0
.

Let

P_{i}=[0,1]\times Y\subset X_{i}

denote the smaller collar of the boundary.

Let us form the non-compact manifold

X_{i}^{\infty}
by attaching the semi-infinite cylinder
[0,\infty)\times Y
along
P_{i}
, and extending the product metric (
2.1
) over the cylindrical end. The resulting metric is denoted
g_{i}^{\infty}
.

Fix
T>0
and consider the finite cylinder:

N=[0,T]\times Y,

equipped with the product metric (
2.1
). Introduce the extended cylinder:

N^{\prime}=[-1,\,T+1]\times Y,

and attach it to
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
as follows: the collar
P_{1}
is identified with the subcylinder
[-1,0]\times Y\subset N^{\prime}
, and
P_{2}
with
[T,\,T+1]\times Y\subset N^{\prime}
. For each
T
, this yields a closed
4
–manifold:

X_{T}=X_{1}\cup N\cup X_{2},

carrying a metric
g_{T}
which equals
g_{i}
on
X_{i}
and the product metric on
N=[0,T]\times Y
.

Inside
N
, let us single out the unit cylinders:

Q_{1}=[0,1]\times Y,\quad Q_{2}=[T-1,\,T]\times Y.

Let

\mathcal{D}=d+d^{*},\quad\Delta=\mathcal{D}^{\,2}

denote the Hodge operator and the Hodge Laplacian.

Let
L^{2}(X_{i}^{\infty})
denote the space of

L^{2}
–integrable differential forms on
X_{i}^{\infty}
with respect to the metric
g_{i}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}^{+}(X_{i}^{\infty})
denote
the space of SD harmonic forms on
X_{i}^{\infty}
that are
also
L_{2}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{H}^{+}(X_{i}^{\infty})=\left\{\text{$g_{i}^{\infty}$-self-dual}\right\}\cap\operatorname{ker}\Delta\cap L^{2}(X_{i}^{\infty}).

For a domain
A
, use
\|\cdot\|_{k,A}
to denote the
L^{2}_{k}
–Sobolev norm on
A
.

Introduce cut-off functions
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
such that:

(1)

\rho_{2}=0
on
X_{1}-P_{1}
,
\rho_{2}=1
on
X_{T}-X_{1}
, and
\rho_{2}
interpolates between
0
and
1
on
P_{1}
.

(2)

\rho_{1}=0
on
X_{2}-P_{2}
,
\rho_{1}=1
on
X_{T}-X_{2}
, and
\rho_{1}
interpolates between
0
and
1
on
P_{2}
.

Fix a form
\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{+}(X_{1}^{\infty})
. Construct two sequences of smooth SD forms,

v^{(i)},\ u^{(i)},\quad i\geqslant 1,

such that:

v^{(i)}\in L^{2}(X_{1}^{\infty})\ \text{for $i$ odd},\qquad v^{(i)}\in L^{2}(X_{2}^{\infty})\ \text{for $i$ even},\qquad u^{(i)}\in L^{2}(X_{T})\ \text{for all $i$},

via the recursion:

(a)

v^{(1)}=\psi
.

(b)

If
i
is odd, restrict
v^{(i)}
to
X_{1}\cup N\cup P_{2}
and set:

u^{(i)}=\rho_{1}\,v^{(i)}.

If
i
is even, restrict
v^{(i)}
to
X_{2}\cup N\cup P_{1}
and set:

u^{(i)}=\rho_{2}\,v^{(i)}.

(c)

Suppose
i
is odd (the even case is symmetric). Construct an SD form
v^{(i+1)}\in L^{2}(X_{2}^{\infty})

solving

\mathcal{D}v^{(i+1)}=-\mathcal{D}u^{(i)}.

The construction produces elliptic estimates of the form:

(2.2)

\|v^{(i+1)}\|_{k,\,X_{2}\cup Q_{2}}\;\leqslant\;A_{k}\,\|\Delta u^{(i)}\|_{k-2,\,P_{2}},

with constants
A_{k}
independent of
T
and
i
.
For even
i
, an identical estimate holds on
X_{1}\cup Q_{1}
.

Let us now explain step (c) in detail for odd
i
. The even case is similar.

To begin with, assume
v^{(i-1)}
and
u^{(i-1)}
have already been constructed. Fix numbers
0<T_{1}<T_{2}
and choose a smooth, positive function

\chi\colon[0,\infty)\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

\chi(t,y)=\begin{cases}1,&t\in[0,\,T_{1}],\\[4.0pt]
e^{-2t},&t\geqslant T_{2}.\end{cases}

Extend
\chi
to all of
X_{1}^{\infty}
by setting
it equal to
1
on
X_{1}
. Consider the conformal
rescaling:

\hat{g}_{1}=\chi\,g_{1}^{\infty}.

(X_{1}^{\infty},\hat{g}_{1})
has an asymptotically Euclidean end.
Indeed, setting
r=e^{-t}
yields:

e^{-2t}\bigl(dt\otimes dt+g_{S^{3}}\bigr)=dr\otimes dr+r^{2}g_{S^{3}}.

Thus, the region
[T_{2},\infty)\times Y
endowed with
\hat{g}_{1}
is isometric to the punctured closed ball of radius
e^{-T_{2}}
modulo the antipodal map. Adding the point at infinity produces a compact Riemannian orbifold, smooth away from the added point, where the metric is locally isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{4}/\{\pm 1\}
.

Use
\hat{X}_{1}
to denote the orbifold compactification of
X_{1}^{\infty}
.

Choose a neighborhood
U\subset\hat{X}_{1}
of the orbifold point together with an isometry:

B\xrightarrow{\ \pi\ }B/\{\pm 1\}\xrightarrow{\ \tau\ }U,

where
\pi
identifies antipodal points and
\tau
is the chosen isometry.
A differential form
\phi
is smooth on
U
if
(\pi\circ\tau)^{*}\phi
extends smoothly across
the origin in
B
. A form on
\hat{X}_{1}

is smooth if it is smooth away from the orbifold point and smooth on
U
. On the domain of such smooth forms, the operator
\mathcal{D}
remains elliptic and self-adjoint, so standard Hodge theory applies.

\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}
is supported in
P_{1}\cup P_{2}
.
Let us restrict
\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}
to
X_{1}\cup N
and extend it by zero to the remainder of
\hat{X}_{1}
. Let us continue to denote this extension by
\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}(\hat{X}_{1})
denote the space of harmonic

2
-forms on
(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{g}_{1})
. For any
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}(\hat{X}_{1})
,

\int_{\hat{X}_{1}}\langle\varphi,\Delta u^{(i-1)}\rangle=\int_{\hat{X}_{1}}\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}\rangle=0,

because
\Delta\varphi=0
implies
\mathcal{D}\varphi=0
. Thus,
\Delta u^{(i-1)}
is
L^{2}
–orthogonal to

\mathcal{H}(\hat{X}_{1})
. Hodge theory on the compact orbifold
(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{g}_{1})
then gives a unique
SD form
\hat{v}^{(i)}
such that:

\Delta\hat{v}^{(i)}=-\,\Delta u^{(i-1)},\qquad\hat{v}^{(i)}\perp\mathcal{H}(\hat{X}_{1}).

This can be strengthened:

Lemma 1
.

\mathcal{D}\hat{v}^{(i)}=-\,\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}
for
\hat{v}^{(i)}
defined above.

Proof.

To begin with, observe that
du^{(i-1)}
is an exact
3
-form
on
\hat{X}_{1}
. Indeed:

du^{(i-1)}=d(\rho_{2}v^{(i-1)})=d\rho_{2}\wedge v^{(i-1)}.

Since
H^{2}(Y;\mathbb{R})=0
, there exists a
1
-form
\alpha
on
P_{1}
with
d\alpha=v^{(i-1)}
. Hence:

du^{(i-1)}=-d(d\rho\wedge\alpha)\quad\text{on $P_{1}$.}

Consider
\hat{v}^{(i)}+u^{(i-1)}
. By construction:

{\mathcal{D}}^{2}(\hat{v}^{(i)}+u^{(i-1)})=0.

Thus:

\Delta{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{v}^{(i)}+u^{(i-1)})=\Delta d(\hat{v}^{(i)}+u^{(i-1)})+\Delta d^{*}(\hat{v}^{(i)}+u^{(i-1)})=0.

In particular,
d(\hat{v}^{(i)}+u^{(i-1)})
is harmonic. Since it is also exact, it must vanish. Both
\hat{v}^{(i)}
and
u^{(i-1)}
are self-dual; therefore:

\mathcal{D}(\hat{v}^{(i)}+u^{(i-1)})=d(\hat{v}^{(i)}+u^{(i-1)})-*\,d(\hat{v}^{(i)}+u^{(i-1)})=0,

and the lemma follows. ∎

Elliptic regularity now gives, for each
k
,

\|\hat{v}^{(i)}\|_{k,\hat{X}_{1}}\;\leqslant\;A_{k}\,\|\Delta u^{(i-1)}\|_{k-2,\,\hat{X}_{1}}=A_{k}\,\|\Delta u^{(i-1)}\|_{k-2,\,P_{1}},

with constants
A_{k}
depending only on
\hat{g}_{1}
.

Restrict
\hat{v}^{(i)}
to the cylindrical-end manifold:

v^{(i)}=\hat{v}^{(i)}\big|_{X_{1}^{\infty}}.

Choosing
T_{2}
large enough, assume
\chi=1
on
X_{1}\cup Q_{1}
. On that region, the metrics
g_{1}^{\infty}
and
\hat{g}_{1}
agree, and hence:

\|v^{(i)}\|_{k,\,X_{1}\cup Q_{1}}\;\leqslant\;\|\hat{v}^{(i)}\|_{k,\hat{X}_{1}}\;\leqslant\;A_{k}\,\|\Delta u^{(i-1)}\|_{k-2,\,P_{1}},

which is precisely (
2.2
).

The
L^{2}
–norm of
2
–forms is
conformally invariant; therefore, if

\hat{v}^{(i)}
is
L^{2}
for
\hat{g}_{1}
, then it also
L^{2}
for
g_{1}^{\infty}
.

Finally, the equation

\mathcal{D}v^{(i)}=-\,\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}

holds on
(X_{1}^{\infty},\hat{g}_{1})
by construction.
To see that it also holds for
g_{1}^{\infty}
, note that the kernel of
\mathcal{D}
is locally conformally invariant, so the only issue is the region where

\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}
is supported. But
\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}
is supported in
P_{1}
, where
\chi=1
and
g_{1}^{\infty}=\hat{g}_{1}
. This completes step (c).

Iterating this procedure one obtains sequences
\{u^{(i)}\}
and
\{v^{(i)}\}
as above. Formally, one expects:

\mathcal{D}\!\left(\sum_{i}u^{(i)}\right)=0.

More precisely, for each
i
:

•

the support of
\mathcal{D}u^{(i)}
is contained in
P_{1}\cup P_{2}
;

•

on
P_{2}
we have
\mathcal{D}u^{(i)}=-\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}
for even
i
,
while on
P_{1}
we have
\mathcal{D}u^{(i)}=-\mathcal{D}u^{(i-1)}
for odd
i
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
k>0
. For all large enough
T
, the series

u=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u^{(i)}

converges in the
L^{2}_{k}
-norm on
(X_{T},g_{T})
, and its tail satisfies:

\|\,u-u^{(1)}\,\|_{k,\,X_{T}}=O(e^{-2T}),\quad\|\,u-u^{(1)}-u^{(2)}\,\|_{k,\,X_{T}}=O(e^{-4T}).

Proof.

It is enough to find constants
C_{k}>0
, independent of
T
and
i
, such that:

(2.3)

\|u^{(i)}\|_{k,\,X_{T}}\;\leqslant\;C_{k}\,e^{-2T}\,\|u^{(i-1)}\|_{k,\,X_{T}}.

The needed convergence then follows by summing a geometric series.

To this end, let us study harmonic forms on the cylindrical neck. On
N=[0,T]\times Y
, the Hodge Laplacian splits as:

\Delta=-\partial_{t}^{2}+\Delta_{3},

where
\Delta_{3}
is the Hodge Laplacian on
Y
, acting in the
y
-variables only.
Since
\psi
is harmonic on
X_{1}^{\infty}
, its restriction to
N
admits a Fourier expansion:

(2.4)

\psi=\sum_{\lambda}\bigl(\alpha_{\lambda}+\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\small$\displaystyle{*}$}}{*}{*}{*}}\alpha_{\lambda}\bigr)\,e^{-\lambda t},

where
\lambda
ranges over square roots (positive and negative) of eigenvalues of
\Delta_{3}
acting on
2
-forms, the
\alpha_{\lambda}
are
\Delta_{3}
-eigenforms with eigenvalue
\lambda^{2}
, and
\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\small$\displaystyle{*}$}}{*}{*}{*}}
is the Hodge star on
Y
.

Eigenvalues and eigenforms of
\Delta_{3}
are known (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
).
{\mathcal{D}}\psi=0
forces

d\alpha_{\lambda}=0
. Hence, only closed eigenforms occur.
The smallest eigenvalue of the Hodge Laplacian on closed
2
-forms on the unit sphere
S^{3}
is
4
, and these eigenforms descend to
\mathbb{RP}^{3}
. Thus, the smallest eigenvalue for closed
2
-forms on
Y
is also
4
. Consequently, every
\lambda
in (
2.4
)
satisfies
\lambda\geqslant 2
. (Only the fact that the smallest eigenvalue is positive will be used below.)

The
L^{2}
condition on
X_{1}^{\infty}
rules out negative
\lambda
in
(
2.4
), so we may rewrite:

\psi=\sum_{\lambda\geqslant 2}(\alpha_{\lambda}+\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\small$\displaystyle{*}$}}{*}{*}{*}}\alpha_{\lambda})\,e^{-\lambda t}.

For
s\in[0,T]
, define the translated unit cylinder:

Q_{1}+s=[s,s+1]\times Y\subset N.

Since the
\alpha_{\lambda}
are independent of

,

\|\psi\|^{2}_{0,Q_{1}+s}=\sum_{\lambda\geqslant 2}\int_{s}^{s+1}2\,e^{-2\lambda t}\bigg(\int_{Y}\langle\alpha_{\lambda},\alpha_{\lambda}\rangle\bigg)\,dt\;\leqslant\;e^{-4s}\,\|\psi\|^{2}_{0,Q_{1}}.

Thus:

(2.5)

\|\psi\|_{0,Q_{1}+s}\;\leqslant\;e^{-2s}\,\|\psi\|_{0,Q_{1}}.

Choose
\delta>0
small so that
g_{T}
remains product
on the longer collars

[-\delta,\,1+\delta]\times Y
and
[T-1-\delta,\,T+\delta]\times Y
for all large
T
. Interior elliptic estimates for the translation-invariant operator
\Delta
imply, for each
k
,

(2.6)

\|\psi\|^{2}_{k,\,Q_{1}+s}\leqslant B^{\prime}_{k}\Bigl(\|\psi\|^{2}_{0,\,Q_{1}+s+\delta}+\|\psi\|^{2}_{0,\,Q_{1}+s-\delta}\Bigr),

with a constant
B^{\prime}_{k}
independent of
s
and
T
. Combining
(
2.5
) and (
2.6
), we find:

\|\psi\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}+s}\leqslant B_{k}e^{-4s}\,\|\psi\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}}

for a suitable
B_{k}
independent of
s
and
T
. Summing over

s=0,1,\dots,T-1
, we obtain:

\|\psi\|^{2}_{k,N}=\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\|\psi\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}+j}\leqslant D_{k}\,\|\psi\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}},

and at the far end of the neck,

\|\psi\|^{2}_{k,P_{2}}=\|\psi\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}+T}\leqslant B_{k}e^{-4T}\,\|\psi\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}}.

B_{k},D_{k}
depend only on
(Y,g_{Y})
and the choice of

\delta
. Hence, the same estimates hold with
v^{(i)}
in place of
\psi
:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\|v^{(i)}\|_{k,N}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant D_{k}\,\|v^{(i)}\|_{k,Q_{1}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|v^{(i)}\|_{k,P_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant B_{k}e^{-4T}\,\|v^{(i)}\|_{k,Q_{1}}^{2},

\displaystyle\text{if $i$ is odd},

\displaystyle\|v^{(i)}\|_{k,N}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant D_{k}\,\|v^{(i)}\|_{k,Q_{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|v^{(i)}\|_{k,P_{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant B_{k}e^{-4T}\,\|v^{(i)}\|_{k,Q_{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\text{if $i$ is even}.

On
Q_{1}
(for odd
i
) and on
Q_{2}
(for even
i
), we have
u^{(i)}=v^{(i)}
. Since

u^{(i)}
and
v^{(i)}
also agree on
N
, (
2.7
)
implies:

(A)

\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,N}\leqslant D_{k}\,\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}}\quad\text{for $i$ odd,}\qquad\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,N}\leqslant D_{k}\,\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,Q_{2}}\quad\text{for $i$ even.}

\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are smooth, so for we obtain:

\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,P_{2}}\leqslant E_{k}\|v^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,P_{2}}\quad\text{for $i$ odd,}\qquad\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,P_{1}}\leqslant E_{k}\|v^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,P_{1}}\quad\text{for $i$ even,}

Combining this
with (
2.7
), we find:

(B)

\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,P_{2}}\leqslant F_{k}e^{-4T}\,\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}}\quad\text{for $i$ odd,}\qquad\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,P_{1}}\leqslant F_{k}e^{-4T}\,\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,Q_{2}}\quad\text{for $i$ even,}

where
F_{k}=B_{k}E_{k}
.

Finally, using (
2.2
) together with continuity of

, we obtain:

(C)

\displaystyle\|u^{(i)}\|_{k,\,X_{1}\cup Q_{1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant A_{k}\,\|\Delta u^{(i-1)}\|_{k-2,\,P_{1}}\leqslant G_{k}\,\|u^{(i-1)}\|_{k,\,P_{1}},

\displaystyle\text{if $i$ is odd},

\displaystyle\|u^{(i)}\|_{k,\,X_{2}\cup Q_{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant A_{k}\,\|\Delta u^{(i-1)}\|_{k-2,\,P_{2}}\leqslant G_{k}\,\|u^{(i-1)}\|_{k,\,P_{2}},

\displaystyle\text{if $i$ is even},

for some
G_{k}
.

Let us now estimate
\|u^{(i)}\|_{k,X_{T}}
for odd
i
(the even case is symmetric). We compute:

\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,X_{T}}=\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,X_{1}}+\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,N}+\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,P_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{A}}}{{\leqslant}}\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,X_{1}}+D_{k}\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}}+\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,P_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{B}}}{{\leqslant}}\\
\leqslant\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,X_{1}}+D_{k}\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}}+e^{-4\,T}F_{k}\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,Q_{1}}\leqslant H_{k}\|u^{(i)}\|^{2}_{k,X_{1}\cup Q_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{C}}}{{\leqslant}}\\
\leqslant H_{k}G_{k}^{2}\|u^{(i-1)}\|^{2}_{k,P_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{B}}}{{\leqslant}}e^{-4\,T}F_{k}H_{k}G_{k}^{2}\|u^{(i-1)}\|^{2}_{k,Q_{2}}\leqslant e^{-4\,T}C_{k}^{2}\|u^{(i-1)}\|^{2}_{k,X_{T}}.

This completes the proof. ∎

Let us now isolate the contribution of
u^{(1)}
to the full series
u
. Along the neck, write the Fourier expansion of
\psi
as:

(2.8)

\psi=\sum_{\lambda\geqslant 2}\psi_{\lambda},\qquad\psi_{\lambda}=a_{\lambda}e^{-\lambda t},

where each
\psi_{\lambda}
is an SD eigenform of

-\partial_{t}^{2}+\Delta_{3}
with parameter
\lambda
.

Let
v^{(2)}
be as above. Decompose it as follows:

v^{(2)}=\sum_{\lambda\geqslant 2}v^{(2)}_{\lambda},

where
v^{(2)}_{\lambda}\in L^{2}(X_{2}^{\infty})
solves

{\mathcal{D}}v^{(2)}_{\lambda}=-{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{1}\psi_{\lambda}).

Define
u^{(2)}_{\lambda}=\rho_{2}v^{(2)}_{\lambda}
on
X_{T}
so that

u^{(2)}=\sum_{\lambda\geqslant 2}u^{(2)}_{\lambda}.

Let
c>0
be such that

is the next eigenvalue of
\Delta_{3}
on closed
2
-forms after the minimal eigenvalue
4
. Let us show
that

is well-approximated by the part
corresponding to the lowest eigenvalue.

Lemma 3
.

For each
k
, we have:

\bigg\|\sum_{\lambda>2}u^{(2)}_{\lambda}\bigg\|_{k,X_{2}}=O(e^{-cT}),

for

as above.

Proof.

By the definition of

, we have:

\mathcal{D}\!\left(\sum_{\lambda\geqslant c}u^{(2)}_{\lambda}\right)=-\,\mathcal{D}\!\left(\rho_{1}\sum_{\lambda\geqslant c}\psi_{\lambda}\right).

Using (

), we find:

\bigg\|\sum_{\lambda\geqslant c}u^{(2)}_{\lambda}\bigg\|_{k,X_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{C}}}{{\leqslant}}G_{k}\Big\|\rho_{1}\sum_{\lambda\geqslant c}\psi_{\lambda}\Big\|_{k,P_{2}}\leqslant G_{k}\sqrt{E_{k}}\,\Big\|\sum_{\lambda\geqslant c}\psi_{\lambda}\Big\|_{k,P_{2}}.

For the tail

repeat the decay argument used to prove (

), but using
that now

. This gives:

\Big\|\sum_{\lambda\geqslant c}\psi_{\lambda}\Big\|_{k,P_{2}}\leqslant B_{k}e^{-cT}\Big\|\sum_{\lambda\geqslant c}\psi_{\lambda}\Big\|_{0,Q_{1}}=O(e^{-cT}),

3. Resolution formula

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian
4
–orbifold, and let
p\in M
be an isolated
orbifold point modelled on
\mathbb{R}^{4}/\{\pm 1\}
with the standard flat metric.
Thus there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
p
and an isometry:

U\simeq(B/\{\pm 1\},\,g_{0}),

where
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
is a ball centered at the origin,
g_{0}
is the flat metric (the action of
\{\pm 1\}
is orthogonal).

Choose a linear complex structure on

\mathbb{R}^{4}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
g_{0}
is Kähler. Then view
U
as a complex orbifold domain. Let
\omega
denote the Kähler form on
U
associated to
g
, and let

r\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
be the distance to
p
with respect to

g
. On
U
one has:

\omega=-\,d\,d^{\mathbb{C}}r^{2}.

After rescaling
g
, we may assume:

U=\{x\in M\mid r(x)<1\}.

The unitary group
U(2)
preserves
(\mathbb{C}^{2},g_{0})
and commutes with the action of
\{\pm 1\}
; hence, it induces an isometric action on
U
that leaves both
r
and
\omega
invariant.

Let
Y\subset U
be defined as:

Y=\{x\in M\mid r(x)=1\},

and let
g_{Y}
be the induced metric. Geometrically,
Y
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
modulo the antipodal map.

Choose
a>0
so small that
a\cdot e<1
(
\ln e=1
), and set:

N=\{x\in U\mid a\leqslant r(x)\leqslant a\cdot e\}.

To apply Lemma
2
, introduce the annuli:

P_{1}=\{x\in U\mid a\cdot e\leqslant r(x)\leqslant a\cdot e^{2}\},\qquad P_{2}=\{x\in U\mid a\cdot e^{-1}\leqslant r(x)\leqslant a\},

which agree with the notation in §
2
.

The metric on
N
has the form:

dr\otimes dr+r^{2}g_{Y}.

Hence,
N
is conformally equivalent to the cylinder

[0,1]\times Y
with the product metric:

dt\otimes dt+g_{Y}.

Let us modify
M
as follows. Consider the neighbourhood of
p
defined as:

U_{a}=\{x\in U\mid r(x)<a\}\subset M.

This neighbourhood contains the cuff
P_{2}
but not the neck
N
.
Let

\sigma\colon X_{2}\longrightarrow U_{a}

be the minimal resolution of
U_{a}
, obtained by blowing up the orbifold point
p
. The exceptional set is an embedded sphere
C\subset X_{2}
with self–intersection
(-2)
.

Consider a smaller neighbourhood of
p
defined as:

U_{\delta}=\{x\in U\mid r(x)<\delta\}\subset M.

By standard complex–analytic results (see, e.g., Ch. 1 of
[
6
]
), the complex surface
X_{2}
admits a strictly
plurisubharmonic function
h\colon X_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

h=r^{2}\quad\text{on }X_{2}-\sigma^{-1}(U_{\delta}).

Define a Kähler form:

\omega^{\prime}=-\,d\,d^{\mathbb{C}}h\quad\text{on }X_{2},

and let
g^{\prime}
be the associated Riemannian metric. By construction,
g
and
g^{\prime}
agree away from
U_{\delta}
.

Let us now build a manifold
X
by cutting out
U_{a}
from
M
and gluing in
(X_{2},g^{\prime})
along the common boundary:

X=(M-U_{a})\,\cup_{\partial X_{2}}\,X_{2}.

Introduce a conformal transformation of the metric on
X
.
Let
\chi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive function such that the
\chi
-rescaled metric on
P_{1}\cup N\cup P_{2}
takes the form:

dt\otimes dt+g_{Y},

and such that
\chi=1
away from a small neighbourhood of
P_{1}\cup N\cup P_{2}
. For
T>0
, let
(X_{T},g_{T})
denote the space obtained from
X
by replacing the factor
[0,1]
in
N
by
[0,T]
.
When
T
is large,
(X_{T},g_{T})
contains a long cylindrical region.

Lemma 4
.

Let
M
,
X
, and
X_{T}
be as above, and let
C\subset X_{2}
be the exceptional

(-2)
–sphere. Assume that
M
carries an SD harmonic
form
\psi
. Let
\langle\psi,\omega\rangle_{p}
denote the pointwise inner product with respect to
g
, evaluated at
p
. Then, for all large enough
T
, there exists an SD
harmonic form
u
on

X_{T}
such that:

\|u-\psi\|_{C^{0},\,X_{1}}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }T\to\infty,

and

(3.1)

\int_{C}u=A\,\langle\omega,\psi\rangle_{p}\,e^{-2T}+O(e^{-cT})

for some constants
A>0
and
c>2
independent of
T
.

To clarify the quantity
\langle\omega,\psi\rangle_{p}
: choose a uniformizing chart

\pi\colon(B,g_{0})\to(U,g)
. Both
\omega
and
\psi
are smooth on
U
; thus their lifts

\pi^{*}\omega
and
\pi^{*}\psi
are smooth,
\{\pm 1\}
-invariant forms on
B
. Define
\langle\omega,\psi\rangle_{p}
by

\langle\omega,\psi\rangle_{p}=\big\langle\pi^{*}\omega,\,\pi^{*}\psi\big\rangle_{0\in B}
, which is independent of the chosen uniformizing chart.

Proof.

In the neighbourhood
U
of
p
, the form
\psi
admits a Fourier expansion:

\psi=\sum_{\lambda\geqslant 2}\psi_{\lambda},\qquad\psi_{\lambda}=a_{\lambda}\,r^{\lambda},

where each
\psi_{\lambda}
is an SD harmonic form on
U
.
Smoothness at the orbifold point forces all exponents to be positive.
Introducing the cylindrical coordinate
t
via
r=e^{-t}
, the series takes the form used in (
2.8
).

Let
P_{1},P_{2}
be as above, and let
\chi
be the function such that the
\chi g
satisfies:

dt\otimes dt+g_{Y}\quad\text{on }P_{1}\cup N\cup P_{2},

and such that
\chi
equals
1
away from a small
neighbourhood of
P_{1}\cup N\cup P_{2}
.

Replace the original neck
N
by a longer cylindrical region
[0,T]\times Y
, and analytically continue
\psi
from
P_{1}
to
P_{2}
along this neck. On
P_{2}
, the continued
form has the expansion:

\psi=\sum_{\lambda\geqslant 2}\psi_{\lambda}\,e^{-\lambda T},

and this series converges on all of
M_{2}
. For
\lambda>2
, the norm of
\psi_{\lambda}
tends to
0
as
r\to 0
. Hence, the leading contribution to
\langle\psi,\omega\rangle
at
p
comes from the
\lambda=2
mode, giving:

\langle\psi,\omega\rangle_{p}=e^{-2T}\,\langle\psi_{2},\omega\rangle_{p}.

Choose holomorphic coordinates
(z,w)
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and set:

\Omega=dz\wedge dw.

The form
\Omega
is invariant under the action of
\{\pm 1\}
, so it descends to
U
. The eigenspace of the Laplacian on
Y
with eigenvalue
4
is
3
–dimensional, and we may write:

\psi_{2}=a_{1}\,\omega_{1}+a_{2}\,\omega_{2}+a_{3}\,\omega_{3},\qquad a_{i}\in\mathbb{R},

where:

\omega_{1}=\omega,\qquad\omega_{2}=\operatorname{Re}(\Omega),\qquad\omega_{3}=\operatorname{Im}(\Omega).

These satisfy:

\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{j}\rangle=2\,\delta_{ij}\quad\text{on }U,

so in particular:

\langle\psi_{2},\omega\rangle=2\,a_{1}.

Replacing
U_{a}
by
X_{2}
produces
X_{T}
. Using Lemma
2
, construct an SD harmonic form
u
on
X_{T}
such that:

u=\rho_{1}\psi+u^{(2)}+O(e^{-4T}),

with
d\rho_{1}
supported in
P_{2}
. Using Lemma
3
, when restricted to
X_{2}
, we obtain:

u^{(2)}=u^{(2)}_{2}+O(e^{-cT}),\qquad\mathcal{D}u^{(2)}_{2}=-\,e^{-2T}\,\mathcal{D}(\rho_{1}\psi_{2}),

for some
c>2
. To solve

\mathcal{D}u^{(2)}_{2}=-\,e^{-2T}\,\mathcal{D}(\rho_{1}\psi_{2}),

observe that
\psi_{2}
extends from
P_{2}
to a closed SD form on all of
X_{2}
:

\eta=a_{1}\,\omega^{\prime}+a_{2}\,\operatorname{Re}(\sigma^{*}\Omega)+a_{3}\,\operatorname{Im}(\sigma^{*}\Omega).

Indeed, the holomorphic form
\sigma^{*}\Omega
is
g^{\prime}
–self–dual, and
\omega^{\prime}
is Kähler for
g^{\prime}
; both remain self–dual after introducing the conformal factor
\chi
.

Thus:

u^{(2)}_{2}=e^{-2T}\,(1-\rho_{1})\,\eta.

Since
\rho_{1}=0
near the exceptional sphere

, we obtain:

u=e^{-2T}\,\eta+O(e^{-cT})\quad\text{near }C.

We compute:

\int_{C}u=e^{-2T}\int_{C}\eta+O(e^{-cT})=e^{-2T}\int_{C}a_{1}\,\omega^{\prime}+O(e^{-cT})=e^{-2T}\,\langle\psi_{2},\omega\rangle_{p}\,A+O(e^{-cT}),

for some constant
A>0
independent of

. This completes the proof. ∎

4. Proof of Theorem

\mathbb{R}^{4}/\{\pm 1\}

There exist neighbourhoods

5. Near-contact forms

In this section we consider contact forms with isolated, nondegenerate singularities on
3
-manifolds. The goal is to show how Eliashberg’s
h
-principle gives a complete classification of such forms up to deformation.

Let
Y
be a
3
-manifold.
A
1
-form
\lambda
on
Y
is called a
near-contact form
if:

(1)

\lambda
is transverse to the zero section of
T^{*}_{Y}
, so its zero set

\lambda^{-1}(0)=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
is a discrete (for simplicity, finite) set of points;

(2)

there exists a volume form
\operatorname{vol}
on
Y
such that, writing

\lambda\wedge d\lambda=f\,\operatorname{vol}.

the function
f
is
positive on
Y-\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
, and each
p_{i}
is a nondegenerate local minimum of
f
. (This condition then holds for any volume form inducing the same orientation as
\lambda\wedge d\lambda
.)

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that
p
is a transverse zero of
\lambda
and that
\lambda
is contact in a small punctured neighbourhood of
p
; then
d\lambda
also vanishes at
p
.

Proof.

Choose a connection
\nabla
near
p
. Since
\lambda
is contact in a punctured neighbourhood of
p
, we have:

\nabla(\lambda\wedge d\lambda)\big|_{p}=0.

Using

\nabla(\lambda\wedge d\lambda)=\nabla\lambda\wedge d\lambda+\lambda\wedge\nabla d\lambda,

we obtain:

\nabla\lambda|_{p}\wedge d\lambda|_{p}=0.

The transverse vanishing of
\lambda
at
p
now implies

d\lambda|_{p}=0
. This completes the proof. ∎

If
p
is a transverse zero of a
1
-form
\lambda
and

d\lambda
also vanishes at
p
, then the bilinear form:

(5.1)

A(\xi,\eta)=\nabla_{\xi}\lambda(\eta)\quad\text{for }\xi,\eta\in T_{Y}|_{p},

is symmetric and nondegenerate. Since
\lambda|_{p}=0
, the form
A
does not depend on
\nabla
.

Lemma 7
.

If a near-contact
1
-form
\lambda
vanishes at
p
, then
the form
A
defined by (
5.1
) is not definite.

Proof.

Choose a flat metric
g
near
p
, let
\nabla
be its flat connection,
and let
\operatorname{vol}_{g}
be its volume form.
Let
Y
be the vector field dual to
d\lambda
with respect to
\operatorname{vol}_{g}
; that is,

\iota_{Y}\operatorname{vol}_{g}=d\lambda.

Then:

L_{Y}\operatorname{vol}_{g}=(\operatorname{div}Y)\,\operatorname{vol}_{g}=0.

Thus, the endomorphism field
\nabla Y\colon T_{Y}\to T_{Y}
is traceless everywhere. In particular, the linear map:

B(\xi)=\nabla_{\xi}Y\in T_{Y}|_{p}\quad\text{for all }\xi\in T_{Y}|_{p},

satisfies
\operatorname{tr}B=0
. We compute:

\lambda\wedge d\lambda=\lambda(Y)\,\operatorname{vol}_{g}.

Since
\lambda
and
Y
both vanish at
p
, it
follows that:

\nabla_{\xi}\nabla_{\eta}\lambda(Y)\big|_{p}=A(\xi,B\eta)+A(\eta,B\xi)\quad\text{for all }\xi,\eta\in T_{Y}|_{p}.

Assume for contradiction that
A
is definite.
Choose
g
so that
g=A
at
p
. Set:

H(\xi,\xi)=\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\lambda(Y)\big|_{p}=2g(\xi,B\xi).

For any
g
-orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
, we obtain:

\operatorname{tr}_{g}H=\sum_{i=1}^{3}H(e_{i},e_{i})=2\,\operatorname{tr}B=0.

Hence, the function
f=\lambda(Y)
cannot have a nondegenerate maximum or minimum at
p
. This contradiction completes the proof. ∎

Define the index of a transverse zero
p
of
\lambda
as:

6. Near-symplectic forms

In this section we review the
notion of a near-symplectic form; see

[
2
,
20
,
12
,
10
,
5
]
for background. Our goal is to prove Proposition
3
.

Let

be a
4
-manifold.
A
2
-form
\omega
on
X
is called

near-symplectic form
if:

(1)

d\omega=0
;

(2)

\omega
is symplectic away
from its zero set, i.e., for each

with
\omega_{x}\neq 0
,

\omega_{x}\wedge\omega_{x}\neq 0
;

(3)

the zero set

is a smooth
1
-dimensional submanifold (a union of embedded circles and arcs), and for each

and
\xi\in T_{X}|_{x}
,

(\nabla_{\xi}\omega)_{x}\wedge(\nabla_{\xi}\omega)_{x}\neq 0.

If
X
has boundary,

is additionally required to be transverse to
\partial X
.

This definition is equivalent to the following.
Let
\omega
be a closed
2
-form with zero set

. Then
\omega
is near-symplectic iff
Z
is a smooth
1
-manifold and there exists a volume form
\operatorname{vol}
on

such that:

\omega\wedge\omega=f\,\mathrm{vol},

where

on

and

is a Morse-Bott critical submanifold of

; that is,

along

and the Hessian of

is nondegenerate on the normal bundle of

.

Endow

with a Riemannian metric and let
\omega
be a closed SD form. Viewing
\omega
as a section of
\Lambda^{2}_{+}T^{*}_{X}
, assume it is transverse to the zero section; that is, for each

,

\nabla\omega_{x}
has rank

. Then

is a smooth
1
-manifold, and
\omega
is necessarily near-symplectic: for each

and
\xi\in T_{X}|_{x}

is nonzero; hence, its wedge
square is also nonzero.

\omega

Proof.

To begin with, assume that all

By Lemma

(d_{Y}\lambda_{t})_{p_{i}}=0
