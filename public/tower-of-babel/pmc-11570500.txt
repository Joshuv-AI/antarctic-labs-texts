Nurse leaders’ recommendations for implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in health systems: A qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Hyunmin Yu, April J Ancheta, Dalmacio Dennis Flores, Stephen Bonett, Steven Meanley, Seul Ki Choi, José A Bauermeister
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiscrimination against lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer or questioning, and other sexual and gender diverse (LGBTQ+) individuals in healthcare settings remains a priority issue. Nurse leaders, who oversee the largest healthcare workforce, are critical in implementing inclusive practices to address LGBTQ+ discrimination, yet implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices and effective strategies is inconsistent. This qualitative descriptive study explored nurse leaders’ perceptions of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices across the United States to inform recommended strategies within health systems.MethodsTwenty-one nurse leaders (e.g., chief nurse executives) from diverse healthcare settings (e.g., children's hospitals and public health centers) in 12 states across the United States participated in semi-structured interviews from October to December 2023. Interview transcripts were analyzed using content analysis to (1) describe nurse leaders’ definitions of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices and (2) identify effective strategies for their implementation.ResultsNurse leaders defined LGBTQ+ inclusive practices to include delivering culturally responsive and competent care, fostering safe and affirming environments, integrating inclusivity into daily interactions and organizational culture, and supporting the needs of both LGBTQ+ staff and patients. Key implementation strategies included fostering a flexible organizational culture through open communication; a diverse workforce; and inclusive mission statements, policies, and strategic plans. Supportive infrastructure involved effective LGBTQ+ training and adapting electronic health record systems to include information on sexual orientation and gender identity. Active and interdisciplinary engagement from staff and leadership, as well as systematic data collection on patient and staff experiences, were essential for driving inclusivity. The advocacy highlighted included legislative support, discourse within the nursing profession, and the incorporation of LGBTQ+ inclusion into healthcare regulations and accreditation standards. Additionally, multisectoral partnerships with LGBTQ+ health experts and external organizations, along with multilevel evaluations, were recommended to develop tailored interventions and promote LGBTQ+ inclusive practices.ConclusionImplementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare is a multifaceted effort influenced by strategies at various levels. Promoting a supportive organizational culture, establishing comprehensive support structures, encouraging active engagement, and fostering community partnerships can address the needs of LGBTQ+ individuals more effectively. Future implementation efforts should tailor strategies to accommodate variability across healthcare settings and regions, ensuring that practices are both adaptable and sustainable.Keywords: Sexual and gender minorities, LGBTQ+ persons, Discrimination, Inclusive practices, Health equity, Nurse leaders

What is already known about the topic

• Discrimination against LGBTQ+ individuals in healthcare settings remains a significant issue, despite growing awareness of its impact.

• Researchers have highlighted the need for LGBTQ+ inclusive practices to address this discrimination effectively and have examined frontline nurses’ perspectives. However, little is known about how nurse leaders define and implement these practices in diverse healthcare settings.

Introduction

Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer or questioning, and other sexual and gender diverse (LGBTQ+) persons in the United States (U.S.) are a growing population (Gallup, 2024) who have historically experienced health disparities (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2019; James et al., 2024; National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine, 2020). They experience higher rates of mental health issues, substance misuse, sexually transmitted infections, cancer-related outcomes, victimization, and cardiovascular diseases compared to non-LGBTQ+ populations (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2022; National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine, 2020; Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, 2020). LGBTQ+ individuals often encounter structural and social barriers to accessing healthcare, including discrimination, stigmatization, and a lack of culturally responsive care, leading to healthcare avoidance (Hafeez et al., 2017; James et al., 2024; Karakaya & Kutlu, 2021). A 2023 nationwide survey of LGBTQ+ cancer patients and survivors found that 37% of participants had faced discrimination in healthcare settings, 50% had concerns about anticipated discrimination, and 26% had avoided seeking care (American Cancer Society Cancer Action Network, 2023). Recent qualitative studies confirm that LGBTQ+ individuals continue to face discrimination, bias, and inadequate care within healthcare settings (Apodaca et al., 2022; Bristowe et al., 2018; LaVaccare et al., 2018; Malik et al., 2019).

Following guidance from national institutions (Joint Commission, 2011; Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, 2020), clinical stakeholders in the U.S. have increasingly acknowledged the critical need to adopt organizational-level LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. Incorporating LGBTQ+ inclusive practices into healthcare involves several key strategies. First, it is essential to ensure that health records systematically document and affirm a wide range of sexual orientations and gender identities, reinforcing their recognition and validity. Second, it is important to provide individualized care that acknowledges the unique health needs and experiences of LGBTQ+ individuals, fostering environments that are both supportive and affirming. Additionally, healthcare services should be designed to be both clinically effective and culturally responsive, providing regular screenings, preventive care, and targeted treatments that address the specific health concerns faced by LGBTQ+ individuals (Coleman et al., 2022; Joint Commission, 2011; National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine, 2020; National LGBTQIA+ Health Education Center, 2016). Without these inclusive practices, clinical environments become non-affirming, heightening the vulnerability of LGBTQ+ individuals to stigmatization and discrimination (National LGBTQIA+ Health Education Center, 2015, 2017).

Nurses play a vital role in creating an inclusive healthcare environment and addressing discrimination against LGBTQ+ individuals (Hughes et al., 2022; Medina-Martínez et al., 2021). As the largest segment of the healthcare workforce, they interact directly with patients, fostering supportive spaces through compassionate care and active listening. Nurses are key in implementing inclusive practices, such as accurately reflecting diverse sexual orientation and gender identities in health records and advocating for individualized care plans tailored to LGBTQ+ patients’ needs. Through continuous education and training, nurses stay updated on best practices, promoting a culture of respect and equity and reducing the risk of stigmatization, thus ensuring high-quality, affirming care for all patients (Yu et al., 2023a). While implementing inclusive practices holds promise for enhancing healthcare experiences and outcomes for LGBTQ+ individuals (National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine, 2020; National LGBTQIA+ Health Education Center, 2022), ongoing reports of discrimination against these individuals in healthcare (James et al., 2024; Kaiser Family Foundation, 2024) indicate that understanding and application of these practices may vary across healthcare settings.

Recent studies have predominantly focused on the perceptions of frontline healthcare providers in outpatient settings regarding LGBTQ+ inclusive practices (Mullins et al., 2023; Pearce & Di Lorito, 2023; Seretlo & Mokgatle, 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Willging et al., 2022). However, there is a significant gap in understanding the perspectives of healthcare leadership across various systems, despite their crucial role in shaping organizational policies (DiMaggio & Garip, 2012; Friedkin, 2001). While some qualitative studies have explored leadership perspectives, they have been limited to federally qualified health centers or community mental health centers (Fish et al., 2022; Gagnon et al., 2022; Loo et al., 2023). Moreover, there is a scarcity of studies exploring the perceptions of nurse leaders specifically, who oversee the largest segment of the U.S. healthcare workforce. Examining nurse leaders’ perspectives on LGBTQ+ inclusivity could offer valuable insights for advancing healthcare. Transformational leadership theory, which underscores the ability of leaders to inspire and drive change, suggests that nurse leaders are well-positioned to influence organizational culture and policies to better support LGBTQ+ practices (Bass, 1985; Bass & Riggio, 2006). By understanding their insights, we can address systemic challenges and leverage opportunities to enhance LGBTQ+ inclusivity in healthcare. This can lead to the development of evidence-based strategies, such as tailored training programs and integration of LGBTQ+ competencies into nursing education, ensuring that the healthcare system is more responsive to the unique needs of LGBTQ+ individuals (Hughes et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2023a).

To ensure the successful implementation of new evidence-based practices, effective implementation strategies are crucial. These strategies involve systematic approaches designed to facilitate the adoption, integration, and sustainability of interventions within specific settings by overcoming barriers and leveraging facilitators (Nilsen, 2020). In this context, the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change framework is a valuable resource (Powell et al., 2015). Developed through expert consensus in implementation science and clinical practice, this framework provides a comprehensive taxonomy of strategies tailored to enhance the adoption and integration of new practices in health and mental health settings (Powell et al., 2015). Rather than serving as a checklist, the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change framework is a resource for selecting and combining strategies to tailor implementation plans and standardize the discussion of diverse implementation strategies (Powell et al., 2015). This framework offers a structured method for identifying and categorizing evidence-based strategies that can be utilized to implement LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare settings. This systematic approach ensures that the chosen strategies are both relevant and effective in real-world healthcare environments.

The objectives of this qualitative study were to (1) explore how nurse leaders describe LGBTQ+ inclusive practices across various healthcare systems in the U.S., and (2) identify implementation strategies applicable to healthcare settings and categorize them using the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change framework. The findings aim to offer guidance to healthcare organizations planning or implementing LGBTQ+ inclusion initiatives, thereby enhancing their likelihood of successful implementation.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative descriptive design was employed to explore how nurse leaders define LGBTQ+ inclusive practices and to identify effective strategies for implementing these practices. We conducted individual semi-structured interviews with nurse leaders across various healthcare systems in the U.S.

Sampling and recruitment

Purposive and snowball sampling were used to identify potential participants for the study (Patton, 1990). We aimed to recruit nurse leaders from a range of leadership roles across different healthcare settings. Inclusion criteria for the study required participants to meet the following criteria: (1) being a nurse, (2) holding a leadership role equivalent to or higher than a nurse director (e.g., chief nurse executive, chief nursing officer, or nurse director) involved in policy design and oversight of multiple units or departments, (3) working in any type of healthcare facility, (4) located in any region within the U.S., and (5) proficient in English.

In June 2023, the first author (HY) presented an overview of the study at a workshop with nurse leaders from various healthcare organizations and regions. During the workshop, he collected email addresses from attendees. Following Institutional Review Board approval from the University of Pennsylvania (Protocol # 854,419), invitations were sent to these nurse leaders. Interested individuals were evaluated through a screening process to determine if they met the inclusion criteria. Those who met the criteria were scheduled for individual semi-structured interviews conducted via Zoom. Additionally, a snowball sampling method was employed, allowing participants to recommend other eligible nurse leaders. The final number of participants was determined once data saturation was achieved, indicating that additional interviews did not yield new information (Bowen, 2008; Hennink et al., 2017). Invitations were sent to a total of 136 nurse leaders, of whom 30 responded (22.1%), and 21 (15.4%) agreed to participate in the study.

Data collection

We conducted in-depth semi-structured interviews to gather nurse leaders’ perspectives on LGBTQ+ inclusive practices across various healthcare systems. The first author, a male PhD student at the time of the study with training in qualitative research and a research focus on LGBTQ+ health equity, recruited and interviewed participants via Zoom with audio recording from October to December 2023. Before each interview, participants were informed about the study's goals, methods, and potential impact, and written consent was obtained. Participants was asked to select a pseudonym to maintain anonymity (Novak, 2014). Interviews lasted between 31 and 64 min, with an average duration of 53 min. Data saturation (Bowen, 2008; Hennink et al., 2017) was reached after 18 interviews, and three additional interviews were conducted to confirm data saturation.

The interviews used two main instruments: a pilot-tested semi-structured interview guide (Supplementary Material) and a demographic questionnaire. The interview guide was developed to explore nurse leaders’ perceptions on the definition of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices, as well as the facilitators, barriers, and effective strategies for implementation. Interview items specific for this study included: (1) how nurse leaders define LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in their healthcare settings and (2) their recommendations for effective strategies to implement these practices. The demographic questionnaire was used to collect data on participants’ age, sexual orientation, gender identity, race, ethnicity, education, leadership role, religious affiliation, political views, length of service, and current position. Participants received a $50 gift card as compensation. Field notes were recorded after each interview to capture key points and non-verbal cues (Phillippi & Lauderdale, 2018), and professional transcriptionists transcribed the interviews verbatim to ensure accuracy for analysis.

Data analysis

The transcribed interviews were analyzed using a content analysis method (Graneheim & Lundman, 2004; Hsieh & Shannon, 2005), which is suitable for this study design as it seeks to describe perspectives with limited existing theory (Sandelowski, 2000). NVivo software version 14 was utilized to facilitate the systematic encoding of the data. Initially, the first coder (HY) developed a preliminary codebook after multiple readings of the interview transcripts. This codebook was designed to address the study's objectives, specifically to (1) capture nurse leaders’ definitions of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices and (2) identify effective strategies for their implementation. The codebook included detailed codes, definitions, and examples to ensure clarity and consistency. Subsequently, the second coder (AA) refined the codebook to enhance its accuracy and consistency. Both coders then jointly coded two transcripts to establish alignment in their coding approach. Following this, they independently applied the refined codebook to the remaining transcripts. Intercoder agreement was achieved through consensus, with any discrepancies resolved via joint discussions during weekly meetings (Campbell et al., 2013; O'Connor & Joffe, 2020). After the initial coding, both coders independently reviewed the coded excerpts. They convened to discuss and establish a shared definition of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices among the nurse leader participants. They also clarified and selected key themes and exemplary quotes related to the effective implementation strategies recommended by the participants.

To further analyze and contextualize the findings, the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change framework (Powell et al., 2015) was employed. Although this framework was not used to conceptualize the study, it was instrumental in linking the study's findings to the broader literature on implementation strategies. This connection not only enhanced the relevance of our research within the field but also provided a structured lens through which to interpret our results. After identifying all the effective implementation strategies recommended by the participants, we systematically matched and categorized these strategies into one or more of the 73 strategies outlined in the framework. This process involved a thorough examination of each recommended strategy, considering its specific elements and intended outcomes, and aligning it with the corresponding category within the framework. By categorizing the identified strategies according to the framework, we aimed to evaluate their alignment with strategies recognized as effective in real-world clinical settings. Additionally, contextualizing our findings within this established framework allowed us to identify which strategies in the framework were frequently cited by participants, thereby enabling an assessment of their potential effectiveness in promoting LGBTQ+ inclusivity in healthcare settings. The framework provided a structured approach to understanding and evaluating implementation strategies, making it an invaluable tool for examining how our identified strategies fit within established best practices in implementation science.

Reflexivity and positionality

Two of the coauthors led the data collection and analysis: HY, a male nurse practitioner working in a hospital setting and PhD student with a research focus on LGBTQ+ health equity, and AA, a female nurse and postdoctoral research fellow focused on LGBTQ+ youth well-being. HY's academic and clinical experiences shaped his understanding of the challenges LGBTQ+ individuals face in healthcare, fostering an environment where nurse leaders felt comfortable discussing inclusivity. His awareness of LGBTQ+ health disparities allowed him to ask relevant follow-up questions, deepening the quality of the data collected. AA's expertise in LGBTQ+ youth well-being provided a complementary perspective during analysis, contributing to the exploration of effective strategies. Their collaboration promoted rigorous discussions within the research team and refined the interpretation of the findings.

To maintain reflexivity, they engaged in ongoing dialogues about their biases and assumptions, aiming to minimize their influence on the research and ensure that the voices of nurse leaders remained central. This reflective practice contributed to a robust analysis of strategies for implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare settings.

While participants varied in their level of engagement with LGBTQ+ inclusion initiatives, they all shared their experiences related to diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives addressing LGBTQ+ issues within their institutions. These initiatives included gender pronoun awareness and education, integration of gender pronouns and information on sexual orientation and gender identity into electronic health record systems, provision of LGBTQ+ specific training for staff and leaders, organization of LGBTQ+ awareness events within the hospital and local community, establishment of LGBTQ+ mentoring programs for staff, formation of LGBTQ+ employee resource groups, updating language in policies and forms to enhance LGBTQ+ inclusivity, and collaboration with LGBTQ+ community organizations for conducting organizational assessments on LGBTQ+ inclusivity and developing training programs.

Consensus definition of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices

The consensus definition of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices, as outlined by nurse leader participants, included several key elements. These practices involved providing culturally responsive and competent care that meets the unique needs of LGBTQ+ individuals, extending beyond simple acknowledgment to actively address the diverse experiences and challenges these individuals face. A crucial component was fostering safe and affirming environments where LGBTQ+ individuals feel accepted, respected, and supported without the fear of discrimination. Participants considered inclusivity an ongoing journey, not a one-time intervention, integrating seamlessly into daily healthcare interactions and organizational culture to ensure that diversity is celebrated and every individual feels valued. Moreover, LGBTQ+ inclusivity was considered a vital component of broader diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives, complementing efforts aimed at achieving racial and ethnic equity. Additionally, participants highlighted healthcare institutions should consider the needs and experiences of LGBTQ+ staff alongside those of patients, recognizing that a supportive environment for LGBTQ+ employees enhances their well-being and improves the quality of care provided to LGBTQ+ patients.

Theme I – flexible organizational culture

This overarching theme underscores the necessity of creating an adaptable and responsive organizational environment and culture that promotes inclusivity and addresses the unique needs of LGBTQ+ individuals within healthcare settings.

Inclusive mission, policies and strategic plans

Participants recommended having LGBTQ+ inclusive mission statements, policies, and strategic plans. Clear and explicit statements and policies demonstrated an organization's commitment to LGBTQ+ inclusivity, setting a consistent tone across the institution. These documents guided staff at all levels, ensuring a unified approach to LGBTQ+ individuals and laying the foundation for an inclusive environment that fosters trust and improves healthcare experiences. This approach was consistent with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of developing a formal implementation blueprint that outlines goals, scope, timeframe, milestones, and performance measures.

“LGBTQ+ inclusive practices involve examining our policies and procedures specifically through the lens of this population. It's about dedicating time and attention to making sure that our policies are not excluding them from our services.” (Meg, nurse director)

Open communication

Participants highlighted open and transparent communication between leaders and staff as a crucial strategy. Such communication created an internal environment where staff felt empowered to voice concerns, including discomfort, biases, and beliefs related to LGBTQ+ issues. By encouraging staff to share their experiences, participants expressed leaders could better understand the challenges LGBTQ+ individuals face within the organization. This dialogue enabled leaders to address concerns, customize support systems, and implement changes to enhance LGBTQ+ inclusivity. This aligned with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of promoting network weaving by leveraging high-quality relationships within the organization to foster information sharing and collaborative problem-solving.

“It's essential for staff to feel empowered to speak up if they're uncomfortable, acknowledging their own biases and beliefs, and creating a culture of trust where transparent communication is valued. Leaders and staff members should share their experiences, including mistakes and their consequences, to foster understanding and growth and promote LGBTQ+ inclusion.” (Denise, chief operating officer)

Prioritize human needs

Participants emphasized that prioritizing human needs and safeguarding human rights were fundamental justifications for implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare and that these principles should be shared throughout the organization. Placing human needs at the forefront of implementation shifted the emphasis toward ensuring that healthcare services were tailored to meet the specific and often unique requirements of LGBTQ+ individuals. This approach also aligned with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of promoting network weaving, building strong internal relationships to foster a unified vision for implementing innovations.

“When you think about healthcare delivery, humanity is at the core of it. You have to set aside your biases. Fundamentally, your purpose is about serving humanity, regardless of the individual's group, religion, color, gender, or sexual orientation. It's all about serving humanity and meeting human needs, so you have to see past any of the things that make us different to see the core of things that make us all the same.” (Cat, chief executive officer)

Workforce diversity

Participants highlighted that workforce diversity is essential for LGBTQ+ inclusivity, as it introduces varied perspectives and insights into decision-making. They stressed the importance of a staff that reflects the broader community, including LGBTQ+ individuals, to better understand and address their needs. Diverse leadership also played a crucial role, fostering an inclusive culture and building trust among LGBTQ+ communities. This was consistent with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of creating new clinical teams by incorporating diverse team members to enhance the delivery and success of clinical innovations.

“Having a diverse workforce ensures that questions addressing various perspectives can come up. For example, when someone is developing a form, policy, or workflow, the diverse workforce serves as a gut-check, creating checks and balances to evaluate how these initiatives impact individuals from different backgrounds. This includes people of color, individuals from varying socioeconomic statuses, those with disabilities, and different sexual orientations.” (Tom, chief nursing officer)

Theme II – infrastructure support

This overarching theme highlights several key infrastructure components necessary for implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices within healthcare settings.

Dedicated team and leader

Participants stressed the need for a dedicated team to manage LGBTQ+ inclusive diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives effectively. Such a team ensured that practices were systematically planned, implemented, and evaluated, facilitating resource allocation, goal setting, and cross-departmental coordination. By providing expertise and support, this team fostered a collaborative and cohesive approach to inclusivity. This corresponded with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of identifying and preparing champions who drive implementation and overcome resistance.

“LGBTQ+ inclusion requires a team. Our interprofessional team includes physicians, social workers, and other professionals. Their entire work is really focused on how to advance LGBTQ+ initiatives within the organization.” (Cat, chief executive officer)

Effective LGBTQ+ training and awareness events for staff and leaders

Participants universally emphasized LGBTQ+ specific training for healthcare leaders and staff as essential. They noted that such training enhanced professionals’ knowledge, skills, and sensitivity to provide culturally responsive care. Additionally, they stressed the importance of training leaders to foster inclusivity and align organizational policies accordingly. Participants also underscored the value of LGBTQ+ awareness and celebration events in healthcare systems, which promote education, advocacy, and community building. These events facilitated dialogue and collaboration to address the unique needs of LGBTQ+ populations. This aligned with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of conducting ongoing training and making training dynamic, adapting delivery methods to various learning styles and work contexts.

“Consistency and ongoing, regularly scheduled LGBTQ+ education or programs are important. You can do a bolus of education, but then it goes away. For me, it's about how to keep it at the forefront of a person's mind. There are many initiatives we implement that get sidelined or compartmentalized until needed. The key for our DEI [diversity, equity, and inclusion] team and our executive team is keeping this conversation healthy, growing, and a priority.” (Dave, chief nursing officer)

Inclusive electronic health record systems with clear explanations of their purpose

Participants recommended adapting electronic health record systems to better serve LGBTQ+ individuals. They highlighted that incorporating fields for sexual orientation, gender identity, and preferred pronouns was essential for comprehensive care, enabling healthcare providers to address patients’ unique needs and ensuring effective communication among healthcare teams. However, inconsistent processes and unclear explanations about the purpose of collecting information on sexual orientation and gender identity can lead to uncertainty and discourage accurate reporting. Participants stressed the need for staff training on respectful and effective sexual orientation and gender identity data collection and communication. This approach aligned with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of changing records systems to enhance implementation and clinical outcomes.

“It's important to collaborate with the IT [information technology] team to ensure that our medical records support the needs of LGBTQ+ patients for assessments and clinical documentation. This includes structuring the review of progress notes to be inclusive and ensuring that pertinent topics are included to facilitate better communication.” (Elizabeth, nurse director)

Theme III – active staff and leadership engagement

This overarching theme emphasizes the critical role of active engagement from both staff and leadership in implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices within healthcare settings.

Active leadership engagement

Participants emphasized senior leadership support and active engagement as vital strategies. They highlighted that leaders should not only endorse but also actively participate in inclusive initiatives, demonstrating commitment through concrete actions such as advocating for policy changes, allocating resources, and participating in LGBTQ+ or diversity, equity, and inclusion training. This proactive leadership was essential for cultivating an organizational culture that values diversity and inclusion, effectively communicating its significance to staff while fostering a sense of accountability. This was consistent with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of involving executive boards in the implementation process.

“LGBTQ+ inclusion has to be continuous and led from the top, because it can't be just a one-time learning and put it out there. It has to be led and supported by the most senior leaders. From a cultural standpoint, at the top, we've decided it's a priority. It has to be continuous and evolving, integrated into the culture. It's an ongoing dialogue, and people like me and my president have to be brave enough to talk about it. When people in our institution see us discussing it, they know it's okay to talk about it.” (Sue, chief nurse executive)

Collect and share data to drive action

Participants recommended employing data and personal stories to drive LGBTQ+ inclusivity. Systematic data collection on LGBTQ+ patient demographics, outcomes, and experience helped identify gaps and measure initiative impact. Sharing personal narratives humanized the need for inclusivity, fostering empathy and advocacy. Participants stressed the importance of leadership in promoting transparent data sharing and prioritizing patient-centered stories to support organizational change and advance LGBTQ+ equity. This approach aligned with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of facilitating the relay of clinical data to providers in real-time to enhance the use of targeted innovations.

“In healthcare, one thing that consistently resonates with people is connecting on an emotional level. Hearing personal stories from LGBTQ+ people about how someone made a difference in their lives can be incredibly impactful because it's a choice employees made or are actively participating in. Beyond just providing standardized education from an organization, making it more personalized could be key.” (Nicole, chief nurse executive)

Interdisciplinary engagement

Participants underscored the vital role of interdisciplinary collaboration in advancing LGBTQ+ inclusivity in healthcare. They highlighted the importance of uniting professionals from diverse specialties and backgrounds to collectively pursue inclusivity goals. This approach leveraged varied expertise, perspectives, and resources, fostering shared ownership and accountability among team members. This corresponded with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of creating a learning collaborative by forming groups of providers to improve the implementation of innovations.

“We try to raise awareness of LGBTQ+ issues among all staff, not just the clinical team. Whether it's housekeeping, the finance department, or clinical staff, it's something everyone in any discipline should be aware of for successful implementation.” (Chris, nurse director)

Theme IV – advocacy at multiple levels

This overarching theme stresses the need for comprehensive advocacy efforts at multiple levels to support the implementation of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices within healthcare settings.

Advocate for legislative support of LGBTQ+ laws

Participants recommended advocating for legislative support of LGBTQ+ laws. They emphasized that such advocacy could drive systemic change and legal protections for LGBTQ+ individuals, influencing policies at local, state, and national levels. Supporting laws that combat discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity in healthcare was essential for creating a supportive environment. Additionally, these efforts can bolster societal acceptance of LGBTQ+ rights and reinforce organizational commitments to diversity, equity, and inclusion. This approach aligned with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of changing liability laws to encourage clinicians to embrace innovative practices.

“We have two experts that were tapped as medical mental health experts that participated in a bipartisan training and hearing sessions that the legislator on bills related to gender-affirming care in transgender youth. We've been a pretty big proponent of this because, ‘if not us, who?’ This new age now, this large group of folks will no longer be able to access care in a way that they could a year ago. We can't just close the door and not provide the services, the services part of health and human services. We must learn and educate ourselves, so that we can learn how to navigate and exist within whatever the current policy, laws, and rules are. We cannot sit back and be unresponsive. We have to be leading the way, especially when laws, rules, and policies change that impact, the way that we're going to be able to conduct our work.” (Sue, chief nurse executive)

Advocacy from the nursing profession

Participants highlighted the critical role of LGBTQ+ advocacy within the nursing profession, identifying the absence of dialogue as a major barrier to advancing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare. They stressed that ongoing conversations and advocacy efforts in nursing are essential for driving institutional change, raising awareness, and ensuring equitable care for LGBTQ+ individuals. This approach was consistent with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of facilitation, which involves interactive problem-solving and support within a context of recognized need and supportive relationships.

“As a healthcare industry and as a nursing profession, we're probably still at the beginning of this LGBTQ+ inclusion journey. We need to start having more conversations and understanding the beliefs and challenges of our nurses regarding providing care to LGBTQ+ patients.” (Meggy, chief nurse executive)

Advocate for LGBTQ+ inclusion in healthcare regulation & accreditation standards

Participants underscored the crucial role of healthcare regulatory and accreditation bodies in advancing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. They noted that organizations faced increasing pressure from entities like the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, the Joint Commission, and the Magnet Recognition Program, which have recently incorporated diversity, equity, and inclusion criteria into their evaluation processes. This external pressure drove organizational change and increased accountability. However, participants pointed out that current standards lack explicit emphasis on LGBTQ+ inclusivity, a critical area that requires further improvement and advocacy. This aligned with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of changing accreditation or membership requirements to encourage or mandate the use of clinical innovations.

“Healthcare regulations recently included language regarding how hospitals manage diversity, equity, and inclusion for both colleagues and patients, which has been helpful. However, they often fail to specify how these goals should be achieved. Most hospitals tend to focus on aspects such as gender, race, ethnicity, religion, age, and disability status. Unfortunately, LGBTQ+ inclusive practices may not be as forefront as these other aspects.” (Tom, chief nursing officer)

Theme V – multisectoral partnership

This overarching theme highlights the importance of collaborative efforts and partnerships across multiple sectors to effectively implement LGBTQ+ inclusive practices within healthcare settings.

LGBTQ+ health expert guidance

Participants recommended consulting LGBTQ+ health experts. These experts brought specialized knowledge and insights into LGBTQ+ health issues, which were essential for guiding effective implementation. Their expertise helped healthcare organizations develop tailored interventions, policies, and practices that address the unique needs of LGBTQ+ patients. By incorporating their guidance, organizations ensured that their efforts were informed, culturally responsive, and aligned with best practices. This approach corresponded with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of using implementation advisors for expert guidance.

“LGBTQ+ inclusion is something you have to be really intentional about, and you need to have subject matter experts to help you do it well.” (Bianca, chief nursing officer)

Collaborations with external organizations

Participants also recommended collaborating with external organizations. Partnerships with academic institutions for training development or LGBTQ+ community groups for needs assessments provided healthcare organizations with valuable expertise, resources, and community insights. These collaborations enabled the creation of tailored interventions and initiatives that address the needs of LGBTQ+ individuals, thereby advancing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. This aligned with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of building a coalition by recruiting and nurturing relationships with key partners in the implementation effort.

“We partnered with a local LGBTQ+ organization. They helped us develop policies and processes and worked through some issues from the LGBTQ+ patient's perspective. One of their members came to talk to the leadership team about their experiences, providing insight into the challenges they faced and helping us build empathy and understanding.” (Sky, nurse director)

Multilevel evaluation

Participants highlighted the necessity of collecting patient-level outcomes to evaluate LGBTQ+ inclusive practices, focusing on LGBTQ+ individuals’ experiences, satisfaction, quality of care, access, and health outcomes. This data enabled organizations to assess effectiveness, identify areas for improvement, and monitor disparities compared to non-LGBTQ+ patients. Additionally, they emphasized the importance of staff-level outcomes to gauge staff experiences, attitudes, competencies, and organizational culture regarding LGBTQ+ inclusivity, which enhanced job satisfaction and staff engagement. Institution-level outcomes, such as achieving recognition through the Healthcare Equality Index, were also noted as crucial for demonstrating commitment, improving reputation, and fostering continuous enhancement of LGBTQ+ inclusive healthcare practices. This was consistent with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change strategy of obtaining and using patients/consumers and family feedback.

“Get HEI [Healthcare Equality Index], then become a top performer and be able to advertise and say, ‘here's what's in place and we're doing it. Then take that feedback that comes in and say, ‘okay, we need to make it different. We need to do better.’ You need a map that shows where you are and has lots of stops along the way and where you want to be. Where are you? Where do you want to go? Who are the people that you need to bring in to get where you're going?” (Paul, nurse director)

Discussion

This qualitative study provides a shared definition of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices as articulated by nurse leaders. Findings show that nurse leaders’ definitions align with national recommendations, including recognizing diverse sexual orientation and gender identities, delivering culturally responsive care, creating safe and affirming environments, and seamlessly integrating LGBTQ+ considerations into healthcare practices (Coleman et al., 2022; Joint Commission, 2011; National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine, 2020; National LGBTQIA+ Health Education Center, 2016). This study also identified effective strategies for implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices at multiple levels, which were further categorized within the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change framework (Powell et al., 2015). Employing the implementation science typology from the framework offers a broad, evidence-based approach to advancing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. By aligning strategies with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change framework, the study ensures that identified strategies are not only comprehensive and contextually relevant but also sustainable in the long term in healthcare settings.

Key implementations strategies include fostering a flexible organizational culture through open communication, a diverse workforce, and inclusive mission statements, policies and strategic plans. Supportive infrastructure involves effective LGBTQ+ training and adapting electronic health record systems to include information on sexual orientation and gender identity. Active and interdisciplinary engagement from staff and leadership, as well as systematic data collection on patient and staff experiences, are essential for driving inclusivity. Advocacy should focus on legislative support, discourse within the nursing profession, and incorporating LGBTQ+ inclusion into healthcare regulations and accreditation standards. Additionally, multisectoral partnerships with LGBTQ+ health experts and external organizations, along with multilevel evaluations, are recommended to develop tailored interventions and promote LGBTQ+ inclusive practices.

Countering anti-LGBTQ+ laws and policies is essential for advancing LGBTQ+ inclusion in healthcare. Nurse leaders, particularly those in states where anti-LGBTQ+ health-related legislation has recently been introduced or advanced, emphasized the detrimental impact of these legislative barriers on their inclusion efforts. They underscored the need for health system-level advocacy to support LGBTQ+ laws in collaboration with legal experts and LGBTQ+ community or advocacy groups. With the significant rise of anti-LGBTQ+ bills (American Civil Liberties Union, 2023), it is crucial for healthcare leaders and policymakers to advocate for the repeal or reform of such laws to ensure equitable care for LGBTQ+ populations. In states with legal constraints, healthcare organizations must navigate these barriers while advocating for policy changes by establishing clear non-discrimination policies, implementing effective training, and supporting staff well-being (Hughes et al., 2021; Mallory et al., 2023; McNamara et al., 2023). Collaborating with LGBTQ+ advocacy groups and legal experts can help address legal challenges and foster positive change. A multifaceted approach involving policymakers, healthcare leaders, and LGBTQ+ communities is necessary to promote inclusivity and combat discrimination.

Regulatory bodies and accreditation programs should strengthen their guidelines with specific measures for LGBTQ+ inclusivity. While organizations like the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, the Joint Commission, and the Magnet Recognition Program have started incorporating broad diversity, equity, and inclusion principles (Bryant et al., 2022; Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2023; Joint Commission, 2022), detailed guidelines on LGBTQ+ inclusivity are still lacking. To advance health equity for LGBTQ+ populations, these bodies should include comprehensive LGBTQ+ cultural competency training, explicit nondiscrimination policies, and LGBTQ+ affirming healthcare services. Additionally, healthcare organizations should be encouraged to collect and report data on LGBTQ+ health outcomes and disparities, providing insights for targeted interventions. Prioritizing funding for LGBTQ+ inclusive diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives can further enhance successful implementation. By incorporating these measures, regulatory bodies can drive systemic change and foster inclusivity for all individuals.

To ensure the sustainability and advancement of LGBTQ+ inclusive infrastructure within healthcare organizations, fostering a flexible and inclusive organizational culture is paramount. Nurse leaders play a crucial role in this process by actively promoting and modeling the values of inclusivity and respect. They should encourage and facilitate open dialogue about LGBTQ+ issues, helping to create a supportive environment where uncomfortable conversations are welcomed rather than avoided. By leading these discussions, nurse leaders can drive cultural change and ensure that LGBTQ+ inclusivity becomes a core component of the organizational ethos. Transparent communication between leadership and staff is essential in this process, allowing for the exchange of ideas, feedback, and concerns related to LGBTQ+ inclusivity initiatives. Nurse leaders should use their position to champion these conversations, addressing concerns and advocating for necessary changes to improve inclusivity. Moreover, emphasizing shared accountability among all stakeholders by embedding LGBTQ+ inclusivity into the organization's core values and mission ensures that everyone within the organization contributes to promoting these principles. Nurse leaders, by integrating these values into their leadership practices and daily operations, can significantly influence the organizational culture, ensuring that LGBTQ+ inclusivity is not just a policy but a lived practice.

Consistent with prior studies (Loo et al., 2023; Table et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Willging et al., 2022; Wilson et al., 2023), active engagement from nurse leaders is essential for advancing LGBTQ+ inclusivity. This engagement involves not only endorsing but actively participating in LGBTQ+ training, allocating a dedicated team and resources, and leading initiatives. Nurse leaders should advocate for LGBTQ+ inclusivity within their organizations, ensuring the implementation of essential infrastructure such as electronic health record systems with features relevant to sexual orientation and gender identity (Mullins et al., 2023) and effective LGBTQ+ training for both staff and leaders (Yu et al., 2023b). They should also advocate for LGBTQ+ inclusivity in the community by engaging with LGBTQ+ individuals and organizations, establishing LGBTQ+ advisory boards or task forces within healthcare organizations (National LGBTQIA+ Health Education Center, 2022), and partnering with community organizations for outreach and education. These efforts, including health fairs and workshops, raise awareness, promote preventive care, and build trust, demonstrating a commitment to LGBTQ+ inclusivity and enhancing both care quality and health equity.

Diverse representation in leadership is also essential. This aligns with the American Hospital Association's health equity roadmap, which recommends diversifying leadership ranks to reflect the populations and communities served, enhancing cultural competency, and ensuring diverse perspectives in decision-making (American Hospital Association, 2024). Despite a national study showing limited diversity among health equity officers in U.S. community hospitals (Weissman et al., 2023), the extent of LGBTQ+ representation in healthcare leadership remains unclear. Therefore, additional research and efforts are needed to promote LGBTQ+ inclusivity across all levels of healthcare organizations. When full diversity within leadership is not immediately feasible, nurse leaders can bridge the gap by actively engaging with LGBTQ+ individuals and participating in effective LGBTQ+ inclusive diversity, equity, and inclusion training, gaining firsthand understanding of their experiences and needs to better advocate for inclusivity within the organization.

Nurse leaders, as transformational leaders (Bass, 1985; Bass & Riggio, 2006), are pivotal in advancing LGBTQ+ inclusivity within the nursing profession. National nursing associations such as the Commission on Collegiate Nursing Education, the American Association of Colleges of Nursing, and the American Nurses Association shape education and practice standards, but existing position statements on LGBTQ+ inclusion may need reassessment to better address evolving health needs. Nurse leaders can leverage their influence to advocate for these updates and engage with organizations to promote LGBTQ+ inclusivity in nursing. This advocacy is crucial given recent findings of inadequate LGBTQ+ health content in nursing education (Hughes et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2023a), highlighting the need for enhanced cultural competence. By championing LGBTQ+ inclusivity in education and practice standards, nurse leaders can foster a more equitable healthcare system for all.

The study's limitations should be considered when interpreting findings, conclusions, and recommendations. Although we purposefully recruited participants from across the nation to ensure diversity in geographic location, rural settings, healthcare settings, leadership roles, and representation of underserved minority populations, it is important to note that the sample might not fully represent all nurse leaders in the U.S. Additionally, given our use of purposive and snowball sampling methods, these approaches may have favored participation from nurse leaders and healthcare systems already inclined to support LGBTQ+ health and combat systemic discrimination. Nearly half of the institutions in our study voluntarily participated in the Healthcare Equality Index in 2023, achieving high scores that indicate increased LGBTQ+ inclusivity. This limitation impacts our understanding of how nurse leaders in regions or states with more entrenched or protected forms of discrimination address LGBTQ+ health issues. Similarly, our data saturation may not have captured the perspectives from leaders in states with contrasting political climates, which could provide valuable insights into effective strategies for reducing discrimination and delivering inclusive care. To fully capture the experiences of nurse leaders regarding LGBTQ+ inclusion efforts, it is essential to gain insights into the perspectives and practices of nurse leaders and health systems in states that are currently enacting legislation promoting discrimination and inequity. Future research should aim to include a broader range of participants from more restrictive settings to provide a deeper understanding of how nurse leaders navigate LGBTQ+ health in diverse contexts.

Future efforts should also focus on testing recommended strategies for implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices across diverse healthcare settings. Future research should investigate the impact of new diversity, equity, and inclusion requirements by regulatory and accreditation bodies on both patient and employee outcomes. Additionally, future research should explore the role of organizational culture and leadership in promoting LGBTQ+ inclusivity, including the effects of leadership training on fostering inclusive practices within healthcare organizations.

Conclusion

We have provided insights into nurse leaders’ perspectives on LGBTQ+ inclusive practices and strategies for their implementation in healthcare systems. Implementing these strategies can foster more inclusive, affirming, and equitable environments for LGBTQ+ patients, staff, and communities. This effort is crucial not only for ethical reasons but also for achieving optimal health outcomes, health equity, and promoting social justice, irrespective of sexual orientation and gender identity. Despite significant legal challenges, collaborative efforts between institutions and LGBTQ+ communities hold promise in overcoming these barriers and advancing inclusive healthcare environments. Strengthening partnerships across healthcare systems, academic institutions, and LGBTQ+ community organizations is essential for ensuring equitable access to inclusive and affirming healthcare services in the future.
