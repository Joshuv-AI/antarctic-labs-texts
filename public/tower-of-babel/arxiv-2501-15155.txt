Sampling with time-changed Markov processes


Abstract
. We study time-changed Markov processes to speed up the convergence of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms. The time-changed process is defined by adjusting the speed of time of a base process via a user-chosen, state-dependent function. We explore the properties of such transformations and apply this idea to several Markov processes from the MCMC literature, such as Langevin diffusions and piecewise deterministic Markov processes, obtaining novel modifications of classical algorithms and also re-discovering known MCMC algorithms. We prove theoretical properties of the time-changed process under suitable conditions on the base process, focusing on connecting the stationary distributions and qualitative convergence properties such as geometric and uniform ergodicity, as well as a functional central limit theorem. We also provide a comparison with the framework of space transformations, clarifying the similarities between the approaches. Throughout the paper we give various visualisations and numerical simulations on simple tasks to gain intuition on the method and its performance. Finally, we provide numerical simulations to gain intuition on the method and its performance on benchmark problems. Our results indicate a performance improvement in the context of multimodal distributions and rare event simulation.

1.
Introduction

The need to generate samples from complex probability distributions is ubiquitous in Bayesian statistics and beyond (e.g.
Brooks et al. 2011
,
Stuart 2010
). The standard technique for achieving this is Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), in which a Markovian process is constructed such that its distribution converges to the target distribution as the process evolves. The outcomes of the process are then used as samples from the distribution of interest. While the original MCMC algorithms used discrete-time processes, continuous-time processes have since gained popularity. These include diffusion processes
(
Roberts and Tweedie 1996
)
, Markov jump processes
(
Green 1995
;
Power and Goldman 2019
)
, and piecewise deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs)
(
Fearnhead et al. 2018
)
.
However, many of these processes struggle with targets commonly encountered in practical settings, such as multi-modal or heavy tailed distributions. For example, in the case of multi-modal targets, the processes tend to be attracted to the mode they find themselves in, to the extent that they struggle to hop to another mode. In the case of heavy-tailed distributions, they fail to explore the tails of the distribution in a systematic and consistent way.

In this paper, we introduce and discuss the framework of sampling using
time-changed Markov processes
to obtain more efficient continuous-time MCMC algorithms for challenging tasks, such as those described above. The fundamental idea is to apply a
random time-change
to a
base
Markov process in such a way that time accelerates in regions where we want the process to visit more frequently. At the same time, this ensures that the process does not spend too much time in these regions, which is crucial for the process to satisfy a law of large numbers. The interpretation of these regions depends on the specific task at hand. For instance, they may correspond to low-density areas that separate modes in a multimodal target setting or to the tails of the target distribution in the case of rare events estimation. In practice, the time-change is regulated by a
speed function
,
s
, which maps the state space to strictly positive real numbers and accelerates or decelerates time based on the current state of the process.

Throughout the paper, we assume that the base process, denoted by
Y
, is a well-studied Markov process. We provide conditions on
Y
and
s
that ensure desirable properties of the time-changed process
X
, such as the law of large numbers (LLN), qualitative convergence rates to the stationary distribution, and functional central limit theorems. In particular, suitable choices of
s
ensure that the convergence of
X
is exponential in time, even if
Y
converges only polynomially or more slowly. Moreover, we provide conditions for the uniform ergodicity of
X
, which refers to the exponential convergence of its law to the target, independently of the initial condition. Throughout the paper, we highlight the numerous connections to the existing literature and apply our framework to several Markov processes that are widely used in the context of MCMC algorithms.

Related work

The idea of sampling from a measure
\mu
with a time-change of a Markov process was first discussed by
Roberts and Stramer 2002a
. The authors consider a time-changed overdamped Langevin diffusion corresponding to speed functions of the particular form
s(x)=\mu^{-\alpha}(x)
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, where
\mu
is the target distribution. The focus of
Roberts and Stramer 2002a
is then limited to a specific choice of process and speed function, whereas in this paper we define and study the framework in generality.
The time-change of the Zig-Zag process (ZZP), a particular PDMP, was already considered by
Vasdekis and Roberts 2023
, but the process was introduced with ad-hoc arguments and without indicating this connection.
The approach of time-change is also deeply connected to importance sampling
(
Robert and Casella 2004
;
Owen 2013
)
. Indeed, computing ergodic averages with respect to a time-changed process is equivalent to obtaining re-weighted ergodic averages of the base process for a random time horizon (see Equation (
9
) below).
In this direction,
Chak et al. 2023
studies the optimal choice of “proposal distribution”
\tilde{\mu}
to use as target for the overdamped Langevin diffusion, then adjusting the obtained sample by importance weights. We shall revisit this connection in
Example

3.8
. It is worth mentioning that the speed function
s(x)=\mu^{-\alpha}(x)
with
\alpha=1
was shown to be optimal across all diffusion coefficients for the overdamped Langevin diffusion in a specific (multimodal) setting when the state space is included in a compact set
(
Lelièvre et al. 2024
)
. Time-changed Langevin diffusions were also considered by
Leroy et al. 2024
, who established some first theoretical properties of such processes and proposed suitable discretisation schemes for their practical simulation.
In the discrete-time world,
Andral et al. 2024
use as base process a Markov chain with stationary distribution different than the target and adjust it by letting it spend a random number of iterations at each state. This is closely connected to some of the ideas we discuss in
Section

2.2
. Similar ideas were introduced by
Malefaki and Iliopoulos 2008
in a semi-Markov implementation.
The frameworks of time changes and space transformations are similar when the main goal is to increase the frequency of visits to the tails, or equivalently to obtain a uniformly ergodic process. This aspect is discussed in
Section

5
, where we particularly focus on the connection to
Johnson and Geyer 2012
and
Yang et al. 2024
.
Finally, we note that a first version of this work appeared in the PhD thesis of the first author
(
Bertazzi 2023
)
.

2.
Framework

In this section we lay the foundations for using time-changed Markov processes to sample from a target probability distribution
\mu
.
Section

2.1
introduces a process
X
as a time-change of a base process
Y
. The main result of this section,
Theorem

2.3
, gives weak conditions on
Y
that ensure
X
has
\mu
as stationary distribution, and moreover that it satisfies a strong law of large numbers.
Section

2.2
discusses different strategies to take advantage of time-changed Markov processes to estimate expectations of observables with respect to
\mu
.

2.1.
Time-changes of Markov processes

We propose to sample from a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathrm{E}
using a Markov process
X
defined as a time change of a base process
Y
, in the sense that

(2)

X_{t}=Y_{r(t)},\text{ where }\,\,r(t):=\int_{0}^{t}s(X_{u})\textnormal{d}u.

Throughout the document,
Y
will be a càdlàg, time-homogeneous Markov process with state space
\mathrm{E}
and should be interpreted as a well-studied process such as a Langevin diffusion
(
Roberts and Tweedie 1996
)
, or a piecewise deterministic Markov process (PDMP)
(
Davis 1984
)
such as the Zig-Zag process (ZZP)
(
Bierkens et al. 2019a
)
.
The function
s
, called the
speed function
, regulates the time change applied to
Y
. We require that
s
satisfies the following condition.

Assumption 2.1
.

The speed function
s:\mathrm{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a continuous function, it satisfies

(3)

\mu(s):=\int_{\mathrm{E}}s(y)\mu(\textnormal{d}y)<\infty,

and there exists a constant
\underline{s}>0
such that
s(x)\geq\underline{s}
for all
x\in\mathrm{E}
.

We observe that since
s
is lower bounded by a constant, a change of variables gives
t=\int_{0}^{r(t)}\frac{1}{s(Y_{u})}\textnormal{d}u,

and thus we find the inverse relation

(4)

Y_{t}=X_{r^{-1}(t)}\,\,\,\text{ for }\,\,\,r^{-1}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{s(Y_{u})}\textnormal{d}u.

Intuitively, the time-changed process
X
follows the same paths of
Y
, with the modification that it speeds up when
s
is large and it slows down when
s
is small. As a consequence, the two processes cannot have the same invariant measure. In particular, for
X
to target
\mu
, we now show that
Y
should have stationary distribution

(5)

\tilde{\mu}(\textnormal{d}x):=\frac{1}{\mu(s)}s(x)\mu(\textnormal{d}x),

that is a well-defined probability measure under
2.1
.
The key assumption to ensure
X
is
\mu
-stationary is that the base process
Y
satisfies a law of large numbers (LLN) with respect to
\tilde{\mu}
.

Assumption 2.2

(LLN for the base process)
.

For any
f\in L^{1}(\tilde{\mu})
, and any initial condition
x\in\mathrm{E}
,

(6)

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(Y_{u})\textnormal{d}u\,\,\xrightarrow[a.s.]{T\rightarrow\infty}\,\,\int_{\mathrm{E}}f(y)\tilde{\mu}(\textnormal{d}y).

The assumptions we have introduced above are sufficient to obtain that
X
has stationary distribution
\mu
and also that it satisfies a LLN.

Theorem 2.3

(Invariance and LLN for the time-changed process)
.

Suppose
2.1
and
2.2
hold. Then, the process
X
has
\mu
as unique stationary distribution. Furthermore, for any
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
, and all initial conditions
x\in\mathrm{E}
, we have

(7)

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t})\textnormal{d}t\,\,\xrightarrow[a.s.]{T\rightarrow\infty}\,\,\int_{\mathrm{E}}f(y)\mu(\textnormal{d}y).

Proof.

The proof can be found in
Appendix

A
.
∎

The weakness of the required assumptions guarantees that the results of
Theorem

2.3
holds essentially in all cases of practical interest. Therefore, the time-changed process
X
can generally be used to sample from a given target distribution and to estimate its expectations.

2.2.
Estimating expectations with time-changed Markov processes

In this section we describe three asymptotically exact approaches to estimate
\mu(f):=\int_{\mathrm{E}}f(y)\mu(\textnormal{d}y)
. These are based respectively on (1) the direct simulation of
X
, (2) a re-weighting of a path of
Y
, (3) a jump process
X
with biased jump kernel
\widetilde{Q}
and rate
s
.

(1)

When it is possible directly simulate the time-changed process
X
, one can rely on
Theorem

2.3
and use the ergodic average

(8)

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t})\textnormal{d}t,

or alternatively any discrete ergodic average obtained with a uniform step size. This is the approach used e.g. by
Vasdekis and Roberts 2023
;
Roberts and Stramer 2002a
.

(2)

In cases where simulating the process
X
is challenging, we can estimate
\mu(f)
by re-weighting paths of the base process
Y
. Indeed, following the proof of
Theorem

2.3
(see
Appendix

A
),
the change of variables
u=r(t)
, with Jacobian
s(Y_{u})
, gives

(9)

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t})\textnormal{d}t\,=\,\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{r(T)}\frac{f(Y_{u})}{s(Y_{u})}\textnormal{d}u.

Theorem

2.3
guarantees that the right hand side of (
9
) is a consistent estimator of
\mu(f)
.
The estimator (
9
) requires the simulation of
Y
for a random time
r(T)
, which is not always streightforward to calculate. Alternatively, we may fix a deterministic time horizon
T
and use the estimator

(10)

\frac{1}{r^{-1}(T)}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{f(Y_{t})}{s(Y_{t})}\textnormal{d}t\,\,=\,\,\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{f(Y_{t})}{s(Y_{t})}\textnormal{d}t}{\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{s(Y_{t})}\textnormal{d}t}\,\,,

which is equivalent to simulating
X
until time
r^{-1}(T)
. For the right hand side of (
10
) we used the expression given by (
4
)for the quantity
r^{-1}(T)
.

The estimator (
10
) essentially coincides with doing self-normalised importance sampling for the base process
Y
, where the unnormalised weights are
(s(Y_{t}))^{-1}
. The estimator (
10
) is considered in
Chak et al. 2023
for a particular choice of the base process,
Y
.
Similarly to importance sampling, this approach allows the estimation of the normalising constant of
\mu
. Indeed, let
\mu(x)=Z^{-1}\exp(-\mathrm{U}(x))
, where
Z
is unknown, but where we have access to the function
\mathrm{U}
. As will be seen in the proof of
Theorem

2.3
(see
Appendix

A
), we can estimate
Z
using

(11)

\frac{r^{-1}(T)}{T\int_{\mathrm{E}}s(x)\exp(-\mathrm{U}(x))\textnormal{d}x}\,\,\xrightarrow[a.s.]{T\rightarrow\infty}\,\,Z.

This can be used when one has access to the quantity
\int_{\mathrm{E}}s(x)\exp(-\mathrm{U}(x))\textnormal{d}x
.

(3)

We now provide a third option in the case where directly simulating
X
or the random time-change
r^{-1}(T)
is not feasible. An alternative strategy is to use as base process
Y
a jump process with jump rate
\tilde{\lambda}(y)=1
for all
y\in\mathrm{E}
, and jump kernel
\widetilde{Q}
, that has
\tilde{\mu}
as stationary distribution.
The kernel
\widetilde{Q}
can be any kernel from the literature on discrete time MCMC algorithms, or the transition kernel of a continuous time Markov process after some fixed time
\bar{t}
. In either case, the time changed process
X_{t}=Y_{r(t)}
is a jump process with rate
\lambda(x)=s(x)
and kernel
Q=\widetilde{Q}
(see
Theorem

3.1
below), with stationary distribution
\mu
.
Fixing a time horizon
T
, and a starting position
x
, we can directly simulate the time-changed process
X
using the procedure described in Algorithm
1
.

Input:

Time horizon
T
, starting position
x
, speed function
s
, target
\mu
, a Markov transition kernel
\widetilde{Q}
that leaves
\tilde{\mu}
as in
eq.

5
invariant

Output:

Jump process
X
targeting
\mu
.

Set
n\leftarrow 0
,
T_{0}=0
,
X_{0}=x
;

while

T_{n}<T

do

Simulate
\tau_{n+1}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(s(X_{T_{n}}))
. Set the next jumping time to be
T_{n+1}=T_{n}+\tau_{n+1}
;

For all
t\in\left[T_{n},\min\{T,T_{n+1}\}\right)
set
X_{t}=X_{T_{n}}
;

Simulate the next jump of the process
X_{T_{n+1}}\sim\widetilde{Q}(X_{T_{n}},\cdot)
;

return

\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,T]}
.

Algorithm 1

Time-changed Markov jump process implementation

This process is straightforward to simulate and has stationary distribution
\mu
under the conditions of
Theorem

2.3
.
This procedure suggests the following estimator

(12)

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t})\textnormal{d}t\,=\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}}f(X_{T_{n}})(T_{n+1}-T_{n})\,,

where
N_{T}
is the time of the last jumping event before time
T
. One can “regularise” the estimator (
12
) by instead discretising the path
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, that is using

(13)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(X_{n\delta})

for some step size
\delta>0
and an integer
N
, such that
N\delta=T
. This has the effect of ignoring with high probability the skeleton states
X_{T_{n}}
where the process spends a small amount of time, that is when
T_{n+1}-T_{n}
is very small. Intuitively, these are points where the target distribution is small if the speed function is chosen well.
Both estimators (
12
) and (
13
) require simulating a random number of jumps
N_{T}
. We can alternatively fix a computational budget, i.e. the number of jumps
N
for the process
X
, and use the same ideas of (
12
) and (
13
).
In this case, (
12
) can be seen as a continuous-time counterpart of
Andral et al. 2024
, or as a Markovian counterpart of
Malefaki and Iliopoulos 2008
.
Howerver, it is not hard to see that this is equivalent to doing self-normalised importance sampling with the discrete time Markov chain
(X_{T_{n}})_{n=0}^{N}
with random weights, and hence there is then no practical advantage in doing this over its deterministic counterpart.

Remark 2.4
.

All three strategies discussed above are asymptotically unbiased as
T\to\infty
(or as
N\to\infty
), but the first two strategies can be modified to make them easier to implement at the cost of introducing a bias. In particular, in the first strategy we can instead discretise
X
, while in the second strategy, given a path of
Y
, we can approximate the one-dimensional integral
r^{-1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(s(Y_{u}))^{-1}\textnormal{d}u
with a numerical solver.

Remark 2.5
.

Equation (
9
) reveals a connection between sampling using time-changed processes and importance sampling. In importance sampling, samples from a measure
\tilde{\mu}
are re-weighted by
\mu/\tilde{\mu}
to approximate expectations with respect to
\mu
. Similarly, the paths of the time-changed process
X
are obtained by re-weighting paths from the
\tilde{\mu}
-stationary base process, where
\tilde{\mu}
is defined in (
5
). Here, the time change assigns importance weights, where large weights are assigned to states where the speed function is small, i.e. where the time-changed process moves slowly. The speed function, defined as
\tilde{\mu}/\mu
up to a constant factor, can then be interpreted as the reciprocal of the importance weight. Intuitively, a well-chosen speed function defines a distribution
\tilde{\mu}
that makes the task of interest easier to solve, e.g. connecting the modes of
\mu
when it is multimodal. As in importance sampling, it is essential to choose speed functions bounded below by a strictly positive constant, ensuring that
\tilde{\mu}
has heavier tails than
\mu
.

3.
Examples

3.1.
Time-changes of piecewise deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs)

In this section we show that time-changes of general PDMPs are also PDMPs and we identify their characteristics.
The PDMPs considered in the MCMC literature are typically of the form
(X,V)
, where
X
is the position vector and
V
can be interpreted as a velocity vector. With an abuse of notation, we denote the stationary distribution of the PDMP as
\mu(\textnormal{d}x,\textnormal{d}v)=\mu(\textnormal{d}x)\nu(\textnormal{d}v)
, where
\mu(\textnormal{d}x)
is the target distribution and
\nu
is an auxiliary distribution. In particular, the position vector is the object of interest in the context of MCMC since it is marginally invariant with respect to the target distribution.
A PDMP is identified by the triple
(\Phi,\lambda,Q)
, which governs the dynamics of the process as follows:

•

The smooth vector field
\Phi
defines the deterministic motion of the process through the system of ODEs
\textnormal{d}\varphi_{t}(y,w)=\Phi(\varphi_{t}(y,w))\textnormal{d}t

where
\varphi_{t}
denotes the integral curve that solves the ODE, and satisfies
\varphi_{0}(y,w)=(y,w)
. The process starting from
(y,w)
follows the deterministic path
\left(\varphi_{t}(y,w)\right)_{t\geq 0}
.

•

Noise comes into the system at random times
\tau
following the probability distribution

\mathbb{P}_{y,w}(\tau>t)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(\varphi_{u}(y,w))\textnormal{d}u\right),

where
\lambda:\mathrm{E}\to[0,\infty)
is the event rate and
(y,w)
is the initial condition.

•

At these random times the state of the PDMP is updated by drawing from the probability kernel
Q(\varphi_{\tau}(y,w),\textnormal{d}(y^{\prime},w^{\prime}))
.

Now we connect the characteristics of the base PDMP with its time change.

Theorem 3.1

(Time change of a PDMP)
.

Assume that
2.1
holds. Consider a PDMP with characteristics
(\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{\lambda},\widetilde{Q})
. Then the time-changed process with speed function
s
is the PDMP with characteristics
(\Phi,\lambda,Q)=(s\tilde{\Phi},s\tilde{\lambda},\widetilde{Q})
.

Proof.

The proof is presented in
Section

C.1
.
∎

Importantly, the vector field
\Phi
and rate
\lambda
are obtained multiplying the ones of the base PDMP with the speed function
s
, while the jump kernel
Q
is not affected and remains equal to
\widetilde{Q}
.

3.1.1.
Running example: the Zig-Zag process

The Zig-Zag process (ZZP)
(
Bierkens et al. 2019a
)
is a PDMP
(Y,W)
with state space
\mathrm{E}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1,+1\}^{d}
.
The vector
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the position of the ZZP, while
W\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}
represents its velocity.
The ZZP is designed so that it has a unique stationary distribution with density
\tilde{\mu}(y,w)=\tilde{\mu}(y)\times 2^{-d}
on
\mathrm{E}
, where
\tilde{\mu}(y)\propto\exp(-\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y))
is the density of a given probability measure
\tilde{\mu}(\textnormal{d}y)=\tilde{\mu}(y)\textnormal{d}y
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This process evolves according to simple deterministic dynamics between random event times, at which the process changes direction. The deterministic dynamics follow the system of ODEs,
\textnormal{d}Y_{t}=W_{t}\,\textnormal{d}t,\textnormal{d}W_{t}=0,
i.e. the process evolves with constant velocity.
The ZZP has
d
types of random events, each consisting in a change of sign of the corresponding coordinate of the velocity vector. The jump mechanism for the coordinate
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
is described by the following pair of jump rate and kernel

(14)

\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(y,w)=(w_{i}\partial_{i}\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y))_{+}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(y),\quad\widetilde{Q}_{i}((y,w),(\textnormal{d}\tilde{y},\tilde{w}))=\delta_{(y,R_{i}w)}(\textnormal{d}\tilde{y},\tilde{w})

for all
(y,w),(\tilde{y},\tilde{w})\in\mathrm{E}
, where
\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(y)\geq 0
are refreshment rates that can be set to zero and
R_{i}
is the map
w\mapsto(w_{1},\dots,w_{i-1},-w_{i},w_{i+1},\dots,w_{d})
. Here we used the notation
a_{+}=\max\{0,a\}
, and
\partial_{i}
denotes the partial derivative with respect to
y_{i}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Notice that PDMPs with more than one type of jump, each with their own rate and kernel
(\tilde{\lambda}_{i},\widetilde{Q}_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}
, can be described in the formulation of
Section

3.1
by setting
\tilde{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\lambda}_{i}
and
\widetilde{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\nicefrac{{\tilde{\lambda}_{i}}}{{\tilde{\lambda}}})\,\widetilde{Q}_{i}
. This has the interpretation that event times are governed by the total rate
\tilde{\lambda}
, and at each event time there is probability
\nicefrac{{\tilde{\lambda}_{i}}}{{\tilde{\lambda}}}
of obtaining the new state applying
\widetilde{Q}_{i}
.

Now we characterise the time change of the ZZP, enforcing that the resulting process has the target distribution
\mu\propto\exp(-\mathrm{U})
as marginal stationary measure for the position vector. As given by
Theorem

2.3
, this can be achieved considering as base process a standard ZZP with marginal invariant distribution
\tilde{\mu}\propto s\mu
, that is the distribution with density
\tilde{\mu}(y)\propto\exp(-\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y))

Proposition 3.4

(Time-changed ZZP)

Let

\displaystyle\textnormal{d}X_{t}=s(X_{t})V_{t}\,\textnormal{d}t,\quad\textnormal{d}V_{t}=0,

Proof.

Example 3.5

(Bouncy Particle Sampler (BPS)
(
Bouchard-Côté et al. 2018
)
)
.

The BPS is a PDMP
(Y,W)
defined on
\mathrm{E}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with position-velocity decomposition and with deterministic dynamics identified by the vector field
\tilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{BP}}(y,w)=(w,0)
. We now describe the standard BPS with stationary distribution with density
\tilde{\mu}(y,w)\propto\exp(-\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y))\nu(w)
, where
\nu
is a
d
-dimensional standard normal distribution.
The event rate of BPS is
\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathrm{BP}}(y,w)=\langle w,\nabla\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y)\rangle_{+}+\lambda_{r}
, where
\lambda_{r}>0
is the refreshment rate, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the usual inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The jump mechanism is given by

\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{BP}}((y,w),(\textnormal{d}y^{\prime},\textnormal{d}w^{\prime}))=\frac{\langle w,\nabla\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y)\rangle_{+}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathrm{BP}}(y,w)}\,\delta_{(x,\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{BP}}(x)w)}(\textnormal{d}y^{\prime},\textnormal{d}w^{\prime})+\frac{\lambda_{r}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathrm{BP}}(y,w)}\,\delta_{x}(\textnormal{d}y^{\prime})\nu(\textnormal{d}w^{\prime}),

where
\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{BP}}
is the operator that defines elastic reflections on the level curves of the potential
\tilde{\mathrm{U}}
, that is
\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{BP}}(y)w=w-2\frac{\langle w,\nabla\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y)\rangle}{\lvert\nabla\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y)\rvert^{2}}\nabla\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y).

At event times, the process is either reflected according to
\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{BP}}
, or the velocity is refreshed by an independent draw from
\nu
.

Bouchard-Côté et al. 2018
prove that if
\lambda_{r}>0
and
\tilde{\mathrm{U}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
, the process has
\tilde{\mu}
as stationary distribution and a LLN (i.e.
2.2
) is satisfied.

Applying
Theorem

3.1
we find that the time changed BPS,
(X_{t},V_{t})=(Y_{r(t)},W_{r(t)})
, with speed
s
satisfying
2.1
, is the PDMP with characteristics
(s\tilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{BP}},s\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathrm{BP}},\widetilde{Q}_{\mathrm{BP}})
.
Theorem

2.3
gives that, if
\lambda_{r}>0
and
\tilde{\mathrm{U}},s\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, this process has invariant distribution with density
\mu(x,v)=\mu(x)\nu(v)
for
\mu(x)\propto\exp(-\mathrm{U}(x))
when
\tilde{\mathrm{U}}=\mathrm{U}-\ln s
. Simple calculations show that the time-changed BPS has event rate
\lambda(x,v)=\langle v,s(x)\nabla\mathrm{U}(x)-\nabla s(x)\rangle_{+}+\lambda_{r}s(x)
and deterministic motion given by (
15
). We note that the jump kernel
Q
consists of bounces on the contour lines of
\tilde{\mu}
.

Example 3.6

(Randomised Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (RHMC)
(
Bou-Rabee and Sanz-Serna 2017
)
)
.

RHMC is a PDMP which follows Hamiltonian dynamics with momentum refreshments at random times.
The RHMC with stationary distribution
\tilde{\mu}(y,w)\propto\exp(-\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(y)-(1/2)\lvert w\rvert^{2})
has dynamics described by the system of ODEs

(18)

\frac{\textnormal{d}Y_{t}}{\textnormal{d}t}=W_{t},\quad\frac{\textnormal{d}W_{t}}{\textnormal{d}t}=-\nabla\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(Y_{t}),

while at random times with rate
\lambda_{r}
the velocity is refreshed with a draw from the standard normal distribution.

The time-changed RHMC with speed
s
has unique invariant distribution with density
\mu(x,v)\propto\exp(-\mathrm{U}(x)-(1/2)\lvert v\rvert^{2})
for
\tilde{\mathrm{U}}=\mathrm{U}-\ln s
. This process is a PDMP with deterministic dynamics

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}X_{t}}{\textnormal{d}t}=s(X_{t})V_{t},\quad\frac{\textnormal{d}V_{t}}{\textnormal{d}t}=-s(X_{t})\nabla\mathrm{U}(X_{t})+\nabla s(X_{t}).

Such process moves on the level curves of the Hamiltonian
H(x,v)=\mathrm{U}(x)-\ln s(x)+(1/2)\lvert v\rvert^{2}
with speed given by
s(x)
. The jump mechanism consists again of velocity refreshments from the standard normal distribution with rate
s(x)\lambda_{r}
. With analogous ideas it is possible to define a time-changed version of RHMC samplers with different choices kinetic energy, considered e.g.
Nishimura et al. 2021
.

Now we consider the setting of a target distribution

Example 3.10
.

Power and Goldman 2019
introduced a pure jump process that jumps at random times from a current state

to a neighbouring state

\mathcal{N}(x)\subset\mathrm{E}

4.
Convergence properties of time changed Markov processes

In this section we focus on convergence properties of a time changed process
X
under suitable assumptions on the base process
Y
.

4.1.
Ergodicity of time changed processes

In this section we are interested in the qualitative convergence of the law of the time-changed process,
X
, to its stationary distribution. We shall obtain conditions under which
X
satisfies, for any
x\in\mathrm{E}
,

(21)

\lVert\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\tilde{\mu}(\cdot)\rVert_{\text{TV}}\leq\mathrm{M}(x)\,\rho^{t}

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\text{TV}}
is the total variation (TV) distance,
\rho\in(0,1)
is a constant, and
\mathrm{M}:\mathrm{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. When (
21
) holds,
X
is
geometrically ergodic
, while we say it is
uniformly ergodic
when
\mathrm{M}
is a constant function. This type of convergence can be proved under
drift conditions
for the generator of the process
(
Down et al. 1995
)
.

Next we define some necessary notions of stability (see e.g.
Meyn and Tweedie 1993b
).
The process
Y
is said
\psi
-irreducible
if there exists a non-trivial measure
\psi
such that for any measurable set
\mathrm{B}
with
\psi(\mathrm{B})>0
it holds
\mathbb{E}_{y}[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{Y_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\,\textnormal{d}t]>0
. A set
C
is
petite
if there exists a probability measure
\alpha
on
(0,\infty)
such that for all
y\in C

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{y}(Y_{t}\in\cdot\,)\,\alpha(\textnormal{d}t)\geq\eta\nu(\cdot).
The process is called
aperiodic
if there exists a petite set
C
and
T_{0}\geq 0
, such that
\mathbb{P}_{x}(Y_{t}\in C)>0
for all
t\geq T_{0}
and all
x\in C.

We will also use the notion of the weak generator of the process
Y
, defined as follows.
Let
\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}})
be a set of measurable functions
f:\mathrm{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
be an operator defined on
\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}})
.
The pair
(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}))
is the
weak generator
of the process
Y
if for any
f\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}})
, and any starting point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the process

(22)

M_{t}=f(Y_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f(Y_{s})\textnormal{d}s

is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration of the process. We will write
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}))
to denote the weak generator of the time-changed process
X
, which is defined in the same way.

In the following assumption we state conditions on
Y
which will be used in
Theorem

4.4
to obtain geometric and uniform ergodicity of
X
.

Assumption 4.1
.

Y
is a
\psi
-irreducible Markov process with generator
(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}))
and stationary distribution
\tilde{\mu}
. Moreover,
Y
satisfies the following conditions.

(1)

There exist bounded sets
C\subset D
, constants
t_{0}>0
,
\eta>0
,
\varepsilon>0
, and a non-trivial measure
\nu
on
C
such that for all
y\in C
and for any measurable set
A
it holds that

\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}_{y}(Y_{t}\in A,Y_{u}\in D\textnormal{ for all }u\in[0,t])\textnormal{d}t\geq\eta\nu(A).

(2)

There exist a function
\mathrm{V}:\mathrm{E}\to[1,\infty]
, with
\mathrm{V}\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}})
, a function
\mathrm{W}:\mathrm{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and a constant
\gamma\geq 0
such that for all
y\in\mathrm{E}

(23)

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\mathrm{V}(y)\leq-\mathrm{W}(y)\mathrm{V}(y)+\gamma\mathbbm{1}_{C}(y),

where
C
is the same set of point
(1)
.

4.1
(1) is enough to ensure that the bounded set
C
is petite for the time-changed process (see
Lemma

B.3
for the proof). For this, we require that
Y
satisfies a petite set condition while staying inside a bounded set during a finite time horizon. We note that for most local algorithms, such as our running example, and other constant velocity PDMPs,
4.1
(1) is met. The function
\mathrm{W}
appearing in
4.1
(2),
is indicative of the convergence of the process
Y
. In particular,
Y
is geometrically ergodic when
\mathrm{W}(y)\geq\eta
for some
\eta>0
, and uniformly ergodic when in addition
\mathrm{V}(y)\leq\beta
for
\beta<\infty

(
Down et al. 1995
)
. Informally,
Y
has slower convergence when these conditions are not met.

In order to present our results, we need the following technical definition, required to translate (
23
) to a drift condition for the time-changed process.

Definition 4.2
.

We define
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L})\cap\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}})
to be the set of measurable functions
f:\mathrm{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for all
x\in E
,

(24)

\mathcal{L}f(x)=s(x)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f(x).

As we discuss in
Section

B.1
, the set
\mathcal{A}
typically contains a large class of functions, for which the generator of the time-changed process is indeed of the form
s\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
. Nonetheless, this is in general not enough to ensure that the function
\mathrm{V}
appearing in
4.1
(2) is in
\mathcal{A}
, as required to prove
Theorem

4.4
. Throughout this section, we shall then assume that
\mathrm{V}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Finally, we will also be making the following assumption on the time-changed process.

Assumption 4.3
.

X
is aperiodic.

Aperiodicity of
X
is crucial in order to establish its convergence to the target. We conjecture that
4.3
holds under aperiodicity of
Y
and some regularity conditions. Indeed, the speed function is bounded away from zero, hence the time-change cannot force the process to get stuck anywhere in the space.
We note that the time-changed ZZP is proven to be aperiodic under mild regularity conditions on the target and the speed function
(

Remark 4.7

The relation (

Connection to space transformations

(a)

Time-changed ZZP with speed
s(x)=(1+\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{(d+1)/2}
.

(b)

Space transform of the time-changed ZZP, with transformation
\mathrm{H}^{-1}(x)=x(1+\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{-1/2}
.

(c)

Space-transformed ZZP, with transformation
\mathrm{H}(y)=y(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{-1/2}
for
y\in\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\lvert z\rvert<1\}.

(d)

Base process for the space-transformed ZZP, that is a standard ZZP with target
\tilde{\mu}(y,w)=\mu(\mathrm{H}(y),w)s(\mathrm{H}(y))
with
y\in\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\lvert z\rvert<1\}.

Figure 3
.
Comparison between time-change and space transformation, using the standard ZZP as base process and a two-dimensional, standard normal target distribution. The choices of the speed function and diffeomorphism are as in (
34
) and (
33
).
The colours and the scale on the right of each plot represent the logarithm of the speed of each process, calculated as distance travelled in a small time unit divided by the length of the time unit.

Johnson and Geyer 2012
suggested another approach to improve convergence of MCMC algorithms in the context of heavy tailed target distributions relying on a diffeomorphism
\mathrm{H}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Their idea is to apply the change of variables
x=\mathrm{H}(y)
to transform the original target density
\mu
to
\tilde{\mu}(y)=\mu(\mathrm{H}(y))\left\lvert\det\mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{H}}(y)\right\rvert
where
\mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{H}}
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
\mathrm{H}
.
Choosing
\mathrm{H}
suitably gives that
\tilde{\mu}
has lighter tails than
\mu
and thus it can be easier to target using a Markov process
Y
that is stationary with respect to
\tilde{\mu}
. Then, one can apply the map
\mathrm{H}
to define the process
X_{t}=\mathrm{H}(Y_{t})
, which can be used to obtain asymptotically exact samples from
\mu
.
It is straightforward to check that the process
X
has stationary distribution
\mu
and inherits the convergence properties of
Y
, hence enabling e.g. geometric convergence even when
\mu
is heavy tailed.

The approaches of space and time transformations are strongly related. In order to see this, it is helpful to rewrite
\tilde{\mu}
as

(32)

\tilde{\mu}(y)=\mu(x)\left\lvert\det\mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathrm{H}^{-1}(x))\right\rvert,\quad\text{for }x=\mathrm{H}(y).

This shows that both strategies are built on a base process that targets a distribution
\tilde{\mu}
of the form
\tilde{\mu}\propto s\mu
, where
s
represents either the speed function that defines the time change or the Jacobian determinant term of the diffeomorphism
\mathrm{H}
. It is indeed reasonable to interpret the Jacobian determinant as a speed function, since it measures how infinitesimal volumes are affected by the map
\mathrm{H}
and hence how fast the process
X
moves through a unit of volume.

Following this connection, we can obtain novel speed functions as Jacobian determinants of a suitable space transformation.
For instance, consider the diffeomorphism defined for points
y\in\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lvert z\rvert<1\}
as

(33)

\mathrm{H}(y)=\frac{y}{(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\,.

This has inverse
\mathrm{H}^{-1}(x)=x(1+\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{-1/2},
which maps points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to points
y=\mathrm{H}^{-1}(x)
inside the
d
-dimensional unit sphere. It is not difficult to obtain that the associated Jacobian determinant satisfies
\det\mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{H}}(y)=(1-\lvert y\rvert^{2})^{-(d+1)/2}
, which motivates using as speed function
s(x)=\lvert\det\mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathrm{H}^{-1}(x))\rvert
, i.e.

(34)

s(x)=(1+\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{(d+1)/2}\,.

Intuitively, this transformation is helpful when one wants to explore the tails of a distribution that is centred around zero. In the case of time-changed ZZP or the time-changed BPS, using a speed function of the form (
34
) has the additional advantage that the deterministic dynamics can be simulated analytically, without the need of numerical integration
(
Vasdekis and Roberts 2023
)
. In
Figure

3(a)
and
Figure

3(c)
we compare the approaches of time and space transformations obtained respectively with speed (
34
) and diffeomorphism (
33
), using the ZZP as base process in each case. The time-changed ZZP, shown in
Figure

3(a)
, moves in straight lines with speed that increases with the distance from the origin, regardless of the direction of the process.
Figure

3(b)
shows the trace plot of the
process obtained applying the transformation
\mathrm{H}^{-1}
to the position vector of the time-changed ZZP. This process has constant speed when it moves radially, while it speeds up when it moves with a tangential component. Because such base process is compactly supported and has speed that is lower bounded by a positive constant (see
Figure

3(b)
), it is then reasonable to expect that it can be uniformly ergodic, and as a consequence so is the time-changed ZZP
(
Johnson and Geyer 2012
)
.
On the other hand, the space-transformed ZZP, shown in
Figure

3(c)
, moves in curved lines and speeds up when moving radially away from the origin, while it slows down when moving with tangential component to the level curves of the Jacobian determinant.

Similarly, we can obtain other speed functions that are the Jacobian determinant of diffeomorphisms from (a compact subset of)
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A related example is the stereographic map, recently considered by
Yang et al. 2024
, that transforms points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to points on the surface of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
(excluding the North pole). The Jacobian determinant of the stereographic projection turns out to be (up to a constant factor)
s(x)=(1+\lvert x\rvert^{2})^{d},
that is similar to (
34
).

7.
Discussion

In this paper we have discussed the use of time-changes of Markov processes in the context of MCMC algorithms. As we have seen, this concept is closely related to performing importance sampling on the paths of a base process with biased stationary distribution.
We discussed suitable speed functions that can be beneficial in the context of multimodal and heavy tailed distributions and illustrated their strengths with simple numerical simulations.
A conceptual comparison to the framework of space transformations
(
Johnson and Geyer 2012
)
highlighted that time-changed processes can be more intuitive to use for tail exploration (see
Section

5
). Similarly, it appears simpler to find a good speed function in the context of multimodal distributions compared to a good diffeomorphism that enhances mode exploration. On the other hand, space transformations seem preferable when the target has complex geometry
(
Girolami and Calderhead 2011
)
.

There are several directions which we leave as topics for future research. For example, although we gave intuitive choices of speed function, these tend to depend on a parameter (e.g.
s(x)=\mu^{-a}(x)
for
a\in(0,1)
). It is then natural to wonder how to learn this parameter, or also how to let it dynamically evolve in order to alternate between exploration and exploitation phases.
At the same time, another application we have not explored is the use of time-changes to obtain more accurate approximations of stochastic processes. Indeed, it can be useful to take advantage of a time-changed process to slow down a process to incur in smaller numerical errors.

\displaystyle=\frac{r(T)}{T}\frac{1}{r(T)}\int_{0}^{r(T)}f(Y_{u})\frac{1}{s(Y_{u})}\textnormal{d}u.

Therefore, due to
2.2
, we find

(38)

\frac{T}{r(T)}\,\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t})\textnormal{d}t=\frac{1}{r(T)}\int_{0}^{r(T)}f(Y_{u})\frac{1}{s(Y_{u})}\textnormal{d}u\xrightarrow[a.s.]{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\mu(s)}\int f(y)\mu(\textnormal{d}y).

This holds for any
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
. In particular, it holds when
f\equiv 1
, in which case (
38
) gives

(39)

\frac{r(T)}{T}\xrightarrow[a.s.]{T\rightarrow\infty}\mu(s).

Therefore, from (
37
) we get

(40)

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t})\textnormal{d}t\,\xrightarrow[a.s.]{T\rightarrow\infty}\,\int f(y)\mu(\textnormal{d}y),

as required.

Now, let
A\subset E
be a borel set with
\mu(A)>0
. Using the law of large numbers we get that

(41)

\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{X_{t}\in A}=\infty\right)=1,

meaning that
X
is Harris recurrent in the sense of
Meyn and Tweedie 1993a
, and therefore it possesses a unique invariant measure
\nu
(see e.g.
Azéma et al. 1969
,
Meyn and Tweedie 1993a
). From (
40
) we get that
\nu=\mu
and this concludes the proof.

Appendix B

B.1.
Weak Generator

We turn our attention to the
weak generator
of the time-changed process, a mathematical object that is essential in the analysis of Markov processes. The weak generator was defined in
Section

4.1
.

We shall assume throughout that the martingale problem for
Y_{t}
is well posed, that is it admits a unique solution. The following theorem shows that for a large class of functions, the generator of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is obtained by multiplying the generator of
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by the speed function
s
. Heuristically, this is to be expected since the generator represents the derivative of a test function with respect to the dynamics of the process, so speeding up the time by a function
s
should be reflected in the derivative. We have the following.

Proposition B.1
.

Suppose that the Markov process
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has a weak generator
(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}))
. Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}})\cap C_{b}(\mathrm{E})
be such that for any
f\in\mathcal{C}
,
s\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f
is a bounded function. Then,
\mathcal{C}
is in the domain of the weak generator of the time-changed process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, defined via (
2
), Furthermore, for the weak generator
\mathcal{L}
of the process
X
, we have that for all
f\in\mathcal{C}
,

(42)

\mathcal{L}f=s\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f,

meaning that
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is defined in
Definition

4.2
.

Proof.

The proof is obtained using the same argument as in the proof of
[
Ethier and Kurtz 2009
, Theorem 1.3, Chapter 6]
.
∎

Proposition

B.1
partially justifies a technical assumption we make throughout our main results in
Section

4
, i.e. that the Lyapunov function
\mathrm{V}\in\mathcal{A}
. The proposition is also used in
Section

C.2
to study time-changes of diffusions. In most practical cases, the set
\mathcal{C}
, and therefore the set
\mathcal{A}
as well, will contain a large family of functions. For example, for our running example, the Zig-Zag process, the infinitely differentiable functions with compact support are a subset of
\mathcal{C}
. The same holds for other PDMPs that move around the space with constant speed, such as the Bouncy Particle Sampler with velocity refreshments chosen from the unit sphere, but also for diffusion processes as well.

B.2.
Irreducibility

Let us consider the concept of
irreducibility
, which is important to obtain convergence of the law of the process to its stationary distribution. The process
Y_{t}
is
\psi
-irreducible if for some non-trivial measure
\psi
, for any measurable set
\mathrm{B}
such that
\psi(\mathrm{B})>0
it holds
\mathbb{E}_{y}[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{Y_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\,\textnormal{d}t]>0
. In the next proposition we show that irreducibility of
X_{t}
follows from irreducibility of
Y_{t}.

Proposition B.2
.

Let
Y_{t}
and
X_{t}
on
\mathrm{E}
be two Markov processes related by (
2
), where
s
is bounded on bounded sets.
Let
\psi
be a measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure.
Then,
X_{t}
is
\psi
-irreducible if and only if
Y_{t}
is
\psi
-irreducible.

Proof.

Assume
Y_{t}
is
\psi
-irreducible and recall the relation (
4
). Then for any
\mathrm{B}
such that
\psi(\mathrm{B})>0
we have by a change of variables

(43)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\textnormal{d}t\right]=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{Y_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\,\frac{1}{s(Y_{t})}\textnormal{d}t\right].

If
\mathrm{B}
is a bounded set, then there exists
\overline{s}_{\mathrm{B}}<\infty
such that
s(x)\leq s_{\mathrm{B}}
for all
x\in\mathrm{B}
. Combined with the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{Y_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\textnormal{d}t\right]>0
, from the irreducibility of
Y
, we get that
\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\textnormal{d}t\right]>0.
Suppose now
\mathrm{B}
is not bounded but has non-zero Lebesgue measure. In this case there exists
\mathrm{B}_{1}\subset\mathrm{B}
that is bounded and has positive Lebesgue measure. Then
\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\textnormal{d}t\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}\in\mathrm{B}_{1}}\textnormal{d}t\right]
which is strictly positive as shown above.
Therefore,
X_{t}
is
\psi
-irreducible.

The reverse statement can be obtained observing that, since
s(x)\geq\underline{s}>0
for all
x\in\mathrm{E}
,

(44)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{Y_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\,\textnormal{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\,s(X_{t})\,\textnormal{d}t\right]

(45)

\displaystyle\geq\underline{s}\,\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}\in\mathrm{B}}\,\textnormal{d}t\right]

which is positive since
X_{t}
is irreducible.
∎

B.3.
Petite sets

Let us now introduce the important notion of
small set
, which is often used to establish ergodicity of a Markov processes
Down et al. 1995
and MCMC algorithms
Roberts and Rosenthal 2004
. A set
C\subset\mathrm{E}
is
(t_{0},\eta,\nu)
-small for a process
Y_{t}
if there exist a constant
\eta>0
and a non-trivial probability measure
\nu
on
\mathrm{E}
, i.e. such that
\nu(\mathrm{E})>0
, such that for all
y\in C

\mathbb{P}_{y}(Y_{t_{0}}\in\cdot\,)\geq\eta\nu(\cdot).

A related, weaker condition is that of
petite set
: there exists a probability measure
\alpha
on
(0,\infty)
such that for all
y\in C

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{y}(Y_{t}\in\cdot\,)\,\alpha(\textnormal{d}t)\geq\eta\nu(\cdot).

We are interested in proving that under suitable conditions a small or petite set for
Y_{t}
can be petite for the time changed process
X_{t}
. For this to hold, we are imposing
4.1
(1) from
Section

4.1
.

The goal of this section is to show that
4.1
(1) is sufficient to conclude that
C
is petite for the time-changed process
X
. The essential idea is that on the event in which
Y_{t}
does not leave the bounded set
C
it is possible to bound the time-change
r(t)
. Notice that
4.1
(1) holds e.g. when
Y
satisfies a uniform small set condition of the form
\mathbb{P}_{y}(Y_{t}\in\cdot\,)\geq\eta\nu(\cdot)
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+\varepsilon]
and
y\in C
, and also
Y_{t}\in D
almost surely for
t\leq t_{0}+\varepsilon
whenever
Y_{0}\in C
. This will be the case for any PDMP with deterministic dynamics having a bounded velocity, such as the Zig-Zag sampler, Bouncy particle sampler, and the Boomerang sampler.

Lemma B.3
.

Suppose
Y
satisfies
4.1
(1) for some sets
C\subset D
, and moreover that
2.1
holds.
Then,
C
is petite for the time-changed process
X
.

Proof.

Let
\beta
be the uniform distribution on the interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
for some
t_{1}<t_{2}
that will be defined later, and denote
\Delta t=t_{2}-t_{1}
.
For any
x\in C
and for any measurable set
A
it holds that

(46)

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A)\beta(t)\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\,\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A)\,\textnormal{d}t

(47)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\,\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{Y_{r(t)}\in A}\textnormal{d}t\right].

In the last line we applied Fubini’s theorem. Applying the change of variables
t^{\prime}=r(t)
we find

(48)

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A)\beta(t)\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{r(t_{1})}^{r(t_{2})}\frac{1}{s(Y_{t})}\,\mathbbm{1}_{Y_{t}\in A}\textnormal{d}t\right]

(49)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\overline{s}_{C}\Delta t}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{r(t_{1})}^{r(t_{2})}\,\mathbbm{1}_{Y_{t}\in A,\,Y_{u}\in D\textnormal{ for all }u\in[0,t]}\,\textnormal{d}t\right].

Now notice that
r(t_{2})\geq\underline{s}t_{2}
almost surely. Similarly, on the event that
Y_{u}\in D\textnormal{ for all }u\in[0,t]
for
t\geq r(t_{1})
, we have
r(t_{1})\leq\overline{s}_{C}t_{1}
almost surely.
Therefore we find

(50)

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A)\beta(t)\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\overline{s}_{C}\Delta t}\int_{\overline{s}_{C}t_{1}}^{\underline{s}t_{2}}\mathbb{P}_{x}(Y_{t}\in A,Y_{u}\in D\textnormal{ for all }u\in[0,t])\textnormal{d}t

since we are effectively restricting the domain of integration. Choosing
t_{2}=\frac{t_{0}+\varepsilon}{\underline{s}}
and
t_{1}=\frac{t_{0}}{\overline{s}_{C}}
gives

(51)

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in A)\beta(t)\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\overline{s}_{C}\Delta t}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}_{x}(Y_{t}\in A,Y_{u}\in D\textnormal{ for all }u\in[0,t])\textnormal{d}t

(52)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\overline{s}_{C}\Delta t}\eta\nu(A),

which concludes the proof.
∎

C.1.
Characterisation of time-changed PDMPs

Let us recall the construction of a time-homogeneous PDMP
(Y,W)
with characteristics
(\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{\lambda},\widetilde{Q})
. Let
(E_{m})_{m=1,2,\dots}
and
(U_{m})_{m=1,2,\dots}
denote two independent sequences of i.i.d. random variables respectively with the exponential distribution with parameter

and the uniform distribution on

. Denote as

the flow map corresponding to the ODE
\textnormal{d}(y_{t},w_{t})=\tilde{\Phi}(y_{t},w_{t})\textnormal{d}t
. We introduce the function

such that for

it holds that
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{F}((y,w),U)\in A)=\widetilde{Q}((y,w),A)
for all measurable sets
A
and all
(y,w)\in\mathrm{E}.

We inductively construct the event times
(\tilde{T}_{m})_{m=0,1,2,\dots}
We first set
\tilde{T}_{0}=0
. Assume that
\tilde{T}_{1},\dots,\tilde{T}_{n}
and the realisation of the path
(Y_{t},W_{t})_{t\leq T_{n}}
are known. We shall show how

and

can be obtained. First of all,
\tilde{T}_{n+1}=\tilde{T}_{n}+\tilde{\tau}_{n+1}
where the interarrival time

\tilde{\tau}_{n+1}=\inf\left\{t>0:\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\lambda}(\tilde{\varphi}_{u}(Y_{\tilde{T}_{n}},W_{\tilde{T}_{n}}))\textnormal{d}u\geq E_{n+1}\right\}.

(Y_{\tilde{T}_{n+1}},W_{\tilde{T}_{n+1}})=\mathrm{F}(\tilde{\varphi}_{\tau_{n+1}}(Y_{\tilde{T}_{n}},Y_{\tilde{T}_{n}}),U_{n+1}).

The whole path of the PDMP
(\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{\lambda},\widetilde{Q})
can be constructed inductively with this approach.

Now we show that the process defined by
(X_{t},V_{t})=(Y_{r(t)},W_{r(t)})

can be constructed following the same inductive procedure above, and is therefore a PDMP. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
T_{n}=r^{-1}(\tilde{T}_{n})
, with the convention that
T_{0}=0
. Assume that the process has been constructed until time
T_{n}
for some
n\in\{0,1,\dots\}
.

Let us define
\tau_{n+1}=T_{n+1}-T_{n}
, that is the
(n+1)
-th interarrival time of
(X,V)
. Since
(Y,W)
follows a deterministic movement in the interval
[\tilde{T}_{n},\tilde{T}_{n+1})
, so does
(X,V)
in the interval
[T_{n},T_{n+1})
. In particular, for any
t\in[0,\tau_{n+1})
, a simple use of the chain rule yields

(54)

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}\left(X_{T_{n}+t},V_{T_{n}+t}\right)}{\textnormal{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\textnormal{d}\left(Y_{r(T_{n}+t)},W_{r(T_{n}+t)}\right)}{\textnormal{d}t}

(55)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Phi}\left(Y_{r(T_{n}+t)},W_{r(T_{n}+t)}\right)s\left(Y_{r(T_{n}+t)},W_{r(T_{n}+t)}\right)

(56)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Phi}\left(X_{T_{n}+t},V_{X_{T_{n}+t}}\right)s\left(X_{T_{n}+t},V_{X_{T_{n}+t}}\right).

Therefore, the deterministic motion of
(X,V)
is described by the ODE with vector field
\Phi=s\tilde{\Phi}
.
Now, we let us focus on the law of the interarrival time
\tau_{n+1}
. Notice that by a change of variables
k=r(u)
, we have

(57)

\displaystyle\int_{T_{n}}^{T_{n+1}}\tilde{\lambda}\left(X_{u},V_{u}\right)s\left(X_{u},W_{u}\right)\textnormal{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{T_{n}}^{T_{n+1}}\tilde{\lambda}\left(Y_{r(u)},W_{r(u)}\right)s\left(Y_{r(u)},W_{r(u)}\right)\textnormal{d}u

(58)

\displaystyle=\int_{r(T_{n})}^{r(T_{n+1})}\tilde{\lambda}\left(Y_{k},W_{k}\right)\textnormal{d}k

(59)

\displaystyle=\int_{\tilde{T}_{n}}^{\tilde{T}_{n+1}}\tilde{\lambda}\left(Y_{k},W_{k}\right)\textnormal{d}k

(60)

\displaystyle=E_{n+1}.

Therefore
\tau_{n+1}
is the first arrival time of a Poisson process with jump rate
\lambda=s\tilde{\lambda}
.

Finally, at time
T_{n+1}
the process
(X,V)
jumps according to the the jump kernel
Q
, which is clearly equal to
\widetilde{Q}
since
(X_{T_{n+1}},V_{T_{n+1}})=(Y_{\tilde{T}_{n+1}},W_{\tilde{T}_{n+1}})
and
(X_{T_{n+1}-},V_{T_{n+1}-})=(Y_{\tilde{T}_{n+1}-},W_{\tilde{T}_{n+1}-})
and hence
(X_{T_{n+1}},V_{T_{n+1}})=\mathrm{F}((X_{T_{n+1}-},V_{T_{n+1}-}),U_{n+1})
.

We have shown that the time changed process
(X,V)
is defined following the standard construction of a PDMP with characteristics
(\Phi,\lambda,Q)=(s\tilde{\Phi},s\tilde{\lambda},\widetilde{Q})
.

Monmarché 2016
, Theorem 1.1]
for the standard ZZP.
We consider a target of the form
\mathrm{U}_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{U}/\varepsilon
, where
\mathrm{U}
is a double well potential, and we characterise the behaviour of the process as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Let
\tau:=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}=x_{1}\},
i.e. the time elapsed before the process finally moves from its initial condition
(X_{0},V_{0})=(x_{0},-1)
to
x_{1}
. In the next proposition we state our result for speed functions of the form
s_{\varepsilon}(x)=s(x)^{1/\varepsilon}
, where
s
is a fixed speed function, satisfying the properties discussed in
Section

3.1
.

Proposition F.1
.

Consider a smooth one-dimensional double well potential
\mathrm{U}>0
with
\mathrm{U}^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})>0
and let
s:\mathbb{R}\to[1,\infty)
be a smooth function. Define
\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(x)=\mathrm{U}(x)-\ln s(x)
and assume
\tilde{\mathrm{U}}
is a double well potential with same maxima and minima of
\mathrm{U}
.
Then the time changed ZZP with target
\mu_{\varepsilon}\propto\exp(-\mathrm{U}/\varepsilon)
and speed function
s_{\varepsilon}(x)=s(x)^{1/\varepsilon}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{(x_{0},-1)}[\tau]\leq\sqrt{\frac{8\mu\varepsilon}{\tilde{\mathrm{U}}^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}}\exp\left(\frac{\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(x_{1})-\tilde{\mathrm{U}}(x_{0})}{\varepsilon}\right)(1+o\left(\varepsilon\right)),

where
o\left(\varepsilon\right)
indicates a function that tends to zero as
\varepsilon
tends to zero.

Proof of Theorem
F.1
.

Let
\tilde{\tau}=r(\tau)
be the first time at which the ZZP with stationary distribution
\propto s_{\varepsilon}\mu_{\varepsilon}=\exp\left\{-\left(U_{\varepsilon}-\ln s_{\varepsilon}\right)\right\}
reaches
x_{1}
. Because
s\geq 1
, we find
\mathbb{E}_{(x_{0},-1)}[\tau]\leq\mathbb{E}_{(x_{0},-1)}[\tilde{\tau}].

Under our assumptions
\mathrm{U}-\ln s
is itself a double well potential and hence we can apply
[
Monmarché 2016
, Theorem 1.1]
to obtain the result.
∎

Remark F.2
.

Let us compare the result of
Proposition

F.1
between the case of the standard ZZP (i.e. choosing
s(x)=1
)
and when
s(x)=\exp(a\mathrm{U}(x))
for
a\in(0,1)
. In the former case we obtain from
[
Monmarché 2016
, Theorem 1.1]

that

\mathbb{E}_{(x_{0},-1)}[\tau]\leq\sqrt{\frac{8\mu\varepsilon}{\mathrm{U}^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{U}(x_{1})-\mathrm{U}(x_{0})}{\varepsilon}\right)(1+o\left(\varepsilon\right)),

while in the latter case we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\tau]\leq\sqrt{\frac{8\mu\varepsilon}{(1-a)\mathrm{U}^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}}\exp\left((1-a)\frac{\mathrm{U}(x_{1})-\mathrm{U}(x_{0})}{\varepsilon}\right)(1+o\left(\varepsilon\right)).

As
\varepsilon
tends to
0
, we see that the upper bound on the hitting time of
x_{1}
of SUZZ increases with a lower rate as that of ZZS, indicating an improved performance of the time-transformed process when targeting a multi-modal distribution with very high density at the modes.
