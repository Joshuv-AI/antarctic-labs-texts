The infinitesimal structure of manifolds with non-continuous Riemannian metrics
Vanessa Ryborz

Abstract
This paper investigates the failure of certain metric measure spaces to be infinitesimally Hilbertian or quasi-Riemannian manifolds, by constructing examples arising from a manifold M M endowed with a Riemannian metric g g that is possibly discontinuous, with g , g − 1 ∈ L loc ∞ g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}} and g ∈ W loc 1 , p g\in W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}} for p < dim M − 1 p<\dim M-1 . † † footnotetext: MSC2020: 53C20, 53C23 Keywords . Riemannian manifolds of low regularity, metric measure spaces, infinitesimal Hilbertianity

Introduction

Manifolds with (semi)-Riemannian metrics of low regularity (in particular below
C^{2}
) have been of growing interest and importance in the field of geometric analysis. They may arise from gluing constructions, or in the case of Lorentzian signature
(-,+,\ldots,+)
, serve to understand physically relevant phenomena from general relativity (see for instance
[
5
]
).

One approach to generalise Riemannian manifolds is to drop any underlying smoothness assumption and study metric measure spaces. A metric measure space is a triplet
(X,\mathsf{d},\mathfrak{m})
, where
(X,\mathsf{d})
is a complete and separable metric space and
\mathfrak{m}
is a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure on
X
(playing the role of the volume measure). For a similar generalisation of Lorentzian manifolds see
[
22
,
6
]
.

Various concepts from Riemannian geometry can be generalised to metric measure spaces such as synthetic notions of the Ricci curvature bounded below by
K
and the dimension bounded above by
N
for some
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in[1,\infty]
; the curvature dimension condition
\mathsf{CD}(K,N)
defined by Lott-Villani
[
25
,
24
]
and Sturm
[
35
,
36
]
.

In this paper however, we will focus on the generalised first order calculus on metric measure spaces.
It emerged through the search for a criterion on metric measure spaces that distinguishes those arising from Finslerian structures from metric measure spaces arising from Riemannian structures. This lead to the study of Sobolev calculus on metric measure spaces. After the approaches from Cheeger
[
7
]
, Hajłasz
[
19
]
, and Shanmungalingam
[
32
]
, Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré introduced a Sobolev calculus based on the notion of test plans in
[
2
]
that they proved to be equivalent to the approaches in
[
7
]
and
[
32
]
, leading to the definition of the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(X)
, and a Laplace operator.

In
[
14
]
, Gigli studied conditions under which the Laplacian is linear and proposed the notion of
infinitesimal Hilbertianity
. A metric measure space is infinitesimally Hilbertian, if the associated Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(X)
is a Hilbert space, or equivalently if the Cheeger energy (a generalisation of the Dirichlet energy to metric measure spaces) is a quadratic form. Then the Laplacian is automatically linear and the theory on Dirichlet forms provides various tools to better understand the Laplacian and the heat flow.

Examples of infinitesimally Hilbertian metric measure spaces include geodesically complete weighted Riemannian manifolds
[
26
]
, locally
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
-spaces equipped with a positive and locally finite Radon measure
[
11
]
, and sub-Riemannian manifolds equipped with an arbitrary Radon measure
[
23
]
.
Relevant notions to study the Cheeger energy, are the metric speed of absolutely continuous curves (
[
1
]
,
[
21
]
) and the slope of Lipschitz continuous functions.
In
[
3
]
, Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré proposed the Riemannian curvature dimension condition,
\mathsf{RCD}(K,\infty)
, a refinement of the curvature dimension condition which combines the
\mathsf{CD}(K,\infty)
-condition with infinitesimal Hilbertianity.
The
\mathsf{RCD}(K,N)
-condition for
N<\infty
was proposed by Gigli in
[
13
]
.
Using this terminology, an earlier result obtained by Ohta and Sturm in
[
30
]
states that
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\norm{\cdot},\mathcal{L}^{n})
is an
\mathsf{RCD}(0,N)
-space if and only if
\norm{\cdot}
arises from an inner product, hence in that case infinitesimal Hilbertianity indeed rules out Riemannian structures among Finslerian ones.

A smooth
d
-manifold
M
, equipped with a continuous Riemannian metric
g
can naturally be seen as a metric measure space, where the measure is induced by the volume form (locally given via
{\rm d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\sqrt{\det g}\,{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d}
) and the Riemannian distance
\mathsf{d}_{g}
is defined as the infimum over lengths of piecewise smooth curves (see (
2.3
)).

However, there are several relevant classes of Riemannian metrics that are non-continuous, for instance the Geroch-Traschen class of metrics
[
12
]
, i.e. those Riemannian metrics
g
such that
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(M)
and
g\in W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}(M)
. These regularity assumptions are minimal to compute the distributional Ricci curvature on
M
(see
[
18
]
,
[
16
]
) and have been studied in both Riemannian (see for example
[
17
]
) and Lorentzian signature (see
[
33
]
). Moreover, in the context of potential theory for uniformly elliptic operators, the even broader class of (Riemannian) metrics
g
such that
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(M)
has been studied in
[
31
]
,
[
34
]
,
[
29
]
and
[
9
]
.

If the metric tensor is only measurable, Norris (cf.
[
29
]
) and De Cecco-Palmieri (cf.
[
9
]
) defined a distance
\mathsf{d}_{g}
that avoids the difficulty that piecewise smooth curves
\gamma
might lie on a null set where
g
is not well-defined (see Section
3
).

This paper is driven by the following questions, which arise naturally from the results on smooth Riemannian manifolds:

Question 1

(see also
[
10
]
)
.

(Under which regularity assumptions on
g
) does
(M,\mathsf{d}_{g},g)
satisfy that for any Lipschitz continuous function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
its slope equals the norm of its gradient almost everywhere, i.e.
|Df|=\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{|f(y)-f(x)|}{\mathsf{d}_{g}(x,y)}=|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}
almost everywhere?

This question has been posed by De Giorgi in
[
10
]
as the positive answer would be a necessary condition for
(M,\mathsf{d}_{g},g)
to be a quasi-Riemannian manifold, as defined in
[
10
]
, see also Definition
3.6
. A related question:

Question 2
.

(Under which regularity assumptions on
g
) does the metric speed of “almost every” absolutely continuous curves equal the norm of its derivative, i.e.
\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t+h})}{|h|}=|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{g}
for almost every
t\in[0,1]
?

What exactly “almost every” curve refers to is clarified in Section
2
.

Question 3
.

(Under which regularity assumptions on
g
) is
(M,\mathsf{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
infinitesimally Hilbertian?

This particular question refers back to the original purpose of infinitesimal Hilbertianity to identify the Riemannian-like structures among metric measure spaces.

It is already known that assuming
g\in C^{0}
is sufficient for the second and third question to have positive answers (see
[
4
]
,
[
28
]
). Moreover, it is known that for
g\in C^{0}
and
f\in C^{1}
, the slope
|Df|
of
f
equals the norm of its gradient
[
28
]
.
In this paper, we will provide examples showing the necessity of those regularity assumptions.

In Section

.
In Subsection

.
Questions
1
and
2

, where we prove:

Theorem 1
.

For
d\geq 3
,
p\in[1,d-1)
there exists a
d

M
and a Riemannian metric

on
M
such that
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}
,
g\in W^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(M)

There exists a test plan that gives positive measure to curves for which the metric speed is strictly smaller than the norm of the derivative for almost every time

(ii)

|Df|,|Df|_{w}>|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}

Question

, where we prove:

.

For
d\geq 3
,
p\in[1,d-1)
there exists a
d
-dimensional manifold
(M,g)
with

and

Notation

Given a manifold
M
, a Riemannian metric
g
, and a locally Lipschitz continuous function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
,
|Df|
will always denote its slope (with respect to the distance
\mathsf{d}_{g}
),
|Df|_{w}
its minimal weak upper gradient,
\nabla_{g}f
its gradient with respect to
g
and
\nabla_{euc}f
its differential with respect to chosen local coordinates (which will be specified).

Given an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
, we will for almost every
t\in[0,1]
denote its metric speed at time
t
by
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|
, and its derivative at time
t
by
\dot{\gamma}_{t}\in T_{\gamma_{t}}M
. To avoid confusion, if we take the norm of the derivative, we will always write
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{g}
for
\sqrt{g(\dot{\gamma}_{t},\dot{\gamma}_{t})}
and
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{euc}
for
\sqrt{\langle\dot{\gamma}_{t},\dot{\gamma}_{t}\rangle_{euc}}
(given specified local coordinates).

When working in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
e_{i}
denotes the
i
-th unit vector,
\mathcal{L}^{d}
the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure, and
Id
denotes the identity matrix.

For any measure space
(X,\mathfrak{m})
and
\mathfrak{m}
-measurable set
A\subset X
,
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
denotes the characteristic function of
A
.

2
Basic definitions on metric measure spaces

In this section we will recall notions from first order calculus on metric measure spaces and their identification in metric measure spaces that arise from manifolds equipped with an at least continuous Riemannian metric.

2.1
First order calculus on metric measure spaces

We will briefly summarise some tools to define a generalised notion of modulus of gradients and Sobolev functions in metric measure spaces that was introduced by Cheeger in
[
7
]
and further analysed by Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré
[
2
]
. The structure of this section has been inspired by
[
28
]
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be a complete and separable metric space. The slope (or local Lipschitz constant) of a real valued function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle|Df|(x):=\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\mathsf{d}(x,y)},

if
\{x\}
is not isolated, and
0
otherwise.

We endow
(X,\mathsf{d})
with a non-negative
\sigma
-finite Borel measure
\mathfrak{m}
, obtaining the metric measure space
(X,\mathsf{d},\mathfrak{m})
.
Throughout the rest of this work, we assume that there exists a bounded Borel Lipschitz map
V:X\to[0,\infty)
such that (cf.
[
2
, Ch. 4]
)

\begin{split}V\ \mathrm{is\ bounded\ on\ each\ compact\ set\ }K\subset X\ \mathrm{and}\\
\int_{X}e^{-V^{2}}\,{\rm d}\mathfrak{m}\leq 1.\end{split}

(2.1)

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\gamma\in C([0,1],X)
be a curve. Then we say that
\gamma
is absolutely continuous if there exists a function
g\in L^{1}([0,1],[0,\infty))
such that for all
0\leq s<t\leq 1
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(\gamma_{s},\gamma_{t})\leq\int_{s}^{t}g(r)\,{\rm d}r.

There exists a minimal such function
g
, which we call the metric speed of
\gamma
and denote it by
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|
.

The metric speed of an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
is given by
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|:=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{d}(\gamma_{t+h},\gamma_{t})}{|h|}
which exists for almost every
t\in[0,1]

[
1
]
, (see also
[
21
]
).

We next recall the notion of test plan and weak upper gradient. We will use the conventions of
[
15
]
, after
[
2
]
. For any metric space
(Y,\mathsf{d}_{Y})
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}(Y)
the set of Borel probability measures on
Y
. Given two topological spaces
X_{1},X_{2}
, a Borel-measurable map
T:X_{1}\to X_{2}
, and a Borel measure
\mu
on
X_{1}
, the pushforward measure
T_{\#}\mu
on
X_{2}
is the Borel measure defined by
T_{\#}\mu(B)=\mu(T^{-1}(B))
for any Borel set
B\subset X_{2}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,1],X))
. We say that
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is a
test plan
if there exists a constant
C(\boldsymbol{\pi})>0
such that

\displaystyle(e_{t})_{\#}\boldsymbol{\pi}\leq C(\boldsymbol{\pi})\mathfrak{m}\ \mathrm{for\ all\ }t\in[0,1]

and

\displaystyle\int\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{\rm d}t{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma)<\infty.

We use the convention that if
\gamma
is not absolutely continuous, then
\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{\rm d}t=\infty
.

In Question
2
, “almost every absolutely continuous curve” refers to a set of curves
\Gamma\subset C([0,1],M)
such that for every test plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}
as defined above,
\boldsymbol{\pi}(\Gamma)=1
.

Definition 2.3
.

Given
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
a
\mathfrak{m}
-measurable function, then a
\mathfrak{m}
-measurable function
G:X\to[0,\infty]
is called a weak upper gradient of
f
, if

\displaystyle\int|f(\gamma_{1})-f(\gamma_{0})|\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma)\,\leq\int\int_{0}^{1}G(\gamma_{t})|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|\,{\rm d}t{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma)<\infty,\quad\mathrm{for\ all\ test\ plans}\ \boldsymbol{\pi}.

(2.2)

The discussion in
[
2
]
, Prop. 5.9 and Def. 5.11 shows the existence of a weak upper gradient
|Df|_{w}
such that
|Df|_{w}\leq G

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. for all other weak upper gradients
G
. We will call it the
minimal weak upper gradient
of
f
.

Definition 2.4
.

The Cheeger energy is defined in the class of
\mathfrak{m}
-measurable functions by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ch}(f):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}\int_{X}|Df|^{2}_{w}\,{\rm d}\mathfrak{m}&\mathrm{if}\ f\ \mathrm{has\ a\ weak\ upper\ gradient\ in}\ L^{2}(X,\mathfrak{m}),\\
\infty&\mathrm{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

with proper domain
D(\mathrm{Ch})=\{f:X\to[0,\infty],\mathfrak{m}\mathrm{-measurable},\ \mathrm{Ch}(f)<\infty\}
.

Definition 2.5
.

A metric measure space is called
infinitesimally Hilbertian
if
\mathrm{Ch}
is a quadratic form.

2.2
Manifolds with continuous Riemannian metrics

Consider a
d
-dimensional manifold
M
equipped with a Riemannian metric
g\in C^{0}(M)
. Then
g
induces a volume measure whose density is locally given by
{\rm d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\sqrt{\det g}\,{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d}
.
The distance induced by
g
is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{g}(x,y)=\inf\Big\{\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma_{t}}|_{g}\,{\rm d}t:\,\gamma\ \mathrm{piecewise\ }C^{\infty},\gamma_{0}=x,\gamma_{1}=y\Big\}.

(2.3)

In
[
4
]
the metric structure for such spaces
(M,\mathsf{d}_{g})
has been thoroughly studied. In
[
28
]
, the Cheeger energy and related notions were studied and expressed in terms of classical Sobolev spaces on manifolds (cf.
[
20
]
). We will recall some of these results to point out that in the case of a Riemannian manifold with a continuous metric, several notions from Subsection
2.1
can be identified with expected classical objects from calculus on manifolds.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
4
]
Prop. 4.1 and Thm 3.15)
.

The metric (
2.3
) turns
M
into a length space.

A standard fact on the slope of functions is that if
f\in C^{1}(M)
, then
|Df|(x)=|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}(x)
for every
x
.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
4
]
, Proposition 4.10)
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
be an absolutely continuous curve. Then the metric speed
|\dot{\gamma_{t}}|
coincides a.e. with
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{g}=\sqrt{\langle\dot{\gamma}_{t},\dot{\gamma}_{t}\rangle_{g}}
, where
\dot{\gamma}_{t}
denotes the (a.e. existing) derivative.

In the case of a manifold with a continuous Riemannian metric, it also turns out that the minimal weak upper gradient of a
C^{1}
-function
f
can be identified as follows:

Proposition 2.8

(
[
28
]
Prop. 4.24)
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold,
g
a
C^{0}
-Riemannian metric on
M
.
Let
f
be a
C^{1}
-function. Then

\displaystyle|Df|_{w}(x)=|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}(x),\quad{\text{for $\mathrm{vol}_{g}$-a.e. x.}}

That was a crucial ingredient to conclude that:

Proposition 2.9

(
[
28
]
, Cor. 4.27)
.

For a manifold
M
equipped with a continuous Riemannian metric
g
, the metric measure space
(M,\mathsf{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
is infinitesimally Hilbertian.

3
The distance on manifolds with non-continuous Riemannian metrics

From now on we will consider a smooth
d
-dimensional manifold
M
with a Riemannian metric
g
such that
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}
.
Then
(M,g)
falls into the class of LIP-Riemannian manifolds (see for example
[
37
,
9
]
). These manifolds and their metric structures, have been studied by Norris
[
29
]
, and De Cecco and Palmieri
[
9
]
, as well as Saloff-Coste
[
31
]
and Sturm
[
34
]
with emphasis on potential theory for uniformly elliptic operators.
Note that LIP-manifolds shall not be confused with metric tensors whose coefficients are locally Lipschitz continuous.

If
g
is only measurable, it might not be well-defined everywhere, hence the definition of the metric
\mathsf{d}_{g}
from the continuous case (
2.3
), cannot be used for non-continuous Riemannian metrics.
However, Norris
[
29
]
and De Cecco-Palmieri
[
9
]
gave a definition of a distance
\mathsf{d}_{g}
on
M
arising from
g
that avoids this difficulty. We will briefly summarise their results and then study the metric speed of curves, test plans, and the Cheeger energy on the corresponding metric measure space.

In the case of a Riemannian manifold with
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}
, Norris defines the distance
\mathsf{d}
via

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(x,y):=\sup\left\{\left[w(x)-w(y)\right],\ w\mathrm{\ is\ Lipschitz\ continuous\ and\ }|\nabla_{g}w|_{g}\leq 1\ \mathrm{a.e.}\right\}.

Moreover, for
\rho\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{1}^{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0),[0,\infty))
, with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho\,{\rm d}x=1
, define
\rho_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})
and
g_{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\varepsilon}*g
(for details on the convolution on manifolds see
[
18
,
16
]
). The metrics
g_{\varepsilon}
are continuous and hence the distance
\mathsf{d}_{g_{\varepsilon}}
is given as in (
2.3
). Norris then proves that for
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\mathsf{d}_{g_{\varepsilon}}
converges to a distance
\mathsf{d}_{0}
that is locally equivalent to the underlying Euclidean metric induced from a coordinate chart; in particular,
[
29
, Theorem 3.6]
states that
\mathsf{d}_{0}=\mathsf{d}=:\mathsf{d}_{g}
.

From the properties of
g
, it follows that all points outside a null set
N_{g}\subset M
are Lebesgue points of
g,g^{-1}
. For any null set
N\subset M
, define the set

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}_{N}(x,y,M):=\{\gamma\in C^{0,1}_{\rm{loc}}([0,1],M):\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y,\mathcal{L}^{1}(\{t\in[0,1]:\gamma(t)\in N\})=0\}.

(3.1)

If
\gamma\in C([0,1],M)
satisfies
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\{t\in[0,1]:\gamma(t)\in N\})=0
, we say that
\gamma
is transversal to
N
and write
\gamma\perp N
.

The reference measure is, as in the continuous case, given by
{\rm d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\sqrt{\det g}{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d}
.
From now on, we will make a further assumption (see
[
9
, (1.13)]
):

Assumption 3.1
.

The manifold
(M,g)
satisfies one of the following:

(i)

The manifold
M
has finite
\mathrm{vol}_{g}
-volume.

(ii)

We can cover
M
with coordinate charts such that the smallest eigenvalue of
g
is uniformly bounded below in these charts and
M
is complete or the interior of a complete manifold (where the completeness is with respect to the distance induced by charts).

Finally, De Cecco and Palmieri proved in
[
9
, Theorem (2.18) and Theorem (6.1)]
that under Assumption
3.1
, it holds
\mathsf{d}_{g}=\boldsymbol{\delta}
, where the distance
\boldsymbol{\delta}
is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}(x,y)=\sup_{N\subset M,\mathrm{vol}_{g}(N)=0}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{N}(x,y),

(3.2)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{N}:=\inf\{L_{g}(\gamma),\gamma\in\mathrm{Lip}_{N}(x,y,M)\},

(3.3)

and
L_{g}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{g}{\rm d}t
.
In
[
8
, Theorem 4.4]
, it is shown that for each null set
N_{0}\subset M
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}:=\sup_{N,\mathrm{vol}_{g}(N)=0}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{N\cup N_{0}}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
be a
\mathsf{d}_{g}
-geodesic such that
\gamma\perp N_{g}
. Then, for almost every
t\in[0,1]
, it holds

\displaystyle|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|\leq|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{g}.

Proof.

For a Lipschitz curve
\gamma\perp N_{g}
, and any
t\in[0,1]
,
h\neq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t+h})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathsf{d}_{g_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t+h})\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{h}|\dot{\gamma}_{t+\tau}|_{g_{\varepsilon}}\,{\rm d}\tau=\int_{0}^{h}|\dot{\gamma}_{t+\tau}|_{g}\,{\rm d}\tau,

where the last equality follows from the dominated convergence theorem and the fact that
\rho_{\varepsilon}*g\to g
pointwise outside
N_{g}
. Now the characterisation
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t+h})}{|h|}
a.e. together with the Lebesgue differentiation theorem yields the proposition.
∎

There are more observations that can be made on the metric speed of absolutely continuous curves. The first one is that the metric speed depends on the derivative of the curve.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold and
g
a Riemannian metric such that
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}
.
Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
be a Lipschitz continuous curve and
t\in[0,1]
such that the metric speed
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|
exists at
t
and
\gamma
is differentiable at
t
. Let
c:[0,1]\to M
be another curve such that
c_{t}=\gamma_{t}
,
c
is differentiable at
t
and
\dot{c}_{t}=\dot{\gamma}_{t}
. Then the metric speed of
|\dot{c}_{t}|
of
c
at
t
exists and
|\dot{c}_{t}|=|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|
.

Proof.

We work in local coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and write
\gamma_{t}=c_{t}=:p
and
\dot{c}_{t}=\dot{\gamma}_{t}=v
. Furthermore, we may restrict to a small neighbourhood
U
around
p
and assume that there exist
\lambda,\Lambda>0
such that
\lambda Id\leq g\leq\Lambda Id
. Then, by the definition of the derivative, we get that

\displaystyle\gamma_{t+h}=p+hv+a_{\gamma}(h),\ c_{t+h}=p+hv+a_{c}(h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{|a_{\gamma}(h)|_{euc}+|a_{c}(h)|_{euc}}{|h|}=0.

(3.4)

Now, we can compute that

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma_{t+h},c_{t+h})\leq\sqrt{\Lambda}(|a_{\gamma}(h)|_{euc}+|a_{c}(h)|_{euc}),

(3.5)

and hence

\displaystyle 0\leq\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma_{t+h},c_{t+h})}{|h|}\leq\lim_{h\to 0}\sqrt{\Lambda}\frac{|a_{\gamma}(h)|_{euc}+|a_{c}(h)|_{euc}}{|h|}=0.

But then

\displaystyle\limsup_{h\to 0}\bigg|\frac{\mathsf{d}_{g}(c_{t},c_{t+h})}{|h|}-\frac{\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t+h})}{|h|}\bigg|\leq\limsup_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma_{t+h},c_{t+h})}{|h|}=0.

∎

Next, we establish that the metric speed depends locally Lipschitz-continuously on the derivative of a curve.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2}:[0,1]\to M
be two Lipschitz continuous curves that are contained in a compact set
K
inside one coordinate patch
V
and
t\in[0,1]
such that
\gamma^{1}_{t}=\gamma^{2}_{t}
and such that the metric speeds
|\dot{\gamma}^{1}_{t}|
and
|\dot{\gamma}^{2}_{t}|
as well as their derivatives
\dot{\gamma}^{1}_{t},\dot{\gamma}^{2}_{t}\in T_{\gamma^{1}_{t}}M
exist. Then there exists a constant
C=C(K)
such that

\displaystyle||\dot{\gamma}^{1}_{t}|-|\dot{\gamma}^{2}_{t}||\leq C|\dot{\gamma}^{1}_{t}-\dot{\gamma}^{2}_{t}|_{g}.

(3.6)

Proof.

We may assume to be working in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Now there exist
\lambda,\Lambda>0
such that
\lambda Id\leq g\leq\Lambda Id
on
K
.
Denote
v:=\dot{\gamma}^{1}_{t}
and
w:=\dot{\gamma}^{2}_{t}
. By translation we may assume that
t=0
and denote
p:=\gamma^{1}_{0}=\gamma^{2}_{0}
.
By the previous proposition, we may assume that
\gamma^{1}_{t}=p+tv
and
\gamma^{2}_{t}=p+tw
. But then

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma^{1}_{0},\gamma^{1}_{h})}{|h|}-\frac{\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma^{2}_{0},\gamma^{2}_{h})}{|h|}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{d}_{g}(p+hv,p+hw)}{|h|}\leq\sqrt{\Lambda}\frac{\mathsf{d}_{euc}(p+hv,p+hw)}{|h|}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Lambda}|v-w|_{euc}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}}|v-w|_{g}=\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}}|\dot{\gamma}^{1}_{t}-\dot{\gamma}^{2}_{t}|_{g}.

∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,1],M))
be a test plan and
N\subset M
a
\mathrm{vol}_{g}
-null set. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}\Big(\{\gamma\in C([0,1],M):\gamma\perp N\}\Big)=1.

(3.7)

Proof.

We argue by contradiction. Assume that
\boldsymbol{\pi}\Big(\{\gamma\in C([0,1],M):\gamma\perp N\}\Big)<1
.
Then as

\displaystyle\{\gamma\in C([0,1],M):\gamma\not\perp N\}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{\gamma\in C([0,1],M):\mathcal{L}^{1}(\gamma^{-1}(N))\geq\frac{1}{n}\},

there exists an
n\in\mathbb{N}
and an
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}(\{\gamma\in C([0,1],M):\mathcal{L}^{1}(\gamma^{-1}(N))\geq\frac{1}{n}\})=\varepsilon>0.

(3.8)

Denote
\{\gamma\in C([0,1],M):\mathcal{L}^{1}(\gamma^{-1}(N))\geq\frac{1}{n}\}=:B_{n}
and note that for each curve
\gamma\in B_{n}
, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}_{N}(\gamma_{t})\,{\rm d}t\geq\frac{1}{n},

(3.9)

hence, with Fubini’s theorem, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{C([0,1],M)}\mathbbm{1}_{N}(\gamma_{t})\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma){\rm d}t=\int_{C([0,1],M)}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}_{N}(\gamma_{t})\,{\rm d}t{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{n}>0.

(3.10)

By the definition of a test plan, we have that for all
t\in[0,1]
it holds
(e_{t})_{\#}\boldsymbol{\pi}\ll\mathrm{vol}_{g}
.
As
N
is a
{\rm d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}
-null set, we get that for all
t\in[0,1]
, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{M}\mathbbm{1}_{N}(y)\,{\rm d}(e_{t})_{\#}\boldsymbol{\pi}(y)=0.

That gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{C([0,1],M)}\mathbbm{1}_{N}(\gamma_{t})\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma){\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{M}\mathbbm{1}_{N}(y)\,{\rm d}(e_{t})_{\#}\boldsymbol{\pi}(y){\rm d}t=0,

which contradicts (
3.10
). This proves the Lemma.
∎

We conclude this section with the definition of a quasi-Riemannian manifold proposed by De Giorgi in
[
10
]
, that we will refer to later:

Definition 3.6
.

Given a manifold
M
, a distance
\mathsf{d}
on
M
, and a measurable positive definite inner product
g
on
TM
, we
call
(M,\mathsf{d},g)
a quasi-Riemannian manifold if there exists an atlas
\mathcal{A}=\{(W,\psi)\}
such that the following conditions hold:

(i)

In each
(W,\psi)
, there exist constants
\lambda,\Lambda>0
such that for any
x,y\in\psi(W)
, it holds

\displaystyle\lambda\leq\sum_{i,j}(\psi_{*}g)^{ij}\partial_{x^{i}}\mathsf{d}(\psi^{-1}(x),\psi^{-1}(y))\partial_{x^{j}}\mathsf{d}(\psi^{-1}(x),\psi^{-1}(y))\leq\Lambda.

(ii)

For any
f\in C^{0}_{c}(W)
, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{W}f{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\mathsf{d}}=\int_{W}f\sqrt{\det g}\,{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d},

where
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\mathsf{d}}
denotes the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure with respect to the distance
\mathsf{d}
.

(iii)

For any Lipschitz continuous
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
,
and almost every
x\in M
it holds

\displaystyle\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\mathsf{d}(x,y)}=|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}.

4
Construction of examples

In this section, we will construct Riemannian manifolds with non-continuous metrics that together with the distance
\mathsf{d}_{g}
as defined in the previous section, do not give rise to quasi-Riemannian manifolds or infinitesimally Hilbertian metric measure spaces.

4.1
A first example of a non-quasi Riemannian manifold

We will now construct a metric
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}
and such that there exists a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
|Df|_{w},|Df|>|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}
on a set of positive measure.

Let
q_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
be a counting of
\mathbb{Q}
. Pick a
\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{8})
and construct the following open set

\displaystyle O:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big(q_{i}-\frac{\kappa}{2^{i}},q_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{2^{i}}\Big).

(4.1)

Then
\mathcal{L}^{1}(O)\leq 2\kappa
and
O
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
. We now define

\displaystyle\theta:=(2-\mathbbm{1}_{O})\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=\theta(x_{1})Id\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}).

Then
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g)
satisfies (ii) from Assumption
3.1
and
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathsf{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
satisfies condition (
2.1
).
We observe that

\displaystyle(2-2\kappa)\leq\int_{(0,1)}\theta\,{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\mathcal{L}^{1}\Big(\big\{\theta<\frac{3}{2}\big\}\cap(0,1)\Big)+2\mathcal{L}^{1}\Big(\big\{\theta\geq\frac{3}{2}\big\}\cap(0,1)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\Big(1-\mathcal{L}^{1}\Big(\big\{\theta\geq\frac{3}{2}\big\}\cap(0,1)\Big)\Big)+2\mathcal{L}^{1}\Big(\big\{\theta\geq\frac{3}{2}\big\}\cap(0,1)\Big).

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{1}\Big(\big\{\theta\geq\frac{3}{2}\big\}\cap(0,1)\Big)\geq 1-4\kappa\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(4.2)

Now for any point
y\in(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
consider the curve
\gamma^{y}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d},t\mapsto y+te_{2}
.

Claim.

For all
t\in(0,1)
,
h\in(0,1-t)
it holds

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma^{y}_{t},\gamma^{y}_{t+h})=h.

(4.3)

Proof of the claim.
Fix
t,h
as above and fix an
\varepsilon>0
. Note that from
g\geq Id
, we get that
\mathsf{d}_{g}\geq\mathsf{d}_{euc}
, hence
\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma^{y}_{t},\gamma^{y}_{t+h})\geq h
. Thus, it remains to prove that
\mathsf{d}_{g}(\gamma^{y}_{t},\gamma^{y}_{t+h})\leq h
. There exists a
q\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
such that
|x_{1}(y)-q|\leq\varepsilon
. Let
i\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
q_{i}=q
in the chosen counting. Then define the curves

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d},s\mapsto\gamma^{y}_{t}+s(q-x_{1}(y))e_{1},

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}:[0,h]\to\mathbb{R}^{d},s\mapsto\gamma^{y}_{t}+(q-x_{1}(y))e_{1}+se_{2},

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d},s\mapsto\gamma^{y}_{t}+(q-x_{1}(y))e_{1}+he_{2}-s(q-x_{1}(y))e_{1}.

Denote by
\lambda
the concatenation of those three curves and note that it yields a piecewise smooth curve that connects
\gamma^{y}_{t}
and
\gamma^{y}_{t+h}
.
Now, for all
\varsigma>0
, it holds
g_{\varsigma}=g*\rho_{\varsigma}\leq 2Id
, so we get that
L_{g_{\varsigma}}(\lambda_{1})+L_{g_{\varsigma}}(\lambda_{3})\leq 4\varepsilon
.
For
\varsigma\leq\frac{\kappa}{2^{i+2}}
that
g_{\varsigma}|_{\{x_{1}=q\}}\equiv Id
, hence
L_{g_{\varsigma}}(\lambda_{2})=h
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varsigma\to 0}\mathsf{d}_{g_{\varsigma}}(\gamma^{y}_{t},\gamma^{y}_{t+h})\leq\lim_{\varsigma\to 0}L_{g_{\varsigma}}(\lambda)\leq h+4\varepsilon.

As
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, this proves the claim.

By (
4.2
), we can choose a set
E^{\prime}\subset\{\theta\geq\frac{3}{2}\big\}\cap(0,1)
such that
\mathcal{L}^{1}(E^{\prime})>0
. Denote
E:=E^{\prime}\times(0,1)^{d-1}
. Define the test plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}(C^{\infty}((0,1),\mathbb{R}^{d}))
via

\displaystyle{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(E)}{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d}(y)&\mathrm{if}\ \gamma=\gamma^{y},\ \mathrm{for\ some}\ y\in E,\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Note that this is indeed a feasible test plan, as
{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d}\leq{\rm d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}\leq 2^{\frac{d}{2}}{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d}
.
But now for all
y\in E
, almost all
t\in[0,1]
, we get that
g(\gamma^{y}_{t})\geq\frac{3}{2}Id
and
\dot{\gamma}^{y}_{t}=e_{2}
and
|\dot{\gamma}^{y}_{t}|_{g}\geq\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}
. But by (
4.3
), we get that the metric speed
|\dot{\gamma}^{y}_{t}|=1
for all
y,t
, hence we found a test plan that gives positive measure to curves whose metric speed and the norm of the derivative are not equal.

We will now give an example of a function
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for which the norm of the gradient is strictly smaller than the minimal weak upper gradient and the metric slope on a set of points with positive measure. The function is given by
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\mapsto x_{2}
. Then for all
y\in E
, we have that
|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}(y)\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}
. But now for any point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that

\displaystyle|Df|(y)=\limsup_{z\to y}\frac{|f(z)-f(y)|}{\mathsf{d}_{g}(z,y)}\geq\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{|f(y+he_{2})-f(y)|}{\mathsf{d}_{g}(y+he_{2},y)}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{|h|}{|h|}=1>\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}.

(4.4)

This shows that for
y\in E^{\prime}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, it holds
|Df|(y)>|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}(y)
.

Finally, the test plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}
shows, that
|Df|_{w}>|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}
on a set of positive measure. Indeed, we note that

\displaystyle\int_{C([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})}|f(\gamma_{1})-f(\gamma_{0})|\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma)=\int_{E}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(E)}\,{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d}=1.

On the other hand, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{C([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla_{g}f(\gamma_{t})|_{g}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|\,{\rm d}t{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma)\leq\int_{E}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(E)}\,{\rm d}t{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}.

Hence, we have proved the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

For
d\geq 2
, there exists a Riemannian metric
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}
and such that

(i)

There exists a test plan that gives positive measure to curves for which the metric speed is strictly smaller than the norm of the derivative for almost every time
t\in[0,1]
.

(ii)

There exists a function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for which the minimal weak upper gradient and the metric slope (with respect to
\mathsf{d}_{g}
) are strictly larger than the norm of the gradient on a set of positive measure, i.e.
|Df|,|Df|_{w}>|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}
on a set of positive
\mathrm{vol}_{g}
-measure.

4.2
A first example of non-infinitesimal Hilbertianity

In this subsection, we will more closely analyse the infinitesimal structure of a manifold similar to the previous example.
This following example metric resembles the previous one, with the sole difference, that we only perturb the metric inside the interior of the unit cube
(0,1)^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and leave it constant outside that set. We will prove that the resulting metric measure space is not infinitesimally Hilbertian.

Let
q_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
be a counting of
\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
,
\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{8})
and define

\displaystyle O:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big(q_{i}-\frac{\kappa}{2^{i}},q_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{2^{i}}\Big).

(4.5)

Then
\mathcal{L}^{1}(O)\leq 2\kappa
and
O
is dense in
(0,1)
. Define

\displaystyle\theta:=(2-\mathbbm{1}_{O})\in L^{\infty}((0,1)),

\displaystyle g:=2\mathbbm{1}_{((0,1)^{d})^{c}}Id+\mathbbm{1}_{(0,1)^{d}}\theta(x_{1})Id\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}).

Note that
(\mathbb{R}^{d},g)
satisfies (ii) from Assumption
3.1
,
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathsf{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
satisfies (
2.1
), and
g
is constant outside the precompact set
(0,1)^{d}
. Inside
(0,1)^{d}
,
g
the metric space arising from
(M,g)
will behave just as in the previous subsection, we only restrict this behaviour to a compact set to conclude the lack of infinitesimal Hilbertianity.

To see that we will first quantitatively determine the difference between the metric speed of curves and the norms of their derivatives.

We start with a preparatory lemma:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz continuous function and
a<b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{f^{-1}((a,b))}f^{\prime}\,{\rm d}t\in[a-b,b-a].

(4.6)

Proof.

We define the function
h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle h(t):=\max(a,\min(f(t),b)).

Then
h
is Lipschitz continuous and admits a weak derivative
h^{\prime}
. Moreover, in the open set
f^{-1}((a,b))\subset[0,1]
, we have that
f=h
, hence
h^{\prime}=f^{\prime}
almost everywhere in
f^{-1}((a,b))
.
Now, on
f^{-1}((-\infty,a])
we have that
h\equiv a
.

Claim.
For almost every
t\in f^{-1}((-\infty,a])
, it holds
h^{\prime}=0
.

Proof of the claim.
Indeed, choose a Lebesgue point
t
of both
f^{-1}((-\infty,a])
and
h^{\prime}
and suppose for a contradiction that
h^{\prime}(t)\neq 0
.
Then there exists a neighbourhood
U\ni t
such that
h(s)\neq h(t)
for every
s\in U\setminus\{t\}
.
But as
t
is a Lebesgue point of
f^{-1}((-\infty,a])
, we have that
(f^{-1}((-\infty,a])\cap U)\setminus\{t\}\neq\emptyset
. Hence, there exists a point in
s\in f^{-1}((-\infty,a])
such that
h(s)\neq h(t)
, which contradicts the definition of
h
. As those Lebesgue points have full measure, this proves the claim.

We similarly get that on
f^{-1}([b,\infty))
, it holds
h^{\prime}\equiv 0
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{f^{-1}((a,b))}f^{\prime}\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{1}h^{\prime}\,{\rm d}t=h(1)-h(0).

(4.7)

The lemma follows as
h(0),h(1)\in[a,b]
.
∎

By Proposition
3.3
, we get that for any curve
\gamma
and
t\in[0,1]
such that
\gamma_{t}\in O\times(0,1)^{d-1}\cup((0,1)^{d})^{c}
and such that
\gamma
is differentiable at
t
, it holds
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|=|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{g(\gamma_{t})}
.

Next suppose that
y\in(0,1)^{d}
is a Lebesgue point of
\{g=2\}
.
In this argument, we will work with the definition of the metric
\mathsf{d}_{g}
via (
3.2
).
We will at first determine the shape and length of an almost distance minimising curve between two points that are close to each other. Fix an
\varepsilon>0
. Then, by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, there exists a
\delta>0
such that for each open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
with
x_{1}(y)\in I
, and
\mathcal{L}^{1}(I)\leq\delta
, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}^{1}(I)}\int_{I}|\theta-2|\,{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{1}\leq\varepsilon.

(4.8)

Fix the null set
N^{(1)}_{g}\subset\mathbb{R}
as the set of non-Lebesgue points of
\theta
and define
N_{g}:=N^{(1)}_{g}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. We may assume that
O\cap N^{(1)}_{g}=\emptyset
.

Let
z\in(0,1)^{d}
be such that
\mathsf{d}_{euc}(y,z)\leq\frac{1}{8}\mathrm{dist}_{euc}(y,\partial(0,1)^{d})
. Then any
2
-shortest curve connecting them, i.e. any curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
from
y
to
z
such that
\gamma\perp N_{g}
and
L_{g}(\gamma)\leq 2\mathsf{d}_{g}(y,z)
, has to lie inside
(0,1)^{d}
. Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to(0,1)^{d}
be a Lipschitz continuous curve transversal to
N_{g}
such that
\gamma(0)=y
and
\gamma(1)=z
.
We will now estimate
\mathsf{d}_{g}(y,z)
depending on
z-y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We assume that
x_{1}(z)\geq x_{1}(y)
and
|x_{1}(z)-x_{1}(y)|<\delta
. The case
x_{1}(z)\leq x_{1}(y)
is analogous.

Step 1:
Reduction to curves with uniformly bounded
x_{1}
coordinates.

Pick
a_{y}\leq x_{1}(y)
and
a_{z}\geq x_{1}(z)
such that
a_{y},a_{z}\in O\cap(0,1)
, and

\displaystyle a_{z}-a_{y}\leq\delta,\ x_{1}(y)-a_{y}\leq\varepsilon,\ a_{z}-x_{1}(z)\leq\varepsilon,a_{z}-a_{y}\leq 2|z-y|_{euc}.

(4.9)

Now define
\tilde{\gamma}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
via

\displaystyle x_{1}(\tilde{\gamma})(t)=\max(a_{y},\min(a_{z},x_{1}(\gamma_{t}))),

\displaystyle x_{i}(\tilde{\gamma})(t)=x_{i}({\gamma}_{t})\ \mathrm{for}\ i=2,\ldots,d.

(4.10)

Then
\tilde{\gamma}
is Lipschitz continuous and transversal to
N_{g}
. Indeed, on
(x_{1}\circ\gamma)^{-1}((-\infty,a_{y}]\cup[a_{z},\infty))
, we get that
\tilde{\gamma}\in\{a_{y},a_{z}\}\times(0,1)^{d-1}\subset N^{c}_{g}\cap\{g\equiv Id\}
and on

, we have that
\gamma=\tilde{\gamma}
. Note that the methods from the proof of Lemma

yield that

\displaystyle|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}|_{euc}\leq|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{euc}\ \mathrm{a.e.\ on}\ [0,1].

(4.11)

Now, we can compute

\displaystyle L_{g}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]}\sqrt{\theta(x_{1}(\gamma_{t}))}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{euc}\,{\rm d}t

Now we observe that for all points

Proposition 4.6
.

The metric measure space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathsf{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})

Proof.

\displaystyle|D\tilde{f}_{i}|_{w}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|\nabla_{euc}\tilde{f}_{i}|_{euc}\ \mathrm{in}\ ((-1,2)^{d})^{c}.

More precisely, this gives

\displaystyle|D\tilde{f}_{i}|_{w}=|\nabla_{g}\tilde{f}_{i}|_{g}\ \mathrm{a.e.\ in}\ (O\times(0,1)^{d-1})\cup((0,1)^{d})^{c},

(4.42)

and

\displaystyle|D\tilde{f}_{1}|_{w}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\ \mathrm{a.e.\ in}\ (0,1)^{d}\setminus O\times(0,1)^{d-1},

\displaystyle|D\tilde{f}_{2}|_{w}=|D\tilde{f}_{3}|_{w}=|D\tilde{f}_{4}|_{w}=1\ \mathrm{a.e.\ in}\ (0,1)^{d}\setminus(O\times(0,1)^{d-1}).

Moreover, we have that
\tilde{f}_{3}=\tilde{f}_{1}+\tilde{f}_{2}
and

and all four functions are in

\displaystyle=2n\mathrm{vol}_{g}(S\times(0,1))+\int_{(-3,3)^{d-1}\times((-2,-1)\cup(1,2))}2|Df_{1,1}|^{2}_{w}+2|Df_{2,1}|^{2}_{w}-|Df_{3,1}|^{2}_{w}-|Df_{4,1}|^{2}_{w}\,{\rm d}\mathrm{vol}_{g},

where in the last two steps, we first used the fact that
2|\nabla_{g}f_{1,n}|_{g}^{2}+2|\nabla_{g}f_{2,n}|_{g}^{2}-|\nabla_{g}f_{3,n}|_{g}^{2}-|\nabla_{g}f_{4,n}|_{g}^{2}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and then applied (
4.92
).
We have that
|Df_{i,1}|^{2}_{w}\in L^{\infty}
and hence the last integral in the above equation is finite, so as
\mathrm{vol}_{g}(S\times(0,1))>0
, we get that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}2\mathrm{Ch}(f_{1,n})+2\mathrm{Ch}(f_{2,n})-\mathrm{Ch}(f_{3,n})-\mathrm{Ch}(f_{4,n})\infty.

(4.93)

In particular, there exists an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2\mathrm{Ch}(f_{1,n})+2\mathrm{Ch}(f_{2,n})-\mathrm{Ch}(f_{3,n})-\mathrm{Ch}(f_{4,n})\neq 0
.
That shows:

Proposition 4.12
.

The metric measure space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathsf{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
is not infinitesimally Hilbertian.

This proves Theorem
2
.

Remark 4.13
.

For
d\geq 4
, the above example shows that we can find a Riemannian metric
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the Geroch-Traschen class that induces a non-infinitesimally Hilbertian metric measure space.

Remark 4.14
.

For a manifold with a
W^{1,p}_{\rm loc}
-Riemannian metric such that
p>d
, the Sobolev embedding theorem together with the results from
[
28
]
immediately give us that
(M,\mathsf{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
is infinitesimally Hilbertian. Theorem
2
shows that
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}\cap W^{1,p}_{\rm loc}
for
p<d-1
is not sufficient for infinitesimal Hilbertianity. This works towards answering Question
3
leaving only the interval of
p\in[d-1,d]
, where it is unknown whether
g,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}\cap W^{1,p}_{\rm loc}
is sufficient to get infinitesimal Hilbertianity.

Remark 4.15
.

One motivation behind the definition of infinitesimal Hilbertianity was to distinguish Finslerian structures from Riemannian ones, in particular in the context of synthetic lower Ricci curvature bounds. The distance in this example arises from a Riemannian metric of low regularity; however the corresponding metric measure space does not satisfy infinitesimal Hilbertianity.
The directional dependence of the metric speed of curves established in (
4.3.2
) reveals the asymptotic shape of metric balls in the manifold and it is readily seen that the space
(M,\mathsf{d}_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
is not locally Minkowskian (see
[
30
]
and
[
27
]
for a discussion of infinitesimal Hilbertianity for locally Minkowskian spaces).
Moreover, the construction immediately implies that this space does not satisfy neither distributional lower Ricci curvature bounds nor the
\mathsf{CD}(K,\infty)
-condition for any
K\in\mathbb{R}
.
