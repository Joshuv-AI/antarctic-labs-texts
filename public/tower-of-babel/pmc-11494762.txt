Effect of baseline anemia on the efficacy of docetaxel and ramucirumab for advanced non-small cell lung cancer treatment
Authors: Yoshitaka Saito, Yoh Takekuma, Jun Sakakibara-Konishi, Yasushi Shimizu, Ichiro Kinoshita, Mitsuru Sugawara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDocetaxel (DOC) and ramucirumab (RAM) is one of the most effective regimens for advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treatment. In our previous study, baseline anemia was identified as a preventive factor against the development of severe adverse effects during the first treatment cycle. It was hypothesized that anemia directly promotes tumor angiogenesis, leading to the elevation of RAM efficacy with increased DOC delivery to tumors, while reducing DOC delivery to other organs, potentially mitigating severe adverse effects. If this hypothesis is correct, patients with baseline anemia may have better clinical outcomes than those with normal hemoglobin levels. In this study, we aimed to investigate the effect of baseline anemia on the efficacy of DOC + RAM in treating advanced NSCLC in a real-word setting.MethodsPatients with advanced NSCLC receiving DOC + RAM (n = 72) were retrospectively assessed. They were categorized into a control group with normal baseline hemoglobin levels and an anemia group with baseline anemia. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS) evaluation.ResultsPatients in the anemia group had a significantly shorter PFS than that of patients in the control group (median PFS: 3.2 and 6.2 months; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.2–4.8 and 4.3–9.9 months, respectively;P = 0.008). In addition, the disease control rate in the anemia group was 65.8%, which was significantly lower than that in the control group (93.6%; P = 0.007). Overall survival tended to be shorter in patients with anemia than in controls, although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.07). Multivariate Cox hazard analysis suggested that baseline anemia was a singular risk factor for poor PFS (adjusted hazard ratio 1.84, 95% CI 1.08–3.13; P = 0.02). The incidence of severe adverse effects did not differ between the two groups.ConclusionsThis study suggests that the PFS of patients with anemia treated with DOC + RAM for advanced NSCLC is shorter than that of those without the symptoms.

Background

The incidence of lung cancer is increasing, and it is a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1, 2]. A combination of ramucirumab (RAM) with docetaxel (DOC) is an effective regimen in second or later line treatments in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [3, 4]. However, this combination is associated with increased occurrence and severity of adverse effects, such as hematotoxicity, febrile neutropenia (FN), peripheral edema, oral mucositis, and anorexia, compared to DOC monotherapy [3, 4]. These severe symptoms significantly reduce the quality of life of patients, leading to treatment delays and/or dose reduction in 20–40% of patients [3–6]. We previously reported that patients with baseline anemia or those who receive prophylactic granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) administration have a lower risk of treatment modifications, such as dose reduction or discontinuation, owing to the severe adverse effects associated with DOC + RAM treatment [6]. Previous small number phase II or retrospective studies have also shown that there is a possibility that prophylactic G-CSF administration contributes to FN reduction [7–9]. On the other hand, several reports have suggested a relationship between anemia and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [10–14]. Furthermore, anti-VEGF treatment leads a pressure gradient across the vasculature, enhancing drug penetration into tumors [15, 16]. Consequently, we hypothesized that anemia directly promotes tumor angiogenesis, leading to the elevation of RAM efficacy with increased DOC delivery to tumors while reducing DOC delivery to other organs, potentially mitigating severe adverse effects. If this hypothesis is correct, patients with baseline anemia may have better clinical outcomes than those with normal hemoglobin levels. In this study, we aimed to investigate the effect of baseline anemia on the efficacy of DOC + RAM in treating advanced NSCLC in a real-world setting.

Methods

Patients

In this retrospective observational study, we assessed data of patients with advanced NSCLC who underwent DOC + RAM treatment between September 2016 and June 2023. Eligibility baseline criteria of the patients were (1) age ≥ 20 years; (2) absolute neutrophil count ≥ 1.5 × 103 cells/µL and platelet count ≥ 1.0 × 105 cells/µL; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG-PS) score of 0 to 2; (4) sufficient patient information available from medical records; and (5) acceptable liver or renal function for treatment initiation. Patients who discontinued both agents after the first cycle, were transferred to another hospital during chemotherapy, and those with previous DOC administration were excluded. The patients were categorized into two groups, including a control group with normal baseline hemoglobin levels between September 2016 and June 2023, and an anemia group with baseline anemia between October 2016 and June 2023. The range of normal hemoglobin levels at our facility is 13.7–16.8 g/dL for men and 11.6–14.8 g/dL for women. The present study was approved by the Ethical Review Board for Life Science and Medical Research of Hokkaido University Hospital (approval number: 023–0104) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE guidelines. As the present study was retrospective in nature, the requirement for informed consent from the participants was not mandated by the Ethical Review Board for Life Science and Medical Research of Hokkaido University Hospital, and the opt-out system was adopted.

Treatment methods

DOC (60 mg/m2) and RAM (10 mg/kg) were administered intravenously every 3 weeks [3, 4]. Dexamethasone 6.6 mg was intravenously administered to all patients as premedication, based on national antiemetic guidelines [17]. Treatment was modified or discontinued depending on the adverse effects at the physician’s discretion, according to the criteria stated in previous reports [3, 4]. Supportive care medications were also administered at the physician’s discretion.

Evaluation of treatment efficacy and adverse effects

All the required information was extracted from the patients’ medical records. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), and the secondary endpoints were assessment of overall survival (OS), disease control rate (DCR), and safety. PFS was calculated from the date of DOC + RAM treatment initiation until disease progression, death, or last follow-up. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause or last follow-up. Tumor response was classified as complete response, partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease, according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (version 1.1), with 2–3 months interval evaluation. Adverse effects were evaluated by referring to the treatment diary and/or medical interview, in accordance with the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0, at every visit, by physicians and pharmacists. Laboratory tests were also performed at every visit, at least on treatment day. The incidence and severity of adverse effects during all treatment periods were evaluated and the worst grade was determined.

Statistical analysis

Differences in baseline clinical characteristics between the control and anemia groups were compared using Fisher’s exact probability test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. PFS and OS were described using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. DCR was assessed using Fisher’s exact probability test. The incidences of adverse effects and dose modifications were compared using Fisher’s exact probability test. Relative dose intensity (RDI) was evaluated using the Student’s t-test. Cox proportional hazard regression analyses were performed to adjust the PFS outcomes based on baseline clinical variables including sex; age; ECOG-PS score; postoperative recurrence setting; histology; presence of driver mutations; programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) tumor proportion score positive (≥ 1%); presence of brain, bone, and liver metastasis; body surface area (BSA); neutropenia; anemia; thrombopenia; hypoalbuminemia; liver dysfunction (grade ≥ 1 aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin elevation); renal dysfunction (creatinine clearance calculated by the Cockcroft–Gault formula < 60 mL/min); C-reactive protein (CRP) elevation; smoking history; treatment line; history of immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) administration; dose reduction from treatment initiation; and prophylactic G-CSF administration according to previous studies [6, 18–20]. Variables that demonstrated a potential association in the univariate analysis (P < 0.20) were included in the multivariate analysis.

All analyses were performed using JMP version 16.1 statistical software (SAS Institute Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Differences were considered statistically significant at P-values < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

Data of 72 patients (32 controls and 40 with anemia) with advanced NSCLC were analyzed in this study (Fig. 1). The baseline patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. No differences were observed between the two groups regarding age; ECOG-PS score; staging; histology; presence of driver mutation; positive PD-L1 tumor proportion score; presence of brain, bone, and liver metastases; BSA; neutrophil and platelet counts; serum albumin levels; liver dysfunction; creatinine clearance; CRP elevation; smoking history; alcohol intake; treatment line; number of prior cytotoxic regimens; prior ICI administration; duration between progressive disease or treatment discontinuation owing to adverse effects of previous treatment and treatment initiation; dose reduction from treatment initiation; or prophylactic G-CSF administration. However, the anemia group included significantly higher number of male patients compared to the control group, which may be attributed to different cutoff levels of normal hemoglobin, with significantly lower baseline hemoglobin levels. One patient in the control group and two in the anemia group had minor epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations (exon 18 or 20 mutations), and others harbored major mutations (exon 19 deletion or L858R). Approximately half of the patients received a prophylactic G-CSF administration (all pegfilgrastim) at treatment initiation.

Evaluation of anemia during the treatment

The median worst hemoglobin levels during all subsequent treatment cycles were 11.4 g/dL (range 8.9–13.1 g/dL) in the control group and significantly lower 10.0 g/dL (7.0–12.6 g/dL) in the anemia group (P < 0.0001). In contrast, the worst variation in hemoglobin levels during all cycles was − 2.0 g/dL (− 4.9–0.4 g/dL) and − 1.2 g/dL (− 3.5–0.1 g/dL) in the control and anemia groups, respectively, which was more reduced in the control group (P = 0.001).

Treatment efficacy

Figure 2 shows Kaplan–Meier plots of PFS and OS after DOC + RAM treatment. Patients in the anemia group had a significantly shorter PFS than that of patients in the control group (median PFS: 3.2 and 6.2 months; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.2–4.8 and 4.3–9.9 months, respectively; P = 0.008; Fig. 2a). In addition, DCR was significantly lower in the anemia group than in the control group (65.8% [PR for 13.2% and SD for 52.6%] and 93.6% [PR for 22.6% and SD for 71.0%], respectively; P = 0.007). Patients with anemia tended to have poor OS compared to controls, although the difference was not statistically significant (12.9 [7.9–15.1] and 15.5 [12.5–38.8] months, respectively; P = 0.07; Fig. 2b).

Adverse effects and dose modification of the treatment

Table 2 presents adverse effects during all DOC + RAM treatment cycles. Compared to patients in the anemia group, those in the control group exhibited significantly higher rates of all-grade thrombopenia (78.1 vs. 47.5%, P = 0.01), and tended to experience all-grade neutropenia (75.0 vs. 55.0%, P = 0.09) and diarrhea (34.4 vs. 15.0%, P = 0.09) without significant differences. Conversely, patients in the anemia group exhibited all-grade anemia at a higher rate than that of patients in the control group (100 vs. 87.5%, P = 0.04). However, the incidence of severe adverse effects during all treatment cycles did not significantly differ between the two groups. There were no patients who succumbed to treatment-related death.

Prophylactic G-CSF was finally administered to 80.0% of patients with anemia, which did not differ from controls (81.3%, P = 1.00). In contrast, dose modification was significantly less common in the anemia group than in the control group (52.5% and 78.1%, respectively; P = 0.03). Final RDI of DOC in the anemia group was 91.5 ± 10.0%, which was significantly higher than that in the control group (84.5 ± 10.9%, P = 0.006). In contrast, RDI of RAM was not different between the two groups (73.4 ± 37.1% for control and 81.5 ± 38.3% for anemia groups, P = 0.37).

Association between baseline patient factors and PFS

We also evaluated the association between baseline patient characteristics and PFS in DOC + RAM treatment (Table 3). Baseline anemia was identified as a significant risk factor for poor PFS in the univariate Cox hazard regression analysis, and the ECOG-PS score, bone metastasis existence, BSA, anemia, renal dysfunction, and CRP elevation (P < 0.20 in the univariate analysis), were assessed in multivariate analysis. Multivariate analysis suggested that baseline anemia was a singular risk factor for poor PFS (adjusted hazard ratio 1.84, 95% CI 1.08–3.13; P = 0.02). Patients with baseline CRP elevation tended to have poor PFS, although this association did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

In our previous study evaluating the factors associated with severe adverse effects during the first cycle of DOC + RAM treatment, baseline anemia was identified as an independent preventive factor [6]. We hypothesized that anemia promotes tumor angiogenesis, enhancing DOC delivery to tumors through RAM efficacy, reducing delivery to other organs, consequently leading to a decrease in severe adverse effects. If our hypothesis is valid, patients with baseline anemia may have better clinical outcomes than those with normal hemoglobin levels. Therefore, we investigated the effect of baseline anemia on the efficacy of DOC + RAM in treating advanced NSCLC in a real-world setting.

Our findings revealed that contrary to our hypotheses, individuals with baseline anemia had a significantly shorter PFS and shorter OS than those with normal hemoglobin levels. Anemia is reportedly associated with poor prognosis in various types of malignancies [21–26]. Additionally, it is associated with poor NSCLC treatment outcomes, although few reports have specifically addressed advanced NSCLC treatment and none focused on anti-VEGF therapy [18, 19, 27–34]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to demonstrate poor treatment outcomes in patients with anemia receiving DOC + RAM for treating advanced NSCLC.

The etiology of anemia in patients with cancer is multifactorial, encompassing cancer treatment including chemotherapy or radiotherapy, disease progression, malnutrition, inflammation, and renal or bone marrow involvement [35, 36]. The precise mechanism underlying for the association between anemia and poor prognosis in patients with cancer remains unclear; one possible hypothesis is hypoxia in cancer tissues. Dunst et al. reported that tissue hypoxia induced by anemia is a major stimulus for the upregulation of VEGF [10, 11]. Linderholm et al. suggested that patients with breast cancer with elevated VEGF levels have a worse prognosis than those with normal VEGF levels [37]. Krzystek-Korpacka et al. reported that mild pretreatment anemia is associated with cancer metastasis, which appears to be mediated by angiogenic factors [13]. Furthermore, resistance to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors correlates with increased VEGF expression [18, 38]. These findings suggest that anemia can result in a poor prognosis via the upregulation of VEGF. We guess that our previous pharmacological hypothesis may have been correct, although it is nothing more than a speculation; however, tumor resistance exceeded the antitumor efficacy of these chemotherapeutic agents, leading to insufficient chemotherapeutic efficacy. Another possible mechanism involves interleukin (IL)-6, which plays an important role in many chronic inflammatory diseases [18]. IL-6 is involved in the regulation of tumor growth and metastasis [18, 39]. Additionally, IL-6 potently induces the production of hepcidin, a key regulator of iron metabolism, leading to a decrease in iron levels, resulting in anemia [40, 41]. Furthermore, higher hepcidin levels are associated with worse progression in patients with NSCLC [42]. In the present study, CRP elevation tended to worsen PFS, although it was not significant, possibly because of low statistical power. As IL-6 is essential for inducing CRP synthesis [43], CRP elevation can reflect an increase in IL-6 levels, which may support the hypothesis.

These may explain a part of the mechanisms underlying the relationship between anemia and the poor efficacy of DOC + RAM treatment, and it is considered that there is a high possibility that hypoxia may be a contributing factor, although further studies are needed.

In our previous study, patients with anemia experienced fewer adverse effects during the first cycle of DOC + RAM [6]. However, the incidence and severity of adverse effects were not statistically different, except for all-grade thrombocytopenia and anemia in all treatment cycles, although patients with anemia exhibited less severe adverse effects such as severe neutropenia or FN in the first cycle, resulting in a significantly lower dose reduction rate in the second cycle (53.1% vs. 27.5% in the control and anemia groups, respectively; P = 0.03). Consequently, we believe that the potent toxicities caused by this regimen over longer treatment periods than those in our previous study resulted in equivalent severe adverse effects in this study. Therefore, close monitoring, particularly in patients with normal baseline hemoglobin levels in the first cycle, is essential for early detection and appropriate management of adverse effects [6]. In addition, as a prophylactic G-CSF reduces severe adverse effects [6–9], it may be beneficial to commence with its administration at treatment initiation. Furthermore, as longer administration can induce broader and more severe adverse effects, adequate symptom management by a medical team is important for maintaining patient’s quality of life.

The present study has some limitations. First, the study was retrospectively performed including a small patient population from a single institution. Second, we assessed anemia using hemoglobin levels alone and did not assess its cause. As many factors induce anemia and can be associated complicatedly, further evaluation of the detailed anemic factors is necessary. Third, due to the limited number of patients, we included individuals from several treatment lines. There are many treatment strategies depending on driver mutations or PD-L1 scores, which could have possibly affected treatment efficacy, particularly OS results. Thus, evaluation in an integrated treatment line and patient population is needed. Fourth, we adopted the institutional cutoff of hemoglobin levels for anemia, considering a real-world analysis. However, there are several definitions of anemia. Therefore, further studies with other cutoffs are required to support the obtained outcomes. Finally, we did not evaluate polymorphisms in drug-metabolizing enzymes or transporters, such as cytochrome P450 3A4 and 3A5, ABCB1, ABCC2, and SLCO1B3, which can affect the pharmacokinetics of DOC [44], and VEGF or IL-6 levels. Therefore, our preliminary findings require validation in future research.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that the PFS of patients with anemia treated with DOC + RAM for advanced NSCLC is shorter than that of those without symptoms. Further studies are required to elucidate the rationale for the development of better treatment strategies.
