Massive Inflationary Amplitudes: New Representations and Degenerate Limits
Zhong-Zhi Xianyu and Jiaju Zang, [5mm] Department of Physics, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084, China

Abstract
The particle model building of cosmological collider physics often involves boost-breaking bilinear mixing between a heavy particle and the nearly massless inflaton mode. In cosmological correlators, such a mixing is obtained by taking a folded limit of a generic tree graph, which is a special case of degenerate kinematics. In this work, we continue our exploration of massive inflationary amplitudes with a focus on degenerate kinematics. With a suitable change of variables, we derive new differential equations and full analytical solutions for generic tree graphs, making it trivial to take the folded limit and partial-energy limit at a vertex. Our result shows that folded tree graphs generally involve functions of smaller transcendental weights than their nondegenerate counterparts. In particular, the inflaton bispectrum with triple massive exchanges can be expressed in terms of a trivariate Kampé de Fériet function and simpler hypergeometric functions.

Phenomenology of bilinear mixings

It is easy to see why bilinear mixing is important to phenomenology: The inflaton bispectrum (3-point correlator) is the lowest-point correlator that can carry scale-invariant CC signals, and bilinear mixing is the only option if we want a heavy particle to generate tree-level CC signals in the 3-point function.

\sigma

Theoretical development

The reduction of symmetry in the bilinear mixing implies that the computation of graphs involving such mixings is not entirely trivial. Nevertheless, the numerical integration of graphs with mixings was successfully implemented in the early studies
[

On the analytical frontier, an analytical expression for the mixed propagator was found in

More generally, as mentioned above, we may want to compute tree graphs with any number of mixings by taking folded limits of the general results in
[
1
]
. This is indeed possible, barring a minor issue of cancelling spurious folded divergences among different terms. However, less obvious is that the transcendental weight of the graph is reduced when we take a folded limit. This fact cannot be seen by directly taking the folded limits of results in
[
1
]
. A change of variables is essential here, as noticed previously
[
77
,
110
,
80

. The reduction of transcendental weight not only helps to speed up numerical computation, but also points to potentially new mathematical relations linking hypergeometric functions of different numbers of variables. Thus, it would be interesting to see whether the weight reduction is a general phenomenon for graphs with an arbitrary number of mixings. It would also be interesting to use this fact to find an analytical expression for the good old triple-exchange graph. In this work, we will report our new progress along these directions.

Thus, it would be interesting to see if the reduction of transcendental weight still holds for more complicated tree graphs with more folded limits taken. Also, it remains unclear how to make the change of variables for more complicated graphs. We will explore these questions in the following sections.

3  New Representations for General Trees

In this section, we return to the most general tree-level graphs reviewed in App.
A

y_{i}\rightarrow\displaystyle\frac{\,{u_{\alpha j}}\,}{u_{\alpha i}+u_{\alpha j}}y_{i}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathcal{E}}_{i}}\,}{{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}}y_{i}.

(35)

With the rescaled
y_{i}
, the integral (
3.1
) is further reduced to:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\,{u_{\alpha i}^{p_{j}+4}u_{\alpha j}^{p_{i}+4}}\,}{(u_{\alpha i}+u_{\alpha j})^{p_{ij}+5}}\sum_{\mathsf{a}_{i}}\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}_{i}\int{\mathrm{d}}y_{i}\,(-y_{i})^{p_{ij}+4}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}_{i}(2-\bm{u}_{ij})y_{i}}\cdots.

(36)

The upshot is that all
y_{i}
-dependence in the original integral is replaced by
({\mathcal{E}}_{i}/{\mathcal{E}}_{ij})y_{i}
, and all
y_{j}
replaced by
({\mathcal{E}}_{j}/{\mathcal{E}}_{ij})y_{j}
. So, we get a new graph with Vertices
i
and
j
pinched together, with a new dressed vertex energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}
and a new twist
p_{ij}+4
, which is nothing but the contraction
\mathsf{C}_{\alpha}[{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}]
. This completes the derivation of (
22
).

To complete this subsection, we comment on our definition of dressed vertex energy (
13
). One may wonder if
\mathsf{s}_{\alpha i}
can be arbitrary real numbers instead of just being
\pm 1
. This question can be easily answered by examining the steps leading to the differential equations (
22
). First, the disappearance of line energy
K_{\alpha}
in the contracted graph
\mathsf{C}_{\alpha}[{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}]
requires
s_{\alpha i}+s_{\alpha j}=0
; See (
34
). Second, when we go from the equation for the propagator (
23
) to the equation for the correlator (
22
), there is a subtle simplification. To explain this point, consider the Klein-Gordon operator in the original equation (
23
). If we count the dimension of each term in the differential operator under the rescaling
y_{i}\rightarrow\lambda y_{i}
, the Euler operator
\vartheta_{y_{i}}
and the mass term
m_{\alpha}^{2}
are dimensionless, while the term
u_{\alpha i}^{2}y_{i}^{2}/4
is dimension 2. Thus, if we use a series ansatz
\sim\sum c_{n}y^{n}
to solve this equation, the Klein-Gordon operator will lead to a second-order recursion relation for
c_{n}
. In contrast, in the final correlator equation (
22
), all terms in the differential operator have
u
-dimension either 0 or 1, implying that the corresponding recursion is of first order. This reduction of recursion order is originated from the second substitution in (
24
). The above derivations show that the resulting operator is of first order only when
\mathsf{s}_{\alpha i}^{2}=1
. It is for this reason that we choose to define dressed energies as in (
13
). Other definitions with more general
\mathsf{s}_{\alpha i}
may also lead to valid differential equations for correlators, although we expect them to be more complicated.

3.2
Completely inhomogeneous solution as massive family trees

In the current and next sections, we solve the equation (
22
) to get the full expression for the graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}
. Again, the procedure is very similar to
[
1
]
. The solution is the sum of a completely inhomogeneous solution (CIS) and appropriate homogeneous solutions. In this subsection, we derive the CIS in terms of MFT functions; In the next subsection, we derive the homogeneous solutions as cuts of the CIS.

Figure 3:
An illustration of the differential equation (
22
).

Recursion formulae

The CIS is constructed by recursively adding new lines to an existing graph. This is achieved by a recursive formula linking the CISs of two graphs before and after the new line is added. As shown in Fig.
3
, we consider an arbitrary tree graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}
. We want to add a new line to Vertex
j
, which we call Line
\alpha
, whose other endpoint is called
i
. Then, the new graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{V+1}^{\prime}
satisfies the following equation:

\displaystyle\Big[(\eta_{{\alpha j}}-\mbox{$\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}$})^{2}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\alpha}^{2}-\mathsf{s}_{\alpha j}u_{\alpha j}(\eta_{\alpha j}-1)(\bm{\eta}_{j}+p_{j}+1)\Big]{\mathcal{G}}_{V+1}^{\prime}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{u_{\alpha i}^{p_{j}+4}u_{\alpha j}^{p_{i}+4}}\,}{(u_{\alpha i}+u_{\alpha j})^{p_{ij}+5}}{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}\Big|_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&E_{j}\rightarrow E_{ij}\\
&p_{j}\rightarrow p_{ij}+4\crcr}}}.

Suppose the source term has the following series solution:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}\Big|_{p_{j}\rightarrow p_{ij}+4}=\sum_{\{n\}}c_{\{n\}}\prod_{\beta\in\mathcal{N}(j)}u_{\beta j}^{q_{\beta}}\times\cdots,

(39)

Here and below, we use the abbreviation
\sum\limits_{\{n\}}=\sum\limits_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\cdots
to collect all summation variables. Also, we only show
u
variables containing Vertex
j
since they are the only relevant variables when doing the recursion. The exponents
q_{\beta}
can depend on summation variables
\{n\}
and other parameters, so is the coefficient
c_{\{n\}}
.

The energy shift
E_{j}\rightarrow E_{ij}
required in (
3.2
) is effected by the change of variable
u_{\beta j}\rightarrow u_{\beta j}u_{\alpha i}/(u_{\alpha i}+u_{\alpha j})
. Then, we work with the region of
u_{\alpha j}<u_{\alpha i}
and express the source as a series of small
u_{\alpha j}
.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\,{u_{\alpha i}^{p_{j}+4}u_{\alpha j}^{p_{i}+4}}\,}{(u_{\alpha i}+u_{\alpha j})^{p_{ij}+5}}{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}\Big|_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&E_{j}\rightarrow E_{ij}\\
&p_{j}\rightarrow p_{ij}+4\crcr}}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell,\{n\}}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}(\ell+1)_{q_{1\cdots N}+p_{ij}+4}}{\Gamma(q_{1\cdots N}+p_{ij}+5)}c_{\{n\}}

\displaystyle\times u_{\alpha j}^{3}\Big(\frac{u_{\alpha j}}{u_{\alpha i}}\Big)^{\ell+p_{i}+1}\prod_{\beta\in\mathcal{N}(j)}u_{\beta j}^{q_{\beta}}\times\cdots,

(42)

The form of this source motivates us to consider the following ansatz for the inhomogeneous solution of
{\mathcal{G}}_{V+1}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{Inh}}_{K_{\alpha}}\big[{\mathcal{G}}_{V+1}^{\prime}\big]=\sum_{\ell,m,\{n\}}d_{\ell m\{n\}}u_{\alpha j}^{m+3}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{u_{\alpha j}}\,}{u_{\alpha i}}\Big)^{\ell+p_{i}+1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathcal{N}(j)\\
\beta\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}u_{\beta j}^{q_{\beta}}\times\cdots,

(43)

Putting (
43
) and (
3.2
) into both sides of the equation (
3.2
), and requiring all powers of
u
to match, we get the coefficients of the series solution for
{\mathcal{G}}_{V+1}^{\prime}
as simple functions of the coefficients of the series solution for the contracted graph:

\displaystyle d_{\ell m\{n\}}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{(-1)^{\ell}\mathsf{s}_{\alpha j}^{m}(\ell+p_{i}+3)_{m}(q_{1\cdots N}+p_{ij}+5)_{\ell+m}}\,}{\ell!(\ell+p_{i}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\alpha})_{m+1}}c_{\{n\}}.

(44)

Since all expressions here are expanded around small
u_{\alpha j}
, the recursion relation (
44
) works when
{\mathcal{E}}_{j}>{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
. As in
[
1
]
, we use the concept of “energy flow” defined for every internal line, which flows from the site of larger energy to the smaller. In this terminology, we can say that (
44
) allows us to add an outgoing line to any existing tree graph. We stress that,
a priori
, the energy flow has nothing to do with the sign labels
\mathsf{s}_{\alpha i}
that were introduced to define
u
variables.

Likewise, we can build an “ingoing” recursion relation which works when
{\mathcal{E}}_{j}<{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
. We can use it to add an ingoing new line to any existing tree graph. However, as proved in
[
1
]
, adding an ingoing line yields the same result for the CIS as adding an outgoing line. Therefore, we will neglect the discussion of ingoing recursion, and consider only outgoing cases. The resulting solution will automatically apply to graphs with ingoing directions.

Massive family tree

As indicated above, we can find the CIS of
{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}
in the following way. We first choose a site of maximal (dressed) vertex energy, say
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
. Then, the choice of the maximal energy site automatically generates a unique energy flow for the entire tree graph. Then, starting from the maximal energy site
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
, we can use the outgoing recursion relation (
44
) to recursively add new lines, which are always outgoing, until we reach the full graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}
. The starting point of this recursive construction is a one-site graph:

{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{\prime}=\sum_{\mathsf{a}_{1}=\pm}\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\mathrm{d}}y_{1}\,(-y_{1})^{p_{1}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}_{1}y_{1}}=2\cos(\pi p_{1}/2)\Gamma(p_{1}+1).

(45)

At this point, it is useful to use the notation for MFT
\big\llbracket{\cdots}\big\rrbracket
introduced in
[
1
]
. See App.
A
for an introduction. Due to the different overall factors involved in defining dimensionless graphs
{\mathcal{G}}_{V}
(for
r
variables) and
{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}
(for
u
variables), we rescale the MFT accordingly, and also add a prime like
\big\llbracket{\cdots}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}
for distinction. Explicitly, the relation between the two versions is:

\displaystyle\big\llbracket{\mathscr{P}(1\cdots V)}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}=2^{3I}\prod_{i=1}^{V}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathcal{E}}_{i}}\,}{E_{i}}\Big)^{1+p_{i}}\big\llbracket{\mathscr{P}(1\cdots V)}\big\rrbracket.

(46)

Here
\mathscr{P}
denotes a particular partial order of the family tree.
For the 1-site graph, the two versions happen to be the same,
\big\llbracket{1}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}=\big\llbracket{1}\big\rrbracket
. Then, we can repeat the exercise in
[
1
]
and construct CIS for tree graphs. This time, it is of no surprise that we also get a closed-form CIS for any
V
-site tree graphs
{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}
in
u
variables. To write down this expression, we choose the partial order induced by the maximal energy site
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
, denoted as
\mathscr{P}(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu1\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\cdots V)
. With this partial order, we can identify the label
\alpha
of an internal line with the label
i
of the vertex to which the line points (i.e., the daughter site). In particular, every sign label can all be written as
\mathsf{s}_{i}=\mathsf{s}_{ii}
, which means the sign label of Line
i
with respect to its
daughter site
, and it is automatically understood that the sign label of Site
i
with respect to its mother site is
-\mathsf{s}_{i}
. Then, for each but the maximal-energy site, we assign two summation labels
\ell_{i},m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
(
i=2,\cdots,V
), together with a
family parameter

q_{i}
which encodes the family-tree structure
\mathscr{P}
:

q_{i}\equiv\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\ell\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}+\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mum\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}+\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mup\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}+4N_{i},

(47)

where a hatted parameter, say
\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\ell\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}
, means the sum of corresponding parameters over all descendant sites of
i
. With this notational preparation, we can now give the expression for the CIS of
{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}
as:

\displaystyle\text{CIS}\,\big[{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}\big]=

\displaystyle~\big\llbracket{\mathscr{P}(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu1\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\cdots V)}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}=2\cos(\pi p_{1\cdots V}/2)\sum_{\{\ell,m\}}\Gamma(p_{1}+q_{1}+1)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i=2}^{V}\frac{\mathsf{s}_{i}^{m_{i}}(-1)^{\ell_{i}}(\ell_{i}+q_{i}+p_{i}+3)_{m_{i}}}{\ell_{i}!(\ell_{i}+q_{i}+p_{i}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i})_{m_{i}+1}}\Big(\frac{2K_{i}}{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}\Big)^{m_{i}+3}\Big(\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{i}}{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}\Big)^{\ell_{i}+p_{i}+1}.

(48)

As in previous work
[
1
]
, this series can be viewed as the definition of a
2(V-1)
-variate hypergeometric function in the sense of Horn. The series is well convergent at least when the maximal energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
is much larger than all other energies, while the relative sizes of other energies are not important. Beyond the region of convergence, the function is defined by analytic continuation. Importantly, the choice of the maximal energy site is arbitrary. In a region where some other
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
instead of
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
is maximal, we can write down the corresponding CIS expanded in large
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
. The two expressions are analytical continuations of each other up to homogeneous solutions to be determined below. Thus, the many ways (
V
ways for
{\mathcal{G}}_{V}^{\prime}
) of choosing the maximal energy site provide many transformation-of-variable formulae which partially achieve the analytical continuation of the original series (
3.2
) beyond its convergence region.

Curiously, (
3.2
) has a similar yet distinct form with the previously obtained MFT using
r
variables in (
A
). In both cases, a family tree is written as
2I
-variate hypergeometric series, but the variables are different, and are related by (the square of) linear fractional transformation as in (
19
). Thus, we immediately get yet another set of infinitely many transformation-of-variable formulae for these hypergeometric functions through (
46
), which are essentially multivariate generalizations of known quadratic transformations of Gauss hypergeometric function
{}_{2}\text{F}_{1}

[
111
]
.

3.3
Homogeneous solutions as cuts of family trees

Next, we consider homogeneous solutions. As in
[
1
]
, we mean by a homogeneous solution the piece in the full graph that is annihilated by at least one of
2I
differential operators in (
22
). Given Line
\alpha
connecting Vertices
i
and
j
, it is always true that, if a homogeneous solution solves the sourceless differential equation for
u_{\alpha i}
, it also does for
u_{\alpha j}
. In this case, we call Line
\alpha
is
cut
, because the corresponding time integrals over
y_{i}
and
y_{j}
are fully factorized. In the
signal region
where all vertex energies are larger than all line energies, we have a one-to-one relation between the solution being homogeneous/inhomogeneous and the line being cut/uncut. So the homogeneous solutions can be specified by the cut lines, and thus there are a total of
2^{I}-1
distinct homogeneous solutions.

Single cut

Now we briefly describe how to get a single-cut solution from the CIS, i.e., a piece annihilated by nothing but
u_{\alpha i}
and
u_{\alpha j}
operators. As always, the homogeneous solutions are fixed by appropriate boundary conditions, which can be conveniently imposed from various squeezed limits. At these limits, the time integrals become analytically computable, so let us turn to these time integrals.

The cut over Line
\alpha
produces two subgraphs, one containing Vertex
i
and the other containing Vertex
j
. With this bipartition, the time integrals over
y_{i}
and
y_{j}
can be written as:

{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}=\sum_{\mathsf{a}_{i},\mathsf{a}_{j}=\pm}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\prod_{n=i,j}\Big[{\mathrm{d}}y_{n}\,\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}_{n}(-y_{n})^{p_{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}_{n}(1-\bm{u}_{n}/2)y_{n}}\Big]\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}_{i}\mathsf{a}_{j}}^{(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\alpha})}\big(\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}u_{\alpha i}y_{i},\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}u_{\alpha j}y_{j}\big)\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{a}_{i}}^{\text{(L)}}\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{a}_{j}}^{\text{(R)}},

(49)

where
\mathcal{I}^{(L)}_{\mathsf{a}_{i}}
and
\mathcal{I}^{(R)}_{\mathsf{a}_{j}}
collect remaining time integrals of left and right subgraphs, respectively. Without loss of generality, we assume that the maximal energy site of the whole graph (Site 1 with energy
E_{1}
) belongs to the left subgraph after the cut, so that the (locally) maximal energy of the right subgraph is smaller than that of the left. Anticipating that the integral will be factorized by the cut, we rearrange the exponential factors in the integrand as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}(1-\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}\bm{u}_{n})=\mathrm{i}(1-\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\bm{u}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{n})-\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}\mathrm{i}\,\mathsf{s}_{\alpha n}u_{\alpha n},

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\bm{u}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{n}\equiv\bm{u}_{n}-\mathsf{s}_{\alpha n}u_{\alpha n}.~~~(n=i,j)

(50)

Since the locally maximal energy is larger on the left subgraph, we apply the cutting rule
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm}^{(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\alpha})}\Rightarrow\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mp\pm}^{(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\alpha})}
to get the single cut of the graph
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{Cut}}_{K_{\alpha}}\big[{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}\big]=

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\mathrm{d}}y_{i}{\mathrm{d}}y_{j}\,(-y_{i})^{p_{i}}(-y_{j})^{p_{j}}\Big[e^{\mathrm{i}(1-\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\bm{u}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}/2)y_{i}}\mathcal{I}^{\text{(L)}}_{+}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{s}_{i}u_{i}y_{i}/2}-e^{-\mathrm{i}(1-\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\bm{u}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}/2)y_{i}}\mathcal{I}^{\text{(L)}}_{-}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{s}_{i}u_{i}y_{i}/2}\Big]

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{I}^{\text{(R)}}_{+}e^{\mathrm{i}(1-\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\bm{u}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{j}/2)y_{j}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{s}_{j}u_{\alpha j}y_{j}/2}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\alpha})}_{-+}\big(\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}u_{i}y_{i},\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}u_{j}y_{j}\big)+\text{c.c..}

(51)

Notably, this asymmetric cutting rule endows the cut with a direction that flows from the left subgraph to the right, i.e., from the larger locally maximal energy to the smaller. Then, the above integrals factorize into two separated integrals for
y_{i}
and
y_{j}
, since the opposite-sign propagators
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-+}^{(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)}_{-+}\big(\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}u_{i}y_{i},\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}u_{j}y_{j}\big)=\sigma\big(\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}u_{i}y_{i}\big)\sigma^{*}\big(\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}u_{j}y_{j}\big),

where

\displaystyle e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b}uy/2}\sigma\big(\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}uy\big)=\sum_{\mathsf{c}=\pm}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}A_{m,\mathsf{c}}^{\mathsf{b}}(u)(-y)^{m+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{c}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+3/2},

\displaystyle A_{m,\mathsf{c}}^{\mathsf{b}}(u)\equiv\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}e^{\pi\mathsf{c}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu/2}\,\text{csch}\,(\pi\mathsf{c}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)\Gamma\Bigg[\begin{matrix}\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}+m+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{c}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\\

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{Cut}}_{K_{\alpha}}\,\big[\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\big]=\sum_{\{\mathsf{c},m\}}\Big[A^{+\mathsf{s}_{i}}_{m_{1},\mathsf{c}_{1}}(u_{i})A^{-\mathsf{s}_{j}*}_{m_{2},-\mathsf{c}_{2}}(u_{j})\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{c}_{1}}_{+}\mathcal{R}^{\mathsf{c}_{2}}_{+}+A^{-\mathsf{s}_{i}}_{m_{1},\mathsf{c}_{1}}(u_{i})A^{-\mathsf{s}_{j}*}_{m_{2},-\mathsf{c}_{2}}(u_{j})\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{c}_{1}}_{-}\mathcal{R}^{\mathsf{c}_{2}}_{+}\Big]+\text{c.c.}.

(55)

Here, the two integrals
\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{c}_{1}}_{\pm}
and

are defined by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{c}_{1}}_{\pm}\equiv

\displaystyle\pm\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathrm{d}y_{i}(-y_{i})^{p_{i}+m_{1}+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{c}_{1}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+3/2}e^{\pm\mathrm{i}(1-\bm{u}^{\text{(L)}}_{i}/2)y_{i}}\mathcal{I}^{\text{(L)}}_{\pm},

(56)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\mathsf{c}_{2}}_{\pm}\equiv

\displaystyle\pm\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathrm{d}y_{j}(-y_{j})^{p_{j}+m_{2}+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{c}_{2}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+3/2}e^{\pm\mathrm{i}(1-\bm{u}^{\text{(R)}}_{j}/2)y_{j}}\mathcal{I}^{\text{(R)}}_{\pm}.

They are the time integrals of two subgraphs after the cut. Here,

4  Folded and Partial-Energy Limits

As advocated before, a main advantage of our new representation of correlators is that taking certain limits becomes a straightforward task. In this section, we consider two kinematic limits. One is the folded limit, or colinear limit, which can be taken at any leaf site and is relevant to our discussion of degenerate correlators with bilinear mixings. The other is the partial-energy limit, which is an unphysical limit (unreachable in physical regions) but is physically interesting as it probes the short-distance behavior of a subgraph. In the following, we will be more detailed in folded limits and only briefly touch upon partial-energy limits.

4.1
Folded limit

As mentioned, a folded limit is defined with respect to any leaf site. A leaf site is a site to which only one internal line is attached. It is possible to use our new variables to consider similar folded limits but we will leave them to interested readers. Let Site
i
be an arbitrary leaf site with vertex energy
E_{i}
, and it is attached to a line with line energy
K_{i}
. (Since the line is unique, we can identify the subscript of
E_{i}
and
K_{i}
without ambiguities.) Then, the folded limit refers to
E_{i}-K_{i}\rightarrow 0
; See Fig.
4
.

Figure 4:

). Here, we are considering an example with 3 external lines attached to Site

(

The folded limit suggests that we take the sign label at Site

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{Fold}}_{i}\,\big[\mathcal{G^{\prime}}\big]=\lim_{E_{i}\rightarrow K_{i}}\Big(\frac{2K_{i}}{E_{i}-K_{i}}\Big)^{p_{i}+1}\mathcal{G^{\prime}},

where we introduce the factor

Given our full solution to the graph in (

CIS

Using our new variables, the folded limit for a CIS is trivial. Recall that, once we have chosen a maximal dressed vertex energy, say

, the CIS of a graph

\displaystyle\big\llbracket{\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu1\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\cdots\mathring{i}\cdots}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}\equiv\mathop{\text{Fold}}_{i}\,\big\llbracket{\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu1\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\cdots i\cdots}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}.

(68)

Now, let us consider an arbitrary MFT with an arbitrary nonzero number of leaves taking folded limits, called

. We denote the set of folded leaves by

. Then, the folded limit of the CIS follows trivially from (

\displaystyle\big\llbracket{\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu1\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\cdots\mathring{\mathcal{F}}}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}=~

\displaystyle 2\cos(\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mup\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{1}/2)\sum_{\{\ell,m\}}\Gamma(p_{1}+q_{1}+1)\prod_{i\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{(p_{i}+3)_{m_{i}}}{(p_{i}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i})_{m_{i}+1}}\Big(\frac{2K_{i}}{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}\Big)^{m_{i}+p_{i}+4}

(69)

That is, the summation over

Cut

Next, we consider the folded limits for the cut part of the graph. Again, we identify the subscript of the line energy

Without loss of generality, we can consider a single cut over Line

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{Fold}}_{E_{i}}\Big\{\mathop{\text{Cut}}\limits_{K_{i}}\big[{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}\big]\Big\}=\big\llbracket{\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu1\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\cdots j^{\sharp}\cdots}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}\lim_{E_{i}\rightarrow K_{i}}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{2K_{i}}\,}{E_{i}-K_{i}}\Big)^{p_{i}+1}\Big\{\big\llbracket{i^{\sharp}}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}+\big\llbracket{i^{\flat}}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}\Big\}+(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}\rightarrow-\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}).

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{2K_{i}}{E_{i}-K_{i}}\Big)^{p_{i}+1}\big\llbracket{i^{\sharp}}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}=

4.2
Partial-energy limit

As a second example, we consider very briefly the partial-energy limit at any given site of the graph. In general, for an arbitrary tree graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, a partial-energy limit is discussed with respect to a subgraph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and defined to be the limit of the magnitude sum of all momenta flowing into
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
going to zero while each momentum is held finite. Clearly, this is an unphysical limit, but it probes the early-time limit (and thus the high-energy/short-distance/flat-space limit) of an inflationary correlator, as has been discussed a lot in the literature.

Here, we only comment that our new variables make it trivial to take the partial-energy limit of a graph at any given
site
. That is, we only consider subgraphs
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
containing only one site, say Site
i
. Then, we can choose the sign labels
\mathsf{s}_{\alpha i}=+1
for all lines attached to this site and define:

{\mathcal{E}}_{i}=E_{i}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(i)}K_{\alpha}.

(75)

Then, the partial-energy limit at Site
i
is simply
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0
.

It is by now standard knowledge that a correlator may develop singularities in the partial-energy limit. Here, we present a complete (not only the leading order) expression for the singular part of the correlator at
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0
. To properly present this singularity, it is useful to go back to the unprimed graph
{\mathcal{G}}
defined in (
122
). This is because the primed graph
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
is related to the correlator by, among other factors, a factor of
1/{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{1+p_{i}}
which is by itself singular when
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0
; See (
20
).

As is well known, the partial-energy singularity at
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0
can be understood as a failure of the convergence of the time integral (
123
): There are terms in the integrand of (
123
) whose early-time behavior is controlled by the factor
e^{\mathrm{i}{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\tau_{i}}
. When
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0
, the
\mathrm{i}\epsilon
-prescription fails to work, and we get a divergent result. Now, our method of decomposing a correlator into an MFT and its cuts says that, whenever Site
i
is connected to another site
j
, the integral over
\tau_{i}
is always bounded by
\tau_{i}>\tau_{j}
and there are never divergences in this case, given that all other energy combinations remain finite. Thus, the only terms that can possibly contribute to partial-energy singularities at
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0
must have all lines attached to Site
i
cut.
9
9

9

See
[
112
]
for a more complete discussion of partial-energy limits of arbitrary massive inflationary correlators at both tree and loop levels, and
[
113
]
for related discussions on partial-energy singularities of (normal) family trees.

We can make use of the above observation to write down a complete expression for the singular part of
{\mathcal{G}}
at
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0

{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}\backslash\{i\}
breaks into

\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime\,\sharp_{1}},\cdots,\mathcal{H}_{M}^{\prime\,\sharp_{M}}

{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0
of
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\lim_{{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0}{\mathcal{G}}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2^{3(V-1)}}\prod_{j=1}^{V}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{E_{j}}\,}{{\mathcal{E}}_{j}}\Big)^{1+p_{j}}\times\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime\,\sharp_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{H}_{M}^{\prime\,\sharp_{M}}\sum_{T_{1},\cdots,T_{M}=\sharp,\flat}\big\llbracket{i^{T_{1}\cdots T_{M}}}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}+\text{regular terms}.

(76)

Here, the prefactors
1/2^{3(V-1)}\prod_{j}(E_{j}/{\mathcal{E}}_{j})^{1+p_{j}}
account for the difference between
{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
. The
T

\big\llbracket{i^{T_{1}\cdots T_{M}}}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}
can be found from the general expression (
3.3
) of multi-fold tuning. However, the resulting series is divergent since the partial energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\rightarrow 0
sits in the denominator. Nevertheless, for this particular example, it is easy to analytically continue the divergent series and find a healthy series expression as

and all others are regular. Since we are only interested in the partial-energy singularity, we only write down the singular series:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{p_{i}+1}}\sum_{T_{1},\cdots,T_{M}=\sharp,\flat}\big\llbracket{i^{T_{1}\cdots T_{M}}}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{2\cos\big[\mbox{$\frac{\,{\pi}\,}{2}$}(p_{i}+\Delta^{+}_{1\cdots M})\big]}\,}{{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{p_{i}+1}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\{m\}}\Gamma[p_{i}-m_{1\cdots M}+M+1]\prod_{\alpha=1}^{M}\displaystyle\frac{\,{\Gamma[m_{\alpha}-1+\Delta^{\pm}_{\alpha}]}\,}{m_{\alpha}!\text{sech}(\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\alpha})}\Big(\frac{-{\mathcal{E}}_{i}}{2K_{\alpha}}\Big)^{m_{\alpha}-1}.

(77)

The regular terms, as well as the derivation of (
4.2
), are collected in App.
C
.

5  Degenerate Stars

Given the importance of bilinear mixings to CC phenomenology, we use this section to discuss a simple set of tree graphs with bilinear mixings, which we call
degenerate stars
. In a degenerate star, all internal massive particles are linearly mixed with external massless or conformal scalars with arbitrary twists, as shown in Fig.
5
. In other words, all leaves are folded in a degenerate star. Thus, we can directly use our new representation to work out its full solution. In this section, we change the convention slightly and call the root site
E_{0}
(instead of
E_{1}
previously).

The degenerate star graph with
N
mixed propagators.

We denote the correlator of an
N
-pointed degenerate star graph by
\mathcal{S}_{N}
, and we adopt yet another overall factor to make

dimensionless. Explicitly, the SK time integral for

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{N}(E_{0};K_{1},\cdots,K_{N})\equiv

\displaystyle~{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{p_{0}+1}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Big[(2K_{j})^{p_{j}+4}\Big]\sum_{\mathsf{a}_{0},\cdots,\mathsf{a}_{N}=\pm}\int^{0}_{-\infty}{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{0}\,\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}_{0}(-\tau_{0})^{p_{0}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}_{0}E_{0}\tau_{0}}

(78)

where

We don’t have to do this integral again, as it is simply the folded limit of an

(79)

Under this limit, a complete set of

are given by

\displaystyle\bigg[\Big(\eta_{i}-\displaystyle\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}\Big)^{2}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}^{2}-u_{i}(\eta_{i}-1)(\bm{\eta}+p_{0}+1)\bigg]\mathcal{S}_{N}=\mathsf{C}_{i}\big[\mathcal{S}_{N}\big],

It should be noted that this limit does not change the analytical property with respect to

Complete solution

Once again, the solution to the equation (

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{N}=\text{CIS}\,\big[\mathcal{S}_{N}\big]+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathop{\text{Cut}}_{K_{j}}\big[\mathcal{S}_{N}\big]+\sum_{i\neq j}\mathop{\text{Cut}}_{K_{i},K_{j}}\big[\mathcal{S}_{N}\big]+\cdots+\mathop{\text{Cut}}_{\text{all~}K}\big[\mathcal{S}_{N}\big].

All these components can be gathered from the last section. First, the CIS is an MFT with all leaves folded, and can be obtained from (

\displaystyle\text{CIS}\,\big[\mathcal{S}_{N}\big]\equiv

\displaystyle~\big\llbracket{0({\mathring{1}})\cdots({\mathring{N}})}\big\rrbracket^{\prime}

\displaystyle~2\cos(\mbox{$\frac{\,{\pi}\,}{2}$}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\Delta\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{0}^{0})\sum_{\{m\}}\Gamma(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mum\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{0}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\Delta\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{0}^{0}-3)\prod_{i=1}^{N}\displaystyle\frac{\,{(\Delta_{i}^{0}-1)_{m_{i}}u_{i}^{m_{i}+\Delta_{i}^{0}}}\,}{(\Delta_{i}^{0}+\Delta_{i}^{\pm}-3)_{m_{i}+1}}.

\Delta_{i}^{{\mathsf{A}}}=\begin{cases}p_{i}+4,&({{\mathsf{A}}}=0\text{~and~}i=0,1,\cdots,N)\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}.&({{\mathsf{A}}}=\pm\text{~and~}i=1,\cdots,N)\end{cases}

The physical meaning of the scaling dimension

6.1
Squeezed limit

In CC physics, the oscillatory signal is most prominent in the squeezed limit

All three cases feature similar behavior in the squeezed limit, including a smooth background and an oscillatory signal. The scaling behaviors of the background and the signal as functions of

7  Conclusion and Outlook

The analytical studies of massive inflation correlators have revealed their rich and interesting underlying structures, which have certainly enriched our understanding of QFT amplitudes in cosmological spacetimes. Meanwhile, there is an intriguing interplay between this analytical program of correlators and high-energy particle phenomenology, which has received great boosts through the program of cosmological collider physics. The interplay is nicely illustrated by the degenerate kinematics from the bilinear mixing between the massless inflaton mode and a massive field, which is the central topic of this work.

To study degenerate kinematics, we proposed to use a new class of kinematic variables. After adopting this change of variables, the result gives us two small but nice surprises: First, when we use a series ansatz to solve the PDE system, we get first-order recursion relations, making it much easier to find a general term formula in terms of hypergeometric functions. Second, when we take the folded limit to reach bilinear mixings, the result features a reduction of transcendental weight. These are surprises to us since we are not mathematically sophisticated enough to elucidate the underlying reasons. Nevertheless, we can exploit these facts and find significantly simpler expressions for graphs with bilinear mixings, including the classic examples of degenerate stars that are of great importance for CC phenomenology.

There are obvious applications of our new variables, including graphs with spins and chemical potentials. They certainly deserve future explorations, but we do not have much to say about them here. Instead, we want to finish this work with a discussion on a “tension” between the transcendental weight of a graph and its degenerate kinematics.

It may be useful to note that the reduction of transcendental weight is not automatic in two senses: A trivial one is that it does not show up with the “old variables” of
r_{(\alpha i)}=K_{\alpha}/E_{i}
; Less trivial is that, even with the new variables, the weight reduction does not happen for
every
degenerate limit. We have examples for the latter: Consider the two-site chain
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}(E_{1},E_{2},K)
, which is a weight-2 object, namely, with two-layer summations. When taking a single folded limit
E_{2}\rightarrow K
, we get a weight-1 result as elaborated in this work. However, when we take one more folded limit
E_{1,2}\rightarrow K
, the result is a number independent of any kinematic variable but is still a weight-1 series evaluated at a particular kinematic point. Another example is the 3-point function with triple massive exchange. It can be thought of as a particular limit of the 4-site star graph
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}(E_{0},E_{1},E_{2},E_{3},K_{1},K_{2},K_{3})
which is a weight-6 object. In this work, we have shown that taking three folded limits
E_{i}\rightarrow K_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
) all reduce the weight, leading to the 3-pointed degenerate star
\mathcal{S}_{3}
which is weight-3. However, it seems that the weight does not reduce further when we take one more limit
E_{0}\rightarrow 0
. Currently, we are not aware of any new variables that could achieve this reduction, and we suspect that the
E_{0}\rightarrow 0
limit does not reduce weight whatsoever. In our opinion, this may be the main reason for the difficulty of computing the triple-exchange bispectrum that people have considered for many years.

At a more conjectural level, the above observation also applies to loop graphs: From known examples, loop graphs are also represented by hypergeometric series. In this regard, increasing the number of loops would increase the weight but not the number of independent variables. Thus, a loop graph can also be thought of as a graph with degenerate kinematics. The absence of weight reduction for these “degenerate kinematics” may be a reason for the difficulty of loops as well.

Certainly, it remains a very interesting problem to find a series expression for the triple-exchange bispectrum that converges in at least part of the physical region. On the other hand, for tree graphs with degenerate kinematics, we have good numerical strategies to find their values quite efficiently. Thus, finding such a series expression seems to be more of a theoretical curiosity than a practical necessity. On the other hand, loops remain formidable even numerically. Therefore, we expect that our methods here may be of some use in tackling those more challenging problems.

In this appendix, we collect special functions used in the main text. First, following our previous works on similar topics, we use shorthand notations for the products and fractions of Euler
\Gamma

\displaystyle\Gamma\left[z_{1},\cdots,z_{m}\right]\equiv

\displaystyle~\Gamma(z_{1})\cdots\Gamma(z_{m}),

(156)

\displaystyle\Gamma\left[\begin{matrix}z_{1},\cdots,z_{m}\\

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{\Gamma(z_{1})\cdots\Gamma(z_{m})}\,}{\Gamma(w_{1})\cdots\Gamma(w_{n})}.

(157)

In the main text, we also frequently use the Pochhammer symbol
(a)_{n}\equiv\Gamma(a+n)/\Gamma(a)
.

Next, we list useful hypergeometric functions. In the literature, a collection of hypergeometric series have been well studied and designated with special names and we mention a few used functions here. More details about these functions can be found in
[
115

114
]
. First, consider the hypergeometric functions with a single variable. The (generalized) hypergeometric function
{}_{p}\text{F}_{q}
is defined by the following way when the series converges:

\displaystyle{}_{p}\text{F}_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1},\cdots,a_{p}\\
b_{1},\cdots,b_{q}\end{matrix}\middle|z\right]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\,{(a_{1})_{n}\cdots(a_{p})_{n}}\,}{(b_{1})_{n}\cdots(b_{q})_{n}}\displaystyle\frac{\,{z^{n}}\,}{n!}.

(158)

In the main text, we use the following dressed version of the hypergeometric function:

\displaystyle{}_{p}\mathcal{F}_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1},\cdots,a_{p}\\
b_{1},\cdots,b_{q}\end{matrix}\middle|z\right]=

\displaystyle~\Gamma\begin{bmatrix}a_{1},\cdots,a_{p}\\
b_{1},\cdots,b_{q}\end{bmatrix}{}_{p}F_{q}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1},\cdots,a_{p}\\
b_{1},\cdots,b_{q}\end{matrix}\middle|z\right]

\displaystyle~\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Gamma\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1}+n,\cdots,a_{p}+n\\
b_{1}+n,\cdots,b_{q}+n\end{matrix}\right]\displaystyle\frac{\,{z^{n}}\,}{n!}.

(159)

\text{F}_{1},\cdots,\text{F}_{4}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}\left[a\middle|\begin{matrix}b_{1},b_{2}\\

(161)

\displaystyle{}^{p+q}\mathcal{F}_{r+s}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1},\cdots,a_{p}\\
