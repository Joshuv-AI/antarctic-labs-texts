RPAS-derived orthomosaic dataset of Southern Elephant Seal breeding colonies on King George Island (2019–2024)
Authors: Katarzyna Fudala, Robert Józef Bialik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe KGI-SESMAP is a high-resolution dataset of 88 orthophotomosaics documenting southern elephant seal (Mirounga leonina) breeding colonies on King George Island, Antarctica. Acquired using remotely piloted aircraft systems (RPASs) between 2019 and 2024, surveys were conducted annually during the breeding season across three Antarctic Specially Protected Areas (ASPAs 128, 132, and 151). Imagery was collected with DJI RPASs and processed using a standardized photogrammetric workflow in Pix4Dmapper Version 4.6.4. The resulting georeferenced orthomosaics have ground sampling distances (GSDs) between 1.04 and 4.89 cm/pixel. This dataset captures reproductive phenology, fine-scale seal distributions, and environmental background conditions. It supports remote wildlife monitoring and enables high-resolution spatial analyses to study ecological dynamics and climate-related change in the rapidly warming Antarctic Peninsula region. All materials are openly available through the figshare data repository and include detailed quality control documentation. Beyond wildlife monitoring, the dataset is valuable for remote sensing validation and climate impact assessment, providing a robust scientific basis for developing management and conservation strategies within ASPAs.Subject terms: Population dynamics, Conservation biology, Marine biology

Background & Summary

The southern elephant seal (Mirounga leonina, SES) is a key predator in the Southern Ocean ecosystem, primarily feeding on mesopelagic prey such as cephalopods and fish1,2. The species forms four genetically distinct stocks3. Three of these occur within the Southern Ocean, each in a different sector: the South Georgia stock in the South Atlantic, the Kerguelen/Heard stock in the southern Indian Ocean, and the Macquarie stock in the southern Pacific sector. The fourth stock, Peninsula Valdés, is located on the Atlantic coast of Argentina4. Across all four stocks, the global population is estimated at approximately 749,385 individuals5. Among these, the South Georgia stock is by far the largest, accounting for over 50% of the global population, with about 96% of its individuals concentrated on South Georgia itself (~397,054 individuals6), and also including smaller subpopulations across the wider South Atlantic sector of the Southern Ocean5. These occur in the South Orkneys, South Shetlands, Avian Island, Falkland Island and the South Sandwich Islands, as well as more peripheral colonies on Gough and Bouvet. Within this broader distribution, the South Shetland Islands, according to estimates from the 2010s, represent only a small fraction (~14,276 individuals5) of the total abundance. The current status of this population remains unknown, yet the South Shetlands provide one of the few Antarctic breeding areas of the South Georgia stock, giving them particular value as a regional reference point for demographic and phenological studies in an Antarctic context. The available estimates for the South Shetland Islands were based on counts from King George Island, Nelson Island, Livingston Island, and Elephant Island. The dataset presented here7 focuses specifically on King George Island, encompassing all sites where the presence of breeding colonies of SES has been historically confirmed.

Although predominantly pelagic, SES exhibit a highly synchronised annual cycle that links breeding and moulting, both of which occur during haul-outs on land, with foraging at sea in between these two periods8. On the South Shetland Islands, males arrive in September and establish dominance hierarchies, leading to the formation of harems—temporary social units composed of a dominant bull and associated females9. Females haul out from the end of September to November10, often spending several days on the beach prior to giving birth, after which they nurse a single pup for about 23 days, fasting throughout and losing substantial body mass11. Pups are born with a dark lanugo coat and rapidly accumulate blubber, quadrupling their weight before weaning10. After separation, ‘weaners’ shed their natal coat and take on a lighter, brownish-grey appearance.

Following breeding, females return to sea for ~70 days to rebuild reserves before coming ashore again between December and February to moult, undergoing a second prolonged fasting period12. Moulting in males follows a distinct sequence: younger, non-breeding males generally haul out earlier, while large, dominant males that invested heavily in reproduction delay moulting until later in the season, once their condition has recovered13.

During the breeding season, individuals aggregate in coastal colonies within the species’ range, demonstrating strong site fidelity5. This phenomenon enables direct observation of the species’ responses to environmental variability14–16. Such responses can be assessed through indicators such as body condition, reproductive success, and population-level parameters including the number of breeding females, pup production, sex ratio, spatial distribution of breeding colonies, and the phenology of reproduction.

Climate change has emerged as a driver of ecological shifts in the Southern Ocean17 influencing sea surface temperature, sea ice extent and duration18, and large-scale oceanographic processes such as El Niño-Southern Oscillation and the Southern Annular Mode19–21. These processes have affected pinniped movement, foraging efficiency, reproductive timing, and survival3,22,23. Superimposed on these climatic pressures is a long history of anthropogenic disturbance. Commercial sealing and whaling from the 18th to the 20th centuries significantly disrupted trophic dynamics by drastically reducing populations of top predators24. The southern elephant seal itself was heavily exploited during the early sealing era25. Although contemporary industrial fisheries do not directly target SES prey species, they continue to influence Southern Ocean food webs. Changes at lower trophic levels can propagate through the ecosystem, ultimately affecting the foraging success and physiological condition of marine predators26,27.

In addition to these climatic and anthropogenic pressures, the health and stability of pinniped populations are increasingly threatened by the emergence of infectious diseases. Recent outbreaks of highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) among sub-Antarctic28 and Antarctic wildlife29 have underscored the vulnerability of pinniped populations to viral epizootics. Documented cases of HPAI-associated mortality in SES30 highlight the risk for disease transmission within dense breeding colonies, with potentially severe demographic consequences31.

The cumulative impacts of climatic, ecological, and anthropogenic stressors remain challenging to quantify due to regional variability, ecological complexity, and the scarcity of long-term datasets. Nevertheless, the SES is increasingly recognized as a sentinel species for monitoring the health and stability of Southern Ocean ecosystems5,27,32.

Recent advances in research technologies, particularly the use of remotely piloted aircraft systems (RPAS), have enabled non-invasive monitoring of Antarctic fauna across vast, remote, and often inaccessible areas33–36. RPAS-based aerial imagery facilitates the assessment of population size, spatial structure, and phenological dynamics at the landscape scale37. Repeated surveys produce a series of high-resolution orthomosaics that serve both as a permanent visual archive and as a valuable resource for comparative ecological studies.

An orthomosaic image, also referred to as an orthoimage or orthophoto, is a high-resolution aerial product generated, among other methods, from images acquired by an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)38. Depending on the operational context, data collection may be carried out through fully autonomous flight missions or by means of RPAS. Using specialized photogrammetric software and an automated orthorectification process, multiple high-overlap images are geometrically corrected and combined into a seamless, distortion-free representation of the surveyed area39. Orthorectification compensates for perspective, camera tilt, and lens distortion, thereby ensuring geometric uniformity across the mosaic while preserving the spatial resolution of the original imagery. Unlike standard aerial photographs, orthorectified imagery enables direct measurement of distances and areas, as each pixel is spatially consistent and uniformly scaled.

In workflows that incorporate ground control points (GCPs), orthomosaics can be anchored to real-world coordinates with high absolute positional accuracy, supporting precise geographic alignment across datasets and survey dates40. In the absence of GCPs, relative accuracy can instead be ensured through the acquisition of high-overlap imagery and consistent processing workflows. High forward and side overlap increases the number of tie points that can be automatically identified across adjacent images, which enhances the robustness of the photogrammetric bundle adjustment. A dense network of tie points reduces the propagation of positional errors and improves the internal consistency of the model, resulting in more accurate relative positioning of objects within the orthomosaic. This approach ensures reliable internal geometry, preserving relative distances and object shapes within a single orthomosaic, thus forming a basis for morphometric analyses and quantitative assessments. Nevertheless, systematic offsets in x, y, and z coordinates (representing the horizontal and vertical dimensions) occur without GCPs, as reflected in the automatically generated processing quality reports, and absolute geographic positioning is therefore less accurate compared to GCP-based approaches. The orthomosaics in this dataset7 were produced without GCP, relying on high-overlap imagery to ensure accurate relative positioning. In this case, georeferencing relied on the onbard GNSS positions recorded in the image EXIF metadata, which provided meter-level absolute accuracy but sufficient anchoring for downstream processing.

The dataset presented here7 was collected as part of the long-term monitoring programme of birds and pinnipeds conducted on King George Island by the Institute of Biochemistry and Biophysics of the Polish Academy of Sciences, supported logistically by the H. Arctowski Polish Antarctic Station. Since 2019, this programme has included a dedicated component focused on the breeding ecology of the SES, enabled by the application of RPAS-based remote sensing methods (KGI-SESMAP, 2019–2024). This dataset7 comprises 88 high-resolution, georeferenced RPAS-derived orthomosaics documenting SES breeding colonies over six reproductive seasons between 2019 and 2024. Data collection was conducted annually between September and December on King George Island (South Shetland Islands, Antarctic Peninsula region), one of the fastest-warming areas in the Southern Hemisphere41,42. Imagery was acquired within three Antarctic Specially Protected Areas (ASPAs): ASPA No. 128 (Western Shore of Admiralty Bay), ASPA No. 132 (Potter Peninsula), and ASPA No. 151 (Lions Rump), as presented in Fig. 1.

Temporal analysis of the dataset7 reveals pronounced seasonal and interannual patterns. Annual map production was highest in 2020 (19 maps), followed by 2023 (16), 2021 and 2024 (15 each), 2019 (13), and lowest in 2022 (10). Across all years, imagery acquisition was strongly concentrated between mid-September and mid-November, with October consistently representing the peak month. Notably, October 2020 alone accounted for 13 maps, reflecting the single most intensive mapping interval within the five-year monitoring period.

To date, only a subset of the dataset7 has been utilized for ecological analyses. Orthomosaics acquired in 2019 within ASPA 128 served as the basis for two published studies. The first investigated intra-seasonal changes in SES colony structure and abundance43, while the second used one of the orthomosaics as a reference layer to assist in identifying adult females and pups within a high-resolution time-series satellite image44. These studies underscore the dataset’s7 utility for both ground-truthing remote sensing products and conducting site-specific ecological assessments.

The orthomosaics offer spatially explicit visualizations of breeding aggregations, enabling detailed analyses of intra- and inter-seasonal variation in reproductive timing, colony size and structure, and spatial organization. An example visualization of such spatial structure is provided in Fig. 2, showing the distribution of harems at Patelnia Point on 24 October 2024. Importantly, by providing a consistent and standardised visual record across seasons and sites, this dataset7 contributes meaningfully to the long-term monitoring of SES populations and their responses to environmental change. The imagery offers a retrospective framework for evaluating shifts in phenology, population dynamics, and colony structure, particularly in the context of rapid climate-driven transformations occurring in the Antarctic Peninsula region, including glacier retreat, altered precipitation regimes, and diminishing sea ice. A georeferenced environmental context of the dataset7 supports integration with habitat maps, topographic models, and climate data, enabling interdisciplinary research. Methods

Site description

All surveys were conducted within three Antarctic Specially Protected Areas (ASPAs): ASPA 128 (Western Shore of Admiralty Bay), ASPA 132 (Potter Peninsula), and ASPA 151 (Lions Rump). ASPA 128 includes several sub-sites: Patelnia Point, Blue Dyke and Paradise Cove.

There are currently 76 Antarctic Specially Protected Areas (ASPAs) established under the Antarctic Treaty System to preserve unique environmental, scientific, historic, aesthetic, and wilderness values46. These areas are strictly regulated through Management Plans and entry requires official permits. The boundaries of all ASPAs are provided as shapefiles and, together with the corresponding management plans, can be accessed through the Antarctic Protected Areas Database maintained by the Secretariat of the Antarctic Treaty47. King George Island, the largest island of the South Shetland archipelago, contains seven ASPAs. Among them, ASPA 128, 132, and 151 are key sites for long-term ecological monitoring and conservation of representative ice-free terrestrial ecosystems, offering diverse habitats, landforms, and biological communities.

ASPA 128 Western Shore of Admiralty Bay (62°10′60″ S, 58°27′0″ W) covers approximately 18.04 km² and includes coastal and subcoastal habitats along the western shore of Admiralty Bay47. It hosts breeding colonies of Adélie (Pygoscelis adeliae), chinstrap (P. antarcticus) and gentoo (P. papua) penguins, as well as SES. The area has been the focus of continuous ecological and ornithological research since the 1970s. It was designated as Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI) No. 8 under Recommendation X-548.

ASPA 132 Potter Peninsula (62°15′0″ S, 58°38′60″ W) encompasses 2.17 km² of ice-free terrain characterized by complex topography, freshwater lakes, moss-dominated vegetation, and glacially influenced landscapes and is located on the east coast of Maxwell Bay47. It supports breeding colonies of Adélie and gentoo penguins, as well as SES, and serves as an important site for studies on glacial retreat and terrestrial ecosystem dynamics. It was designated as SSSI No. 13 under Recommendation XIII–849.

ASPA 151 Lions Rump (62°7′60″ S, 58°7′0″ W) is a smaller site (1.32 km²) located at the entrance of King George Bay47. It includes raised marine terraces, cliffs, and tundra vegetation. Due to minimal human disturbance, it serves as a valuable reference site for assessing natural ecosystem variability. Since the 2014/15 austral summer, ASPA 151 has also been designated as a CCAMLR Ecosystem Monitoring Program (CEMP) site, where standardized long-term monitoring of seabird populations is conducted50. It was designated as SSSI No. 34 under Recommendation XVI-251.

In the first two seasons, 2019 and 2020, aerial monitoring was conducted exclusively on the Patelnia Point locality within ASPA 128. The resulting orthomosaics provide a detailed depiction of reproductive phenology, however, other SES breeding sites known for those years within ASPA 128 were not surveyed. Ground-based observers recorded females accompanied by pups at the Blue Dyke and Rondel during the same seasons. From 2021 onwards, the scope of aerial surveys was progressively expanded. In 2021, the surveys encompassed all breeding sites known for that year within ASPAs 128 and 151. In 2022, nearly all breeding sites confirmed for that year within ASPA 128 were documented, except for Block Point and Paradise Cove, where only single females with pups were observed. That year, aerial surveys additionally included ASPA 132; however, unlike ASPAs 128 and 151, no ground surveys were conducted, and the flights did not cover the extreme western part of the area. Consequently, the presence of breeding aggregations in this part of ASPA 132 cannot be definitively ruled out. In 2023, all breeding sites known for that year within ASPAs 128 and 151 were surveyed. During the 2024 season, aerial coverage again included all breeding sites known for that year within ASPAs 128 and 151, with the exception of Llano Point in ASPA 128, where two females with pups were observed.

The spatial extent of individual breeding locations varied between seasons. These differences reflect interannual changes in the spatial distribution of breeding aggregations, as well as topographical variation driven by changes in beach extent due to tidal fluctuations. A detailed description of the areas surveyed in each season is provided in the following sections of this data descriptor. For standardization purposes, all surveyed locations were assigned alphanumeric zone labels (A–P), as shown in Fig. 1. These zone codes correspond directly to those listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Data collection

Between 2019 and 2024, high-resolution RGB imagery was collected through RPAS surveys, resulting in the generation of 88 orthomosaics. Survey timing was carefully aligned with the SES reproductive cycle. Flights were conducted from mid-September to early December, matching the seasonal phenology of the species, with peak female harem attendance at King George Island historically occurring around 25–28 October9,43,52. This phenologically targeted scheduling improved the accuracy of population estimates and ecological assessments, as illustrated in Fig. 4, which shows that the majority of RPAS flights were conducted near the peak haul-out period.

Weekly distribution (7-day bins) of RPAS flight activity across all survey seasons (2019–2024), conducted over breeding aggregations of the southern elephant seal (Mirounga leonina). Flight scheduling was concentrated around the expected maximum haul-out of breeding females (ca. 25–28 October), to coincide with peak colony attendance and enhance comparability across years.

Two types of RPASs were used: DJI Inspire 2 (hereafter Inspire) with a Zenmuse X5S camera (15 mm lens) and DJI Phantom 4 Pro v2.0 (hereafter Phantom) with an integrated 8.8 mm RGB camera. Both platforms enabled the collection of nadir RGB imagery during pre-programmed and/or manual flights, depending on terrain complexity and weather conditions. Automated grid missions (parallel flight lines with fixed image overlap) were employed where feasible, while manual flights (with adaptive control) were conducted in areas of rugged topography or dense wildlife aggregations requiring flexible and non-intrusive coverage.

Although manual operations introduced variability in flight geometry and image quality, they enabled broader and more adaptive spatial coverage. A hybrid strategy, combining automated and manual RPAS missions, was adopted to balance mapping consistency with operational flexibility.

Access to ASPAs and the conducting of RPAS flights over wildlife was authorised under Permits Nos. 2/2019, 2/2020, 4/2021, 1/2022, 13/2022, and 9/2023, issued by the Polish Antarctic Programme and approved by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Poland. RPAS operators held Aviation Personnel Certificates of Qualification issued in accordance with Polish licensing regulations. Between 2019 and 2022, operators were required to possess an Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Operator Certificate issued by the Polish Civil Aviation Authority. From 2023 onward, compliance with both the EU-wide certification system—holding at least an A1–A3 open category license—and national Polish authorization under the NSTS-06 standard scenario became mandatory for specific operations. Although the flights described in this study were carried out in Antarctica, outside the jurisdiction of the European Union and Polish national airspace, all operators held valid European licenses to ensure high safety standards. Final authorization for the missions, including assessment of environmental impact, was granted by the Polish Antarctic Programme. All flight operations conducted over breeding groups of SES adhered to the environmental guidelines53 for the use of RPAS near wildlife in Antarctica. Survey altitudes ranged from 50 to 220 meters above ground level (mean~79.8 m, median = 80 m). RPAS flights over SES were conducted in accordance with internally established monitoring guidelines, maintaining a minimum altitude of 50 m, which was never breached, even though no behavioral responses of the animals were observed. Most flights occurred between 65 and 80 m. Launch sites were generally situated ≥ 50 m from harems and other animals; in cases where terrain constraints required closer positioning, RPASs always ascended vertically to the minimum altitude before approaching animals, thereby avoiding low-altitude horizontal flights over breeding groups. In some instances, surveys were performed from a rigid inflatable boat (RIB), with RPAS likewise ascending to the prescribed minimum altitude prior to approaching the animals. These precautionary procedures are consistent with best-practice guidelines for RPAS use in ecological research54, emphasizing minimal disturbance. While species-specific responses to RPASs remain incompletely documented, the adoption of a conservative altitude threshold and adherence to these operational protocols ensure ethically responsible survey practices and minimize potential impacts on sensitive wildlife populations. Exact flight altitudes for all missions are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Image acquisition for the dataset7 achieved a mean ground sampling distance (GSD) of 1.77 cm/pixel, with values ranging from 1.04 to 4.89 cm/pixel. Across 88 survey missions, image overlap was most commonly approximately 80% frontal and 70% lateral (48 missions), with other frequently applied settings of 80–75% (18 missions) and 85–75% (9 missions). Lower overlaps, such as 75–70% or 60–50%, were observed only occasionally (Supplementary Table 1). Surveyed areas per mission ranged from 0.31 ha up to 132.56 ha. Patelnia was the most extensively covered site, with 58,796 images collected there. In total, across all locations and seasons 68,605 images were acquired, providing broad spatial and temporal coverage of the study sites.

Image processing

All imagery was processed using Pix4Dmapper version 4.6.4 (Pix4D S.A., Lausanne, Switzerland) with the “3D Maps” template. The workflow included image alignment and keypoint matching, followed by camera calibration and bundle adjustment. Subsequently, dense point clouds were generated, and digital surface models (DSMs) as well as orthomosaics were rendered, producing high-resolution, georeferenced outputs suitable for quantitative analysis.

To document survey context in a directly actionable way, six annual “Survey Conditions” figures were compiled (2019–2024) that accompany the dataset7: SurveyConditions_2019.png– SurveyConditions_2024.png. For each flight, the panels display the local survey window (UTC-3) with a ± 30-min buffer, the predicted tidal height (m) from CATS2008 v2023 tide model55 via Tidal Model Driver ver. 3.056 for MATLAB (predictions for central part of Admiralty Bay, −62.10°, −58.38°, coordinate format as output by the TMD) and the daily mean air temperature (T¯) for the survey date. Air temperature data for the survey dates originated from the automatic weather station (Arctowski AWS 62°09′34″ S, 58°28′06″ W) on the western shore of Admiralty Bay. Both the values for 2019–2023, as well as the technical specifications of the AWS and the associated data acquisition and processing methods, are documented in the data article57. These data are published in the dataset Daily meteorological data from automatic weather station Arctowski, King George Island, during 2018–202358. Values for 2024 were provided directly by Arctowski Station for the purpose of survey condition compilation. Site codes: PA.P. = Patelnia Point; BD. = Blue Dyke; LR. = Lions Rump; PC. = Paradise Cove; PO.P. = Potter Peninsula. Flights were conducted under dry, operational wind conditions (typically ~3–6 m s−1), therefore wind and precipitation time series are not included. These figures make explicit whether flights occurred on rising/falling tide and within which tidal range, aiding reuse and interpretation.

Data Records

The KGI-SESMAP dataset is available via the figshare repository7 and comprises a total of 88 RPAS-derived orthomosaics in GeoTIFF format, each accompanied by a corresponding Pix4D Quality Report in PDF format. Moreover, six annual context figures (SurveyConditions_YYYY.png) accompany the dataset and are described in Data collection.

All files are provided as individual, standalone items—without hierarchical folder structures by location or year—in order to ensure compatibility with the data repository and to avoid complications when downloading large files, many of which exceed 1 GB in size. This flat file organization reflects the structure presented in Supplementary Table 1. Files follow a consistent naming convention: Location_YYYY_MM_DD_orthomosaic.tif for orthomosaics and Location_YYYY_MM_DD_report.pdf for the corresponding Pix4D Quality Reports.

The complete dataset7 has a total size of 116 GB, and individual orthomosaic files range from 35.31 MB to 2.97 GB, depending on spatial extent and resolution. The data are provided in a structure optimized for integration with standard GIS platforms.

Technical Validation

Each output was georeferenced to WGS 84/UTM Zone 21S (EPSG: 32721) using onboard GNSS. The “3D Maps” template ensured consistent processing across all missions.

Each orthomosaic in the dataset7 is accompanied by a Pix4D Quality Report. These reports are automatically generated and may appear complex, but they contain interactive hyperlinks to Pix4D’s online documentation that explain each metric in detail. Users are not required to have specialized expertise in photogrammetry to interpret them.

Another common artifact is “ghosting”, caused by the movement of animals between consecutive images59. While higher image overlap generally improves the geometric quality of reconstruction, in the case of moving animals it may increase the likelihood of duplicated or blurred representations in the final orthomosaic (Fig. 5c,d). The target species exhibits relatively low mobility during the breeding season; nonetheless, ghosting artifacts may affect the interpretation of the number of animals in specific fragments of the orthomosaic (Fig. 5c).

Photogrammetric accuracy without GCPs/RTK

All orthomosaics in this dataset7 were produced without ground control points (GCPs), and none of the RPAS used RTK. As stated in the Pix4D documentation, GCPs are required to achieve centimeter-level absolute accuracy; without them, positional accuracy is limited to the meter scale. This limitation may lead to systematic horizontal or vertical offsets between survey dates, such that the same geographic location may not perfectly coincide across mosaics. For instance, in the Lions Rump_2024_10_23 survey, the Absolute Geolocation Variance reported by Pix4D indicated horizontal RMS errors of ~6 m and a vertical error exceeding 20 m, illustrating the limits of absolute positioning without GCPs/RTK.

Internal consistency of orthomosaics

At the same time, internal consistency was high. Mean reprojection errors were typically in the range of 0.11–0.17 px across projects. When combined with the ground sampling distance, this yields sub-centimeter measurement precision. For example, with a GSD of 1.7 cm/pixel and an MRE of 0.2 px, the positional uncertainty is ~0.34 cm. For adult individuals typically exceeding 2 m in length, this error is negligible, ensuring that 2D morphometric measurements derived from the orthomosaic are generally reliable; however, some features may be affected by the posture of the animal as captured and by image artifacts (e.g., ghosting).

Comparison across survey series

The survey series in this dataset7 differ in scale, resolution, and absolute geolocation accuracy. Blue Dyke comprises small-extent surveys (2.09–10.77 ha) with fine ground sampling distances (1.09–1.75 cm/pixel) and consistently low mean reprojection errors (0.132–0.209 px), supporting precise relative measurements. Patelnia is the largest and most variable series (9.32–132.56 ha) with ground sampling distances spanning 1.04–4.89 cm/pixel and mean reprojection errors 0.106–0.300 px, reflecting heterogeneous platforms and conditions. Lions Rump covers larger single blocks (38.20–111.70 ha) at 1.69–3.91 cm/pixel, with stable internal precision (0.111–0.163 px MRE) but the weakest absolute geolocation performance (multi-meter horizontal RMS and, in one case, a > 20 m vertical RMS in 2024.10.23). ASPA 132 (Potter Peninsula) occupies an intermediate niche (9.81–42.82 ha, 1.47–2.15 cm/pixel) with consistently low MRE (0.139–0.178 px). Paradise Cove includes a well-covered survey on 2021.11.05 (GSD 2.13 cm/pixel; MRE 0.166 px; RMS geolocation X = 1.31 m, Y = 4.54 m, Z = 1.33 m) and a very small, targeted flight on 2023.11.02 (GSD 1.07 cm/pixel; MRE 0.140 px; RMS geolocation X = 1.69 m, Y = 2.43 m, Z = 1.98 m).

Considerations for biological interpretation

The dataset7 exclusively documents one of the two principal terrestrial events in the annual cycle of SES: the breeding season. It does not encompass the moulting period, during which individuals haul out asynchronously at variable locations. While some temporary aggregations may occur during moulting, they are irregular and less cohesive than during reproduction, rendering systematic surveys more challenging. Consequently, the dataset7 allows quantification only of adult females, dominant males, and pups present in colony during the breeding season. Young males and non-breeding females are absent from the imagery at this time, as they remain at sea. Estimation of total population size therefore necessitates the application of species-specific correction factors or demographic models11,60,61.

During the female breeding haul-out, dominant males establish harems and maintain exclusive access to females, expelling subordinate males9, which may exhibit less pronounced sexual dimorphism than dominant bulls. This ensures that misidentification of males as females in orthomosaics is unlikely during the postpartum nursing phase. Female presence on the beach is staggered over several weeks, with births and a three-week lactation period creating a dynamic turnover; therefore, even surveys conducted at the apparent peak of female attendance will not capture all breeding females for the season.

Over the course of several weeks following the onset of the lactation period, females wean their pups10, and the spatial and social aggregation patterns within the colony progressively shift towards the weaners, which gradually form temporary groups on land before eventually dispersing to the marine environment. They also utilize nearby freshwater pools or shallow coastal waters to practice swimming and diving, making some individuals undetectable in aerial imagery. Similar to adult females during the breeding haul-out, weaners may exhibit a peak in onshore abundance; however, this does not imply that all individuals are simultaneously present, as their departure from the colony occurs over an extended interval.

On orthomosaics from the late phase of the breeding season, typically in late November, most females have already departed while weaners remain aggregated on land or in coastal pools. At this stage, young males may begin to appear at haul-out sites. Because they are not yet fully dimorphic, they may be erroneously identified as adult females in orthomosaic imagery. In parallel, juvenile individuals of both sexes may also occur, and given their intermediate body size and morphology, they may pose a risk of being misclassified as weaners in survey imagery.

According to the Management Plans of ASPAs 128, 132, and 151, these areas serve as habitat for a diverse assemblage of avian species and pinnipeds, some of which are opportunistically recorded in the orthomosaics. Fragments of Pygoscelis penguin colonies are visible at Patelnia (e.g., Patelnia_2019_11_20, a breeding groups of chinstrap penguins), Lions Rump (e.g., Lions Rump_2024_10_23, breeding groups of Adélie and gentoo penguins), and Potter Peninsula (e.g., Potter_Peninsula_2022_10_14, incipient breeding aggregations of gentoo penguins); it should be noted, however, that the dataset7 provides only partial spatial coverage of these taxa. Snowy sheathbills (Chionis albus) can also be observed in orthomosaics foraging within SES breeding colonies (e.g., Patelnia_2020_10_11), where their occurrence is closely associated with the phenology of SES, exploiting placental remains. Other bird species and pinniped individuals may also be present in the dataset7. It is important to emphasize that both the temporal window and the spatial extent of the aerial surveys were explicitly designed to document SES breeding aggregations. Consequently, the dataset7 does not provide systematic coverage of non-SES taxa and is not intented for deriving robust population estimates for species other than SES.
