The PRECISE framework: a structured approach to antibiogram interpretation for antimicrobial optimization at the bedside
Authors: Claudio Pérez-Oliva, Andrés Giglio, Marcio Borges-Sa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance claimed an estimated 4.95 million lives in 2019. When clinicians prescribe empiric therapy that follows guidelines, mortality drops by roughly a third; when they de-escalate on the basis of culture results, it drops by more than half. Yet the document that should drive both decisions, the antibiogram, is often read superficially or misinterpreted, particularly by physicians without infectious-disease training. Existing stewardship frameworks address prescribing principles, timing, or ecological impact, but none walks the clinician step by step through an individual susceptibility report and out the other side with a treatment plan. PRECISE is a seven-step mnemonic designed for that purpose. Each letter corresponds to a clinical action: Pathogen assessment, Resistance phenotype interpretation, Evaluate MIC, Curtail spectrum, Individualize PK/PD, Switch route, and Endpoint optimization. Three clinical vignettes (including one in which the steps deliberately collide) illustrate how the framework operates in practice.Keywords: Anti-bacterial agents, Antimicrobial stewardship, Microbial sensitivity tests, Drug resistance, Sepsis, Clinical decision-making

The antibiogram interpretation gap

Antimicrobial resistance is now among the leading causes of death worldwide, associated with an estimated 4.95 million deaths in 2019 [1]. Ask an internal-medicine resident how to read an antibiogram, and you will likely get a hesitant answer. In a US survey, 22% of residents did not know how to access their own institution’s cumulative antibiogram, and fewer than half had completed an infectious-disease rotation [2]. This is not a trivial knowledge gap. The Schuts meta-analysis showed that guideline-concordant empiric therapy reduces mortality by 35% (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.54–0.80) and that culture-guided de-escalation reduces it by 56% (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.30–0.66) [3]. In other words, how a clinician reads the susceptibility report has a measurable effect on whether the patient survives.

The stewardship community has built an impressive set of tools over the past two decades. The “5 D’s” (Diagnosis, Drug, Dose, Duration, De-escalation) provide a simple checklist [4]. Start Smart Then Focus structures the prescribing arc into initiation and a 48–72-hour review [5]. The AHRQ Four Moments framework offers four decision nodes [6]. Australia’s MIND ME captures prescribing principles in six letters [7]. The antibiotic time-out concept systematizes reassessment at 48–72 hours [8]. The WHO AWaRe classification sorts drugs by ecological risk [9]. These are useful, but they all share a limitation: none of them tell the clinician what to do with the specific susceptibility report sitting in front of them.

On the laboratory side, the European tradition of interpretive reading, systematized by Courvalin in the 1990s and codified in the EUCAST expert rules [10,11], offers a rigorous method for deducing resistance mechanisms from phenotypic patterns. But it was built by microbiologists for microbiologists. It stops at mechanism identification. It does not extend to drug selection, dosing, route, or duration.

The result is a gap between two established traditions: stewardship frameworks that address the “when” and “what” of prescribing and interpretive reading that addresses the “what mechanism.” Neither provides a systematic “how” for the frontline clinician at the moment of antibiogram review. Three recent developments have made this gap clinically urgent. First, the EUCAST redefinition of “I” as “Susceptible, Increased Exposure” (v9.0, 2019) demands that clinicians understand PK/PD optimization to use “I” results correctly [12,13]. Second, mechanism-specific drugs for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales now require knowing the carbapenemase type (KPC, OXA-48, or NDM) to select the right agent [14]. Third, a wave of landmark trials (BLING-III, BALANCE, POET, OVIVA, and SABATO) has redefined how we dose, for how long, and by what route, but none of this evidence reaches the patient unless it is integrated into bedside reasoning.

This viewpoint proposes PRECISE, a seven-step mnemonic that guides non-ID clinicians from antibiogram receipt to treatment optimization.

The PRECISE framework

Each letter in PRECISE represents a clinical action. The steps are intentionally sequential: the output of one feeds the input of the next, creating a structured pathway from organism identification through treatment endpoint. Figure 1 summarizes the seven steps and their sequence.

P – Pathogen: identify and validate

Nothing else matters if the organism on the report is a contaminant. Before touching the susceptibility panel, the clinician should ask: “is this a true pathogen?”

For blood cultures, the answer is usually straightforward. S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and Candida species represent true bacteremia in over 90% of episodes. Coagulase-negative staphylococci are contaminants in the majority of cases; the decision hinges on the number of concordant sets (≥2 with matching species and antibiogram), species identity (S. lugdunensis is always a pathogen), and the clinical picture [15]. The CDC benchmark for contamination is <3% [16].

Intrinsic resistance serves as a built-in quality check. If an Enterococcus appears susceptible to cephalosporins or P. aeruginosa susceptible to ertapenem, something is wrong: either the identification or the susceptibility test. The EUCAST intrinsic resistance tables (v3.3, 2021) provide the reference [17]. Other critical intrinsic resistances: S. maltophilia versus carbapenems; Proteus mirabilis versus nitrofurantoin; and Serratia marcescens versus ampicillin, first/second-generation cephalosporins, and colistin [17].

Local epidemiology completes the picture. The institutional cumulative antibiogram (CLSI M39) [18] or, where available, a weighted-incidence syndromic combination antibiogram (WISCA) [19] contextualizes individual results and flags unusual phenotypes that warrant confirmation.

R – Resistance: interpret the phenotype

Once the organism is validated, the next question is: what resistance mechanism is driving this phenotype? This was once an academic exercise; it is now therapeutically essential. With mechanism-specific drugs available for CRE, identifying the carbapenemase type directly determines which drug to use [14].

ESBLs: The classic pattern, third-generation cephalosporin resistance with clavulanate synergy and cefoxitin susceptibility, remains the key phenotypic marker. MERINO settled the treatment debate: piperacillin-tazobactam is inferior to meropenem for ESBL-producing E. coli/K. pneumoniae bacteremia [20]. The 2024 IDSA guidance is clear: novel BL/BLI combinations are reserved for CRE, not for ESBL producers [14].

Other indicators, such as the D-test for inducible clindamycin resistance, are mandatory on erythromycin-resistant, clindamycin-susceptible staphylococci and streptococci [25]. Stepwise fluoroquinolone resistance suggests accumulating gyrA/parC mutations or plasmid-mediated mechanisms (PMQR). Selective aminoglycoside resistance patterns point to specific modifying enzymes that determine which agent retains activity [10].

E – Evaluate MIC: calibrate beyond S/I/R

The S/I/R category is a summary, not the whole story. The actual MIC value matters in several clinical scenarios [26]:

Site-specific breakpoints: EUCAST sets distinct thresholds by infection site. Penicillin for S. pneumoniae: S ≤0.06 mg/L in meningitis versus S ≤2 mg/L in pneumonia. Missing this distinction can mean treating meningitis with a drug that the laboratory technically calls “susceptible”.

MIC creep: For MRSA bacteremia, vancomycin MICs ≥1.5 mg/L by Etest correlate with higher treatment failure and mortality even within the susceptible range [27]. When the MIC approaches the breakpoint, alternative agents (daptomycin, ceftobiprole) deserve consideration.

The EUCAST “I” redefinition: Since Breakpoint Table v9.0, “I” means “Susceptible, Increased Exposure”: not intermediate, not a grey zone, but an explicit instruction to use higher doses, prolonged infusions, or both [12,13]. For P. aeruginosa, where wild-type organisms fall into “I” for multiple antipseudomonals, this means optimized dosing is not optional: it is the default.

C – Curtail spectrum: de-escalate with confidence

The instinct to keep broad coverage “just in case” is among the hardest habits to break in antimicrobial prescribing. The evidence, however, is unambiguous: de-escalation is safe.

A systematic review found reduced ICU mortality with de-escalation (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.52–0.88) [29]. A target trial emulation in 36,924 patients with community-onset sepsis across 67 hospitals found that de-escalation from anti-MRSA or antipseudomonal coverage was associated with similar 90-day mortality, fewer antibiotic days (RR 0.91), and shorter hospital stays (RR 0.88) [30]. Despite this, the DIANA study found that only 16% of ICU patients actually undergo de-escalation, a striking gap between evidence and practice [31].

Not all antimicrobials are equal in their collateral damage. A large case-control study (159,404 CDI cases) quantified the hierarchy: clindamycin topped the list (OR 25.39), followed by later-generation oral cephalosporins (OR 9–12) and amoxicillin-clavulanate (OR 8.53), while doxycycline was neutral and minocycline was actually protective (OR 0.79) [32]. Fluoroquinolone restriction programs have driven substantial reductions in CDI, largely through the elimination of fluoroquinolone-resistant lineages [33]. The WHO AWaRe classification provides an additional lens for assessing ecological impact [9]. The practical rule is simple: once you know the pathogen and its susceptibility profile, pick the narrowest agent that can achieve adequate exposure at the site of infection. Which leads directly to the next step.

I – Individualize PK/PD: match drug to patient and site

Choosing the right drug is not enough. Delivering it at the right dose, through the right infusion strategy, to the right anatomical compartment is what separates adequate from optimal therapy.

PK/PD targets: β-lactams are time-dependent (target: 100% fT>MIC in critical illness); aminoglycosides are concentration-dependent (Cmax/MIC ≥8–10); vancomycin and fluoroquinolones are AUC/MIC-driven (vancomycin AUC/MIC 400–600 per the 2020 ASHP/IDSA/PIDS/SIDP consensus) [34,35].

Prolonged β-lactam infusion: The evidence for prolonged infusion is now strong, though not unequivocal. In BLING-III (7,031 patients, 104 ICUs), continuous infusion did not significantly reduce 90-day mortality in the primary analysis (24.9% vs 26.8%; OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.81–1.01; p=0.08). The companion Bayesian meta-analysis (17 RCTs, 9,014 patients) found a risk ratio of 0.86 (95% credible interval 0.72–0.98), a 99.1% posterior probability of benefit, and a number needed to treat of 26. Seven professional societies endorse prolonged infusion as preferred practice [36–38]. Intermittent bolus dosing of β-lactams in critically ill patients should now be considered suboptimal.

S – Switch route: transition to oral when appropriate

Three randomized trials have fundamentally changed how we think about IV-to-oral transition:

POET (NEJM 2019) randomized 400 patients with stable left-sided endocarditis to oral step-down versus continued IV after ≥10 days. Oral was noninferior (composite endpoint 9.0% vs. 12.1%); hospital stays dropped from 19 to 3 days. At a 5.4-year follow-up, mortality was actually lower with an oral switch: 23.4% versus 35.2% (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.42–0.88) [41,42].

OVIVA (NEJM 2019) enrolled 1,054 patients with bone and joint infections. Oral was noninferior to IV (failure 14.6% vs. 13.2%), and IV catheter complications dropped from 9.4% to 1.0% [43].

SABATO (Lancet Infect Dis 2024) showed noninferiority of early oral switch in low-risk S. aureus bacteremia, though it was terminated early. The larger SNAP trial is ongoing [44].

A 2025 systematic review (38 studies) confirmed non-inferiority of oral switch in bacteremia, with hospital stay reduced by 5.19 days [45]. The key to success: high-bioavailability agents. Levofloxacin (~99%), linezolid (~100%), TMP-SMX (~90–100%), metronidazole (~100%), fluconazole (~90%), and clindamycin (~90%) are the workhorses of step-down therapy.

E – Endpoint: optimize duration and assess response

The final step asks two questions: “How long should we treat?”, and “is the treatment working?”

Duration: BALANCE (NEJM 2025; 3,608 patients, 74 hospitals, 7 countries) established that 7 days is noninferior to 14 for non-S. Aureus bacteremia (90-day mortality 14.5% vs. 16.1%) [46]. STOP-IT showed 4±1 days suffice for complicated intraabdominal infections with source control [47]. ATS/IDSA 2019 endorses ≥5 days for CAP [48]. The pattern is consistent: shorter courses, when supported by clinical stability, perform as well as longer ones.

Positioning PRECISE within the stewardship landscape

The Cochrane review by Davey et al. (221 studies) demonstrated a 15% increase in prescribing compliance and a 1.95-day reduction in treatment duration with stewardship interventions [53]. Mnemonic-based educational tools improve pharmacotherapy decision-making in a randomized trial [54]. Yet the field suffers from a behavioral science deficit: only 4 of 2,014 records in a scoping review described theory-informed acute care stewardship interventions [55].

PRECISE targets the capability domain of the COM-B behavioral model [56] by providing a cognitive scaffold for a task that most non-ID clinicians find difficult. Structured antibiogram training increased resident comprehension from 57% to 89% (p < 0.001) in a quality improvement study [57]. Integration with clinical decision support systems (CDSS) could amplify the effect: a meta-analysis of 57 AMS-CDSS studies found an OR of 2.28 for appropriate therapy and an 18% relative mortality reduction [58]. In Spain, national PROA consensus documents and educational, non-restrictive stewardship programmes have shown that structured interventions can improve prescribing and reduce antimicrobial consumption without compromising outcomes [59,60]; PRECISE is designed to operationalize that philosophy at the level of the individual susceptibility report. Limitations and future directions

PRECISE is a proposed framework, not yet validated. Its main limitations are the absence of prospective outcome data, dependence on MIC availability (not universal in resource-limited settings), the assumption of adequate laboratory infrastructure, and the well-documented difficulty of translating any structured tool into sustained behavioral change [55,56]. Priority next steps include before-and-after implementation studies, cluster-randomized educational trials comparing PRECISE against standard stewardship training, CDSS integration studies, and adaptation research in low- and middle-income settings where the AMR burden is highest but microbiological resources are most constrained.

Conclusion

The antibiogram remains one of the most powerful and underused tools in clinical medicine. PRECISE offers a structured pathway from susceptibility report to treatment plan, integrating interpretive microbiology with therapeutic optimization evidence. Each step rests on robust data: BLING-III for prolonged infusion [36], BALANCE for duration [46], and POET/OVIVA/SABATO for route switching [41–44]. Together with its Spanish-language companion MICROBE, PRECISE provides a globally applicable, evidence-grounded framework for what may be the most consequential daily decision in hospital medicine: how to turn a susceptibility report into the right treatment for the patient in front of you.
