Surface layers and linearized water waves: a boundary integral equation framework
Travis Askham, Tristan Goodwill, Jeremy Hoskins, Peter Nekrasov, Manas Rachh

Abstract
The dynamics of surface waves traveling along the boundary of a liquid medium are changed by the presence of floating plates and membranes, contributing to a number of important phenomena in a wide range of applications. Mathematically, if the fluid is only partly covered by a plate or membrane, the order of derivatives of the surface-boundary conditions jump between regions of the surface. In this work, we consider a general class of problems for infinite depth linearized surface waves in which the plate or membrane has a compact hole or multiple holes. For this class of problems, we describe a general integral equation approach, and for two important examples, the partial membrane and the polynya, we analyze the resulting boundary integral equations. In particular, we show that they are Fredholm second kind and discuss key properties of their solutions. We develop flexible and fast algorithms for discretizing and solving these equations, and demonstrate their robustness and scalability in resolving surface wave phenomena through several numerical examples.

Introduction

Linear water wave theories assume that water is an inviscid, incompressible, and
irrotational fluid and that the amplitudes of surface waves are small relative
to their wavelength and the depth of the water. These assumptions allow the fluid motion to be modeled by potential flow, with the kinematic and dynamic boundary conditions prescribed on a fixed surface. The simplicity of the resulting equations
makes them well-suited to analytical and numerical calculations, and the theory has a long history of success in predicting physical phenomena at many scales, from the coarsening and flexing of biological membranes
[
22
]
to the arrival of transoceanic infragravity waves at Antarctic ice shelves
[
14
]
.

The aim of the present work is to demonstrate that, particularly for “deep water” models in which the fluid occupies a half-space,
there is an effective, simple, and unified approach to the numerical solution of these mixed-order boundary condition problems
based on integral equation formulations. More concretely, we consider linear water waves in the time harmonic setting,
in which the spatial dependence of the fluid flow is written in terms of a velocity potential
\phi:H^{-}\to\mathbb{C}

where

\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We let
\Omega
be a bounded domain on the fluid surface

denote

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (\Delta+\partial_{z}^{2})\phi&=0\,,\,&&\text{ in }H^{-}\,,\\
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{ext}}[\phi,\partial_{z}\phi]&=0\,,\,&&\text{ in }D\setminus\overline{\Omega}\,,\\
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{int}}[\phi,\partial_{z}\phi]&=f\,,\,&&\text{ in }\Omega\,,\\
\mathcal{B}[\phi,\partial_{z}\phi]&=g\,,\,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\,,\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\Delta:=\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}
is the Laplace operator in two dimensions. See
Figure

1
for an illustration. The first equation corresponds to an incompressibility condition in the fluid bulk, the second and third conditions correspond to dynamic boundary conditions on the exterior and interior regions of the fluid surface, respectively, and the last equation corresponds to an in-plane boundary condition which may be vector-valued, depending on the order of the operators
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{ext}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{int}}
.

The inputs to
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{ext}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{int}}
should be understood as the traces of
\phi
and
\partial_{z}\phi
on
D
.
Note that the trace of
\partial_{z}\phi
on
D
is the vertical velocity of the surface of the fluid, which is related to the vertical displacement through a kinematic condition, while
\phi
is related to the pressure of the fluid through Bernoulli’s principle. These two quantities are connected through
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{ext}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{int}}
, which have the following general form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}[\phi,\partial_{z}\phi]=p(-\Delta)\partial_{z}\phi-\phi\;,

(1.2)

where
p
is a polynomial. Quadratic terms in
p
account for flexural effects, linear terms for
elastic and surface tension effects, and constant terms for inertial and gravitational effects.
In this work, we assume that the polynomial degree is greater for the
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{ext}}
operator than it is
for the
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{int}}
operator, though the case where this condition is reversed can be treated with similar tools.

Problems of a similar flavor have been considered in a number of different contexts. Many studies have examined the effects of continuous changes in the surface properties such as the thickness of an ice cover
[
59
,
72
,
7
,
54
,
3
]
or variations in surface tension
[
20
,
64
]
. These papers typically look at simplified geometries or idealized settings using semi-analytical approaches. It is also possible to have a discontinuous jump in the coefficients on the surface, where it is typically necessary to prescribe additional transmission or continuity conditions. Some early work looked at discontinuities in the surface tension for simple geometries, such as on an infinite flat boundary with the help of a Fourier-type integral representation
[
31
]
or the Wiener-Hopf method
[
28
]
, or on circular domains with the help of truncated Bessel series
[
19
]
or the first order Born approximation
[
20
]
.

Perhaps the most closely related method is that of
[
8
]
, which focuses on the polynya problem in the finite depth case. In their approach, the solutions in the ice-covered and ice-free regions are expanded in terms of vertical modes. Green’s identities are then employed to reduce the problem to a system of integro-differential equations along the boundary of these two regions, which are solved using a Galerkin method. While this method offers the flexibility needed to incorporate draft and finite depth, it involves discretizing the fluid where no significant dynamics occur. In contrast, our approach seeks to reduce the problem to an integral equation defined solely on the surface of the polynya or, more generally, the object responsible for scattering. We remark that it is similar in spirit to the
surface wave preconditioners
developed in
[
6
,
5
]
for singular waveguides and topological insulators.

The numerical approach advocated here is characterized by the use of a nested integral representation of the
solution. The original three dimensional problem in
D
is first reduced to a two-dimensional integro-differential
equation on the surface
D
using a
single layer
integral representation for
\phi
. The resulting integro-differential
equation, defined on the infinite interface
D
, is further reduced to the compact set
\Omega

using the Green’s function for the integro-differential analog of the
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{ext}}
operator. We show that it
is relatively straightforward to select this second representation so that the resulting equation is a Fredholm second
kind integral equation system defined on
L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\partial\Omega)^{m}
, where
m
denotes the number of boundary conditions imposed by
\mathcal{B}
. More details of this overall framework are provided in
Section

2
and the
Green’s functions and their properties are derived in
Section

3
.
We derive appropriate representations for two common applications and discuss the invertibility of
the resulting integral equation systems in
Section

4
.

Once suitable integral representations are defined for these problems, the corresponding integral equations can be
discretized using standard tools. We describe a discretization scheme in
Section

5.1
,
based on a high-order triangulation of
\Omega
and panelization of
\partial\Omega
. To treat a mild singularity
which develops in
\partial_{z}\phi
near the interface, we apply adaptive refinement near the boundary of
\Omega
. The resulting linear systems are dense and require acceleration to scale to larger problems. While the system matrices have similar rank structure
to the matrices resulting from integral equation representations of PDE solutions, namely that
submatrices corresponding to well-separated sets of source and target points are low rank, many of the common
fast algorithm techniques do not apply directly to these systems. We describe a simple and efficient scheme for
accelerating matrix-vector multiplication for these systems based on pre-corrected FFTs in
Section

5.2
.
The scheme uses
proxy annuli
to efficiently compress interactions of points which are physically well-separated. Similar ideas have been employed for compressing kernels arising in Gaussian process regression, see
[
52

, for example. Finally, in
Section

6
we present several numerical examples illustrating the efficacy of the proposed framework. We conclude with
Section

7

2
Overview of the method

We represent the velocity potential
\phi
in the fluid domain using the three-dimensional Laplace single layer
\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}
applied to an unknown density
\sigma
defined on the surface (see also
[
20
,
30
,
23
,
57
,
3
]
):

\phi(\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}})=\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}[\sigma](\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}):=\operatorname{p.\!v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}-\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}^{\prime}|}\sigma({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,,

(2.1)

where
\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}=[x,y,z]^{T},\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}^{\prime}=[x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0]^{T},\,{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}=[x^{\prime},y^{\prime}]^{T}
, and the principal value integral (
\operatorname{p.\!v.}
) is defined as

\operatorname{p.\!v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{|{\mathbf{r}}|\leq R}f({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,.

The principal value definition can accommodate slowly decaying but oscillatory densities, like the ones we will obtain for certain parameters. Applying the ansatz
eq.

2.1
ensures that
\phi
satisfies Laplace’s equation and decays in the fluid region
H^{-}.
All that remains is to choose the density
\sigma
to satisfy the boundary conditions on
D.

The standard jump relations for the Laplace single layer potential
[
43
]
imply that the limit of
\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}[\sigma]
can be taken continuously to the boundary for any sufficiently smooth density
\sigma
with mild conditions on the decay and oscillations at infinity, i.e. letting
\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}=[x,y,0]^{T},\,{\mathbf{r}}=[x,y]^{T},\,\mathbf{z}=[0,0,1]^{T}
, and
h>0
we have

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\to 0\end{subarray}}\mathcal{S}_{{\operatorname{3d}}}[\sigma](\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}-h\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{S}_{{\operatorname{3d}}}[\sigma](\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}})\,,

while the normal derivative is given by

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\to 0\end{subarray}}\partial_{z}\mathcal{S}_{{\operatorname{3d}}}[\sigma](\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}-h\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{2}\sigma({\mathbf{r}})+\partial_{z}\mathcal{S}_{{\operatorname{3d}}}[\sigma](\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}})=\frac{1}{2}\sigma({\mathbf{r}})\;.

Substituting the single layer potential representation for
\phi
into (
1.2
) and applying these jump relations allows us to write the boundary operator (
1.2
) in terms of the density
\sigma({\mathbf{r}})
. We call this new integro-differential operator
\mathcal{L}[\sigma]
:

\mathcal{L}[\sigma]({\mathbf{r}}):=\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}[\sigma],\sigma/2]({\mathbf{r}})=\frac{1}{2}p(-\Delta)\sigma({\mathbf{r}})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{4\pi|{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}|}\sigma({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\;.

(2.2)

Similarly, substituting our ansatz for
\phi
into the in-plane boundary operator
\mathcal{B}

in (
1.1
) yields another operator
\mathcal{M}[\sigma]:=\mathcal{B}[\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}[\sigma],\sigma/2]
. This converts the three-dimensional boundary value problem problem given by (
1.1
) into a two-dimensional surface problem of the form:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ext}}[\sigma]&=0\,,\,&&\text{ in }D\,\backslash\,\overline{\Omega}\,,\\
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{int}}[\sigma]&=f\,,\,&&\text{ in }\Omega\,,\\
\mathcal{M}[\sigma]&=g\,,\,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\,,\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(2.3)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ext}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{int}}
are integro-differential operators of the general form (
2.2
), with the order of the highest derivative of
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ext}}
being strictly greater than the order of the highest derivative in
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{int}}
, and
\mathcal{M}
a possibly vector-valued operator.

In order to solve this problem, we introduce the following new ansatz involving both a
surface-volume
density
\mu\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and a
surface-boundary
density
\eta\in(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{m}
:

\sigma({\mathbf{r}})=\int_{\Omega}V({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\mu({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})+\int_{\partial\Omega}B({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\cdot\eta({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,,

(2.4)

where
V({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}):\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
,
B({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}):\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
, and
m
is the number of vector components of
\mathcal{M}
.
If the kernels
V({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})
and
B({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})
are constructed out of the Green’s function corresponding to the exterior operator, then the ansatz (
2.4
) will satisfy the homogeneous equation in the exterior. We defer a detailed proof of this to
Section

3
. Thus, the only constraints on the densities
\mu
and
\eta
result from the failure of the ansatz (
2.4
) to satisfy the interior equation
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{int}}[\sigma]=f
in
\Omega
and the boundary condition
\mathcal{M}[\sigma]=g
on
\partial\Omega
. Substituting our ansatz into these two equations results in a system of coupled surface-volume and surface-boundary integral equations defined on
L^{2}(\Omega)\times(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{m}\to L^{2}(\Omega)\times(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{m}
:

\displaystyle T_{0}\mu({\mathbf{r}})+\int_{\Omega}K_{00}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\mu({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\partial\Omega}K_{0j}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\eta_{j}({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=f({\mathbf{r}})\,,

(2.5)

\displaystyle T_{i}\eta_{i}({\mathbf{r}})+\int_{\Omega}K_{i0}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\mu({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\partial\Omega}K_{ij}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\eta_{j}({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=g_{i}({\mathbf{r}})\,,

(2.6)

for
i=1,\dots,m
. There are two different ways in which the identity term
T
appears. In the surface-volume equation (
2.5
) the term
T_{0}\mu
appears because the interior operator
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{int}}
applied to the kernel
V({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})
contains a delta function which is handled explicitly. In the surface-boundary equations (
2.6
) the identity terms
T_{i}\eta_{i}
appear when taking the exterior traces of the layer potentials at the boundary. In other words, we define
T_{i}\eta_{i}
as the difference between the off-surface limit of the integral operator and its on-surface value, i.e. for
{\mathbf{r}}\in D\setminus\overline{\Omega}
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{0}\in\partial\Omega
we have:

T_{i}\eta_{i}({\mathbf{r}}_{0}):=\lim_{{\mathbf{r}}\to{\mathbf{r}}_{0}}\int_{\partial\Omega}K_{ii}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\eta_{i}({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})-\operatorname{p.\!v.}\int_{\partial\Omega}K_{ii}({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\eta_{i}({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})

where the limit is taken in the normal direction. Then, the kernels in the integral equations (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) are defined as:

\displaystyle K_{00}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{int}}[V(\ \cdot\ ,{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})]({\mathbf{r}})-T_{0}\delta({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,,

\displaystyle K_{0j}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{int}}[B_{j}(\ \cdot\ ,{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})]({\mathbf{r}})\,,

\displaystyle K_{i0}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{i}V({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,,

\displaystyle K_{ij}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{i}B_{j}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,.

In this paper, through two representative examples, we show how to construct the kernels
V({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})
and
B({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})
so that equations (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) form a system of Fredholm integral equations of the second kind. In general, a Fredholm integral equation of the second kind is an integral equation of the form:

(I+K)u=f

for
u,f\in X
, where
X
is a Banach or Hilbert space,
I
is the identity operator, and
K:X\to X
is a compact integral operator acting on
X
. Such equations have a number of numerical and analytical advantages, the most notable being that the condition number of the corresponding discrete linear system remains bounded under suitable refinement of the discretization
[
43
]
.

The solution
\phi
to the boundary value problem (
1.1
) can be retrieved from the ansatz (
2.4
) through the following formula:

\phi(\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}-\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}^{\prime}|}\int_{\Omega}V({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}})\mu({\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\\
+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}-\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}^{\prime}|}\int_{\partial\Omega}B({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}})\cdot\eta({\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})\,,

(2.7)

where
\mathbf{r}_{\operatorname{3d}}^{\prime}=[x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0]^{T}
and
{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}=[x^{\prime},y^{\prime}]^{T}
. Later, we show that the integrals above can be interchanged and that simple analytical formulas are available for
\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}[V(\,\cdot\,,{\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}})]({\mathbf{r}})
and
\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}[B(\,\cdot\,,{\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}})]({\mathbf{r}})
when
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime\prime},{\mathbf{r}}\in D
. Using these formulas,
\phi
can be computed on surface by evaluating integrals over finite regions.

Notation and assumptions

In the remainder of the paper, we assume that
\Omega
is a bounded, open domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, whose boundary,
\partial\Omega
,
is a smooth, regular curve. The in-plane outward-pointing normal, positively-oriented tangent, and
signed curvature at a point
{\mathbf{r}}\in\partial\Omega
are denoted by
{\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{r}})
,
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})
, and
\kappa({\mathbf{r}})
,
respectively, and the dependence on

is dropped when it is clear from context. For a function of two variables,

, normal derivatives are denoted by

\partial_{{\mathbf{n}}}K({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})={\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{r}})\cdot\nabla_{{\bf w}}K({\bf w},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})|_{{\bf w}={\mathbf{r}}}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\partial_{{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}}K({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})={\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{\bf w}K({\mathbf{r}},{\bf w})|_{{\bf w}={\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}}\;,

where

, as appropriate. A similar notation is used for tangential derivatives. Regularity results below are described using the standard Sobolev spaces, denoted
H^{s}
. By
H^{s}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we
mean the space of functions

such that
\varphi f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for any compactly-supported,
smooth
\varphi
.

For several quantities in the analysis, the restriction of a function
to the boundary

Let
\tilde{\Omega}\subset D

\overline{\Omega}
.
For a function

denotes the trace of

\partial\Omega

which is well-defined provided the restriction

Likewise,
\gamma_{0}^{+}f
denotes the trace of

on

[[f]]:=\gamma_{0}^{+}f-\gamma_{0}^{-}f
. The notations

and

In many cases a layer potential with an

sense), even though the

\Omega

might not have sufficient regularity

3
Green’s functions and regularity of integral operators

Let the operator
\mathcal{L}
be in the form of (
2.2
) and let

d_{p}
be the degree of
p
. The fundamental solution
G_{\operatorname{S}}
satisfying the equation

\mathcal{L}[G_{\operatorname{S}}(\ \cdot\ ,{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})]({\mathbf{r}})=\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\;,

(3.1)

together with suitable radiation conditions at infinity, can be explicitly constructed using standard Fourier methods. In this section, we give a brief sketch of this derivation, along with several analytic properties of
G_{\operatorname{S}}
and integral
operators derived from
G_{\operatorname{S}}
, which will be used in later sections.

We begin by noting that the translational invariance of
\mathcal{L}
immediately implies that
G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
and so, without loss of generality we may assume that
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}=0.
Taking a Fourier transform of (
3.1
) yields

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2}p(\xi^{2})-\frac{1}{2|\xi|}\right]\tilde{G}(\xi)=1\;,

(3.2)

where
\tilde{G}
is the Fourier transform of
G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}}).
The bracketed quantity in the above equation can be written in the form
P(|\xi|)|\xi|^{-1}
where
P(\xi)=(\xi p(\xi^{2})-1)/2
is a polynomial of degree
d_{P}=2d_{p}+1.
Let
\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{d_{P}}
denote the roots of
P.
Then, performing a partial fraction decomposition, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(\xi)=|\xi|\sum_{j=1}^{d_{P}}\frac{c_{j}}{|\xi|-\rho_{j}},\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad|\xi|\neq 0,\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{d_{P}}\;,

where
c_{j}=\lim_{z\to\rho_{j}}(z-\rho_{j})/P(z).
Here we assume for simplicity that all of the roots of
P
are simple. Similar results can be obtained for roots of higher multiplicity.

As a consequence of Cauchy’s theorem, the coefficients
c_{j}
satisfy the moment conditions:
\sum_{j=1}^{d_{P}}c_{j}\rho_{j}^{\ell}=0
for
\ell=0,\cdots,d_{P}-2
and
\sum_{j=1}^{d_{P}}c_{j}\rho_{j}^{d_{P}-1}=\frac{1}{a_{d_{P}}}
, where

is the leading order coefficient of

In particular, it follows that for
d_{P}\geq 3

\tilde{G}(\xi)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{P}}\frac{c_{j}\rho_{j}}{|\xi|-\rho_{j}},

again excluding the set
|\xi|=\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{d_{P}}.

For

an extra constant term is present in the above expression leading to a delta function in the final expression for

For

[

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}[1/(|\xi|-\rho_{j})]({\mathbf{r}})=\frac{1}{2\pi|{\mathbf{r}}|}+\frac{\rho_{j}}{4}{\rm\bf K}_{0}(-\rho_{j}|{\mathbf{r}}|)\;,

where
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
is the inverse Fourier transform and

is the Struve function
[
26

\rho_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},
the limit

the upper half of the complex plane is

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}[1/(|\xi|-\rho_{j}-i\epsilon)]({\mathbf{r}})=\frac{1}{2\pi|{\mathbf{r}}|}-\frac{\rho_{j}}{4}{\rm\bf K}_{0}(\rho_{j}|{\mathbf{r}}|)+\frac{i\rho_{j}}{2}H_{0}^{(1)}(\rho_{j}|{\mathbf{r}}|)\,,

giving an

contribution to the fundamental solution, where

denotes the zeroth order
Hankel function of the first kind.

Remark 1

In most applications there is at most one positive root and the outgoing solution can be
obtained by applying a limiting absorption principle. For multiple positive roots one would expect a limiting absorption principle to also apply, though the choice of branch cuts may depend on the problem setting.

Using these formulas, as well as the moment conditions, we have that for
d_{P}\geq 3,

\displaystyle G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}})=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\rho_{j}\notin\mathbb{R}^{+}}c_{j}\rho_{j}^{2}{\rm\bf K}_{0}(-\rho_{j}|{\mathbf{r}}|)+\sum_{\rho_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}c_{j}\rho_{j}^{2}\left[-\frac{1}{4}{\rm\bf K}_{0}(\rho_{j}|{\mathbf{r}}|)+\frac{i}{2}H_{0}^{(1)}(\rho_{j}|{\mathbf{r}}|)\right]

is a solution of

3.1
.
For

additional terms involving delta functions and multiples of

Similarly, if we define

\displaystyle G_{\phi}({\mathbf{r}})=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{\rho_{j}\notin\mathbb{R}^{+}}c_{j}\rho_{j}{\bf K}_{0}(-\rho_{j}|{\mathbf{r}}|)+\sum_{\rho_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\frac{c_{j}}{2}\rho_{j}\left[-\frac{1}{4}{\bf K}_{0}(\rho_{j}|{\mathbf{r}}|)+\frac{i}{2}H_{0}^{(1)}(\rho_{j}|{\mathbf{r}}|)\right].

In many problems of physical interest, there is only one propagating frequency in the Green’s function.
Furthermore, if there is dissipation added to the surface, then there are no slowly decaying modes.
This is summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
p(z)=a_{d_{p}}z^{d_{p}}+\cdots+a_{1}z+a_{0}
with

.
If
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d_{p}-1}\geq 0
,
a_{d_{p}}>0
, and
a_{0}
is real, then

has
precisely one positive real root, which we
take to be
\rho_{1}
. In this case,

and

radiate like the Hankel function

, i.e.

(3.7)

and likewise for their derivatives, as
|{\mathbf{r}}|\to\infty\;.

In the case that the coefficients
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d_{p}}
are real and

a_{0}
has non-zero imaginary part, none of the roots of

are real. Then,

G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}})=O\left(\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{r}}|^{3}}\right)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad G_{\phi}({\mathbf{r}})=O\left(\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{r}}|^{3}}\right)\;,

(3.8)

and likewise for their derivatives.
More generally, these decay rates hold whenever none of the roots of
P
are
real and positive.

Proof.

The facts about the roots follow from elementary properties of
polynomials. The decay conditions follow by applying the large

asymptotics of Struve and Hankel functions
[
26
]
and the moment conditions to

3.4
and

3.6

The slow decay of the Green’s functions in the real coefficient case adds additional technical difficulties

Definition 3

The coefficients in the polynomial

The small

Details of the formulas can be deduced from the small

Lemma 4
.

Suppose

Then

admits a small

and

has an expansion of the form

G_{\phi}({\mathbf{r}})=A_{\phi}(|{\mathbf{r}}|^{2})+|{\mathbf{r}}|^{d_{P}-2}B_{\phi}(|{\mathbf{r}}|^{2})+|{\mathbf{r}}|^{d_{P}+1}\log|{\mathbf{r}}|C_{\phi}(|{\mathbf{r}}|^{2})\;,

where the

,

, and

functions are infinitely differentiable.

In particular, when

\frac{a_{1}}{2}G_{\operatorname{S}}+G_{\rm L}\in H^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),
where

is the standard Laplace Green’s function. Similarly, for
p(z)=a_{2}z^{2}+a_{1}z+a_{0}

\frac{a_{2}}{2}G_{\operatorname{S}}-G_{\rm B}\in H^{4}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),
where

is the standard biharmonic Green’s function.

Consider the volume integral operators

\mathcal{V}_{\rm S}[\mu]({\mathbf{r}}):=\int_{\Omega}G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\mu({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\rm d}A({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathcal{V}_{\rm\phi}[\mu]({\mathbf{r}}):=\int_{\Omega}G_{\phi}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\mu({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\rm d}A({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\;.

We summarize some smoothing properties of these operators in the following lemma. Because the Green’s functions
are not PDE Green’s functions, higher regularity is described for compactly contained subsets. The
arguments for these properties are standard and omitted.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that
d_{P}\geq 3
, let

be an open set compactly contained in
\Omega
, and let

be given. Then,
\mathcal{V}_{\operatorname{S}}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{d_{P}-1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\mathcal{V}_{\phi}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{d_{P}}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\mathcal{V}_{\operatorname{S}}:H^{m}(\Omega)\to H^{m+d_{P}-1}(A)
, and
\mathcal{V}_{\phi}:H^{m}(\Omega)\to H^{m+d_{P}}(A)
.
The “loc” may be dropped in the dissipative regime.

The regularity of the corresponding boundary-to-volume operators can also be established.

Lemma 6
.

Let

K_{\rm S}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\partial_{{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}}^{\ell}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}}^{m}G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\quad\textrm{and}\quad K_{\phi}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\partial_{{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}}^{\ell}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}}^{m}G_{\phi}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\;,

where
\ell+m\leq d_{P}-2
. Let
\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{S}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
be the corresponding integral operators

\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{S}}[\eta]({\mathbf{r}})=\int_{\partial\Omega}K_{\rm S}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\eta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathcal{K}_{\phi}[\eta]({\mathbf{r}})=\int_{\partial\Omega}K_{\phi}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\eta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\;.

Then,

\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{S}}:H^{s}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{s+d_{P}-3/2-\ell-m}(\Omega)
for
0\leq s\leq 3/2
and

\mathcal{K}_{\phi}:L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{d_{P}-1-\ell-m}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Recall that
d_{P}\geq 3
is odd. For
G_{\operatorname{S}}
, the dominant term
in the expansion provided by
Lemma

4
is
|{\mathbf{r}}|^{d_{P}-3}\log|{\mathbf{r}}|
and up to

d_{P}
derivatives of the other terms are bounded. The dominant term is the Green’s function
of an elliptic PDE of order
d_{P}-1
; the pseudo-differential theory, e.g. Theorem 8.5.8
in
[
36
]
, then implies that the contribution of the dominant term
gains
d_{P}-3/2
derivatives for boundary-to-volume. For
G_{\phi}
, the dominant term is

|{\mathbf{r}}|^{d_{P}-2}
, which is not a PDE kernel. Observe that taking
d_{P}-1
derivatives of the
dominant term results in singularities of the form
1/|{\mathbf{r}}|
, which give bounded operators from

L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
to
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Given these regularity results and asymptotics, it is relatively straightforward to derive the
compactness and jump properties of the integral operators we define for specific boundary value
problems in the next section. We will make frequent use of some standard results from Sobolev

For the volume-to-boundary operators, regularity of the trace can be established by combining

5

[

Theorem 7
(Trace theorem).

We have
\gamma_{0}^{-}:H^{1}(\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and

\gamma_{0}^{+}:H^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega})\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)

Moreover, if

Compactness can then be inferred

Lemma 8
(Rellich’s lemma).

Let
s>t

H^{s}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{t}(\Omega)

and
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{t}(\partial\Omega)
are
compact.

As observed above, the

kernel is a relatively smooth perturbation of a (poly)-harmonic

Lemma 9

Suppose that

{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},

Before proceeding, we show that volume potentials and boundary layer potentials defined using the Green’s

For PDE Green’s functions this is a trivial step, but here the nonlocal terms in

Lemma 10

Suppose that
d_{P}\geq 3
and that the coefficients are in the dissipative regime. Let

\eta\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)

K_{\rm S}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\partial_{{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}}^{j}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}}^{k}G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{S}}[\eta]({\mathbf{r}})=\int_{\partial\Omega}K_{\rm S}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\eta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\;.

Then, for
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}\left[\mathcal{V}_{\operatorname{S}}[\mu]\right]({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}_{\phi}[\mu]({\mathbf{r}})\;,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}\left[\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{S}}[\eta]\right]({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{\phi}[\eta]({\mathbf{r}})\;,

(3.10)

where
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
is the integral operator for the kernel

K_{\phi}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\partial_{{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}}^{j}\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}}^{k}G_{\phi}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

Proof.

We begin by letting

I({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{r}}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}|}|G_{S}(\tilde{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})|\,{\operatorname{d}}A(\tilde{\mathbf{r}})\quad\textrm{and}\quad J({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{r}}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}|}|K_{\rm S}(\tilde{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})|\,{\operatorname{d}}A(\tilde{\mathbf{r}})\;.

The value

exists for all
{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
because
G_{S}(\tilde{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=O(|\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|^{-3})
as

, and is bounded because

has at worst a logarithmic singularity. Similarly,

has similar decay properties and is at worst

for

and standard estimates show that it has at worst a

The integrals

\int_{\Omega}I({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})|\mu({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})|\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

are thus finite by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The desired formulas are therefore a consequence of the Fubini-Tonelli theorem and (
3.5

4
The integral representations

Because of the nonlocal nature of (
2.3
), it is convenient to construct a global representation of the solution from a single Green’s function. Since we require that the equation be automatically satisfied in the unbounded exterior region
D\,\backslash\,\overline{\Omega}
, we use the exterior Green’s function, and its derivatives with respect to the ‘source’ variable. We illustrate this construction with two examples: capillary-gravity waves and flexural-gravity waves. A key feature of our approach is that the surface-boundary portions of the representation can be constructed using ingredients from standard BIE representations for solving PDEs for exterior problems. Roughly speaking, if an integral representation exists for the solution
u
to the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{ext}}[0,u]&=0\,,\quad&&{\rm in}\,\,D\setminus\overline{\Omega}\,,\\
\mathcal{B}[0,u]&=g\,,\quad&&{\rm on}\,\,\partial\Omega\,,\\
\end{aligned}\end{cases}

then, by substituting
G_{\operatorname{S}}
into the integral representation for
u
, we obtain a suitable candidate function
B_{j}
in our representation (
2.4
). The surface-volume portion of the representation
V({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}})
we obtain by taking appropriate linear combinations of
G_{\operatorname{S}}
and its derivatives.

The uniqueness of the specific PDE boundary value problems treated below can be established with standard
arguments in the dissipative regime, assuming certain decay conditions on the
potential
\phi
and its derivatives. It is then possible to establish the uniqueness of solutions
of the corresponding integro-differential boundary value problem, assuming that
\sigma
satisfies the
same radiation condition as
G_{\operatorname{S}}
(see
Proposition

2
). Finally, the uniqueness of the
solutions of the integro-differential boundary value problem can be used to establish
the invertibility of the corresponding integral equation systems, and even their invertibility
in the non-dissipative regime by the Gohberg-Sigal theory (with the possible exception of a nowhere
dense set of parameters). These arguments were taken up in detail previously for
variable rigidity flexural-gravity wave scattering
[
3
]
. For the sake of
brevity, we simply assume the uniqueness of solutions of the integro-differential boundary
value problem when establishing the invertibility of the integral equations below.

4.1
Exterior capillary-gravity waves

The first example we consider is the exterior capillary-gravity wave problem, which arises in the case of porous or partial membranes
[
77
,
40
,
48
,
25
]
and surface films of finite area
[
27
,
29
]
. In this case, we
consider a system of the form

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{ext}}[\phi,\partial_{z}\phi]&:=(-\beta\Delta+\gamma)\partial_{z}\phi-\phi=0\,,&&\text{ in }D\setminus\overline{\Omega}\,,\\
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{int}}[\phi,\partial_{z}\phi]&:=\partial_{z}\phi-\phi=f\,,&&\text{ in }\Omega\,,\\
\mathcal{B}_{D}[\phi,\partial_{z}\phi]&:=\gamma_{0}^{+}\partial_{z}\phi=g\,,\ \text{ or }\ \mathcal{B}_{N}[\phi,\partial_{z}\phi]:=\gamma_{1}^{+}\partial_{z}\phi=g\,,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\,,\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(4.1)

where
\beta\geq 0
is related to the surface tension. Two of the common boundary conditions
[
77

for the interface

\partial\Omega
are provided and the appropriate data

depend on the boundary condition
selected. The Neumann condition
\mathcal{B}_{N}
corresponds to a freely floating membrane while the Dirichlet condition
\mathcal{B}_{D}
corresponds to a membrane which is fixed using, for instance, a loop or a string. For ease of exposition, we have set the coefficient in front of the
\partial_{z}\phi

\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{int}}
to be unity, though the approach in this section applies for any nonzero coefficient after suitable rescaling.

We now turn to the derivation of the integral equation formulation of this problem. Setting
\phi=\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}[\sigma]
as before, the system
eq.

4.1

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ext}}[\sigma]&:=\frac{1}{2}(-\beta\Delta+\gamma)\sigma-\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}[\sigma]=0\,,&&\text{ in }D\setminus\overline{\Omega}\,,\\
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{int}}[\sigma]&:=\frac{1}{2}\sigma-\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{3d}}[\sigma]=f\,,&&\text{ in }\Omega\,,\\
\mathcal{M}_{D}[\sigma]&:=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}^{+}\sigma=g\,,\ \text{ or }\ \mathcal{M}_{N}[\sigma]:=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}^{+}\sigma=g\,,&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\,.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(4.2)

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ext}}
is then in the general form

2.2

in the notation of

The design of our integral representation
of
\sigma

is a relatively
smooth perturbation of the Laplace Green’s function, i.e.

has continuous

One immediate consequence is that the jump properties of the boundary layer potentials are analogous
to the standard jump properties of Laplace boundary layer potentials.

Lemma 13

Let

be the Green’s function for
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ext}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\rm S}[\eta]({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\eta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\;,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\rm S}[\eta]({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}{\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}}G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\eta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\;.

(4.4)

If

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}^{\pm}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm S}[\eta])({\mathbf{r}}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mp\frac{1}{\beta}\eta({\mathbf{r}}_{0})+\underbrace{\operatorname{p.\!v.}\int_{\partial\Omega}{\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}}G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\eta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}_{=:\mathfrak{D}_{\rm S}[\eta]({\mathbf{r}}_{0})}\;,

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(\mathcal{S}_{\rm S}[\eta])({\mathbf{r}}_{0})

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{\beta}\eta({\mathbf{r}}_{0})+\underbrace{\operatorname{p.\!v.}\int_{\partial\Omega}{\mathbf{n}}({\mathbf{r}}_{0})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}G_{\operatorname{S}}({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\eta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}_{=:\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}_{\rm S}[\eta]({\mathbf{r}}_{0})}\;.

Moreover,
\mathcal{S}_{\operatorname{S}}[\eta]
for

are continuous across the boundary and

Proof.

By

4

G_{\operatorname{S}}+2/\beta G_{\text{L}}
has a continuous gradient and its second derivative

Consider the system for the Neumann boundary condition. To obtain a second kind equation on the boundary, a single layer
potential is used, by analogy with the Laplace case. For the volumetric term, since convolution with

Theorem 14
.

The system of integral equations given by

4.5

It suffices to show that the integral operators with kernels

The reasoning for the Dirichlet case is similar.

The following result establishes the invertibility of our integral equations.

Theorem 15
.

Suppose that the coefficients are in the dissipative regime and that solutions of the integro-differential boundary value problems

4.2

Proof.

By the Fredholm alternative and

14

Suppose that

satisfy the homogeneous version of

2.5
and
2.6

5

6

13

applying the regularity results to the integral equations,
implies that
\mu\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and that
\eta\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
in the Neumann case and
\eta\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
in the Dirichlet case.

Let
\sigma
be the corresponding surface density defined by the
ansatz
eq.

2.4
, which satisfies the interior equation and boundary condition in
eq.

4.2
by construction and the exterior equation in
eq.

4.2
by

12

Uniqueness of the integro-differential equation

4.2
implies that

Recalling the representation of

0=\sigma({\mathbf{r}})=\mu({\mathbf{r}})+\frac{|\gamma|-\gamma}{2}\mathcal{V}_{\operatorname{S}}[\mu]({\mathbf{r}})+\mathcal{V}_{\phi}[\mu]({\mathbf{r}})+\int_{\partial\Omega}B({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\eta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\operatorname{d}}s({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\;,\quad{\mathbf{r}}\in\Omega\;.

Re-arranging this expression for

We then apply the exterior operator (

Now, we examine the Neumann and Dirichlet problems separately. For the Neumann problem, the standard jump relations applied to

The first equation implies that

For the Dirichlet problem, we observe that

4.2

5
Numerical implementation and scalability

While the kernels that appear in the integral equations above
are non-standard, there has been significant progress in recent decades in

kernel independent
methods for the high-order accurate
discretization of singular integrals and the fast solution of linear systems
with certain rank structures. We briefly describe a scalable numerical scheme for solving these integral equations using such methods in the
sections below.

Remark 22
.

Both for the flexural and capillary problems, the interior normal derivative of the surface-volume density
\mu
(and of the solution
\partial_{z}\phi
itself) has a logarithmic singularity at the boundary. Such behavior is expected due to the presence of
1/r
-type surface-volume terms in our integral equations. In our numerical methods, we partially alleviate this issue by simply refining adaptively near the boundary. In principle, special discretization methods could be developed which capture these singularities with many fewer degrees of freedom. We note that a straightforward consequence of Lemma
5
is that for smooth data the surface-volume densities are smooth away from the boundary.

5.1
Discretization of integral operators

We numerically solve the integral equations of
Sections

4.1
and
4.2
using
a collocation scheme
[
32
]
. The boundary curve
\partial\Omega
is initially discretized by a
16th-order panelization and the interior of
\Omega
is represented as an 8th-order curved triangular
mesh, with each panel of the boundary corresponding to an edge of a triangular element. The discretization
nodes on each triangular element are scaled “Vioreanu-Rokhlin” nodes
[
69
]
, which
are stable for high-order interpolation in the total degree polynomial basis, and the nodes on the boundary
are scaled Legendre nodes; see
Figure

2
for an illustration.

The integral operators were applied to a smooth density on a disk and compared to a reference value for a point in the domain. The dashed line represents 8th-order convergence.

To discretize the integral
operators, which are generally weakly singular, we apply special tables of high-order accurate quadrature
rules designed for general kernels with known singularity type for targets interior to a patch, we
apply adaptive quadrature for targets on the boundary of a patch or near a patch, and we apply a fixed,
high-order quadrature rule for well-separated targets.
Letting
\{{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\}_{1}^{N}
be the nodes of the triangular mesh, this quadrature method produces
a discretization of the
surface-volume to surface-volume
operators of the form

\int_{\Omega}K({\mathbf{r}}_{i},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\mu({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\operatorname{d}}A({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\approx\sum_{j\in J_{i}}w_{ij}\mu_{j}+\sum_{j\not\in J_{i}}w_{j}K({\mathbf{r}}_{i},{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\mu_{j}\;,\quad i=1,\ldots,N\,,

(5.1)

where
K
is some integral kernel and
J_{i}
is a set of indices of volume nodes that are sufficiently
close to
{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
to require special quadrature. The other operators have similar discrete forms.

5.2
Scaling to large examples

If the maximum triangle diameter and panel length is bounded by
h
, then the naive dense application of the system matrix will take
O(h^{-4})
time, and a dense solve will take
O(h^{-6})
time. In order to avoid this, we use the precorrected-FFT method
[
58
,
56
,
15
,
75
,
46
]
to apply our system matrix in
O(h^{-2}\log(h^{-1}))
time and solve the system iteratively using GMRES
[
62
]
. In short, the precorrected-FFT method in two dimensions is a method for computing
N
-body calculations:

u_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}K({\mathbf{x}}_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\zeta_{j},\quad i=1,\ldots

(5.2)

where
K
is a translationally invariant kernel that is smooth away from
0
, the
\zeta_{i}
are arbitrary complex numbers, and the
{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
are a collection of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In this work,
K
will be
G_{\operatorname{S}}
,
G_{\phi}
, or some combination of their derivatives. The method is based on the observation that if the points
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
happen to be located on an equispaced grid, then (
5.2
) is a convolution and thus can be computed efficiently using the FFT. In order to take advantage of this observation, let
{\mathbf{z}}_{i}
be an equispaced grid covering
\Omega
. For any
{\mathbf{y}},
let
X_{{\mathbf{y}},n}
be the
n\times n
subset of the grid centered near
{\mathbf{y}}.
For a given

r>0
, let
\chi_{i}({\mathbf{y}})
be equivalent charges such that

K({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}})\approx\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\in X_{{\mathbf{y}},n}}K({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{z}}_{i})\chi_{i}({\mathbf{y}}),

(5.3)

for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{r}({\mathbf{y}})
with
X_{{\mathbf{y}},n}\subset\overline{B_{r}({\mathbf{y}})}
. Similarly, let
\psi_{i}({\mathbf{x}})
denote effective weights so that

K({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}})\approx\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}_{j}\in X_{{\mathbf{x}},n}}\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\in X_{{\mathbf{y}},n}}K({\mathbf{z}}_{j}-{\mathbf{z}}_{i})\psi_{j}({\mathbf{x}})\chi_{i}({\mathbf{y}}),

(5.4)

holds for
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
satisfying
B_{r}({\mathbf{x}})\cap B_{r}({\mathbf{y}})=\emptyset
. Returning to the
N
-body calculation, let

Q_{i}=\{{\mathbf{x}}_{j}|B_{r}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})\cap B_{r}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})\neq\emptyset\}.

We can then write

\displaystyle u_{i}\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}\in X_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i},n}}\psi_{\ell}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})\left(\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\neq{\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}}K({\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}-{\mathbf{z}}_{k})\left(\sum_{j\in\{j\,|\,{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\in X_{{\mathbf{x}}_{j},n}\}}\zeta_{j}\chi_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})\right)\right)

(5.5)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j\in Q_{i}}\zeta_{j}\left[K({\mathbf{x}}_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{j})-\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\in X_{j,n}}\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}\in X_{i,n}}K({\mathbf{z}}_{k}-{\mathbf{z}}_{\ell})\psi_{\ell}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})\chi_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{j})\right].

The first term in (
5.5
) computes the sum as if the expansion (
5.4
) were valid between every pair of source and target points
({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j}).
The second term in (
5.5
) corrects the error in the first sum for sources and targets which are too close by subtracting the contribution from the first sum for these points, and adding the correct contribution. The advantage of this decomposition is that the first term in (
5.5

\chi_{k}
to the vector of charges
\vec{\zeta}=\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N},
applying a 2D-Toeplitz matrix (the sum over the equispaced grid points), and applying a second sparse matrix with entries given by the
\psi_{\ell}.
The second term in (
5.5

\vec{\zeta}.
The sparse matrices have
O(N)
non-zero entries, assuming the
|Q_{i}|
are bounded independent of
N

\mathcal{O}(N\log{N})
work, assuming the equispaced grid has

7
Discussion and future work

In this paper we present a general framework for solving linear surface wave problems where the order of the derivatives in the surface-boundary condition experiences a jump between the interior and exterior regions. By representing the velocity potential as a single layer, this class of problems reduces to integro-differential equations on the unbounded surface of the fluid. Using the Green’s function of the integro-differential operator, we derive second-kind integral equations with densities supported only in the interior region and its boundary.

We illustrate the application of this framework to problems involving capillary-gravity and flexural-gravity waves. Under certain natural assumptions, we prove that the resulting second-kind Fredholm equations are invertible. We then present a flexible and fast method for their numerical solution based on the precorrected FFT method. The scalability of our approach was demonstrated through several representative numerical examples, including a ‘rift’ geometry based on the WR2 rift in the Ross Ice Shelf with realistic choices of physical parameters.

While the methods discussed in this work are applied to models in which the exterior boundary condition has more derivatives than the interior boundary condition, similar techniques also apply when the interior region has more derivatives than the exterior region. This extension is being vigorously pursued. Moreover, the numerical methods presented in this work apply to a broader class of integro-differential equations, and nonlocal problems. While we use standard tools and extra refinement to treat the singularity present in
\mu
near the boundary, it is relatively straightforward to develop more efficient tools based on tailored bases. A more specialized discretization approach, as well as the technical details of the fitted discretizations, fast quadrature generation, and numerical analysis of the proxy-annuli approach for computing precorrected FFT quadrature corrections, will be reported in an upcoming manuscript.
