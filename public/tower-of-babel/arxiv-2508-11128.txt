The NHIM development scenario in an asymmetric binary system of dwarf galaxies
Christof Jung, Francisco Gonzalez Montoya

Abstract
We study the development scenario of a three-degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian system. We observe how the fundamental normally hyperbolic invariant manifolds (NHIMs) form a homoclinic/heteroclinic tangle. As the energy increases, the NHIMs begin to lose normal hyperbolicity and decay, creating tangential transient effects. We also observe a certain kind of coordination between the development scenarios of these NHIMs. We analyse this phenomenon using Poincaré maps and phase space indicator functions. As an example of demonstration, we use a relatively simple model of a test particle moving in the gravitational field of a pair of interacting dwarf galaxies.



1
Introduction

In dynamical systems showing chaos, unstable invariant subsets are the geometrical structures in the phase space which determine
the global dynamics and, in particular, the creation of chaos to a large extent. As the simplest example, we can think of the
unstable fixed points in a 2-dimensional map
whose stable and unstable manifolds build up the chaotic invariant set, which can be displayed pictorially as a horseshoe construction
traced out by the intersecting stable and unstable manifolds of the most important unstable fixed points. In maps with a domain of
higher dimension, the role played in 2-dimensional maps by unstable fixed points is taken over by normally hyperbolic invariant manifolds
(standard abbreviation NHIMs) of codimension 2; for general information on NHIMs see
[
1
]
. These subsets have stable and
unstable manifolds of codimension 1, which can form
walls, channels, and tubes in the domain of the map and thereby direct the global dynamics. This is the direct generalisation of the
role of stable and unstable manifolds of hyperbolic fixed points in 2-dimensional maps. Then it is easy to understand that the
parameter dependence of the central NHIMs has an enormous influence on the parameter dependence of the whole dynamics. Therefore,
we are interested in a good understanding of the typical development scenarios of NHIMs under parameter changes. In autonomous
Hamiltonian systems (the class of systems we deal with in the present article), the most important parameter is in many cases
the total energy
E
, i.e. the conserved numerical value of the generating function
H
of the dynamics.

Hamiltonian systems have an effective potential; for the general recipe to construct it for a given Hamiltonian
function, see
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. And usually, the most important NHIMs of codimension 2 sit over the index-1 saddle
points of this effective potential. Then these NHIMs act as transition states over these saddles and direct the flow over
these saddles
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. We can imagine that from parts of the stable and unstable manifolds of the NHIMs, we glue
together tubes (cylinders) which guide the flow over the saddle of the effective potential. This property demonstrates the
importance of the NHIMs in the generation of the global properties of the dynamics.

For Hamiltonian systems with three degrees of freedom (3-dof), we are at the moment far away from a complete understanding of the
typical development scenarios of codimension-2 NHIMs. We only have hard mathematical persistence theorems which state that NHIMs
survive perturbations as long as the normal instability remains larger than the tangential instability, for various versions of
proofs see
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
, chapter 3 in
[
1
]
, and chapter 3 in
[
10
]
. Many numerical examples
indicate that NHIMs really decay in regions where the tangential instability surpasses the normal instability. In the present situation,
it is worth studying many examples of development scenarios of NHIMs numerically to gain more experience with the typical and
frequent development scenarios.

There are some investigations of the loss of normal hyperbolicity of invariant surfaces
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Unfortunately,
these publications use 2-dof systems or use a simplification which is equivalent to the reduction to 2 essential degrees
of freedom. And they are mainly concerned with the consequences of the loss of normal hyperbolicity to transition state theory and
to the flow rates over the saddles. They are not concerned with the disintegration of the NHIM surface into (partially lower-dimensional) invariant fragments, a topic into which we will delve in the present article. For some further observations of
qualitative changes of NHIMs under parameter changes, see
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

NHIMs of dimension 2 or more in a map have an internal dynamics which can be displayed graphically by plotting the Poincaré map restricted
to the NHIM itself, which we will call the restricted map for short. For an early example of the use of such a restricted map, see
Fig.1 in
[
11
]
. It is the same basic idea as the restriction of the Poincaré map
to the centre manifold of the saddle point, see for example Fig.6 in
[
19
]
. For maps with 4 dimensions in total, this restricted map for NHIMs
of codimension 2 is itself 2-dimensional and can be plotted conveniently. This restricted map, as a function of the parameters of
the system, is an excellent way to display graphically the development scenario of the NHIMs. The numerical method to follow the
NHIMs under parameter changes and to display the restricted map have been explained in detail in
[
20
]
.

In various examples, we have observed that in systems with several fundamental NHIMs the development scenarios of these NHIMs are
coordinated and important qualitative changes of one particular NHIM occur in the same small parameter interval in which also some other NHIMs suffer
important changes. One of these examples has been a simple model for the motion of a test particle in the effective potential of a
rotating symmetric system of two spherical dwarf galaxies
[
21
]
. This system has three collinear Lagrange points and
codimension-2 NHIMs over these saddle points of the effective potential. The two outer NHIMs are symmetry-related, whereas the
middle one is qualitatively different from the outer ones. Nevertheless, the development scenario of the middle NHIM is coordinated
with the development scenario of the two outer NHIMs. Now the reader can ask whether the discrete left-right symmetry of the complete
system is responsible for this coordination of the development scenarios. Motivated by this question, we investigate in the
present article the asymmetric generalisation of the model from
[
21
]
. In the present article, we set the masses of the two
spherical dwarf galaxies to different values in contrast to
[
21
]
, where these two masses have been equal.

Another example of the occurrence of coordination effects in the development scenario of NHIMs has been observed in
[
22
]
.
This system is the motion of a test particle in a rotating double-barred galaxy. The model has four codimension-2 NHIMs over
index-1 saddles of the effective potential. These four NHIMs fall into two nonequivalent groups, where the two NHIMs within each
group are symmetry related. Nevertheless, we found a coordination of the scenarios of all four NHIMs, i.e. also between the
nonequivalent groups.

In the treatment of these previous systems, we did not observe any important role of the transient outer parts of partially decayed
NHIMs. In the meantime, two interesting cases of such transient effects have been observed, see
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
.
Interestingly, in the asymmetric case of the two dwarf galaxies, the topic of the present article, the
transient outer parts of the decaying NHIMs influence the coordination of the development and decay scenario and thereby of the
creation and development of chaos. This is a new event, not noticed in previous studies. We consider it a remarkable new result of
the investigation presented here. For general information on transients and in particular on transient chaos, see the textbook
[
25
]
.

In section 2, we introduce the asymmetric model and explain the important extremal points of its effective potential. Section 3 displays
graphically the development scenario of the 3 NHIMs and of the most important periodic orbits related to these NHIMs. In section 4,
we have a closer look at the creation of chaos and at heteroclinic connections between NHIMs, including the role of transient effects.
In section 5, we use the delay time as an indicator function to obtain more information about the phase space structure, and in section 6 we draw conclusions and give final remarks.

2
The model

In
[
21
]
, the functional form of the model has already been given for general values of the masses
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
of the two
galaxies. Therefore, we give here only a brief repetition of the most important properties of the model. The total mass of the two galaxies taken
together is
M_{t}=M_{1}+M_{2}
. The distance between the centres of the two galaxies is
R
. We describe the dynamics in a corotating
frame with Cartesian coordinates
x,y,z
where the origin is the centre of mass of the whole system. The
z
axis is the axis of rotation
with the angular velocity
\Omega
. In the rotating frame, the centres of the two individual galaxies are located along the
x

axis at the points
x_{1}=-RM_{2}/M_{t}
and
x_{2}=RM_{1}/M_{t}
respectively and independently of the time.
The gravitational potential for a test particle at a general point
x,y,z
is given as

V_{g}=-\frac{GM_{1}}{r_{1}}-\frac{GM_{2}}{r_{2}},

(1)

where
G
is the gravitational constant and

\displaystyle r_{1}^{2}=(x-x_{1})^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+c^{2},

(2)

\displaystyle r_{2}^{2}=(x-x_{2})^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+c^{2}.

Here
c
is a size measure for the galaxies, which we take equal for the two galaxies. The two galaxies rotate around each other
and when
R
is large compared to
c
then we can approximate the rotational angular velocity
\Omega
by the Kepler value

\Omega=\sqrt{\frac{GM_{t}}{R^{3}}}.

(3)

Accordingly, the effective potential in the rotating frame is given as

V_{eff}(x,y,z)=V_{g}(x,y,z)-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2}).

(4)

We use the same units as in the symmetric case; they are explained in detail in section 2 of
[
21
]
.

In these units, the parameters of the system used in the following are
c=0.25
,
R=6
,
M_{1}=0.06
and
M_{2}=0.14
. Note that
accordingly
M_{t}=0.2
, exactly the same value as the one used for the symmetric case in
[
21
]
. Also, the values of
c
and
R
are chosen the same as in the symmetric case. We hope that this makes a comparison between the symmetric and asymmetric cases easier.
The momenta conjugate to the Cartesian position coordinates are
p_{x},p_{y},p_{z}
. In these coordinates, the generating function of the dynamics (the Hamiltonian function) is given as

H(x,y,z,p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})=\frac{1}{2}(p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2}+p_{z}^{2})+V_{g}(x,y,z)-\Omega L_{z}.

(5)

Here
L_{z}=xp_{y}-yp_{x}
is the
z
component of the angular momentum. We describe the dynamics in a corotating system. Therefore, the
numerical value of the function
H
is not the energy of the test particle as measured by an observer in an inertial system. This energy,
measured by the inertial observer, is not conserved because the potential seen by the inertial observer is explicitly time dependent.
For the inertial observer, the conserved generating function from Eq.5 is the Jacobi conserved quantity. In the following, we use the letter

E
for the numerical value of the function
H
, and call this number the energy, even though it is not the energy as measured by the
inertial observer.

In later sections, a knowledge of some symmetries of this system will be useful. First, we notice that the effective potential is
symmetric under
y\rightarrow-y
and under
z\rightarrow-z
. If a trajectory starts with
z=0
and
p_{z}=0
, then this trajectory will
lie completely in the horizontal plane
z=0
of the position space. Therefore, this horizontal plane is an invariant 2-dof subsystem.
Because we describe the dynamics in a rotating frame, we do not find the usual time reversal symmetry.
However, if we combine the transformation
t\rightarrow-t
with a reflection in the plane
y=0
, then the orientation of rotation is
also reversed and the Hamiltonian equations of motion remain the same. Therefore, the whole dynamics is invariant under the following
transformation

(t,x,y,z,p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})\rightarrow(-t,x,-y,z,-p_{x},p_{y},-p_{z}).

(6)

This transformation can be used to transform stable manifolds of invariant subsets into unstable manifolds of the corresponding
y
-reflected
invariant subsets. This transformation will become useful in subsection 4b.

x-y
plane in Fig.

Finally, we give the motivation for our choice of the mass ratio between the two galaxies. As already mentioned, we want to keep
M_{t}
fixed
at the value 0.2. And then, it is clear that we have chosen

3
Numerical results for the development and decay scenario of the NHIMs

3.1
Development scenario of the most important periodic orbits

From now on, we use the conserved numerical value
E
of the Hamiltonian as the perturbation parameter of the system and study the development
scenario of the dynamics as a function of
E
. Out of the index-1 saddles and out of the relative minima of
V_{eff}
grow the most
important periodic orbits of the system, which are partly also the most important periodic orbits within the NHIMs. Therefore, a detailed
knowledge of the development scenario of these periodic orbits helps a lot to understand the development scenario of the NHIMs and, in
the end, the development scenario of the whole dynamics. The important properties of these periodic orbits are displayed in the
bifurcation diagram plotted in Fig.
2
in an energy interval which is relevant for the topic of the present article.

Figure 2:
Bifurcation diagram of the most important periodic orbits. Red curves represent
horizontal orbits. Dark green curves represent vertical orbits, blue curves represent tangentially
stable tilted loop orbits, and the magenta curve represents tangentially unstable tilted loop orbits.
Pitchfork bifurcations are marked as open black squares, and the loss of normal hyperbolicity is
marked by eight-pointed black stars.

The horizontal axis of the plot gives the
x
coordinate of the periodic orbit at the moment when it crosses the plane
y=0

space in negative orientation. The vertical axis of the plot gives the value
E
of the generating function
H

Out of each minimum of the potential grows three fundamental periodic orbits, two of them are horizontal, i.e. completely contained in the
plane
z=0
of the position space, and one of them goes mainly into the vertical direction. One horizontal orbit has a negative sense of
rotation, and the other one has a positive orientation of rotation. The horizontal orbits with positive orientation are not important for the development scenario of the dynamics and are not discussed in the following, nor included in Fig.
2
in order not to overload the plot. In contrast,
all the periodic horizontal orbits with a negative orientation and with period one with respect to the negative intersection with the
plane
y=0
are central for the development scenario and are included in Fig.
2
as the red lines. There is one red curve growing out of each of the potential minima. Note that each one of these two red curves runs through a symmetry-broken pitchfork bifurcation,
whereby each one turns into 3 red curves for higher values of the energy. For the curve coming out of the minimum
P_{1}
, this happens for

x\approx-3,E\approx-0.063
, and for the one growing out of the minimum
P_{2}
, it happens for
x\approx 2.8,E\approx-0.074
.
For the event of symmetry-broken pitchfork bifurcations, see section 20.3 and Fig. 20.3.2 in
[
26
]
.

The approximately vertical periodic orbits are represented in Fig.
2
as the dark green curves. Again, the
x
value of the orbit at the
moment of the negative intersection with the plane
y=0
is plotted as a function of
E
.

Out of each one of the 3 collinear Lagrange points grows one horizontal Lyapunov orbit with negative orientation of rotation in the
x-y

plane, again represented in Fig.
2
as red curves, and one approximately vertical Lyapunov orbit, again represented in the plot as dark
green curves. In the following, we call lhj and lvj the horizontal Lyapunov orbit and the vertical Lyapunov orbit, respectively, coming out of
the Lagrange point
L_{j}
. These Lyapunov orbits are the most important and central periodic orbits within the NHIMs created over the collinear
Lagrange points. And these Lyapunov orbits are born at
E_{j}
as normally hyperbolic and tangentially elliptic, where the words normally
and tangentially refer to the NHIM surface.

Pitchfork bifurcations play an important role in the scenario, and they are indicated by open black squares in the plot. In such a
pitchfork bifurcation, each one of the horizontal Lyapunov orbits splits off a pair of tilted loop orbits, which go out of the horizontal plane.
The two members of such a pair are transformed into each other by a reflection in
z
and are represented by a single blue curve in the
Fig.
2
. For higher energy, these tilted loop orbits disappear in saddle-centre bifurcations with pairs of further tilted loop orbits
(also represented by blue curves) being created in pitchfork bifurcations of the horizontal orbits coming out of the potential minima.
The tilted loop orbits of this type created over the Lagrange point
L_{j}
will be called ltj in the following. The
orbit lh1 runs through a further pitchfork bifurcation at
E\approx-0.045
, where it splits off a further pair of tilted loop orbits
which are represented by the magenta curve in the plot. These tilted loop orbits will be called lu1. With increasing energy, these new
tilted loop orbits lu1 turn vertical very rapidly and are absorbed by the vertical Lyapunov orbit lv1 in an inverse pitchfork bifurcation
at
E\approx-0.043
.

The tangential stability properties of the periodic orbits contained within the NHIMs and their changes during all these bifurcations can
be visualised best in the restricted Poincaré maps of the NHIMs. They will be explained in detail below in the subsections describing
the development scenarios of the NHIMs.

At still higher energy, the horizontal Lyapunov orbits coming out of the collinear Lagrange points are destroyed in saddle-centre
bifurcations together with horizontal periodic orbits coming out of the potential minima or the horizontal orbits created in the broken pitchfork
bifurcations suffered by the horizontal orbits coming out of the potential minima. Shortly before their destruction, the horizontal
Lyapunov orbits lose their normal hyperbolcity; their loss of normal hyperbolicity is marked in the plot by a black eight-pointed star.

2

and a large part of the orbits coming out of

the old plot of the symmetric case augmented by the orbits coming out of

and of

We show the internal dynamics of NHIMs by the Poincaré map restricted to the NHIM surface with the intersection condition
z=0

where the intersection orientation is irrelevant because of the symmetry under
z
reflection. In the full 4-dimensional Poincaré map,
the NHIM is a 2-dimensional curved surface embedded in the 4-dimensional domain of the
map having coordinates
x,y,p_{x},p_{y}
. To display the restricted map graphically, we show the projection of the NHIM surface into some
appropriate 2-dimensional coordinate surface. Here, the choice of the
y-p_{y}
coordinate plane gave the best graphical results. Therefore, we will
use this projection for all restricted Poincaré maps throughout the article.

The central and most important NHIM is the one over the Lagrange point
L_{1}
. Therefore, we start with the graphical presentation of the
development scenario of this NHIM. It is created at the energy
E_{1}
. Very close to this energy, the internal dynamics of the NHIM is
a two degrees of freedom (2-dof) oscillator, and accordingly, the Poincaré plot in Fig.
3
(a) for
E=-0.064
looks like that the one of a
2-dof oscillator. The central fixed point (a green dot in the figure) represents the vertical Lyapunov orbit lv1, and the boundary
(shown as a solid red curve) is the horizontal Lyapunov orbit lh1, which lies completely in the horizontal plane. For such low energies,
both of these Lyapunov orbits are tangentially elliptic. Now we increase the energy and show plots of the qualitatively changed NHIM1
appearance after each important bifurcation event, which is related to an important bifurcation event of the Lyapunov orbits, presented
in the previous subsection.

At an energy
E\approx-0.0638
, lh1 runs through its first pitchfork bifurcation, where it becomes tangentially hyperbolic and splits
off a pair of tangentially elliptic tilted loop orbits. In the restricted map, these tilted loop orbits are represented by new fixed
points which, at the bifurcation energy, come out of the boundary, i.e. out of lh1. In the figures, these tilted loop fixed points are
represented by blue dots. The tilted loop orbits are surrounded by KAM islands, which grow larger
with increasing energy. Fig.
3
(b) shows the situation for
E=-0.046
. Here, the central island around lv1 is already rather small; the large
majority of the area of the NHIM1 is occupied by the islands around the tilted loop orbits. In its first pitchfork bifurcation, lh1
has become tangentially unstable and has become part of a separatrix structure which divides the various islands. Strictly speaking,
this separatrix structure is a fine chaos strip. However, it is so fine that in the figure it looks like lines. The tangential instability
of lh1 is here still smaller than its normal instability. Accordingly, lh1 still belongs to the NHIM1.

At an energy a little below
E=-0.045
, lh1 runs through its second
pitchfork bifurcation, where it splits off a new pair of tangentially hyperbolic tilted loop orbits, while lh1 itself returns to tangentially
elliptic. The Fig.
3
(c) for the energy
E=-0.044
shows how these new tilted loop orbits run along the line
y=0
closer to
the central point for increasing energy. The 2 fixed points in the map, which correspond to these 2 new tilted loop orbits, are marked by
magenta dots. The unstable manifolds of the new tangentially unstable tilted loop orbits create a fine chaos strip, which in the figure looks
like a separatrix structure. This separatrix divides all the various disjoint regular islands. First, we have the very small
remnant of the central island around lv1. Then we have the island pair around the tangentially elliptic tilted loop orbits. And last, we have a new regular region of KAM curves close to the boundary (which is still lh1), which has now become tangentially elliptic again.

At the energy
E\approx-0.043
, the tangentially hyperbolic tilted loop orbits reach the central point and are absorbed by this central point in an
inverse pitchfork bifurcation. Thereby, the central point turns tangentially hyperbolic and becomes the central point of the separatrix structure.
The island around lv1 has been lost completely in this bifurcation.
The Fig.
3
(d) shows the NHIM1 for
E=-0.042
. We see how the separatrix structure has become a chaos strip of a size which is already clearly
visible in the plot. In addition, we notice that the tilted loop islands start to disintegrate from the outside, i.e. from the neighbourhood
of the chaos strip. Now we are in the energy region, which is most important for the whole development scenario. Therefore we show in Fig.
3
(e)
the NHIM1 for the nearby energy
E=-0.041
, even though no important bifurcation event happens during this further increase of the energy. Now the
separatrix has already turned into a large-scale chaos region. It is important to emphasise that up to this energy, all elements in NHIM1
are still normally hyperbolic, i.e. in all points of the NHIM1, the normal instability is still larger than the tangential instability. This also
holds for the large chaos region.

At
E\approx-0.04035
, a very drastic event happens to the NHIM1. Here, the Lyapunov orbit lh1 loses its normal hyperbolicity. Accordingly, for
higher values of the energy, lh1 no longer belongs to NHIM1, and it can no longer act as the boundary of NHIM1. At this energy, NHIM1 starts to
decay. At first sight, the plot of NHIM1 for
E=-0.04
in Fig.
3
(f) does not look very different from the plot in Fig.
3
(e). Nevertheless, there are some
important qualitative differences which we have to point out. As already mentioned, lh1 is no longer the boundary of NHIM1. Therefore,
this orbit has been included in the plot as a broken line only. Now the large chaos strip no longer has any boundary curve on its outside, which
prevents trajectories from diffusing chaotically to the outside in the long run and leaving the NHIM1 tangentially. Therefore, strictly
speaking, the chaos strip is no longer invariant and accordingly no longer a part of the NHIM1. Trajectories only stay temporarily in the
apparent chaos strip of the NHIM1. We can say that this apparent chaos strip only has a transient existence as an invariant surface. For more
details on this type of transient behaviour on decaying NHIMs, see
[
23
]
.

3.5
Some comments on the NHIM development scenarios

Important events happen in NHIM1 and also in NHIM3 in the same relatively small energy interval between
E=-0.043
and
E=-0.04
. With
increasing energy in NHIM3, the loss of normal hyperbolicity happens before the development of large-scale chaos in the internal
dynamics. We see large-scale chaos in the surroundings of the remnant parts of NHIM3 only as a transient effect. In contrast, in the
internal dynamics of NHIM1, we observe the development of large-scale chaos before the beginning of any decay of this NHIM. The
difference is that NHIM3 starts its decay because the normal instability is becoming small, whereas in NHIM1, the development of
the growing tangential instability is important for the beginning of the decay.

When we compare the development scenario of NHIM1 on one hand with the development scenarios of NHIM2 and NHIM3 on the other hand,
then we notice the following qualitative difference. On NHIM1, the horizontal Lyapunov orbit runs through two consecutive pitchfork
bifurcations, where in the second one, a pair of unstable tilted loop orbits is created, while the horizontal Lyapunov orbit returns
to tangential stability. This second pair of tilted loop orbits turns vertical and is finally absorbed by the vertical Lyapunov
orbit in an inverse pitchfork bifurcation. Thereby, the vertical Lyapunov orbit becomes tangentially unstable, and this event triggers
the creation of large-scale chaos inside of NHIM1.

In contrast, in NHIM2 and in NHIM3, tangentially unstable tilted loop orbits are never created, which has two consequences. First,
the horizontal Lyapunov orbit remains tangentially unstable, which favours the beginning of the decay of these NHIMs from the
separatrix structure, which contains lh2 or lh3, respectively. Second, the vertical Lyapunov orbit remains tangentially stable, which favours the survival of the central island of these NHIMs up to very high energy.

We made a surprising observation of the connection of these properties with the behaviour of the time periods of the Lyapunov
orbits as a function of the energy. In Fig.
6
, we plot these time periods as a function of the energy. As before, the red curves belong
to the horizontal Lyapunov orbits, and the green curves belong to the vertical Lyapunov orbits. The blue curves belong to the tangentially
stable tilted loop orbits. And the magenta curve belongs to the tangentially unstable tilted loop orbits. The solid curves belong to
NHIM1, the dotted curves belong to NHIM2 and the broken curves belong to NHIM3. In NHIM1, the periods of the horizontal and
the vertical Lyapunov orbit cross each other; one pitchfork bifurcation happens energetically before the crossing, and the other
one happens after the crossing. In NHIM2 and NHIM3, we do not find any analogous crossing of the horizontal and vertical periods.

Figure 6:
Periods of Lyapunov orbits and tilted loop orbits as a function of the energy.

Now the reader might ask whether this observation is a strange feature of the particular example or whether it is common.
Interestingly, the experience with other systems suggests that this behaviour is common. In the system of a test particle in a
barred galaxy
[
27
]
and for the dynamics of a test particle in a double-barred galaxy
[
22
]
, we found two consecutive
pitchfork bifurcations creating first a pair of tangentially stable tilted loop orbits and later a pair of tangentially unstable
tilted loop orbits. Also, a crossing of the periods of the horizontal and the vertical Lyapunov orbits occurs between
these two pitchfork bifurcations. However, in these two examples, the tilted loop orbits come out of the vertical Lyapunov orbit.
On the other hand, in the system of a test particle in the gravitational field of an open star cluster
[
28
]
, there is
no crossing of periods, and there is only a single pitchfork bifurcation creating a pair of tangentially stable tilted loop orbits.

In a classical model for a hydrogen atom in a rotating external field
[
29
]
, we find the following scenario. First the horizontal
Lyapunov orbit splits off a pair of tangentially stable tilted loop orbits. Later, we have a crossing of the periods of the
horizontal and vertical Lyapunov orbits. However, at an energy a little higher, the horizontal Lyapunov orbit collides with the
singularity of the Coulomb system at the origin, and this event throws the scenario out of its usual path. At an energy still
a little higher, the horizontal Lyapunov orbit is absorbed by an orbit coming out of the central potential hole in an inverse
period doubling bifurcation. So the horizontal Lyapunov orbit ends its existence before it has the opportunity for a further
pitchfork bifurcation.

The conclusion of this subsection is the following. We have seen the creation of tangentially unstable tilted loop orbits only
in cases where the periods of the horizontal and the vertical Lyapunov orbits cross. The system of the two dwarf galaxies is
a nice example where we see in the same system the case with crossing and the case without crossing. Certainly, the crossing of
the periods indicates some kind of 1:1 resonance between the horizontal and the vertical motion. And such a resonance is needed
for the creation of tilted loop orbits out of the Lyapunov orbits. Unfortunately, so far we are not yet able to give a detailed
explanation of all these connections.

4.3
Some remarks on transients and heteroclinic connections

The existence of the heteroclinic trajectories starting or ending on transient parts of decaying NHIMs might lead us to the following interesting speculation: The beginning of the decay of NHIM3 and the formation
of the corresponding transient chaotic regions is synchronised with the formation of a large-scale chaotic region on NHIM1.
Such a synchronisation is supported by the existence of heteroclinic trajectories like the one presented in Fig.
9
(b).

After having seen numerical evidence for transient effects and their influence on the general dynamics, let us finish this section
with some further remarks on the decay of NHIMs and the related appearance of transient behaviour in the
region of the former NHIM. As long as the NHIM is a smooth 3-dimensional invariant surface of the flow in the 5-dimensional energy
shell (or a corresponding 2-dimensional surface in the 4-dimensional domain of the map), its stable manifold is a smooth 4-dimensional surface in the energy shell (or a corresponding 3-dimensional surface in the map). It must have codimension 1 in
the energy shell and also in the domain of the map. Then a 1-dimensional curve
C
in an appropriate region of the energy shell
close to the NHIM intersects the local branch of the stable manifold transversally. The curve
C
can be chosen to lie in the domain
of the map, and then it also serves in the domain of the map for the same purpose. This is the basic idea behind the search strategy
for the NHIM as explained in all details in
[
20
]
.

Now imagine that the NHIM decays and that in some region only a lower-dimensional fractal collection of remnants of the NHIM remains.
These remnants certainly contain unstable periodic orbits. The stable manifolds of these remnants have a lower dimension, i.e. higher
codimension than the stable manifold of the former complete NHIM. Then, in general, the curve
C
will no
longer intersect exactly the stable manifolds of the remnants. Accordingly, the search strategy for the NHIM mentioned above will
no longer find these lower-dimensional remnants. However, if the curve
C
is chosen in an appropriate region, it still contains
points leading to a high time delay in the neighbourhood of the remnants. This is the explanation of the transient effects.
To find again the stable manifolds of such lower-dimensional remnants of NHIMs, it would be necessary to scan initial
conditions not only along a 1-dimensional curve but on a higher-dimensional surface, where the dimension of this surface of initial
conditions should be larger than or at least equal to the codimension of the stable manifold we want to catch.

6
Conclusions and final remarks

The system analysed in the present article is an instructive example of Hamiltonian dynamics with 3 not symmetry-related NHIMs, and
for the effects of coordination of the development scenario of the NHIMs. A new feature in the present article is the inclusion of
tangentially transient behaviour on decaying NHIMs into the formation of the global scenario. We saw indications that also the
decaying transient parts of NHIMs give important contributions to the heteroclinic tangle, i.e. to the global chaotic invariant set
created by the NHIMs and their stable and unstable manifolds.

In the study of the development scenario of the NHIMs and the comparison with the corresponding left-right symmetric system
investigated in
[
21
]
, we made the following interesting observation: Also, when we destroy the symmetry between the two outer
NHIMs by the transition to different masses of the two dwarf galaxies, there remains a partial qualitative symmetry in the
development scenarios of the two outer NHIMs. This partial symmetry includes the sequence of the important bifurcations and their
order for the Lyapunov orbits contained in the individual NHIMs. But it does not include the coordination of the creation of chaos.
Here, in this example, only NHIM1 and NHIM3 make a coordinated development into the direction of chaos; NHIM2 does not participate
in this coordination. This happens even though we have heteroclinic trajectories between any pair of NHIMs. The horizontal Poincaré map presented in subsection 5.2 gives at least some indication of the relatively weak heteroclinic coupling of NHIM2
to the other two fundamental NHIMs of the system.

It seems that NHIM1 and NHIM3 become susceptible to tangential instabilities at a similar energy interval, while NHIM2 is not yet
ready for the development of large-scale chaos. Of course, in the symmetric case studied in
[
21
]
, NHIM2 has exactly the
same development scenario as NHIM3, and the two NHIMs develop large-scale chaos in exactly the same energy interval.
The horizontal horseshoe plot of Fig.
11
suggests that the large-scale KAM island over the potential minimum
P_{2}
prevents a
rapid and efficient heteroclinic connection between NHIM1 and NHIM2 along horizontal trajectories. In our collection of
heteroclinic orbits, we do not have a single one which starts or ends on NHIM2, runs over the potential hole at
P_{2}
and
does not go to
z
values far away from
z=0
. As we have seen in subsection 5.1, long and complicated trajectories can do it.
As Figs.
8
(e) and
9
(a) show, also trajectories going to the outside and circling around
L_{4}
or
L_{5}
can do it. But these long trajectories seem to be little efficient in the creation of coordination effects in the development scenarios between NHIMs.
Of course, also trajectories using the vertical degree of freedom can establish heteroclinic connections, remember the
trajectory from Fig.
9
(d). However, we have seen here (and also in other model systems) that in systems with a

z
reflection symmetry and accordingly an invariant horizontal subsystem, the energy exchange between the vertical degree of
freedom and the horizontal degrees of freedom is rather slow. This prevents an efficient coupling between the NHIMs via
trajectories having a large part of their energy in the vertical motion. All these features prevent any efficient coupling
of the development scenario of NHIM2 to the development scenario of the other two NHIMs.

One important aspect of the development scenario, which we can not yet explain precisely, but which certainly deserves a further more fundamental investigation, is the following consideration: For stable and unstable manifolds of NHIMs, there holds a foliation theorem which loosely speaking says that these manifolds are the Cartesian product of a line with the internal NHIM structure.
Thereby, these manifolds transport the internal NHIM structure along them to all regions of the phase space heavily influenced by
the homoclinic/heteroclinic tangle of the NHIMs. As a consequence, the whole homoclinic/heteroclinic tangle is at least qualitatively
similar to a Cartesian product of a lower-dimensional homoclinic/heteroclinic tangle (in our case, the horizontal horseshoe presented
in subsection 5.2) and the internal NHIM structures. This property has been noticed before and investigated in more detail
in
[
34
]
. Also, the system investigated in this reference is 3-dof and has, in a natural form, an invariant horizontal 2-dof
subsystem. Such a tendency of higher-dimensional homoclinic/heteroclinic tangles to show an approximate product structure
has also been observed by another group in
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
. This tendency seems to be common and needs a more
profound explanation.

The appearance of the tangential transient effects shows that, also after the beginning of its decay, the remnants of a NHIM have
an effect on their environment in the phase space and on the formation of the homoclinic/heteroclinic tangle very similar to the
effect the complete NHIM had before the start of the decay. The fragments and transient parts continue without heavy qualitative
differences.

The NHIMs sitting over the Lagrange points of the effective potential direct the transport of test particles over these saddle
points. In the interpretation of our model as describing two dwarf galaxies in interaction, this model describes the exchange of
stars between the two galaxies (motion over the central Lagrange point
L_{1}
) or the loss of stars from the binary galaxy
system (motion over the outer Lagrange points
L_{2}
and
L_{3}
). The projection of the unstable manifolds of the NHIMs into
the position space then gives the position of tidal arms and tidal bridges going out from the dwarf galaxies. Of course, our
model system could also be interpreted as a simple model for two nearby globular clusters in interaction. Here, it is interesting
to mention that in the astronomical literature, there is a claim of observational indications for a tidal bridge between the
interacting globular clusters NGC5024 and NGC5053
[
38
]
.
