Reliability of Three Image‐Based Instrumental Color‐Measurement Methods
Authors: Eloah Nunes de Almeida, Ido Luiz de Azevedo Feiten, Luis Felipe J Schneider, Larissa Maria Assad Cavalcante, André Luis Faria‐e‐Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo compare the precision of three clinically applicable, image‐based color assessment methods—smartphone imaging, a clinical colorimeter (Optishade), and a digital scanning device (Borea Cobra)—against a benchtop spectrophotometer for measuring CIELAB coordinates and CIEDE2000 (ΔE 00) color differences of composite resin shades.Materials and MethodsThree specimens from eight composite shades were measured using a benchtop spectrophotometer (reference) and the three test devices. Agreement for L*, a*, and b* was evaluated using linear regression and Bland–Altman analyses. ΔE 00 values relative to the reference and intershade ΔE 00 across shade pairs were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA and regression.ResultsOptishade showed the closest agreement with the reference—with slopes near unity, high R 2 values—and the smallest biases and limits of agreement. Borea Cobra demonstrated good overall performance and strong agreement for a* and b*, though with greater data dispersion and device‐specific distortions in intershade differences. Smartphone measurements displayed systematic positive bias, lower slopes for chromatic axes, and wider limits of agreement, indicating reduced precision and compression of color differences.ConclusionsOptishade provided the most reliable quantitative measurements; Borea Cobra offered acceptable performance with specific limitations. Smartphone‐based imaging preserved shade ordering but was unsuitable for precise color quantification.Keywords: clinical color assessment, color measurement, composite resin, digital shade matching, instrumental colorimetry, spectrophotometry

Introduction

Color evaluation plays a central role in determining the clinical effectiveness of numerous esthetic dental procedures, including tooth bleaching, enamel microabrasion, and the shade matching of esthetic restorations [1, 2]. The color perceived by the human eye depends on complex interactions among optical phenomena such as transmittance, absorption, reflectance, and diffraction of light [3]. Although visual assessment remains the most common method for determining the success of an esthetic intervention, human color perception is inherently subjective and can be influenced by factors such as evaluator experience, ambient conditions, surrounding colors, and timing of the evaluation [4, 5, 6]. Consequently, instrumental methods are frequently employed in clinical research either as complementary tools to visual assessment or as primary measures of color changes and discrepancies [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15].

Clinical spectrophotometers are widely used for instrumental color measurement due to their high accuracy and repeatability [7, 8, 9, 10]. In addition to identifying corresponding shade guide tabs (e.g., VITA Classical), many devices provide numerical color coordinates based on color systems established by the Commission Internationale de l'Éclairage (CIE) [16]. Among these systems, CIELAB is the most frequently used in dentistry. It characterizes color according to three coordinates—lightness (L*), red–green chromaticity (a*), and yellow–blue chromaticity (b*)—following the tristimulus concept [17]. Color differences can be quantified using formulas such as CIE76 (ΔE ab), which calculates the Euclidean distance between two colors in the CIELAB space [18]. However, as the CIELAB space is not perceptually uniform, the CIEDE2000 (ΔE 00) formula has been recommended for more accurate representation of perceived color differences [19, 20]. Despite their advantages, some clinical spectrophotometers employ measurement apertures that may limit the assessment of highly localized color variations, particularly when small restorations or confined regions are evaluated. In such situations, the averaging effect inherent to spot‐based measurements can reduce sensitivity to subtle color differences between restorations and adjacent enamel.

Image‐based approaches have emerged as an alternative for assessing color in more localized areas [21, 22, 23, 24]. A simple and widely used method involves capturing standardized photographs with a digital camera or smartphone in the presence of an 18% gray card to enable white balance correction, thereby enhancing the accuracy of color measurements obtained from the images [25, 26, 27]. The availability of adjunctive illumination devices specifically designed for dental photography has further increased the feasibility of this technique in clinical settings [28]. In addition, dedicated image‐based color measurement devices have been introduced to improve shade communication between clinicians and dental laboratories [29, 30]. Beyond facilitating shade selection, some of these systems generate CIELAB coordinates, making them potentially valuable tools for clinical research that requires quantification of localized color differences—for example, evaluating the color match between a restoration and adjacent tooth structure. However, the precision of these image‐based approaches, including those obtained using smartphones or digital color scanners, remains uncertain when compared with high‐precision benchtop spectrophotometers. Moreover, determining whether any observed discrepancies are clinically relevant requires interpretation based on established CIEDE2000 perceptibility and acceptability thresholds [31].

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine the precision of color measurements of resin composite specimens in various shades obtained using (1) smartphone‐acquired images, (2) a clinical colorimeter, and (3) a scanner designed for color assessment, relative to measurements from a high‐accuracy benchtop spectrophotometer. The ability of each method to detect color differences among composite shades was also examined, considering that the evaluated techniques allow color‐difference estimations from a single image. The hypothesis of this study was that dedicated dental color‐measurement devices would produce color values with significantly closer agreement to those obtained using a benchtop spectrophotometer than the values derived from smartphone‐based measurements.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Design

This in vitro study assessed color measurements and color‐difference values of composite resin discs using three color‐assessment methods: (1) images acquired with a smartphone, (2) a clinical colorimeter (Optishade, Style Italiano, Vicenza, Italy), and (3) a digital shade‐scanning device (Borea Cobra, Borea, Limoges, France). Measurements obtained with a benchtop spectrophotometer (CM‐36d, Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan) served as the reference standard. The dependent variables were the CIELAB color coordinates (L*, a*, and b*) and the overall color difference (ΔE 00) calculated between composite shades.

Sample Preparation

A single‐translucency resin composite (Filtek Universal Restorative, 3 M ESPE, Minnesota, USA) was used. Three specimens were fabricated for each of the following eight shades: B1, B2, A1, A2, A3, A3.5, A4, and D3. The encapsulated composite was dispensed into a stainless‐steel mold (diameter: 8 mm; thickness: 2 mm) using a capsule dispenser (Filtek Universal Dispenser, Solventum, Minnesota, USA). The material was covered with a microscope glass slide to achieve a flat surface and light‐cured using a Bluephase PowerCure unit (Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) in High‐Power mode (≈1200 mW/cm2 for 20 s). After polymerization, specimens were removed from the mold and stored dry at room temperature until color measurements were performed.

Color Measurements Procedures

All color measurements were performed by a single trained operator to minimize potential interoperator variability. For each specimen and device, five measurements were obtained at predefined central locations and averaged to derive representative CIELAB values. Contact‐based devices were positioned perpendicular (90°) to the specimen surface with direct contact, ensuring consistent measurement geometry. Smartphone‐based imaging was standardized with respect to distance, angulation, and illumination using a dedicated positioning system. All measurements were conducted on the same day using a neutral gray background (ColorChecker 3 Step Grayscale, Calibrite, Wilmington, DE, USA) to ensure consistency across devices.

Measurements obtained with the benchtop spectrophotometer (reference standard) were performed using an 8‐mm Small Area View aperture, illuminant D65, and a 2° standard observer. CIELAB coordinates were recorded directly from the device output.

For Optishade, the device tip was positioned perpendicular to and in direct contact with the specimen surface. Images were transferred to the manufacturer's iOS application (iPad, Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA). Readings were obtained from the central region of each specimen using the cursor‐based sampling tool, carefully avoiding specimen edges. Device calibration was performed immediately before measurements using the supplied white reference.

For Borea Cobra, the scanner tip was placed in direct contact with the specimen surface, ensuring a parallel image plane relative to the surface. Images were transmitted to the Vision software (Borea, Limoges, France), which automatically delineated the specimen area; manual adjustments were made when necessary. Measurement points were selected within the delineated area, and CIELAB coordinates were extracted and averaged.

For smartphone‐based imaging, specimens were photographed individually using a Google Pixel 9 Pro smartphone (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) mounted on a Smile Line MDP 2 positioning system (Smile Line, St‐Imier, Switzerland) to standardize distance, alignment, and illumination. The system incorporates three LED light groups and was operated at maximum intensity with the use of both a diffuser and a polarizing filter to minimize specular reflections. Images were captured in RAW format using automatic focus and exposure settings. RAW files were imported into Adobe Photoshop (Adobe Systems Inc., San Jose, CA, USA), where white‐balance correction was performed using the Levels tool to standardize the gray background to L* = 50, a* = 0, and b* = 0. Nonoverlapping regions of interest were selected within the central area of each specimen, avoiding edges, shadows, and reflections. CIELAB values were recorded and averaged. Color Difference Calculations

Color differences between each clinical measurement method (Optishade, Borea Cobra, Smartphone) and the reference spectrophotometer were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula (ΔE 00) with parametric factors K_L = K_C = K_H = 1 [19].

Additionally, pairwise intershade ΔE 00 values were computed for all combinations of the eight composite shades. Because three specimens were produced per shade, each pairwise comparison yielded nine ΔE 00 values (3 × 3) for every measurement method.

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi software (version 2.6.45, Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). Prior to inferential testing, data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene's test. Two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to analyze ΔE 00 values, with measurement device and composite shade (or shade pair, where applicable) treated as fixed factors. Interaction effects between factors were also examined. When significant main effects or interactions were identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey's honestly significant difference adjustment to control for multiple comparisons. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

All numerical data were reported using a consistent number of decimal places based on the resolution of the measurement devices and standard reporting conventions. CIELAB coordinates (L*, a*, b*) and ΔE 00 values were reported to two decimal places. Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) and regression parameters were rounded according to conventional rules for significant figures. Statistical test results were reported with exact p values, rounded to three decimal places when applicable.

Agreement for Individual CIELAB Color Coordinates

For each CIELAB coordinate (L*, a*, b*), agreement between the benchtop spectrophotometer (reference method) and the alternative devices was evaluated using ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression. In these analyses, benchtop values were treated as the dependent variable, and measurements obtained from Optishade, Borea Cobra, or smartphone images were treated as independent variables. Regression coefficients (slope and intercept), coefficients of determination (R 2), root mean square error (RMSE), and residual distributions were calculated. Scatter plots with fitted regression lines and 95% confidence intervals were generated, along with the identity line (y = x), to facilitate visual interpretation of proportional and fixed bias.

Agreement between the benchtop spectrophotometer and each alternative device was also assessed using Bland–Altman analysis. Differences were calculated as benchtop minus method and plotted against the mean of the two measurements. Mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (mean bias ±1.96 standard deviations) were calculated and graphically displayed.

CIEDE2000 Color Differences Relative to the Reference

Color differences between each device and the reference were computed using the CIEDE2000 formula (ΔE 00), with the benchtop spectrophotometer values serving as the reference for all calculations. For each shade and device, ΔE 00 was calculated at the specimen level and summarized as mean and standard deviation. Two‐way ANOVA was conducted independently for each shade to test for differences in ΔE 00 among the three alternative measurement methods.

Intershade Color Difference and Device Agreement

To evaluate how each device represented intershade color spacing, ΔE 00 values were calculated for all possible shade pairs within each device. For each shade pair, every specimen from one shade (n = 3) was compared with every specimen from the other shade (n = 3), yielding nine ΔE 00 values per device and shade pair. These values were averaged to produce a single mean ΔE 00 per device for each of the 28 unique shade comparisons. The resulting data were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA, with pairwise comparison and device as factors.

Agreement between device‐specific mean ΔE 00 values and the benchtop reference was assessed using OLS linear regression, with benchtop mean ΔE 00 values as the dependent variable and the corresponding device mean ΔE 00 values as independent variables. Regression coefficients, coefficients of determination (R 2), RMSE, and residuals were calculated for each device. Residuals versus fitted values were plotted to assess homoscedasticity and detect any systematic trends.

Results

Agreement for Individual CIELAB Color Coordinates

Linear regression analyses revealed high correlations between the benchtop spectrophotometer and both clinical devices for all three CIELAB coordinates (Figure 2). For L* values, Optishade demonstrated slopes close to unity and the highest coefficients of determination, indicating strong agreement with the reference method and minimal scatter. Borea Cobra showed greater dispersion and lower R 2 values for L*, whereas smartphone‐derived values exhibited systematic offsets and reduced agreement.

Linear regression plots of CIELAB color parameters (A—L*, B—a*, C—b*) comparing Minolta measurements (dependent variable) with Optishade, Borea Cobra, and smartphone measurements (independent variables). Each point represents a single specimen. Solid lines indicate ordinary least squares regression, and the dashed line indicates the identity line (y = x).

For the a* and b* coordinates, both Optishade and Borea Cobra showed strong linear relationships with the benchtop spectrophotometer, although proportional bias was observed, particularly for b* values. Smartphone measurements demonstrated the greatest deviations from the reference, with lower slopes and wider residual distributions, indicating compression of chromatic differences.

For the L* parameter, Bland–Altman analysis revealed the lowest bias and the narrowest limits of agreement for Optishade, followed by Borea Cobra (Figure 3). For the b* parameter, Optishade also showed the lowest bias, with Borea Cobra (the narrowest limits of agreement) ranking second. In contrast, for the a* parameter, Borea Cobra demonstrated the lowest bias and the narrowest limits of agreement. For all parameters, smartphone measurements exhibited the greatest bias.

Bland–Altman plots comparing color parameters measured using the benchtop spectrophotometer (reference) with Optishade, Borea Cobra, and smartphone for each color parameter (A—L*, B—a*, and C—b*). The solid horizontal line indicates mean bias (Reference − method); dashed lines indicate the 95% limits of agreement. LLA: Lower limit agreement, LLU: Upper limit agreement.

Color Difference Analysis Across Composite Shades

Two‐way ANOVA demonstrated that both shade (p < 0.001) and device (p < 0.001) had a significant effect on ΔE 00 values, and a significant shade × device interaction was also observed (p = 0.022). Across all evaluated shades, ΔE 00 values obtained with Optishade and Borea Cobra generally ranged between 4 and 6 and were frequently not significantly different from one another within the same shade (Figure 4). These results suggest a comparable level of color agreement between the two clinical devices when referenced to the benchtop spectrophotometer.

Forest plot of mean ΔE 00 values calculated using the benchtop spectrophotometer as the reference, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals according to the composite shade and measurement device.

In contrast, smartphone‐derived measurements consistently produced substantially higher ΔE 00 values, typically ranging from approximately 8 to more than 15, and were statistically worse than both clinical devices for nearly every shade. Forest plots of mean ΔE 00 values and 95% confidence intervals highlighted the consistently larger deviations associated with smartphone imaging and the narrower confidence intervals observed for Optishade, reflecting lower variability.

Intershade Color Difference and Device Agreement

Regression analyses (Figure 5) using benchtop inter shade ΔE 00 values as the dependent variable demonstrated strong linear relationships for Optishade and Borea Cobra, with high R 2 values. Optishade showed slopes slightly below unity, indicating mild compression of inter shade differences, whereas Borea Cobra demonstrated slopes greater than unity, suggesting expansion of larger color differences. Smartphone measurements exhibited the weakest agreement, with lower R 2 values and higher RMSE, reflecting greater inconsistency in representing inter shade spacing.

Residual plots from linear regressions of Minolta versus Optishade, Borea Cobra, and smartphone for ΔE 00 values calculated from pairwise comparisons among different composite shades. Each point represents a single shade pair comparison. Solid lines indicate ordinary least squares regression, and the dashed line represents the identity line (y = x).

Two‐way ANOVA showed that both shade (p < 0.001) and device (p < 0.001) had significant effects on ΔE 00 values, with a significant shade × device interaction also observed (p < 0.001). The smartphone generally produced higher ΔE 00 values, particularly for shade pairs with larger color differences, whereas the benchtop spectrophotometer yielded lower values with narrower confidence intervals (Figure 6). Optishade and Borea Cobra demonstrated intermediate performance, with confidence intervals largely overlapping those of the benchtop device for most comparisons. For shade pairs with higher ΔE 00 values, Borea Cobra tended to underestimate ΔE 00 relative to the benchtop spectrophotometer, whereas the opposite trend was observed for the other devices.

Forest plot showing mean ΔE 00 values with 95% confidence intervals for each pairwise shade comparison across devices.

Figure 7 shows a heatmap visualization of ΔE 00 differences relative to the benchtop spectrophotometer, highlighting device‐specific patterns. When interpreted using established perceptibility (PT = 0.8) and acceptability (AT = 1.8) thresholds, Optishade showed that most intershade ΔE 00 differences were below the perceptibility threshold. Only one shade comparison approached the acceptability threshold (approximately +1.4 ΔE 00), and none exceeded the AT, indicating predominantly imperceptible and clinically acceptable deviations.

Heatmap of bias (difference between the benchtop spectrophotometer and other devices) for each pairwise composite shade comparison. Positive values (red) indicate ΔE 00 values higher than those measured by the benchtop spectrophotometer, whereas negative values (blue) indicate lower values.

For Borea Cobra, intershade ΔE 00 differences were generally small but exhibited a tendency toward negative bias. Most values remained below the perceptibility threshold; however, several shade pairs showed negative deviations exceeding the PT. Importantly, no Borea Cobra comparison exceeded the acceptability threshold, indicating that although some differences would be perceptible, they would remain clinically acceptable.

In contrast, smartphone‐based measurements demonstrated substantially larger positive deviations across numerous shade pairs. The majority of intershade ΔE 00 differences exceeded the perceptibility threshold, and multiple comparisons surpassed the acceptability threshold, indicating frequent perceptible and clinically unacceptable discrepancies relative to the reference method.

Discussion

This in vitro study evaluated the precision of color measurements obtained using three clinically applicable, image‐based methods for assessing localized color differences, such as those encountered in restored teeth. The findings demonstrated that both Optishade and Borea Cobra exhibited strong linear relationships with the benchtop spectrophotometer and produced relatively small ΔE 00 deviations, indicating good agreement with the reference standard. In contrast, smartphone‐derived measurements showed substantially lower concordance across all CIELAB parameters and consistently overestimated color differences. Therefore, based on these results, the hypothesis of this study was accepted.

The superior performance of Optishade and Borea Cobra compared with the smartphone can be explained by fundamental differences in their design and intended purpose. Both devices were specifically developed for color measurement in dentistry and incorporate controlled illumination, standardized optical geometry, and calibrated sensors engineered to approximate CIE colorimetric response functions [11, 29]. In contrast, the smartphone is a general‐purpose imaging device whose sensor characteristics and internal image‐processing algorithms are optimized for aesthetic rendering rather than objective colorimetry [29]. Although auxiliary lighting systems can enhance standardization, the present results show that even with structured illumination and postprocessing white‐balance correction, smartphone‐based measurements displayed excessively wide limits of agreement and notable compression of chromatic differences. These findings reinforce that, despite efforts to improve reproducibility, the smartphone remains primarily a qualitative imaging tool and is not suitable for precise, quantitative color assessment in dentistry.

Among the dedicated devices, Optishade demonstrated the strongest overall performance, showing the smallest measurement biases and the narrowest limits of agreement for the L* and b* parameters, as well as the highest coefficients of determination in the linear regression analyses for L*. This performance reflects the device's high repeatability, likely attributable to its design as a clinical colorimeter with factory calibration and standardized internal processing. The precision demonstrated by Optishade is consistent with the behavior expected of instruments specifically developed for dental shade matching, which have been shown to achieve high accuracy under controlled conditions and to mitigate the inherent limitations of visual assessment [11, 29]. Nevertheless, a systematic positive bias was identified for L* and b*, indicating that Optishade tends to yield slightly lower lightness and yellow‐axis values than the benchtop spectrophotometer. This bias is plausibly linked to differences in optical geometry and sensor spectral responsivity—factors known to influence colorimetric outputs and contribute to interdevice variability [12, 13, 16]. The comparatively weaker correlations commonly observed for the a* coordinate in digital color‐measurement systems, as also noted here, further support the influence of device‐specific optical configuration on chromatic accuracy [27].

Borea Cobra likewise demonstrated good agreement with the reference, particularly for the b* axis, for which it showed the smallest bias and a slope closest to unity. This enhanced performance in the yellow‐blue dimension may reflect sensor characteristics optimized for that spectral range. The device also reproduced the lightness scale adequately, with an L* slope near unity; however, greater scatter reduced overall precision. This increased dispersion is likely related to the contact‐based measurement geometry, which can heighten sensitivity to minor surface irregularities, subtle variations in pressure or angulation, and small inconsistencies in positioning. The comparatively weaker performance for a* suggests that the red spectral region is captured with less fidelity by this sensor. Interdevice discrepancies are well documented, as is the tendency of some clinical instruments to exhibit lower precision than benchtop spectrophotometers [14, 15]. These patterns align with the present observations and underscore the role of optical design and geometry in device‐dependent performance.

By contrast, the smartphone consistently produced the poorest results. Regression slopes for the chromatic axes were the lowest, indicating marked compression of color differences; less yellowish specimens tended to appear even less yellow, evidencing distortion in b* capture. The device also showed the largest bias for L* and the widest limits of agreement across all parameters. These outcomes reflect the absence of colorimetric sensor calibration, proprietary image‐processing pipelines that modify hue and contrast, and the practical difficulty of fully standardizing photographic variables even with auxiliary lighting and stabilization. Although light‐correcting accessories have been reported to enable clinically acceptable shade matching in restorative workflows [28], the present findings demonstrate that for precise color quantification of individual composite shades against a spectrophotometric reference, systematic biases persist and errors are amplified in more chromatically saturated specimens. The consistently higher values observed across all color coordinates compared with the spectrophotometric reference corroborate a systematic positive bias, with divergence increasing as a* and b* values rise—further evidencing error amplification in saturated regions [24]. Additionally, a trend of increasing discrepancy relative to the reference was observed for higher a* and b* values, indicating that error amplifies in more saturated specimens.

Despite the limitations observed in absolute color‐coordinate measurement, all devices demonstrated a reasonable ability to order resin shades from lightest to darkest and from least to most saturated, as reflected by the high coefficients of determination. This suggests that for clinical scenarios in which relative shade selection is more critical than exact color quantification, all three methods may offer some utility. The preservation of relative shade ordering, despite discrepancies in absolute values, aligns with previous reports showing strong correlations between smartphone‐based ΔE 00 values and spectrophotometric references [24]. However, the magnitude of intershade differences was distorted in distinct device‐specific ways: Optishade mildly compressed differences, Borea Cobra tended to expand them, and the smartphone produced more pronounced compression.

A particularly noteworthy finding was that although Borea Cobra generally expanded intershade differences, it underestimated high‐contrast shade pairs because of its negative intercept, a pattern also clearly reflected in the heatmap analysis. Clinically, this implies that Borea Cobra may underestimate differences between distant shades while amplifying subtle distinctions among close shades, a behavior that may be advantageous or problematic depending on the clinical objective. The smartphone, despite being the least precise, preserved the correct ordering of shades, indicating that it may still provide a rough qualitative estimate of relative shade differences when dedicated equipment is unavailable; however, its consistent overestimation of color differences underscores the risk of misinterpretation if values are taken literally.

When interpreted using established perceptibility and acceptability thresholds, Optishade and Borea Cobra demonstrated predominantly clinically acceptable agreement with the benchtop spectrophotometer, whereas smartphone‐based imaging frequently produced perceptible and unacceptable overestimations of intershade color differences. The frequent exceedance of perceptibility and acceptability thresholds observed for smartphone‐derived ΔE 00 values indicates a limited ability to reliably quantify intershade color differences when compared with dedicated image‐based color‐measurement devices. These findings carry important implications for both clinical practice and dental research. Optishade proved to be the most reliable instrument for quantitative color measurement, offering high repeatability and predictable, correctable systematic errors. Borea Cobra may be particularly useful when differentiating very closely related shades, although caution is warranted when comparing shades from different families because of its tendency to distort larger color differences. In contrast, the smartphone is not recommended for applications requiring precise color quantification, though standardized photographs continue to play an important role in clinical documentation and communication.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The study employed flat composite resin specimens, which do not replicate the optical complexity of natural teeth, including layered structure, translucency gradients, and surface morphology. Intraoral measurements are further complicated by distracting structures such as mucosa, lips, and tongue position. Only eight shades from a single resin system were tested, limiting generalizability across brands and materials. Future investigations should explore intra‐ and interoperator reproducibility, evaluate device performance directly on natural teeth, and expand testing to include a wider variety of restorative materials such as ceramics and alternative resin systems. Clinical studies are particularly warranted given the additional complexities of the oral environment.

Conclusion

Image‐based color‐measurement devices differed in their agreement with the benchtop spectrophotometer. Optishade demonstrated the closest agreement and the highest precision for CIELAB coordinates and ΔE 00 values, exhibiting the smallest biases and narrowest limits of agreement. Borea Cobra also showed good agreement with the reference standard, although greater variability and device‐specific distortions in intershade color differences were observed. Smartphone‐based measurements showed the weakest agreement, with systematic positive bias, reduced precision, and distorted representation of color differences.
