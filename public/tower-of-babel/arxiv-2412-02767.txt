Abstract
Linear regressions with endogeneity are widely used to estimate causal effects. This paper studies a framework that involves two common practical issues: endogeneity of the regressors and heteroskedasticity that depends on endogenous regressors, i.e. , endogenous heteroskedasticity. To address the inconsistency of the two-stage least squares estimator in this scenario, and recover the causal parameters of interest, we develop a framework for practical estimation and inference based on the control function approach allowing for discrete and continuous regressors. In particular, we suggest a simple two-step estimation procedure. We establish the limiting properties of the estimator, namely, consistency and asymptotic normality. In addition, we develop practical valid inference methods by proposing an estimator for the asymptotic variance-covariance matrix, and formally establishing its consistency. Monte Carlo simulations provide evidence on the finite-sample performance of the proposed methods and evaluate different implementation strategies. We revisit an empirical application on job training to illustrate the methods.



Keywords
: Two-stage least squares, instrumental variables, heteroskedasticity, control function.

Introduction

This paper studies estimation and inference of linear models with endogeneity and heteroskedasticity. At the estimation stage, endogeneity is typically addressed using instrumental variables (IV) and two-stage least squares (2SLS) procedures. Heteroskedasticity, on the other hand, is usually handled with robust inference techniques. However, when heteroskedasticity is caused by an endogenous regressor, a situation referred to as endogenous heteroskedasticity, the 2SLS estimator may become inconsistent because the necessary exogeneity condition fails. The literature on econometric models that allow for endogenous heteroskedasticity is limited (see, e.g.,
Chen (2008)
,
Florens et al. (2008)
,
Chen and Khan (2014)
, and
Abrevaya and Xu (2023)
).

In this paper, we focus on this issue and develop methods for estimation and inference of parameters in linear triangular models with endogeneity and endogenous heteroskedasticity that employ a control function (CF) approach and allow for continuous and discrete regressors. Endogenous heteroskedasticity models can be viewed as a special case of nonseparable models, see, e.g.,
Chesher (2003)
,
Chernozhukov et al. (2007)
,
Florens et al. (2008)
,
Imbens and Newey (2009)
and
Jun (2009)
. These general methods usually employ nonparametric modeling and estimation together with a CF. Differently from these papers, our interest lies in practical linear regression models in the presence of multiple covariates, such that nonparametric methods become impractical. Thus, we rely on a feasible parametric model using the CF approach.

There is a large literature on the CF approach. See, e.g., among many others,
Newey et al. (1999)
,
il Kim and Petrin (2022)
, and
Han and Kaido (2025)
. Different alternatives have been studied in the literature. See the survey by
Blundell and Powell (2008)
, and
Imbens and Newey (2009)
for nonparametric and semiparametric models.
We start by building on the general identification results in
Florens et al. (2008)
and tailoring the conditions to accommodate continuous and discrete endogenous variables, as well as exogenous covariates, such that we are able to write the parameter of interest as an explicit function of observables, and thus derive the estimator of interest.
The key restrictions for identification are a CF condition with a flexible parameterization, along with another parameterization of the skedastic function in the structural equation.
Given a valid CF constructed from the first stage, and the structure of the skedastic function, the parameter of interest in the second stage can be identified using a standard ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. We highlight that our proposed approach includes the 2SLS as a special case when there is no endogenous heteroskedasticity and there is no correction for the first-stage heteroskedasticity, a condition that depends on the CF assumption.

Estimation is implemented in a two-step procedure. First, the skedastic function in the first step can be estimated using flexible scale function methods suggested in
Romano and Wolf (2017)
. Given the estimate of the skedastic function, the CF can be computed as the normalized errors from the first-stage regression.
In the second step, one performs a simple linear regression with a CF approach. Empirically, this step uses an OLS regression of the dependent variable on the endogenous, exogenous, and CF variables augmented with their interacting terms. We establish the limiting statistical properties of the proposed two-step estimator. Mild sufficient conditions are provided for the estimator to have desired asymptotic properties, namely, consistency and asymptotic normality. The limiting theory presented here incorporates elements of both the CF and generated regressors.

In addition, we develop practical statistical inference procedures by suggesting an estimator for the asymptotic variance-covariance matrix with the generated regressors. We formally establish the consistency of the variance-covariance matrix estimator. The proposed methods provide foundation for general inference procedures. Testing for general linear hypotheses is easily accommodated by Wald type tests, and non-linear hypotheses are readily available by employing the Delta method. We also suggest a test for endogenous heteroskedasticity.

We evaluate the finite sample performance of the proposed methods using Monte Carlo exercises. Numerical simulations confirm that the presence of endogenous heteroskedasticity in the structural equation induces bias in the 2SLS estimator. Moreover, heteroskedasticity in the first-stage equation may amplify the bias. The proposed CF method is able to produce estimates that concentrate around the true value of the target parameter. In addition, results improve when the sample size increases.

Finally, we present an empirical application that illustrates the framework discussed in this paper. We apply the estimator to study the effect of public-sponsored training program Job Training Partnership Act (JTPA) on future earnings. We estimate the effect of interest using the CF, and for comparison, the 2SLS. Empirical results show evidence that the 2SLS-IV estimator (the typical application in other papers that used the JTPA data) is substantially smaller than the alternative CF methods.

The literature considering the effects of endogenous heteroskedasticity is restricted.
Chen (2008)
studies a wage model in which the variance of potential wages depends on the endogenously chosen level of schooling, and addresses the resulting selection with a parametric, Heckman-type control-function approach.

Florens et al. (2008)
use the CF approach to identify the average treatment effect and the effect of treatment on the treated in models with a continuous endogenous regressor allowing for endogenous heteroskedasticity. They consider a nonparametric model for the endogenous variable and a flexible polynomial for the skedastic function. However, their focus is on the case of no additional exogenous covariates, and estimation and inference are not provided.

Chen and Khan (2014)
discuss identification and estimation of models with endogenous heteroskedasticity in binary treatments using semiparametric IV and pairwise matching. Their main object of interest is different from ours, as they focus on the variance of potential outcomes.

Abrevaya and Xu (2023)
show that when a binary treatment is endogenous and there is endogenous heteroskedasticity, that is, the endogenous treatment indicator also affects the scale of the outcome variable, then standard methods for estimation of average treatment effects fail. In particular, they consider the standard IV estimator with binary treatment and show that it is an inconsistent estimator for the average treatment effect. After nonparametric identification is established, a nonparametric IV estimator is provided for the mean and variance treatment effect, as well as the average treatment effect on the treated.

2
A linear model with endogenous heteroskedasticity

In this section, we first describe the model of interest, which contains endogenous heteroskedasticity. Second, we motivate the proposed methods by reviewing the inconsistency of the two-stage least squares in this scenario.

2.1
Model

We consider the following model:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=D\alpha_{1}+X^{\prime}\alpha_{2}+g(D,X)\varepsilon

(1)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=Z^{\prime}\pi_{1}+X^{\prime}\pi_{2}+h(X,Z)V,

(2)

where the main parameter of interest is the scalar
\alpha_{1}
. The vector of exogenous variables
X
is
(d_{x}-1)
-dimensional (including a constant), and the instrumental variable (IV)
Z
is
d_{z}
-dimensional. The variables
\varepsilon
and
V
are unobservable and their correlation makes
D
potentially endogenous.

The model in equations (
1
) and (
2
) has several important features. Despite the linearity in
D
in (
1
), it allows for heterogeneous effects on
Y
due to the term
g(D,X)\varepsilon
. Because of this, the parameter of interest has an interpretation as an average effect. Indeed, if we define the potential outcome for unit
i
when
D=d
as
Y_{di}=d\alpha_{1}+X_{i}^{\prime}\alpha_{2}+g(d,X_{i})\varepsilon_{i}
, then
E[Y_{di}-Y_{d^{\prime}i}]=(d-d^{\prime})\alpha_{1}
.

3

See Appendix
A.1
for more about the interpretation of
\alpha_{1}
.

However, the exogeneity conditions
E[\varepsilon|X,Z]=0
and
E[V|X,Z]=0
are not sufficient to identify
\alpha_{1}
via 2SLS, since
E[g(D,X)\varepsilon|X,Z]
may not be zero. Since the conditional variance of the error term in

1
)
is
g(D,X)^{2}Var[\varepsilon|D,X]
, we refer to it as “endogenous heteroskedasticity,” a terminology borrowed from
Abrevaya and Xu (2023)
.

The model above is a particular case of non-additive triangular models, where the interest lies in estimating the average linear effect,
\alpha_{1}
. This specification would allow us to study the role of the skedastic function
g
in the 2SLS bias for
\alpha_{1}
and specific solutions using the control function approach. A comparable model is used in
Abrevaya and Xu (2023)
to study the role of endogenous heteroskedasticity in treatment effects models. Furthermore, equation (
2
) could be rewritten as a general model
D=m(Z,X,V)
but we use a similar skedastic structure to match equation (
1
). The parametric model in the first stage is mainly used due to the curse of dimensionality problem, since most empirical work uses multiple control variables. In a nonparametric case, estimation could rely on methods suggested by
Jin et al. (2015)
and
Linton and Xiao (2019)
.

2.2
Motivation: Inconsistency of the 2SLS estimator

The 2SLS is a popular method to address endogeneity in linear models. In this section, we show that the 2SLS estimator estimates the average effect
\alpha_{1}
with a bias in the presence of endogenous heteroskedasticity. Although the result here is simple, intuitive, and similar issues have been highlighted in the literature, we include this section to motivate the linear representation above.

The failure of 2SLS to identify
\alpha_{1}
in the presence of endogenous heteroskedasticity was highlighted by
Abrevaya and Xu (2023)
in the case of a binary
D
. When a binary IV is available, they offer a detailed analysis of the bias and propose a novel identification and estimation strategy. In a similar context with a continuous
D
,
Florens et al. (2008)
argue that the usual exogeneity IV requirements are not sufficient for identification.

The following example illustrates the bias in the 2SLS procedure in a model described by (
1
)–(
2
).

Example 1
.

Consider the following simple linear case of model (
1
)–(
2
) with one endogenous variable, one instrument,
g(D)=1+\delta D
and
h(Z)=1+Z\gamma
:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=D\alpha_{1}+(1+D\delta)\varepsilon

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=Z\pi_{1}+(1+Z\gamma)V.

Here,
E[D\varepsilon]\neq 0
through the possible correlation between
\varepsilon
and
V
. We assume that
E[\varepsilon|Z]=E[V|Z]=0
. The 2SLS estimator of
\alpha_{1}
is the sample counterpart of
\frac{Cov[Z,Y]}{Cov[Z,D]}
. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle Cov[Z,Y]

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}Cov[Z,D]+Cov[Z,\varepsilon]+\delta Cov[Z,D\varepsilon].

Now,
E[\varepsilon|Z]=0
implies
Cov[Z,\varepsilon]=0
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{Cov[Z,Y]}{Cov[Z,D]}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}+\delta\frac{Cov[Z,D\varepsilon]}{Cov[Z,D]}.

The exogeneity assumptions on
Z
do not imply that
Cov[Z,D\varepsilon]=0
. Therefore,
\alpha_{1}
is not identified by the 2SLS moment conditions whenever there is heteroskedasticity in the structural equation, i.e.,
\delta\neq 0.

Under the stronger independence assumption:
Z\perp\!\!\!\perp(V,\varepsilon)
(typical in experimental settings when
Z
is randomly assigned), the expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\frac{Cov[Z,Y]}{Cov[Z,D]}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}+\delta\gamma\frac{E[V\varepsilon]}{\pi_{1}}

In this case, the joint heteroskedasticity together with the endogeneity (in the form of
E[V\varepsilon]\neq 0
) is inducing the bias. Thus, in this case, one could have a situation where there is heteroskedasticity in the structural equation with
\delta\neq 0
, but if there is no heteroskedasticity in the first stage,
\gamma=0
, then 2SLS is able to correctly identify the parameter of interest.

Next, we provide a formal result deriving the asymptotic bias in the 2SLS estimator under the endogenous heteroskedasticity. This is not a novel result
per se
, but we specify it to our particular setting.

We maintain the following assumptions.

Assumption 1

(i)

We provide some further remarks to understand the intuition and nature of the result in Lemma
1
.

1.

The asymptotic bias of the 2SLS estimator in equation (
3
) can be succinctly written as
\Sigma_{h}^{-1}\pi_{1}^{\prime}E[\overline{Z}g(D,X)\varepsilon]
, but this might obscure the fact that

is also playing a role through

, and could, in principle, amplify the bias.

2.

If
g(\cdot)\equiv 1
, i.e., there is no heteroskedasticity in the structural equation, then there is no asymptotic bias in the 2SLS estimator because
E[\overline{Z}{g(D,X)\varepsilon}]=E[\overline{Z}{\varepsilon}]=0
by Assumption
1
. The lack of bias is regardless of
h(\cdot)
. For a general

, the form of

may matter.

3.

If
h(\cdot)\equiv 1
, i.e., there is no heteroskedasticity in the first stage, then the asymptotic bias depends only on the form of
g(\cdot)
.

4.

The strength of the instrumental variable, through the magnitude of
\pi_{1}
, can alleviate the bias. To see this, suppose that
d_{z}=1
. Then, the asymptotic bias is inversely related to
\pi_{1}
:

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}_{1}=\alpha_{1}+\frac{E[\overline{Z}{g(D,X)\varepsilon}]}{\pi_{1}E[\overline{Z}^{2}]}.

Even in this simple case, the sign of the bias is not totally obvious.

5.

If it is suspected that
b_{0}\leq E[\overline{Z}g(D,X)\varepsilon]\leq b_{1}
, then partial identification of
\alpha_{1}
is possible. Otherwise, this can be used in a sensitivity analysis.

6.

In this paper, we focus on the 2SLS estimator. Nevertheless, we highlight the same result is valid for a GMM estimator. The main intuition is that, under endogenous heteroskedasticity, the moment condition used for estimation would be misspecified through the function
g(\cdot)
.

3
A control function approach

This section discusses sufficient conditions, based on the control function (CF) approach, that allow for identification of the parameters of interest in the model given in equations (
1
)–(
2
), in the presence of both endogeneity and general heteroskedasticity. In the next section, we will suggest a practical estimator and develop inference procedures. Identification in additively separable models with endogenous heteroskedasticity using a CF strategy was initially proposed by
Florens et al. (2008)
.
Our analysis builds on their results, with the key distinction being a trade-off between making a stochastic polynomial assumption on the CF in our approach versus allowing for a general nonparametric function in their approach. The advantage of our strategy is reflected in the practicality of the estimator. We will show that, by specializing the CF to be a polynomial, the identification result shows that the parameter of interest can be written as a simple explicit function of the data, which in turn can be used for estimation and inference. Moreover, our approach allows the 2SLS-IV estimator to be nested within our model, and thus being a special case.

We maintain the following assumption.

Assumption 3
.

Control Function Condition:
E[\varepsilon|D,X,Z]=E[\varepsilon|V]=r(V)
.

Assumption
3
is a standard control function condition, see, e.g.,

Blundell and Powell (2008)
. This is the same condition as Assumption A-3 in
Florens et al. (2008, p.1196)
. Under this condition, the conditional expectation of the response variable in equation (
1

\displaystyle E[Y|D,X,Z]

\displaystyle=D\alpha_{1}+X^{\prime}\alpha_{2}+g(D,X)E[\varepsilon|D,X,Z]

\displaystyle=D\alpha_{1}+X^{\prime}\alpha_{2}+g(D,X)r(V).

(4)

It should be noted that, from the previous display, the skedastic term in the structural equation does not depend only on the control function, but also on
g(\cdot)
. The next example, where we assume that both
D
and
Z
are continuous, shows that further assumptions are required to identify
\alpha_{1}
.

Example 2
.

Consider the model in Example
1

E[Y|D,Z]=D\alpha_{1}+g(D)r(\psi(D,Z))

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial D}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}+g_{D}r+gr_{V}\psi_{D}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial Z}

\displaystyle=gr_{V}\psi_{Z},

where the derivatives are denoted by subscripts, and we omit the arguments in each function. The left hand-sides are identifiable. By multiplying the second equation of the above display by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial D}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}+g_{D}r+\frac{\partial f}{\partial Z}\psi_{Z}^{\prime}(\psi_{Z}\psi_{Z}^{\prime})^{-1}\psi_{D}.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial D}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial Z}\psi_{Z}^{\prime}(\psi_{Z}\psi_{Z}^{\prime})^{-1}\psi_{D}

6

6

For a block matrix

\displaystyle\det E[RR^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\det E[WW^{\prime}]\cdot\left(E[D^{2}]-E[DW^{\prime}]E[WW^{\prime}]^{-1}E[WD]\right)

Remark 2
.

The no-constant normalization of Assumption
5
is a simple

, but it is not equivalent to it. Intuitively, the same covariate cannot appear both in the structural linear function and in the implied control function expanded by the skedastic function. Every

with

, so

contains no pure

; a constant in

would break this. On the other hand, Assumption

may hold
with

provided

has no linear term—an alternative normalization to that of Assumption

can fail even when

has no constant, for reasons unrelated to it: for instance a binary

Remark 3
.

Note that if

is a dummy variable, as in the empirical application, then there is no need to consider a polynomial model of it and the regression is simplified. The identification result in Lemma

above shows that the parameter of interest

in equation (

Remark 4
.

When

is discrete,

(X,Z)
, the control function

has finite support. For example, in the binary

(X,Z)
. However, this does not prevent identification, because the rank condition in Assumption

concerns the

. Since

is governed by the fitted value

remain linearly independent. If

4
Illustrative examples

To illustrate the methods we study in this paper, this section provides two simple examples considering different economic models. The examples help to motivate the proposed econometric methods and required assumptions into different economic models with endogenous heteroskedasticity. The first example, inspired by
Florens et al. (2008)
, relates education (the choice variable) and wages.
7
7

7

This example is also related to the identification of potential wage distributions by education in
Chen (2008)
.

The second, inspired by
Kitagawa (2014)
, relates labor (the choice variable) and a production function. In both, it is an optimization problem that generates an endogenous choice variable, while the outcome equation exhibits heteroskedasticity. To keep matters simple, we abstract from exogenous covariates in all cases.

4.1
Heterogeneous returns to education and linear costs

As mentioned before, this is a version of the model considered by
Florens et al. (2008)
. Suppose that the economic agent receives wages,
Y_{d}
, for given years of education
d
, and wages are given by

\displaystyle Y_{d}=\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}d+\frac{\phi_{2}}{2}d^{2}+d\varepsilon,

(9)

where
\varepsilon
is the unobserved heterogeneity in the
wage level. The endogenous heteroskedasticity term is
d\varepsilon
: two individuals with the same education differ in wages by an amount that is increasing in
d
, so that more education yields not only higher but also more dispersed earnings. Because the agent chooses
d
optimally after observing
\varepsilon
, education is self-selected on the very heterogeneity that governs its dispersion, so
\varepsilon
and
d
are correlated.

On the other hand, suppose that education costs,
C_{d}
, are linear in
d
as follows:

\displaystyle C_{d}=c_{0}+c_{1}Z+c_{2}Zd+d\varepsilon_{1},

(10)

where
\varepsilon_{1}
is the unobserved heterogeneity in the cost of schooling.
8
8

8

The model in
Florens et al. (2008)
has quadratic costs
d^{2}
interacted with a function of
Z
. We rule out this case in order to have a linear in
Z
first stage.
The variable
Z
is a factor that only affects the cost of schooling, such as the price of education, for example. The agent optimizes the difference,
Y_{d}-C_{d}
, by choosing the schooling level
d
. Assuming continuity, the (stochastic) first order condition is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(Y_{d}-C_{d})}{\partial d}=\alpha_{1}+\phi_{2}d+\varepsilon-c_{2}Z-\varepsilon_{1}=0

(11)

and the optimal
d
, provided
\phi_{2}<0
(so that the second-order condition for a maximum holds), is

\displaystyle d=-\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\phi_{2}}+\frac{c_{2}}{\phi_{2}}Z+\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon}{\phi_{2}}.

(12)

Comparing this optimal choice in (
12
) with the first stage (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\pi_{1}=\frac{c_{2}}{\phi_{2}},\qquad\pi_{2}=-\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\phi_{2}},\qquad h(X,Z)\equiv 1,\qquad V=\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon}{\phi_{2}}.

(13)

Thus, the first stage is homoskedastic (
h=1
), and the control function
V
is a fixed linear combination of the primitive shocks
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{1})
. Conditional on
Z
, the optimal choice (
12
) is a one-to-one (affine) transformation of
V
. Hence
E[\varepsilon|D,Z]=E[\varepsilon|V,Z]
. Now, if
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{1})
is independent of
Z
, then
E[\varepsilon|V,Z]=E[\varepsilon|V]
. This is exactly the control function condition in Assumption
3
, with
r(V)=E[\varepsilon\mid V]
.

Substituting the optimal
d
from (
12
) into the wage equation (
9
) yields the realized outcome

\displaystyle Y=\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}D+\frac{\phi_{2}}{2}D^{2}+D\,\varepsilon,

(14)

which is of the form in equation (
1
) with a single constant covariate (
X=1
, so that
X^{\prime}\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{2}
), skedastic function
g(D)=D
, and augmented by the quadratic term
\tfrac{\phi_{2}}{2}D^{2}
.

Now, for Assumption
5
to hold, we need some primitive structure on the error terms. For example, a well-known one is that
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{1})
are jointly normal with mean zero.
9
9

9

Note that the same results can be obtained by directly assuming linearity of the conditional expectation without referring to joint normality. When studying the wage equation under endogenous heteroskedasticity,
Chen (2008)
uses a Heckman-type two-step method that is a special case of the control function approach.
In this case,

\displaystyle r(V)=E[\varepsilon|V]=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon,V)}{\operatorname{Var}(V)}V.

(15)

This is a polynomial of degree
k_{v}=1
in
V
with no constant term, which is precisely Assumption
5
.

4.2
Heterogeneous returns to scale in production

We present a second economic model that maps into the econometric model in (
1
)–(
2
), building on a simple production-function example in
Kitagawa (2014, Section 1)
, which is itself based on
Marschak and Andrews (1944)
. This example shows that a single productivity shock to the output elasticity of labor is what separates the homoskedastic benchmark, where 2SLS is consistent, from a model with endogenous heteroskedasticity of the form
g(D)=D
, where 2SLS fails.

Consider a firm that produces a homogeneous good,
Q
, using labor
L
, with the Cobb-Douglas technology

\displaystyle Q(L)=e^{\alpha_{2}}\,L^{\alpha_{1}+\varepsilon},

(16)

where
\alpha_{1}
, the output elasticity of labor, is the parameter of interest,
\alpha_{2}
is a constant, and
\varepsilon
is an unobserved mean-zero shock to
\alpha_{1}
, which satisfies
0<\alpha_{1}+\varepsilon<1
almost surely.
10
10

10

Kitagawa (2014)
places the unobserved productivity shock on the constant
\alpha_{2}
instead:
Q(L)=e^{\alpha_{2}+\eta}L^{\alpha_{1}}
. Such a shock enters the log-output equation additively and yields the standard, homoskedastic linear IV model, in which 2SLS is consistent. Placing the shock on the output elasticity
\alpha_{1}
instead is what generates the endogenous heteroskedasticity.
Writing
D=\log L
for the (endogenous) log-input, and
Y=\log Q(L)
for log-output, the structural equation that (
16
) gives rise to is

\displaystyle Y=\alpha_{1}D+\alpha_{2}+\varepsilon\,D.

(17)

The structural equation (
17
) is exactly of the form (
1
), with a single constant covariate (
X=1
, so that
X^{\prime}\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{2}
), skedastic function
g(D)=D
, and structural error
\varepsilon
. The endogenous heteroskedasticity can be interpreted economically as heterogeneity in the elasticity of output with respect to labor. Firms with
\varepsilon>0
have a higher labor-output elasticity,
\alpha_{1}+\varepsilon
, and therefore experience a larger percentage increase in output for a given percentage increase in labor input. Note that these differences in firm-specific elasticities reflect unobserved heterogeneity across firms, arising from differences in production technologies, work organization, or other firm-specific factors that affect the responsiveness of output to labor input.

Turning to the firm’s behavior, it is assumed that firms face a common output price
p
and a firm-specific wage
w
. Profits are then
pQ(L)-wL
. Endogeneity arises due to the optimal choice of labor because the firm chooses its input
after
observing its productivity, so
D=\log L
is correlated with
\varepsilon
.

Solving for the optimal log-labor demand yields

\displaystyle D=\frac{\alpha_{2}+\log(\alpha_{1}+\varepsilon)+Z}{1-\alpha_{1}-\varepsilon}.

(18)

where
Z:=\log(p/w)
is the (log) price ratio. This is a linear random coefficients model in the instrument
Z
, and

\displaystyle D=\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{1}Z,\qquad\gamma_{0}=\frac{\alpha_{2}+\log(\alpha_{1}+\varepsilon)}{1-\alpha_{1}-\varepsilon},\quad\gamma_{1}=\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{1}-\varepsilon}.

(19)

Decomposing each coefficient into its mean and a deviation,
\gamma_{0}=\pi_{2}+a
and
\gamma_{1}=\pi_{1}+b
with
\pi_{2}=E[\gamma_{0}]
,
\pi_{1}=E[\gamma_{1}]
, equation (
19
) becomes

\displaystyle D=\pi_{2}+\pi_{1}Z+U,

(20)

where
U:=a+bZ.
Our parametric first stage (
2
) writes the error in the multiplicatively separable form
h(X,Z)V
, with a single scalar
V
. The exact error
U=a+bZ
in (
20
) is not of this form, because
a
and
b
are two distinct nonlinear functions of the single shock
\varepsilon
; it instead falls under the general first-stage representation
D=m(Z,X,V)
noted after (
2
).

In this example the same scalar
\varepsilon
is both the structural shock and the unobservable driving the first stage: since
D
is a one-to-one function of
\varepsilon
given
Z
, the control function may be taken to be
V=\varepsilon
itself, so

\displaystyle r(V)=E[\varepsilon\mid V]=E[\varepsilon\mid\varepsilon]=\varepsilon=V.

(21)

Assumption
5
therefore holds
exactly
, with
r(V)=V
(that is,
k_{v}=1
,
\mu_{1}=1
, and no constant), and
without
the normality assumption required in the wage/cost example: a single-index unobservable makes the control function exact by construction. The price for this cleaner identification is that the first stage is heteroskedastic (
h\not\equiv 1
) and only approximately separable. This trade-off is itself informative: the very additivity that makes the standard production-function IV model tractable is what rules out endogenous heteroskedasticity, and relaxing it to obtain a heterogeneous causal effect reintroduces the difficulty that our control-function approach is designed to address.

5
Estimation and inference

This section proposes a practical estimator for the parameter of interest
\alpha_{1}
, and establishes its statistical limiting properties. We consider an estimator that consists of a two-step procedure. The overall procedure is as follows. In the first stage, one estimates the control function (CF),
V
. In the second-stage, one performs a simple OLS regression of the dependent variable
Y
on the regressors
(D,X^{\prime})^{\prime}
and the estimated CF,
\widehat{V}
, as well as on their polynomial interactions. In addition, we provide practical inference procedures.

5.1
First stage estimator: obtaining
\hat{V}

We employ OLS in the first stage and regress the endogenous variable
D
on the instruments,
Z
, and exogenous variables
X
, to compute the residuals

\displaystyle\check{V}_{i}

\displaystyle=D_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{1}-X_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{2}.

Next, we compute the first-stage skedastic function
h(\cdot)
. In practice, to estimate the skedastic function we employ the parametric models in
Romano and Wolf (2017)
or
Wooldridge (2012, ch. 8)
. For instance,

h(X,Z;\gamma)=\left(|Z^{\prime}|\gamma_{1}+|X^{\prime}|\gamma_{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(22)

or

h(X,Z;\gamma)=\left(\exp\left(|Z^{\prime}|\gamma_{1}+|X^{\prime}|\gamma_{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}.

(23)

The coefficients of these models can be estimated using (possibly non-linear) OLS regressions with
\check{V}^{2}_{i}
or
ln(\check{V}^{2}_{i})
as the dependent variable and the absolute value of the instruments and exogenous covariates. The resulting estimator is
\widehat{h}_{i}=h(X_{i},Z_{i};\widehat{\gamma})
. Using the estimated skedastic function,
\widehat{h}
, we obtain the normalized errors as

\widehat{V}_{i}=\check{V}_{i}/\widehat{h}_{i}.

The following assumption imposes a standard regularity condition on the skedastic function determining its smoothness and existence of moments of the conditional heteroskedasticity.

Assumption 8
.

Assume that

, a parametric function with parameter
\gamma
.
(i) The map

is twice differentiable with gradient denoted by
\nabla_{\gamma}h(x,z;\gamma)
; (ii)
E[\nabla_{\gamma}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)\nabla_{\gamma}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)^{\prime}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)^{2}]
has full rank; (iii) the vectors

and the components of

,
X_{i}h(X_{i},Z_{i})V_{i}
and
\nabla_{\gamma}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)\left(V_{i}^{2}-1\right)h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)^{3}

Denote
\phi:=(\pi_{1}^{\prime},\pi_{2}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime}
. The next result is the asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\phi}=(\hat{\pi}_{1}^{\prime},\hat{\pi}_{2}^{\prime},\hat{\gamma}^{\prime})^{\prime}

is detailed in the appendix.

Proposition 1

Under Assumptions

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\phi}-\phi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\phi,i}+o_{p}(1)\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega_{\phi}),

where

Second stage estimator: augmented control function estimator

given in equation (

5.3
Inference

Now we turn our attention to inference in the model under endogenous heteroskedasticity, and suggest a practical estimator for the asymptotic variance-covariance matrix. We also formally establish its consistency. Based on the result of Theorem
1
, for inference purposes, we deem
\hat{\alpha}_{1}
as approximately normal, centered at
\alpha_{1}
, and with variance given by
\Omega_{\alpha,(1,1)}/n
.

Estimation of
\Omega_{\alpha,(1,1)}
can be carried out by resorting to the sample counterpart of
\Omega_{\alpha}
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\psi}_{\alpha,i}\hat{\psi}_{\alpha,i}^{\prime}.

with

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\alpha,i}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{j}\widehat{R}_{j}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{U}_{i}+\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{j}\widehat{R}_{j}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{U}_{j}J_{\hat{\phi},j}-\widehat{R}_{j}\widehat{\alpha}^{\prime}J_{\hat{\phi},j}\right)\right)\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i},

where

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{i}

\displaystyle=Y_{i}-D_{i}\widehat{\alpha}_{1}-W_{i}(\widehat{\phi})^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha}_{w}=Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha},

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Sigma}_{\phi}^{-1}\hat{m}_{i},

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}&Z_{i}X_{i}^{\prime}&0\\
X_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}&X_{i}X_{i}^{\prime}&0\\
0&0&2[\nabla_{\gamma}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\widehat{\gamma})\nabla_{\gamma}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\widehat{\gamma})^{\prime}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\widehat{\gamma})^{2}]\\
\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{i}\left(D_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{1}-X_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{2}\right)\\
X_{i}\left(D_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{1}-X_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{2}\right)\\
\nabla_{\gamma}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\widehat{\gamma})\left(\hat{V}_{i}^{2}-1\right)h(X_{i},Z_{i};\widehat{\gamma})^{3}\end{bmatrix}.

Next, we state a result establishing the consistency of the above estimator.

Lemma 3
.

If Assumptions
8
and
9
hold,
E[\|R\|^{4}]<\infty
,
E[\|J_{\phi}\|^{4}]<\infty
,
E[U^{4}]<\infty
,
E[\|Z\|^{4}]<\infty
,
E[\|X\|^{4}]<\infty
,
E[|h(X,Z;\gamma)V|^{4}]<\infty
,
c_{i}(\phi):=\nabla_{\gamma}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)\left(V_{i}^{2}-1\right)h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)^{3}
is differentiable in
\phi
with derivative

\nabla_{\phi}c_{i}(\phi)
,
E\big[\|\nabla_{\phi}c_{i}(\phi)\|^{2}\big]<\infty
, and satisfies a uniform law of large numbers, then
\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}\overset{p}{\to}\Omega_{\alpha}.

Given the result in Lemma
3
and Theorem
1
, inference is standard. A
100(1-\theta)\%
confidence interval for
\alpha_{1}
can be constructed as
\widehat{\alpha}_{1}\pm z_{\theta/2}\sqrt{\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha,(1,1)}/n}
, where
\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha,(1,1)}
is the
(1,1)
element of
\widehat{\Omega}_{\alpha}
, and

is the corresponding critical value (
\theta/2

Moreover, given the asymptotic normality in Theorem
1

\alpha_{1}
, or more generally
\alpha
, can be accommodated by simple Wald-type tests. The
Wald process and associated limiting theory provide a natural foundation for the hypothesis
R\alpha=r
when
r
is known, with a Chi-square limiting distribution. Non-linear testing can be easily performed by employing the Delta method.

5.4
Testing for endogenous heteroskedasticity

In this section we propose a simple test for endogenous heteroskedasticity, that is, a test of whether the skedastic function
g(D,X)
actually depends on the endogenous regressor
D
. Writing the identifying regression (
6
) as
E[Y|D,X,Z]=D\alpha_{1}+W^{\prime}\alpha_{w}
as in Lemma
2
, the regressor vector
W
contains, among its components, the interactions
D^{s}V^{j}
for
s=1,\dots,k_{g}
and
j=1,\dots,k_{v}
; denote by

\beta_{sj}=\theta_{s}^{d}\mu_{j}

the coefficient on
D^{s}V^{j}
.

Endogenous heteroskedasticity is present exactly when
g(D,X)
depends on
D
,
i.e.
, when
\theta_{1}^{d},\dots,\theta_{k_{g}}^{d}
are not all zero. Since
\beta_{sj}=\theta_{s}^{d}\mu_{j}
and the control function is nondegenerate (some
\mu_{j}\neq 0
), this is equivalent to not all the interaction coefficients
\beta_{sj}
being zero. The null hypothesis of no endogenous heteroskedasticity is therefore

H_{0}:\ \beta_{sj}=0\quad\text{for all }s=1,\dots,k_{g},\ j=1,\dots,k_{v},

(25)

a set of linear restrictions on the regression coefficients that can be tested with a standard Wald statistic.

Example 3
.

In the simplest case
k_{g}=k_{v}=1
there is a single interaction, and the regression is

\displaystyle E[Y|D,X,Z]

\displaystyle=D\alpha_{1}+X^{\prime}\alpha_{2}+\mu V+\beta\,DV+\eta\,X^{\prime}V,

with
\beta=\theta^{d}\mu
the coefficient on
DV
and
\eta=\theta^{x}\mu
that on
X^{\prime}V
. The test of no endogenous heteroskedasticity is then the single-restriction test
H_{0}:\beta=0
: one simply checks whether the term
DV
belongs in the regression.

Collect the interaction coefficients into the vector
\beta=(\beta_{sj})
, of dimension
q=k_{g}k_{v}
, and let
R
be the selection matrix that extracts
\beta
from the full coefficient vector
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{w}^{\prime})^{\prime}
, so that
H_{0}
reads
R\alpha=0
. The Wald statistic is

\mathcal{W}_{n}=n\,(R\hat{\alpha})^{\prime}[R\hat{\Omega}R^{\prime}]^{-1}(R\hat{\alpha}),

(26)

where
\hat{\Omega}
is the consistent variance estimator of Lemma
3
.

Corollary 1
.

Under
H_{0}
and the conditions of Theorem
1
and Lemma
3
,

\mathcal{W}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\chi_{q}^{2},

where
q
is the number of restrictions.

The proof is omitted since it follows directly from the continuous mapping theorem, the asymptotic normality of the estimator in Theorem
1
, and the consistency of the asymptotic variance estimator in Lemma
3
.

Monte Carlo simulations

This section provides numerical simulations to assess the finite sample performance of the proposed methods. We use the following version of the model in equations (
1
) and (
2
),

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=D\alpha_{1}+X\alpha_{2}+g(D,X)\varepsilon,

(27)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=Z\pi_{1}+X\pi_{2}+h(X,Z)V,

(28)

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=U+\lambda V.

(29)

This allows for endogeneity and different types of heteroskedasticity in both the first and second stages. The parameter
\lambda
allows for
D
being exogenous (i.e.,
\lambda=0
) or endogenous (i.e.,
\lambda\neq 0
) in the structural equation (
27
).

We compute simulation results for the cases of
U\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
V\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
Z
following a folded normal distribution, that is,
Z\sim|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|
. Additionally, they are all independent of each other. We set
X\equiv 1
so that all models have a constant term. Moreover, we use sample sizes of
n\in\{250,500,1000\}
. The number of replications is set to
2000
.

We consider different types of heteroskedastic models given by

\displaystyle g(D,X)

\displaystyle=D\delta_{1}+D^{2}\delta_{2}+X\delta_{3},

(30)

\displaystyle h^{2}(X,Z)

\displaystyle=Z\gamma_{1}+X\gamma_{2}.

(31)

Parameters
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
allow for heteroskedasticity of the endogenous variable in the structural equation (i.e.,
\delta_{1}\neq 0
or
\delta_{2}\neq 0
) and nonlinear effects (i.e.,
\delta_{2}\neq 0
), while the parameter
\delta_{3}
allows for heteroskedasticity of the exogenous variable, and in this case to have a constant term. Since the model includes a constant, this ensures that the variance is not zero under the absence of endogenous heteroskedasticity.
The parameter
\gamma_{1}
also allows for
D
having conditional homoskedasticity (i.e.,
\gamma_{1}=0
) or heteroskedasticity (i.e.,
\gamma_{1}\neq 0

The parameters are

,

,
\delta_{1}\in\{0,1\}
and
\delta_{2}\in\{0,0.2\}
, thus covering a wide range of scenarios.

We present results for different estimators: OLS, standard 2SLS (note that this is equivalent to a standard CF model where
\check{V}
is included as an additional regressor in the structural equation), and the proposed CF with one interaction term CF1
(\widehat{V},\widehat{V}D)
, as well as with two interaction terms CF2
(\widehat{V},\widehat{V}D,\widehat{V}D^{2})
.

For the CF models we consider one type of skedastic function given in
Romano and Wolf (2017)
, as given in equation
(

h(X,Z;\gamma)=(\gamma_{0}+|Z|\gamma_{1})^{1/2}

\check{V}
be the residuals of the first-stage regression of
D
on

and a constant, then

is estimated as the regression of
\check{V}^{2}
on

and a constant. Note that this is not the correctly specified skedastic function given in equation (

).

\hat{V}D

8
Conclusion

This paper shows that the estimation of linear models with endogenous heteroskedasticity using the popular 2SLS method may not be estimating the parameter of interest. As an alternative we provide a simple-to-implement augmented control function estimator. The CF approach provides an intuitive modeling strategy to handle endogeneity and lies in the center of general approaches to this particular set-up. We show that this can be adapted to the least-squares linear estimation methods to correct for the 2SLS bias. In fact, the proposed solution is to augment the standard CF model (which corresponds to 2SLS) with interactions of the structural equation variables with the residuals from the first-stage.

A.1
Interpretation of
\alpha_{1}

It is important to attach an interpretation to the parameter
\alpha_{1}
in the proposed set-up. To that end, define
y(d,x)
to be the potential responses of the outcome to different values of the treatment,
d
, and covariates,
x
. Consider the following simple case:

\displaystyle y(d,x)=d\alpha_{1}+x^{\prime}\alpha_{2}.

First, we discuss the case where
d
is binary. Then

\displaystyle y(1,x)-y(0,x)=\alpha_{1},

in which case the parameter of interest is the average treatment effect (ATE), because there is no treatment heterogeneity.

Alternatively, we could define the potential responses to be

\displaystyle y(d,x)=d\alpha_{1}+x^{\prime}\alpha_{2}+g(d,x)\varepsilon,

and now we have heterogeneity, because

\displaystyle y(1,x)-y(0,x)=\alpha_{1}+(g(1,x)-g(0,x))\varepsilon.

And then the ATE is

\displaystyle E[y(1,x)-y(0,x)]

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}+(g(1,x)-g(0,x))E[\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}

if we assume that
E[\varepsilon]=0
. This is similar to the approach taken by
Abrevaya and Xu (2023)
, where the term
(g(1,x)-g(0,x))\varepsilon
reflects the variability/deviations from ATE for different individuals/subpopulations. As noted by those authors, the 2SLS bias, i.e., the discrepancy between the ATE and the LATE, depends on “the degree to which heteroskedasticity depends upon treatment, as well as the average error disturbance for the compliers” (p.4).

Now, consider the case where
D
is allowed to take a continuum of values. Now, a more general modeling as in
Florens et al. (2008)
might be useful. Consider the quantity

\displaystyle\frac{y(d+\Delta,x)-y(d,x)}{\Delta}=\alpha_{1}+\frac{g(d+\Delta,x)-g(d,x)}{\Delta}\varepsilon,

and the ATE is
\alpha_{1}
regardless of
d
, and the size of the jump
\Delta
. If this is the model, then this fits our 2SLS model in the example above.

An alternative is to model in the following way:

\displaystyle y(d,x)=\varphi(d)+x^{\prime}\alpha_{2}+g(d,x)\varepsilon

for some smooth function
\varphi
. Now we have

\displaystyle\frac{y(d+\Delta,x)-y(d,x)}{\Delta}=\frac{\varphi(d+\Delta)-\varphi(d)}{\Delta}+\frac{g(d+\Delta,x)-g(d,x)}{\Delta}\varepsilon,

and taking the limit we get

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta\to 0}\frac{y(d+\Delta,x)-y(d,x)}{\Delta}=\varphi^{\prime}(d)+g^{\prime}(d,x)\varepsilon,

and the ATE at
d
is
\varphi^{\prime}(d)
, while the “average ATE” is
E[\varphi^{\prime}(D)]
. So a linear model

\displaystyle y=E[\varphi^{\prime}(D)]d+x^{\prime}\alpha_{2}+u

implies that

\displaystyle u=g(d,x)\varepsilon+(\varphi(d)-E[\varphi^{\prime}(D)]d).

This is different from our model but it shows that the simple linear representation above can accommodate more general models.

\|ab^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\|a\|\,\|b\|
, and the triangle inequality,

\|\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}-R_{i}R_{i}^{\prime}\|\leq 2\|R_{i}\|\,\|e_{i}\|+\|e_{i}\|^{2},\qquad\|\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}-R_{i}R_{i}^{\prime}\|^{2}\leq C\big(\|R_{i}\|^{2}\|e_{i}\|^{2}+\|e_{i}\|^{4}\big)

(A.16)

for some constant
C>0
.

To show
T_{21}=o_{p}(1)
, we use (
A.16
):

\displaystyle T_{21}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}-R_{i}R_{i}^{\prime}\|^{2}\leq C\left\{\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|R_{i}\|^{4}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{4}\Big)^{1/2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{4}\right\}.

Therefore, since we have
E\|R_{i}\|^{4}<\infty
and if
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{4}=o_{p}(1)
, then
T_{21}=o_{p}(1)
. Now, to analyze
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{4}
, we have by an element-by-element application of the mean value theorem
e_{i}:=R_{i}(\widehat{\phi})-R_{i}(\phi)=\nabla_{\phi}R_{i}(\tilde{\phi})(\widehat{\phi}-\phi)
,
so that
\|e_{i}\|\leq\|\nabla_{\phi}R_{i}(\tilde{\phi})\|\|\widehat{\phi}-\phi\|
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{\phi}-\phi\|^{4}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\phi}R_{i}(\tilde{\phi})\|^{4}

\displaystyle=O_{p}(n^{-2})(E[\|J_{\phi}\|^{4}]+o_{p}(1)).

(A.17)

where we assumed that the Jacobian
J_{\phi}
has finite fourth moments.

Now we will show that
T_{22}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{4}=O_{p}(1)
. We can write

\displaystyle Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha}

\displaystyle=U_{i}-e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha}-R_{i}^{\prime}(\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha),

and for some constant
C
,

\displaystyle(Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{4}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\{U_{i}^{4}+(e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{4}+(R_{i}^{\prime}(\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha))^{4}\right\}.

Averaging gives

\displaystyle T_{22}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{4}\leq C\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}\alpha)^{4}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{4}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{i}^{\prime}(\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha))^{4}\right\}.

The first term, since
E[U^{4}]<\infty
, is
O_{p}(1)
. For the second term, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle(e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{4}\leq\|\widehat{\alpha}\|^{4}\,\|e_{i}\|^{4}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{4}\leq\|\widehat{\alpha}\|^{4}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{4}.

Since
\widehat{\alpha}=O_{p}(1)
and as shown in (
A.17
),
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{4}=o_{p}(1)
, then the second term is
o_{p}(1)
. For the third term, again by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle(R_{i}^{\prime}(\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha))^{4}\leq\|R_{i}\|^{4}\,\|\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha\|^{4}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{i}^{\prime}(\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha))^{4}\leq\|\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha\|^{4}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|R_{i}\|^{4}.

Since
E\|R_{i}\|^{4}<\infty
and
\|\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha\|^{4}=O_{p}(n^{-2})
, then the third term is
o_{p}(1)
. Therefore,
T_{2}=o_{p}(1).

Finally, consider the third term, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}R_{i}^{\prime}\left[(Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}-(Y_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|R_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}-(Y_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}\right]^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Since the fourth moments of
\|R_{i}\|
are finite, then
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|R_{i}\|^{2}=O_{p}(1)
. Now, for the next term, consider

\displaystyle(Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}-(Y_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}

\displaystyle=(Y_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha}-e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}-(Y_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}

\displaystyle=(e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}-2e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha}(Y_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})

\displaystyle=(e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}-2e_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha}(U_{i}+R_{i}^{\prime}(\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha)).

Therefore, for a constant
C
, we get

\displaystyle\left[(Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}-(Y_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\{\|e_{i}\|^{4}\|\widehat{\alpha}\|^{4}+\|e_{i}\|^{2}\|\widehat{\alpha}\|^{2}|U_{i}|^{2}+\|e_{i}\|^{2}\|\widehat{\alpha}\|^{2}\|R_{i}\|^{2}\|\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha\|^{2}\right\}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(Y_{i}-\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}-(Y_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\alpha})^{2}\right]^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{4}\|\widehat{\alpha}\|^{4}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{2}\|\widehat{\alpha}\|^{2}|U_{i}|^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|^{2}\|\widehat{\alpha}\|^{2}\|R_{i}\|^{2}\|\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha\|^{2}\right\}.

As shown in (
A.17
), first term is
o_{p}(1)
. The second and third terms are also
o_{p}(1)
. Therefore,
T_{3}=o_{p}(1).
In conclusion,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{R}_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{U}_{i}^{2}\overset{p}{\to}E[RR^{\prime}U^{2}].

Now, the goal is to show:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}^{\prime}=\hat{\Sigma}_{\phi}^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{m}_{i}\hat{m}_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\Sigma}_{\phi}^{-1}\overset{p}{\to}\Sigma_{\phi}^{-1}E[mm^{\prime}]\Sigma_{\phi}^{-1}=\Omega_{\phi}.

By Assumption
8
,
\hat{\Sigma}_{\phi}^{-1}\overset{p}{\to}\Sigma_{\phi}^{-1}
.

Now, for the outer product
\hat{m}_{i}\hat{m}_{i}^{\prime}
, recall that
\check{V}_{i}=D_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{1}-X_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{2}
, and denote
\hat{c}_{i}:=\nabla_{\gamma}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\widehat{\gamma})\left(\hat{V}_{i}^{2}-1\right)h(X_{i},Z_{i};\widehat{\gamma})^{3}
, we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{i}\widehat{m}_{i}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}\check{V}_{i}^{2}&Z_{i}X_{i}^{\prime}\check{V}_{i}^{2}&Z_{i}\check{V}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}\\[3.99994pt]
X_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}\check{V}_{i}^{2}&X_{i}X_{i}^{\prime}\check{V}_{i}^{2}&X_{i}\check{V}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}\\[3.99994pt]
\hat{c}_{i}\check{V}_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}&\hat{c}_{i}\check{V}_{i}X_{i}^{\prime}&\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}.

Consider
Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}\check{V}_{i}^{2}
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}\check{V}_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}(D_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{1}-X_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}(Z_{i}^{\prime}\pi_{1}+X_{i}^{\prime}\pi_{2}+h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)V_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{1}-X_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\pi}_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}\left(Z_{i}^{\prime}(\pi_{1}-\widehat{\pi}_{1})+X_{i}^{\prime}(\pi_{2}-\widehat{\pi}_{2})+h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)V_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\prime}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)^{2}V^{2}+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\to}E[ZZ^{\prime}h(X,Z;\gamma)^{2}V^{2}]

because
E[\|Z\|^{4}]<\infty
,
E[\|X\|^{4}]<\infty
, and
E[|h(X,Z;\gamma)V|^{4}]<\infty
. Similar results hold for
Z_{i}X_{i}^{\prime}\check{V}_{i}^{2}
and
X_{i}X_{i}^{\prime}\check{V}_{i}^{2}
. Now consider
Z_{i}\check{V}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\check{V}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\check{V}_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\check{V}_{i}(\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\left(Z_{i}^{\prime}(\pi_{1}-\widehat{\pi}_{1})+X_{i}^{\prime}(\pi_{2}-\widehat{\pi}_{2})+h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)V_{i}\right)c_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\check{V}_{i}(\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}h(X_{i},Z_{i};\gamma)V_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\check{V}_{i}(\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i})^{\prime}+o_{p}(1)

because by Assumption
8
, the second moments are finite. Now for the second term we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\check{V}_{i}(\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i})^{\prime}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|Z_{i}\check{V}_{i}\right\|^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\right\|^{2}.

Write
c_{i}(\phi)
for the population quantity and
\hat{c}_{i}=c_{i}(\widehat{\phi})
,

c_{i}=c_{i}(\phi)
.
We have that it is differentiable in
\phi
with derivative

\nabla_{\phi}c_{i}(\phi)
and
E\big[\|\nabla_{\phi}c_{i}(\phi)\|^{2}\big]<\infty.
By a mean value expansion, for each
i
there exists
\tilde{\phi}_{i}
on the line
segment between
\widehat{\phi}
and
\phi
such that

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}=\nabla_{\phi}c_{i}(\tilde{\phi}_{i})(\widehat{\phi}-\phi).

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\nabla_{\phi}c_{i}(\tilde{\phi}_{i})(\widehat{\phi}-\phi)\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{\phi}-\phi\|^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\nabla_{\phi}c_{i}(\tilde{\phi}_{i})\big\|^{2}.

Since
\widehat{\phi}
is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent,
and
\nabla_{\phi}c_{i}(\tilde{\phi}_{i})
satisfies a uniform law of large numbers, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\|^{2}=O_{p}(n^{-1})\cdot O_{p}(1)=o_{p}(1).

A similar result holds for
X_{i}\check{V}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}
. Finally,
we want to show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}\overset{p}{\to}E[c_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}].

Decompose

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}-E[c_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}\right\}+\underbrace{\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}-E[c_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}]\right\}}_{\overset{p}{\to}0}.

The second term is
o_{p}(1)
because
E\|c_{i}\|^{2}<\infty
.
For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}-c_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i})\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}+c_{i}(\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i})^{\prime}.

Hence

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i})\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}\|+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|c_{i}(\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i})^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\|\,\|\hat{c}_{i}\|+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|c_{i}\|\,\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\|.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to each sum separately

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\|\,\|\hat{c}_{i}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{c}_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|c_{i}\|\,\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|c_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

From the previous argument we have
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{c}_{i}-c_{i}\|^{2}=o_{p}(1)
. Moreover, by the uniform law of large numbers, we have
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|c_{i}\|^{2}=O_{p}(1)
and
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{c}_{i}\|^{2}=O_{p}(1)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}\right\|=o_{p}(1)\cdot O_{p}(1)+O_{p}(1)\cdot o_{p}(1)=o_{p}(1).

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\prime}\overset{p}{\to}E[c_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}].

Finally, we take care of the cross term in
\hat{\psi}_{\alpha,i}\hat{\psi}_{\alpha,i}^{\prime}
.
We only need to show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{U}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}^{\prime}\overset{p}{\to}E[RU\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}].

Define

\displaystyle e_{i}^{R}

\displaystyle:=\widehat{R}_{i}-R_{i},

\displaystyle e_{i}^{U}

\displaystyle:=\widehat{U}_{i}-U_{i},

\displaystyle e_{i}^{\psi}

\displaystyle:=\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}-\psi_{\phi,i},

so that

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{i}

\displaystyle=R_{i}+e_{i}^{R},

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{i}

\displaystyle=U_{i}+e_{i}^{U},

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}

\displaystyle=\psi_{\phi,i}+e_{i}^{\psi}.

Then

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{U}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(R_{i}+e_{i}^{R})(U_{i}+e_{i}^{U})(\psi_{\phi,i}+e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=R_{i}U_{i}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}+e_{i}^{R}U_{i}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}+R_{i}e_{i}^{U}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}+R_{i}U_{i}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime}

\displaystyle+e_{i}^{R}e_{i}^{U}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}+e_{i}^{R}U_{i}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime}+R_{i}e_{i}^{U}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime}+e_{i}^{R}e_{i}^{U}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime}.

Averaging over
i
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{U}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}U_{i}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}}_{=:S_{0}}+\sum_{k=1}^{7}S_{k},

where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{R}U_{i}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime},

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}e_{i}^{U}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime},

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}U_{i}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime},

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{R}e_{i}^{U}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime},

\displaystyle S_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{R}U_{i}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime},

\displaystyle S_{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}e_{i}^{U}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime},

\displaystyle S_{7}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{R}e_{i}^{U}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime}.

By the law of large numbers and the moment conditions in Assumptions
8
and
9
, we have

\displaystyle S_{0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}U_{i}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}\overset{p}{\to}E[RU\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}].

for

. First, we take care of

,

, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{R}\|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{R}\|^{4}\right)^{1/2}=o_{p}(1).

By arguments analogous to those used for

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|e_{i}^{U}|^{2}=o_{p}(1).

For

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{\psi}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\phi}^{-1}-\Sigma_{\phi}^{-1}\|^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{m}_{i}\|^{2}+2\|\Sigma_{\phi}^{-1}\|^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{m}_{i}-m_{i}\|^{2}.

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{\phi}^{-1}\overset{p}{\to}\Sigma_{\phi}^{-1},\qquad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{m}_{i}\|^{2}=O_{p}(1),\qquad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{m}_{i}-m_{i}\|^{2}=o_{p}(1),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{\psi}\|^{2}=o_{p}(1).

Additionally, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\psi_{\phi,i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=O_{p}(1),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}\|^{2}

Term

.

\displaystyle\|S_{1}\|

.

Term

.

\displaystyle\|S_{3}\|

\displaystyle\|S_{4}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{R}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|e_{i}^{U}|^{2}\|\psi_{\phi,i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=o_{p}(1)\cdot o_{p}(1)=o_{p}(1).

Term

.

\displaystyle\|S_{5}\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{R}U_{i}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime}\right\|\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{R}\|^{2}U_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{\psi}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=o_{p}(1)\cdot o_{p}(1)=o_{p}(1),

using

and the
o_{p}(1)
bounds on
\frac{1}{n}\sum\|e_{i}^{R}\|^{2}
and
\frac{1}{n}\sum\|e_{i}^{\psi}\|^{2}
.

Term
S_{6}
.

\displaystyle\|S_{6}\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}e_{i}^{U}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime}\right\|\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|R_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|e_{i}^{U}|^{2}\|e_{i}^{\psi}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq O_{p}(1)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|e_{i}^{U}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{\psi}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=O_{p}(1)\cdot o_{p}(1)\cdot o_{p}(1)=o_{p}(1).

Term
S_{7}
.

\displaystyle\|S_{7}\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{R}e_{i}^{U}(e_{i}^{\psi})^{\prime}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{R}\|\cdot|e_{i}^{U}|\cdot\|e_{i}^{\psi}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{R}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|e_{i}^{U}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}^{\psi}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=o_{p}(1)\cdot o_{p}(1)\cdot o_{p}(1)=o_{p}(1).

Therefore,
S_{k}=o_{p}(1)
for all
k=1,\dots,7
, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{U}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}U_{i}\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}+o_{p}(1)\overset{p}{\to}E[RU\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}].

This establishes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{R}_{i}\widehat{U}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{\phi,i}^{\prime}\overset{p}{\to}E[RU\psi_{\phi,i}^{\prime}],

and completes the proof.
