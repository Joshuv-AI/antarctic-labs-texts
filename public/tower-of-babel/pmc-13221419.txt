Methods for Analyzing RNA Pseudoknots via Chord Diagrams and Intersection Graphs
Authors: Rayan Ibrahim, Allison H. Moore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA molecules are known to form complex secondary structures including pseudoknots. A systematic framework for the enumeration, classification and prediction of secondary structures is critical to determine the biological significance of the molecular configurations of RNA. Chord diagrams are mathematical objects widely used to represent RNA secondary structures and to analyze structural motifs, however a mathematically rigorous enumeration of pseudoknots remains a challenge. We introduce a method that incorporates a distance-based metric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau $$\end{document}τ to analyze the intersection graph of a chord diagram associated with a pseudoknotted structure. In particular, our method formally defines a pseudoknot in terms of a weighted vertex cover of a certain intersection graph constructed from a partition of the chord diagram representing the nucleotide sequence of the RNA molecule. In this graph theoretic context, we introduce a rigorous algorithm that enumerates pseudoknots, classifies secondary structures, and is sensitive to three-dimensional topological features. We implement our methods in MATLAB and test the algorithm on pseudoknotted structures from the bpRNA-1m database. Our findings confirm that genus is a robust quantifier of pseudoknot complexity.

Introduction

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) is a molecule essential to many functions of life, notably gene expression, cellular communication, and the storage and transfer of genetic information. The primary structure of RNA refers to the sequence of its four nitrogenous bases adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C), and uracil (U), attached along a sugar-phosphate backbone (Elliott and Ladomery 2023). It is well known that RNA molecules fold into a variety of secondary and tertiary structures related to their natural functions via complementary Watson-Crick base pairings and other pairings (Watson et al. 2013; Elliott and Ladomery 2023). Common secondary structure motifs include hairpin loops, stems (i.e. ‘stacks’), bulges, interior loops, multiloops, single-stranded regions, and pseudoknots (Reidys 2011), (Danaee et al. 2018, Figure 1) (see Figure 4). A pseudoknot is a secondary structure motif representing a three-dimensional folding pattern. Pseudoknots were first recognized in the study of the turnip yellow mosaic virus (Rietveld et al. 1982; Dumas et al. 1987), but the term was coined in Studnicka et al. (1978). The simplest type of pseudoknotted structure is an H-type pseudoknot, formed when nucleobases along the loop of a hairpin bond with nucleobases elsewhere along the sequence (Brierley et al. 2007). A variety of pseudoknot motifs have been characterized including H-type, K-type, L-type, and M-type motifs (Kucharík et al. 2015; Andrikos et al. 2022).

RNA secondary structures may be represented graphically with chord diagrams, objects common in enumerative combinatorics and topology. In the representation of an RNA secondary structure, bonded pairs are indicated by arcs (‘chords’) along a line segment or circle. A precursor to a chord diagram representing secondary structures appears in Waterman (1978) as a connection to predictive models using base-pairing matrices (Tinoco et al. 1971). There, RNA secondary structures were defined as simple planar graphs on a set of n labeled points such that a path along the n points represents the primary structure, with other edges representing bonds between bases (Waterman 1978, Definition 2.1). These planar graphs correspond to crossingless chord diagrams, which have since been studied extensively as models for secondary structures (Bon et al. 2008; Reidys et al. 2011; Kabak et al. 2004; Waterman and Smith 1978; Waterman 1979; Penner and Waterman 1993; Schmitt and Waterman 1994). Pseudoknots occur only when the corresponding chord diagrams contain chords that cross each other (Bon et al. 2008; Kabak et al. 2004; Reidys et al. 2011). Despite the relative simplicity of a chord diagram, there is no agreed-upon method for quantifying the complexity of RNA pseudoknotting. A naive count of crossings overemphasizes contributions from helical stacking, and different methods for reducing parallel bonds may yield different enumerations of pseudoknots. Moreover, existing methods may ignore some topological features of the 3D conformation. We investigate these discrepancies in Sections 2.2 and 3.1.

Our goal is to construct a mathematically rigorous and topologically robust framework for quantifying pseudoknot complexity in RNA, presented in the familiar language of graph theory and building upon conventions implicitly assumed in the bpRNA method (Danaee et al. 2018) and bpRNA-1m database (Danaee et al. 2018). The bpRNA-1m database aggregates over 100K RNA secondary structures from seven sources (Cannone et al. 2002; Zwieb 2003; Rosenblad 2003; Juhling et al. 2009; Brown 1998; Griffiths-Jones 2003; Berman et al. 2000). Internal annotation routines and the enumeration of pseudoknots and other structural motifs are conducted via the algorithmic tool bpRNA (Danaee et al. 2018). This database provides the primary test case for our graph theoretic procedures. The strategy presented here verifies the reproducibility of both our methods and those of the bpRNA tool (Danaee et al. 2018), while illustrating discrepancies resulting from topological conformations.

Section 2 develops a mathematical formulation of pseudoknotting using chord diagrams and intersection graphs. The relevant graph theoretical background is reviewed in Section 2.1, and we prove a relationship between vertex cover numbers and the genus of a chord diagram in Theorem 2. In Section 2.2, we investigate precedent theories of pseudoknotting (particularly those in Danaee et al. 2018) and convey these notions into our mathematical framework. Specifically, we use a weighted vertex cover of an intersection graph constructed from a partition of the chord diagram corresponding to an RNA molecule to give a precise enumeration of pseudoknots (note that enumeration via vertex cover appeared in Kabak et al. (2004)). The intersection graph G of a chord diagram D is the graph whose vertices are the chords of D, and such that two vertices in G are adjacent if their corresponding chords in D form a crossing. The use of intersection graphs is well established (Mckee and Mcmorris 1999). Variations on the concept of an intersection graph arise numerous times in the biology literature under different names. For example, the intersection graph of an arc diagram is referred to as an “incompatibility graph" in Kabak et al. (2004), whereas in Kucharík et al. (2015), a “conflict graph” has a vertex set comprised of helices of the RNA structure, with edges representing crossings of chords corresponding to the helices. In Shu et al. (2008) the concept of an “element-contact graph” is introduced, in particular the stem-loop-contact graph (SLCG) (Shu et al. 2008, Figure 7). The segment graph used in the bpRNA database (Danaee et al. 2018) is another example of an intersection graph.

Pseudoknotted Structures

Analyzing the role of pseudoknots in various RNA processes motivates the study of pseudoknot complexity for comparison, characterization of motifs, and prediction of RNA secondary structure (Danaee et al. 2018; Bon et al. 2008; Reidys et al. 2011; Staple and Butcher 2005). Practical definitions of a pseudoknot vary in the biological literature (Kabak et al. 2004; Bon et al. 2008; Reidys et al. 2011). Because we will ultimately be interested in quantifying pseudoknots aggregated by the bpRNA-1m database (Danaee et al. 2018), we start from definitions presented there. In this database, a pseudoknotted structure is characterized as having base pair positions that cross in the sense of Definition 1(a); the working definition of a ‘pseudoknot base pair’ is one belonging to a minimal set of base pairs that results in a pseudoknot-free structure once removed. There is some ambiguity in these concepts; for example, a pair of kissing hairpins with intersection graph weighted [a, a+b, b] demonstrates that such a minimal set is not unique. Further ambiguities may result from helices formed in secondary structures (see Section 2.4). By default, the number of pseudoknots in any given secondary structure reported in Danaee et al. (2018) is the number returned by an algorithm that identifies some minimal set of pseudoknot base pairs. We review their algorithm, and translate corresponding notions of pseudoknotting into the language of chord diagrams, as follows.

To make the notion of a pseudoknot, and more specifically the annotation of multiple pseudoknots, more precise, Danaee et al. (2018) introduces the notion of a segment of RNA secondary structure. An RNA segment is described as a region of duplexed RNA, possibly containing bulges or internal loops. Definition 5

(Pseudoknotted Structures - bpRNA Segment Graph Method) A secondary structure is pseudoknotted if its segment graph contains at least one edge and is called pseudoknot-free otherwise. Qualitatively Similar Pseudoknotted Structures

Identifying nested chords in a chord diagram is a common strategy for reducing the complexity of the combinatorial analysis of secondary structures because it allows for the reduction of helices to a single chord, or alternatively to a single vertex in an intersection graph. For example, nested chords are identified as segments in Danaee et al. (2018) (Definition 3 above), as ‘stacks’ in Bon et al. (2008), as ‘shadows’ in Reidys et al. (2011), or as edges in tree diagrams of pseudoknot-free structures in Benedetti and Morosetti (1996) and elsewhere. Such reductions also preserve some invariants of interest, for example the topological genus (see Definition 4 and Bon et al. (2008)). In contrast to r-nestings, r-crossings in chord diagrams are typically left untouched by simplification algorithms. Consequently, the existence of an r-crossing implies the existence of at least r pseudoknots according to the conventions of Danaee et al. (2018).

Consider the following two examples.

Example 2

(Discrepancies due to weights) Consider two weighted segment graphs, each isomorphic to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_3$$\end{document}P3, with weights (1, 5, 3) and (1, 5, 4), respectively. Such graphs represent nearly identical K-type secondary structures which differ only by a single bonded pair in the third stem. By Danaee et al. (2018) and Definition 5, the minimum cardinality over vertex covers of minimum weight determines the number of pseudoknots: 2 and 1, respectively. Note that the addition of one pair results in a decrease in the number of pseudoknots.

Discussion

We applied the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau $$\end{document}τ-Segment Graph Method with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =0$$\end{document}τ=0 to independently verify the quantities reported in bpRNA-1m(90) (Danaee et al. 2018). By Definition 5, structures are pseudoknotted when their segment graphs contain at least one edge, and the number of pseudoknots is quantified by the minimum cardinalities of vertex covers of minimum weight. In agreement with Danaee et al. (2018), we obtained 3,320 graphs containing at least one edge in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{{\mathcal {S}}}$$\end{document}GS from RNA structures and a total quantity of 7,164 pseudoknots, as determined by the sum over the cardinalities of the vertex covers. Applying the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau $$\end{document}τ-Segment Graph Method with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =\infty $$\end{document}τ=∞ (the augmented segment graph method) for every structure in bpRNA-1m(90), we found a minimum vertex cover for each structure. The number of pseudoknots (the sum of vertex cover numbers over all graphs) was 6,548 with this method.

With \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =0$$\end{document}τ=0, we found 31 unique RNA structures containing 13 or more pseudoknots. These structures are listed in Table 1. When the method was applied with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =\infty $$\end{document}τ=∞, we found that the same 31 structures contained the most pseudoknots amongst all structures in the database. With the exception of the last structure, Oceanobacillus iheyensis, from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =0$$\end{document}τ=0 to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =\infty $$\end{document}τ=∞ there was a uniform decrease in pseudoknotting by two that resulted from a single 3-crossing being consolidated into one segment by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau $$\end{document}τ-reduction. This uniformity in behavior is explained by the fact that all but the last structure are of type 23S prokaryotic ribosomal RNA, originating in various bacterial organisms (Elliott and Ladomery 2023).

Over the entire bpRNA-1m(90) database, a total of 573 structures had a decrease in numbers of pseudoknots when analyzed with the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =\infty $$\end{document}τ=∞ versus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =0$$\end{document}τ=0 methods. Of these, 531 structures decreased in quantity of pseudoknots by 1, 41 structures by 2, and 1 structure decreased by 3 (bpRNA_CRW_55316, Plasmodium falciparum). Of the 41 structures which decreased by 2, there were 6 unique RNA types with 36 of them being of type 23S. Structures that changed from having a nonzero quantity of pseudoknots to zero pseudoknots are shown in Table 2. Of these, one structure (Homo sapiens) decreased from 2 to 0 pseudoknots. All other structures decreased from 1 to 0 pseudoknots.

Remark 5

Lastly, we remark that relative to k-nestings, pseudoknots resulting from k-crossings are comparatively rare (Danaee et al. 2018; Reidys et al. 2011). Although the present work is focused on the enumeration of pseudoknots resulting from all such structures, the definitions and algorithms presented in Section 3.1 remain valid in the absence of k-crossings. One may simply remove chords contained in k-crossings from a chord diagram and proceed with the current algorithm to analyze pseudoknots resulting from nestings alone. However, one may reasonably have the goal of treating crossings and nestings asymmetrically. In principle, one could modify the definition of the augmented \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau $$\end{document}τ-partition in Section 3.1 to incorporate two distance parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _n$$\end{document}τn and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _c$$\end{document}τc, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _n$$\end{document}τn defining a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _n$$\end{document}τn-near k-nesting, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _c$$\end{document}τc defining a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _c$$\end{document}τc-near k-crossing. By setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _c=\infty $$\end{document}τc=∞, one could force the consolidation of any k-crossing to a single element in the partition, while retaining flexibility with the nesting distance parameter.
