Abstract
In this work, we consider the problem of efficient decoding of codes from insertions and deletions. Most of the known efficient codes are codes with synchronization strings which allow one to reduce the problem of decoding insertions and deletions to that of decoding substitution and erasures. Our new approach, presented in this paper, reduces the problem of decoding insertions and deletions to that of list recovery. Specifically, any ( ρ , 2 ​ ρ ​ n + 1 , L ) (\rho,2\rho n+1,L) -list-recoverable code is a ( ρ , L ) (\rho,L) -list decodable insdel code. As an example, we apply this technique to Reed–Solomon (RS) codes, which are known to have efficient list-recovery algorithms up to the Johnson bound. In the adversarial insdel model, this provides efficient (list) decoding from t t insdel errors, assuming that t ⋅ k = O ⁡ ( n ) t\cdot k=O(n) . This is the first efficient insdel decoder for [ n , k ] [n,k] RS codes for k > 2 k>2 . Additionally, we explore random insdel models, such as the Davey-MacKay channel, and show that for certain choices of ρ \rho , a ( ρ , n 1 / 2 + 0.001 , L ) (\rho,n^{1/2+0.001},L) -list-recoverable code of length n n can, with high probability, efficiently list decode the channel output, ensuring that the transmitted codeword is in the output list. In the context of RS codes, this leads to a better rate-error tradeoff for these channels compared to the adversarial case. We also adapt the Koetter-Vardy algorithm, a famous soft-decision list decoding technique for RS codes, to correct insertions and deletions induced by the Davey-MacKay channel.

Introduction

Error-correcting codes (codes, for short) are designed to enable the recovery of original information from data that has been corrupted.
The primary corruption models studied are substitutions or erasures. In these models, each symbol in the transmitted word is either replaced with a different symbol from the alphabet (a substitution) or with a ’?’ (an erasure).

Another important model that has been considered since the works of Shannon and Hamming is that of
insertions and deletions
(insdel errors, for short). An insertion error is when a new symbol is inserted between two symbols of the transmitted word and a deletion is when a symbol is removed from the transmitted word.
For example, over the binary alphabet, when
100110
is transmitted, we may receive the word
1101100
, which is obtained from two insertions (
1
at the beginning and
0
at the end) and one deletion (one of the
0
’s at the beginning of the transmitted word).
The insdel error model is as natural as that of substitutions and erasures; however, with the loss in synchronization between the sender and the receiver, insdel errors are much more challenging to handle.

Motivated by applications such as over-/under-samping and DNA-based data storage, in recent years many researchers studied and designed codes that can correct from insdel errors

Preliminaries

Throughout,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
will denote a finite field of order

We denote by

We shall use the notation
\binom{\mathbb{F}_{q}}{\ell}

\mathbb{F}_{q}

Finally, we write

.
With these notations, we are ready to define a
list recoverable code
.

Definition I.1
.

For
\rho\in[0,1]
and integers

, we say that a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is
(\rho,\ell,L)
-
list-recoverable

S\in\binom{\mathbb{F}_{q}}{\ell}^{n}
there are at most
L
different codewords

which satisfy that
d_{H}(c,S)\leq\rho n
.

List recoverable codes are an important class of codes that have found applications in domains such as pseudorandomness
[
33
]
, algorithms
[
34
]
, combinatorial group testing
[
35
]
, hashing, and many more. As such, studying the limitation and constructing (efficient) list recoverable codes is a very active area of research
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
, just to name a few.
The Johnson bound for list recoverable codes (see e.g.,
[
37
, Theorem 2.3]
) states that a code
\mathcal{C}
with relative Hamming distance
\delta
is
(\rho,\ell,L)
for any
\ell<\frac{(1-\rho)^{2}}{1-\delta}
with

.
Observe that a
(\rho,1,L)
-list-recoverable code is a list decodable code. Thus, list-recoverable codes are a generalization of the more widely studied list-decoding model.

Recall that we are dealing with insdel errors in this paper, and the metric that we are going to use is the
edit
metric.

Definition I.2
.

The
edit distance
between
{\bm{s}}
and
{\bm{s}}^{\prime}
, denoted by
d_{\textup{ed}}({\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime})
, is the minimal number of insertions and deletions needed in order to turn
{\bm{s}}
into
{\bm{s}}^{\prime}
.

For a code
\mathcal{C}
we write,
d_{\textup{ed}}(\mathcal{C})=\max_{{\bm{c}},{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C},{\bm{c}}\neq{\bm{c}}^{\prime}}d_{\textup{ed}}({\bm{c}},{\bm{c}}^{\prime})
.
Note that list decoding can also be defined in the insdel model. Namely, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is
(\rho,L)

list decodable insdel code
if for every
{\bm{y}}
, it holds that
|\{{\bm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}\ |\ d_{\textup{ed}}({\bm{c}},y)\leq\rho n\}|\leq L
.

We now formally define RS codes.

Definition I.3
.

Let
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
where
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
for every distinct

[n,k]_{q}

RS codes are among one of the most widely used codes in theory and practice. They have efficient unique decoding algorithms up to half of the minimum distance and also efficient list decoding algorithms up to the Johnson bound
[
20
]
. Note here that since RS codes have
R=1-\delta

\rho<1-\sqrt{\ell R}

I-B

Our Results

In this paper, we present a connection between list recoverable codes and insdel codes. This connection is achieved by a simple reduction where we take an input to an insdel decoding algorithm and transform it to an input to a list recovery algorithm. This implies that if one has a code with an efficient list recovery algorithm, then the code has also an efficient insdel decoding algorithm.

Then, we also apply our idea to probabilistic errors and show that over random insdel channels we can slightly improve the rate-error correction trade-off.

More specifically, we prove the following statements.

•

Any
(\rho,2\rho n+1,L)
-list-recoverable code
\mathcal{C}
is an
(\rho,L)
-list decodable insdel code. Moreover, if
\rho n
is smaller than half the edit distance of
\mathcal{C}
, then the decoding is unique (see
Theorem

II.1
for a formal description).

•

Let
P_{\mathrm{i}},P_{\mathrm{d}}\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
(E+\varepsilon,O(n^{1/2+0.01}),L)
-list-recoverable code, where
E
is a constant depending on
P_{\mathrm{i}}
and
P_{\mathrm{d}}
.
Then,
\mathcal{C}
is a list decodable code in the random insdel model (the Davey-MacKay channel
[
31
]
with a constraint on the maximum length of an insertion burst) with insertion and deletion probabilities being
P_{\mathrm{i}}
and
P_{\mathrm{d}}
respectively. Namely, with high probability, upon transmitting any codeword
{\bm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
through this channel, we can efficiently list decode the output so that
{\bm{c}}
is in the output list (see

III.6
for the formal statements). An analogous result for the probabilistic deletion channel is also presented in
Theorem

.

Finally, we adapt the Koetter-Vardy algorithm
[
32
]
to insdel channels. This is the only decoding algorithm for RS codes that can leverage soft information. Additionally, we show how to jointly decode multiple received sequences, which is useful in applications like DNA data storage that provide multiple erroneous copies

III-A

The Deletion Channel

We begin by examining a specific case of the Davey-MacKay channel that does not produce insertion errors, i.e.,
P_{\mathrm{i}}=0
. This is commonly known as the deletion channel, wherein each transmitted symbol is deleted independently with probability
P_{\mathrm{d}}\in(0,1)
. By utilizing the statistical properties of the drift variable

, we establish the following bound concerning the error probability of a list-recovery decoder that uses sufficiently large lists for each transmitted symbol.

Proposition III.2
.

Assume a sequence
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is transmitted over a channel with deletion probability
P_{\mathrm{d}}
, resulting in the received sequence
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{r}
, where
r<n
. If for all
m\in[n]
, we assign
S_{m}\xleftarrow{}\{y_{a},y_{a+1},\ldots,y_{b}\}
, where
a=\max(m+\mathbb{E}[D_{m}]-C,1)
and
b=\min(m+\mathbb{E}[D_{m}]+C-1,r)
, where
C
is chosen such that
|S_{m}|\leq 2C=n^{\frac{1}{2}+0.001}
and let
I=\{i|x_{i}\not\in S_{i}\}
, then it holds that

\Pr[|I|\geq(P_{d}+\varepsilon)n]\leq\exp(-\Omega(n^{0.002})).

Proof:

We begin by observing that for any
m\in[\varepsilon n,n]
,
\Pr[x_{m}\not\in S_{m}|x_{m}\textup{ was transmitted}]=\Pr[|D_{m}-\mathbb{E}[D_{m}]|>C]
. Since

can be seen as a sum of

, we apply Hoeffding’s inequality
[

and the fact that

to arrive at

\displaystyle\Pr[x_{m}\not\in S_{m}|x_{m}\textup{ was transmitted}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2e^{-{2C^{2}}/{m}}\leq e^{-\Omega(n^{0.002})}.

By the union bound, we further deduce that the probability that there exists an

such that

was transmitted and

is at most
n\cdot\exp(-\Omega(n^{0.002}))=\exp(-\Omega(n^{0.002}))
.

Also note that since the event that any

is transmitted, can be modeled by a Bernoulli random variable with success probability

]
that

Thus, to conclude, we bound the probability that

was transmitted and

.
The proposition follows.

Proposition III.5
.

Assume a Davey-MacKay channel with insertion and deletion probabilities

Proof:

can be seen as the sum of

D_{1}
and thus bounded to
[-1,B]

denotes the maximum length of a burst of insertions. By applying Hoeffding’s inequality

]
and

\displaystyle\Pr[x_{m}\not\in S_{m}|x_{m}\textup{ was transmitted}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\Big(-\frac{n^{0.002}}{2(B+1)^{2}}\Big).

Once again, the union bound lets us deduce that the probability that there exists an
i\in[\varepsilon n,n]
such that
x_{i}
was transmitted and

is at most

Since

\displaystyle\Pr[|{\bm{y}}|<(1+\mathbb{E}[D_{1}]-\varepsilon)n]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr[D_{n}-\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]<-\varepsilon n]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\Big(-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}n}{(B+1)^{2}}\Big)\leq e^{-\Omega(n)}.

such that

was transmitted and
x_{i}\notin S_{i}
.

IV
Koetter-Vardy Algorithm for Insdels

In this section, we adapt a decoding algorithm for Reed-Solomon codes that generalizes list decoding and list recovery, namely the Koetter-Vardy (KV) algorithm
[
32
]
, to correct insdel errors. This algorithm extends the Guruswami-Sudan (GS) algorithm of RS codes by incorporating soft information concerning the received symbols. It accomplishes this by using the reliability information of these symbols to form a multiplicity matrix that assigns unequal weights to the interpolation points of the RS code.

IV-A

Reliability Matrix for Insdels

The primary obstacle to adapting the KV algorithm for use over channels susceptible to insertions and deletions alongside substitutions lies in the computation of the reliability matrix. Since such channels are not memoryless, we rely on the concept of the generalized reliability matrix
[
32
, Eq. (46)]
. Specifically, if we denote a fixed ordering of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
as
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{q}
, then the
(i,j)
th entry of the reliability matrix, denoted as
\Pi
, should quantify the probability that the
i
th symbol in the transmitted codeword
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
equals
\alpha_{j}
, for all
j\in[q]
and
i\in[n]
, given a specific received sequence, say
{\bm{y}}
, i.e.,
\pi_{i,j}\triangleq\Pr[{\bm{x}}_{i}=\alpha_{j}|{\bm{y}}]
. To compute this quantity, we first note that in the context of an insertion-deletion channel, any transmitted symbol
x_{i}
either undergoes transmission with a specific drift, or deletion. Thus, given an
[n,k]_{q}
RS code
\mathcal{C}
, and a vector
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{r}
that results from the transmission of some
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{C}
over the Davey-MacKay channel,
\Pr[x_{i}=\alpha_{j}|{\bm{y}}]
can be expanded as follows.

\displaystyle\Pr[x_{i}=\alpha_{j}|{\bm{y}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{C}\\
x_{i}=\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}]}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{C}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}]}\approx\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\\
x_{i}=\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}]}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}]}.

The approximation is necessary since marginalizing
\Pr[{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}]
over (a subset of)
\mathcal{C}
is computationally expensive. The quantity
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}]
signifies the probability of receiving a specific sequence
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{r}
given that a vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
was transmitted and is constant for all
{\bm{y}}
of a specific length
r
, if all transmitted sequences are equally likely. For brevity of notation, we denote this by
P_{n}({\bm{y}})
and can compute it as the sum of weights of all paths on a two-dimensional lattice from coordinates
(0,0)
to
(n,r)
, where paths consist of horizontal, vertical and diagonal steps with weights
P_{\mathrm{i}}/q
,
P_{\mathrm{d}}
and
P_{\mathrm{t}}/q
respectively
[
31
,
49
]
. Note that horizontal edges are absent in the final row, as insertions cannot follow the transmission of the final symbol. This process accounts for every possible sequence of insertion, deletion, and transmission events, thereby emulating the Davey-MacKay channel depicted in Figure 1. Next, we recall that
x_{i}
is either deleted or transmitted with a certain drift, and proceed by decomposing
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},x_{i}=\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}]
as follows.

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},x_{i}=\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}},x_{i}\text{ deleted}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{r}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{i-1}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}}_{1}^{l-1},{\bm{x}}]\Big)P_{\mathrm{d}}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-i}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}}_{l}^{r},{\bm{x}}]\Big),

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},x_{i}=\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}},x_{i}\text{ transmitted}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{r}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{i-1}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}}_{1}^{l-1},{\bm{x}}]\Big)P_{\mathrm{t}}\phi(y_{l}|\alpha_{j})\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-i}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{y}}_{l+1}^{r},{\bm{x}}]\Big),

where
\phi(\alpha|\beta)=1
if
\alpha=\beta
and
0
otherwise. These equations allow us to write

\displaystyle\pi_{i,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\mathrm{d}}\sum_{l=1}^{r}P_{i-1}({\bm{y}}_{1}^{l-1})P_{n-i}({\bm{y}}_{l}^{r})}{P_{N}({\bm{y}})}

(2)

\displaystyle+\frac{P_{\mathrm{t}}\sum_{l=1}^{r}\phi(y_{l}|\alpha_{j})P_{i-1}({\bm{y}}_{1}^{l-1})P_{n-i}({\bm{y}}_{l+1}^{r})}{P_{n}({\bm{y}})}.

In case of multiple received sequences, say
{\bm{y}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{y}}^{(M)}
, which result from independent transmissions of the encoding of the same information vector, say
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
, where
k<n
, the reliability matrix can be computed in a similar spirit as before, i.e.,
\pi_{i,j}\triangleq{\Pr[{\bm{x}}_{i}=\alpha_{j}|{\bm{y}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{y}}^{(M)}]}
. Note that

\displaystyle\Pr[x_{i}=\alpha_{j}|{\bm{y}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{y}}^{(M)}]\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\\
:x_{i}=\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{u}},{\bm{y}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{y}}^{(M)}]}{\sum_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\Pr[{\bm{u}},{\bm{y}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{y}}^{(M)}]}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\\
:x_{i}=\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\!\Pr[{\bm{u}}]\prod_{h=1}^{M}\!\Pr[{\bm{y}}^{(h)}|{\bm{u}}]}{\sum_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\!\Pr[{\bm{u}}]\prod_{h=1}^{M}\Pr[{\bm{y}}^{(h)}|{\bm{u}}]}

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{h=1}^{M}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\\
:x_{i}=\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{u}},{\bm{y}}^{(h)}]}{\prod_{h=1}^{M}\sum_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\Pr[{\bm{u}},{\bm{y}}^{(h)}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{h=1}^{M}\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{C},x_{i}=\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{(h)}]}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{C}\end{subarray}}\Pr[{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{(h)}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{h=1}^{M}\Pr[x_{i}=\alpha_{j}|{\bm{y}}^{(h)}],

where
(i)
follows from the fact that the received vectors are acquired from independent transmissions of the same codeword and
(ii)
results from uniform distribution of all information vectors.

Observe that the two-dimensional lattice used to compute
P_{n}({\bm{y}}_{1}^{r})
, as discussed earlier, can also be flipped along the off-diagonal, enabling the traversal of the finite state machine in reverse, i.e., from the ending to the starting state. Hence, the order of received and transmitted symbols is altered: the columns, from left to right, correspond to the symbols
y_{r},\ldots,y_{1}
, while the rows, from top to bottom, represent the symbols
x_{n},\ldots,x_{1}
. This change may impact the second term in (
2
) due to asymmetry introduced by the non-uniform diagonal edge weights
P_{t}\phi(y_{r}|\alpha_{j})
. For instance, when using the forward lattice, one or more symbols in
{\bm{y}}^{r-1}_{1}
affect
\pi_{n,j}
, but this is not the case in the reversed model. To remedy this, we use a bidirectional version of the computation of
\pi_{i,j}
presented in (
2
), i.e., we compute
\pi_{i,j}
on the forward and the reversed lattice, and ultimately use the average of the two.
