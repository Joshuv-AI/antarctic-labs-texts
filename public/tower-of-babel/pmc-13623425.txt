Cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis with predominant IgM or IgG immune deposits on direct immunofluorescence: What does it mean in clinical practice and how does it fit within the dermatologic addendum to the 2012 revised International Chapel Hill Consensus Conference Nomenclature of Vasculitides? A retrospective review of 14 patients at Mayo Clinic
Authors: Kathleen M Fletcher, Julia S Lehman, David A Wetter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cutaneous IgM/IgG immune complex vasculitis is a newly recognized provisional subtype of single-organ vasculitis that is clinically indistinguishable from IgA vasculitis. This retrospective case series evaluated the presence, clinicopathologic characteristics and outcomes of patients with IgM and/or IgG dominance or codominance on direct immunofluorescence testing (2010–23).

Cutaneous IgM/IgG immune complex vasculitis is a single-organ cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis that presents clinically with palpable purpura with a predilection for dependent areas of the body and accompanying direct immunofluorescence (DIF) microscopy revealing prominent superficial perivascular deposition of IgM and/or IgG.

Clinicians have recently defined a subset of cutaneous small-­vessel vasculitis that, from a cutaneous perspective, appears clinically indistinguishable from skin-limited IgA vasculitis. However, direct immunofluorescence (DIF) reveals IgM and/or IgG dominant or codominant perivascular immune deposition without evidence of accompanying IgA deposition.1,2 This provisional cat­egory of cutaneous vasculitis has been termed ‘­cutaneous IgM/IgG immune complex vasculitis’ and was not initially included in the 2012 International Chapel Hill Consensus Conference Nomen­clature of Vasculitides.1,2 This retrospective review serves to evaluate the presence, clinicopathological characteristics and outcomes of 14 patients with cutaneous ­small-vessel vasculitis with IgM and/or IgG deposition on DIF who do not otherwise belong in one of the other defined categories of cutaneous vasculitis ­diagnosed from January 2010 through September 2023 at Mayo Clinic.

Patients and methods

As Mayo Clinic uses standardized DIF templates, a natural language search of DIF reports (January 2010 through September 2023) was conducted to identify reports containing at least one of the following: ‘IgG: Granular deposition within’; ‘IgG: Perivascular’; ‘IgG4: Granular deposition within’; ‘IgG4: Perivascular’; ‘IgM: Granular deposition within’; or ‘IgM: Perivascular’. Inclusion criteria were palpable purpura with predilection for dependent areas of the body, skin biopsy demonstrating leucocytoclastic vasculitis, and DIF demonstrating IgM and/or IgG dominant or codominant perivascular immune deposits. Cases were excluded if ­leucocytoclastic vasculitis was not identified on histopathology, if IgA was present on DIF or if the final clinical diagnosis was not consistent with cutaneous vasculitis.

We identified 293 patients, 77 of whome had accompanying electronic health records at Mayo Clinic for review. Of the 77 reviewed patients, 14 were identified as having cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis with IgM and/or IgG dominant or codominant perivascular deposition on DIF, without IgA perivascular deposition. Cases were divided into five subgroups, including idiopathic, vasculitis associated with underlying autoimmune connective tissue disease, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis, cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis and vasculitis associated with a probable aetiology (e.g. drug-induced, infection or malignancy-associated) (Figure 1).

Results

Of the 14 cases identified, 5 (36%) were found to be idiopathic in nature and were retrospectively diagnosed as cutaneous IgM/IgG immune complex vasculitis. Two (14%) were associated with an underlying autoimmune connective tissue disorder, 1 (7%) was diagnosed as ANCA-associated vasculitis, 1 (7%) was associated with cryoglobulinaemia and 5 (36%) were associated with a probable aetiology (Figure 1). Patients with associated autoimmune connective tissue disease and underlying probable aetiologies more commonly presented with more severe cutaneous disease than patients with idiopathic disease at the time of diagnosis, as evidenced by necrotic-ulcerative lesions occurring in both patients (100%) with underlying connective tissue disease and one patient (20%) associated with a probable aetiology, and haemorrhagic bullae occurring in one (20%) patient associated with a probable aetiology. Regarding systemic symptoms, two (40%) patients with idiopathic cases had associated myalgias and arthralgias, and the patient with ANCA-associated vasculitis presented with pulmonary symptoms and was diagnosed with eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis. Among the patients in the idiopathic subgroup, laboratory data collected at the time of presentation were retrospectively reviewed to rule out any potential aetiology inducing the cutaneous vasculitis. Available data included complete blood count, a comprehensive metabolic panel, a monoclonal gammopathy screen, antinuclear antibodies, extractable nuclear antigens, complement levels, rheumatoid factor, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies, ANCA, serum inflammatory markers, urinalysis, urine microscopy, cryoglobulins, cryofibrinogens, cold agglutinins, peripheral blood smear, recent chest imaging, hepatitis panel, HIV, group A streptococcus swab and serology, as well as a history of recent infection, medication changes or vaccine administration.3–7 No trigger was identified in any of the cases of cutaneous IgM/IgG immune complex vasculitis.

In all five cases of idiopathic cutaneous small-vessel IgM/IgG immune complex vasculitis, palpable purpura was limited to the lower extremities (Figure 2), with accompanying DIF revealing superficial perivascular deposition of IgM, C3 and intermittent fibrinogen (Figure 3). No patients demonstrated IgG or IgG4 perivascular deposition. Four (80%) patients were managed with observation, topical corticosteroids alone, oral corticosteroids alone, or a combination of topical and oral corticosteroids, and subsequently experienced complete resolution without recurrence (Table 2).

Discussion

This study provides further data about the provisional category of cutaneous IgM/IgG immune complex vasculitis as a distinct subtype of single-organ, cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis affecting primarily postcapillary venules in the skin, as previously proposed.1,2 To our knowledge, this report describes only the second study to date to characterize this unique population.2

In this study, all five patients with idiopathic cutaneous IgM/IgG immune complex vasculitis presented with palpable purpura limited to the lower extremities with accompanying DIF revealing predominance of perivascular IgM deposition. Interestingly, early analysis of this provisional entity by Marzano et al.2 also revealed a marked IgM predominance (39/41 patients showed IgM alone, 2 showed IgM and IgG codominance, and none showed IgG alone). None of the patients in this cohort demonstrated IgG or IgG4 perivascular deposition. Further studies are needed to determine whether the presence of IgG has clinical implications and whether the proposed terminology is appropriate for this provisional entity. Clinically, more severe disease at presentation was observed in cases associated with either underlying connective tissue disease or a probable aetiology, but not in idiopathic cases. This finding suggests that patients presenting with more severe disease at the time of diagnosis should be thoroughly evaluated for potential underlying causes.

Regarding treatment and long-term outcomes, our findings were similar to those of the prior report of this entity,2 which also revealed mainly single-episode cases without systemic involvement and treatment predominantly with topical and oral corticosteroids that resulted in complete resolution. The similarities further suggest that this entity is a single-organ vasculitis affecting primarily the skin.

Additionally, nine patients (64%) with cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis with IgM and/or IgG dominant or codominant deposition on DIF were found to have an underlying autoimmune connective tissue disease, cryoglobulinaemia, ANCA-associated vasculitis, infection or a drug-related cause (Figure 1). Therefore, it is imperative that these patients undergo a thorough investigation that includes testing for these entities, performing a thorough review of prescription and over-the-counter medications, and evaluating for potential underlying infection.

Limitations of this study include a retrospective design with a small cohort size, which may have underpowered it to assess IgG perivascular deposition, a rare finding.8 This probably underestimated the prevalence of this entity due to the inability to access many electronic health records, as a large number of external DIF cases are sent to Mayo Clinic for analysis without accompanying clinic visits for evaluation. Additionally, it is likely that not all patients who clinically presented with leucocytoclastic vasculitis had a biopsy specimen sent for DIF, and the timing and location of the skin biopsy could not be standardized because of the study’s retrospective design, leading to potential sampling error and underdetection of immune deposits. Lastly, due to the retrospective study design, data collection was inconsistent, owing to the absence of a standardized protocol for ordering laboratory tests during clinical evaluations. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to further characterize this provisional entity.

This study examined the provisional entity of cutaneous IgM/IgG immune complex vasculitis, with the conclusion that certain DIF findings may prompt additional evaluation, which must be completed before a diagnosis of idiopathic cutaneous IgM/IgG vasculitis can be assigned. Further studies are needed to evaluate the presence, clinical presentation, association with systemic disease and long-term outcomes of this provisional subset of cutaneous vasculitis, as well as to draw comparisons with IgA vasculitis and DIF-negative vasculitis.
