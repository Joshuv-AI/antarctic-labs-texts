Formation of singularities for the relativistic membrane equation with radial symmetry
Lv Cai, Jianli Liu

Abstract
. The relativistic membrane equation can be rewritten as a first order hyperbolic system. Making use of the characteristic decomposition method, a new blow-up theorem is established. As an application, it demonstrates the formation of singularities for the relativistic membrane equation. Indeed, the singularity occurs when the hypersurface turns from being timelike to being null. This generalizes the result of Kong, Sun and Zhou’s work for one-dimensional case [J Math Phys 47(1): 013503, 2006].

1. Introduction

The study of singularity for nonlinear wave equations has long been a central topic in geometric analysis and mathematical physics. Among these, the relativistic membrane equation occupies a particularly significant position as it describes the dynamics of timelike extremal hypersurfaces in the Minkowski spacetime
\mathbb{R}^{1+(n+1)}(n\geq 2)
.

Mathematically, the relativistic membrane equation can be derived as the Euler-Lagrange equation for the volume functional of timelike hypersurfaces.
Let
(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{n+1})
be coordinates in the
(1+(n+1))
-dimentional Minkowski spacetime endowed with the metric

(1.1)

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{d}x_{i}^{2}.

We consider the hypersurface
S
denoted by a graph

(1.2)

x_{n+1}=\phi(t,x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}).

Then the induced metric on the hypersurface
S
becomes

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}x_{i}^{2}+\mathrm{d}\phi^{2}

\displaystyle=-(1-|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2})\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(1+|\partial_{i}\phi|^{2})\mathrm{d}x_{i}^{2}+2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{i}\phi\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x_{i}

\displaystyle+2\sum\limits_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{i,j=n}\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi\mathrm{d}x_{i}\mathrm{d}x_{j}

\displaystyle\triangleq(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}x_{1},\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n})G(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}x_{1},\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n})^{\mathrm{T}},

where

G=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}-(1-|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2})&\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{1}\phi&\cdots&\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{n}\phi\\
\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{1}\phi&1+|\partial_{1}\phi|^{2}&\cdots&\partial_{1}\phi\partial_{n}\phi\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{n}\phi&\partial_{1}\phi\partial_{n}\phi&\cdots&1+|\partial_{n}\phi|^{2}\end{array}\right).

We assume that this hypersurface is timelike, i.e., the induced metric is Lorentzian

(1.4)

1+|\nabla_{x}\phi|^{2}-|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}>0.

Thus, the volume functional for the hypersurface is

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sqrt{-\mathrm{det}G}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{x}\phi|^{2}-|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n}.

A relativistic membrane is a critical point of the volume functional (
1.5
). Consequently, we can get

(1.6)

\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\frac{\partial_{t}\phi}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{x}\phi|^{2}-|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\frac{\partial_{i}\phi}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{x}\phi|^{2}-|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}}}=0,

where
\nabla_{x}\phi=(\partial_{1}\phi,\partial_{2}\phi,\cdots,\partial_{n}\phi)
and
\partial_{t}\phi,\partial_{i}\phi
denote partial differentiation with respect to
t,x_{i}
, respectively. When
n=1
, the equation (
1.6
) is the relativistic string equation, associated with Born-Infeld theory
[
3
,
8
,
9
]
. Before we state our main result, let us introduce some necessary works in regard to the relativistic string and membrane.

As shown above, the geometric significance of the equation (
1.6
) is manifested through the induced metric (
1.3
) on the hypersurface, where the timelike condition (
1.4
) ensures the Lorentzian character of the metric. This condition is essential to the hyperbolicity of the equation (
1.6
), see
[
7
,
26
]
. The system is totally linearly degenerate in the sense of Lax
[
17
]
, and the local well-posedness for smooth initial data was proved in
[
2
]
. On the other hand, (
1.6
) is a quasilinear wave equation whose nonlinearity satisfies the ‘double null condition’
[
10
]
, and it can be reformulated as an equation in divergence form
[
19
]
. Global existence results for small initial data were obtained in
[
1
,
19
]
by exploiting these good structures from the perspective of the wave equation. The global existence of the initial boundary value problem of time-like extremal surface equation was proved in
[
21
,
22
]
and the asymptotic behavior to global classical solutions was given in
[
20
]
.

In the study of large initial data leading to a globally smooth solution,
a useful way is to examine the stability of non-trivial solutions
[
5
]
. Recently, Liu and Zhou
[
23
]
gave the existence and global nonlinear stability of traveling wave solutions to the timelike extremal hypersurface equation in the Minkowski space. Another attempt to study the large-data problem for nonlinear wave equations involves the short-pulse method introduced by Christodoulou in
[
4
]
. It turns out that this method was successfully utilized to prove the formation of black holes for the vacuum Einstein equation. Later, the ideas were adapted to the relativistic membrane equation in the two and three dimensions
[
24
]
, proving the global existence of smooth solution with large data. All of these results regarding small perturbations of the trivial background solution depend on the ‘null structure’ inherent to the equations.

For one-dimensional case, Wang and Wei
[
25
]
addressed the global existence for the relativistic string within the setting that the ‘right travelling wave’ was non-small while the ‘left travelling wave’ had to be small enough. By the approach of characteristics, Kong and Tusji
[
12
]
proposed sufficient and necessary conditions for global solutions to the general quasilinear system with linearly degenerate characteristics, which were applied to the relativistic string in
[
15
]
.

However, formation of singularities for the relativistic membrane equation remains less understood. Early work by Hoppe
[
9
]
explored singularities in relativistic membranes using algebraic methods. Eggers and Hoppe
[
6
]
constructed swallowtail singularities and observed that solutions to the relativistic membrane equation approximate those of the eikonal equation near singularities
[
7
]
. Therefore, global results for large data mentioned above
[
24
,
25
]
were built on a framework that the timelike condition (
1.4
) holds for all time. We refer to
[
13
,
14
,
16
]
for singularity formation of other models with linearly degenerate characteristics.

This paper investigates the formation of singularities for the radially symmetric relativistic membrane equation with

(1.7)

\phi(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=\phi(t,r),

where
r=\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. The volume functional (
1.5
) is

(1.8)

\mathrm{V}(\phi)=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\iint_{\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{R}^{+}}}r^{n-1}\sqrt{1+\phi_{r}^{2}-\phi_{t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}r,

where
\Gamma(n)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-x}x^{n-1}\mathrm{d}x
is the Gamma function. Then the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equation (
1.6
) becomes

(1.9)

\left(\frac{r^{n-1}\phi_{t}}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)_{t}-\left(\frac{r^{n-1}\phi_{r}}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)_{r}=0,

where
\Delta\triangleq 1+\phi_{r}^{2}-\phi_{t}^{2}
. For
r>0
, it can be rewritten as a quasilinear wave equation

(1.10)

(1+\phi_{r}^{2})\phi_{tt}-2\phi_{r}\phi_{t}\phi_{tr}-(1-\phi_{t}^{2})\phi_{rr}=\frac{(n-1)\phi_{r}\Delta}{r}.

Consider the Cauchy problem with initial data

(1.11)

t=0:\phi=\phi_{0}(r)\in H_{rad}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\phi_{t}=\phi_{1}(r)\in H_{rad}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where
s>\frac{n+2}{2}
. For the axially symmetric case, Wong
[
27
]
identified the singularity arising from the degeneration of the principal symbol of the evolution, distinct from shock-type singularities. In this work, we make progress by developing a new characteristic decomposition for the radially symmetric case, establishing precise conditions under which the timelike condition fails in finite time, as well as providing a geometric interpretation of the singularity formation process.

In this paper, we reformulate the relativistic membrane equation as a first order hyperbolic system (
2.8
). A blow-up result of (
2.8
) was established by using the characteristic decomposition method, c.f. Theorem
3.1
. As an application, we can prove that for radially symmetric initial data satisfying certain natural conditions, the solution will develop a singularity in finite time through the loss of hyperbolicity. This complements and extends Kong, Sun and Zhou’s work
[
11
]
in one-dimensional case.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we rewrite the relativistic membrane equation as a first order system. In Section
3
, we establish and prove a new blow-up theorem leading to the formation of singularities for the the relativistic membrane equation.

2. Preliminaries

Let

(2.1)

\displaystyle s_{1}\triangleq\phi_{r},\ s_{2}\triangleq-\frac{\phi_{t}}{\sqrt{\Delta}},

and define

(2.2)

\displaystyle u\triangleq\frac{s_{1}s_{2}}{\sqrt{(1+s_{1}^{2})(1+s_{2}^{2})}},\ v\triangleq\sqrt{(1+s_{1}^{2})(1+s_{2}^{2})}.

The transformation maps
(\phi_{t},\phi_{r})
to
(u,v)
as

(2.3)

\displaystyle u=-\frac{\phi_{r}\phi_{t}}{1+\phi_{r}^{2}},\ v=\frac{1+\phi_{r}^{2}}{\sqrt{\Delta}}.

We can rewrite the equation (
1.10
) as the following first order quasilinear system.

Lemma 2.1
.

The relativistic membrane equation (
1.10
) can be reduced to the following system

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}u_{t}+uu_{r}+v^{-3}v_{r}=-(n-1)r^{-1}v^{-2}\mathcal{F}(u,v)&\\
v_{t}+vu_{r}+uv_{r}=-(n-1)r^{-1}uv,&\end{array}\right.\end{split}

where the nonlinear term

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(u,v)\triangleq\frac{1}{2}[(v^{2}-u^{2}v^{2}-1)-\sqrt{(v^{2}-u^{2}v^{2}-1)^{2}-4u^{2}v^{2}}].

Proof.

By the direct calculation, one has

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=(-\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}-\phi_{tt}\phi_{r})(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-1}+2\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}\phi_{r}^{2}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-2}

\displaystyle=(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-2}[-\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}(1-\phi_{r}^{2})-\phi_{tt}\phi_{r}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})],

\displaystyle u_{r}

\displaystyle=(-\phi_{rr}\phi_{t}-\phi_{tr}\phi_{r})(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-1}+2\phi_{rr}\phi_{t}\phi_{r}^{2}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-2}

\displaystyle=(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-2}[-\phi_{rr}\phi_{t}(1-\phi_{r}^{2})-\phi_{tr}\phi_{r}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})],

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=2\phi_{tr}\phi_{r}\Delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}+(1+\phi_{r}^{2})(\Delta^{-\frac{1}{2}})_{t}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-\frac{3}{2}}[\phi_{tr}\phi_{r}(1+\phi_{r}^{2}-2\phi_{t}^{2})+\phi_{tt}\phi_{t}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})],

\displaystyle v_{r}

\displaystyle=2\phi_{rr}\phi_{r}\Delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}+(1+\phi_{r}^{2})(\Delta^{-\frac{1}{2}})_{r}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-\frac{3}{2}}[\phi_{rr}\phi_{r}(1+\phi_{r}^{2}-2\phi_{t}^{2})+\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})].

Then,

\displaystyle u_{t}+uu_{r}+v^{-3}v_{r}

\displaystyle=(-\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}-\phi_{tt}\phi_{r})(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-1}+2\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}\phi_{r}^{2}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-2}

\displaystyle-\phi_{t}\phi_{r}[(-\phi_{rr}\phi_{t}-\phi_{tr}\phi_{r})(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-2}+2\phi_{rr}\phi_{t}\phi_{r}^{2}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-3}]

\displaystyle+[\phi_{rr}\phi_{r}(1+\phi_{r}^{2}-2\phi_{t}^{2})+\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})](1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-3}

\displaystyle=-(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-2}\phi_{r}[\phi_{tt}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})-2\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}\phi_{r}-\phi_{rr}(1-\phi_{t}^{2})].

Noting the equation (
1.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle u_{t}+uu_{r}+v^{-3}v_{r}=-(n-1)r^{-1}v^{-2}\phi_{r}^{2}.

It follows from the representation (
2.2
) that the new variables (
u,v
) satisfy

\displaystyle 0\leq u\leq 1,v\geq 1.

Besides, by (
2.3
), if
\phi_{r}=0
, then
u=0
. Otherwise, if
\phi_{r}
does not vanish, one can derive the equation of
\phi_{r}^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{u^{2}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{2}}{\phi_{r}^{2}}=(1+\phi_{r}^{2})-\frac{(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{2}}{v^{2}},

or equivalently,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\phi_{r}^{4}-(v^{2}-u^{2}v^{2}-1)\phi_{r}^{2}+u^{2}v^{2}=0.

We take

(2.7)

\displaystyle\phi_{r}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}[(v^{2}-u^{2}v^{2}-1)-\sqrt{(v^{2}-u^{2}v^{2}-1)^{2}-4u^{2}v^{2}}]=\mathcal{F}(u,v).

Here we note that by (
2.2
), one has

\displaystyle v^{2}-u^{2}v^{2}-1=s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}\geq 0,

\displaystyle(v^{2}-u^{2}v^{2}-1)^{2}-4u^{2}v^{2}=(s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2})^{2}-4s_{1}^{2}s_{2}^{2}\geq 0,

which ensures that (
2.7
) makes sense. On the other hand,

\displaystyle v_{t}+uv_{r}+u_{r}v

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-\frac{3}{2}}[\phi_{tr}\phi_{r}(1+\phi_{r}^{2}-2\phi_{t}^{2})+\phi_{tt}\phi_{t}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})]

\displaystyle-\Delta^{-\frac{3}{2}}\phi_{t}\phi_{r}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-1}[\phi_{rr}\phi_{r}(1+\phi_{r}^{2}-2\phi_{t}^{2})+\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})]

\displaystyle+\Delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})^{-1}[\phi_{rr}\phi_{t}(-1+\phi_{r}^{2})-\phi_{tr}\phi_{r}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})]

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-\frac{3}{2}}\phi_{t}[\phi_{tt}(1+\phi_{r}^{2})-2\phi_{tr}\phi_{t}\phi_{r}-\phi_{rr}(1-\phi_{t}^{2})].

Again by (
1.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle v_{t}+uv_{r}+u_{r}v=-(n-1)r^{-1}uv.

The proof of Lemma
2.1
is completed.

\Box

If one rescales the new variables
\hat{t}=(n-1)t,\ \hat{r}=(n-1)r
and still denotes by
t,\ r
, then (
2.4
) becomes

(2.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}u_{t}+uu_{r}+v^{-3}v_{r}=-r^{-1}v^{-2}\mathcal{F}(u,v)&\\
v_{t}+vu_{r}+uv_{r}=-r^{-1}uv.&\end{array}\right.\end{split}

3. A Blow-up Result

The initial data we consider are

(3.1)

\displaystyle(u,v)(r,0)=(\bar{u},v_{0})(r),\ \ r\in[r_{1},r_{2}].

The following assumptions are made throughout this paper.

(3.2)

\displaystyle\text{(A1)}\ 0<r_{1}<r_{2},\ v_{0}\text{ is a positive constant},\ \bar{u}(r)\in C^{1}[r_{1},r_{2}],\ \bar{u}(r_{2})>v_{0}^{-1};

\displaystyle\text{(A2)}\ \bar{u}^{\prime}(r)+\frac{\bar{u}(r)+\beta\mathcal{F}_{0}(r)}{r}<0,\text{ for }\ r\in[r_{1},r_{2}],

\displaystyle\text{ where }1\leq\beta<\infty\text{ is a given constant },\mathcal{F}_{0}(r)=\mathcal{F}_{0}(\bar{u}(r),v_{0});

\displaystyle\text{(A3)}\ \text{there exists an interval }(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\in(r_{1},r_{2})\text{ such that }

\displaystyle 0<\frac{\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}}{\bar{u}(\eta_{1})-\bar{u}(\eta_{2})-2v_{0}^{-1}}

\displaystyle<\min\left\{\frac{\eta_{1}-r_{1}}{\bar{u}(r_{1})-\bar{u}(\eta_{1})+2v_{0}^{-1}},\frac{r_{2}-\eta_{2}}{\bar{u}(\eta_{2})-\bar{u}(r_{2})+2v_{0}^{-1}}\right\}.

Note that if
\bar{u}(\eta_{1})-\bar{u}(\eta_{2})>2v_{0}^{-1}
and
\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}
is sufficiently small then the assumption (A3) can be satisfied. By analyzing the dynamics along characteristic curves and applying the assumptions (A1)-(A3), we shall show that the variable

v
of the classical solution to system (
2.8
) with initial data (
3.1
) blows up in finite time, leading to the loss of hyperbolicity.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a
t_{*}>0
such that system (
2.8
) with initial data (
3.1
) admits a classical solution in
\Lambda(t_{*})
with

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow t_{*}^{-}}\left\|v(\cdot,t)\right\|_{0;\Lambda(t)}=+\infty.

Here
\Lambda(t)
is a domain defined in (
3.26
).

3.1. Characteristic decompositions

In this subsection, we derive a group of suitable characteristic decompositions for system (
2.8
). One can easily get the eigenvalues of system (
2.8
)

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{+}=u+v^{-1},\ \lambda_{-}=u-v^{-1},

with the corresponding left eigenvectors
l_{\pm}=(\pm v,v^{-1})
. Multiplying (
2.8
) by
l_{\pm}
from the left-side yields the characteristic equations

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}v\partial_{+}u+v^{-1}\partial_{+}v=-r^{-1}(u+v^{-1}\mathcal{F}(u,v))&\\
-v\partial_{-}u+v^{-1}\partial_{-}v=-r^{-1}(u-v^{-1}\mathcal{F}(u,v)),&\end{array}\right.\end{split}

where

(3.6)

\partial_{\pm}\triangleq\partial_{t}+(u\pm v^{-1})\partial_{r}.

The equations (
3.5
) can be rewritten as

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\partial_{+}u=-v^{-2}\partial_{+}v-r^{-1}(uv^{-1}+v^{-2}\mathcal{F}(u,v))\\
\partial_{-}u=v^{-2}\partial_{-}v+r^{-1}(uv^{-1}-v^{-2}\mathcal{F}(u,v)).\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Clearly, one has

(3.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}=\frac{v(\partial_{+}+\partial_{-})}{2},\ \partial_{t}=\frac{v\left[(u+v^{-1})\partial_{-}-(u-v^{-1})\partial_{+}\right]}{2}.

In this paper, we do not estimate the derivatives of the Riemann variants of system (
2.8
). Instead, we will estimate
\partial_{\pm}v
. In what follows, we shall first derive some characteristic equations for
\partial_{\pm}v
. To this end, we need to introduce the following commutator relation.

Proposition 3.1
.

One has the following commutator relation

(3.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{+}\partial_{-}-\partial_{-}\partial_{+}=-\frac{u}{r}(\partial_{+}-\partial_{-}).

Proof.

By (
3.6
), (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{+}\partial_{-}-\partial_{-}\partial_{+}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{t}+\lambda_{+}\partial_{r})(\partial_{t}+\lambda_{-}\partial_{r})-(\partial_{t}+\lambda_{-}\partial_{r})(\partial_{t}+\lambda_{+}\partial_{r})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\lambda_{-}\partial_{r})+\lambda_{+}\partial_{t}\partial_{r}+\lambda_{+}\partial_{r}(\lambda_{-}\partial_{r})

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}(\lambda_{+}\partial_{r})-\lambda_{-}\partial_{t}\partial_{r}-\lambda_{-}\partial_{r}(\lambda_{+}\partial_{r})

\displaystyle=(\partial_{t}\lambda_{-})\partial_{r}+\lambda_{+}(\partial_{r}\lambda_{-})\partial_{r}-(\partial_{t}\lambda_{+})\partial_{r}-\lambda_{-}(\partial_{r}\lambda_{+})\partial_{r}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{+}\lambda_{-}-\partial_{-}\lambda_{+})\partial_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2uv^{-1}}{r}\cdot\frac{v(\partial_{+}-\partial_{-})}{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{u}{r}(\partial_{+}-\partial_{-}).

This completes the proof of Proposition
3.1
.
\Box

The next proposition states the characteristic decomposition for the variable
v
in (
2.8
).

Proposition 3.2
.

One has the following characteristic decomposition about the solution
v

(3.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{+}\partial_{-}v&=2v^{-1}\partial_{+}v\cdot\partial_{-}v+\frac{1}{2r}(2u-v^{-2}\partial_{u}\mathcal{F})\left(\partial_{+}v+\partial_{-}v\right)\\
&\ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{2r}\left(-u+v^{-1}-2v^{-1}\mathcal{F}+\partial_{v}\mathcal{F}\right)\left(\partial_{+}v-\partial_{-}v\right)\\
&\ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left(2u^{2}v-uv^{-1}\partial_{u}\mathcal{F}\right),\\
\partial_{-}\partial_{+}v&=2v^{-1}\partial_{+}v\cdot\partial_{-}v+\frac{1}{2r}(2u-v^{-2}\partial_{u}\mathcal{F})\left(\partial_{+}v+\partial_{-}v\right)\\
&\ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{2r}\left(u+v^{-1}-2v^{-1}\mathcal{F}+\partial_{v}\mathcal{F}\right)\left(\partial_{+}v-\partial_{-}v\right)\\
&\ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left(2u^{2}v-uv^{-1}\partial_{u}\mathcal{F}\right).\end{split}

Proof.

Applying the commutator relation (
3.9
) for variables
v
and
u
respectively, we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{+}\partial_{-}v-\partial_{-}\partial_{+}v=-\frac{u}{r}\left(\partial_{+}v-\partial_{-}v\right),

and

(3.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{+}\partial_{-}u-\partial_{-}\partial_{+}u=-\frac{u}{r}(\partial_{+}u-\partial_{-}u).

Inserting (
3.7
) into the left side of (
3.12
) yields that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{+}[v^{-2}\partial_{-}v+r^{-1}(uv^{-1}-v^{-2}\mathcal{F})]-\partial_{-}[-v^{-2}\partial_{+}v+r^{-1}(uv^{-1}+v^{-2}\mathcal{F})]

\displaystyle=[\partial_{+}(v^{-2})\partial_{-}v+\partial_{-}(v^{-2})\partial_{+}v]+v^{-2}(\partial_{+}\partial_{-}v+\partial_{-}\partial_{+}v)

\displaystyle+r^{-1}[\partial_{+}(uv^{-1})+\partial_{-}(uv^{-1})]+r^{-1}[-\partial_{+}(v^{-2}\mathcal{F})+\partial_{-}(v^{-2}\mathcal{F})]

\displaystyle+(uv^{-1}-v^{-2}\mathcal{F})\partial_{+}(r^{-1})+(uv^{-1}+v^{-2}\mathcal{F})\partial_{-}(r^{-1})

\displaystyle\triangleq v^{-2}[-4v^{-1}\partial_{+}v\cdot\partial_{-}v+(\partial_{+}\partial_{-}v+\partial_{-}\partial_{+}v)]+I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

where

(3.14)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\triangleq r^{-1}[\partial_{+}(uv^{-1})+\partial_{-}(uv^{-1})]

\displaystyle=r^{-1}[v^{-1}(\partial_{+}u+\partial_{-}u)+u(\partial_{+}(v^{-1})+\partial_{-}(v^{-1}))]

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\{v^{-1}[-v^{-2}(\partial_{+}v-\partial_{-}v)-2r^{-1}v^{-2}\mathcal{F}]-uv^{-2}(\partial_{+}v+\partial_{-}v)\}

\displaystyle=v^{-2}[-r^{-1}v^{-1}(\partial_{+}v-\partial_{-}v)-r^{-1}u(\partial_{+}v+\partial_{-}v)-2r^{-2}v^{-1}\mathcal{F}],

(3.15)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\triangleq r^{-1}[-\partial_{+}(v^{-2}\mathcal{F})+\partial_{-}(v^{-2}\mathcal{F})]

\displaystyle=r^{-1}[\mathcal{F}(-\partial_{+}(v^{-2})+\partial_{-}(v^{-2}))+v^{-2}(-\partial_{+}\mathcal{F}+\partial_{-}\mathcal{F})]

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\{2v^{-3}\mathcal{F}(\partial_{+}v-\partial_{-}v)+v^{-2}[\partial_{u}\mathcal{F}(-\partial_{+}u+\partial_{-}u)+\partial_{v}\mathcal{F}(-\partial_{+}v+\partial_{-}v)]\}

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\{2v^{-3}\mathcal{F}(\partial_{+}v-\partial_{-}v)+v^{-2}[\partial_{u}\mathcal{F}(v^{-2}(\partial_{+}v+\partial_{-}v)+2r^{-1}uv^{-1})

\displaystyle-\partial_{v}\mathcal{F}(\partial_{+}v-\partial_{-}v)]\}

\displaystyle=v^{-2}[r^{-1}v^{-2}\partial_{u}\mathcal{F}(\partial_{+}v+\partial_{-}v)+r^{-1}(2v^{-1}\mathcal{F}-\partial_{v}\mathcal{F})(\partial_{+}v-\partial_{-}v)

\displaystyle+2r^{-1}uv^{-1}\partial_{u}\mathcal{F}],

and

(3.16)

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle\triangleq(uv^{-1}-v^{-2}\mathcal{F})\partial_{+}(r^{-1})+(uv^{-1}+v^{-2}\mathcal{F})\partial_{-}(r^{-1})

\displaystyle=-r^{-2}(u+v^{-1})(uv^{-1}-v^{-2}\mathcal{F})-r^{-2}(u-v^{-1})(uv^{-1}+v^{-2}\mathcal{F})

(3.42)

\displaystyle r_{-}(t)-r_{0}(t;\eta_{2})>r_{2}-\eta_{2}-(\bar{u}(\eta_{2})-u_{2}+2v^{-1}_{0})t,

\displaystyle r_{0}(t;\eta_{1})-r_{+}(t)>\eta_{1}-r_{1}-(u_{1}-\bar{u}(\eta_{1})+2v^{-1}_{0})t,

which yields that

(3.43)

\displaystyle r_{0}(t;\eta_{2})-r_{0}(t;\eta_{1})<\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}-(\bar{u}(\eta_{1})-\bar{u}(\eta_{2})-2v^{-1}_{0})t.

Hence, it follows from (A3) that if system (
2.8
) with initial data (
3.1
) admits a classical solution, then there exists a
0<t^{*}<\frac{\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}}{\bar{u}(\eta_{1})-\bar{u}(\eta_{2})-2v^{-1}_{0}}
such that

\displaystyle r_{+}(t^{*})<r_{0}(t^{*};\eta_{2})=r_{0}(t^{*};\eta_{1})<r_{-}(t^{*}),

and

\displaystyle r_{+}(t)<r_{0}(t;\eta_{2})<r_{0}(t;\eta_{1})<r_{-}(t)\ \ \ \ \text{for }t<t^{*}.

Consequently, by (
3.35
) we obtain that there exists a
t_{*}\in(0,t^{*}]
such that system (
2.8
) with initial data (
3.1
) admits a classical solution in
\Lambda(t_{*})
and

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow t_{*}^{-}}\left\|v(\cdot,t)\right\|_{0;\Lambda(t)}=+\infty.

This completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We end up this article with an application of Theorem
3.1
showing the singularity formation of the relativistic membrane equation. Recall that

(3.44)

\displaystyle u=-\frac{\phi_{r}\phi_{t}}{1+\phi_{r}^{2}},\ v=\frac{1+\phi_{r}^{2}}{\sqrt{\Delta}},

and
\Delta=1+\phi_{r}^{2}-\phi_{t}^{2}
. The vanishing of
\Delta
causes the blow-up of
v
since
\phi_{r}
is assumed to be regular in the domain
\Lambda(t_{*})
. This
precisely characterizes the nature of singularities. Indeed, the singularity occurs when the hypersurface turns from being timelike to being null.
