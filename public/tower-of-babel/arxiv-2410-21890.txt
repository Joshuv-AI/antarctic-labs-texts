Numerical Boundary Control of Multi-Dimensional Hyperbolic Equations Thanks: ∗ Corresponding author: Kai Hinzmann


Abstract
. Existing theoretical stabilization results for linear, hyperbolic multi–dimensional problems are extended to the discretized multi-dimensional problems. In contrast to existing theoretical and numerical analysis in the spatially one–dimensional case the effect of the numerical dissipation is analyzed and explicitly quantified. Further, using dimensional splitting, the numerical analysis is extended to the multi-dimensional case. The findings are confirmed by numerical simulations for low-order and high-order DG schemes both in the one-dimensional and two-dimensional case.

1.
Introduction

Stabilization of spatially one–dimensional systems of hyperbolic balance laws has been a subject of many investigations in the mathematical and engineering community, respectively, see, e.g., the monographs
[
2
,
4
,
33
,
35
]
. In particular, the boundary control of typically one–dimensional hyperbolic systems, like the isentropic Euler equations or the Saint–Venant equations has been a subject of recent research, see e.g.
[
26
,
17
,
27
,
15
,
34
,
9
,
7
,
6
]
and references therein. The boundary control applied has been of feedback type and has been aimed to stabilize the dynamics at a desired equilibrium. The main analytical tool in the spatially one–dimensional setting
is
the analysis of a family of weighted Lyapunov functions. They have been proposed as (exponentially) weighted
L^{2}
– (or
H^{s}
–) norms of the solution. Provided that the boundary conditions are dissipative, exponential decay of the Lyapunov function
can be established, cf.
[
7
,
10
,
5
,
6
]
.
Furthermore, a comparison to other stability concepts has been provided
[
8
]
.
Extensions towards input–to–state stability have also been investigated, e.g. in
[
37
]
, as well as extensions towards nonlocal systems, see e.g.
[
11
,
13
,
12
]
. While most of the results aim to stabilize the linearized dynamics, there are also results on stability of nonlinear systems, see e.g.
[
6
,
2
]
.

Most of the existing literature focuses on analytical results and on the spatially one–dimensional case. Decay rates of the corresponding
numerical discretization
in the spatially one–dimensional case have been established only recently for linear hyperbolic systems with symmetric source term
[
1
,
38
]
in
L^{2}
, for linear hyperbolic balance laws in
H^{s}

[
23
,
24
]
, and for scalar hyperbolic equations using second–order schemes
[
18
,
19
]
. Those results can be seen in part as a numerical counterpart to the analytical results reviewed above.

Furthermore, only recently, the Lyapunov stability for
multi–dimensional
hyperbolic has been investigated from an analytical perspective using similar techniques as cited above, see
[
30
,
29
]
. In
[
30
]
, the authors study (general) linear, hyperbolic multi–dimensional systems of balance laws in a bounded domain. Using again a weighted norm as Lyapunov function, they derive boundary feedback control that stabilizes the dynamics at a steady–state. Exponential decay of the Lyapunov function has been established, however, without explicit rates of the decay. In the multi-dimensional case, there exists also an approach tailored to the shallow-water equations, where boundary conditions have been designed to control the decay of the energy, see
[
16
]
.
Hyperbolic multi–dimensional systems satisfying the strong stability condition are studied in
[
42
]
. A study on the relation between the approaches given in
[
30
]
and
[
42
]
is provided in
[
28
]
.
Further, in
[
39
]
a
L^{2}
-Lyapunov function for multi–dimensional scalar conservation laws has been studied.

The goal of the present paper is to complement the existing stabilization results for linear, hyperbolic multi–dimensional systems
[
29
,
30
,
42
]
by an analysis of the corresponding discretized systems. Here, we will focus on the exponential stabilization using the family of Lyapunov functions
introduced in
[
29
]
. In contrast
to the analysis in the spatially one–dimensional case
[
1
,
38
,
23
]
, we will also investigate and quantify the effect of the numerical dissipation. This dissipation has already been observed in the numerical results, e.g. in
[
1
]
, but has not been quantified. On coarse grids, this leads to an additional decay of the Lyapunov function compared with the analytical result. Furthermore, we use dimensional splitting as numerical method. A careful analysis of the splitting is required in order to obtain the exponential decay rate. Following the analytical result of

[
29
]
,
the numerical analysis will be presented for linear, multi–dimensional systems, and first–order schemes. Numerical results will also be presented using higher–order schemes, in particular, to illustrate further the effect of the numerical dissipation.

2.
Description of the Problem

We first introduce the continuous setting before turning to the discretization.
To this end we recall the problem and theoretical results of
[
29
]
.

The feedback control problem in the multi–dimensional case is given by a linear system of hyperbolic partial differential equations.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with
piecewise
smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
.
We consider the following IBVP for a linear system of hyperbolic partial differential equations as in
[
29
]
.

Problem

(General IBVP)
.

For a given bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
piecewise
smooth boundary
\partial\Omega

find a solution
\mathbf{w}:[0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

to the IBVP

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\partial_{x_{k}}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0},\quad(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\Omega

(1a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}(0,\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega

(1b)

\displaystyle{w_{i}}(t,\mathbf{x})={u_{i}}(t,\mathbf{x}),\quad(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\Gamma^{-}_{{i}},\ {i=1,\ldots,d}.

(1c)

Further, we assume that
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})
for
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
and
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})
are sufficiently smooth and bounded
m\times m
real matrices. In addition, all matrices
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})
are assumed to be diagonal matrices.

The boundary feedback control is realized through the function
\mathbf{u}
in the boundary condition (
1c
).
Here, we prescribe for each component
w_{i}
with
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
boundary values given by some function
\mathbf{u}(t,\mathbf{x})
at the inflow part of the boundary
\partial\Omega
characterized by

\Gamma_{i}^{-}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega:\mathbf{a}_{i}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})<0\},

where the vector of diagonal entries of the matrices
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
is denoted by

\mathbf{a}_{i}:=(a_{ii}^{(1)},\dots,a_{ii}^{(d)})
.
The outflow part of the boundary
\partial\Omega
for component
i
will be denoted by

\Gamma_{i}^{+}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega:\mathbf{a}_{i}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\}.

Remark 2.1
.

Since Dirichlet boundary conditions can only be prescribed at
\Gamma_{i}^{-}
but not at
\Gamma_{i}^{+}
we refer to these boundary parts as controllable and uncontrollable part, respectively.
Analogous to
[
29
]
, we further divide
\Gamma_{i}^{-}
into the disjoint sets
\mathcal{C}_{i}\subset\Gamma_{i}^{-}
where we set some control
u_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})
componentwise and
\mathcal{Z}_{i}\subset\Gamma_{i}^{-}
where we set
u_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})=0
for the numerical simulations in Section
4
.

We are interested in the exponential stabilization
at zero
of the solution
\mathbf{w}
to
IBVP
(
1
)
by applying a boundary feedback control
\mathbf{u}
.
As in
[
29
]
,
we consider smooth solutions
\mathbf{w}\in C^{1}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega))^{m}
for
s\geq 1+\frac{d}{2}
of
IBVP
(
1
).
If the initial condition and the boundary data are sufficiently smooth, there exists such a solution, see
[
14
]
. Moreover, we assume that the solution is unique.
Exponential stability is defined as follows.

Definition 2.2

(Exponentially stable)
.

A solution
\mathbf{w}\in C^{1}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega))^{m}
for
s\geq 1+\frac{d}{2}
of
IBVP (
1
)
is called exponentially stable in the
L^{2}
–sense if and only if there exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{w}(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\operatorname*{exp}(-Ct)\left\lVert\mathbf{w}(0,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall t\in[0,T).

In this setting the following theorem has been obtained.
Note that Def.
2.2
can be extended to exponential stability in the
H^{s}
-norm.

Theorem 2.3

(Exponential stability)
.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in C^{1}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega))^{m}
for
s\geq 1+\frac{d}{2}
be a solution to
IBVP (
1
).
A Lyapunov function is given by

\displaystyle L(t)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})^{T}\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\>d\mathbf{x},

(2)

where

\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x})):=\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{exp}(\mu_{1}(\mathbf{x})),\dots,\operatorname{exp}(\mu_{m}(\mathbf{x})))

(3)

for functions
\mu_{i}(\mathbf{x})\in H^{s}(\Omega)
for
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
that satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\mathcal{M}^{(k)}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}+\partial_{x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\right)+\mathcal{D}&=-\operatorname{diag}\!\left(C_{L}^{(i)}\right)\\
\text{or written rowwise}\quad\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\nabla\mu_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i}+\mathcal{D}_{ii}&=-C_{L}^{(i)},\qquad C_{L}^{(i)}\geq C_{L}>0\end{aligned}

(4)

for some
C_{L}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}):=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\partial_{x_{k}}\mu_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\partial_{x_{k}}\mu_{m}(\mathbf{x})\right).

(5)

Here,
\mathcal{D}
is assumed to be a constant matrix which satisfies

\displaystyle-\mathbf{v}^{T}\left(\mathbf{B}^{T}\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}))+\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{B}\right)\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{v}\qquad\forall\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(6)

Then, for every
\mathbf{u}(t,\mathbf{x})=(u_{1}(t,\mathbf{x}),\dots,u_{m}(t,\mathbf{x}))^{T}
such that

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{-}}u_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}(\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{n})\operatorname{exp}(\mu_{i}(\mathbf{x}))\>d\mathbf{x}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{+}}w_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}(\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{n})\operatorname{exp}(\mu_{i}(\mathbf{x}))\>d\mathbf{x},

(7)

the Lyapunov function satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}L(t)\leq-C_{L}L(t)\quad\text{and}\quad L(t)\leq\exp(-C_{L}t)L(0)\qquad\forall t\in[0,T).

(8)

The proof of Theorem
2.3
can be found in

[
29
]
.

Remark 2.4
.

In addition to the Hamilton–Jacobi equations, Theorem
2.3
can also be applied to the diagonal systems of the multi–dimensional discrete–velocity kinetic models which are discussed in
[
41
]
since these are also covered by the IBVP
(
1
).

2.1.
Feedback Control Problem with Viscosity

Since any finite volume scheme will introduce numerical viscosity, we first study the viscous approximation to
IBVP (
1
).
To investigate the effect of viscosity on the decay of the Lyapunov function, we consider a viscous control problem adding diffusion to (
1a
) on a multi-dimensional rectangular domain. For simplicity we confine to the scalar case neglecting zero order terms.

Problem

(Scalar IBVP with diffusion)
.

For a given bounded domain
\Omega=[\mathbf{{\overline{a}}},\mathbf{{\overline{b}}}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

find a solution

w:[0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

to the IBVP

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w(t,\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\partial_{x_{k}}w(t,\mathbf{x})={\overline{q}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{k},x_{k}}w(t,\mathbf{x}),\quad(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\Omega

(9a)

\displaystyle w(0,\mathbf{x})=w_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega

(9b)

\displaystyle w(t,\mathbf{x})=u(t,\mathbf{x}),\quad(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\Gamma^{-},

(9c)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}w(t,\mathbf{x})=w_{b,\mathbf{n}}(t,\mathbf{x}),\quad(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\Gamma^{+}.

(9d)

The advection velocities are assumed to be
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
a_{k}\neq 0
, and we assume a
positive viscosity coefficient
{\overline{q}}>0
. By
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}
we denote the derivative in the outer unit
normal direction
\mathbf{n}
to the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

Since (
9a
) is a second order parabolic PDE, we impose two boundary conditions. Here, we choose
Dirichlet conditions and Neumann conditions at the inflow boundary
\Gamma^{-}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\,:\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})<0\}
and the outflow boundary
\Gamma^{+}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\,:\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})>0\}
, respectively.

Now we can proof the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let

w\in C^{1}((0,T);H^{s}(\Omega))

for
s\geq 1+\frac{d}{2}

be a solution to
IBVP (
9
).
The Lyapunov function is given by

\displaystyle L(t)=\int_{\Omega}w(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\>d\mathbf{x},

(10)

where we assume that there exists a function
\mu\in H^{s}(\Omega)
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla\mu(\mathbf{x})=-C_{L}

(11)

for some value
C_{L}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
The control
u
of
boundary condition (
9c
) satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u^{2}(t,\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k})\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k}))\leq w^{2}(t,\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k}-0)\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k}))\,,&a_{k}>0\\
u^{2}(t,\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k})\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k}))\leq w^{2}(t,\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k}+0)\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k}))\,,&a_{k}<0\end{cases}

(12)

at the inflow boundary
\Gamma^{-}_{k}
in the
k
th coordinate direction.
Here
\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k}
and
\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k}=\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k}-({\overline{b}}_{k}-{\overline{a}}_{k}){\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{k}
denote opposing points at the left and the right boundary surface in the
k
th direction, respectively.

Then the Lyapunov function is bounded by

\displaystyle L(t)\leq\operatorname*{exp}(-C_{L}t)L(0)+\int_{0}^{t}R(s;\bar{q})\operatorname*{exp}(-C_{L}(t-s))\>ds

(13)

where
R(t;\bar{q}):=\int_{\Omega}2\bar{q}w\mathcal{E}(\mu)\nabla\cdot\nabla w\>d\mathbf{x}
.

For the proof of Lemma
2.5
we proceed analogously to the proof of Theorem
2.3
. An additional term occurs accounting for the viscosity coefficient
\bar{q}
that needs to be estimated.

Proof.

Inserting (
9a
) in the time derivative of the Lyapunov function (
10
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}L(t)=-2\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}w)-{\overline{q}}\nabla\cdot\nabla w)w\mathcal{E}(\mu)\>d\mathbf{x}.

(14)

By the product rule we have

\displaystyle 2w\mathcal{E}(\mu)\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}w)=\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}w^{2}\mathcal{E}(\mu))-w^{2}\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{E}(\mu).

From this we conclude

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}L(t)=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}w^{2}\mathcal{E}(\mu))-w^{2}\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{E}(\mu)-2{\overline{q}}w\mathcal{E}(\mu)\nabla\cdot\nabla w\>d\mathbf{x}.

(15)

By means of the Gaussian theorem we obtain for the divergence part

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}w^{2}\mathcal{E}(\mu))\>d\mathbf{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\left(\int_{\Gamma^{k}}(w^{2}\mathcal{E}(\mu))(t,\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k}(\mathbf{s}))-(w^{2}\mathcal{E}(\mu))(t,\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k}(\mathbf{s}))\>d\mathbf{s}\right),

where
\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k}(\mathbf{s})
and
\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k}(\mathbf{s})
denote the boundary points of the surfaces

x_{k}={\overline{a}}_{k}
and
x_{k}={\overline{b}}_{k}
in the
k
th coordinate direction, respectively, parameterized by means of the
(d-1)
–dimensional domain
\Gamma^{k}
.
From the boundary condition (
9c
) and the assumptions (
12
) we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{a}w^{2}\mathcal{E}(\mu))\>d\mathbf{x}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\begin{cases}\int_{\Gamma^{k}}w^{2}(t,\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k})\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k}))-u^{2}(t,\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k})\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k}))\>d\mathbf{s}\,,&a_{k}>0\\
\int_{\Gamma^{k}}u^{2}(t,\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k})\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{R}^{k}))-w^{2}(t,\mathbf{x})_{L}^{k})\mathcal{E}(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{L}^{k}))\>d\mathbf{s}\,,&a_{k}<0\end{cases}\geq 0.

For the remaining integral in (
15
) we obtain

\displaystyle I(t):=\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{E}(\mu)+2{\overline{q}}w\mathcal{E}(\mu)\nabla\cdot\nabla w\>d\mathbf{x}=\int_{\Omega}-C_{L}w^{2}\mathcal{E}(\mu)+2{\overline{q}}w\mathcal{E}(\mu)\nabla\cdot\nabla w\>d\mathbf{x}.

By using
{\nabla}\mathcal{E}(\mu)=\mathcal{E}(\mu){\nabla}\mu

as well as (
11
)
we may estimate (
15
) by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}L(t)\leq I(t)=-C_{L}L(t)+R(t;\bar{q}).

Applying Gronwall’s Lemma gives

\displaystyle L(t)\leq\operatorname*{exp}(-C_{L}t)L(0)+\int_{0}^{t}R(s;\bar{q})\operatorname*{exp}(-C_{L}(t-s))\>ds

∎

Remark 2.6
.

Note that
\bar{q}=0
implies
R(t;0)=0
, i.e., in this case we obtain
the same decay as in Theorem
2.3

for the inviscid feedback control problem.
Furthermore, we note that diffusion has no impact on the decay rate
C_{L}
, but the decay will be perturbed by a residual.
Later, we will also quantify the term
R(\cdot\ ;\bar{q})
numerically.
Therefore, we expect for a numerical scheme a
faster
decay of the Lyapunov function compared with the continuous result.

2.2.
Numerical analysis of the discretized problem

In Sect.
2.1
we showed that viscosity in the feedback control problem, see Eq. (
9
), does not affect the exponential decay rate but influences the overall decay behavior due to an additional residual term, see Eq. (
13
). We now investigate whether numerical dissipation in the inviscid control problem (
1
) has a similar effect on the numerical decay behavior. For this purpose, we consider a simplified configuration of a multi–dimensional scalar feedback control problem.
This is similar to IBVP (
9
) where again we neglect source terms in (
1a
). Note that neglecting the source terms the IBVP (
1
) decouples in
m
independent scalar problems justifying to consider only a single scalar problem even in the case of higher spatial dimensions.

Problem

(Scalar IBVP)
.

For a given bounded domain
\Omega=[\mathbf{{\overline{a}}},\mathbf{{\overline{b}}}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

find a solution

w:[0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

to the IBVP

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w(t,\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\partial_{x_{k}}w(t,\mathbf{x})=0

\displaystyle,\quad(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\Omega

(16a)

\displaystyle w(0,\mathbf{x})=w_{0}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega

(16b)

\displaystyle w(t,\mathbf{x})=u(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle,\quad(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\Gamma^{-}.

(16c)

Here, the advection velocities are assumed to be
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
a_{k}\neq 0
. Note that boundary conditions can only be imposed on the inflow boundary
\Gamma^{-}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\,:\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})<0\}
where
\mathbf{n}
denotes the unit outer normal direction
\mathbf{n}
to the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

To approximate (
16
) numerically, we perform in each time step a dimensional splitting where for each direction we solve a quasi-one dimensional problem. For this purpose, we discretize the intervals

[{\overline{a}}_{k},{\overline{b}}_{k}]
,
k=1,\ldots,d
, for the
k
th direction by

\displaystyle x_{j-1/2}^{k}={\overline{a}}_{k}+(j-1)\Delta x_{k},\ x_{j}^{k}=(x_{j-1/2}^{k}+x_{j+1/2}^{k})/2,\ \Delta x_{k}=({\overline{b}}_{k}-{\overline{a}}_{k})/M_{k},

\displaystyle I_{j}^{k}=(x_{j-1/2}^{k},x_{j+1/2}^{k}),\ j\in\{0,\ldots,M_{k}+1\}.

From this we determine the multi–dimensional discretization of the domain
\Omega

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}=(x_{j_{1}}^{1},\ldots,x_{j_{d}}^{d}),\ V_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}=({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$1$}}/2},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$1$}}/2})=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{d}I_{j}^{k},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{J}}^{+}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{d}\{0,\ldots,M_{k}+1\}

Note that
\Omega=\bigcup_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{J}}}V_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}
with
{\mathcal{J}}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{d}\{1,\ldots,M_{k}\}
. The elements corresponding to the set
{\mathcal{J}}^{+}\backslash{\mathcal{J}}
denote the ghost cells at the boundary.
In the
k
th direction the ghost cells are

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{k}_{L}=\{(l_{1},\ldots,l_{i-1},0,l_{i+1},\ldots,l_{d})\,:\,l_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,M_{j}\},\ j\neq k\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{k}_{R}=\{(l_{1},\ldots,l_{i-1},M_{i}+1,l_{i+1},\ldots,l_{d})\,:\,l_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,M_{j}\},\ j\neq k\}.

The bounded time interval
[0,T]
is discretized by
t_{n}=n\Delta t
,
n\in\{0,\dots,N\}
, with

N\Delta t=T
. The time step
\Delta t
is chosen such that the
\operatorname*{CFL}
condition

\displaystyle\max_{k=1,\ldots,d}\lambda_{k}\left\lvert a_{k}\right\rvert=\max_{k=1,\ldots,d}\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x_{k}}\left\lvert a_{k}\right\rvert\leq 1\qquad\text{for}\qquad\lambda_{k}=\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x_{k}}

(17)

holds.

In each direction
k=1,\ldots,d
we successively apply a linear 3–point finite volume scheme

\displaystyle w^{n,k}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}=w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}-\frac{a_{k}\lambda_{k}}{2}(w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}-w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}})+\frac{q_{k}}{2}(w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}-2w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}+w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}})

(18)

with boundary conditions at the left and right boundary in the
k
th direction

\displaystyle w^{n,k-1}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}=\begin{cases}u(t_{n},x_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}/2})\,,&a_{k}>0\\
w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}\,,&a_{k}<0\end{cases},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{k},

(19a)

\displaystyle w^{n,k-1}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}=\begin{cases}u(t_{n},x_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}/2})\,,&a_{k}<0\\
w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}\,,&a_{k}>0\end{cases},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{R}^{k}.

(19b)

Note that there is only flow in the
k
th direction but no flow in the other directions. Thus, the update along all the lines in the
k
th direction are independent of each other.
Essentially all linear 3–point finite volume schemes in 1D can be written in the form
(
18
) only differing in the choice of the numerical viscosity coefficient
q_{k}
.
In particular, if the lines are unbounded the scheme
is
l_{2}
–stable if the numerical viscosity coefficient is chosen as

\displaystyle(\lambda_{k}a_{k})^{2}\leq q_{k}\leq 1.

For
q_{k}=1
it corresponds to the Lax–Friedrichs scheme. In general it is of first order except for
q_{k}=(\lambda_{k}a_{k})^{2}
where it is of 2nd order corresponding to the Lax-Wendroff scheme, see
[
25
]
.

We emphasize that the
k^{th}
intermediate step does not correspond to the intermediate time
t_{n+(k-1)/d}
because in each step we perform a full time step
\Delta t
instead of
\Delta t/d
.
This becomes important in equation (
24
).
Here,
w^{n,0}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}=w^{n}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}
and
w^{n,d}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}=w^{n+1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}
correspond to
t_{n}

and
t_{n+1}
, respectively.
The initial condition
w_{0}
is approximated in each cell by the average over this cell, i.e.

w_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}^{0}=\frac{1}{|V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}|}\int_{V_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}}w_{0}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})\>d{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}\quad\text{for}\quad{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{J}}.

(20)

The Lyapunov function (
2
) is discretized at time
t_{n}
and
at
the
intermediate stages of the dimensionally splitted scheme
by means of the midpoint rule, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n}:=\sum_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{J}}}\left(w_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}|V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}|,\quad\mathcal{L}^{n,k}:=\sum_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{J}}}\left(w_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}^{n,k}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}|V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}|,\ k=1,\ldots,d,

(21)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}:=\operatorname*{exp}(\mu({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}))
and
|V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}|=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\Delta x_{k}=|V|
.
Note that
\mathcal{L}^{n}=\mathcal{L}^{n,0}
and
\mathcal{L}^{n+1}=\mathcal{L}^{n,d}
.

For the discrete Lyapunov function we can verify the following result.

Theorem 2.7

(Decay of the discrete Lyapunov function in multi–dimension)
.

Let
\left(w_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}^{n}\right)_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{J}}^{+},n=0,\dots,N}
be a bounded approximate solution to
IBVP (
16
)
computed by the numerical scheme (
18
) with boundary data (
19
) and initial data (
20
)
for some
\Delta t>0
which satisfies

\displaystyle\max_{k=1,\ldots,d}\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x_{k}}\left\lvert a_{k}\right\rvert\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\left(1-C_{L}\Delta t\right)>0.

(22)

The discrete Lyapunov function is given by (
21
)
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}:=\operatorname*{exp}(\mu({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}})),\quad\mu({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mu_{k}(x_{k})

(23)

where we assume that there exist functions
\mu_{k}\in H^{s}(\Omega)
,
s\geq 1+\frac{d}{2}
,
satisfying

\displaystyle a_{k}\mu_{k}^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{C_{L}}{d},\quad k=1,\ldots,K

(24)

for some constant value
C_{L}>0
.
The boundary values (
19
) corresponding to the boundary conditions (
16c
) are for all directions
k=1,\ldots,d
and all time steps
n=0,\ldots,N

\displaystyle w^{n,k-1}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}=\begin{cases}u^{n,k}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}\,,&a_{k}>0\\
w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}\,,&a_{k}<0\end{cases},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{k},\quad w^{n,k-1}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}=\begin{cases}u^{n,k}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}\,,&a_{k}<0\\
w^{n,k-1}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}\,,&a_{k}>0\end{cases},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{R}^{k},

(25)

where
u^{n,k}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a_{k}\left(u^{n,k}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}+\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}}{2}\leq a_{k}\left(w_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+(M_{k}+1){\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}^{n}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+M_{k}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}+\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+(M_{k}+1){\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}}{2}\,,&a_{k}>0,\ {\mbox{\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{k}\\
a_{k}\left(u^{n,k}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}}{2}\geq a_{k}\left(w_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-(M_{k}+1){\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}^{n}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-(M_{k}+1){\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}+\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-M_{k}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}}{2}\,,&a_{k}<0,\ {\mbox{\boldmath$j$}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{R}^{k}\end{cases}.

(26)

Then the discrete Lyapunov function is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n}\leq\operatorname*{exp}(-C_{L}n\Delta t)\mathcal{L}^{0}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\operatorname*{exp}(-C_{L}(k-1+(i-1)d)\Delta t/d)\mathcal{R}^{n-i,d-k}

(27)

for a residual

\mathcal{R}^{n,k}=\mathcal{R}^{n,k}(\Delta t,\Delta x_{k};a_{k},q_{k})
,
k=1,\ldots,d
,
defined in (
64
).

Some remarks are in order.

Assumption on the function
\mu
.

Note that the condition on

\mu(\mathbf{x})
in (
23
) is only needed due to
the dimensional splitting.

Comparison of the decay for the discrete and continuous Lyapunov functions
.
Comparing the estimate (
27
) of the discrete Lyapunov function corresponding to the discretization of the inviscid feedback control problem and
the estimates (
13
) and (
8
) for the continuous Lyapunov function for the viscous and the inviscid feedback control problems, see
IBVP (
9
)
and
(
1
), respectively,
we note that
(i) the exponential decay rate is the same and
(ii) the numerical viscosity in the discretization causes a
residual term in the discrete decay that is an approximation of the continuous residual of the viscous problem.

Boundedness of discrete data.

The boundedness of the discrete solution
\left(w_{i}^{n}\right)_{i=0,\dots,M+1}^{n=0,\dots,N}
follows from the fact that the numerical flux is monotone if
the CFL condition (
17
) holds. This holds provided the initial data and the boundary data given by the Dirichlet boundary condition are bounded. In this case the data at the new time level
t_{n+1}
are bounded by the minimum and the maximum of the cell data and the boundary data at the old time level
t_{n}
, see
[
25
]
.

Consistency of control.

Note the similarity of the constraints on the control in the discrete condition (
26
) and the continuous condition (
12
).
In the discrete case the mean values
(\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}}+\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}+{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}})/2
and
(\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-(M_{k}+1){\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}}+\mathcal{E}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$j$}}-M_{k}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}_{k}})/2
approximate the point values
\mathcal{E}(\mu({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{L}^{k}))
and
\mathcal{E}(\mu({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{R}^{k}))
.
Furthermore, we note that
(
23
) and (
24
) imply
(
11
).

Consistency of decay rate.

The estimate (
27
) reveals that numerical viscosity has no influence on the decay rate of the discrete Lyapunov function (
21
). This is consistent to the decay rate of the exact Lyapunov (
2
) in the estimate (
8
).
Instead, the numerical viscosity causes a perturbation in the decay of the discrete Lyapunov function that is similar to the perturbation in the decay of the exact Lyapunov function for the diffusive
IBVP (
9
). In
[
31
]
consistency of the discrete estimate (
27
)
and the continuous estimates (
8
) (no diffusion) and (
13
) (with diffusion) is verified in the 1D case. The
discrete perturbation vanishes for
\Delta x\to 0
,
\lambda=const
, i.e.,
{\overline{q}}=0
in
IBVP (
9
), whereas the discrete perturbation is consistent with perturbation in the diffusive case for
\Delta x\to
,
\lambda/\Delta x=const
, if
{\overline{q}}=1/2q\lambda/\Delta x
, i.e., the viscosity coefficient
{\overline{q}}
is proportional
to the numerical viscosity coefficient
q
.

Accuracy of discretization and accuracy of decay.

As confirmed
by numerical investigations in Section
4.2
a higher order discretization of the PDE does not necessarily lead to a higher order approximation of the exact rate

L(0)\operatorname*{exp}(-C_{L}t)
in Theorem
2.3
. However, a higher order approximation introduces less numerical viscosity affecting the perturbation term in the estimate of the decay (
21
) of the discrete Lyapunov function (
21
). Typically this results in a decay
that is closer to the exact decay.

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.7

We now prove Theorem
2.7
in two steps. In Sect.
3.1
we first consider the one–dimensional case. This result is extended to the multi–dimensional case in Sect.
3.2
using a dimensional splitting argument.

3.1.
Setting and one–dimensional analysis

For ease of representation we state
the problem, its discretization and the theorem for the one–dimensional case.

Problem

(One–dimensional scalar IBVP)
.

For a given bounded domain
\Omega=[{\overline{a}},{\overline{b}}]\subset\mathbb{R}

find a solution

w:[0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

to the IBVP

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w(t,x)+a\partial_{x}w(t,x)=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\Omega

(28a)

\displaystyle w(0,x)=w_{0}(x),\quad x\in\Omega

(28b)

\displaystyle w(t,x)=u(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\Gamma^{-}

(28c)

with non-vanishing advection velocity
a\neq 0
and boundary condition only imposed on the inflow boundary
\Gamma^{-}=\{{\overline{a}}\}
or
\Gamma^{-}=\{{\overline{b}}\}
if
a>0
and
a<0
, respectively.

This problem is approximated on a uniform discretization for the interval
\Omega=[{\overline{a}},{\overline{b}}]
by the elements

\displaystyle x_{j-1/2}={\overline{a}}+(j-1)\Delta x,\ x_{j}=(x_{j-1/2}+x_{j+1/2})/2,\ \Delta x=({\overline{b}}-{\overline{a}})/M,

\displaystyle I_{j}=(x_{j-1/2},x_{j+1/2}),\ j\in{\mathcal{J}}^{+}:=\{0,\ldots,M+1\}.

Note that
\Omega=\bigcup_{j\in{\mathcal{J}}}V_{j}
with
{\mathcal{J}}=\{1,\ldots,M\}
. The elements corresponding to the set
{\mathcal{J}}^{+}\backslash{\mathcal{J}}=\{0,M+1\}
denote the ghost cells at the boundary.
The bounded time interval
[0,T]
is discretized by
t_{n}=n\Delta t
,
n\in\{0,\dots,N\}
, with

N\Delta t=T
. The time step
\Delta t
is chosen such that the
\operatorname*{CFL}
condition

\displaystyle\lambda\left\lvert a\right\rvert=\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left\lvert a\right\rvert\leq 1\qquad\text{for}\qquad\lambda=\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}

holds.

In the 1D case a general
3–point finite volume scheme (
18
) in viscous form, see
[
25
]
, Chap. 3.3, reads

\displaystyle w^{n_{+}1}_{j}=w^{n}_{j}-\frac{a\lambda}{2}(w^{n}_{j+1}-w^{n}_{j-1})+\frac{q}{2}(w^{n}_{j-1}-2w^{n}_{j}+w^{n}_{j+1})

(29)

with Courant number
\lambda a
and numerical viscosity coefficient
q
.
The boundary conditions at the left and right boundary given by (
19
) are determined by

\displaystyle w^{n}_{0}=\begin{cases}u(t_{n},x_{1/2})\,,&a>0\\
w^{n}_{1}\,,&a<0\end{cases},\ w^{n}_{M+1}=\begin{cases}u(t_{n},x_{M+1/2})\,,&a<0\\
w^{n}_{M}\,,&a>0\end{cases}.

(30)

The initial condition
w_{0}
is approximated in each cell by the average over this cell, i.e.

\displaystyle w_{j}^{0}=\frac{1}{|V_{j}|}\int_{V_{j}}w_{0}(x)\>dx\quad\text{for}\quad j\in{\mathcal{J}}.

(31)

We control the solution at the inflow boundary and observe at the
outflow boundary. We apply
a discrete boundary feedback control
u^{n}=u^{n}\!\left(w_{M+1}^{n}\right)
and
u^{n}=u^{n}\!\left(w_{0}^{n}\right)
if
a>0
and
a<0
, respectively, by setting
w_{0}^{n}:=u^{n}
and
w_{M+1}^{n}:=u^{n}
in the left (right) ghost cell and extend the solution constantly at the right (left) ghost cell by setting
w_{M+1}^{n}=w_{M}^{n}
and
w_{0}^{n}=w_{1}^{n}
, respectively.
Thus, the control enters the flow field by

\begin{cases}w_{1}^{n+1}=w_{1}^{n}-\frac{\lambda a}{2}\left(w_{2}^{n}-u^{n}\right)+\frac{q}{2}\left(w_{2}^{n}-2w_{1}^{n}+u^{n}\right)\,,&a>0\\
w_{M}^{n+1}=w_{M}^{n}-\frac{\lambda a}{2}\left(u^{n}-w_{M-1}^{n}\right)+\frac{q}{2}\left(u^{n}-2w_{M}^{n}+w_{M-1}^{n}\right)\,,&a<0\end{cases}.

(32)

At the outflow boundary we have

\begin{cases}w_{M}^{n+1}=w_{M}^{n}-\frac{\lambda a}{2}\left(w_{M}^{n}-w_{M-1}^{n}\right)+\frac{q}{2}\left(w_{M}^{n}-2w_{M}^{n}+w_{M-1}^{n}\right)\,,&a>0\\
w_{1}^{n+1}=w_{1}^{n}-\frac{\lambda a}{2}\left(w_{2}^{n}-w_{1}^{n}\right)+\frac{q}{2}\left(w_{2}^{n}-2w_{1}^{n}+w_{1}^{n}\right)\,,&a<0\end{cases}

(33)

and then we set

\begin{cases}w_{M+1}^{n+1}=w_{M}^{n+1}\,,&a>0\\
w_{0}^{n+1}=w_{1}^{n+1}\,,&a<0\end{cases}.

(34)

The Lyapunov function (
2
) is discretized at time
t_{n}
by means of the midpoint rule, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n}:=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{J}}}\left(w_{j}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{j}\Delta x,

(35)

where

\mathcal{E}_{j}:=\operatorname*{exp}(\mu(x_{j}))
.
If
w
is continuous, then
\mathcal{L}^{n}
is a second order approximation of
L(t_{n})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle L(t_{n})=\mathcal{L}^{n}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{2}).

3.1.1.
Decay rate of the discrete Lyapunov function

With the one–dimensional setting described above
the decay of the discrete Lyapunov function can be estimated by the following theorem that is an analogue to Theorem
2.7
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\left(w_{i}^{n}\right)_{i=0,\dots,M+1}^{n=0,\dots,N}
be a bounded discrete solution to
IBVP (
28
)
computed by the numerical scheme (
29
)-(
34
)
for some
\Delta t>0
which satisfies

\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left\lvert a\right\rvert\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\left(1-C_{L}\Delta t\right)>0.

(36)

The discrete Lyapunov function is given by (
35
)
with
\mathcal{E}_{j}:=\operatorname*{exp}(\mu(x_{j}))

where we assume that there exists a function
\mu(x)\in H^{s}([{\overline{a}},{\overline{b}}])
,
s\geq\frac{3}{2}
. satisfying

a\mu_{x}(x)=-C_{L}

(37)

for some constant value
C_{L}>0
.
The boundary condition for (
28c
) is realized by setting

\begin{cases}w_{0}^{n}=u^{n}\,,&a>0\\
w_{M+1}^{n}=u^{n}\,,&a<0\end{cases}\quad\text{for }n\in\{0,\dots,N\},

(38)

where
u^{n}
satisfies

\begin{cases}a\left(u^{n}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}}{2}\leq a\left(w_{M+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{M}+\mathcal{E}_{M+1}}{2}\,,&a>0\\
a\left(u^{n}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{M}+\mathcal{E}_{M+1}}{2}\geq a\left(w_{0}^{n}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}}{2}\,,&a<0\end{cases}.

(39)

Then the discrete Lyapunov function is bounded by

\mathcal{L}^{n}\leq\operatorname*{exp}(-C_{L}n\Delta t)\mathcal{L}^{0}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname*{exp}(-C_{L}(i-1)\Delta t)\mathcal{R}^{n-i}

(40)

for a residual
\mathcal{R}^{n}=\mathcal{R}^{n}(\Delta t,\Delta x;a,q)
.

Proof.

For the proof we mimic the steps in the proof of Lemma
2.5
for the partial differential equation with diffusion in the discrete case.
For better readability, we divide the proof into four parts.

Part I.
For some fixed
n\geq 0
the discrete derivative of the Lyapunov function (
35
) is given by

\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n+1}-\mathcal{L}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\left(w_{i}^{n+1}\right)^{2}-\left(w_{i}^{n}\right)^{2}}{\Delta t}\mathcal{E}_{i}\Delta x=-\sum_{i=1}^{M}2aw_{i}^{n}\frac{w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}}{2\Delta x}\mathcal{E}_{i}\Delta x+R_{1}^{n}(\lambda,a,q)

(41)

with residual

\displaystyle R_{1}^{n}(\lambda,a,q)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{M}qw_{i}^{n}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-2w_{i}^{n}+w_{i-1}^{n}\right)\mathcal{E}_{i}

(42)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(-\frac{\lambda a}{2}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}\right)+\frac{q}{2}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-2w_{i}^{n}+w_{i-1}^{n}\right)\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i}.

Here we make use of (
29
) that provides us with

\displaystyle\left(w_{i}^{n+1}\right)^{2}-\left(w_{i}^{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\lambda aw_{i}^{n}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}\right)+qw_{i}^{n}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-2w_{i}^{n}+w_{i-1}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(-\frac{\lambda a}{2}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}\right)+\frac{q}{2}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-2w_{i}^{n}+w_{i-1}^{n}\right)\right)^{2}.

Part II.
Inspired by the proof of Lemma
2.5
we derive a discrete analogue to the continuous derivative
\left(aw^{2}\mathcal{E}\right)_{x}
.
For this purpose, we replace this derivative by the central difference

\mathcal{D}_{i}:=\frac{a\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i+1}-a\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i-1}}{2\Delta x}.

(43)

This term can be rewritten as

\mathcal{D}_{i}=aw_{i+1}^{n}\frac{w_{i+1}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{i+1}-w_{i}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{i}}{2\Delta x}+aw_{i}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{i}\frac{w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}}{2\Delta x}+aw_{i-1}^{n}\frac{w_{i}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{i}-w_{i-1}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{i-1}}{2\Delta x}.

(44)

Replacing
w_{i+1}^{n}=w_{i}^{n}+\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i}^{n}\right)
and
w_{i-1}^{n}=w_{i}^{n}+\left(w_{i-1}^{n}-w_{i}^{n}\right)
in the first and third term we obtain after some rearrangement

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{i}

\displaystyle=a\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{i+1}-\mathcal{E}_{i}}{2\Delta x}+a\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{i}-\mathcal{E}_{i-1}}{2\Delta x}+2aw_{i}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{i}\frac{w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}}{2\Delta x}

\displaystyle+a\mathcal{E}_{i}\frac{\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i}^{n}\right)^{2}}{2\Delta x}-a\mathcal{E}_{i}\frac{\left(w_{i}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}}{2\Delta x}.

Summation over all cells and using (
43
) yields

\sum_{i=1}^{M}2aw_{i}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{i}\frac{w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}}{2\Delta x}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{a\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i+1}-a\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i-1}}{2\Delta x}-\frac{1}{\Delta x}R_{\mathcal{E}}^{n}(a)-\frac{1}{\Delta x}R_{2}^{n}(a)

(45)

with residuals

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{E}}^{n}(a)

\displaystyle:=\frac{a}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{i+1}-\mathcal{E}_{i}\right)+\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}-\mathcal{E}_{i-1}\right)\right),

(46)

\displaystyle R_{2}^{n}(a)

\displaystyle:=\frac{a}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{E}_{i}\left(\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i}^{n}\right)^{2}-\left(w_{i}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}\right).

(47)

Inserting (
45
) in (
41
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n+1}-\mathcal{L}^{n}}{\Delta t}=

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}a\mathcal{E}_{i+1}-\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}a\mathcal{E}_{i-1}}{2\Delta x}-\frac{1}{\Delta x}R_{\mathcal{E}}^{n}(a)-\frac{1}{\Delta x}R_{2}^{n}(a)\right)\Delta x+R_{1}^{n}(\Delta t,\Delta x;a,q).

By an index shift and the boundary conditions (
38
) the sum reduces to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}a\mathcal{E}_{i+1}-\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}a\mathcal{E}_{i-1}}{2\Delta x}\Delta x=

\displaystyle\frac{a}{2}\left(-\left(w_{0}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{0}-\left(w_{1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{1}+\left(w_{M}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{M}+\left(w_{M+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{M+1}\right)=

\displaystyle-\frac{a}{2}\begin{cases}\left(u^{n}\right)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}\right)+\left(\left(w_{1}^{n}\right)^{2}-\left(u^{n}\right)^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{1}-\left(w_{M+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{M}+\mathcal{E}_{M+1}\right)\,,&a>0\\
\left(w_{0}^{n}\right)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}\right)+\left(\left(u^{n}\right)^{2}-\left(w_{M}^{n}\right)^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{M}-\left(u^{n}\right)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{M}+\mathcal{E}_{M+1}\right)\,,&a<0\end{cases}.

The constraints (
39
) on the control
u^{n}
provides the inequality

-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}a\mathcal{E}_{i+1}-\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}a\mathcal{E}_{i-1}}{2\Delta x}\Delta x\leq R_{u}^{n}(a),

where the residual on the right-hand side is determined by

R_{u}^{n}(a)=\frac{a}{2}\begin{cases}\left(\left(w_{1}^{n}\right)^{2}-\left(u^{n}\right)^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{1}\,,&a>0\\
\left(\left(u^{n}\right)^{2}-\left(w_{M}^{n}\right)^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}_{M}\,,&a<0\end{cases}.

(48)

Finally, the discrete time derivative of the discrete Lyapunov function can be estimated by

\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n+1}-\mathcal{L}^{n}}{\Delta t}\leq R_{\mathcal{E}}^{n}(a)+R_{u}^{n}(a)+R_{2}^{n}(a)+R_{1}^{n}(\Delta t,\Delta x;a,q).

(49)

Part III.
Now we consider the term
R_{\mathcal{E}}^{n}(a)
given by (
46
).
We start by replacing the forward and the backward difference of
\mathcal{E}_{i}=\operatorname*{exp}(\mu(x_{i}))
by the first derivative of the function
\mathcal{E}(x):=\mathcal{E}(\mu(x))=\operatorname*{exp}(\mu(x))
. By Taylor expansion we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i+1}-\mathcal{E}_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i+1}^{\prime}\Delta x-\mathcal{E}_{i+1}^{\prime\prime}\frac{\Delta x^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi_{i}^{+})\frac{\Delta x^{3}}{6},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}-\mathcal{E}_{i-1}=\mathcal{E}_{i-1}^{\prime}\Delta x+\mathcal{E}_{i-1}^{\prime\prime}\frac{\Delta x^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi_{i}^{-})\frac{\Delta x^{3}}{6}

for
\xi_{i}^{+}\in[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
and
\xi_{i}^{-}\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
, where we use the notation
\mathcal{E}_{j}^{(k)}=\mathcal{E}^{(k)}(x_{j})
.
Inserting these in (
46
) gives

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{E}}^{n}(a)=R_{\mathcal{E},0}^{n}(a)\Delta x+R_{\mathcal{E},1}^{n}(a)\Delta x^{2}+R_{\mathcal{E},2}^{n}(a)\Delta x^{3}

(50)

with the three terms

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{E},0}^{n}(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{a}{2}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i+1}^{\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{a}{2}\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i-1}^{\prime},

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{E},1}^{n}(a)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{a}{4}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i+1}^{\prime\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{a}{4}\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i-1}^{\prime\prime},

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{E},2}^{n}(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{a}{12}\left(\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi_{i}^{+})+\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi_{i}^{-})\right).

In the following we rewrite these terms. We start by considering the first term.
By using the derivative

\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\prime}=\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname*{exp}(\mu(x))\big|_{x_{j}}=\mu_{x}(x_{j})\operatorname*{exp}(\mu(x_{j}))\overset{(\ref{discr_Bed_mu})}{=}-\frac{C_{L}}{a}\mathcal{E}_{j}

(51)

and performing an index shift we obtain with the definition of the discrete Lyapunov function (
35
)

R_{\mathcal{E},0}^{n}(a)\Delta x=-C_{L}\mathcal{L}^{n}+R_{0}^{n}\Delta x

(52)

with the residual

R_{0}^{n}:=-\frac{1}{2}C_{L}\left(\left(w_{0}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{0}-\left(w_{1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{1}-\left(w_{M}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{M}+\left(w_{M+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{M+1}\right).

(53)

For the second term we proceed similarly.
Using

\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\prime\prime}=\left(\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}\overset{(\ref{der_E_j})}{=}\left(-\frac{C_{L}}{a}\mathcal{E}_{j}\right)^{\prime}=-\frac{C_{L}}{a}\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\prime}\overset{(\ref{der_E_j})}{=}\left(\frac{C_{L}}{a}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{j},

(54)

and performing an index shift we get

R_{\mathcal{E},1}^{n}(a)=\frac{C_{L}^{2}}{4a}\left(\left(w_{0}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{0}+\left(w_{1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{1}-\left(w_{M}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{M}-\left(w_{M+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}_{M+1}\right).

(55)

In the third term we replace the third derivate using (
51
) and (
54
) by

\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=\left(\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(x)\right)^{\prime}=\left(\frac{C_{L}}{a}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x)=-\left(\frac{C_{L}}{a}\right)^{3}\mathcal{E}(x)

to obtain

R_{\mathcal{E},2}^{n}(a)=-\frac{1}{12}\frac{C_{L}^{3}}{a^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\left(w_{i+1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}(\xi_{i}^{+})+\left(w_{i-1}^{n}\right)^{2}\mathcal{E}(\xi_{i}^{-})\right).

(56)

Combining (
49
), (
50
) and (

) gives the decay

\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n+1}-\mathcal{L}^{n}}{\Delta t}\leq-C_{L}\mathcal{L}^{n}+\mathcal{R}^{n}

(57)

with the residual at time
t_{n}
given by

\mathcal{R}^{n}:=R_{0}^{n}\Delta x+R_{\mathcal{E},1}^{n}(a)\Delta x^{2}+R_{\mathcal{E},2}^{n}(a)\Delta x^{3}+R_{u}^{n}(a)+R_{2}^{n}(a)+R_{1}^{n}(\lambda,a,q),

(58)

where
R_{0}^{n}
is defined by (
53
),
R_{\mathcal{E},1}^{n}(a)
by (
55
),
R_{\mathcal{E},2}^{n}(a)
by (
56
),
R_{u}^{n}(a)
by (
48
),
R_{2}^{n}(a)
defined by (

) is simplified to

R_{2}^{n}(a)=\frac{a}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{E}_{i}\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-w_{i-1}^{n}\right)\left(w_{i+1}^{n}-2w_{i}^{n}+w_{i-1}^{n}\right)

(59)

and

Part IV.

Finally, we conclude from (

\mathcal{L}^{n}\leq(1-C_{L}\Delta t)\mathcal{L}^{n-1}+\Delta t\mathcal{R}^{n-1}.

Since this inequality holds for all

(60)

Since

4.2.
Multi–d stabilization using higher order schemes

To perform higher order computations in the multi–dimensional case we adapted the in–house C++–library
MultiWave
to the
IBVP (
1
).
This library provides a multi–dimensional high order Runge–Kutta discontinuous Galerkin scheme with grid adaptation that can be applied for solving hyperbolic balance laws, see
[
36
]
.

Solver.
We briefly summarize the main ingredients of
MultiWave
.
Here we apply a
P^{th}
–order DG scheme using piecewise polynomial elements of order
P=1,2,3

and a
P^{th}
–order explicit SSP–Runge–Kutta method with
P
stages for the time discretization.
For a numerical flux we choose the local Lax-Friedrichs flux that for our linear fluxes coincides with the classical Lax–Friedrichs flux.
Thus, in case of
P=1
the scheme reduces to a 3-point scheme as (
18
).
The Gibbs phenomena near to discontinuities is suppressed by the minmod limiter from
[
3
]

for
P\geq 2
.
Because of the explicit time discretization we restrict the
time step by means of a CFL condition.
The efficiency of the scheme is improved by local grid adaption where we employ the
multiresolution concept based on multiwavelets.
The key idea is to perform a multiresolution analysis on a sequence of
nested grids providing a decomposition of the data on a coarse scale and a sequence
of details that encode the difference of approximations on
subsequent resolution levels. The detail coefficients become small when the underlying
data are locally smooth and, hence, can be discarded when dropping below a level–dependent
threshold value
\varepsilon_{l}=2^{l-\overline{L}}c_{thresh}h_{\overline{L}}
where
\overline{L}
denotes the largest refinement level and
h_{\overline{L}}
the smallest cell diameter on this level. The parameter
c_{thresh}
is chosen proportional to the smallest amplitude in the initial data.
By means of the thresholded sequence a new, locally refined grid
is determined.
Details can be found in

[
32
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
.

For all examples, we set the CFL number to
CFL=0.7
. The
cell size at the coarsest refinement level is

\Delta\mathbf{x}_{0}=(1/3,1/3)
.
Due to the dyadic grid hierarchy in
MultiWave
the cell size at refinement level
l
is
\Delta\mathbf{x}_{l}=2^{-l}\Delta\mathbf{x}_{0}
, for
l=0,\dots,\overline{L}
.

Configuration.

In order to have
a direct comparison to the results reported in
[
40
]
, we investigate a configuration from this reference.
This allows to verify the implementation of the boundary feedback control.

For the bounded domain
\Omega=(0,1)\times(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
we consider the IBVP (
1

5.
Outlook

We investigated the influence of numerical dissipation on the decay of the Lyapunov function. For this purpose, we analyzed the stabilization problem using boundary feedback control with viscosity for the solution of a linear, parabolic multi–dimensional scalar problem. In contrast to the inviscid problem the decay of the Lyapunov function cannot be estimated only by an exponentially decaying term but by an additional integral term accounting for viscosity.

Since numerical dissipation of any numerical scheme acts similar to physical viscosity, it has been long suspected that numerical dissipation will trigger a similar term in the estimate of the decay of the discrete Lyapunov function. This has now been confirmed and quantified by general finite volume schemes applied to the inviscid feedback control problem.
Results in the one-dimensional problem have been given as well as for the multi-dimensional case.

Finally, the analytical results have been confirmed by numerical computations for the one-dimensional and two-dimensional inviscid feedback control problem.
The main findings are that numerical dissipation leads to a faster decay of the discrete Lyapunov function in comparison with the decay of the theoretical Lyapunov function.
This becomes most evident for first-order schemes. Increasing the order of the numerical scheme decreases the numerical dissipation and, the decay of the discrete Lyapunov function converges to the decay rate of the exact Lyapunov function.
