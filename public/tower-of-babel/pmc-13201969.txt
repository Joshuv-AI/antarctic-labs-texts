Bilateral persistence of the proatlantal intersegmental arteries in moyamoya syndrome: illustrative case

Fetal carotid-vertebrobasilar anastomoses normally regress during embryonic development; however, persistence beyond this period has been reported and has clinical significance. One such connection is the proatlantal artery, a rare conduit with two subtypes.1 Type I is the persistent proatlantal intersegmental artery (PPIA) arising from the internal carotid artery (ICA), and type II is the C1 intersegmental artery branching from the external carotid/occipital artery. Both enter the posterior fossa via the foramen magnum to join the vertebral artery.1,2 In moyamoya syndrome (MMS)—a steno-occlusive arteriopathy of the distal ICA—coexisting PPIA may materially shape anterior-posterior collateralization and procedural strategy, including superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery bypass planning and endovascular access. While cases of unilateral and bilateral PPIA have been described,1,3–5 we present bilateral PPIA occurring with bilateral MMS. The coexistence holds clinical relevance as the embryonic vascular pathway can provide collateral flow bypassing stenosis of the distal ICA. This report explores the clinical implications of this concurrence.

Illustrative Case

Presentation

A 28-year-old woman with Down syndrome presented to an outside hospital with sudden severe headache followed by unresponsiveness. Noncontrast head CT demonstrated large bilateral intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH).

The patient was transferred for neurosurgical care. On arrival, the patient was intubated and sedated, and her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 6. Urgent management included bedside navigated external ventricular drain placement and a stat CT angiography study with/without contrast, which showed large bilateral IVH with complete opacification of the left lateral ventricle, obstructive hydrocephalus, and a small left periatrial parenchymal hemorrhage (Fig. 1). Imaging

CT angiography demonstrated diffusely small intracranial ICAs with marked narrowing and poor opacification of the proximal anterior cerebral arteries and middle cerebral artery branches, concerning for MMS. Digital subtraction angiography showed bilateral distal ICA stenosis with relative preservation of the posterior circulation (Fig. 2). Arteries arising from each ICA coursed posteriorly through the foramen magnum to the vertebral arteries, consistent with bilateral PPIA (Fig. 3). On left subclavian injection, no left vertebral artery was identified (Fig. 4A). The right vertebral artery was rudimentary and was not directly catheterized due to its small caliber but demonstrated retrograde filling on ICA injection (Figs. 3D, 4B, and 4C). Overall, the posterior circulation opacified predominantly via bilateral PPIA, with retrograde filling of the V4 segments and basilar artery from carotid injections, and minimal antegrade vertebral contribution. Extensive leptomeningeal and meningeal collateral vessels connected anterior and posterior territories. A tortuous left posterior choroidal branch was noted without a definite aneurysm or treatable lesion. Given the hemorrhagic presentation and collateral burden, interval imaging surveillance was planned. Hospital Course

Subsequent transcranial Doppler studies demonstrated robust collateral flow through the proatlantal-vertebrobasilar pathway with elevated velocities in multiple territories, consistent with MMS physiology and compensatory hemodynamics. A follow-up cerebral angiography study again confirmed bilateral PPIA and redemonstrated the irregular left posterior choroidal branch suspicious for a small intraventricular aneurysm without a clear embolization target. The patient was weaned from CSF diversion and discharged on hospital day 19 after a course complicated by deep vein thrombosis (managed with inferior vena cava filter placement in the setting of recent hemorrhage), Clostridioides difficile colitis, and urinary tract infection.

Follow-Up

Cerebral angiography 47 days after discharge showed stable cervical and intracranial hemodynamics with advanced bilateral MMS, bilateral PPIA, and no recurrent abnormality of the previously concerning left posterior choroidal branch—now interpreted as a vascular loop with distal filling. At clinic follow-up nearly 3 months after discharge, the patient had returned to baseline functional status but had persistent right eyelid ptosis, and her caregiver reported difficulty maintaining oral hydration. Given stable serial angiograms and no treatable aneurysm, after discussion with the caregiver, an as needed follow-up plan was selected.

Informed Consent

The patient was obtunded and sedated at presentation and could not provide consent; she required emergency treatment/workup. Once stabilized, surrogate consent was obtained as the patient remained intubated.

Discussion

Observations

The primary observation in this case is hemorrhagic presentation of MMS in a young woman with Down syndrome, with bilateral PPIA serving as the dominant posterior inflow, extensive leptomeningeal and meningeal collateral vessels, and a posterior choroidal “aneurysm” later recognized as a vascular loop on follow-up. These findings are best interpreted in an embryological context.

In early embryogenesis (approximately 4- to 5-mm stage), transient carotid-vertebrobasilar anastomoses (hypoglossal, otic, trigeminal, and proatlantal) link the primitive carotid system to the developing posterior circulation.2,6 As the posterior communicating arteries mature, the first three typically involute, whereas the proatlantal arteries persist until formation of the vertebral arteries during the 7- to 12-mm stage.4,7 The vertebral arteries arise from longitudinal anastomoses among the cervical intersegmental arteries, beginning cranially with the proatlantal segment and descending caudally. With normal regression, the distal proatlantal segment contributes to the horizontal (C1) vertebral segment; failure of regression yields a persistent proatlantal artery.6,8

Persistence of one of these anastomoses into postnatal life is uncommon, and the proatlantal artery is one of the least common among the persistent carotid-vertebrobasilar anastomoses, with an incidence of approximately 0.020%.8 The bilateral persistence of this fetal anastomosis is exceedingly rare, and its incidence is not available due to sparse published data. Most cases are asymptomatic and detected incidentally, but recognition is important for procedural safety, stroke risk assessment, and endovascular/surgical planning.7,9 There are several reports of endovascular treatments via persistent fetal anastomoses, such as thrombectomy, angioplasty, and stenting.10–13

Our patient also had radiographic evidence of MMS, a progressive cerebrovascular disease characterized by progressive stenosis at the distal end of the ICA that is associated with Down syndrome.14 The most common clinical presentations for MMS include transient ischemic attack, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, seizures, headache, and cognitive impairment.15 Clinical presentation varies, but age-dependent patterns are well described. For example, the most common presentation among children is transient ischemic attack with seizures and intellectual disability also being common, while adults usually present with intraventricular or intercerebral hemorrhage.14–16 Rarely, MMS can be associated with the persistence of embryonal vasculature; for example, there are reports of persistent trigeminal artery or primitive PPIA.17–20 This is the first report of bilateral PPIA with MMS.

In addition to anatomical considerations, genetic susceptibility, particularly in the context of Down syndrome, may further contextualize this presentation. RNF213 is a principal susceptibility gene for moyamoya disease, with the p.R4810K variant conferring increased risk.21 Experimental studies suggest that the RNF213 protein participates in endothelial and vascular smooth muscle signaling pathways related to angiogenesis, inflammation, and nitric oxide–mediated vascular regulation, supporting a biological framework for interaction with systemic or genetic comorbidities.22,23 RNF213 variants have also been shown to be significantly associated with quasi-moyamoya phenotypes, including cases occurring in the setting of Down syndrome, indicating a modifier rather than causative role.24,25 Reviews of moyamoya genetics propose that molecules implicated in Down syndrome–related vascular biology may intersect with pathways associated with the RNF213 protein, potentially increasing susceptibility to MMS.

Lessons

The most important lesson from this case is that the persistence of fetal carotid-vertebrobasilar anastomoses necessitates meticulous angiographic mapping of each vessel’s origin, course, dominance, and collateral dependencies because this anatomy and its angiographic features can alter bypass targeting, dictate endovascular access routes, and create imaging pitfalls in high-flow beds. This is especially relevant when additional vascular pathology is present.

In MMS, distal ICA inflow is constrained.14,15 PPIA provides a preexisting carotid-vertebral conduit that can redistribute or supplement posterior circulation flow, altering baseline anterior-posterior collateralization. Persistence of the proatlantal artery could be associated with MMS as it uses the vessel to supply blood past the stenosed ICA vessel.17 On the other hand, this may contribute to stenosis as flow is shunted away from the distal ICA through the anastomosis. Depending on vessel calibers and resistance beds, this pathway may augment perfusion to posterior territories, participate in leptomeningeal cross-fill to anterior territories, or create competitive/steal dynamics that further depress distal ICA flow. These effects help explain our Doppler and angiographic observations. However, mechanistic inferences about hemodynamics are hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

In this case, stable vascular imaging and absence of a targetable hemorrhagic source supported conservative management. Established management principles for MMS remain applicable despite the presence of bilateral PPIA, with intervention typically guided by symptomatic status, evidence of hemodynamic compromise, and disease progression.14–16 If such anatomy were recognized prior to hemorrhage, management would similarly follow established moyamoya paradigms. Asymptomatic patients would warrant surveillance with serial imaging and clinical follow-up, whereas those with ischemic symptoms or evidence of impaired cerebrovascular reserve would be evaluated for revascularization. In this setting, physiological assessment of perfusion and reserve is particularly important, as dominant carotid-vertebrobasilar conduits may influence target selection and increase the risk of competitive graft flow. Thus, identification of bilateral PPIA would not fundamentally alter management thresholds but would necessitate careful hemodynamic mapping when operative intervention is considered.

Although the external carotid system was patent and superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery bypass would have been technically feasible, the patient had no recurrent ischemic symptoms and demonstrated robust collateralization, making the risk-benefit profile of surgical revascularization less favorable. Accordingly, management decisions were individualized to angiographic stability, clinical recovery, and established moyamoya best practices.

This case discusses the management of a patient that presented with extensive IVH, encephalopathy, and poor GCS score and addresses the possible impact a PPIA could have on patients with MMS. The occurrence of these vessels is rarely described in the literature, and even more infrequently described bilaterally or with other cerebrovascular pathology. Although PPIA is present from development and MMS is a progressive arteriopathy, this case highlights a hemodynamic interplay between the two. Further dedicated studies are necessary to determine the extent to which the embryonic vessels aid in bypassing stenosis caused by MMS. Limitations of our findings include its single-case nature and lack of long-term follow-up. However, this case underscores the importance of the awareness that neurointerventionalists and radiologists must harbor for these connections due to the possible clinical and procedural implications.
