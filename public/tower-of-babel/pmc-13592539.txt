The Effectiveness of a Prenatal Educational Programme on Fear of Childbirth Among Primigravida Women: A quasi-experimental pretest–post-test study
Authors: Mozah Al Mamari, Amal Al Hadi, Fahima Al Harthy, Shamsa Al Ghusaini, Kadhiya Al Riyami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:Fear of childbirth is commonly reported among primigravida women and may negatively influence labour and birth outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured prenatal educational programme in reducing fear of childbirth among primigravida women.Methods:A quasi-experimental single-group pretest-post-test design was conducted from January to June 2025. The study included primigravida women attending antenatal clinics in selected health centres in Muscat Governorate, Oman. No control group was included. Participants completed the baseline assessment during the third trimester (28–40 weeks of gestation) prior to the intervention. Post-intervention assessment was conducted at two weeks postpartum using the Self-Evaluation Questionnaire.Results:A total of 79 primigravida women were included. Following the intervention, the mean fear-of-childbirth score decreased from 4.35 ± 0.63 at baseline to 1.79 ± 0.74 at 2-week postpartum follow-up, representing a mean reduction of 2.56 points (95% CI: 2.38–2.74; t = 27.8; P <0.001; Cohen's d = 3.12), indicating a statistically significant improvement after participation in the prenatal educational programme.Conclusion:The structured prenatal educational programme was associated with a reduction of fear of childbirth among primigravida women. Incorporating such programmes into routine antenatal care may enhance maternal psychological well-being in Oman and in similar maternal health settings.

1. Introduction

Pregnancy and childbirth are natural physiological processes and major physical, emotional and social milestones in a woman's life.1 Fear of childbirth is nevertheless common and may adversely affect maternal well-being and birth outcomes, including prolonged labour, heightened pain perception and an increased likelihood of elective or instrumental delivery.23 Fear may also persist into the postpartum period and has been associated with delayed recovery, postpartum depression and higher levels of post-traumatic stress symptoms following childbirth.24

Structured antenatal education is an important strategy to support a positive pregnancy and childbirth experience. Evidence from systematic reviews and clinical studies suggests that such programmes may reduce fear of childbirth and improve maternal self-efficacy, with potential benefits for birth outcomes, including mode of delivery.5 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis has shown that antenatal education significantly reduces fear of childbirth and improves childbirth-related psychological outcomes.6 In addition, antenatal education programmes have demonstrated improvements in maternal confidence and childbirth self-efficacy in primiparous women.78 Furthermore, prenatal education has been shown to enhance maternal understanding of pregnancy and childbirth-related topics and support better preparedness for delivery through structured educational interventions.15 However, evidence on the effect of antenatal education on fear of childbirth remains inconsistent. A Cochrane systematic review reported mixed findings, with considerable variability in intervention content, timing and outcome measures across studies, limiting the certainty of conclusions.9 This highlights the need for further evaluation of structured prenatal education programmes in different healthcare contexts.

In Oman, structured antenatal education is largely confined to two tertiary referral institutions in Muscat. Most women, however, receive routine antenatal care in primary healthcare centres, where structured education is not typically provided. As a result, many women may have limited access to organised preparation for childbirth and the postpartum period, particularly in relation to labour and birth, postpartum care and newborn care.10 Furthermore, there is limited local evidence regarding the effectiveness of structured antenatal education in addressing fear of childbirth among Omani primigravida women. Generating such evidence is important to inform clinical practice and strengthen woman-centred antenatal care within primary healthcare settings. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured prenatal educational programme in reducing fear of childbirth among primigravida women. The null hypothesis was that there would be no difference in fear-of-childbirth scores before and after participation in the programme, whereas the alternative hypothesis was that participation in the programme would result in a reduction in fear-of-childbirth scores.

2. Methods

This study employed a single-group pre-test–post-test design to compare fear-of-childbirth scores before and after a structured prenatal educational programme among primigravida women. A quasi-experimental pre-post design was considered appropriate given the need to evaluate this intervention in a real-world primary healthcare setting, where randomisation and control conditions are often not feasible. The study was structured using the PICO framework. The population (P) comprised primigravida women attending four antenatal clinics at selected primary healthcare centres in Muscat Governorate, Oman, from January–June 2025. The intervention (I) was a structured prenatal educational programme. There was no comparison group (C), as the study used a quasi-experimental pre–post design. The outcome (O) was fear of childbirth among primigravida women. This design is commonly applied in health professions education and clinical intervention research to assess change following educational programmes in real-world settings where randomisation is not feasible or may disrupt routine care delivery.11

A purposive sampling approach was used to recruit primigravida women with singleton pregnancies. Participants were included if they were willing to take part in the structured prenatal educational programme and able to complete the study procedures and assessments. Four participants were recruited before the third trimester to facilitate recruitment and ensure achievement of the required sample size. However, all baseline assessments of fear of childbirth and the intervention were conducted when participants reached the third trimester (28–40 weeks’ gestation). Women with medical or obstetric complications were excluded. Purposive sampling was used to ensure recruitment of participants who met the eligibility criteria and were expected to benefit from the intervention.

Sample size was calculated using PASS, Version 24.0.2 (NCSS Statistical Software, Kaysville, Utah, USA) for a paired t-test, assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen's d ≈ 0.5) as supported by evidence of moderate effects of antenatal education reported in the literature.12 With a 5% significance level, 80% power and a 20% attrition rate, the minimum required sample was 60 participants.

The intervention comprised a structured prenatal educational programme delivered by trained healthcare professionals through group workshops and individual counselling. The programme included two sessions, each lasting approximately 60–90 minutes. Content covered stages of labour, pain management strategies, relaxation and breathing techniques, psychological preparation for childbirth and postpartum care. Sessions were delivered face-to-face and supported by videos, visual aids and anatomical models. Educational materials were developed using validated medical resources.

Fear of childbirth was assessed at baseline (third trimester) and at 2-week postpartum follow-up using the Structured Evaluation Questionnaire (SEQ). The SEQ is a researcher-developed instrument consisting of four sections: socio-demographic characteristics (age, educational level, employment status, marital status and gestational age); fear-of-childbirth items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree); prior exposure to prenatal educational programmes; and perceived effectiveness of the current intervention in reducing fear of childbirth [Supplementary Appendix 1 – Study Questionnaire].

Fear-of-childbirth items covered emotional, cognitive and behavioural responses to childbirth. Content validity was established through expert review by three experts in maternal health and midwifery, and the tool was pilot tested for clarity and feasibility among primigravida women. Internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach's α = 0.82 at baseline and 0.85 at follow-up).

Eligible participants were escorted to a private room within the health centre to ensure privacy and confidentiality. Participants completed the pre-intervention questionnaire prior to the educational sessions. Follow-up assessment was conducted 2 weeks postpartum using the same fear-of-childbirth items to capture early postpartum levels of childbirth-related fear. A follow-up period of 2 weeks postpartum was selected to minimise loss to follow-up while keeping the recall period short. The investigator was available to clarify questionnaire items as required.

Potential sources of bias included self-reported responses and variability in participants’ engagement with the educational sessions. These were addressed by standardising the educational content, ensuring consistent delivery by trained professionals and allowing time for questions and clarification. Data were stored securely in password-protected software accessible only to the research team.

Data were coded and checked for completeness and consistency prior to analysis; no missing data were identified. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. Normality of the within-participant difference scores was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test to confirm suitability for paired t-test analysis. Paired t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-intervention fear-of-childbirth scores. Mean differences were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Effect size was calculated using Cohen's d for paired samples to quantify the magnitude of change and interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5) and large (0.8). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Python, Version 3.10 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, Delaware, USA), with the Pandas and SciPy packages. Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to examine whether changes in fear-of-childbirth scores differed by maternal age, educational level and employment status using independent t-tests or one-way ANOVA as appropriate. Because follow-up occurred postpartum, the pre-post comparison reflected change from late pregnancy to early postpartum and may be influenced by the childbirth experience.

The mean fear-of-childbirth score decreased from 4.35 ± 0.63 at baseline to 1.79 ± 0.74 at 2-week postpartum follow-up, representing a mean reduction of 2.56 points. This reduction was statistically significant (t = 27.8; P <0.001). The mean difference was 2.56 (95% CI: 2.38–2.74). The effect size was very large (Cohen's d = 3.12) indicating a strong intervention effect.

At post-intervention evaluation, 76 participants (96.2%) reported a reduction in fear of childbirth, while 2 (2.5%) reported no change and 1 (1.3%) reported increased fear. Most participants strongly agreed that they felt more confident at the time of birth (62.1%), while 43.0% strongly agreed and 54.5% agreed that they were prepared emotionally and psychologically. Furthermore, 78.5% strongly agreed and 19.0% agreed that they would recommend the programme [Table 6].

A subgroup analysis was conducted to examine whether fear-of-childbirth scores differed according to maternal age and educational level. A one-way ANOVA demonstrated a statistically significant difference in mean fear of childbirth scores across maternal age groups (F = 3.31; P = 0.042). Women aged 28–40 years had a mean fear score of 26.36 ± 3.46, compared with 21.00 ± 6.24 among women aged 13–27 years and 25.00 in the single participant within the remaining age category. These findings suggest that fear of childbirth varied according to maternal age. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in fear-of-childbirth scores across educational levels (F = 1.94; P = 0.151). Mean fear scores ranged from 25.36 ± 3.15 to 26.97 ± 3.42 across the three education categories, indicating that educational attainment was not significantly associated with fear of childbirth in this sample.

4. Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of a structured prenatal educational programme in reducing fear of childbirth among primigravida women. In this study, many women had (48.1%) a bachelor's degree and 57.0% were aged 26–35 years. These characteristics may reflect the high educational profile of women attending primary care in Muscat and, possibly, a later age at first pregnancy in this setting.

High baseline levels of fear of childbirth were observed across several domains, including pain, complications, perceived lack of support during labour, prolonged labour, episiotomy and caesarean section. Fear of episiotomy emerged as the most prominent concern, which is consistent with the international literature identifying episiotomy as a key contributor to childbirth-related fear.1314 These findings underline the need for targeted counselling and structured antenatal education addressing specific fears, including episiotomy. Elevated fear during pregnancy has been associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including operative delivery, postpartum haemorrhage, severe preeclampsia and neonatal complications such as birth asphyxia.15

The majority of the participants (73.4%) reported no prior attendance at a structured prenatal educational programme, while some (26.6%) reported previous attendance. This pattern of limited baseline utilisation aligns with a randomised controlled trial conducted by Francis et al., who reported that only 6.67% of total participants fully completed all ten recommended sessions in the group care model.16 This low completion rate was driven primarily by inflexible timings or childcare constraints (23.19%), personal preferences for private care (16.43%) and a lack of strong family support systems.16 This widespread limited uptake supports the need for structured, accessible antenatal programmes delivered through primary healthcare services. Among those who had previously attended a prenatal educational programme in the current study, healthcare providers were the most frequently cited source of information (48.1%), followed by the internet (30.4%). This underscores that healthcare providers are vital to informing women about prenatal education and actively encouraging them to attend these sorts of programmes.

Nearly all participants (97.5%) perceived benefit from the programme, particularly in relation to a reduction in fear of childbirth and improved preparedness for labour. This is supported by a prospective randomised trial by Kaya et al. which reported that a structured 6-week programme reduced fear of childbirth, with greater reduction in Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ) scores in the intervention group (P <0.0001), alongside documented decreases in maternal stress and enhancements in postpartum mental well-being.17 However, the study included only women with severe baseline fear and employed an intensive multidisciplinary programme, which may have contributed to the magnitude of the observed effect and may limit the generalisability of the findings to routine primary healthcare settings.

Structured prenatal education has also been associated with improved maternal knowledge, informed decision-making and better preparation for labour and postpartum care. The World Health Organization's Compendium on Respectful Maternal and Newborn Care highlights the importance of woman-centred care that ensures effective communication, information provision and psychosocial support throughout maternity services, thereby promoting informed and respectful childbirth experiences.18 From a policy perspective, these findings may support piloting structured antenatal education programmes in primary healthcare settings.

Interestingly, in the current study, participants recommended additional topics for future antenatal education programmes, such as stages of labour, emotional and psychological aspects of childbirth and postpartum care. These findings suggest that women have broader educational needs beyond reducing fear of childbirth; this highlights the importance of comprehensive antenatal education within primary healthcare settings. Healthcare providers need to consider the importance of expanding the content of the antenatal educational programme which may play a key role in fulfilling women's needs, increase their satisfaction and improve the overall childbirth experience.

Prenatal education was associated with a statistically significant reduction in fear-of-childbirth scores (t = 27.8; P <0.001). The findings are broadly consistent with a systematic review, in which 40 studies involving 1,116 pregnant women reported improvements in childbirth self-efficacy and reductions in fear of childbirth following antenatal education, although effect sizes varied considerably (self-efficacy: standardised mean difference [SMD] = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.06–2.95; fear of childbirth: SMD = -1.26, 95% CI: -1.79 to -0.74).12 On the other hand, there are some studies reported mixed or inconclusive findings, which may be small sample sizes, heterogeneous interventions and risk of bias.619 These inconsistencies underscore the need for well-designed, context-specific evaluations of structured prenatal educational programmes, particularly in settings where local evidence remains limited.

Subgroup analysis revealed a significant association between maternal age and fear of childbirth, whereas educational level was not significantly associated with fear scores. This result is consistent with recent evidence indicating that maternal age may be associated with variations in fear of childbirth, with younger women in particular often reporting higher levels of anxiety and fear related to labour and delivery.2021 In regards to the educational level, several recent studies have reported no significant association between educational level and fear of childbirth which supports the finding of the current study.22 However, findings are inconsistent across populations, with some studies reporting that younger women and those with lower educational attainment experience higher levels of fear of childbirth, while other studies show no statistically significant associations after adjustment for confounding factors.246 Overall, these findings suggest that the relationship between sociodemographic factors and fear of childbirth may be context-specific rather than acting as isolated predictors.

This study has several important strengths. It provides locally relevant evidence on the effectiveness of a structured prenatal educational programme in reducing fear of childbirth among primigravida women in Oman, thereby contributing to the limited body of knowledge on this topic within the local context. The study also identified specific areas of childbirth-related fear among primigravida women, with fear of episiotomy emerging as the most prominent concern. These findings provide valuable insights for healthcare providers and may assist in developing targeted educational interventions that address women's specific fears and informational needs, thereby supporting holistic, woman-centred care. In addition, the findings provide evidence that structured prenatal education may improve psychological preparedness for childbirth and reduce childbirth-related fear. Such evidence may inform the development of future policies, guidelines and standards related to antenatal education and maternal health services. The results may also support efforts to strengthen routine antenatal care, enhance women's childbirth experiences and improve the quality of maternity care.

The study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. The absence of a comparison group in the single-group pre-test-post-test design means that the observed change in fear scores cannot be attributed to the intervention with certainty, as other factors over time may have contributed. This design was selected to provide a preliminary evaluation of the programme in a real-world primary healthcare setting and to assess its potential effectiveness before undertaking more rigorous controlled studies. In addition, fear of childbirth was measured using self-reporting, which may be influenced by response tendencies such as social desirability bias or recall bias following childbirth. To minimise this risk, participants completed questionnaires in a private setting and were assured of confidentiality.

Although the single-group pre–post design limits causal inference, this study provides locally relevant evidence and may inform the development and evaluation of structured antenatal education programmes within primary healthcare settings in Oman.

5. Conclusion

This study suggested that a structured prenatal educational programme may contribute to reducing fear of childbirth among primigravida women in a primary healthcare setting. The findings highlighted the potential value of integrating structured antenatal education into routine maternity care, particularly within primary healthcare services where such programmes are limited. Further research using controlled study designs, larger samples and multi-site implementation is recommended to confirm effectiveness and assess the long-term impact of antenatal education on fear of childbirth.

Ethical approval was granted from Health Studies and Research Approval Committee (HSRAC) [MOH/CSR/24/29142], Ministry of Health, Oman. Participant confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study and participants were informed that they could withdraw at any stage without impact on their care. Clinical trial registration was not pursued because this study was designed as a single-group quasi-experimental pre-test–post-test evaluation of a prenatal educational programme implemented within routine primary healthcare services.
