Strategies for replacing phosphates in meat processing
Authors: Tae-Kyung Kim, Min Hyeock Lee, Dong-Min Shin, Yea-Ji Kim, Samooel Jung, Yun-Sang Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhosphates are widely used in processed meat products because of their multifunctional technological properties, including enhancing water-holding capacity, improving myofibrillar protein solubilization, stabilizing emulsions, increasing cooking yield, and supporting oxidative stability. These functions make phosphate an essential ingredient in comminuted and whole-muscle meat systems. However, increasing consumer demand for clean-label foods, concerns about the excessive intake of highly bioavailable inorganic phosphates, and sustainability issues related to finite phosphate resources have intensified the interest in phosphate reduction strategies. This review summarizes the physicochemical characteristics and functional roles of phosphates in meat systems, and discusses their potential health implications and regulatory considerations. Furthermore, recent strategies for phosphate reduction have been critically evaluated, including ingredient-based approaches (plant fibers, modified starches, functional proteins, alkaline salts, and enzymatic systems) and processing-based technologies (high-pressure processing, ultrasound, and optimized tumbling). Although no single substitute fully replicates the multifunctional effects of phosphates, integrated or hurdle-based approaches combining multifunctional ingredients with advanced processing technologies show considerable promise. Partial replacement is generally more feasible than complete elimination, particularly for emulsified meat products. Future research should focus on elucidating protein–water interaction mechanisms, developing clean-label multifunctional systems, and validating long-term quality and consumer acceptance. Strategic phosphate reduction is technologically achievable and aligns with global trends toward healthier, sustainable, and clean-label meat products.Keywords: Phosphate reduction, Meat processing, Water-holding capacity, Clean-label, Phosphate substitutes

Introduction

Phosphates are multifunctional additives that are extensively used in processed meat products to improve technological functionality, product quality, and manufacturing efficiency. Combined with sodium chloride, phosphates enhance the water-holding capacity (WHO 2002), increase myofibrillar protein solubility, promote protein extraction, and improve emulsion stability and texture in comminuted meat systems (Feiner 2006, Long et al. 2011). These functional properties contribute to the improved cooking yield, juiciness, tenderness, oxidative stability, and overall sensory acceptability of meat products (Cheng and Ockerman 2003, Glorieux et al. 2017). Therefore, phosphates have become indispensable ingredients in various meat products, including sausages, ham, bacon, and restructured meat products.

Despite their technological advantages, the growing consumer demand for clean-label and minimally processed foods has intensified the scrutiny of synthetic additives in meat products (Yong et al. 2021). Clean-label trends emphasize reducing or eliminating chemically synthesized additives and promoting the use of natural alternatives (Thangavelu et al. 2019). Although phosphates are classified as generally recognized as safe (GRAS) by regulatory authorities when used according to good manufacturing practices (GMP) (CODE 2020), excessive dietary phosphorus intake has been associated with mineral imbalance, kidney burden, and potential long-term health risks (WHO 2002, EFSA 2015). Moreover, the increasing awareness of phosphate sustainability and environmental concerns related to phosphate rock depletion further supports the need to explore alternative strategies (Vaccari et al. 2019).

Although phosphate reduction has attracted increasing attention due to clean-label trends and concerns regarding excessive intake, such reduction should not be considered universally beneficial without technological qualification. Technologically, the functional roles of phosphates in meat systems are complex. Phosphates increase meat pH above the isoelectric point (pI) of myofibrillar proteins, thereby enhancing electrostatic repulsion, protein swelling, and water retention (Puolanne et al. 2001). Additionally, they dissociate actomyosin complexes and chelate divalent metal ions, which improves protein extraction and reduces lipid oxidation (Cheng and Ockerman 2003, Fonseca et al. 2011). For this reason, phosphate reduction should be approached as a balanced reformulation strategy that considers both consumer-driven demands and technological necessity. Phosphate functionality depends on the molecular structure, degree of polymerization, pH, solubility, ionic strength, and interactions with other ingredients, including salts and hydrocolloids (Feiner 2006, Long et al. 2011). In addition, the relative importance of phosphate functionality depends on product type, formulation, and processing conditions (Feiner 2006, Long et al. 2011, Ellinger 2018). Phosphate replacement is not a simple one-to-one substitution. It is a product-specific reformulation challenge. Multiple quality and processing parameters must be balanced to optimize it.

Recent studies have investigated various strategies for reducing or replacing phosphates in meat products (Yong et al. 2020). These methods include using plant fibers, citrus fibers, starches, proteins, hydrocolloids, alkaline salts (e.g., sodium bicarbonate), enzymatic systems, and novel processing technologies such as high-pressure processing and ultrasound (Thangavelu et al. 2019, Garmyn et al. 2020, Powell et al. 2019). Although some alternatives partially compensate for water retention or texture, achieving equivalent multifunctional performance remains challenging. Consequently, a systematic evaluation of phosphate replacement strategies is essential to maintain product quality, safety, and consumer acceptance.

Therefore, this review aims to summarize the physicochemical characteristics and functional roles of phosphates in meat systems, discuss their impact on product quality and human health, examine current regulations governing phosphate use, and critically evaluate ingredient- and processing-based strategies for phosphate reduction or replacement in meat products. This comprehensive assessment provides scientific insights and technological guidance for developing clean-label meat products with reduced phosphate content.

The characteristics of phosphate added to meat products

Production of phosphate

The production of food-grade phosphate, known as phosphate salt, varies; however, most phosphate is obtained from rock phosphorus. Briefly, phosphoric acid obtained from rock phosphorus is cleaned several times until it reaches food grade. Phosphoric acid is subsequently neutralized using a strong alkali. Consequently, monophosphates are produced and melted to polymerize (Feiner 2006). As the main source of elemental phosphorus, phosphate rock is produced worldwide, and its quality can be determined by the percentage of phosphorous pentoxide (P2O5) (Cisse and Mrabet 2004, Toama 2017). The beneficiation process can increase the phosphate concentration of phosphate rock, which could be used as a raw material for fertilizers, phosphoric acid, and other compounds. Most phosphoric acid is consumed in fertilizer production and food, and approximately 1% of phosphoric acid is used by the food and beverage industry (Toama 2017). Wet and dry processes are used to produce phosphoric acid. Approximately 90% of the world’s phosphoric acids are produced using sulfuric, hydrochloric, and nitric acids. However, this process may be inappropriate for high-quality phosphoric acid, which is required for food-grade phosphate production. Although its output is lower than that of the wet method, the dry process produces pure phosphoric acid, which is sufficient for use as a food ingredient. Elemental phosphorus obtained by heating the phosphate rock reacts with coal and condensed silica sand to produce phosphorus oxide. Phosphoric acid is obtained via hydration with water (Hocking 2016). Phosphoric acid, combined with metal ions—mostly sodium, calcium, and phosphate salts—can be manufactured (Gilmour 2013). Sodium phosphate is the most widely consumed type of phosphate in the food industry. Sodium hydroxide or sodium carbonate has been used to determine the grade of phosphate salts, including monosodium, disodium, and trisodium phosphates (Toama 2017). As mentioned previously, food-grade phosphates are produced using limited resources, various chemical sources, and complicated and difficult processes. Additionally, because phosphate rock is exhausted and no direct substitutes exist, alternatives for sodium phosphate or other phosphates should be developed (Vaccari et al. 2019).

Structural characteristics of linear phosphates

The basic forms of phosphates can be classified into three groups (ring, linear, and a combination of ring and linear phosphates). Linear phosphates have been widely used in meat products, and the physicochemical characteristics of phosphates—including pH, solubility, binding capacity of meat ions, and buffer capacity—can vary with chain length (Feiner 2006). The basic chemical structure of phosphate is shown in Fig. 1. Phosphates can be categorized into two groups based on their ion polymerization. First, the physicochemical properties of phosphate salts can be determined using polymeric metal ions. These phosphates can be produced using sodium hydroxide or sodium carbonate, and the sodium/phosphate ratio determines the degree of polymerization. Linear phosphates can also be modified through anion polymerization. The sodium/phosphate ratio not only affects polymerization but also the polymerization temperature (Toama 2017). This is because the physicochemical properties of phosphates can be determined by the polymerization of metal ions and phosphates. Monophosphates have a high buffering capacity; however, their calcium-binding capacity and cold-water solubility are the poorest compared to those of diphosphate, triphosphate, and polyphosphate. Additionally, short phosphates (diphosphates) have a higher active component that can act on proteins than short-chained phosphates (Feiner 2006). The difference in pH values when dissolved in water also changed according to the phosphate type. The pH of phosphates with polymeric metal ions and polymeric phosphates is higher than that of non-polymerized phosphates (Thangavelu et al. 2019). Therefore, appropriate phosphate selection is crucial because their physicochemical properties vary significantly.

Considered condition of phosphate in meat products

The physicochemical properties of phosphate—including pH, buffering capacity, solubility, activated components, and binding capacity for calcium ions—should be considered when preparing brine or processing meat products (Feiner 2006). The specific characteristics of phosphates are determined by their structures. Longer-chain phosphates (tri- or poly) are more suitable than shorter-chain phosphates for preparing brine solutions because their higher ion sequestration and buffering capacity in cold water than shorter (Ellinger 2018, Capita et al. 2000). Thereby improving brine performance and helping control oxidation-related quality defects such as warmed-over flavor and discoloration (Capita et al. 2000, Trout and Dale 1990). The active site on the phosphate protein is important in emulsified meat products, and short-chain phosphates operate more easily. The short-chain such as pyrophosphates are often more directly linked to myofibrillar protein extraction and actomyosin dissociation, which are critical for water retention, emulsion formation, and textural development in emulsified products (Long et al. 2011, Trout and Dale 1990). A high number of active sites allows proteins to be solubilized, enabling effective extraction of proteins with technical functions, including gelling and emulsifying capacities (Feiner 2006). Since each phosphate has a different pH and buffer capacity based on its structure, their highest efficiency can be achieved by blending them according to the recommended pH value when preparing meat products. Buffer capacity can also affect the maintenance of meat quality. However, longer chains, specifically those longer than two phosphorus atoms, could have a poor buffer capacity. From a meat-processing perspective, the technological relevance of phosphate type is more important than its general industrial production route. Short-chain phosphates such as pyrophosphates are often associated with stronger effects on actomyosin dissociation and myofibrillar protein extraction, whereas longer-chain phosphates are typically valued for solubility and metal-ion sequestration in brine systems (Feiner 2006, Long et al. 2011). Therefore, phosphate selection in meat products should be discussed in relation to buffering behavior, brine stability, extraction efficiency, and oxidative control rather than chemistry alone (Long et al. 2011).

The above conditions describe the typical characteristics of phosphates and external factors that should be considered when preparing meat products. The raw meat quality should be considered because meat with a high pH value does not need phosphates to increase its pH (Puolanne et al. 2001). Water type should also be considered when preparing brine solutions. Soft water is more suitable than hard water because metal ions in hard water can interfere with the functionality of phosphates. Metal ions can form complexes with phosphate anions, decreasing the number of active phosphate sites (Feiner 2006). Additionally, phosphates should be dissolved first at room temperature because of their low solubility in cold water. The temperature of the meat is also important, as phosphate diffusion increases with higher meat temperatures (Ünal et al. 2006). Adding other ingredients, including NaCl, hydrocolloids, and biocarbonate, could affect protein solubility, ionic strength, and WHC of meat protein (Long et al. 2011).

Meat product quality in relation to phosphate

Phosphates play various roles in determining the quality of meat products. Phosphate addition can improve desired color, appearance, texture, and flavor, while also extending shelf-life and providing anti-oxidation effects (Fernández-López et al. 2004). The detailed effects of phosphates are described, focusing on their impact on the color, texture, flavor, and shelf-life of meat products.

Color

Phosphate effects on meat color can be attributed to pigment characteristics and light-scattering properties. Adding phosphates can prevent light scattering on the surface of meat caused by moisture exudation. Meat products with phosphates may exhibit lower CIE L* values than those without phosphates due to the relative difference in WHC (Feiner 2006, Fernández-López et al. 2004, Roldan et al. 2014). As phosphates alter the intrinsic pH of meat and the pI, more moisture can be retained in the muscle (Feiner 2006). However, the high moisture diffusion rate caused by phosphate addition can induce excessive loss of moisture in dry sausages. Fonseca et al. (Fonseca et al. 2011) showed that adding phosphate to “chorizo” decreases CIE L* and CIE b* values and results in lower moisture contents than “chorizo” without phosphate. Myoglobin (Mb) is the major color pigment in meat, and its various forms produce different colorations in muscle (Honikel 2008). When oxygen binds to the original Mb, its color changes to bright red (oxymyoglobin, MbO2). If MbO2 undergoes oxidation or deoxidation, its color changes to brown (metmyoglobin, MMb) or purple-red (deoxymyoglobin, DMb). Both MMb and DMb are affected by sodium tripolyphosphate (Fernández-López et al. 2004). Although phosphate ions have an antioxidant effect, the MMb content in meat increases and DMb content decreases with elevated triphosphate ion concentration (Fernández-López et al. 2004). However, because of the high stability of MbO2, the CIE a* and b* values remained unchanged regardless of phosphate addition. Therefore, phosphates can be used as color stabilizers for lean meat. Curing agents, including nitrite, are useful ingredients for increasing the attractive red color of meat products. Nitrite is converted to nitric oxide, which reacts with Mb to form nitrosomyoglobin, a dark-red pigment (Honikel 2008). However, phosphates with a pH value greater than 10.0 are unsuitable for brine solution or curing agents because of their deleterious effects on curing color formation and nitrite-to-nitric oxide conversion (Feiner 2006).

Texture

Various studies have demonstrated that the textural properties of meat and meat products can be enhanced by adding phosphates. With increasing gaps between the pI and pH of meat, the WHC could be improved, and textural properties, including tenderness, hardness, springiness, and chewiness, may be affected (Long et al. 2011). During storage at 0–4 °C for 30 days, emulsion-type sausage with phosphates had more stable hardness and decreased purge loss, while higher phosphate levels further increased emulsion stability and cooking yield (Wang et al. 2009). With increasing WHC and cooking yield, the hardness and chewiness of phosphate-treated goat meat decrease (Gadekar et al. 2014). To obtain acceptable textural properties, a minimum of 0.06% tetra-sodium pyrophosphate (TSPP) should be added to cooked sausages (Gadekar et al. 2014). In addition to ground meat, the textural properties of whole muscle can also be affected by adding phosphates. According to a study by Kim et al. (2019), adding phosphates to brine could improve chicken breast tenderness, with a significant effect on the shear force depending on salt concentration. Beef meat enhanced with phosphate also exhibited a more tender texture than non-enhanced beef with phosphate (Garmyn et al. 2020). Although phosphate addition improved cooking yield and WHC, the hardness and shear force of sous-vide–cooked injected lamb loin also increased (Roldan et al. 2014). Because a more swollen sample or excessive denaturation requires more compression forces, sous-vide samples with high moisture content and phosphates might have higher hardness and shear force values. In conclusion, although phosphate addition improved tenderness in most conventionally cooked meat products, it was inappropriate for the sous-vide method.

Flavor

The effect of phosphates on flavor can be understood through direct and indirect mechanisms. Although various studies reported that phosphate addition has no significant effect on the flavor of meat products, a minor direct effect on overall flavor perception has been observed because of bitterness or alkaline/soapy aftertaste depending on phosphate type and concentration (Long et al. 2011). The recommended level of phosphate addition is 0.3–0.5% to prevent unacceptable flavors (Ranken 2000). Phosphate type may also influence the flavor characteristics of meat products because of its different pH and solubility (Feiner 2006). Tetrapotassium pyrophosphate (TPP) had lower scores for flavor, and TSPP had relatively higher scores than the TPP treatment (Gadekar et al. 2014). In addition, phosphates can retard oxidation through metal-ion sequestration, thereby reducing the development of warmed-over flavor and other storage-related off-flavors during refrigerated storage (Ellinger 2018, Trout and Dale 1990, Chambers Iv et al. 1992) because lipid oxidation is a major cause of warmed-over flavor in cooked meat products. Garmyn et al. (Garmyn et al. 2020) showed that enhanced Australian beef fajitas had higher scores for flavor than non-phosphate treatment, and the overall liking was also high. They reported that the high juiciness of enhanced meat was correlated with a strong flavor (Garmyn et al. 2020). By improving water-holding capacity and juiciness, phosphates can influence flavor release, mouthfeel, and overall palatability, while their contribution to emulsifying and gelling properties also helps maintain overall product quality (Long et al. 2011, Goemaere et al. 2021). The sensory role of phosphates should be understood not only in terms of intrinsic taste effects, but also in terms of their broader function in preserving flavor quality through improved moisture retention and oxidative stability.

Shelf-life

Phosphates have been used to extend the shelf-life of meat and meat products (Long et al. 2011). Commercial phosphate has a chelating effect on meat and meat products (Fonseca et al. 2011). This chelating characteristic of phosphate could not only affect the actomyosin complex structure but also prevent oxidation during processing or storage (Glorieux et al. 2017, Fonseca et al. 2011). Alkali phosphate can bind to some prooxidants that can promote lipid oxidation in meat products, and the addition level has a significant effect on the antioxidant activity of phosphate (Cheng and Ockerman 2003, Fonseca et al. 2011). Therefore, the phosphate type and addition level should be considered when incorporating phosphate into meat products.

Influence of phosphate on human health

Phosphorus is an essential mineral involved in skeletal development, energy metabolism (ATP), nucleic acid synthesis, and cellular signaling (Serna and Bergwitz 2020). Approximately 85% of total body phosphorus is stored in bones and teeth, whereas the remaining portion plays a critical role in intracellular metabolic processes (Penido and Alon 2012). Under normal physiological conditions, phosphorus homeostasis is closely regulated by the coordinated actions of the kidney, intestine, bone, parathyroid hormone (PTH), fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF-23), and vitamin D (Bergwitz and Jüppner 2010). Although phosphates are considered GRAS when used according to GMP (CODE 2020), increasing evidence suggests that the excessive intake of inorganic phosphate additives may present health concerns. Unlike organic phosphorus naturally present in meat and dairy products, inorganic phosphates added to processed foods exhibit high bioavailability (approximately 90–100%), resulting in more rapid intestinal absorption and increased serum phosphate levels (EFSA 2015). High dietary phosphate intake is associated with disturbances in mineral metabolism, particularly in susceptible populations, including those with chronic kidney disease. Elevated serum phosphate levels may stimulate the increased secretion of PTH and FGF-23, leading to altered calcium metabolism, vascular calcification, bone demineralization, and increased cardiovascular risk (WHO 2002). Epidemiological studies have reported correlations between high serum phosphate concentrations and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality even within the upper normal physiological ranges (Torrijo-Belanche et al. 2024). In addition to systemic effects, excessive phosphate intake may influence the gut microbiota composition and intestinal barrier function; however, this requires further investigation. Processed meat products represent a significant source of inorganic phosphate in modern diets (Oda et al. 2023). Although the estimated daily intake of phosphorus in most populations remains within the tolerable limits established by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA; 70 mg/kg body weight/day), the cumulative intake from processed foods raises concerns regarding long-term exposure (Dunford and Calvo 2025). From a public health perspective, reducing the amount of phosphate added to processed meat products aligns with clean-label trends and preventive nutrition strategies. Therefore, technological approaches that minimize the phosphate content without compromising product quality are both scientifically and socially relevant.

Strategies for reducing phosphate

Reducing or replacing phosphates in meat products is technologically challenging because they perform multiple simultaneous functions, including increasing pH, improving protein extraction, enhancing WHC, stabilizing emulsions, chelating prooxidants, and improving texture (Glorieux et al. 2017). Therefore, successful phosphate reduction strategies generally require multifunctional systems, rather than single-ingredient substitutions. To facilitate comparison among different ingredient-based strategies, Table 1 summarizes the major categories of phosphate substitute additives, their primary functional mechanisms, technological advantages, and practical limitations. Although these ingredients can compensate for specific phosphate-related functions, particularly water binding and structural stabilization, none fully replicates the multifunctional effects of phosphates. Therefore, formulation optimization and synergistic combinations are frequently required to achieve an acceptable product quality. To use strategies for reducing phosphate, its ability to perform the functions of phosphate must be evaluated. In addition to conventional measurements such as cooking yield, purge loss, shear force, texture profile analysis, and oxidation indices (Feiner 2006, Long et al. 2011). In these days, objective flavor evaluation tools may provide useful information on warmed-over flavor, metallic notes, and overall flavor shifts (O’sullivan et al. 2003). Representative tools include electronic nose/electronic tongue systems (Munekata et al. 2023). With these methodologies, sensory evaluation should be complemented by trained panels using product-specific protocols to assess tenderness, juiciness, flavor intensity, and off-flavor perception (O’sullivan et al. 2003, Munekata et al. 2023).

Phosphate substitute additives

Various compounds have been investigated as partial or complete phosphate substitutes. These substitutes aim to replicate one or more of the technological functions of phosphates, particularly water binding, protein solubilization, and emulsion stabilization. Dietary fibers, including citrus, wheat, bamboo, and oat fibers, have demonstrated strong water-binding capacity owing to their porous structure and hydrophilic functional groups. Citrus fiber has been shown to partially replace sodium phosphate in emulsified meat products, while maintaining an acceptable cooking yield and texture (Powell et al. 2019). However, excessive fiber addition may negatively affect the mouthfeel and cohesiveness. Native and modified starches can improve the water retention and viscosity of meat emulsions (Genccelep et al. 2015). Pregelatinized and resistant starches enhance gel formation and reduce purge loss (Chen et al. 2024). However, starch does not significantly improve myofibrillar protein extraction, limiting its ability to completely replace phosphate functionality. Milk (caseinate and whey proteins), blood plasma, and plant (soy protein isolate and pea protein) proteins can improve emulsion stability through interfacial stabilization and gel formation (Hinderink et al. 2019). These proteins compensate for the reduced phosphate-induced protein solubilization. Nevertheless, allergen labeling and flavor effects should be considered. Sodium bicarbonate and potassium carbonate increase the pH, partially mimicking the pH-shifting effect of phosphates (Li et al. 2022). Garmyn et al. (Garmyn et al. 2020) demonstrated that sodium bicarbonate improved the tenderness and juiciness of enhanced beef. However, excessive use may result in a soapy flavor or excessive alkalinity. Transglutaminase (TGase) catalyzes the covalent cross-linking between proteins, improving gel strength and texture (Kieliszek 2026). TGase does not increase WHC directly, but enhances structural integrity in reduced-phosphate systems.

Processing technology for reducing phosphate

Beyond ingredient substitution, innovative processing technologies can improve protein functionality and water retention, thereby reducing reliance on phosphates (Ji et al. 2024). A structured overview of processing-based strategies for phosphate reduction is presented in Table 2. These technologies primarily enhance protein functionality and water retention through physical modification of muscle proteins, thereby reducing reliance on chemical additives (Stanišić et al. 2025). Among these, combination strategies that integrate processing optimization with multifunctional ingredients appear to offer the greatest potential for industrial applications. High-pressure processing modifies the protein conformation and enhances protein unfolding, thereby increasing gelation and WHC. Moderate pressures (100–300 MPa) can improve emulsion stability without additives (Zhao et al. 2022). However, equipment costs are high. Ultrasound treatment enhances mass transfer and protein extraction through cavitation. It increases the solubility of myofibrillar proteins and improves the functional properties of low-phosphate systems. Vacuum tumbling improves brine penetration and protein extraction efficiency, potentially allowing reduced phosphate levels when optimized for salt concentration and temperature control (Ge et al. 2022). Adjusting the NaCl concentration and ionic strength can partially compensate for the reduced phosphate by enhancing protein solubilization (Choi and Chin 2024). However, sodium reduction targets should also be considered. The most promising approach is to combine ingredient substitutes using a processing technology. The equipment requires a significant initial investment and is therefore unsuitable for small-scale factories. Consequently, it should be combined with equipment suitable for small-scale use, such as vacuum tumblers, and the aforementioned ingredients.

Regulations for using phosphates as food additives

Currently, various phosphates have been used as additives in food products for different purposes, including preventing staining on the exterior of canned goods, removing hair from carcasses, decreasing the amount of cooked meat products, facilitating the rendering of animal fats, and increasing the effectiveness of antioxidants in lard and shortening products (Ellinger 2018). Therefore, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) considers phosphate food additives to be GRAS when used in accordance with GMP or feeding practices (Code 2020). Table 3 presents food phosphates classified as GRAS by the FDA. However, as shown in Table 4, certain phosphate applications in meat products are limited regarding the quantity that can be used (Ellinger 2018, CODE 2020). According to European legislation, phosphates can be used in meat preparations, minced meat, and meat products, but are prohibited in fresh meat. The permitted maximum level of phosphates in meat and meat products is 5 g/kg as P2O5 individually or in combination with the finished product (Long et al. 2011). Table 5 illustrates the permitted amounts of phosphates used as food additives in Europe (European Parliament and Council Directive No 95/2/EC, 2006). The FDA and the European Parliament and Council have similar regulations regarding the use of phosphates as additives in meat products. For example, according to the FDA and European regulations, the acceptable maximum levels of monopotassium phosphate used to decrease the amount of cooked-out juices of meat food products are 0.5% of the total product and 5 g/kg, respectively. However, as presented in Table 6, most phosphates, except for calcium glycerophosphate, sodium aluminum phosphates, and calcium phosphates (mono-, di-, and tribasic), are allowed to be used in minimum amounts to achieve the purposes of nutrients and/or dietary supplements, acidity regulators, and leavening agents by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, Republic of Korea (Korean Food Additives CODE 2021). Additionally, the JECFA established the Maximum Tolerable Daily Intake of phosphorus from all food sources as 70 mg/kg body weight based on the lowest concentration of phosphorus (6600 mg/day) that caused nephrocalcinosis in rats (WHO 2002). According to EFSA (EFSA 2015), the major sources of phosphorus intake are foods with high protein content (milk and dairy products, followed by meat, poultry, fish, grain products, and legumes). The daily phosphorus intake in European Union countries is 265–531, 641–973, 750–1202, 990–1601, and 1000–1767 mg/day for infants, children aged 1–3 years, children aged 3–10 years, children aged 10–18 years, and adults, respectively, which are acceptable amounts proposed by the JECFA.

Conclusion

Phosphates play a multifunctional role in processed meat systems by improving the WHC, enhancing myofibrillar protein solubilization, stabilizing emulsions, increasing cooking yield, improving texture, and contributing to oxidative stability. These properties make phosphates essential ingredients in many comminuted and whole-muscle meat products. However, growing consumer demand for clean-label products, concerns about excessive inorganic phosphate intake, and sustainability issues related to phosphate resources have increased the need for reduction strategies. Replacing phosphates is technologically challenging because no single ingredient can replicate their combined effects on pH adjustment, protein extraction, ionic strength, or metal chelation. Partial replacement is generally more feasible than total elimination, particularly in emulsified meat systems. Ingredient-based approaches can compensate for specific functions but require careful formulation to maintain sensory quality. Processing technologies, including high-pressure processing, ultrasound, and optimized tumbling, can further enhance protein functionality and water retention, thereby reducing dependence on phosphates. Integrated strategies that combine multifunctional ingredients with optimized processing conditions are the most promising. Future research should focus on mechanistic understanding, clean-label ingredient development, sensory and storage stability, and regulatory alignment. Not only in meat and meat products, but phosphates are also used in various foods, and phosphates are used as a major additive in alternative foods as well. Overall, phosphate reduction in food matrix system is achievable and is consistent with global trends toward healthier and more sustainable food systems.
