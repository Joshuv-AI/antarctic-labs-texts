Qingre Liangxue Acupuncture for Psoriasis: A Cholinergic Neuroimmune Framework
Authors: Lin Liu, Wei Liu, Ru Jia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disorder sustained by reciprocal interactions among keratinocytes, immune cells, vascular elements, and neural signals. This mechanism-oriented narrative review examines Qingre Liangxue (clearing heat and cooling blood) acupuncture through the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway (CAP) and the cutaneous non-neuronal cholinergic system. Qingre Liangxue acupuncture is treated here as a therapeutic principle implemented through a family of related, rather than universally fixed, acupoint prescriptions. We distinguish four evidence sources: direct human psoriasis evidence, psoriasis-like animal or cellular evidence, cross-disease CAP/acupuncture evidence, and theoretical mechanistic inference. Direct human evidence supports the presence and potential relevance of cutaneous cholinergic signaling but does not establish that acupuncture engages CAP in plaque psoriasis. Psoriasis-like models suggest that α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor signaling may restrain myeloid and keratinocyte inflammatory programs, whereas α9-associated signaling may contribute to keratinocyte turnover and epidermal homeostasis. Cross-disease studies show that site- and parameter-dependent acupuncture inputs can access autonomic anti-inflammatory circuits, but these findings cannot be assumed to translate directly to psoriasis. Psoriasis-related acupuncture studies report changes in lesion severity, Th17/Treg balance, neuropeptide signaling, and keratinocyte proliferation, although the evidence is predominantly preclinical or methodologically heterogeneous. Clinically, acupuncture is best considered an adjunctive and mechanistically exploratory intervention rather than an alternative to topical therapy, phototherapy, conventional systemic treatment, or biologics. Overall, CAP provides a testable neuroimmune framework linking upstream autonomic regulation to local immune-epidermal responses, with α7 and α9 receptors representing distinct candidate effector nodes. Confirmation requires standardized, sham-controlled studies that integrate clinical outcomes with autonomic, receptor, cytokine, and keratinocyte measurements.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, relapsing, immune-mediated inflammatory disorder in which keratinocyte dysfunction, IL-23/IL-17-centered immune activation, vascular changes, sensory-neural signaling, and tissue memory contribute to lesion persistence and recurrence,1–6 Current management is determined by disease extent, location, symptoms, comorbidities, and treatment goals. Topical agents remain central for limited disease, whereas phototherapy, conventional systemic agents, targeted small molecules, and biologics are used for more extensive or clinically burdensome psoriasis.7,8 These therapies have substantially improved skin clearance and patient outcomes, although relapse after withdrawal, incomplete response, treatment burden, and persistent tissue memory continue to motivate investigation of complementary regulatory mechanisms.9

The inflammatory reflex and CAP provide one framework for examining how autonomic signals may influence inflammatory intensity,10–12 Within the skin, this systemic framework intersects with a non-neuronal cholinergic system in which keratinocytes, immune cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and adnexal structures can synthesize or respond to acetylcholine.13–15 α7nAChR-related signaling has been associated with restraint of inflammatory transcription in myeloid cells and keratinocytes, whereas emerging α9nAChR evidence points to local immune-epidermal communication and epidermal homeostasis.16–18 These findings establish biologically plausible receptor-level interfaces but do not demonstrate that CAP is the dominant mechanism of plaque psoriasis or that acupuncture recruits this pathway in patients.

In this review, Qingre Liangxue acupuncture refers to a therapeutic principle of clearing heat and cooling blood that may be implemented through a family of related clinical prescriptions rather than a universally fixed acupoint formula. Representative acupoints used in relevant clinical protocols include Quchi (LI11), Xuehai (SP10), Zusanli (ST36), and Sanyinjiao (SP6),19 although the complete acupoint combination may vary across studies according to the treatment protocol, lesion distribution, and clinical presentation. This definition distinguishes the broader therapeutic principle from any single standardized trial protocol. Within contemporary psoriasis care, acupuncture should not be regarded as a replacement for topical therapy, phototherapy, conventional systemic agents, or biologic treatment. Its more plausible current role is as a potential adjunct for symptom and pruritus control rather than as an established disease-modifying intervention; use during maintenance or for recurrence prevention remains to be validated. Its mechanistic value lies in probing somato-autonomic regulation and cutaneous neuroimmune interactions.20–22

This article is a mechanism-oriented narrative review that seeks to interpretively integrate the proposed relationships among the vagus nerve-mediated cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, the local cutaneous cholinergic system, and the pathological network of psoriasis. Evidence was identified and supplemented primarily around the themes of psoriasis, the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, the cutaneous non-neuronal cholinergic system, and the neuroimmune regulation associated with acupuncture. Studies were selected according to mechanistic relevance, argumentative coherence, and relevance to the proposed framework, with particular emphasis on basic mechanistic research, animal models, translational studies, and acupuncture-related research in psoriasis. Where necessary, evidence from inflammatory models in other organs concerning acupuncture-mediated recruitment of autonomic anti-inflammatory circuits was also incorporated to examine whether relevant neuroimmune circuits are functionally accessible. In evaluating the literature, priority was given to distinguishing direct human psoriasis evidence, psoriasis-like experimental evidence, cross-disease CAP/acupuncture evidence, and theoretical inference, rather than treating materials of different evidentiary levels as equivalent. This review was neither preregistered nor conducted according to systematic review standards, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis was performed. Its purpose, therefore, is not to adjudicate the therapeutic efficacy of acupuncture by means of evidence grading, but to propose and critically examine a falsifiable and further testable neuroimmune mechanistic framework.

Throughout this narrative review, the mechanistic discussion is organized according to the source and directness of the available evidence. Direct human psoriasis evidence includes clinical, lesional-tissue, cellular, and biomarker studies involving patients with psoriasis or human psoriasis-derived materials. Psoriasis-like experimental evidence encompasses imiquimod-induced and related animal models, together with psoriasis-relevant cellular systems. Evidence concerning the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway or acupuncture in other inflammatory disorders is used only to demonstrate the functional accessibility of the relevant neuromodulatory pathways and is not interpreted as evidence of psoriasis-specific therapeutic efficacy. Where these evidence streams are integrated to propose additional mechanistic connections, the resulting interpretation is identified as theoretical inference. These distinctions concern the origin and directness of the evidence rather than formal certainty, as this narrative review did not undertake a systematic risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative evidence grading.

Mechanistic Basis of CAP: The Inflammatory Reflex and the Cholinergic Anti-Inflammatory Pathway

The cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, CAP, is not merely a local anti-inflammatory effect mediated by a single transmitter, but an important effector route of the inflammatory reflex in peripheral immune regulation. Recent studies of neural circuits indicate that peripheral inflammation is not passively “perceived” by the brain. Rather, it can be encoded by vagal sensory neurons, whereby pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines activate distinct vagal sensory neuron populations and relay information to central brainstem nodes such as the nucleus tractus solitarius, thereby initiating subsequent autonomic regulatory programs.23,24 This suggests that the initiation of CAP does not simply depend on changes in local acetylcholine levels within inflammatory lesions. Instead, inflammatory states are first translated into neural signals, integrated centrally, and then converted into regulatory outputs that shape the intensity of peripheral immune responses.

In the efferent arm, the classical CAP model proposes that brainstem-integrated vagal output can functionally engage spleen-associated sympathetic pathways, thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory responses in the spleen and peripheral myeloid cells. This mechanism is commonly summarized as a stepwise “NA–ACh–α7nAChR” anti-inflammatory relay, in which norepinephrine released from splenic nerve terminals promotes acetylcholine production by choline acetyltransferase-positive immune cells, followed by activation of α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on macrophages and related cells, ultimately suppressing the production of TNF and other pro-inflammatory cytokines.25 However, recent evidence indicates that this T cell-mediated cholinergic relay is not an absolute prerequisite for CAP activity. In some animal models and in human and porcine splenocyte systems, the anti-inflammatory effect may depend more directly on norepinephrine signaling through β2-adrenergic receptors on myeloid cells and macrophages.26 Merchant et al further proposed that key molecular components of CAP may be organized predominantly in extrasplenic or perisplenic autonomic networks, rather than within a traditionally assumed complete intrasplenic cholinergic loop.26 Therefore, based on current evidence, the more cautious interpretation is that CAP has a well-supported anti-inflammatory function, but its specific anatomical connections and cellular relay steps are context dependent, with different organs and inflammatory models potentially recruiting distinct neuroimmune subcircuits.

Although the precise transmission pattern of upstream CAP circuits remains to be further clarified, the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor remains one of the most evidence-supported effector nodes identified to date. Its role is not confined to the downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF, but also extends to the migration, recruitment, and functional remodeling of monocyte–macrophage populations under inflammatory conditions. Keever et al demonstrated that the inflammatory regulatory effects of α7nAChR are reflected not only in the suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine production, but also in the recruitment of monocyte-derived macrophages to inflamed tissues. This process may be associated with the regulation of integrin αMβ2 expression, suggesting that α7 signaling may contribute to inflammatory microenvironment remodeling by modulating macrophage migration.27 Nakamura et al further showed that activation of α7 receptors enhanced interactions among splenic macrophages, reduced TNF-α output, and was accompanied by organ-protective effects.28 These findings indicate that CAP does not exert its anti-inflammatory effects merely through suppression of inflammatory mediator release, but may participate in the broader regulation of inflammatory responses at multiple levels, including cell migration, intercellular communication, and cytokine transcription.

In the context of psoriasis, this framework provides a useful upstream regulatory perspective for understanding the sustained activation of inflammatory responses. Psoriasis should not be regarded simply as a consequence of aberrant IL-23/IL-17 axis activation. Rather, persistent amplification among keratinocytes, myeloid cells, and neural signals may jointly contribute to the maintenance and recurrence of psoriatic lesions. At the epidermal effector level, Stegemann et al reported that α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor-mediated stimulation inhibited TNF-α-induced IL-6 and IL-8 expression in primary human epidermal keratinocytes, whereas blockade of α7 signaling attenuated this inhibitory effect.18 These findings suggest that α7-related cholinergic signaling acts not only on classical immune cells, but may also directly modulate the pro-inflammatory response threshold of keratinocytes, thereby influencing the maintenance of inflammatory amplification loops within psoriatic lesions. Accordingly, the role of CAP in psoriasis may extend beyond systemic anti-inflammatory regulation and may also involve interference with the pathological positive feedback between keratinocytes and immune cells that sustains lesional inflammation. Importantly, cholinergic regulation in psoriasis is not limited to the α7 receptor. Foffi et al found that B cell-derived acetylcholine maintained skin homeostasis through α9 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors expressed on keratinocytes, whereas impaired cholinergic synthesis in B cells or loss of α9 signaling aggravated imiquimod-induced skin inflammation.17 This evidence suggests that cholinergic regulation in psoriasis involves not only classical immune anti-inflammatory mechanisms, but also local cholinergic communication between immune cells and epidermal cells. Mechanistically, α7-related pathways appear to be more closely associated with immune-inflammatory regulation, whereas α9-related pathways are more closely linked to the maintenance of keratinocyte homeostasis. Together, these two pathways may contribute to the persistence and amplification of inflammation within psoriatic lesions.

From a translational medicine perspective, the significance of CAP is no longer confined to mechanistic inference derived from animal experiments. Existing clinical studies have shown that transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation can reduce disease activity and downregulate CRP levels in patients with psoriatic arthritis.29 In refractory Crohn’s disease, vagus nerve stimulation has been associated with improvement in clinical symptoms, attenuation of endoscopic inflammation, and reductions in fecal calprotectin and serum inflammatory mediators.30 A recent randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial in rheumatoid arthritis further suggested that vagus nerve modulation targeting the inflammatory reflex achieved superior clinical responses compared with sham stimulation and showed a favorable safety profile.31

Dermatologic evidence for direct vagal modulation is emerging but remains predominantly experimental. An invasive VNS study in an imiquimod-induced psoriasis-like model reduced cutaneous TNF-α and IL-6 and altered macrophage-associated inflammatory patterns.32 A recent dermatologic review has further synthesized the emerging preclinical and early clinical rationale for vagal modulation across inflammatory skin diseases.33 Together, these sources support the biological modifiability of cutaneous inflammation by vagal stimulation, but the evidence remains preclinical or indirect and cannot establish that somatic acupuncture input recruits the same circuit in patients with plaque psoriasis. No controlled human study has established CAP engagement in plaque psoriasis.

Taken together, cross-disease clinical studies and psoriasis-like experimental findings show that vagal modulation is an intervention-accessible neuroimmune strategy. They do not, however, establish CAP as the dominant therapeutic mechanism in plaque psoriasis or demonstrate pathway equivalence between direct VNS and acupuncture. CAP is therefore used here as an upstream, evidence-bounded framework rather than as a validated single mechanism. The Cutaneous Cholinergic System and Psoriasis-Related Immunity

Foundational work on the epidermal non-neuronal cholinergic system demonstrated that human keratinocytes express a structured repertoire of acetylcholine receptors and that cholinergic signaling contributes to proliferation, differentiation, adhesion, migration, apoptosis, and tissue homeostasis.13–15 Studies from Grando et al further showed that disruption of α7nAChR signaling alters keratinocyte maturation and cutaneous homeostatic programs.15,34 These findings establish the biological competence of the epidermal cholinergic system and broaden the discussion beyond a macrophage-centered CAP model. They do not, however, demonstrate a psoriasis-specific effect of acupuncture.

Within psoriasis-related inflammation, the skin can be regarded as a local neuro–immune–epithelial regulatory unit capable of inflammatory signal sensing, integration, and effector translation. Recent studies have shown that a relatively complete non-neuronal cholinergic system exists within the skin, in which keratinocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and multiple immune cell populations may participate in the synthesis, release, and response to acetylcholine, thereby contributing to barrier homeostasis, microcirculatory regulation, and inflammatory control through local autocrine or paracrine mechanisms.35,36

This local regulatory capacity is relevant because psoriatic inflammation is sustained by interactions among keratinocytes, myeloid cells, sensory nerves, and local stromal cells, that reinforce one another within lesional tissue. Current single-cell and spatial studies suggest that lesional tissue contains a local amplification microenvironment centered on IL-36 and related inflammatory signals, while the sensory nerve-associated ASIC3-CGRP axis may further reinforce this lesional ecosystem.37,38 The resulting question is whether local cholinergic signaling can be embedded within this pathological network as an endogenous counter-regulatory mechanism, exerting negative regulatory effects at key points of inflammatory amplification, cellular interaction, and tissue-level effector translation.

In light of the current evidence, the most representative receptor node remains the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor. Its relevance in psoriasis can no longer be simply narrowed to the classical CAP-mediated suppression of macrophage-derived TNF release. Studies have shown that α7nAChR is basally expressed in normal epidermis and is further upregulated in psoriatic lesions from patients and in imiquimod-induced murine lesions, with predominant localization in keratinocytes and macrophages.16 Together with recent advances in the cutaneous cholinergic system, α7nAChR appears to be broadly distributed across epidermal cells, stromal cells, and multiple immune cell populations, suggesting that it may constitute an important receptor interface linking local epithelial signaling, immune responses, and tissue microenvironmental regulation.34 Further functional studies showed that the selective α7nAChR agonist PNU-282987 significantly alleviated psoriasis-like skin lesions, improved abnormal epidermal thickening and the imbalance of keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation, and concomitantly downregulated pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. In vitro, this agonist also inhibited IL-6- or IL-22-induced STAT3 activation and TNF-α-induced NF-κB activation in HaCaT cells, whereas the α7nAChR antagonist methyllycaconitine produced opposite effects.16 Consistent with these findings, nicotinic cholinergic signaling in keratinocytes has also been shown to suppress TLR2-mediated pro-inflammatory responses, reduce the expression of IL-6, IL-8, and related effector molecules, and inhibit NF-κB pathway activation.39 Taken together, the current evidence supports a more appropriate positioning of α7nAChR in psoriasis as a key receptor node linking cholinergic anti-inflammatory input with epidermal inflammatory transcriptional programs. On one side, it receives CAP-related neuroimmune regulation; on the other, it directly influences keratinocyte responses to inflammatory stimuli in terms of proliferation, differentiation, and gene expression. Accordingly, the α7 branch may not regulate a single isolated cytokine, but rather the overall mode by which lesional epidermis responds to inflammatory signaling.

Compared with the α7 branch, α9-related evidence further suggests that cutaneous cholinergic regulation is not merely a simple local projection of classical CAP, but has a more prominent role in maintaining epithelial homeostasis. Foffi et al showed that choline acetyltransferase-expressing immune cells in the skin are mainly B cells. When B cells specifically lose the capacity to synthesize acetylcholine, IMQ-induced inflammatory skin disease in mice is markedly aggravated, as reflected by epidermal thickening, enhanced keratinocyte proliferation, and upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Chrna9 deficiency reproduced this aggravated phenotype, whereas Chrna7 deficiency did not fully recapitulate these changes.17 This suggests that α9nAChR may serve as an important receptor interface through which local acetylcholine regulates keratinocyte turnover and epidermal homeostasis. When considered together with the α7-related evidence discussed above, the α7 branch appears to be more closely involved in constraining inflammatory transcriptional programs and immune effector responses, whereas the α9 branch is more directly associated with the control of keratinocyte proliferation and the maintenance of epidermal homeostasis. It should be noted that the current α9-related evidence still derives mainly from animal models, and its histological localization and functional significance in human plaque psoriasis remain to be further validated. Mechanistically, however, this finding has already extended cholinergic regulation beyond the classical macrophage-centered model toward a new level of bidirectional immune cell–epidermal cell coupling. It also further suggests that the cutaneous cholinergic system is not a passive terminal extension of CAP, but may represent a local tissue interface for the execution, amplification, and translation of the inflammatory reflex. For this reason, the next question to be addressed is no longer simply whether a local cholinergic counter-regulatory mechanism exists in the skin, but whether acupuncture, as a peripheral neuromodulatory input, can recruit and reshape this neuroimmune pathway, thereby inducing functionally meaningful anti-inflammatory translational effects within psoriatic lesions. Based on the evidence outlined above, the local cutaneous cholinergic counter-regulatory mechanism may be more appropriately understood as a potential effector interface for neuro–immune–epithelial interactions within psoriatic lesions. Acupuncture Intervention and Mechanistic Reconstruction: Neuroimmune Pathways for CAP Recruitment

Evidence in this section derives primarily from cross-disease CAP/acupuncture studies and psoriasis-like experimental models, while direct human psoriasis evidence remains limited. The evidence discussed above suggests that the cutaneous cholinergic system may constitute an important effector interface for local inflammatory regulation in psoriasis. The central question is whether peripheral interventions can recruit and modulate CAP-related neuroimmune pathways. Available evidence supports examining acupuncture as a peripheral sensory input capable of accessing autonomic regulatory networks, rather than merely as a transient response induced by local physical stimulation. Neuroanatomical and functional studies have shown that acupuncture or electroacupuncture applied to specific body surface regions can activate different types of somatosensory afferent fibers, undergo segmental integration in the spinal cord and transduction through brainstem autonomic centers, and subsequently influence vagus-related or sympathetic-related immunoregulatory pathways. These effects may vary according to stimulation site, stimulation intensity, and disease state.20,21,40–42 These findings support circuit accessibility but do not establish the same pathway as the dominant mechanism of acupuncture in psoriasis. In psoriasis, the framework instead proposes that acupuncture may regulate the maintenance conditions of local inflammatory networks within psoriatic lesions from an upstream level by influencing inflammatory reflex-related circuits.

Acupuncture as a Parameter-Dependent Peripheral Neural Input and Its Potential Modulation of the Inflammatory Threshold in Psoriasis

Body-Region Selectivity and Parameter-Dependent Peripheral Neural Input

Current evidence suggests that acupuncture-induced anti-inflammatory effects are not uniform, but show clear body-region selectivity and stimulation-parameter dependence.42 Liu et al demonstrated that low-intensity electroacupuncture applied to the hindlimb ST36 region can drive a vagus-related anti-inflammatory reflex, whereas stimulation of the abdominal ST25 region at the same intensity fails to produce comparable effects.41 This difference cannot be explained solely by acupoint nomenclature or surface location, but is closely related to the distribution of sensory nerve endings within local tissues. Deep fascia, periosteum, and muscle tissues in the hindlimb are enriched with PROKR2 sensory neuron endings capable of accessing relevant reflex pathways. These fibers are more likely to relay somatic stimulation to brainstem autonomic centers and subsequently induce catecholamine release. Accordingly, the neurobiological basis of acupoint effects should not be reduced to surface location alone. Rather, it may be more appropriately understood in terms of whether a given stimulation region has the structural conditions required to enter the corresponding autonomic reflex pathway, and whether stimulation intensity reaches the threshold necessary to activate that pathway. This view is also consistent with recent summaries of somato-autonomic reflexes, in which body surface region, tissue layer, and stimulation intensity jointly determine which type of autonomic output a peripheral input is more likely to engage.43

Neuroimmune Interpretation of the Inflammatory Threshold in Psoriasis

Within the pathological context of psoriasis, the significance of this mechanistic framework does not lie in seeking a simple neuroanatomical correspondence for traditional acupoint selection, but in redefining the level at which acupuncture intervention may act. Persistent disease activity in psoriasis is more likely to reflect a hyper-responsive state jointly maintained by sensory nerve activation, neuropeptide release, immune cell recruitment, and aberrant keratinocyte responses, rather than a static manifestation of increased local inflammatory markers alone.16,37,38 Therefore, the mechanistic interpretation of acupoint selection and stimulation parameters may be more appropriately focused on which combinations of body surface regions and stimulation intensities are more likely to deliver peripheral sensory input into autonomic reflex pathways capable of regulating inflammatory responses.44,45 In other words, the effects of acupuncture may not be limited to the short-term downregulation of inflammatory mediators. Rather, acupuncture may influence the conversion process between upstream somatosensory input and autonomic anti-inflammatory output, thereby altering the conditions that maintain immune recruitment, inflammatory amplification, and aberrant keratinocyte proliferation within psoriatic lesions.46 Recent psoriasis-related studies also suggest that sensory neural input itself may participate in the sustained amplification of the lesional microenvironment. ASIC3-mediated activation of sensory neurons can aggravate psoriasis-like inflammation by promoting CGRP release, while sensory neuron-derived CGRP can also directly promote keratinocyte proliferation.38,47 Accordingly, the so-called “inflammatory threshold” may be better understood as a susceptibility state formed by the coupling between neurogenic signals and local immune–epidermal networks, which provides a more biologically plausible explanation for understanding acupuncture intervention from the perspective of upstream neural regulation.

Cross-Disease Evidence Supporting Acupuncture-Mediated Recruitment of CAP-Related Pathways

Studies from organ inflammation models have provided functional support for the upstream neural input logic described above. In an acute pancreatitis model, Zhang et al found that electroacupuncture at ST36 increased vagal activity, reduced systemic inflammation, alleviated inflammatory infiltration and tissue injury in the pancreas, and increased the proportion of α7nAChR-positive macrophages within pancreatic tissue. These protective effects were markedly weakened after vagotomy or α7nAChR blockade.48 Yang et al further showed that electroacupuncture at ST36 suppressed intestinal inflammation and improved gastrointestinal motility, and that these effects were associated with enhanced vagal efferent activity and activation of macrophage α7nAChR-mediated JAK2/STAT3 signaling. These effects were also significantly attenuated under conditions of vagotomy or α7nAChR inhibition.49 These studies support the capacity of acupuncture input to recruit vagus-related cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathways in non-psoriatic inflammatory models, but they do not establish that the same circuit mediates lesion improvement in psoriasis. In psoriasis-like models, acupuncture, electroacupuncture, and fire needling reduced lesion severity, epidermal thickening, keratinocyte proliferation, T-cell infiltration, and local inflammatory mediator expression.46 In the comparative study, electroacupuncture produced larger changes in several inflammatory and neuropeptide-related indices.46 Consistent with a neurogenic mechanism, electroacupuncture at DU20 and SP10 also attenuated psoriasiform inflammation in association with modulation of substance P–neurokinin-1 receptor signaling.50 A further study found that electroacupuncture combined with methotrexate improved psoriasiform inflammation while regulating Th17/Treg balance and NF-κB activation.51 None of these psoriasis-related studies simultaneously measured the principal CAP circuit nodes; they therefore support downstream effects in psoriasis-like inflammation without demonstrating CAP engagement.

On this basis, when the clinical prescription structure of acupuncture for psoriasis is reconsidered within the neuro–immune regulatory framework proposed in this review, Qingre Liangxue acupuncture may be more appropriately understood as a composite body-surface input pattern formed by the coordinated use of core and adjunctive acupoints. Existing evidence suggests that the mechanical stimulation induced by acupuncture can be converted into bioelectrical and chemical signals at the levels of the skin and muscle, and may further act on nerve fibers, cytokines, and related receptor networks, thereby influencing peripheral and central transmission of pruritus.52 A randomized controlled trial protocol for acupuncture in psoriasis reported by Du et al adopted a combination of acupoint regions including Xuehai, Quchi, Zusanli, Sanyinjiao, and related adjunctive acupoints.19 This suggests that current clinical prescription design places greater emphasis on coordination between the limbs and trunk, as well as the parallel integration of local regulation and systemic intervention, rather than a simple additive effect of single acupoints. Within the pathological context of psoriasis, acupoint regions such as Quchi and Xuehai, which are relatively closely associated with lesion activity and pruritic phenotypes, may be more involved in the regulation of local neuro–immune interfaces. In contrast, adjunctive acupoints such as Zusanli, Sanyinjiao, and back-shu points may be more closely related to the reorganization of the systemic inflammatory background, autonomic regulatory state, and host response conditions. Accordingly, the modern significance of Qingre Liangxue acupuncture does not lie in assigning a single acupoint to an isolated molecular event. Rather, it may lie in the coordinated organization of body-surface inputs at different levels, which provides a more biologically plausible explanatory route for acupuncture signals to access CAP-related neuroimmune regulatory networks and to influence, at a more upstream level, the maintenance conditions of persistent inflammation in psoriasis. Given that the overall quality of existing systematic reviews on acupuncture for psoriasis remains limited, this interpretation is currently better regarded as an explanatory understanding that connects clinical prescription structure with available mechanistic evidence.53 It may also serve as the basis for the next section, which further discusses the role of different acupuncture modalities in differential pathway recruitment.

Differential Activation of CAP-Related Pathways by Different Acupuncture Modalities

Differences among acupuncture modalities should not be distinguished solely by technical nomenclature, but may be more appropriately understood in terms of the peripheral sensory input they generate, the stability of activation thresholds, and their likelihood of accessing specific autonomic reflex pathways. With respect to the current CAP-related evidence, more direct mechanistic support still mainly derives from electroacupuncture studies. This is not only because electroacupuncture has been widely used, but also because its stimulation intensity, frequency, and duration are relatively controllable, making it more suitable for observing how body-surface input is integrated into specific autonomic outputs. Liu et al showed that the recruitment of autonomic pathways by acupuncture input is body-region specific and intensity dependent. Hindlimb and abdominal stimulation do not enter entirely identical reflex networks, suggesting that body surface region and stimulation intensity may jointly influence which anti-inflammatory regulatory pathway acupuncture signals ultimately tend to engage.41 Kim et al further suggested that even within the same acupoint region, electroacupuncture at different frequencies may produce non-identical autonomic effects, indicating that frequency itself is also an important parameter influencing pathway differentiation.54 Zhang et al also showed in a sepsis model that the anti-inflammatory effect of electroacupuncture at ST25 was associated with a specific stimulation intensity range and adrenal sympathetic nerve-mediated processes.55 Together with the current understanding of neuromodulatory anti-inflammatory networks, the advantage of electroacupuncture may be better understood as its ability to generate quantifiable, reproducible somatosensory input that is relatively more likely to reach an effective activation threshold. It is therefore more suitable for demonstrating relatively clear pathway correspondence under experimental conditions. For psoriasis, this point is particularly relevant, because lesional inflammation is more likely to exist within a dynamic network continuously coupled with sensory nerve activity, neuropeptide release, and immune amplification.

Compared with electroacupuncture, manual acupuncture may also influence CAP-related autonomic regulation, but its input dose is more dependent on manipulation technique, deqi, needle retention time, practitioner experience, and the patient’s physiological state. As a result, fluctuations in stimulation threshold and response direction may occur more readily across different studies and individuals. Existing studies have reported that manual acupuncture may be accompanied by changes in heart rate variability, hemodynamics, and certain neurophysiological indicators, suggesting its potential to modulate autonomic activity.56,57 At the same time, relevant reviews have emphasized that the effects of manual acupuncture are clearly parameter dependent, although parameter quantification and standardization remain relatively insufficient.58 Together with current understanding of the cutaneous neuroimmune axis, a more cautious interpretation is not to define manual acupuncture simply as a modality “weaker than electroacupuncture”, but to regard it as a more practitioner-dependent and context-sensitive form of peripheral input.59 For psoriasis, this form of input may also act on inflammation-related neuro–immune interfaces. However, the current evidence remains insufficient to stably assign it to a specific CAP branch or to a fixed pattern of autonomic output.

The type of evidence supporting fire needling is not entirely the same as that for electroacupuncture or manual acupuncture. Current clinical evidence on fire needling for psoriasis mainly derives from systematic reviews and quantitative syntheses, suggesting that it may be associated with improvement in skin lesions. However, conclusions regarding recurrence control and long-term stability of therapeutic effects remain limited by the quality and heterogeneity of the included studies, and are therefore insufficient to support definitive mechanistic inference.60 From the perspective of experimental research, acupuncture, electroacupuncture, and fire needling have all been shown to improve imiquimod-induced psoriasis-like lesions in mice, reduce epidermal thickness, and attenuate local inflammation. However, electroacupuncture exerted more pronounced effects on some inflammatory indicators and neuropeptide-related molecules.46 Another study suggested that fire needling reduced psoriasis-like lesion severity, epidermal thickness, Ki-67 expression, and the levels of IL-17, IL-22, TNF-α, and p-STAT3/STAT3, indicating that its effects may involve regulation of Th17/STAT3-related inflammatory pathways.61 Thus, fire needling is more likely to contain three components simultaneously: acupuncture-related sensory input, thermal stimulation, and local tissue responses. Its biological effects are not necessarily unrelated to CAP-associated networks, but the evidence at this stage more directly supports local anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative effects, rather than a clearly established pattern of upstream CAP pathway recruitment.

Different acupuncture modalities generate distinct peripheral input profiles, but available evidence does not support assigning any single modality to a specific CAP branch. Electroacupuncture currently provides the clearest experimental platform for pathway mapping; manual acupuncture is more strongly influenced by practitioner technique and clinical adaptation; and fire needling combines local tissue stimulation with anti-inflammatory and antiproliferative effects. Downstream Effector Translation Mediated by Acupuncture

The preceding discussion focused on how acupuncture input may access neuroimmune circuits related to the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. The remaining question is how such upstream regulation might translate into pathological changes within psoriatic lesions. Disease persistence reflects interacting keratinocyte–immune and stromal programs, including IL-23/IL-17- and IL-36-related signaling and fibroblast-supported inflammatory amplification.62,63 Psoriasis-like experimental studies further indicate that oxidative stress can interact with epidermal abnormalities and local inflammatory activity.64 Keratinocyte-derived cytokines, chemokines, extracellular vesicles, and metabolic signals then sustain macrophage and neutrophil recruitment and reinforce intercellular inflammatory loops.65–69 Neurogenic inputs and vascular remodeling provide additional support for lesion maintenance,38,47,70,71 while persistent keratinocyte states and tissue-resident immune memory contribute to recurrence after apparent clinical resolution.72–77 Within this context, any downstream effect of Qingre Liangxue acupuncture would be expected to involve coordinated modulation of these maintenance processes rather than a transient reduction in a single inflammatory marker.

The persistent activity of psoriatic lesions is largely associated with the bidirectional amplification network formed between keratinocytes and immune cells. Current evidence suggests that keratinocytes are not merely terminal target cells. Under inflammatory signals such as IL-17 and TNF-α, they can integrate and amplify pathogenic transcriptional programs, induce the expression of multiple cytokines, chemokines, and related effector molecules, and thereby continuously participate in immune cell recruitment and local inflammatory maintenance. Kamata et al showed that IL-17 and TNF-α synergistically upregulate a group of psoriasis-associated inflammatory genes in keratinocytes, indicating that keratinocytes themselves may constitute a key integrative node for pathological amplification.65 Goldstein et al further found that IL-36R signaling in keratinocytes plays an important role in early IL-23, IL-17, and IL-22 expression, as well as neutrophil infiltration, in psoriasis-like inflammation.66 In addition, Xu et al demonstrated that an IL-17-induced autocrine IL-25 loop in keratinocytes promotes keratinocyte proliferation through STAT3 and enhances the production of inflammatory factors and chemokines, thereby contributing to the sustained amplification of psoriasis-like dermatitis.67 At the level of intercellular communication, Jiang et al observed that LRG1-enriched extracellular vesicles released by psoriasis-like keratinocytes promoted macrophage recruitment and M1-like polarization, and upregulated the expression of IL-23A, IL-17A, IL-1β, and TNF-α. Hao et al further found that upregulation of FABP5 in keratinocytes promoted neutrophil chemotaxis and infiltration through VCP-related NF-κB signaling.68,69 These findings suggest that acupuncture-mediated downstream effects should not be understood merely as a passive reduction in local inflammatory burden, but may instead reflect multi-level regulation of keratinocyte-driven immune recruitment, intercellular amplification loops, and pathological maintenance states.

If this upstream regulation is to be further translated into a more stable remission of psoriatic lesions, the key may lie not only in suppressing the inflammatory output of immune cells, but also in restoring local cholinergic braking signals, together with the consequent remodeling of keratinocyte renewal programs, the inflammatory vascular niche, and the threshold of recurrence-associated memory. Chen et al showed that selective activation of α7nAChR alleviated inflammatory responses in both an imiquimod-induced psoriasis-like model and in vitro keratinocytes, improved epidermal thickening, and corrected aberrant differentiation markers such as K16 and K17, with these effects being associated with inhibition of STAT3 and NF-κB signaling.16 Foffi et al further found that B cell-derived acetylcholine could reduce keratinocyte proliferation and attenuate cutaneous inflammation through keratinocyte α9nAChR, suggesting that cholinergic signaling within psoriatic lesions should not be understood solely in terms of classical immune suppression, but also as directly participating in the maintenance of epidermal renewal rhythm and barrier homeostasis.17

At the same time, the persistent state of psoriasis is also jointly supported by an abnormal vascular microenvironment and recurrence-associated local memory. Endothelial dysfunction in psoriasis has both pathogenic and disease-driven features, while VEGFA-related signaling not only drives inflammatory angiogenesis, but may also further reinforce keratinocyte- and infiltrating immune cell-associated responses, thereby collectively maintaining the conditions for lesional inflammatory amplification.70,71 More broadly, even after clinical lesions have resolved, local tissues may retain inflammatory memory involving tissue-resident memory T cells, antigen-presenting cells, and structural cells, while keratinocytes themselves are also associated with phenotypic switching and the maintenance of recurrence susceptibility.72–77 On this basis, it may be inferred that the downstream effects of acupuncture following CAP recruitment are more likely to converge on the integrated regulation of aberrant keratinocyte programs, inflammatory vascular niches, and recurrence-associated local memory. This also means that the connection between upstream neural regulation and improvement of terminal lesional phenotypes may be better understood as the loosening and remodeling of local pathological maintenance conditions.

Taken together, the available studies support an evidence-bounded model in which Qingre Liangxue acupuncture may influence autonomic and local neuroimmune regulation and, downstream, immune recruitment, keratinocyte inflammatory programs, neuropeptide-associated amplification, vascular support, and recurrence-related tissue states. The evidence does not yet form a direct causal chain from acupuncture input to CAP activation and durable clinical remission in plaque psoriasis. This distinction provides the basis for the translational limitations considered below.

Evidence Boundaries and Translational Considerations

Clinical studies of acupuncture for psoriasis remain limited by heterogeneity in acupoint selection, needling technique, stimulation dose, treatment frequency, treatment duration, comparator design, and follow-up.53,60 Practitioner dependence is especially relevant to manual acupuncture, while sham procedures may themselves produce sensory and physiological effects and do not always provide an inert control. Blinding of practitioners is generally infeasible, participant blinding can be incomplete, sample sizes are commonly small, and reporting of concomitant dermatologic therapy is inconsistent. Consequently, reported changes in lesion severity, pruritus, or recurrence should not be interpreted as evidence of durable disease modification without adequately powered, prospectively registered, sham-controlled trials and longer follow-up.

Direct VNS and acupuncture should also be distinguished mechanistically. Invasive or transcutaneous VNS applies defined stimulation to the vagus nerve or an intended vagal territory, whereas acupuncture initiates an indirect somatosensory input whose autonomic output depends on body region, tissue depth, stimulation parameters, and physiological context.20–22,40–42,78–81 Evidence that either intervention influences inflammatory activity does not establish pathway equivalence. Recent dermatologic VNS evidence includes an invasive VNS experiment in an imiquimod-induced psoriasis-like model32 and a review integrating emerging preclinical and early clinical observations.33 This literature supports the modifiability of cutaneous inflammation by vagal stimulation but remains insufficient to demonstrate that acupuncture engages the same circuit in human plaque psoriasis.

Psoriasis is clinically and molecularly heterogeneous, and recent transcriptomic analyses have identified gene signatures associated with disease endotypes and severity.82 Age at onset, metabolic comorbidity, pruritus burden, prior systemic treatment, and molecular endotype may plausibly modify autonomic state or responsiveness to cholinergic modulation, but this possibility has not been directly tested. A single CAP-centered response pattern should therefore not be assumed across all patients. Future clinical studies should prespecify plaque-psoriasis populations and concomitant therapies, standardize and fully report acupuncture parameters, incorporate credible sham controls, and assess PASI, pruritus, quality of life, adverse events, maintenance of response, and recurrence. Mechanistic validation would be strengthened by concurrent measurement of heart-rate variability or other autonomic indices, lesional α7nAChR and α9nAChR expression, local cytokine profiles, and keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation markers.

Conclusions and Outlook

Qingre Liangxue acupuncture is best understood as a therapeutic principle implemented through related acupoint prescriptions rather than as a single fixed intervention. CAP offers a biologically plausible framework through which such somatosensory input might influence upstream autonomic regulation and local immune–epidermal responses.78,81,83 Within this framework, α7nAChR is more closely associated with restraint of myeloid and keratinocyte inflammatory signaling, whereas α9nAChR is more closely associated with normalization of keratinocyte turnover and maintenance of epidermal homeostasis.16,17 However, direct evidence that acupuncture recruits these pathways in human plaque psoriasis remains insufficient. The proposed CAP–acupuncture–psoriasis axis should therefore be regarded as a testable explanatory framework, not as a confirmed causal pathway or the sole mechanism of acupuncture.

Clinical translation requires standardized and fully reported acupoint prescriptions, needling or electroacupuncture parameters, treatment schedules, concomitant therapies, and sham-control procedures. Adequately powered trials in plaque psoriasis should prespecify PASI, pruritus, quality of life, adverse events, maintenance of response, and recurrence outcomes. Within the same participants, heart-rate variability or other autonomic measures should be integrated with lesional α7nAChR and α9nAChR expression, local cytokine profiles, and markers of keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation, including Ki-67, K16, and K17. Such multimodal longitudinal designs would help clarify whether autonomic changes precede local cholinergic and immune–epidermal remodeling and whether these biological changes are associated with clinically meaningful benefit.
