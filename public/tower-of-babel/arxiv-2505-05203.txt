LAPSO : A Unified Optimization View for L earning- A ugmented P ower S ystem O perations
Wangkun Xu, Zhongda Chu, Fei Teng

Abstract
With the high penetration of renewables, traditional model-based power system operation is challenged to deliver economic, stable, and robust decisions. Machine learning has emerged as a powerful modeling tool for capturing complex dynamics to address these challenges. However, its separate design often lacks systematic integration with existing methods. To fill the gap, this paper proposes a holistic framework of Learning-Augmented Power System Operations (LAPSO, pronounced as Lap-So ). Adopting a native optimization perspective, LAPSO is centered on the operation stage and aims to break the boundary between temporally siloed power system tasks, such as forecast, operation and control, while unifying the objectives of machine learning and model-based optimizations at both training and inference stages. Systematic analysis and simulations demonstrate the effectiveness of applying LAPSO in designing new integrated algorithms, such as stability-constrained optimization (SCO) and objective-based forecasting (OBF), while enabling end-to-end tracing of different sources of uncertainties. In addition, a dedicated Python package- lapso is introduced to automatically augment existing power system optimization models with learnable components. All code and data are available at https://github.com/xuwkk/lapso_exp .

Furthermore, although tasks are temporally connected, in which the earlier decisions inform subsequent ones, the conventional decision-making framework often designs these tasks in isolation, without fully accounting for their interdependencies. From an operational perspective, siloed decision-making does not fully leverage grid flexibility to meet growing economic and stability demands. For example, renewable forecasting is typically accuracy-driven, but this isolated design overlooks its cascading impact on the economics of downstream PSOs

Moreover, due to the different characteristics and objectives of ML and optimization, most existing algorithms are developed on a case-by-case basis. Analytically, the relationship between ML and model-based methods remains opaque in the absence of a general framework for end-to-end performance evaluation and uncertainty quantification. This underscores a second key transformation: the need for

II
The LAPSO Framework

Power system decision-makings are composed of sequentially connected tasks with different time resolutions, such as forecast/modeling, operation, and control. This paper centers on a sequence of power system operation problems, each of which can be compactly written as an optimization problem,

\boxed{\mathcal{P}_{basic}}:\quad\min_{\bm{z}}\;f(\bm{z};\bm{y})\quad\text{s.t.}\;\bm{g}(\bm{z};\bm{y})\leq\bm{0}\\

The problem
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
is considered as
basic operation
where
\bm{z}
are decision variables and
\bm{y}
are parameters such as load and renewable profiles. In this study,
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
is restricted to a partially convex mixed-integer optimization problem, which means that
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
becomes convex if integers are fixed. Note that the general formulation can be extended to nonconvex settings.

With the increasing penetration of renewable energy and IBRs, to ensure the stability and robust operation of the system, new optimization components, such as new parameters and constraints, have been added to
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
. Most of the recent advances are in data-driven approaches in which the uncertainty in renewable and complex IBR dynamics can be adequately captured due to their strong representative capacity. When such model is encoded into
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
, a new
learning-augmented operation
model can be constructed,

\boxed{\mathcal{P}_{lapso}}:\quad\min_{\bm{z}}\;\tilde{f}(\bm{z};\bm{y},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\quad\text{s.t.}\;\tilde{\bm{g}}(\bm{z};\bm{y},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\leq\bm{0}

where the objective and constraints are extended to include machine learning parameter
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
.

Definition 1
.

A power system operation
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
is learning augmented if some of its components, e.g. objective and constraints, are represented by parametric models, as in
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
.

From the optimization point of view, one unique characteristic is that
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
maintains the original structure of model-based optimization
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
. Moreover, the optimality by solving
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
is guaranteed by proper design, which is more interpretable and reliable, compared to pure machine learning techniques.
Nonetheless, for a proper design of
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
, the following principle is summarized,

“In the LAPSO framework, the ML model is trained so that the resultant
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
can fully respect the sequence, structure, objective, constraints, etc. of the original PSO
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
s."

Based on this principle, a design triangle is illustrated in Fig.
3
. Under different applications, the modeling target can be the dynamics of renewable energy, stability indices and/or control laws; The ML techniques include both traditional ML such as linear regression, decision trees, etc and more advanced NNs; The type of
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
represents the optimization structure of PSOs such as UC, ED, AC OPF, etc. When ML is considered as a modeling tool, a suitable ML technique needs to be selected to better represent the modeling target, in terms of inference accuracy and generalization to unseen scenarios. The perspective of ML as learnable component of optimization requires a new trade-off upon the traditional ML use case: the impact of encoded ML model on the complexity, optimality, and conservatism of
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
needs to be carefully controlled. To demonstrate the flexibility of the LAPSO framework and how the above principle can be used to design specific LAPSO applications, this paper uses SCO and OBF as examples.

Figure 3
:
The design triangle for LAPSO. Three entities, including targets, ML techniques, and PSO
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
interact with each other.

III
Design on Special Types of LAPSO

III-A

Stability-Constrained Optimization

III-A
1
Formulation

To encode stability constraints at the operation stage, an ML model
u(\bm{z};\bm{y},\bm{\theta}_{u}^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}
, as a map from decision variables and parameters into the stability index, is trained offline to assess the grid stability. The training loss can be written as

\boxed{\mathcal{P}^{sco}_{train}}:\quad\min_{\bm{\theta}_{u}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{(\bm{z},\bm{y})\times\xi\in\mathcal{D}}-H(\xi,u(\bm{z},\bm{y};\bm{\theta}_{u}))

where
H
is the binary cross entropy loss;
(\bm{z},\bm{y})\times\xi\in\mathcal{D}
is a sampled data with
\xi\leq 0
representing a stable sample. Because
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{sco}
does not interact with the
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
during training, it is referred to as
accuracy-based
or
open-loop
training. Once it is trained,
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
becomes,

\boxed{\mathcal{P}^{sco}_{inf}}:\quad\min_{\bm{z}}\;f(\bm{z})\quad\text{s.t.}\;\bm{g}(\bm{z};\bm{y})\leq\bm{0},u(\bm{z},\bm{y};\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{u})\leq 0

i.e., a new stability constraint
u(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{u}^{\star})
is added to
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
.

III-A
2
Design Principle

The complexity of a parametric
u(\cdot;\bm{\theta})
is controlled by the hypothesis/structure space to which it belongs and the number of parameters
[
25
]
. By considering the stability constraint as a learnable component in an optimization problem, the LAPSO framework can provide new angles for controlling its complexity. Referring to Fig.
3
, the complexity determines,

•

Accuracy and Generalization.
When ML in
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{sco}
is considered as a modeling tool, its complexity determines the accuracy and generalization of the assessor and will eventually be reflected in real-time grid stability performance.

•

Complexity, Optimality, and Conservatism.
When unified as a learnable component in
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
, the structure of
u(\cdot;\bm{\theta})
strongly determines the convergence and computational burden of the solution algorithm, and the conservativeness will inevitably influence the operation cost.

Based on the above analysis, learning for stability constraint becomes an
optimization-aware multi-objective model selection
process such that,

“A good stability assessor should not overly complicate the type of
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
and increase the operational cost while achieving a high assessment accuracy."

For example, if the original problem is a mixed integer linear program (MILP), such as the standard UC, the stability constraint should ideally be a convex function (such as linear or second-order cone) or at least can be represented as a mixed integer linear (MIL) function such that
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
has the same optimization type as
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
. Such consideration has been explored in previous research
[
8
,
26
,
3
,
9
]
, etc., by training a convex assessor. Otherwise, the global optimality of
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
may not be achieved. Therefore, explicit approximation and iterative algorithm may be required
[
10
]
. However, since the actual stability criterion is not a convex function of
\bm{z}
and
\bm{y}
or can only be achieved by solving ad-hoc differential equations, there always exist unstable samples that cannot be detected.
In addition, due to strong non-linearity, NNs cannot be easily encoded in
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
and directly solved as a mathematical program
[
10
]
. When an NN-based assessor
u(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{u}^{\star})
is composed of piecewise linear layers and activations, it can be equivalently reformulated as a set of mixed integer linear (MIL) constraints as a function of input
(\bm{z},\bm{y})
(see our previous work
[
17
]
).

III-B

Objective-based Forecasting

In this section, the LAPSO framework is demonstrated to effectively train the energy forecaster to align with the cost of a sequence of power system operations.

III-B
1
Formulation

The OBF inference problem specializes
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
as,

\boxed{\mathcal{P}^{obf/basic}_{inf}}:\quad\min_{\bm{z}}\;f(\bm{z})\quad\text{s.t.}\;\tilde{\bm{g}}(\bm{z})\leq\bm{F}\cdot\underbrace{\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{h})}_{\hat{{\bm{y}}}}

where
\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{h})
is a trained energy forecaster with parameter
\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{h}
and input feature
\bm{x}
(e.g. previous energy profile, weather forecast and calender information, etc);
\bm{F}
is a matrix with suitable dimension, highlighting the affine relationship to
\hat{\bm{y}}
.

Similarly to the SCO,
\bm{h}(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{h})
can be trained independently to maximize the forecast accuracy, as
accuracy-based forecast
:

\boxed{\mathcal{P}^{abf}_{train}}:\quad\min_{\bm{\theta}_{h}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{\bm{x}\times\bm{y}\in\mathcal{D}}\|\bm{y}-\hat{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}

where
\bm{y}
is the true load or renewable profile.
However, to better align the forecast error with the more economic operation cost,
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
can be explicitly encoded during training. Therefore, it is named as
objective-based forecasting
,
smart predict-and-optimize
, or
closed-loop forecast
. Under this setting, the training stage reformulation becomes the following bi-level optimization problem,

\displaystyle\boxed{\mathcal{P}^{obf/basic}_{train}}:\quad\min_{\bm{\theta}_{h}}

\displaystyle\sum_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{D}}\ell(\hat{\bm{z}},\hat{\bm{y}},\bm{y})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\hat{\bm{z}};\hat{\bm{y}},\bm{y})\leq\bm{0}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{y}}=\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{h}),\quad\forall(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
is mathematical program with equilibium constraints (MPEC)
[
27
]
in which the lower level problem for each sample
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is considered as equilibrium constraint of single or a sequence of
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
s. The implicit constraint
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
takes the forecast
\hat{\bm{y}}
and the true
\bm{y}
as inputs, whose optimal decision
\hat{\bm{z}}
will be reflected as empirical objective in the upper level. The idea of
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
is straightforward, that is, to find forecaster parameter
\bm{\theta}_{h}
such that a realistic empirical economic cost is optimized subject to the real-time inference problem
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
over the entire sample space.

III-B
2
Design Principle

We discuss
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
and
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
in terms of forecasting accuracy, training loss, complexity, and the corresponding solution algorithm based on design triangle in Fig.
3
.

Choice of Training Loss.
To start, the choice of training loss
\ell(\cdot)
of
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
should respect the objective, functionality and the decision sequence of
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
s. When a multistage setting is considered and the actual operating cost is settled, such as UC/dispatch (DP) followed by economic dispatch (ED) or day-ahead bidding followed by real-time clearing in the energy market,
\ell(\cdot)
is composed of the exact settled cost associated to the sequence of
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}

[
14
,
15
,
17
,
16
,
20
]
. In the literature, it is commonly referred to as
self-supervised
learning as the true settled cost (the label) is not explicitly shown in
\ell(\cdot)
. However, in the authors’ opinion,
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
is still “supervised”, as the true parameter
\bm{y}
is included as the label in
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
. In contrast, when the cost is not settled and/or only part of the decision making chain is considered, a
supervised
loss
\ell(\cdot)
is designed in which the true sample-wise decision or objective of
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
is applied as the label
[
28
,
18
,
29
]
, which is also known as
regret
loss.

Accuracy and Generalization.
Moreover, unlike the traditional ML-based forecaster, the
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
is directly associated to the specific choice of
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
, which demonstrates another layer of uncertainties if
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
at inference stage is different to it considered during training. An immediate example is that the different choices of training loss will result in different optimal forecaster parameters, which cannot be well generalized between each other
[
17
]
. More discussion is presented in the next section.

Complexity, Optimality, and Conservatism.
In addition, it is observed that the decision
\bm{z}
and the forecaster
\hat{\bm{y}}
are disentangled in
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
, making the parametric program affine. Note that this property does not exist in the SCO case
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
. In addition, the affine structure of
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
suggests that, in the inference stage,
\mathcal{P}^{obf/basic}_{inf}
can also be decomposed into separate forecast
\hat{\bm{y}}=\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{h})
and optimization
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
, which uncovers the exact decision-making sequence in real time. Therefore, unlike the SCO case, the complexity of the forecaster
\bm{h}(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{h})
does not influence the efficiency of solving
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
.
However, solving the training stage problem
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
can be challenging, depending on complexity/structure of the machine learning model
h(\cdot;\bm{\theta})
and the type of optimization
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
. Some examples are highlighted in Table
I
. Notably, for convex
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
, the equilibrium
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
is represented as mathematical program with complementarity contraints through the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions at optimality
[
27
]
, which allows the mathematical program (MP) to be solved if the upper level of
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
is (partially) convex. In contrast, when NN-based forecaster is designed, implicit differentiation (ID) or differentiable optimization is needed to find the gradient through the optimization problem in the backward pass after the exact
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
is solved in the forward pass
[
30
]
.

III-C

Summary From the Unification View

In this section, two distinct applications, namely SCO and OBF, are discussed. Although their objectives and training methodologies differ, both are centered on the operation stage and represent specialized instantiations of the LAPSO framework. In both cases, ML is used as a
system modeling
or system identification method that improves decision making performance at different stages. Specifically, renewable forecasting captures time series dynamics, whereas stability constraints reflect the dynamic behavior of IBRs (see Fig.
2
). Moreover, the triangle provided in Fig.
3
demonstrates a highly unified analytical procedure for designing ML as a
learnable component of optimizations
.

Although the paper focuses on SCO and OBF, the LAPSO is broadly applicable. For example, optimization models can serve as valuation metrics in data markets
[
31
,
33
]
. OBF can be extended to general data-driven control frameworks for microgrid optimization
[
34
]
and energy storage arbitrage
[
35
]
as long as the control problem is formulated as an optimization problem. Similarly, NN encoded as constraints can address challenges beyond stability, such as voltage regulation in AC OPF
[
36
]
, or modeling battery energy storage systems for a wide range of energy management tasks
[
37
]
.
To further illustrate the versatility of LAPSO, the next section presents extensions of SCO and OBF that support more flexible and uncertainty-aware designs, deepening the integration between model-based optimization and machine learning.

IV
Extend LAPSO to New Applications

IV-A

Extension to OBF with SCO

Referring to Fig.
1
, the LAPSO applications on SCO and OBF only integrate part of the forecast-operation-control chain. To close the loop, a more flexible formulation can be considered in the OBF
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
by replacing
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
with the corresponding
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
(see Fig.
2
). Then the inference problem becomes,

\displaystyle\boxed{\mathcal{P}^{obf/sco}_{inf}}:\;\min_{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle f(\bm{z})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{g}}(\bm{z})\leq\bm{F}\cdot\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{h}),\;u(\bm{z},\hat{\bm{y}};\bm{\theta}_{u}^{\star})\leq 0

Although more flexible training can be designed by jointly training
\bm{\theta}_{u}
and
\bm{\theta}_{h}
, we focus on fixed assessor parameter
\bm{\theta}_{u}^{\star}
trained by
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{sco}
and adapting
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
into the following training formulation,

\displaystyle\boxed{\mathcal{P}^{obf/sco}_{train}}:\quad\min_{\bm{\theta}_{h}}

\displaystyle\sum_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{D}}f^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{z}})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{sco}(\hat{\bm{z}};\hat{\bm{y}},\bm{y},\bm{\theta}_{u}^{\star})\leq\bm{0}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{y}}=\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{h}),\quad\forall(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{D}

where
\mathcal{F}_{sco}(\cdot)
represents the equilibrium conditions for
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
.

As discussed in Section
III-B2
, a forecaster trained to capture the characteristic of
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
is unlikely to achieve the same optimality as a forecaster trained with
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
. In general, this problem is summarized as the
uncertainty of the downstream optimization
. To evaluate the consistency of the two trained forecasters with different down-stream optimizations for one sample, the cosine similarity or Pearson correlation coefficient between the two gradients can be computed.

For
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{abf}
, the gradient can be computed either by analytical or automatic differentiation method,

\bm{g}^{abf}(\bm{x},\bm{y}):=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{h}}\|\bm{y}-\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{h}^{\star})\|_{2}^{2}

(1)

Moreover, let
p(\bm{x},\bm{y};\bm{\theta}_{h}^{\star})
be the value function of
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
or
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/sco}
on sample
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
. By taking the bilevel optimization view of LAPSO and the chain rule, the gradient is,

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{obf}(\bm{x},\bm{y}):=\partial_{3}p(\bm{x},\bm{y};\bm{\theta}_{h}^{\star})=\partial f^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{z}})\cdot\partial\hat{\bm{z}}(\hat{\bm{y}})\cdot\partial_{2}\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{h}^{\star})

(2)

\displaystyle-\partial f^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{z}})\cdot[\partial_{1}\mathcal{F}(\hat{\bm{z}};\hat{\bm{y}},\bm{y})]^{-1}\cdot\partial_{2}\mathcal{F}(\hat{\bm{z}};\hat{\bm{y}},\bm{y})\cdot\partial_{2}\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{h}^{\star})

if the equilibrium condition
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
follows the implicit function theorem
[

]
where
\partial_{\star}(\cdot)
represents the partial Jacobian operator to the
\star
-th element.

IV-B

Uncertainty-Aware LAPSO

In previous studies, it has been shown that the forecaster can be trained to fit different downstream optimization problems, such as
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf}
or
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
, and under different choices of losses, such as supervised or self-supervised settings, which introduces another layer of uncertainty into the LAPSO framework. However, considering the different structures of optimizations is intractable and can be meaningless. Therefore, this section regards
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
as an integrated system and investigates its uncertainty by fixing its structural class.

IV-B
1
Source of Uncertainty

In the integrated framework
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
, the sources of uncertainties originate from both learning (
ML-Uncertainty
) and optimization (
Opt-Uncertainty
). To simplify the analysis, the following discussion is made on OBF while the generalization to
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
is straightforward. From a Bayesian perspective, the uncertainty on the ML forecaster
\hat{\bm{y}}=\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{h}^{\star})
is classified as
epistemic/model
uncertainty and
aleatoric/data uncertainty

[
38
]
. The epistemic uncertainty captures the ignorance of the forecaster on unseen data while the aleatoric uncertainty models the inherent noise of the data itself. In optimization, the uncertainty occurs on its “data”. To avoid ambiguity, we continue to use the term parameter as before. Clearly, both sources of ML uncertainties eventually become the uncertainty of
\hat{\bm{y}}
and have an impact on the robustness of
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
.

Moreover, by rewriting
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
as

\boxed{\mathcal{P}^{obf/uncer}_{inf}}:\min_{\bm{z}}f(\bm{z};\bm{\phi})\;\text{s.t.}\;\tilde{\bm{g}}(\bm{z};\bm{\phi})\leq\bm{F}(\bm{\phi})\cdot\underbrace{\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{h})}_{\hat{{\bm{y}}}}

with a new set of uncertain parameter
\bm{\phi}\in\bm{\Phi}
, whose formulation
does not
reply on the ML forecaster, as distinguished from parameter
\hat{\bm{y}}
explicitly. Since this uncertainty is more intrinsic to optimization and less coupled to the decision variable
\bm{\theta}_{h}
, it is named as
Opt-Uncertainty
and the robustness analysis on
\bm{\phi}
should not consider the impact of propagated uncertainty from the ML model. An illustration of different sources of uncertainties and their propagation within LAPSO framework is highlighted in Fig.
4
.

Figure 4
:
Source and propagation of uncertainties in
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obf/basic}
. Similar structure can be drawn for general
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
. Stochastic formulation is taken while others, such as (distributional) robust formulations, can also be generalized to.

IV-B
2
Timings of Uncertainty Realization

Depending on the realization time of the uncertainty and the actions taken in
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{obj/uncer}
, both
here-and-now
and
wait-and-see
formulations can be taken but with different impact on the inference problems. Referring to Fig.
4
, most studies consider ML-Uncertainty under the here-and-now setting, such as probabilistic load forecast with stochastic generator dispatch
[
22
]
and the impact of the input data/aleatoric uncertainty on the optimizations
[
17
,
23
]
. Opt-Uncertainty is considered in
[
24
]
but the impact does not reflect in
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
. Therefore, this paper formulates a new
wait-and-see
robust formulation on
Opt-Uncertainty
where
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
does not directly hedge against the realization of uncertainty. In detail, consider

\displaystyle\boxed{\mathcal{P}^{obf/uncer}_{train}}:\min_{\bm{\theta}_{h}}

\displaystyle\sum_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{\bm{\phi}\in\bm{\Phi}(\bm{x},\bm{y})}\ell(\hat{\bm{z}},\hat{\bm{y}},\bm{y},\bm{\phi})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\hat{\bm{z}};\hat{\bm{y}},\bm{y},\bm{\phi})\leq\bm{0}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{y}}=\bm{h}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{h})\quad\forall(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{D}

where a sample-sensitive uncertainty set
\bm{\Phi}
on optimization parameter
\bm{\phi}
is assigned. The robust formulation ensures that the worst-uncertainty realization on the upper-level objective is compensated when training the forecaster
\bm{h}(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{h})
.

V
Overview of
LAPSO
Package

By observing the frequent interaction between ML and optimizations in both training (such as
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf}
) and inference (such as
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
) stages in LAPSO framework, a dedicated Python package
lapso
is proposed. This package is built upon
CVXPY
, a Python library to model disciplined convex optimization problems. It enables power system optimizations to be formulated in a natural, mathematically intuitive form and solved by most of the back-end solvers. The main purpose of the
lapso
package is to automatically and efficiently extract the structures of the
existing
power system optimization problem
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
, along with a neural network model, and compile them into extended form
\mathcal{P}_{lapso}
, which is then recompiled by
CVXPY
. Currently, the module
lapso.optimization
supports affine parametric quadratic and linear programs, and directly generates the KKT conditions as constraints in problem like
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
,
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/sco}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/uncer}
. In addition, the
lapso.neuralnet
transforms any pytorch
nn.sequential
modules or more complex nested structure composed of piecewise linear layers such as linear, convolutional, max-pooling layers with ReLU activations into the set of mixed-integer linear constraints, such as
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
and
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{sco}
.

Moreover, to facilitate benchmarking of new LAPSO applications, another package
pso
is open-sourced for generating standard power system testbed from
PyPower
, enriched with appropriate nodal weather, load, solar, and wind data from open-source dataset
[
39
]
. Examples of using
lapso
for SCO, OBF, and their variants are demonstrated in the next section and more instructions can be found in the GitHub repository.

VI
Case Study Settings

To demonstrate the potential of LAPSO to unify existing research and identify new challenges, this paper presents several detailed case studies. All simulations utilize the IEEE 14-bus 5-generator system, which further incorporates four integrated solar panels at buses 5, 11, 13, and 14. The grid configuration,
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
s, and preprocessed nodal weather, load, and solar dataset is generated from
pso
package for a year with one-hour resolution (8760 samples in total).

VI-A

SCO

A standard day-ahead mixed-integer linear UC problem, which consists of generator, reserves, and transmission line constraints, is considered as the
\mathcal{P}_{basic}
in this case study. Particularly, the small-signal stability constraint is considered for evaluating SCO. To efficiently assess the grid strength, generalized Short-Circuit Ratio (gSCR) based small-signal stability criterion is considered, e.g.,

\text{gSCR}=\lambda_{min}\left(\operatorname{diag}\left({\bm{v}_{r}^{2}}/{\bm{p}_{r}}\right)\bm{Y}_{red}\right)\geq\text{gSCR}_{lim}

(3)

in which
\operatorname{diag}\left({\bm{v}_{r}^{2}}/{\bm{p}_{r}}\right)
is the diagonal matrix of IBR terminal voltage
\bm{v}_{r}
and invertor output power
\bm{p}_{r}
;
\bm{Y}_{red}
is the reduced nodal admittance matrix after eliminating passive buses and
infinite buses, which is dependent on the topology of the grid, such as generator on/off status
\bm{u}_{g}
and the location of IBRs.
\text{gSCR}_{lim}
is the critical gSCR value, which can be defined in advance. Detailed derivation can be found in
[
3
]
.

Although (
3
) is derived in analytical form, directly encoding into UC with iterative and approximation solution algorithm can cause sub-optimal solution and non-convergence issue, due to the nonlinearity and nonconvexity of the eigenvalue problem. Therefore, data-driven approaches are adopted as the stability assessment model. The training dataset
\mathcal{D}
is prepared on a complete combination of generator status and renewable energy profiles. In detail, each solar profile is sampled 5 times evenly within its maximum generation. Therefore, the total number of samples is equal to
(2^{5}-1)\times 5^{4}=19375
. To better represent the stability boundary, marginally stable samples, whose small signal stability indices are slightly above
\text{gSCR}_{lim}
, are labeled as unstable.

As a binary classification problem, standard logistic regression (LR) with binary cross entropy loss is first considered as a candidate stability assessor. Moreover, a constrained logistic regression problem (cLR) can be solved analytically for
\bm{\theta}_{u}=[\bm{w}_{u},b_{u}]
,

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{w}_{u},b_{u}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{stable}|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}_{stable}}\log(1+e^{\bm{w}_{u}^{T}\bm{p}_{r}^{k}+b_{u}})

(4)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{T}\bm{p}_{r}^{k}+b_{u}\geq 0,\quad k\in\mathcal{D}_{unstable}

where
\mathcal{D}_{stable}
and
\mathcal{D}_{unstable}
are the stable and unstable sub-dataset. As a hyperplane is placed between the stable and unstable samples in LR, a conservative constraint is added in cLR to ensure that all unstable samples are classified correctly. Although (
4
) is always feasible, it will inevitably increase the False Positive Rate (FPR) on stable samples, which will be demonstrated in simulations. In addition, NN-based stability assessors with various number of linear layers and ReLU activations are trained by
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{sco}
to represent the stability criterion. Note that the sigmoid function is considered as part of the BCE loss so that the trained NN can be represented as mixed-integer linear constraints.

Once the data-driven assessor is trained, the stability constraint
u([\bm{u}_{g}^{T},(\bm{p}_{r}-\bm{p}_{c})^{T}]^{T};\bm{\theta}_{u}^{\star})\leq 0
is encoded into
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
where
\bm{p}_{r}
and
\bm{p}_{c}
are the renewable generation and the curtailment decision, respectively. The
\mathcal{P}_{inf}^{sco}
can be conveniently implemented by the

OBF/Uncer

In (
5
), the loads are assumed to be well known during the
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}

s are the same for both DP and RD. In a more realistic setting, the load forecast can be provided by different operators, and the uncertainty of which should be compensated during training. In detail, at the RD stage, it is assumed that the load is subject to a bounded sample-dependent
\ell_{\infty}
uncertainty set, e.g.,
\bm{\phi}_{rd}^{i}\in[\underline{\bm{\phi}}_{rd}^{i},\bar{\bm{\phi}}_{rd}^{i}],\forall i=1,\cdots,|\mathcal{D}|
. Note that when
(\bm{W}_{h},\bm{b}_{h})
are fixed, (
5
) becomes single level RD as
\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i},\forall i=1,\cdots,|\mathcal{D}|
in the dispatch problem can be optimized explicitly for given
\hat{\bm{y}}^{i}
. Therefore, the worst case for each sample
i
is obtained independently as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i}):=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\tilde{f}_{dp}(\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i})}_{\text{Const.}}+\max_{\bm{\phi}_{rd}^{i}\in\bm{\Phi}_{rd}^{i}}f_{rd}(\Delta\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i},\hat{\bm{z}}_{rd}^{i})

(6)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i},\hat{\bm{z}}_{rd}^{i}=\arg\min\{f_{rd}(\Delta\bm{p}_{g},\bm{z}_{rd}):

\displaystyle(\Delta\bm{p}_{g},\bm{z}_{rd})\in\mathcal{C}_{rd}(\bm{y}^{i},\bm{\phi}^{i}_{rd},\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i})\}

with given
\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i},\forall i=1,\cdots,|\mathcal{D}|
. Because (
6
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{dp}(\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i})+\max_{\bm{\phi}_{rd}^{i}\in\bm{\Phi}_{rd}^{i}}\min_{\Delta\bm{p}_{g}^{i},\bm{z}_{rd}^{i}}f_{rd}(\Delta{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i},{\bm{z}}_{rd}^{i})

(7)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle(\Delta{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i},{\bm{z}}_{rd}^{i})\in\mathcal{C}_{rd}(\bm{y}^{i},\bm{\phi}^{i}_{rd},\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i})

an equivalent single level problem can be solved by either transforming the lower level of (
6
) as the KKT equilibrium or replacing the inner minimization in (
7
) by its dual problem.

Considering a “wait-and-see” setting to hedge against the worst-case load realization at RD (
6
), one formulation of
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/uncer}
can be designed as,

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{w}_{h},\bm{b}_{h}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\tilde{f}_{dp}(\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i})+\max_{\bm{\phi}_{rd}^{i}\in\bm{\Phi}^{i}_{rd}}f_{rd}(\Delta\hat{\bm{p}}^{i}_{g},\hat{\bm{z}}_{rd}^{i})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i},\hat{\bm{z}}_{rd}^{i}=\arg\min\{f_{rd}(\Delta\bm{p}_{g},\bm{z}_{rd}):

\displaystyle(\Delta\bm{p}_{g},\bm{z}_{rd})\in\mathcal{C}_{rd}(\bm{y}^{i},\bm{\phi}^{i}_{rd},\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i})\}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i},\hat{\bm{z}}_{dp}^{i}=\arg\min\{f_{dp}(\bm{p}_{g},\bm{z}_{dp}):

\displaystyle(\bm{p}_{g},\bm{z}_{dp})\in\mathcal{C}_{dp}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{i},\bm{\phi}^{i})\}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{y}}^{i}=\bm{W}_{h}\bm{x}^{i}+\bm{b}_{h},\hskip 56.9055pt\forall i=1,\cdots,|\mathcal{D}|

which turns into a two-stage bilevel robust optimization. Note that this setting is fundamentally different from the commonly discussed “here-and-now” follower in which the DP hedges the ML-uncertainty by itself. Similarly to
\mathcal{P}_{train}^{obf/basic}
, the lower level DP is first represented by the KKT conditions (denoted as
\mathcal{DP}(\cdot)
) and linearized by the big-M method.
This paper then takes the column and constraint generation (C&CG) method as it is not difficult to show that the worst-case iteration number is
\mathcal{O}(2^{|\bm{\phi}|})
, which depends only on the cardinality of the discrete uncertainty set
\bm{\Phi}^{i}
.
The algorithm is designed based on
[
40
]
into Step 1. to 5. In detaisl,
Step 1.
Set
LB=-\infty
,
UB=+\infty
, and
k=0
.
Step 2.
Solve the main problem,

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{W}_{h},\bm{b}_{h},\eta^{i}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\tilde{f}_{dp}(\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i})+\eta^{i}

(8)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\eta^{i}\geq f_{rd}(\Delta\bm{p}_{g}^{i,(l)},\bm{z}_{rd}^{i,(l)}),\;\eta^{i}\geq 0

\displaystyle(\Delta\bm{p}_{g}^{i,(l)},\bm{z}_{rd}^{i,(l)})\in\mathcal{C}_{rd}(\bm{y}^{i},\bm{\phi}_{rd}^{i,(l),\star},\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i})\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{DP}(\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i},\hat{\bm{z}}_{dp}^{i};\bm{\phi}^{i},\hat{\bm{y}}^{i})\leq\bm{0},\hskip 14.22636pt\forall l=1,\cdots,k

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{y}}^{i}=\bm{W}_{h}\bm{x}^{i}+\bm{b}_{h},\hskip 14.22636pt\forall i=1,\cdots,|\mathcal{D}|

with optimum
(\bm{W}_{h}^{(k+1),\star},\bm{b}_{h}^{(k+1),\star},\eta^{(k+1),\star},\hat{\bm{p}}^{i,(k+1),\star}_{g},\forall i)
and update
LB=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\tilde{f}_{dp}(\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i,(k+1),\star})+\eta^{i}
.
Step 3.
For
\forall i=1,\cdots,|\mathcal{D}|
, solve (
6
) using the KKT reformulation for given
\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i,(k+1),\star}
and update
UB=\min\{UB,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\mathcal{Q}(\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i,(k+1),\star})\}
.
Step 4.
If
UB-LB\leq\epsilon
, return
(\bm{W}^{(k+1),\star},\bm{b}^{(k+1),\star})
and terminate. Otherwise, add a new set of
optimality cut
to (
8
), for
\forall i=1,\cdots,|\mathcal{D}|
,

\displaystyle\eta^{i}

\displaystyle\geq f_{rd}(\Delta\bm{p}_{g}^{i,(k+1)},\bm{z}_{r}^{i,(k+1)})

\displaystyle(\Delta\bm{p}_{g}^{i,(k+1)},\bm{z}_{rd}^{i,(k+1)})

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{C}_{rd}(\bm{y}^{i},\bm{\phi}_{rd}^{i,(k+1),\star},\hat{\bm{p}}_{g}^{i})\}

Step 5.
Set
k:=k+1
and return to Step 2.

In Step 3), the subproblems (
6
) can be solved in parallel. In Step 4), a new cut is added to the main problem if the gap between the upper and lower bounds is greater than a predefined threshold
\epsilon
. Note that, as slack variables have been added to the RD, (
6
) is always feasible. I.e., the
relatively complete recourse assumption
is always satisfied.

VIII
Conclusion

This paper proposes a unified framework for learning-augmented power system operations (LAPSO) and an open source package
lapso
to support the ongoing transformation of integrated ML-optimization nexus in power system decision making. Considering the ML as a dynamic modeling tool and a learnable component in optimization, LAPSO provides a comprehensive modeling language to explain the interaction between optimization, ML training, and inference problems. It also provides a unique angle on understanding the trade-off between the ML target, such as accuracy, and the optimization requirement, such as solution efficiency, conservatism, and economic compensation. Using SCO and OBF as examples, this paper demonstrates that the unification perspective can benefit designing new task-aware algorithms and evaluation metrics, as well as quantifying the new uncertainty sources and timings within the end-to-end setting.
