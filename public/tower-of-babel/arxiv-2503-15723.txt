Abstract
. Given an arbitrary subgraph H = H n H=H_{n} and p = p n ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p=p_{n}\in(0,1) , the planted subgraph model is defined as follows. A statistician observes the union of the “signal,” which is a random “planted” copy H ∗ H^{*} of H H , together with random noise in the form of an instance of an Erdős–Rényi graph G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) . Their goal is to then recover the planted H ∗ H^{*} from the observed graph. Our focus in this work is to understand the minimum mean squared error (MMSE), defined in terms of recovering the edges of H ∗ H^{*} , as a function of p p and H H , for sufficiently large n n . A recent paper [ MNS+23 ] characterizes the graphs for which the limiting (as n n grows) MMSE curve undergoes a sharp phase transition from 0 0 to 1 1 as p p increases, a behavior known as the all-or-nothing phenomenon, up to a mild density assumption on H H . However, their techniques fail to describe the MMSE curves for graphs that do not display such a sharp phase transition. In this paper, we provide a formula for the limiting MMSE curve for any graph H = H n H=H_{n} , up to the same mild density assumption. This curve is expressed in terms of a variational formula over pairs of subgraphs of H H , and is inspired by the celebrated subgraph expectation thresholds from the probabilistic combinatorics literature [ KK07 ] . Furthermore, we give a polynomial-time description of the optimizers of this variational problem. This allows one to efficiently approximately compute the MMSE curve for any dense graph H H when n n is large enough. The proof relies on a novel graph decomposition of H H as well as a new minimax theorem which may be of independent interest. Our results generalize to the setting of minimax rates of recovering arbitrary monotone boolean properties planted in random noise, where the statistician observes the union of a planted minimal element A ⊆ [ N ] A\subseteq[N] of a monotone property and a random Ber ⁡ ( p ) ⊗ N \operatorname{Ber}(p)^{\otimes N} vector. In this setting, we provide a variational formula inspired by the so-called “fractional” expectation threshold [ Tal10 ] , again describing the MMSE curve (in this case up to a multiplicative constant) for large enough n n .

Kahn–Kalai thresholds and Bayesian approaches.
An easy application of Bayes’ rule shows that the posterior distribution of the planted subgraph model is the uniform distribution among all the copies of
H=H_{n}
in the observed graph
G=H^{*}\cup G_{0},G_{0}\sim G(n,p)

H

G
that have a given overlap with the planted copy
H^{*}
.

Because the planted graph
H=H_{n}
may be arbitrary, naïve first and second moment method arguments fail to provide good estimates for these subgraph counts. To circumvent this issue, we derive inspiration from probabilistic combinatorics in the study of the “null” model

The expectation thresholds relate to the following question about subgraph counts: given an (unlabeled) graph

This has been studied for specific choices of

]

It should also be noted that these results generalize well beyond subgraph inclusion properties, and identify the critical thresholds for arbitrary monotone properties on a random discrete universe of points under the product Bernoulli measure.

While the original proof of tightness of the fractional expectation threshold in
[

]

Can one go the other direction, and use the expectation thresholds to characterize the MMSE for a large family of subgraph models?

Indeed, in this work, we prove that for large

1.1.
Main Contributions

As mentioned, our main result is a characterization of the MMSE for the planted subgraph model for all weakly dense graphs

Assuming the validity of the subgraph version of the Kahn–Kalai conjecture,

Beyond (weakly dense) graphs

Related work.
The limiting MMSE of various high dimensional statistical models has been analyzed in a number of recent influential works. Notable characterizations of the limiting MMSE include the exact computation for the spiked rank-one matrix model under Gaussian noise
[
DMK+16
,
LM17
]
(conjectured in
[
LKZ15
]
) and the random linear estimation problem with a Gaussian feature matrix
[
MT06
,
RP19
]
(conjectured in
[
Tan02
,
GV05
]
). In many cases, the approach begins with a non-rigorous yet remarkably precise calculation of the model’s free energy via the “replica” or “cavity” methods, which also yields a conjectured exact formula for the limiting MMSE.
In some cases, such as those mentioned above, the validity of these conjectured formulas has later been rigorously proven. However, our techniques fundamentally differ from those used here, and operate in a setting where typical assumptions in statistical physics literature do not hold. For instance, for our main result
Theorem

1.1
, the planted subgraph
H=H_{n}
is only assumed to be weakly dense for each
n
, and in particular no product-like structure is assumed on its entries as is often the case for statistical physics techniques. Moreover, for our general results on monotone properties (
Theorem

4.5
), no assumption whatsoever is made on the planted discrete structure other than it being of a known cardinality.

In another related line of work, the goal is to prove properties of the limiting MMSE curve for specific planted models. Notable examples include
[
RXZ20
]
that understood the limiting MMSE curve in sparse linear regression and proved the AoN phenomenon, and
[
DWXY23
,
GSXY25
]
that studied the MMSE for the planted matching problem. Our work again differs from these results in that it applies to a larger family of priors with much less structure—for instance, the planted matching model is a special case of our planted subgraph model with
H
a perfect matching so
Theorem

4.5
is directly applicable (as the perfect matching is not a weakly dense graph, we cannot apply the stronger
Theorem

3.1
). Moreover, our proof crucially differs in the proof techniques we employ to the previous works. Most of the above works are based on involved second moment methods (e.g., in

[
RXZ20
]
) or algorithmic construction arguments (e.g., in
[
DWXY23
]
). Yet, in this work we analyze the limiting MMSE curves by leveraging Bayesian techniques, such as the planting trick
[
ACO08
]
, alongside state-of-the-art combinatorics results and arguments using the structure of the (fractional) expectation thresholds, such as the duality trick of Talagrand
[
Tal10
]
. We hope that the combinatorics techniques introduced in this work will be useful in understanding more minimax rates or MMSE curves in the high-dimensional statistics literature.

2.1.
Graph Theory

We start by introducing a few definitions from graph theory.

First, as mentioned in the introduction, in this paper we identify a graph
T

V(T)
is the set of all vertices in

to refer to the number of edges in
T

T.
Finally, we define it’s

We now define the notion of a weakly dense graph.

Definition 2.1
.

Consider a sequence of graphs

. We say that a graph
H=H_{n}
is weakly dense if

\liminf_{n}\frac{|H_{n}|}{v(H_{n})\log v(H_{n})}=+\infty.

We often simplify notation in this work and refer to
H=H_{n}
as a graph, instead of a sequence of graphs indexed by
n
.

Now, we introduce a non-standard definition in graph theory, which we term a “graph-cut,” which can intuitively be viewed as a graph “modulo” a specific subset of its vertices. This definition will be useful in understanding the properties of the onion decomposition of a graph
H
(see
Section

5
).

Definition 2.2
.

A “graph-cut” on
n
vertices is defined by a triplet
J=(V,S,E)
, where
S\subseteq V\subseteq[n],
and

E\subseteq K_{V}\setminus K_{S}=\left\{\{u,v\}:u,v\in V\text{ and at most one of $u,v$ is in $S$}\right\}.

We further denote its number of edges
|J|\coloneqq|E|
, its number of vertices
v(J)\coloneqq|V\setminus S|
, and if
S\subsetneq V
, its density

(2.1)

\displaystyle\rho(J)\coloneqq\frac{|J|}{v(J)}=\frac{|E|}{|V\setminus S|}.

If
V\setminus S=\emptyset,
we define
\rho(J):=+\infty,
using the convention that
\frac{1}{\rho(J)}=\frac{1}{\infty}=0.

Note that any graph can trivially be considered a graph-cut by choosing
S=\emptyset
.

Towards succinctness, given a graph
T_{1}
and subgraph
T_{2}\subseteq T_{1}
, we use
T_{2}|T_{1}
to denote the graph-cut
(V(T_{1}),V(T_{2}),T_{1}\setminus T_{2})
. In particular,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\rho(T_{1}|T_{2})\coloneqq\frac{|T_{1}|-|T_{2}|}{|V(T_{1})\setminus V(T_{2})|}.

2.2.
Monotone Properties and Expectation Thresholds

Now we turn to some required background from combinatorics. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite universe of points. We start with defining a monotone property.

Definition 2.3
.

A monotone property
\tilde{A}
in the finite universe
\mathcal{X}
is a collection of subsets of
\mathcal{X},
such that for any
A\in\tilde{A}
if
A\subseteq B
then
B\in\tilde{A}.
An element
A\in\tilde{A}
is called a minimal element of
\tilde{A}
if there is no
A^{\prime}\in\tilde{A}
with
A^{\prime}\subsetneq A.

Observe that a monotone property
\tilde{A}
is fully characterized by the set of minimal elements of
\tilde{A}
. We can define the fractional expectation threshold of a monotone property as in
[
Tal10
]
.

Definition 2.4

(Fractional expectation threshold)
.

Fix any monotone property
\tilde{A}
in a finite universe
\mathcal{X}
. We define

p_{\mathrm{FE}}(\tilde{A})\coloneqq\max_{w\in W}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{w}^{-1}(1/2)\right\},

where
\mathcal{E}_{w}(p)\coloneqq\sum_{T\subseteq\mathcal{X}}w(T)p^{|T|},

and
W
is the set of all “fractional covers”
w
which are functions
w:2^{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
for any minimal element
A
of
\tilde{A}
,
\sum_{T\subseteq A}w(T)\geqslant 1
.

It turns out that that the fractional expectation threshold is a natural lower bound for the critical probability
p_{c}(\tilde{A})
(via a relatively simple “fractional” union bound argument) for the property of interest to be satisfied under the product
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
measure on
\mathcal{X}

[
Tal10
]
. Importantly, because of its fractional nature, a strong duality trick observed by Talagrand in
[
Tal10
]
leads to a representation of
p_{\mathrm{FE}}(\tilde{A})
in terms of so-called “spread” measures on the minimal elements of
\tilde{A}

[
ALWZ20
]
. Then, the breakthrough work
[
FKNP21
]
used this representation and proved that
p_{\mathrm{FE}}(\tilde{A})
also upper bounds
p_{c}(\tilde{A})
, up to a logarithmic multiplicative factor. As the critical probability
p_{c}(\tilde{A})
is not the focus of this work, we refer the interested reader to
[
FKNP21
]
for more details. We only highlight that the same duality trick is in fact crucial in our work in order to obtain our general connection between minimax rates and variants of the fractional expectation thresholds in
Section

7
.

3.
Main Results I: Planting a Weakly Dense
H

3.1.
Characterizing the MMSE curve via the
\varphi_{q}
thresholds

We start with our first and main result in this section. For any
q\in[0,1)
, we identify the noise level
p
(up to
1+o(1)
error) at which the MMSE curve crosses the threshold
1-q
. Recall the definition of
\varphi_{q}
from (
1.2
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider any (sequence of) weakly dense
H=H_{n}
and
q\in[0,1)
. Then, for the planted subgraph model corresponding to
H
, the following holds as
n
grows to infinity.

(a)

If
\liminf_{n}p/\varphi_{q}>1
, then

\liminf_{n}\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)\geqslant 1-q.

(b)

If
\limsup_{n}p/\varphi_{q}<1
, then

\limsup_{n}\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)\leqslant 1-q.

In particular, for any constant
q\in[0,1],
there exists some sequence of noise levels
p=p_{n}
satisfying

\lim_{n}p/\varphi_{q}=1

for which it holds

\lim_{n}\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-q.

The proof of the theorem is deferred to
Section

6
.

Remark 3.2

(The MMSE curve using the discontinuities of
\varphi_{q}
)
.

Theorem

3.1
can be used to extract non-trivial structure for the
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)
curve from the behavior of the points
(\varphi_{q})_{q\in[0,1)}
.
Observe that
\varphi_{q}
is a piecewise constant function of
q,
since for any
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(H)>0
small enough,
\varphi_{q}=\varphi_{q+\varepsilon}
.
For example, this holds if
\lfloor(q+\varepsilon)|H|\rfloor=\lfloor q|H|\rfloor
since in both these cases the same collection of subsets
S\subseteq H
is considered inside the minimum operation in the definition of
\varphi_{q}
.

This implies that one can choose the minimal possible integer
0\leqslant M\leqslant|H|
for which there exist points
1=q_{M}>q_{M-1}>\ldots>q_{1}>q_{0}=0
such that
\varphi_{q}
is constant on the intervals
[q_{i+1},q_{i}),i=0,1,\ldots,M-1
. In terms of the MMSE,
Theorem

3.1
implies that if for some
i=0,1,\ldots,M-1
we have
\varphi_{q_{i}}=(1+\Omega(1))\varphi_{q_{i+1}}
, then for any
\delta>0
if
p
satisfies

(1+\delta)\varphi_{q_{i+1}}\leqslant p\leqslant(1-\delta)\varphi_{q_{i}}

then

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-q_{i+1}+o(1),

and if
p\geqslant(1+\delta)\varphi_{q_{0}}
,
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-o(1)
.

Indeed, if
p\leqslant(1-\delta)\varphi_{q_{i}}
then for any
\varepsilon>0
we have
p\leqslant(1-\delta)\varphi_{q_{i+1}-\varepsilon}
and therefore
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)\leqslant 1-q_{i+1}+\varepsilon+o(1)
by
Theorem

3.1
(b). Since
\varepsilon>0
can be made arbitrarly small, we conclude
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)\leqslant 1-q_{i+1}+o(1)
. For the other direction, we have
(1+\delta)\varphi_{q_{i+1}}\leqslant p
and thus
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)\geqslant 1-q_{i+1}-o(1)
again by
Theorem

3.1
(a) and the fact that
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)
is a non-increasing function of
p
. The result follows. The proof when
p\geqslant(1+\delta)\varphi_{q_{0}}
is analogous.

Based on
Remark

3.2
, to further understand the MMSE curve one needs to understand the discontinuity points of the piecewise constant function,
(\varphi_{q})
. At first sight,
\varphi_{q}
is a minimax problem over exponentially many subgraphs, suggesting that such an analysis would be very challenging. Despite this, our next result is a full combinatorial characterization of
\varphi_{q}
based on a peeling process which we term the
onion decomposition
of
H
.

3.2.
Simplifying the
\varphi_{q}
thresholds via the onion decomposition of
H

We start with formally defining the onion decomposition here. Recall from
Definition

2.2
that
\rho(J_{1}|J_{2})\coloneqq\frac{|J_{1}\setminus J_{2}|}{|V(J_{1})\setminus V(J_{2})|}
for any graphs
J_{2}\subseteq J_{1}.

Definition 3.3

(Onion decomposition of
H
)
.

Let
H=H_{n}
be an arbitrary graph
H=H_{n}
. Consider the following procedure generating an increasing sequence of subgraphs
J^{(0)},J^{(1)},\cdots
of
H
.

(i)

Set
J^{(0)}=\emptyset
.

(ii)

For each
t>0,
let
J^{(t)}
be a maximal subgraph in
\arg\max_{J\supsetneq J^{(t-1)}}\rho(J|J^{(t-1)})
6
6

6

Maximality here means that there is no
J^{\prime}\supsetneq J^{(t)}
such that
J^{\prime}
also maximizes the density
\rho(J|J^{(t-1)})
.
.

(iii)

If
J^{(t)}=H
, stop.

Let
M=M(H)\leqslant|H|
be the number of steps required until the above process terminates. We refer to the outcome of this process,
J^{(t)},t=0,1,2,\ldots,M
, as the
onion decomposition
of
H
.

In words, the decomposition proceeds by setting
J^{(1)}
as the densest subgraph of
H,
then removes
J^{(1)}
and denotes by
J^{(2)}
the new densest subgraph, and so on.

Remark 3.4
.

Observe for intuition that if the graph is balanced
[
ER60
]
, that is
\max_{J\subseteq H}|J|/v(H)=|H|/V(H),
then
M=1
and
J^{(0)}=\emptyset,J^{(1)}=H.

Remark 3.5
.

Note that we refer to
Definition

3.3
as
the
onion decomposition of
H
. This is because, as stated in
Theorem

3.6
below, the choice of
J^{(t)}
turns out to be unique for all
t.

The following key combinatorial lemma establishes an important relation between the onion decomposition and the function
(\varphi_{q})_{q\in[0,1]}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

For any graph
H=H_{n}
, let
J^{(t)},t=0,1,2,\ldots,M
be its onion decomposition from
Definition

3.3
. The following holds.

(a)

For any
t=0,1,2,\ldots,M-1
, given
J^{(t)}
, the choice of
J^{(t+1)}
is unique in step (ii) of the procedure. That is, the onion decomposition of a given graph
H
is unique.

(b)

For any
q\in[0,1)
, let
t=t(q)
be such that
|J^{(t)}|/|H|\leqslant q<|J^{(t+1)}|/|H|
. Then

\varphi_{q}=n^{-\frac{|V(J^{(t+1)})\setminus V(J^{(t)})|}{|J^{(t+1)}\setminus J^{(t)}|}}=n^{-\frac{1}{\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)})}},

i.e., the pair
(S=J^{(t)},J=J^{(t+1)})
is a minimax optimal pair in the definition of
\varphi_{q}
in (
1.2
).

(c)

The onion decomposition of
H
, and in particular, the function
(\varphi_{q})_{q\in[0,1]}
can be computed in time which is polynomial in
|H|
.

The proof of these parts are based on a novel minimax duality principle that could be of independent interest. We defer the proof to
Section

5
.

Remark 3.7
.

(On the computability of
\varphi_{q}
.) Recall that
\varphi_{q}
is defined using the minimax optimization problem where both the minimum and the maximum operation is defined over exponentially many subsets of
H
. Fortunately, the simple structural characterization of
Theorem

3.6
(a) and (b) allows us to reduce the problem to a modification of the densest subgraph problem, for which a polynomial time algorithm is known due to
[
Cha00
]
. The fact that we are working with graph-cuts introduces some complications, see
Section

5.3
for details.

Now notice that
Remark

3.2
characterizes the MMSE using
\varphi_{q},
and
Theorem

3.6
characterizes
\varphi_{q}
using the onion decomposition of
H
. Hence, their combination allows us to obtain the following corollary directly, which characterizes the MMSE at a much finer detail than before.

Corollary 3.8
.

For any weakly dense graph
H=H_{n}
let
J^{(t)},t=0,1,2,\ldots,M
be its onion decomposition from
Definition

3.3
. If for some
t=0,1,2,\ldots,M-1
it holds that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{|V(J^{(t+1)})\setminus V(J^{(t)})|}{|J^{(t+1)}\setminus J^{(t)}|}-\frac{|V(J^{(t)})\setminus V(J^{(t-1)})|}{|J^{(t)}\setminus J^{(t-1)}|}=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right),

then for any
\delta>0
if

(3.2)

\displaystyle(1+\delta)n^{-\frac{|V(J^{(t+1)})\setminus V(J^{(t)})|}{|J^{(t+1)}\setminus J^{(t)}|}}\leqslant p\leqslant(1-\delta)n^{-\frac{|V(J^{(t)}\setminus V(J^{(t-1)})|}{|J^{(t)}\setminus J^{(t-1)}|}},

where in (
3.2
) we treat for convenience
n^{-0/0}\coloneqq 1
, it holds that

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-|J^{(t)}|/|H|+o(1).

Moreover, for any
\delta>0
if
p\leqslant(1-\delta)n^{-\frac{|V(J^{(M)}\setminus V(J^{(M-1)})|}{|J^{(M)}\setminus J^{(M-1)}|}}
then

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-|J^{(M)}|/|H|+o(1)=o(1).

The proof is deferred to
Section

8
. Some remarks are in order.

Remark 3.9
.

(On the “jump” assumption (
3.1
).)
Assumption (
3.1
) asks that at the
(t+1)
th step of the onion decomposition, the maximum density
|J^{(t)}|/|V(J^{(t)})|
reached has a non-trivial “jump” from the
t
th step. The role of the assumption is to ensure that the interval for
p
considered in (
3.2
) is non-vacuous. Besides being of a technical nature, we highlight that the assumption is in fact satisfied for many
H
of interest: for example, it can be straightforwardly checked to be satisfied for all steps
t
for any graph
H
with size sub-logarithmic, i.e., satisfying
|H|=o(\log n)
(also see
Section

3.4
for more examples of such
H
.)

The reason we require the assumption is that, in our main result
Theorem

3.1
, a small
1+\Omega(1)
room between
p
and
\varphi_{q}
is needed to derive the asymptotic bounds on
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)
. We believe studying the behavior of the MMSE for
p
being
(1+o(1))
-close to the thresholds
\varphi_{q}
, i.e., understanding the “sharpness” of these transitions
[
FB+99
,
GMZ23
]
, to be an interesting direction for future work.

Remark 3.10
.

(Interpretation of
Corollary

3.8
and the subgraph expectation threshold) For this remark, assume a weakly dense
H
such that the assumption (
3.1
) holds for all steps
t
of the onion decomposition. Then, we highlight two interesting implications of
Corollary

3.8
in terms of the different values the MMSE can take and the thresholds that are the MMSE discontinuity points.

First, notice that
Corollary

3.8
tells us that for any noise level
p
satisfying (
3.2
), the Bayes-optimal performance is achieved by an estimator that recovers exactly the edges of the
J^{(t)}
subgraph in
H^{*}
, up to an additive
o(1)
error. One can therefore interpret our result as saying that for all noise levels
p
, the maximal possible subgraphs of
H
that can be recovered is always a prescribed element of the onion decomposition of
H
. As the noise level
p
decreases from one to zero, the first subgraph that can be recovered should be the densest one, i.e.,
J^{(1)},
the second should be the densest subgraph containing
J^{(1)},
i.e.,
J^{(2)},
and so on, until we can recover all of
J^{(M)}=H
. It is arguably surprising that these critical subgraphs for recovery are so well structured for any weakly dense
H
.

Moreover,
Corollary

3.8
implies that the thresholds at which the MMSE “jumps” are exactly given as functions of the onion decomposition via the formula

p=n^{-\frac{|V(J^{(t+1)}\setminus V(J^{(t)})|}{|J^{(t+1)}\setminus J^{(t)}|}}=n^{-\frac{1}{\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)})}},t=1,\ldots,M.

In particular, assuming the mild condition
|J^{(1)}|/|H|=\Omega(1)
on
H
—a small variant of this natural condition is referred to as begin “delocalized” in
[
MNS+23
]
—the above observation implies that for
t=1
the MMSE jumps from being trivial, i.e. taking the value
1-o(1),
to its first non-trivial value
1-|J^{(1)}|/|H|+o(1)=1-\Omega(1)
exactly at the threshold

p=n^{-\frac{|V(J^{(1)}|}{|J^{(1)}|}}=\max_{J\subseteq H}n^{-\frac{|V(J)|}{|J|}},

which is the subgraph expectation threshold for
H
defined by Kahn and Kalai in
[
KK07
]
, thus proving
Corollary

1.3
. In other words, as mentioned in the introduction, the weak recovery threshold equals to the subgraph expectation threshold of
H
for any such weakly dense graph
H
. In particular, our work gives a statistical meaning to the
exact
subgraph expectation threshold of
H
from probabilistic combinatorics in Bayesian statistics, without the need to account for any additional
O(\log|H|)
-factor.

Remark 3.11

(A characterization of the AoN phenomenon)
.

Recall that AoN happens for the planted subgraph model for some
H=H_{n}
if the
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)
curve converges to a step function from
0
to
1
as
n
grows to infinity. An immediate consequence of
Theorem

3.1
is that the AoN phenomenon happens for a weakly dense graph if and only if for every
q,q^{\prime}\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{n}\frac{\varphi_{q}}{\varphi_{q^{\prime}}}=1.

Interestingly, this implication can be seen as a generalization of one of the main results in
[
MNS+23
]
, which requires an assumption of
H
being “delocalized” (see
[
MNS+23
, Definition 4.3]
) to obtain a similar such AoN characterization.

3.3.
On the convergence of the MMSE curve

Observe that all the above results discuss the behavior of the MMSE curve when
n
is finite but large. This is slightly diverging compared to the study of the limiting MMSE of other high dimensional statistical models, like compressed sensing
[
RP19
]
or the planted clique problem
[
MNS+23
]
, where the MMSE curve is (pointwise) converging as
n
grows to infinity to a specific curve and the curve is characterized, often via methods from statistical physics. This discrepancy is fundamental in our result as we consider a much larger family of priors compared to the above results. Furthermore, we do not assume any joint structure on
H_{n}
across different values of
n
; in other words, the planted subgraph
H_{n}
, and hence our prior, may change arbitrarily with
n
. For example,
H
could be a
10\log n
-clique for even
n
, and a
\log\log n
-clique for odd
n
, leading to two convergent MMSE subsequences with different limits. More specifically, in this case, if say
p=1/2
, it can be easily proven via the AoN theory from
[
MNS+23
]
that the (normalized) MMSE equals
o(1)
for even
n
and equals to
1-o(1)
for odd
n
, hence the MMSE as a curve is
not converging
as
n
grows to infinty. Yet, under the assumption that the sequence of graphs
H_{n}
is such that the pointwise limit of the MMSE
does
exist, we have the following asymptotic formula as a direct corollary of
Theorem

3.1
.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
H=H_{n}
be a weakly dense graph and
p=p_{n}\in(0,1).
Let the function
G:(0,1]\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
defined by
G(q)=\varphi_{q}.
Consider the sequence
\alpha_{n}\in(0,1)
defined by

\alpha_{n}=\inf G^{-1}([0,p]),n\in\mathbb{N}.

Then any limit point
x\in[0,1]
of
\{\alpha_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\},
corresponds in an one-to-one fashion to a limit point of the sequence
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p).
In particular, if the
\lim_{n}\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)
exists, then the
\lim_{n}\alpha_{n}
exists and it holds,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{n}\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-\lim_{n}\alpha_{n}.

The proof is based on simple calculus and is deferred to
Section

8
.

3.4.
A simple example

We now work through a simple example to apply our established MMSE theory on weakly dense
H=H_{n}
that illustrates the diversity of possible MMSE curves that one can obtain as the planted graph
H=H_{n}
is varied. We emphasize at the outset that, while the simple example below can be solved directly by other methods, the calculation illustrates how our main theorem gives a general recipe that can be applied to compute the MMSE curve of any planted graph which is weakly dense.

A union of disjoint cliques.
Let
m=m_{n}
and
r_{1}>\cdots>r_{m}\geqslant 1,
be an arbitrary sequence of integers with

1/r_{i+1}-1/r_{i}\geqslant\delta/\log n

for some constant
\delta>0.
Such sequences include the cases
r_{i}=(C_{i}+o(1))\log n,i=1,2,\ldots,m
for some constants
C_{1}>C_{2}>\ldots>C_{m}>0
, or
r_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
is an arbitrary decreasing sequence of positive integers with
r_{1}=o(\sqrt{\log n}).

We then consider
H
to be the
disjoint
union of
m
cliques of sizes
r_{1}>\cdots>r_{m}\geqslant 1,
so that

H=\bigcup_{s\leqslant m}K_{r_{s}}

and
k\coloneqq|H|=\sum_{s\leqslant m}\binom{r_{s}}{2}
.

In terms of the onion decomposition of
H
(
Definition

3.3
), we have
J^{(0)}=\emptyset
and the densest subgraph of
H
can be straightforwardly checked to be
J^{(1)}=K_{r_{1}}.
Similarly, a simple inductive argument implies that
M=M(H)=m
and

J^{(t)}=\bigcup_{s\leqslant t}K_{r_{s}},t=1,2,\ldots,m.

Moreover, from
Theorem

3.6
, we know that for any
1>q\geqslant 0
, if
m-1\geqslant t\geqslant 0
is maximal such that
|J^{(t)}|/|H|\leqslant q
, it holds

\varphi_{q}=p_{t}\coloneqq n^{-\frac{|V(J^{(t+1)})\setminus V(J^{(t)})|}{|E(J^{(t+1)})\setminus E(J^{(t)})|}}=n^{-\frac{2}{r_{t+1}-1}}.

We also set
p_{m}\coloneqq\varphi_{1}=0
and
p_{-1}\coloneqq 1.

Then using
Corollary

3.8
we have that for any
\delta>0
and for each
-1\leqslant t\leqslant m-1,
if
(1+\delta)p_{t+1}\leqslant p\leqslant(1-\delta)p_{t},
then

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-\frac{\sum_{s\leqslant t+1}\binom{r_{s}}{2}}{k}+o(1).

For example, if
r_{i}=(C_{i}+o(1))\log n,i=1,2,\ldots,m
for some constants
C_{1}>C_{2}>\ldots>C_{m}>0
we conclude that
p_{t}=e^{-2/C_{t+1}}+o(1)
for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant m-1
and
p_{m}=0,p_{-1}=0.
Hence, we have that for any
\delta>0
and for each
-1\leqslant t\leqslant m-1,
if

(1+\delta)e^{-2/C_{t+2}}\leqslant p\leqslant(1-\delta)e^{-2/C_{t+1}},

then it holds

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-\frac{\sum_{s\leqslant t+1}C^{2}_{s}}{\sum_{s\leqslant m}C^{2}_{s}}+o(1),

where set
e^{-2/C_{m+1}}\coloneqq 0,e^{-2/C_{0}}\coloneqq 1
.

If
m=1
and
C_{1}=2/\log 2
, we recover the AoN phenomenon for the planted clique model from
[
MNS+23
]
where the the clique is of size
k=(2+o(1))\log_{2}n
and the critical AoN noise level is
p=e^{-2/C_{1}}=1/2
.

4.
Main Results II: Planting general monotone properties

We now turn to a more general model, referred to as the planted subset model in
[
MNS+23
]
. In this general setting, we provide a weaker characterization of the MMSE curve as
n
grows, but under
no
assumptions on the planted structure. In particular, for the planted subgraph model discussed previously, this more general result applies to the MMSE curve for any
H=H_{n}
, even if not weakly dense.

To define this, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and fix some arbitrary family of
k=k_{N}
-sets
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
in a finite universe
\mathcal{X}
of
N
elements, and some noise level
p=p_{N}
. Now, the “signal” is an element
A
from
\mathcal{A}
which is sampled from an (arbitrary but known) prior
\pi
on
\mathcal{A}
. Then, the statistician observes

Y=A\cup X_{p}

where the “noise”
X_{p}
is a sample from the
p
-biased product measure on
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
X_{p}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(p)^{\otimes\mathcal{X}},
and aims to recover
A
from
Y
.

Remark 4.1
.

One may want to imagine
\mathcal{A}
as the set of minimal elements of some monotone property of interest (see
Section

2.2
for the standard definitions on monotone properties). Then the statistician aims to recover the planted “certificate” of this property in the presence of Bernoulli noise. The question of interest is: for what noise levels
p
is it possible to infer the certificate of the monotone property?

For example, the planted subgraph model is a special case of the planted subset model. Consider the universe
\mathcal{X}
to correspond to the
N=\binom{n}{2}
edges of
K_{n},
the complete graph on
n
vertices. In that case, the Bernoulli noise
X_{p}
equals in distribution to
G(n,p)
. Now, for any subgraph
H=H_{n}
of
K_{n}
, let
\mathcal{A}
to be the collection of all subsets of
\mathcal{X}
(i.e., all subgraphs of
K_{n}
) which are copies of
H
in
K_{n}.
Under this choice of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{A}
, the planted subset model becomes the planted subgraph model (it is easy to prove that the “worst” prior for planted subgraph models is the uniform one, see
Lemma

A.4
for the details).

Of course, the planted subset model generalizes well beyond the planted subgraph model to other interesting models, such as planting a connectivity certificate (i.e., a spanning tree) in
G(n,p)
or planting arbitrary monotone properties on random hypergraphs.

As in the previous section, we focus on the MMSE of the model as a function of
p
, defined as

\operatorname{MMSE}_{N}(p,\pi)=\frac{1}{k}\min_{\hat{A}}\mathbb{E}_{A\sim\pi,Y=A\cup X_{p},\hat{A}}\left[\|\mathbf{1}_{A}-\hat{A}(Y)\|^{2}_{2}\right]=\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}_{A\sim\pi,Y=A\cup X_{p}}\left[\|\mathbf{1}_{A}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{A}|Y]\|^{2}_{2}\right],

where the minimum is over all randomized estimators
\hat{A}:2^{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
. As the prior is arbitrary in this model, we focus on the MMSE of estimating
A
under the “worst” possible prior
\pi
, which using the strong duality of linear programs can be written as

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{MinMax}(\mathcal{A},p)\coloneqq\max_{\pi}\operatorname{MMSE}_{N}(p,\pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\min_{\hat{A}}\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{A,Y,\hat{A}}\left[\|\hat{A}(Y)-\mathbf{1}_{A}\|^{2}_{2}\right].

Remark 4.2
.

We note that the study of minimax rates of estimation, like
\mathrm{MinMax}(\mathcal{A},p)
, where one accounts for the worst-case prior is a topic of intense study for many decades in the literature of theoretical statistics. Moreover, the optimizers
\pi,\hat{A}
in the definition of
(
4.1
)
are called the “least favorable prior” and the “minimax estimator”, respectively.

Our main result is to identify the order of the noise level
p
for which
\mathrm{MinMax}(\mathcal{A},p)
achieves a given fixed value of interest in
(0,1]
. Recall that in the planted subgraph model for a weakly dense
H
and under a uniform prior,
Theorem

3.1
implies that
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-q+o(1)
for some
p=(1+o(1))\varphi_{q}
where the threshold
\varphi_{q}
is a modified subgraph expectation threshold. In the planted subset model, for any
q\in[0,1),
the desired threshold is given by a modification of the so-called
fractional expectation
thresholds of the monotone property generated by
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 4.3
.

Fix any family of
k
-sets
\mathcal{A}
in a finite universe
\mathcal{X}
of
N
elements. For any
q\in[0,1),
we define

\psi_{q}\coloneqq(\psi_{q})_{N}=\min_{S\subseteq\mathcal{X},|S|\leqslant qk}\left\{\max_{w\in W_{S}}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{w}^{-1}(1/2)\right\}\right\},

where given
S\subset\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{E}_{w}(p)\coloneqq\sum_{T\subseteq\mathcal{X}}w(T)p^{|T\setminus S|},

and
W_{S}
is the set of all “fractional covers”
w
which are functions
w:2^{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow[0,1]
satisfying the following two properties.

(a)

(“Support condition”) Unless
|T|\geqslant qk
and
S\subseteq T
, it holds
w(T)=0
.

(b)

(“Fractional cover condition”) For all
A_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,M
where
S\subseteq A_{i}
we have

\sum_{T\subseteq A_{i}}w(T)\geqslant 1.

We finally set
\psi_{1}\coloneqq 0
.

Remark 4.4
.

We remark that the case
q=0
and
S=\emptyset
corresponds exactly to the fractional expectation threshold
[
Tal10
]
of the monotone property with minimal elements
\mathcal{A}
, see
Definition

2.4
. In particular, the value of the fractional expectation threshold has been calculated up to constants for a number of monotone properties, including the
H
-inclusion property for a subgraph
H=H_{n}
of the complete graph
K_{n}

[
FKNP21
,
MNSZ22b
]
.

4.1.
Characterizing the Minimax curve via the
\psi_{q}
thresholds

We now present our general result in this setting.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
q\in[0,1)
, and
\mathcal{A}
be an arbitrary
k
-uniform family of subsets of
[N]
. Then, as
N\to\infty
,

(a)

If
\lim_{N}p/\psi_{q}=+\infty,
then

\liminf_{N}\mathrm{MinMax}(\mathcal{A},p)\geqslant 1-q.

(b)

If
\lim_{N}p/\psi_{q}=0,
then

\limsup_{N}\mathrm{MinMax}(\mathcal{A},p)\leqslant 1-q.

The proof is deferred to
Section

7
.

Remark 4.6
.

We remark that while we are able to efficiently compute
\varphi_{q}
in the setting of weakly dense graphs using the onion decomposition of
H
, we do not yet have a way to efficiently compute an optimizer for
\psi_{q}
for arbitrary monotone properties. It is an interesting question for future work if a similar onion-peeling process exists for any monotone property.

Despite this, given the success of probabilistic combinatorics in computing the fractional expectation thresholds for various different properties
[
FKNP21
]
, we find the calculation of
\psi_{q}
for specific monotone properties an interesting direction—by
Theorem

4.5
, this would have implications for the minimax rates of such problems. We next go over such a calculation for the well-studied case where
H
is a perfect matching.

4.2.
A simple example

We now work through a simple example in this more general case.

Perfect matching.
Let us consider the case that
H
is the perfect matching on
n
vertices (
n
even). This is a model that has received significant interest in the community (see e.g.,
[
DWXY23
,
GSXY25
]
and references therein). In this setting, the minimax rate is equal to the MMSE for the uniform distribution over planted matchings (see
Lemma

A.4
).

We will show via direct computation that for any
q\in(0,1)
,

\psi_{q}=(1+o(1))\frac{e}{n}.

Applying
Theorem

4.5
, we can conclude the following.

•

If
p=o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
, then the MMSE will be
o(1)
for any prior. We note that this is a result already established in
[
DWXY23
]
, by a direct analysis in this setting.

•

If
p=\omega\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
, then there exists a prior (the uniform distribution) for which the MMSE will be
1-o(1)
. We note that this was very recently independently proved by
[
GSXY25
]
, using a direct analysis.

Recall that

\psi_{q}=\min_{S\subseteq\mathcal{X},|S|\leqslant qk}\left\{\max_{w\in W_{S}}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{w}^{-1}(1/2)\right\}\right\}.

First, we observe that for any choice of S, the max is the fractional Kahn–Kalai threshold
\tau_{k}
for a perfect matching on
k=n-v(S)
vertices. This is because any
S
will be disconnected from the rest of
H
, so the resulting graph-cut is essentially just a normal graph.
Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\psi_{q}

\displaystyle=\min_{S\subseteq\mathcal{X},|S|\leqslant qk}\left\{\tau_{n-v(S)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{k\geqslant(1-q)n}\tau_{k}

It remains to compute
\tau_{k}
, the fractional Kahn Kalai threshold for a perfect matching in a size
k
graph.

From
[
MNSZ22b
]
, for subgraph inclusion properties, the fractional Kahn–Kalai threshold is equal to the following:

\tau_{n}=\max_{J\subseteq H}\left(\frac{H[J]}{K_{n}[J]}\right)^{1/|J|}

where
G[J]
for a graph
G
refers to the number of unlabeled copies of
J
in
G
.

In the case of perfect matchings,
\tau_{k}
can be expressed as

\tau_{k}=\max_{0\leqslant r\leqslant k/2}\left(\frac{M_{k/2}[M_{r}]}{K_{k}[M_{r}]}\right)^{2/r}=\max_{1\leqslant r\leqslant k/2}\left(\frac{\binom{k/2}{r}}{\binom{k}{2r}\cdot(2r-1)!!}\right)^{2/r},

with
M_{t}
denoting the matching with
t
edges. We shall show that the above is maximized for
r=k/2
. Letting
a_{r}
be the expression inside the parentheses, we have for
r\geqslant 1
,

a_{r}=\frac{\binom{k/2}{r}}{\binom{k}{2r}\cdot(2r-1)!!}=\frac{\binom{k/2}{r-1}}{\binom{k}{2r-2}\cdot(2r-3)!!}\cdot\frac{\frac{k/2-r}{r}}{\frac{(k-2r)(k-2r+1)}{2r(2r-1)}\cdot(2r-1)}=a_{r-1}\cdot\frac{1}{k-2r+1}.

A simple inductive argument then implies that
a_{r}\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{k-2r-1}\right)^{r}
. Indeed, this is trivially true for
r=1
. For larger
r
, we have by induction that
a_{r}=\frac{1}{k-2r+1}\cdot a_{r-1}\leqslant\frac{1}{k-2r+1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{k-2r+1}\right)^{r-1}\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{k-2r-1}\right)^{r}
as desired. Given this,

a_{r}^{1/r}=\left(\frac{1}{k-2r+1}\cdot a_{r-1}\right)^{1/r}\geqslant\left(f(r-1)^{1/r-1}\cdot a_{r-1}\right)^{1/r}=a_{r-1}^{1/(r-1)}.

It follows that the maximum is attained for
r=k/2
. Thus,

\displaystyle\tau_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{(k-1)!!}\right)^{2/k}

Observe that this is minimized at
k=n
. Approximating
(n-1)!!=\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n/2+o(1)}
, we have that

\displaystyle\tau_{n}

\displaystyle=\left((e/n)^{n/2+o(1)}\right)^{2/n}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{e}{n}.

Hence we conclude that

\psi_{q}=(1+o(1))\frac{e}{n}

for all
q\in[0,1]
, as desired.

5.
Onion Decomposition: Proof of
Theorem

3.6

In this section, we establish some key results on the onion decomposition that will be useful in multiple proofs throughout the paper and also prove
Theorem

3.6
.

We start with recalling the
\rho
notation from
Section

2
. Notice that our key quantity
(\varphi_{q})
can be defined via
\rho
; if we define for
q\in[0,1]
the quantity

(5.1)

\displaystyle\rho_{q}\coloneqq\min\left\{\max\left\{\rho(J|S):J\supseteq S\right\}:S\subseteq H,|S|\leqslant q|H|\right\}

then it clearly holds

\varphi_{q}=n^{-\frac{1}{\rho_{q}}}.

5.1.
Key lemmas

We now establish four useful combinatorial lemmas for the “density”
\rho
function, that follow from elementary graph theory arguments. We defer their proofs to
Section

8
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A,B
be subgraphs. Then,

\rho(A\cup B|A)\geqslant\rho(B|A\cap B).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
K|T
be a graph-cut that is “balanced”, in that for all
T^{\prime}
such that
T\subseteq T^{\prime}\subseteq K
,
\rho(T^{\prime}|T)\leqslant\rho(K|T)
. Then, for all
T^{\prime}
such that
T\subseteq T^{\prime}\subseteq K
it also holds

\rho(K|T^{\prime})\geqslant\rho(K|T).

The third lemma implies that modulo any subgraph
A,
there exists a unique maximizer of the density
\rho(J|A),J\supseteq A.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
A
be a subgraph and consider the map
\varphi_{A}
which maps any subgraph
J
containing
A
to
\varphi_{A}(J)=\rho(J|A).
If
J^{\prime}
and
J^{\prime\prime}
are two subgraphs containing
A
and at least one of them is balanced, then
\varphi_{A}(J^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime\prime})\geqslant\min\{\varphi_{A}(J^{\prime}),\varphi_{A}(J^{\prime\prime\prime})\}
. In particular, if
J^{\prime},J^{\prime\prime}
are two maximizers of
\varphi_{A}
, then
J^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime\prime}
is also a maximizer of
\varphi_{A}
, so there is a unique maximal maximizer of
\varphi_{A}.

The fourth lemma asserts that the densities in the onion decomposition are non-increasing.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
A
be a subgraph and
B=\arg\max_{J\supseteq A}\rho(J|A).
Then for all
C\supseteq B
,

\rho(C|B)\leqslant\rho(B|A).

In particular, for
J^{(t)},t=0,1,\ldots,M
the onion decomposition of any subgraph
H
the sequence
\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)}),t=0,1,\ldots,M-1
is non-increasing.

5.2.
Simplifying
\varphi_{q}
: Proofs of
Theorem

3.6
(a) and (b)

The above lemmas allow us to conclude parts (a) and (b) of
Theorem

3.6
, restated for convenience.

See
3.6

Lemma

5.3
applied to
A=J^{(t)}
immediately implies (a).

Proof of (b).

Fix some
q\in[0,1)
and let
(S^{*},J^{*})
be a minimax pair in the definition of
\rho_{q}
, which in particular also satisfies

\varphi_{q}=n^{-1/\rho(J^{*}|S^{*})}.

In particular, our goal is to show that for
t
such that
|J^{(t)}|\leqslant q|H|<|J^{(t+1)}|
, it holds that
\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)})=\rho(J^{*}|S^{*})
.

We start with choosing
t
to be the step of the onion decomposition such that
J^{(t)}\subseteq S^{*}
but
J^{(t+1)}\not\subseteq S^{*}
. We first prove that for this choice of
t
,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)})=\rho(J^{*}|S^{*})

By the definition of the onion decomposition,
J^{(t+1)}\in\argmax_{J\supseteq J^{(t)}}\rho(J|J^{(t)})
. Combining this with the optimality of
S^{*}
, that means we have

\rho\left(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)}\right)=\max_{J\supseteq J^{(t)}}\rho(J|J^{(t)})\geqslant\rho(J^{*}|S^{*}).

Next, defining
\widetilde{J}=J^{(t+1)}\cup S^{*}
and
\widetilde{S}=J^{(t+1)}\cap S^{*}
,
Lemma

5.1
implies that

\rho(\widetilde{J}|S^{*})\geqslant\rho(J^{(t+1)}|\widetilde{S}).

Finally, by
Lemma

5.2
,

\rho(J^{(t+1)}|\widetilde{S})\geqslant\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)}).

Hence, combining the last three displayed inequalities, we have

\rho(\widetilde{J}|S^{*})\geqslant\rho(J^{*}|S^{*}).

But by the optimality of
J^{*}
given
S^{*}
,
\max_{J\supseteq S^{*}}\rho(J|S^{*})=\rho(J^{*}|S^{*})
, all the above four displayed inequalities should be equalities, proving (
5.2
).

Thus, for all
q\in[0,1]
,

\rho_{q}\in\{\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)}):0\leqslant t\leqslant q|H|\}.

But again as by definition for all
t
,
\rho\left(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)}\right)=\max_{J\supseteq J^{(t)}}\rho(J|J^{(t)})
it also holds by setting
S=J^{(t)},t\leqslant q|H|
and the definition of
\rho_{q}
that

\rho_{q}\leqslant\min\{\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)}):0\leqslant t\leqslant q|H|\}.

Hence,

\rho_{q}=\min\{\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)}):0\leqslant t\leqslant q|H|\}.

But by
Lemma

5.4
we have that
\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)})
is a non-increasing function of
t
. Therefore the last displayed equation gives
\rho_{q}=\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)})
for the maximum possible
t\leqslant q|H|
, from which (b) follows.
∎

5.3.
Computability of
\varphi_{q}
: Proof of
Theorem

3.6
(c)

We further have the following lemma, which shows that this procedure can be implemented in polynomial-time.

Proof.

We first show that there is an efficient algorithm to find the densest subgraph in a graph-cut. Specifically, given a graph-cut
G|S
, we show how to find a
J\subseteq G|S
attaining the value
f(G|S)=\max_{J\subseteq G|S}\rho(J)
.

The algorithm and its analysis are based on Charikar’s Linear Programming (LP) approach to find a densest subgraph in a graph
[
Cha00
]
. We consider the following LP:

maximize

\displaystyle\sum_{ij\in G\setminus S}x_{ij}

subject to

\displaystyle x_{ij}\leqslant y_{i}\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall i\in V(G)\setminus V(S)

\displaystyle x_{ij}\leqslant y_{i}\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall j\in V(G)\setminus V(S)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in V(G)\setminus V(S)}y_{i}\leqslant 1

\displaystyle x_{ij},y_{i}\geqslant 0\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall ij\in G|S.

Let
v^{*}
be the optimal value of this LP. To see that
v^{*}\geqslant f(G|S),
note that for every
J\subseteq G|S,
letting
y_{i}=\frac{1}{v(J)}\mathbf{1}\{i\in V(J)\}
and
x_{ij}=\frac{1}{v(J)}\mathbf{1}\{ij\in J\}
, we get
\sum_{ij\in G|S}x_{ij}=\rho(J)
. Moreover,
\sum_{i\in V(G)\setminus V(S)}y_{i}=1
, and the other two constraints are trivially satisfied.

Next, given a solution to the LP with value
v^{*},
we show how to extract a choice of
J
such that
\rho(J)\geqslant v^{*}.
Let
\{\overline{x}_{ij}\}_{ij}\cup\{\overline{y}_{i}\}_{i}
be such a solution. Note that we can assume without loss of generality that
\overline{x}_{ij}=\min\{\overline{y}_{i},\overline{y}_{j}\}
when
i,j\in V(G)\setminus V(S)
,
\overline{x}_{ij}=\overline{y}_{i}
when
j\in V(S)
, and
\overline{x}_{ij}=\overline{y}_{j}
when
i\in V(S).
For each
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0},
consider the subgraph
E(r)=\{ij:\overline{x}_{ij}\geqslant r\}\subseteq G\setminus S
and denote by
V(r)\coloneqq\{i:\overline{y}_{i}\geqslant r\}
. Note that for each
r
, by our assumption, for any edge of the subgraph
E(r)
,
ij\in E(r)
it can be easily checked to hold
\{i,j\}\setminus V(S)\subseteq V(r)
, and therefore

(5.3)

\displaystyle V(E(r))\setminus V(S)\subseteq V(r).

Now, we claim that there exists
r
such that
\rho(V(r),E(r))\geqslant v^{*}.
Indeed, since
\int_{0}^{\infty}|E(r)|dr=\sum_{ij\in G|S}\overline{x}_{ij}
and
\int_{0}^{\infty}|V(r)|dr=\sum_{i\in V(G)\setminus V(S))}\overline{y}_{i}\leqslant 1
, we get

\displaystyle v^{*}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}|E(r)|dr}{\int_{0}^{\infty}|V(r)|dr}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{r}\frac{|E(r)|}{|V(r)|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{r}\frac{|E(r)\setminus S|}{|V(E(r))\setminus V(S)|}.

But then note that we can efficiently enumerate all combinatorially distinct
E(r)
to find a
J=E(r)
such that

\rho(J)=\rho(E(r))=\frac{|E(r)\setminus S|}{|V(E(r))\setminus V(S)|}\geqslant v^{*},

as desired.
Given the above, we can efficiently construct sequences
\emptyset=\widetilde{J}^{0},\widetilde{J}^{1},\dots,\widetilde{J}^{\ell}
and
\widetilde{J}^{(t)}=\cup_{s\leqslant t}\widetilde{J}^{s}
so that, for each
t\leqslant\ell-1
,
\widetilde{J}^{t+1}
is a maximizer of
J\mapsto\rho(J|\widetilde{J}^{(t-1)})
among
J\supseteq\widetilde{J}^{(t-1)}.

To efficiently construct the onion decomposition, we need yet to show something slightly stronger: we need to efficiently construct the sequence
\emptyset=J^{0},J^{1},\dots,J^{m}
such that, for
J^{(t)}=\cup_{s\leqslant t}J^{s}
, we have that
J^{t+1}
is the
maximal

J
maximizing the function
J\mapsto\rho(J|\widetilde{J}^{(t-1)})
among
J\supseteq\widetilde{J}^{(t-1)}
(the uniqueness of the maximal
J
follows by
Theorem

3.6
).

We do this in a greedy fashion as follows. Let
s_{1}\geqslant 1
be maximal such that

\rho(\widetilde{J}^{1}|\widetilde{J}^{(0)})=\cdots=\rho(\widetilde{J}^{s_{1}}|\widetilde{J}^{(s_{1}-1)}),

and let
J^{1}=\cup_{s\leqslant s_{1}}\widetilde{J}^{s}.
Then let
s_{2}
be maximal such that

\rho(\widetilde{J}^{s_{1}+1}|\widetilde{J}^{(s_{1})})=\cdots=\rho(\widetilde{J}^{s_{2}}|\widetilde{J}^{(s_{2}-1)}),

and let
J^{2}=\cup_{s_{1}<s\leqslant s_{2}}\widetilde{J}^{s},
and so on. Notice that using
Lemma

5.4
we have for all
t,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\rho(\widetilde{J}^{s_{t}+1}|\widetilde{J}^{(s_{t})})<\rho(\widetilde{J}^{s_{t}}|\widetilde{J}^{(s_{t}-1)}).

Now, it is straightforward to check that for all
t

J^{t}
maximizes
J\mapsto\rho(J|J^{(t-1)}).
Hence, it remains to show that
J^{t}
is in fact a
maximal
such maximize for all. Suppose that this is not the case for some
t
. Then by
Lemma

5.3
, there exists
J^{\prime}\not\subseteq J^{t}
so that
J^{\prime}\cup J^{t}
is the maximal maximizer of
J\mapsto\rho(J|J^{(t-1)}).
In particular, it holds

\rho(J^{\prime}\cup J^{t}|J^{(t-1)})=\rho(J^{t}|J^{(t-1)})=\rho(\widetilde{J}^{s_{t}}|\widetilde{J}^{s_{t-1}})

which implies for
J^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq J^{\prime}\setminus J^{t}
that

\rho(J^{\prime\prime}|\widetilde{J}^{s_{t}})=\rho(\widetilde{J}^{s_{t}}|\widetilde{J}^{s_{t-1}}).

We can therefore guarantee that

\rho(\widetilde{J}^{s_{t}+1}|\widetilde{J}^{(s_{t})})\geqslant\rho(J^{\prime\prime}|\widetilde{J}^{s_{t}})=\rho(\widetilde{J}^{s_{t}}|\widetilde{J}^{(s_{t}-1)}),

a contradiction with (
5.4
).
∎

6.
The MMSE for weakly dense

: Proof of
Theorem

In this section, we give an overview of the main technical steps involved in the proof of our main result,
Theorem

3.1
. Our first step is a “positive” result, showing that if
p
is smaller than a threshold
\widetilde{\varphi}_{q},
then the noise is small enough so that the limiting MMSE is at least
1-q
. The second step is a “negative” result, proving that if
p
is larger than
\varphi_{q}
then the noise is high enough so that the limiting MMSE is at most
1-q
. Our final step shows that these two thresholds,
\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}
and
\varphi_{q}
, are in fact equal.

6.1.
Two lemmas: the importance of the order of the min and max operators

Our first lemma, proved in
Section

6.4
, shows that for

below a modified version of the
\varphi_{q}
threshold defined in (

) (where importantly the min and max operators have been exchanged) it is possible to achieve MMSE at most

.

Lemma 6.1

(Possibility of Recovery)
.

Suppose
H=H_{n}
is a weakly dense
H
. Given
q\in[0,1)
, define the threshold

\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}=(\widetilde{\varphi}_{q})_{n}=\max\left\{\min\left\{n^{-\frac{|V(J)\setminus V(S)|}{|J\setminus S|}}:S\subseteq J,|S|\leqslant q|H|\right\}:J\subseteq H\right\}.

Then for any
p=p_{n}
with
\limsup_{n}p/\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}<1,
it holds

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)\leqslant 1-q.

The lemma is proven by showing that for
p
below this new
\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}
threshold, with high probability there does not exist an isomorphic copy
H^{\prime}
of the planted graph
H^{*}
in the observed graph
G=H^{*}\cup G_{0},G_{0}\sim G(n,p)
which overlaps with
H^{*}
in fewer than
(q-o(1))|H|
edges. We then prove via the Nishimori identity that the MMSE is equal to one minus the expected overlap between a random isomorphic copy of
H
and
H^{*},
from which we can directly upper bound the MMSE by
1-q+o(1).
The proof is deferred to
Section

6.4
.

The intuition behind the variational formula in
Lemma

6.1
for
\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}
is as follows. In order to avoid having a copy
H^{\prime}
of
H
in
G
with overlap
\leqslant q|H|
with the planted
H^{*},
we need to understand for which noise levels
p
we can rule out such structures. Let us denote by
S
this potential intersection set of edges between a copy
H^{\prime}
of
H,
and
H^{*}.
Once we fix such an
S,
a bound on the largest value of
p
such that no copy of
H
appears in the observed graph
G
overlapping with
H^{*}
exactly on
S
can be obtained by a standard first moment method (i.e, a vanilla union bound). Then optimizing the bound to account for the worst-case
S,
leads to a bound on
p
to have MMSE at most
1-q.
However, in many cases this bound will not be tight. To improve it, using the intuition from the tightness of the expectation thresholds from
[
KK07
]
, one can fix a subgraph
J\subseteq H
and instead upper bound for any
p
the probability that a copy
H^{\prime}
of
H
in
G
overlaps
H^{*}
on
S
by the probability that
a copy of
J
in
G
overlaps with
H^{*}
in at most
\leqslant q|H|
edges (simply because
J\subseteq H
). Then, the same standard first moment method argument as above can be performed with
J
in place of
H
to obtain a new upper bound on the probability that a copy
H^{\prime}
of
H
in
G
overlaps
H^{*}
on
S
, which depends on
J
and
S
. Now, notice that by optimizing in a worst-case sense over
S
(to guarantee an MMSE at most
1-q
), and then optimizing over all subgraphs
J
(now, in favor of the statistician so choosing the “best-case”
J
), we arrive exactly at the max-min formula for
\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}
giving
Lemma

6.1
.

We next describe our second lemma, proved in
Section

6.5
, which shows that above the
\varphi_{q}
threshold, the MMSE must be worse than
1-q
.

Lemma 6.2

(Impossibility of recovery)
.

Suppose
H=H_{n}
is a weakly dense
H
. Given
q\in[0,1)
, recall the threshold
\varphi_{q}
from (
1.2
).
Then for any
p=p_{n}
with
\liminf_{n}p/\varphi_{q}>1
, it holds

\displaystyle\liminf_{n}\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)\geqslant 1-q.

The proof shows that when
p
crosses
\varphi_{q}
, the posterior distribution of
H^{*}
, which is the uniform distribution over the copies of
H
in the observed graph, is concentrated on copies with overlap less than
q|H|
with
H^{*}.
Our proof follows from an appropriate leverage of the planting trick
[
ACO08
]
similar to its application in the recent Bayesian proof of the spread lemma
[
MNSZ22b
]
. The proof is deferred to
Section

6.5
.

Here, the intuition for
\varphi_{q}
is as follows. Given
S\subseteq H
with
|S|\leqslant q|H|
, one can define a statistically easier version of the estimation task of interest, in which the statistician is also given as side information the
S
part of the planted graph
H^{*}.
An application of the data processing inequality implies that the inability to recover a given fraction of
H^{*}
in this easier problem implies the same inability in the original statistical problem (Such techniques are often called “genie” lower bound arguments, e.g,
[
RG13
]
).

To prove the MMSE lower bound it suffices to determine the noise levels
p
for which the MMSE is at least
1-q
when the statistician is given
S
as side information, and then optimizing over
S
to find the minimum such noise level. For this reason, we start by fixing any
S
with
|S|\leqslant q|H|
and assume that the
S
part of
H
is given to the statistician. Then a simple posterior calculation implies that MMSE is at least
1-|S|/|H|\geqslant 1-q
, if a typical copy
H^{\prime}
(of
H
) in
G=H^{*}\cup G_{0}
that contains
S
does not overlap an
\Omega(1)
-fraction of edges in
H^{*}\setminus S
. Equivalently, this is true if most copies
H^{\prime}
containing
S
are such that
H^{\prime}\setminus S
is (almost) fully included in
G_{0}
.

For this reason, it makes sense to study the threshold of
p
at which many (near-complete) copies of
H\setminus S
start appearing in
G_{0}\sim G(n,p)
. The intuition from the literature on Kahn–Kalai thresholds suggests that this threshold is given by the subgraph expectation threshold of
H\setminus S
, which is precisely the maximizing problem in the definition of
\varphi_{q}
.
While the tightness of this threshold is still at a conjectural phase and expected to hold only up to a logarithmic error, we prove that the approximate version of it (recall we want
H\setminus S
to only approximately appear in
G
) that holds exactly above the subgraph expectation threshold. Optimizing over all subgraphs
S
to determine the best such bound results in
\varphi_{q}
, the minimum possible subgraph expectation threshold of
H\setminus S
among all
S
.

6.2.
Exchanging the max and min operators

To conclude
Theorem

3.1
, we must relate the thresholds in the two lemmas above. It is somewhat dissatisfying that the natural probabilistic way to argue about
Lemma

6.1
and
Lemma

6.2
leads to almost the same threshold for
p
, but with the max and the min operators being exchanged in the two results. While there are plenty of “minimax results” in the literature for exchanging the min and the max operators (e.g., in the literature of game theory and of optimization), no such result appears applicable in our discrete vertex-symmetric setting. Yet, a careful combinatorial argument allows us to indeed exchange the operators. This minimax result is the content of the next lemma, proved in
Section

6.6
, and is proved by employing the onion decomposition of
H
.

Lemma 6.3

(Minimax result)
.

For any
n
,
q=q_{n}\in[0,1)
, and
H=H_{n}
,
\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}=\varphi_{q}
.

We remark that the duality lemma above is an exact, non-asymptotic equality: it holds for all graphs
H=H_{n}
and for all
n
. In order to prove it, our main tool is a structural result for the max-min and min-max optimal pairs
(S,J)
in
\varphi_{q}
. Observe that the pair
(S,J)
can take exponentially many values. Yet, by considering the onion decomposition
(J^{(t)})
of
H
(
Definition

3.3
), it turns out that, as explained in
Section

5
, the minimax optimization defining
\varphi_{q}
has a simple optimizing pair: it is exactly of the form
(S=J^{(t)},J=J^{(t+1)})
, where
t
is maximal such that
|J^{(t)}|\leqslant q|H|
. For instance, for
q
close to 1, the optimal pair is to choose
S=J^{(1)}
, the densest subgraph of
H
and
J=J^{(2)}
the “second” densest subgraph in the onion decomposition. The knowledge of the optimizers greatly simplifies
\varphi_{q}
and is key to establishing
Lemma

6.3
.

6.3.
Putting it all together

Notice that the above lemmas immediately imply
Theorem

3.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Parts (a) and (b) follow immediately from
Lemmas

6.1
,
6.2
and
6.3
.

The last part follows from the fact that
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)
is a non-increasing polynomial function
p
(see
Lemma

A.2
) together with a simple analysis argument to construct the desired sequence. Indeed, for any
\varepsilon>0,
and for large enough
n
, parts (a), (b) imply

\operatorname{MMSE}((1-\varepsilon)\varphi_{q})\leqslant 1-q+\varepsilon

and

\operatorname{MMSE}((1+\varepsilon)\varphi_{q})\geqslant 1-q-\varepsilon.

That means that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(\varepsilon)
large enough such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
there exists
p=p_{n}\in[(1-\varepsilon)\varphi_{q},(1+\varepsilon)\varphi_{q}]
for which

1-q-\varepsilon\leqslant\operatorname{MMSE}(p)\leqslant 1-q+\varepsilon.

Iterating this argument for
\varepsilon=1/m,m=1,2,\ldots
leads to (potentially distinct) sequences of noise levels
p^{(m)}=p^{(m)}_{n}
such that for all
m
, there exists a (without loss of generality strictly increasing)
G(m)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geqslant G(m),

p^{(m)}_{n}\in[(1-1/m)\varphi_{q},(1+1/m)\varphi_{q}]
and

1-q-1/m\leqslant\operatorname{MMSE}(p^{(m)}_{n})\leqslant 1-q+1/m.

Now, “diagonalizing” this argument, consider the sequence defined as follows: for each
n\in[G(m),G(m+1))
, let

p=p_{n}\coloneqq p^{(m)}_{n}

(for
n<G(1),
set
p_{n}=0
). Then notice that clearly for this sequence it holds
\lim_{n}p_{n}/\varphi_{q}=1
and also
\lim_{n}\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p_{n})=1-q
as desired.
∎

6.4.
Proof of
Lemma

6.1
: Recovery for
p\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}

In this section, we prove
Lemma

6.1
. In particular, we assume that for some
\varepsilon>0
and
n
large enough it holds
p\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\varphi_{q}
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta>0
to be a constant and set
k\coloneqq|H|
. It suffices to show that the
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)
is at most

1-q+\delta+o(1).

Since
\delta
is arbitrary, we may eventually take
\delta
to
0
to obtain the desired statement.

Observe that by Bayes’ rule, the posterior of the model is simply the uniform distribution over all copies of
H
in the observed graph. Using the Nishimori identity (
Lemma

A.1
), we have

1-\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{G}\sim G(n,p)\\
H^{*}\sim\text{uniform copy of $H$ in $K_{n}$}\end{subarray}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{H^{\prime}\sim\text{copies of $H$ in $\bm{G}\cup H^{*}$}}|H^{\prime}\cap H^{*}|\right].

We bound this as follows,

\displaystyle 1-\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)

\displaystyle\geqslant(q-\delta)-\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{G}\sim G(n,p)\\
H^{*}\sim\text{uniform copy of $H$ in $K_{n}$}\end{subarray}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{H^{\prime}\sim\text{copies of $H$ in $\bm{G}\cup H^{*}$}}|H^{\prime}\cap H^{*}|\cdot\mathbf{1}_{|H^{\prime}\cap H^{*}|\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant(q-\delta)-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{G}\sim G(n,p)\\
H^{*}\sim\text{uniform copy of $H$ in $K_{n}$}\end{subarray}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{H^{\prime}\sim\text{copies of $H$ in $\bm{G}\cup H^{*}$}}\mathbf{1}_{|H^{\prime}\cap H^{*}|\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant(q-\delta)-\mathbb{P}_{\bm{G},H^{*}}\left[\text{$\exists$ a copy $H^{\prime}$ of $H$ in $G$ such that $|H^{*}\cap H^{\prime}|\leqslant(q-\delta)k$}\right]

It suffices to show that

\mathbb{P}_{\bm{G},H^{*}}\left[\text{$\exists$ a copy $H^{\prime}$ of $H$ in $G$ such that $|H^{*}\cap H^{\prime}|\leqslant(q-\delta)k$}\right]=o(1).

For starters, let
J^{*}
be an optimal choice of
J
in the definition of
\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}
. Then, using the notation
\cong
to denote the graph isomorphism relation, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\text{$\exists$ $H^{\prime}\subseteq G\cup H^{*}$ such that $H^{\prime}\cong H$ and $|H^{*}\cap H^{\prime}|\leqslant(q-\delta)k$}\right]\\
\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left[\text{$\exists$ $J^{\prime}\subseteq G\cup H^{*}$ such that $J^{\prime}\cong J^{*}$ and $|H^{*}\cap J^{\prime}|\leqslant(q-\delta)k$}\right]

This can be bounded further by Markov’s inequality and the definition of the
G(n,p)
noise to give,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\text{$\exists$ $J^{\prime}\subseteq G\cup H^{*}$ such that $J^{\prime}\cong J^{*}$ and $|H^{*}\cap J^{\prime}|\leqslant(q-\delta)k$}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leqslant\sum_{\ell\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\mathbb{E}[\left|\text{$J^{\prime}\subseteq G\cup H^{*}$ such that $J^{\prime}\cong J^{*}$ and $|H^{*}\cap J^{\prime}|=\ell$}\right|]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leqslant\sum_{\ell\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\mathbb{P}_{J^{\prime},H^{\prime}\sim K_{n}}\left[|J^{\prime}\cap H^{\prime}|=\ell\right]\cdot M_{J}p^{L-\ell},

where
M_{J}
is the number of copies of
J
in
K_{n}
, and
L=|J|\geqslant qk
(by
Theorem

3.6
(b)) and by
\mathbb{P}_{J^{\prime},H^{\prime}\sim K_{n}}
we refer to the joint measure of
J^{\prime}
, and copy of
J^{*}
in
K_{n}
and
H^{\prime}
an independent copy of
H
in
K_{n}
.

By employing
[
MNS+23
, Lemma 4.4]
,

\mathbb{P}_{J^{\prime},H^{\prime}\sim K_{n}}\left[|J^{\prime}\cap H^{\prime}|=\ell\right]\leqslant v(H)^{O(v(H))}\cdot n^{-v_{\ell}(J)},

where
v_{\ell}(J)
is the minimal number of vertices in a subgraph of
J
with
\ell
edges.
Therefore,

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)-(1-q+\delta)\leqslant v(H)^{O(v(H))}\sum_{\ell\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\cdot n^{-v_{\ell}(J)}\cdot M_{J}p^{L-\ell}

Now observing that
M_{J}=(n)_{v(J)}/|\mathrm{Aut}(H)|\leqslant n^{v(J)}
, we get that

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)-(1-q+\delta)\leqslant\sum_{\ell\leqslant(q-\delta)k}v(H)^{O(v(H))}\cdot n^{v(J)-v_{\ell}(J)}\cdot p^{L-\ell}.

By the definitions of
\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}
and
J^{*}
, we have that for all
\ell\leqslant(q-\delta)k
,

\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}\leqslant n^{-\frac{v(J)-v_{\ell}(J)}{L-\ell}}.

Thus, for large enough
n
, since
p\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\widetilde{\varphi}_{q},
we also have

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)-(1-q+\delta)\leqslant v(H)^{O(v(H))}\sum_{\ell\leqslant(q-\delta)k}(1-\varepsilon)^{L-\ell}\leqslant v(H)^{O(v(H))}k(1-\varepsilon)^{\delta k}=o(1),

as desired.

∎

6.5.
Proof of
Lemma

6.2
: Impossibility of recovery for
p\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\varphi_{q}

In this section, we prove
Lemma

6.2
. The proof of this lemma will essentially be a formalization of the intuition mentioned above.

By definition of
\varphi_{q}
, there exists some
S\subseteq H
with
|S|\leqslant qk
, for which we have

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\max\left\{n^{-\frac{|V(T)\setminus V(S)|}{|T\setminus S|}}:T\subseteq H\right\}.

Let
\delta>0
be a fixed but arbitrary small constant, which we will eventually shrink it to
0
. In particular, it must hold

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\max\left\{n^{-\frac{|V(T)\setminus V(S)|}{|T\setminus S|}}:T\subseteq H,|T\setminus S|\geqslant\delta k.\right\}.

Our goal will be to show that, for all
p
satisfying the inequality above, it will be impossible to recover better than a
\delta
-fraction of the set of edges
S^{c}.
This will imply by standard Bayesian tools that

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)\geqslant 1-q-O(\delta)-o(1),

and thus
Lemma

6.2
, since
\delta
can be taken arbitrarily small.

More concretely, our technique will be to show that even if the statistician is given the
S^{*}
-part of the planted graph
H^{*}
as side information, it will be impossible for the statistician to recover an
\Omega(1)
fraction of the rest of
H^{*}\setminus S^{*}
; in particular, with side information the statistician must miss at least a
1-q-o(1)
fraction of
H*
, and then via a data processing argument, we can conclude the same for the original setting without side information.

To define this alternate problem, consider the following inference problem defined on slightly modified graphs where the edges of a particular subgraph of
H
is removed from the observations.

Definition 6.4

(Planted
S^{c}
-graph)
.

Let
H=H_{n}
be a graph and
H^{*}
be a uniformly random draw from the isomorphism class of
H
in
K_{n}
. Let also
S
be a subgraph of
H
and
S^{*}
an arbitrary copy of
S
in
H^{*}
. The
planted
S^{c}
-graph
problem is the task to infer
H^{*}\setminus S^{*}
given a draw from
G=(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})\cup G_{0}
, where
G_{0}
is the product Bernoulli measure with parameter
p
only on the edges of
K_{n}\setminus S^{*}
. Moreover, we define the MMSE of the problem,

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n,S}(p)=\frac{1}{|H|-|S|}\mathbb{E}[\|1_{H^{*}\setminus S^{*}}-\mathbb{E}[1_{H^{*}\setminus S^{*}}\|^{2}_{2}|G].

Notice that in the planted
S^{c}
graph problem, the observed
G
is only supported on the edges of
K_{n}\setminus S^{*}
.

We start with the following reduction from the original problem to the planted graph-cut problem for
S
, at an MMSE loss of a multiplicative factor
1-q.

Lemma 6.5

(Reduction)
.

Let
S\subseteq H
with
|S|\leqslant q|H|
. Recall
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)
is the MMSE of the original planted problem and
\operatorname{MMSE}_{n,S}(p)
is the MMSE in the planted
S^{c}
-graph-cut problem. Then it holds

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n,S}(p)(1-q)\leqslant\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p).

Proof.

Given a copy
\widetilde{H}
of
H
, let us denote by
S(\widetilde{H})
the part of
\widetilde{H}
corresponding to a copy of
S
(resolving ties arbitrarily). Notice that for any fixed copy
S_{0}
of
S
in
K_{n},
by the law of total variance we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=\frac{1}{|H|}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Var}(H^{*}|G)\geqslant\frac{1}{|H|}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Var}(H^{*}|G,S\widetilde{(}H)=S_{0})

But now consider the statistical model where the prior is uniform over all copies
H^{\prime}
of
H
so that
S(H^{\prime})=S_{0},
and the noise is as usual an independent instance of
G(n,p).
The posterior
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S_{0}}
in this model is uniform among all copies
H^{\prime}
of
H
in the observed graph with
S(H^{\prime})=S_{0}
, and therefore the
unnormalized
MMSE in this model satisfies

\operatorname{MMSE}^{\prime}_{S_{0},n}(p)=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Var}(H^{*}|G,S\widetilde{(}H)=S_{0}).

In particular,

\frac{1}{|H|}\operatorname{MMSE}^{\prime}_{S_{0},n}(p)\leqslant\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p).

Moreover clearly the posterior in the planted
S_{0}^{c}
-graph model is the marginal of
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S_{0}}
on
K_{n}\setminus S_{0}
. Therefore by two applications of the Nishimori identity
Lemma

A.1
(identifying graphs on
n
vertices with vectors in
\{0,1\}^{\binom{n}{2}}),

\operatorname{MMSE}^{\prime}_{S_{0},n}(p)=|H|-\mathbb{E}_{v,v^{\prime}\sim^{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathbb{P^{\prime}}_{S_{0}}}\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle=|H|-(|S|+\mathbb{E}_{v,v^{\prime}\sim^{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathbb{P}_{S}}\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle)=(|H|-|S|)\operatorname{MMSE}_{S_{0},n}(p)

The second equality follows because the bits about the edges of
S_{0}
are always equal to one under
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S_{0}}.
The result follows since for any copy
S_{0}
of
S
, clearly
\operatorname{MMSE}_{S_{0},n}(p)=\operatorname{MMSE}_{S,n}(p),
and of course
|H|-|S|\geqslant(1-q)|H|.

∎

Given
Lemma

6.5
, to complete the proof of impossibility of recovery, we require the following impossibility result for the planted graph-cut problem.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
q\in[0,1),

H=H_{n}
a weakly dense graph and
S=S_{n}\subseteq H,|S|\leqslant q|H|.
Consider the planted
S^{c}
-graph problem for the planted graph
H
. Then, assume that for some constants
\varepsilon,\delta>0,
we have for large enough

,

p\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\max\left\{n^{-\frac{|V(T)\setminus V(S)|}{|T\setminus S|}}:T\subseteq H,|T\setminus S|\geqslant\delta|H|.\right\}.

Then

\operatorname{MMSE}_{S,n}(p)\geqslant 1-\delta/(1-q)-o(1).

Proof.

Let
k\coloneqq|H|-|S|
. Let also
\widetilde{G}=(H^{*}\setminus S)\cup G_{0}
be a sample from the planted
S^{c}
-graph distribution as defined in

6.4
. In particular, let
H^{*}
the planted copy of
H
and
S^{*}
the (arbitrarily chosen) copy of

in

First notice that it suffices to show that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})|}{k}\geqslant\delta/(1-q)\right]

\displaystyle=o_{N}(1),

where
H^{\prime}
is a uniformly random isomorphic copy of
H
containing
S^{*}
that is present in the graph
\widetilde{G}\cup S^{*}.
Indeed, a simple application of Baye’s rule implies that the posterior of the planted
S^{c}
-graph problem for
H
is uniform on all
H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*}
where
H^{\prime}
is a copy of
H
in
\widetilde{G}\cup S^{*}
containing
S^{*}
. Given that (
6.1
) and Nishimori’s identity (
Lemma

A.1
) imply the result.

In the calculation below, we always condition on
S^{*}.
For convenience, we introduce some new notation. We denote by
(S^{*})^{c}
-graph, any graph on edges only in
K_{n}\setminus S^{*}.
We define
G^{\prime}
to be an independent sample from the product
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)
measure on
K_{n}\setminus S^{*}
(that is
G^{\prime}
has no planted copy
H^{*}\setminus S^{*}
) and
\delta^{\prime}\coloneqq\delta/(1-q)
.
We also denote for an
(S^{*})^{c}
-graph
G
,

(a)

by
Z(G)
the number of isomorphic copies
H^{\prime}
of
H
containing
S^{*}
in
G\cup S^{*},

(b)

by
Z_{\ell}(H^{*},G)
the number of copies
H^{\prime}
of
H
containing
S^{*}
in
G\cup S^{*}
with

|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})|=\ell,

and

(c)

by
Z^{2}(\ell,G)
is the number of pairs of copies

of

containing
S^{*}
in

with

|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{\prime\prime}\setminus S^{*})|=\ell.

For any
\varepsilon>0
We have that by direct calculations (similar to the proof of

where the universe are all edges minus the edges in

)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})|}{k}\geqslant\delta^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{Z(\widetilde{G})}\sum_{\ell\geqslant k\delta^{\prime}}Z_{\ell}(H^{\prime},\widetilde{G})\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}[Z(\widetilde{G})\leqslant\varepsilon\mathbb{E}Z(G^{\prime})]+\frac{1}{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}Z(G^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\sum_{\ell\geqslant k\delta^{\prime}}Z_{\ell}(H^{*},\widetilde{G})

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}Z(G^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\sum_{\ell\geqslant k\delta^{\prime}}Z_{\ell}(H^{*},\widetilde{G})

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\varepsilon(\mathbb{E}Z(G^{\prime}))^{2}}\mathbb{E}\sum_{\ell\geqslant k\delta^{\prime}}Z^{2}(\ell,G^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sum_{\ell\geqslant k\delta^{\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{P}_{H^{\prime}}(|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})|=\ell)}{p^{\ell}}

where in the two last inequalities we used a Bayesian inference idea called the “planting trick”
[
ACO08
]
, as executed in
[
MNS+23
, Lemmas 3.9, 3.10]
, which allows us to pass from the planted observation graph
\tilde{G}
to the “null” graph
G^{\prime}
. Moreover, in the last equality under
\mathrm{P}_{H^{\prime}}
,
H^{\prime}
is a uniform copy random isomorphic copy of
H
in
K_{n}
containing
S^{*}
and
H^{*}
is without loss of generality any fixed arbitrary copy of
H
in
K_{n}
containing
S*
. Hence it suffices to show that

\sum_{\ell\geqslant k\delta^{\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{P}_{H^{\prime}}(|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})|=\ell)}{p^{\ell}}=o_{N}(1).

Now we employ an idea
[
MNS+23
, Lemma 4.4]
to show this, by adjusting it to our
S^{c}
-graph setting. Fix some
\ell\geqslant\delta^{\prime}k
. Notice that for a copy
H^{\prime}
to satisfy
|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})|=\ell
it must hold
|V(H^{\prime}\cap H^{*})\setminus V(S^{*})|\geqslant v_{\ell,S^{*}}(H)
where
v_{\ell,S^{*}}(H)
is the minimum value of
|V(T)\setminus V(S^{*})|
among all subsets
T
of
(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})
with
|T|=\ell
edges. But to choose the random copy
H^{\prime}
of
H
containing
S^{*}
,
|V(H^{\prime}\cap H^{*})\setminus V(S^{*})|
is simply an Hypergeometric distribution with parameters
(v(H)-v(S^{*}),v(H)-v(S^{*}),n-v(S^{*}))
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{H^{\prime}}(|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})|)

\displaystyle\leqslant\binom{n-v(S^{*})}{v(H)-v(S^{*})}^{-1}\sum_{w=v_{\ell,S^{*}}(H)}^{v(H)-v(S^{*})}\binom{v(H)-v(S^{*})}{w}\binom{n-v(H)}{v(H)-v(S^{*})-w}

\displaystyle\leqslant v(H)^{O(v(H))}\sum_{w=v_{\ell,S^{*}}(H)}^{v(H)-v(S^{*})}\frac{(n-v(H))_{v(H)-v(S^{*})-w}}{(n-v(S^{*}))_{v(H)-v(S^{*})}}

\displaystyle\leqslant v(H)^{O(v(H))}\frac{(n-v(H))_{v(H)-v(S^{*})-v_{\ell,S^{*}}(H)}}{(n-v(S^{*}))_{v(H)-v(S^{*})}}

\displaystyle\leqslant v(H)^{O(v(H))}(n-v(H))^{-v_{\ell,S^{*}}(H)}

\displaystyle\leqslant v(H)^{O(v(H))}n^{-v_{\ell,S^{*}}(H)}.

Notice let
T_{1}\subseteq H^{*}\setminus S^{*}
be such that
|T_{1}|=\ell
and
|V(T)\setminus V(S^{*})|=v_{\ell,S^{*}}(H)
. Hence for
T=T_{1}\cup S^{*}
it must hold
|T\setminus S^{*}|=\ell
and
|V(T)\setminus V(S^{*})|=v_{\ell,S*}(H).
Now by assumption on
p
since
\ell\geqslant\delta^{\prime}k=\delta(|H|-|S|)/(1-q)\geqslant\delta|H|
it must hold that
p^{|T\setminus S^{*}|}n^{|V(T)\setminus V(S^{*})|}\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)^{|T\setminus S^{*}|}
and therefore

p^{\ell}n^{v_{\ell,S^{*}}(H)}\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)^{\ell}.

Combining the last two displayed equations we have for all
\ell\in[\delta^{\prime}k,k],

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{P}_{H^{\prime}}(|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})|)}{p{\ell}}

\displaystyle\leqslant v(H)^{O(v(H))}(1+\varepsilon)^{-\ell},

hence

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\geqslant k\delta^{\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{P}_{H^{\prime}}(|(H^{\prime}\setminus S^{*})\cap(H^{*}\setminus S^{*})|)}{p{\ell}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\ell\geqslant k\delta}\frac{v(H)^{O(v(H))}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=v(H)^{O(v(H))}(1+\varepsilon)^{-\delta^{\prime}k}=o(1)

where we have used that
\ell\geqslant k\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}(|H|-|S|)/(1-q)\geqslant\delta|H|=\omega(v(H)\log v(H))
where the last inequality is by the weakly dense assumption on
H
. This concludes the proof.
∎

6.6.
Proof of
Lemma

6.3
: A minimax result

In this section, we proof
Lemma

6.3
, which is the last missing part of proving
Theorem

3.1
. To do so we establish the following stronger claim.

Theorem 6.7
.

For
q\in(0,1)
, define

\rho_{q}=\min\left\{\max\left\{\rho(J|S):J\supseteq S\right\}:S\subseteq E(H),|S|\leqslant qk\right\}

and

\widetilde{\rho}_{q}=\max\left\{\min\left\{\rho(J|S):S\subseteq J,|S|\leqslant qk\right\}:J\subseteq E(H)\right\}.

Then,

(a)

\rho_{q}=\widetilde{\rho}_{q}
. Equivalently,
\varphi_{q}=\widetilde{\varphi}_{q}
.

(b)

There exists an optimizer
(S^{*},J^{*})
in the definition of
\rho_{q}
(and
\widetilde{\rho}_{q}
) such that
|J^{*}|\geqslant qk
.

Clearly,
Theorem

6.7
(a) immediately yields
Lemma

6.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

6.7
.

It is not difficult to see that
Theorem

3.6
(b), in conjunction with
Theorem

6.7
(a), immediately yields
Theorem

6.7
(b). It remains to prove (a).

Let us first show the easier direction, that
\widetilde{\rho}_{q}\leqslant\rho_{q}
.
Let
(S^{*},J^{*})
be an optimizer in the definition of
\widetilde{\rho}_{q}
, and
S\subseteq H
be arbitrary. Our goal is to show that there exists
J^{\prime}\supseteq S
such that
\rho(J^{\prime}|S)\geqslant\rho(J^{*}|S^{*})
. Set
J^{\prime}=J^{*}\cup S
and
T=J^{*}\cap S
. Then, by
Lemma

5.1
,

\rho(J^{\prime}|S)\geqslant\rho(J^{*}|T)\geqslant\rho(J^{*}|S^{*}),

where the second inequality is because
\rho(J^{*}|S^{*})=\min_{S\subseteq J}\rho(J^{*}|S)
, so
\widetilde{\rho}_{q}\leqslant\rho_{q}
.

Next, suppose that
(J^{(t)},J^{(t+1)})
is an optimizer in the definition of
\rho_{q}
, using
Theorem

3.6
(b). We shall show that

\widetilde{\rho}_{q}\geqslant\min_{S\subseteq J^{(t+1)}}\rho(J^{(t+1)}|S)\geqslant\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)})=\rho_{q}.

The first inequality follows from the definition of
\widetilde{\rho}_{q}
and that
|J^{(t+1)}|>q|H|
, so it remains to show the middle inequality. Let
S\subseteq J^{(t+1)}
be arbitrary, and for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant t+1
, set
S_{i}=S\cap J^{(i)}
. First off, note that
\left(\rho(J^{(r+1)}|J^{(r)})\right)_{r\geqslant 0}
is a non-increasing sequence in
r
by
Lemma

5.4
. By
Lemma

5.2
, for all
0\leqslant i\leqslant t
,
\rho(J^{(i+1)}|S_{i+1}\cup J^{(i)})\geqslant\rho(J^{(i+1)}|J^{(i)})
. As a result, for all
i\leqslant t,

\rho(J^{(i+1)}|S_{i+1}\cup J^{(i)})\geqslant\rho(J^{(t+1)}|J^{(t)})=\rho_{q}.

But by leveraging the increasing structure of the
J^{(i)},0\leqslant i\leqslant t+1
, it holds

\displaystyle\rho(J^{(t+1)}|S)

\displaystyle=\frac{|J^{(t+1)}\setminus S|}{|V(J^{(t+1)})|-|V(S)|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{t}|(J^{(i+1)}\setminus J^{(i)})\cap(J^{(t+1)}\setminus S)|}{\sum_{i=0}^{t}|V(J^{(i+1)})|-|V(J^{(i)}\cup S_{i+1})|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{t}|(J^{(i+1)}\setminus J^{(i)})\cap(J^{(i+1)}\setminus S)|}{\sum_{i=0}^{t}|V(J^{(i+1)})|-|V(J^{(i)}\cup S_{i+1})|}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{t}|J^{(i+1)}|-|J^{(i)}\cup S_{i+1}|}{\sum_{i=1}^{t}|V(J^{(i+1)})|-|V(J^{(i)}\cup S_{i+1})|}\geqslant\min_{0\leqslant i\leqslant t}\rho(J^{(i+1)}|S_{i+1}\cup J^{(i)})\geqslant\rho_{q},

as desired.
∎

7.
The Minimax rate for any monotone property: Proof of
Theorem

4.5

We now prove
Theorem

4.5
. We do this by first establishing the positive result (part (b)), and then the negative result (part (a)).

7.1.
The positive result: Proof of
Theorem

4.5
(b)

The proof follows by an appropriate application of a “fractional” first moment method. While this method has been used in the probabilistic combinatorics literature, we are not aware of any such application in the high dimensional statistics literature.

Proof.

We establish the following property. For any
\delta>0
, we have that with high probability for any
A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
with
A_{i}\subseteq Y
it must hold
|A_{i}\cap A|\geqslant(q-\delta)k
.

Notice that this property is sufficient to conclude the result. Indeed, Baye’s rule implies that for an arbitrary prior
\pi
on
\mathcal{A}
the posterior is assign mass to each
A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
given by the formula
\hat{\pi}(A_{i})\propto\pi(A_{i})\mathbf{1}(A_{i}\subseteq Y).
In particular, the suggested property implies that with high probability the posterior outputs with high probability an
A_{i}
with
|A_{i}\cap A|\geqslant(q-\delta)k.
Therefore by Nishimori’s identity (
Lemma

A.1
),

\displaystyle\operatorname{MMSE}_{N}(p,\pi)\leqslant 1-(q-\delta)+o(1).

Since
\delta>0
is arbitrary we conclude the desired result.

Now to prove the desired property, first observe that since
Y=A\cup\mathcal{X}_{p}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{A,Y}\left[\text{$\exists$ $A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}$ such that $A_{i}\subseteq Y,|A\cap A_{i}|\leqslant(q-\delta)k$}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A}\sum_{S\subseteq A,|S|\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\mathbb{P}_{Y}\left[\text{$\exists$ $A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}$ such that $A_{i}\subseteq Y,A\cap A_{i}=S$}\right]

(7.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A}\sum_{S\subseteq A,|S|\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\left[\text{$\exists$ $A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}$ such that $A_{i}\setminus S\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{p},A_{i}\subseteq S$}\right].

Now for any
S
we can choose
w_{S}\in W_{S}
with
\sum_{T}w_{S}(T)(\psi_{q})^{|T\setminus S|}\leqslant 1/2
. Notice that since by definition of
w_{S}
we have
\min_{A_{i}\in\mathcal{A},S\subseteq A_{i}}\sum_{T\subseteq A_{i}}w_{S}(T)\geqslant 1,
it also holds

\mathbf{1}\left[\text{$\exists$ $A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}$ such that $A_{i}\setminus S\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{p}$}\right]\leqslant\sum_{T}w_{S}(T)1(T\setminus S\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{p})

and therefore

\mathbb{E}_{A}\sum_{S\subseteq A,|S|\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\left[\text{$\exists$ $A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}$ such that $A_{i}\setminus S\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{p}$}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{A}\sum_{S\subseteq A,|S|\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\sum_{T}w_{S}(T)p^{|T\setminus S|}.

Combining with (
7.1
) gives,

\mathbb{P}_{A,Y}\left[\text{$\exists$ $A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}$ such that $A_{i}\subseteq Y,|A\cap A_{i}|\leqslant qk$}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{A}\sum_{S\subseteq A,|S|\leqslant(q-\delta)k}\sum_{T}w_{S}(T)p^{|T\setminus S|}.

Since
w
is only supported on
T
containing
S
, for all
T
in the inside summation we have
|T\setminus S|=|T|-|S|\geqslant\delta k
, and therefore using also the
p<\psi_{q}
and the definition of
w_{S}
,

\mathbb{P}_{A,Y}\left[\text{$\exists$ $A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}$ such that $A_{i}\subseteq Y,|A\cap A_{i}|\leqslant qk$}\right]\leqslant 2^{qk}(p/\psi_{q})^{\delta k}\sum_{T}w_{S}(T)\psi_{q}^{|T\setminus S|}\leqslant 2^{qk}(p/\psi_{q})^{\delta k}.

Since
p=o(\psi_{q})
we conclude the result.
∎

7.2.
The negative result: Proof of
Theorem

4.5
(a)

We now prove the negative result of the theorem. The proof follows by an interesting use of a duality trick of Talagrand between fractional covers and spread measures.

Proof.

By definition, there exists an
S\subseteq\mathcal{X},|S|\leqslant qk
such that

\max_{w\in W_{S}}\sum_{T\subseteq\mathcal{X}}w(T)\psi_{q}^{|T\setminus S|}\leqslant 1/2.

Using the strong duality of linear programs, we conclude that there exists some function
\pi^{\prime}
from the set
\mathcal{A}(S)\coloneqq\left\{A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}:S\subseteq A_{i}\right\}
to
[0,1]
such that

(a)

\displaystyle\sum_{A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}(S)}\pi^{\prime}(A_{i})=1/2
.

(b)

For all
T
such that
S\subseteq T
and
|T|\geqslant qk
,

\sum_{A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}(S),T\subseteq A_{i}}\pi^{\prime}(A_{i})\leqslant(\psi_{q})^{|T\setminus S|}.

Hence, setting
\pi(A_{i})=2\pi^{\prime}(A_{i})
for all
A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}(S)
we conclude the existence of a measure on
\mathcal{A}(S)
such that for all
T
such that
S\subseteq T
and
|T|\geqslant qk
it holds

(7.2)

\displaystyle\pi_{X\sim\pi}(T\subseteq X)\leqslant 2(\psi_{q})^{|T\setminus S|}.

Remark 7.1
.

In the language of probabilistic combinatorics, if (
7.2
) was true for all
T
, the measure would be called
\psi_{q}
-spread. Yet, in our setting we only know that the “spread” condition holds for a subset of
T
’s. Interesting, it is this spread-type of condition that will allow us to use
\pi
as a “non-favorable enough” prior to conclude our tight minimax rate lower bound.

We use the “spread”
\pi
as our prior and prove that
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{MMSE}_{N}(p,\pi)\geqslant 1-q
which concludes the proof. By the Nishimori identity (
Lemma

A.1
), it suffices to prove that for any
1-q>\delta>0
it holds
\mathbb{P}(|A^{\prime}\cap A|\geqslant(q+\delta)k)=o(1)
where
A\sim\pi
and
A^{\prime}
is sampled from the posterior of
A
given
Y=A\cup X_{p}
.

For any
A\in\mathcal{A}(S)
,
Y\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}
and
t\in[q+\delta,1]
, let

(7.3)

Z_{Y}(A,t)\coloneqq\sum_{A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}(S)}\pi(A_{i})\mathbf{1}\{A_{i}\subseteq Y,|A\cap A_{i}|\geqslant tk\}.

Also denote for simplicity
Z_{Y}\coloneqq Z_{Y}(A,0).
A quick application of Baye’s rule gives that the posterior distribution is
\mathbb{P}(A|Y)=\pi(A)\mathbf{1}\{A\subseteq Y\}/Z_{Y},A\in\mathcal{A}(S),
and therefore we have the identity

\mathbb{P}(|A^{\prime}\cap A|\geqslant(q+\delta)k)=\mathbb{E}_{A,Y}Z_{Y}(A,q+\delta)/Z_{Y}.

Now we claim that if we establish
p^{|S|-k}\mathbb{E}_{A,Y}Z_{Y}(A,q+\delta)=o(1),
then we can conclude
\mathbb{E}_{A,Y}Z_{Y}(A,q+\delta)/Z_{Y}=o(1)
.

This implication follows by a careful “change of measure” argument. To see this denote by
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
the product law of
Y=X_{p}\setminus S,
and by
\mathbb{P}_{p}
the “planted” law of
Y=(A\setminus S)\cup(X_{p}\setminus S)
for independent
A\sim\pi,X_{p}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{p}.
Also recall that
\pi
is always supported on sets containing
S
. Combining these, it holds for all
Y\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that

\mathbb{P}_{p}(Y\setminus S|A)=p^{|Y|-k}\mathbf{1}(A\subseteq Y)=p^{|S|-k}\mathbf{1}(A\subseteq Y)\mathbb{Q}_{p}(Y\setminus S),

and therefore the likelihood ratio between
\mathbb{P}_{p}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
take the following form for all
Y\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{p}(Y\setminus S)}{\mathbb{Q}_{p}(Y\setminus S)}=Z_{Y}p^{|S|-k}.

In particular, for all
\varepsilon>0,

\mathbb{P}_{p}(Z_{Y}\leqslant\varepsilon p^{k-|S|})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}[\frac{\mathbb{P}_{p}(Y\setminus S)}{\mathbb{Q}_{p}(Y\setminus S)}\mathbf{1}(Z_{Y}p^{|S|-k}\leqslant\varepsilon)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}[Z_{Y}p^{|S|-k}\mathbf{1}(Z_{Y}p^{|S|-k}\leqslant\varepsilon)]\leqslant\varepsilon.

Hence, for any
\varepsilon>0,
since almost surely

Z_{Y}(A,q+\delta)/Z_{Y}\leqslant 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{A,Y}Z_{Y}(A,q+\delta)/Z_{Y}\leqslant\frac{1}{\varepsilon}p^{|S|-k}\mathbb{E}_{A,Y}Z_{Y}(A,q+\delta)+\varepsilon.

From the last inequality by optimizing over
\varepsilon>0
we conclude

\mathbb{E}_{A,Y}Z_{Y}(A,q+\delta)/Z_{Y}\leqslant 2\sqrt{p^{|S|-k}\mathbb{E}_{A,Y}Z_{Y}(A,q+\delta)},

from which the claimed implication follows.

Now by linearity of expectation and (
7.3
) (for the first equality), (
7.2
) (for the second inequality) and direct counting arguments,

\displaystyle p^{|S|-k}\mathbb{E}_{A,Y}Z_{Y}(A,q+\delta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geqslant(q+\delta)k}\pi^{\otimes 2}(|A\cap A^{\prime}|=\ell)p^{|S|-\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\ell\geqslant(q+\delta)k}\mathbb{E}_{A}\sum_{T\subseteq A,|T|=\ell}\pi(T\subseteq A^{\prime})p^{|S|-\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{\ell\geqslant(q+\delta)k}\binom{k-|S|}{\ell-|S|}(\psi_{q}/p)^{\ell-|S|}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{\ell\geqslant(q+\delta)k}(ke/(\ell-|S|)^{\ell-|S|}(\psi_{q}/p)^{\ell-|S|}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{\ell\geqslant(q+\delta)k}(e/\delta)^{\ell-|S|}(\psi_{q}/p)^{\ell-|S|}

\displaystyle=O\left(((\psi_{q}e)/(\delta p))^{\delta k}\right).

Since
p=\omega(\psi_{q})
the last quantity is
o(1)
and the proof is complete.
∎

In this section we prove some standard results that are needed for the main body of this work.

We start with the Nishimori identity we use often in this work.

Lemma A.1

(Nishimori’s identity)
.

Suppose a Bayesian inference setting where the parameter
\theta
is sampled from some prior
\pi
supported on
S^{N-1}
, i.e.,
\theta\sim\pi,
and we have observations
Y\sim P_{\theta}
. Let also
\pi^{\prime}(Y)
be the posterior distribution of
\theta
given
Y
. Then,

\operatorname{MMSE}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}[\|\theta-\mathbb{E}[\theta|Y]\|^{2}_{2}]=1-\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi,Y\sim P_{\theta},\theta^{\prime}\sim\pi^{\prime}(Y)}[\langle\theta,\theta^{\prime}\rangle].

In particular, for the planted subgraph model for a graph
H
it holds

\operatorname{MMSE}_{n}(p)=1-\frac{1}{|H|}\mathbb{E}[|H^{*}\cap H^{\prime}|],

where
H^{\prime}
is a uniform random copy of
H
in the observed graph
G.

Proof.

See
[
NZ23
, Lemma 2]
for the proof of the identity. The application for the normalized MMSE of the planted subgraph model follows immediately from the fact that the posterior in the planted subgraph model is the uniform measure on the copies of
H
in the observed graph
G
.
∎

The following is a standard lemma on the MMSE curve.
