Hemodialysis dose and frequency should be considered in subgroup analysis
Authors: Lu Hu, Zhonghua Wang, Xiang He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe article by Wang et al. titled “Exploring the mortality and cardiovascular outcomes with SGLT-2 inhibitors in patients with T2DM at dialysis commencement: a health global federated network analysis” demonstrated that new SGLT-2i use in T2DM patients at the onset of dialysis was associated with a reduced long-term risk of all-cause mortality and MACE over a median follow-up duration of 2.0 years. However, the hemodialysis dose and frequency, which are significant confounding factors, were not included in the study’s subgroup analysis. We raise concerns about this limitation, which may affect the study’s findings.

We read the article by Wang et al. [1] with great interest. The study indicated that new SGLT-2i use in T2DM patients at the onset of dialysis was associated with a reduced long-term risk of all-cause mortality (aHR = 0.49, p < 0.001) and MACE (aHR = 0.52, p < 0.001) over a median follow-up of 2.0 years. Hemodialysis is associated with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with or without T2DM. The hemodialysis dose (Kt/V or Kt as the common and clinically valid measure) and frequency, significant confounding factors, were not included in the study’s subgroup analysis. We note the limitations and discuss why the hemodialysis dose and frequency should be considered in the subgroup analysis.

The current BMC nephrology guidelines [2] recommend the hemodialysis dose be targeted to achieve a minimum Kt/V over 1.2 for patients receiving treatment thrice weekly. Nevertheless, the effects of the hemodialysis dose and frequency on mortality and cardiovascular outcomes among patients undergoing hemodialysis are somewhat controversial. In a prospective cohort (n = 558) of maintenance hemodialysis patients [3], dialysis adequacy was assessed via baseline Kt/V; compared with the low-dose group, the high-dose group had a 33% lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.47–0.98). Notably, the high-dose group was associated with both lower all-cause (HR = 0.46, 95% CI = 0.26–0.81) and CVD mortality (HR = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.20–0.88) in patients aged < 65 years. A prospective cohort study by Béguin L et al. [4] reported that a higher dialysis dose was consistently associated with better survival, and when the causes of death in the four Kt quartiles were compared, mortality related to cachexia was significantly greater in the lower Kt group and decreased with increasing Kt (p < 0.0001). In addition, an international prospective cohort study reported a significant relationship between Kt/V and the RR of mortality, independent of TT; for every 0.1 higher Kt/V, the RR of mortality was 2% lower (p < 0.05) [5]. Conversely, in the HEMO study, the primary outcome, all-cause mortality, was not significantly influenced by the dose, in which the relative risk of death in the high-dose group compared with the standard-dose group was 0.96 (95% CI = 0.47–0.98, p = 0.53) in patients undergoing hemodialysis thrice weekly [6]. Similarly, in terms of hemodialysis frequency, in the FHN Trial [7], compared with those in the conventional hemodialysis group, patients in the frequent hemodialysis group averaged 5.2 per week, which was associated with significant benefits concerning both coprimary composite outcomes (HR for death or increase in left ventricular mass, 0.61, 95% CI = 0.46–0.82; HR for death or a decrease in the physical‒health composite score, 0.70, 95% CI = 0.53–0.92).

In summary, the hemodialysis dose and frequency can affect all-cause mortality and/or cardiovascular outcomes. If we do not include confounding factors in the study and conduct subgroup analysis, the trial’s accuracy and rigour will be compromised.
