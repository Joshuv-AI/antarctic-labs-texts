Co-opted ATG2 lipid transfer protein delivers phospholipids for biogenesis of viral replication organelles
Authors: Yuanrong Kang, Judit Pogany, Peter D Nagy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPositive-strand RNA viruses, which are important pathogens of humans, animals and plants, subvert cellular membranes and induce de novo membrane proliferation to generate viral replication organelles (VROs) that support virus replication. Tomato bushy stunt virus (TBSV), an extensively-studied plant virus replicating in yeast model host and plants, hijacks ATG2 (autophagy-related 2), a lipid transfer protein (LTP) that transports lipids between adjacent organelles at membrane contact sites, for the biogenesis of their membranous VROs. Subversion of ATG2 by TBSV is important to enrich VRO membranes with phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylserine (PS) and the phosphoinositide phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate [PI(3)P], which are all required for viral replication. TBSV replication protein directly interacts with ATG2 leading to recruitment to VRO membranes independently of the autophagy machinery.

The biogenesis of the membranous VROs requires robust de novo membrane proliferation supported by virus-driven rewiring of several cellular pathways and subversion of host proteins. TBSV genomic RNA codes for p33 replication protein, which is the master regulator of VRO biogenesis. TBSV p33 replication protein subverts peroxisomal and ER membranes as primary sources of VRO biogenesis, causing peroxisome and ER aggregation (Figure 1, central scheme). Recent findings have revealed that the autophagy and autophagy-related (ATG) proteins are exploited by TBSV in two ways to both support virus replication and suppress the antiviral role of autophagy. The first one involves the subversion of selective autophagy. The second one is characterized by the co-opting of individual ATG proteins, such as ATG2, for pro-viral functions through a role in VROs establishment and function. Specifically, induction of selective autophagy by TBSV is proposed to lead to the formation of phagophores, which are hijacked by TBSV p33 replication protein through the targeting the selective autophagy receptor NBR1 (neighbor of brca1) and ATG8f (LC3 in mammals) into VROs (Figure 1, boxes #2 & #3). The co-opted phagophore is proposed to contribute membranes that are enriched in PE and other phospholipids to enhance VRO membrane biogenesis. The hijacked phagophore also favors the recruitment of VPS34-containing PI3K (phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase) complex to produce PI(3)P in the VROs. It has also been predicted that the subverted phagophores contribute to the delivery of the ATG2 LTP and the ATG9 lipid scramblase to VROs (Figure 1, box #2). We found that TBSV p33 replication protein hijacks ATG2 into VROs in yeast even in the absence of the ATG9 or ATG18 genes [1]. This suggests that p33-driven recruitment of ATG2 could take place independently of the canonical machinery of autophagy. The co-opted ATG2 was shown to be required for enrichment of phospholipids PE, PS and PI(3)P in VRO membranes.

Models describing the possible subversion mechanisms of ATG2 LTP to support TBSV replication.

We hypothize that the co-opted ATG2 is present at p33-induced MCSs between the ER and the co-opted peroxisome membrane, which we call the virus-induced vMCS. The vMCS contains p33 replication protein and several co-opted host proteins, including the ER-resident VAP (VAMP-associated protein) tethering proteins (e.g., SCS2 in yeast, VAP27-1 in plants), the selective autophagy adaptor ATG11 (FIP200 in mammals), the mitochondrial fission protein FIS1 and phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate [PI(4)P] phosphatase SAC1 (Figure 1, box #1). In addition, a subdomain of ER containing the ER-resident SNARE complex (such as UFE1, USE1 and SEC20) is critical for vMCS formation. vMCS also comprises oxysterol-binding proteins (OSBP in mammals, Osh proteins in yeast, and ORP proteins in plants), shuttle-type LTP proteins that deliver sterols from ER in exchange for PI(4)P phosphoinositide from the VROs (Figure 1, box #1). Sterols in VRO membranes are needed to stabilize the small spherules (Figure 1, central scheme) that contain the viral replicase complexes producing progeny viral RNAs.

ATG2 is known to transfer phospholipids on the cytosolic leaflet of both the donor and acceptor membranes, which causes membrane asymmetry and membrane irregularities that may lead to membrane deformations. To overcome this problem, the autophagy machinery includes ATG9 lipid scramblase to dissolve the potential membrane asymmetry between the membrane leaflets. Because TBSV hijacks ATG9 lipid scramblase into VROs for pro-viral functions in plants [1], we suggest that subversion of ATG9 lipid scramblase might reduce membrane asymmetry within VROs (Figure 1, boxes #1 and #2).

What is the advantage for TBSV in hijacking ATG2 LTP? Altogether, the advantage of co-opting selective autophagy and ATG2 LTP is to contribute phospholipids to TBSV VRO membranes. The de novo generation of protein-poor new membranes for VROs with the assistance of ATG2 LTP and the phagophore facilitates the activity of TBSV p33 and p92p°l replication proteins to build new viral replicase complexes without steric hindrances or other interference from host membrane proteins [1]. Moreover, TBSV infection inhibits the autophagy flux by sequestering ATG2 LTP and other core autophagy proteins to VROs. This in turn very likely damps the canonical antiviral function of autophagy. Altogether, TBSV manipulates the selective autophagy pathway for its own benefit.

Based on the critical role of ATG2 LTP in TBSV VRO membrane biogenesis, we propose that development of inhibitors that disrupt the interactions between viral replication proteins and lipid transfer proteins, including but not limited to ATG2, could impede the formation of functional VROs and inhibit viral infections.
