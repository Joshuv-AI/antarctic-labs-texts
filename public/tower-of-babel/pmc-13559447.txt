Integrated drainage-analgesia in thoracoscopic surgery: cautionary optimism amidst evolving multimodal guidelines
Authors: Justyna Danel, Joanna Zając, Szymon Białka
Published: 2026

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has revolutionized thoracic care by reducing surgical trauma and accelerating recovery. However, postoperative pain remains a significant clinical challenge. A major contributor to this pain is the chest drain, which causes intense noxious stimulation of the parietal pleura and intercostal nerves, impairing pulmonary mechanics, limiting cough efficacy, and delaying mobilization (1). Furthermore, current perioperative trends strongly emphasize aggressive opioid-sparing or even opioid-free anesthetic strategies combined with regional block techniques, which have been shown to significantly reduce early postoperative pain scores and cumulative opioid consumption in VATS procedures (2).

To optimize recovery, contemporary enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) and procedure-specific pain management protocols offer clear, evidence-based recommendations (1). The PROSPECT guidelines for VATS (2022) explicitly recommend regional analgesia—identifying thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) or erector spinae plane block (ESPB) as core components—while not recommending intrapleural analgesia due to limited procedure-specific evidence (3). Similarly, the Italian VATS Group ERAS perioperative guidelines (2018) prioritize TPVB, followed by intercostal nerve block (ICNB) or serratus anterior plane block (SAPB), making no endorsement of intrapleural techniques (4). While thoracic epidural analgesia (TEA) historically represented the gold standard, its routine adoption in minimally invasive thoracic surgery has waned due to its steep learning curve, technical complexity, failure rates, and potential for severe complications. Consequently, regional interfascial plane blocks have emerged as the practical standard of care (5,6).

Historically, intrapleural analgesia—the instillation of local anesthetics into the pleural cavity—has been viewed as an appealing theoretical alternative. By acting directly on free nerve endings of the visceral and parietal pleura, intrapleural local anesthetics aim to diffuse across the subpleural space to block intercostal nerves. While a few smaller studies (e.g., in thoracic sympathectomy, pediatric scoliosis, or thoracic resections) and recent trial protocol registrations suggest potential efficacy—including a notable 2025 study demonstrating that intrapleural liposomal bupivacaine reduced length of stay and rescue analgesia compared to systemic patient-controlled analgesia (PCA)—the literature remains divided (7-10). Comparative trials, such as a 90-patient randomized study on thoracoscopic sympathectomy, confirmed that TPVB offers significantly longer and superior analgesia compared to intrapleural administration (11). Furthermore, in routine practice, intrapleural local anesthetic delivery is frequently performed as an informal, off-label rescue measure when standard regional blocks fail. Delivering local anesthetics into a large closed body cavity carries inherent risks, including rapid systemic absorption, toxicity, and variable drug distribution washed out by chest tube drainage.

In a recent issue of the Journal of Thoracic Disease, Tang and colleagues address the ergonomics of intrapleural delivery through an innovative hardware approach. In their randomized controlled trial entitled “Targeted intrapleural analgesia through an integrated chest tube after thoracoscopic lung resection: a randomized controlled trial”, the authors introduce a novel drainage-analgesia integrated (DAI) chest tube. The device features a dedicated, axially continuous injection channel embedded within the drain wall, allowing localized local anesthetic delivery through lateral microporous side holes while maintaining negative-pressure chest drainage (12).

In their study of 47 patients undergoing uniportal VATS, patients received either the DAI chest tube with programmed intrapleural ropivacaine/lidocaine infusion or a standard (STD) chest tube. Both groups received identical systemic IV-PCA sufentanil. The DAI group demonstrated significantly lower Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain scores at 24 and 48 hours postoperatively, alongside higher Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) scores at 48 hours and drain removal.

While these findings are structurally innovative, a critical appraisal of the study highlights important methodological, pharmacological, and clinical caveats that necessitate cautious optimism.

Confounding device mechanics and lack of placebo control

A critical methodological oversight in the study design is the inability to separate the pharmacological effect of the local anesthetic from the physical properties of the drainage catheter itself. Patients in the intervention group simultaneously received two distinct variables: a structurally novel chest tube (with unique dimensions, material rigidity, cross-sectional geometry, and tissue-contact dynamics) and an active local anesthetic mixture. Conversely, the control group received a conventional chest drain without a placebo infusion through a DAI assembly. As a result, the physical mechanics of the new drain and its local pharmacological action are completely confounded. It is impossible to determine whether the observed reductions in early pain scores were driven by intrapleural analgesia or merely by superior ergonomics, reduced tissue drag, or lower parietal pleural irritation inherent to the DAI tube’s design. To conclusively demonstrate efficacy, future trials must incorporate a control group utilizing the DAI chest tube infused with a saline placebo.

Pharmacological rationale and local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) concerns

The use of a combined solution of ropivacaine and lidocaine delivered intermittently into the pleural space raises significant pharmacological and safety concerns:

❖ Dubious rationale for drug combination: the safety and efficacy of combining intrapleural lidocaine with long-acting local anesthetics such as ropivacaine or bupivacaine warrant rigorous scrutiny. Pulsatile administration of this dual-drug mixture into the highly vascularized pleural space carries a substantial risk of rapid systemic absorption, particularly given that LAST is strictly additive. Growing pharmacological and clinical evidence specifically advises against mixing bupivacaine or ropivacaine with lidocaine; studies demonstrate that such combinations do not provide superior or faster-onset analgesia compared to monotherapy, but instead increase the risk of adverse events and may even shorten the overall duration of the blockade (13-18).

Statistical vs. clinical significance and economic feasibility

Although the authors report statistically significant differences (P<0.05) in VAS and QoR-15 scores, they do not evaluate these findings against established minimal clinically important differences (MCID)—typically requiring a ≥1.5–2.0 cm reduction in VAS and a ≥6–8 point increase in QoR-15. Without presenting absolute mean differences with 95% confidence intervals or calculating the number needed to treat (NNT), it remains unclear whether these numerical changes were truly felt as meaningful recovery improvements by the patients. From an implementation perspective, an economic analysis is missing: it is doubtful whether marginal statistical variations justify the added financial cost, device complexity, and pharmacokinetic risks of deploying this novel hardware.

High baseline opioid infusion and the “ceiling effect”

A major point of critique is that overall 48-hour cumulative sufentanil consumption was completely equivalent between groups (P=0.49). The authors explicitly acknowledge: “Overall, the DAI system was associated with lower early postoperative pain scores and fewer PCA activation attempts within the first 48 hours after VATS, but it did not reduce total systemic sufentanil consumption.”

All patients received a heavy continuous background sufentanil infusion (2 µg/h). Notably, 14 out of 25 patients in the control group did not activate the PCA device a single time, indicating that the baseline systemic opioid regimen was already potent. This creates two possible interpretations:

Sample size and randomization limitations

The trial enrolled a small sample of only 47 patients (22 in DAI vs. 25 in STD) across a single center. The open-label design—unavoidable due to obvious physical differences between the devices—introduces performance and response bias in subjective metrics like VAS and QoR-15. Small sample sizes carry inherent risks of baseline randomization imbalances. While the lack of early complications is reassuring, it demonstrates preliminary feasibility rather than definitive safety. Larger, multicenter, double-blinded (or double-dummy) trials are essential.

Divergence from modern ERAS regional pathways

The most critical clinical limitation is the complete omission of routine regional anesthesia (e.g., TPVB, ESPB, or ICNB). Because major international guidelines (PROSPECT, Italian VATS Group) explicitly endorse interfascial plane or paravertebral blocks over intrapleural instillation, the true utility of the DAI system remains completely undefined. Additionally, because patients were continuously attached to an infusion pump, their ability to fully mobilize was restricted, thereby compromising a fundamental tenet of ERAS pathways—early patient mobilization.

The ongoing research landscape reflects that intrapleural delivery remains an unsettled topic—evidenced by active prospective multicenter trial protocols evaluating TPVB combined with drainage-tube PCA.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Tang and colleagues provide an ingenious hardware modification that cleverly addresses the mechanics of chest-tube-related pain. The DAI system offers an interesting alternative for localized analgesia, proving technically feasible and capable of reducing early breakthrough pain attempts. However, given the small sample size, open-label design, absence of total opioid reduction, lack of regional anesthesia comparison, questionable dual-drug pharmacology, and non-conformance with current PROSPECT recommendations, the clinical enthusiasm for routine adoption must remain cautiously guarded. Future multicenter trials comparing integrated drain analgesia directly against or in combination with gold-standard regional blocks are required before this technology can claim a routine place in thoracic ERAS pathways.
