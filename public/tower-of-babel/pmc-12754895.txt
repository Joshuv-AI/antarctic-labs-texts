Is occlusive retinal vascular disease linked to hereditary spherocytosis postsplenectomy? A case series
Authors: Frida Velcani, Shivesh Shah, Brian E. Zaugg, Michael P. Teske, Nikhil N. Batra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo present two separate cases of occlusive retinal vascular disease with secondary cystoid macular edema in patients with a past medical history significant for hereditary spherocytosis and splenectomy.Case presentationWe present two patients with atypical ocular changes in the setting of a distant history of splenectomy with underlying hereditary spherocytosis. The first patient is a 52-year-old Caucasian male with findings consistent with adult-onset Coats disease, predominantly presenting as type 1 macular telangiectasia. The second patient is a 79-year-old Caucasian female with acute branch retinal vein occlusion with a remote history of bilateral nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy.ConclusionThere is limited literature exploring the association between retinal vascular disease, hereditary spherocytosis, and splenectomy. These cases highlight that the link between vascular events and a remote history of splenectomy remains inconclusive, especially in the absence of evidence to differentiate it from other potential diagnoses. Both patients in our cases had secondary macular edema, which responded to treatment with intravitreal antivascular endothelial growth factor medications.

Background

Hereditary spherocytosis (HS) is the most common genetic cause of hemolytic anemia, caused by mutations in structural proteins that maintain erythrocyte stability including ankyrin-1, band 3, alpha spectrin, beta spectrin, and protein 4.2. These mutations result in malformed, spherical-shaped red blood cells (RBCs), called spherocytes, which can become trapped in the spleen and destroyed. Patients present clinically with anemia, jaundice, and splenomegaly. Splenectomy is a typical treatment for patients with moderate-to-severe forms of HS to help increase the lifespan of RBCs and stabilize hemoglobin levels, resulting in less tissue hypoxia and lower levels of serum bilirubin [1].

While HS can lead to retinal complications, such as retinal artery or vein occlusion, to our knowledge, retinal vascular disease associated with HS and splenectomy has been less frequently reported in the literature [2, 3]. The causality of these events is unclear, but potentially related to abnormal circulating RBCs and thrombocytosis, among other biomarkers [1]. However, further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanism. In this report, we present two cases of occlusive retinal vascular disease with secondary cystoid macular edema in patients with a past medical history significant for HS with splenectomy.

Case presentations

Case 1

A 52-year-old Caucasian male presented with distortion of his vision in the right eye associated with central blurring. He reported painless vision loss that had progressed slowly over the course of at least 2 months prior to examination and did not improve with refraction. His medical history included HS with splenectomy 33 years prior. Systemically, the patient complained of peripheral limb claudication, for which he had started taking clopidogrel a few weeks prior. Since starting clopidogrel, he reported a drastic improvement in his claudication and only mild improvement in vision.

On presentation, his best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/20–2 in the right eye and 20/15 in the left eye. Intraocular pressures were 12 mmHg in both eyes. External and anterior segment slit lamp examination findings were normal in both eyes. Dilated fundus exam of the right eye revealed exudates in the temporal and superior macula with microaneurysms and prominent telangiectasias (Fig. 1A). The left eye showed microaneurysms in the inferotemporal macula without exudates on exam, though these findings are not clearly discernible on imaging (Fig. 1B). The vitreous and optic nerves were normal in both eyes. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the right eye confirmed a broad area of intraretinal cystic fluid in the temporal macula corresponding to the areas of exudates and microaneurysms (Fig. 2). Our differential diagnosis included diabetic retinopathy with macular edema, juxta-foveal telangiectasia, Coats disease, a hemoglobinopathy, or occlusive disease secondary to splenectomy. The evidence in this case, including the unilateral presentation and predominance in males, may be most consistent with type 1 macular telangiectasia associated with Coats disease. Exam, fundus imaging, and fluorescein angiography revealed macular capillary pruning, dilation, and enlargement of retinal capillaries, peripheral capillary nonperfusion with telangiectasias, and extensive intraretinal exudates. Overall, this presentation could reflect an undetected, asymptomatic case of childhood Coats disease. Alternatively, while rare, it could be a case of adult-onset Coats disease of which the average age of diagnosis is 47 years old. This variant is more localized with slower progression and less potential of worsening during the follow-up period. While the etiology of childhood Coats disease is largely considered to be idiopathic, the associated systemic conditions when diagnosed in adulthood include hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia [4, 5]. These comorbidities were not observed in our patient.

The direct link between HS and our patient’s presentation remains unclear. The patient’s only laboratory abnormality was mild thrombocytosis in the upper 400 s, which is expected after splenectomy. However, if vascular obstruction secondary to thrombocytosis were the causative mechanism, we would expect to see acute vascular obstruction as opposed to a silent microangiopathy. Hemoglobinopathy is also unlikely as none of the classic features were observed including salmon patches, black sunburst spots, retinal or vitreous hemorrhage, and sea-fan neovascularization.

We initially elected to observe given the patient’s excellent presenting vision. In addition to clopidogrel, the patient’s hematologist also started amlodipine and atorvastatin for vasodilation and lowering cholesterol levels respectively. Approximately 2 years after initial presentation, his vision had worsened to 20/30 in the right eye owing to increasing macular edema. We then began treatment with intravitreal antivascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy (bevacizumab 1.25 mg / 0.05 ml). He had subjective improvement of vision and decreased macular edema as measured by central retinal thickness on OCT (Fig. 2) after receiving 12 intravitreal injections of bevacizumab over an 18-month period with ongoing treatment. The initial fluorescein angiography findings remained stable throughout his course.

Case 2

A 79-year-old Caucasian female presented to our clinic with a 1-week history of blurry vision in both eyes that began suddenly and was associated with flashes of light. Her past ocular history included bilateral peripheral vision loss secondary to nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) at the age of 26, with associated bilateral inferior visual field loss and a history of episcleritis. Her medical history included HS treated with a splenectomy more than 50 years ago.

On exam, BCVA was 20/400 in the right eye and 20/40 in the left eye. She had bilateral inferior visual field defects noted on confrontational testing. Her external exam and anterior segment slit lamp exam was only notable for 2 + nuclear sclerotic cataracts in both eyes. Dilated fundus exam of the right eye showed a branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) of the inferotemporal retina accompanied by nerve fiber layer hemorrhages, exudates, cotton wool spots, and retinal edema. There was notable optic disc pallor consistent with her history of NAION. In her left eye, a pale optic disc and retinal pigment epithelial mottling within the macula was noted (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Case 2. We began treatment with intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy (ranibizumab 0.5 mg / 0.05 ml) for cystoid macular edema associated with her BRVO. She was referred to her primary care doctor to evaluate for any treatable cardiovascular risk factors. The patient was found to have normal blood pressure and cholesterol, with overall low cardiovascular risk. Over a 12-month period, she received a total of seven injections of intravitreal ranibizumab. BCVA improved to 20/150 in the right eye with OCT showing macular thinning and improvement of cystoid macular edema (Fig. 8). While the patient’s BRVO could be linked to atherosclerosis, her cardiovascular risk profile is low, with advanced age as the only notable factor. In addition, her history of NAION occurred over 50 years ago. Discussion and conclusion

Occlusive retinal vascular disease may result from systemic conditions such as diabetes, hemoglobinopathies, or embolic causes from carotid atherosclerosis. Localized ocular diseases such as juxta-foveal telangiectasia, Coats disease, and Eales disease are also known to cause occlusive retinal vascular disease. Our case series describes two patients with atypical ocular changes in the setting of HS and remote splenectomy. The first patient presented with findings resembling adult-onset Coats disease, predominantly type 1 macular telangiectasia. The second patient presented with acute BRVO with a history of bilateral NAION. Sickle cell disease, potential coexisting myeloproliferative disorders, and other hemoglobinopathies including hereditary stomatocytosis were ruled out in both cases by laboratory testing. Both patients had secondary macular edema that responded to intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy. A similar case reported a vascular event in a patient with HS complicated by macular edema and successfully treated with intravitreal ranibizumab injections [3]. Our patients had a remote history of splenectomy, but whether this directly attributed to their risk of vascular events is not certain, especially given more likely alternative explanations.

After splenectomy, patients may generally experience persistent spherocyte formation, thromboembolic complications, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and other life-threatening events [1]. The mechanism by which vascular events occur after splenectomy for hematologic disorders has not been clearly defined in the literature; however a number of hypotheses exist. Once the spleen is removed, it can no longer clear cellular debris, abnormal red cells, or more narrowly, cell-derived microparticles which express phosphatidylserine and are thought to have procoagulant activity [6, 7]. With lower bilirubin levels, an elevated risk of coronary artery disease and myocardial infarction has also been shown [8, 9]. After correcting for anemia in HS, higher levels of serum cholesterol may be observed, thereby increasing the risk of adverse arterial events. For example, in an experiment with lab mice, the spleen demonstrated a protective immune effect among mice with elevated cholesterol levels that reduced their progression of atherosclerosis [10]. There is also some evidence of elevated levels of platelets, hematocrit, hemoglobin, C-reactive protein, leukocytes, and whole blood viscosity following splenectomy, all of which can independently increase the risk of thrombosis [2, 11–18]. These studies add to our understanding of complications postsplenectomy that may explain the development of retinopathy down the line.

Reports of arterial and venous thrombosis associated with HS and splenectomy are limited to few cross-sectional studies and case reports [2, 19–23]. Schilling showed that the rate of having at least one arteriosclerotic event (which includes stroke, myocardial infarction, and coronary or carotid artery surgery) in patients with HS over 40 years was about five times higher after splenectomy than in patients with a spleen [22]. An expanded study confirmed that there was greater cumulative incidence of arterial and venous events at 70 years following splenectomy for HS compared with those who did not undergo splenectomy, with a hazard ratio of 7.2 and 3.3 for arterial and venous events, respectively [2]. The occurrence of stroke in a patient with HS following splenectomy has also been documented [23]. In trauma patients, studies highlight an increased risk of occlusive vascular events after splenectomy, affecting both arterial and venous systems [3, 22, 24–26]. Reported complications include priapism, osteonecrosis in Gaucher disease, spinal cord infarction, and glaucoma [27–31].

Our cases highlight unique ocular findings in the setting of HS and splenectomy, with rare depictions of this in the literature. A similar case was reported of a patient whose history is significant for unilateral anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, similar to our patient in case 2 with a bilateral presentation of NAION [20]. However, there remains question as to whether occlusive retinal vascular events in these patients should be attributed to splenectomy or HS—both of which can independently lead to increased occurrence of retinal vascular events [3, 26, 32–34]. More research is needed to better elucidate the mechanism of elevated thromboembolic risk after splenectomy in patients with HS.
