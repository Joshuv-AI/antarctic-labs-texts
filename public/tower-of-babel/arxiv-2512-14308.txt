Improving the Accuracy of Amortized Model Comparison with Self-Consistency
Šimon Kucharský, Aayush Mishra, Daniel Habermann, Stefan T. Radev, Paul-Christian Bürkner

Abstract
Amortized Bayesian inference (ABI) offers fast, scalable approximations to posterior densities by training neural surrogates on data simulated from the statistical model. However, ABI methods are highly sensitive to model misspecification: when observed data fall outside the training distribution (generative scope of the statistical models), neural surrogates can behave unpredictably. This makes it a challenge in a model comparison setting, where multiple statistical models are considered, of which at least some are misspecified. Recent work on self-consistency (SC) provides a promising remedy to this issue, accessible even for empirical data (without ground-truth labels). In this work, we investigate how SC can improve amortized model comparison conceptualized in four different ways. Across two synthetic and two real-world case studies, we find that approaches for model comparison that estimate marginal likelihoods through approximate parameter posteriors consistently outperform methods that directly approximate model evidence or posterior model probabilities. SC training improves robustness when the likelihood is available, even under severe model misspecification. The benefits of SC for methods without access of analytic likelihoods are more limited and inconsistent. Our results suggest practical guidance for reliable amortized Bayesian model comparison: prefer parameter posterior-based methods and augment them with SC training on empirical datasets to mitigate extrapolation bias under model misspecification.



1
Introduction

Bayesian model comparison (BMC) is a principled approach for ranking competing probabilistic models according to how well they explain observed data
(
22
)
. By combining prior model probabilities with evidence provided by the data, BMC yields posterior model probabilities that directly quantify plausibility of each model under question. However, practical use of BMC is often hindered by the need to compute high dimensional integrals to obtain the evidence brought about by the data, integrals that often turn out to be analytically intractable or computationally prohibitive to approximate
(
21
)
.

Modern machine learning approaches to approximate complex (often intractable) probability distributions emerge as a promising candidate to address this challenge. We leverage neural networks as surrogates for densities required for evidence calculation, circumventing the need to solve high-dimensional integrals. Typically, these neural networks are trained on data simulated from the statistical models under consideration
(
1
, falling under the umbrella of simulation-based inference;)
. Once trained, these neural surrogates can rapidly approximate quantities that would otherwise demand costly re-computation for each new dataset. In this way, we trade initial training cost for substantial gains in efficiency during deployment, enabling large-scale or real-time inference. This property is called
amortization
, giving the name Amortized Bayesian Inference (ABI).

However, neural networks are notoriously susceptible to out-of-distribution
(
39
, OOD;)
data. In standard ABI, neural networks are trained data simulated from the statistical models in question. If some of the candidate models are misspecified relative to the true data-generative process, the real observed data might be OOD, and inferences based on the neural networks are arbitrarily wrong
(
28
;
9
)
. When comparing different models, there is an inherent assumption that some models fit the data better than others, and some (or even all) models might be misspecified. This creates a tension: traditional ABI may not be trustworthy for misspecified models, but proper BMC may require accurate inferences for poorly fitting models.

A recently proposed self-consistency (SC) loss family
(
29
;
13
;
23
)
is a promising candidate to help solve the issue of OOD data. SC does not require model simulations and can be even used with real-world data. This allows the neural networks to generalize to spaces inaccessible during pure simulation-based training. In this article, we study the benefits of SC for model comparison purposes.

2
Bayesian Inference and Model Comparison

Given data
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and a set of
K
candidate models
M_{1},\dots,M_{K}
with model-specific parameter vectors
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{K}
, the parameter posterior follows the Bayes’ rule

p(\theta_{k}\mid y,M_{k})=\frac{p(\theta_{k}\mid M_{k})\;p(y\mid\theta_{k},M_{k})}{p(y\mid M_{k})},

(1)

where the denominator is the
marginal likelihood
(model evidence),

p(y\mid M_{k})=\int p(\theta_{k}\mid M_{k})\;p(y\mid\theta_{k},M_{k})d\theta_{k}.

(2)

Posterior model probabilities (PMPs) also follow the Bayes’ rule,

p(M_{k}\mid y)=\frac{p(M_{k})\;p(y\mid M_{k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}p(M_{j})\;p(y\mid M_{j})},

(3)

where the models are ranked according to their marginal likelihoods weighted by their prior model probabilities
p(M_{k})
. A ratio of two marginal likelihoods
\text{BF}_{ij}=\frac{p(y\mid M_{i})}{p(y\mid M_{j})}
expresses the relative probability of the data under the two models. Such ratio is called the
Bayes factor
and is sometimes being reported over PMPs, as it represents the evidence provided by the data in favour of
M_{i}
compared to
M_{j}
, regardless of the prior model probabilities
(
17
;
15
)
.

Unfortunately, for non-trivial models, marginal likelihood (integral in Eq.
2
) is often intractable. Methods for approximating the marginal likelihood
(
11
, e.g., bridge sampling,)
require sampling many draws from the posterior
\theta_{k}^{(s)}\sim p(\theta_{k}\mid y,M_{k})
using MCMC
(
20
;
18
)
, which is on itself computationally demanding. This motivates the use of amortized methods for model comparison.

3
Amortized Bayesian Inference

ABI can be viewed as approximating a conditional distribution of latent variable
z
conditioned on observables
y
,
p(z\mid y)
with a conditional generative neural network
q_{\phi}(z\mid y)
, trained on simulated pairs
(z^{(n)},y^{(n)})\sim p(z,y)
. By minimizing a scoring rule,

\hat{\phi}=\arg\min_{\phi}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}S\!\big(q_{\phi}(\cdot\mid y^{(n)}),\,z^{(n)}\big),

(4)

we train
q_{\phi}(z\mid y)\to p(z\mid y)
as
N\to\infty
, provided that
q_{\phi}
is sufficiently expressive and
S
is a strictly proper scoring rule
(
10
, e.g., the log score
-\log q_{\phi}(z^{(n)}\mid y^{(n)})
;)
. For reasons that will become apparent below, we use network architectures for which the density
q_{\phi}
is easily computed, such as coupling flows
(
27
;
2
;
3
)
.

The advantage is that
p(z,y)
needs to be available only in a form of simulator, i.e., a computer program generating realizations from the joint distribution, but evaluating the density of
p(z,y)
does not need to be available, making it a simulation-based inference framework
(
1
)
.

Once the network has been trained, the distribution
p(z\mid y^{\text{obs}})
can be approximated easily by sampling draws
z\sim q_{\psi}(z\mid y^{\text{obs}})
, for any observed data set
y^{\text{obs}}
. This step takes only a little amount of time and so inferences on real data is very efficient: most of the resources are expended during training. This property of inferences via neural networks is called
amortization
.

4
Amortized Model Comparison

We consider four different ways of amortized model comparison. The first two methods estimate the marginal likelihood. By rearranging the Bayes’ rule for parameter posterior (Eq.
1
) we obtain an expression for the marginal likelihood in terms of prior, likelihood, and posterior,

p(y\mid M_{k})=\frac{p(\theta_{k}\mid M_{k})\times p(y\mid\theta_{k},M_{k})}{p(\theta_{k}\mid y,M_{k})}.

(5)

If the posterior density
p(\theta_{k}\mid y,M_{k})
was available analytically, marginal likelihood is exact for any
\theta_{k}\in\Theta_{k}
.

Neural posterior estimation (NPE)

We use a generative neural network
q_{\phi}
to approximate the posterior
p(\theta_{k}\mid y,M_{k})
. Substituting
q_{\phi}
into Equation
5
and taking the log yields

\displaystyle\log p(y\mid M_{k})\approx

\displaystyle\log p(\theta_{k}\mid M_{k})+\log p(y\mid\theta_{k},M_{k})-

(6)

\displaystyle\log q_{\phi}(\theta_{k}\mid y,M_{k}),

for any
\theta_{k}\in\Theta_{k}
. However, the estimate based on a single
\theta_{k}
may be unreliable due to approximation error of
q_{\phi}
. To mitigate this, we compute a Monte Carlo estimate over
S
samples drawn from the posterior surrogate
\tilde{\theta}_{k}^{(s)}\sim q_{\phi}(\theta_{k}\mid y,M_{k})
,

\displaystyle\log p(y\mid M_{k})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\big[\log p(\tilde{\theta}_{k}^{(s)}\mid M_{k})\;+

(7)

\displaystyle\log p(y\mid\tilde{\theta}_{k}^{(s)},M_{k})-\log q_{\phi}(\tilde{\theta}_{k}^{(s)}\mid y,M_{k})\big].

The training data for the simulation-based training is generated from a specific model,

\displaystyle\theta_{k}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\sim p(\theta_{k}\mid M_{k})

(8)

\displaystyle y^{(n)}

\displaystyle\sim p(y\mid\theta_{k}^{(n)},M_{k}),

and the simulation-based training is set up to minimize the negative log likelihood,

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}=\arg\min_{\phi}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log q_{\phi}\big(\theta_{k}^{(n)}\mid y^{(n)},M_{k}^{(n)}\big).

(9)

Neural posterior and likelihood estimation (NPLE)

When the likelihood
p(y\mid\theta_{k},M_{k})
is unavailable as well (e.g, implicit simulators), we can approximate both with neural posterior
q_{\phi}(\theta_{k}\mid y,M_{k})
and neural likelihood
q_{\psi}(y\mid\theta_{k},M_{k})
estimation
(
26
, NPLE;)
. The marginal likelihood estimate becomes

\displaystyle\log p(y\mid M_{k})\approx

\displaystyle\log p(\theta_{k}\mid M_{k})+\log q_{\psi}(y\mid\theta_{k},M_{k})-

(10)

\displaystyle\log q_{\phi}(\theta_{k}\mid y,M_{k}),

and is estimated over multiple parameter draws from the posterior surrogate as in the NPE case (Eq.
7
).

The simulation-based training of the posterior and likelihood networks is typically simultaneous on the model simulations (Eq.
8
). However, the likelihood network could be in principle trained on simulated data with a separate proposal distribution for the parameters;
\theta_{k}^{(n)}\sim p_{c}(\theta_{k}),~y^{(n)}(y\mid\theta_{k}^{(n)},M_{k})
. As long as the observables
y
are generated from the correct data distribution, the likelihood network will approximate the correct likelihood (in a different region of the parameter space).

The next two approaches sidestep approximating the parameter posterior by approximating the marginal likelihood or posterior model probabilities
directly
. The training data are
N
model-data pairs
(M_{k}^{(n)},y_{k}^{(n)})
, simulated from the
model ensemble
(a probabilistic collection of all models under consideration),

\displaystyle M_{k}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\sim p(M_{k})

(11)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\sim p(\theta_{k}\mid M_{k}^{(n)})

\displaystyle y^{(n)}

\displaystyle\sim p(y\mid\theta_{k}^{(n)},M_{k}^{(n)}).

Neural evidence estimation (NEE)

The marginal likelihood (evidence)
p(y\mid M_{k})
is approximated with a generative neural network
q_{\kappa}
directly from the simulations in Eq
11
. We call this approach
neural evidence estimation
(NEE). The simulation-based training is set up to minimize the negative log likelihood,

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}=\arg\min_{\kappa}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log q_{\kappa}\big(y^{(n)}\mid M_{k}^{(n)}\big).

(12)

Neural Posterior Model Probabilities (NPMP)

Alternatively, we can instead directly estimate posterior model probabilities (PMPs) conditioned on data
p(M_{k}\mid y)
. PMPs can be estimated via a classifier
q_{\delta}
(e.g., an MLP with a softmax output layer) trained with a cross-entropy loss
(
25
;
5
)
,

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}=\arg\min_{\delta}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log q_{\delta}\big(M_{k}^{(n)}\mid y^{(n)}\big).

(13)

5
Self-consistency

Trained on finite synthetic data generated from the statistical model, networks can be arbitrarily wrong if applied to OOD data
(
28
;
9
)
. One option to make inferences more trustworthy for empirical data is imposing
self-consistency
(SC) objective on the networks
(
29
;
23
)
. SC builds on the idea of expressing the marginal likelihood in terms of prior, likelihood, and posterior (Eq.
5
) and emphasizes that for a given dataset
y
, the marginal likelihood is always constant, regardless of which parameter values
\theta_{k}\in\Theta_{k}
are plugged in the equation. Estimates of the marginal likelihood with surrogate densities (Eq.
6
and Eq.
10
) should therefore also be invariant to different parameter values.

Importantly, the data sets used for calculating the marginal likelihood need not be labeled (i.e., come as a parameter-observables
(\theta^{(m)},y^{(m)})
pair as is the case for simulation-based training), and so even empirical data without ground truth parameter values can be used for SC training. This gives us an opportunity to train the networks on data far away from the generative scope of the statistical models.

To help the posterior network (or posterior and likelihood networks) to converge towards self-consistency, we define the SC loss as the variance of the log marginal likelihood estimate (Eq.
6
), averaged over M datasets
y^{(1)},y^{(m)},\dots,y^{M}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textrm{SC}}\frac{1}{M}

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{Var}_{\tilde{\theta}_{k}\sim p_{c}(\theta_{k})}\big[\log p(\tilde{\theta}_{k}\mid M_{k})+

(14)

\displaystyle\log p\big(y^{(m)}\mid\tilde{\theta}_{k},M_{k}\big)-\log q_{\phi}\big(\tilde{\theta}_{k}\mid y^{(m)},M_{k}\big)\big].

with a proposal distribution
p_{c}
set to the current approximate posterior
q_{\phi}(\theta_{k}\mid y^{(m)},M_{k})

(
23
)
. During training, this variance is approximated with a finite number of posterior samples
\tilde{\theta}_{k}\sim q_{\psi}(\theta_{k}\mid y^{(m)},M_{k})
. When the likelihood is unknown, it is replaced with its surrogate (Eq.
10
),

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textrm{SC}}\frac{1}{M}

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{Var}_{\tilde{\theta}_{k}\sim p_{c}(\theta_{k})}\big[\log p(\tilde{\theta}_{k}\mid M_{k})+

(15)

\displaystyle\log q_{\psi}\big(y^{(m)}\mid\tilde{\theta}_{k},M_{k}\big)-\log q_{\phi}\big(\tilde{\theta}_{k}\mid y^{(m)},M_{k}\big)\big].

However, with both posterior and likelihood being approximate, the SC loss is not strictly proper
(
23
)
. We use the SC loss to update the posterior network from the density evaluation and stop its gradients to the likelihood network.

With approaches that do not approximate the parameter posteriors, but rather target the evidence or PMP directly, the same logic can be applied on the level of the model ensemble, where rearranging Eq.
3
yields,

p(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}p(M_{j})p(y\mid M_{j})=\frac{p(M_{k})\,p(y\mid M_{k})}{p(M_{k}\mid y)},

(16)

which holds for every model
M_{k}
. This implies that the marginal likelihood of the ensemble model
p(y)
can be expressed in terms of the prior model probabilities, marginal likelihood of the individual models, and the posterior model probabilities. By plugging in the marginal likelihood estimates from NEE and PMP estimates with NPMP, we obtain the following SC loss,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textrm{SC}}\frac{1}{M}

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{Var}_{\tilde{M}_{k}\sim p_{c}(M_{k})}\big[\log p(\tilde{M}_{k})+

(17)

\displaystyle\log q_{\kappa}\big(y^{(m)}\mid\tilde{M}_{k}\big)-\log q_{\delta}\big(\tilde{M}_{k}\mid y^{(m)}\big)\big].

Here,
p_{c}
can be a simple proposal distribution, such as the prior model probabilities or a discrete uniform distribution over the models. SC training can be done for NEE and NPMP only if trained concurrently. Since the SC loss is improper in this case as well, we only use it to update NPMP and stop its gradients to the evidence network.

Related work

Amortized BMC via classifiers was proposed by
25
and applied across multiple domains
(
16
;
32
;
31
, e.g.,)
.
5
extended the method to hierarchical settings, and

enabled joint parameter inference and model comparison.
14

4
used deep ensembles for OOD detection. The JANA approach estimates evidence from a joint approximation of posterior and likelihood
(
26
)
.

proposed post-processing posterior samples with a learned harmonic mean estimator, and

used normalizing flows to estimate evidence and its uncertainty from posterior samples.

showed that the SC loss improves evidence estimation for well-specified models. None of these works address repairing the “extrapolation bias”
(
9
)
of BMC under model misspecification. We adapt the SC loss of
29
and
23
to assess its potential for reliable BMC in misspecified settings. This article expands previous work on NPE and NPLE with SC for model comparison

.

7.1
Multivariate Gaussian: Estimating Marginal Likelihood

In our first case study, we focus on a standard toy example of a
D
-dimensional Gaussian location model
(
23
)
, and our inference target is estimating the marginal likelihood. The model generates
N
IID observations,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\,\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{D})

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\,\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{D})

\displaystyle\text{for }i=1,\dots,N.

We set
\sigma_{\mu}^{2}=N
so that the total amount of information about
\boldsymbol{\mu}
remains approximately constant regardless of
N
.

We use NPE and NPLE, both with or without SC training. For the SC loss, we use
M=32
datasets drawn from
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{5},\,\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{D})
,
i.e.
, a mean shift away from the prior location. Additionally, we also estimate the marginal likelihood with NEE, but without SC training (since there is no model ensemble for concurrent NPMP training).

During training, we either fix the prior scale (
\sigma_{\mu}^{2}=1
) or randomize it by sampling
\log\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-3,3)
. In the latter case,
\sigma_{\mu}
is provided to the networks as an additional conditioning variable, effectively specifying different models with varying prior concentration. This variation alters the implied posterior while leaving the likelihood unchanged. By exposing the likelihood network in NPLE to data generated under a broader range of prior scales than
\sigma_{\mu}^{2}=1
, we expect the learned likelihood to become more robust to prior misspecification.

Results

Figure
1
presents example results for
N=100
and
D=20
. For datasets drawn within the scope of the generative model (
\mu=0
), all methods achieve highly accurate estimates. With analytic likelihood (NPE), SC training consistently improves marginal likelihood estimation. When the generative model is misspecified but aligns with the SC training distribution (
\mu=5
), SC-trained models yield nearly perfect marginal likelihood estimates. For test data further beyond both the generative and SC training regimes (
\mu=8
), SC training still brings the estimated marginal likelihood close to the analytic value, though with a small residual error.

In contrast, without analytic likelihood (NPLE and NEE), results are less definitive. SC training does not consistently improve marginal likelihood estimation. Nonetheless, when the likelihood network is trained on a broader range of prior specifications, SC training can provide noticeable improvements in NPLE, but it also helps with NEE even without SC. Further, the effect of SC in NPLE remains variable and inconsistent across experimental settings (further results are shown in the Appendix
A
).

7.2
Multivariate Gaussian: Estimating Bayes Factors

In the second case study we compare all four methods. Since NPMP needs to be trained on simulations from a model ensemble rather than a single model, we require multiple models for NPMP. Two random vectors are drawn from a unit variance multivariate Gaussian,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{x},\,\mathbf{I}_{D})

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{y},\,\mathbf{I}_{D})

\displaystyle\text{for }i=1,\dots,N.

Under the null model, the means of the two vectors are set to be equal,
M_{0}:\boldsymbol{\mu_{x}}=\boldsymbol{\mu_{y}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\,\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{D})
. Under the alternative model, the means are drawn independently,
M_{1}:\boldsymbol{\mu_{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\,\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{D}),\ \boldsymbol{\mu_{y}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\,\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{D})
. As in the previous case study, we either fix the prior scale (
\sigma_{\mu}^{2}=1
) or randomize it by sampling
\log\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-3,3)
.

We train NPE, NPLE, NEE, and NPMP, all with or without SC training. For the SC loss, we use
M=32
datasets drawn from
M_{1}
, but the two random vectors shifted
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}+\delta
,
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{\delta}
, with
\delta=3
. Both of the models are therefore misspecified, though in general, the data favors
M_{1}
.

Results

Figure
2
shows the results for an experiment with
D=5
,
N=10
. When the test data is generated from
M_{1}
(
\delta=0
), the approximated log Bayes factors are relatively precise for all methods. Once the models are misspecified, neither NEE or NPMP are able to approximate the log Bayes factor accurately, with or without SC training. SC training helps estimating log Bayes factors for NPE and NPLE, mainly when the test data is similar to that used for SC training (
\delta=3
). When the test data is even more extreme (
\delta=6
), SC improves the estimates, although its bias cannot be completely rectified. Training with a wider range of priors (
\log\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-3,3)

shows results from further experiments. Overall, NPE and NPLE vastly outperform NEE and NPMP, regardless of SC training. SC improves log Bayes factor approximation especially for NPE and on test data similar to that used for SC training (
\delta=3

7.3
Racing Diffusion Model of Decision Marking

We analyze empirical data from the lexical decision task of
38
(Experiment 1), in which 17 participants classified letter strings as words or non-words under either speed or accuracy instructions. Decision outcomes and response times are modeled using the racing diffusion model
(
37
)
, which models the decision as a noisy evidence accumulation process represented by a Wiener process with a drift. Two evidence accumulators race toward a decision threshold
\alpha
. Under the null model,
\alpha
is constrained to be constant across conditions; under the alternative model, it is allowed to vary between the two conditions. Here we only evaluate NPE and NPLE. We also experimented with NEE and NPMP, but both yielded chance-level performance even during training on in-distribution simulated data; we therefore omit them from further empirical evaluation. For the SC loss, we incorporate data from
M\in{0,10,17}
participants (with
M=0
corresponding to no SC training). Additional details on the models and network architectures are provided in Appendix
C
.

Results

We compare the log marginal likelihoods for all 17 participants, obtained with NPE, NPE+SC, NLPE, and NLPE+SC, to those from bridge sampling
(
11
)
using NUTS, as implemented in Stan
(
36
;
35
)
, which is a highly accurate (but slow) gold standard, since analytic marginal likelihoods are not available for these models. Figure
3
summarizes the results. Without SC (top row), log marginal estimates deviate substantially from the gold standard. With all data sets included in the SC training (bottom row), NPE yields almost perfect results, whereas NPLE does not improve. When some of the data sets are left out from the SC training, NPE still improves estimates (albeit imperfectly).

Air Passenger Traffic Forecasting

As last experiment, we analyze the European air passenger traffic dataset
(
7
;
6
;
8
)
, which has been used previously in the context of ABI
(
12
;
23
)
. This dataset contains annual counts of air passengers traveling from 15 European countries to the United States between 2004 and 2019, alongside each country’s GDP and household debt indicators, and has been used in the previous work on SC for parameter estimation

Discussion & Conclusion

In this article, we investigated four methods for amortized model comparison and studied whether their generalization to OOD data can be improved by training with a self-consistency (SC) loss. Across synthetic and empirical case studies, our results show clear ranking among amortized model comparison approaches. Generally, our experiments favor methods that approximate the parameter posterior (NPE, NPLE) over those that directly approximate only the marginal likelihood (NEE) or posterior model probabilities (NPMP), regardless of whether SC training was used. In both multivariate Gaussian case studies, NPE and NPLE provide substantially more accurate marginal likelihoods and Bayes factors than NEE and NPMP, particularly with increasing dimensionality and model misspecification.

NPE benefits the most from SC training. When the test data is close to the data used during training, NPE with SC can yield very accurate results, even when the models are misspecified. This effect is also evident in real-world applications: in the racing diffusion and air traffic examples, NPE-based estimates trained with SC closely track bridge sampling, whereas exhibit larger deviations without SC training. This is not a surprising result, since NPE has the benefit of training on the analytic likelihood. These findings suggest that, whenever feasible, amortized inference for model comparison should be built on posterior surrogates that, in addition of the traditional simulation-based loss, should be also trained with an SC loss based on analytic likelihoods. On the other hand, NPE+SC is limited to models that are possible to fit with likelihood-based methods as well (e.g., MCMC with bridge sampling), and not on models with intractable likelihood densities. NPE still might be the preferred option in that case for superior speed during inference, but other than that it is not applicable to purely simulation-based model comparison.

When both posterior and likelihood are approximated (NPLE), the SC loss is no longer strictly proper and its effect becomes less predictable; in some settings it appears to help, in others it has little impact. Our interpretation of the results is that for NPLE to work well with SC, the likelihood surrogate must be trained well in the vicinity of true parameter posterior. This is obviously a challenge, since the true parameter posterior is not available. One possible remedy could be to iteratively retrain the likelihood surrogate on data generated from the simulator conditioned on parameter samples from the current posterior approximation. We believe such training regimes could be a viable focus for future work. For NEE and NPMP, which lack access to parameter posteriors, such remedy is unavailable, and similar approaches cannot be attempted. Hence, we believe that NPLE remains the most promising candidate for estimating marginal likelihoods without analytic likelihoods.

Taken together, our findings lead to several practical recommendations. For practitioners interested in scalable and trustworthy Bayesian model comparison, we recommend (i) using NPE as the default amortized strategy when likelihood evaluations are available, (ii) augmenting NPE with SC training on carefully chosen empirical datasets to improve robustness under misspecification, and (iii) resorting to NPLE primarily when likelihoods are unavailable, while being cautious about the reliability of SC in this regime. Contrary to previous recommendations
(
25
;
5
)
, methods that directly target evidence or PMPs without parameter posteriors (NEE, NPMP) appear less attractive for reliable model comparison, especially under OOD conditions.

Our results support the view that supplementing simulation-based training with training based on empirical data might be a powerful tool to address model misspecification in amortized methods. However, the limitations of the cases without access to analytic likelihoods leave relevant gaps for further improvements.

Appendix C
Racing Diffusion Model of Decision Making

The racing diffusion model
(
37
)
assumes that each response option (here, correct vs. incorrect) is associated with a dedicated noisy evidence accumulator. Each accumulator begins at zero and evolves according to a Wiener process with drift (where
W_{t}
is the standard Wiener process),

X_{t}=\nu~\mathrm{d}t+\sigma~\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}=0.

(23)

A response is initiated when one of the accumulators first reaches the decision boundary
\alpha
. The observed response time is the hitting time of the winning accumulator plus a non-decision component
t_{0}
. Thus, the model produces response times (
rt
, in seconds) and accuracies (
d
, correct/incorrect) on each trial. For training purposes, we recoded incorrect responses as negative values of the corresponding
rt
, enabling the networks to condition on a single variable capturing both speed and accuracy.

The experiment alternated between speed and accuracy instruction blocks. Within each block, participants were instructed either to respond as quickly as possible or to prioritize accuracy. Each block comprised 96 trials. For the present analysis, we selected the first four blocks (two per condition) for each participant, yielding datasets of 384 trials (192 per condition).

Under the null model,
\alpha
is held constant across conditions:

\displaystyle M_{0}:\text{Fixed }\alpha\text{ across conditions},\ \log\alpha\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5),

(24)

whereas under the alternative model, the decision boundary varies by condition:

\displaystyle M_{1}:\text{Varying }\alpha\text{ across conditions},\ \log\alpha_{\text{speed}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5),\ \log\alpha_{\text{accuracy}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5).

(25)

The alternative model thus reflects the hypothesis that participants adapt their response caution (i.e., the amount of evidence required before responding) in accordance with task instructions.

Under both models, the nuisance parameters follow the priors

\displaystyle\log\nu_{\text{correct}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5),\ \log\nu_{\text{incorrect}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5),\ \mathrm{logit},\tau

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(26)

with
\sigma=1
. The parameter
\tau
determines the non-decision time via

t_{0}=\min(rt)\frac{\tau}{1-\tau}.

(27)

This reparameterization permits estimation of
\mathrm{logit}~\tau
on an unconstrained scale while ensuring
t_{0}\in(0,\min(rt))
.

Both the neural posterior and neural likelihood estimators are implemented as normalizing flows
(
2
)
with six affine coupling layers. Each coupling layer contains two dense layers with 256 hidden units and mish activations
(
24
)
. The summary network follows a DeepSet architecture
(
40
)
comprising two equivariant modules (each with two dense layers and 64 hidden units), an invariant module with two dense layers and 32 hidden units in both the inner and outer components, an additional invariant module with two dense layers of 32 hidden units, and an output layer with 24 units. All layers except the output layer use SiLU activations.

Training of the NPE/NLPE models follows an online regime (300 epochs, 64 steps per epoch, batch size 64). Because the SC loss can be unstable with randomly initialized networks, especially when both posterior and likelihood are approximate, we employ a warm-start schedule:
\lambda_{\mathrm{SC}}=0
for epochs 1–100, increasing linearly to 1 over epochs 101–200, and held at 1 thereafter. We use
S=16
posterior samples to approximate the variance of the log marginal likelihood in the SC loss (Eq.
14
), and
S=128
samples at test time to approximate the log marginal likelihood.
