Recent highlights from the international journal of Cardiology Heart & Vasculature: Advancing atrial fibrillation care
Authors: Dominik Linz, Dobromir Dobrev
Published: 2026

Herein we provide a brief update on manuscripts focussing on atrial fibrillation (AF) recently published in the International Journal of Cardiology Heart & Vasculature [1].

AF is a complex clinical condition with incompletely understood pathophysiology [2], [3]. Although ablation procedures led to an improvement of AF treatment, these approaches can be applied only to a limited number of patients, with antiarrhythmic drugs still being the cornerstone of AF therapy [4], [5]. Overall, the efficacy of contemporary treatment approaches for AF is still moderate and appears to depend on AF burden (the time spend in AF) rather than on the clinical manifestation form of the arrhythmia [6]. A better arrhythmia detection and classification are expected to unmask endophenotypes and allow a tailored therapy and management of this very common clinical entity [7].

Clinical outcomes and complications of AF

AF is associated with substantial cardiovascular morbidity across a wide range of clinical settings. In patients with heart failure (HF), both newly diagnosed and pre-existing AF increase the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, mortality, and hospitalization, with prior AF conferring the greatest long-term risk [8]. AF also remains a major cause of thromboembolic complications, and recent evidence suggests that stroke risk prediction can be improved beyond the CHA₂DS₂-VA score by incorporating left atrial appendage morphology and metabolic biomarkers such as lipoprotein(a) [9]. Beyond cardiovascular complications, AF may also contribute to cognitive decline through impaired cerebral perfusion [10]. Although most studies report reduced cerebral blood flow and poorer cognitive performance in patients with AF, particularly in those with concomitant HF, the available evidence remains limited by methodological heterogeneity, uncertainty, and lack of high-quality studies.

Despite the growing evidence that early rhythm control significantly improves the outcome of AF patients, rhythm control is still underutilized in most regions of the world [11], [12]. Contemporary registry data further show that catheter ablation use has more than doubled over recent years, particularly among older patients, although only a small proportion of eligible AF patients undergo ablation, indicating persistent underutilization [13], [14]. In parallel, a European survey revealed substantial variation in antiarrhythmic drug prescribing practices and frequent deviations from guideline recommendations, with physicians often prioritizing perceived efficacy over safety when selecting therapy [15], [16].

Recent evidence demonstrated superior outcomes with catheter ablation compared with pacemaker implantation in patients with tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome, reducing AF recurrence, stroke, and mortality [17]. In patients hospitalized with acute decompensated HF, early catheter ablation following clinical stabilization safely reduced HF hospitalization rate compared with delayed intervention [18], [19].

Role of Comorbidities and Risk Factors in AF

The burden of comorbidities plays a central role in AF progression and prognosis [20], [21]. Multimorbidity is highly prevalent among AF patients and is consistently associated with increased risks of cardiovascular death, HF hospitalization, stroke, and other adverse outcomes [22]. Likewise, patients with multiple comorbidities undergoing catheter ablation experience higher rates of arrhythmia recurrence, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive patient assessment before intervention [23], [24]. Several studies further identified specific comorbid conditions, including HF, sleep apnea, obesity, pulmonary hypertension, and reduced physical activity, as important determinants of AF occurrence or recurrence [25], [26], [27], [28]. Continuous monitoring using implantable cardiac devices may facilitate dynamic risk assessment by detecting changes in HF status, physical activity, and sleep-disordered breathing [29], [30], [31]. Despite guideline recommendations promoting integrated care through the ABC pathway and systematic comorbidity management [32], patient awareness of their own risk factors remains limited, indicating that additional educational strategies are needed to optimize long-term management [33], [34].

In the perioperative setting, postoperative AF (POAF) remains a frequent complication after cardiac surgery [35]. Reduced preoperative interleukin-2 concentrations independently predicted POAF following coronary artery bypass grafting, while increased epicardial adipose tissue density was identified as an independent predictor of POAF after aortic valve replacement, highlighting the potential value of inflammatory and structural biomarkers for perioperative risk stratification [35], [36], [37], [38].

Advances in Rhythm Control and Procedural Innovations

Recent advances in AF ablation focus on improving efficacy while minimizing procedural risks. Pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as a promising non-thermal alternative to cryoballoon ablation (CBA), demonstrating comparable safety and efficacy with shorter procedure times and a trend toward lower AF recurrence, particularly in paroxysmal AF. Ongoing randomized trials will further clarify its role in clinical practice [39], [40], [41], [42], [43].

Several studies also investigated strategies to optimize ablation outcomes. Three-dimensional mapping during PFA improved first-pass pulmonary vein isolation without prolonging procedures, AF-inducibility may have some prognostic information, while empirical superior vena cava isolation reduced atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence without increasing complications [44], [45], [46], [47]. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated that baseline left atrial size and function, as well as post-ablation remodeling, are associated with AF recurrence, supporting its use for patient selection and risk stratification [48]. Routine preprocedural computed tomography did not improve procedural outcomes but frequently detected previously unrecognized coronary artery disease [49]. Peri-procedural respiratory complications during catheter ablation were common and were associated with obesity, sleep apnea, pulmonary disease, and prolonged sedation, emphasizing the need for individualized procedural planning and risk assessment [50], [51], [52], [53], [54], [55].

Overall, recent evidence, including studies published in the International Journal of Cardiology Heart & Vasculature, underscore the evolving landscape of AF research, emphasizing the importance of integrated risk assessment, comprehensive comorbidity management, and personalized rhythm-control strategies. Catheter ablation use has increased but remains underutilized. Advances in mapping, imaging, pharmacotherapy, artficial intelligence, and trial design aim to improve outcomes, safety, patient selection, and evidence quality in AF management [56]. However, many AF trials are discontinued or unpublished, highlighting persistent gaps in research transparency and evidence generation [57], [58]. AF remains an important and relevant reseach topic, and new advances in AF care will help to improve outcomes and ensure a better life in our patients.
