Updated Wilkins Score Predicting Success of Balloon Mitral Valvuloplasty in Rheumatic Mitral Stenosis
Authors: Ply Chichareon, Nitchanun Jinpisoot, Prayuth Rasmeehiran, Tasalak Thonghong, Suchart Aroonsiriwattana, Saranyou Suwanugsorn, Premanan Manoret, Seththawut Kosallavat, Thanapon Nilmoje, Sirichai Cheewatanakornkul, Treechada Wisaratapong, Sunti Limumpornpetch, Watchara Lohawijarn, Noppadol Chamnarnphol, Mann Chandavimol, Pannipa Suwannasom, Woravut Jintapakorn, Krissada Meemook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Wilkins score evaluates mitral valve morphology and predicts percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty (PBMV) success in rheumatic mitral stenosis (MS). The 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend considering 5 clinical and 2 anatomical factors when selecting PBMV candidates.ObjectivesThe authors aimed to update the Wilkins score by integrating clinical variables and compared its performance with the conventional score.MethodsWe studied 1,128 patients with rheumatic MS who underwent PBMV in 4 hospitals. PBMV success was defined as postprocedural mitral valve area (MVA) ≥1.5 cm2, irrespective of percentage increase, mitral regurgitation ≤ grade 2, with no more than a 1-grade increment in severity and without in-hospital complications. The Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score was developed from a multivariable logistic regression predicting PBMV success, incorporating age, prior commissurotomy, NYHA functional class IV, atrial fibrillation, right ventricular systolic pressure, pre-PBMV MVA, severe tricuspid regurgitation, and Wilkins score.ResultsThe median age was 45 years and 82.2% were female. Prevalence of prior commissurotomy, NYHA functional class IV, atrial fibrillation, and pulmonary hypertension was 4.3%, 1.5%, 48.4%, and 44.5%, respectively. The median Wilkins score was 8 and 65.8% and 4.2% had very small MVA and severe tricuspid regurgitation. The procedural success rate was 48.6%. C-statistic of the Wilkins score and the Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score was 0.543 (95% CI: 0.477-0.608) and 0.658 (95% CI: 0.595-0.721), respectively. The Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score showed good calibration and clinical utility on decision curve analysis.ConclusionsThe Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score better predicts procedural success in patients with rheumatic MS who underwent PBMV.

Percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty (PBMV) is an effective treatment for rheumatic mitral stenosis (MS).1 The suitability and success of PBMV primarily depend on mitral valve morphology. Among available scoring systems designed to predict PBMV success, the Wilkins score is widely recognized and extensively used in clinical practice.2 This scoring system consists of 4 anatomical components: leaflet mobility, thickness, calcification, and subvalvular thickening. However, previous studies have demonstrated limited predictive performance of the conventional Wilkins score.3,4 In addition, a multicenter registry indicated changes in the clinical characteristics of patients with rheumatic MS and a progressive decline in clinical outcomes over time.5 Therefore, reassessment and updating of the Wilkins score are necessary to more accurately identify patients who may have low chances of successful PBMV and for whom surgical treatment might be preferable.

Clinical factors such as age, NYHA functional class, atrial fibrillation (AF), and severe pulmonary hypertension have been associated with both optimal PBMV results and long-term clinical outcomes.6,7 International guidelines recommend that clinical and anatomical characteristics guide decision making for PBMV,8 whereas the U.S. guidelines predominantly emphasize anatomical features (pliable valve, absence of left atrial thrombus, and less than moderate mitral regurgitation).9 It remains uncertain whether integrating clinical characteristics into anatomical assessments significantly improves the prediction of PBMV success.

Therefore, we aimed to update the traditional Wilkins score by incorporating clinical predictors acknowledged by the European Society of Cardiology guidelines.8 Furthermore, we evaluated and compared the predictive performance of this Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score against the traditional anatomical Wilkins score.

Methods

Study design and population

The design was a retrospective study including all patients with rheumatic MS who underwent PBMV in 4 hospitals in Thailand. Patients who had missing information on PBMV success were excluded from the study. Information was collected at a patient level. In patients who underwent PBMV more than 1 time during the study period, the first procedure was used as the index procedure.

The Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of Prince of Songkla University, Faculty of Medicine of Chiang Mai University, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, and Suratthani Hospital approved the study protocol and informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

PBMV

Intracardiac clot and severity of mitral regurgitation (MR) were assessed using transesophageal echocardiography in all cases before the procedure. The procedure was postponed if thrombus was present in the left atrium. PBMV was performed using the single-balloon technique with either an Inoue or Accura balloon with an antegrade transseptal approach under local anesthesia. Transseptal puncture was performed under transesophageal echocardiographic or fluoroscopic guidance. The initial balloon size was selected based on the height of patients. A stepwise balloon dilatation technique was performed and the decision to adjust balloon size and re-inflate was at the discretion of each operator.

Clinical characteristics and echocardiographic data before PBMV

Patient information and echocardiographic parameters were extracted from the medical record or database of each hospital. Prior commissurotomy was defined as a history of prior PBMV or surgical commissurotomy. Echocardiographic parameters, including the Wilkins score and mitral valve area (MVA), were assessed by an experienced cardiologist at each participating site. Pre-PBMV MVA derived from the 2-dimensional planimetry method was used for the analysis. Pressure half time–derived MVA was used if the planimetry-derived MVA was not available. Right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) was calculated from the peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity derived from the continuous-wave Doppler echocardiography and right atrial pressure. Severity of mitral and tricuspid regurgitation (TR) was evaluated using qualitative Doppler assessment based on color flow jet area and extent of jet penetration into the atrium.

The Wilkins score was assessed semiquantitatively based on 4 components of mitral valve morphology: leaflet mobility, valve thickening, subvalvular fibrosis, and valvular calcification.2 Each component was scaled from 1 to 4. The lowest and highest possible Wilkins score was 4 and 16, respectively. The lower Wilkins score indicated more favorable valvular morphology for intervention.

PBMV success definition

PBMV success was defined as postprocedural MVA ≥1.5 cm2, irrespective of the percentage increase, and MR severity grade ≤2, with no more than a 1-grade increment in severity and without in-hospital complications.10 This strict definition was selected based on criteria previously shown to be associated with favorable long-term outcomes.10 Sensitivity analyses were performed using postprocedural MVA ≥1.5 cm2 and MR severity grade ≤2, without in-hospital complications as alternative success criteria.11

Sample size calculation

Because of the retrospective design of the study, formal sample size calculation was not performed before data analysis. All consecutive patients with available information on PBMV success were included into the study to maximize study power. In addition, we assessed the power of our analysis using the method proposed by Riley et al12 to ensure precision of the estimations and to minimize overfitting. The pmsampsize package was used for calculating the minimum sample size requirement.

Statistical methods

The predictors with missing data were imputed using multivariable imputation with chained equations. The multiple imputation procedure was performed using the MICE package in R software13 creating 17 imputed datasets The number of imputations were based on missingness level.14 Additional details on included variables, types of imputation for each variable, and diagnostic used for the imputation models are provided in the Supplemental Materials.

The Wilkins score was handled as a continuous variable in the prediction model. The coefficient of Wilkins score was not reported in the developmental study; therefore, Wilkins score was fitted to PBMV success in the logistic regression model to derive predicted probability for PBMV success. The Wilkins score was updated by fitting 8 predictors (Wilkins score, preprocedural MVA [cm2], age [years], history of prior commissurotomy, NYHA functional class IV, history of AF, RVSP [mm Hg], and severe TR) in the logistic regression model. Multicollinearity of variables in the models was assessed using variance inflation factor. Age, RVSP, and pre-PBMV MVA were assessed for nonlinearity using restrict cubic spline in univariable logistic regression analysis. P values for nonlinearity were derived from Wald chi-square tests of the nonlinear spline components.

Predictive performance of the 2 models (Wilkins score and Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score) were assessed. Discrimination was assessed by C-statistic and classification plots.15 Classification plot showed true positive and false positive rate among different risk thresholds derived from each model. Internal validation was performed using bootstrapping to correct for optimism in the C-statistics.16 Calibration was assessed with calibration plot.

Continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement were used to assess the improvement in outcome prediction by the Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score compared with the Wilkins score.15 Thresholds of 0.05 and 0.1 were used for meaningful change in reclassification for continuous NRI calculation.17 The 95% CI of the NRI was derived using bootstrapping with 200 iterations. Decision curve analysis was performed to assess clinical utility of the 2 models.18

All statistical analyses were performed with R software version 4.0.3.19 The rms, rmda, and nricens packages were used for regression analyses and evaluation of predictive performance.

Results

Of 1,282 patients with rheumatic MS treated with PBMV in 4 hospitals between June 2002 and May 2023 (Supplemental Table 1), 154 patients had missing information on PBMV success. Our study cohort comprised 1,128 patients (88%) for whom the status of PBMV success was available (Figure 1, Table 1). Supplemental Table 2 shows the characteristics of 1,128 patients compared with 154 patients who had missing data on PBMV success. Most patients in the study cohort were female (82.2%) with a median age and body mass index of 45 years and 21.9 kg/m2, respectively (Table 1). The prevalence of AF, NYHA functional class IV, and prior commissurotomy was 48.4%, 1.5%, and 4.3%, respectively. The median pre-PBMV MVA was 0.9 cm2 and 65.8% of patients had pre-PBMV MVA equal or less than 1 cm2. The median Wilkins score was 8 with 64.6% of patients having a Wilkins score equal to or less than 8. The distribution of Wilkins score is provided in Supplemental Figure 1. The median RVSP was 49 mm Hg. Forty-seven patients (5.5%) had severe TR. PBMV was successful according to the PBMV success definition used in the present study in 548 patients (48.6%). Twenty-two patients (2%) underwent emergency surgery, and 4 patients suffered ischemic stroke during the index admission. Discussion

This approach highlights the added value of combining anatomical and clinical factors to provide a more comprehensive and individualized assessment of PBMV success using routinely available preprocedural data. The Wilkins-Integrated Clinical Percutaneous Balloon Mitral Valvuloplasty Score to Predict Percutaneous Balloon Mitral Valvuloplasty Success

Summarized is the development and performance of the Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score for predicting procedural success in patients with rheumatic MS undergoing PBMV. In a multicenter cohort of 1,128 patients, anatomical Wilkins score components were integrated with key clinical variables using a multivariable logistic regression model. This approach highlights the added value of combining anatomical and clinical factors to provide a more comprehensive and individualized assessment of PBMV success using routinely available preprocedural data. AF = atrial fibrillation; MVA = mitral valve area; MS = mitral stenosis; PBMV = percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty; RVSP = right ventricular systolic pressure; TR = tricuspid regurgitation.

Anatomical characteristics of the mitral valve and commissural calcification patterns significantly influence PBMV outcomes. Extensive calcification and subvalvular fibrosis can hinder effective valve dilation, and asymmetric calcification increases the risk of commissural tearing and severe MR. Consequently, echocardiographic evaluation using the Wilkins score is crucial for predicting procedural success. Although higher Wilkins scores generally indicate poorer outcomes, previous studies have noted that almost half of patients with scores above 8 may still achieve optimal PBMV results.2,3 In line with previous research, our findings revealed that the conventional Wilkins score provided limited discrimination between optimal and suboptimal outcomes.

Recent European Society of Cardiology guidelines defined unfavorable clinical characteristics for PBMV as the presence of old age, AF, NYHA functional class IV, prior commissurotomy, severe TR, and severe pulmonary hypertension.8 Older patients with rheumatic MS often have systemic hypertension, AF, and reduced left ventricular compliance.5 Impaired left ventricular relaxation may cause elevated left ventricular diastolic pressure and is manifested as low-gradient severe MS.21 This phenotype of severe rheumatic MS often achieves less favorable outcomes with valvuloplasty than those with normal gradient.22 In addition, older patients are often unaware of their symptoms and the diagnosis usually came at late presentation or when complications have occurred.23 Chronic elevation of left atrial pressures predisposes patients to AF and severe pulmonary hypertension, indicating advanced rheumatic MS. Concomitant TR is not uncommon in patients with severe rheumatic MS.24,25 Severe TR was associated with high pulmonary vascular resistance, smaller pre-PBMV MVA, and higher valve score.26 PBMV results seemed to be less optimal, as shown by smaller post-PBMV MVA, in patients with severe TR.26 TR may be improved after successful PBMV; the presence of significant TR before PBMV was significantly associated with poor long-term outcomes.7,24,26 These clinical factors impact the clinical outcomes after PBMV. Our study demonstrated a marked improvement in predictive accuracy when these clinical characteristics were integrated into the Wilkins score. Importantly, these parameters are routinely collected during preprocedural evaluations, facilitating practical clinical application.

The predictive value of individual Wilkins score components varies across studies. Among the 4 components of the Wilkins score, valvular thickening was identified as a significant predictor in one study3 whereas leaflet mobility was found in another study as a factor associated with PBMV success.27 Two studies showed that only extension of calcification and subvalvular thickening were predictive for PBMV success.4,28 In the original Wilkins cohort, the predictive value arose from the combination of components rather than individual elements.2 Given its familiarity and widespread use, we retained the conventional Wilkins score structure and enhanced it by incorporating the clinically relevant factors highlighted by contemporary guidelines.8,9

The pre-PBMV MVA is an essential marker of disease severity. Nunes et al4 previously reported that smaller MVAs (≤1 cm2) are predictive of suboptimal PBMV outcomes. Similarly, our study identified a positive correlation between pre-PBMV MVA and procedural success. Patients with smaller MVAs typically exhibit advanced valvular fibrosis requiring aggressive dilation, increasing the risk of severe MR. To mitigate these risks, operators often use conservative, stepwise dilation strategies. In addition, our cohort primarily comprises Asian women, typically with smaller body sizes compared with Western populations, influencing procedural strategies toward moderate valve enlargement rather than aggressive dilation.

Although repeat PBMV procedures have historically shown favorable results,29,30 prior commissurotomy emerged as a significant independent predictor of suboptimal outcomes in our analysis. Structural changes such as fibrosis and scarring following the initial procedure likely increase the technical difficulty of subsequent interventions. Thus, mitral valve repair or replacement should be strongly considered for patients with prior commissurotomy who present complex valve anatomy or low likelihood of successful repeat PBMV.

Despite demonstrating significantly improved predictive ability compared with the anatomical Wilkins score, the C-statistic of Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score was only 0.658. This finding highlights the complexity of predicting PBMV success and suggests that further refinement of predictive models is warranted. Given that the primary mechanism of PBMV is enlargement of the MVA through commissurotomy, detailed information on the severity and distribution of commissural calcification may further enhance prediction of procedural success. Prior studies have shown that calcification patterns—particularly asymmetrical commissural remodeling—significantly influence procedural outcomes; for example, a commissural area ratio greater than 1.25 has been associated with suboptimal PBMV results.4 In addition, grading the severity of commissural calcification using a commissural calcification score has been shown to stratify patients according to their likelihood of achieving an optimal postprocedural MVA.31 These findings suggest that incorporating more granular anatomical or advanced imaging data could further improve the prediction of PBMV success. Therefore, the Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score should be viewed as complementary to, rather than competitive with, detailed anatomical assessments, helping clinicians optimize patient selection and procedural outcomes.

Real-time 3-dimensional echocardiography and multimodality imaging may offer superior predictive insights compared with traditional 2-dimensional assessments.32,33 Moreover, biomarkers such as natriuretic peptides or inflammatory markers may potentially improve the prediction of PBMV success.34,35 However, such advanced imaging modalities and biomarkers are often unavailable in resource-limited settings and require specialized expertise for imaging interpretation. Consequently, the simplicity and clinical accessibility of our Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score provide practical advantages in everyday clinical practice.

Study limitations

Limitations of this study include its retrospective design and reliance on data from 4 tertiary hospitals within a single country, potentially limiting generalizability to different populations. Missing data, a common limitation in retrospective analyses, was addressed through multiple imputation, allowing maximal utilization of available data and ensuring analytical robustness. Our results were derived from cohorts undergoing single-balloon techniques; hence, applicability to other techniques such as double-balloon or percutaneous metallic mitral commissurotomy36 is uncertain. Furthermore, echocardiographic parameters were extracted from standard hospital reports rather than centralized core laboratory assessments, potentially affecting data reliability.37 Our database was collected several years ago, with raw echocardiographic data unavailable for most patients. The relatively low prevalence of severe TR in our study, compared with other cohorts,38,39 may have resulted in wide CIs, thereby limiting the statistical precision of the coefficient. Alternative quantitative methods for assessing TR severity, such as vena contracta width, may offer more reliable and accurate measurements as continuous variables and potentially enhancing the diagnostic precision. However, such detailed echocardiographic parameters were not available in our data. Although the NYHA functional class is simple and widely used in practice, the more objective 6-minute walk distance may reclassify the patient-reported NYHA functional class and could potentially improve predictive performance.40 Even though symptomatic improvement, such as changes in NYHA functional class, represents a patient-center clinical endpoint, these data are not available in our database. Finally, the Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score was developed and internally validated with adjustment for potential optimism within a specific Thai population, predominantly young women. Although our study cohort was sufficiently large to support robust model development (Supplemental Table 6),12 prospective external validation in an independent cohort—particularly among older patients with rheumatic MS and a higher burden of comorbidities—is necessary to confirm its generalizability before clinical adoption.

Conclusions

Integrating clinical characteristics into the conventional Wilkins score significantly enhances its predictive accuracy. The Wilkins-Integrated Clinical PBMV score has potential to improve patient selection for PBMV. Nevertheless, external validation is required before clinical adoption.
