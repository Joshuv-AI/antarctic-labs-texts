Estimation of the sample size to determine gas flux in finishing steers under confined conditions using alternative statistical approaches
Authors: Juan de J Vargas, Leticia M Campos, Fidel Maureira, Maya Swenson, Ashley K Schilling-Hazlett, Marisa Werner, Leonardo G Sitorski, Eduardo M Paula, Pedro H V Carvalho, Kimberly R Stackhouse-Lawson, Sara E Place
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn ruminants, gas flux evaluation (ie methane [CH4] and carbon dioxide [CO2] production and oxygen [O2] consumption) has primarily been conducted in experimental research conditions. Despite its importance for accurate gas flux estimation, to our knowledge, limited guidance exists on how many animals are required to be sampled to represent a specific population of animals adequately. This study aimed to predict the number of growing steers required to characterize gas flux within contemporary groups during the finishing phase using alternative statistical approaches. The analysis included data from six research studies conducted during the finishing phase using Angus cross-bred steers (n = 572 cattle, 552 ± 60 kg of body weight). In each experiment, gas flux was individually determined using an automated head-chamber system. The required number of steers was estimated using three independent approaches: a conventional statistical sampling method, a bootstrap evaluation, and a Monte Carlo simulation. The number of steers required to determine CH4 production varied between 15 and 55 (ie between 15 and 125% of the original population), while to determine CO2 production and O2 consumption varied between 5 and 14 (ie between 6 and 43% of the original population) according to each experimental condition. Conventional methods could be applied a priori, whereas computational techniques can only be performed a posteriori. These results illustrate how methodological choices influence the estimated sample size for gas flux evaluation in finishing steers.Keywords: beef cattle, carbon dioxide, methane, oxygen, sampling size

Introduction

In the last decades, there has been considerable effort to understand and reduce enteric methane (CH4) emissions from ruminants (Hristov et al. 2013; Beauchemin et al. 2020; Vargas et al. 2022). Production of CH4 represents an energy loss for ruminants that varies between 2 and 12% of the gross energy intake (Johnson and Johnson 1995), and it is a potent greenhouse gas with 27 times more global warming potential than carbon dioxide (CO2) (Masson-Delmotte et al. 2022). However, determining CH4 emissions under commercial production conditions is challenging due to technical and economic limitations, but it is essential to support the reduction of greenhouse gases in the livestock sector and the monitoring, reporting, and verification programs in carbon markets (Perosa et al. 2023; Tedeschi and Beauchemin 2023).

Gas flux evaluation has been used to determine animal energetics and enteric CH4 emissions in ruminants (Johnson and Johnson 1995; Proctor et al. 2024). Initially, gas flux evaluation required trained animals, and it was conducted under restrained conditions using indirect calorimetric chambers (Blaxter and Clapperton 1965; Hammond et al. 2016). Although indirect calorimetry is considered the “gold standard” technique to determine gas flux, its applicability to commercial production conditions is limited due to methodological restrictions (Hammond et al. 2016; Llonch et al. 2018). Therefore, gas flux has been primarily evaluated on an individual or group basis under experimental research conditions (Hammond et al. 2016; Hristov et al. 2018). However, the constrained number of experimental units and conditions due to physical, logistical, or economic factors limits the ability to extrapolate gas flux measurements to commercial production systems.

In research experiments, the number of required experimental units is calculated a priori using predefined values according to results from previous experiments, such as expected variability or the difference between treatment means. However, computational techniques methods allow evaluating the required number of experimental units a posteriori using the distribution of a specific population. Differently, models used to estimate CH4 emissions in ruminants have been reported in the literature (Kebreab et al. 2008; Ellis et al. 2009; van Lingen et al. 2019) but tend to have low predictability due to the potential to misrepresent conditions in current production systems, especially during the finishing phase (Ellis et al. 2014; Moraes et al. 2014; Vargas et al. 2025).

The increasing availability of portable gas flux measurement technologies may allow the evaluation of CH4, CO2, and oxygen (O2) in commercial production conditions (Hegarty 2013; Dorich et al. 2015), potentially resulting in more practical quantification, although it is expected that greater variability will occur due to less controlled conditions, such as environmental or animal behavior variability (Hammond et al. 2016; Hristov et al. 2018). In the literature, there are methodological recommendations to evaluate gas flux using portable chambers (O’Connor et al. 2024) or automated head-chamber system (AHCS) (Hegarty 2013; Dressler et al. 2023). However, those recommendations are mainly designed for individual animals evaluated under experimental research conditions, and there is limited guidance regarding the number of animals that should be evaluated to represent the gas flux of contemporary groups. Thus, this study aimed to determine the required number of animals to represent the gas flux of contemporary groups of growing steers during the finishing phase, utilizing alternative statistical approaches. The experimental hypothesis was that different methods would result in similar recommendations regarding the required number of animals to represent gas flux.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This analysis included data from six studies conducted during the finishing phase of beef production at the Climate Smart Research Facility at the Agricultural Research, Development, and Education Center at Colorado State University, CO, USA. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all procedures involving animal handling (experiment one: 4072, experiment two: 3712-13, experiment three: 4689, experiment four: 4689, experiment five: 5403, experiment six: 1526).

Animals and feed management

A total of 572 Angus crossbred steers were similarly managed in six independent experiments, where animal performance and gas flux were evaluated. Briefly, steers were weighed during two consecutive days at the beginning and end of each experimental period. Steers received a total mixed ration (TMR, Table 1) and water ad libitum. Daily intake was individually recorded using the SmartFeed intake monitoring system (C-Lock, Rapid City, SD). In experiment 1, 166 steers (518 ± 39.9 kg of initial body weight [BW]) were evaluated over 69 days. In experiment two, 170 steers (345 ± 22.3 kg of initial BW) were assessed over 96 days. In experiment three, 51 steers were classified according to the initial BW as heavy (n = 23 and 578 ± 24.0 kg of initial BW) or light (n = 38 and 512 ± 36.5 kg of initial BW), and evaluated over 52 and 80 days, respectively. In experiment four, 36 steers (548 ± 31.2 kg of initial BW) were assessed over 76 days. In experiment five, 23 steers (608 ± 51.4 kg of initial BW) were evaluated over 49 days. In experiment six, 126 steers (555 ± 47.0 kg of initial BW) were assessed over 53 days.

Gas flux determination

In each experiment, steers were located in research pens (max capacity: 50 cattle/pen) containing one AHCS (GreenFeed, C-Lock, Rapid City, SD) for gas flux measurement (ie CH4 and CO2 production and O2 consumption). Before using the units, steers received a radio frequency identification electronic ear tag (Allflex, Merck & Co, Rahway, NJ) in the left ear using a manual applicator. Steers were exposed to the AHCS during an acclimation period of at least one week before data collection. After the acclimation period, headbox wings and cattle panels were used to ensure that only one animal had access to the AHCS unit.

Steers were allowed to visit the AHCS unit every 4 hours (up to 6 visits/day) and consume up to 6 alfalfa pellet drops (approximately 35 g as fed/drop) with 30-second intervals between drops during each visit. This encouraged steers to visit the units throughout the day and ensured animals stayed at the AHCS for a minimum duration of 2 minutes for gas flux measurement.

Recovery tests of CO2 were performed monthly throughout the experiment and at the beginning and end of each experiment to ensure the performance of the gas flux system. Recovery tests were 100 ± 3.3%, 100 ± 3.9%, 101 ± 1.4%, 99 ± 2.6%, 98 ± 1.7%, and 98 ± 3.5% for experiments one, two, three, four, five, and six, respectively. Additionally, the manufacturer performed zero and span calibrations of the CH4, CO2, and O2 gas analyzers every three days via an onboard auto-calibration system. Raw collection data were validated by C-Lock Inc., which included adjustments for head proximity, visit length, and airflow and wind corrections. Gas flux data were excluded when the length of the visit was less than 2 min and more than 8 min, and the airflow was less than 26 L/s (Arthur et al. 2017; Gunter and Beck 2018; Vargas et al. 2024). In addition, the evaluated steers were required to visit the AHCS units at least 55 times during the evaluation period (Vargas et al. 2024). Thus, individual gas flux was determined as the average CO2 and CH4 production and O2 consumption during the evaluation period.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated using three independent approaches. Initially, a conventional method was used to predict the number of steers required to represent gas flux within a contemporaneous group, following the statistical framework outlined by Daniel and Cross (2018). For the computational techniques, a common threshold was pre-determined to ensure consistency across methods. Thus, two stability criteria were arbitrarily applied: (i) the percent change in the mean of posterior means between adjacent sample sizes < 5% and (ii) the coefficient of variation of posterior means < 2.5%. Stability was declared at the smallest n meeting both criteria. The bootstrapped method was implemented in Rstudio v 2025.09.0 and R v4.5.1 using the package data.table (Barret et al. 2025) to determine the minimum number of steers empirically required that meet the predefined threshold of mean and standard deviation. This method involved repeatedly drawing random subsamples without replacement of an increasing size from each group and calculating mean flux values (eg CH4, CO2, O2) across a large number of iterations (eg 10,000). Within each iteration, random subsamples were taken from each project, and group means were calculated with missing values removed. All results were indexed by sample size and iteration, ensuring reproducibility through a fixed random seed.

Sample size determination was evaluated using a parametric Monte Carlo-based subsampling simulation (Williams et al. 2007). Analyses were performed using RStudio v 2025.09.0. Within each subsample, an intercept-only model was fitted using a Bayesian framework with conjugate priors, implemented through the MCMCpack: MCMCregress function (Martin et al. 2011). The model estimated the population-level mean without inclusion of covariates, thereby isolating sampling-driven variability in the mean estimate. Models were fitted within a Bayesian framework, in which prior information was formally combined with the likelihood to obtain a posterior distribution for model parameters. Conjugate normal-normal priors were specified for the mean parameter (µ) following a normal distribution centered on the observed gas-specific mean and standard deviation (SD) reported by Vargas et al. (2025). The likelihood assumed normally distributed residuals. The model was set for 10,000 iterations with a burn-in of 1000. Posterior means, posterior SD, and Geweke z-scores were extracted for diagnostic evaluation. Across the ten replicates at each sample size, the mean and SD of posterior means were computed. Data handling and visualization were performed using R packages dplyr and tidyr (Wickham et al. 2023), purr (Wickham and Henry 2025), and ggplot2 (Wickham 2016). Model fitting used MCMCpack (Martin et al. 2011), and diagnostics employed coda (Plummer et al. 2006). Within each interaction, the sample mean and standard deviation were calculated to assess the stability of estimates as the sampling number of steers increased.

Results and discussion

In this study, a total of 572 steers from six different experiments were evaluated under confined conditions during the finishing phase of beef production. Steers produced daily between 167 and 192 g of CH4, 10,197 to 12,571 g of CO2, and 7047 to 8864 g of O2 during the finishing phase (Table 2). Similar results have been reported in growing steers in confined conditions (Gunter and Beck 2018; Beauchemin et al. 2022a; Dressler et al. 2023). In addition, there was a positive and significant (P < 0.0001) relationship between CH4 and CO2 production (r = 0.60) and CH4 production and O2 consumption (r = 0.51), similar to previous reports (Ryan et al. 2022). Gas flux varies according to DMI and the physiological state of the animal (Beauchemin et al. 2022b). In this regard, it is expected that animals with greater DMI produce more CH4 and CO2 due to greater intake capacity. However, the relationship between CH4 and DMI is weaker when animals consume high-concentrate diets relative to high-forage diets (Hristov et al. 2018; van Lingen et al. 2019; Vargas et al. 2025), possibly due to other limiting factors, such as the energy ingestion (NASEM 2016).

In this study, the number of steers required to determine a representative CH4 production varied between 15 and 55 (ie between 15 and 125% of the original population), while to determine CO2 production and O2 consumption varied between 5 and 14 (ie between 15 and 125% of the original population; Table 2). Similar to previous reports, the greater number of animals required to determine CH4 production compared to CO2 production and O2 consumption was due to greater variability for CH4 production (Arthur et al. 2017; Della Rosa et al. 2025). In this study, the coefficient of variation (CV) among experiments ranged between 15% and 18% for CH4 production, while the CV for CO2 production and O2 consumption ranged between 5% and 10%. It is important to recognize that in this analysis, steers were managed similarly among experiments, and only steers that met the minimum criteria (ie > 55 AHCS visits, > 2 min visit duration) were included in this analysis (Vargas et al. 2024).

The application of three different methodologies (ie conventional statistical sampling method, bootstrap analysis, and Monte Carlo simulation) revealed important differences in sample size recommendations across gases and experimental contexts. Regarding CH4 production, both the conventional and bootstrap methods consistently estimated greater minimum sample sizes than the Monte Carlo simulation (Table 3). Notably, the required sample sizes for CH4 exceeded the actual number of animals sampled in experiments 4 and 5 using the conventional and bootstrap methods, suggesting that group-level estimates in these trials may be underpowered and subject to elevated uncertainty. Monte Carlo simulation produced lower sample size estimates for CH4 production in experiments 3 to 5, which may reflect its reliance on prior and likelihood assumptions or its tendency to stabilize earlier under certain distributional conditions. These results reinforce the value of computational techniques such as bootstrap and Monte Carlo, which can capture the stability of mean estimates under replication.

The three methods resulted in a more consistent minimum number of animals regarding CO2 production and O2 consumption, where bootstrap and Monte Carlo often stabilized on a minimum of 10 animals per group. The conventional statistical method occasionally suggested slightly higher (eg 12 vs. 10 for CO2 production in experiment 2) or lower (eg 5 vs. 10 for O2 consumption in experiment 3) thresholds (Table 3), likely reflecting the data analysis (ie increasing five animals during the sampling evaluation) and the sensitivity to variance structure in those datasets. This divergence highlights the importance of aligning methodological choice with the underlying data structure and research objectives. In addition, these findings underscore the practical implications of methodological selection in experimental design. While conventional methods offer a quick, a priori assumption-based benchmark, computational techniques such as bootstrap and Monte Carlo provide posteriori insights into sampling sufficiency under real-world variability.

The methodological framework developed in this study, particularly the bootstrap approach, offers applications beyond confined finishing systems. In contexts where animal dispersion, environmental heterogeneity, and temporal variability introduce additional layers of complexity (French et al. 2025), subsampling-based approaches can help to quantify the uncertainty of gas flux estimates under field conditions. Thus, applying the bootstrap analysis to datasets could support robust sampling designs, improve comparability across experiments, and enhance the reliability of assessments (Ghassemi Nejad et al. 2024). However, it is important to note that the effectiveness of bootstrap analysis depends on the availability of sufficiently large and well-characterized datasets. When sample sizes are too limited, subsampling may fail to capture true variability, potentially leading to misleading conclusions (Efron and Tibshirani 1998).

Monte Carlo simulation provides a parametric, model-based framework able to represent uncertainty through simulation of underlying probability distributions. This attribute enables the incorporation of prior information, formal estimation of posterior variability, and direct assessment of stability. In contrast to purely non-parametric resampling methods, which rely on the observed data distribution, the Monte Carlo-based approach offers a stronger inferential power by drawing from defined probabilistic models. The main limitations of Monte Carlo simulation compared to bootstrap approaches are the reliance on model assumptions and prior specifications, limiting its application a priori. Because it draws from predefined probability distributions rather than the observed data itself, results are sensitive to incorrect model structure or poorly informed priors. Additionally, Monte Carlo simulations are computationally intensive and require convergence diagnostics, whereas empirical methods are generally simpler and less assumption-dependent (Tempelman 1998; Sorensen and Gianola 2007).

Conclusion

A total of 15 to 55 steers (ie between 15 and 125% of the original population) are required to determine gas flux in confined conditions during the finishing phases in beef production systems, according to the variability of the contemporaneous group. Conventional sample size calculations and bootstrap analysis required more animals than Monte Carlo simulations, especially for CH4 production determination. However, conventional methods could be applied a priori, whereas simulation approaches could only be performed a posteriori. These results illustrate how methodological choices influence the estimated sample size for gas flux evaluation in finishing steers. Therefore, methodological choices should align with study objectives, resource constraints, and data availability to ensure accurate and reproducible assessments of gas flux emissions in beef cattle production systems.
