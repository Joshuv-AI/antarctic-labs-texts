Difficult differential diagnosis of delayed mesh infection following inguinal hernia surgery involving appendiceal cancer: A case report
Authors: Yusuke Kitagawa, Shigeo Hirasaki, Michiya Bando
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe present a case of post-inguinal hernia repair delayed mesh infection that was initially misdiagnosed as appendiceal cancer.Presentation of caseThe patient was an 82-year-old man who underwent right inguinal hernia repair with a plug mesh 7 years before he presented with a lump in the right inguinal region. No skin infection signs were evident; blood tests revealed no inflammation or abnormal tumor markers. These infections may mimic other conditions such as appendiceal cancer, stressing the need for vigilance and careful evaluation. Introduction

Mesh repair is a common and safe treatment option for inguinal hernia; however, mesh infection has also been reported as a complication [1]. The reported frequency of its incidence ranges from 0 % to 3.5% [2]. However, some reports have suggested rates as high as 8% [3]. In cases where signs of inflammation such as redness, swelling, and pain in the inguinal area raise suspicion of infection [1], mesh removal is often necessary for infection control. While reports of surgery for suspected mesh migration leading to a lump exist [4], there have been no reports of appendiceal cancer suspected preoperatively in cases with symptoms and tests showing inflammation limited to the inguinal lump alone. Here, we describe a case of a patient who presented with an inguinal lump without signs of inflammation and was initially misdiagnosed as having appendiceal cancer before being eventually diagnosed with delayed mesh infection. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [5].

Presentation Of Case.

An 82-year-old man presented with the chief complaint of a lump in the right inguinal region. Seven years prior to this presentation, he had undergone hernia repair surgery with a mesh plug for a right inguinal hernia. Upon examination, a 30-mm lump was detected on palpating the right inguinal area, and the patient complained of mild tenderness. No signs of skin infection were observed. Blood tests revealed the following findings: white blood cell count, 6430/mm; C-reactive protein level, 1.31 mg/dl; carcinoembryonic antigen level, 2.5 ng/ml, and carbohydrate antigen 19–9 level, 4.6 IU/ml. Abdominal contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) showed a tumorous lesion that appeared contiguous with the cecum from its outer aspect (Fig. 1a, b). Swelling of the lymph nodes along the ileocolic artery and a tumor in the right lung lobe were observed (Fig. 1c), leading to a diagnosis of appendiceal cancer with associated lymph node and right lung metastases. Lower gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed extrinsic compression of the cecal wall but no mucosal changes suggestive of cancer (Fig. 2.). Planned ileocecal resection with lymph node dissection was performed. During the dissection of adhesions between the cecum and abdominal wall, a pus-filled abscess was found, and the mesh in the inguinal region was exposed (Fig. 3). Consequently, the tumor identified on preoperative CT was diagnosed as an abscess caused by the mesh infection. After separating the mesh adhered to the cecum and detorsed the ileocecal region. During the surgery, we deliberated whether to perform an ileocecal resection or limit it to cecal resection. Given the absence of tumorous lesions in the cecal portion, the likelihood of cancer was deemed extremely low. As a result, we opted for a cecal resection, with the plan to perform an additional ileocecal resection if a cancer diagnosis was made in the permanent specimen. Pathological testing did not reveal cancer, confirming the diagnosis of delayed mesh infection. No fistula was observed between the intestine and mesh (Fig. 4). Bacterial cultures of the abscess were negative. The patient was discharged without postoperative complications. Follow-up CT scans showed resolution of the lymph node enlargement along the ileocolic artery, and monitoring was continued for the lung nodule. Clinical and imaging findings made it challenging to suspect a mesh infection, and differentiation from appendiceal cancer was difficult.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a patient who presented with an inguinal lump without signs of inflammation and was initially misdiagnosed as having appendiceal cancer before being eventually diagnosed with delayed mesh infection.

Although mesh repair is a common curative treatment for inguinal hernia, mesh infection resulting in reoperation is a significant complication [1]. The reported frequency of its incidence ranges from 0 % to 3.5% [2]. Reports of conservative treatment for mesh infections have been sporadic [4,6,7] and there appear to be cases where drainage alone suffices. However, mesh removal through surgery becomes necessary when conservative treatment is insufficient [8]. In the case of our patient, mesh removal was not performed and peritoneal closure was performed after plug mesh removal. This was because appendiceal cancer could not be ruled out in the preoperative diagnosis, observation of the onlay mesh was not possible after plug mesh removal during the operation, and there were no preoperative signs of infection. Subsequently, there were no postoperative infections or hernia recurrences.

Reports of plug mesh migration into intra-abdominal organs exist. According to international guidelines published in 2018, many plug meshes have been reported to migrate early after surgery [2], while occurrences of flat mesh migration are rare. In the case of our patient, hernia repair was performed using a plug mesh, which suggested that migration occurred early postoperatively, leading to abscess formation in the deep tissue. Regarding infection associated with mesh migration, Asano et al. [9] primarily attributed it to inadequate peritoneal suturing in the plug and Kugel meshes, resulting in mesh exposure within the abdominal cavity, adhesion to the intestine, and potential for fistula formation due to inflammation from a foreign body reaction. Maeda et al. [10] also mentioned the possibility of chronic contact between the intestine and plug mesh, leading to fistula formation and subsequent infection.

There have been limited previous reports on the association between superficial and mesh infections [11]. In our case, there was awareness only of the lump being present and there were no signs of superficial infection. In the differential diagnosis of tumors, reports exist where entry into the perivesical region via the preperitoneal space led to suspicions of urinary tract tumors and necessitated surgery [4,12]. Considering the reports of hernia recurrence after infected hernia mesh removal [13], we are proceeding with caution.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge posed by delayed mesh infection after inguinal hernia repair, particularly when typical clinical and imaging indications are absent. In our patient, the mesh infection mimicked appendiceal cancer, leading to a diagnostic dilemma. Early recognition and accurate differentiation are crucial for avoiding unnecessary extensive surgeries and complications. Furthermore, in the context of hernia repair using plug mesh, surgeons should be aware of the risk of migration even in the absence of classic signs of infection, and when dealing with abdominal wall abscesses following the use of plug mesh, consideration should be given to the possibility of migration. Further research is needed to better understand the factors contributing to mesh migration and infection as well as to develop effective management strategies for such cases.
