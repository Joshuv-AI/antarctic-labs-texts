Comparative genomics and epidemic success of Mycobacterium tuberculosis lineage 4 in eastern China
Authors: Zong-Lei Zhou, Qi Wu, Zheng-Wei Liu, Wei-Bing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) lineage 4 (L4) remains as a crucial component of the pathogen. Genetic factors potentiate the spread of Mtb, while the genomic feature and epidemic success at individual level of L4 strains have not been fully illustrated, especially in China. Therefore, the current study aimed to obtain additional understanding on the pan-genome architecture and transmission of the pathogen.ResultsThe results first revealed a low proportion of soft-core genes (28.66%) and high genetic diversity for Mtb L4 strains in eastern China. The pan-genome size increased with added genomes following an exponential growth trend, with no obvious plateau observed. Additionally, our results suggested a greater gene loss rate (mean: 0.0112) compared to gene gain rate (mean: 0.0039), suggesting its important roles in the course of adaptive evolution for L4 strains. Pan-genome was enriched in 120 KEGG pathways, of which the majority was metabolism (75.57%). Furthermore, we identified significant associations between the bacterial success and 5 genes (espK, ilvD, mazF7, aftD, ilvA), the presence of which suggested high transmissibility of L4 strains.ConclusionThis study highlights an open and dynamic pan-genome structure of Mtb L4 strains in eastern China. The local strains are undergoing massive changes in gene families that shape its gene repertoires, thereby potentially conferring the adaptive potential to environment and bacterial transmission. Collectively, our findings allow for deeper insights into the pan-genome composition, phylogenetic relationship, evolutionary dynamic, and epidemic success of Mtb L4 strains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13035-9.Keywords: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Pan-genome, Comparative genomics, Epidemic success, Lineage 4

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), resulting from the infection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), continues to be the top cause of mortality from single infectious disease [1]. Recent genome data suggested a wide spectrum of Mtb lineages, which primarily included seven human-adapted types and at least nine animal-adapted types [2]. Mtb of lineage 4 (L4), also referred as Euro-American lineage, has been reported as the most geographically widespread and genetically diverse sub-lineage [3, 4]. Notably, L4 isolates differ in distinct geographic scenarios, the epidemiology and host-pathogen interplay benefit its evolution into various sub-lineages [4, 5]. In China, a nationwide spoligotyping of 16621 isolates suggested L4 constituted roughly 17% of circulating strains, with L4.2, L4.4, and L4.5 holding the major portion [6]. Compared to ancient lineages (L1 and L5), the L4 lineage demonstrates significantly a higher transmission rate and a broader geographical distribution, partially attributed to the bacterial evasion of host immune defenses [7]. Therefore, extensive understanding on the molecular mechanisms underlying the transmission of L4 lineage is imperative for effective TB control.

Most previous reports on Mtb L4 strains focused exclusively on genes or mutations conferring drug resistance and evolution history, while comprehensive investigations on genome composition and biological functions, as well as dynamic evolution of gene families for L4 isolates were rarely implemented. Intriguingly, existing evidence corroborates phylogeographically structured Mtb population in specific human individuals, implicating stable sympatric host-pathogen associations within the same region over time [8]. Therefore, the evolution and genetic structures of the pathogen might vary across distinct populations, while there exits knowledge gaps for L4 strains circulating in Chinese TB patients [9]. Analyses on multiple genomes of Mtb strains allow to reveal the genetic diversity, which can be largely explained by the differences in genes and transposable element repertoire [10]. Through literature review, we found several studies [11–13] exploring the epidemic success and risk factors for recent transmission from a perspective of population, like genetic clusters and basic reproduction numbers. However, such approaches mix the effects on epidemic success for Mtb strains with distinct genomic features and fail to determine the transmission capacity at individual level. By contrast, time-scaled haplotype density (THD) method helps evaluate strain-specific effects on transmission [14, 15]. Furthermore, these studies presented limited information on genes contributing to high or low transmission capacity of the pathogen.

Hence, our study aimed to provide new insights into the genome structure, composition, and evolution of Mtb L4 strains through comparative genomic analyses at genomic level. Furthermore, we sought to analyze the transmission capacity of the strains at individual level using THD method and explore related genetic factors by pan-genome-wide association analyses, thereby offering further evidence for TB control.

Methods

Study population and sample collection

This study prospectively collected and sequenced 773 isolates from Changshan and Jiangshan City in Zhejiang Province from 2015 to 2021, of which 155 belonged to L4 according to the lineage identification using TB-Profiler version 4.1.1 [16]. Details of study design and population have been documented in our prior report [17]. In this study, we removed duplicate samples (n = 9) by checking case ID and those without clinical characteristics (n = 16), followed by drug resistance assessment implemented as previously reported [18]. To obtain reliable assembled genomes, we further excluded samples with a mean read depth less than 40X (n = 1) and those with G + C content less than 60% (n = 2). Afterwards, 127 isolates were employed for final analyses (Figure S1). Detailed information on demography and clinical characteristics was presented in Table S1.

De novo assembly and annotation

Quality of raw sequencing data were check using FastQC version 0.12.1 [19], followed by exclusion of adapters, unpaired reads, and low-quality sequences with Trimmomatic version 0.36 [20]. After trimming, we employed FastUniq version 1.1 [21] to remove PCR duplicates. SPAdes version 4.0 [22] was used for read error correction and De novo assembly of Mtb genomes using a list of k-mer sizes “21,33,55,77,99,127”. We filtered reads with sequencing depth less than 20X, and only contigs greater than 300 bp were retained in final assembled genomes [23]. Base correction and assembly polishing were performed using Pilon version 1.24 [24], followed by the evaluation of genome assembly quality with QUAST version 5.2.0 [25] (Table S2). Prokka version 1.13 [26] was employed to annotate assembled genomes with an e-value threshold of 1⋅10− 6. Briefly, this genome annotation tool employed Prodigal version 2.6.3 [27] to predict proteins coding genes and identify gene coordinates, and the feature of putative gene products was described using BLAST+ search against UniProt database [28].

Pan-genome construction

In this study, we employed GET_HOMOLOGUES software [29] to calculate the core- and pan-genome of 127 Mtb L4 strains using draft genomes assembled above. First, get_homologues.pl script was implemented to compute sequence cluster (both nucleotide and protein) with minimum sequence alignment coverage of 75% and minimum sequence identity of 90% [30, 31]. Herein, we employed compare_clusters.pl script to calculate the intersected clusters based on results obtained by COGtriangles [32] and OrthoMCL [33] algorithms, which were considered as core-genome shared by all strains. Afterwards, we performed genome composition analysis to measure the size of core- and pan-genome, by adding “-c” flag in the get_homologues.pl script. To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we also decomposed the genetic structures of L4 strains using BDBH algorithm [29]. Pan-Genome Profile Analyse Tool (PanGP) software version 1.0.1 was used to analyze the trend change in the size of core-genome, pan-genome, and new genes, and to create fitting curves using the exponential model as described by Tettelin et al. [10, 34]. Another auxiliary script parse_pangenome_matrix_pl was employed to classify pan-genome into four classes: core, soft-core, shell, and cloud. Herein, soft-core genes were determined if appearing in at least 95% of all genomes, cloud genes were those found in two or fewer genomes, and the remaining genes present in several genomes were viewed as shell genes [29].

Pan-genome phylogenetic analysis and average nucleotide identity calculation

To reveal the phylogenetic relationships between isolates, a pan-genome matrix (PGM) indicating gene presence (1) or absence (0) was created using the shared orthogroups obtained through COGtriangles and OrthoMCL algorithms (get_homologues.pl -t 0 -S 90 -G/M). The script estimate_pangenome_phylogenies.sh in GET_PHYLOMARKERS pipeline [35] was employed to create maximum-likelihood pan-genome phylogenetic trees using the resulting PGM. We selected GTR2 + FO+R4 as the optimal nucleotide substitution model, and the confidence of phylogeny was assessed via ultrafast bootstrap iteration with 1000 replicates. Furthermore, we inferred a maximum-likelihood phylogeny using IQtree software version 3.0.1 [36] with concatenated SNP alignment (see below), and bootstrap approximation with 1000 replicates was performed to assess the reliability for internal branches. To quantitively evaluate the congruence between trees, the normalized Robinson-Foulds (nRF) and normalized matching split (nMS) distances were calculated by TreeCmp program version 1.1 [37], with high values indicating greater inconsistence (range: 0–1). Comparison between phylogenetic trees was visualized using cophylo function in phytools package [38] with R software.

The script average_nucleotide_identity.py in pyani version 0.3.0 was used to calculate the average nucleotide identity (ANI) between genomes, by running MUMmer algorithm as previously described [39]. Further, we employed the pheatmap packages in R software version 4.3.1 to visualize the obtained matrix. A cut-off value of 98% for pairwise ANI was used to differentiate subtypes within same species [40].

Pan-genome evolution analysis

To reveal genome evolution of L4 strains, we employed Count software version 10.04 [41] to evaluate gene gain and loss events using a maximum-likelihood birth-and-death model. Briefly, PGM returned by compare_clusters.pl script and the maximum-likelihood pan-genome phylogeny were subjected to Count software.

First, the gain-loss-duplication model with a Poisson distribution of family size at root was used for rate optimization, and the same gain-loss and duplication-loss ratio was set for lineage-specific variation. We assumed a gamma-distributed rate variation across gene family and ran rate optimization procedure for 100 times, with convergence threshold of 0.01 for likelihood computation. Then, the optimized rate was used to calculate gene family history and predict gene gain and loss events across the phylogeny using Dollo parsimony method. For each branch, the gene turnover was computed as gene gain rate divided by gene loss rate.

Epidemic success estimation

First, we employed BWA-MEM version 0.7.18 [42] to align reads to reference genome (GenBank accession number: NC_000962.3), and SAMtools version 1.6 [43] was used to create indexed BAM files. Picard version 2.9.2 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) was employed to remove duplicate reads, followed by base quality recalibration and realignment of reads around insertion and deletion regions using GATK version 3.8 [44]. We used VarScan2 software [45] to perform SNP calling and VCFtools version 0.1.14 [46] to filter SNP in PE/PPE/PGRS family gene, mobile genetic elements, phage sequence, repeat regions, and genes conferring drug resistance. SNP loci with a minimum mapping quality of 30, a minimum base quality score of 20, a minimum read depth of 20, and a minimum variant allele frequency of 75% were retained to create alignment sequences [47].

Further, we used the dist.dna function in ape R package to generate the matrix of pairwise SNP distance, which was employed to compute strain-specific success index with THD method [15]. The calculation of THD success index was implemented through thd package in R software, assuming the following parameters: a mean evolutionary rate of 1⋅10− 7 mutations per genome per site, an effective genome size of 4⋅106 (number of loci for SNP calling), and a time scale of 200 years [15].

Function prediction and pan-genome-wide association analyses

In this study, the function of gene clusters was inferred using BPGA software version 1.3 [48] with GeneBank annotation files and PGM as inputs. We employed the functional annotation tool of BPGA software to obtain best-hits from KEGG and COG databases, using USEARCH clustering method [49]. An E-value cut-off of 10E-5 was used to detect orthologous groups, and other parameters were set as default values. The above analysis enables us to annotate the metabolic pathways and COG categories within core genome, dispensable genome, and unique genes.

Due to the explorative nature of current study, Mtb isolates were further categorized into two classes based on the 75th percentile of THD index [15]. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression was implemented adjusting for patient age, gender, and the genotypes and drug resistance of L4 isolates. We ran the pipeline with default parameters, and adjusted P-value of 0.05 by Bonferroni’s correction was used to corroborate between-group difference. To validate the robustness of gene-transmission associations, linear regression fitting with aforementioned covariates adjusted was carried out to examinate the links between THD index and specific genes. Furthermore, between-group comparison of THD index distribution was conducted using Kruskal-Wallis H test, with a two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 suggesting statistical significance.

Results

General genomic features

The 127 Mtb isolates were sequenced to an average depth of 294.15±129.32X, and the mean coverage was 99.14±1.23%. The number of contigs ranged from 68 (JK-0981) to 1283 (lzwG29C06), with a mean of 286 contigs. The average N50 and N90 were 101256 (ranging from 5239 to 204876) and 32649 (ranging from 1549 to 67527), respectively. The G + C content ranged from 64.56% (lzwG29C06) to 65.60% (J25/J8/J99/JK-0957/JK-0986), with genome fraction of 97.21% and duplication ratio of 1.001 on average. Furthermore, genome annotation analysis predicted a minimum number of 4047 protein-coding sequences (CDS) in the strain LD21F62X and the maximum of 4379 CDS in the strain coded as 2018483. Additionally, a median of 53 tRNA and 3 rRNA were annotated in 127 Mtb genomes, respectively (Table S2).

Pan- and core-genome analyses

To determine the complete gene repertoire of Mtb L4 strains, we employed COGtriangles and OrthoMCL algorithms to performed genome composition analyses. Accordingly, a total of 1890 core genes were yielded to account for 18.69% of pan-genome (Figs. 1 and 2). Consistently, we obtained 1900 core genes by leveraging BDBH algorithm, with a consensus of 1857 genes across three approaches (Fig. S2). In addition to core set, accessory genome comprising dispensable genes and strain-specific (unique) genes constituted a total of 10111 gene clusters (Fig. S3). Moreover, genome composition analysis suggested 2898 gene clusters (28.66%) in soft-core compartment, and shell class encompassed 2672 gene clusters, accounting for 26.43% of the pan-genome. The remaining 44.91% corresponded to 4541 gene clusters were in cloud genome (Figs. 1 and 2). The proportion of soft-core genes in each Mtb genomes ranged from 69.29% (lzwG29C06) to 80.01% (J19), with a median of 79.20% (Fig. S4). The median proportion of shell genes was 20.26%, with a range of 19.27%-24.73%. Similarly, we also detected a variation in the proportion of cloud genes (median, 0.05%; IQR, 0.36%-1.20%).

Frequency distribution of gene clusters against the number of added genomes

Furthermore, the evolution of core- and pan-genome size was explored in exponential decay and growth models, respectively. The fitted core-genome curve suggested that gene size exhibited a downward trend as new genomes were added and flattened to an approximation of 1920 gene clusters (Fig. 3A). Conversely, the exponential growth model demonstrated a continuous increase in pan-genome size and no obvious plateau was observed (Fig. 3A). Moreover, we found additional new gene clusters when further genomes were added (Fig. 3B), both suggesting L4 strains were likely to have an open pan-genome.

Phylogenetic analyses and genomic similarity

Pan-genome phylogeny of Mtb L4 strains indicated tight evolutionary relationships between subtypes, where most strains clustered within same sub-lineages (Fig. 4). In addition, we compared the order and phylogenetic position of terminal branches between trees constructed using pan-genome and SNP sites (Fig. S5). We detected preserved tree topology in most clades and branches, while distinct phylogenetic positions were found for several isolates, like lzwG10C06, lzwG66B24, LD21C58X, and JK-0995. The nMS and nRF were calculated to be 0.779 and 0.824, respectively. Notwithstanding these differences, we observed generally congruent topology between pan-genome tree and SNP alignment tree, suggesting that pan-genome can offer a comprehensive and deeper resolution to ascertain the phylogenic relationships between Mtb strains.

Additionally, we calculated the ANI indicator to quantitatively assess the genomic similarity across the 127 Mtb isolates. The results indicated that the minimum ANI between genomes was 99.922%, and the maximum value equaled 99.995%. Isolates were clustered by sub-lineages, and those belonging to the same sub-lineage had greater genomic similarity (Fig. 5).

Dynamic evolution of gene families

To reconstruct ancestral state and reveal genome dynamics of Mtb L4 isolates, a phylogenetic birth-and-death model was employed. As shown in Fig. 4, the evolution of gene repertoire suggested a loss rate of 0.0112 on average, higher than gain rate (mean: 0.0039). Additionally, we observed a higher rate of both gene loss and gain for terminal branches (mean loss rate: 0.0183, mean gain rate: 0.0063), when comparing with internal nodes (mean loss rate: 0.0040, mean gain rate: 0.0014). All the gene turnover values of terminal branches were less than 1, with a range from 0.3274 to 0.3578. Interestingly, L4.5 experienced a relatively higher gene turnover rate (mean: 0.3546) versus L4.2 and L4.4 (mean: 0.3522 and 0.3521). Furthermore, pan-genome evolutionary analysis demonstrated a median gain of 6 genes and a median loss of 72 genes, respectively.

Functional annotation of pan-genome

Functional profiles of core genomes, accessory genomes, and unique genes were assigned using the KEGG pathway database, respectively (Fig. 6). Core genes were enriched in 6 major pathways, the highest frequency of which was metabolism (95.25%) involving carbohydrate (19.64%), amino acid (15.80%), energy (9.75%), and lipid (7.61%), etc. Genes within dispensable genome suggested enrichments in organismal systems (18.04%), human diseases (17.47%), and environmental information processing (14.91%), in addition to metabolism-related pathways (67.19%). Similarly, strain-specific genes were assigned to human diseases (28.57%), organismal systems (13.49%), and genetic information processing (12.70%).

Functional annotation of different pan-genome compartments using KEGG pathway database

Furthermore, we also inferred pan-genome function by searching against the COG database (Fig. S6). The results indicated 20 COG categories for core genomes, the frequencies of which from high to low were [R] General function prediction only (16.19%), [I] Lipid transport and metabolism (8.05%), [Q] Secondary metabolites biosynthesis, transport, and catabolism (7.80%), and [K] Transcription (7.68%), etc. The most frequently observed assignments for dispensable genes were [R] General function prediction only (12.69%), [N] Cell motility (8.36%), [Q] Secondary metabolites biosynthesis, transport, and catabolism (8.36%), followed by [E] Amino acid transport & metabolism (6.83%). As for strain-specific genes, the five largest COG categories were as follows: [R] General function prediction only (12.34%), [C] Energy production & conversion (9.36%), [Q] Secondary metabolites biosynthesis, transport, and catabolism (8.51%), [L] Replication, recombination, and repair (7.23%), and [I] Lipid transport and metabolism (7.23%). Intriguingly, we detected greater ratios of functional enrichment in [V] Defense mechanisms and [N] Cell motility for strain-specific genes compared to those within core genomes.

Identification of genes associated with epidemic success

THD analyses indicated a median success index of 5.67⋅10− 4 for Mtb L4 strains in eastern China (Fig. 7), with greater transmissibility detected for L4.4 (6.99⋅10− 4) and L4.5 (5.11⋅10− 4). Furthermore, we implemented pan-genome-wide association analyses to unravel the genes linked to the bacterial transmission. To reduce the probability of family wise error rate (FWER), adjusted P-values were calculated using Bonferroni’s correction method. The results suggested that the presence of five genes was correlated with the success of L4 strains, including espK, ilvD, mazF7, aftD, and ilvA, which may confer high transmission capacity (Table 1). The name and THD index of isolates having the aforementioned genes were listed in Table S3. Likewise, comparable findings regarding the relationships between Mtb success and the above identified genes were revealed via multivariate linear regression analysis (Table S4).

Discussion

This study first revealed the pan-genome composition of Mtb L4 isolates in China and provided further insights into the phylogenetic relationship between strains. Additionally, we evaluated the transmission capacity of each strain at individual level using THD method and explored relevant genes through pan-genome-wide association analyses. Comparative genomics analyses suggested a soft-core genome of 2898 genes (28.66%), fewer than that found in western Ethiopia [50] and Colombia [9], indicating relatively greater genomic variability and plasticity of L4 strains in China. Intriguingly, the size of core set reached the plateau regardless of added genomes, possibly due to its conservative role in bacterial growth, reproduction, and survival [51]. Meanwhile, the highly clonal nature of Mtb strains offers the potential of genetic variation [52], and that is why we identified the accessory genes shared by partial strains. In support of this finding, the number of predicted CDS varied across strains, similar to other species of Mycobacteria, like M.marinum [53], M.avium [54], and M.bovis [23].

Furthermore, the fitting curve showed a continued increase in pan-genome size with inputted genomes, suggesting an open pan-genome of L4 isolates. These results were in consistence with published reports by Reis et al. [23] and Hurtado-Páez et al. [9], but different from other relevant findings [55, 56], and this discrepancy may be partially associated with fewer genomes used in the current study. Of genes in pan-genome, 44.91% were assigned as cloud genes, and some isolates in this study had a higher proportion of cloud genes than others, like the strains 2018483 and 2018484. As crucial components of pan-genome, cloud and shell genes are responsible for species diversity and can offer selective advantage [57]. In this study, the observed heterogeneity in pan-genome composition can be attributed to several mechanisms, including transposable elements, copy number variations (CNV), and a high frequency of mutations during incubation period [58–60]. Furthermore, gene loss and gain events during bacterial evolution also contribute to varied pan-genome [9]. Existing evidence has underscored the genetic difference across Mtb complex in determining the mycobacterial transmission between divergent hosts, drug resistance and latency period [61]. Accordingly, genetic diversity provides an indispensable prerequisite for the survival and host adaptability of Mtb when under selection pressure exerted by external environment.

In this study, we constructed a pan-genomic phylogenetic tree of Mtb L4 isolates to evaluate their genetic relationships based on gene presence or absence of pan-genome. Phylogenetic topology showed most strains clustered within sub-lineages, demonstrating robust phylogenetic inference using genes. Likewise, the genetic affinity was further supported by ANI index between isolates. Moreover, we compared phylogenetic trees constructed using pan-genome and SNP alignment, and the results indicated generally consistent evolutionary relationships. Notably, there suggested a certain degree of dissimilarity in phylogenetic positions for some isolates. This discrepancy could be the results of the presence of accessory genes in L4 lineage strains [62]. Collectively, our work suggested that phylogenetic analyses based on pan-genome not only enable the differentiation between different lineages, but also allow for the reveal of a deeper genetic relationship between isolates.

Previous findings have suggested that bacterial competitiveness and environmental adaptability can be enhanced by gene gain and loss during evolution [63]. For Mtb L4 strains, the dynamic evolution of pan-genome was demonstrated by gene family expansion and contraction on each branch, which also suggested the variety and plasticity of gene repertoires. Additionally, gene gain and loss exhibited variations within L4 strains, with a greater rate found in terminal branches compared to internal nodes. In this study, we found that the gene loss rates of all strains were higher than gene gain rates, and the trend toward gene loss might suggest its crucial roles in adaptive evolution of L4 strains [63]. In comparison with lineage L4.2 and L4.4, the genome of L4.5 possessed a higher gene turnover rate, and the differential gene dynamics might contribute to the heterogeneity in transmission risk between sub-lineages [50]. However, given the small sample size of L4 isolates and genome-level inference, a population-based study with large samples will be necessary to verify our findings.

Functional annotation using KEGG database indicated that core genes were enriched in metabolism pathways (e.g., carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid), followed by genetic information processing. Similarly, the top COG category after [R] General function prediction only was [I] Lipid transport and metabolism. As generally known, carbohydrate metabolism and protein metabolism are essential life activities for bacterial survival and multiplication. In line with our findings on Mtb, a recent study on Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens reported the highest number of genes involving protein metabolism and carbohydrate metabolism [64]. Lipid constitutes the major component of Mtb cell membrane [65], which interfaces the cell and physical environment. Beyond its crucial roles in bacterial structure, lipid has been demonstrated to get involved in multiple biological functions, like virulence, cell invasion, and host immune system evasion [60, 66]. Contrary to core genes, the top two KEGG categories assigned to strain-specific and dispensable genes after metabolism were human diseases and organismal systems. Dispensable and unique genes are shared by partial Mtb strains, and such diversity provides possibility of strain-specific transmission attributes. While not essential for growth, partially shared and strain-specific genes offer selective benefits like adapting to distinct hosts and resisting antibiotics [57]. Previous report documented dispensable genes are correlated with adaptive functions, like virulence and pathogenesis [29]. Niemann et al. [67] and Yang et al. [68] demonstrated that Beijing lineage has greater transmissibility and virulence than non-Beijing lineage, which could be partially explained by the difference in these accessory genes. The presence or absence of dispensable and strain-specific genes might stem from the selective pressure exerted on the pathogen due to host immune response and environment [57, 59].

Pan-genome-wide association study suggested five genes were associated with a high success index of Mtb isolates. Among these genes, espK gene encodes ESX-1-associated protein EspK, the secretion of which greatly impacts the mature and function of ESX-1 protein, a major driver of Mtb virulence [69]. A recent study involving 13582 whole genome sequences has underscored the mutations in espK region significantly correlate with bacterial clustering and TB transmission [70]. Moreover, aligning to our observations, Singh et al. [71] documented the upregulated expression of ilvD gene during the early exponential phase of Mtb growth in both normal and stress conditions. MazF7 and other homologues (e.g., MazF5 and MazF6) constitute the mycobacterial toxin-antitoxin (TA) system, which plays a vital role in bacteriostasis and can render the pathogen into a dormant state under surviving stress, thereby leading to persistent Mtb infection [72, 73]. Furthermore, our work found that the presence of aftD could confer a transmission advantage of Mtb strains. aftD encodes the essential glycosyltransferase AftD to assemble glycolipids, the disrupt of which contributes to defects in cell division and impeded growth [74]. Notably, the associations with Mtb transmission were marginally significant for the aforementioned genes, our findings should be interpreted with caution and need further validation. Additionally, the transmission capacity of Mtb isolates was merely measured in statistical scenarios, necessitating additional experimental evidence to bridge this gap. Since the success of Mtb strains can be influenced by multiple factors [75], like treatment regiments, hosts’ vulnerability for TB, social-economic condition and TB control programs at local region, the links of Mtb spread with identified genes may simply reflect the local TB epidemic rather than the intrinsic properties of L4 strains.

Conclusion

In this study, we revealed the pan-genome composition, function, phylogenetic structure, and evolutionary dynamics of Mtb L4 strains from eastern China and demonstrated a high genetic diversity and variability. Pan-genome phylogeny could provide a complementary perspective to infer evolution history and disclose deeper genomic relationships between strains. Local L4 strains are undergoing changes in gene families, with a greater rate of gene loss found in these strains, suggesting gene loss might play an important role in adaptive evolution. Additionally, we found espK, ilvD, mazF7, aftD, and ilvA genes were associated with higher transmission capacity of the pathogen. However, further laboratory investigation and epidemiological surveys are necessary to confirm the association, and the generality of results should be testified in strains of other lineages.
