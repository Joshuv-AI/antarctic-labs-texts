Risk of raised posterior corneal elevation associated with cornea and sublayer thicknesses 5 years post keratorefractive lenticule extraction
Authors: Huazheng Cao, Mengyuan Shan, Shuang Liang, Guoxi Wu, Vishal Jhanji, Yan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the risk of posterior corneal forward shift associated with central corneal thickness (CCT), residual bed thickness (RBT), cap thickness and lenticule thickness after keratorefractive lenticule extraction (KLEx) over 5-year follow-up period, providing evidence for preoperative assessment to improve safety.Methods and analysisRetrospective cohort study that included patients (n=165) who underwent KLEx surgery. Changes in posterior central elevation (PCE) were measured at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year and 5 years post-KLEx. Elevation measurements were obtained at 27 prespecified points and separated into central, peripheral zones and nasal, temporal, superior and inferior quadrants for analysis. Risk ratios for raised PCE were compared across thickness groups.ResultsOverall, posterior corneas exhibited a slight forward shift over the early postoperative period and ultimately returned to their original state within 5 years. Central cornea shifted backward, whereas peripheral cornea shifted forward at each follow-up examination. Preoperative CCT had a significant effect on PCE (p<0.05), whereas cap thickness did not (p>0.05). RBT had a weakly negative correlation with PCE (β=−0.12, p=0.01), whereas lenticule thickness was positively correlated (β=0.02, p=0.03). Each 10 µm loss of CCT resulted in up to 3.5 µm forward shift of the PCE (p<0.001). CCT <500 µm increased the risk ratio of postoperatively raised PCE by 2.9–5.6 (p<0.05).ConclusionsIntended sublayer thicknesses were strongly correlated with posterior corneal shift post-KLEx. Although long-term PCE remained stable, a preoperative CCT <500 µm was associated with greater forward displacement of the posterior cornea and a higher likelihood of elevated tomographic parameters, suggesting a need for cautious preoperative assessment.Keywords: Cornea, Treatment Lasers

Introduction

Keratorefractive lenticule extraction (KLEx) is femtosecond, flap-free, minimally invasive corneal refractive surgery.1 First introduced in 2011, its safety, efficacy, predictability and stability have been well-established, and long-term effects are being continuously evaluated.2 3 Iatrogenic corneal ectasia is described as one of the most serious complications following corneal refractive surgery, characterised by morphological imbalance and protrusion.4 Compared with laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK), KLEx results in less tissue damage and preserves better corneal biomechanical stability.5 6 However, ectatic corneas after KLEx have occurred.7 While risk factors for ectasia post-LASIK have been identified, including age, low corneal thickness, high myopia, abnormal corneal morphology and insufficient residual bed thickness (RBT), further research is needed to identify long-term risk factors for ectasia following KLEx.8

Tomography is the most reliable diagnostic tool for detecting corneal ectasia, including post-refractive surgery ectasia.9 Measuring posterior corneal elevation has allowed for early ectasia detection and to evaluate post-refractive surgery corneal stability with a high specificity and efficiency.10,13 Previous studies have suggested that a forward shift of the posterior cornea after keratorefractive surgery is an important clue for corneal instability with an increased risk of ectasia.14 While some preliminary results have shown stable postoperative posterior corneal surfaces following KLEx and a correlation between the ablation ratio (lenticule thickness divided by central corneal thickness (CCT)) and posterior corneal changes,15,17 other studies have provided conflicting results.18 Due to the limited observation points, short follow-up periods, and small sample sizes, this indeterminacy has been confusing surgeons for a long time.

Unmasking the quantitative relationship between cornea and sublayer thicknesses and postoperative posterior elevation is highly valuable for clinical practice. This would provide robust evidence for preoperative risk assessment, predicting corneal stability and selecting suitable candidates for surgery. Moreover, surgeons can use this information to design safe surgeries by precisely removing and preserving corneal tissues. Our study used a large number of measuring points and a continuous long-term follow-up to investigate the 5-year changes in posterior corneal elevation after KLEx, longitudinally analyse the association between corneal sublayer thicknesses and posterior corneal stability, and evaluate the potential risk of postoperative posterior corneal forward shift in corneas with different thicknesses.

Materials and methods

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Study design and patient selection

A retrospective review of the medical records of all patients who underwent a KLEx procedure for myopia correction at our institution between August 2014 and August 2015 with 5-year follow-up data. Inclusion criteria were ≥18 years of age, corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) ≥20/25, astigmatism <−5.0 D, spherical equivalent (SE) <−10 D, RBT >250 µm and stable refraction for 2 years. Patients were advised to discontinue wearing rigid lenses for at least 4 weeks and soft contact lenses for at least 2 weeks before surgery. Exclusion criteria were the presence of previous ocular surgery, signs of suspicious keratoconus, active ocular disease and systemic diseases such as connective tissue disorders or diabetes. One eye was randomly selected from each patient for analysis. All patients provided informed consent prior to surgery.

Examinations

In all patients, a preoperative assessment was conducted, including slit-lamp examination, measurement of uncorrected and CDVA, non-contact intraocular pressure evaluation, corneal morphology scanning and manifest refraction measurement. Subsequently, follow-up assessments were performed at 3 and 6 months, and at 1 and 5 years postoperatively. Posterior corneal surface elevations were measured at each follow-up time using anterior segment tomography (Pentacam-HR, Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany). Only measurements that produced ‘OK’ statements and had no extrapolation within the central 8 mm corneal area were included for analysis.

Elevation data were collected with respect to a best-fit sphere in the central 8 mm corneal area. The preoperative best-fit sphere of each eye was used as a consistent reference surface at each follow-up visit. Posterior elevations were extracted at the central, thinnest and maximal points, as well as in eight predetermined locations on each concentric circle at 1, 2 and 3 mm from the centre. These points were distributed along eight semi-meridians (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, 180°, 225°, 270° and 315°), with the right horizontal semimeridian of the right eye defined as 0° and clockwise positive angles.

Table 1 provides the definitions for each measurement of posterior corneal elevation. The posterior average elevation (PAE) was obtained by calculating the elevation at the centre and 24 additional predetermined measurements. Elevations on each concentric ring were measured at posterior central elevation (PCE0), PCE2, PCE4 and PCE6, with eight points on each circle. These predetermined points were symmetrically divided into four quadrants: nasal and temporal, separated by using the 90°−270° meridian, and superior and inferior quadrants, separated by the 0°−180° meridian. Elevation changes (△elevation) were calculated by subtracting the baseline elevation from the postoperative one. A positive value meant a forward shift in the posterior corneal displacement.

Surgical technique

The surgical design and technical specifications are outlined in online supplemental file 1. The RBT measurements, along with the maximum lenticule thickness referred to as lenticule thickness in this study, were collected from the operation records.

Statistical analyses

A sample size of 162 eyes with a target power of 90% was reckoned to detect a 0.22 µm difference in posterior elevation at a significance level of 5%.17 The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Continuous variables with normal and non-normal distribution as well as categorical variables are displayed as mean±SD, median with IQR and frequency with percentage, respectively. Comparison of changes in posterior elevation at different time points was performed with a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Kruskal-Wallis test or one-way ANOVA was used for multiple independent comparisons, as appropriate. In different areas, quadrants and radii, average changes in posterior corneal elevations were compared with the paired t-test. In addition, generalised estimation equations were used to longitudinally analyse changes in elevations according to corneal thickness indices, and to compare changes between groups with different corneal thicknesses over time. The adjustment variables were set as age, gender, preoperative refraction, optical zone and other confounding factors. To mitigate potential multicollinearity, variables included in a single model were selected based on the variance inflation factor. The sublayer thickness, strongly correlated with posterior elevation, was assessed as both a continuous and categorical parameter. Specifically, we used the χ² test and relative risks (RR) to evaluate the impact of corneal thickness <500 µm on the risk of postoperative posterior corneal forward shift. We conducted all statistical analyses using SPSS V.23.0 (IBM), and considered a two-sided p<0.05 to be statistically significant.

Results

The study included 165 eyes (72 male and 93 female). Mean SE was −5.43±1.64 (range, −1 to −9.13) D, and CCT was ≥480 µm in all eyes. Online supplemental table 1 presents demographic and preoperative clinical information.

Varying patterns of posterior corneal elevation in time and space dimensions

Online supplemental table 2 illustrates differences in postoperative posterior corneal elevation, and figure 2A–C shows trends in △elevation over time. No significant changes over postoperative time were observed for any of the target variables on the time scale (all p>0.05). However, postoperative changes became statistically significant when compared with baseline levels, except for the △PAE at 5 years (p=0.15) and △posterior maximal elevation (PME) at 3, 6 and 12 months (p=0.19, 0.17 and 0.13, respectively). Within the 6 mm central ring, the average displacement of the posterior corneal surface was 0.26±0.76 µm at 3 months postoperatively (p<0.001), 0.30±0.87 µm at 6 months (p<0.001), 0.31±0.94 µm at 1 year (p<0.001), and 0.13±1.51 µm at 5 years (p=0.15). Specifically, posterior thinnest elevation (PTE) and PCE were significantly shifted backward at each follow-up examination (3 months: p<0.001 and p=0.001, respectively; 6 months: p<0.001 and p=0.002, respectively; 1 year: p<0.001 and p=0.001, respectively; 5 years: both p<0.001). PME also showed a backward displacement at 5 years postoperatively (p<0.001). On the spatial scale of the cornea, the central area (PCE0–2) was displaced backward, while the peripheral area (PCE4–6) was displaced forward (between-area difference, 3 months: p<0.001, p<0.001, p=0.001 and p<0.001, respectively; 6 months: p=0.002, p=0.013, p<0.001 and p<0.001, respectively; 1 year: p=0.001, p=0.013, p<0.001 and p<0.001, respectively; 5 years: p<0.001, p<0.001, p=0.017 and p<0.001, respectively). Elevations in the superior quadrant were significantly higher than those in the inferior quadrant at all time points (between-quadrant difference: p<0.001). Similarly, elevations in the temporal quadrant (TPCE) were higher than those in the nasal quadrant (NPCE) at 3 months, 6 months and 1 year (between-quadrant difference: p=0.04, 0.03 and 0.01, respectively).

Association between cornea and sublayer thicknesses and posterior corneal stability

Online supplemental table 3 shows the correlations between corneal thickness indices (CCT, RBT, cap thickness and lenticule thickness) and posterior corneal elevations. Our multivariate analysis, adjusting for variables such as age, gender, preoperative refraction, optical zone and observation time point, revealed significant correlations between CCT and changes in posterior corneal elevation, including PTE (β= –0.35, p<0.001), PAE (β= –0.04, p=0.036), PCE (β= –0.02, p=0.01), PCE2 (β = –0.01, p=0.02), PCE4 (β= –0.07, p=0.047), PCE6 (β=0.03, p=0.001) and TPCE (β = –0.06, p=0.036). However, cap thickness did not demonstrate a significant correlation with any △elevation measurements (p>0.05). Conversely, RBT was negatively correlated with △PTE (β = −0.12, p=0.01), whereas AD was positively correlated with △PTE (β=0.02, p=0.03). Our results indicate that, for every 10 µm decrease in corneal thickness, posterior corneal elevation shifted forward by an average of 0.12–3.5 µm.

Risk of raised posterior corneal elevation

Patients were divided into the following groups based on their CCT: thin cornea (≤500 µm), moderately thick cornea (501–550 µm) and thick cornea (>550 µm) (table 2). Changes in posterior corneal elevations were assessed after adjusting for confounding variables such as age, gender, optical zone, RBT and time point. The results indicated significant differences in changes in several posterior corneal elevations between groups, including PTE (p=0.001), PAE (p=0.04), PCE (p=0.002), NPCE (p=0.01), TPCE (p=0.04), PCE2 (p=0.001), PCE4 (p=0.02) and PCE6 (p<0.001). Thinner corneas were associated with greater forward displacement and increased elevation fluctuations in these measurements compared with thicker corneas. The centre of the cornea (0−2 mm zone) showed a similar trend, but an opposite trend was observed in the periphery (4−6 mm zone). Discussion

The current investigation aimed to delineate the temporal and spatial patterns of posterior corneal elevation changes following KLEx surgery by tracking alterations in three locations (central, thinnest and maximal points), on three concentric rings (1, 2 and 3 mm from the centre), and in four quadrants (nasal, temporal, superior and inferior) over a 5-year observation period. On this basis, the impact of corneal and sublayer thickness on the degree of posterior elevation shift was assessed, and the potential risk of posterior corneal forward shift following KLEx surgery in individuals with thin corneas was evaluated through intraindividual and intragroup comparisons.

In this study, we observed backward shifts in PTE (–0.99±2.71 µm) and PCE (−1.12±2.63 µm) 5 years post-KLEx, consistent with prior reports at 2 and 3 years post-KLEx.19 Kim et al20 reported a decreased mean PTE of 3.9±5.1 µm before photorefractive keratectomy to a mean PTE of 1.0±9.0 µm after surgery, while Sy et al21 found no significant difference in mean PCEs (5.06±2.29 µm vs 4.55±2.34 µm) pre-LASIK and post-LASIK, respectively. Early postoperative inflammatory responses and wound remodelling may contribute to changes in these parameters,22 while postoperative flattening of the posterior corneal surface from peripheral stromal thickening without ablation may also contribute to posterior displacement of the PCE and PTE.23 Although previous studies reported anterior protrusion after LASIK or laser-assisted subepithelial keratectomy, most described the forward elevation reported by Orbscan as a false-positive result.24 25 The 5-year change in PME (−1.54±4.40 µm) observed in the present study was consistent with the 2-year change reported by Zhou et al,19 but inconsistent with the 3-year change reported by Zhao et al26 or the 5-year change reported by Li et al.17 These contradictory results may be due to differences in the central areas analysed.

This study revealed a significantly higher elevation in the temporal quadrant than in the nasal quadrant at nearly all time points, which is consistent with previous reports.19 22 This may be related to the high incidence of astigmatism, especially in younger patients, where differences in the horizontal and vertical meridians could be a contributing factor.27 This study also observed opposite direction displacement in the central (backward) and peripheral (forward) corneas. Similar findings have been reported after LASIK,22 but opposing outcomes (forward and backward displacement in central and peripheral areas, respectively) were observed after KLEx in patients with myopia over –10 D.19 The deep ablation and thick lenticules used in correcting severe myopia may contribute to a large stromal pocket between the cap and residual, resulting in increased central posterior elevation compared with the periphery, due to higher residual tensile strength in the uncut periphery compared with the central cornea.19 Overall, the posterior cornea after KLEx demonstrated a slight anterior protrusion during the early postoperative period before reverting to its original level and remaining stable in the long term. Unlike previous studies after LASIK,22 no significant differences in posterior elevation over time were found in the present study, indicating the long-term stability of post-KLEx corneas.

In this study, a strong correlation was noted between preoperative corneal thickness and changes in posterior corneal elevation after KLEx. Specifically, for every 10 µm reduction in corneal thickness, the posterior cornea showed a forward displacement of up to 0.35 µm. Although the change seemed not clinically relevant, it would translate to a dramatic corneal morphological difference between patients with very thin and thick corneas. In such cases the possible severe protrusion is worth considering, because this linear relationship was not previously determined in literature.16 19 It has been shown that stromal strength is weakened nonlinearly with increasing depth, with the posterior-60% stroma demonstrating a weakness of over 50%.27 Therefore, the risk of ectasia in thin corneas may be higher with a deep ablation into the posterior corneal stroma. Corneal thickness <500 µm has been identified as an independent risk factor for ectasia post-LASIK.28 Even if corneal biomechanical strength is less strongly influenced by KLEx than by LASIK, caution should be exercised when considering KLEx in patients with corneal thickness <500 µm, as KLEx generally indicates a deeper stromal tissue removal.5 6 There are some claims of satisfactory results with KLEx in eyes with CCT <500 µm over a short-term observation period.29 Nevertheless, Kirmaci Kabakci et al30 still emphasised the need for more careful screening before surgery for corneas with a thickness of <500 µm. In a cohort study, a significant protrusion was noted at the PCE in thin corneas (480–499 µm).26 In this longitudinal study, thin corneas exhibited more forward displacement during the 5-year study period. A preoperative CCT<500 µm increased the potential risk of posterior corneal forward shift by a factor of 2.9–5.6 compared with thick corneas (>550 µm). This was confirmed by a few cases of post-KLEx ectasia in which a relatively insufficient preoperative corneal thickness was described as a major contributing factor.31 This evidence supports that surgeons should not be careless about laser procedures in patients with a thin cornea, instead, strict preoperative screening and diligent postoperative follow-up are recommended to monitor a long-term stability.30

In accordance with previous studies,32 33 the present study found a positive correlation between lenticule thickness and △PTE, and a negative correlation between RBT and △PTE. Li et al17 also reported that a larger lenticule-thickness-to-preoperative-CCT ratio represented a more anterior shift of PTE and PCE. However, the opposite correlation was observed 1 year after the surgery, where △PCE was positively correlated with RBT and negatively correlated with lenticule thickness.34 Reinstein et al5 reported that corneal tensile strength increased by 0.08% per μm when lenticule tissue ablation depth increased and RBT decreased. Another study reported that KLEx with greater lenticule thickness had a weaker impact on corneal biomechanics.35 Nevertheless, further research is warranted to determine the association between lenticule thickness, RBT, and corneal biomechanics.

This study is not without limitations. First, no patients with clinical ectasia were included. Being that KLEx is considered to be a relatively safe procedure with few reported cases of postoperative ectasia in the literature,7 the risk of unstable cornea after KLEx was evaluated by examining changes in postoperative posterior corneal elevation, which has been identified as a prerequisite for diagnosing ectatic disease in a global consensus.9 Nevertheless, it should be noted that an increase in posterior corneal elevation does not equate to clinical ectasia in the absence of progressive thinning and visual impairment, but is considered a tomographic risk marker for biomechanical weakening and is associated with ectasia risk in the literature.14 Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting these findings. Third, the study lacked direct biomechanical measurements, precluding a more comprehensive biomechanical interpretation of the potential risk suggested by the elevation data. Future concurrent biomechanical data would strengthen the correlation between the observed morphological shifts and actual corneal stiffness or resistance.

Overall, this study confirmed the long-term tomographic stability of the posterior cornea following KLEx in a myopic population, with no cases of clinical ectasia observed over 5 years. Although slight protrusions were observed in the early postoperative period and peaked at 1 year, they have reverted to baseline within 5 years postoperatively. Preoperative CCT was strongly correlated with changes in posterior corneal surface elevation, with more forward displacement observed in thin corneas (CCT <500 µm) and a correspondingly higher risk of raised posterior corneal elevation (by a factor of 2.8–5.6). These findings suggest that extra care may be needed when performing corneal refractive surgery on thin corneas.
