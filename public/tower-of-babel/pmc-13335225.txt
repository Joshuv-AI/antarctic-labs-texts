Effects of a community-based structured group exercise program on cognitive and physical function among older adults with dementia: a randomized controlled trial study protocol
Authors: Al Amin, Kazi Md Azman Hossain, Mohammad Ramiz Uddin, Suraiya Yesmin Sharna, Mehedi Hasan Badsha, Sabuj Biswas, Md. Feroz Kabir, K M Amran Hossain, Sharmila Jahan, Ehsanur Rahman, Md. Kabir Hossain, Md. Zahid Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDementia is a neurocognitive disorder that involves deterioration in cognitive and daily functioning, leading to loss of independence and reduced quality of life. While pharmacological treatments offer limited symptomatic relief, non-pharmacological approaches such as exercise have shown promise in improving cognitive and functional outcomes. However, evidence from community-based structured exercise interventions remains limited, particularly in low-resource settings. This trial aims to evaluate the effects of a community-based structured group exercise program on cognitive and physical function among older adults with dementia.MethodsThis assessor-blinded, community-based randomized controlled trial will be conducted from February to September 2026 in Bangladesh. A total of 120 older adults diagnosed with dementia will be randomly allocated (1:1) to either the experimental group (structured group exercise program with healthy lifestyle advice) or the control group (healthy lifestyle advice alone). The intervention will consist of supervised sessions three times a week for 12 weeks, followed by a 12-week follow-up period. The outcome will be measured on both cognitive and physical function bases. Cognitive function will be evaluated using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination Revised (ACE-R), Trail Making Test (TMT) A–B, and Digit Span Test (DST). Physical function will be measured using the Senior Fitness Test (SFT), Berg Balance Scale (BBS), Timed Up and Go (TUG) Test, and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living scale (IADLs). All outcomes will be measured at baseline, posttest (12 weeks), and follow-up period (12 weeks). Data will be analyzed using SPSS following the intention-to-treat principle.DiscussionThe findings are expected to support the integration of physiotherapist-led, community-based exercise into dementia care, offering a feasible, safe, and cost-effective approach for improving cognitive and physical function in older adults with dementia. Despite potential limitations in generalizability, the study’s community implementation and follow-up design will contribute valuable insights into sustainable rehabilitation practices in resource-limited settings.Trial registrationThis trial is registered prospectively in the Clinical Trial Registry India; ID: CTRI/2025/10/096618; Registered on: 29/10/2025. Introduction

Dementia, also known as major neurocognitive disorder in the DSM-5, is a clinical syndrome characterized by a significant decline in one or more cognitive domains—such as memory, executive function, attention, language, or visuospatial skills—along with functional impairment that disrupts independence in daily activities [1]. It is not a single disease but an umbrella term encompassing a range of conditions with heterogeneous etiologies, most commonly neurodegenerative processes (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia) and vascular pathology (e.g., vascular dementia), resulting in varied clinical presentations and trajectories. Consequently, cognitive decline is not uniform: some forms initially present with memory impairment, while others predominantly affect attention, executive function, visuospatial skills, language, or behavior [2, 3]. Dementia represents a major and growing global public health challenge: an estimated 57.4 million people were living with dementia in 2019, a figure projected to rise to 152.8 million by 2050, driven largely by population aging, particularly in low- and middle-income countries with constrained healthcare resources [3]. In Bangladesh, approximately 0.6 million individuals are currently affected, with substantial increases anticipated in the coming decades [4]. These trends underscore the urgent need for scalable, sustainable strategies to reduce functional decline and support long-term care for affected individuals in community settings.

Current clinical management of dementia mainly emphasizes symptomatic pharmacological treatments, which offer modest improvements in cognitive or neuropsychiatric symptoms but do not change the underlying neurodegenerative processes [2]. For example, cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil and rivastigmine are frequently prescribed to treat cognitive symptoms linked to Alzheimer’s disease, while dopaminergic agents like levodopa may be used to manage motor and related cognitive symptoms in Parkinson’s disease dementia [2, 5]. Pharmacological treatment of behavioral and mood symptoms often involves antidepressants, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors like citalopram [6]. Although these medications can provide symptomatic relief for some individuals, their effectiveness varies among patients and does not halt disease progression [5]. Additionally, pharmacological management of behavioral disturbances—especially the use of antipsychotic or sedative medications—has been linked to significant adverse effects in older adults, such as increased risks of sedation, falls, cerebrovascular events, and death. As a result, there is growing interest in non-pharmacological approaches that may enhance cognitive function, physical health, and quality of life for people living with dementia [2, 5, 6].

Physical exercise is increasingly recognized as a non-pharmacological approach with potential benefits for dementia, supported by experimental and clinical evidence linking it to improved physical performance, mobility, and independence [6, 7]. Mechanistic studies suggest that exercise may exert neurobiological effects through increased cerebral blood flow, enhanced neurotrophic signaling (including brain-derived neurotrophic factor), modulation of synaptic plasticity, and reduced neuroinflammation—pathways that likely contribute to the observed cognitive and functional improvements [8, 9]. Evidence from randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses indicates that structured exercise programs—especially multicomponent plans combining aerobic, strengthening, and flexibility exercises—can produce modest gains in overall cognition, physical ability, and daily living activities [8, 10]. However, the effect size and the cognitive responses vary across studies due to differences in intervention design, intensity, duration, and methodology. Additionally, most existing research is limited by being conducted mainly in institutional or high-income settings, with fewer studies on community-based programs and low- and middle-income populations, where healthcare resources and implementation environments differ significantly [8–10]. Filling these gaps is crucial for understanding the feasibility and effectiveness of scalable exercise programs in real-world community settings and for clarifying their role in comprehensive dementia care.

Therefore, this randomized controlled trial aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a multicomponent structured group exercise program on cognitive and physical functions in community-dwelling older adults with dementia. This study implements a physiotherapist-led program that includes joint mobility, aerobic, strengthening, stretching, and breathing exercises within a structured community-based setting. Given the multidimensional nature of dementia and the intervention’s integrated design, cognitive and physical/functional outcomes will be evaluated with equal priority rather than categorized as primary or secondary outcomes. We hypothesize that participants who receive the exercise-based intervention will experience greater improvements or slower declines in cognitive and physical function than those who receive only healthy lifestyle advice.

Methods

Study design

This study will be an assessor-blinded community-based randomized controlled trial, taking place from February to September 2026. There will be a 1:1 split of eligible participants into two groups: either the experimental group (structured group exercise program with healthy lifestyle advice: Group A) or the control group (healthy lifestyle advice alone: Group B). Each group intervention will last 12 weeks, consisting of three sessions per week, followed by a 12-week follow-up period. Cognitive and physical functional measurements will be measured at three time points. Logistical support will be provided by the Department of Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation at Jashore University of Science and Technology, as well as by the designated community clinics serving as study sites. To ensure transparency and completeness of reporting, the study will follow the 2013 SPIRIT guidelines (see Table 1). Additionally, the CONSORT statement guidelines will be used to confirm transparent reporting of the findings (see Fig. 1).

Study settings

This study will be employed at the community level in six designated areas near local community clinics in Jashore, Bangladesh. Participants meeting the criteria will be assigned to either the experimental or control group at the study site.

Sample size calculation

To determine the sample size for this study, we used G*power 3.1.9.7 and set α = 0.05 with a power (1 - β) = 0.80. Although this study has no definitive primary or secondary outcomes, we calculated the effect size using the most relevant cognition-based measure, MMSE, drawing from prior multicomponent intervention research that reported an effect size of 0.56 [10]. This led us to conclude that including at least 52 participants in each group, for a total of 104 participants, was necessary. Therefore, after accounting for a 15% dropout rate, we targeted a total of 120 participants, with 60 in each group, for this study.

Participants and recruitment

A total of 120 older adults diagnosed with dementia will be recruited from surrounding communities through online and offline advertisements, including social media, flyers, posters, leaflets, and announcements in religious institutions within designated areas near local community clinics of Jashore, Bangladesh. Recruitment activities will begin with advertisements during the first 15 days of February, followed by 12 community-based offline seminars during the remaining days of the month to raise awareness and enroll eligible participants. No compensation will be provided to the study participants. The intervention is scheduled from March 1 to June 30, after which follow-up assessments will be conducted approximately 12 weeks following the post-intervention evaluation. Given the large community catchment area, the study is expected to be completed by September without requiring timeline extensions. A trained research assistant will screen participants for dementia and eligibility, and written informed consent will be obtained from each participant and their caregiver/legal guardian prior to enrollment. Additional consent from physician will be required for participants with unstable medical conditions, recent hospitalization, or comorbidities that may pose potential risks during exercise participation, to ensure safety and medical clearance. Participants can withdraw at any time without affecting their care. After baseline assessment, they will be randomly and confidentially assigned to one of two groups. During the consent process, participants will be informed about the interventions the groups may receive, but this will depend entirely on the randomization process and will not be based on the participants’ choices.

Eligibility criteria

Trained research assistants under the guidance of healthcare professionals will assess eligibility criteria at the designated community area for recruiting a total of 120 participants.

Inclusion criteria

Participants will be included if: (1) they are community-dwelling males and females aged between 60 and 80 years; (2) they have a clinically confirmed prior diagnosis of dementia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-V) by health professionals [11]; (3) they have a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score between 10 and 24 (exclusive); (4) they are able to sit on a chair and walk 10 feet (3.05 m) without physical assistance [4, 12]; (5) able to carry out a structured exercise program based on the intervention protocol (6) participants and caregiver/legal guardians consent to participate in the intervention and assessments of this study.

Exclusion criteria

Participants will be excluded if: (1) they have serious neurological disorders, severe psychiatric conditions, uncontrolled arterial hypertension, or severe cardiorespiratory or musculoskeletal problems that prevent safe participation in exercise; (2) they have been diagnosed with cancer or have undergone major surgery in the past year; (3) they have severe vision or hearing impairments that interfere with communication or assessment; (4) they have a history of alcohol or drug abuse [12].

Randomization and blinding

Participants will be randomly assigned to either the experimental group or control group using a computer-generated block randomization sequence in a 1:1 ratio, ensuring balanced group sizes throughout the enrollment period. Allocation concealment will be maintained by independent personnel who will generate the randomization sequence and remain uninvolved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery, or outcome assessment. Each participant will receive a unique identification number and randomization code at enrollment, and group assignment will only be revealed after baseline assessments are completed. To ensure blinding of outcome assessors, a project manager will provide alphanumeric codes for participants, masking group allocation throughout the data collection process. Intervention providers (physiotherapists) will be aware of group allocation due to the nature of the exercise program. Participants will be informed about the intervention details and variations in two groups, as this study will be conducted at the community level, making it impossible to keep the participants blind. But all assessors measuring outcomes and the data analyst will remain blinded to participant groupings. This procedure will minimize bias and ensure that participant assessment and statistical evaluation are conducted in a blinded and impartial manner.

Interventions

Both groups of participants will follow a 12-week protocol specific to their assigned group. All participants will receive healthy lifestyle advice over the 12-week period, including educational sessions with weekly talks on diet, hydration, sleep, and healthy ageing. A printed booklet with healthy lifestyle advice will be provided. Additionally, participants in the experimental group will undergo an intervention that includes joint mobility, aerobic, strengthening, stretching, and breathing exercises. The intervention will be delivered by 12 experienced physiotherapists, each with at least 3 years of clinical experience. Participants will be treated in small groups of four to five, with sessions conducted three times per week. Community-based structured group exercise programs are outlined in Table 2, including dosage and progression [12–15]. Control group participants will only receive brief healthy lifestyle advice and will not participate in any structured exercise programs. They will attend biweekly sessions at the respective study sites, with each session lasting approximately 45 min to 1 h. During these sessions, participants will receive standardized healthy lifestyle advice and will have the opportunity to discuss feedback, concerns, or issues related to the study. Each session will be facilitated by a responsible physiotherapist at each site. However, they will have the opportunity to access the designed exercise program after completing the study. Although the intervention will be delivered in small groups, randomization will be performed at the individual level. Grouping is solely for logistical delivery and does not define the unit of allocation. The principal investigator and four research assistants will monitor and assess adherence to the study protocol during each session, but will not be directly involved in delivering the intervention.

Progression of interventions

Participants’ initial group exercise program will last more than an hour, and subsequent sessions will be available three days a week, each lasting 45 min to one hour. In practice, the frequency and intensity of exercise programs may vary depending on the individual’s circumstances or health conditions. To ensure standardized monitoring, trained research assistants will assess and record each session using a structured checklist. Frequency will be documented as the number of prescribed sets and repetitions completed for each exercise. Intensity will be recorded based on participant-reported rating of perceived exertion along with observable effort during performance. Duration will be measured as the total time actively engaged in the exercise session. Participants will be advised to avoid additional unsupervised exercise during the intervention period. Any extra physical activity will be systematically recorded at each session through participant self-report, including type, frequency, and approximate duration, and documented on a separate structured sheet by the research assistant.

Outcome measurements

Socio-demographic information will be collected at a single time point at baseline. The socio-demographic-related information will include age, gender, body mass index (BMI), presence of comorbidities as measured by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), previous fall history, smoking history, and regular physical activity level. Outcomes will be assessed across both cognitive and physical function domains, without distinguishing between primary and secondary outcomes, to provide more comprehensive evidence of the interventions’ effectiveness; it will be collected at three time points: baseline, posttest (12 weeks), and follow-up period (12 weeks) by trained, blind assessors. Cognitive and physical functional abilities will be assessed using validated tools to determine the effectiveness of the intervention.

Cognitive function

Mini-mental state examination (MMSE)

Cognitive function will be assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), a widely used screening tool. The MMSE includes 30 items across five domains: orientation, attention and concentration, memory, language, and visuospatial skills. Each correct answer scores 1 point, with a maximum of 30 points. Higher scores indicate better cognitive function. The assessment is administered verbally and typically takes 5–10 min. It is regarded as reliable, with Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) values ranging from 0.83 to 0.99 [16, 17].

Addenbrooke’s cognitive examination-revised (ACE-R)

Cognitive function will be assessed using the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-Revised (ACE-R), a brief screening tool for cognition. The ACE-R evaluates five domains: attention/orientation (18 points), memory (26 points), verbal fluency (14 points), language (26 points), and visuospatial ability (16 points), with a total score of up to 100. Higher scores indicate better cognitive performance. The assessment will be conducted orally and is expected to take approximately 12–20 min (average 16 min). It has high reliability, with ICC values between 0.88 and 0.97 [16, 18].

Trail making test (TMT) A–B

Cognitive function will be evaluated using the Trail Making Test (TMT), which measures attention, processing speed, and executive function. TMT-A involves connecting 25 numbered dots in order, while TMT-B requires alternating between numbers and letters (1, A, 2, B, etc.). Both parts should be completed as quickly and accurately as possible. The test will be stopped after 5 min if not finished. The completion time will be recorded, with shorter times indicating better performance. The TMT shows good reliability, with ICC values ranging from 0.79 to 0.89 [16, 19].

Digit span test (DST)

Attention and working memory will be evaluated through the Digit Span Test (DST), which includes forward (DST-F) and backward (DST-B) sequences. The participant will repeat progressively longer digit sequences presented by the examiner. The test ends when the participant fails a sequence or reaches the maximum length. Scoring: 2 points for correctly repeating both sequences at a given level, 1 point for one correct, and 0 for none. Total scores will be summed, with higher scores indicating better attention and working memory. The DST is reliable, with ICC values of 0.80–0.88 [16, 20].

Physical function

Senior fitness test (SFT)

Physical function will be assessed using the Senior Fitness Test (SFT), which measures strength, endurance, balance, agility, and flexibility through six tasks. The Arm Curl uses 2 kg for women and 3.5 kg for men; flexibility will be measured in centimeters. The process may take approximately 30–40 min. The SFT is simple to perform, requires no specialized equipment, and is reliable for older adults with or without cognitive impairment. ICC = 0.93–0.99 [16, 21].

Berg balance scale (BBS)

Balance will be assessed using the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), which comprises 14 functional tasks scored from 0 to 4 (total score 0–56). Scores below 45 indicate a higher risk of falling. Higher scores reflect better balance and independence. The assessment typically takes 15–20 min, requires no special equipment, and has been shown to be highly reliable in older adults, including those with cognitive impairment. ICC = 0.98 [16, 22].

Timed up and go (TUG) test

Mobility and functional performance will be evaluated using the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test. The participant will rise from a chair, walk 3 m, turn around, return, and sit down. The time taken will be measured in seconds; shorter times indicate better mobility. The TUG is quick (~ 3–5 min), easy to administer, and reliable (ICC = 0.87–0.99) in older adults, including those with dementia [23].

Instrumental activities of daily living scale (IADLs)

Independent living skills will be assessed through the IADLs, covering eight areas: telephone use, shopping, food preparation, housekeeping, laundry, transportation, medication management, and finances. All participants will be scored in all areas (0–8). Each area scores 1 if performed independently, 0 if assistance is required. Higher scores reflect greater independence. The assessment takes approximately 10–15 min and is reliable for use with older adults, including those with or without cognitive impairment. ICC = 0.84–0.91 [16, 24].

Follow-up phase

After 12 weeks of study enrollment and post-intervention assessments, participants will be encouraged to maintain a healthy lifestyle as previously advised. And no exercise will be advised for home use. If someone wants to perform, they can do an aerobic workout, such as walking or a previous workout they are familiar with, but this will be tracked using a tracker sheet given to all participants after post-intervention assessments. Peer support, interactive health education, and individual feedback will be provided monthly during discussion sessions to boost motivation and address common physical activity challenges. After 12 weeks of follow-up, a final assessment will be conducted to determine the intervention’s long-term effects.

Data management and monitoring

Data authenticity will be maintained through regular record verification for accuracy and completeness, with all details double-checked by assessors. The final trial dataset will be reviewed and confirmed by assessors and made accessible to the trial manager, principal investigator, and data monitoring personnel. All data will be de-identified using unique participant codes, with any corresponding identifying information stored separately in a secure, encrypted departmental database. Both hard and soft copies of the data will be securely retained by the principal investigator, with access limited to authorized personnel only. Statistical analyses will be conducted on anonymized data, and results will be reported collectively to preserve confidentiality. Double manual data entry will be performed to minimize entry errors. Three independent, non-trial staff will monitor intervention adherence, adverse events, and participant enrolment, and will also perform preliminary data checks in each session. Any protocol modifications or adverse findings will be promptly reported to the ethical review board.

Statistical analysis

All data will be recorded and analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A blinded statistician will conduct the analysis to minimize bias. Data normality will be assessed through visual inspection of histograms and bell curve patterns, as well as the statistical Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD for continuous data and frequency/percentage for categorical data) will summarize baseline characteristics. Between-group comparisons will be performed using the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, while within-group changes will be analyzed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Repeated measures ANOVA will be used to assess time and group-by-time effects for normally distributed data. If normality assumptions are violated, the non-parametric Friedman test will be used as an alternative. To account for potential intra-group correlation arising from group-based intervention delivery, mixed-effects models with random effects for exercise group will be used for primary analyses. All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, with missing data addressed using appropriate imputation methods assuming data are missing at random. No interim analysis was planned for this study. Statistical significance will be set at p < 0.05.

Safety measures and adverse effects management

Although no major adverse effects are anticipated from the intervention, potential minor consequences may occur among the older population involved. Monitoring staff will closely observe participants during and after the intervention for any unexpected events and promptly inform the appropriate healthcare professionals if necessary. Any adverse events will be documented in the treating physiotherapist’s SOAP notes and reported to the principal investigator. In cases of pain, irritation, or discomfort, participants will be advised to contact their physiotherapist immediately. If any serious adverse events occur, detailed information will be recorded and addressed before continuing the trial. However, due to the vulnerability of older people, two healthcare professionals will be available to provide emergency medical services or other healthcare services. Any protocol modifications made in response to such events will be reported by the principal investigator to the ethical review board.

Study status

The current protocol is version 1.0, dated January 31, 2026. Participant enrolment and intervention are scheduled to begin in February 2026, subject to the established eligibility criteria. The intervention, post-assessments, and follow-up evaluations are expected to be completed by September 2026.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial aims to determine whether a structured, physiotherapist-led group exercise program can slow or improve decline in cognitive and physical functions among community-dwelling older adults with dementia. The intervention combines joint mobility, aerobic, strengthening, stretching, and breathing exercises conducted in supervised group sessions over 12 weeks, followed by a post-intervention period to assess the longevity of potential benefits. The main hypothesis is that regular participation in this program will result in measurable improvements in overall cognitive performance and gains in physical capacity, mobility, balance, and independence in daily activities.

From a cognitive standpoint, the intervention is expected to impact several areas commonly affected in dementia. Improvements may be seen in overall cognitive function, as measured by standardized assessments such as the Mini-Mental State Examination and Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-Revised, indicating changes in orientation, memory, and language skills. Additionally, exercise-triggered neuroplastic mechanisms—including increased cerebral blood flow, enhanced neurotrophic signaling, and better synaptic connectivity—may especially support executive functions and attentional control [8, 9]. As a result, domains evaluated through the Trail Making Test and Digit Span Test, which assess executive function, processing speed, and working memory, might show noticeable improvement or stabilization compared to the control group. Importantly, in chronic neurodegenerative diseases like dementia, maintaining cognitive abilities or reducing the rate of decline can be a significant clinical outcome, even if large improvements are not evident [25].

Physical outcomes form another key focus of the intervention. Multicomponent exercise programs have reliably shown benefits for muscle strength, balance, mobility, and functional endurance in older adults, including those with cognitive impairment [10]. As a result, this intervention is expected to improve multiple physical areas assessed in this study, such as functional strength and endurance (Senior Fitness Test), postural stability (Berg Balance Scale), mobility and dynamic balance (Timed Up and Go test), and independence in complex daily tasks (Instrumental Activities of Daily Living scale). Improvements in these areas are especially important for individuals with dementia because functional decline is closely linked to higher fall risk, institutionalization, and lower quality of life [9]. Even modest gains in physical function may therefore lead to meaningful improvements in independence and safety within the community.

The current protocol aims to address several gaps in existing evidence. First, the intervention is based on a multicomponent exercise program that uses progressive intensity and multiple training components targeting both neuromuscular and cardiovascular systems. Second, the study evaluates multiple cognitive domains simultaneously instead of relying solely on global cognitive screening tools, providing a more comprehensive assessment of cognitive changes. Third, including both physical performance and functional independence outcomes allows for examining the wider clinical significance of exercise interventions in dementia care. Lastly, the follow-up assessment offers an opportunity to determine whether any observed benefits last beyond the active intervention period, an area that has been insufficiently explored in many previous trials.

Another key contribution of this study concerns its implementation model. Many exercise trials for dementia have been conducted in clinical or institutional settings, which may limit scalability and accessibility [8–10]. In contrast, this study assesses a community-based group exercise program, delivered in small groups under physiotherapist supervision. This approach reflects real-world rehabilitation conditions and could provide a practical framework for integrating non-pharmacological interventions into community health services. If proven effective, such programs could be a cost-efficient way to promote healthy aging and maintain functional independence among older adults with dementia, especially in resource-limited healthcare systems. The study also offers methodological contributions by implementing rigorous randomized allocation, assessor blinding, standardized outcome measurement, and intention-to-treat analysis. These design features strengthen internal validity and reduce potential bias in outcome evaluation. Moreover, the use of multiple validated assessment tools across cognitive and physical domains enables a multidimensional evaluation of intervention effects, providing richer evidence regarding the potential mechanisms and clinical benefits of exercise in dementia populations.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this trial. First, the study is conducted in a limited number of community settings within a single geographic region, which may limit how well the results apply to other populations or healthcare contexts. Second, adherence to exercise programs may be difficult for individuals with cognitive impairment, and participant retention during the intervention and follow-up periods may affect study outcomes. Third, although the 12-week follow-up period provides a preliminary view of sustained effects, longer follow-up periods are necessary to determine whether exercise interventions can impact long-term disease progression or functional changes. Finally, since participants cannot be blinded to exercise participation, there remains a potential risk of performance bias despite the assessor and statistician being blinded.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths that boost its potential impact on dementia research and rehabilitation practice. If the expected benefits are confirmed, the findings could have significant implications for clinical practice and public health. Physiotherapist-led exercise programs might be integrated into community rehabilitation services as part of a comprehensive, non-pharmacological management strategy for dementia. Such interventions may help sustain cognitive engagement, improve mobility and functional independence, and potentially lower caregiver burden and healthcare costs related to disability and institutional care.

Future research should extend these findings by exploring longer intervention durations, larger multicenter trials, and comparative analyses of different exercise modalities and intensities. Additionally, integrating behavioral, neurobiological, and digital monitoring approaches may help clarify the mechanisms through which physical activity influences cognitive trajectories in dementia. Ultimately, establishing scalable and evidence-based exercise models could play an important role in improving the quality of life and functional capacity for the growing population of older adults living with dementia.
