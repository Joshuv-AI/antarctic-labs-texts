The aging epigenome: integrative analyses reveal intersection with Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Wei Zhang, David Lukacsovich, Juan I Young, Lissette Gomez, Michael A Schmidt, Brian W Kunkle, Xi Chen, Eden R Martin, Lily Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging is the strongest risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease (AD), yet the role of age-associated DNA methylation (DNAm) changes in blood and their relevance to AD remains poorly understood. We performed a meta-analysis of blood DNAm samples from 475 dementia-free subjects aged over 65 years across two independent cohorts, the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) at Exam 9 and the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). We adjusted for sex and immune cell-type proportions and corrected batch effects and genomic inflation. Integrative analyses included pathway enrichment, mQTL analysis, colocalization with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia (ADRD) GWAS summary statistics, brain-blood DNAm correlations, and comparison to independent AD methylation studies. We identified 3758 CpGs and 556 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) consistently associated with chronological age in both cohorts at a 5% false discovery rate. Our pathway enrichment analyses highlighted metabolic regulation and synaptic signaling, processes previously implicated in Alzheimer’s disease. Colocalization with ADRD GWAS summary statistics identified 32 genomic regions consistent with shared genetic signals for DNAm and ADRD risk. Roughly one-third of aging-associated CpGs overlapped CpGs associated with AD or AD neuropathology in external studies. Finally, we prioritized nine promoter CpGs (including those located in PDE1B, ELOVL2, and PODXL2) showing strong positive blood-to-brain methylation concordance and external AD associations, nominating them as candidate blood-based biomarkers. Our study demonstrated that late-life aging signatures in blood DNAm converge on processes implicated in AD and intersect with dementia genetics. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia worldwide; it already affects more than 6 million people in the USA alone; its prevalence is projected to grow to 13.8 million by 2060 as the population ages [1]. Chronological age remains the single strongest risk factor, with the majority of cases occurring after age 65. Understanding the molecular changes that accompany “normal” aging, and how they intersect with pathologic processes that culminate in AD, is therefore central to both risk stratification and early-intervention strategies.

Alterations to the epigenome, particularly DNA methylation (DNAm), accumulate throughout life and can influence transcriptional programs that underlie immune, metabolic, and neurodegenerative pathways [2]. Multi-tissue “epigenetic clocks” such as Horvath’s 353-CpG model demonstrate that a subset of age-related DNAm changes can accurately predict chronological age across diverse tissues. Deviations from predicted age (age acceleration) and other DNAm-derived pace-of-aging measures have been evaluated for associations with cognitive impairment and dementia, including analyses in ADNI and FHS (e.g., Sugden et al., 2022 [3]). In parallel, large-scale blood epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) have identified thousands of CpG sites and differentially methylated regions (DMRs) that show reproducible age-dependent shifts [4–7]. Growing literature also links DNAm signatures to both incident dementia (including our study [8]) and early alterations in AD neuropathology [9]. However, the specific CpGs/DMRs that reproducibly define late-life blood methylation changes with aging, and the extent to which these loci intersect with AD-related epigenetic findings, remain incompletely characterized, because most AD EWAS treat age as a confounder rather than primary variable of interest.

Prior clock-based analyses in ADNI and FHS evaluated whether a single DNAm-derived aging summary (e.g., age acceleration or pace-of-aging) is associated with cognitive impairment or dementia. In contrast, our goal is to map the reproducible CpG- and region-level late-life blood aging landscape across two cohorts and then interpret those loci through integrative analyses to identify aging-associated DNAm changes most relevant to AD biology and biomarker development.

Several recent studies have explored the molecular intersections of aging and AD. In the temporal cortex, age‑related and AD‑associated transcriptional shifts show extensive concordance, with most genes changing in the same direction [10]. Meng et al. [11] further demonstrated that epigenomic aging signatures across multiple brain regions converge with AD‑responsive genes in immune and developmental pathways [11]. In Li et al. [12], genome‑wide blood DNAm profiles associated with aging were systematically compared to brain DNAm profiles in AD, and the authors provided the first evidence that peripheral epigenetic aging signatures partially overlap with AD‑associated changes in the brain.

However, these previous studies have used DNAm samples measured by the older 27 k/450 k Illumina arrays and did not directly compare age-associated blood DNAm with AD-associated blood DNAm. In addition, DNAm-to-aging associations vary across the life course, with dementia onset typically occurs during late life. Most published blood DNAm aging EWAS and meta-analyses include participants across adulthood (including younger and middle-life ages) [4, 13, 14]. Thus, some of the association signals identified in these aging studies with wide age ranges may reflect developmental or mid-life aging patterns rather than late-life biology most relevant to dementia. Finally, as dementia has a long preclinical phase and is frequently underreported, it is important to carefully select samples to exclude subjects who later develop dementia.

To address these gaps, we performed a comprehensive analysis to examine aging-associated blood DNAm and its implications for dementia, by analyzing previously generated DNAm data measured in blood from 475 adults older than 65 years. Because our objective is to characterize DNAm differences associated with aging in late life, we restricted our analyses to participants aged ≥ 65 years in ADNI and FHS. These DNAm data were previously generated by the parent studies, the Framingham Heart Study (Exam 9) and the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), using the Illumina HumanMethylation EPIC BeadChip (> 850,000 CpGs) [15]. To ensure that the observed changes represent DNAm in normal aging, we excluded participants who developed dementia during follow-up.

We then performed a meta-analysis to identify age-associated CpGs and DMRs, mapped their genomic distributions, and evaluated their transcriptional and functional relevance by integrative eQTm (DNAm-to-gene expression associations), brain-to-blood correlations in DNA methylation levels, and pathway analyses. In addition, we explored shared genetic architecture of the aging processes and dementia by integrating our results with large-scale mQTL resources and genome-wide association summary statistics from recent dementia GWAS and performing colocalization analysis. Finally, we compared our findings with independent AD methylation studies external to FHS and ADNI datasets. An overview of the analysis workflow is shown in Supplementary Figure 1.

Methods

Study cohorts

Our meta-analysis included 475 participants from two cohorts: 282 individuals from the FHS Offspring cohort at Exam 9 (FHS9) and 193 individuals from the ADNI. For both studies, we included samples from self-reported non-Hispanic white individuals over 65 years of age. In ADNI, we analyzed each participant’s earliest visit with available DNAm data. In FHS9, to avoid inflation due to family structure, we selected only one individual per family, prioritizing the sample with the highest bisulfite conversion rate.

The participants were followed for up to 11.11 and 7.72 years after their blood draw in ADNI and FHS9, respectively. We excluded individuals who had dementia at baseline or developed it during follow-up to minimize reverse causation from early AD-related changes, which can precede clinical onset by many years and alter peripheral immune and metabolic biology detectable in blood DNA methylation. This exclusion helps to ensure that the observed methylation changes reflect normal aging rather than pre-symptomatic neurodegenerative processes.

Pre-processing of DNA methylation data

This study did not generate new DNA methylation data. Illumina EPIC array DNAm data were previously generated by FHS and ADNI and obtained through controlled-access repositories (ADNI portal and dbGaP) for secondary analysis. Supplementary Table 1 shows the number of CpGs and samples at each quality control (QC) step. The pre-processing procedures were previously described elsewhere [8]. Briefly, for each dataset, the QC of probes involved selecting probes with a detection P-value < 0.01 in 90% or more of the samples, probes that start with “cg,” and removing probes that are located on X and Y chromosomes, are cross-reactive [16], or located close to single nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs). The QC of samples included removing samples with bisulfite conversion rates lower than 85%, as well as samples for which the DNAm predicted sex status differed from the recorded sex status. In addition, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) using the 50,000 most variable CpGs to identify outliers. Samples outside the range of ± 3 standard deviations from the mean of PC1 and PC2 were excluded. The quality-controlled data was next normalized using the dasen method, as implemented in the wateRmelon R package [17]. To correct batch effects from methylation plates, we used the BEclear R package [18].

Immune cell-type proportions (B lymphocytes, natural killer cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, monocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils) were estimated using the EpiDISH R package [19]. Granulocyte proportions were computed as the sum of neutrophil and eosinophil proportions [20–22], since both neutrophils and eosinophils are classified as granular leukocytes.

Association of DNA methylation at individual CpGs with chronological age

For each dataset, the association between CpG methylation levels and chronological age at blood draw was assessed using linear statistical models. Given that methylation M-values (logit transformation of methylation beta values) have better statistical properties (i.e., homoscedasticity) for linear regression models [23], we used the M-values as the outcome variable in our statistical models. For both the ADNI and FHS9 datasets, we adjusted for potential confounding factors including sex and estimated major immune cell-type proportions in the samples. The linear model we used is methylation M-value ~ age + sex + immune cell-type proportions (B, NK, CD4T, Mono, Gran).

Inflation assessment and correction

Genomic inflation factors (lambda values) were estimated using both the conventional approach [24] and the bacon method [25], which was proposed specifically for EWAS. Using the conventional approach, the estimated λ values were 1.097 for ADNI and 1.229 for FHS9. The inflation factors estimated by the bacon approach (λ.bacon) were 1.033 and 1.136 for the ADNI and FHS9 datasets, respectively. The estimated bias from the bacon method was −0.048 for ADNI and −0.044 for FHS9.

After genomic correction using the bacon method [25], as implemented in the bacon R package, the estimated bias was −1.35 × 10−4 and 4.70 × 10−4, and the estimated inflation factors were λ = 1.049 and 1.063, and λ.bacon = 1.002 and 1.006 for the ADNI and FHS9 datasets, respectively. The bacon method was then used to compute bacon-corrected effect sizes, standard errors, and P-values for each dataset.

Meta-analysis

To meta-analyze individual CpG results across both the FHS9 and ADNI datasets, we used the inverse-variance weighted fixed-effects model [26], as implemented in the meta R package. As demonstrated by Rice et al. [26], the fixed effects model can be interpreted as a weighted average of study-specific effects, regardless of whether the true study-specific effects are heterogeneous [26]. To correct for multiple comparisons, we computed the false discovery rate (FDR). We considered CpGs with an FDR less than 5% in the meta-analysis of the FHS9 and ADNI datasets, with a consistent direction of change in estimated effect sizes in both cohorts, and a nominal meta-analysis P-value less than 1 × 10−5 to be statistically significant.

Differentially methylated regions analysis

To identify differentially methylated regions (DMRs) significantly associated with chronological age, we used two analytical approaches, the comb-p [27] approach and the coMethDMR [28] approach, and selected significant DMRs identified by both methods. Briefly, comb-p takes single CpG P-values and locations of CpG sites to scan the genome for regions enriched with a series of adjacent low P-values. In our analysis, we used meta-analysis P-values of the two blood sample cohorts as input for comb-p. We used the default parameter setting for our comb-p analysis, with parameters --seed 1e-3 and --dist 200, which required a P-value of 10−3 to start a region and extend the region if another P-value was within 200 base pairs. As comb-p uses the Sidak method [29] to adjust for multiple comparisons, we selected DMRs with Sidak P-values less than 0.05. In addition, we further required the selected DMRs to have at least 3 CpGs and a consistent direction of change across all CpGs mapped within the region.

In the coMethDMR approach, the “contiguous genomic regions” are genomic regions on the Illumina array covered with clusters of contiguous CpGs where the maximum separation between any two consecutive probes is 200 base pairs. First, coMethDMR selects co-methylated sub-regions within the contiguous genomic regions. Next, we summarized methylation M-values within these co-methylated sub-regions using medians and tested them against chronological age at blood draw, adjusting for sex, and estimated immune cell-type proportions using linear regression models. The bacon method [25] was next applied to cohort-specific coMethDMR test statistics to obtain inflation-corrected effect sizes, standard errors, and P-values, which were then combined by inverse-variance weighted meta-analysis models using R package meta. We considered co-methylated DMRs with at least 3 CpGs, with a consistent direction of change in estimated effect sizes in both datasets, and a meta-analysis FDR < 0.05 to be significant in the coMethDMR analysis. Finally, we selected significant DMRs identified by both comb-p and coMethDMR approaches for subsequent analyses.

Functional annotation of significant methylation associations

Significant methylation at individual CpGs and DMRs was annotated using both the Illumina (UCSC) gene annotation and Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool (GREAT) software [30], which associates genomic regions with target genes.

Enrichment test of over- and under-representation of aging-associated DNAm across genomic annotations

For each genomic annotation, we used Fisher’s exact test to compare the proportion of significant CpGs (foreground) mapping to the annotation (e.g., CpG island) to the proportion among all tested CpGs (background). For region-based analyses, Fisher’s exact test compared the proportion of CpGs within significant DMRs (foreground) mapping to each annotation to the proportion among CpGs in contiguous genomic regions on the array (described under “Differentially methylated regions analysis” above) (background). We defined genic regions using gene start–end boundaries (TxDb UCSC knownGene, hg38) and assessed whether loci overlapped these genic intervals versus intergenic regions. Given a total of 13 genomic annotations, we considered P-values less than 3.84 × 10−3 (i.e., 0.05/13) to be statistically significant.

Pathway analysis

To identify biological pathways enriched with significant DNA methylation differences, we performed gene set enrichment analysis using the methylRRA function in the methylGSA R package [31]. We used CpG-level P-values from the meta-analysis of FHS9 and ADNI for all CpGs that passed QC and were included in the meta-analysis as input. CpGs were mapped to genes using the Illumina MethylationEPIC annotation (UCSC RefGene gene annotation). This mapping yielded 26,644 unique genes with at least one mapped CpG, which defined the background gene universe for enrichment testing. The methylRRA procedure then aggregates CpG-level evidence into a single gene-level score while adjusting for probe-number bias (genes represented by different numbers of CpGs on the array). Gene-level scores were ranked and evaluated using a pre-ranked Gene Set Enrichment Analysis [32] framework against KEGG and Reactome gene sets. Pathways with FDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Integrative analyses with gene expression, genetic variants, and brain-to-blood correlations

To evaluate the effect of DNA methylation on the expression of nearby genes, we overlapped our aging-associated CpGs, including both significant individual CpGs and those located within DMRs, with eQTm analysis results in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 of Yao et al. [33].

For correlation and overlap of aging-associated CpGs with genetic susceptibility loci, we searched the GoDMC database. To select significant blood mQTLs of aging-associated CpGs in GoDMC, we used the same criteria as the original study [34], that is, considering a cis P-value smaller than 10−8 and a trans P-value smaller than 10−14 as significant. The genome-wide summary statistics for genetic variants associated with dementia described in Bellenguez et al. [35] were obtained from the European Bioinformatics Institute GWAS Catalog under accession no. GCST90027158.

We performed colocalization analysis using the coloc R package to determine whether the association signals at overlapping loci (variant to CpG methylation levels and variant to ADRD status) were attributable to a single shared causal variant or to distinct nearby variants. For each aging-associated CpG with a cis-mQTL, we analyzed the set of SNPs overlapping between the mQTL summary statistics and results from the Bellenguez et al. [35] ADRD GWAS. DNAm was modeled as a quantitative trait and ADRD was modeled as a case–control trait, using default priors in the coloc.abf () function. Coloc estimates posterior probabilities for five hypotheses; we focused on PP3 (H3), where both traits are associated in the region but through distinct causal genetic variants, and PP4 (H4), where both traits are associated and share a single causal genetic variant. Following Guo et al. [36], we defined strong evidence for colocalization using the criteria PP3 + PP4 > 0.90, PP4 > 0.80, and PP4/PP3 > 5 (shared-variant model at least 5 times more likely than distinct-variant models).

To assess the correlation of aging-associated CpGs and DMRs methylation levels in blood and brain samples, we used the London dataset, which consisted of 69 samples with matched PFC and blood samples [37]. We assessed the association of brain and blood methylation levels at aging-associated CpGs by performing an adjusted correlation analysis using methylation residuals (rresid), in which we first removed the effects of estimated neuron proportions in brain samples (or estimated immune cell-type proportions in blood samples), array, age at death for brain samples (or age at blood draw for blood samples), and sex from DNA methylation M-values.

Validation using independent datasets

To compare our results with previous independent AD studies, we searched aging-associated CpGs (both significant individual CpGs and those located in DMRs) using the CpG Query tool in the MIAMI-AD database. For input on phenotype, we selected “AD Biomarker,” “AD Neuropathology,” “Dementia Clinical Diagnosis,” and “Mild Cognitive Impairment.” Only studies external to the ADNI and FHS datasets were included. The use of external datasets for comparison helps ensure that any observed overlap between aging- and AD-associated loci is less likely to be driven by dataset-specific features of the FHS or ADNI datasets, by avoiding within-cohort confirmation bias and cohort-specific technical or ascertainment effects (e.g., study-specific sample processing, array/batch structure, or recruitment/clinical assessment differences).

Results

Participant characteristics

The average follow-up durations for the subjects were 5.0 ± 2.3 years in FHS9 and 5.9 ± 3.0 years in ADNI. All participants were over 65 years of age. In FHS9, the average age was 74.3 ± 6.6 years, with 56.7% females. In ADNI, the average age was 77.0 ± 6.5 years, with 50.8% females (Table 1, Fig. 1).

Among the 491 hypermethylated DMRs, the majority (75.2%, 369 DMRs) were found in gene promoter regions. On the other hand, among the 65 hypomethylated DMRs, most (56.9%, 37 DMRs) were located in distal regions.

Our formal test of enrichment of the aging-associated methylation differences in blood against multiple genomic annotations revealed enrichment patterns that were largely consistent between CpGs and DMRs. Both significant hypermethylated individual CpGs and DMRs were strongly enriched in CpG islands and promoter-proximal annotations (including TSS200, TSS1500, 5′UTR, and 1 st exon) and depleted in CpG-poor annotations (open sea, shelf, and shore) and distal annotations such as gene body and intergenic regions (Supplementary Tables 4–5, Fig. 3). Overall, hypermethylated loci were enriched in genic regions (CpGs OR = 1.30, P-value = 3.09 × 10−7; DMRs OR = 1.27, P-value = 6.41 × 10−11), indicating that age-associated hypermethylation preferentially occurs at loci annotated to genes.

Enrichment of DNA methylation significantly associated with chronological age in meta-analysis of individual CpGs and DMRs. A two-sided Fisher’s test was used to determine over or under-representation of the significant CpGs in individual CpGs analysis and CpGs mapped within significant DMRs in various genomic annotations. Because a total of 13 genomic annotations were analyzed, we considered P-values less than 3.84 × 10−3 (i.e., 0.05/13) to be statistically significant (indicated by the symbol *)

In contrast, hypomethylated individual CpGs and DMRs showed enrichment patterns that were less concordant between CpGs and DMRs. Hypomethylated CpGs were significantly depleted in CpG islands and promoter/genic-proximal annotations (TSS200, 1 st exon), while being significantly enriched in CpG-poor/distal annotations including open sea, shores, TSS1500, and intergenic regions (Supplementary Tables 4–5). In contrast, hypomethylated DMRs were significantly depleted in CpG islands and significantly enriched in shores and shelves. Neither hypomethylated CpGs nor DMRs showed significant enrichment in genic regions.

Pathway enrichment of aging-associated DNA methylation differences highlights biological hallmarks of aging and Alzheimer’s disease pathways

The pathway enrichment results for methylGSA are based on gene-level scores obtained by aggregating CpG-level meta-analysis evidence across all tested CpGs mapped to each gene (rather than on the counts of significant CpGs or DMRs alone). At a 5% false discovery rate (FDR), we identified 26 KEGG pathways and 27 Reactome pathways significantly enriched with aging-associated DNAm (Supplementary Table 6, Fig. 4). Consistent with the 12 established biological hallmarks of aging [38], these significant pathways highlight a number of critical molecular and cellular processes implicated in age-related functional decline. Several enriched pathways, such as Type II diabetes mellitus, Integration of energy metabolism, and Peptide hormone metabolism, are associated with the hallmark of deregulated nutrient sensing, which encompasses insulin/IGF-1 signaling and metabolic dysregulation in aging [38]. Pathways associated with the altered intercellular communication hallmark include Gap junction, Tight junction, Cell-adhesion molecules, ECM–receptor interaction, and Protein-protein interactions at synapses, underscore that impaired cell-cell communication and structural remodeling accompany aging [39]. Moreover, pathways such as the Wnt signaling pathway and Hedgehog signaling pathway are associated with the stem-cell exhaustion hallmark and altered developmental cues, highlighting aging-related deterioration in regenerative capacity and tissue homeostasis [40]. Finally, mitochondrial dysfunction hallmark is represented by enrichment of the Calcium signaling pathway and Purine metabolism, which affect mitochondrial energy production and oxidative stress [41].

The methylGSA software identified (A) 26 KEGG pathways and (B) 27 Reactome pathways significantly enriched with aging-associated CpGs at 5% FDR

Importantly, several pathways enriched with aging-associated DNAm overlap with biological processes that have been implicated in AD. For example, the most significant KEGG pathway was the Calcium signaling pathway, a broad intracellular signaling pathway. In blood, this enrichment likely reflects general signaling and stress-response biology. Nonetheless, dysregulation of neuronal calcium homeostasis has been proposed as a contributor to amyloid/tau-related processes and synaptic dysfunction in AD [42]. Pathways related to neuronal communication and synaptic function, including Neurotransmitter receptors and postsynaptic signal transmission, Long-term potentiation, and Protein-protein interactions at synapses, have been associated with cognitive impairment observed in AD [43]. Additionally, the Wnt signaling pathway has been implicated in AD through its influence on synaptic plasticity, neuronal survival, and amyloid-beta production [44]. ECM–receptor interaction, Cell adhesion molecules, and Focal adhesion pathways have roles in neuronal connectivity and integrity, processes often compromised during AD pathogenesis [45]. Moreover, enrichment of the Type II diabetes mellitus and Integration of energy metabolism pathways is consistent with late-life metabolic dysregulation as a hallmark of aging, and these processes have also been previously implicated as contributors to dementia risk [46]. Notably, the KEGG pathway Alzheimer’s disease is significantly enriched with aging-associated CpGs (P-value = 2.07 × 10−4, FDR = 0.0038). However, our dementia-free cohort design does not test AD-specific associations, and these enrichments should be interpreted primarily as aging-related signatures.

Correlation of significant DNAm with expression of nearby genes

To better understand the functional role of aging-associated DNAm, we overlapped the significant aging-associated CpGs and DMRs with previously identified DNAm-to-RNA associations (eQTm), which were computed using over 4000 paired blood DNA methylation and gene expression data collected from the same subjects in FHS [33]. We identified 73 CpGs significantly correlated in cis (within 500 kb of the CpG) with nearby transcript expression (Supplementary Table 7). Because some CpGs were associated with multiple transcripts, this corresponded to a total of 103 significant CpG-transcript pairs, of which more than half (62.1%, 64 out of 103) were negative. Enrichment or depletion of cis-eQTm loci was not statistically significant for either CpGs or DMRs in either the hypermethylated or hypomethylated DNAm differences.

Co-localization analysis nominated genetic variants associated with both dementia and aging-associated DNA methylation

To further understand the functional roles of the aging-associated CpGs and DMRs in AD, we performed integrative analyses of aging-associated DNAm with genetic data. First, to identify methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTLs) for the significant aging-associated DNAm differences, we searched the GoDMC database [34]. Among the 3758 aging-associated CpGs (Supplementary Table 2) and the 2409 CpGs located in significant DMRs (Supplementary Table 3), we found that 1696 CpGs (45.1%) had 311,054 mQTLs (Supplementary Table 8). This proportion is consistent with findings from a recent large mQTL meta-analysis in blood, which estimated that approximately 45% of CpGs on the Illumina array are influenced by genetic variants [34].

As the aging processes and dementia may share common genetic factors, we next evaluated whether these mQTLs overlapped with genetic risk loci implicated in dementia, by comparing them with the genetic variants identified in a recent ADRD (Alzheimer’s and related dementia) meta-analysis [35]. While no mQTLs overlapped with genome-wide significant loci (P-value < 5 × 10−8) for ADRD, we found 1168 mQTLs overlapped with genetic variants reaching a suggestive significance threshold at P < 1 × 10−5 (Supplementary Table 9).

Given the observed overlap between mQTLs and ADRD genetic risk loci, we sought to determine whether the association signals at these loci (variant to CpG methylation levels and variant to ADRD status) were driven by a single shared causal variant or by distinct variants in proximity. To this end, we performed a co-localization analysis using the method described in Giambartolomei et al. [47]. The results provided strong evidence [36] supporting a shared causal variant in 32 genomic regions influencing both traits (“Methods”). Among the associated CpGs, several were located in promoter regions of genes including ABI3, RELN, ZNF233, DLG4, HIVEP3, DNTT, HSD3B7, RNF39, PPM1E, and HSD3B7 (Supplementary Table 10).

Aging-associated DNAm differences are significantly associated with Alzheimer’s disease in independent studies

To further investigate the relevance of aging-associated DNAm differences to AD, we next examined their presence in previous AD-related DNAm studies using the MIAMI-AD database (https://miami-ad.org/) [48]. We considered AD phenotypes including clinical diagnoses of mild cognitive impairment (MCI), AD, or dementia; AD-related neuropathology in brain tissue; and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) AD biomarkers. To ensure independence from our aging study, we excluded datasets that used samples from the ADNI or FHS studies.

Applying a stringent Bonferroni correction for 5362 CpGs (including 3758 significant individual CpGs and 1604 CpGs located within DMRs, at a significance threshold of P-value < 9.32 × 10−6), we identified 33 CpGs that were also significant in independent studies of AD. These studies analyzed brain tissue from the prefrontal cortex (PFC), temporal cortex (TC), parahippocampal gyrus (PHG), or middle temporal gyrus (MTG) (Supplementary Table 11). Notably, all these external studies adjusted for age and other covariates, so the observed DNAm-to-AD associations are independent of age effects.

These 33 CpGs were located in the promoter regions of ACADS, CHST9, DIO3, EDARADD, GP5, GPR56, HTR4, LIMD1, PENK, and SLC24A3 genes and other genomic regions. At the more relaxed nominal significance threshold (P-value < 0.05), we found that 1846 (34.4%) of the 5362 aging-associated CpGs were associated with AD phenotypes in prior studies. Among them, 1159 (62.8%) were identified in brain-based DNAm studies, 503 (27.2%) in blood-based studies, and 184 (10.0%) were observed in both blood and brain DNAm studies of AD. The higher number of overlapping CpGs in brain studies may reflect both the greater number of brain-based AD studies compared to blood-based studies included in the MIAMI-AD database, and the larger biological variability in blood-based DNAm data [20], which can reduce statistical power relative to brain-based studies.

Limiting to blood-based studies, we next compared the direction of methylation changes in aging and AD among the 687 CpGs (503 + 184 CpGs). We found less than half (278 CpGs, 40.5%) showed concordant changes: 81 CpGs were hypomethylated and 197 CpGs were hypermethylated in both aging and AD. The remaining 409 CpGs (59.5%) were discordant: 45.4% of the CpGs (312 CpGs) were hypermethylated in aging but hypomethylated in AD, and the remaining 14.1% CpGs (97 CpGs) showed hypomethylation in aging but hypermethylation in AD.

At a 5% FDR, enrichment analysis showed that CpGs with concordant DNAm changes in aging and AD were significantly over-represented in the phasic smooth muscle contraction pathway, reflecting vascular dysfunction common in AD, as well as in the developmental pathways tripartite regional subdivision and anterior/posterior axis specification in embryo. On the other hand, CpGs showing discordant DNAm changes between aging and AD were significantly enriched in neuroactive ligand signaling and neuron migration pathways, key regulators of neuronal communication and synaptic plasticity that are dysregulated in AD (Supplementary Table 12).

Brain-to-blood DNAm correlation analysis identified aging-associated blood DNAm with concordant cross-tissue changes

Among the 3758 significant individual CpGs associated with aging and 1604 CpGs located in aging DMRs, DNAm at 23 CpGs showed significant brain-to-blood correlations (FDR < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 13). All 23 CpGs showed a significant positive association, ranging from 0.423 to 0.626. These CpGs were located in the promoter of CNTNAP2, PODXL2, MARCH11, OCIAD2, SCGN, ZNF233, C3orf18, ELOVL2, ZNF442, and PDE1B genes and other genomic regions. The consistent cross-tissue methylation patterns at these CpGs may indicate important shared regulatory roles during aging.

By intersecting these 23 CpGs with the 1846 CpGs significantly associated with both aging and AD described above, we identified 9 CpGs, located in the promoter regions of PDE1B, ELOVL2, and PODXL2 genes and other genomic regions, that showed both strong concordance in brain-to-blood DNAm levels, as well as association with AD diagnosis or AD neuropathology in independent studies (Supplementary Table 14). One notable example is cg26019680 in the promoter of PODXL2, which showed high correlation of DNAm levels between blood and four brain regions (prefrontal cortex, entorhinal cortex, superior temporal gyrus, and cerebellum) (Supplementary Fig. 2). This CpG is part of a hypermethylated DMR associated with aging and also displays hypermethylation in male AD cases [22]. Such CpGs represent promising candidates for future biomarker development.

Discussion

We performed a comprehensive meta-analysis of two large, independent, blood-based DNAm datasets generated by the FHS and ADNI studies, which were measured using the same Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip. Prior work in these cohorts has tested whether DNAm-based aging algorithms (including first- and second-generation epigenetic clocks and newer pace-of-aging measures) are associated with cognitive impairment and dementia outcomes (e.g., [3]). Our study addresses a complementary question: rather than summarizing DNAm into a single aging score, we identify specific CpGs and DMRs that reproducibly associate with chronological age in late life across two EPIC-array datasets, while restricting to participants who remain dementia-free during follow-up to better capture normative aging. We then performed blood mQTL mapping for the aging-associated loci and tested their ADRD relevance via colocalization with ADRD GWAS loci, blood-brain concordance, and systematic overlap with independent AD methylation studies, which enables locus-level biological interpretation and biomarker prioritization beyond what clock-based associations provide.

At a 5% FDR, we identified 3758 CpGs and 556 DMRs consistently associated with chronological age in both the FHS and ADNI datasets. Specifically, consistent with prior blood-based aging EWAS [5–7, 49], we observed that age-associated hypermethylation was preferentially located in promoter-proximal regions, while age-associated hypomethylation was more frequently observed in distal regions (> 2 kb from the TSS). Importantly, our formal enrichment analyses accounting for non-uniform EPIC array coverage further demonstrated that hypermethylated CpGs and DMRs are over-represented in CpG islands and promoter-proximal gene annotations (e.g., TSS200/TSS1500, 5′UTR, and 1 st exon) and depleted in CpG-poor contexts (open sea, shelf, and shore) and distal categories such as gene body and intergenic regions. In addition, hypermethylated loci showed overall enrichment in genic regions, indicating that a substantial proportion of age-associated hypermethylation overlaps gene-annotated loci. In contrast, hypomethylated CpGs and DMRs showed less concordant enrichment patterns across analysis levels, with hypomethylated CpGs enriched in CpG-poor/distal annotations (including open sea/shores, TSS1500, and intergenic regions) while hypomethylated DMRs were enriched in shores and shelves; neither hypomethylated CpGs nor DMRs showed evidence of significant enrichment (or depletion) in genic regions. Notably, this pattern also reflects fundamental differences in baseline methylation distributions across genomic annotations: CpG islands/promoters are typically lowly methylated, while intergenic “open sea” CpGs are often highly methylated. As a result, the direction and detectability of change can be partly constrained by floor/ceiling effects and dynamic range, and genomic-context enrichment should be interpreted as reflecting both biological regulation and the underlying methylation landscape.

While age-related promoter hypermethylation is often linked to reduced gene expression, the transcriptional consequences are context-dependent and do not imply uniform gene silencing [14, 50, 51]. Most recently, a large-scale meta-analysis of more than 32,000 blood methylomes reported increasing methylation in bivalent and Polycomb regions along with more uniform methylation loss in quiescent chromatin regions characterized by low levels of active histone marks [4]. This underscores that age-associated changes are genomic-context-specific rather than a uniform genome-wide hypomethylation. Importantly, our results are limited to the probe content of the Illumina EPIC array, which is enriched for regulatory CpGs. Future studies using whole genome sequencing-based approaches will be needed to quantify global methylation changes with age.

Importantly, a review of recent literature revealed that many of the aging-associated CpGs and DMRs we identified in this study have previously been implicated in AD. Among the top 20 CpGs (Table 2), the three most significant CpGs were located in the ELOVL2 gene, whose hypermethylation has consistently been demonstrated to be a robust epigenetic biomarker of chronological aging across multiple tissues, including brain [52], blood [53], and saliva [54]. Additionally, the EpiAge clock, built solely on the top three CpGs (cg16867657, cg21572722, and cg24724428) identified in this study, shows significant acceleration in individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) compared to controls [54]. A recent GWAS also identified a genetic variant in ELOVL2 significantly associated with an increased risk of AD, potentially through alterations in lipid metabolism [55]. Among other genes associated with top CpGs, TRIM59 is another gene that shows age-associated hypermethylation. A recent study reported hypermethylation of TRIM59 in blood samples from AD patients compared to healthy controls [56]. This hypermethylation was correlated with abnormalities in DNA repair and cell cycle regulation, two critical processes involved in AD pathology. CACNA1G encodes a calcium channel, and its expression decreases with age in both human and mouse brains, a decline further exacerbated in AD. This downregulation may disrupt calcium homeostasis, promote amyloid-beta production, and contribute to cognitive decline [57]. Finally, FHL2 gene is also hypermethylated with age; it regulates inflammatory responses and adipose tissue metabolism, both of which are increasingly recognized as important contributors to AD pathophysiology [58].

Similarly, among the top 20 most significant DMRs (Table 3), PCDHAC1 is a member of the protocadherin family involved in synapse formation and stabilization, processes whose disruption has also been implicated in AD pathology [59]. The GALNT17 gene encodes an enzyme in the GALNT family, which initiates glycosylation (the addition of sugar molecules to proteins), a process recently linked to microglial-driven neuroinflammation and exacerbation of AD pathology in the brain [60]. Also, the ALDH1A2 gene encodes a key enzyme in retinoic acid (RA) synthesis, a pathway known to support neuroplasticity and memory. Reduced ALDH1A2 expression has been observed in multiple AD mouse models, even at early disease stages. This decline may play an initiating role in AD pathogenesis, and pharmacologic restoration of RA signaling has shown therapeutic promise [61]. Another noteworthy gene is NEFM (Neurofilament Medium Polypeptide). In AD mouse models, elevated NEFM expressions were associated with neuronal dysfunction, including axonal damage and disrupted cytoskeletal integrity. Notably, reducing NEFM gene expression through IGF1R inhibition provided neuroprotection, leading to improved neuronal function and reduced neuroinflammation [62].

Finally, GPR158 encodes a G protein-coupled receptor predominantly localized to neurons in the cortical and hippocampal regions, areas critical for synaptic architecture and plasticity, which are disrupted in AD. In a recent functional genomics study [63], GPR158 was identified as one of five hub genes significantly downregulated in the temporal cortex of AD patients. Gene ontology analysis revealed that these hub genes, including GPR158, are enriched in pathways related to synaptic function and memory processes, suggesting that their reduced expression may contribute to synaptic failure in AD. Notably, GPR158 expression was inversely correlated with β-secretase (BACE1) activity in AD brain samples, indicating that lower GPR158 levels are associated with increased BACE1 activity, which could in turn enhance Aβ production from Amyloid Precursor Protein (APP).

In addition to corroborating previous findings, our analyses also identified several novel differentially methylated genes that have potential implications in AD. For instance, in the comparison of aging-associated CpGs with those reported in prior AD studies, cg02336827, located in the promoter region of the LIMD1 gene and hypomethylated with age, emerged as one of the most significant CpGs, showing a P-value of 4.63 × 10−7 and consistent hypomethylation in AD (Supplementary Table 11) [64]. LIMD1 encodes a scaffold protein involved in key cellular functions, including transcriptional repression, microRNA-mediated gene silencing, and cytoskeletal organization. Although LIMD1 itself has not been directly implicated in AD, other LIM domain-containing proteins have been linked to neurodegenerative processes. Notably, LIM kinase 1 (LIMK1), which regulates actin cytoskeleton dynamics, has been associated with synaptic dysfunction in AD [65]. These findings suggest that LIMD1 may represent a novel candidate for future AD studies.

To reveal additional biological insights linking age-associated blood methylation to dementia risk, we performed several additional integrative analyses. Our pathway enrichment analysis revealed that aging-associated CpGs were significantly overrepresented in KEGG and Reactome pathways related to calcium signaling, Wnt/Hedgehog signaling, extracellular matrix (ECM) and cell adhesion, and metabolic regulation, biological processes that are prominent in late-life aging and which have also been previously implicated in AD (Fig. 4). Colocalization analyses identified 32 loci where the same causal variant likely influences both DNAm and ADRD risk. Notably, shared signals that harbored both mQTLs and AD GWAS hits were observed at ABI3, RELN, and DLG4, genes involved in microglial activation, synaptic organization, and neuronal plasticity [66–68].

Furthermore, we found that approximately one-third of aging-associated CpGs were also associated with AD or AD neuropathology in independent AD DNAm studies after adjusting for age and other covariates, with about 40% displaying concordant direction of effect (Supplementary Table 11). Divergent patterns (e.g., hypermethylation with aging but hypomethylation in AD) may reflect a dysregulation of epigenetic programs in normal aging, whereby protective or adaptive methylation changes that typically accumulate with age are either not established or are actively reversed in AD. This supports the model proposed by Berger and colleagues that AD is not merely an acceleration of aging but also involves a disruption of homeostatic epigenetic regulation that contributes to neurodegeneration [69]. Taken together, our results suggest that a number of age-related epigenetic changes in peripheral blood function not only as biomarkers of aging, but also reflect relevant changes in AD or AD neuropathology, and may be influenced by genetic risk variants for AD.

We further demonstrated that 23 aging-associated CpGs showed significant positive correlations between blood and brain frontal cortex (Supplementary Table 13). Among the genes associated with these CpGs, the CNTNAP2 gene encodes a cell adhesion molecule involved in various critical neuronal functions, including axonal organization, synaptic regulation, and neuronal migration. Recent studies have identified genetic variants in the CNTNAP2 gene significantly associated with AD [70], and altered CNTNAP2 expression levels have been observed in the brains of AD patients [71]. Also, the SCGN gene encodes the calcium-binding protein secretagogin. Importantly, SCGN has been postulated to have neuroprotective effects against neurodegeneration, as neurons expressing SCGN were largely resistant to cell death in human hippocampus [72]. Moreover, 9 of the 23 CpGs, including loci in ELOVL2, PODXL2, and PDE1B, were also significantly associated with AD or AD neuropathology in independent datasets, after adjusting for age and other covariates (Supplementary Table 14). ELOVL2, in particular, is a well-established component of several epigenetic clocks [14, 50, 73] and has been linked to both neuronal lipid homeostasis and cognitive decline [74]. Prospective evaluation of these CpGs in longitudinal cohorts will clarify their utility for early dementia risk stratification.

Because epigenetic clock and pace-of-aging associations with dementia have already been evaluated in ADNI and FHS [3], our focus here was not to re-test those composite measures, but to interpret late-life age-associated DNAm at locus and regional resolution and integrate it with AD-related genomic evidence. We did not compare our age-associated CpGs to the CpG lists used in established epigenetic clocks. First, probe-wise clocks have been shown to have lower test-retest reliability and greater sensitivity to technical variation than principal component (PC)-based epigenetic clocks [75, 76]; PC clocks aggregate signal across all assayed CpGs, improving reliability but yielding no fixed CpG set, so CpG-level overlap is not meaningful. Second, age-associated CpGs often occur in highly co-methylated clusters [77], and clock algorithms typically select a small number of representative CpGs from many correlated sites. Thus, the inclusion or omission of any single CpG provides limited biological insight, as different correlated subsets can achieve similar predictive performance. For these reasons, we did not perform CpG-list comparisons in this study.

The strengths of this study include the careful selection of samples, rigorous harmonization and quality control procedures, and robust analytical approaches applied to two well-characterized cohorts. In addition to meta-analyses of individual CpGs and genomic regions, we also performed comprehensive integrative analyses that incorporated information from eQTMs, mQTLs, ADRD GWAS, brain-to-blood DNAm correlations, pathway enrichment, and the manually curated MIAMI-AD database.

Several limitations of this study are in order. First, the DNAm was measured in whole blood, which may not fully capture cell-type-specific changes. Future work leveraging single-cell technology will offer more insight into the specific cell types affected by the aging-associated DNAm differences discovered in this study. Second, given that brain and blood derive from distinct cell lineages, blood-based DNAm markers may not accurately reflect brain-specific methylation changes. To address this, we prioritized DNAm changes with concordant brain-to-blood associations using a large publicly available DNAm dataset with matched brain and blood DNA methylation levels. Third, both the ADNI and FHS cohorts are predominantly of European ancestry, limiting generalizability to more diverse populations. Fourth, because age-associated CpGs were derived from cross-sectional differences across individuals (rather than repeated measures within individuals), they may not fully capture longitudinal DNAm change with aging. Future longitudinal methylation studies will be important to more rigorously define aging-related loci. Finally, we did not comprehensively model late-life comorbidities (e.g., diabetes) across cohorts. Diabetes status was not available in ADNI. In FHS Exam 9, 15% of participants (135/898) had diabetes, and diabetes status was not significantly associated with age, suggesting it is less likely to confound age-DNAm associations. As a sensitivity analysis in FHS, we rerun age-DNAm associations additionally adjusting for diabetes status and repeated pathway enrichment; the metabolic pathways remained significantly enriched (Type II diabetes mellitus: P-value = 2.23 × 10−4, FDR = 0.017, Integration of energy metabolism: P-value = 3.56 × 10−5, FDR = 0.0021). These findings suggest the metabolic enrichments are not driven entirely by diabetes case status and may reflect broader late-life aging-related metabolic biology.

In summary, our results show that in late-life peripheral blood, among CpG sites covered by the EPIC array, age-associated DNAm differences are enriched for promoter-proximal hypermethylation and distal-region hypomethylation. These aging-associated DNAm differences converge on metabolic and synaptic pathways implicated in AD. We further prioritized a small set of CpGs where DNAm is influenced by mQTLs colocalizing with dementia risk loci, or show strong blood-to-brain concordance and are associated with AD in independent studies, highlighting their promise as blood-based AD biomarkers in future research.
