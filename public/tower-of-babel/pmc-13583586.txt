Diversity in Care Trajectories in Specialist Visits for Preventing Cardiovascular Events After New-Onset Diabetes: A Population-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Sarah O’Connor, Claudia Blais, Jérémie Sylvain-Morneau, Awa Diop, Miceline Mésidor, Jacinthe Leclerc, Paul Poirier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpecialist physicians may play a role in preventing major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs; myocardial infarction/stroke/cardiovascular deaths) in patients with diabetes. Given the heterogeneity in patients and the specific needs in established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), this study sought to describe the trajectories of visits to specialists among diabetes patients with vs without ASCVD and explore their associations with MACEs.MethodsUsing Quebec’s administrative databases, we identified new-onset diabetes patients aged ≥ 20 years with no history of MACEs in 2013. Individual trajectories based on outpatient visits to a specialist (cardiologist, internist, or endocrinologist) were compiled for consecutive 3-month periods, up to 2 years after diagnosis, and grouped using latent class growth analysis. We assessed the risk of MACEs using weighted Cox regression models according to ASCVD status, from year 2 postdiagnosis up to December 31, 2019.ResultsFour trajectory groups were identified, as follows: (i) regular/frequent visits (n = 1810); (ii) early visits only (n = 1420); (iii) regular/sparse visits (n = 8670); and (iv) primary care only (n = 18,050). Patients with ASCVD consulted more specialists compared to those without ASCVD. Among patients with ASCVD (n = 4800), the early-visits-only group had a lower risk of MACEs (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR]: 0.70 [95% confidence interval {CI}:0.49-0.97]), compared with the primary-care-only group, whereas for patients without ASCVD (n = 25,150), the risk of MACEs was higher in the early-visits-only (aHR:1.76 [95% CI: 1.44-2.12]) and regular/sparse-visits groups (aHR:1.26 [95% CI: 1.13-1.41]).ConclusionsThis study highlights great diversity in diabetes care trajectories according to ASCVD status. We found conflicting results in the risk of MACEs; some results should be interpreted with caution.

In Canada, the incidence of diabetes has steadily increased since 2000-2001, with a crude prevalence of 9.6% among Canadians aged ≥ 1 year in 2022-2023.1 Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) associated with atherosclerosis (eg, myocardial infarction [MI], stroke, cardiovascular death) are major complications of diabetes.2 Diabetes increases the risk of MACEs 2- 4-fold, making atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) the main cause of morbidity and mortality.3,4

In Canada, after a first diagnosis of diabetes, patients are usually followed in primary care settings,5, 6, 7 although specialists also play key roles in care pathways.8,9 Given their expertise, specialists often use more screening tests and prescribe a wider variety of pharmacologic agents, compared to general practitioners (GPs).8,10, 11, 12 In Canada, GP referrals are required to access specialists, suggesting that referred patients may have higher cardiovascular risk or established ASCVD. Discrepancies in care trajectories might reflect the heterogeneity in the cardiovascular risk and characteristics of patients with diabetes, but they also provide insight on use of healthcare services.13 However, diversity in care trajectories involving specialists, and the expected frequency of follow-up given patient indications, remains largely unexplored in Quebec, Canada. In addition, the impact of this heterogeneity on the frequency and regularity of visits to specialists, after a new diagnosis of diabetes, in the prevention of MACEs remains unclear.8,11,14

Therefore, this population-based cohort study sought to describe trajectories of outpatient visits to specialists among new cases of diabetes with and without established ASCVD. We hypothesize that patients who follow different care trajectories have distinct sociodemographic characteristics and health statuses, per their ASCVD status at the time of diagnosis. As a secondary objective, we sought to assess whether care trajectories are associated with the advent of a MACE according to ASCVD status. We hypothesize that regular and consistent involvement of specialists likely would have protective effects in secondary prevention of MACEs, for patients with diabetes, both with and without ASCVD.

Methods

Data sources

This study used the Quebec Integrated Chronic Disease Surveillance System (QICDSS), composed of health administrative databases linked by an anonymized unique identifier for each citizen covered by the provincial health insurance.15 We used the following databases: (i) health-insurance registry (demographic information); (ii) hospital discharges; (iii) physician claims; and (iv) vital statistics.15 The QICDSS databases start in 1996 and are updated every year.15

Study population

We included patients with incident cases of diabetes (all types) aged ≥ 20 years who were diagnosed during fiscal year 2013, which is the year glycated hemoglobin A1c became a principal biomarker for the diagnosis of diabetes and glycemic control in Canada.16 Cases were identified using a validated algorithm (sensitivity: 95%; positive predictive value: 88%; Supplemental Table S1).17, 18, 19 Three exclusion criteria were used to enhance accuracy (Supplemental Table S2). Cases identified before April 1, 2013 were excluded, with a look-back window starting in 1996.

Study design

As depicted in Figure 1, the following 2 periods were determined for each patient after diagnosis: (i) the trajectory period, spanning from diabetes diagnosis until 2 years postdiagnosis (2013-2014); and (ii) the explanatory period, extending from the end of the trajectory period up to the first MACE, death, or the end of follow-up (December 31, 2019).Figure 1Study design. Identification of individual trajectories

Given that the Canadian diabetes guidelines recommend that patients with diabetes have regular glycemic follow-up evaluations every 3-6 months, we recorded the presence or absence of ≥ 1 outpatient visit with a specialist every 3 months,16 from the diabetes incident diagnosis date up to 2 years. The specialists selected were endocrinologists, internists, and cardiologists, specialties chosen for their role in the prevention of cardiovascular diseases among patients with diabetes.16 Each patient with ≥1 code associated to a specialist physician visit during a given 3-month window was considered exposed for this period (Supplemental Table S3). Given that the physician claims database includes only fee-for-services reclamations, ambulatory visits with physicians with other kinds of remuneration (capitation, hour-based salary; mostly GPs) along with visits with diabetes nurses, diabetes educators, or specialized practitioner nurses are not recorded. With the increasing importance given to specialized nurses and other professionals in diabetes care, and alternative modes of physician remuneration (especially in primary care), the number of patients without recorded ambulatory primary care visits was expected to increase during the study period. For this reason, patients without a recorded visit with a specialist during the follow-up period, with or without recorded primary care visits, were considered to have an absence of specialist visits, and were so classified. Patients who had a MACE during the trajectory period were censored (non-fatal MACE would likely affect the likelihood of visiting a specialist after the event), along with patients who left the QCIDSS or had incomplete data.

Study characteristics and other covariates

The characteristics of sex and age were collected at diabetes diagnosis only. The following characteristics were collected at diagnosis and every time-point during the trajectory period for time-varying variables: material and social deprivation quintiles (Q1, least deprived; Q5, most deprived)20,21; and area of residency. We calculated the combined comorbidity index, adjusted by removing the conditions “diabetes, uncomplicated” and “diabetes, complicated.”22 Patients were considered to have a given comorbidity as follows: (i) at first hospitalization and; (ii) with ≥ 2 physician claims within 2 years spaced by ≥ 30 days within the 5 preceding years. We explored whether patients had their diabetes diagnosis during a hospital stay or in outpatient settings. We also assessed healthcare utilization patterns by summarizing the proportion of patients with ≥ 1 visit to a cardiologist/internist/endocrinologist, a visit to a GP, and 1 hospital discharge in the year prior to diagnosis. The involvement of specialists and their role is expected to be greatly influenced by the health status of patients when diabetes is diagnosed. Specialist physicians are more often consulted in cases of suspected or established ASCVD.23,24 Given the differences in indications for specialist consultation, we compared characteristics according to established ASCVD or not at diabetes diagnosis (atherosclerosis, ischemic heart disease, or peripheral artery diseases), with a lookback window from 1996 (Supplemental Table S4). Although they are usually considered ASCVDs, stroke and MI were not considered an ASCVD here, as they were studied as MACEs in exploratory analyses.25

Statistical analysis

To identify trajectory groups, we first classified patients without any outpatient visit to a specialist during the trajectory period into a primary-care-only group. Patients who consulted ≥ 1 specialist were classified into trajectory groups using a latent class growth analysis (LCGA).26 Supplemental Appendix S1, and Supplemental Tables S5 and S6 contain the complete methodology. After classification of individual trajectories using LCGA, characteristics between groups were compared using t-tests and χ2 tests.

Exploratory analyses

The primary outcome was a composite score of incident MACEs (first MI and/or stroke and/or cardiovascular death).27 MACEs were identified using diagnostic codes in the physician claims and/or hospital discharge databases or the death registry (Supplemental Table S7).28,29 Individuals who had a MACE prior to or at the date of the diabetes diagnosis, were excluded (with a lookback window from 1996, corresponding to the creation of the QICDSS). Survival analyses were conducted separately according to ASCVD status and are described in more detail in Supplemental Appendix S2. We estimated weighted marginal Cox proportional models using trajectory groups as the independent variable (reference: primary-care-only) and MACEs as the dependent variable. We calculated combined stabilized weights that were truncated at the 99th percentile, using inverse probability weighting of treatment and censoring, for individuals with vs without ASCVD status separately.30 Briefly, we adjusted logistic regression models to derive propensity scores for each time point, which served to estimate the weights. These models, adjusted for exposure and censoring, were conditional on prior potential confounders and exposures during the trajectory period. Time-fixed covariates included age and sex. Time-varying covariates included annual material / social deprivation quintiles, annual area of residency, known or new comorbidities associated with MACEs (complicated diabetes [renal, ocular, neurologic, vascular, or unspecified complications], hypertension, heart failure, circulatory pulmonary disorders, coagulation disorders, peripheral vascular diseases, renal diseases, valvular disease), annual visits to GPs, annual hospital discharges, annual combined comorbidity score and the incidence of new ASCVD (for those without ASCVD). Balance was assessed by visual inspection of weight distribution between groups and assessment of standardized mean differences (SMDs).31 Inferences were estimated using a robust variance estimator.30 Results were presented as crude hazard ratios (HRs) and adjusted hazard ratios aHR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide version 7.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and the FlexMix and Cobalt packages in R Studio Server (version 1.4.1717; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).26,31 As anonymized databases were used, no informed consent was required. Absolute numbers were randomly rounded to further ensure anonymity.

Results

As presented in Figure 2, we identified 29,950 incident cases of diabetes with no history of MACE prior to or during the first 2 years following diagnosis. As depicted in Figure 3, the following 4 trajectory groups were identified: (i) regular/frequent visits (n = 1810); (ii) early visits only (n = 1420); (iii) regular/sparse visits (n = 8670); and (iv) primary care only (n = 18,050). The regular/frequent visit group is characterized by a consistent and large proportion of patients meeting with a specialist every 3 months, starting at 79.2% and declining to 67.2% of patients after 2 years. All patients in the early-visits-only group met with a specialist in the first 3 months, without any consultation after 6 months. The regular/sparse-visits group is characterized by a consistent yet smaller proportion of patients meeting with a specialist every 3 months (24.7% to 30.5%). Characteristics of patients across trajectory groups

As presented in Table 1, patients in the regular/frequent-visits group were older, were more likely to live in the Montreal metropolitan census area, had more preexisting comorbidities, and demonstrated higher specialist healthcare utilization and hospital discharges in the year prior to diagnosis, compared to the other groups. Patients in the early-visits-only and regular/sparse-visits groups were similar in terms of areas of residency, deprivation quintiles, and comorbidity scores, although patients in the regular/sparse-visits group were older, and were more likely to have consulted specialists or have had a hospital admission in the year prior to diagnosis (Table 1). Patients with ASCVD were older, had higher comorbidity scores, and visited specialists more often in the year prior to diabetes diagnosis (Supplemental Table S8). The same was observed for censored patients during the trajectory period (Supplemental Table S9). Among patients who consulted ≥ 1 specialist, the proportion of visits to cardiologists (48.7%) and internists (47.9%) were similar, whereas 25.9% met an endocrinologist. Among patients who met ≥ 1 specialist, those with ASCVD were more likely to have consulted a cardiologist than were those without ASCVD (75.8% vs 39.6%, P < 0.0001), and they were less exposed to Risk of MACE according to trajectory groups and history of ASCVD

Among patients with established ASCVD, a total of 800 first MACEs occurred (average time to first MACE 2 years after diagnosis, up to December 31, 2019: 25.7 [standard deviation 14.8] months); of these, 280 were MIs, 190 were strokes, and 330 were cardiovascular deaths. As presented in Table 2, compared with the primary-care-only group, the early-visits-only group was associated with a lower risk of MACEs (aHR: 0.70 [95% confidence interval {CI}: 0.49 to 0.97]), whereas no difference was observed for other groups. Among patients without ASCVD, a total of 1435 first MACEs occurred (average time to first MACE, 2 years after diagnosis up to December 31, 2019: 26.1 [standard deviation 14.9] months); of these, 515 were MIs, 520 were strokes, and 400 were cardiovascular deaths. The risk of MACEs was higher in the early-visits-only group (aHR: 1.76 [95% CI: 1.44 to 2.12]) and the regular/sparse-visits group (aHR: 1.26 [95% CI: 1.13 to 1.41]) compared to the primary-care-only group, whereas no difference was observed for the regular/frequent-visits group. In pairwise comparison between trajectory groups, we observed a lower risk of MACEs in the early-visit-only group compared with the regular/sparse-visits group among patients with known ASCVD (aHR: 0.71 [95% CI: 0.49 to 0.98]), whereas no difference was observed for the other groups. Discussion

In this population-based cohort study, we identified 4 trajectory groups of outpatient visits to specialists following an incident diagnosis of diabetes. The exposure to specialists among groups varied according to established ASCVD at diagnosis, with 63% of patients with ASCVD meeting with a specialist, compared with 35% for those without ASCVD. Results from exploratory analyses suggested that although the risk of MACE was lower among the early-visits-only group compared to the primary-care-only group among patients with established ASCVD, we observed a higher risk of MACE among patients without ASCVD allocated to the early-visits-only and the regular/sparse-visits groups. Overall, this study pioneers the description of care trajectories relating to visits to specialists in the context of diabetes at a population level in Quebec, along with the exploration of heterogeneity in care.

The proportion of patients with diabetes who received outpatient care from specialists in our study is concordant with previous Canadian studies.8,11,12 We also observed that patients with known ASCVD had a greater exposure to specialist physicians than did those without ASCVD, which was expected according to clinical practice guidelines for diabetes and studies from other jurisdictions.23,24,32 However, only nearly two-thirds of patients with ASCVD met with specialist physicians in the 2 years following diagnosis of diabetes. This figure is concerning, given that all patients with a new diabetes diagnosis and known ischemic heart disease should have access to cardiology care in Canada.23 These results may suggest the presence of barriers to access to specialist care. The LCGA highlighted important variations in the regularity and frequency of visits between groups, which may be influenced by a variety of individual risk factors or determinants of health. A study from Quebec, among patients with incident diabetes, found that, similar to our results, patients with atherosclerosis or osteoporosis had higher rates of consultation with specialists in metropolitan areas vs rural areas, except for internists, who are more likely to practice in rural regions.33 Interestingly, the rates of consultations with cardiologists were significantly lower in rural areas among patients with atherosclerosis, highlighting the disparities in access to specialist care despite the presence of ASCVD.33

The question of whether specialists play a role in the prevention of MACEs in diabetes remains controversial. A cohort study from Ontario observed a lower risk of MACE among patients with complex chronic diseases who met with specialists in the first year following a diagnosis of diabetes, whereas no difference was observed among those without chronic disease.8 In contrast, a cohort study from Saskatchewan observed a higher risk of mortality among patients with diabetes who sought specialist care in the first year of diagnosis, comparted to those who had GP-only care.11

These results, along with ours, may be criticized, as a higher risk of MACEs following consultations with specialists would be unlikely in practice. Hence, this discrepancy may be the result of residual confounding, a well-known challenge in nonrandomized studies.14 The presence of confounding by indication is particularly problematic, notably as patients with worse health conditions are more likely to meet with specialists, along with having a higher risk of MACEs.34 Administrative health databases often miss information on key confounders such as pharmacologic treatments, severity of disease, healthy habits, clinical data, and other important cardiovascular risk factors. Although we used a well-recognized approach for balancing measured confounders between groups, confounding by indication is particularly difficult to address in the presence of unmeasured covariates,34 especially in longitudinal analyses with multiple time points.30 The internal validity of some of our results is likely limited and should thus be interpreted with caution.

This study has several strengths, including a long follow-up period, careful modelling of weights addressing time-varying confounding and treatment-confounder feedback, and a strategy to handle selection bias by modelling the censoring variable. Additionally, the study offers a more granular understanding of heterogeneity in care pathways through trajectory analysis. As for limitations, some assumptions of LCGA may be counterintuitive from a practical standpoint, notably that each time points is assumed to be independent in time.35,36 In addition, misclassification cannot be ruled out, as grouping is based on membership probabilities.35,36 Also, the case definition used of ASCVD, which is mainly based on hospital diagnoses, tends to identify the most severe cases of ASCVD, leaving less-severe cases to be allocated to the non-ASCVD group (eg, intermittent claudication, presence of stenosis not requiring hospitalization or revascularization procedures). This issue increases the heterogeneity in indication in the non-ASCVD group, which may overestimate both the exposure to specialists and the risk of MACE for patients with peripheral arterial disease who were not identified by our case definition. This limitation may have contributed to the higher risk of MACE observed in the specialists’ trajectory groups, compared with primary care, among patients without ASCVD at diagnosis. Similarly, our case definition for diabetes does not differentiate type 2 diabetes from type 1 diabetes. Although we can expect that new diagnoses of diabetes after the age of 20 years are likely type 2 diabetes, we cannot rule out the possibility that a few cases of type 1 diabetes may be included and contribute to the heterogeneity in care trajectories. Additionally, health administrative data miss information on key confounders, which limits our capacity to compare trajectory groups and ensure total removal of confusion bias.

Another limitation is the end of the follow-up period being in 2019, due to the important upheaval in access in care associated with the COVID-19 pandemic and accessibility of data at the time of the study. Finally, censoring patients who had a MACE during the trajectory period may create immortal time bias—that is, patients who remained in the cohort were considered immune from MACE and are expected to have different characteristics than those censored. Although this was taken into account in statistical analyses, residual confounding may have limited our capability to fully control for this bias.

In summary, we identified distinct trajectories of visits to specialists and highlighted the differences between diabetes guidelines and what is done in practice. We found conflicting results regarding whether the trajectory groups are associated with MACEs, and some results should be interpreted with caution. This study opens a pathway to a better understanding of heterogeneity in diabetes care and highlights gaps for future improvement in care.
