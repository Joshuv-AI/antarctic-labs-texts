Abstract
Approximating a tensor in the tensor train (TT) format has many important applications in scientific computing. Rounding a TT tensor involves further compressing a tensor that is already in the TT format. This paper proposes new randomized algorithms for TT-rounding that uses sketches based on Khatri-Rao products (KRP). When the TT-ranks are known in advance, the proposed methods are comparable in cost to the sketches that used a sketching matrix in the TT-format [ 4 ] . However, the use of KRP sketches enables adaptive algorithms to round the tensor in the TT-format within a fixed user-specified tolerance. An important component of the adaptivity is the estimation of error using KRP sketching, for which we develop theoretical guarantees. We report numerical experiments on synthetic tensors, parametric low-rank kernel approximations, and the solution of parametric partial differential equations. The numerical experiments show that we obtain speed-ups of up to 50 × 50\times compared to deterministic TT-rounding. Both the computational cost analysis and numerical experiments verify that the adaptive algorithms are competitive with the fixed rank algorithms, suggesting the adaptivity introduces only a low overhead.

Introduction

A growing range of problems in scientific computing and data analysis involve high-dimensional data represented as tensors—multidimensional generalizations of vectors and matrices. When considering tensors, one must consider the specific tensor format to be used and the associated notions of ranks associated with tensors. Some examples of tensor formats are CP decomposition, Tucker, Tensor Train (TT), Hierarchical Tucker, etc., which have been reviewed in
[
6
,
22
]
. Inspired by the matrix-product-state (MPS), which is widely used in the computational quantum chemistry community
[
35
]
, Oseledets
[
29
]
introduced the TT format to the numerical analysis community. The cost of storing the tensor in the TT-format depends linearly on the number of dimensions. A major challenge is to find a suitable approximation to a tensor in the TT-format with sufficiently small TT-ranks. There are several algorithms to convert a tensor into TT-format, such as TT-SVD
[
29
]
and cross-approximation algorithms
[
13
,
31
,
32
]
.

This paper is focused on TT-rounding, which is a special case of TT approximation. Rounding a TT-tensor involves finding a compressed representation of a tensor which is already in TT-format, but perhaps with large ranks. This is an important problem with applications to parametric low-rank kernel approximation
[
21
]
, quantum physics
[
10
]
, high dimensional partial differential equations
[
37
]
, thermal radiation transport
[
15
]
, and the solution of linear tensor equations
[
7
]
.

Contributions

In this paper, we derive new randomized algorithms for rounding a tensor in the TT-format. The specific contributions of our work are:

1.

We propose two new randomized algorithms (Sections
3.2
and
3.3
) for rounding a TT-tensor. Both algorithms use a random tensor for sketching based on the Khatri-Rao product (KRP). The first algorithm assumes the target ranks are known
a priori
, whereas the second algorithm takes a prespecified tolerance for the relative error and adaptively determines the ranks.

2.

We analyze, in detail, the computational complexity of both algorithms (Sections
3.2
and
3.3
). While the proposed methods are comparable to existing randomized rounding algorithms, the use of KRPs enables adaptivity, which is missing in existing work.

3.

The adaptive randomized algorithm uses a termination criterion based on a randomized Frobenius norm estimator. We also provide an analysis for this estimator (Section
3.4
).

4.

Numerical experiments (Section
4
) on three different sets of applications—synthetic tensors, parametric kernel low-rank approximation, and numerical solution of a parametric partial differential equation—demonstrate the performance of the method. In these applications, we observe speedups of up to
50\times
compared to the deterministic TT-rounding techniques.

Additionally, the code required to reproduce our results is available at
https://github.com/bhisham123/TT-Rounding-using-KRP-structure.git
.

\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times(N\setminus n_{k})}

\|\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}\|=\left(\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}}a_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
and is equivalent to the Frobenius norm of any of its unfoldings, i.e.,
\|\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}\|=\|\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{(k)}\|_{F}
.

We use the symbol
\mathbin{\otimes}
to denote the Kronecker product of vectors or matrices and
\mathbin{\odot}
for the KRP of matrices. For matrices
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}

\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\mathbin{\otimes}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}&\cdots&a_{1n}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}&\cdots&a_{mn}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}\end{bmatrix}

The KRP is defined as a column-wise Kronecker product, i.e., if
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{1}&\dots&\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
and
\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{\mathbf{{b}}}}_{1}&\dots&\bm{{\mathbf{{b}}}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\mathbin{\odot}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{1}\mathbin{\otimes}\bm{{\mathbf{{b}}}}_{1}&\dots&\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{n}\mathbin{\otimes}\bm{{\mathbf{{b}}}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

2.2
Tensor Train

A

-way tensor

2.3
TT-Rounding

First proposed in
[
29
]
, TT rounding is the problem of computing a low-rank TT decomposition of an input already given in a higher-rank TT format. For the
fixed-precision approximation problem
, given suboptimal ranks
r_{k}
, we want to estimate the true ranks
r^{\prime}_{k}\leq r_{k}
while maintaining the prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon
. In the
fixed-rank
problem, given suboptimal ranks
r_{k}
we seek an approximation with target ranks
\ell_{k}
.

To illustrate the TT rounding procedure, we first consider an analogy for matrices. Let
\bm{{\mathbf{{Y}}}}=\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}^{T}
where
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}
is an
m\times r
matrix and
\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}
is
r\times n
with
r\leq\min\{m,n\}
. To obtain a low-rank approximation we could form the product
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}^{T}
and then compute a truncated SVD, but exploiting the multiplicative structure gives us computational advantages. The computation of a rank
\ell<r
approximation can be divided into an orthogonalization step followed by a compression phase. During the orthogonalization step, we want to make
\bm{{\mathbf{{Y}}}}
right orthogonal by computing the thin QR factorization,
\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}=\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{R}}}}
and setting
\bm{{\mathbf{{Z}}}}=\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{R}}}}^{T}
. This initial step gives
\bm{{\mathbf{{Y}}}}=\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}^{T}=\bm{{\mathbf{{Z}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}^{T}
, where
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}^{T}
has orthonormal rows. In the second step,
\bm{{\mathbf{{Z}}}}
is compressed to rank
l
via the SVD,
\bm{{\mathbf{{Z}}}}\approx\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{V}}}}_{Z}^{T}
. The low-rank approximation of
\bm{{\mathbf{{Y}}}}
is then given by
\bm{{\mathbf{{Y}}}}\approx\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}}(\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{V}}}}_{Z})^{T}=\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{V}}}}^{T}
. The classic TT rounding algorithm
[
29
]
is also divided into two phases, orthogonalization followed by compression.

We say a tensor is
right orthogonal
if all its horizontal unfoldings
\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k}\right)
have orthonormal rows, except the first core. We similarly say that a tensor is
left orthogonal
if all its vertical unfoldings
\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k}\right)
have orthonormal columns except the last core.

Right-to-Left orthogonalization
is the procedure in which a TT tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}
is transformed into an equivalent but right orthogonal TT tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}
. Starting from the last core, we compute the thin QR factorization
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{R}}}}=\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{d}\right)^{T}
and set the cores
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{d-1}
and
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{d}
as

\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{d-1}\right)\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{d}\right)=\underbrace{\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{d-1}\right)\bm{{\mathbf{{R}}}}^{T}}_{\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{d-1}\right)}\underbrace{\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}^{T}}_{\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{d}\right)}.

This procedure is continued through the cores from
d-1
to
2
. We could obtain instead a left orthogonal TT tensor similarly by processing the cores from left to right.

After obtaining a right-orthogonal tensor, we perform a compression step, processing the cores from left to right. For each mode, we compute a low-rank approximation of the vertical unfolding
\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)
via the SVD. For the fixed precision problem, the number of singular vectors to keep depends on the threshold
\tau=\frac{\|\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}\|}{\sqrt{d-1}}\varepsilon
. The vertical unfolding is set to
\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)=\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}
while the other two factors
\bm{{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{V}}}}^{T}
are combined with the horizontal unfolding of the next core
\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k+1}\right)
to obtain
\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k+1}\right)=\bm{{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{V}}}}^{T}\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k+1}\right)
. The process ends after
d-1
steps. Details are given in Algorithm

2.4
Randomized Matrix Algorithms

In this section, we discuss the randomized low-rank matrix approximation methods, particularly the randomized range finder and its adaptive version.

Randomized Range Finder

We briefly review the randomized range finder
[
17
, Algorithm 4.1]
. Let
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a given matrix with
m\geq n
, and we want to compute a rank-
r
approximation, where
r\leq n
. To do so, we first chose an oversampling parameter
p
and define
\ell=r+p
. We then generate a random matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
whose entries are independently and identically sampled from a standard normal distribution (with zero mean and unit variance). We refer to this matrix as a Gaussian random matrix.

Using the matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}
, we construct a sketch matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}=\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}
. We then compute the thin QR decomposition of
\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}
, yielding an orthonormal matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell}
whose columns form a basis for the range of
\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}
. If
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}
has rank close to
r
, or if its singular values decay rapidly after the
r
-th one, then the subspace spanned by the columns of
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}
provides a good approximation to the column space of
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}
. In this case,
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}\approx\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}^{T}\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}
and we use
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}^{T}\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}
as a low-rank approximation of
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}
. Assuming the matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}
is stored in a dense format, the computational cost of finding
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}
using this approach is
2mn\ell+O(m\ell^{2})
floating point operations (flops).

This procedure yields a rank-
\ell
approximation of
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}
. We can convert it to desired rank-
r
approximation by additional post-processing, refer to
[
17
]
for more details.

Adaptive Randomized Range Finder

We review the adaptive randomized range finder in
[
27
]
. Suppose we are given a matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and a tolerance
\epsilon
, and we want to find a low-rank approximation of
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}
that is accurate to within precision
\epsilon
. This formulation represents a more practical scenario compared to the fixed-rank setting, as in most applications we do not have prior knowledge of the singular value spectrum of
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}
that would allow us to determine an appropriate rank
r
in advance. The idea of adaptive range finder is to start with a low initial guess for the rank, run the range finder, and check if we are within the desired tolerance. If not, then draw more samples to enrich the basis until the required accuracy is achieved. Algorithm
2
gives a pseudocode of the adaptive range finder procedure.

3
Randomized TT Rounding Algorithms using KRPs

In this section, we propose fixed-rank (Algorithm
3.2
) and adaptive-rank (Algorithm
3.3
) randomized TT-rounding algorithms that utilize a sequence of random matrices with a KRP structure for sketching.

We give a high-level overview of both algorithms. The fixed-rank variant follows a procedure similar to that of the Randomize-then-Orthogonalize algorithm introduced by
[
4
]
, consisting of a randomization phase followed by an orthogonalization phase. The key distinction lies in the randomization step. In
[
4
]
a random Gaussian TT-tensor is employed to generate sketches, a TT-tensor whose cores
\bm{{\mathscr{{R}}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{k-1}\times n_{k}\times l_{k}}
are filled with independent, normally distributed entries with zero mean and variance
1/(\ell_{k-1}n_{k}\ell_{k})
. This is further explained in Section
. Our proposed approach utilizes random matrices with KRP structure for sketching, providing an alternative mechanism to compress the TT tensor efficiently. Our adaptive-rank algorithm applies the randomization followed by orthogonalization steps iteratively until the desired approximation tolerance is achieved. We discuss it in detail in Section
3.3
.
The randomization phase is a core component of both of our proposed algorithms. In this phase, we exploit the structural properties of the TT format along with the KRP structure of the random matrices to compute partial contractions efficiently. We begin with a discussion of this partial contraction (Section
3.1
).

Throughout this section, let
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
in TT format with TT ranks
\{r_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d-1}
and TT cores
\{\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
, so that we can write
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}=[\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{1},\dots,\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{d}]
. We want to find a compressed representation
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}=[\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{1},\dots,\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{d}]
, with ranks
\{\ell_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d-1}
and
\ell_{j}\leq r_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq d-1
. For the analysis, it is convenient to assume that
n_{1}=\dots=n_{d}=n
and the ranks are uniform, i.e.,
r_{1}=\dots=r_{d-1}=r
and
\ell_{1}=\dots=\ell_{d-1}=\ell
.

3.2
Fixed Rank Algorithm

In this algorithm, we assume that the target ranks
\{\ell_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d-1}
are known a priori.

As in the Randomize-then-Orthogonalize algorithm (Algorithm
9
), our proposed algorithm has two phases: a randomization phase, followed by an orthogonalization phase. In the randomization phase, we generate independent Gaussian random matrices
\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times\ell}
for
2\leq k\leq d
, where
\ell=\max_{1\leq k\leq d-1}\ell_{k}
. Next, we perform an efficient contraction between the KRP of these random matrices and the TT-tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}
, as described in Algorithm
3
, to compute the partial contraction matrices
\{\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{k}\}_{k=2}^{d}
. These steps constitute the randomization phase of the algorithm.

In the orthogonalization phase, we construct a left-orthogonal compressed TT-tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}
, and it is similar to the orthogonalization phase of Algorithm
9
. In Step 1,
k=1
and consider the unfolding
\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{(1)}=\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{1}\right)\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{2}\right)(\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{3:d}\right)\otimes\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}})
where
\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}}
is an identity matrix of appropriate size.
We compute the sketch

\displaystyle\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{1}=

\displaystyle\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{(1)}\left(\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{d}(:,1:\ell_{1})\mathbin{\odot}\cdots\mathbin{\odot}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{2}(:,1:\ell_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{1}\right)\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{2}(:,1:\ell_{1}).

We compute a thin-QR factorization
\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{1}=\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{1}\bm{{\mathbf{{R}}}}_{1}
, and compute an approximation

\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{(1)}\approx\underbrace{\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{1}}_{\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{1}\right)}\underbrace{\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{1}^{T}\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{1}\right)\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{2}\right)}_{\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{2}\right)}(\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{3:d}\right)\otimes\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}}).

We set
\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{1}\right)=\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{2}\right)=(\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{1}^{T}\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{1}\right))\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{2}\right)
. Proceeding iteratively, we have the approximation

\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{(1:k)}\approx(\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}}\otimes\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{1:k-1}\right))\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+1}\right)(\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+2:d}\right)\otimes\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}}),\quad 2\leq k\leq d-1.

At the
k
-th step, since the first
k-1
cores of
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}
have already been orthogonalized, the matrix
(\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}}\otimes\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{1:k-1}\right))
has orthonormal columns and does not need to be further compressed.
We compute the sketch
\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}
and its thin-QR factorization as

\displaystyle\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+1}\right)(\left(\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+2:d}\right)\otimes\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}})\left(\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{d}(:,1:\ell_{k})\mathbin{\odot}\cdots\mathbin{\odot}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{k+1}(:,1:\ell_{k})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{k+1}(:,1:\ell_{k})=\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{R}}}}_{k}.

Thus, we have the approximation for a fixed
k

\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}_{(1:k)}\approx(\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}}\otimes\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{1:k-1}\right))\underbrace{\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}}_{\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)}\underbrace{\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{T}\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+1}\right)}_{\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k+1}\right)}(\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+2:d}\right)\otimes\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}}).

The TT-cores are then updated by overwriting

3.3
Adaptive Rank Algorithm

We want to find a compressed TT-tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}
while maintaining the prescribed relative error
\varepsilon
, i.e.,
\|\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}-\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}\|\leq\varepsilon\|\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}\|
. Our adaptive rank algorithm, Algorithm
6
, is motivated by the adaptive randomized range finder, Algorithm
2
. It incrementally builds the core tensors
\{\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
of the left-orthogonal compressed TT-tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}
by combining the randomized range finder and a blocked Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization scheme. We use an estimate of the norm of the residual as the stopping criterion for terminating the iterations in the range finder.

The algorithm requires user-defined parameters
0<f_{\rm init}<1
,
0<f_{\rm inc}<1
representing fractions of the input ranks to be used as an initial sketching sizes and incremental sketching sizes, respectively.
In numerical experiments, we use
f_{\rm init}=0.1
and
f_{\rm inc}=0.05
, but these can be tuned in applications.
First, we generate independent Gaussian random matrices
\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times r}
for
2\leq k\leq d
, where
r=\lceil\max\{r_{k}\}\cdot f_{\rm init}\rceil
.
Then, using these random matrices, we compute the partial contraction matrices
\{\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{k}\}_{k=2}^{d}
by invoking Algorithm
3
.
We evenly distribute the required accuracy
\varepsilon
across the TT-cores
\{\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d-1}
and denote the resulting modewise threshold by
\tau=\varepsilon\|\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}\|/\sqrt{d-1}
. A justification for this allocation is given in
[
6
, Theorem 8.6]
. We begin with
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{1}=\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{1}
. The process of computing the left orthonormal compressed TT-core
\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}
for mode
k
can be divided into two phases for each
k
, where
1\leq k\leq d-1
.

Phase 1: Initialization
This phase consists of computing the
b_{\rm init}^{(k)}
orthonormal basis

b_{\rm init}^{(k)}=\lceil f_{\rm init}\cdot\bar{r}_{k}\rceil,

where
\bar{r}_{k}
denotes the minimum of the number of rows and columns in the updated core
\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)
.
The first phase of the algorithm to initialize the low-rank factors essentially follows the fixed rank case (Algorithm
3
) with
\ell_{k}=b_{\rm init}^{(k)}
.
At line
10
, we compute the sketch matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}
using partial contraction
\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{k+1}
as

\displaystyle\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\underbrace{\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+1:d}\right)(\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{d}(:,1:b_{\rm init}^{(k)})\odot\cdots\odot\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{k+1}(:,1:b_{\rm init}^{(k)}))}_{\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{k+1}(:,1:b_{\rm init}^{(k)})}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{k+1}(:,1:b_{\rm init}^{(k)}).

The QR decomposition of the sketch matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}

yields the orthonormal basis of dimension
b_{\rm init}^{(k)}
, which we denote by
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}
and set
\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k+1}\right)=\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{T}\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+1}\right)
.

Phase 2: Iteration
Let
b_{\text{inc}}^{(k)}
be the incremental block size for
1\leq k\leq d-1
. We define it as

b_{\rm inc}^{(k)}=\lceil f_{\text{inc}}\cdot\bar{r}_{k}\rceil,

where
\bar{r}_{k}
is the minimum of the number of rows and columns in the core
\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)
.
To expand the orthonormal basis, we compute the residual sketch
\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}
and update the partial contraction matrices
\{\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{j}\}_{j=k+1}^{d}
by invoking Algorithm
5
. We explain the working of Algorithm
5
as follows: if the partial contraction matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{k+1}
does not contain
b_{\rm inc}^{(k)}
additional columns (beyond those already used), we generate new random Gaussian matrices
\{\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{j}\}_{j=k+1}^{d}
and construct new partial contractions
\{\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{j}^{\text{new}}\}_{j=k+1}^{d}
using Algorithm
3
, and concatenate them with the existing contraction matrices:

\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{j}\leftarrow\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{j}&\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{j}^{\text{new}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{for all }j=k+1,\ldots,d.

We then form the next sketch matrix:

\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}\leftarrow\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{k+1}(:,\texttt{col}(\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k})+1:\texttt{col}(\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k})+b_{\rm inc}^{(k)}),

(10)

where
\texttt{col}(\cdot)
denotes the current number of columns in the matrix, and orthogonalize it against the existing basis to get a residual sketch:

\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}\leftarrow\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}-\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}\left(\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{T}\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}\right).

(11)

The quantity
\|\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}\|_{F}/\sqrt{b_{\rm inc}^{(k)}}
provides an unbiased estimate of the Frobenius norm of the residual error matrix.
A detailed analysis of this estimate is provided in Section
3.4
.
If the estimate exceeds
\tau
, we compute the QR decomposition of the residual sketch to get new orthonormal directions

\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{k}=\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{new}\,\bm{{\mathbf{{R}}}}_{k}^{new}.

We reorthogonalize the directions in
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{new}
with respect to the current basis
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}
:

\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{new}\leftarrow\texttt{QR}\left(\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{new}-\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}\left(\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{T}\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{new}\right)\right).

This helps to mitigate numerical errors and ensures that the updated basis
\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}\leftarrow\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}&\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{new}\end{bmatrix}
remains numerically orthonormal
[
28
, Section 4.3]
.
We then efficiently update the next core by utilizing the
\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k+1}\right)
computed so far in the previous steps as follows:

\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k+1}\right)\leftarrow\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{T}\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+1}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k+1}\right)\\
\left(\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}^{new}\right)^{T}\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)\mathcal{H}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{k+1}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

We repeat step 2 until the residual norm falls below the threshold
\tau
or the maximum possible rank
\bar{r}_{k}
(i.e., minimum of the number of rows and columns in
\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)
) is reached and set
\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{Y}}}}_{k}\right)=\bm{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{k}
.

See Algorithm
6
for a detailed pseudocode of the above procedure.

.

Remark 1
(Input tensor norm estimation).

In Algorithm
6
, we assume that the norm of the input tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}
is known.
If this is not the case, computing it exactly via orthogonalization or TT inner product requires as much computation as deterministic TT rounding
[
3
]
.
Instead, we can estimate it using the partial contraction computed in line
5
as follows:

\|\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}\|\approx\frac{\|\mathcal{V}\left(\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}_{1}\right)\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{2}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{r}}.

(12)

where
r
denotes the sketch size (number of columns in
\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}_{2}
). An analysis of the norm estimation as well as the residual error estimation in the While loop of Algorithm
6
is given in Section
3.4
.

Remark 2
(Additional Compression).

For
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
, Algorithm
6
incrementally enriches the initially computed orthogonal basis by a block of size
b_{\text{inc}}^{(k)}
until the residual norm falls below the threshold
\tau
. There is the possibility that the ranks of the compressed tensor produced by this algorithm may exceed those obtained from the deterministic TT-rounding algorithm (Algorithm
1
) due to either a large incremental block size or an inaccurate estimate of the residual norm.
The cores of the compressed tensor produced by Algorithm
6
are left-orthonormal (except the last core).
We can reduce the ranks by performing a compression pass on the tensor cores, similar to that in the deterministic TT-rounding algorithm (Algorithm
1
, Lines
7
–
13
), but in the opposite direction, i.e., for
k=d
down to
2
, with truncation threshold
\tau
.

Remark 3
(Sum of TT-tensors).

The adaptive rank algorithm (Algorithm
6
) can be tailored to inputs that are sums of TT tensors.
One approach to handling such inputs is to compute a formal sum of the TT tensors, resulting in a TT tensor whose ranks are the sums of the TT summand ranks but whose cores have sparse structure, and then apply a rounding algorithm.
Instead, we can exploit the property of our randomized algorithm that the sketch of the sum is the sum of the sketches.
That is, we can sketch each TT summand individually using the approach of Algorithm
3
and then accumulate the result efficiently.
We omit the details of this case because it follows the approach described in
[
4
, Section 3.3]
; the only difference for the fixed-rank case is in how the partial contractions are computed to exploit the KRP structure of the sketches.
We also incorporate error estimation and basis augmentation to enable adaptivity, as in the case of a single TT tensor input.
We demonstrate the efficiency of this approach in Algorithm
4.3
.

In the adaptive-rank algorithm (Algorithm
6
), the extra computational overhead compared to the fixed-rank algorithm (Algorithm
5
) arises from the orthogonalization step (Line
12
of Algorithm
5
) and the re-orthogonalization step (Line
18
of Algorithm
6
). However, both of these steps involve operations on smaller matrices and introduce only a minor overhead. As a result, the leading-order computational cost remains the same for both the fixed-rank and adaptive-rank algorithms.

3.4
Analysis of norm estimate

Here, we analyze the accuracy of estimating the Frobenius norm of a matrix using randomized sketches based on KRPs of Gaussian matrices. In the following theorem, we set
N=\prod_{k=1}^{d}n_{k}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
and
\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}=\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{d}\mathbin{\odot}\cdots\mathbin{\odot}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{2}\mathbin{\odot}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{1}
, where each
\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times\ell}
is Gaussian random matrix and
d>1
. Then
\frac{1}{\ell}\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}\|_{F}^{2}
gives an unbiased estimate of the
\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
0<\delta\leq 1
. If the number of samples
\ell
satisfies

\ell\geq 4\max\big(\varepsilon^{-1}C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\ln^{d}(2/\delta),\,\varepsilon^{-2}{C_{\alpha}^{\prime}}^{2}\ln^{2}(2/\delta)\big),

where
C_{\alpha}^{\prime}
and
C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}
are constants depending only on
\alpha\equiv 1/d
, then with probability at least
1-\delta

\left|\frac{1}{\ell}\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}\|_{F}^{2}-\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}\right|\leq\varepsilon\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}.

We introduce some concepts from probability used in the proof of Theorem
4
. Let
X
be a random variable. For
p\geq 1
, the
L^{p}
norm is defined as
\|X\|_{L^{p}}=(\mathbb{E}|X|^{p})^{1/p}
. Let
\alpha>0
,
X
is an
\alpha
–sub-exponential random variable if for
t>0
,
\text{Pr}\left\{|X|\geq t\right\}\leq c\,\text{exp}(-Ct^{\alpha})
, where
c
and
C
are two absolute constants. The quasi-norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Psi_{\alpha}}
is defined as

\|X\|_{\Psi_{\alpha}}=\text{inf}\left\{t>0\;|\;\mathbb{E}\text{exp}\left(|X|^{\alpha}/t^{\alpha}\right)\leq 2\right\}.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

We can easily prove that the estimator is unbiased by showing that
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\ell}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}\|_{F}^{2}\right]=\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}
. The proof of the remaining part is split into two steps. In the first step, we define the sequence of independent random variables
X_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq\ell
, and some properties of the random variables. In the second step, we establish concentration inequalities for the sum of the random variables.

Step 1: Defining the random variables

Define the sequence of independent random variables

\displaystyle X_{i}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}}\left(\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}^{(i)}\|^{2}-\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left((\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{j}\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}^{(i)})^{2}-\|\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right),

for
1\leq i\leq\ell
, where
\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}
denotes the
j
-th row of
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}
and
\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
represent the
i
-th column of
\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}
.
Next, we will analyze the properties of
X_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq\ell
.

Let
\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}=\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}_{d}
where
\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times 1}
for
k=\{1,\ldots,d\}
with
i.i.d.
entries sampled from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. For any arbitrary vector
\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n_{k}}
, by
[
34
, Lemma 3.4.2]
,
\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}_{k}
is subgaussian with norm
\|\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}_{k}\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq C_{S}K\|\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{k}\|_{2}
, where
C_{S}
is an absolute constant and
K
is bound on subgaussian entries of
\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}^{(k)}
. For the standard normal distribution,
K=1
. Next, due to
[
34
, Section 2.5.2]
, we have

\|\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}_{k}\|_{L^{p}}\leq C_{S}\sqrt{p}\|\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{k}\|_{2}\qquad\forall~\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}},\>1\leq j\leq d,\>p\geq 1.

Using
[
1
, Lemma 19]
, whose assumptions we just verified, we have that for any arbitrary vector
\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}

\|\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}\|_{L^{p}}\leq(C_{S}\sqrt{p})^{d}\|\bm{{\mathbf{{y}}}}\|_{2},\qquad\forall p\geq 1.

(13)

By using the triangle inequality and the centering property of
L^{p}
norms
[
18
, Lemma 47]
, we have

\displaystyle\|X_{i}\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|(\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{j}\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}^{(i)})^{2}-\|\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq\frac{2}{\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|(\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{j}\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}^{(i)})^{2}\right\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|\bm{{\mathbf{{a}}}}_{j}\bm{{\mathbf{{\omega}}}}^{(i)}\right\|_{L^{2p}}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}}(C_{s}\sqrt{2p})^{2d}\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}=2\,(C_{s}\sqrt{2})^{2d}\,p^{d}.

As
\|X_{i}\|_{L^{p}}\leq 2(C_{s}\sqrt{2})^{2d}p^{d}
implies
X_{i}
is an
\alpha
–sub-exponential random variable with
\alpha=\frac{1}{d}
. More precisely, by
[
16
, Lemma A.2]

\|X_{i}\|_{\Psi_{\alpha}}\leq(2e)^{d}2(C_{S}\sqrt{2})^{2d}\equiv C_{d}.

Step 2: Concentration inequality

Let
\bm{{\mathbf{{b}}}}:=\frac{1}{\ell}(C_{d},\ldots,C_{d})
be an
\ell
dimensional vector, then
\|\bm{{\mathbf{{b}}}}\|_{2}=C_{d}/\sqrt{\ell}
and
\|\bm{{\mathbf{{b}}}}\|_{\infty}=C_{d}/\ell
. For
\alpha=1/d<1
, by
[
25
, Theorem 3.1]
, we have

\text{Pr}\left\{\left|\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i}\right|\geq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{\ell}}+C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\frac{t^{d}}{\ell}\right\}\leq 2e^{-t}\quad\text{ for }t\geq 0

where the constants
C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\equiv 2eC(\alpha)C_{d}
,
C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\equiv\sqrt{2}e4^{d}C(\alpha)C_{d}
and

C(\alpha)\equiv 4e^{3}(2\pi)^{1/4}e^{1/24}(e^{2/e}/\alpha)^{1/\alpha}

depend only on
\alpha=1/d
. Setting the failure probability
2e^{-t}=\delta
implies
t=\ln(2/\delta)
. Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\left|\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i}\right|\leq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{\ell}}+C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\frac{\ln^{d}(2/\delta)}{\ell}.

Setting
\varepsilon\geq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{\ell}}+C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\frac{\ln^{d}(2/\delta)}{\ell}
, we get an inequality
\ell\geq\beta+\gamma\sqrt{\ell}
, where
\beta=\varepsilon^{-1}C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\ln^{d}(2/\delta)
and
\gamma=\varepsilon^{-1}C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\ln(2/\delta)
. We can solve the equality
\ell=\beta+\gamma\sqrt{\ell}
for positive root
\sqrt{\ell}
as

\sqrt{\ell}_{+}=\gamma/2+\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+4\beta}/2.

The function
\ell-\gamma\sqrt{\ell}-\beta
is a parabola that is concave upward in
\ell
and crossing the real line at the root
\sqrt{\ell}_{+}
.
For
\sqrt{\ell}\geq\sqrt{\ell}_{+}
,
\ell-\gamma\sqrt{\ell}-\beta\geq 0
, so we can take
\sqrt{\ell}\geq 2\max\{\sqrt{\beta},\gamma\}
or
\ell\geq 4\max\{\beta,\gamma^{2}\}
. This gives the minimal number of samples
\ell
as stated in the theorem.

The above theorem gives a relative error bound for the performance of the norm estimation. The corollary below is more useful to get an order-of-magnitude estimate of the norm.

Corollary 5
.

Let
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}
and
\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}
be defined as in Theorem
4
. Let
\zeta>1
be a user-defined parameter and
0<\delta<1
be the failure probability. If the number of samples
\ell
satisfies

\ell\geq 4\max\left(C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\frac{\zeta}{\zeta-1}\ln^{d}(2/\delta),\frac{\zeta^{2}}{(\zeta-1)^{2}}{C_{\alpha}^{\prime}}^{2}\ln^{2}(2/\delta)\right),

where
C_{\alpha}^{\prime}
and
C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}
are constants depending only on
\alpha\equiv 1/d
, then with probability at least
1-\delta

\frac{1}{\zeta}\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\ell}\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\zeta\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof.

Since
\zeta>1
, set
1-\varepsilon=\zeta^{-1}<1
. From Theorem
4
, with probability at least
1-\delta

\zeta^{-1}\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}=(1-\varepsilon)\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\ell}\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{\Omega}}}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\zeta\|\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\|_{F}^{2}.

The last inequality follows since
\varepsilon>0
, so
1-\varepsilon^{2}<1
and
1+\varepsilon<\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}=\zeta
. This completes the proof.

Kernel matrices arise in many applications including machine learning, inverse problems, spatial statistics, etc. Kernel matrices are typically large and dense and computationally challenging to work with. In recent work

While

We propose to use the TT-rounding algorithms proposed in this paper.
We show that for problems with a specified accuracy required (i.e., rank adaptivity), our proposed Algorithm

10\times

[

5
Conclusion

We introduce a new adaptive randomized TT-rounding algorithm where sketching employs matrices with KRP structure.
Underpinned with statistical analysis of the error estimation to provide adaptiveness, the proposed algorithm demonstrates comparable accuracy compared to the deterministic TT-SVD algorithm while achieving up to
50\times
speedup on a number of test cases arising from synthetic data, parameter-dependent PDE, and parameter-dependent kernel matrices.
Compared to previously proposed randomized fixed-rank algorithms, a slight overhead is required in order to guarantee a satisfactory accuracy as demonstrated in our numerical experiments.

We observe a huge benefit from using the randomized TT-rounding in order to compress a sum of TT tensors without assembling the sum prior to compression.
This avoids a massive amount of data movement and therefore results in a fast fused-summation-and-compression algorithm allowing solvers such as TT-GMRES to be practical for tensor equations.

Another observation regarding sketching using matrices with KRP structure is that a significant part of computing the sketches can be performed in parallel with respect to the tensor cores. This can potentially have a significant impact on the derivation of parallel algorithms for TT rounding especially with the intrinsic sequential paradigm of TT-SVD to keep the complexity linear in the number of modes. We aim to address this in future work. Another area of future work is to extend our approach to exploit the structure of MPO-MPS compression as in
[
10
]
.

Appendix D
Rounding of the sum of TT Tensor

The summation of TT tensors is a common operation in TT arithmetic, and it inherently increases the ranks of the resulting tensor. Hence, it often relies on TT rounding, where the goal is to compress a tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}
that is given as a sum of
s
TT tensors:
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}^{(1)}+\cdots+\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}^{(s)}
. A naive approach to compressing the sum of TT tensors is to first form their formal sum, resulting in a TT tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}
with ranks equal to the sums of the individual TT ranks and cores exhibiting a sparse block structure. Then round
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}
using rounding algorithms. However, this method is computationally expensive, exhibiting cubic scaling in the number of summands
s
when using classical TT rounding (Algorithm
1
), and quadratic scaling for randomized methods (Algorithms
9
,
4
,
6
). As
s
grows large, this becomes computationally infeasible in practice.

To handle this, we can exploit the property of the randomized sketching algorithm that the sketch of the sum is the sum of the sketches as suggested in
[
4
]
. That is, we can sketch each TT summand individually using Algorithm
3
and then accumulate the result efficiently. We omit the details of this case because it follows the approach described in
[
4
, Section 3.3]
; the only difference for the fixed-rank case is in how the partial contractions are computed to exploit the KRP structure.

This idea naturally extends to the adaptive-rank variant (Algorithm
6
), which aims to maintain a prescribed relative error when the target TT ranks are not known a priori. The detailed pseudocode of this extension is presented in the Algorithm
11
.

Appendix E
Problem setup

In this section, we give additional details of the problem setups in the Numerical Experiments (Section
4
).

E.1
Problem setup: Parametric kernel low-rank approximation

In this section, we give some of the details of the problem setup in Section
4.2
.

Given two sets of points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{I_{s}}\}
and
Y=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{I_{t}}\}
, called source and target points respectively, and a kernel function
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot;\theta):\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
, a kernel matrix
K(X,Y;\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{s}\times I_{t}}
has entries

[K(X,Y;\theta)]_{i,j}=\kappa(x_{i},y_{j};\theta)\qquad 1\leq i\leq I_{s},1\leq j\leq I_{t}.

The (hyper)parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
control the behavior of the kernel.

In this application, we focus on the Matérn kernel. Given parameters
\theta=(\ell,\nu)
, where
\ell
is the length scale and
\nu
controls the smoothness of the kernel, the Matérn kernel function is defined as

\kappa(x,y;\theta)=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\frac{r}{\ell}\right)^{\nu}\mathcal{K}_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\frac{r}{\ell}\right),

where
\mathcal{K}_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind and
r=\|x-y\|_{2}
. This kernel is an example of an isotropic kernel.

We consider the kernel function
\kappa(x,y;\theta)
defined over a source box
C_{s}=[0,1]^{3}
and a target box
C_{t}=[2,3]^{3}
, where the source and target point sets
X\subseteq C_{s}
and
Y\subseteq C_{t}
. The kernel parameters
\theta=(\ell,\nu)
vary over a 2-dimensional parameter space, with the length scale
\ell
sampled over the interval
[c\cdot D_{b},D_{b}]
and the smoothness
\nu
over
[0.5,3]
. Here,

D_{b}=\|(b,b,b)-(0,0,0)\|_{2}=\sqrt{3}\,b\approx 0.173,

where
b=0.1
defines the extent of the parameter domain, and
c=0.5
controls the lower bound of
\ell
. Thus, the kernel (hyper)parameters satisfy
\theta\in\Theta\subset C_{\theta}
, where
C_{\theta}=[c\cdot D_{b},D_{b}]\times[0.5,3]
.

In this application, we only focus on the compression of the coefficient tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{M}}}}
, as defined in
[
21
, Section 2.4, below equation (2.8)]
. Essentially, it involves the kernel evaluations at Chebyshev grid points of the first kind on domain
C_{s}\times C_{\theta}\times C_{t}
. Note that the compression of
\bm{{\mathscr{{M}}}}
is an important step in PTTK that affects many downstream computations. The respective domains
C_{s},C_{t},C_{\theta}
are discretized using
n=100
Chebyshev nodes of the first kind in each spatial dimension. This results in an 8-way coefficient tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{M}}}}
(3 each for spatial variables
x
and
y
, and
2
for the parameter variables) with each mode size
n=100
.
This high-dimensional tensor is then compressed using a greedy TT-cross approximation with a relative Chebyshev norm tolerance of
10^{-12}
, resulting in a compact tensor-train (TT) representation of the parameterized Matérn kernel. We call the resultant tensor-train representation the Matérn kernel tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{X}}}}
and use it as input to the baseline algorithms in our experimental study.

E.2
Problem Setup: Parametric PDE in the TT format

Consider a square domain
\Omega
with
d
mutually disjoint discs
D_{i}
aligned on a grid. Solving the following PDE for all parameter values
\bm{\rho}=\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
simultaneously is a
d+2
dimensional problem

\displaystyle-\text{div}(\sigma(x,y;\bm{\rho})\nabla(u(x,y;\bm{\rho}))

\displaystyle=f(x,y)

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega

\displaystyle u(x,y;\bm{\rho})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\delta\Omega,

where, for
1\leq i\leq d
,

\displaystyle\sigma(x,y;\bm{\rho})=\begin{cases}1+\rho_{i}&(x,y)\in D_{i}\\
1&\text{ elsewhere}.\end{cases}

Let
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}_{1,1}
be the operator discretized over the domain with constant parameter values, for
2\leq i\leq d
,
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}_{i,1}
the discretization of the operator over the domain times the characteristic function corresponding to the subdomain,
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}_{i,i}
the diagonal matrix with parameter values,
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}_{i,j}
the identity matrix for
j\neq i
. Then we can consider the linear system encapsulating the parametric PDE

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}_{i,1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}_{i,d}\right)\bm{{\mathscr{{U}}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{{F}}}},

where
\bm{{\mathscr{{U}}}}
is the discretized solution tensor representing
u(x,y;\bm{\rho})
over the spatial and parameter domains, and
\bm{{\mathscr{{F}}}}
is the discretized right-hand side tensor representing
f(x,y)
.

In our numerical experiments, we follow the setup from
[
4
, Section 4.2]
with
d=5
parameter dimensions and a piecewise linear finite element discretization. We vary the number of parameter samples
n=n_{2}=\cdots=n_{d}
over the values
\{2^{3},2^{4},\ldots,2^{8}\}
, with
\rho_{i}
values linearly spaced between 1 and 10.
