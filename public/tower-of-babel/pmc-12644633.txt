Impact of multiple steam sterilizations on the mechanical properties of 3D-printed surgical items
Authors: Magdalena Antonowicz-Hüpsch, Piotr Trębacz, Lisa Adele Piras, Agata Piątek, Angelika Auguścik, Klaudia Cholewa, Wiktoria Groelich, Mateusz Pawlik, Luca Manassero, Aleksandra Kurkowska, Anna Barteczko, Davide Mancusi, Michał Czopowicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates how repeated steam sterilization affects the mechanical properties of 3D-printed surgical items made from methacrylate-based Surgical Guide Resin. Unlike single-use instruments, reusable 3D-printed instruments undergo multiple sterilization cycles, which may compromise their durability. Cuboids produced using Digital Light Processing (DLP) were exposed to up to ten steam sterilization cycles (121 °C, 110 kPa), followed by drying. Mechanical tests included three-point bending, Shore D hardness, and microscopic analysis to assess elasticity, hardness, and structural changes. Initial sterilization cycles increased hardness and stiffness due to further polymer cross-linking. However, continued exposure led to visible surface damage, microcracks, and signs of polymer degradation. While bending strength remained relatively stable, cumulative effects ultimately reduced material integrity. These results raise concerns about the long-term reliability of multi-use 3D-printed surgical instruments. The findings suggest that current sterilization practices should be re-evaluated, especially for items intended for repeated use. Future research should focus on improving material resistance to minimize thermal degradation. This work offers critical insights into the safety and performance of reusable 3D-printed surgical items in both veterinary and human medical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25561-9.

Introduction

Additive manufacturing has transformed surgical instrumentation, enabling precise and cost-effective manufacturing of surgical instruments such as guides1,2. These instruments are also increasingly used in veterinary surgery3–6. The universal drill or saw guides can improve surgical precision and efficiency, particularly for breed-specific conditions, e.g., treatment of intracondylar humeral fractures in Beagles, Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, and feline femoral neck fractures7. While their benefits include reduced surgery time and improved postoperative outcomes, their durability after multiple sterilization cycles remains a concern8.

Steam sterilization, commonly used for reusable surgical instruments, involves high-temperature steam at 121 °C and 110 kPa to eliminate microbial contamination9. However, repeated exposure to such conditions can degrade polymer-based materials, causing structural changes such as brittleness, loss of flexibility, and surface defects10. Studies indicate prolonged sterilization can lead to polymer chain scission and microstructural deterioration, compromising surgical precision.

Despite the wide range of patient sizes in veterinary medicine, there are procedures for which universal 3D-printed items can be used, e.g., 3D-printed drill guides11,12 or arthroscopic instruments13. At the same time, many 3D-printed surgical guides are custom-designed based on patient-specific imaging data (CT or MRI), allowing for precise anatomical fit for the planned osteotomy. Although these items are typically intended for single use, re-sterilization and in some cases, multiple sterilization cycles may be required under certain clinical circumstances. Common scenarios include surgical rescheduling due to anesthesia or logistical issues, accidental breach of sterile packaging, or preoperative test fitting. However, their long-term mechanical integrity remains an underexplored area. In veterinary medicine, surgical instruments are most commonly printed from polylactic acid (PLA) or photopolymer resin, with the latter often selected for reusable guides due to its superior dimensional accuracy, mechanical strength, and sterilization resistance11,14,15. Although veterinary surgical instruments lack formal regulatory classification, human medical standards emphasize stringent sterility and material stability requirements. This further reinforces the need for evidence-based evaluation of 3D-printed instruments in veterinary applications.

Additive manufacturing encompasses a broad spectrum of technologies that differ in feedstock form, energy source, resolution, and mechanical performance. Among the most relevant techniques for medical applications are fused deposition modeling (FDM), stereolithography (SLA), and digital light processing (DLP). FDM relies on the extrusion of thermoplastic filament, layer by layer, through a heated nozzle. SLA and DLP, by contrast, are resin-based technologies that cure liquid photopolymer using light. SLA uses a laser that scans the resin surface point by point, while DLP projects an entire layer pattern at once onto the resin surface, curing the whole layer simultaneously. This layer-wide exposure enables faster printing and high-resolution output16.This study investigates how repeated steam sterilization impacts the mechanical properties of 3D-printed cuboids fabricated from Surgical Guide Resin, a popular material used in the additive manufacturing of surgical items17,18. Through mechanical testing, including modulus of elasticity, hardness measurements, and microscopic analysis, this research aims to establish best practices for sterilization protocols, optimize material performance, and enhance patient safety in both veterinary and human applications.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The test samples used in this study were cuboids-shaped speciments measuring 60 × 10 × 3 mm, fabricated from SG-100 Surgical Guide Resin (Graphy Inc, Seoul, South Korea). The STL models were prepared for printing using CHITUBOX slicing software (CBD-Tech, Shenzhen, China), which was used to generate layer-by-layer exposure patterns and configure print parameters such as layer height and exposure time. The samples were manufactured using the Digital Light Processing (DLP) additive manufacturing method on the 22 μm XY resolution Phrozen Sonic Mini 8 K Resin 3D Printer (PHROZEN TECH CO., LTD., Hsinchu, Taiwan). The chosen dimensions and shape of the samples represent a critical part of surgical guides designed for corrective osteotomies, specifically planar guides for saw blade cutting (Fig. 1). Printing parameters included a layer height of 100 μm and exposure time of 10 s per layer, according to resin manufacturer technical data sheet. All samples underwent post-processing, which included mechanical removal of printed supports, rinsing in isopropyl alcohol and post-curing in nitrogen rich atmosphere within UV light chamber for 5 min (Curie Plus nFinity, Ackuretta, Taipei, Taiwan).

Sterilization procedure

The samples underwent steam sterilization in an autoclave (Sun 12-II, Ningbo Mingtai Medical Instrument Co. Ltd., Ningbo, China). Samples were packaged separately for each cycle. The sterilization process was conducted at a temperature of 121 °C under a pressure of 110 kPa, consistent with standard clinical practices. Actual temperature during sterilization phase ranged from 121.1 ± 0.1 to 121.9 ± 0.0 °C. Actual pressure was 112.1 ± 3.7 kPa at the moment of minimum temperature and 114.3 ± 2.7 kPa at the moment of maximum temperature. To simulate real-world scenarios where additional sterilization might be required due to instrument use in many surgical procedures, delayed surgeries, or compromised sterile packaging. 33 samples were divided into 11 sterilization groups, each counting 3 elements and subjected to a specified number of sterilization cycles (ranging from 0 to 10). After each sterilization cycle lasting 20 min, the samples were dried for 15 min to remove excess moisture before proceeding with subsequent cycles. This iterative sterilization and drying protocol was designed to ensure an accurate assessment of the cumulative effects of multiple sterilizations on the material properties of the surgical guides.

Mechanical testing

To evaluate the effect of sterilization on the mechanical properties of the samples, a static three-point bending test was performed in accordance with PN-EN ISO 178:201919.

This test was performed at a constant speed of 1 mm/min, due to the sample thickness of 3 mm, at room temperature, using the MTS Criterion testing machine, Model 45 (MTS Systems Corporation, Minnesota, USA). The distance between the supports was 48 mm, and the diameters of the rollers on which the sample was placed were as follows: upper roller – 5 mm, lower rollers – 4 mm.Five samples were tested from each sterilization group.

Shore D hardness test

Shore D hardness test assessed the surface hardness of the samples, providing insights into their resistance to surface deformation. For each sterilization group, three samples were tested, and measurements were taken at ten points on each sample to ensure uniformity across the entire surface. The measurements were performed using a Shore D durometer (Shore D/100HD, SAUTER, Balingen, Germany) with a measurement range of 100 HD and a resolution of 1.0 HD.

Microscopic analysis

High-resolution optical microscopy was used to examine the samples for surface defects, including cracks, discoloration, and other microstructural changes induced by repeated sterilization. Microscopic images were obtained using Leica DVM6 (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Numerical variables (properties of samples) were presented as the arithmetic mean and standard deviation (SD). The whole analyzed group counted 33 samples – 3 samples examined before sterilization and 3 samples in each of 10 sterilization cycles. Normality of data distribution and equality of variance were evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test and the Brown-Forsythe test, respectively. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to identify significant differences between sterilization cycles, with post-hoc analysis using Dunnett’s test to compare sterilization cycles 1–10 to the baseline values (before sterilization). Statistical significance (α) was set at 0.05, and all statistical tests were two-sided. Analyses were performed in TIBCO Statistica 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA).

Discussion

This study demonstrates the significant impact of multiple steam sterilization cycles on the mechanical properties of the cuboids printed from resin, commonly used for printing surgical items, highlighting the effects of thermal and hydrolytic stress on polymer-based surgical instrumentation. The use of reusable 3D-printed guides and instruments is not well documented in either human or veterinary surgical literature. Most printed items are designed as patient-specific and intended for single use, undergoing only one sterilization cycle prior to surgery. In one of the few available studies, a set of reusable surgical instruments, including hemostats, needle holders, scalpel handles, retractors, and thumb forceps, was printed and then tested on cadavers, demonstrating that functional 3D-printed surgical instruments are feasible20. Reported advantages over conventional manufacturing included cost neutrality despite increasing complexity, faster design-to-production times, and the ability to implement surgeon-specific modifications. However, the study did not evaluate the mechanical performance of these instruments following sterilization. Another investigation presented a 3D-printed Army/Navy retractor fabricated from polylactic acid (PLA), which proved sufficiently strong for surgical use21. The reported production cost per unit was approximately one-tenth that of a comparable stainless steel instrument. The instruments were chemically sterilized using glutaraldehyde; autoclaving was avoided due to concerns about thermal damage and structural degradation of PLA.

In our study, we demonstrated that the mechanical properties of cuboids printed from resins, such as bending strength, remained stable after autoclaving. At the same time, hardness and modulus of elasticity exhibited notable changes. These variations reflect the balance between material composition and sterilization effects, where long-term structural degradation offsets early rigidity improvements. The observed increase in hardness and modulus of elasticity during the initial sterilization cycles may indicate material stiffening due to heat and moisture exposure. One possible explanation is post-curing or further cross-linking of the polymer matrix. However, this remains a hypothesis, as the current study did not employ analytical techniques such as differential scanning calorimetry or infrared spectroscopy to confirm changes in the material’s molecular structure. Future investigations should include these methods to determine whether the observed mechanical behavior correlates with cross-link density or thermal transitions such as changes in glass transition temperature. With further cycles, microstructural damage led to a progressive decline in hardness. This degradation, attributed to polymer chain scission and surface erosion, aligns with previous findings on prolonged steam sterilization effects. Microscopic analysis revealed visible cracks and surface irregularities, confirming that excessive thermal exposure accelerates polymer breakdown and embrittlement. The modulus of elasticity showed a progressive increase, indicating material stiffening over time. While initially beneficial, excessive rigidity beyond a critical threshold, defined as the point at which increased stiffness compromises fracture toughness and resistance to dynamic loading can contribute to microfractures and reduced impact resistance, ultimately affecting the precision of the printed guides or instruments. These results align with studies on methacrylate-based photopolymers, where prolonged thermal exposure leads to densification followed by irreversible degradation22. It is noteworthy that we managed to show the significant effects on hardness and elasticity even at relatively low number of samples compared between sterilization cycles which is inevitably associated with a low power of analysis. It is very likely that including more samples in the experiment would reveal more consistent (in the case of hardness) or earlier (in the case of elasticity) trend of changes. In contrast, the absence of statistically significant differences in bending strength should not be overlooked. This result may in fact indicate a desirable stability of load-bearing capacity across repeated sterilization cycles.

From a clinical perspective, observed material changes raise concerns about the long-term accuracy and reliability of reusable surgical items made from the resin commonly used for surgical guides. While early cycles may improve the stability of the surgical item, extended sterilization can compromise its structural integrity. The microcracks and surface roughness observed after 10 cycles suggest that excessive reuse could cause significant damage to the printed items during surgery. Therefore, selecting the appropriate material is crucial for printing surgical items for medical and veterinary applications23.

Surgical guides, instruments, and implants can be 3D-printed from metal, such as titanium alloy24,25. This would solve the problem of multiple sterilizations. Although plastic and metal 3D-printed surgical items require advanced, high-precision manufacturing equipment, plastic-based systems are significantly more accessible and economical than the substantial investment and infrastructure required for metal additive manufacturing. Additionally, metal powders are not suitable for point-of-care manufacturing due to their flammability and the risk of inhalation-related health hazards. Metal printing involves complex processes and extended production times, and it is usually outsourced to specialized facilities, resulting in several days or weeks of turnaround time. In contrast, inexpensive printers can produce plastic guides in-house within hours, providing greater flexibility and responsiveness20,21. Additive manufacturing makes it possible to create surgical instruments. Given the wide variety of animals that are treated surgically, creating specialized instruments tailored to the anatomical conditions of a given species of animal seems to be an interesting prospect. These instruments can also be reusable. In our work, we have demonstrated that 3D-printed plastic items are sensitive to repeated steam sterilization, a factor that must be considered in everyday use.

This study establishes a foundational understanding of the mechanical resilience of 3D-printed items, e.g., universal drill guides, subjected to repeated steam sterilization. Identifying performance thresholds and material limitations will aid in optimizing surgical workflows and improving the safety and efficacy of reusable 3D-printed instrumentation in both veterinary and human medicine. Standardized sterilization protocols should address these challenges by limiting the number of reuse cycles for 3D-printed instruments made from Surgical Guide Resin. The choice of 10 sterilization cycles reflects a practical upper limit observed in clinical practice, beyond which reusable instruments are typically withdrawn due to safety concerns. However, it should be noted that certain materials may require a higher number of sterilization cycles to fully capture the extent and progression of their degradation. For high-precision applications, minimizing sterilization cycles - ideally to just a few - can help maintain the integrity of 3D-printed surgical items. Future research should focus on material innovations to improve thermal and hydrolytic resistance in 3D-printed surgical instruments. Reinforced photopolymers, nanocomposites, or polymer coatings with enhanced thermal stability could extend the lifespan of reusable guides. Additionally, long-term clinical studies evaluating the performance of sterilized guides in surgical cases will be essential in refining re-use recommendations and ensuring patient safety.

This study does not address cleaning and disinfection procedures carried out prior to sterilization. It also does not consider the potential accumulation or chemical alteration of surface components during repeated processing cycles, or how this influences the mechanical properties of resin-based printed elements. While this may be a limitation of the study, cleaning surgical instruments before re-sterilization is not a complicated procedure in most veterinary surgical practices. Contaminated surgical instruments are typically immersed in fresh, cold water containing an enzymatic cleaner immediately after surgery. Once the debris has dissolved, the instruments are placed in a separate bowl containing fresh, warm water and a surgical instrument cleaner. A soft plastic cleaning brush is then used to scrub the instruments. After scrubbing, the instruments are rinsed thoroughly in cold water to remove all traces of the cleaning solution. Finally, before sterilization, the instruments should be laid out on a clean towel to dry26. It seems that the presented procedure does not require the use of chemicals that could affect the outcome of our experiment.

A second limitation is that the importance of advanced material characterization techniques, such as DSC, FTIR, and DMA, cannot be overstated. However, the primary objective of the present study was to evaluate the performance and practical applicability of 3D-printed components following multiple sterilization cycles rather than conducting an in-depth investigation of degradation phenomena at the material level.

As 3D-printed surgical instruments are becoming increasingly common in veterinary surgery11–13, we examined the hazards of repeatedly steam sterilizing printed resin items from a clinical perspective. Accordingly, our work focused on assessing the dimensional stability and surface condition of component parameters directly relevant to their intended clinical use.

Ultimately, this study determined the maximum force value during the three-point bending tests, and the corresponding results are presented in the supplementary file. However, the main analysis focused on Young’s modulus (E) and hardness, as these parameters provide more detailed insight into how the material behaves after multiple steam sterilization cycles. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the effect of repeated sterilization on the elasticity and surface resistance of the methacrylate-based material, as these properties are directly related to the long-term durability and safety of reusable 3D-printed surgical items. Young’s modulus (E) reflects changes in a polymer’s structural integrity and its ability to resist deformation, indicating potential degradation or additional cross-linking due to thermal exposure. Hardness, on the other hand, is a crucial factor in evaluating surface wear resistance and selecting suitable drilling tools for surgical procedures. While the maximum bending force (Fg) is directly associated with the point of mechanical failure, its value remained relatively stable within the tested sterilization cycles. Therefore, greater emphasis was placed on analyzing E and hardness, as these parameters more effectively capture progressive changes in material performance and help predict loss of structural integrity before ultimate failure occurs.

Conclusion

In surgical items printed from resin, minimizing sterilization cycles is essential for high-precision surgical applications to maintain performance and ensure surgical accuracy. Future research should focus on developing inexpensive, easy-to-print materials with improved thermal resistance. Establishing standardized sterilization protocols tailored to surgical instrumentation printed from plastic will be crucial for optimizing clinical outcomes and ensuring long-term applicability in veterinary and human medicine.
