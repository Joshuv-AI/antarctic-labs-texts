Weak approximation of kinetic SDEs: closing the criticality gap
Zimo Hao, Khoa Lê and Chengcheng Ling

Abstract
. We study the weak convergence of a generic tamed Euler-Maruyama scheme for the kinetic stochastic differential equations (SDEs) with integrable drifts. We show that the marginal density of the considered scheme converges at rate 1 / 2 1/2 to the corresponding marginal density of the SDE. The convergence rate is independent from the criticality gap, which is new compared to previous results. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020): Primary 60H35, 65C30; Secondary 60H10, 60H50. Keywords: Singular SDEs; degenerate noise; weak approximation; kinetic SDEs; Second order SDEs; (tamed-)Euler-Maruyama scheme; regularization by noise



1.
Introduction

Let
d\geqslant 1
be an integer dimension,
\xi,\eta
be vectors in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
and
W
be a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},({\mathscr{F}}_{s})_{s\geqslant 0},{\mathbb{P}})
.
We consider the second order stochastic differential system

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{t}=V_{t}dt,\\
dV_{t}=b(t,X_{t},V_{t})dt+dW_{t},\end{cases}\quad(X_{0},V_{0})=(\xi,\eta)\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

The drift
b:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a measurable function which satisfies the following integrability condition.

Assumption 1.1
.

There exists a constant
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(p_{x},p_{v})\in[2,\infty]^{2}
such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{p}}}:=3\frac{d}{p_{x}}+\frac{d}{p_{v}}<1\quad\text{and}\quad\|b\|_{L_{T}^{\infty}({\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}})}:=\sup_{t}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}|b(t,x,v)|^{p_{x}}dx\right)^{\frac{p_{v}}{p_{x}}}dv\right)^{\frac{1}{p_{v}}}<\infty.

(The Lebesgue integration norm is replaced by
\sup
-norm when
p_{x}=\infty
or
p_{v}=\infty
.)

System (
1.1
) is one of the typical models that describes the Hamiltonian mechanics in the form of Langevin equation (
[
14
,
15
]
). In such context,
X_{t},V_{t}
usually represent respectively the position and velocity of a moving particle at a time
t
.
The probability density
\rho_{t}(x,v)
of
Z_{t}:=(X_{t},V_{t})
(which exists under
1.1
, see
[
12
]
) is a common subject in statistical mechanics, and it satisfies the following Fokker–Planck equation (
[
13
,
17
]
)

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta_{v}\rho+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\rho+b\cdot\nabla_{v}\rho.

Let
n\geqslant 1
be an integer.
We define
k_{n}(t):=\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{n}
for each
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
We let
(b_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of bounded functions that converges to
b
.
Let
\{Z^{n}_{t}\}:=\{(X^{n}_{t},V^{n}_{t})\}
be the solution to the following
tamed Euler scheme

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X^{n}_{t}=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}V^{n}_{s}ds,\\
V^{n}_{t}=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}b_{n}(s,X^{n}_{k_{n}(s)}+(s-k_{n}(s))V^{n}_{k_{n}(s)},V^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds+W_{t}.\end{cases}

The probability density of
Z^{n}_{t}
, which exists, is denoted by
\rho^{n}_{t}
. The goal of the current article is to obtain convergence of
\rho^{n}_{t}
to
\rho_{t}
with a rate that is independent from
d,p_{x},p_{v}
.
To be more precise, we assume that
(b_{n})_{n}
satisfies the following condition.

Assumption 1.2
.

There are finite constants
\zeta\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
,
\vartheta>0
,
\delta\in(1,2)
and
\kappa_{b}>0
such that

(1.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\|b_{n}\|_{L_{T}^{\infty}({\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}})}\leqslant\kappa_{b},

(1.6)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}n^{\vartheta\delta}\|b_{n}-b\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1],{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}})}\leqslant\kappa_{b},

(1.7)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}n^{-\zeta}\sup_{(t,z)\in[0,n^{-1}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|b_{n}(t,z)|\leqslant\kappa_{b},

and

(1.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\sup_{n}(n^{-1/2}\wedge h)\sup_{(t,z)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|b_{n}(t,z)|=0.

In (
1.6
),
{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}
is an anisotropic Besov space of distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
with regularity index
-\delta
.
Precise definitions are provided later in
Section

2
. Heuristically, condition (
1.5
) asserts that
b_{n}
belongs to the same function space as
b
uniformly. The constant
\vartheta
parametrizes and quantifies the approximating sequence
(b_{n})_{n}
. Condition (
1.8
) is a technical one which is necessary for the application of Girsanov theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that
1.1
and
1.2
hold and initial data
(\xi,\eta)\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
is given.
Then for
any
{\boldsymbol{q}}\in[2,\infty)^{2}
with
{\boldsymbol{q}}\geqslant{\boldsymbol{p}}
, there is a constant
C=C(d,{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}},\zeta,\vartheta,\delta,\kappa_{b},\|b\|_{L_{T}^{\infty}({\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}})})>0
such that for all
t\in(0,1]
,

(1.9)

\displaystyle\|\rho_{t}-\rho_{t}^{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime}}\lesssim_{C}n^{-(\vartheta\wedge\frac{1}{2})}\left(t^{-\zeta-\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{q}}}}+t^{-\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{p}}}+{\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{q}}})}\right),

where
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime}=(\frac{q_{x}}{q_{x}-1},\frac{q_{v}}{q_{v}-1})
is the Hölder conjugate of
{\boldsymbol{q}}.

We note that the constant
C
in (
1.9
) is independent from the initial position.
The restriction on the time interval
[0,1]
is conventional which simplifies our exposition. The results discussed herein can be extended straightforwardly to arbitrary time intervals. In such case, the implicit constant in
Theorem

1.3
would depend on the the length of the temporal interval.
We give two typical examples of
(b_{n})_{n}
which satisfies
1.2
.
The first one is given by the convolution

(1.10)

b_{n}=b*\phi_{n},\quad\phi_{n}(x,v):=n^{4d\vartheta}\phi(n^{3\vartheta}x,n^{\vartheta}v),

where
\phi
is any bounded probability density function with
\varphi(x,v)=\varphi(x,-v)
and
\vartheta
is any constant in
(0,\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{p}}})^{-1}]
.
When
((p_{x}\wedge p_{v})-1){\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{p}}}>1
or when
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(\infty,\infty)
, one can choose the other example of taming sequence is obtained through the truncation

(1.11)

\displaystyle b_{n}(t,x,v)

\displaystyle:=1_{|b(t,x,v)|>0}\frac{|b(t,x,v)|\wedge(C_{2}n^{\kappa})}{|b(t,x,v)|}b(t,x,v),

where
\kappa
is any constant in
(0,1/2)
.
To obtain rate
1/2
in (
1.9
), it is sufficient to tune the parameters so that
\vartheta>\frac{1}{2}
in case of (
1.10
) and
\kappa>\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{p}}})
in case of (
1.11
), see details in
Section

2.2
below.

Discussion

Comparing (
1.4
) with the standard Euler-Maruyama scheme

(1.12)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X^{n}_{t}=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}V^{n}_{k_{n}(s)}ds,\\
V^{n}_{t}=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,Z^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds+W_{t},\end{cases}

we have replaced
\int_{0}^{t}V_{k_{n}(s)}^{n}ds
and
\int^{t}_{0}b(s,Z^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds
respectively by
\int_{0}^{t}V_{s}^{n}ds
and
\int_{0}^{t}b_{n}(s,X^{n}_{k_{n}(s)}+(s-k_{n}(s)V^{n}_{k_{n}(s)},V^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds
.
This replacement is more favorable due to the several reasons.

First, even though
\int_{0}^{t}V^{n}_{s}ds
has continuum variable, the scheme (
1.4
) is still recursive. Indeed, let
h:=1/n
, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain from (
1.4
) that

\displaystyle X_{(k+1)h}^{n}

\displaystyle=X_{kh}^{n}+\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}V_{s}^{n}ds,

\displaystyle V_{s}^{n}

\displaystyle=V_{kh}^{n}+\int_{kh}^{s}b^{n}(r,X_{kh}^{n}+(r-kh)V_{kh}^{n},V_{kh}^{n})dr+W_{s}-W_{kh},\ s\in[kh,(k+1)h].

This implies the recursive relation

(1.13)

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}X_{(k+1)h}^{n}\\
V_{(k+1)h}^{n}\end{matrix}\right)=F^{n}_{h}(X_{kh}^{n},V_{kh}^{n})+\xi^{n}_{k},

where

\displaystyle F^{n}_{h}(x,v):=\left(\begin{matrix}x+hv+\int_{0}^{h}(h-r)b^{n}(r,x+rv,v)dr\\
v+\int_{0}^{h}b^{n}(r,x+rv,v)dr\end{matrix}\right)

and
\xi^{n}_{k}=\left(\begin{matrix}\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}(W_{s}-W_{kh})ds\ \ W_{(k+1)h}-W_{kh}\end{matrix}\right)
.
We note that
\xi^{n}_{k}
are i.i.d. Gaussian random variables with covariance matrix

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{h^{3}}{3}\mathbb{I}_{d\times d}&&\frac{h^{2}}{2}\mathbb{I}_{d\times d}\\
\frac{h^{2}}{2}\mathbb{I}_{d\times d}&&h\mathbb{I}_{d\times d}\end{matrix}\right).

Consequently, in the case of time-independent smooth drift
b
, the scheme (
1.4
) is completely implementable through (
1.13
).

Second, we use tamed term
b_{n}
instead of
b
itself, which is known as “taming technique”
[
6
,
8
]
, to aid the hardly controllable case when
b
has singularities. The taming of the drift turns out to be technically beneficial in analyzing convergence rates.

There are limited works on the study of the weak well-posedness of (
1.1
). Sharp conditions given from
[
11
, Theorem 1]
are either
b\in L^{\infty}_{T}(C_{x,v}^{\beta})
with
\beta\in(0,1)
or
b\in L^{\infty}_{T}({\mathbb{L}}^{\infty})
or
L_{T}^{q}({\mathbb{L}}^{p})
with
\frac{2}{q}+\frac{4d}{p}<1
and
p\geqslant 2,q>2
.
[
12
, Theorem 1.4]
obtains, among other things, weak well-posedness for (
1.1
) with unbounded singular drifts in Kato’s class,
which includes
1.1
. Considering the fact that there are few results on weak well-posedness, it seems that the current result is the first work that quantifies the weak convergence rate of order
1/2
for (
1.1
) under
1.1
. The work
[
10
]
also studies the law of the discrete Euler–Maruyama equation but does not provide any quantitative bounds.

When
b=b(t,v)\in{\mathbb{L}}^{(p_{x},p_{v})}
with
p_{x}=\infty
and
p_{v}>d
, the kinetic SDE (
1.1
) reduces to the non-degenerate SDE
dV_{t}=b(t,V_{t})dt+dW_{t}
. The work
[
7
]
considered the convergence of (
1.4
) with the approximating drift

(1.14)

\displaystyle{b}_{n}(t,v):=1_{t>1/n,|b(t,v)|>0}\frac{|b(t,v)|\wedge(Cn^{\kappa})}{|b(t,v)|}b(t,v),\quad\kappa=\frac{d}{p_{v}},

and established a pointwise convergence rate for
\rho^{n}
of order
n^{-\frac{1-d/p_{v}}{2}}
, which vanishes when
d/p_{v}
approaches
1
.
Due to technical reasons,
[
7
]
requires that
b_{n}
vanishes when
t<1/n
, which causes (
1.14
) to violate (
1.6
).
In contrast, our method does not treat the initial time step differently and therefore allows the use of (
1.11
). In this case, by choosing
\kappa>\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{p}}})
,
Theorem

1.3
yields a convergence rate (with respect to a weaker topology) of order
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
,
which is independent from the criticality gap
1-d/{p_{v}}
. While

[
7
]
also considers the case (
1.14
) with
\kappa=1/2
, the choice (
1.11
) with
\kappa=1/2
is ruled out by condition (
1.8
) (see
Remark

4.3
for further details).

Idea of the proof.

We briefly sketch the general arguments showing (
1.9
). By duality, it suffices to estimate
|{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(Z_{t})-{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(Z_{t}^{n})|
, for each measurable function
\varphi
satisfying
\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}}=1
. Applying Itô’s formula to
r\to P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r})
and
P_{t-r}\varphi(Z^{n}_{r})
, we have

\displaystyle|{\langle}\varphi,\rho_{t}-\rho^{n}_{t}{\rangle}|\leqslant

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}(b-b_{n})(r,Z_{r})\cdot\nabla_{v}P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r})dr\Big|

\displaystyle+\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}b_{n}(r)\cdot\nabla_{v}P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r})-b_{n}(r)\cdot\nabla_{v}P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r}^{n})dr\Big|

\displaystyle+\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\big(b_{n}(r,Z^{n}_{r})-\Gamma_{r-k_{n}(r)}b_{n}(r,Z_{k_{n}(r)}^{n})\big)\cdot\nabla_{v}P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r}^{n})dr\Big|

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{1}^{n}+I_{2}^{n}+I_{3}^{n}.

The first term,
I_{1}^{n}
, encodes the taming error arising from approximating
b
by
b_{n}
. The convergence rate of
I^{1}_{n}
is therefore governed by (
1.6
). To utilize (
1.6
), one must effectively control the regularity of the product
\nabla_{v}P_{t-s}\varphi\cdot(b-b_{n})(s)
. Standard product estimates would yield unsatisfactory rates. We overcome this difficulty by using Bony’s paraproduct decomposition, writing
\nabla_{v}P_{t-s}\varphi\prec(b_{n}-b)(s)+\nabla_{v}P_{t-s}\varphi\succcurlyeq(b_{n}-b)(s)
. Each term in Bony’s decomposition possesses a different degree of regularity and integrability, and by treating them separately, we are able to apply condition (
1.6
) effectively (see
Lemma

4.4
).
The second term,
I_{2}^{n}
, has a recursive structure and can be estimated directly in terms of the map
s\mapsto\|\rho_{s}-\rho^{n}_{s}\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}}
. By applying a Grönwall argument, this recursive contribution becomes negligible. The last term,
I^{n}_{3}
, is a weighted quadrature error arising from the discretizations of the time steps. To control it, we use refined estimates related to the transition probabilities associated with
Z
and
Z^{n}
, see
Lemmas

4.5
,
4.6
and
4.7
.

The application of Itô’s formula leading to the decomposition into
I^{n}_{1},I^{n}_{2},I^{n}_{3}
is already presented in literature. For instance, it is used in
[
5
]
in a slightly different context, where the test function
\varphi
is Hölder continuous, which leads to convergence in a Wasserstein metric.
Our analysis of each term is nevertheless novel, and new techniques have been introduced to handle the lack of regularity present in the coefficients and the test functions.

Conventions

A vector
z
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
is often decomposed into two components
z=(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. We denote
\nabla_{x}=(\partial_{x_{k}})_{1\leqslant k\leqslant d}
,
\nabla_{v}=(\partial_{v_{k}})_{1\leqslant k\leqslant d}
and
\nabla=(\nabla_{x},\nabla_{v})
.

We use relations
<,=,\leqslant
and
>,\geqslant
between vectors if each corresponding component shares the same relation, e.g.

{\boldsymbol{q}}:=(q_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}\leqslant{\boldsymbol{p}}:=(p_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
if
q_{i}\leqslant p_{i}
for each
i
.
For each
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(p_{x},p_{v})\in[1,\infty]^{2}
, we denote
{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}:=\frac{1}{{\boldsymbol{p}}}:=(\frac{1}{p_{x}},\frac{1}{p_{v}})
, with convention that
1/\infty=0
.

The notation
a\lesssim b
means that
a\leqslant Cb
for some finite non-negative constant
C
which depends only on the parameters in the corresponding statement. If there are further dependence on another parameter
c
, we incorporate it in the notation by writing
a\lesssim_{c}b
.

2.
Notations and examples

2.1.
Notations

For each
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denotes the classical Lebesgue norm on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, while
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the supremum norm over
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, i.e.
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(x)|
. We note that this differs from the usual
L^{\infty}
Lebesgue norm defined via the essential supremum. This choice is purely for convenience since we consider only functions that are bounded everywhere.

For each vector index
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(p_{x},p_{v})\in[1,\infty]^{2}
, we define the mix
{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}
space as the collection of measurable functions
f
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
such that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}:=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|f(\cdot,v)\|_{p_{x}}^{p_{v}}dv\right)^{1/p_{v}}<\infty\quad\text{if}\quad p_{v}<\infty,

and

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}:=\sup_{v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|f(\cdot,v)\|_{p_{x}}<\infty\quad\text{if}\quad p_{v}=\infty.

When
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(\infty,\infty)
, we abbreviate
\|\cdot\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.

For each
q\in[1,\infty]
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(p_{x},p_{v})\in[1,\infty]^{2}
,
L^{q}_{T}({\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}})
denotes the space of measurable functions
f
on
[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
such that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{T}^{q}({\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}})}:=\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{q}dt\Big)^{1/q}<\infty\quad\text{if}\quad q<\infty

and

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{T}^{\infty}({\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}})}:=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{q}<\infty\quad\text{if}\quad q=\infty.

Anisotropic Besov spaces

For a Lebesgue integrable function
f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, let
\hat{f}
and
\check{f}
respectively be the Fourier transform of
f
and its inverse which are defined by

\hat{f}(\xi):=(2\pi)^{-2d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot z}f(z)dz,\quad\check{f}(\xi):=(2\pi)^{-2d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\rm i}\xi\cdot z}f(z)dz,\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

Put
{\boldsymbol{a}}=(3,1)
and define the anisotropic distance

|z-z^{\prime}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}:=|x-x^{\prime}|^{1/3}+|v-v^{\prime}|,\quad\forall z=(x,v),z^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

For each
r>0
and
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, we denote

B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{r}(z):=\{z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}:|z^{\prime}-z|_{\boldsymbol{a}}\leqslant r\}

and
B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{r}:=B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{r}(0)
.
Let
\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
be a
C^{\infty}_{c}
-function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
which is symmetric in the direction of
x
and
v
with
\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(\xi)=1
if
\xi\in B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}
and
\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(\xi)=0
if
\xi\notin B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
.
We define

\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}(\xi):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(2^{-j{\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi)-\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(2^{-(j-1){\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi),\ \ &j\geqslant 1,\\
&\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(\xi),\ \ &j=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where for each
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
,

2^{s{\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi=(2^{3s}\xi_{1},2^{s}\xi_{2}).

Note that

\displaystyle{\rm supp}(\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j})\subset\big\{\xi:2^{j-1}\leqslant|\xi|_{\boldsymbol{a}}\leqslant 2^{j+1}\big\},\ j\geqslant 1,\quad{\rm supp}(\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0})\subset B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2},

and

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}(\xi)=\chi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(2^{-k{\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi)\to 1,\,\text{as $k\to\infty$},\quad\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be the space of all Schwartz functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
be its topological dual, i.e. the space of tempered distributions.
For given
j\geqslant 0
, the dyadic anisotropic block operator
\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}
is defined on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}f(z):=(\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}\hat{f})\check{\ }(z)=\check{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}*f(z),

where the convolution is understood in the distributional sense.
We conventionally put
{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}:=0
for
i<0
.
We give a definition for anisotropic Besov spaces (cf.
[
16
, Chapter 5]
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

and
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(p_{x},p_{v})\in[1,\infty]^{2}
. The anisotropic Besov space is defined by

{\mathbb{B}}^{s,q}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}:=\left\{f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}:\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s,q}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}:=\left(\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\big(2^{js}\|{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\big)^{q}\right)^{1/q}<\infty\right\}.

When
q=\infty
, we write
{\mathbb{B}}^{s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}:={\mathbb{B}}^{s,\infty}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}
.

For any
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in[1,\infty]^{2}
,
s^{\prime}>s
and
q\in[1,\infty]
, it holds that (
[
2
, Appendix B]
)

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}^{0,\infty}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}},\quad{\mathbb{B}}^{s^{\prime},\infty}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}^{s,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}^{s,q}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in[1,\infty]^{2}
and
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, there is a constant
C=C({\boldsymbol{p}},d)>0
such that for any
f\in{\mathbb{B}}^{m+3n,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{m}_{v}\nabla^{n}_{x}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{m+3n,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

Proof.

We will make use of Bernstein’s inequalities (see
[
18
]
): for any
j\geqslant 0
,

(2.4)

For each

\alpha\in[0,1)
and
\gamma\geqslant 0
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\alpha>\beta>0
. Then there is a constant
C=C(\alpha,\beta)>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-1}2^{-\beta j}\left(1\wedge(2^{\alpha j}\varepsilon)\right)\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}.

Proof.

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}2^{-\beta j}\left(1\wedge\left(\varepsilon 2^{\alpha j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}2^{\beta+1}\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}s^{-\beta-1}(1\wedge(\varepsilon s^{\alpha}))ds\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{-\beta-1}\left(1\wedge\left(\varepsilon s^{\alpha}\right)\right)ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{-\beta-1}(1\wedge s^{\alpha})ds\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}.

∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in[1,\infty]^{2}
. There is a constant
C=C(d,s,{\boldsymbol{p}})>0
such that for all
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
f\in{\mathbb{B}}^{3s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}
,

(A.4)

\displaystyle\|f(\cdot+h,\cdot)-f(\cdot,\cdot)\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}\leqslant C|h|^{s}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{3s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

Proof.

We note that for any
x,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

|f(x+h,v)-f(x,v)|\leqslant\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x+h,v)-{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x,v)|\\
\leqslant\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}\left(|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x+h,v)|+|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x,v)|\right)\wedge\left(h\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{x}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x+sh,v)ds\right).

Then it follows from Bernstein’s inequalities (see (
2.4
)) that

\displaystyle\|f(\cdot+h,\cdot)-f(\cdot,\cdot)\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}\left(|h|\|\nabla_{x}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}}\right)\wedge\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}\left(\left(|h|2^{3j}\right)\wedge 1\right)\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}}\lesssim\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}\left(\left(|h|2^{3j}\right)\wedge 1\right)2^{-3sj}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{3s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|h|^{s}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{3s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}},

provided by (
A.3
), and the proof completes.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
f
be a measurable function defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, define
f_{n}:=f\ast\varphi_{n}
where
\varphi_{n}(x,v):=n^{4d\vartheta}\varphi(n^{3\vartheta}x,n^{\vartheta}v),

\vartheta\geqslant 0
and
\varphi
is a probability density function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
with
\varphi(x,v)=\varphi(x,-v)
. Then for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\beta\in(0,2)
,
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in[1,\infty)^{2}
,
we have

(A.5)

\displaystyle\|f-f_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim n^{-\vartheta\beta}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s+\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

Proof.

By
Definition

2.1
,
\|f-f_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}=\sup_{j}2^{sj}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(f-f_{n})\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
.
Since
\varphi_{n}
is a probability density function,
for any
(x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(f-f_{n})(x,v)=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\varphi_{n}(y,w)\big({\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x,v)-{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x-y,v-w)\big)dydw

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\varphi_{n}(y,w)\big({\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x,v)-{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x,v-w)\big)dydw

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\varphi_{n}(y,w)\big({\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x,v-w)-{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x-y,v-w)\big)dydw

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{1}(j)+I_{2}(j).

Using the symmetry
\varphi_{n}(x,v)=\varphi_{n}(x,-v)
and Bernstein’s inequalities (see (
2.4
)) one sees that

\displaystyle\|I_{1}(j)\|_{\boldsymbol{p}}=

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\varphi_{n}(y,w)\big({\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x,v+w)+{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x,v-w)-2{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{a}f(x,v)\big)dydw\right\|_{\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|\varphi_{n}(y,w)||w|^{2}\|\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}}dydw\lesssim 2^{2j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}}n^{-2\vartheta}.

In view of (
A.4
), we have

\displaystyle\|I_{2}(j)\|_{\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|\varphi_{n}(y,w)||y|^{\frac{2}{3}}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{{\mathbb{B}}^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}}dydw\lesssim\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}n^{-2\vartheta}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{k}2^{2k}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{k}^{a}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}n^{-2\vartheta}\lesssim\sup_{k\sim j}2^{2k}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}n^{-2\vartheta}\lesssim 2^{2j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}n^{-2\vartheta}.

Moreover, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(f-f_{n})\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\leqslant\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}+\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\lesssim\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}.

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\|f-f_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}2^{sj}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(f-f_{n})\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\lesssim\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}2^{sj}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\left(\left(2^{2j}n^{-2\vartheta}\right)\wedge 1\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}2^{-\beta j}\left(\left(2^{2j}n^{-2\vartheta}\right)\wedge 1\right)\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s+\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}},

which by (
A.3
) implies (
A.5
).
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
N>1
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(p_{x},p_{v})\in(1,\infty]^{2}
such that
{\boldsymbol{p}}\neq(\infty,\infty)
. For any
\delta\in(0,((p_{x}\wedge p_{v})-1)({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{p}}}))
, there is a constant
C=C(d,{\boldsymbol{p}},\delta)>0
such that for any
f,g\in{\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
satisfying

|g|\leqslant|f|1_{|f|>N}
,
one has

(A.6)

\displaystyle\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\leqslant CN^{-\delta({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot d/{\boldsymbol{p}})^{-1}}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}^{1+\delta({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot d/{\boldsymbol{p}})^{-1}}.

Proof.

We consider three cases.

Case (i)
(
p_{x}=\infty
,
p_{v}\neq\infty
).
Let
q\in[1,p_{v})
be such that
\delta+\frac{d}{p_{v}}=\frac{d}{q}
. Using embedding
{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{(\infty,q);{\boldsymbol{a}}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}
(
[
2
, Appendix B]
) and (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{(\infty,q);{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|g\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{(\infty,q)}}\leqslant\|f1_{|f|>N}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{(\infty,q)}}.

Note that

\displaystyle\|f(\cdot,v)1_{|f(\cdot,v)|>N}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant\|f(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{\infty}}1_{\{\|f(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{\infty}}>N\}}.

Setting
h(v):=\|f(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{\infty}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|f1_{|f|>N}\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|h1_{|h|>N}\|_{L^{q}}\leqslant N^{-\frac{p_{v}-q}{q}}\|h\|_{L^{p_{v}}}^{\frac{p_{v}}{q}}=N^{-\frac{\delta p_{v}}{d}}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}^{\frac{p_{v}}{q}}.

Case (ii)
(
p_{v}=\infty
).
Let
q\in[1,p_{x})
be such that
\delta+\frac{3d}{p_{x}}=\frac{3d}{q}
. Using embedding
{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{(q,\infty);{\boldsymbol{a}}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}
(
[
2
, Appendix B]
) and (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{(q,\infty);{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|f1_{|f|>N}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{(q,\infty)}}.

For each
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\|f(\cdot,v)1_{|f(\cdot,v)|>N}\|_{L^{q}}\leqslant N^{-\frac{\delta p_{x}}{3d}}\|f(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{p_{x}}}^{\frac{p_{x}}{q}}.

This gives (
A.6
) upon taking supremum over
v
.

Case (iii)
(
p_{x},p_{v}\neq\infty
). We put
k=1+\delta({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\tfrac{d}{{\boldsymbol{p}}})^{-1}
.
Since
\delta\in(0,((p_{x}\wedge p_{v})-1)({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot\frac{d}{{\boldsymbol{p}}}))
, we see that
{\boldsymbol{q}}:=(q_{x},q_{v})=(\frac{p_{x}}{k},\frac{p_{v}}{k})\in[1,\infty)^{2}
and

\displaystyle\delta+\tfrac{3d}{p_{x}}+\tfrac{d}{p_{v}}=\tfrac{3d}{q_{x}}+\tfrac{d}{q_{v}}.

Then by embedding
{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}
(
[
2
, Appendix B]
) and (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\delta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|g\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}}\lesssim\|f1_{|f|>N}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}}.

It is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\|f1_{|f|>N}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}}\leqslant N^{-\frac{p_{x}-q_{x}}{q_{x}}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|f(\cdot,v)\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{p_{x}}}^{\frac{p_{x}q_{v}}{q_{x}}}dv\right)^{\frac{1}{q_{v}}}=N^{-\frac{p_{x}-q_{x}}{q_{x}}}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\frac{p_{v}}{q_{v}}}.

,
the proof completes.
∎

Appendix B
Paraproduct estimates in anisotropic Besov spaces

Recall the Bony decomposition

(B.1)

\displaystyle fg=f\prec g+f\circ g+f\succ g=:f\prec g+f\succcurlyeq g,

where

\displaystyle f\prec g=g\succ f:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}S_{k-1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f{\mathcal{R}}_{k}^{\boldsymbol{a}}g,\quad S_{k}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f:=\sum_{j=0}^{k}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f,\quad f\circ g:=\sum_{|i-j|\leqslant 2}{\mathcal{R}}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}g.

Lemma B.1

(Paraproduct estimates)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}},{\boldsymbol{r}}\in[1,\infty]^{2}
with
1/{\boldsymbol{p}}+1/{\boldsymbol{q}}=1/{\boldsymbol{r}},\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\beta<0.
Then

(B.2)

\displaystyle\|f\prec g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\alpha}_{{\boldsymbol{r}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\alpha}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}

and

(B.3)

\displaystyle\|f\succ g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\alpha+\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{r}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\alpha}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

When
\alpha+\beta>0,

(B.4)

\displaystyle\|f\circ g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\alpha+\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{r}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\alpha}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

Moreover, we have

(B.5)

\displaystyle\|f\circ g\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{r}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\alpha,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\alpha}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}},

(B.6)

\displaystyle\|f\succ g\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{r}}}\leqslant\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\beta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}},

(B.7)

\displaystyle\|f\succ g\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{r}}}\leqslant\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}.

Consequently,

(B.8)

\displaystyle\|f\succcurlyeq g\|_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\lesssim{\mathord{{\rm min}}}(\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}},\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\beta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}).

Proof.

The estimates (
B.2
), (
B.3
) and (
B.4
) are standard (see
[
3
, Lemma 2.11]
for instance). We only show (
B.5
)-(
B.7
), which implies (
B.8
) through the embedding
{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}^{0}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}
(see (
2.2
)).
Concerning (
B.5
), by definition and Hölder inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\|f\circ g\|_{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\lesssim\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=-2}^{2}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{i+\ell}^{\boldsymbol{a}}g\|_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\lesssim\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{\alpha i}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\alpha}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\alpha,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\alpha}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

As for (
B.7
) and (
B.6
), applying Hölder inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|f\succ g\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{r}}}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|S^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{k-1}g{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{k}f\|_{\boldsymbol{r}}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|S^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{k-1}g\|_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\|{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{k}f\|_{\boldsymbol{p}}.

From (
2.1
), we have
\widehat{S^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{k}g}(\xi)=\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(2^{-k{\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi)\hat{g}(\xi)
.
This implies, through Young convolution inequality, that
\|S^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{k}g\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\leqslant\|g\|_{\boldsymbol{p}}
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\|f\succ g\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{r}}}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|g\|_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\|{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{k}f\|_{\boldsymbol{p}}\leqslant\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{0,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\boldsymbol{q}}},

which shows (
B.7
). Furthermore, since
\beta<0
, we have
\|S^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{k}g\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\leqslant 2^{-\beta k}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}
.
This yields that

\displaystyle\|f\succ g\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{r}}}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-\beta k}\|{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{k}f\|_{\boldsymbol{p}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\leqslant\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{-\beta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|g\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta,1}_{{\boldsymbol{q}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}},

which shows (
B.6
).
∎
