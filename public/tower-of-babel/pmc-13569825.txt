Effect of knee and hip joint positions on passive stiffness of the rectus femoris and vastus lateralis in healthy individuals
Authors: Victor Hugo de Souza Ribeiro, Leandro Gomes de Jesus Ferreira, Jeam Marcel Geremia, Roberto de Souza Baptista, Nicolas Babault, Rita de Cássia Marqueti, João Luiz Quagliotti Durigan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Passive muscle stiffness is a key determinant of musculoskeletal function and is influenced by structural components such as titin, connective tissue, and fascia. However, the effects of joint position, muscle depth, and sex on quadriceps passive stiffness remain unclear. To investigate the passive stiffness of the rectus femoris (RF) and vastus lateralis (VL) under different joint configurations, muscle depths, and between sexes using shear wave elastography (SWE). Thirty-six healthy young adults (18 men and 18 women) participated in this randomized crossover study. Passive stiffness was assessed in four positions of knee flexion: supine with 60° (SUP60), supine with 20° (SUP20), sitting with 60° (SIT60), and sitting with 20° (SIT20). SWE measurements (m/s) were obtained from 30 regions of interest (ROIs) per muscle, categorized into superficial, intermediate, and deep levels. Data were analyzed using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE). A significant effect of position was observed, with higher stiffness values in the SUP60 condition for both RF and VL (p < 0.001). Superficial regions consistently exhibited greater stiffness compared to intermediate and deep regions across all positions (p < 0.001). Additionally, men demonstrated significantly higher stiffness values than women (p < 0.001). Significant interactions were found between position and muscle, as well as position and depth. Quadriceps passive stiffness is influenced by joint position, muscle depth, and sex. The SUP60 position elicits the highest stiffness, while superficial muscle regions are consistently stiffer. These findings highlight the non-uniform mechanical behavior of the quadriceps and may have implications for clinical assessment, rehabilitation, and exercise prescription. Introduction

Passive muscle stiffness, determined by the intrinsic properties of muscle and connective tissue, is a fundamental physiological factor that influences biomechanics and musculoskeletal function1. This stiffness is modulated by viscoelastic properties, neural activation, and fascia organization2, as well as structural components like collagen and titin3. These structural properties are especially important in biarticular muscles, such as the rectus femoris (RF), which runs through both the hip and knee, and which suffers significant stretches in certain joint positions4. Muscle adaptation to different lengths is particularly important, as muscle length directly impacts stiffness and force generation5. Several studies have explored the effects of muscle length across various conditions, examining its influence on electromyographic activity, metabolism, fatigue, and clinical rehabilitation outcomes6–9. However, the relationship between stretching and muscle stiffness remains under debate, with some studies showing no effect10 and others reporting reductions11,12. Notably, there is a lack of research on the quadriceps femoris, specifically examining how muscle length affects passive stiffness.

Shear Wave Elastography (SWE) is a reliable ultrasound imaging technique for assessing muscle stiffness, particularly in passive conditions13,14. SWE quantifies stiffness based on shear wave propagation speed (m/s) and on stiffness in kilopascals (kPa), reflecting muscle and connective tissue properties15,16. By providing both quantitative and qualitative data, SWE is a valuable tool for evaluating muscle conditions and therapeutic interventions in clinical and research settings17. In addition, this tool allows detailed regional mapping of muscle stiffness, enabling the analysis of both average stiffness and variations across different regions and depths18,19. This is particularly important since muscles comprise diverse fiber types and structures with distinct mechanical properties, directly influencing performance and function. Furthermore, sex significantly influences passive muscle stiffness due to anatomical, physiological, and hormonal differences, that affect muscle structure and mechanical behavior. These variations have important implications for injury prevention, sports performance, and rehabilitation20,21, RF injuries are highly prevalent in athletes, particularly in soccer, rugby, and sprinting, and among the quadriceps, the RF is the most frequently injured, particularly through muscle distension22, likely due to its bi-articular nature, as it spans both the hip and knee joints. This same biarticular configuration leads to greater length-dependent changes in passive tension compared to monoarticular muscles, meaning stiffness is predominantly influenced by joint position and muscle length. Evidence supports this mechanical behavior by showing that biarticular muscles are more sensitive to joint configuration and length–tension relationships23 However, current findings are inconclusive, with some studies reporting greater stiffness in men and others in women20,21,24.

Joint position is crucial for torque production by the quadriceps femoris, with the optimal muscle length for force generation occurring around 60° of knee flexion6. Previous studies assessed active muscle stiffness using young’s modulus, strength, and muscle architecture in healthy individuals across four positions: supine with 60° knee flexion (SUP60), sitting with 60° knee flexion (SIT60), supine with 20° knee flexion (SUP20), and sitting with 20° knee flexion (SIT20)9,25. The findings indicated that active stiffness, strength, and muscle architecture changes were more pronounced in elongated (60°) than in shortened (20°) positions. To date, no studies have investigated passive quadriceps stiffness across different joint positions, in order to understand its mechanical properties using SWE. Examining passive stiffness in both monoarticular and biarticular components, such as the rectus femoris (RF) and vastus lateralis (VL), may clarify the relationship between muscle length, stiffness, and force production. However, the influence of joint angle and muscle length on stiffness across different quadriceps regions remains unclear. Thus, our aim was to: (1) Assess the passive stiffness of RF and VL at two knee flexion angles (60° and 20°) and in two body positions (supine and sitting) using SWE; (2) evaluate passive stiffness variations at different muscle depths; and (3) investigate differences in passive stiffness between males and females. We hypothesized that greater passive stiffness would be observed in the supine with 60° knee flexion position, with no heterogeneity in stiffness along muscle depth and differences between sexes.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

This study is a crossover, experimental, and randomized trial. All participants provided their informed consent prior to inclusion in the study. The data were collected between July 2023 and May 2024. Approval for the project was obtained from the local ethics committee (CAAE: 99221818.9.0000.0029) following the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. The trial was registered on the clinical trial platform (https://clinicaltrials.gov: NCT05905406).

Participants

The sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.7 assuming α = 0.05 and power (1-β) = 0.80 to detect a large effect (f2 > 0.5). Based on the primary outcome and accounting for a 20% dropout rate, a sample of 36 participants was defined as appropriate. The effect size was estimated based on previous literature that investigated passive muscle stiffness at different muscle lengths using shear wave elastography26. A total of 36 participants (18 men and 18 women) with no history of neuromuscular injury or disease were included in the study. The sample had a mean age of 21.9 ± 3.0 years, a height of 1.6 ± 0.09 m, and a body mass of 66.8 ± 11.0 kg. All participants were classified as eutrophic (BMI: 18.5–24.9 kg/m²) and had not engaged in systematic lower limb strength training in the previous six months. Physical activity levels were assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), to ensure that all participants were physically active, either through recreational or sports activities. Participants were informed that they could request the interruption of the assessments at any time if they deemed it necessary.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Four conditions were randomly presented: SUP60 (lying with the knee at 60º), SUP20 (lying with the knee at 20º), SIT60 (sitting with the knee at 60º), and SIT20 (sitting with the knee at 20°). Computer-generated randomization lists were prepared using the website www.random.org, to sequentially distribute the participants into the four conditions. One researcher (VHSR) prepared sealed, opaque, and numbered envelopes. When each participant was enrolled in the study, the investigator opened the envelope with the smallest item number, which contained the condition order.

Blinding

The participants were blinded to the hypotheses of the study and the numerical order of the joint angles used. They were not informed about which position was expected to result in greater muscle stiffness. However, due to the nature of the positions, it was not feasible to blind the evaluator.

Study design

Participants performed a familiarization session prior to the assessments, which included anthropometric measurements (height and body mass), randomization of the order of joint positioning, and verification of eligibility criteria. The right RF and VL muscles were selected to observe the stiffness behavior of two muscle components within the same muscle group, one being biarticular and the other monoarticular. During the anthropometric assessment, participants were barefoot and wore light clothing. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). Physical activity levels were assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which evaluates the frequency and duration of physical activities over the previous week27. Following familiarization, four sessions were conducted to evaluate stiffness using SWE. Participants sat on an isometric dynamometer (IsoSystem 2.0, Cefise, SP, Brazil) with hip and knee fixation in the evaluation position. Assessments were performed in four different positions: SUP60 (hip at 0° and knee at 60° of flexion), SUP20 (hip at 0° and knee at 20° of flexion), SIT60 (hip at 85° and knee at 60° of flexion), and SIT20 (hip at 85° and knee at 20° of flexion). To ensure a passive condition, relaxation was monitored using Electromyographic (EMG) analysis. The passive state was ensured through standardized verbal instruction and continuous monitoring throughout data collection, and was further confirmed by establishing a baseline muscle signal and monitoring its oscillation during these periods. Subcutaneous fat thickness was controlled, and only participants presenting a maximum thickness of 1 cm in the assessed region were included28.

SWE

The trial was conducted in the Laboratory of Muscle and Tendon Plasticity (LaPlasT) at the University of Brasília. The dynamometer shaft was aligned with the center of the knee joint for passive stiffness assessment. Passive muscle stiffness in the RF and VL was evaluated using shear wave elasticity (kPa) and shear wave velocity (Ms) (Fig. 1). Musculoskeletal configurations ranging from 0 to 300 kPa were selected using the ACUSON Redwood Ultrasound System (Siemens, USA). Specifically, the RF and VL were visualized at 50% and 60% of the thigh length, respectively, measured from the anterior-superior iliac spine to the base of the patella9. Regions of interest (ROIs) were delineated in a circular format (3 mm) from B-mode ultrasound images obtained with a linear transducer (10 - L4 MHz, Siemens, USA). Thirty ROIs were manually measured: 10 in the superficial region of each muscle (superior part in selection box), 10 in the deep region (deep part in selection box), and 10 in the intermediate region, between the superficial and deep regions (medium part in selection box). Muscle tissue stiffness was determined by averaging the shear modulus velocity from these 30 ROIs applied in each depth per image. The stiffness of each muscle was calculated based on the mean shear modulus from three obtained images. This mean shear modulus represented the stiffness of the quadriceps femoris components. The transducer surface was covered with water-soluble transmission gel to ensure acoustic coupling without applying additional pressure on the skin, thereby preventing overestimation of tissue stiffness. No damages were identified.

All SWE evaluations were performed by the same evaluator (VHSR). The evaluation environment was maintained at a temperature between 23 and 25 °C to prevent fluctuations in tissue stiffness29. Three ultrasound images of each tissue (superficial and deep fascia of each muscle, RF and VL) were acquired with the participant in a resting position. To ensure map stabilization, images were captured following a 10-second delay30. During the stiffness evaluation, two key variables are typically considered: shear wave propagation speed (measured in meters per second, m/s) and stiffness (measured in kilopascals, kPa). These variables are interconnected, as the propagation speed directly influences the calculated stiffness values15,16. The Results section presents the shear wave velocity (Ms) outcomes, while outcomes measured from kilopascals (KPa) are presented as Supplementary Data (Supplementary Material 1). Specifically, the ultrasound device was configured with the following acquisition parameters: transparency set at 0%, acquisition depth at 6 cm, frame rate of 35 FPS, compound mode with clarify off, high-frequency setting, line density 3, dynamic range 51 dB, persistence level 3, map E, and tint 5.

Statistical analysis

Shear wave propagation speed (Ms) and stiffness (kPa) are reported as estimated marginal mean with 95% confidence interval. To verify the differences between the stiffness variables of groups, the Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) method was adopted, using “position” and “levels” and “muscles” as factors. The model was adjusted considering “sex” as a factor. The least significant difference (LSD) procedure was used as a post hoc test to locate differences. The GEE procedure was used to examine the effects of position (SUP60, SUP20, SIT60, and SIT20), muscle (RF and VL), and levels (UP, INT, and DEEP) on Ms and KPa. In the absence of a significant three-way interaction, the interaction between the two groups is reported. For the GEE analyses, all 30 ROIs were included. In the position × muscle analysis, all ROIs were analyzed together. For the position × depth analysis, the ROIs were categorized into three levels: the first 10 as superficial, the next 10 as intermediate, and the final 10 as deep, totaling 30 ROIs. The effect sizes were determined using partial eta squared (η 2). Values of η2 > 0.01 are defined as small, η2 > 0.06 as medium, and η2 > 0.14 as large. Cohen’s d (d) was used for the paired comparisons. Values of d = 0.2 are defined as small, d = 0.5 as medium, and d = 0.8 as large. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and for graphical construction the software Graphpad Prism 8.3.0 (San Diego, California, USA). The significance threshold was P < 0.05. To determine measurement reliability, we calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to assess the intra-rater consistency of the single evaluator. The time interval between repeated measurements used for the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis was 10 min. A total of 30 participants were included in the reliability analysis. Reliability was classified as low (< 0.5), moderate (0.5–0.75), good (> 0.75–0.9), or excellent (> 0.9).

Results

Shear wave velocity (SWE, Ms)

Reliability of SWE

After intra-rater ICC analysis, for RF good reliability was observed for SUP60 Ms (mean [95% confidence interval]: 1.99 [1.98–2.01], ICC = 0.88), SUP20 Ms 1.60 [1.58–1.64], ICC = 0.84), SIT60 Ms 1.63 [1.62–1.64], ICC = 0.81), and SIT20 Ms 1.49 [1.46–1.52], ICC = 0.80). For VL, excellent reliability was observed for SUP60 Ms (mean [95% confidence interval] 1.75 [1.74–1.77], ICC = 0.91) and SUP20 Ms 1.53 [1.51–1.57], ICC = 0.91), and good reliability for SIT60 Ms 1.67 [1.65–1.69], ICC = 0.81) and SIT20 Ms 1.42 [1.44–1.46], ICC = 0.82).

Position x muscle

There was a significant interaction between muscle and position for SWE Ms (p < 0.001; Fig. 2). The post hoc analysis indicated that the SUP60 group exhibited significantly higher SWE compared to the other groups for RF (mean [95% confidence interval]: SUP60 = 1.98 [1.91–2.05], SUP20 = 1.60 [1.56–1.65], SIT60 = 1.63 [1.57–1.69], and SIT20 = 1.49 [1.44–1.53]). For VL, the post hoc analysis showed that the SUP60 group exhibited significantly higher SWE values compared to the other groups for VL (mean [95% confidence interval]: SUP60 = 1.75 [1.68–1.82], SUP20 = 1.53 [1.49–1.58], SIT60 = 1.67 [1.61–1.73], and SIT20 = 1.44 [1.40–1.49]).

Sex

The model was adjusted considering “sex” as a factor, and the post hoc analysis revealed a significant interaction for SWE (p < 0.001). The results indicated that men presented significantly higher SWE values compared to women (mean [95% confidence interval]: Men = 1.70 [1.64–1.75], Women = 1.56 [1.52–1.61]).

Discussion

The present study represents the first application of SWE to measure passive muscle stiffness, enabling a more detailed evaluation of the mechanical properties of the different components of the quadriceps under various joint conditions. Our study identified the following findings: (1) Higher stiffness (measured in KPa and m/s) was observed in the SUP60 position for both the RF and VL compared with other positions.;(2) stiffer conditions (KPa and m/s) were consistently found in the superficial regions across all positions compared to the intermediate and deep regions; and (3) higher stiffness was observed in male individuals compared to female individuals (KPa and m/s). Contrary to our hypothesis, quadriceps muscle depth and sex differences were clearly demonstrated. In summary, our results indicate that the quadriceps femoris is not a uniform structure in terms of stiffness and that it responds differently according to joint position, muscle depth, and sex.

The entire sample completed the study, with no sampling losses. Our data analysis revealed that the SUP60 position exhibited higher passive stiffness. Muscle stiffness and capacity are influenced by cross-bridge formation, which correlates with Young’s modulus and serves as an indicator of strength during isometric contractions5; however, passive stiffness is predominantly influenced by structural components such as titin31, connective tissue32,33, and the fascia34,35. In passive conditions, muscle stiffness is primarily influenced by non-contractile components, especially titin and the extracellular matrix (ECM). Evidence indicates that titin is one of the main determinants of passive stiffness at the intracellular level, supporting a large portion of the mechanical load during muscle stretching36 In addition, the contribution of titin increases with sarcomere length, which explains the increase in stiffness at longer muscle lengths, as observed in the SUP60 condition. On the other hand, the collagen-rich extracellular matrix also plays a fundamental role, especially at greater levels of stretch and at higher organizational levels of the muscle. Studies demonstrate that the ECM can contribute substantially to passive stiffness in human muscles37. These structures exhibit viscoelastic behavior and become progressively stiffer as deformation increases, which is consistent with the patterns observed across positions and depths in the present study. Passive muscle stiffness therefore results from the interaction between intracellular components and extracellular components, both of which may depend on the level of muscle stretch38. This behavior could explain the consistent increase in SWE values in the SUP60 position, which places the quadriceps in a more lengthened state compared to seated positions. A similar protocol, using active contraction to assess quadriceps, demonstrated that the Young’s modulus of the quadriceps femoris is higher when the knee is flexed at 60º compared to 20º during voluntary isometric contractions9. Furthermore, the same study indicated that the SUP60 position elicited higher maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) values compared to SUP20, SIT60, and SIT20, suggesting superior force transmission capability in the SUP60 position. Some authors have noted the relationship between muscle strength and shear wave elastography (SWE)14,39, while others40 suggest a correlation between torque production and stiffness in kilopascals (kPa). Joint configuration and fiber arrangement influence both passive and active stiffness of the quadriceps, with changes in stiffness potentially explained by alterations in connective tissues like fascia36,39. This may explain the differing behaviors of the quadriceps components assessed herein, such as the RF (biarticular) and VL (monoarticular), even though the hip does not influence the VL. Nonetheless, changes in hip angle may still alter overall quadriceps mechanics and fascial connections, which could partly explain the observed stiffness behavior in the VL.

The difference in stiffness across various muscle depths is a crucial aspect. Our findings indicate that superficial regions exhibit significantly higher stiffness than intermediate and deep regions, regardless of position. This may be attributed to the greater presence of fascia and dense connective tissue in the superficial layers, which contribute to increased stiffness2. Previous studies suggest that the distribution of muscle stiffness is not homogeneous and may be influenced by muscle architecture and the predominant fiber type in different regions18,19. For example, some authors41investigated muscles such as the biceps brachii, gastrocnemius, and quadriceps femoris (QF), segmenting their images to analyze stiffness values across different muscle depths. The results showed decreasing stiffness values with increasing depth, supporting our observations regarding variations in muscle stiffness across different regions within the same muscle group. Regional differences in stiffness may be partly explained by greater connective tissue content near the myotendinous junction42. demonstrated that subcutaneous fat–corrected echo intensity was higher in distal quadriceps compared to mid-belly regions, suggesting increased tendon-like infiltration in these areas. This connective tissue distribution may contribute to the higher stiffness values observed in superficial or distal regions. Regarding sex-related differences, women typically present lower stiffness values than men, which may be linked to a smaller muscle cross-sectional area, differences in fiber type composition, and hormonal influences43. reported that estrogen reduces collagen synthesis and alters connective tissue turnover, while44 showed that sex-specific differences in collagen expression influence mechanical properties. These findings support our results and help explain the consistently lower stiffness values in females compared to males. The analysis of muscle stiffness at different depths is essential to better understand the mechanical properties of the muscle. Clinically, this approach allows the identification of imbalances in stiffness that can predispose individuals to injuries, optimizing post-injury rehabilitation and sports performance, and assisting in the treatment of neuromuscular diseases45–51.

Our findings indicate that sex influences passive stiffness, with men showing higher values compared to women. Although several studies have investigated the impact of sex on stiffness, there is still no consensus in the literature regarding this effect. Methodological differences in ultrasound equipment and protocols across studies may contribute to the variability in results52–54. For instance, some authors21 used a Myoton device (Myoton AS, Estonia) to compare the passive stiffness of the rectus femoris (RF) and biceps brachii (BB), while others55 used ultrasound SWE. However, both studies found that men had greater passive stiffness than women, even though different equipment was used. An important study56 evaluated the passive stiffness of RF in both older adults and young individuals using SWE and found that men had greater passive stiffness than women. This result contrasts with our findings, and the differences may be explained by the assessment of an older population20, whereas our study focused on young participants. It is also possible that muscle architecture and physiological differences between men and women, along with other contributing factors, may explain this discrepancy between sexes25,57. Our data highlight the importance of understanding sex-specific differences in muscle stiffness, as this knowledge is crucial for tailoring rehabilitation protocols and training programs to optimize performance and prevent injuries25,57,58.

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. Data collection sessions were conducted over a seven-day period, which may have introduced external factors that influence muscle behavior. Future studies should standardize protocols, control for confounding variables like muscle length and adipose tissue, and conduct data collection in a single session to minimize variability. The influence of sex on passive stiffness requires further investigation due to conflicting literature findings. Incorporating active stiffness assessments alongside passive evaluations could enhance understanding of muscle mechanics. Additionally, exploring stiffness in diverse populations, such as those with musculoskeletal disorders or athletes, is essential to assess how joint configurations and stiffness impact performance and recovery. In our study, we assessed intra-rater reliability, as our primary aim was to evaluate the consistency of repeated measurements performed by the same examiner under similar conditions. Although all possible interactions were tested during the analyses, in order to present the data in a clear and objective manner, only those that reached statistical significance were reported. This choice, however, limited the exploration of potential differences between muscles with distinct anatomical characteristics, such as the vastus lateralis (monoarticular) and rectus femoris (biarticular). Consequently, different behaviors between these muscles may not have been fully captured in the reported results. Another limitation of the present study is the absence of echo intensity, which could have contributed to a more comprehensive understanding of the factors underlying the observed stiffness differences, particularly regarding intramuscular fat and fibrotic tissue. Although echo intensity does not directly reflect mechanical properties, it provides important information on muscle composition, and its integration with elastography measures would allow a more global interpretation of muscle behavior59,60. Echo intensity has been shown to be strongly associated with intramuscular adipose tissue and muscle quality58,59. Importantly, studies investigating muscle length–dependent changes typically do not incorporate compositional measures such as echo intensity, suggesting that its inclusion could offer a novel perspective in this field. Therefore, future studies should combine mechanical, architectural, and compositional assessments, particularly in clinical populations, where alterations in muscle composition are more pronounced and may meaningfully influence stiffness behavior. Additional limitations include the absence of muscle strength tests and the lack of consideration of injury history or sport-specific demands, which may limit the clinical and athletic applicability of our findings. This is particularly relevant for the femoral rectum, which shows the highest incidence of lesions between the quadriceps muscles, particularly distension. This increased susceptibility has been attributed to factors such as the biarticular nature of this muscle (crossing both the hip and knee joints), a high proportion of type II fibers22, and its complex musculotendinous architecture, all of which increase its vulnerability to repetitive strain61,62. Future studies should integrate assessments of strength, injury history, and sport-specific demands, to better contextualize stiffness findings and increase their translational relevance for both clinical and athletic populations. Since the primary aim of the current study aim was to characterize passive mechanical properties of the RF and VL, voluntary contractions were not included, to avoid confounding effects such as residual force enhancement and alterations in passive muscle state. Prior work from our group using the same joint configurations9, showed that MVIC torque is highest at 60° knee flexion, consistent with the passive stiffness pattern observed here. For a more comprehensive assessment of muscle function, future studies should include both passive SWE and MVIC assessments in separate sessions, to enable direct correlations between passive stiffness and force production across joint positions and depths.

Conclusion

The results of the current study showed that the quadriceps femoris exhibits non-uniform stiffness across different joint positions, muscle depths, and sexes. Higher stiffness was observed in the SUP60 position for both the RF and VL, and superficial regions consistently showed greater stiffness than deeper regions. In addition, men displayed higher stiffness values compared to women. Clinicians should consider joint position, muscle depth, and sex differences when recommending quadriceps femoris exercises, as these factors influence the mapping and modulation of muscle stiffness. The findings in this study are limited to young, healthy individuals and should be interpreted with caution when considering clinical populations. While they may offer preliminary insights for exercise prescription and rehabilitation strategies, particularly in conditions such as ACL injuries, knee arthroplasty, patellofemoral pain, and tendinopathies, these implications remain speculative and require further validation in clinical populations.
