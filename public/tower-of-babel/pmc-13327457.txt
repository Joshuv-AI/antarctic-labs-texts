Cumulative Cytological Assessment of Repeated Non‐Diagnostic Thyroid Fine Needle Aspirations: A Novel Approach to Reduce Diagnostic Uncertainty
Authors: Shiri Damti Geva, Wafaa Emiel Haj, Yokhanan Spivak, Yara Assaf, Mussa Abed Al Aziz, Daniella Itzhak, Alejandro Livoff, Ohad Ronen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeFine needle aspiration (FNA) is the primary diagnostic tool for evaluating thyroid nodules. However, 10%–21% of FNAs yield non‐diagnostic (ND) results (Bethesda category I). Repeated ND findings occur in 25%–67.5% of cases, creating diagnostic uncertainty and increased healthcare costs. Our aim was to evaluate whether cumulative analysis of repeated ND cytology samples enables reclassification into a diagnostic Bethesda category.MethodsThis retrospective observational study (2013–2024) included patients with ≥ 2 ND FNA results from the same thyroid nodule. Two blinded cytopathologists performed cumulative assessment of cellular material from consecutive ND samples to determine if reclassification into diagnostic Bethesda categories was feasible.ResultsAmong 1861 FNA procedures, 29 patients (1.56%) had ≥ 2 ND results. Cumulative assessment enabled reclassification in 3/29 patients (10.3%), into Bethesda category II (benign).FindingsIn selected cases, cumulative cytological assessment of multiple ND FNAs may provide sufficient diagnostic material to allow reclassification. This novel cost‐effective approach may help reduce diagnostic uncertainty and unnecessary procedures, and improve patient care.Keywords: Bethesda system, cytological adequacy, fine needle aspiration, non‐diagnostic cytology, reclassification, thyroid nodule

Introduction

Thyroid nodules are a common clinical finding, with an estimated occurrence rate of 19% to 68% in the population. When a thyroid nodule is found, a proper diagnostic examination is indicated, particularly due to risk of 5%–15% of being cancerous [1]. In clinical practice, the diagnostic approach often involves the use of ultrasonography and fine needle aspiration (FNA) to evaluate the possibility of malignancy. These methods are crucial for effective diagnosis, enabling clinicians to accurately assess and manage potential cases of cancer [2].

According to the third edition (2023) of The Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (TBSRTC), thyroid fine‐needle aspiration (FNA) cytology is classified into six diagnostic categories: non‐diagnostic (ND), benign, atypia of undetermined significance/follicular lesion of undetermined significance (AUS/FLUS), follicular neoplasm/suspicious for follicular neoplasm, suspicious for malignancy, and malignant. The updated Bethesda system prioritizes standardized terminology, improved risk stratification, and greater flexibility in adequacy assessment based on cytomorphologic context over rigid numerical thresholds alone [3].

To be sufficient for assessment, a cytological sample should have at least 6 groups of well‐preserved, well‐visualized follicular cells, with each group containing ≥ 10 cells [3, 4, 5]. Samples that do not match these requirements are placed in the ND cytology category [3]. Cyst fluid‐only aspirates are likewise classified as ND (Bethesda I) [4, 5]. 10%–21% of patients may encounter this problem as a result of inadequate cytological material for diagnosis [6, 7]. Another difficulty occurs when a second repeated FNA after a nondiagnostic (ND) cytology provides ND results again, with a prevalence ranging from 25% to 67.5% [1, 8].

Managing patients with ND cytology poses a dilemma regarding follow‐up or surgical intervention. According to the 2015 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines, it is advisable to repeat FNA for ND cytology, along with considering an on‐site cytological evaluation if feasible [9]. The optimal interval for repeat FNAs after initial nondiagnostic results remains a topic of debate; however, waiting for 3 months is no longer considered crucial based on ATA guidelines [4].

In addition to clinical challenges, repeated FNAs also carry significant economic implications. According to Wong et al., the mean direct healthcare cost per patient for FNA management was approximately $542 [10]. Another study by Khalid et al. reported the expected cost of ultrasound‐guided FNA to be $1329 [11]. These figures highlight the variability and potential financial burden associated with the procedure in different clinical settings. Thus, multiple repeat procedures can lead to substantial cumulative costs for both patients and health services.

While previous studies have examined factors influencing initial ND rates and outcomes of repeat FNA, no research has specifically investigated in the English literature whether cumulative analysis of cellular material from consecutive ND samples can enable diagnostic reclassification [12, 13, 14]. This represents a potentially valuable but unexplored approach to optimizing diagnostic yield.

We hypothesized that combining cytological material from multiple ND FNA samples of the same thyroid nodule would provide sufficient cellular content to meet Bethesda adequacy criteria and enable diagnostic reclassification in a subset of cases.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at our Medical Center, a tertiary referral hospital. The study period spanned from January 2013 to March 2024. Institutional review board approval was obtained from the local Helsinki Committee, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient Selection

Eligible participants were identified through a comprehensive review of the institutional database of patients who underwent FNA for thyroid nodules during the study period. Inclusion criteria were: [1] age ≥ 18 years, [2] at least two FNA procedures yielding ND (Bethesda category I) results for the same thyroid nodule. Exclusion criteria included incomplete medical records, unavailability of original cytology slides, or ND results from different nodules.

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical information, such as age, gender, and ultrasound features, were obtained from patient medical records. To evaluate diagnostic findings and clinical care decisions, further information about follow‐up clinic visits, following surgical interventions, and final histopathology results was gathered where available.

An experienced head and neck surgeon who specializes in diagnostic ultrasound and ultrasound‐guided biopsies of the thyroid and neck performed all FNA procedures under ultrasound guidance. The specimens were fixed in 95% ethanol and stained with the Papanicolaou method. Rapid on‐site evaluation (ROSE) was not performed.

For each FNA procedure, a standardized protocol of 2–3 needle passes per nodule was performed under real‐time ultrasound guidance. Aspirated material was prepared using direct smear technique fixed immediately in 95% ethanol and stained using the Papanicolaou method; liquid‐based cytology was not utilized in this cohort. Rapid on‐site assessment for adequacy was not performed. The number of slides prepared per procedure varied according to the cellularity obtained: a median of 5 slides (range, 3–10) were generated per FNA pass and reviewed per case. All slides from every FNA procedure for each patient were retrieved and included in the cumulative assessment, with the exception of slides excluded due to obvious artifacts, poor preservation, or insufficient staining.

All original cytology slides were retrieved from the pathology archives and assessed for quality before review. Cytological reevaluation of all 29 cases confirmed the initial ND designation. Each FNA procedure produced multiple cytology slides, all of which were ready for review. The cumulative cytological assessment was carried out by evaluating all slides obtained from both ND FNAs in each case. Slides with obvious artifacts, poor preservation, or insufficient staining were excluded from further examination.

Each case was re‐evaluated by one of two board‐certified cytopathologists who were both blind to the patient's clinical history and previous cytological evaluations. Slide evaluation was done with a Nikon Eclipse Ci‐L microscope (Tokyo, Japan), and representative images were taken with a Nikon DS‐Fi3 camera (Tokyo, Japan). Both FNAs from each case were reviewed concurrently, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus review. (Figure 1).

Bethesda criteria were used to reassess cytological adequacy, which included at least six groups of ≥ 10 well‐visualized follicular cells. A cell group was defined as a cohesive cluster of follicular epithelial cells. Cases that met this threshold using the combined material from two ND FNAs were reclassified as definitive Bethesda categories. When available, surgical histopathology or follow‐up cytology were used to confirm reclassified cases.

Statistical Analysis

The study was conducted mainly as a proof of concept. Due to the small sample size and descriptive nature of the data, statistical analysis was primarily limited to descriptive measures. Continuous variables such as age were summarized using mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables such as sex were reported as frequencies and percentages. Given the exploratory nature of this proof‐of‐concept study, formal power analysis was not performed.

No inferential statistical tests were performed given the limited number of patients eligible for reclassification (n = 3), which precluded meaningful subgroup comparisons or hypothesis testing.

Results

Between 2013 and 2024, a total of 1861 patients underwent fine‐needle aspiration (FNA) for thyroid nodules at the Galilee Medical Center. Of these, 29 patients (1.56%) had at least two FNA procedures yielding ND (Bethesda category I) results for the same thyroid nodule. Upon retrospective review of cytology slides from these 29 patients, three patients (10.3%) were found to have sufficient cumulative cytological material to allow reclassification of their previously ND samples. Cytologic Findings in the 29 ND Cases

Cytologic review of all 29 cases confirmed the initial ND designation. The predominant factors contributing to ND classification across the cohort were low cellularity and insufficient follicular cell representation, with a subset of cases showing blood‐obsured background. Cases with cystic fluid content‐only were excluded from the study. On review of cellular components, follicular cells were identified in a proportion of cases but were generally insufficient for diagnostic interpretation. Colloid and hemosiderin‐laden macrophages were variably present; no cytological atypia or features suspicious of malignancy were identified on cumulative review. Independent blinded assessment by two cytopathologists showed full concordance with the initial non‐diagnostic diagnosis, and no case was reclassified without consensus.

Adequacy Assessment

For each FNA procedure, a total of 2–8 slides were prepared and reviewed. All available slides from both FNA procedures were re‐evaluated during the cumulative analysis by two cytologists and photographed using (Nikon DS‐Fi3 camera). Adequacy was assessed according to the Bethesda Classification System criteria. Specifically, slides were considered adequate if they contained at least six groups of well‐visualized follicular cells, with each group comprising at least 10 cells. In the cumulative analysis, adequacy was determined by combining the cellular material from both FNAs rather than evaluating each specimen in isolation. Of the 29 cases reviewed, none contained more than 60 cells in total across all slides (i.e., no case independently approached the adequacy threshold from a single FNA alone), which is consistent with the original non‐diagnostic classifications. Only three cases achieved the adequacy threshold when material from both FNAs was combined cumulatively.

Interval Between Repeat FNAs

The mean interval between the first and second FNA among the 29 cases was 2.6 months (range, 1–17.7 months). This variability is consistent with current ATA guidelines, which no longer mandate a minimum waiting period before repeat FNA after a non‐diagnostic result.

Surgical Follow‐Up

Of the 29 patients with repeated non‐diagnostic results, only one ultimately underwent surgical intervention (thyroid lobectomy with isthmectomy). Final histopathological examination revealed benign findings (adenomatous goiter). The remaining 28 patients were managed conservatively with ongoing ultrasound surveillance. No malignancy signs, symptoms, or ultrasound findings were identified among the reclassified cases during follow‐up.

Reclassified Cases

Case 1

A 79‐year‐old male presented with a 3.5 cm TIRADS 3 thyroid nodule. The initial FNA sample gave us 5 slides which were predominantly blood‐stained, with scattered follicular cells. A repeat FNA performed 1 month later yielded us 3 slides with similar findings. Cumulative assessment identified eight groups of follicular cells with benign morphology, allowing reclassification to Bethesda category II. Subsequent surgical excision confirmed a benign adenomatous goiter.

Case 2

A 69‐year‐old male with a 1.5 cm TIRADS 3 thyroid nodule had two consecutive non‐diagnostic FNA results due to scant cellularity. The first sample yielded us 6 slides, and the second one gave us 10 slides. Cumulative analysis identified six groups of follicular cells with benign features, allowing reclassification to Bethesda category II. The patient was managed conservatively with ultrasound surveillance over a 33‐month follow‐up period.

Case 3

A 62‐year‐old male with a 1.5 cm TIRADS 3 thyroid nodule underwent two FNAs spaced 5 months apart, the first one having 5 slides and the second one yielding 8 slides. Each sample contained three to four groups of follicular cells. Combined assessment revealed eight groups of follicular cells with colloid, supporting reclassification to Bethesda category II. A third FNA performed subsequently confirmed this diagnosis, also yielding a Bethesda category II result. The patient remained under conservative management with ultrasound surveillance for 27 months.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort, we explored the feasibility of cumulative cytological assessment of consecutive ND thyroid FNA samples as a proof‐of‐concept approach to diagnostic reclassification. Among 1861 patients who underwent thyroid FNA during the study period, only 29 (1.56%) had repeated ND results from the same nodule. Of these, cumulative review enabled reclassification in three cases (10.3%), all to Bethesda category II (benign), underscoring the limited but measurable diagnostic yield of this approach in selected cases.

Importantly, the clinical impact of reclassification was modest. Only one patient avoided further invasive diagnostic procedures, while the remaining two patients underwent additional FNA or surgical intervention despite reclassification. These findings indicate that cumulative cytological assessment may offer incremental diagnostic clarification rather than a definitive alternative to repeat FNA or surgical management.

The feasibility of cumulative assessment depends on several technical and interpretive factors, including slide preservation, staining quality, and cytopathologist expertise. In this study, standardized specimen processing and review by board‐certified cytopathologists supported consistent evaluation. Nevertheless, this approach is applicable only to a specific subset of ND cases in which follicular epithelial cells are present across multiple samples. ND aspirates lacking epithelial elements or consisting solely of cyst fluid are unlikely to benefit from cumulative assessment.

ROSE, which was not performed in this study, represents an established strategy for reducing ND rates by allowing immediate assessment of specimen adequacy. ROSE may be more effective than retrospective cumulative review in preventing repeated ND results and should be considered when available. Therefore, cumulative assessment should not be viewed as a replacement for ROSE but rather as a potential supplementary strategy in selected circumstances.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as only cases with available and adequately preserved slides were included. The small sample size limits generalizability and precludes meaningful statistical analysis. In addition, the single‐center setting and lack of long‐term outcome data restrict conclusions regarding clinical utility and diagnostic accuracy.

Conclusion

This proof‐of‐concept study demonstrates the feasibility of cumulative cytological assessment of repeated non‐diagnostic thyroid FNA samples in a small subset of cases. In this cohort, cumulative review enabled diagnostic reclassification in 3 of 29 patients with repeated non‐diagnostic results, all to Bethesda category II.

Given the limited sample size and modest clinical impact observed, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than practice‐changing. Cumulative cytological assessment may provide additional diagnostic clarification in selected cases but does not replace established management strategies for non‐diagnostic thyroid cytology.

Larger prospective studies with standardized methodologies and extended follow‐up are required to further evaluate the reproducibility, clinical relevance, and potential role of cumulative cytological assessment within existing diagnostic frameworks.
