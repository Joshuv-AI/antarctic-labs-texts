Thermally stable gel particles with optimized performance for high-temperature conformance control
Authors: Qiming Zheng, Chenming Peng, Wei Qian, Xin Wang, Defeng Wang, Kairui Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis investigation developed and evaluated a new type of high-temperature-resistant gel particle system (DPAG), which is specifically designed for conformance control in high-temperature reservoirs. Conventional conformance control systems, including polymer gel systems and foam-based technologies, often suffer from thermal degradation and insufficient plugging stability under high-temperature environments. To address these limitations, DPAG gel particles were synthesized via inverse emulsion polymerization, incorporating thermally stable functional monomers (AMPS and DAC) to create a dual-crosslinking network combining covalent bonds and electrostatic interactions. A comprehensive characterization combining chemical structure assessment, particle size measurement and microscopic morphology examination confirmed the successful synthesis of DPAG gel particles, revealing their uniform size distribution and well-defined morphology. Thermodynamic stability analysis, combined with rheological characterization, revealed that DPAG gel particles exhibit enhanced structural integrity, thermal resistance, and mechanical strength compared to conventional AM gel particles. Swelling kinetic studies revealed that DPAG gel particles exhibit significantly enhanced swelling characteristics, achieved a swelling ratio of 14.6. Experimental results indicated that DPAG microsphere dispersions at concentrations of 0.1% to 0.5% not only maintained excellent injectivity but also achieved remarkable plugging efficiency, reaching up to 98.7%. Particularly under high-temperature conditions, DPAG gel particles displayed outstanding stability, retaining excellent mechanical properties after 30 days with less than 17.4% strength loss. Core flooding experiments demonstrated that DPAG gel particles exhibit both deep migration capability and long-term stability in high-temperature reservoirs, ultimately enhancing oil recovery by 22.4%. These findings strongly indicate the significant potential of DPAG gel particles for well conformance control in high-temperature oilfields.Keywords: Gel particles, Thermal stability, Conformance control

Introduction

A problematic consequence of prolonged waterflooding development is that it exacerbates inter-layer conflicts. Injected water preferentially sweeps high-permeability regions, quickly increasing water cut, while leaving oil in low-permeability areas bypassed, which severely affects the overall development performance [1]. This phenomenon severely limits the overall crude oil recovery efficiency. Deep conformance control technology, which redirects fluid flow to enhance sweep efficiency, is a critical strategy for enhancing the oil reservoir development efficiency under such conditions [2–4]. However, conventional conformance control systems, such as polymer gels and foam-based system, are prone to thermal degradation, crosslinking failure, or dehydration-induced shrinkage under high-temperature reservoir conditions [5–7]. These issues result in weakened plugging strength, insufficient long-term stability, and resulting in water breakthrough again, making deep fluid diversion difficult to achieve. Therefore, developing advanced deep profile control systems suitable for high-temperature reservoirs is essential for improving oil recovery and prolonging the economic viability of oilfields [8].

The plugging agents currently used in high-temperature reservoirs face two major challenges. (1) Insufficient Thermal Stability: Traditional polymers are prone to molecular chain curling or breaking under high temperatures, leading to a sharp decline in viscosity [9, 10]. Gels may suffer from dehydration-induced structural destruction, while foams exhibit poor stability and rapid coalescence [11–13]. (2) Balancing Deep Migration and Effective Plugging: Profile control agents must have sufficient injection capacity to penetrate deep into the formation while maintaining long-term plugging stability at target locations [14, 15].

Currently, high-temperature profile control systems primarily include polymer gels, foams, and gel particles [16–18]. Among these, gel particles have become a research focus due to their excellent thermal resistance, swelling-induced plugging capability, and deep migration potential. The mechanism of gel particles relies on their water-triggered swelling behavior [19–21]. When the accumulated or swollen particles exceed the formation’s pore diameter, they create mechanical bridging at pore throats, diverting subsequent fluid flow toward low-permeability zones. Additionally, their elastic deformation allows them to break through narrow throats under differential pressure, enabling deep dynamic plugging [22]. However, the existing gel particle systems still have significant drawbacks under high-temperature conditions [23, 24]. On the one hand, high temperatures critically influence the expansion characteristics of gel particles, resulting in a decrease in the size compatibility with reservoir pore structures. Excessive swelling may cause injection difficulties or particle rupture, while insufficient swelling cannot form an effective plugging [25]. On the other hand, prolonged exposure to high temperatures induces progressive degradation of the three-dimensional polymer network within gel particles, ultimately compromising their long-term plugging efficacy during extended oil recovery operations. Recent advances in gel-based profile control systems have focused on addressing their inherent thermal stability limitations through innovative material design approaches [26, 27], which are also prevalent in other engineering fields facing high-temperature challenges [28–32]. Two primary research directions have been introduced to enhance high-temperature performance: molecular engineering and nanocomposite reinforcement. In molecular structure design, researchers have achieved significant improvements by incorporating thermally stable functional groups, such as sulfonic acid moieties from 2-acrylamido-2-methylpropanesulfonic acid (AMPS) monomers, which enhance polymer backbone rigidity through the steric effect. A notable advancement was demonstrated by Schuman et al. [33], who designed preformed particle gels (PPGs) based on the stable N,N-dimethylacrylamide (DMA) and comonomers containing benzene rings. Their accelerated aging tests revealed exceptional durability, with the modified PPGs maintaining structural integrity for over 18 months at 120 °C. The second approach involves nanocomposite reinforcement, where inorganic nanomaterials are integrated into polymer networks to create composite systems with enhanced thermal resistance [34–53]. Silica nanoparticles have proven particularly effective, as shown in Liu et al.‘s work with phenolic resin gels [54], where the nanoparticles served dual functions: physically reinforcing the three-dimensional network through surface interactions while simultaneously improving water retention properties. More recently, carbon-based nanomaterials have shown promise for extreme conditions; Zhu et al. [55] developed carbon nanofiber-reinforced PEI gels capable of withstanding ultra-high temperatures (>150 °C) while maintaining both excellent mechanical strength and stable rheological properties. The innovation of these materials has significantly expanded the application scope of the enhanced gel system in challenging reservoir. However, achieving the optimal balance between thermal stability and other key performance parameters (such as injectability and long-term stability) still poses significant challenges.

This investigation presents a thermally stable gel particle system (DPAG) or enhanced oil recovery applications, developed through an innovative electrostatic supramolecular reinforcement strategy. The DPAG particles were synthesized via inverse emulsion polymerization of acrylamide with thermally stable functional monomers (AMPS and DAC), creating a unique dual-crosslinking network architecture that combines covalent bonds with electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged side chains. In addition to the permanent covalent junctions supplied by MBAA, the electrostatic attraction between the anionic sulfonate groups of AMPS and the cationic quaternary ammonium groups of DAC serves as a reversible supramolecular crosslinking point. These dynamic ionic associations act as physical crosslinks that can break and reform, thereby enhancing bound-water retention and thermal tolerance. Together, the covalent and electrostatic interactions constitute the hierarchical dual-crosslinking network of the DPAG system. The synthesis process was systematically optimized by evaluating key parameters, including particle size distribution and mechanical strength, with chemical structure confirmation through FTIR spectroscopy. Comprehensive thermal characterization using TGA and DSC revealed the system’s degradation mechanisms and bound-water stability, while DLS and rheological measurements provided insights into the temperature-dependent swelling kinetics and mechanical property evolution. Core flooding experiments demonstrated the system’s exceptional conformance control capacity and enhanced oil recovery efficiency, confirming its potential for field applications in challenging high-temperature reservoirs.

Results and discussion

Characterization of DPAG gel particles

Analysis of the synthetic product

The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis results of the DPAG gel particles shown in Fig. 1 indicate that the ternary copolymer (AM–AMPS–DAC) was prepared using a free radical copolymerization technique with AM, DAC, and AMPS as monomers. The broad absorption band at 3430 cm−1 arises from the N–H stretching vibration of the primary amide group (–NH2). Conversely, the strong peak at 1664 cm−1 (amide I band) and the peak at 1195 cm−1 are assigned to the stretching vibrations of the C=O and C–N bonds within the amide group, respectively. These three characteristic peaks jointly confirm the successful polymerization of AM monomer. The characteristic peak observed at 961 cm−1 corresponds to the quaternary ammonium group (–N+(CH3)3), confirming the successful incorporation of the DAC monomer. Furthermore, the distinct absorption peak at 1049 cm−1 is assigned to the S=O stretching vibration of the sulfonic acid group (–SO3H), which serves as direct evidence for the inclusion of AMPS in the copolymer. The absence of a detectable peak near 1630 cm−1, coupled with the characteristic peaks of the formed functional groups, confirms the complete consumption of the vinyl groups and the success of the polymerization [56, 57].

Optimization of DPAG gel particles Preparation

This work conducted a systematic investigation into the influence of eight critical parameters in inverse emulsion polymerization on the performance indicators of DPAG gel particles, including average particle size (D50) and elastic modulus (G’) [58–60]. The parameters encompassed: total monomer concentration (20–40%), acrylamide (AM) proportion (70–90%), AMPS to DAC mass ratio (1:4 to 4:1), ammonium persulfate initiator dosage (0.02–0.07%), N,Nʹ-methylenebisacrylamide crosslinker concentration (0.1–0.5%), oil phase to aqueous phase volume ratio (3:5 to 5:3), composite emulsifier (Span-80/Tween-80 = 4:1) content (1–5%), and stirring speed (1000–3000 rpm). The experiment was conducted at 60 °C.

Influence of monomer composition

This study investigated the effects of monomer composition on the performances of DPAG gel particles, focusing on three critical parameters: total monomer concentration, acrylamide (AM) proportion, and AMPS to DAC mass ratio. Initially, under fixed conditions (m(AM):m(AMPS):m(DAC) = 80:14:6, 0.05% initiator, 0.3% crosslinker, 1:1 oil/water ratio, 3% composite emulsifier, and 2000 rpm stirring speed), the influence of total monomer concentration (20%, 25%, 30%, 35%, 40%) was examined. Figure 3a displays the experimental results.

The effects of monomer composition on the performances of DPAG gel particles, a total monomer concentration, b acrylamide (AM) proportion, and c AMPS to DAC mass ratio

Experimental data revealed that as the total monomer concentration increased from 20 to 35%, the viscosity of the polymerization system rose significantly, leading to enhanced droplet coalescence in the inverse emulsion and consequent growth of average particle size from 245 to 357 nm. Simultaneously, higher monomer concentrations promoted increased polymer chain density, resulting in tighter crosslinked networks and a remarkable improvement in elastic modulus from 34.6 to 51.9 Pa. However, when exceeding 35% total monomer concentration, excessive viscosity caused emulsification difficulties, producing irregular particles and broader size distribution. Therefore, the total monomer concentration should be maintained below 35% to balance particle uniformity and mechanical strength. Subsequently, at a fixed 35% total monomer concentration with AMPS: DAC = 3:1, the effect of AM proportion (70%, 75%, 80%, 85%, 90%) was investigated. Compared with DAC and AMPS functional monomers, AM exhibits superior reactivity ratios, and increasing its content facilitates polymer chain extension and molecular weight enhancement. Figure 3b demonstrates a clear correlation between AM content and DPAG gel particle size, where increasing the AM monomer ratio from 70 to 90% resulted in a progressive enlargement of average particle diameter from 269 to 346 nm, followed by size stabilization at higher concentrations. However, beyond 80% AM content, the predominance of linear AM structures reduced crosslinking network rigidity, causing a 10–25% decrease in elastic modulus. Thus, based on experimental data, the optimal AM proportion was determined to be 80%.

Finally, under optimized conditions (35% total monomer and 80% AM content), the AMPS: DAC mass ratio (1:4, 1:3, 1:2, 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1) was thoroughly examined. Compared with DAC, AMPS possesses a reactivity ratio more similar to acrylamide and higher molecular weight. Experimental results (Fig. 3c) demonstrated that as the AMPS: DAC ratio increased from 1:4 to 2:1, the particle size progressively enlarged from 297 to 354 nm. Moreover, the rigid sulfonic acid groups in AMPS significantly enhanced the deformation resistance of the polymer network, increasing the elastic modulus from 24.6 to 50.8 Pa. Nevertheless, when the AMPS: DAC ratio fell below 3:1, weakened electrostatic interactions between anionic AMPS and cationic DAC monomers led to a 7.9% reduction in elastic modulus. Consequently, considering both stability and mechanical performance, the optimal AMPS: DAC ratio should be maintained within the 2:1–3:1 range.

Influence of initiator and crosslinker

This study investigated the influences of initiator and crosslinker on DPAG gel particle performances. Under fixed conditions (35% total monomer, m(AM):m(AMPS):m(DAC) = 80:14:6, 0.3% crosslinker, 1:1 oil/water ratio, 3% emulsifier, and 2000 rpm stirring speed), the influence of initiator concentration (0.02–0.07%) was first examined. When the initiator dosage was increased from 0.02 to 0.05%, the radical concentration in the reaction system progressively rose, thereby effectively accelerating molecular chain propagation [61]. The enlargement of polymer chain structures led to the formation of a tighter crosslinked network. Consequently, the particle size of DPAG gel particles increased gradually from 303 to 357 nm, while the elastic modulus exhibited a significant enhancement from 41.1 to 51.9 Pa (Fig. 4a). However, when the initiator content is greater than 0.05%, the excessively fast reaction rate leads to an increase in the probability of chain termination, resulting in the formation of more low-molecular-weight components, which in turn causes a decrease in the average particle size and mechanical strength of the DPAG gel particles.

The effects of initiator and crosslinker on the performances of DPAG gel particles, a initiator, b crosslinker

At the optimal initiator concentration of 0.05%, the effect of crosslinker concentration (0.1–0.5%) was investigated. As shown in Fig. 4b, when the MBA content was below 0.3%, as the content increased, the particle size increased from 255 to 347 nm and the elastic modulus increased from 22.7 to 51.9 Pa. This behavior can be attributed to the enhanced rigidity of the gel network due to a higher density of crosslinking points. However, beyond 0.3% MBA, electrostatic repulsion and steric hindrance impeded further crosslinking, leading to stabilized particle size and elastic modulus. Specially, excessive MBA resulted in material brittleness, suggesting that 0.3% represents the optimal crosslinker dosage.

Influence of preparation parameters

This study investigated the effects of preparation parameters (oil-to-water ratio, composite emulsifier dosage, and stirring speed) on the performance of DPAG gel particles. The fixed conditions were: total monomer mass fraction of 35% (m(AM):m(AMPS):m(DAC) = 80:14:6), initiator concentration of 0.05%, and crosslinker concentration of 0.3%.

Figure 5a shows that both particle size and mechanical strength first rose then fell with increasing oil phase content. The optimal performance was achieved at an oil-to-water ratio of 1:1, yielding particles with an average diameter of 357 nm and elastic modulus of 51.9 Pa. Mechanistic analysis revealed that insufficient oil phase led to unstable emulsion systems and poor heat dissipation, resulting in uncontrolled polymerization and defective crosslinked networks. Conversely, excessive oil phase produced excessively small droplets with inadequate local monomer concentration, hindering macromolecular chain formation and ultimately reducing particle size and mechanical strength. Under the condition of a fixed Span-80/Tween-80 mass ratio of 4:1, the influence of composite emulsifier dosage (1.0–5.0%) on the DPAG gel particles was investigated. Figure 5b shows that when the dosage was insufficient (< 3.0%), the emulsion droplets were large and unevenly distributed. When the dosage was too high (> 3%), the interface film became too thick, which not only hindered the chain growth reaction but also triggered chain transfer side reactions, significantly reducing the effective concentration of free radicals, and ultimately resulting in smaller gel particle sizes and deteriorated mechanical properties. The stirring speed study (Fig. 5c) revealed that increasing rotation speed (1000–3000 rpm) monotonically decreased particle size while mechanical strength showed an initial increase followed by decrease. This is because the faster the stirring speed, the smaller the volume of the emulsion droplets, and the smaller the final formed gel particles. At the same time, the smaller the droplets, the fewer monomers contained within them, which is not conducive to the formation of large molecular chains and leads to a decrease in the cross-linking effect. Considering all these factors, the optimal stirring speed is 2000 rpm.

The effects of preparation parameters on the performances of DPAG gel particles, a oil-to-water ratio, b composite emulsifier dosage, c stirring speed

Through single-factor experiments, the optimal preparation conditions were determined as: total monomer 35 wt%, m(AM):m(AMPS):m(DAC) = 80:14:6, initiator 0.05%, crosslinker 0.3%, oil/water ratio 1:1, emulsifier 3%, and stirring speed 2000 rpm. The resultant DPAG gel particles exhibited ideal size distribution (D50 = 357 nm) and mechanical strength (G’ = 51.9 Pa), meeting the requirements for deep conformance control in high-temperature reservoirs.

Thermal stability evaluation

The thermal stability of DPAG gel particles is a critical parameter determining their application performance in high-temperature reservoirs. Under high temperatures, the thermal degradation behavior of gel networks directly affects their mechanical strength and volumetric stability. This study systematically investigated the thermal degradation characteristics and bound water retention capacity of DPAG gels.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Figure 6 reveals that both conventional AM gel particles and DPAG gel particles exhibit three distinct weight loss stages during thermal degradation [62]. The reference AM gel particles were synthesized using an AM–MBAA formulation following established literature protocols, with the MBAA molar ratio matched to that used in DPAG. No AMPS or DAC was included, allowing the AM–MBAA network to serve as a clear single-network control. For conventional AM gel particles, the initial weight loss stage occurs between 40 and 196 °C with approximately 10.4% mass loss. Similarly, DPAG gel particles show a comparable first-stage weight loss of about 11.8%, and this weight loss occurs during the heating stage from 40 to 245 °C. This initial mass reduction is mainly due to the hydrophilicity of the polymer network, which causes the absorbed water to evaporate. The second degradation stage for AM gel particles appears between 196 and 348 °C with 29.5% weight loss. In contrast, DPAG gel particles demonstrate their second-stage decomposition (17.3%) during the heating stage from 245 to 357 °C. This stage corresponds to the breakdown of molecular side chains. While AM gel particles contain only –CONH2 side chains from AM units, DPAG gel particles incorporate additional functional groups including –COO(CH2)2N(CH3)3Cl from DAC and –CONHC(CH3)2CH2SO3H from AMPS. These bulkier side chains with higher molecular weights in the DPAG particles exhibit better thermal stability and less weight loss during thermal degradation at this stage. The final degradation stage occurs between 348 and 600 °C for AM gel particles (48.9% weight loss) and 357–600 °C for DPAG gel particles (50.2% weight loss). The final residual mass was 11.2% and 20.7%, consisting predominantly of inorganic salts. This stage represents the primary chain scission and carbon skeleton breakdown under high-temperature conditions. Considering most high-temperature reservoirs operate below 245 °C, DPAG gel particles demonstrate outstanding structural stability that fully meets the requirements for high-temperature reservoir applications.

Swelling performance under high-temperature condition

The swelling behavior of gel particles is a key indicator for evaluating their in-depth profile control capability. This study systematically investigated the swelling characteristics of DPAG gel particles under high-temperature conditions through a comparative analysis with conventional gel particles.

As shown in Fig. 7, DPAG gel particles exhibit unique swelling kinetics under high temperature (130 °C) and high salinity (19,484.6 mg/L) conditions. Experimental results demonstrate that DPAG gel particles require 5 days aging to reach equilibrium swelling ratio (14.6), whereas conventional AM gel particles achieve maximum swelling ratio (12.4) within just 2 days. This unique behavior stems from their fundamental structural differences. The DPAG gel network constructs a tighter three-dimensional crosslinked structure through the electrostatic interaction, as well as the synergistic effect of multiple hydrogen bond networks. This unique supramolecular interaction restricts the free extension of polymer chain segments, thereby regulating the rate at which water molecules penetrate the gel network. Although the swelling rate is relatively slow, the DPAG gel particles ultimately achieve a higher swelling ratio, which is due to their larger charged side chain density enhancing the hydrophilicity. During the conformance control operation in the high-temperature reservoirs, the rapid swelling of gel particles can lead to premature plugging of the gel particles in the near-wellbore area, severely weakening their ability to migrate towards the deeper part of the reservoir. The delayed swelling characteristic of DPAG gel… is inferred to maintain excellent injection performance, thereby allowing the DPAG gel particles to migrate effectively to deeper reservoir regions before undergoing significant swelling and plugging, as supported by the deep-bed pressure responses shown in Fig. 10.

Mechanical strength under high-temperature condition

The mechanical strength of gel particles under high-temperature condition critically influences their plugging efficiency in channeling pathways. This research evaluated the durability and the long-term mechanical performance of DPAG gel particles in high temperature (130 °C) and high salinity (19,484.6 mg/L) environments through rheological analysis.

As shown in Fig. 8a, DPAG gel particles exhibit excellent mechanical properties and thermal stability. Experimental results reveal a 18.2% enhancement in initial elastic modulus (G’ = 51.9 Pa) compared to conventional AM gel particles (G’ = 43.9 Pa), which is attributed to their unique multi-scale crosslinked network where covalent crosslinks form a rigid framework, electrostatic interactions between sulfonate and quaternary ammonium groups create dynamic physical crosslinks, and hydrogen-bonding networks provide supplementary reinforcement, collectively leading to significantly improved elastic modulus. The elastic modulus (G’) is a critical indicator of the gel’s resistance to deformation under stress. In the context of conformance control, a higher G’ suggests that the gel particles can withstand a greater differential pressure before being extruded through a pore throat, directly relating to a higher breakthrough pressure in porous media. During 130 °C accelerated aging tests (Fig. 8b), DPAG gel particles maintain exceptional stability with 86.1% modulus retention after 30 days, outperforming conventional AM gel particles (61.0%). DSC analyses confirm that the superior performance stems from higher bound water content and more stable crosslink density, where hydration layers mitigate thermal stress, dynamic physical networks enable self-healing, and robust covalent linkages resist degradation, ensuring structural integrity and plugging performance under high-temperature reservoirs and making DPAG gel particles an ideal solution for deep conformance control applications.

Plugging capacity

This study systematically investigates the critical factors influencing DPAG gel particles’ plugging performance in high-permeability channels, which directly determines subsequent waterflood sweep efficiency. Two key parameters, the gel particle-to-pore throat size ratio and DPAG concentration, were identified as primary controlling factors [63]. Through designed experimental series, we examined these relationships: (1) maintaining a constant particle concentration of 0.4% while varying the size ratio from 0.1 to 0.5, and (2) fixing the size ratio at 0.3 while adjusting concentrations from 0.1 to 0.4%. The core plugging efficiency results were presented in Fig. 9.

The influence laws of the plugging rate of DPAG gel particles, a the ratio of particle diameter to pore throat size, b DPAG concentration

The experimental results demonstrate a significant influence of particle-to-pore throat size ratio on plugging efficiency (Fig. 9a). When the size ratio ranges from 0.1 to 0.4, the plugging efficiency increases markedly with the ratio, reaching a maximum of 98.0%, which can be attributed to a better geometric match between the particles and the pore channels, thereby enhancing the plugging effect. However, when the ratio exceeds 0.5, the plugging efficiency declines, likely due to excessive interparticle spacing that leads to an uncompact bridging structure. Concentration-dependent tests reveal that increasing particle concentration from 0.1 to 0.5% elevates plugging efficiency from 88.0 to 98.7%, owing to enhanced cooperative plugging effects from denser particle packing. It should be noted that while higher concentrations improve local plugging, they may reduce the effective treatment distance due to premature particle accumulation [64]. This phenomenon is not easily observable in short core experiments, but it is highly detrimental in actual reservoir applications involving long-distance migration.

In-depth migration in core

This study systematically investigates the in-depth migration behavior of DPAG gel particles in formation through sand-packed core flooding experiments. The key characteristics of the long sand-packed cores used for evaluating in-depth migration capability are presented in Table 2, while Fig. 10 illustrates the dynamic pressure variations at three monitoring points (A, B, and C) during DPAG particle injection and migration. After the 1.5 PV water flooding experiment was conducted, a suspension of DPAG gel particles was injected. As the gel particles progressively migrated toward the core outlet, distinct pressure responses were observed at each monitoring point upon arrival of the particle front, demonstrating effective pressure transmission along the flow path. The particles exhibited a characteristic “migrate-plug-remigrate” dynamic behavior: initial particle accumulation and swelling at pore throats resulted in substantial pressure buildup, achieving approximately 97% plugging efficiency at 1.8 PV injection volume, followed by a transient pressure decline indicating breakthrough and redistribution of particles. The injection was continued until reaching 6 PV, revealing a stage of increased secondary pressure and slight fluctuations. At the end of 6 PV injection, the final plugging efficiencies at monitoring points A, B, and C were calculated to be 96.4%, 97.5%, and 97.6%, respectively, using the Eq. (2). The pressures at all three points remained high without significant decline, indicating that the plugging structure was relatively permanent without ultimate breakthrough. This further demonstrates the dynamic blocking process of the gel particles, including “migration, blockage, re-migration, secondary expansion and re-blockage”. The observed dynamic plugging mechanism suggests that DPAG particles can achieve both deep penetration and sustainable conformance control, addressing the critical challenge of balancing injectivity with plugging efficiency in high-permeability zones. The pressure response curves further demonstrate the particles’ ability to successively create multiple barrier effects along the flow path for improved sweep efficiency.

Plugging performance and EOR potential

Figure 11 demonstrates that the injected DPAG gel particles preferentially enter high-permeability water-flooding channels under flow resistance guidance. Through hydration swelling and interparticle aggregation, the particles form dense packing structures that effectively plug dominant flow channels. Plugging experiments show this plugging can reduce channel permeability, significantly increasing flow resistance. According to Darcy’s law, subsequent injected fluids will automatically divert to previously unswept low-permeability zones, significantly enhancing the oil recovery. This mechanism reveals how DPAG gel particles improve oil recovery through the synergistic effects of channel plugging, flow diversion, and residual oil mobilization [65].

Schematic representation of EOR mechanisms by the DPAG gel particles

Building upon previous findings demonstrating the critical influence of DPAG gel particle-to-pore throat size ratio and concentration on plugging efficiency, this study quantitatively investigates the relationship between plugging performance and EOR potential. Through systematic variation of DPAG particle size relative to core pore throat dimensions, we established a series of cores with plugging efficiencies ranging from 75.3 to 98.8% [66–70]. Subsequent secondary oil displacement experiments revealed a well-defined correlation between plugging efficiency and EOR potential, as illustrated in Fig. 12.

The influence of plugging capacity on the EOR potential and typical oil displacement experimental curves, a low plugging rate, b appropriate plugging rate, c high plugging rate, d relationship between plugging rate and EOR potential

As the DPAG gel particle plugging efficiency increased from 75.3 to 96.1%, the enhanced oil recovery showed a corresponding enhancement from 11.1 to 22.4%. However, the study revealed a critical threshold beyond which excessive plugging (> 98%) led to diminished EOR effectiveness. This phenomenon can be attributed to two primary mechanisms: (1) Excessive plugging strength increases the injection pressure required for subsequent displacement, potentially causing irreversible formation damage (e.g., fines migration, clay swelling), thereby impairing their productivity. (2) High plugging rate often correlate with limited deep migration of gel particles, resulting in a predominantly near-wellbore plug. This shallow blocking can be bypassed during continued injection, failing to achieve sustainable in-depth fluid diversion and sweep improvement. The DPAG system partially mitigates this risk through its delayed-swelling behavior under high-temperature, high-salinity conditions. The slow swelling kinetics allow the particles to be transported deeper into the reservoir before developing their maximum plugging strength, thereby minimizing the likelihood of near-wellbore blockage and enabling more uniform in-depth placement. Consequently, field application strategies should be designed to maintain plugging efficiency within the optimal range of 94.7% to 98%. This can be achieved by employing techniques such as staged injection with graded particle sizes, real-time bottom-hole pressure monitoring to avoid formation damage, thereby maximizing sweep efficiency while safeguarding the productivity of the entire reservoir.

Conclusions

In this study, a novel high-temperature-resistant gel particle system (DPAG) was successfully developed and evaluated for deep conformance control in oil reservoirs. Compared with previously reported AMPS- or DAC-based thermal-resistant gels, the DPAG system incorporates a dual-crosslinking network consisting of covalent MBAA bonds and AMPS-DAC electrostatic associations. This structural design enhances bound-water retention, delays swelling, and significantly improves long-term thermal stability, distinguishing DPAG from single-network AMPS or DAC systems. The main conclusions are as follows:

Single-factor experiments identified optimal conditions for DPAG preparation, including a total monomer concentration of 35 wt%, m(AM):m(AMPS):m(DAC) = 80:14:6, initiator dosage of 0.05%, and crosslinker concentration of 0.3%. These parameters yielded particles with ideal size distribution (D50 = 357 nm) and mechanical strength (G’ = 51.9 Pa).

TGA and DSC analyses revealed that DPAG gel particles maintained structural integrity up to 245 °C, with superior bound water retention (78.9% after 10 days at 130 °C) compared to conventional AM gel particles.

Experimental section

Materials

The monomer materials, including acrylamide (AM, ≥ 99%, MW = 71.08 g/mol), 2-acrylamido-2-methylpropanesulfonic acid (AMPS, ≥ 98%, MW = 207.25 g/mol), acryloyloxyethyltrimethyl ammonium chloride (DAC, 80% aqueous solution), and crosslinker N,Nʹ-methylenebisacrylamide (MBAA, 99%), were sourced from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Formation water was simulated with total salinity of 19,484.6 mg/L (detailed ionic composition provided in Table 3). All experimental solutions were formulated using deionized water (resistivity ≥ 18.15 MΩ cm) generated in-house through a purification system.

Preparation of DPAG gel particles

The DPAG were synthesized via inverse emulsion polymerization. First, a homogeneous oil phase was prepared by mixing white oil, Span-80, and Tween-80 under stirring at 300 rpm for 15 min. The polymerization system was prepared by first dissolving the monomers (AM, AMPS, DAC), crosslinker (MBAA), and initiator (APS) in deionized water, followed by pH adjustment to 7.0 ± 0.1 using 20 wt% NaOH solution. Oxygen was removed from the solution through 30 min of purging with high-purity nitrogen under continuous stirring. The resulting deoxygenated aqueous phase was then emulsified with the oil phase by injecting it into the oil under high-shear mixing at 3000 rpm for 15 min, producing a uniform water-in-oil emulsion. After purging with high-purity N2 for 15 min, the emulsion was transferred to a thermostatic magnetic stirrer at 60 °C, and polymerization was carried out at 300 rpm for 4 h. The obtained product was isolated by centrifugation, followed by three washing cycles with anhydrous ethanol. It was then dried under vacuum at 60 °C. A fine powder was acquired as the final product after grinding and sieving the dried material. The chemical structures of the reactive monomers and polymerization crosslinking products of DPAG gel particles are shown in Fig. 13. The prepared DPAG gel particles were dispersed as a standard dispersion solution with a concentration of 0.4 wt%. All subsequent experiments were conducted based on this concentration.

Characterization of DPAG gel particles

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy characterization (FTIR)

The molecular architecture of the DPAG gel particles was investigated using FTIR spectroscopy (KBr pellet technique). Spectral data collection was performed under standardized conditions (25 ± 0.5 °C) across the mid-infrared region (4000–400 cm−1), employing a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1 to ensure precise vibrational mode identification.

Dynamic light scattering analysis (DLS)

The hydrodynamic diameter distribution of DPAG gel particles was measured through DLS measurements conducted at 25.0 ± 0.1 °C. Before measurement, the particles were dispersed in deionized water. All measurements were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Rheology measurement

The viscoelastic behavior of the DPAG gel particles was assessed with a rotational rheometer at 25 °C. The linear viscoelastic region (LVR) was first established via stress sweep tests conducted from 0.1 to 10 Pa at a fixed angular frequency of 6.18 rad/s. Frequency oscillatory sweeps ranging from 0.1 to 100 rad/s were then carried out within the predetermined LVR to measure the elastic modulus (G’) and viscous modulus (G”).

Thermodynamic analysis

The thermal stability of DPAG gel particles was investigated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) under nitrogen purge (50 mL/min). Dried samples (5–10 mg) were analyzed in alumina crucibles with a programmed temperature ramp (25–600 °C at 10 °C/min). The continuous mass loss monitoring facilitates identification of specific decomposition temperatures.

In-depth migration capacity

The long sand-packed core model experiments were used to systematically investigate the in-depth migration capability of DPAG gel particles [15]. As shown in Fig. 15, the experimental system included a 600 mm long sand-packed core (25 mm in diameter) divided into three equal segments (200 mm each) with pressure monitoring points positioned at the injection end (Point A), 200 mm (Point B), and 400 mm (Point C) along the core length. The experimental procedure commenced with careful preparation of the sand pack, where uniformly graded quartz sand was gradually loaded into the core holder under continuous compaction to achieve consistent packing density. The dry weight of the packed core was precisely measured before saturation, which was performed using a vacuum-assisted method with formation water to ensure complete pore space filling. Following saturation, the wet weight was recorded to get the pore volume and porosity through gravimetric analysis. Core permeability was then determined through steady-state brine flooding at a steady injection rate of 0.5 mL/min. The DPAG gel particle suspension was subsequently injected at the same injection rate for a total of 6.0 pore volumes (PV) while continuously monitoring pressure evolution at all three measurement points using pressure sensors.

Oil displacement experiment

Core flooding experiments were performed using the apparatus depicted in Fig. 14 to evaluate the EOR potential of DPAG gel particles. The sandstone cores were first cleaned, dried, and saturated with simulated oil through vacuum pressurization, then kept at 70 °C for 7 days to establish initial reservoir conditions. During testing, the core sample was put in a core holder with 3.0 MPa confining pressure above injection pressure and 0.1 MPa backpressure. Formation water injection continued until water cut stabilized at 98%, followed by injection of 0.5 PV DPAG gel particles at identical injection rate. Following gel treatment, the core was hermetically sealed and subjected to 130 °C thermal aging for 5 days to simulate reservoir conditions, then waterflooding was carried out at a constant 0.5 mL/min injection rate. Real-time monitoring of injection pressure dynamics coupled with comprehensive effluent analysis (oil/water fraction determination) enabled quantitative evaluation of the conformance control performance.
