Multifaceted effects of galU deletion on phenotype and virulence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Jie Yu, Jinying Wang, Yan Yang, Lang Sun, Xinxin Hu, Tongying Nie, Xinyi Yang, Xiukun Wang, Congran Li, Xuefu You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPseudomonas aeruginosa is a widespread Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen in environmental and hospital settings, frequently causing respiratory diseases such as cystic fibrosis (CF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD), and ventilator-associated pneumonia. In our previous study, a galU-deleted clinical P. aeruginosa was found to exhibit increased susceptibility to polymyxins. The galU gene plays an important role in the biosynthesis of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) O-antigen. Here, we systematically evaluated the effects of galU deletion on the phenotype and virulence of P. aeruginosa PAO1. A galU deletion mutant was successfully constructed in P. aeruginosa PAO1 by CRISPR/Cas9, and the complementation was accomplished by pUCP18 plasmid carrying wild-type galU. The changes in phenotype, virulence, and pathogenicity were systemically studied. The results revealed that knockout of galU led to the loss of O-antigen, which affected growth, virulence, and pathogenicity through various ways in P. aeruginosa, and significantly affected the susceptibility of P. aeruginosa to polymyxins. Mechanism study suggested the involvements of quorum sensing, Entner–Doudoroff pathway, and tyrosine metabolism on bacterial virulence and antibiotic susceptibility changes after galU deletion. galU and the related pathways may serve as effective targets for the treatment of P. aeruginosa infection, providing a theoretical basis for the development of novel antibacterial drugs.KEYWORDS: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, galU deletion, virulence, polymyxin resistance, tyrosine metabolism, quorum sensing

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) is a ubiquitous microorganism and common gram-negative bacterium [1]. P. aeruginosa induces various severe infections in hospitals, especially in immunocompromised individuals [2]. P. aeruginosa occurs frequently in drinking water and is extensively resistant to existing antibiotics [3]. Metabolic versatility, multiple virulence factors, biofilm formation, and resistance to numerous antibiotics collectively contribute to the challenges in treating P. aeruginosa infections [4].

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a major component of the outer leaflet of the outer membrane of most gram-negative bacteria and an important virulence factor in P. aeruginosa [5,6]. LPS is composed of three parts: a hydrophobic domain known as lipid A, a non-repeating “core” oligosaccharide, and a hydrophilic O-antigen [7]. The diverse O-antigen is the basis for serotype classification of P. aeruginosa and the receptor of phage to recognize bacteria [5,8]. The galU gene, which encodes UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase in most gram-negative pathogens, is necessary for the synthesis of O-antigen [9–12], and the galU mutant produces a rough-LPS (R-LPS) with a truncated core oligosaccharide [5]. In Salmonella Typhimurium, galU is an important virulence factor, and galU mutants have defects in biofilm formation and motility [11]. In addition, galU knockout mutant of Streptococcus pneumoniae cannot produce capsular polysaccharide, and galU gene is considered a suitable target to inhibit virulence factors in S. pneumoniae [10,12]. A previous study showed that galU was required for corneal infection and efficient systemic spread [13]. In addition, P. aeruginosa isolates from the lungs of patients with advanced cystic fibrosis generally exhibit R-LPS [5,13]. Existing research has primarily evaluated the effects of galU mutations in P. aeruginosa on phage recognition and pathogenicity in vivo [13,14]; a systematic evaluation of galU functions in P. aeruginosa is still lacking. In our previous study, the P. aeruginosa isolates from the sputum of a clinical patient showed large chromosomal deletions containing galU [15] after microevolution during a short period, and the strains had changes in phenotype and were more susceptible to colistin (MIC decreased from 1 to 0.25 μg/mL) and gentamicin (MIC decreased from 2 to 0.25 μg/mL) compared to the parental strain. Thus, to comprehensively investigate the effects of galU gene on the phenotype and virulence of P. aeruginosa and to delve into the functions of galU in bacteria, we constructed a galU-partial deletion mutant in P. aeruginosa PAO1 using CRISPR/Cas9. Changes in phenotype and virulence after galU deletion in P. aeruginosa PAO1 were examined, and the possible mechanisms were explored.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and plasmids

The strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Table 1, and all strains were stored in the CAMS Collection Center of Pathogen Microorganisms (CAMS-CCPM-A). Bacteria were grown at 37°C in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth (BD Difco, USA) with shaking at 220 rpm or on LB plates containing 1.5% agar (Beijing SanYao Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). When required, ampicillin, tetracycline, and carbenicillin were added at 100 μg/mL, 50 μg/mL, and 50 μg/mL, respectively (for E. coli). For P. aeruginosa, carbenicillin, and tetracycline were added at concentrations of 150 and 100 μg/mL, respectively.

Animals

ICR mice (SPF grade, 18–20 g, half male and half female) were purchased from Spelford Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The mice were housed under controlled humidity (40–60%) and temperature (22 ± 2°C) with a 12 h light–dark cycle and free access to food and water. All animal studies followed the animal husbandry guidelines, and all animal experiments were performed according to the national standards for laboratory animals in China (GB/T 35,892–2018) [16] with approval from the Animal Care & Welfare Committee of the Institute of Medicinal Biotechnology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College (approval No. IMB-20250107D901).

Gene deletion by CRISPR/Cas9

Deletion of galU in P. aeruginosa PAO1 using CRISPR/Cas9 was performed as described previously [17,18]. The strains, plasmids, and primers used are listed in Table 1 and Table S1, respectively. Briefly, the related fragments for deletion were amplified by PCR using genomic DNA from P. aeruginosa PAO1 as the template. The plasmid pACRISPR carrying sgRNA, the repair template containing upstream and downstream sequences for galU partial deletion, and the plasmid pCasPA containing Cas9 protein were electroporated into P. aeruginosa PAO1 cells. Partial deletion of galU was checked by PCR amplification of the related region with a pair of primers (ko-check-F/ko-check-R), which will end with a fragment of 1367 bp for the wild type and 541 bp for the galU partial deletion mutant PAO1∆galU (with deletion of −1 to 825 bp of the galU gene).

Complementation of galU

The primer pair galU-F/galU-R (Table S1) was used to amplify the wild-type galU gene from P. aeruginosa PAO1. The fragment was cloned into the pUCP18 vector using EcoRI and HindIII restriction sites, resulting in plasmid pUCP18-galU. The pUCP18-galU plasmid was transformed into P. aeruginosa PAO1∆galU after verification by PCR using a pair of primers pUCP18-F/pUCP18-R and sequencing (Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd.).

LPS isolation and SDS-PAGE

LPS isolation from PAO1 and PAO1∆galU was performed using a Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) Isolation Kit (Sigma-Aldrich Co. LLC, MAK339, USA) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the strains were swept from fresh LB plates, resuspended in 1 × PBS to OD600s higher than 0.6, and washed twice by 1 × PBS. The pellets were weighed, resuspended in lysis buffer provided in the kit at 100 μL/10 mg, and sonicated (3 s on and 3 s off, for 5 min in total) on ice. After incubating on ice for another 10 min, the mixtures were centrifuged, and the supernatants were transferred to clean 1.5 mL tubes. Proteinase K was added to a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL, and the samples were heated at 60°C for 60 min. The heat lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatants containing LPS were collected. For LPS development, the isolated LPS was separated on standard discontinuous sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)–12.5% polyacrylamide gels and visualized by silver staining using a Pierce Silver Stain Kit (Thermofisher, USA).

Determination of MICs for antibiotics

The drug susceptibility of bacteria was determined using the microdilution method according to the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [19]. The test drugs were diluted in cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton (CAMH) broth (BD BBL, USA) and added to a round-bottomed 96-well plate (non-tissue culture treated) (NEST Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). The bacterial concentration was adjusted using sterile saline and 1:10 added to the plate with a final inoculum of 5 × 105 CFU/mL. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 16–18 h. MIC was determined as the minimal drug concentration that inhibits bacterial growth observed with naked eyes.

Growth curves

Three independent cultures per strain were grown in tubes containing 3 mL LB broth and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 220 rpm for 6 ~ 8 h. The cultures were then diluted to about 1 × 106 CFU/mL with fresh CAMH broth and allocated into the wells of a honeycomb plate (four technical repeats for each sample) with 300 μL per well. The cultures were incubated at 37°C, and the OD600 was measured every 10 min using Bioscreen (Bioscreen C, Finland) for 24 h. Growth curves were plotted using the GraphPad Prism 8 software (La Jolla, CA, USA). After subtracting the background at time 0 h, a scatter plot was created using the time values (X-axis) and the base-2 logarithm of absorbances (Y-axis). The relative growth rate (in comparison to PAO1) and the doubling time were calculated using data within the OD600 range of 0.01–0.1.

Biofilm formation

Biofilm formation assay was performed as previously described [20]. Briefly, fresh bacterial colonies were suspended in 1 mL sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), centrifuged at 300 rpm for 10 min, and washed once with the same volume of PBS. The concentration of the bacterial suspension was accurately adjusted to OD600 = 0.5, and 10 μL of the bacterial suspension was mixed with 990 μL of Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (BD Bacto, USA) containing 0.5% glucose. When assessing the impact of exogenous substances on biofilm development, 0.05% (w/v) GABA or 0.01% (w/v) tyrosine was supplemented instead of 0.5% glucose. The bacterial suspension was allocated into a flat-bottomed 96-well plate at 200 μL/well, and the plate was incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The culture medium in the wells was carefully removed and the biofilms grown on the well walls were gently washed twice with PBS. The wells were then baked at 60°C for 45 min and stained with 0.1% safranin for 1 min, before being rinsed twice with PBS. The adhering dyes were dissolved in 30% acetic acid and the absorbances were measured at 490 nm.

Pyocyanin and pyoverdine productions

The strains were grown in LB broth at 37°C with shaking at 220 rpm for 6 h and then 1:100 diluted in 10 mL LB broth. The cultures were incubated at 37°C for 25 h before pyocyanin and pyoverdine extractions. Pyocyanin production was assayed as previously described with slight modifications [1]. Overnight cultures were centrifuged for 5 min at 4000 × g. The supernatants were mixed with 2 mL chloroform and vortexed for 20 s. After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, the top aqueous phases and bottom organic phases were transferred to new tubes. 800 μL of 0.2 M HCl was added to the blue organic phases, and the tubes were vortexed for 10 s, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The OD520s of the top pink phases were measured and multiplied by 17.072 to calculate pyocyanin concentrations (μg/mL). For pyoverdine production, fluorescences of the aqueous phases were measured at an excitation (Ex) wavelength of 405 nm and an emission (Em) wavelength of 460 nm. The results are expressed as the fluorescence intensity (F. I.).

Exopolysaccharides (EPS) production

The medium used for the assay was 2% tryptone (OXOID, LP0042B) containing 1.5% agar and 0.004% Congo Red (MACKLIN, C804149-100 g). The strains were inoculated in LB broth at 37°C until the logarithmic phase, and 2 μL bacterial cultures were dripped onto the surface of the plates and incubated at 30°C for 24 h. EPS could form a complex with Congo Red, thereby displaying a deeper red color. EPS production was determined by comparing the depths of the red color and the size of the areas.

Total proteolytic activity and staphylolytic activity assay

The procedure of the assay was as previously reported [21,22] with some modifications. For total proteolytic activity, culture supernatants of strains (0.1 mL) were incubated with 0.1 mL Difco skim milk (5%) (BD Difco, USA) in 96-well plates at 37°C for 30 min, and the changes in turbidity were measured at OD600. For staphylolytic activity, an overnight culture of Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213 was boiled for 10 min and centrifuged at 10,000 × g to collect the pellet. The pellet was resuspended in 1 × PBS and adjusted to an OD600 of approximately 0.8. A 900-μL aliquot of S. aureus suspension was mixed with 100 μL of P. aeruginosa supernatant. The decrease in turbidity was expressed as the change in OD600 due to staphylolytic activity measured after 1 h.

Motility assays

The motility assay was conducted as previously described [23]. For swimming, the swimming plates (containing 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L NaCl and 0.3% agar) were inoculated with bacteria from 16–18 h cultures on plates at 37°C with sterile toothpicks and wrapped with plastic wraps before incubating at 30°C for 16 h. For swarming, the swarming plates (LB broth supplemented with 0.5% agar and 0.5% glucose) were inoculated with bacteria from 16–18 h cultures onto the surface of the plate using sterile toothpicks and incubated at 37°C for 16–18 h.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The 16–18 h overnight cultures were collected by centrifugation into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. Fresh 2.5% glutaraldehyde was added to the tubes and the samples were fixed at 4°C for 10 h to preserve ultrastructural integrity. After fixation, critical point drying and gold sputtering were performed. The influence of gene deletion on bacterial appearance was then observed using SEM (HITACHI S4800/SU8010/SU8020, Japan) by Beijing Zhongkebaice Technology Service Co., Ltd.

Adhesion and anti-phagocytic assay

The human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cell line A549 was used to compare the adhesion abilities of different strains, and the murine monocyte macrophage RAW264.7 was used to study the anti-phagocytic ability. A549 and RAW264.7 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). For adhesion assay, A549 cells were cultured in 24-well plates at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/well. The suspensions were removed, and the cells were washed twice with 1 × PBS slightly after A549 cells adhered to the wall (this step must not exceed 24 h after plating). Fresh bacterial cultures in LB broth were diluted to 2 × 106 CFU/mL in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA), and 1 mL/well was added to each well. A549 cells and bacteria were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 h. The wells were washed thrice with 1 × PBS to remove planktonic bacteria, and 0.1% SDS was added to each well to lyse the cells. The bacteria adhering to A549 cells were determined by plating the lysed cell suspensions on plates after appropriate dilution. The procedure for the anti-phagocytic assay was similar, except for the 1 h treatment with 200 μg/mL gentamicin to eliminate extracellular bacteria prior to cell lysis.

Pathogenicity toward ICR mouse

Thirty mice were randomly allocated by weight into three groups, with 10 mice per group (half male and half female). The strains were freshly cultured in enrichment broth (CAMH broth supplemented with 0.5% yeast extract) and diluted. Mice were infected by intraperitoneal injection at a dose of about 1 × 108 CFU/mouse. The deaths in each group were recorded daily for 5 d, and the survival rates were calculated. Survival curves were plotted using the GraphPad Prism 8 software. Mice that remained alive at the end of the observation period were euthanized with carbon dioxide in compliance with the 3 R principles. The study adhered to ARRIVE guidelines, and the completed checklist was provided in the supplementary materials.

Transcriptome analysis and RT-qPCR

For each strain, a single clone was inoculated in LB medium with three biological replicates and grown at 37°C overnight for 16–18 h. The overnight cultures were diluted 1:50 using fresh LB and grown to an OD600 of 0.6–0.8. Total RNAs were extracted from the strains using a TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The concentrations of RNAs were determined using NanoDrop and Qubit. To ensure RNA integrity and quantity, the samples were analyzed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Transcriptome sequencing was performed using Novogene (Beijing, China) and Illumina. Libraries were constructed, quality controlled, clustered, and sequenced. All transcriptome sequencing samples included three biological replicates. And FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped fragments) of each gene was calculated based on the length of the gene and the read count mapped to this gene. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq R package (1.18.0). The p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg approach for controlling the false discovery rate. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 by DESeq and |Log2foldchange| >0, and the statistical enrichment of differential expression genes in KEGG pathways was tested by KOBAS software. For RT-qPCR validation, cDNAs were synthesized from the isolated RNAs using TransScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). RT-qPCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q712-02-AA).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assay

The affinity between LPS and colistin was detected using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., UK). The concentrations of the drug and LPS were 0.001 M and 0.02 M respectively. The specific parameters were as follows: temperature, 37°C; reference power, 9.00 μcal/s; feedback, high; stir speed, 800 rpm; initial delay, 60 s. Each experiment included 25 injections and 120 s of spacing between two injections.

Metabolite assay

The concentrations of glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), pyruvate, and succinate in different bacterial cells were detected by G6P Assay Kit with WST-8, Amplex Red Pyruvate Assay Kit, and Amplex Red Succinate Assay Kit, respectively, from Beyotime Biotech Inc (Shanghai, China). The strains were incubated at 37°C overnight and diluted to 1 × 109 CFU/mL with saline the next day. The strain cultures were centrifuged at 4°C, 10,000 × g for 2 min to collect cell pellets. The cell pellets were resuspended in the lysis buffer provided with the corresponding kits, and the absorbances at 600 nm were measured. The bacterial resuspensions were sonicated at 22% power with 3 s on and 10 s off for 30 times. The lysis mixtures were centrifuged at 4°C, 12,000 × g for 2 min, and the supernatants were then used for G6P, pyruvate, and succinate concentration measurements per manufacturers’ instructions. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analyses

Data collection in the study was carried out between February in 2024 and January in 2026. All statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 8 software. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and each biological replicate contained at least three technical replicates. Differences were analyzed by unpaired t-test (between two groups) and one-way ANOVA (more than two groups). The results are presented as mean ± SD.

Results

Construction of galU partial deletion mutant and complementation

CRISPR/Cas9 was used to construct galU partial deletion mutant in PAO1 (resulting in PAO1∆galU), and the pUCP18-galU plasmid was used for galU complementation (resulting in PAO1∆galU C). The resulting strains, PAO1∆galU and PAO1∆galU C, were verified by PCR amplifications (Figure 1(A), Figure S1) and Sanger sequencing (Figure 1(B)). The deletion and complementation of galU were also confirmed by qPCR (Figure 1(C)). In addition, electrophoresis and silver staining of LPS from the strains in this study showed truncation of the bacterial LPS core and a decrease in molecular weight after galU deletion (Figure 1(D), Figure S2), confirming the successful construction of the mutant.

galU deletion affects antibiotic susceptibility of PAO1

The MIC results showed that galU deletion had various effects on antibiotic susceptibility (Table 2). For quinolones and polymyxins, the galU deletion mutant showed twofold decrease in levofloxacin and ciprofloxacin MICs and fourfold decrease in polymyxin B and colistin MICs. In contrast, galU deletion caused twofold increase in gentamicin and meropenem MICs and a fourfold increase in ceftazidime MIC. galU complementation generally recovered the MIC changes, except for ceftazidime, which still demonstrated a twofold increase in comparison with the wild-type PAO1. For amikacin, even though galU deletion did not demonstrate an obvious effect, complementation caused a twofold decrease in the MIC.

galU deletion affects growth and biofilm formation ability of PAO1

The effect of galU deletion on bacterial growth was determined using Bioscreen C. The results showed that the growth of PAO1 slowed down after galU deletion (Figure 2(A)). The growth rate of the early logarithmic phase decreased after galU deletion and recovered after galU complementation, with the relative growth rates of PAO1∆galU and PAO1∆galU C being 0.65 and 1.03 in comparison to PAO1 (Figure 2(B)). The corresponding doubling times were 25.4, 39.4, and 24.6 min for PAO1, PAO1∆galU and PAO1∆galU C, respectively (Figure 2(C)).

Effects of galU deletion on bacterial motility

The swimming and swarming abilities, which depend on flagellum regulated by IV-type secretion system, are important features of motility in P. aeruginosa. The results of the swimming (Figure 3(A)) and swarming (Figure 3(B)) ability measurements showed that the motility of PAO1 was affected to different degrees after galU deletion (with the effect on swarming being more dramatic than that on swimming), and galU complementation recovered the motility to levels similar as the wild-type PAO1.

galU deletion reduces numerous virulence factors

Several virulence-related factors were detected, including the production of pyocyanin, pyoverdine and exopolysaccharide (EPS), proteolytic activity, and staphylolytic activity (LasA activity). The pyocyanin production of PAO1 decreased after galU deletion (being 1.34 μg/mL and 1.13 μg/mL for PAO1 and PAO1ΔgalU), and the color of PAO1∆galU liquid culture was apparently lighter than that of PAO1. Complementation with wild-type galU recovered pyocyanin production, with the level even higher than that of PAO1 (1.58 μg/mL for PAO1ΔgalU C vs 1.34 μg/mL for PAO1, Figure 4(A)). Pyoverdine production in PAO1∆galU was significantly decreased (fluorescence intensity of 36726 for PAO1∆galU vs 62304 for PAO1), and complementation with wild-type galU recovered pyoverdine production to a level similar as PAO1 (Figure 4(B)). The total proteolytic activity of PAO1∆galU markedly decreased compared to that of PAO1 (with an OD600 change of 0.026 for PAO1∆galU in comparison to 0.034 for PAO1), and complementation with wild-type galU recovered the proteolytic activity (Figure 4(C)). The staphylolytic activity related to metalloprotease LasA significantly decreased after galU deletion (OD600 change of 0.083 for PAO1∆galU vs 0.092 for PAO1), and complementation with wild-type galU recovered the staphylolytic activity to a level even higher than that of PAO1 (OD600 change of 0.102 for PAO1∆galU C vs 0.092 for PAO1) (Figure 4(D)). Figure 4(E) shows that the shade of Congo Red for PAO1∆galU is lighter than that of the wild-type strain, indicating lowered production of EPS after galU deletion, and complementation with wild-type galU recovered EPS production, with similar shades of Congo Red for PAO1 and PAO1∆galU C.

galU deletion mutant reduces pathogenicity of PAO1 in cell and animal models

A549, a human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cell line, was used to study the adherence ability of P. aeruginosa. The results (Figure 5(A)) showed that the number of bacteria adhering to A549 cells significantly decreased after galU gene partial deletion, indicating impaired adherence. Complementation with wild-type galU recovered the adhering activity, with adhered bacterial count for PAO1ΔgalU C similar to that of PAO1.

The effects of galU deletion on bacterial virulence and pathogenicity in cell and mouse models.

(A) The capacity of the P. aeruginosa strains adhering to A549 cells; (B) The anti-phagocytic activity of the strains by RAW264.7; (C) The pathogenicity of the strains in mice (n = 10). The statistical analyses used were ordinary one-way ANOVA. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01.

The effects of galU deletion on the ability of bacteria to resist phagocytosis by phagocytes were studied by examining the number of intracellular bacteria in RAW264.7 cells, and the results (Figure 5(B)) showed that the number of PAO1∆galU phagocytosed by RAW264.7 increased comparing with that of PAO1 (1067 CFU/mL vs 333 CFU/mL), indicating lowered anti-phagocytic activity of the strain after galU deletion. Complementation with wild-type galU showed partial recovery, with decreased phagocytosed bacteria counts, even though still higher than that of PAO1.

The in vivo virulence and pathogenicity of the strains were further investigated using an intraperitoneal mouse infection model. The survival rate of mice infected with PAO1 was only 10%, whereas galU deletion caused a significant decrease in virulence, with a survival rate of 80% for PAO1∆galU. After complementation, the survival rate of the mice (20%) returned to a level similar as PAO1 (Figure 5(C)).

Effects of galU deletion on cell structures and affinity of LPS to colistin

The effects of galU deletion on cell structures were observed by SEM, and the results (Figure 6(A)) demonstrated numerous filamentous structures among bacterial cells in wild-type PAO1, probably corresponding to extracellular polysaccharides. In contrast, although outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) were still visible on the surface of PAO1∆galU, the intercellular filamentous structures were significantly reduced. The bacterial cells were both smooth and rod shaped with a complete structure for the two groups. However, a larger number of cells showed a thick and short shape after galU deletion.

The SEM images and ITC results of PAO1 and PAO1∆galU.

(A) For each sample, two images of the same field of view at different magnification levels (20.0 K and 40.0 K) are presented. (B) The ITC titer curves and the calculated Kd values between different LPS and colistin. The Kd values are shown as mean ± SD.

ITC analysis was used to study the effects of galU deletion on the affinity of LPS to colistin, and the results showed that LPS without O-antigen expressed by PAO1∆galU had about 13-fold higher affinity to colistin than that from PAO1, with KDs of 0.0171 mM and 0.228 mM, respectively (Figure 6(B)).

Transcriptome analysis

To investigate the effects of galU deletion on bacterial gene expressions and pathways, transcriptome analysis was performed. Transcriptome profiling revealed that 5,666 genes were co-expressed in PAO1 and PAO1∆galU (Figure 7(A,B)). The galU deletion significantly altered the expression of 30 genes in addition to galU in PAO1. Among them, 19 genes were up-regulated and 11 genes were downregulated (Figure 7(C)). Functional annotation showed that the up-regulated genes were primarily involved in amino acid degradation and transporter complex, whereas the downregulated genes were enriched in two-component system and nucleotidyltransferase activity. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of all differentially expressed genes in the galU deletion mutant vs PAO1 showed their involvement in the two-component systems, amino acid degradation, and metabolism (Figure 7(D)), suggesting the presence of adaptive mechanisms to compensate for LPS biosynthesis defects. As Table 3 shows, there are seven differentially expressed genes included in the two-component systems, with tadZ, flp, rcpA, PA4648, and PA4649 downregulated (0.45–0.56 folds), arnB and PA5169 significantly up-regulated (1.42 ~ 1.80 folds). The PA0265 and PA0266 genes involved in amino acid (like tyrosine) metabolism were up-regulated significantly in PAO1∆galU strain (2.28 ~ 2.35 folds). Notably, many of the differentially expressed genes are associated with the quorum sensing (QS) system [24] (Table 3 and Table S2), including the significantly changed genes PA0603, PA0604, PA1874, PA4306, PA4648, and PA4649 (0.45–3.76 folds), and those not very significantly changed. In addition, some biofilm‑relating operons like the pvc and cup operons were generally downregulated after galU deletion (Table S3), even though the changes were usually not significant for each gene. qPCR was then used to check the expressions of some representative genes involved, and the results showed many representative genes of pvc and cup operons were downregulated after galU deletion and recovered after galU complementation (Figure 7(E)). The downregulations of PA1874 and its regulatory gene brlR (Table S3), which were reported to be associated with tobramycin susceptibility, were also confirmed by qPCR; the results showed significantly decreased expressions in PAO1∆galU strain, and restorations to wild-type levels after complementation (Figure 7(F)). In addition, we also found that many differentially expressed genes such as PA0265, PA0266, PA0603, PA0604 and PA3181 are associated with tyrosine metabolism (Table 3, Table S2), suggesting the participation of tyrosine metabolism relating pathways after galU deletion. Although these genes exhibited moderate expression alterations, subtle transcriptional changes can exert cumulative effects on metabolic networks and signaling cascades. Excluding such genes would overlook functionally important regulators and fail to fully reflect the global pathway disturbance caused by galU deletion.

Tyrosine metabolism relating assays

As the transcriptome sequencing results suggested the participation of tyrosine metabolism relating pathways in the reduced biofilm formation ability of the galU mutant, the levels of three key metabolites (glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), pyruvate, and succinate) involved were determined. The results demonstrated significantly increased levels of the three metabolites in PAO1∆galU compared to PAO1 (1.2–1.8 folds, Figure 8(A-C)), and the increased concentrations significantly dropped after galU complementation. The effects of exogenous supplementation of tyrosine and GABA on biofilm formation were then carried out, and the results showed that biofilm formation was increased in PAO1∆galU after adding GABA and tyrosine (1.33–1.64 folds, Figure 8(D)), compared with the control group without any supplementation.

Discussion

The galU gene is vital for synthesis of O-antigen in LPS [5,11] and is important for virulence, infection, and LPS production in many bacteria [10,11,13]. To systematically study the mechanism and effects of galU deletion on P. aeruginosa, a galU partial deletion mutant in PAO1 was constructed using CRISPR/Cas9 and complemented by a pUCP18 plasmid carrying wild-type galU. The phenotypes of the bacteria, virulence factor productions, and pathogenicity were evaluated by different methods; the possible mechanisms were explored using RNA sequencing and real‑time qPCR, and the involvement of tyrosine metabolism was further assessed.

galU deletion increased the susceptibility of the strain to polymyxins and quinolones (Table 2). The increased susceptibility of P. aeruginosa to polymyxins after galU deletion may be attributed to the lack of O-antigen in the LPS of the strain, which enhanced the affinity of polymyxins for LPS, as demonstrated by ITC, possibly due to smaller steric hindrance. It is well known that the O-antigen is the largest part of LPS located on the surface of the outer membrane. Therefore, polymyxins must first penetrate the O-antigen arranged on the outer surface before binding to lipid A. Hence, the lipid A binding sites were more easily exposed after the loss of the O-antigen for polymyxin binding. PA1874 is a gene of the PA1874-77 adenosine triphosphate-binding cassette (ABC) transport system in P. aeruginosa that removes antibiotics from cells within the biofilm [25]. The operon PA1874-77, whose expression depended on brlR, has been considered a kind of multidrug efflux transporter involved in biofilms and contributing to the resistance of P. aeruginosa in biofilm [26,27]. Zhang et al. [28] demonstrated that the MIC values for tobramycin, gentamicin and ciprofloxacin in the biofilm of the PA1874-77 deletion mutant were lower than those in the biofilm of the wild-type strain. A previous study also demonstrated that PA1874-77 could affect norfloxacin accumulation in PAO1 biofilms [26]. PA1874-77 has been shown to be overexpressed during the biofilm formation process, but deletion of the operon did not affect the ability to form biofilms [27]. In addition, inactivation of the PA1874-77 operon has a significant influence on P. aeruginosa biofilm architecture [26], which possibly contributes to biofilm reduction. Our RT-qPCR results showed that PA1874 and brlR were all significantly downregulated, which may contribute to the increased susceptibility to quinolones in PAO1∆galU. PAO1∆galU was more resistant to the two tested β-lactam antibiotics (meropenem and ceftazidime) than PAO1. PA1874 was reported to be enriched in ceftazidime–avibactam (CZA)-resistant isolates, which is believed to be a possible novel mechanism in CZA resistance [29], suggesting a possible relationship between PA1874 and ceftazidime resistance. And galU mutations were described to link to cephalosporin resistance, which revealed galU gene is relative to cephalosporin resistance in P. aeruginosa [30]. The MIC value of gentamicin showed a twofold increase in PAO1∆galU, consistent with the results of a recent study showing that PAO1 with Tn5-Hg insertion in galU caused a twofold increase in the MICs of aminoglycosides [31,32], indicating the possible involvement of galU in aminoglycoside resistance in P. aeruginosa. In the study, galU deletion increased MICs for ceftazidime (fourfold), meropenem (twofold), and gentamicin (twofold), indicating that galU deletion compromised the efficacy of standard antibiotics, which may affect the safe therapeutic concentrations of these antibiotics in vivo. To explore the effect of impaired galU function on antibiotic MICs in clinical strains, the MICs of ceftazidime, meropenem, and gentamicin against eight clinical P. aeruginosa isolates were determined in combination with uridine diphosphate (UDP), a putative galU inhibitor [33]. The results showed that combination of UDP at both high and low concentrations exerted no apparent effects on the susceptibility of the strains to ceftazidime, meropenem, and gentamicin (Table S4). Possible explanation is that galU knockout represents an extreme genetic perturbation, and pharmacological inhibition of GalU activity may yield phenotypic outcomes distinct from gene deletion. Nevertheless, clinical application of galU inhibitors may carry a potential risk of promoting tolerance to commonly used antibiotics, which warrants careful consideration in subsequent translational investigations.

P. aeruginosa has a complex virulence system and produces multiple virulence factors, which help it adapt to the host environment and contribute to severe infection [2,34]. galU, which is activated by las QS, affects the virulence and pathogenicity of P. aeruginosa through multiple mechanisms, including biofilm formation reduction, virulence factor expression downregulation, motility reduction, QS system damage, decreased capacity for adhesion, and anti-phagocytosis [8]. Our results demonstrated lowered virulence and pathogenicity of galU mutant (Figure 5), together with decreased biofilm formation (Figure 2(D,E)), reduced virulence factor productions (Figure 4), and diminished swimming and swarming ability (Figure 3(A,B)). Pyoverdine for iron chelation is an important siderophore that takes up iron from the environment in P. aeruginosa and plays a dual role in bacterial virulence and infection in the host [35]. After galU deletion, the capacity of bacteria to uptake iron was critically affected, as indicated by the reduced expression of pyoverdine (Figure 4(B)), which possibly contributed to virulence reduction in PAO1ΔgalU. P. aeruginosa produces and secretes several proteases, which are important virulence factors and have close connections with infection and defense mechanisms in the host [21]. The metalloprotease LasA secreted by P. aeruginosa has staphylolytic activity and can enhance elastase activity [21]. LasA is encoded by the PA1871 gene locating close to PA1874, which is slightly downregulated after galU deletion (Table S2). Moreover, the reduction in LasA affects the activation of the elastase precursor and induces an elastase decrease [36]. EPS is a virulence factor that benefits biofilm formation [25] and is also largely decreased after galU deletion (Figure 4(E)). LPS is an important component of the outer membrane that maintains the bacterial morphology. A previous study showed destroyed bacterial morphology after interruption of O-antigen biosynthesis in Escherichia coli, illustrating that O-antigen plays a role in bacterial shape [37]. Hence, we observed the morphology of P. aeruginosa after galU deletion by SEM, and the results were consistent with those of the biofilm and EPS (Figure 6(A)). The capacity for adhesion and colonization in vivo can reflect the virulence and pathogenicity of bacteria. In addition, phagocytosis by macrophages in the host is vital for engulfing and killing bacteria [38,39]. The results showed that the PAO1∆galU strain had a reduced adhesion ability to A549 cells in vitro, and a large number of PAO1∆galU cells were phagocytosed by RAW264.7 in comparison to PAO1. The significantly reduced adhesion and anti-phagocytosis properties of PAO1 after galU deletion could make bacteria be eliminated easily in the host. Bacterial virulence and motility are regulated by QS systems. Our RNA-seq results showed that virulence formation genes were not significantly changed (data not shown); hence, deletion of galU might indirectly affect the production of bacterial virulence factors and motility by downregulating the expression of QS system-related genes (Table 3 and Table S2). Twitching, a form of translocation on a solid surface, is also a type of motility that depends on type IV pili in P. aeruginosa [23]. Unfortunately, we did not obtain consistent results for twitching in the current study. flp (PA4306) gene encoding type IV pili Flp was significantly downregulated in PAO1∆galU (Table 3), consistent with the reduced motility of the strain. Notably, the galU deletion mutant exhibited a pronounced growth defect. Such impaired growth could impose strong selective pressure on bacterial cells, which may drive the emergence of adaptive mutations during in vivo colonization. These compensatory mutations might partially rescue the growth deficiency and possibly alter the virulence phenotype of the galU mutant. Therefore, targeting galU in clinical settings may affect bacterial growth, which would facilitate the occurrence of adaptive mutations and consequently weaken the anti-virulence efficacy of galU-targeted strategies. Nevertheless, the actual clinical outcome remains to be further investigated.

galU deletion demonstrated fitness costs in PAO1 including slowed growth and decreased virulence, which may activate stress responses and alternative energy pathways to offset LPS defects. Actually, some transportation genes relating with nitrogen metabolism and amino acid metabolism pathway were up-regulated in PAO1∆galU in our study, including the upregulation of PA0265, PA0266, PA0603, PA0604, and PA3181 genes, which were reported to be related with tyrosine metabolic pathway or participate in the synthesis of relevant reactants [40,41]. Specifically, tyrosine serves as the precursor for paerucumarin synthesis via the pvc operon pvcABCD, which regulates chaperone/usher pathway (cup) genes modulating biofilm formation in P. aeruginosa [42,43]. PA0266, PA0603, and PA0604 are involved in the conversion of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) to succinate semialdehyde in P. aeruginosa [41,44]. The PA3181 (2-keto-3-deoxy-6-phosphogluconate aldolase, Eda) gene in Pseudomonas takes part in the synthesis of pyruvate, one of the precursors in citrate cycle (TCA cycle) for succinate production in the Entner–Doudoroff (ED) pathway [45]. And succinate also represents a critical intermediate in the tyrosine metabolic pathway [44]. Therefore, galU knockout was likely to up-regulate the tyrosine metabolic pathway, reduce the intracellular tyrosine content of bacteria, further downregulate the expression of pvc and cup operons, and thereby impair the assembly of type IV pili in P. aeruginosa. The deficiency of pili led to attenuated bacterial adhesion and reduced biofilm formation, consequently resulting in decreased virulence [46]. The qPCR and biofilm formation results confirmed our inference (Figure 7(E) and 8(D)). Furthermore, activation of the ED pathway was reported to enhance glucose consumption in E. coli [47,48], and changes in tyrosine, succinate and GABA content showed certain effects on bacterial biofilm production and virulence [49–51]. Consistently, our study also showed that the levels of G6P, tyrosine and succinate were increased in PAO1∆galU strain, and exogenous addition of these substances can stimulate biofilm formation of PAO1∆galU.

Based on the above results, a mechanism was proposed: The deletion of galU directly leads to the intracellular accumulation of G6P, and then high levels of G6P can be converted into free glucose via the reverse reaction of carbohydrate metabolism, ultimately resulting in an increase of glucose content. High levels of G6P and glucose activate the ED pathway, up-regulate PA3181, and increase pyruvate production. As a key TCA cycle intermediate, pyruvate is converted to succinate via several reactions. Elevated succinate up-regulates PA0265, accelerating its downstream conversion in the TCA cycle. Concurrently, rapid succinate turnover raises intracellular demand for this metabolite, up-regulates the GABA transport genes PA0266, PA0603, and PA0604 to catalyze GABA conversion to succinate semialdehyde. Succinate also participates in key tyrosine metabolic reactions, and its accumulation activates tyrosine metabolism, lowering intracellular tyrosine levels. A decrease in tyrosine downregulates the expression of the pvc and cup operon, ultimately reducing biofilm formation and virulence in PAO1∆galU (Figure 9).

Pathway affected by galU knockout. Solid lines indicate direct effects, while dashed lines represent indirect effects via multiple-step reactions. Red icons denote up-regulated genes, and blue icons denote downregulated genes.

In summary, a galU deletion mutant was successfully constructed in PAO1, and its effects were systemically evaluated; the possible mechanisms were analyzed. These findings establish an academic framework for galU-mediating regulation of phenotypic features, QS systems, and antibiotic susceptibility in P. aeruginosa. Future studies may leverage this foundation to explore galU-regulated virulence during infections or develop targeted anti-virulence strategies against multidrug-resistant strains.
