Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders predictors among pregnant and delivery mothers receiving care in public health institutions in Sidama, Ethiopia: a multicenter case control study
Authors: Girma Tenkolu Bune
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD) include conditions like Pre-Eclampsia, Eclampsia, and Gestational Hypertension, impacting 5–10% of pregnancies globally. These disorders are responsible for 16% of maternal deaths in Sub-Saharan Africa and account for 16.9% of maternal mortality in Ethiopia, representing 10% of the country's total maternal deaths.ObjectiveTo identify predictors of PIHD among pregnant and delivery women in public hospitals and primary health care units (health centers) in Sidama, Ethiopia.MethodsA multi-center unmatched case–control study was conducted on women in antenatal care and obstetrics/gynecology departments at 12 randomly selected public health institutions, including one specialized teaching hospital, two general hospitals, three primary hospitals, and six health centers. The study involved 920 women (230 cases and 690 controls) selected from December 2023 to February 2024. Cases included pregnant or delivery women at 20 + weeks gestation with PIHD such as pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, gestational hypertension, and chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia. Controls were normotensive pregnant women or delivering mothers without PIHD after 20 weeks gestation. Data was manually verified, entered, and validated using Epidata software, followed by analysis with SPSS 22. Univariate analysis assessed predictors of PIHD, while binary logistic regression evaluated the relationships between factors. Model fitness was checked using collinearity assessments and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with variables having a P value < 0.05 deemed independent predictors.ResultsOut of 920 planned participants, 686 were included, resulting in a response rate of 94.35%. Factors associated with PIHD in women included age at first conception (AOR = 1.26), mid (AOR = 6.05) and high (AOR = 5.01) wealth index levels, multigravidity (AOR = 4.34), pregnancy age ≥ 42 weeks (AOR = 3.65), maternal mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) (AOR = 1.29), hemoglobin levels of 6.5–10.9 g/dL (AOR = 5.59), pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/M2 (AOR = 0.81), preexisting hypertension (AOR = 8.97), family history of diabetes mellitus (AOR = 20.02), former alcohol consumption (AOR = 0.27), and total physical activity during pregnancy (AOR = 0.54).ConclusionsThe study identifies key predictors of PIHD in women, including age at conception, wealth index, multigravidity, gestational age, and pre-pregnancy BMI. It underscores the link between socioeconomic status and maternal health, highlighting that financial resources alone don’t ensure better outcomes without educational access. The research advocates for a health intervention strategy focusing on socioeconomic factors and public health initiatives to reduce maternal health disparities while exploring the role of partner stability and psychosocial aspects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-024-06886-1.Keywords: Antenatal care, ANC, Delivery service, Obstetrics department, Complications, Hypertension, Maternal, Pregnancy, PIHDD, Public, Women, Sidama, Ethiopia

Background

Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD) include various conditions affecting pregnant and postpartum women, such as Pre-Eclampsia, Eclampsia, Gestational Hypertension, Proteinuria, and Chronic Hypertension with Superimposed Pre-Eclampsia [1–6]. Pre-Eclampsia, which typically arises after 20 weeks of gestation, is marked by elevated blood pressure and often proteinuria. Eclampsia represents a severe form of Pre-Eclampsia, characterized by seizures and heightened health risks. Chronic Hypertension with Superimposed Pre-Eclampsia refers to new hypertensive symptoms in women with pre-existing hypertension during pregnancy. Gestational Hypertension develops after 20 weeks without proteinuria and may resolve post-delivery, but can progress to Pre-Eclampsia [3–5].

PIHD account for 5–14% of maternal deaths worldwide, making them the second leading cause of maternal mortality, especially in low-income areas where complications are significantly more prevalent [1, 7]. In these regions, the incidence of PIHD varies between 1.8% and 16.7%. These disorders have a profound effect on maternal mortality, contributing to 16% of deaths in Sub-Saharan Africa [1, 8]. In Ethiopia, the mortality rate associated with hypertensive disorders has been reported at 16.9%, showing an increase from 4 to 29% over recent decades [9]. PIHD heightens the risk of prenatal mortality and severe complications [1, 7–10], with pre-eclampsia alone accounting for 11% of maternal deaths [1, 9, 10]. In Ethiopia, hypertensive disorders are linked to 62.7% of perinatal complications, leading to serious issues such as low birth weight and stillbirths, along with alarmingly high neonatal mortality rates [1, 9].

The exact cause of PIHD remains unclear; however, it is influenced by various biological, socioeconomic, and psychosocial factors. Specific risk factors, such as advanced maternal age, obesity, a family history of hypertension, and inadequate antenatal care, increase the likelihood of developing these disorders [1, 8, 9]. First-time mothers are at twice the risk of experiencing complications compared to those with prior childbirth experiences [9]. Nevertheless, diagnosing conditions like pre-eclampsia can be challenging due to insufficient laboratory testing, as reliance on clinical judgment alone may fall short, particularly in multiple pregnancy cases. Additionally, partner dynamics significantly impact maternal health, as relationship changes can affect a woman’s psychological well-being and health behaviors during pregnancy [1, 2, 6–10].

In Ethiopia, initiatives to improve maternal health have focused on strengthening healthcare infrastructure and deploying skilled professionals [1]. However, the prevalence of PIHDposes a significant challenge, highlighting the need for further research into its risk factors [2, 7]. The rationale for this study arises from the limited research on predictors of PIHD occurences in Ethiopia including the study region of Sidama, which has largely been restricted to single hospital settings, cross-sectional approaches, and small sample sizes [1, 11, 12], primarily relying on secondary data, resulting in a narrow understanding of the issue [13–15].

To address this, a multicenter case–control study was designed to assess the predictors of PIHD among pregnant women receiving care in public health institutions in Sidama, Ethiopia. The study investigates the influence of various Socio-demographic factors, Current and Past Obstetrics Factors, Past medical and family history related factors, and Llifestyle related factors, on the development of PIHD. The Sidama region was chosen due to its significant challenges, which include high institutional birth rates and considerable barriers to antenatal care, such as a 17% dropout rate and only 14% of women receiving comprehensive care services. Furthermore, the region experiences alarming maternal mortality rates, with a majority of deaths occurring within healthcare facilities [16, 17]. The findings of this study aim to fill existing gaps by providing essential primary data on the predictors of PIHD among pregnant women in Sidama, with a focus on public hospitals and primary healthcare units. Additionally, the results are expected to offer valuable insights for academia and policymakers, facilitating the development of targeted, evidence-based interventions to improve maternal health outcomes in the region and beyond.

Methods and materials

Study contexts, study design and period

2.1 This study was carried out across 12 public health institutions in the Sidama regional state of Ethiopia, specifically involving six hospitals and six health centers. The selected health facilities cater to a demographic with a high prevalence of multigravidity, influenced by various socioeconomic and cultural factors, including early marriage, restricted access to family planning resources, and cultural norms that promote larger families. Consequently, the cohort of pregnant women seeking antenatal care was predominantly composed of multiparous mothers. Sidama, one of Ethiopia’s 11 regional states, was formed in June 2020 after a 98.52% approval for increased autonomy in the 2019 Sidama referendum. It is the second-smallest regional state in the country, bordered by the Oromia Region and the Bilate River, and has a population of approximately 4.3 million people, distributed among 30 rural districts, 6 town administrations, and 536 rural kebeles. The capital, Hawassa, is situated 275 km south of Addis Ababa. According to a report by the Ministry of Health of Ethiopia for the 2020/2021 period, 140,834 pregnant women received antenatal care (ANC) four or more times, resulting in a coverage rate of 91% [16, 17].

A multi-center, institution-based unmatched case-control study was conducted from December 2023 to February 2024. In this context, "unmatched" signifies that the cases (individuals diagnosed with conditions such as Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders) are not paired with specific controls (individuals without the condition) based on predetermined characteristics like age or socioeconomic status. Instead, unmatched studies utilize a broader selection of controls from the same population, which allows for a more extensive representation of cases without being restricted to particular demographic profiles. This methodology increases the pool of available controls and is often more feasible in multi-center research settings. Although it may introduce potential confounding variables, the insights gained from this approach can still be effectively addressed during the analysis process.

Source and study population

The source population for this study includes pregnant women attending antenatal care (ANC) follow-up units with a gestational age of 20 weeks and above, as well as those seeking delivery services in public health facilities in both urban and rural areas of the Sidama regional state. The study population was comprised of women selected using consecutive sampling methods from public health facilities. The participants included those with a gestational age of 20 weeks or more who were receiving antenatal care or seeking delivery services. Women were recruited from prenatal clinics located in both urban and suburban areas, ensuring a diverse representation within the sample.

Case and control identification and eligibility criteria

Case identification

This study defines cases as pregnant women with a gestational age of 20 weeks or more who attend antenatal care (ANC) units or seek delivery services and have been diagnosed with Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD), including pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, gestational hypertension, and chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia.

Control identification

Controls are normotensive pregnant women attending ANC clinics after 20 weeks of gestation or mothers delivering without a PIHD diagnosis, serving as a comparison group for the cases.

Inclusion criteria for cases

Cases must be pregnant women over 20 weeks gestational age seeking antenatal care or delivery services at designated public health facilities and diagnosed with PIHD based on specific blood pressure measurements and urine protein levels.

Inclusion criteria for controls

Controls must be women receiving similar care at selected healthcare facilities without a PIHD diagnosis.

Exclusion criteria

Both cases and controls are excluded if they have: HELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelet count) or severe eclampsia; critical illnesses, including severe infections like sepsis or advanced systemic diseases such as tuberculosis; confirmed chronic hypertension prior to 20 weeks of gestation; abnormal blood pressure readings that normalize upon re-evaluation; epilepsy; or if they did not provide informed consent. Participants with psychiatric disorders affecting comprehension or consent are also excluded. This selection process ensures a cohort likely to yield valid and reliable data [3, 4].

Case Identification: Cases were identified as pregnant women over 20 weeks of gestation seeking antenatal care or delivery services at designated public health facilities. Their diagnosis of PIHD was based on specific blood pressure measurements and urine protein levels. Control Identification: Controls consisted of women receiving similar care at the selected healthcare facilities who have not been diagnosed with PIHD [3, 4].

Women diagnosed with HELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelet count) and severe cases of eclampsia were excluded from the study due to significant overlap with PIHD, which could confound results. Additionally, women in critically ill conditions (including severe infections like sepsis and advanced systemic illnesses such as tuberculosis), those with confirmed chronic hypertension prior to 20 weeks of gestation, individuals with abnormal blood pressure readings that normalized upon re-assessment, women with epilepsy, and those who did not provide informed consent were also excluded. Furthermore, participants with psychiatric disorders that could affect their ability to understand or give consent to the study were excluded, ensuring that the cohort comprised individuals who were most likely to provide valid and reliable data [3, 4].

Sample size determination, sampling procedure, and technique

Sample size was calculated using OpenEpi Version 3.01 with the assumption of a 1:3 ratio of Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders cases to controls, power of 80, confidence level of 95%, and odds ratio of 1.77 due to the significant association of alcohol consumption with PIHD. The hypothetical proportion of controls with exposure was derived as 4.5% from a systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted in Ethiopia, where it was 40%. This calculation yielded a sample size of 836 (209 cases and 627 controls) [13]. Adjusting for a 10% nonresponse rate, the total sample size became 920 study participants (230 cases and 690 controls) (Table S1 attached as additional data).

The sampling procedure and technique involved categorizing public health facilities in the Sidama region into Specialized Teaching Hospitals, General Hospitals, and Health Centers based on their level of service. A total of 12 public health institutions were randomly selected for the study, including one Specialized Teaching Hospital, two General Hospitals, three Primary Hospitals, and six Health Centers. The hospitals (H) included in the study were Hawassa University Specialized Teaching H, Yirgalem General Hospital (GH), Bona GH, Aleta Chuko PH, Boricha PH, and Loca Abaya PH. The Health Centers (HC) included were Millinium HC, Hulla HC, Hawella HC, Titicha HC, Wensho HC, and Daye HC. These facilities were chosen based on the availability and quality of ANC, delivery, and gynecology services. The selected institutions represented over 50% of the population served by all health facilities in the region. The sample size for the study participants (920 in total, consisting of 230 cases and 690 controls) was distributed among the 12 health care institutions based on the proportion of their ANC and delivery service attendants. Lastly, cases were consecutively sampled from ANC and delivery services at the selected institutions until the specified sample size for each facility was achieved. For each case, two controls—women without Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD) during ANC and delivery services—were selected from the source population at the designated health care facilities (Table S1 attached as additional data, Fig. 1 attached at the end of the manuscript).

Study variables

Dependent variable

Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD) in this study were defined as cases involving pregnant women at 20 weeks of gestation or later who were diagnosed with conditions including preeclampsia and gestational hypertension. Cases were identified during visits to antenatal care (ANC) clinics for initial or follow-up appointments after the 20-week mark, or among mothers who delivered at healthcare facilities and were normotensive prior to being diagnosed with PIHD by gynecologists, general practitioners, midwives, or clinical nurses. We adhered to established clinical diagnostic criteria focusing on the detection of hypertension and associated symptoms [3, 4]. We adhered to established clinical diagnostic criteria, which emphasize the identification of hypertension and associated symptoms. However, we recognize that this approach has inherent limitations, particularly in multiparous women with a history of elevated blood pressure. To mitigate potential misdiagnosis, we recommend incorporating laboratory testing, such as proteinuria assessments, into our diagnostic protocols in future research and clinical practice. Pre-eclampsia: The new onset of hypertension with systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, within two readings taken 4–6 h apart, accompanied by proteinuria and possibly edema, developing after 20 weeks of gestation [3, 4]. Eclampsia: Mother with DBP ≥ 110 mmHg after 20 weeks of gestation and, in addition to the features of preeclampsia, having one or more episodes of convulsion or coma plus proteinuria + 2 or more [3, 4]. Chronic Hypertension with Superimposed Preeclampsia: Mothers with hypertension before pregnancy or before 20 weeks of gestation who develop signs of preeclampsia after 20 weeks of gestation [3, 4]. Proteinuria: Proteinuria is defined as urinary protein excretion of ≥ 300 mg/24 h urine samples or ≥ 1 + on qualitative dipstick reading and protein/creatinine ratio of ≥ 0.3 in the absence of urinary tract infections [5]. Gestational Hypertension: Characterized by elevated blood pressure after 20 weeks of gestation, often near term, without accompanying proteinuria [3–5].

No PIHD or Controls: Denotes randomly chosen mothers receiving antenatal and delivery care services without Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (normotensive mothers) at the same location and time [3, 4].

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors

Data regarding socioeconomic and demographic factors, such as age of women at first conception (measured in years), place of residence, marital status, educational attainment, family size, and women's occupational status, along with health outcomes were gathered through surveys and medical records. In the analysis, while age and family size-related factors were treated as continuous variables, all socioeconomic and demographic factors—except for the wealth index—were categorized as binary (Yes = 1 or No = 0). The wealth index, used in the EDHS 2016 as a composite measure of socioeconomic status, was created through principal component analysis (PCA). Initially, 28 household asset variables were prepared, and key factors were identified using frequency analysis. Binary variables were coded as 0 and 1, while categorical options were transformed into dummy variables, with missing values also marked as 0. Eight variables were removed due to low communality scores, leaving 20 for further PCA. These variables included indicators such as electricity access, appliances, cooking fuels, cooking locations, kitchen setups, roofing materials, and land or livestock ownership. The PCA revealed eight components that explained 56.6% of the total variance, which surpassed the minimum requirement. The final wealth index was calculated by summing the scores of these components and categorizing them into three segments: 0 for the lowest quintile, 1 for the middle quintile, and 2 for the highest quintile.

Current and past obstetrics factors

The obstetric history collected includes pre-pregnancy contraceptive use, previous instances of Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD), abortions, stillbirths, and low birth weights. Current obstetric details encompass pregnancy status, gravidity, parity, multiple pregnancies, anemia at the initial visit, pregnancy interval, gestational age, as well as both current and pre-pregnancy factors, all categorized as Yes = 1 or No = 0. Weight and height were treated as continuous variables, while mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI), and hemoglobin levels were analyzed as both categorical and continuous variables. Pre-pregnancy BMI was classified according to established thresholds: underweight (BMI < 18.5), normal (BMI 18.5–24.9), and overweight/obese (BMI ≥ 25.0) [3–5].

Past medical and family history related factors

Preexisting Hypertension, Pre-gestational Diabetes Mellitus, History of Kidney Diseases, History of Asthma, Family History of Diabetes Mellitus, Family History of Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders, and Family History of Hypertension. All of these factors were assessed in this study by questioning the women about the conditions or, if necessary, by reviewing the client's documents and then categorizing them as yes = 1 and No = 0.

Lifestyle related factors

Data on lifestyle behaviors, including mothers' coffee consumption status, frequency of coffee intake, fruit and vegetable intake during pregnancy, alcohol consumption status, smoking habits, and physical activity levels, were gathered through surveys and medical records. Fruit and vegetable intake in this study was assessed based on how often the women consumed these foods, categorized as daily, every three days, or weekly. A "Yes = 1" response indicated that the women consumed fruits or vegetables 3 to 5 days a week on an occasional basis during pregnancy [5].The study also examined lifestyle-related factors, including alcohol consumption and smoking status. Alcohol consumption was categorized into two groups: lifetime abstainers, coded as 0, and alcohol consumers, coded as 1. Smoking status was classified into three categories: non-smokers, current smokers, and past smokers, with the coding scheme of No = 0 and Yes = 1 to indicate smoking status. Physical activity during pregnancy was assessed by aggregating participants' responses regarding work (W), transportation (T), and recreation (R) activities at varying intensity levels (vigorous, moderate, or low). Participants were then classified as active (coded as 1) if they reported any activity, while those who reported no activity were categorized as inactive (coded as 0).

Data collection tools, personnel, and procedures

Data collection tools, personnel, and procedures were meticulously organized for this study. A validated questionnaire, adapted from previous studies in Ethiopia, served as the primary data collection instrument. In this tools, questions concerning diet, fruit, vegetable intake, and physical activity were sourced from the WHO dietary assessment STEEP tool, which had been validated in Ethiopian contexts [18]. Sixty data collectors and 12 supervisors, proficient in Sidamic and Amharic, were carefully selected from maternity departments in healthcare institutions.They were divided into 12 groups, each consisting of a diverse team of health professionals, including Gynecologists/Obstetricians or General Practitioners, midwives,nurses, and BSc nurses. Supervision was provided by department heads at selectedhospitals and health centers. Prior to data collection, both data collectors andsupervisors underwent a comprehensive two-day on-site training session to ensure standardized procedures.The data collection methods encompassed face-to-face interviews, review of medical records, and the measurement of blood pressure and protein levels at the time of diagnosis. Height, weight, and blood pressure were accurately measured using calibrated instruments. Interviews were conducted at suitable locations and times, especially for women receiving delivery services, to ensure convenience and cooperation. Data collection commenced after the confirmation of diagnoses through laboratory tests and physical examinations, maintaining the accuracy and reliability of the gathered information.

Data quality assurance

Data quality assurance procedures were meticulously followed. The study questionnaire was initially developed in English, then translated into Sidama (Sidaamu Afoo) and Amharic to ensure accuracy. The translated versions were back-translated into English by a language expert. After validation involving 46 women at Tula Primary Hospital, adjustments were made to the questionnaire a week before data collection. Data collectors and supervisors underwent two days of intensive on-site training covering critical aspects such as data collection format, timely retrieval, completeness, confidentiality, and consistency. Supervisors closely monitored the data collection process, promptly addressing issues, while the principal investigator ensured overall data consistency, transparency, and completeness. These rigorous procedures aimed at maintaining high data quality standards throughout the study. The collected and verified data were coded, cross-checked, and entered into EpiData 3.5.2 software. Subsequently, the data was exported to SPSS version 22 for further analysis and cleansing. Several measures were implemented to mitigate bias, including using uniform data gathering techniques and selecting participants through representative sampling to address selection bias. Clear inclusion and exclusion criteria were established, with variables accounted for in the analysis. Techniques like logistic regression were employed for multifactor analysis, focusing on minimizing non-response bias through transparent communication.

Data analysis

The data exported to SPSS 22 underwent frequency analysis for descriptive statistics to ensure cleanliness and readiness for further analysis. Univariate analysis was performed, presenting data as frequencies and means. The influence of each predictor variable on Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD) was assessed through cross-tabulation frequencies to determine percentage effects. Associations between the predictors and the outcome variable were explored via tabulation. Binary logistic regression was conducted, calculating crude associations to select variables for multivariable analysis at a significance level of P < 0.25. Following this, multivariable analysis was carried out to identify predictors of the outcome variables. The adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and its corresponding 95% confidence intervals were computed, with P values < 0.05 considered indicative of independent predictors of PIHD. Collinearity checks were conducted using variance inflation factor statistics, and model fitness was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test statistics, where a P value > 0.05 indicated good model fit. It is important to note that due to the low representation of primigravida women in our cohort, we did not conduct a detailed subgroup analysis of atypical findings related to this population. This absence of analysis limits our ability to fully understand the unique predictors of PIHD among primigravida mothers, which are crucial for developing targeted interventions.

Result

The socio-demographic factors

The study involved a total of 686 participants, which included 217 cases of Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD) and 651 control subjects, achieving a high response rate of 94.35%. The 5.65% non-response rate was attributed to several factors, such as scheduling conflicts, misunderstandings about the study's objectives, apprehensions regarding potential pain or complications from tests, family emergencies, transportation challenges for individuals in remote areas, recruitment during busy periods, and negative past experiences with inadequately conducted studies.

Among the 686 participant women, PIHD cases were primarily diagnosed through clinical assessments based on patients' histopathological hypertension history. While this is a standard approach for diagnosing preeclampsia, the reliability may be compromised, especially in multiparous participants lacking laboratory confirmation. Specifically, the distribution of diagnoses among the PIHD cases included gestational hypertension (25.8%), pre-eclampsia/eclampsia (44.2%), and chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia (30.0%). The average age of cases was 32.96 years (± 4.68), in contrast to the controls, who had an average age of 27.29 years (± 5.89). Notably, older mothers aged 35 and above were significantly more prevalent among cases (45.6%, n = 99) compared to controls (10.9%, n = 71) (P < 0.001). Additionally, rural residents represented a smaller proportion of cases (33.2%, n = 72) than controls (39.0%, n = 254), though this difference was not statistically significant (P < 0.13). Examining educational attainment, among women with secondary education, 30.0% (n = 65) of cases were documented, while 35.8% (n = 233) of controls were found in this category (P = 0.07). Furthermore, only 11.5% (n = 25) of cases completed secondary education compared to 19.7% (n = 128) of controls (P = 0.03). In terms of religious affiliation, 17.5% (n = 38) of cases identified as orthodox Christians, as did 26.7% (n = 174) of controls, with a significant difference noted (P = 0.004).

The result revealed that the maximum family size reported was 8, with a mean family size of 4.29 (± 1.23) for cases and 4.07 (± 0.28) for controls. Almost 67% of respondents had a family size of five or more, observed in 74.7% (n = 162) of cases compared to 65.0% (n = 423) of controls (P = 0.009). Moreover, a substantial proportion of participants in both groups belonged to the highest wealth index, with 47.0% (n = 102) of those with PIHD and 39.9% (n = 260) of those without PIHD classified as belonging to the highest socioeconomic status (P = 0.01). indicating they belong to the highest socioeconomic status. The current and past obstetric factors

The study found that 14.7% of participants with Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD) and 23.5% of controls had planned their pregnancies, with a significant association to the utilization of antenatal care (ANC) services (P = 0.01). In terms of Prevention of Mother-To-Child Transmission of HIV (PMTCT) services, 60% of cases accessed these services compared to 50% of controls, showing a significant difference (P < 0.001). Additionally, among the women surveyed, a majority were multigravida, with 203 (93.5%) of those with PIHD and 522 (80.2%) of those without PIHD, yielding a crude odds ratio (COR) of 3.58 for multigravidity, although this value was not adjusted for maternal age (P = 0.01).

Maternal mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) was classified as below or above 28.01 cm, revealing that 34% of the cases had a MUAC of 28.01 cm or above, compared to just 6% of the controls (P = 0.01). Mean pre-pregnancy weights were 50.91 ± 5.11 kg for women with PIHD and 55.65 ± 8.93 kg for those without, with pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) averages of 19.11 ± 2.27 kg/m2 versus 20.64 ± 3.73 kg/m2, both of which were statistically significant with a p-value of 0.01.Hemoglobin levels during recent pregnancies averaged 11.56 ± 2.81 g/dl for cases and 11.90 ± 1.72 g/dl for controls, with 60.8% of cases and 85.5% of controls reporting hemoglobin levels ≥ 11.0 g/dl (P < 0.001). Approximately 25.8% of mothers with PIHD reported the use of contraceptives prior to pregnancy, compared to 17.8% of those without PIHD (P = 0.01). Notably, 21% of participants had a history of PIHD, with differences observed between cases (21.7%) and controls (13.2%) (P < 0.01). The study also highlighted differences in stillbirth rates (30.4% in cases vs. 36.3% in controls, P = 0.12) and low birth weight rates (27.6% in cases vs. 21.7% in controls, P = 0.07). The study found that 33.5% of participants had chronic conditions, with a higher prevalence in cases (55.3%) than in controls (26.3%) (P < 0.001). A larger percentage of mothers with PIHD (15.1%) had hypertension before pregnancy, and 18.8% had experienced anemia in a previous pregnancy. Additionally, 41.5% of individuals with hypertension were cases, while only 6.3% were controls (P < 0.001). The study also showed no significant differences in the history of rheumatoid arthritis, asthma, pre-gestational diabetes, and kidney disorders between cases and controls (P ≥ 0.25) (Table 3).

Llifestyle related factors

Coffee consumption was observed to be higher among women with Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD), with 89.9% of cases consuming coffee compared to 86.8% of controls, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.24). Furthermore, within the PIHD group, 89.9% reported a frequency of coffee intake, while the figure for controls was slightly lower at 86.2%, also not reaching statistical significance (P = 0.20). However, a statistically significant difference was noted in the consumption of three or more cups per occasion, where only 16.6% of cases reported this level of intake compared to 20.7% of controls (P = 0.04). The study highlighted dietary differences, revealing that the intake of fruits and vegetables was notably lower among women with PIHD (70.5%) compared to controls (93.1%), with statistical significance (P = 0.001).

In terms of smoking prevalence, our data indicated that smoking was not a significant risk factor for PIHD in this population. Among women diagnosed with PIHD, there were 27 individuals who were past smokers (defined as having smoked for at least one month), with a crude odds ratio (COR) of 0.72 (P = 0.16). Additionally, 23 women were classified as current smokers (having smoked within the past 30 days), yielding a COR of 0.96, which was not statistically significant (P = 0.89). Approximately 77% of participants with PIHD were former smokers, revealing variations in smoking history between the cases and controls.

Regarding physical activity during pregnancy, 47.9% of cases engaged in physical activity, in contrast to 61.0% of controls, with this difference being statistically significant (P = 0.001). Detailed information can be found in Table 4 (Table 4 attached separately at the end of this manuscript).

Predictors of PIHD

The study employed binary logistic regression at a 95% confidence level to identify determinants of Pregnancy-Induced Hypertension (PIHD). Specifically, multivariable logistic regression was utilized to pinpoint predictors of PIHD. Out of 33 initial variables, 20 were retained in the final model, which demonstrated adequacy based on various metrics, including -2 Log likelihood, R Square values, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow Test. The model exhibited strong predictive power, with an overall predicted accuracy of 89.9%. To mitigate potential confounding, we controlled for several factors, including socioeconomic status, obstetric history (both past and present), existing health conditions, and lifestyle variables.

The analysis revealed that age at first conception significantly impacted the risk of PIHD, with an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.19, 1.33). Additionally, higher wealth index levels were correlated with increased odds of PIHD, wherein the middle wealth index demonstrated an AOR of 6.05 (95% CI: 2.53, 14.45), and the highest wealth index indicated an AOR of 5.01 (95% CI: 2.12, 12.84). Moreover, multigravida women were found to have a 4.34 times higher risk of PIHD compared to primigravida women (AOR: 4.34, 95% CI: 2.01, 11.18), underscoring the heightened risk among multiparous women when adjusted for maternal age and other covariates. This methodological design enhances the validity of our findings regarding the association between multiparity and PIHD by addressing the influence of confounding variables.

Furthermore, the results indicated that gestational age at or beyond 42 weeks increased the odds of PIHD by 3.65 times (AOR: 3.65, 95% CI: 1.37, 9.68). A 1 cm increase in Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) was associated with a 1.29 times increase in the odds of PIHD (AOR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.20, 1.39). Hemoglobin levels ranging from 6.5 to 10.9 g/dL significantly heightened the risk of PIHD by 5.59 times (AOR: 5.59, 95% CI: 3.18, 9.83). Interestingly, higher pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI) was found to decrease the likelihood of PIHD by 1991.9% (AOR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.74, 0.88). Additionally, preexisting hypertension markedly increased the odds of developing PIHD (AOR: 8.97, 95% CI: 4.85, 16.61), while a family history of diabetes elevated the risk significantly by 20.02 times (AOR: 20.02, 95% CI: 8.45, 45.15). Conversely, former alcohol use (AOR: 0.27, 95% CI: 0.10, 0.73) and physical activity during pregnancy (AOR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.31, 0.96) were identified as protective factors, suggesting that alcohol consumption a year prior and engagement in physical activity during pregnancy reduced the likelihood of PIHD by 73% and 46%, respectively. Discussions

This study identified several key predictors of Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD), including age at first conception, wealth index, multigravidity, gestational age, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), hemoglobin levels, pre-pregnancy BMI, pre-existing hypertension, family history of diabetes mellitus, former alcohol use, and physical activity during pregnancy.

Notably, advanced maternal age at first pregnancy emerged as a significant risk factor for PIHD, which aligns with findings from studies conducted in.Ethiopia [9, 13, 19–21], Kenya [22], and India [7]. The increased risk associated with advanced maternal age might stem from the cardiovascular changes that occur during pregnancy [19, 21]. However, a contrasting study in Bahir Dar, Ethiopia [5], reported consistent PIHD incidence across different age groups, suggesting that regional genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors could influence these outcomes. The finding implicates monitoring older pregnant women becomes essential to mitigate potential complications.

Moreover, this study uncovers an unexpected link between socioeconomic factors and PIHD, demonstrating that women from middle (AOR = 6.05) and higher (AOR = 5.01) wealth index categories face a significantly higher risk for PIHD compared to those from lower-wealth backgrounds [21]. This finding challenges the prevailing assumption that lower socioeconomic status is directly associated with increased pregnancy complications, as prior research has often linked poverty to maternal health risks due to restricted access to healthcare, inadequate nutrition, and heightened stress levels [5, 23–25]. Wealthier women may engage in health-neglecting behaviors due to a misguided confidence in their health status, choosing to prioritize career demands over preventive healthcare measures. The complexities surrounding healthcare access suggest that financial resources alone do not ensure effective healthcare utilization, revealing a critical gap in maternal health strategies [5, 23–25]. The study also highlights a paradoxical relationship between educational attainment and PIHD, indicating that the anticipated benefits of education may not always correlate with improved health outcomes. Initial unadjusted odds suggested an education-related protective effect; however, the adjusted analysis reveals that other factors might overshadow the advantages conferred by education, suggesting a need for deeper investigation into how education influences health behaviors, particularly among affluent women who have access to information but may not apply it effectively. Additionally, lifestyle factors such as physical activity and alcohol consumption significantly influence the likelihood of PIHD, emphasizing the need to consider behavioral aspects. The psychological stresses associated with multiple pregnancies exacerbate risk factors, underscoring the complexities of maternal health.

The study also brings to light a change in our understanding of risk factors for PIHD, particularly in multigravida women, who are found to have a 4.34 times greater likelihood of developing PIHD compared to their primigravida counterparts. Our analysis of PIHD predictors revealed a crude odds ratio (COR) of 3.58 linked to multiparity, indicating the initial relationship without accounting for confounding variables, such as maternal age. However, after adjusting for maternal age and other relevant factors, the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) rose to 4.34, reinforcing that multiparity is a substantial predictor of PIHD even when controlling for potential confounders [20]. This finding challenges the traditional notion that first-time mothers are the most susceptible to hypertensive disorders due to their lack of experience. Instead, it suggests that previous pregnancies may elevate the risk in multiparous women, likely attributable to cumulative physiological stress, lingering cardiovascular impacts from earlier hypertensive pregnancies, and the psychological demands associated with caregiving [5, 11, 22, 26]. The finding underscores the significance of socioeconomic determinants in maternal health, advocating for healthcare interventions that address these factors, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Additionally, we acknowledge certain limitations of our study, including the lack of data regarding the impact of new paternal factors on the risk of PIHD in multiparous women. Moreover, we recognize the critical importance of laboratory tests, including proteinuria assessment, in enhancing the reliability of preeclampsia diagnoses. As such, we recommend an integrated approach that combines clinical evaluation with laboratory confirmation in order to reduce the risks of misdiagnosis and improve maternal outcomes. In generall, the findings advocate for comprehensive screening protocols, targeted educational initiatives, and future exploration of how new paternity and partner health impact maternal outcomes, aiming to improve prenatal care and health results for vulnerable women [5].

The study also identifies a significant association between post-term pregnancies (defined as those reaching or exceeding 42 weeks of gestation) and an elevated risk of PIHD, with an Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) of 3.65. This finding underscores the importance of investigating the underlying physiological mechanisms contributing to this relationship, which is vital for refining prenatal care strategies. Extended gestation is linked to a higher prevalence of hypertensive conditions, such as preeclampsia and gestational hypertension, which may arise due to factors like placental dysfunction, increased vascular resistance, and hormonal changes. Thus, it is essential for healthcare providers to prioritize close monitoring of maternal health and implement effective interventions during this critical period.Regular blood pressure monitoring and fetal assessments should be integrated into the care for pregnant women, particularly those approaching or surpassing their due dates. The emphasis on timely management strategies, such as early labor induction when clinically indicated, is crucial for reducing risks associated with prolonged pregnancies. Healthcare professionals have a pivotal role in mitigating the dangers linked to post-term pregnancies. As highlighted in earlier research, the adoption of standardized protocols for monitoring and managing post-term pregnancies has shown potential in reducing the incidence of hypertensive disorders [7, 11]. Evidence from Ethiopia suggests that thorough monitoring can lead to decreased hypertensive complications, reinforcing the need for structured care approaches. It is imperative for healthcare systems to develop clear guidelines for prenatal care, especially for women at risk of PIHD due to post-term pregnancies or other contributing factors [11]. In resource-limited areas like Sidama, Ethiopia, obstacles to accessing adequate prenatal care can heighten the risks associated with post-term deliveries. Therefore, addressing these disparities is critical for ensuring that all pregnant women receive comprehensive prenatal services. Public health initiatives should aim to enhance access to quality healthcare while also raising community awareness to empower women to seek timely medical attention. Interestingly, the inconclusive findings from research conducted in the Wolaita zone regarding the relationship between gestational age and PIHD risk highlight the necessity for context-specific investigations [3]. Factors influencing maternal health outcomes can vary significantly across different populations, necessitating localized studies to comprehend the complex interactions among socioeconomic, cultural, and healthcare system-related factors [7, 11]. This variability emphasizes the challenges of managing pregnancies within diverse demographic contexts.

In addition to these findings, the study points out that woman with a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) of ≥ 28.0 cm present with a significantly higher risk of PIHD, indicated by an AOR of 1.29. In contrast, a higher pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI) classified as overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 25.0) is associated with a lower risk of PIHD (AOR = 0.81). This observation calls for an investigation into the underlying mechanisms that link MUAC—often reflective of upper body fat distribution and metabolic responses—to the incidence of PIHD. Elevated MUAC may also correlate with complications such as inflammation and insulin resistance. The existing literature supports MUAC as a reliable indicator of nutritional status and body composition during pregnancy, potentially shedding light on adiposity that BMI alone may not capture [23]. For instance, research by Mekie et al. (2020) indicates that mothers with a MUAC of 25 cm or more face double the odds of developing PIHD, while Tefsa et al. (2020) found a MUAC of 21 cm offers a 49% protective effect against PIHD [13]. Generallyl, these findings suggest that MUAC may serve as a more sensitive metric for assessing body fat distribution and nutritional status in pregnant women than BMI [13, 23].

Furthermore, the study reveals a surprising trend that women with higher pre-pregnancy BMI report a lower likelihood of developing PIHD, challenging the traditional belief that a BMI over 30 poses a significant risk [8, 26]. This assertion is contrary to findings from earlier research in various contexts, including studies conducted in India [7]. The existing literature indicates that oversimplifying BMI classifications may obscure the complexity of its relationship with PIHD. A more nuanced analysis that employs continuous BMI measurements is likely to uncover varied and potentially non-linear associations with the risk of PIHD. Our study exemplifies this dual approach, using categorical BMI in descriptive analyses to pinpoint at-risk groups and continuous BMI in multivariable assessments to explore its dynamic influence on PIHD. This comprehensive methodology allows for an enhanced understanding of BMI's relationship with PIHD, although it may also complicate interpretation of the results. Our findings may diverge from those of established studies, which might reflect unique pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the relationships between MUAC, BMI, and PIHD. Environmental and lifestyle factors play significant roles in how obesity affects PIHD risk; thus, understanding cultural differences related to diet, physical activity, body image, and healthcare access becomes crucial [8, 26]. For instance, the Sidama community's emphasis on balanced nutrition and higher levels of physical activity might mitigate PIHD risk. Additionally, genetic predispositions and socioeconomic factors significantly impact body composition and health outcomes, highlighting the need to reevaluate BMI’s universal application as a health risk indicator. Overall, our analysis emphasizes the necessity for refined approaches to BMI assessment in maternal health, aimed at enhancing clinical practice and improving outcomes for both mothers and their children [8, 26]. Future research should consider integrating alternative measures of body composition, such as body fat percentage or waist circumference, to more accurately reflect the multifaceted nature of obesity and obesity-related health outcomes in diverse demographics.

Moreover, the relationship between maternal BMI and birth outcomes, especially regarding the risk of small for gestational age (SGA) infants and PIHD, is intricate and multifaceted. The results of this study reveal a protective association between lower pre-pregnancy BMI and PIHD, with an Adjusted Odds Ratio of 0.81. While this finding aligns with some existing literature [3, 4], it challenges traditional views that commonly associate low BMI with a reduced risk of preeclampsia and an increased prevalence of SGA [1, 2, 6, 7, 10]. Given the inherent limitations of our dataset, this study could not include continuous birthweight centiles in the analysis, which may have provided deeper insights into these relationships. Therefore, future research should prioritize the exploration of these associations by incorporating detailed birthweight data and gestational age information to further enhance our understanding of the complex interactions between maternal health, BMI, and fetal outcomes.

A notable correlation was also discovered between a history of anemia and low maternal hemoglobin levels, which are associated with an increased risk of developing PIHD. This finding underscores the urgent need for consistent and effective monitoring and management of anemia throughout the prenatal care continuum. The relevance of this connection is supported by existing literature [5, 26, 27]. However, it is worth noting that a study conducted by Belayhun et al. in 2021 challenged the prevailing notion that a history of anemia is definitively a risk factor for the development of PIHD [3]. Generally, the findings indicate that anemia during pregnancy presents significant risks that not only jeopardize maternal health but also potentially jeopardize fetal well-being and development. This dual impact accentuates the necessity for proactive intervention and diligent attention from healthcare providers to optimize outcomes for both mothers and their babies throughout the prenatal period [5, 26, 27].

Interestingly, the analysis revealed that the control group of normotensive women experienced a higher incidence of stillbirths (36.3%) compared to those with PIHD (30.4%), shedding light on broader implications related to these conditions. Several factors may contribute to this discrepancy, including the presence of undiagnosed health issues in the control group, such as higher body mass index and untreated infections, which can adversely affect pregnancy outcomes. Furthermore, disparities in healthcare access and the quality of prenatal care are evident; typically, women with PIHD receive more rigorous monitoring, while normotensive women may underestimate the importance of regular medical check-ups. Socioeconomic status also plays a crucial role, as women from lower socioeconomic backgrounds in the control group may face increased barriers to accessing quality healthcare, thereby heightening their vulnerability to stillbirth. Risky lifestyle behaviors, including smoking and substance use, along with inadequate nutrition, further exacerbate the situation [28, 29]. These findings highlight the urgent need for enhanced maternal healthcare and comprehensive prenatal support, particularly focusing on social determinants of health for at-risk normotensive women.

Furthermore, the study result reveals the strong connection between chronic medical conditions, particularly pre-existing hypertension, and the likelihood of developing PIHD [9]. Women with a history of chronic hypertension face a significantly elevated risk of experiencing PIHD compared to their counterparts without such medical histories, corroborating findings from prior studies [3, 5, 15, 26]. This established link emphasizes the importance for healthcare providers to prioritize the monitoring and management of blood pressure in pregnant individuals with existing conditions. Existing evidences affirmed that early detection and intervention could mitigate risks and enhance outcomes for both mothers and their fetuses [5, 9, 15, 30, 31].

Moreover, we identified a significant correlation between a family history of diabetes mellitus and an increased risk of PIHD. Research conducted in Ethiopia supports this correlation, indicating the influence of genetic and environmental factors on both diabetes and PIHD [13, 21]. This relationship enriches our understanding of maternal health, suggesting that genetic predispositions, coupled with modifiable lifestyle factors, can collectively impact pregnancy outcomes. Further investigation into the mechanisms linking diabetes—such as insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome—could enhance targeted screening practices within obstetric care.

Transitioning to lifestyle choices, this study underscores their vital role in influencing the development of PIHD [9, 13, 32]. Notably, our findings reveal that abstaining from alcohol—often recommended by healthcare professionals—acts as a protective factor against PIHD, consistent with existing literature that advocates for avoiding alcohol during pregnancy [9, 32]. However, previous studies have presented conflicting results regarding this issue, emphasizing the methodological differences in assessing alcohol consumption and highlighting the need for standardized methodologies in both research and clinical settings [3]. These findings reinforce the necessity for uniform evaluation techniques and effective counseling during prenatal visits to educate expecting mothers about the detrimental effects of alcohol on both maternal and fetal health.

Additionally, regular physical activity is identified as a protective factor against PIHD, prompting healthcare providers to incorporate personalized exercise regimens into prenatal care. Women who engage in regular physical activity exhibit a lower risk of developing PIHD, advocating for public health initiatives that clarify definitions and ensure consistent measurement metrics for physical activity. Such an approach would enhance understanding and implementation across healthcare frameworks [32–35]. To address the discrepancies in findings regarding the impact of physical activity on PIHD, the study calls for clear definitions and standardized measurements, which could enhance clarity in public health initiatives [21].

Furthermore, the study scrutinizes the dietary habits of pregnant women. Although 70.5% of women with PIHD reported consuming fruits and vegetables, this figure was significantly higher at 93.1% among the control group (P = 0.001). Initial analyses suggested a possible link between dietary habits and PIHD; however, subsequent investigation did not yield conclusive connections. This inconsistency with existing research—where low fruit and vegetable intake is consistently associated with an increased risk of PIHD—highlights the complexities of maternal nutrition and its impact on health outcomes [13, 23]. While women with PIHD display notable differences in dietary consumption compared to the control group, further exploration into potential nutritional deficiencies is warranted. Socioeconomic status and access to nutritious foods significantly influence these outcomes, emphasizing the importance of potassium-rich foods for blood pressure regulation. Public health initiatives aimed at enhancing nutritional education and food accessibility are crucial for improving maternal health. A comprehensive approach to maternal care—which integrates medical management with lifestyle counseling, alongside proactive interventions like lifestyle screenings and resource provision—could substantially reduce the incidence of PIHD. Collaborative efforts among healthcare providers, local governments, and community organizations are essential for empowering pregnant women to make informed dietary choices, ultimately fostering healthier outcomes for mothers and their children. Additional research is critical to elucidate the intricate causal relationships and mechanisms linking dietary habits and lifestyle choices to the development of PIHD. Qualitative studies that explore women's perceptions of nutrition and physical activity, paired with larger quantitative studies establishing clear connections between specific lifestyle modifications and health outcomes, will be vital in developing targeted interventions to enhance maternal health.

Finally, this study presents valuable insights into the predictors of PIHD among women in the Sidama region of Ethiopia. However, it is essential to acknowledge certain limitations that may impact the generalizability and interpretation of the findings. One notable limitation is the underrepresentation of primigravida women, which constrains our capacity to effectively analyze predictors unique to this subgroup. Additionally, the study did not factor in partner health status or the history of previous pregnancies with different partners. This omission limits our assessment of paternal influences, especially among multiparous women. The small number of participants with high Body Mass Index (BMI) also restricts our conclusions regarding this demographic. Although the research provides critical insights, it is important to recognize the inherent limitations surrounding the clinical diagnosis of preeclampsia, particularly when laboratory confirmation is lacking. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data related to lifestyle behaviors introduces potential biases. The case–control design of the study also limits our ability to establish causal relationships. Moreover, the sample may not adequately represent all socioeconomic strata, particularly within rural populations. We also acknowledge the exclusion of individuals with conditions closely associated with PIHD, such as HELLP syndrome and severe eclampsia, which could restrict the broader applicability of our findings. Lastly, the possibility of recall bias due to limited evaluations during facility visits warrants consideration. While standardized data collection methods and thorough training were implemented to mitigate response bias, the risks of selection bias, information bias, and confounding variables persist. These factors highlight the complexity surrounding PIHD and necessitate careful interpretation of our results, emphasizing the need for further research to expand upon these findings.

Conclusions

The study identifies key predictive factors for Pregnancy-Induced Hypertensive Disorders (PIHD), including age at first conception, wealth index, multigravidity, gestational age, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), hemoglobin levels, pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI), pre-existing hypertension, family history of diabetes, alcohol consumption, and physical activity during pregnancy. It reveals the intricate relationship between socioeconomic status and maternal health, emphasizing that financial resources alone do not ensure better outcomes, particularly without educational access. The findings challenge conventional views on wealth and education's roles in maternal health, underscoring the need for comprehensive interventions. The study advocates for targeted public health initiatives to tackle these disparities and recommends monitoring key health indicators for at-risk women. A holistic approach integrating financial and educational factors into health planning is essential. Future research should investigate partner stability's impact on maternal health, considering psychosocial and clinical factors. Improved diagnostic methods and thorough health evaluations are critical for enhancing maternal healthcare strategies, especially in resource-limited settings like Sidama, Ethiopia. The research also stresses addressing anemia, stillbirth rates, and chronic conditions' effects on pregnancy outcomes to create effective interventions. Overall, it supports a well-rounded prenatal care approach that includes lifestyle modifications and standardized screenings, with the goal of reducing health disparities and improving maternal and fetal outcomes across populations. This study lays the groundwork for further exploration of PIHD factors and promotes an integrative perspective on maternal health that incorporates biological and sociocultural aspects.
