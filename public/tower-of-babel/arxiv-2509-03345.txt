Language Models Do Not Follow Occam’s Razor: A Benchmark for Inductive and Abductive Reasoning
Yunxin Sun & Abulhair Saparov

Abstract
Reasoning is a core capability in artificial intelligence systems, for which large language models (LLMs) have recently shown remarkable progress. However , most work focuses exclusively on deductive reasoning, which is problematic since other types of reasoning are also essential in solving real-world problems, and they are less explored. This work focuses on evaluating LLMs’ inductive and abductive reasoning capabilities. We introduce a programmable and synthetic dataset, InAbHyD (pronounced in-a-bid ), where each reasoning example consists of an incomplete world model and a set of observations. The task for the intelligent agent is to produce hypotheses to explain observations under the incomplete world model to solve each reasoning example. We propose a new metric to evaluate the quality of hypotheses based on Occam’s Razor. We evaluate and analyze some state-of-the-art LLMs. Our analysis shows that LLMs can perform inductive and abductive reasoning in simple scenarios, but struggle with complex world models and producing high-quality hypotheses, even with popular reasoning-enhancing techniques such as in-context learning and RLVR. 1 1 1 All data and generation code are freely available at https://github.com/byrantwithyou/inabhyd/ .

Introduction

Reasoning is a hallmark of human intelligence. Peirce categorizes reasoning into deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning
(
Burch 2024
)
. Deductive reasoning draws a specific conclusion from a set of premises. For example, given “All humans are moral” and “Socrates is a human”, we can conclude that “Socrates is moral”. Inductive reasoning aims to identify a general principle from multiple individual cases. For example, if we see many cute cats, we may inductively reason that all cats are cute. Abductive reasoning seeks to produce hypotheses to explain observations. For example, if we hear a bird sound outside, we may abductively reason that there is a bird. The goal in both types of reasoning is to find explanations to explain observations (inductive reasoning focuses on inducing a more general rule, while abductive reasoning focuses on finding a ground fact or axiom).

The composition of inductive and abductive reasoning is ubiquitous in real-world scenarios. For example, given (i)
Amy has a skin rash and leg pain and has been diagnosed with a new unknown virus.
(ii)
Sam has a skin rash and leg pain and has been diagnosed with the same new unknown virus.
(iii)
Tom also has a skin rash and leg pain.
We can first induce the rule:
The new unknown virus causes skin rashes and leg pain
, and then we can abduce that
Tom is infected with this new virus
.

A fundamental difference between deductive and inductive or abductive reasoning is that deductive reasoning is deterministic. The conclusion in deductive reasoning is deterministically correct if the proof is sound and the premises are correct.
However, inductive and abductive reasoning are inherently probabilistic. Technically, there may exist many conclusions, some
more probable (high-quality)
and some
less probable (low-quality)
. For example, in the above bird sound case, a
less probable
conclusion is that someone outside is playing the bird sound on a speaker. In this regard, we view inductive and abductive reasoning as the inverse processes of deductive reasoning and study them through a unified lens. In many real-world scenarios, all three reasoning modalities are indispensable. Deductive reasoning is widely used in mathematical problem-solving, inductive reasoning is commonly used in scientific research and discovery, and abductive reasoning is often employed in medical diagnosis. Knowing and applying the principles of parsimony / Occam’s Razor is essential for LLMs to be utilized in areas such as scientific discovery, which require inductive and abductive reasoning, yet this is often overlooked. Physicists, for example, strive for the simplest explanations to account for as many observations as possible. According to our findings, LLMs struggle to generate high-quality hypotheses that match human-level intelligence. To the best of our knowledge, prior studies of inductive and abductive reasoning did not consider parsimony / Occam’s Razor in their evaluation.

The high-level research question we want to answer in this paper is:
Do LLMs reason inductively and abductively? If so, how well?
There is a significant body of work on evaluating the deductive reasoning capabilities of LLMs
(
Saparov and He 2023
;
Saparov et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Tafjord et al. 2021
;
Han et al. 2024
)
, as well as techniques to improve deductive reasoning capabilities, such as in-context learning and reinforcement learning with verifiable reward,
RLVR

(
Lambert et al. 2024
;
Su et al. 2025
)
. However, there is relatively little exploration into the inductive and abductive reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). This work systematically evaluates whether and to what extent LLMs can reason abductively and inductively. We also assess whether popular reasoning-enhancing techniques, such as in-context learning
(
Zhou et al. 2022
;
Dong et al. 2024
)
and RLVR
(
Lambert et al. 2024
;
Su et al. 2025
)
, which primarily aim to improve deductive reasoning capabilities, can also benefit inductive and abductive reasoning.

Techniques to enhance reasoning.

A large body of work has focused on improving the
(deductive) reasoning capabilities of LLMs and related downstream tasks such as mathematics and coding
(
Li 2025
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Yao et al. 2023
;

;
Su et al. 2025
;
Lambert et al. 2024

(

(

3
Approach

In this section, we describe how we generate the dataset and evaluate LLMs on this dataset. At a high level, the dataset consists of synthetically-generated reasoning examples in natural language.
The question description is a user prompt, and the format specification is a system prompt to LLMs. We parse LLMs’ output to evaluate their inductive and abductive reasoning capabilities on metrics such as accuracy. We acknowledge that the potential space of inductive and abductive reasoning examples is vast, so we restrict ourselves to what is expressible in first-order logic. By composing the inductive and abductive reasoning steps in Table
2
, our evaluation framework can readily cover all inductive and abductive reasoning questions in first-order logic.

Each reasoning example contains a fictional world model and a set of observations. We use real person names (such as Amy) and real property names (such as strong), but we do not use real concept names (such as tiger). Instead, we use fictional concept names (such as wumpus). In doing so, LLMs will not be confused by these fictional relations, as we never generate facts that contradict those about real concepts.

LLMs must produce hypotheses to
explain
the observations. Our
hypothesize-explain
framework can cover a wide range of inductive/abductive reasoning scenarios.

Next, we describe each step in more detail.

World model generation

In our approach, the world model is an ontology tree in first-order logic. Each node in the ontology tree is a concept (e.g.
cat
) with properties (e.g.
All cats are cute
, or equivalently in logical form
\forall x(\texttt{cat}(x)\rightarrow\texttt{cute}(x))
) and members (e.g.
Amy is a cat
, or equivalently in logical forms,

cat(Amy)
). Each edge in the ontology tree denotes a subtype relationship (e.g., the
ragdoll
node being a child of the
cat
node implies
Each ragdoll is a cat
, or equivalently in logical form,

\forall x(\texttt{ragdoll}(x)\rightarrow\texttt{cat}(x))
).
We first generate the topology of the ontology tree with the height as a controllable parameter. Each non-leaf node has two or three child nodes. The height of the ontology tree is a variable that characterizes the complexity of the world model. We then make two passes of the tree in level order traversal. In the first pass, for each node, we allocate a concept name, three properties,
4
4

4

The properties can also be negated properties, e.g.,
All cats are not slow
.
and three members.
After the first pass, we have a
complete
world model with three types of axioms:

(1)

properties (e.g.,
All cats are cute
),

(2)

membership relations (e.g.,
Amy is a cat
),

(3)

subtype relations (e.g.,
Each ragdoll is a cat
).

In the second pass, we randomly mark some axioms as hidden. These axioms are the ground truth hypotheses. After the second pass, we have an
incomplete

Observation generation

Next, we generate observations so that LLMs have sufficient information to produce the ground truth hypotheses. The observations must be complex enough that the reasoning example is non-trivial, yet they should also ensure the
reasoning is feasible.

•

To generate an example where the task is to infer a property (e.g.,
All cats are cute
), we first identify the type in the ontology with this property (e.g.,
cat
), then we sample members of this type and subtypes (e.g.,
Amy is a cat
;
Sally is a ragdoll
;
Polly is a shorthair
), and add observations where the members have this property (e.g.,
Amy is cute
;
Sally is cute
;
Polly is cute
).

•

To generate an example where the task is to infer a membership relation (e.g.,
Amy is a cat
), we first identify the type in the ontology with this member (e.g.,
cat
), then we sample properties of this type and supertypes (e.g.,
All cats are cute
;
All felines are not slow
;
All mammals are hairy
), and add observations where this member have these properties (e.g.,
Amy is cute
;
Amy is not slow
;
Amy is hairy
).

•

To generate an example where the task is to infer a subtype relation (e.g.,
All cats are felines
), we first identify the subtype in the ontology (e.g.,
cat
), then we sample members of this type and its subtypes (e.g.,
Amy is a cat
;
Sally is a ragdoll
;
Polly is a shorthair
), and add observations where these members belong to the supertype (e.g.,
Amy is a feline
;
Polly is a feline
;
Sally is a feline

Translate theories and observations into natural language

We use the same grammar as in
PrOntoQA
for translating logical forms into natural language. Properties are translated into the form:

All cats are cute
; subtype relations are randomly translated into one of three forms:
Each ragdoll is a cat;

Every ragdoll is a cat;

All ragdolls are cats
; membership relations are translated into the form
Amy is a cat
.

We define a metric to evaluate the quality of hypotheses
based on the principles of parsimony, or Occam’s Razor (i.e., the priciple that, other things being equal, simpler theories are better;
Baker 2022
).
In this regard, we encourage one hypothesis to explain as many observations as possible and penalize unnecessary hypotheses.

\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{H}^{*}

\mathcal{O}

\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{H}

q(\mathcal{H})

Results

4.1
Experimental setup

In each experiment configuration, we generate 100 independent and identically distributed reasoning examples. Each reasoning example is pure text, a user prompt with an incomplete world model and observations. We also specify the desired output format in the system prompt. To answer each reasoning example, LLMs must produce hypotheses to explain all observations. We evaluate hypotheses on three metrics: weak accuracy, strong accuracy, and quality. We compute 95% confidence intervals for both accuracy metrics using the Wilson interval
(
Wilson 1927
)
.
We use the OpenAI API to evaluate
GPT-4o
and
together.ai
platform to evaluate
Llama3-70B
,
Gemma3-27B
,
DeepSeek-V3
, and
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B
.

In our experiments, we vary the following variables:

(1)

Different types of tasks (i.e., infer property, infer membership relation, or infer subtype relation),

(2)

Single hypothesis or multiple hypotheses,

(3)

The height of the ontology tree,

(4)

Whether the reasoning example contains in-context demonstrations and whether they are in-distribution or out-of-distribution.

LLMs have shown extraordinary generalization capabilities with few-shot examples coupled with CoT prompts
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
. We are also interested in whether in-context learning can help with inductive and abductive reasoning. We add eight demonstrations to each reasoning example, with the demonstrations’ ground truth hypotheses and CoTs. The CoT consists of each observation’s step-by-step proof. We have two settings for in-context learning:

(1)

In-distribution demonstrations, where the demonstration has an ontology tree of the same height as the test question.

(2)

Out-of-distribution demonstrations, where each demonstration has a single hypothesis with a height-1 ontology tree.

In both settings, we ensure demonstrations cover all types of tasks.

The in-distribution demonstrations are slightly more helpful than out-of-distribution demonstrations. This result is consistent with the hypothesis that in-context learning mimics Bayesian inference
(
Xie et al. 2022
)
. We also observe that in-context learning with in-distribution demonstrations improves strong accuracy and quality in ontology trees with heights 3 and 4. We conjecture that LLMs can learn how to navigate the ontology hierarchy and produce high-quality hypotheses through in-context demonstrations. In contrast, we do not observe a significant performance improvement from out-of-distribution demonstrations.

4.5
Analysis of common errors

To further understand
how
and
why
LLMs fail, we manually inspect mistaken and low-quality hypotheses. We summarize some of the most common types of errors here for further insights. We provide example responses in each category in Appendix
E
.

(1)

Wrong ontology direction.
LLMs sometimes produce hypotheses in the wrong direction. For example, the ground truth hypothesis is
All cats are mammals
, while LLMs will produce the hypothesis
All mammals are cats
.

(2)

Ignore the ontology and produce unnecessary hypotheses.
LLMs sometimes can produce unnecessary hypotheses because they ignore the ontology. For example, the ground truth hypothesis is
All cats are cute
, while LLMs produce two hypotheses:
All cats are cute; All ragdolls are cute
.

(3)

Fall back to trivial hypotheses.
Technically, the observation is also a valid hypothesis, but it is considered low-quality because it can only explain itself. LLMs sometimes reuse observations as hypotheses.

(4)

Hallucinated entities.
LLMs sometimes produce hypotheses with hallucinated entities (including concepts, properties, members). Usually, this happens when the entity appears in the in-context demonstrations, but LLMs use them in the test question.

(5)

Confuse members with concepts.
We use fictional names for concepts and real-person names for members. However, LLMs sometimes do confuse these two. For example, the ground truth hypothesis is
Amy is a wumpus
, while LLMs produce
Wumpus is Amy
.

5
Conclusions and future work

In this study, we introduced a programmable and synthetic dataset,

InAbHyD

. Our dataset serves as an out-of-distribution evaluation of the inductive and abductive reasoning abilities of LLMs. We do not claim that success in our benchmark automatically implies success in real-world tasks, but rather, our benchmark serves as a checkpoint for developing models that are capable of robust real-world abductive and inductive reasoning.

We systematically studied LLMs’ inductive and abductive reasoning capabilities. Our results suggest that current LLMs can’t generate high-quality hypotheses that match human-level intelligence. For example, suppose we use LLMs as a scientific discovery assistant. In that case, they may generate superficial hypotheses that can explain observations but fail to uncover more general hypotheses that reveal hidden structures or connections in science. Our results can serve to better inform practitioners of LLMs of their limitations, especially in applications that rely heavily on inductive and abductive reasoning. The dataset itself opens new avenues for future work, such as training or fine-tuning models to improve their inductive and abductive reasoning capabilities.

One limitation of this work is that first-order logic (FOL) limits the expressiveness of the world model and the reasoning example. We leave it as a future work to use higher-order logic (HOL) to generate more complex world models and reasoning examples, which would require translating from HOL into natural language and vice versa. The other crucial future direction is improving the quality of LLMs’ hypotheses. For that, LLMs may benefit from RLVR
(
Lambert et al. 2024
)
that explicitly conveys principles of parsimony in inductive and abductive reasoning with an appropriate reward. Another interesting future direction will be to determine whether large language models fine-tuned on this synthetic dataset (using supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) could improve their performance on real-world tasks involving inductive and abductive reasoning. If this is the case, this synthetic dataset is more valuable because we can essentially generate an infinite number of synthetic data points, without worrying about the bottleneck of sampling data from the real world.
