Score-based sampling without diffusions: Guidance from a simple and modular scheme


Abstract
Sampling based on score diffusions has led to striking empirical results, and has attracted considerable attention from various research communities. It depends on availability of (approximate) Stein score functions for various levels of additive noise. We describe and analyze a modular scheme that reduces score-based sampling to solving a short sequence of “nice” sampling problems, for which high-accuracy samplers are known. We show how to design forward trajectories such that both (a) the terminal distribution, and (b) each of the backward conditional distribution is defined by a strongly log concave (SLC) distribution. This modular reduction allows us to exploit any SLC sampling algorithm in order to traverse the backwards path, and we establish novel guarantees with short proofs for both uni-modal and multi-modal densities. The use of high-accuracy routines yields ε \varepsilon -accurate answers, in either KL or Wasserstein distances, with polynomial dependence on log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) \log(1/\varepsilon) and d \sqrt{d} dependence on the dimension.

1.1
A geometric view

Let us provide some intuition and context for the modular scheme
analyzed in this paper. For a given positive integer
K
, we begin
with the standard forward noising process

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}X+\sqrt{1-\theta_{k}^{2}}\;W_{k}\qquad\mbox{with $W_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$,}

(1)

for
k=0,1,\ldots K-1
. Setting
\theta_{0}=1
ensures that

Y_{1}=X
, and
(\theta_{1},\ldots\theta_{K-1})
are scalars in the
interval
(0,1)
to be chosen. In the standard diffusion approach,
the sequence (
1
) represents a discretization of
a variance-preserving SDE
34
;
53
. The
trajectory length
K=K(\varepsilon)
is chosen as a function of the
target accuracy
\varepsilon
so as to ensure accurate tracking of the
reverse-time SDE or ODE; for this reason, one essential

\varepsilon
-dependent bottleneck is the discretization error, and
leads to iteration complexities that scale linearly in dimension
d
,
and polynomially in
(1/\varepsilon)
.

In contrast, our modular scheme avoids any consideration of
discretized diffusions, and makes use of trajectory lengths
K
that
are independent of the target accuracy
\varepsilon
, and satisfy the
following two properties:

(T)

It is easy to sample from the
terminal
marginal
distribution
Y_{K}\sim p_{K}
, and

(B)

Each of the
backward
conditional distributions

Y_{k}\mid Y_{k+1}
are strongly log-concave (SLC) with constant
condition number, and hence easy for sampling.

When these two properties hold, we can carry out each transition in
the reverse chain by exploiting fast samplers for SLC distributions.
Our analysis quantifies the size of the steps
\theta_{k}
and hence
trajectory length that guarantee these two properties.

A simple graphical example helps to illustrate how it is possible to
take large (independent of
\varepsilon
) steps while ensuring that
both properties
(T)
and
(B)
hold. Starting with
X
drawn from a
simple multi-modal distribution in
2
, we used the
recursion (
1
) to construct a path of length
K=5
, in particular using the sequence
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{5})=(1.0,0.93,0.85,0.78,0.60)
.
Panel (a)
in
Figure

1
shows contour plots of the marginal densities

p_{k}\equiv p_{\mathsf{y_{k}}}
generated by this forward process;
while multi-modal for the first four rounds, the final marginal
p_{5}

is a simple (uni-modal) sampling problem, so that property
(T)
holds.

Thus, given a sample
Y_{5}
from the final marginal
p_{5}
, the
remaining challenge is to traverse the backward path of conditional
distributions, thereby generating a backwards sequence
(Y_{5},Y_{4},\ldots,Y_{1})
, such that
Y_{1}\sim p_{1}
. The difficulty of this
backward path depends on the structure of the backwards conditional
distribution
p_{k\mid k+1}
of
Y_{k}\mid Y_{k+1}
. Panels (b)
through (d) in
Figure

1
plot the contours of these backward
densities, as defined by three different choices of the
Y
-sequence.
The key observation is that—in
marked contrast
to the forward
problems—each of these backwards sampling problems is defined by a
relatively simple unimodal density. In fact, as shown by our theory,
there are simple stepsize selection rules that ensure that all of these
backwards transitions are strongly log-concave (SLC), so that property

(B)
holds.

The main contribution of this paper is to describe and analyze this
general modular scheme in two main settings. In particular:

•

In
Theorem

1
, we study the problem of sampling from a
strongly log-concave density with condition number
\kappa
. This
is a very well-studied problem, and there are various high-accuracy
procedures that, based on first-order information from

p_{\mathsf{x}}
, draw samples with iteration complexity scaling
with
\kappa
; see
Section

2.1
for further discussion. By
adapting our modular scheme to this setting, we exhibit a sampler
with iteration complexity scaling as
\widetilde{O}\big(\sqrt{d}\log(\kappa)\log^{3}(1/\varepsilon)\big)
. Thus, knowledge of
annealed scores are very useful, since dependence on the condition
number becomes logarithmic.

•

In
Theorem

2
, we study the use of our modular scheme
for sampling from an general multi-modal density. We specify a
forward trajectory of length
K
, specified by an
adaptive
stepsize sequence
, that ensures that both properties
(T)
and

(B)
hold. This leads to concrete bounds on sampling complexity in
terms of trajectory length
K
, and the complexity of solving these
sub-problems, and we exhibit a sampler with iteration complexity

\widetilde{O}(K\sqrt{d}\log^{3}(1/\varepsilon))
.
In
Corollary

1
, we provide a worst-case bound on
K
in
terms of a geometric Lipschitz constant, but suspect that this
guarantee can be improved.

We discuss connections between these results and related work in more
detail in the next section, as well as following the statements
of
Theorem

1
and
Corollary

1
, along with open
questions and extensions in
Section

4.1.2
.

2.2
Annealed Stein scores

Score-based sampling procedures operate on a sequences of random
variables that are transformed by a simple linear operation with
Gaussian noise. In particular, given a random vector
U\in\real^{d}
and a pair of positive scalars
a
and
b
, consider the
update

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=aU+bW\qquad\mbox{where $W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$ is standard Gaussian.}

(4)

For instance, when
a=1
and
b=\sigma
, then
V
corresponds
to a version of
U
corrupted by additive Gaussian noise with
covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}
. This transformation can be
viewed as a form of annealing: the density
p_{\mathsf{v}}
of
V
will
be smoother than the density
p_{\mathsf{u}}
of
U
, since it is
obtained by convolving
p_{\mathsf{u}}
with the Gaussian density.

The classical Robbins–Tweedie
formula
46
;
41
;
30
relates the

Stein score
function
\nabla\log p_{\mathsf{v}}
to the conditional
expectation
\mathbb{E}[U\mid V=v]
. In particular, for the
model (
4
), it guarantees that

\displaystyle-\nabla\log p_{\mathsf{v}}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\Big\{v-a\mathbb{E}[U\mid V=v]\Big\}.

(5)

Knowledge of this score function allows us to draw samples from

p_{\mathsf{v}}
using the score-based procedures described
in
Section

2.1
.

For our scheme, it is essential that the
conditional score

\nabla_{u}\log p_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}
also has a simple representation. In
particular, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}\log p_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}(u\mid v)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{u}\log p_{\mathsf{u}}(u)+\nabla_{u}\log p_{\mathsf{v}\mid\mathsf{u}}(v\mid u)\;=\;\nabla_{u}\log p_{\mathsf{u}}(u)-\frac{a}{b^{2}}\big(au-v\big),

(6)

where the second equality follows from the fact that
(V\mid U=u)\sim\mathcal{N}(au,b^{2}\mathbf{I})
, and the form of the
Gaussian density. Consequently, knowledge of the marginal score

\nabla_{u}\log p_{\mathsf{u}}(u)
gives us knowledge of the conditional
score.
In particular, we can then
apply the fast algorithms from
Section

2.1
to
draw samples from the backwards conditional distribution
p_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}
.

In summary, for the
1
-step model
V=aU+bW
, knowledge of the
marginal score functions enable us to exploit fast algorithms for both
(a) generating samples from the marginal distributions
p_{\mathsf{u}}
and

p_{\mathsf{v}}
, and (b) generating samples from the backward conditional

p_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}
.

2.3
Second-order Tweedie and Hessian structure

Instead of focusing on the score function—that is, the first
derivative of the log density—the bulk of our analysis is instead
focused on second derivatives. More precisely, still focusing on the
update
V=aU+bW
, we introduce the two Hessian matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{u}}(u)\coloneqq-\nabla^{2}\log p_{\mathsf{u}}(u)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{v}}(v)\coloneqq-\nabla^{2}\log p_{\mathsf{v}}(v),

(7)

associated with the marginal distributions
p_{\mathsf{u}}
and
p_{\mathsf{v}}

over
U
and
V
respectively, along with the Hessian

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}(u,v)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\nabla_{u}^{2}\log p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}(u\mid v)

(8)

associated with the conditional distribution of
U\mid V
.

To be clear, our analysis makes central use of these second-order
objects, but the standard sampling schemes that we use to solve
sub-problems are still based on first-order information only. The
following lemma plays a key role in our analysis:

Lemma 1

(Forward and conditional Hessians)
.

We have the second-order Tweedie formula

\displaystyle\hskip-36.135pt\mbox{\bf{Second-order Tweedie:}}\qquad\underbrace{\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{v}}(v)}_{-\nabla^{2}\log p_{\mathsf{v}}(v)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\Big\{\mathbf{I}-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\operatorname{cov}(U\mid V=v)\Big\}.

(9a)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\hskip-72.26999pt\mbox{\bf{Backward conditional Hessian:}}\qquad\quad\underbrace{\mathbf{J}_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}(u,v)}_{-\nabla^{2}_{u}\log p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}(u\mid v)}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{u}}(u)+\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\mathbf{I}.

(9b)

The second-order Tweedie formula (
9a
) is a
known result (see the
papers
30
;
9
;
27
;
5

for variants), whereas the backward conditional Hessian
formula (
9b
) follows directly from the structure of
the joint distribution. For completeness, we provide a proof of both
claims in
Appendix

A
.

3.1
Procedure and its guarantee

We initialize
Y_{0}=X
, and then construct a forward path of the form

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}=a_{k}Y_{k}+\sqrt{1-a_{k}^{2}}W_{k}\qquad\mbox{where $W_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$,}

(10)

and
a_{k}\in(0,1)
are stepsizes to be chosen. The

sub-problems
to be solved in traversing the backward path are
(i) sampling from the terminal marginal
p_{K}
, and (ii) for each
k=K-1,\ldots,0
, sampling from the backward conditional
p_{k\mid k+1}
. Recall from
Section

2.2
that access to annealed
score functions gives us access to the required gradients for both
marginal and backward condition sampling.

Theorem 1

(Logarithmic reduction to SLC black box sampling)
.

Given any
(m,M)
-strongly log-concave target density

p
, there is a forward trajectory (
10
) of
length at most

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\leq 1+\log_{2}\big(M/m\big),

(11a)

along with stepsizes
\{a_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
that can be chosen to ensure that:

(a)

All sampling sub-problems are SLC with condition number at
most
2
.

(b)

For each distance
D\in\{\sqrt{m}\mathscr{W}_{2}\}
, we
can perform
\varepsilon
-accurate
sampling (in distance
D
)
from the target
p
in at most

\displaystyle T(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}{N}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{825862}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{825862}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{729956}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{729954}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}}\big(s_{k}\varepsilon\big)

(11b)

first-order queries, where
\{s_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
is any positive sequence
such that
\sum_{k=0}^{K}s_{k}=1
.

Choice of
s_{k}
:

In stating the guarantee (
11b
), we have allowed
for some freedom in choosing the sequence
\{s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, since it
might be desirable to solve earlier problems to lower accuracy than
later problems. Disregarding this degree of freedom, the simplest
choice is the constant one
s_{k}=1/(K+1)
,
in which case the
total number of rounds scales as

\displaystyle T(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=(K+1)\,{N}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675609}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675609}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675610}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675609}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}}\big(\varepsilon/(K+1)\big)\;=\;\big\{2+\log_{2}(\kappa)\big\}\;{N}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675609}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675609}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675610}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675609}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\log(\kappa)}\Big).

(12a)

Explicit consequence:

Theorem

1
is a general reduction that allows
for any black-box SLC sampler to be used in solving the sub-problems.
Let us develop its specific consequences for the high-accuracy
samplers with iteration complexity
{N}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675609}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675609}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675610}{262143}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{675609}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}}(\varepsilon)=\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{d}\log^{3}(1/\varepsilon)
, as previously discussed in
equation (
3
). Substiuting this choice into
equation (
12a
), we find an overall complexity of

\displaystyle T(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{O}\Big(\sqrt{d}\,(2+\log_{2}(\kappa))\log^{3}\big(\frac{2+\log(\kappa)}{\varepsilon}\big)\Big).

(12b)

Overall, we have a sub-linear dimension dependence (
\sqrt{d}
)
accompanied by the logarithmic dependence
\log\kappa
. This latter
property should be contrasted with the polynomial dependence on

\kappa
exhibited by standard samplers, including both ULA and MALA
and variants thereof.

It is also worth comparing this guarantee to known bounds on the query
complexity for log-concave sampling; these lower bounds apply to
algorithms that
only
have access to first-order information
associated with the target distribution. Under this model, for an SLC
problem with condition number
\kappa
, it is known

that any procedure requires at least
\tilde{\Omega}\big(\min\{\sqrt{\kappa}\log d,\>d\}\big)
first-order queries. Our
guarantee (
12b
) violates this lower bound, but there
is no contradiction, since our modular scheme makes use of additional
annealed score information. This provides concrete evidence for the
computational benefits of knowing annealed scores.

3.2.1
Rescaling argument

It is convenient, as a first step, to reduce the problem of sampling
from an
(m,M)
-conditioned distribution to an equivalent
one that is
(1,M/m)
-conditioned.
In order to do
so, letting
X\sim p
be the original distribution, we define the
rescaled vector
Y=\sqrt{m}X
. If the original distribution

p
is
(m,M)
-conditioned, then the rescaled vector
Y

has a distribution that is
(1,M/m)
-conditioned.

Now suppose that we can generate samples of random vector
\widetilde{Y}

whose distribution is
\varepsilon
-close to that of
Y
in a given
distance
D
. We then define
\widetilde{X}=\widetilde{Y}/\sqrt{m}
,

and consider the quality of the
\widetilde{X}
samples as approximations to
the original
X
. The argument is slightly different, depending on
whether
D
is the KL distance or the Wasserstein distance.

When
D
is the KL distance, it is invariant to this linear
transformation, so that we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{757834}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\text{KL}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{757834}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\text{KL}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{672677}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{KL}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{672678}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{KL}$}}}}}}(p_{\widetilde{X}},p_{X})

\displaystyle=D_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{757834}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\text{KL}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{757834}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\text{KL}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{672677}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{KL}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{672678}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{KL}$}}}}}}(p_{\widetilde{Y}},p_{Y})\;=\;\varepsilon.

Consequently, the samples
\widetilde{X}
are
\varepsilon
-close to
X
in KL
distance.

When
D
is the Wasserstein distance, we use
\mathscr{W}_{2}(X,Y)
denote
the Wasserstein distance between
p_{X}
and
p_{Y}
. In this case, we
have

\displaystyle\sqrt{m}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\widetilde{X},X)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{W}_{2}\big(\sqrt{m}\widetilde{X},\sqrt{m}X\big)\;=\;\mathscr{W}_{2}(\widetilde{Y},Y)\;=\;\varepsilon

so that the samples are
\varepsilon
-close in the rescaled Wasserstein
norm
\sqrt{m}\mathscr{W}_{2}
. (Note that the theorem statement
involves this rescaled Wasserstein distance.)

Thus, for the remainder of the proof, we study the rescaled problem
with initial values

and
M_{0}=\frac{M}{m}
. We prove bounds in terms of
M_{0}
,
and then recall this transformation.

3.2.2
Forward recursion with adaptive stepsize choice

We initialize the forward recursion (
10
) with

Y_{0}=Y=\sqrt{m}X
, as defined in the previous section, and
then implement the recursion with adaptively chosen stepsizes
\{a_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
that depend on how the conditioning of the problem at

More specifically, given the initial value
M_{0}
, and we choose
the initial parameter

\displaystyle M_{k+1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\frac{M_{k}}{M_{k}(1-a_{k}^{2})+a_{k}^{2}},\quad\mbox{and}\quad a_{k}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{=}}\frac{M_{k}}{1+M_{k}}\qquad\mbox{at each round.}

We adopt the convenient shorthand

Lemma 2

(Properties of forward trajectory)

(a)

We have the Hessian sandwich

\displaystyle 1\;\preceq\;\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{k}}(y)\;\preceq\;M_{k}\qquad\mbox{at each round $k\in[K]$.}

(b)

After

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}\;\preceq\;\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{K}}(y)\;\preceq\;2\mathbf{I}\qquad\mbox{uniformly in $y\in\real^{d}$,}

\sup\limits_{y\in\real^{d}}\operatorname{cond}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{K}}(y))\leq 2

At each round

\displaystyle 1+M_{k}\;\preceq\;\mathbf{J}_{k}(y)\;\preceq\;2M_{k}\qquad\mbox{uniformly in $y\in\real^{d}$},

\sup\limits_{y\in\real^{d}}\operatorname{cond}(\mathbf{J}_{k}(y))\leq 2
.

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{k}(r)(\cdot)

3.3.1
Propagation of spectral control

We begin with an auxiliary result used to control the structure of the

\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{v}}(v)

Lemma 4

(Propagation of spectral control)
.

(a)

Suppose there is some
m\geq 0
such that

\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{u}}(u)\succeq m\mathbf{I}
uniformly in
u\in\real^{d}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{v}}(v)

\displaystyle\succeq\;\frac{m}{mb^{2}+a^{2}}\mathbf{I}\qquad\mbox{uniformly in $v\in\real^{d}$.}

(18a)

(b)

Suppose there is some
M<\infty
such that

\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{u}}(u)\preceq M\mathbf{I}
uniformly in
u\in\real^{d}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{v}}(v)

\displaystyle\preceq\;\frac{M}{Mb^{2}+a^{2}}\mathbf{I}\qquad\mbox{uniformly in $v\in\real^{d}$.}

(18b)

Let us now prove each of these two claims in turn.

Proof of the lower bound (
18a
):

Combining the representation of
\mathbf{J}_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}
in
equation (
9b
) with our assumed lower bound on

\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{u}}
, we have the uniform lower bound
\mathbf{J}_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}(u)\succeq\big(m+\tfrac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\big)\mathbf{I}
. Thus, the conditional
distribution
p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}
is strongly log-concave, so that the
Brascamp–Lieb inequality (cf.
Appendix

B
) is in force. It allows
us to argue that

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}(U\mid V=v)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\preceq}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}}[\big(\mathbf{J}_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}(U)\big)^{-1}\big]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\preceq}}\;\big(m+\tfrac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{I},

where step (i) follows from the bound (
41b
)
in
Appendix

B
; and step (ii) follows from the uniform lower bound on

\mathbf{J}_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}(u)
.

Consequently, we have the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\operatorname{cov}(U\mid V=v)

\displaystyle\succeq\Big(1-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\frac{1}{m+\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}}\Big)\mathbf{I}\;=\;\Big(\frac{mb^{2}}{mb^{2}+a^{2}}\Big)\mathbf{I}.

Combining with the second-order Tweedie formula (
9a
)
from
Lemma

1
, we have shown that
\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{v}}(v)\succeq\frac{m}{mb^{2}+a^{2}}\mathbf{I}
, as claimed.

Proof of the upper bound (
18b
):

Returning to the second-order Tweedie
representation (
9a
), we see that upper bounds on

\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{v}}
require lower bounds on the covariance matrix
\operatorname{cov}(U\mid V=v)
. By a suitable embedding of our model into a
parametric family, we can obtain such a lower bound via the
Cramer–Rao approach, as we now describe.

Fix a realization
v\in\real^{d}
, and for each vector
\theta\in\real^{d}
, define the shifted density
q_{\theta}(u)\coloneqq p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}(u-\theta)
. (To keep the notation clean, we are suppressing the
dependence on
v
, since it remains fixed throughout the argument.)
We can now apply the Cramer–Rao bound to the parametric family
\{q_{\theta}\mid\theta\in\real^{d}\}
. By construction, we have

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\log q_{\theta}(u)\Big|_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{2}_{u}\log p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}(u)\;=\;\mathbf{J}_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}(u).

Consequently, the Fisher information for estimating
\theta=0
is
given by
\mathbf{F}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{U\sim p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}}[\mathbf{J}_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}(U)]
. From
the representation (
9b
) of
\mathbf{J}_{\mathsf{u}\mid\mathsf{v}}
and our
assumed upper bound on
\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{u}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}}\Big[\mathbf{H}_{\mathsf{u}}(U)+\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\mathbf{I}\Big]\;\preceq\Big(M+\tfrac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\Big)\mathbf{I},

Inverting and negating the relation, we have
-\mathbf{F}^{-1}\preceq-\Big(M+\tfrac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\Big)^{-1}\mathbf{I}
.

Now observe that
\psi(u)=u
is an unbiased estimate of
\theta=0

in this model, so that the Cramer–Rao bound implies that
\operatorname{cov}(U\mid V=v)\succeq\mathbf{F}^{-1}
. Putting together the pieces, we
have

4
Multi-modal setting

We now turn to the more challenging setting of a general multi-modal
distribution. In this case, given some
\delta>0
that is
user-specified, our goal is to draw samples from the random vector
Z\coloneqq\widetilde{X}+\delta W_{0}
, where
W_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
. As in
diffusion analyses, we refer the parameter
\delta
as the early
stopping error. With this parameter fixed, our goal is to develop
algorithms that produce
\varepsilon
-accurate samples from
the distribution
p_{Z}
of
Z
.

In order to do so, it is convenient to work with the rescaled random
vector
Y_{1}=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{2}\,\delta}
. In the case of the KL
distance
D_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{757834}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\text{KL}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{757834}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\text{KL}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{672677}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\text{KL}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{672678}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\text{KL}$}}}}}}
, this rescaling has no effect on distances, whereas in
the case of the Wasserstein distance
\mathscr{W}_{2}
, a

\varepsilon
-accurate approximation to
Z
can be achieved by
obtaining a
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\varepsilon/\delta
accurate
approximation to
Y_{1}
.

Thus, we have reduced our problem to sampling from
p_{1}\equiv p_{Y_{1}}
. For the purposes of analysis, we note that
Y_{1}
can
be written as

\displaystyle Y_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}X+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}W_{0}

(22a)

where
X\coloneqq\widetilde{X}/\delta
is a rescaled version of the
original variable
\widetilde{X}
. In our analysis, we state conditions
directly on
X
. Starting with
Y_{1}
, we then evolve the sequence
forward as

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}=a_{k}Y_{k}+\sqrt{1-a_{k}^{2}}W_{k},

(22b)

where

are stepsizes to be chosen in an adaptive way.

4.1
Main guarantee

Our adaptive choice of stepsizes depends on the sequence

\displaystyle B_{k}\>\coloneqq\>\sup_{y\in\real^{d}}|\!|\!|\operatorname{cov}(X\mid Y_{k}=y)|\!|\!|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}},

(23)

defined for each
k=1,2,\ldots
in the forward trajectory. We
construct the forward sequence with a stepsize sequence
\{a_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
based on the initialization
a_{0}=1/\sqrt{2}
, and for
k=1,2,\ldots
, the updates

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\>\coloneqq\>4\,B_{k}\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}a_{\ell}^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad a_{k}^{2}=\frac{2\lambda_{k}+2}{2\lambda_{k}+3}.

(24a)

Our main result applies to a trajectory length
K
chosen to ensure
that

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=0}^{K-1}a_{\ell}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{8B_{K}}.

(24b)

As in the statement of
Theorem

1

\{s_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
be
any positive sequence such that
\sum_{k=0}^{K}s_{k}=1
.

Theorem 2

(SLC reduction for multi-modal case)
.

Given the stepsize choice (
24a
) and a trajectory

satisfying condition (
24b

(a)

The terminal stage marginal

is strongly log-concave

(b)

Each of the backward sampling problems

(c)

\varepsilon

\displaystyle\mbox{\bf{KL distance:}}\qquad T(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}{N}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{825862}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{825862}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{729956}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{729954}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{{SLC}}$}}}}}}\big(s_{k}\varepsilon\big),\qquad\mbox{and}

Focusing on the KL divergence guarantee (
25a
),
suppose that we choose the parameters

for all

,

4.1.1
Trajectory length under worst-case assumptions

We now turn to an analysis of the trajectory length
K
under
a particular worst-case assumption, namely that there is
some

such that

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle\leq B_{\max}\qquad\mbox{for all $k=1,2,\ldots$,}

(27)

but no further structure is given.

Corollary 1
.

Under the uniform bound (

), the guarantees
of

2
apply with trajectory length at most
K\leq 14\,B_{\max}
.

See
Section

4.4
for the proof.

Let us consider one concrete setting in which an uniform upper bound

B_{\max}
is immediate. Suppose that the original data vector
\widetilde{X}

has its support contained within the Euclidean ball

\|\widetilde{X}\|_{2}\leq R
.
By construction, the rescaled vector
X=\widetilde{X}/\delta
then satisfies the bound
\|X\|_{2}\leq R/\delta
,
from which it follows that the worst-case bound (
27
)
holds with
B_{\max}=R^{2}/\delta^{2}
. Substituting the upper bound
K\leq 14(R/\delta)^{2}
from

1
into our earlier
bound (

), we find a worst-case iteration
complexity scaling as

\displaystyle T_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1571141}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle worst$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1571141}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle worst$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1351222}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle worst$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{1351223}{327680}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle worst$}}}}}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{O}(\big(R/\delta)^{2}\sqrt{d}\log^{3}(1/\varepsilon)\Big).

(28)

This result can be directly compared to past
results
9
;
13
on discretized
diffusions; their bounds exhibit a quadratic dependence on a
smoothness condition, and
d/\varepsilon^{2}
dependence on
(d,\varepsilon)
, as opposed to
\sqrt{d}\log^{3}(1/\varepsilon)
in the
guarantee (

).

Open question:

State-of-the-art
results on discretized
diffusions

;
5
;
40

provide iteration complexities with

scaling,
inferior to the bound (
28
), but with a milder
poly-logarithmic dependence on quantities of the type

are thus led to a natural open question: is it possible to obtain a

(R/\delta)
and
(1/\epsilon)
? We suspect that
this improvement should be possible, using our modular scheme, by

exploiting additional structure of the conditional covariance process.

4.1.2
Other open questions and extensions

Our scheme and results suggest a variety of other open questions and
extensions. Let us comment on a few of them here.

Reductions beyond the SLC class:

Theorem

2
shows
how to construct a trajectory such that all sampling sub-problems are
strongly log-concave (SLC). Doing so allows us to exploit fast SLC
sampling algorithms. However, there are many other classes of
distributions for which fast samplers are available, including those
satisfying geometric relations such as log-Sobolev (LSI) or
Poincarë inequalities. For the instance, the class of LSI
distributions is a strict superset of the LSC class, allowing for
considerable multi-modality depending on the LSI constant. There are
various fast samplers available for LSI distributions
(e.g.,
54
;
15
;
2
), so that
the scope of our scheme could be enlarged substantially by instead
reducing to a sequence of well-conditioned LSI problems.

Milder dimension dependence:

An attractive feature of the general
reduction of
Theorem

2
is that it allows for the use of any
sampler for the SLC problems that define the backward trajectory. In
deriving concrete results thus far, we have assumed the use of
sampling routines with
\sqrt{d}
-dependence, so that our summary
results include this scaling. However, it is worth noting that there
are various first-order schemes with even milder dimension dependence
when additional structure is available. For instance, under a
higher-order smoothness condition, Mou et al.
43

exhibit a sampling algorithm, one that uses only first-order
information but makes use of a lifted form of dynamics, with
d^{1/4}

scaling. On the other hand, Chen and Gamirty
15
provide a
refined analysis of the MALA algorithm that yields square-root
dependence on the Hessian trace, as opposed to
\sqrt{d}
. Any of
these improved samplers can be used within our modular scheme, and it
inherits any improved dimension dependence in the final iteration
complexity.

Manifold and other low-dimensional structures:

As noted in
Section

1.2
, there is a rapidly evolving line of
work on diffusion sampling when the original distribution exhibits
some type of low-dimensional structure
(e.g.,
27
;
44
;
26
;
10
;
4
),
with recent results giving linear scaling in effective
dimension
45
;
35
. It would be
interesting to prove analogous results for our modular scheme, so as
to benefit both from the poly-logarithmic scaling in

(1/\varepsilon)
, and adaptivity to low-dimensional structure. For
our scheme, one key question to address is the iteration complexity of
samplers for SLC distributions obtained by annealed smoothing of a
low-dimensional distribution.

5
Discussion

In this paper, we have described a modular approach to sampling from a
given target distribution
{p_{\mathsf{x}}}
based on the availability of
annealed score functions. It can be understood as a form of
“divide-and-conquer”, showing how the original sampling problem is
reducible to a sequence of sub-problems, each defined by a
well-conditioned and strongly log-concave (SLC) distribution. Using
this reduction, we proved novel results both for uni-modal and
multi-modal distribution. For sampling from a SLC distribution
(
Theorem

1
), our results lead to logarithmic dependence on the
condition number
\kappa
, along with the usual
\sqrt{d}
dependence.
on the dimension. For multi-modal distributions
(
Theorem

2
), with a form of smoothness imposed, our scheme
leads to
\sqrt{d}\log^{3}(1/\varepsilon)
scalings to achieve accuracy

\varepsilon
, improving upon the best known results for diffusion
samplers.

Our work leaves open various questions and potential extensions.
First, the analysis of this paper is predicated upon knowledge of the
exact annealed score functions. It would provide useful guidance to
also track errors in estimated scores, as has been done for diffusion
samplers. Second, we derived one concrete result
(
Corollary

1
) for multi-modal sampling under a worst-case
assumption, with no further structure. In our analysis, this leads to
quadratic dependence on certain smoothness parameters, as opposed to a
more desirable logarithmic dependence obtained in recent work on
diffusion samplers; see the discussion following
Corollary

1

for more details. We suspect that it should be possible to reduce
this dependence, so as to obtain a “best-of-both-worlds” guarantee.

Lastly, there are a variety of possible refinements and extensions to
this work, as outlined in
Section

4.1.2
. The modular scheme in this
paper was based on reducing to a sequence SLC distributions; it could
be enriched by instead reducing to richer classes of distributions for
which fast schemes are available (e.g., those satisfying a log-Sobolev
inequality). Finally, we exhibited a
\sqrt{d}
-dependence in
general, but also see various ways in which it might be further
reduced, depending on the availability of additional structure to the
problem.

Appendix B
Elementary consequence of Brascamp–Lieb inequality

Consider a density on
d
of the form
p(x)\propto\exp(-\psi(x))
where
\psi
is twice differentiable and strictly
convex. The Brascamp–Lieb inequality asserts that for any
differentiable function
f:\real^{d}\rightarrow\real
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}_{p}(f(X))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{p}\Big[\left\langle\nabla f(X),\,(\nabla^{2}\psi(X))^{-1}\nabla f(X)\right\rangle\Big].

(41a)

Let us derive an elementary consequence that leads to an upper bound
on the matrix
\operatorname{cov}_{p}(X)
. For any given
v\in\real^{d}
, define
the linear function
f_{v}(x)=\left\langle v,\,x-\mu\right\rangle
, where
\mu\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[X]
. Computing the derivative
\nabla f_{v}(x)=v
and then
applying inequality (
41a
) yields the upper bound

\displaystyle v^{T}\operatorname{cov}_{p}(X)v\;=\;\operatorname{var}_{p}(f_{v}(X))\;\leq\;v^{T}\mathbb{E}_{p}\big[(\nabla^{2}\psi(X))^{-1}\big]v.

Since the choice of
v\in\real^{d}
was arbitrary, it follows that

\operatorname{cov}_{p}(X)\preceq\mathbb{E}_{p}\Big[(\nabla^{2}\psi(X))^{-1}\Big]
. In
particular, when
\nabla^{2}\psi(x)\succeq m\mathbf{I}
uniformly in
x
,
it follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}_{p}(X)

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{I}.

(41b)

Appendix C
Wasserstein stability of the backward kernel

Consider the Markov kernel
q\mapsto\mathcal{P}(q)(\cdot)\coloneqq\int_{\real{}^{d}}p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}(\cdot\mid v)q(v)dv
where, for some

\beta\in\real
, the conditional density takes the form

\displaystyle p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}(u\mid v)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\Big\{-\psi(u)+\beta\left\langle u,\,v\right\rangle\Big\},

(42a)

and the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\psi
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\psi(u)

\displaystyle\succeq\alpha\mathbf{I}\qquad\mbox{uniformly
over $u\in\real^{d}$.}

(42b)

Lemma 8

(Wasserstein stability of the backward kernel)
.

Under the above conditions, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{2}\big(\mathcal{P}(q),\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{q})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\beta|}{\alpha}\,\mathscr{W}_{2}(q,\widetilde{q})\qquad\mbox{valid for
any pair of distributions $q,\widetilde{q}$.}

(43)

Proof.

The Wasserstein distance is defined via an infimum over couplings;
thus, by a standard “gluing” argument, it is sufficient to show that

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{2}\big(p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}(\cdot\mid v),\;p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}(\cdot\mid\widetilde{v})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\beta|}{\alpha}\;\|v-\widetilde{v}\|_{2}\qquad\mbox{for all $v,\widetilde{v}\in\real^{d}$.}

(44a)

In order to do so, it is convenient to introduce the
d
-dimensional
exponential family

\displaystyle r_{\theta}(u)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exp\big\{\left\langle\theta,\,u\right\rangle-\psi(u)-A(\theta)\big\}

where
A(\theta)=\log\int e^{\left\langle\theta,\,u\right\rangle-\psi(u)}du
is
the log normalization constant. By construction, for each
v\in\real^{d}
, we have
p_{\mathsf{u\mid v}}(u\mid v)\equiv r_{\theta(v)}(u)
where

\theta(v)\coloneqq\beta v
. Consequently, in order to prove the
bound (
44a
), it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{2}\big(r_{\theta},r_{\widetilde{\theta}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\|_{2}\qquad\mbox{for each
$\theta,\widetilde{\theta}$.}

(44b)

Since
\nabla^{2}\psi(u)\succeq\alpha\mathbf{I}
uniformly in
u\in\real^{d}
, the density
r_{\widetilde{\theta}}
is

\alpha
-strongly-log-concave. Hence, Talagrand’s
T_{2}
-bound
holds. (In particular, the Bakry–Emery criterion implies that

r_{\widetilde{\theta}}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality (LSI) with
parameter
2/\alpha
, and the Otto–Villani translation from LSI
to
T_{2}
then implies that
r_{\widetilde{\theta}}
satisfies Talagrand’s

T_{2}
-inequality with parameter
2/\alpha
. See Corollary 7.3
and Theorem 8.12 in the survey paper
32
for
details.) Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}^{2}_{2}(r_{\theta},r_{\widetilde{\theta}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}D_{\mbox{\tiny{KL}}}(r_{\theta}\|r_{\widetilde{\theta}}),

(45a)

where
D_{\mbox{\tiny{KL}}}
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between

r_{\theta}
and
r_{\widetilde{\theta}}
. From the exponential family
structure
56
, we have
D_{\mbox{\tiny{KL}}}(r_{\theta}\|r_{\widetilde{\theta}})=A(\widetilde{\theta})-A(\theta)-\left\langle\nabla A(\theta),\,\widetilde{\theta}-\theta\right\rangle
. Taking one more derivative, we can write

\displaystyle D_{\mbox{\tiny{KL}}}(r_{\theta}\|r_{\widetilde{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{\theta}-\theta)^{\top}\nabla^{2}A(\bar{\theta})(\widetilde{\theta}-\theta),

(45b)

where
\bar{\theta}
is some vector on the line joining
\theta
and

\widetilde{\theta}
. Thus, it remains to bound the Hessian
\nabla^{2}A
.
Again using standard properties of exponential
families
56
, we have
\nabla^{2}A(\bar{\theta})=\operatorname{cov}_{\bar{\theta}}(U)
, where
\operatorname{cov}_{\bar{\theta}}
denotes the covariance
computed under the exponential family density
r_{\bar{\theta}}
. Since

r_{\bar{\theta}}
is defined by a potential function that is

\alpha
-strongly-convex, the Brascamp–Lieb inequality (see
equation (
41b
) in
Appendix

B
) can be applied to assert
that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}A(\bar{\theta})=\operatorname{cov}_{\bar{\theta}}(U)

\displaystyle\;\preceq\;\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{I}\;\qquad\mbox{uniformly for all
$\bar{\theta}$.}

(45c)

Applying the Brascamp–Lieb bound (
45c
) to
equation (
45b
), we find that the KL can be upper bounded as

\operatorname{KL}(r_{\theta}\mid r_{\widetilde{\theta}})\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}
. Combining with our

T_{2}
-bound (
45a
), we find that

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}^{2}_{2}(r_{\theta},r_{\widetilde{\theta}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}D_{\mbox{\tiny{KL}}}(r_{\theta}\|r_{\widetilde{\theta}})\;\leq\;\frac{2}{\alpha}\;\Biggr\{\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}\Biggr\}\;=\;\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}.

Taking square roots, we have proved the desired
bound (
44b
).
∎
