Regulation of aluminum induced oxidative stress in Brassica via roles of sulfur metabolites and antioxidant responses mediated by a melatonin selenium nanocomposite
Authors: Nilakshi Chakraborty, Sayan Pal, Abir Das, Soumita Roy, Malay Kumar Adak, Mohammad Reza Fayezizadeh, Mirza Hasanuzzaman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMelatonin (MT) and selenium (Se) are quite known in application in regulation of abiotic stress tolerance where metals remain important. Undoubtedly, MT being a signalling molecule and Se, a multivalent metalloid have been effective in metal stress alleviation individually, however, a limitation exists for insights in synergistic application. Thus, we formulated a special MT-dopped nano-selenium (SeNP-MT) against aluminum (Al) toxicity through oxidative stress is Brassica. This is more specified with illustration of Brassica seedlings where molecular and physiological regulation against Al stress remain less explored with exogenous application of SeNP-MT. Therefore, this study addresses the knowledge gap over the existing insights of nano-composites with special reference to integrated approaches of SeNP-MT that may modulate the regulatory pathways of Al tolerance.ResultsThe synthesis of SeNP-MT was verified through opto-physical characteristics covering sizes, crystallinity, amorphous and repulsive nature, variations in functional bonds on surface. The investigation revealed that under 500 µM aluminum chloride (AlCl3) stress caused significant cellular lysis of root tissues. This was accompanied by the reduction of plant growth when plants were treated with varying doses of SeNP-MT1-3 (0.02—0.1 gL−1). The application of SeNP-MT2 showed an improvement in relative growth rate (RGR) (172.72%) along with minimization of Al uptake (21.87%). Similarly, tissue specific distribution of reactive oxygen species (ROS), chlorophyll fluorescence, photosystem II photochemical activities and malate decarboxylation reactions were regulated by SeNP-MT. Maximum accumulation of total sulfur (165.17%), cysteine (104.54%), non-protein thiol (22.50%), and phytochelatin (5.13%) content was maximum under SeNP-MT2 under Al stress. Additionally, SeNP-MT1-3 induced antioxidation pathways as recoded with increased glutathione peroxidase (GPX) and catalase (CAT) activities whereas maximum ratio (20.40%) of ascorbate to dehydro-ascorbate under SeNP-MT2 was observed. The genotoxicity tolerance was improved by modulating the loss of DNA studied through comet assay and related parameters.ConclusionThis study is scientific document which critically bridges the knowledge gap between individual effects of Se and MT and their synergistic impacts in a nanocomposite. The key regulatory pathways were found more effective under SeNP-MT application to mitigate the phytotoxicity of Al with Brassica and thereby establishes an emerging and bio-compatible nano-assisted tool for stress tolerance. The key findings in the present experiment emphasised that genomic stability, improved organic acid turnover are two essential pathways to regulate Al detoxification and bio-exclusion respectively. Thereby, the multifaceted activities underscore the efficiency of SeNP-MT might intervein Al tolerance in species that develops a biocompatible strategy for crop cultivation in acid rich Al polluted soil. Overall, the key findings emphasised the potential of nano augmented MT-Se formulation may open the unexplored pathways of Al tolerance at molecular level imparting resilience to other stresses also.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07951-3.Keywords: Phytochelatin, Malate metabolism, Comet assay, Nanocomposite, ROS

Introduction

The cultivation of the crops in acid soil typically suffers from a pH stress. In such condition Al accounts nearly 1/3rd in abundance and represents 30 to 40% of earth crust mostly in oxides and silicates with significant impediment of crop growth and productivity. The toxicity of Al salts is based on a decreased soil pH (≤ 5) that simultaneously dominates proton (H+) stress also [1]. Accumulation of Al mostly in trivalent (Al3+) form reports to be most vulnerable for suppression of cell division, elongation and viabilities of roots. The downstream toxicity extends into the elongated root apices, resulting in the loss of root hairs and consequently hindering ion absorption [2]. At the cellular level, Al3+ toxicity may progress to the nucleus involving interactions with the plasma membrane, nucleic acids, histidine, and other nucleophilic residues within the cytoskeleton, which are key to its phytotoxic effects [3]. Covalent interactions between Al3+ and membrane components can distort ion equilibrium, inhibit signalling pathways mediated by Ca2+-binding proteins, and induce cytoskeletal disorganization. In physiological response, a significant exudation of acids and phenolics immobilize the ions through external or internal bio-sequestration [4]. Ultimately, these responses are manifested at the cellular level through the activation of antioxidative pathways, which counteract the oxidative stress in root cells [5]. Thus, Al3+ induced antioxidation pathways cover the protection of membrane and organelle protein, photosystem II (PSII), pigments, lipids residues from oxidative stress. Different crop species show varying sensitivity to oxidative stress induced by Al3+ toxicity. This variation is due to genotypic plasticity as revealed through the upregulation of antioxidative enzyme activities and the accumulation of antioxidants. This mechanism helps to protect against ROS-induced damages with exercising antioxidants including several important residues mostly phenolics (e.g. flavonoids, iso-flavonoids), carotenoids (e.g. lutein, zeaxanthin) [6].

The Al tolerance in plants may be improved by improvisation of signals from rhizosphere into cellular responses by exogenous application of chemical residues. Likewise, melatonin (MT) is chemically N-acetyl 5-methoxytryptamine shows a better impact on seed germination, photosynthetic membrane activity, osmotic adjustment, retardation of senescence, redox responses concerning abiotic stress tolerance [7]. Exogenous application of MT effects in reversal of drought, salt, metal, oxidative exposure [8]. In response to metal stress, MT plays key role in the internal sequestration of ions, the formation of phytochelatin complexes, and the enhancement of antioxidative activities [9]. In rice, the expression of biosynthetic genes: tryptamine 5 hydroxylase (T5H), tryptophan decarboxylase are over expressed on heavy metal stress to secure MT biosynthesis [10]. Therefore, MT happens to be an important supplementation in tolerance to metal induced oxidative stress allied to specific ion toxicity. Different combinations of MT and other metal/metalloid-based NPs widen the genotypic plasticity of Brassica against abiotic stresses. Likewise, Brassica boosted an extended antioxidation cascades under application of MT and zinc oxide NPs combination [11]. The melatonin-nanocomposite (MT-NP) effectively controls the oxidative degeneration against heavy metals covering cobalt (Co), cadmium (Cd), arsenic (As) along with minimized bioaccumulation [12]. Moreover, exogenous application with MT-ZnO-NP composite on seeds recorded satisfactory improvement on photosynthetic efficiencies, upregulation of ROS resistance genes in Brassica establishing the synergistic modalities in application exceeding their individual effects. Therefore, the validity of interaction for NPs and MT would be also more imperative to justify plant responses under metal stress to modulate the tolerance.

It is quite interesting to trace the relationship between Se and sulfur owing to their chemical similarity in synthesis of major antioxidants like non-thiol residues. Now, under Al stress, Se also enhances the activity of antioxidizing enzymes and overall sulfur reduction pathways aiding ROS neutralization. On the other hand, MT boosts sulfur metabolism also, where enzymes like ATP sulforylase are induced leading GSH production [13]. Thus, it is relevant to enquire the relationship between SeNP-MT composite with regards to Se and sulfur metabolism to favour the plants in stress tolerance with reference to genomic stability, acid exudation, bio-sequestering of Al and antioxidative resistances.

Meanwhile, application of MT is widened when some conjugates are used that may augment the stress tolerance. As reported earlier, the composites of NPs with binding agents like chitosan, small lipid residues, growth regulators are used to enhance the antioxidation activity [14]. For instance, copper NP dopped chitosan and gibberellin-chitosan composite positively affected root biomass, seed germination, and water conductivity [15]. The solubility of MT in aqueous and lipids is higher contributing better translocation over the membrane when conjugated with polar substances. This inspires the use of Se to improve the effectivity of MT for antioxidation, solubility, adsorption capacity over cellular membrane as already reported [16]. Moreover, Se in nano form (SeNP) reported to improve water stress tolerance by induction of thiol metabolism when composited with nitric oxide [17]. It is assumed that SeNP with its interconvertible forms (Se3+ to Se0+ by oxidation) may enhance the antioxidation activity. This occurs when MT binds with Se. Thus, understanding the beneficial impacts of SeNP and MT composite (i.e. SeNP-MT) may be worth of improved tolerance in crops. In this study, we synthesized SeNP-MT, characterized its opto-physical properties, and applied the formulation to Brassica seedlings subjected to Al stress. Brassica is most economically important oil crop, moderately grown under acid soil, where Al3+ toxicity is predominant reducing growth and yield [18]. Understanding the establish antioxidative roles of MT and SeNP, we have exercised the SeNP-MT for expected protection against oxidative stress. We hypothesized that SeNP-MT may confer protective roles in Brassica in complementation of Al signaling and antioxidation. The accumulation of sulfur-containing compounds, such as glutathione serving as a key mechanism for plants to adapt and overcome environmental stresses like heavy metal toxicity [19]. We evaluated the plant tolerance by assuming MT bio-accumulation, sulfur and malate metabolism, reduced nuclei damage and cell viability. We also examined the photosynthetic and related physiological activities induced under Al stress. Both Se and sulfur share common properties by which those are assumed to be compatible in reactions, particularly, occurring in metabolism. Primarily, plasma membrane bearing some specific channels co-transporting sulfur and Se to enrich sulfur rich residues [20]. Sulfur and Se are regarded as chalcogens as those occur in the same group of periodic tables having non-metal or metalloid in nature and chemical similarities with 6 valance electrons [21]. Importantly, sulfur nutrition in plants under oxidative stress is materialized with selenocysteine, selenomethionin like residues where incorporation of Se into amino acids escalates the antioxidation [22]. With earlier background, role of hormones, growth regulators, and nano entities individually against metal stress, the present study seems to be unique for a detailed exploration of melatonin functionalized Se nano entities (SeNP-MT) against Al stress. Despite of the promising effects of MT and Se nanoparticles on mitigating stress in plants, their combined application and mechanism under Al stress remains underexplored. This study aims to assess the potentials of SeNP-MT in amelioration of Al3+ induced phytotoxicity in Brassica with special reference to sulfur metabolism, oxidative revelation, genome stability and metal bio-exclusion. Secondarily, the study fills the research gap by evaluating the synergistic effects of SeNP-MT composites over individual effects in modulation of stress tolerance for crops under acid rich Al soil.

Materials and methods

Seed germination, development of seedling and chemical treatments

Viable seeds of Brassica juncea (L.) Czern. cv. Kranti were collected from agricultural farm of the university, sterilized with 0.1% sodium hypochlorite solution following repeated washing and germinated on Petri plates in seed germinator (BTR Kolkata, LAB-X). Following germination, seedlings were uniformly distributed in pots (10 cm2 dia) in the Murashige and Skoog media (MS media) filled with cocopeat soil and vermicompost (8:2 w/w) with aeration for proper growth up to 15 days. To induce Al stress, 500 µM of AlCl3 was added to 1/4th strength nutrient solution. Treatment beyond 500 µM of AlCl3 shows an inhibitory effect in root growth and relative water content (RWC) to fix as threshold concentration with de-folding of maximum tolerance (Fig. S1, S2). Likewise, sensitivity of MT was adopted as 100 μM as optimum from a preliminary observation (data not shown) and reported earlier [23]. For Se also we have chosen the best concentration 20 ppm as monitored form the two major parameters (chlorophyll content and relative growth rate) exhibited by the plants (Fig. S3, S4) as well as from earlier reports. [24]. For SeNP-MT composite, we have selected three doses 0.02, 0.05 and 0.1 gL−1, however, beyond the maximum plant showed significant mortality. Therefore, for the core experiment, we have utilised within 0.02–0.1 gL−1 [25].

Thus, 21 days old plants were transferred to treatment sets formulated accordingly: control (0 µM Al), Al stress (500 µM), Al + MT (500 µM + 100 µM), Al + Se (500 µM + 20 µM), Al + SeNP-MT1 (0.02 gL−1), Al + SeNP-MT2 (0.05 gL−1), Al + SeNP-MT3 (0.1 gL−1) supplemented with 4.0 mM calcium chloride (pH 4.5). The treatment sets were replicated thrice with randomized block design containing 15 seedlings in each under laboratory conditions [photoperiod (16D/8 N), illumination (≈1100 µE m−2 s−1), temperature (27 ± 1ᴼC), and relative humidity (40%)] and continued for 3 weeks. The renewal of solution was done in every alternate day to maintain original concentration. On completion, all plants were thoroughly washed with sterile water, measured biomass and immediately stored at −80ᴼC for enzymatic and biochemical assays. For metal quantification, samples were dried in oven to constant weight and grinded in fine powder.

Synthesis of SeNP-MT composite and characterization

This method presents the synthesis of composite by chemical reduction method where 100 µM MT added to 20 µM sodium salt of selenate (Na2O4Se) in a mixture with constant staring. After half an hour with formation of selenious acid, thoroughly we added 45 mM ascorbic acid drop wise to initiate the redox reaction under same condition. The mixture was turned into orange red by minimum addition of deionized water recorded with pH 6.2 after 12 h incubation. The particles were recovered by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min room temperature following freeze drying (Buchi, Lyovapor™ L-200). Initially, SeNP-MT was evaluated by reading absorption maxima (λmax) (200–800 nm) by UV–vis spectrophotometry (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan, UV-1800). The crystallinity and shape of particles were determined with X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Ultima diffractometer, Japan) following standard derivation (d = Kλ/ß cosθ) [26]. The surface charges and the distribution in suspension of the particles were determined by measuring zeta potential (mV) within (−200 to 200 mV) adopting dynamic light scattering (DLS; Nano-Zetasizer, Malvern). The synthesized particles were analyzed for 3-D morphological shape, topography, porosity by atomic force microscopy (AFM). The purified particles after drop-casting on glass were completely dried at room temperature, and then AFM micrographs were taken under frequency (300 kHz) in multimode SPM [27]. Purified SeNP-MT particles were determined for biomolecules on surface of SeNP when fabricated with MT as well as stabilization of composites. The Fourier transformation infrared (FTIR) spectra from Na2O4Se (bulk precursor), MT and SeNP-MT composite was computed within the wavelength 4000–400 cm−1 in a dry pellet of potassium bromide solution using infrared spectrophotometer (FTIR, Bruker, Germany). The elemental mapping in the SeNP-MT was determined using Energy Dispersive X-Ray Analysis with predominant composition of Se and other elements like sodium (Na), oxygen (O), potassium (K) as shown the particles on carbon-coated copper grids (CARL ZEISS, USA).

Growth analysis, relative water content and metal bio-accumulation

Plant samples (5 g) from each treatment was dissected into roots and shoots, completely dried in oven till constant weight. The distribution of the dry matter into plant parts and relative growth rate (RGR) were measured according to [28].

RGR = ​ln (W2​) − ln (W1​)​/T2​ − T1 [where, W2 = Final plant dry biomass at time T2, and W1 = Final plant dry biomass at time T1].

For root length analysis, seedlings were randomly selected and washed. Then precise measurements of roots were taken and computed accordingly. Growth analysis like RGR were computed dry weight basis [29].

Relative water content (RWC) was measured using the following formulae taking 2.0 g of fresh tissue from different replications under treatments. [30].

The plant tissue (1.0 g) was grinded into fine powder and digested in tri-acid mixture (HCl: H2SO4: HClO4 = 1:1:1) on constant boiling until transparent in color and filtered (Whatman 42). The concentration of Al in the solution was determined by appropriate dilution with Milli-Q water following atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS, AAnalyst 800) according to [31].

Determination of chlorophyll content and fluorescence, PSII photochemical activity, NADP-ME activity and polymorphic variation

For chlorophyll fluorescence, 100 mg fresh leaf tissue was thoroughly macerated under cold condition and extracted with 80% acetone. The supernatant was collected from centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min and read the fluorescence energy at excitation (440 nm) and emission (640 nm) by spectrofluorometer (Hitachi F-7100) according to [32]. The chlorophyll content was also estimated adopting the equation referred by [33].

Activity of PSII photochemical reaction was assayed following the extraction of chloroplast from uniform leaf discs (20 cm diameter) in isolation buffer: 10 mM sodium chloride (NaCl), 10% sucrose, 5% glycerol (v/v), 5 mM magnesium chloride (MgCl2). The supernatant was saved and diluted with 15 mM sorbitol containing the same buffer and centrifuge at 10,000 × g, 4οC for 15 min. The pellet was resuspended again in 15 mM sorbitol and stored under ice cold condition. The photochemical activity of PSII was determined incubating the suspension of chloroplast (10 mg/L) in assay mixture containing 1.0 mM 2,6-Dichlorophenolindophenol (DCPIP) and transferred to illumination (1000–1200 E m−2 s−1) for 2 min. Another set was prepared with the same combination of reagents, however, kept under complete dark as used as blank. Reading the absorbance at 580 nm, the activity of PSII was computed [34] and expressed as µg DCPIP reduced chl−1 h−1.

The activity and assay of NADP-ME (EC 1.1.1.40) was done [28]. The uniform leaf discs from plants under different treatments were illuminated for 1 h and thoroughly macerated into fine powder. The extraction was done in a buffer [1.0 mm ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.3), 100 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1.0 mM phenyl methyl sulfonyl fluoride, 0.1 mM protease inhibitor cocktail, 2% glycerol, 25 mM MgCl2, 10 mM dipotassium hydrogen phosphate (K2HPO4)] and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min, 4οC. The supernatant was thoroughly washed in 10 mM K2HPO4 and incubated in an assay buffer (1 ml): 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM NADP-sodium salt, 10 mM EDTA, 4.0 mM L-malate. The whole sets were incubated under illumination after immediate adding of 100 μg protein from extract and read the absorbance (340 nm) with 30 s interval through 3 min. The activity was expressed as nmol NADP(H) produced min/mg protein with bovine serum albumin as standard.

For in-vivo detection, same enzyme extract run in a native PAGE with 15 mA, 70 V for separation of the isoforms. Then the gel incubated in a mixture containing 50 mM Tris–HCL (pH 7.5), 0.55 mg/ml NBT, 0.5 m nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP), 2 mM MnCl2, 18 mM malate and 0.1 mg/ml phenazine methosulfate (PMS) for 2 h. Then 5% acetic acid solution was added to fix the band to resolve the bands followed by analysis of band intensities through Gel Analyzer software.

Histochemical detection of ROS, callose deposition and aluminum bio-accumulation

Roots of seedlings under each treatment was carefully dissected and thoroughly washed with Milli-Q water to eliminate Al deposits on surface. For detection of the ROS, root samples were completely suspended in 0.1% (w/v) 2',7'-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA, Sigma Aldrich) solution under dark for 15 min. Repeated washing was done with 1X phosphate saline buffer (PBS) to release out excess stain. The images of the stained roots were captured mounting on glass slides with fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss™ microscope) using excitation and emission wavelengths at 485 nm and 530 nm respectively [35]. For callose deposition in root, roots were stained with 1% (w/v) aniline blue, then washed in Sorensen’s phosphate buffer and observed at light intensity (5 µM photons m−2 s−1) at 358 nm excitation and 460 nm emission (Carl Zeiss™ epifluorescence microscope) [36].

For Al bio-accumulation, specific staining was used where 0.2% (v/v) solution of Hematoxylin in 0.02% potassium iodide (w/v) solution, pH 4.2 was used. Root apex was stained in the solution for 30 min and observed with stereoscopic microscope [37].

Determination of superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, root membrane injury and NADP(H)-oxidase

Superoxide (O2•−) content of the tissue was determined from plant tissue homogenized in 65 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) and supernatant were collected by 8,000 × g for 30 min, 4οC. The aliquot was added with a mixture containing 10 mM hydroxyl amine in 65 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.8). On incubation at room temperature, the solution was reacted with assay mixture (10 mM sulphanilamide with 7 mM α-napthyl amine) and kept under dark for 1 h. The absorbance was read at 530 nm in with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Cecil, CE7200) against a standard solution of potassium nitrite to determine the O2• − content [38]. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the tissue was extracted by extraction buffer made with 1% (W/V) trichloro acetic acid (TCA) solution. The supernatant was collected from centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and 0.1 ml of aliquot was reacted with assay buffer containing 1 mM potassium iodide in 10 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.5). On dark incubation the absorbance was read at 390 nm using 30% (v/v) aqueous H2O2 and the concentration as determined using the molar extinction coefficient (1.75 × 105 L·mol⁻1·cm⁻1) according to [39]. Evans blue staining was used to assess root membrane injury, as described by [40]. The activity of NADP(H) oxidase (NOX) (EC 1.6.3.1) through in gel staining was determined according to [41]. The plant extract was done in an ice-cold buffer containing 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) containing 0.1% triton X, 0.2 mM EDTA, 1% (W/V) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), 1% leupeptine, 0.1% DTT, 1 mM proteinase K, 5 mM ascorbate and centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4οC for 20 min. The supernatant was saved and partially purified by 30% ammonium sulfate cut followed by dissolving in the same buffer in minimum volume with 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and quantified using Bradford reagent. In an assay mixture 100 µg protein was incubated in a 1 ml solution containing 5 mM manganese chloride or MnCl2, 0.1 mm DTT, 0.1 mM p-coumaric acid, 0.05 mM NADP(H), 30 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 7.5) and incubated under dark. The absorbance was read at 340 nm using a blank solution excluding the protein and activity was determined using molar extinction coefficient of NADP(H) as 6.22 × 103 L·mol⁻1·cm.

Histochemical detection of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide

In vivo detection of tissue specific ROS (O2• − and H2O2) on leaves through infiltration [37]. Leaf sheaths from each treatment were incubated in 5 mM DAB, pH 6.8 under vacuum overnight at dark. The sheaths were dechlorophyllized with a solution of glycerol: ethanol Lactic acid: (4:1:1- v/v/v) under gentle warming and images was captured. For detection of O2•−, the sheaths were incubated in 6 mM NBT dissolved in PBS (pH 4.8) for infiltration. Leaves were made transparent and formazan complex as dark blue spots was detected and captured [42].

Assay of antioxidative enzymes, antioxidants, malondialdehyde and polymorphic expression of proteins

The assay of antioxidative enzymes as guaiacol peroxidase (GPX, E.C. 1.11.1.7.) and catalase (CAT, E.C. 1.11.1.6.) was done according to [43]. The fresh tissue was thoroughly homogenised in liquid nitrogen following extraction in ice-cold buffer containing100 mM Tris–Cl (pH 7.8) buffer containing 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM PMSF, 10 mM DTT, 100 mM EDTA, and 2% PVP, 0.1% protease inhibitor. The supernatant was collected from centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C and protein was quantified with Bradford reagent. For assay of GPX, crude enzyme protein (100 µg equivalent) was incubated in assay mixture containing 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), 0.5% H2O2 and 0.5 mM ortho-dianisidine. Change of absorbance (470 nm) was read through 15 min before plateau and from Δt value, the activity was calculated using molar extinction coefficient (H2O2: 0.485) according to [44]. Separation of the isozymic enzymes was done running the protein on a 1.2% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel in running buffer under 1 V/lane within electric field. The bands of isozymic bands were resolved staining in a solution containing 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer, 1.0 mg/ml o-dianisidine (Sigma Aldrich) in methanol and varying concentration (1–3 mM) of 30% v/v H2O2 solution under dark. For the CAT activity in vitro the same extraction buffer weas used and 100 µg protein was incubated in assay mixture in 10 mM H2O2 in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The activity was determined from the absorbance (240 nm) using the extinction coefficient of H2O2 (0.036 mM−1 cm−1). For isozymic detection, the partially purified protein was loaded in 12% non-denaturing gel and electrophoresed in 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 6.8) under 1 V/lane against the tracking dye as bromophenol blue. The intensity of the polypeptide bands was developed immersing the gel in a staining solution of same buffer mixed with 1% (w/v) ferric chloride and potassium ferricyanide (1:2) for 10 min under dark. Reaction was stopped with addition of dilute HCl on the gel and excess stain was removed with deionized water [45]. The concentration of ascorbic acid (AsA) was determined from extracted fresh sample in 6% (w/v) trichloro acetic acid (TCA) solution and the supernatant was recovered by centrifugation 14,000 × g for 15 min, 4οC. For AsA, the supernatant was diluted by 250 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and mixed with 0.5% N-ethyl maleimide (NEM). Deproteinization of the solution was done by adding 10% TCA, 44% H3PO4, 3% FeCl3, 4% 2,2 ′-dipyridyl in 70% ethanol. The whole mixture was incubated at 37οC temperature for 1 h and read the absorbance at 525 nm. In a different aliquot, the same extract was initially added with 200 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and 200 μL 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) to reduce all dehydroascorbate (DHA) into AsA and continued the same reaction as mentioned. The concentration of AsA was determined using a standard curve and the value was subtracted to deduce the DHA content [28]. Lipid peroxidation through accumulation of malondialdehyde (MDA) was determined by homogenizing the tissue in ice-cold mixture of 20% (v/v) TCA containing 0.5% thio barbituric acid (TBA) (1:1). The mixture was thoroughly shaken under 90οC for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min, 4οC. The absorbance of the supernatant was read at 532 and 600 nm to determine the concentration of MDA [46].

Detection of DNA loss from nuclei

Nuclear membrane lysis following DNA loss was determined on isolated nuclei from the root tissue [47]. Nuclear suspension was prepared from chopped root sample in phosphate buffer (pH 7) on ice. Grease free slides were precoated with 0.5% low melting agarose with successive coating on top by 0.6% agarose embedded with nuclei suspension. The slides were incubated under alkaline solution of 250 mM sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and equilibrated with 1 mM EDTA (pH ≥ 12.0). In an alkaline electrophoresis system, the slide was placed under 0.80 V cm−1 for 45 min under cold condition. Following this slide were transferred in 0.4 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, semi dried under aseptic condition and stained with 0.1% (w/v) ethidium bromide (EtBr) solution. Finally, the nuclei were observed through detection of EtBr fluorescence under excitation (480 nm) and emission (550 nm) by fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss™, Germany). Different quantitative analysis to detect the variables of DNA loss was done through Comet Score software.

Root topography by scanning electron micrography

Fresh root segments (1 cm) from each treatment were fixed with 1 M PBS (pH 7.3) containing 2.5% (w/v) glutaraldehyde for 1 day. The samples were carefully rinsed with 1X PBS and completely dehydrated through graded solution of ethyl alcohol containing hexamethyldisilazane (Sigma-Aldrich). The specimen was coated within gold layer, mounted and observed through scanning electron microscopy (Carl ZEISS EVO 15).

Assay of sulfur metabolites

Total sulfur content of the plant tissue was determined by gravimetric method from the dried sample. 10 g of plant was completely dried, ground into fine powder, dissolved in deionized water and neutralized with dilute HCl. The solution was filtered and reacted with saturated solution of barium chloride (BaCl2). The suspension was filtered through Whatman 42 and the filtrate wad checked with excess BaCl2. The precipitate on filtrate as barium sulfate was repeatedly washed with hot water and air dried. The precipitate on paper was burned under 550οC in a crucible to make complete ash. From the weights taken before and after ash development of the precipitate was used to determine the sulfur content and expressed on dry weight basis [48]. For cysteine content, 0.5 g of fresh tissue was thoroughly homogenized in liquid nitrogen and extracted with 5% (w/v) perchloric acid under cold condition and centrifuged at 12,000 × g, 15 min. The supernatant was reacted with 10 mM acid ninhydrin solution dissolved in 80% ethanol and 0.2 mM citrate buffer (pH 5.0) using the diluent solution (water: n-propanol = 1:1). The mixture was boiled for 20 min, cooled and absorbance was read at 570 nm. Using, a standard solution of L-cysteine content was determined and expressed in nmol g−1 fresh weight [49]. For non-protein thiol (NPTs) content, the fresh plant sample was extracted with 5% sulfosalicylic acid and the supernatant was saved form 10,000 × g, 15 min, 4 οC. The supernatant was reacted with 10 mM dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) in 0.2 M Tris–HCl (pH 8.2). On incubation at 37οC for 30 min the reaction mixture was read the absorbance at 412 nm [50]. The fresh sample was extracted from 10% TCA solution, centrifuged 10,000 × g, for 10 min under cold condition and the supernatant was incubated in an assay mixture [0.2 M phosphate buffer, 15 mM dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid)] and diluted with water for 30 min. The clear supernatant was read the absorbance (412 nm) to determine the concentration of GSH against a diluted standard (0.1–1 μM). Now, the content of phytochelatin (PC) was determined from the concentration of NPTs subtracted by GSH (i.e. NPT – GSH) and expressed on fresh weight basis as (nmol g−1) [51].

Statistical analysis

The data on each observation were represented from average ± SE, where (n = 3) in different replicated treatments with independent sampling. One was analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey test recorded significant variation were computed. Pearson’s correlation matrix was constructed at significant level (p ≤ 0.05) through SPSS software (IBM version 26).

Results

Opto-physical characterization of selenium melatonin nanocomposite and evaluation

On chemical reduction by ascorbic acid in aqueous solution of sodium selenate and MT, the synthesis of SeNP-MT was done and confirmed with different opto-physical characterization. On initial observation with synthesis of SeNP-MT was determined through the colour changes into brick red [selenate (Se4+) reduced to selenium (Se0)] in colloidal dispersal. From absorption maximum (λmax) at 270 nm by sharp peak indicating presence of SeNP in the complex that was further characterized (Fig. 1A). Hydrodynamic volume taking the core substance as Se in the composite revealed the particle size by dynamic light scattering (DLS). Likewise, zeta-potential calculated with satisfactory value (−19.07 mV) indicated better stabilization of the composite within colloidal suspension (Fig. 1C). The homogeneity of the particles in the solution was determined by polydispersity index (PDI: 0.2388) within the average size (119.1 nm) (Fig. 1B). The particles were homogenous, mono-dispersive and almost uniform in size. More analysis of the particles revealed their crystalline shape as detected from XRD with peak (22.06ο, 24.25ο, 31.75ο, and 37.77ο) correspondingly revision plane for Se. Whereas peaks 2θ = 24.25ο, 25.08ο, and 26.15ο were markedly characterized for MT, for SeNP-MT new peaks developed at 23.54ο, 29.75ο, 31.58ο, and 43.72ο which indicates the composite moiety would be in crystalline lattice formation where ionic interaction would be predominant (Fig. 2D, E, F). Discussion

Formulation of SeNP-MT qualifies for nanocomposite with good stability and surface plasmon response

Advances in nano-biotechnology has been well adopted for abiotic stress alleviation in crop species through modulation of physiological responses. Similarly, the present experiment evaluates the potential of SeNP-MT to induce physiological and cellular responses in Brassica that helps to mitigate Al stress in different angles. In the present study, formulations of SeNP-MT synthesized using MT and reduced Se (i.e. Se0) has also been reported elsewhere. This formulation has direct interactions with the cellular redox under stress but not with Al like toxic metal [52]. Such complexes have already been thoroughly characterized with regards to impact on redox interaction under environmental stresses. Thus, the influence on Al toxicity would be more valid herein with regards to any chemical ligands (i.e. SeNP-MT). This special formulation falls within optimum size, distribution within matrix, absorption maximum (λmax), specific surface bound charges, stability and non-dispersiveness, and relevant functional groups. All of these constituted a potential complex capable of adhering onto cell wall [53]. More importantly, the occurrence of secondary or tertiary amino groups and stretching would be responsible for strong affinity of Se to MT in the complex. The stability of the SeNP-MT within the colloidal matrix is indicated by a high zeta potential which reflects strong interparticle repulsion. Additionally, it also suggests a strong electrostatic attraction to oppositely charged components of the cell membrane [54]. The red-shift of absorbance compared to control solution would be another indication for improved surface plasmon resonance when synthesized from chemical pathways. The red-shift compared to the control samples is more pertinent as revealed from our experiment that may suggest an enhanced electron oscillation on the NP surface. This could be due to the chemical synthesis processes that also corroborate the moderate aggregation and advanced optical characteristics. The plasmon response of NP refers the conductions of electrons on free surface due to oscillation when those incidents with light. This might also fit for well characterizing feature for synthesized SeNP-MT complex to have moderate aggregation. Finally, the development of crystallinity or amorphous nature of the complex derived from XRD analysis is important for plant interaction. The crystallinity of the complex increases the surface area coupled with reduction of lattice energy. These factors collectively offer more solubility in aqueous phase of plant fluids (ascent of sap) [55]. This otherwise facilitates the bio-availability and sustained release of Se from SeNP-MT complex when administered in the rhizosphere. From the AFM image, our formulation exhibits distinct topological characteristics with roughness by analysis of root mean square values on the surface. This is important in non-structural integrity of the SeNP-MT complex within the root surface. Here a possible increase in surface diffusion is expected that senses elements (e.g. Se here) to adsorbed onto it [56]. The surface topography of the particles was detected with presence of Se through EDX spectrum. The intramolecular exchange/interaction of our sample would be interesting when a distinct variations in shifts of the components like Se, MT and SeNP-MT was analysed through FTIR spectra. The major characteristic changes in minimization of bond energies were documented which denotes mostly the presence of C-H, C = O in the SeNP-MT complex more adsorptive in nature. Specifically, N–H stretching would be liable for presence of amide/and indole residues either exudated from plant body that would be supporting for amino acid metabolism [57]. Other stretching of bonds like C = O, C-N though not precisely investigated with their roles, still, the interaction with cell surface might be amenable. A possibility may arise from interaction between Se and MT via N–H and C = O bonding implies of a stable nano composite as in our case [58]. On plant soil interaction, the others bonds may extend the interaction with specific transporter protein(s) for availability of nutrients as well as induction of signalling pathways where abscisic acid (ABA), ROS, reactive nitrogen species are important. Alternatively, the formulation of SeNP-MT for its strong affinity on Al as adsorbed on roots surface that might be accessed for better bio-exclusion [59]. In our study, we assumed that Al bio-exclusion could primary be the strategy to limit the intracellular migration of the metal into roots and thereby reducing phytotoxicity. The XRD analysis of SeNP-MT also improvises amorphization or surface interactions with different functional groups. This interaction otherwise enhanced the sustained release of Al from soil onto roots. Thus, we might have the support for our formulation of SeNP-MT complex with their different opto-physical characteristics qualified in control of oxidative stress with up/down regulation of related genes following earlier documentation [60].

Melatonin-selenium composite may be potential for aluminum bio-exclusion supporting sustainable growth

The chemical conversion of selenite [Se (IV)] to elemental Se [Se+0] via selenate [Se (VI)] and its nano state has been well documented in bio-exclusion of different metals including Al [61]. The chemical changes or transformation of Se into nano structures is facilitated with less toxic forms, however, with higher reactivity due to impact on cellular redox. Still, in a stable complex preferably with organic residues like MT (as used in our experiment) is less frequently reported. This complex has potential to alter the heavy/toxic ion absorption in plants. Likewise, specific cysteine rich residues like metallothionine are important for formation of coordinate bonds with metal ions. This is expected to enhances the capacity of SeNP-MT composite with chelate formation to immobilize the toxic residues in rhizosphere or root-soil interphase. The initial bio-exclusion of Al ions by application of SeNP-MT in varying proportions (SeNP-MT1–3) is satisfactory in our study where Brassica seedlings were relieved in realizations of other growth attributes like RGR, NAR. Roots in plants being more vulnerable to Al toxicity must be considered for its growth. In our experiment we observed a decreased RGR. It also appears that seedlings accumulate dry matter accumulation through photosynthetic rate (i.e. measured with NAR herein) under Al stress. The retrieval of RGR and NAR occurs with functional modules of total leaf biomass [62]. It is the capacity of plants to improve the NAR which otherwise restores the photosynthetic efficiency and that possibly supports controlling of oxidative stress and improved stomatal activity (conductance of CO2, H2O vapour, O2 and other gasses) with SeNP-MT exposure. The excess bio-accumulation of Al in the root tissues otherwise involves possible denaturing of the membrane structure and functional integrity besides absorption as evident from the SEM images. The opposing trend of Al acquisition in the roots and RGR under SeNP-MT1–3 otherwise signs the effectivity of NPs to counteract Al phytotoxicity. The synthesized SeNP to restore the root volume and possible water relation parameters must be amenable for less binding of Al on root membrane that otherwise reduces the phytotoxicity of Al in rice [63]. This could be possible when SeNP-MT could stabilize the aquaporin or downregulate the ROS induced breakdown of membrane proteins those collectively improved the root hydraulic conductivity. This finding would be strengthening our experimental view where complimentary roles of MT might be improvised when complexed with SeNP. This is practically realised in our experiment where both inhibition of Al bio-accumulation as well as its impact on metal induced physical or oxidative injuries. There remains ample possibility to minimize this phenomenon by application of SeNP-MT1–3 complex. The distortions of root surfaces by invading Al ions interacts with bio-sequestration processes where metal binds onto apoplastic wall [64]. The interactions of metals on cell wall residues are based on the shifting of bond energies by lowering the cell rigidity. Our experiment revelled root surface becomes more vulnerable to Al toxicity. A significant compression of the hypodermal tissues according to Al exposure was noticed. Still, patches with restricted cell volume and minimized furrows on cell wall were normalized by SeNP-MT1–3 complex. This reflects the potentials of SeNP for recovery of tissue damage. Such damage is typically caused by invading ions crossing the root membranes into conducting tissues or regulation of peroxidative reactions. Root growth vulnerable to Al toxicity was recovered by MT where major concern included oxidative stress [65]. Therefore, it would be elusive whether function of MTs is exclusively required for radical/ROS scavenging or binding of metal ions those collectively or complementarily contribute the recovery of root damage under Al stress. More research awaits on genomic or metabolomic level to decipher SeNP-MT impact to the levels of cell viability or minimizations of developmental constraints under Al overaccumulation.

Energy conversion pathways are controlled under SeNP-MT application

Metal stress is essentially influenced with sort of moisture deficits/dehydration in tissues with alteration of osmotic potential by ions. The ionic imbalance under metal stress commonly is switched over to osmotic stress. This results reduced water potential in tissues, and finally impairing the cell expansion of root, stomatal conductance like turgor dependent phenomena [66]. Likewise, in crops Al overaccumulation would be realizing with dehydrated status of tissues where photosynthetic energy yielding mechanism are compromised. So, dehydration stress developed due to metal acquisition would be affected on stomatal closer. This in turn may modify the energy quenching pathways. In this line of thoughts, stomatal regulations most important to reduce intercellular CO2 concentration. This may shift the flow of electron through chloroplastic electron transport chain more towards non-photochemical quenching to control the photo-oxidative damage. The in vivo fluorescence in our experiment is maximally downregulated under Al toxicity. However, SeNP-MT1–3 were quite efficient to retrieve the fluorescence energy probably ensuring the energy dissipation pathways. Metal toxicity affects how light energy is distributed through the photosystems, and this reduction is more severe under Al stress compared to normal conditions. This is evident from the reduced activity of PSII documented herein. It lowers oxygen production in chloroplasts and the generation of NADPH, a key molecule for energy ultimately reduced the photosynthesis. Thus, the availability of NADPH may limit the regeneration of Calvin cycle intermediate residue which exerts with negative effects of Al stress on carbon fixation, metabolism and overall biomass acquisition. Aluminum toxicity studied both against fluorescence and PSII photochemical activity and its retrieval by the modulation with SeNP-MT1–3 treatment that would be factor for membrane protection against oxidative stress [67]. Thus, sustenance of chlorophyll concentration either through its de novo biosynthesis or regulation of its oxidation might be encountered by SeNP already referred earlier as complimented by MT complex [68]. On the other hand, metal induced stomatal closure obviously restricting the CO2 concentration within chloroplast stroma where decarboxylation of organic acid would be a substitutive pathway [69]. We measured the in vitro activities of major malate metabolism enzyme as NADP-ME which appeared to be significantly varied all through the treatments. Brassica, like C3 plant is also sensitized by activities of NADP-ME not necessarily with photosynthetic CO2 enrichment, however, with other developmental growth processes [70]. In C3 plants, NADP-ME is mostly focused with donation of CO2 residues in biosynthetic pathways as well as maintaining of redox with reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate [NADP(H)] + H+ as equivalent. The increased activity of NADP-ME under nano composite treatments would be as usual as a triggering of reducing ionic/metal load, oxidative redox, tissue moisture deficit etc. Moreover, malate metabolism by this enzyme plays a key role in interconversion of carbon residues in contributing biosynthetic pools of metabolites [71]. Under Al tolerance, malate metabolism would be more crucial for internal bio-exclusion of ions coupled with generation of NADP(H) as regeneration of glutathione (GSH) like major antioxidants in ROS scavenging.

Aluminum toxicity is also transformed into a H+ stress, particularly, in acidic soil (pH ≤ 5). Aluminum co-occurring with H+ stress ensures acidolysis of root membrane and disrupts ion homeostasis. In acid soil, the maximum solubility and thereby bioavailability of Al3+ exacerbates the rhizotoxicity which alternatively establishes a H+ stress by depolarization of membrane potential. Contextually, decarboxylation of malate by NADP-ME leads to formations of phosphoenol pyruvate (as acid anion) which is complexed with other alkali metals like sodium, potassium [72]. Malate metabolism in chloroplast and mitochondria often induces co-transport of other cation channels which also stabilizes the H+ gradient over the membrane mitigating the stress [73]. Conclusively, the activation of malate mediated anion/cation exchange is most important to recover pH homeostasis followed by amelioration of H+ induced cytosolic acidification.

Regulation of oxidative redox: a key to tolerance for aluminum stress

Brassica is well understood as a hyperaccumulatory species where stabilization of redox is the prime consideration for sustenance as already referred with different heavy metals. The stabilization of cellular redox is purely based on activity of glutathione-ascorbate cycle. This cycle essentially neutralizes the ROS/free radicals and also responsible for differential regulation of specific regulatory proteins (transcription factors etc.) sensitive to redox. Moreover, exogenous application of pro-oxidants (e.g. Se) and any special form like nano-Se pointed out the sulfur metabolism for stabilizations of redox. In Brassica, an amalgamation of SeNP with other organic residues in more bio-compatible form realized an enhanced stress tolerance where glutathione metabolism was important [74]. In our experiment, Brassica seedlings responded well to SeNP-MT1–3 interacting oxidative stress developed by Al bio-accumulation with a significant modulation of O2•− and H2O2 content. Similar findings strengthen our observation where SeNP specially dopped with plant residue recorded a downregulation of ROS generation in rice under Al stress [75]. The attenuation of ROS suggests the activation of antioxidants and enzymatic proteins like SOD, NADP-ME, CAT. These enzymes were executed in the present experiment which are upregulated under Se treatment. Melatonin itself is a stabilizing residue in plant system must be well compatible in modulation of oxidative burst. It is likely more possible complex of SeNP-MT1–3 complex contribute to ROS regulation. This was also reflected in the regulated NOX activity in our experiment as well as directs quenching of ROS energy. The lysis of cellular membrane by lipid peroxidation which is visually documented by Evans blue uptake in root tissue in our experiment also established Al sensitivity. Both Se and MT were referred distinctly to reduce the Evans blue uptake with sustenance of membrane integrity that secured improved Al tolerance in rice [76]. Aluminum toxicity in root is also manifested into cellular necrosis causing cell death with progressive loss of membrane integrity where Evans blue uptake is more facilitated. Thus, evaluating cell death in root through Evans blue uptake and its regulation would be justified by the potential of Al + SeNP-MT1–3 treatments. Understanding the major roles for tissue disintegration in roots by ROS, the visualization with fluorescence-based imaging with H2DCFDA is important in Brassica seedling herein. Oxidative stress may directly proportionate with ROS like H2O2, which in oxidised state binds with acetate groups formed by induced intracellular esterase activity. The suppression of such esterase and other hydrolytic activities by MT was referred in plant roots under oxidative exposure and may possibly suggest the regulation of ROS generation [77]. In our experiment, Brassica roots restricted their sensitivity to ROS generation or lysis under Al + SeNP-MT1–3 treatment over Al toxicity must be supportive to that view. The diminished intensity of fluorescence by H2DCFDA stained roots indicates regulation of ROS acquisition as well as enhanced neutralization by activities of glutathione peroxidase or peroxiredoxin related proteins. The moderate callose deposition ultimately preventing excessive blockage of plasmodesmata and maintaining cellular communication secure tolerance to Al stress [78]. In our treatments, application of SeNP-MT or singly with each component ultimately improved the stress tolerance in acidic soil by refining the ROS scavenging mechanism following different antioxidative pathways. The sensitivity to Al is predominantly focused with meristematic tissues on root tips. Internal bio-sequestering results in a concentrated density of Al3+ occurs surroundings the root tips. These ions accumulate both in vacuoles and apoplastic spaces [79]. In the present study this was evident through distinct growth inhibition, browning and over all degeneration as visualized Haematoxylin staining. The Al3+ ionic form has good affinity to bind with pectin residues of cell wall in root tips can extend the cytotoxicity in the meristematic cells in concerned tissues. The variations through the treatments specially with SeNP-MT1–3 diluted the concentration of Al-Haematoxylin complex in tissues and thereby lowered the colour intensities indicating ameliorative function. The sensitivity of such staining under diluted concentration of Al is based on flexibility of cell wall polymer residues in chemically altered form [80]. Therefore, degenerative tissues in root tips are the most likely targets for diffusion of Al (specifically in Al3+ ionic form) that complex with Haematoxylin. So, it would be logical to understand the restoration of native configuration of root tip by potential of SeNP-MT1–3 reduces the Al susceptibility in plants. All the histochemical detection of Al induced cellular degenerations was observed in corroboration with accumulation of ROS (like O2•−, H2O2) in Brassica roots experiencing the ameliorative effects of SeNP-MT1–3. Therefore, maintaining cell wall integrity by regulation of cell wall hydrolysing/modifying enzymes such as pectin methyl esterase would be prime contributary to sustain the root apical viability against Al stress.

Selenium-melatonin composite discriminates the antioxidation pathways contributed by enzymes and antioxidants

Initially, Al toxicity was intensified by typical symptoms in root degeneration where a turnover of redox into oxidative burst was the key [81]. Primarily, Al toxicity is initiated with root degeneration where a turnover of cellular redox into more oxidized state or establishment of oxidative burst is ensured. The results in the present experiment distinctly indicated the downregulation of enzyme activity through SeNP-MT1–3 doses as compared to only Al treatment. To control peroxidative and carbonylation reactions by ROS, the tolerant species maintain a steady antioxidation pathways [82]. The plant species showing tolerance must maintain a steady antioxidation pathways to either degenerate the ROS or inhibit its biosynthesis that physiologically marked with regulated peroxidative and carbonylation reactions. In our experiment, the roles of antioxidative proteins and antioxidants were markedly observed. Treatments including Se, MT and its complex (SeNP-MT1–3) responded well. The capability of MT for stabilizations of enzymatic proteins including antioxidative ones as well as a steady supplementation of electron donor from GSH cycle in Al tolerance were clearly described [83]. Melatonin could stabilize the ionic concentration which in turn optimizes enzymatic activities within definite pH. This stabilizing role is similarly performed by played by SeNP. Although Se in bulk (i.e. non nano form) form is a redox metal, is helps sustain ROS homeostasis but only within a safe threshold of cellular concentration [84]. Selenium itself a redox metal, however, in bulk form and still it contributes ROS homeostasis within a threshold concentration of its safer application. Therefore, it would be quite obvious for realization of impact for antioxidative enzyme(s) activity from the conjugant effects of SeNP-MT in our experiment. Interestingly, Brassica herein activated its constitutive antioxidation activity by maximizing GPX and CAT activity to secure the control of ROS as evident form the trend under Al treatment only. Aluminum hyperaccumulation in roots partially co-transports with other metals [including cupper (Cu), arsenic (As), lead (Pb) like heavy metals] those induces de novo synthesis of antioxidative proteins in Brassica [85]. These possibly indicate the genotypic plasticity of the species to induce existing enzymatic antioxidation cascades. Thus, insensitivity of SeNP-MT1–3 as shown comparing to Al treatment indirectly indicates the species genotypic potential for ROS scavenging might be independent to any supplements. Notably, opposing trend of antioxidants (AsA/DHA) and oxidative degeneration (MDA content out of lipid peroxidation) herein, would be indicating the stringent regulation of oxidative stress ensuring plant tolerance [86]. It recorded an increased AsA/DHA ratio corresponding decreased MDA content under SeNP-MT1 treatment secured the plants from oxidative stress through independent enzymatic pathways. In complex of SeNP-MT1–3, SeNP functions as an antioxidizing residue, while MT may function in signaling paths. Evidences suggesting such a moiety often quenches the energy under basal or sub-optimal cellular ROS concentration and thereby, reduces the proportionate contribution of ROS scavenging by enzymes and antioxidants [87]. The increased ratio of AsA/DHA all through SeNP-MT1–3 treatments is strongly adjoined with the contribution of antioxidants for direct quenching of ROS against Al stress. Thereby, Brassica plants registered its oxidative tolerance through exercising scavenging ability of antioxidants, however, not being fully dependent on antioxidative enzymes.

Polymorphic expression of isozyme patterns is sensitized with aluminum stress under modulation of SeNP-MT

The polymorphic expression of isozymic profile essentially deals with variations in genotypic tolerance mediated by genes that govern specific biochemical reactions. Polymorphic expression of few enzymatic profiles as isozyme reflects the strong genotypic regulation for tolerance under stress, which sparingly governs the specific biochemical reactions adhered to stress tolerance. In Al tolerance, bio-sequestration of metals by malate metabolizing gene sets conception of biomarker for tolerance. In our study, the isozymic profiles resolved through non-denaturing gel recorded distinct variations in polypeptide concentrations, although the total number of bands remained consistent. The validation of polypeptide variations in concentration through different treatments resolved by non-denaturing gel electrophoresis, however, despite a consistent band numbers advocates only the quantitative changes. The pattern of these changes primarily reflects quantitative shifts in polypeptide concentrations under different treatments. It does not indicate qualitative variations from the same or different loci, as previously observed in Brassica [88]. Notably, the presence of a single band for GPX, CAT, however, not in NADP-ME across all treatments suggests a high sensitivity of a single/different locus/loci to Al toxicity in this experimental setup. The isozymic diversity arisen from same or different gene pools on a single chromosome or its any homologue specifically those may response to Al induction is important for selection of genotypes. [89]. This is clarified further with dose dependent variations of SeNP-MT1–3 to express the concern polypeptide. This variation may play crucial role in genes expression. The variation in expression of polypeptide remains dose-dependent that highlights its potential to modulate the gene expression under different environmental stresses. In Brassica, transcriptome profiling under heavy metal stress identified sets of genes variable expression. This change occurs in a dose and time dependent by specific regulatory system [90]. Collectively, shift of polypeptide concentrations from low to high through control via Al toxicity arises the possibility to develop biomarkers against Al contamination. Conclusively, the overall enzyme expression pattern for GPX, CAT and NADP-ME, SeNP-MT. However, concentration dependent changes of polypeptide through doses of treatment might reflect in enzyme activities in vitro. Future research awaits to strengthen the relationship between activity of enzyme with expression under oxidative stress as plants respond through real time analysis of transcripts.

Alteration of oxidative redox: a combinational interaction with SeNP and MT

Undoubtedly, genotoxicity with Al is referred with an establishment of oxidative redox in sensitive genotype at molecular level. Redox induced loss of cell viability results in degeneration of DNA from nucleus. Genotoxicity is referred with oxidative damages of DNA, particularly, the sequences associated with active transcription and remodelling of chromatin under Al induction. This includes damage to genes involved in cell wall biosynthesis, osmoregulation, metal homeostasis, photosynthetic energy transfer under Al stress [91]. Selenium itself is redox metal that can induce oxidation of nucleic acid crossing the threshold concentration. However, in nano form it regulates the same mostly with induction of antioxidation. This behaviour was also evident in our study, where nuclear sensitivity to Al induced oxidative reaction was assessed by analysing tail length, olive tail moment and tail DNA %. The comet assay has been a reliable tool or indicators for damages of nuclear DNA and its quantitative pattern directly corroborating intensity of stresses. Interestingly, MT singly (i.e. Al + MT) in our study could exceed the regulation of tail length and others than Se. Regulations of nuclear membrane damage through reduction of tail length (which is amount of DNA released from nuclei entering comet tail) was reported against genotoxicity of Al in Brassica juncea [92]. The metal chelating protein like metallothionine also participates in buffering of redox as well as DNA repair in addition to its generic metal chelating activities. The ROS induced peroxidation and mostly with damage by free radicals like OH− are most sensitive to loss of DNA by depurination. Additionally, olive tail moment with an arbitrarily unit refers extent of DNA release in proportion to total DNA in nucleus [93]. Regardless of parameters, SeNP is well mentioned in different crops to reduce the genotoxicity by sustaining nuclear integrity where both regulation of the ROS or/and shielding of major domains of nucleic acid. Melatonin is usually familiar with regulation of genotoxicity through induction of antioxidation with conventional enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants. Still, MT in bulk or nano form is also efficient in upregulation of DNA base excision and other repair mechanism [94]. Regulatory factors from NAM, ATAF1/2, and CUC2 and WRKYGQK in its DNA-binding domain (WRKY, NAC) have been identified to play the co-regulation of metal induced gene expression and thereby assist genomic stability. A number of transcription factors (NAC, WRKY) under drought or metal stress are co-expressed with MT biosynthesis to regulate oxidative stress tolerance [95]. Thus, the combinational use of SeNP-MT1–3 may offer more efficient regulation of DNA damage. Sustenance of nuclear stability under SeNP-MT1–3 treatment could reflect integrated cellular processes covering antioxidation activity, sequestering of metal and DNA repair like major coordinating events for stress tolerance. This approach shows promising results in mitigating genomic effects in Brassica seedlings in our experiment.

Induction of sulfur assimilation: compatibility with SeNP-MT for antioxidation

Aluminum not being a heavy metal still show a significant affinity for oxidative redox with different nucleophilic reaction to develop variants of ROS [96]. The ROS being central to oxidative damages, its detoxification is expected to be mediated by sulfur containing compound where Se and sulfur act as chalcogens being most important. The interaction of sulfur residues with ROS is mostly facilitated through the donation of electron from GSH, the key antioxidant. This occurs via specific pathway involved in reduction processes. Aluminum triggers a competitive transport of selenate (SeO42−) and sulfate (SO42−) over the plasmalemma through specific sulfate transporters [97]. The direct conversions of oxidized sulfur into reduced form particularly, in methionine and cysteine has the ability to contribute antioxidation directly. Still, on absorption of SO42−, there is a direct incorporation into methionine also forming selenomethionin. So, involvement in antioxidation under Al or other metal stress, Se rich residues are more aggravated than sulfur metabolites [98]. In our experiment, Se singly or in combination (SeNP-MT1–3) resulted in over accumulation of sulfur containing compounds. These include total sulfur, cysteine, NPT and PC content. Interestingly, PC content showed hardly any dose dependent changes by SeNP-MT1–3 over the Al toxicity in plants. The PC level surprisingly recorded hardly dose-dependent response to SeNP-MT1–3 that possibly suggests role(s) of PC and biosynthesis. This may not be exclusively dependent on inorganic sulfur transformation into reduced moieties from total sulfur pools [99]. Selenium induced accumulations of PC reported in Allium established many of the enzymes in sulfur assimilations are targeted to methionine and cysteine enrichment. Thus, the maximum increase in cysteine content in Brassica herein would be realized by SeNP-MT1–3 mediated supplementation to reduce oxidative stress. This is more supported with the findings where sulfur and Se accumulation in a few crop that depends on the rapid transformation of ionic forms into inorganic organic residues. The Se-PC composite also helps in detoxification of Se inducing oxidative stress in its excess concentration. During cellular detoxification, Se binds with PC by either inducing GSH synthesis as precursor or regulating PC synthase under oxidative stress [100]. The incorporation of Se is mostly accredited both in selenite (oxidized form) and selenocysteine (reduced form) to sequester excess Se onto apoplastic or non-cellular spaces. In case of SeNP, the concentration of Se toxicity is maximally reduced in plants than that of bulk Se [101]. Thereby, the subcellular compartmentalization would be critical to minimize the excess cellular toxicity while rendering its metabolic outcomes or benefit. For spatial allocation of those compounds in cytosol mostly is required to facilitate other metabolic function [102]. Regardless, Al tolerance in Brassica, is more favorably achieved by converting oxidized sulfur into organic residues. These reduced forms are readily integrated into antioxidation cascades, potentially enhanced by SeNP-MT1–3.

Sulfur assimilations become most crucial with the repository of cysteine as precursor for major thiol containing compounds (NPTs, PCs etc.) as elucidate in the present experiment. The experiment also recorded Al + SeNP-MT2 as best combination to restore sulfur and cysteine concentrations and, thereby, a sulfur based antioxidation pathway is secured. The data clearly suggested the emphasis of sulfur reduction pathways under stress would be more lenient to reduce lipid peroxidation of membrane and thus restored root integrity. Additionally, the photosynthetic recovery through improved decarboxylative pathways (i.e. NADP-ME activity) would be the result of adequate biomass accumulation under Al stress. Overall, a strong direct correlation is the final goal to focus sulfur status of Brassica seedlings for assured Al tolerance. Collectively these findings underscore the sustenance of sulfur rich metabolites from any source of nutritions, fertilizers, bio-stimulants or from exogenous application of advanced chemicals where SeNP-MT1–3 found more reliable.

The present work deals with the effectivity of SeNP in composite with one of the growth regulators like MT in Brassica seedlings to moderate the Al toxicity. The bio-compatibility of SeNP is well adjusted from the modified molecular characterizations in nano formulation probably extent the strong bondage with MT. Toxicity of the metal associated with root functioning with regards to growth and oxidative damages was primarily reduced by singly and in combination of SeNP and MT. The major foci were restricted on reduction of Al bio-sequestration, oxidative damage and other related cellular responses in tolerance. Thus, we understand Se with inherent antioxidation capacity is more pronounced in nano form (i.e. SeNP) specifically when synthesized with MT supplementation. Still, whether SeNP or MT binds with any specific regions of cellular compartments to modulate the reactions is not elucidated herein. Despite this, plants typically manage Al phytotoxicity via ROS degradation pathway, which may be achieved by SeNP application. Alternatively, MT in conjugation with Se may improvise gene expression supporting alternative pathways for CO2 enrichment oxidizing malate (the major Al binding residue) by NADP-ME. The SeNP with its background in induction of photosynthetic regulation could modulated the energy dissipation as documented herein to minimize over oxidation of chloroplasts. Thus, distinct cellular recovery in Brassica roots advocated the MT mediated membrane repair as supported earlier. Plants are attained with an adequate pool of reducing equivalent as NADP(H) as byproduct of NADP-ME mediated oxidative decarboxylation of malate. In consistent with Al bio-sequestering for detoxification of metal toxicity, a significant variation with polymorphisms of different antioxidative proteins suggests de-folding of genetic plasticity against metal in Brassica. It is also a bonus for SeNP-MT application where toxicity of Se (if any) could also be minimized by antioxidative properties of MT. Brassica itself being a sulfur rich crop may be fortified with better exercising of sulfur reduction pathway under SeNP-MT application to contribute thiol residues in antioxidation. Finally, mitigation of genotoxicity by SeNP-MT opens avenues for exploring nucleic acid modifying genes sensitive to Al using molecular techniques. Consequently, amelioration of Al genotoxicity under coverage of SeNP-MT exposure may open a new line of experiment where nucleic acid modifying gene were targeted, however, with appropriate molecular probing. Nevertheless, more in-depth studies await to bridge the unexplored areas of Al toxicity and SeNP-MT interaction. Conclusively, SeNP-MT is evident as a biocompatible advanced chemical exhibiting a dual-function system with SeNP based antioxidative cascades and MT mediated cellular detoxification against Al stress in crops.
