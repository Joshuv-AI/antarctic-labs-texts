A dual-table technique facilitates vascular control and successful repair of a suprarenal inferior vena cava injury
Authors: Catherine Kilada, Jonathan Robitsek, Katherine McKenzie, Juan C Jimenez, Mina Guerges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPenetrating inferior vena cava (IVC) injuries are rare and highly lethal vascular emergencies. We report the successful open primary repair of a suprarenal IVC injury caused by a retained knife in a hemodynamically stable 24-year-old man. Preoperative imaging facilitated operation planning, and a coordinated multidisciplinary approach optimized patient positioning in the operating room, vascular exposure, and staged foreign body extraction after proximal and distal caval control were established. Primary venorrhaphy preserved caval continuity, and completion venography confirmed repair integrity. This case highlights that complex penetrating suprarenal IVC injuries can be successfully managed with open reconstruction through meticulous planning, secure vascular control, and multidisciplinary collaboration.Keywords: Inferior vena cava, Penetrating trauma, Vascular injury, Suprarenal IVC, Vena cava ligation

Inferior vena cava (IVC) injuries occur in approximately 0.5% to 5% of penetrating abdominal trauma cases and represent one of the most lethal forms of vascular injury, with reported prehospital mortality rates of 30% to 50% and in-hospital mortality rates of 20% to 60%.1, 2, 3 Survival is mainly determined by the injury location, patient physiology at presentation, and the ability to achieve rapid hemorrhage control. Injuries involving the suprarenal and retrohepatic IVC are associated with particularly poor outcomes because of challenging exposure, difficult vascular control, and the potential for massive hemorrhage.4 Definitive management options include primary repair, patch reconstruction, interposition grafting, and, less optimally, ligation. Although ligation may be lifesaving in damage-control situations, particularly for infrarenal injuries, it is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, especially when performed in the suprarenal segment.5,6 Preservation of caval continuity is therefore desirable whenever technically feasible. Despite advances in endovascular technology, open surgical repair remains the cornerstone of management for most penetrating IVC injuries.7,8 We present the case of a penetrating injury caused by a retained knife blade traversing the suprarenal IVC. This report highlights the importance of meticulous preoperative planning, optimal operating room logistics, coordinated multidisciplinary management, and secure vascular control in facilitating the successful open primary repair of a frequently fatal injury. The patient has consented to publication of the case details and images.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old man presented as a level 1 trauma activation after sustaining a stab wound to the mid-back with a retained knife at the L1 region, projecting approximately 4 to 5 cm externally (Fig 1, A and B). On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15, intact motor function, and palpable distal pulses. Given his stability, computed tomography (CT) was performed to evaluate the trajectory of the retained weapon. CT demonstrated transluminal penetration of the IVC with an associated Zone 1 retroperitoneal hematoma and active contrast extravasation anterior to the L1 vertebral body (Fig 2, A-C). The patient was taken urgently to the operating room for surgical intervention. A key strategic challenge was safe patient positioning in order to preserve knife stability. Two operating tables were placed side by side with a 2-3-inch gap, allowing the patient—initially prone—to be carefully rotated into a supine position while the knife projected freely between the tables (Fig 3). This avoided anatomic displacement before vascular control. A massive transfusion protocol was activated preemptively, and blood products were immediately available.

Operating room organization of two tables side by side with a 2 to 3-inch gap allowing the initially prone patient to be rotated supine with the knife projecting freely between the tables.

Following midline laparotomy and Cattell–Braasch medial visceral rotation with a Kocher maneuver, a large Zone 1 retroperitoneal hematoma was exposed. The knife was found to traverse the subhepatic suprarenal IVC, with approximately 2 cm of the blade projecting through the anterior lumen of the vessel. Proximal and distal IVC control was achieved using sponge sticks rather than formal clamping, minimizing additional endothelial injury. After communication with the anesthesia team to ensure adequate resuscitation, the knife was withdrawn in a controlled staged manner from the patient’s back, through the gap in the tables by an off-field team member. Through primary venorrhaphy, the posterior and anterior walls of the IVC were repaired using Prolene sutures. Completion venography via right femoral venous access demonstrated a widely patent IVC without residual injury or stenosis.

No major arterial, renal, ureteral, hepatic, or hollow viscus injuries were identified. Owing to physiologic stress and concern for an associated retroperitoneal injury, the abdomen was temporarily closed with negative pressure therapy, and the patient was transferred to the surgical intensive care unit in a stable condition.

Discussion

Penetrating IVC injuries remain among the most lethal vascular emergencies. The high mortality associated with these injuries is due to rapid exsanguination, difficult retroperitoneal exposure, and frequent associated injuries, particularly in the suprarenal and retrohepatic segments.2,4 A 13-year analysis of the National Trauma Databank involving approximately 25,000 patients demonstrated persistently poor outcomes following penetrating major vascular trauma. Although mortality has improved for blunt vascular injuries, likely reflecting the increased utilization of endovascular techniques, mortality associated with penetrating venous injuries, including IVC trauma, has increased over time.5,6 These findings underscore the continued importance of optimizing open surgical management strategies for penetrating IVC injuries.7,8

The present case highlights several principles that contributed to a successful outcome. First, the patient’s hemodynamic stability allowed preoperative imaging, which confirmed caval violation and defined the trajectory of the retained knife, facilitating operation planning. Second, the retained weapon was left in place until definitive vascular control could be established, preventing uncontrolled hemorrhage. Most importantly, successful repair required close multidisciplinary coordination among trauma surgery, vascular surgery, anesthesia, nursing, and operating room personnel. A unique setup in the operating room with two adjacent operating tables facilitated safe patient positioning while maintaining the retained knife in situ and providing adequate exposure for laparotomy and vascular control. This planning enabled the controlled extraction of the blade only after the proximal and distal control of the IVC had been achieved.

Primary repair was successfully performed, preserving caval continuity and avoiding the morbidity associated with IVC ligation. Although ligation may be necessary in damage-control situations, outcomes vary significantly by the injury location.7,9,10 Infrarenal ligation is often tolerated but remains associated with venous morbidity, whereas suprarenal ligation is associated with substantially higher mortality because the interruption of renal venous drainage may result in a severe acute kidney injury and other complications.7,8,11 Ligation also predisposes patients to venous thromboembolism, lower extremity edema, and, in severe cases, compartment syndrome.9,11, 12, 13, 14 Preservation of caval flow through primary repair therefore remains desirable whenever technically feasible.

This case demonstrates that even complex penetrating suprarenal IVC injuries can be successfully managed with open primary repair. Careful preoperative planning, strategic operating room organization, coordinated multidisciplinary communication, secure vascular control before foreign body extraction, and definitive venous reconstruction were the key factors contributing to a favorable outcome despite the historically high mortality associated with these injuries.

Conclusions

The successful management of complex penetrating suprarenal IVC injuries requires coordinated logistical organization before and during the operation and by all members of the operating room team. This case highlights the importance of precise operation planning, maintenance of anatomic stability of the retained foreign body until definitive vascular control is established, coordinated multidisciplinary teamwork, and vessel-preserving reconstruction whenever feasible. Adherence to these principles enabled successful primary repair and the preservation of caval patency despite a potentially fatal injury.
