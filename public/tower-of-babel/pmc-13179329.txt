A nanobody-based proteolysis-targeting chimera offers broad-spectrum protection against diverse influenza virus infections
Authors: Yichao Zhuang, Lei Chen, Chen Qin, Puze Chen, Lulu Feng, Weipeng Lin, Jiaqi Li, Yaping Zhang, Congcong Wang, Jianzhong Shi, Guohua Deng, Xianying Zeng, Pengfei Cui, Yanbing Li, Yongping Jiang, Hualan Chen, Huihui Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfluenza A viruses continue to pose a major threat to global public health. In addition to H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes causing seasonal epidemics that result in an estimated 3–5 million severe cases and 290,000–650,000 deaths annually, other subtypes, including avian H5, H7, and H9, have shown cross-species transmission potential, leading to thousands of human infections in multiple countries. The development of broad-spectrum antiviral drugs capable of inhibiting different influenza virus subtypes is key for alleviating the severity of diseases caused by influenza viruses and reducing mortality rates. Here, we constructed five nanobody-based proteolysis-targeting chimeras (Nb-PROTACs) by fusing NP-specific nanobodies to the α-domain of the Von Hippel‒Lindau (VHL) E3 ubiquitin ligase. We found that two of these chimeras (VHL-Nb135 and VHL-Nb170) efficiently induced NP degradation across all 16 recognized influenza A subtypes (H1–H16). VHL-Nb135 and VHL-Nb170 efficiently inhibited the replication of human (H1N1, H3N2) and avian (H5N1, H7N9, H9N2) influenza viruses in vitro. In animal studies, when VHL-Nb170 was administered intratracheally to mice via adeno-associated virus serotype LungM3 (AAV-LungM3), virus replication was significantly inhibited in the respiratory tract, and 90% and 80% of the mice survived infection with lethal H1N1 and H5N1 viruses, respectively. Our study indicates that Nb-PROTACs offer a robust platform for the development of broad-spectrum therapies against influenza viruses and hold potential for clinical translation as innovative antiviral candidate drugs.Subject terms: Infectious diseases, Microbiology

Introduction

Influenza A viruses (hereafter referred to as influenza viruses) are highly diverse pathogens that primarily originate from wild aquatic birds and cause infectious outbreaks across multiple species, including humans. Influenza viruses are classified into different subtypes on the basis of the antigenic or genetic properties of their surface glycoproteins, hemagglutinin (HA, H1–H19) and neuraminidase (NA, N1–N11).1,2 Human seasonal influenza is caused mainly by the H1 and H3 virus subtypes. These two subtypes circulate primarily during winter and spring, resulting in an estimated 3–5 million severe cases and 290,000–650,000 deaths worldwide each year.3 Over the past several decades, various subtypes of avian influenza viruses have exhibited zoonotic transmission, posing a major threat to public health.4–9 For example, H9N2 low pathogenic avian influenza virus has caused more than 100 human infections in 10 different countries.10,11 H5 and H7 subtype influenza viruses have caused more than 2600 human infections, with fatality rates exceeding 40%.12,13 Recently, outbreaks caused by an H5N1 virus of clade 2.3.4.4b have been reported among dairy cattle and associated personnel in the United States.14–16 These viruses have undergone genetic changes that may increase their potential for human-to-human transmission, further underscoring the pandemic risk posed by avian influenza viruses.17,18

Vaccination and antiviral therapy are the two primary strategies employed to combat influenza viruses.19–23 However, mutations in the HA gene frequently result in antigenic drift, thereby reducing the efficacy of vaccines administered to both humans and animals. Consequently, vaccines must be regularly updated to remain effective against circulating virus strains.24 Antiviral agents, including neuraminidase inhibitors (i.e., oseltamivir, zanamivir, peramivir, and laninamivir) and M2 ion channel blockers (i.e., amantadine and rimantadine), have been widely used to mitigate the severity of influenza in humans.25–27 However, the emergence of drug-resistant variants has significantly reduced the effectiveness of these therapeutic interventions. Recent studies have demonstrated that although the novel antiviral agents baloxavir and favipiravir exhibit broad-spectrum antiviral activity, they may facilitate the emergence of drug-resistant variants,28–30 thereby further complicating influenza treatment. Therefore, innovative strategies that target and inhibit viral components while exhibiting broad-spectrum antiviral activity are needed.

Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have emerged as a highly effective and selective approach for targeted protein degradation. The underlying mechanism involves harnessing the intracellular ubiquitin‒proteasome system to recognize and degrade the protein of interest.31–36 Unlike traditional inhibitors, which prevent protein function by binding to their active sites, PROTACs exert their function simply by binding to the protein of interest and facilitating its degradation. The first generation of PROTACs are small molecule-based PROTACs. Since the initial report of a PROTAC designed to target methionine aminopeptidase-2 (MetAp-2) by recruiting an E3 ligase through a peptide ligand, many small molecule-based PROTACs have been developed to combat various viruses.37–42 Recently, peptide-based or antibody-based PROTACs have also been developed.43–45 Montrose et al. constructed a PROTAC in which the target protein binding domain was tethered to the proteasome recognition domain and included a cell-penetrating peptide at its N-terminus to combat hepatitis B virus.43 Vartak et al. engineered a cetuximab-based PROTAC that downregulates EGFR in non-small cell lung cancer.44 Wang et al. reported an ROR1-targeting antibody-based PROTAC conjugate that induces BRD4 degradation for solid-tumor therapy.45

Nanobodies, distinguished by their small molecular size, high stability, and excellent tissue penetrability, have become an important complement to conventional antibodies, offering potential in the fields of precision medicine and novel drug development.46,47 Fulcher et al. and RÖth et al. developed PROTACs by fusing different protein-targeting nanobodies to the E3 ligase domain and successfully degraded different proteins in human cancer cell lines.48,49 Deng et al. designed a tumor microenvironment-responsive bispecific nanobody-based PROTAC (Nb-PROTAC) to target PD-L1 and Survivin to increase antitumor efficacy.50 However, the efficacy of Nb-PROTAC as an antiviral agent, particularly its in vivo antiviral activity, has yet to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we constructed novel Nb-PROTACs targeting the highly conserved nucleoprotein (NP) of influenza viruses and evaluated their potential as anti-influenza therapeutics. We found that one Nb-PROTAC efficiently induced the degradation of NPs across multiple influenza virus subtypes in vitro and inhibited the replication of different influenza viruses in cells. It also provided at least 80% protection to mice challenged with different lethal viruses. Nb-PROTACs thus show promise as broad-spectrum antivirals for the treatment of diverse influenza virus infections.

Results

Design and validation of NP nanobody-based PROTACs

Schmidt et al. identified several specific nanobodies that target the NP protein of influenza virus via lentiviral screening technology and demonstrated that these nanobodies inhibited viral replication in A549 cells to varying degrees.51 Here, we selected five nanobodies reported by Schmidt et al. [two strong inhibitors (Nb52 and Nb77) and three weaker ones (Nb135, Nb170, and Nb355)] to construct our Nb-PROTACs. Each nanobody was fused to the α-domain of Von Hippel‒Lindau (VHL) to generate VHL-Nb constructs (Fig. 1a). To avoid the cell-penetrating problem of purified Nb-PROTACs, we constructed overexpression plasmids for each of these five VHL-Nbs. To assess their ability to promote the degradation of NPs, we cotransfected human HEK293T (293T) cells with plasmids encoding either VHL-Nb or the corresponding Nb, together with a plasmid expressing NP-eGFP [NP protein from A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1, PR8) fused to eGFP at the C-terminus]. Compared with cells cotransfected with Nb and NP-eGFP, cells cotransfected with VHL-Nb and NP-eGFP presented markedly reduced fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1b). In contrast, cells expressing VHL-Nb-Spike (a control construct in which the α-domain of VHL was fused to a SARS-CoV-2 spike-targeting Nb) and NP-eGFP presented a fluorescence intensity similar to that of cells cotransfected with plasmids expressing the anti-spike nanobody (Nb-Spike) and NP-eGFP.

To rule out the possibility that the eGFP tag might affect NP degradation, we performed Western blot analysis using lysates from cells transfected with plasmids expressing NPs with no tag, together with plasmids expressing Nb or VHL-Nb. We found that the NP level in cells transfected with VHL-Nb-Spike was similar to that in cells transfected with Spike-targeting Nb; however, the relative NP levels in the cells transfected with VHL-Nb52, VHL-Nb77, VHL-Nb135, VHL-Nb170, and VHL-Nb355 were 71.5%, 71.6%, 30.0%, 26.2%, and 75.1%, respectively, of those in their corresponding Nb-transfected cells (Fig. 1c). These results indicate that VHL-Nb135 and VHL-Nb170 have a strong capacity for NP degradation, even though they showed weak inhibitory effects in Schmidt’s study.

We then investigated how VHL-Nb135 and VHL-Nb170 affect influenza virus replication. Human lung epithelial (A549) cells stably expressing different Nbs or VHL-Nbs constructed through lentiviral transduction were infected with H1N1 virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01. Viral titers in the supernatants were quantified at different time points postinfection. We found that the viral titers in cells expressing VHL-Nb-Spike were similar to those in cells expressing Nb-Spike (Fig. 1d); however, the viral titers in cells expressing VHL-Nb135 were significantly lower than those in cells expressing Nb135, and the viral titers in cells expressing VHL-Nb170 were significantly lower than those in cells expressing Nb170 (Fig. 1d). Collectively, these data demonstrate that NP-specific nanobodies can be engineered into Nb-PROTACs, which effectively promote NP degradation and thereby prevent viral replication in cells.

VHL-Nb135 and VHL-Nb170 can promote the degradation of NPs from different influenza virus subtypes

VHL-Nb135 and VHL-Nb170 efficiently promoted the degradation of NPs from the H1N1 virus (PR8 strain) and inhibited its replication, but are they effective against other influenza virus subtypes? To answer this question, we first evaluated their binding to the NP proteins of 16 different subtypes of influenza viruses (Supplementary Table 1) via coimmunoprecipitation. We cotransfected 293T cells with plasmids expressing NP together with either VHL-Nb135-Myc or VHL-Nb170-Myc. Forty-eight hours posttransfection, the cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with anti-Myc antibodies, and the levels of NP and VHL-Nb135-Myc or VHL-Nb170-Myc were analyzed via Western blotting. We found that VHL-Nb135-Myc (Fig. 2a) and VHL-Nb170-Myc (Fig. 2b) coprecipitated with the NPs of all 16 influenza virus subtypes.

VHL-Nb135 and VHL-Nb170 reduce the replication of different influenza viruses in A549 cells

Since VHL-Nb135 and VHL-Nb170 exhibited broad-spectrum degradation activity against NPs across all 16 influenza virus subtypes, we next evaluated their antiviral efficacy against viruses from five major subtypes: two human seasonal influenza strains [A/Sichuan/1/2009 (H1N1) and A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2)] and three avian influenza viruses with zoonotic potential [A/wild duck/Hebei/SD012/2021 (H5N1), A/Anhui/1/2013 (H7N9), and A/chicken/Guangxi/S11583/2019 (H9N2)].8,52,53 A549 cells expressing Nbs or VHL-Nbs were infected with the indicated influenza virus at an MOI of 0.01, and viral titers were measured at different time points postinfection. We found that these five viruses replicated in a similar manner in both VHL-Nb-Spike-expressing and Nb-Spike-expressing cells (Fig. 4a, b). However, viral titers in cells expressing VHL-Nb135 were significantly lower than those in Nb135-expressing cells (Fig. 4a). Similarly, the titers of these five viruses in VHL-Nb170-expressing cells were significantly lower than those in Nb170-expressing cells (Fig. 4b). These findings indicate that the development of nanobodies into PROTACs can significantly increase their antiviral activity against influenza viruses.

Protective efficacy of VHL-Nb170 against different influenza viruses in mice

Although VHL-Nb135 and VHL-Nb170 exhibited broad-spectrum antiviral activity in A549 cells, VHL-Nb170 inhibited influenza virus replication more effectively than did VHL-Nb135 (Fig. 4c). We therefore evaluated the protective efficacy of VHL-Nb170 in vivo. We first constructed mouse VHL-Nb170 (mVHL-Nb170) and mVHL-Nb-Spike (by replacing the human VHL protein with its mouse counterpart) and tested their antiviral activity in MLE-12 cells (a mouse lung epithelial cell line). Lentivirus-transduced MLE-12 cells overexpressing mVHL-Nb170 or mVHL-Nb-Spike were infected with an H5N1 virus (A/wild duck/Hebei/SD012/2021, SD012) at an MOI of 0.01. We found that the viral titers in MLE-12 cells overexpressing mVHL-Nb170 were significantly lower than those in MLE-12 cells overexpressing mVHL-Nb-Spike (Fig. 5a).

We then evaluated the protective efficacy of mVHL-Nb170 in mice against infections with different influenza viruses. We used an adeno-associated virus serotype LungM3 (AAV-LungM3) vector, which does not cause any side effects in mice (Supplementary Fig. 1) and is a delivery system widely utilized in contemporary clinical gene therapy applications,54 to introduce mVHL-Nb170 into mice. Groups of 14 BALB/c mice received two doses of 5 × 1010 vector genomes (vg) of AAV-LungM3-mVHL-Nb170 or AAV-LungM3 vector viruses intratracheally at three-day intervals (Fig. 5b). Three weeks after the second dose, 14 mice from each group were challenged intranasally with 5 MLD (50% mouse lethal dose) of H1N1 virus (PR8) or H5N1 virus (SD012). Four mice from each group were euthanized on day 3 postchallenge, and their nasal turbinates and lungs were collected for virus titration.

In the H1N1 virus-challenged groups, the viral titers in the AAV-LungM3-mVHL-Nb170-inoculated mice were significantly lower than those in the AAV-LungM3 vector-inoculated mice, and compared with those in the mice that received AAV-LungM3, the average viral load in the nasal turbinates of the mice that received AAV-LungM3-mVHL-Nb170 was reduced by 99.94%, and the average viral load in their lungs was reduced by 94.51% (Fig. 5c). All of the AAV-LungM3 vector-inoculated mice lost body weight and died within 10 days of H1N1 virus challenge, whereas the AAV-LungM3-mVHL-Nb170-inoculated mice lost less than 10% of their body weight, and nine of the 10 mice survived the virus challenge (Fig. 5c). In the H5N1 virus-challenged groups, the viral titers in the AAV-LungM3-mVHL-Nb170-inoculated mice were also significantly lower than those in the AAV-LungM3 vector-inoculated mice, and compared with those in the mice that received AAV-LungM3, the average viral load in the nasal turbinates of the mice that received AAV-LungM3-mVHL-Nb170 was reduced by 96.71%, and the average viral load in their lungs was reduced by 93.86% (Fig. 5d). The AAV-LungM3 vector-inoculated mice lost body weight and died within 14 days of H5N1 virus challenge, whereas the AAV-LungM3-mVHL-Nb170-inoculated mice did not show clear body weight loss, and eight of the 10 mice survived H5N1 virus challenge (Fig. 5d). These studies demonstrate that nanobody-based PROTAC technology designed to target NP degradation effectively inhibits influenza virus replication in vivo and protects at least 80% of mice from lethal infection with different influenza virus subtypes.

VHL-Nb170-mediated NP ubiquitination is degraded through the autophagy‒lysosome pathway

Ubiquitinated proteins are degraded through the proteasomal pathway or the autophagy‒lysosome pathway.55 Therefore, we investigated the degradation mechanism of VHL-Nb170-mediated ubiquitinated NPs. Ubiquitination analysis via coimmunoprecipitation indicated that VHL-Nb170 significantly enhanced the binding of ubiquitin to the NPs (Fig. 6a). MG132 and LY294002 are inhibitors of the proteasome and autophagy‒lysosome pathways, respectively, and Western blotting analysis indicated that MG132 treatment did not affect NP degradation, whereas LY294002 treatment dramatically inhibited NP degradation (Fig. 6b), suggesting that ubiquitinated NP is degraded through the autophagy‒lysosome pathway.

To confirm this observation, we established a 293T cell line (293T-ATG5-/-) that lacks ATG5 (Supplementary Fig. 2), an essential protein in the autophagy-lysosomal pathway.56 Wild-type 293T cells and 293T-ATG5-/- cells were then transfected with pCAGGS-NP-eGFP together with either pCAGGS-Nb170 or pCAGGS-VHL-Nb170, and the fluorescence intensity of the cells was analyzed at 48 h posttransfection. For wild-type 293T cells, the fluorescence intensity was notably greater in the cells cotransfected with pCAGGS-NP-eGFP and pCAGGS-Nb170 than in the cells cotransfected with pCAGGS-NP-eGFP and pCAGGS-VHL-Nb170 (Fig. 6c); however, in the 293T-ATG5-/- cells, the fluorescence intensity was comparable between the cells cotransfected with pCAGGS-NP-eGFP and pCAGGS-Nb170 and the cells cotransfected with pCAGGS-NP-eGFP and pCAGGS-VHL-Nb170. Western blot analysis revealed that in 293T cells, the level of NP in the cells cotransfected with pCAGGS-NPs and pCAGGS-VHL-Nb170 was significantly lower than that in the cells cotransfected with pCAGGS-NPs and pCAGGS-Nb170; however, in 293T-ATG5-/- cells, the level of NP in the cells cotransfected with pCAGGS-NPs and pCAGGS-VHL-Nb170 was comparable to that in the cells cotransfected with pCAGGS-NPs and pCAGGS-Nb170 (Fig. 6d), indicating that ATG5 knockout significantly impaired the degradation of ubiquitinated NP.

Proteins targeted for degradation through the autophagy‒lysosome pathway may need to interact with specific autophagy receptors, such as p62, OPTN, NDP52, and TOLLIP.57 To identify the cargo receptor that specifically recognizes VHL-Nb170-mediated ubiquitinated NPs, coimmunoprecipitation assays were performed using lysates from cells transfected with plasmids encoding VHL-Nb170, the NP of the PR8 strain, and one of four HA-tagged receptors. The results demonstrated that the ubiquitinated NPs generated via VHL-Nb170 selectively interact with the cargo receptor OPTN but not with the other tested receptors (Fig. 6e). These results demonstrate that VHL-Nb170-mediated NP ubiquitination is degraded through the autophagy‒lysosome pathway mediated by the receptor OPTN.

Discussion

Here, we successfully developed a novel nanobody-based PROTAC, VHL-Nb170, that exhibits broad-spectrum anti-influenza activity. VHL-Nb170 can degrade the NP of all 16 HA subtypes and inhibit the replication of human H1N1 and H3N2 influenza viruses and avian H5N1, H7N9, and H9N2 viruses, which have pandemic potential. More importantly, when mice were administered mVHL-Nb170 by use of the clinically relevant AAV-LungM3 vector and subsequently challenged with lethal doses of influenza virus, 90% of the mice survived H1N1 virus infection, and 80% survived infection with the highly pathogenic H5N1 strain, demonstrating its potential for translational development as a broad-spectrum antiviral prophylactic.

The antiviral efficacy of Nb-PROTACs could be affected by the amount of target protein. The eight gene segments of influenza virus encode at least 11 distinct proteins that are expressed at various levels, and the abundance of the NP is second only to that of M1.58 In the present study, even though the Nb-PROTAC VHL-Nb170 targeted influenza virus NPs, it still efficiently reduced influenza virus replication in mice compared with that in mice administered AAV-LungM3. Mice receiving AAV-LungM3-mVHL-Nb170 exhibited an average reduction in H1N1 viral load of 94.51% and a 93.86% reduction in H5N1 viral load in their lungs (Fig. 5c, d), which enabled more than 80% of the mice to survive the lethal influenza virus infection. If an Nb-PROTAC is designed to target a viral protein with lower abundance, such as the polymerases PB2, PB1, or PA, its antiviral efficacy may be further enhanced.

The effectiveness of Nb-PROTACs largely depends on their ability to degrade the target protein, and the choice of nanobody is therefore critical for Nb-PROTAC efficacy. In our study, two of the five Nb-PROTACs we constructed were better able to degrade the influenza virus NP than the other three (Fig. 1c), and subsequent experiments revealed that VHL-Nb170 more potently inhibited the replication of influenza viruses than VHL-Nb135. As reported by Schmidt et al.51, Nb170 is a relatively weak inhibitor; however, in our study, VHL-Nb170 demonstrated the most potent inhibition of influenza virus replication, highlighting a fundamental distinction between inhibition and targeted degradation strategies. Indeed, when Nbs are expressed intracellularly, their efficacy is primarily determined by their ability to disrupt critical stages of the viral life cycle. The antiviral activity of Nb-PROTACs depends on whether Nbs can position the E3 ligase in a spatial conformation favorable for the formation of a productive ternary complex and efficient ubiquitination of the target protein. Our findings support the notion that the most effective antiviral Nbs may not inherently serve as optimal candidates for targeted protein degradation, as degradation efficiency relies on synergistic interactions within the ternary complex comprising the target protein, E3 ligase, and other associated factors.59,60

The E3 ubiquitin ligase VHL has been commonly used for the construction of PROTACs. Previous studies have indicated that VHL-mediated ubiquitinated proteins, such as kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog,48 src homology 2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase-2,49 and apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD,49 are degraded through the proteasomal pathway; however, the VHL-mediated ubiquitinated protein NP in our study and hypoxia-inducible factor-2α (HIF-2α) reported by Liu et al. are degraded through the autophagy‒lysosome pathway.61 We speculate that the recognition of autophagy receptors by ubiquitinated proteins, such as the interaction between HIF-2α and p6261 and that between NP and OPTN, may represent a primary mechanism underlying their degradation through the autophagy‒lysosome pathway. These findings indicate that the degradation pathway of ubiquitinated proteins may depend on the intrinsic properties of the target proteins themselves rather than on the specificity or activity of ubiquitin ligases.

VHL-Nb170 delivered via the AAV-LungM3 vector has been demonstrated to be an effective antiviral prophylactic agent. With ongoing advancements in novel technologies, the use of Nb-PROTACs may emerge as a highly promising therapeutic strategy. mRNA-based delivery has become increasingly feasible,62,63 as demonstrated by the clinical success of LNP-formulated mRNA vaccines against SARS-CoV-2. Moreover, inhalable mucus-penetrating LNPs have enabled pulmonary mRNA therapy in mice,64 and selective organ-targeting LNPs support tissue-specific mRNA delivery.65 Therefore, given the broad-spectrum antiviral effects demonstrated in this study, further investigation into the therapeutic potential of Nb-PROTACs against influenza virus via pulmonary mRNA delivery is warranted.

In conclusion, we established a novel antiviral strategy by converting nanobodies into PROTACs that selectively degrade the NP of influenza virus and demonstrated that Nb-PROTAC could efficiently inhibit the replication of different influenza viruses in vitro and in vivo. This proof-of-concept study paves the way for the development of novel antiviral strategies targeting diverse viruses, particularly those prone to antigenic drift or shift, such as influenza viruses and coronaviruses, as well as viruses that evade host immunity, such as African swine fever virus.66

Materials and methods

Ethics statements and facilities

All experiments involving highly pathogenic avian influenza viruses, such as H5 and H7 viruses, as well as a human-transmissible strain (A/Sichuan/01/2009, H1N1), were conducted in the enhanced animal biosafety level 3 (ABSL3) facility at the Harbin Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (HVRI, CAAS). Experiments involving low pathogenic influenza viruses were conducted in the enhanced biosafety level 2 (BSL-2 + ) facility at HVRI, CAAS. The study was performed in accordance with the recommendations of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Ministry of Science and Technology of China. The protocol for the animal studies was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of HVRI, CAAS (approval number: 240318-01-GJ). The animals used in this study were housed in individually ventilated cage systems.

Cells and viruses

The human embryonic kidney cell line 293T, murine lung epithelial cell line MLE-12, and human lung epithelial cell line A549 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). 293T and MLE-12 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, USA), whereas A549 cells were cultured in Ham’s F-12K medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS. Madin‒Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 5% newborn calf serum (NCS; Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) and maintained in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with 0.3% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma‒Aldrich, USA). All culture media were supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Procell, China). All the cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

All viruses used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Avian influenza A viruses were propagated in 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs, whereas human influenza viruses were grown in MDCK cells cultured in MEM supplemented with 0.3% BSA and 1 µg/mL L-1-tosylamide-2-phenylmethyl chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)-treated trypsin (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA). All viral stocks were stored in our laboratory.

Construction of plasmids

The genes encoding the α-domain of the human von Hippel‒Lindau (VHL) tumor suppressor (GenBank accession no. NP_000542.1), mouse VHL (GenBank accession no. NP_033533.1), and anti-NP nanobodies (Nb52, Nb77, Nb135, Nb170, and Nb355) and an anti-SARS-CoV-2 Spike nanobody (GenBank accession no. QNA42481.1) were synthesized and cloned into the lentiviral expression vector pLVX-CMV-Puromycin (Addgene plasmid #164589) and/or the pCMV-Myc vector (Clontech, 635689). The NP gene of the PR8 virus was cloned into the pCAGGS vector with no or an eGFP tag at its C-terminus, resulting in the constructs pCAGGS-NP and pCAGGS-NP-eGFP. The NP genes of 16 different influenza A virus subtypes (H1–H16; see Supplementary Table 1) were subsequently cloned into the pCAGGS vector. The ubiquitin gene was cloned into the pCAGGS vector with an HA tag at its C-terminus.

Degradation analysis

293T cells were seeded into 60-mm dishes or 6-well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 70% confluence. The cells were then cotransfected with pCAGGS-Nb or pCAGGS-VHL-Nb and either pCAGGS-NP-eGFP or pCAGGS-NP. To prepare the transfection complex, we diluted the plasmid DNA in 300 μL of Opti-MEM, followed by the addition of PEI at a ratio of 1 μg of DNA to 4 μL of PEI. The mixture was gently pipetted and incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes. The resulting DNA‒PEI complexes were added dropwise to the cells across the culture surface. Six hours posttransfection, the medium was replaced with fresh Opti-MEM to remove residual transfection reagents. At 48 h posttransfection, fluorescence signals were observed via a fluorescence microscope to assess NP-eGFP degradation, and the NP expression levels were analyzed via Western blotting.

Generation of stable-expressing cells

A second-generation lentiviral packaging system was used to generate stable mammalian cells that expressed the target genes. 293T cells were transfected as described for the transfection assay via a plasmid mixture consisting of the target expression plasmid, psPAX2, and pMD2.G at a ratio of 4:3:2 was diluted in Opti-MEM. Viral supernatants collected from the transfected 293T cells were used to transduce A549 and MLE-12 cells. At 48 h postinfection, the culture medium was replaced with puromycin-containing Opti-MEM selection medium (1 μg/mL for A549 cells and 1.5 μg/mL for MLE-12 cells). After antibiotic selection, the surviving cell population was cultured for further analysis.

Generation of the ATG5-knockout 293T cell line

The CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing system was used to generate an ATG5-knockout 293T (293T-ATG5-/-) cell line. Single-guide RNA (sgRNA) oligonucleotides (sgRNA-1: 5′-CACCAACTTGTTTCACGCTATATC-3′ and sgRNA-2: 5′-AAACGATATAGCGTGAAACAAGTT-3′) were cloned into the lentiCRISPRv2 vector (Addgene plasmid #98290). Then, the 293T cells were cotransfected with lentiCRISPRv2-sgRNA, psPAX2, and pMD2.G plasmids at a ratio of 4:3:2 via Lipofectamine LTX. Lentiviral supernatants were collected 48 h posttransfection. The 293T cells were then infected with the collected lentivirus. At 48 h post infection, the transduced cells were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 3–5 days to eliminate noninfected cells. Following selection, single puromycin-resistant cells were isolated via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) via a MA900 high-speed cell sorter (Sony, Japan) and seeded into 96-well plates to establish monoclonal populations. The knockout cell clones were validated at the DNA level by PCR and at the protein level by Western blotting.

Viral growth kinetics assay

To analyze viral growth kinetics in vitro, A549 and/or MLE-12 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and infected with viruses at an MOI of 0.01. Cells infected with human seasonal H1N1 and H3N2 viruses were incubated at 33 °C, whereas those infected with PR8, avian H5N1, H7N9, or H9N2 viruses were incubated at 37 °C. After 1 h of viral infection, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed three times with MEM. The cells were then maintained at either 33 °C or 37 °C in MEM/BSA supplemented with 0.2 μg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin. Culture supernatants were collected from triplicate wells at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h postinfection. Viral titers were determined via plaque assays in MDCK cells. All the experiments were performed in triplicate.

Viral titration

Plaque assays were performed to determine viral titers in the collected samples. Supernatants from A549 and MLE-12 cells infected with influenza viruses were harvested for titration. Briefly, MDCK cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured until they reached 100% confluence. The monolayers were washed three times with MEM and then infected with 10-fold serial dilutions of virus-containing supernatants prepared in MEM/BSA. After 1 h of adsorption at 33 °C or 37 °C, the inoculum was removed, and the cells were washed twice with MEM. The cells were overlaid with 1% Sea Plaque agarose in MEM/BSA containing 1 μg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin. Following incubation for 2–3 days, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS, and plaques were counted to determine viral titers.

Western blotting

Plasmid-transfected cells were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer supplemented with Halt protease inhibitor cocktail and benzonase for 1 h at 4 °C. The cell lysates were subsequently centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were mixed with SDS‒PAGE loading buffer and boiled for 10 minutes. Protein samples were separated by electrophoresis on a 4%–20% ExpressPlus PAGE gel and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (0.45 μm). The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After five washes with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20), the membrane was incubated for 1 h at room temperature with IRDye 800CW goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000) or IRDye 680RD goat anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000) secondary antibodies. After three additional washes with PBST, the immunolabeled proteins were visualized via an Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences, USA). Band intensities were quantified via ImageJ software.

Coimmunoprecipitation assay

For coimmunoprecipitation assays, 293T cells transfected with the indicated plasmids were lysed as described in the Western blotting procedure. The supernatants from the cell lysates were first incubated with 30 μL of Protein G agarose beads for 4 h at 4 °C on a flip shaker to preclear nonspecific binding proteins. After this incubation, the beads were removed via centrifugation, and the resulting supernatants were collected. The precleared supernatants were then incubated with 30 μL of anti-His-tag or anti-Myc-tag antibody-conjugated agarose beads overnight (i.e., > 12 h) at 4 °C on a shaker. Following this incubation, the beads were washed five times with cold NP-40 lysis buffer to remove nonspecific proteins. The washed beads were resuspended in PBS, mixed with SDS‒PAGE loading buffer, and boiled for 10 minutes. The samples were then analyzed by Western blotting.

Drug treatment assay

For the MG132 and LY294002 treatment assays, 293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with the indicated expression plasmids. After a 24 h incubation at 37 °C, the cells were treated with either DMSO (control), MG132 (10 μM), or LY294002 (20 μM) for 8 h. Following treatment, the cells were lysed, and the NP protein expression levels were analyzed via Western blotting. Band intensities were quantified via ImageJ software.

Intratracheal administration of AAV-LungM3 to mice

AAV-LungM3 constructs, including the AAV-LungM3-eGFP vector and AAV-LungM3-eGFP-mVHL-Nb170 (1 × 1013 vg/mL each), were obtained from OBiO Technology (Shanghai Co., Ltd.). Groups of 14 five-week-old female BALB/c mice (Vital River Laboratories, Beijing, China) were anesthetized with 3–5% isoflurane delivered via a gas anesthesia system (Yuyan Instruments Co., Ltd., China) and maintained under anesthesia with continuous isoflurane inhalation. For AAV-LungM3-mediated gene delivery, each mouse was positioned on an angled surgical platform and received two intratracheal administrations of AAV-LungM3 at 5 × 1010 vg per dose in 50 μL of PBS, with administrations spaced 3 days apart. The AAV-LungM3 suspension was delivered intratracheally via a microsprayer aerosolizer device (Yuyan Instruments Co., Ltd., China). Three mice per group were euthanized at 3 weeks post-administration. The efficacy of intratracheal delivery was validated by immunohistochemistry (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Mouse experiment

Three weeks after AAV-LungM3 administration, groups of mice (14 per group for AAV-LungM3-VHL-Nb170 and AAV-LungM3-eGFP vectors) were lightly anesthetized with 3–5% isoflurane and intranasally challenged with 5 MLD50 of H1N1 (PR8) or H5N1 (SD012) virus in a total volume of 50 μL. At 3 days postchallenge, four mice from each group were euthanized, and their organs were collected for virus titration in 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs. The remaining mice were monitored daily for changes in body weight and survival for up to 14 days. The criterion for determining mortality in mice was defined as a 25% reduction in body weight.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Viral replication kinetics were assessed via a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test, and degradation efficacy was analyzed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
