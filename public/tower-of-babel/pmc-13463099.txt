Mechanistic insights into phage dynamics to overcome intracellular bacterial reservoirs in urinary tract infections
Authors: Simone C Lieberknecht-Jouy, Dajana Pavlovic, Carmen Aguilar, Simon Peters, Lucas J Fein, Mathias Müsken, Tobias Weirauch, Annika Y Classen, Olaf Tyc, Silvia Würstle, Thomas A Wichelhaus, Maria J G T Vehreschild
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecurrent urinary tract infections frequently arise from uropathogenic bacteria invading bladder epithelial cells, where they establish persistent intracellular reservoirs. Phages represent a promising therapeutic alternative to antibiotics owing to their host specificity, ability to circumvent antibiotic resistance, and capacity to disrupt biofilms. In this study, we investigated whether lytic phages can gain access to human bladder epithelial cells and are able to target internalized bacteria using an in vitro bladder infection model. We show that phages can enter the bladder epithelium either via direct cellular uptake or through co-invasion with extracellularly infected bacteria. Notably, the presence of extracellular uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) substantially enhanced intracellular phage uptake, revealing a previously unrecognized bacteria-assisted pathway for intracellular phage delivery that may facilitate access to intracellular bacterial reservoirs. Phage treatment prevented epithelial infection by UPEC and preserved urothelial cell integrity. Moreover, phages reduced intracellular bacterial burden, although complete eradication was not achieved and efficacy was limited to early stages of infection. Together, these findings advance understanding of phage–pathogen–human interactions to optimize phage-based strategies for recurrent urinary tract infections.Subject terms: Microbiology, Urology

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are a major global public health issue affecting over 400 million people globally each year1,2. A defining clinical feature of UTIs is their high rate of recurrence, with many patients experiencing repeated episodes over months or years. Recurrent infection not only diminishes quality of life but also increases the risk of severe complications such as pyelonephritis and urosepsis3,4. Current management strategies largely rely on repeated courses of antibiotics or long-term, low-dose prophylaxis5. Although these approaches can provide temporary symptom control, they contribute to the escalating global crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) by selecting for drug-resistant uropathogens. The limitations of antibiotic-based strategies underscore the need for alternative antimicrobial approaches and more precise interventions capable of preventing or eliminating persistent infections.

Bacteriophages (phages) - viruses that infect and lyse bacteria - have re-emerged as an alternative to conventional antibiotic treatments. Their inherent biological properties, including high host specificity, widespread environmental abundance, and the ability to infect antibiotic-resistant bacteria6,7 or to disrupt bacterial biofilms8–10, make them attractive therapeutic agents. These characteristics have renewed interest in phage-based therapeutics as part of a broader effort to expand the antimicrobial arsenal in the face of rising resistance.

Among the pathogens responsible for UTIs, uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) accounts for the majority of both uncomplicated and complicated cases3,11. Bacteria causing recurrent UTI (rUTI) are often phenotypically or genotypically identical to the strain that caused the initial infection. This phenomenon may be explained by the ability of UPEC to be internalized upon contact with epithelial cells of the bladder mucosa. There, they can replicate forming a persisting reservoir undetected by immune surveillance mechanisms and protected from antibiotics12,13. The intracellular localization of UPEC raises a critical question for antimicrobial therapies that primarily act in the extracellular environment. Although bacteriophages do not infect eukaryotic cells or replicate using host cellular machinery, growing evidence suggests that phage particles can nevertheless enter mammalian cells14–16. Several mechanisms of uptake have been proposed, including nonspecific micropinocytosis14,15,17,18 and phagocytosis19–21, as well as receptor-mediated endocytosis involving clathrin- or caveolae-dependent pathways22. Once internalized, phages have been observed to traffic through intracellular compartments such as endosomes and lysosomes14,23. These interactions appear most prominent in professional phagocytes21,24–26, but phage internalization has also been reported in a variety of non-phagocytic cell types15,27.

Despite these observations, the mechanisms governing phage uptake in bladder cells, intracellular trafficking, and interactions with intracellular bacteria remain poorly defined. Here, we investigate the cellular dynamics of phage interactions with human bladder epithelial cells in the context of UPEC infection. By combining mechanistic analyses with an in vitro infection model, we examine how phages enter epithelial cells and explore the implications of these processes for targeting bacterial populations associated with persistent and recurrent urinary tract infections.

Results

Bladder epithelial cells internalize bacteriophages

Bladder epithelial cells are highly specialized to withstand a challenging environment characterized by microbial exposure, toxic substances, mechanical stretch, and both hydrostatic and osmotic pressures28. Building on a modified version of the bladder model described by Mulvey et al.29, we investigated if phages are able to penetrate the bladder wall and, if so, by which mechanisms this occurs (see also Methods section “Phage invasion assays”). To this end, human cancer-derived 5637 HTB-9 bladder epithelial cells were infected either with a single phage (MM02 or G10400; final titer: 10⁸ PFU/mL) or with the corresponding phage–UPEC combination (MM02 with UPEC 8923 or G10400 with UPEC 7958; final bacterial concentration: 10⁷ CFU/mL) for 3 or 24 h. Extracellular bacteria were eliminated by 2 h of gentamicin treatment, and extracellular phages were subsequently inactivated with ferrous ammonium sulfate (FAS), a virucidal agent that selectively inactivates free phage particles. Positive controls containing only bacteria with bladder cells and negative controls with only phages (without bladder cells, to account for phage adhesion to the plastic surface) were included.

In negative control wells containing phages only (without bladder epithelial cells), residual titers were 6.2 × 10² PFU/ml for MM02 and 8.0 × 10² PFU/ml for G10400, indicating that ~0.0006–0.0008% of phage particles adhered to the plastic surface and survived FAS treatment. To determine the accurate number of intracellular phages, the phage counts detected in the negative controls were subtracted from those measured in the experimental groups. This correction additionally accounted for the fact that, in contrast to the experimental wells, the bottom surface of the negative control wells was not covered by cells, resulting in a substantially larger exposed plastic surface area. In all experimental setups, phage particles were internalized by bladder cells. Notably, for both phages, intracellular phage uptake was markedly higher when phages were co-administered with UPEC (MOI = 10) than when phages were applied alone. After 3 h of incubation, corrected intracellular titers of phage MM02 alone were 8.69 × 10³ PFU/mL, whereas titers increased significantly to 9.99 × 10⁴ PFU/mL (p = 0.002) when UPEC 8923 was added simultaneously to the extracellular medium. Similarly, intracellular titers of phage G10400 alone reached 5.76 × 10³ PFU/mL after 3 h, while co-incubation with UPEC 7958 resulted in titers of 2.59 × 10⁵ PFU/mL (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). These findings demonstrate that phages enter the eukaryotic epithelium not only via cellular uptake mechanisms but particularly while residing within extracellularly infected bacterial hosts, underscoring the contribution of host-mediated phage uptake.

Furthermore, extending the cellular exposure time from 3 to 24 h resulted in higher intracellular phage counts under both conditions, while uptake remained consistently greater in the phage–bacterial co-infection group, suggesting that phage uptake is time-dependent. After correction for phage particles attached to the plastic surface, the intracellular titers in the phage-only groups were 1.39 × 105 PFU/mL for MM02 and 2.59 × 10⁴ PFU/mL for G10400. Simultaneous infection with corresponding UPEC over 24 h yielded corrected phage titers as high as 4.73 × 105 PFU/mL for MM02 and 8.63 × 105 PFU/mL for G10400 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1 B).

Ultrastructural evidence of myovirus phage internalization into urothelial cells

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed to substantiate the hypothesis that phage particles can access bladder cells within extracellularly infected hosts. The phages G10400 and MM02 used in this study belong to the class Caudoviricetes and exhibit a myovirus morphotype. TEM analysis revealed that both phages measured a total length of 246/251 nm (head dimension 122 × 89/124 × 95 nm for phage MM02/G10400) (Fig. 2A, B). We observed phage adsorption to and phage replication within UPEC 7958 (Fig. 2C, D). Importantly, phage particles were also seen within bladder epithelial cells in co-invasion experiments involving phage G10400 and UPEC 7958, where they localized inside intracellular vacuoles, demonstrating that phages are capable of entering urothelial cells (Fig. 2E, F).

Phages prevent UPEC Invasion but Show Limited Activity Against Established Intracellular Bacteria

To assess the ability of phages to prevent bacterial infection of the bladder epithelium, HTB-9 cells were simultaneously exposed for 3 h to either UPEC alone, phage alone, or matched phage–host combinations (UPEC 8923 with phage MM02 or UPEC 7958 with phage G10400) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 (see also Methods section “Phage invasion assays”). Mean intracellular bacterial titers reached 4.2 × 10³ CFU/mL for UPEC 8923 and 3.8 × 10³ CFU/mL for UPEC 7958 after 3 h of infection. In contrast, no intracellular UPECs were detected when phages were co-administered with their respective bacterial hosts, indicating that phage treatment effectively prevented bacterial invasion of the bladder epithelial cell layer (Fig. 3A).

Next, we evaluated whether phages could target established intracellular UPEC within a human urothelial cell model (see also Methods section “Intracellular bacterial killing”). HTB-9 bladder epithelial monolayers were infected with either UPEC 8923 or UPEC 7958 for 3 h, followed by medium replacement containing gentamicin (200 µg/mL) to eliminate extracellular bacteria for 2 h. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with a lower maintenance dose of gentamicin (50 µg/mL), administered alone or together with phages MM02 or G10400 at an MOI of 10. At 24 h post-infection, cells were lysed, and intracellular bacterial loads were quantified. Positive-control wells were treated identically, except that no phages were added. Mean intracellular viable bacterial counts in control wells were 2.86 × 10³ CFU/mL for UPEC 8923 and 2.3 × 10³ CFU/mL for UPEC 7958 24 h after infection. In phage-treated wells, bacterial counts were 2.87 × 10³ CFU/mL for UPEC 8923 and 2.3 × 10³ CFU/mL for UPEC 7958 (Fig. 3 B). Thus, phage treatment initiated 5 h after bacterial infection did not reduce intracellular bacterial loads, indicating that phages were ineffective against established intracellular UPEC.

To investigate whether the timing of phage administration affects their ability to target intracellular pathogens, we performed a subsequent experiment in which the interval between UPEC infection and phage treatment was shortened. UPEC 8923, which rapidly invades bladder epithelial cells, was allowed to infect HTB-9 cells for 0.5, or 1 h, followed by a 30-minute treatment with gentamicin to eliminate extracellular bacteria. Phage MM02 was then added and maintained 24 h post-infection. Quantification of intracellular bacteria following host cell lysis showed that phage treatment 1.5 h after infection onset reduced the intracellular UPEC burden by 24% (mean of biological replicates: 5.96 × 102 CFU/mL versus 4.5 × 102 CFU/mL; estimated ratio of geometric means, 0.74). Addition of phage at 1 h post infection resulted in a 34% reduction in intracellular load (mean of biological replicates: 2.09 × 102 CFU/mL versus 1.38 × 102 CFU/mL; estimated ratio of geometric means, 0.64) (Fig. 4). Although these reductions did not reach statistical significance, they suggest biologically meaningful bacterial killing, with the greater reduction observed when phage treatment was initiated earlier post-infection.

Role of lysosomal pH in intracellular bacterial elimination

To examine whether the low pH conditions found in lysosomes affect intracellular bacteriophage-mediated bacterial killing, we evaluated the ability of phages G10400 and MM02 to lyse their host bacteria at pH 4.5 (representative of lysosomal conditions)30 compared with standard LB growth medium at pH 7. After 6 h of co-incubation (a time frame preceding the development of phage resistance for these phages), bacterial counts were determined. Phage treatment in standard LB medium resulted in complete (MOI 0.01) or substantial (MOI 1) reduction of bacterial counts, whereas treatment with the same phages at the same concentrations in LB medium adjusted to pH 4.5 resulted in only marginal reductions in bacterial numbers (Fig. 5).

Bacteriophages preserve bladder epithelial cell integrity

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is a cytoplasmic enzyme present in a wide range of cell types and serves as a marker for cytotoxicity due to its release following membrane damage31,32. To evaluate the impact of phages and bacteria on bladder cell viability, LDH release was quantified as an indicator of membrane integrity and cytotoxicity. Exposure to UPEC strains 8923 or 7958 for 24 h resulted in a marked increase in LDH release compared with the untreated cell-only control, indicating substantial cytotoxicity. In contrast, treatment with the phages the MM02 or G10400 alone appeared to exert a protective effect, as LDH activity in these wells was lower than that observed in the control (Fig. 6A, B). Furthermore, when UPEC and the corresponding phage were added simultaneously at an MOI of 10 for 24 h, LDH levels were below those measured in the control condition and significantly lower than in wells exposed to UPEC alone (Fig. 6 A, B). This trend suggests that the phages successfully lysed the bacteria before they could damage the urothelial cell layer.

Discussion

Escherichia coli capable of persisting within bladder epithelial cells are a key contributor to the high recurrence rates of urinary tract infections. This intracellular niche provides protection from urinary washout and host immune defenses12,13,33. Despite increasing antimicrobial resistance and the limited intracellular penetration of most antibiotics - more than two-thirds of which fail to efficiently accumulate in mammalian cells - antibiotics remain the primary treatment for recurrent UTI34–37. These limitations underscore the need for alternative therapeutic strategies. Bacteriophages have emerged as promising natural antimicrobials against extracellular bacterial pathogens; however, their interactions with mammalian cells and their potential activity against intracellular bacterial populations remain poorly understood.

Accumulating evidence indicates that bacteriophages can enter a variety of mammalian cell types, with uptake routes and capacities strongly influenced by cellular function, such as professional phagocytes versus non-phagocytic cells15,21,24–27,38. The principal internalization mechanisms described to date involve micropinocytosis, clathrin- or caveolae-mediated endocytosis, and phagocytosis, often acting in combination14,15,17,19–21,39. Seminal studies by Lehti et al.27 and Bichet et al.40 revealed that mammalian cells can accumulate substantial numbers of phage particles. Phage internalization rates varied markedly among cell lines, with some exhibiting high uptake (e.g., A549 lung epithelial cells) and others showing minimal internalization (e.g., HT29 colonic epithelial cells and BJ fibroblasts)15. Despite evidence that E. coli bacteriophages can enter mammalian cells27,41, their interactions with human bladder epithelial cells remain largely unclear.

Here, we demonstrate that bacteriophages are actively internalized by human bladder epithelial cells, with uptake significantly enhanced in the presence of extracellular UPEC, highlighting a previously unrecognized role of bacteria in facilitating phage entry into urothelial cells. Quantification of intracellular phage titers revealed that counts in the combined phage-UPEC condition exceeded those observed with phage exposure alone by up to 2 log units. These findings suggest an additional host-associated uptake pathway. Such a “Trojan horse” mechanism, whereby phages are engulfed together with extracellularly infected UPEC, has been described for Chlamydiophila spp.42, Staphylococcus aureus43 and Mycobacteria spp.44. Our findings are consistent with a similar co-internalization mechanism in bladder epithelial cells, although this conclusion is based on indirect evidence. The underlying endocytic pathways remain to be elucidated.

Phage internalization increased with longer exposure times. Importantly, internalized phages remained stable for at least 24 h and retained infectivity upon recovery. These findings demonstrate that phages remain viable following cellular uptake. Multiple strategies have been developed to enhance phage delivery into mammalian cells, including chemical surface modification, liposomal encapsulation, and genetic engineering45–48. From a therapeutic perspective, our findings raise the possibility that a phage-first treatment strategy may enhance intracellular efficacy by preserving extracellular bacteria that facilitate phage uptake.

Concurrently, assessment of LDH release indicated that phage exposure did not compromise epithelial cell viability. LDH release is a well-established surrogate marker of membrane damage and cytotoxicity31,32. In the present study, exposure of bladder epithelial cells to UPEC strains 8923 and 7958 resulted in a pronounced increase in LDH release, confirming the pathogenic potential of the tested UPEC strains. In contrast, treatment with phages MM02 or G10400 alone did not induce cytotoxicity; rather, LDH levels were lower than those observed in untreated control cells, indicating excellent tolerability. Simultaneous exposure of urothelial cells to UPEC and the corresponding phage completely abrogated UPEC-induced cytotoxicity, as evidenced by LDH falling below baseline levels. Phage treatment effectively prevented UPEC-mediated membrane damage, likely through rapid bacterial lysis prior to host cell injury. Together, these results demonstrate that phages are non-toxic to bladder epithelial cells and can fully neutralize UPEC-induced cytotoxicity under the conditions tested.

In this study, despite robust intracellular phage accumulation, none of the tested phages reduced intracellular UPEC burdens when infection was established for more than 5 h. Consistent with our observations, a human bladder organoid model showed that phage HP3 significantly reduced UPEC adherence but failed to eliminate intracellular reservoirs49. In contrast, Møller-Olsen et al.41 demonstrated phagocytosis-mediated uptake of engineered K1F phage into T24 bladder epithelial cells, resulting in intracellular UPEC killing. Similarly, Zhang et al. reported that S. aureus phage vB_SauM_JS25 penetrated bovine mammary epithelial cells and eliminated intracellular S. aureus in a time-dependent manner50. These divergent outcomes may partly reflect differences in host cell types. As HTB-9 cells are bladder carcinoma-derived, altered endocytic and trafficking properties of transformed cells may also have influenced our findings. Supporting the notion that the ability of phages to reach their intracellular host depends, at least in part, on the eukaryotic cell type, Joshi et al.51 reported a phage-mediated reduction of intracellular UPEC in VK2 vaginal epithelial cells but not in HTB-9 bladder epithelial cells, the same cell line used in the present study. Similarly, cell-penetrating peptide (CPP)-displaying phages significantly reduced intracellular Salmonella in only one of three cell lines, an effect that correlated with differences in cytosolic bacterial localization. The antibacterial effect was attributed to the selective eradication of cytosolic Salmonella, as intracellular replication and cytosolic residency vary across cell lines48.

Beyond differences in cell lines, an analysis of previous reports indicates that the timing of phage treatment may impact outcomes. Studies demonstrating phage effectiveness against intracellular hosts typically added phages shortly after bacterial infection, whereas studies that failed to show intracellular phage activity introduced phages after UPEC infection had been established for a longer period. In our initial experimental setup, phages were added 5 h after UPEC infection and failed to reduce intracellular bacterial loads. Similar outcomes were reported when natural phages were added 3 h49,51, or 12 h48 post-infection, despite satisfactory intracellular phage uptake. In contrast, studies in which phages were administered 1 h after bacterial infection demonstrated intracellular bacterial reduction41,52. Notably, an engineered E. coli phage displaying epidermal growth factor (EGF) entered mammalian cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis, bypassed phagocytic pathways, and more efficiently eliminated intracellular bacteria than its wild-type counterpart52.

Distinct routes of phage entry into mammalian cells likely predetermine intracellular trafficking and fate. Likewise, internalized UPEC may reside either freely in the cytosol53,54 or within membrane-bound compartments55,56. Physical separation of bacteria and phages into distinct vesicles may restrict phage access to their host receptors. Moreover, intracellular stress conditions and nutrient limitation within endosomal or lysosomal compartments may alter bacterial metabolism, including growth rate, thereby impairing phage replication57–59. Cytosolic E. coli can additionally produce outer membrane vesicles to counteract xenophagy60,61, a phenomenon that has also been linked to reduced susceptibility to phage infection62. Together, these observations suggest that bacterial compartmentalization and metabolic state could critically determine susceptibility to intracellular phage attack. Possibly, only if phage and UPEC are internalized in close temporal proximity, their phagosomes may fuse allowing phage attachment to their host receptors unhindered by a lipid membrane. Although transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 2) confirmed the presence of phages within intracellular vesicles, the precise subcellular localization of phages relative to intracellular bacteria remains to be determined. In particular, it is unclear whether both entities share the same intracellular compartments, which is likely a key factor influencing phage access to bacterial targets. Advanced imaging approaches, such as confocal microscopy with co-localization analysis, will be instrumental in further elucidating these spatial relationships.

To directly test the influence of timing, we reduced the interval between UPEC infection and phage exposure from 5 h to 1.5 h and 1 h. Under these conditions, intracellular UPEC loads were modestly but consistently reduced, with greater reductions observed at shorter intervals. Our findings indicate that the efficacy of phages against intracellular bacteria is strongly influenced by the timing of phage administration, which correlates with the intracellular processing status of both phages and bacteria, defining a critical window for effective intervention. Importantly, UPEC do not undergo net intracellular replication in HTB-9 bladder epithelial cells; instead, intracellular bacterial numbers remain stable or gradually decrease over time. We acknowledge that increasing extracellular bacterial abundance during the course of infection may contribute to higher phage availability and uptake. However, this factor alone is unlikely to fully explain the observed differences in intracellular phage activity. Accordingly, Fig. 4 should be interpreted as evidence for a temporal window of intracellular phage activity rather than as an assessment of maximal antibacterial efficacy.

Given that lysosomes constitute a major intracellular compartment encountered following phagocytic uptake63, lysosomal acidification may represent a key barrier to effective intracellular phage activity. Our findings demonstrate that acidic conditions resembling the lysosomal environment markedly impair bacteriophage-mediated bacterial killing. Whereas phages G10400 and MM02 efficiently reduced bacterial counts in standard LB medium at pH 7, their antibacterial activity was substantially diminished at pH 4.5 despite identical phage concentrations and incubation conditions. These results suggest that low pH directly interferes with critical steps of the phage infection cycle, ultimately reducing lytic efficacy64,65. Of note, lysosomes containing UPEC have been reported to fail to undergo normal acidification, a process ordinarily required for efficient bacterial clearance66. This impaired lysosomal maturation may create a more permissive intracellular environment for phage infection. Whether altered lysosomal acidification in UPEC-infected cells modulates intracellular phage activity in vivo remains to be determined.

Taken together, our results, together with current evidence, show that phage targeting of intracellular bacteria is feasible but strongly depends on eukaryotic cell type and the timing of phage administration. These findings highlight both the potential and current limitations of phage therapy against intracellular bacterial reservoirs. Overcoming intracellular barriers through optimized treatment timing, improved intracellular delivery, and combination approaches with antibiotics or phage-derived therapeutics will be important for clinical translation. A better understanding of phage trafficking and activity within host cells will be essential for developing effective treatments for recurrent and persistent urinary tract infections.

Methods

Bacterial strains

The two uropathogenic gentamicin-susceptible E. coli isolates used in this study, UPEC 7958 and UPEC 8923 were provided by the Department of Medical Microbiology and Infection Control at the University Hospital Frankfurt. They were clinical urine isolates from patients with clinically confirmed UTI ( ≥ 100.000 CFU/mL with leukocyturia). Bacteria were aerobically grown at 37 °C in either Lysogeny Broth (LB; Roth, Germany) at 180 rpm or on LB 1,5% agar plates67. All isolates were stored in 25% glycerol stocks at −74 °C until further use.

Phage stocks

The two phages vB_EcoM-MM02 (hereafter referred to as phage MM02) and vB_EcoM-G10400 (hereafter referred to as phage G10400), along with their respective host strains, were kindly provided by the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ), Braunschweig, Germany68. They belong both to the class of Caudoviricetes with the morphotypes myovirus. Phage stocks were obtained using the double agar overlay method (DAO). Briefly, an overnight culture of the host strain was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 and grown for 1 h to an ∼OD600 of 0.2 at 37 °C, using a rotary shaker at 180 rpm. 100 μL of the bacterial culture and 100 μL of phage stock at a dilution that yielded a web pattern in preliminary experiments were added to 3 mL pre-warmed (55 °C) LB soft agar of 0.4% and poured onto 90 mm round LB 1.5% agar plates. The plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. The plates were then agitated for 4 h with 2 mL SM buffer. The top layer was removed and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was filter-sterilised through a 0.45 μm pore size filter, followed by a filtration step through a 0.22 μm pore size filter (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). The resulting phage stock titer was determined using plaque assays. Stocks were stored at 4 °C. Phage G10400 (NCBI accession no. MK327937) was propagated on E. coli DSM 103266 and phage MM02 (NCBI accession no. MK373784) on E. coli DSM 498.

Tissue cell culture

The human urinary bladder epithelial cell line, 5637 HTB-9 was acquired from CLS Cell Lines Services (Germany). This cell line was originally derived from a grade II bladder carcinoma of a male patient. The HTB-9 cells were cultured in uncoated T175 flasks containing modified RPMI Medium (ThermoFisher, USA) supplemented with 10% v/v heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Merck, Germany) and were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 under a humidified atmosphere.

This study constitutes basic research and did not involve human participants, human data, animals, or animal-derived material. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Double agar overlay method (DAO) - MD/SP (multiple dilutions on single plates)

Phage titers were determined using the MD/SP (multiple dilutions on single plates) double agar overlay method69–71. Here, serial dilutions of phage(s) proliferate in a soft top agar layer, while bacteria are fed from an underlying film of solid agar. Bacterial cultures were grown overnight, adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.1, and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with shaking (180 rpm) to reach an OD600 of approximately 0.2. Aliquots (150 µl) of bacterial culture were mixed with 4 mL of molten 0.4% (w/v) LB agar maintained at 55 °C and poured onto 120-mm square LB agar plates (1.5% w/v). Serially diluted phage suspensions (5 µl) were spotted onto the overlays, air-dried, and plates were incubated inverted overnight at 37 °C.

Phage invasion assays

For invasion assays, the HTB-9 bladder epithelial cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of ~2 × 105 cells/mL in 500 µl RPMI medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and allowed to grow for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂29. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates and three technical replicates per condition. For phage-only conditions, phage MM02 or phage G10400 were added to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁸ PFU/mL. For co-infection experiments, UPEC strain 8923 or UPEC strain 7958 (OD₆₀₀ = 0.3; ~1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was added simultaneously with phages at the same titers used in phage-only conditions to achieve a final bacterial concentration of 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL (MOI = 10). Wells containing RPMI medium only received phages to control for nonspecific adherence to plastic. After 3 (Fig. 1A; Fig. 3A; Fig. 7, group A) or 24 (Fig. 1B; Fig. 7, group B) h of incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, wells were washed three times with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Merck, Germany). Extracellular bacteria were eliminated by incubation with gentamicin (300 µg/mL) for 2 h. Extracellular phage particles were inactivated by treatment with 0.2 M ferrous ammonium sulfate (Merck, Germany) for 15 min72,73, followed by another washing step with DPBS. Cells were then lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100 to recover intracellular phages and bacteria. Intracellular phage titers were determined using the double-agar overlay method, and intracellular bacterial counts were quantified by serial dilution and plating on LB agar.

Phage invasion assays.

HTB-9 bladder epithelial cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured to confluence for 24 h. The cells were then exposed to either UPEC alone or UPEC plus phage (MOI = 10) for 3 h (A) or 24 h (B). Extracellular bacteria were eliminated by gentamicin (300 µg/mL) for 2 h and extracellular phages were inactivated with FAS.

To correct for phage attachment to the plastic surface, phage titers obtained from phage-only control wells (phages added to wells without bladder cells) were subtracted from those of the experimental groups. In these controls, the well bottom was not covered by cells, in contrast to the experimental conditions, resulting in a substantially larger exposed plastic surface area. Therefore, accurate determination of intracellular phage numbers required adjustment of the experimental counts using the negative control values while accounting for the increased exposed plastic surface in the controls. To estimate this contribution, the surface area of the well bottom was related to the total medium-contacting surface of the well at a filling height of 4.1 mm (corresponding to a volume of 500 µL) and a diameter of 16.2 mm. Based on the formulas for circular area (A = πr²) and cylindrical surface area (S = πr² + 2πrh), the bottom surface area accounted for 49% of the total medium-contacting surface. Consequently, phage titers obtained from the negative controls were multiplied by a factor of 0.51 prior to subtraction from the phage titers of the experimental groups. In preliminary experiments, phage susceptibility to the virucide FAS was assessed, and stability in Triton X-100 and RPMI medium was evaluated (Methods section “Virucide susceptibility” and “Phage stability in Triton X-100 and RPMI medium”). Intracellular bacterial killing

HTB-9 bladder epithelial cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of ~1 × 10⁴ cells/mL and grown to confluency for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂29. Three experimental conditions were performed to evaluate intracellular UPEC elimination, including positive controls (bladder cells infected with UPEC only) and a negative control (bladder cells only). All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates and three technical replicates per condition.

For the primary experimental setup, cells were infected with UPEC 8923 (for phage MM02) or UPEC 7958 (for phage G10400) for 3 h. Cells were then washed three times with PBS, and fresh medium containing gentamicin (300 µg/mL) was added for 2 h to eliminate extracellular bacteria. The medium was aspirated, cells were washed twice with PBS, and phages MM02 or G10400 were added at a MOI of 10 (each reaching a final concentration of 108 PFU/ml) in RPMI medium containing gentamicin (50 µg/mL). After 19 h of incubation (and thus 24 h after beginning of experiment), extracellular phages were inactivated by treatment with 0.2 M FAS, followed by three washes with PBS. Cells were lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100, and lysates were plated on LB agar to quantify surviving intracellular bacteria (Fig. 3B; Fig. 8, group A).

Effect of acidic pH

To evaluate the effect of lysosomal pH on bacteriophage-mediated bacterial killing, phages G10400 and MM02 were tested for their ability to lyse their respective host bacteria under acidic conditions. Bacterial cultures were incubated with phages in LB medium adjusted to pH 4.5, representative of lysosomal conditions, or in standard LB medium at pH 7 as a control. Phages were added at multiplicities of infection (MOIs) of 1 and 0.01. Following 6 h of static co-incubation at 37 °C bacterial survival was assessed by enumeration of colony-forming units (CFUs). The 6-hour incubation period was selected to minimize the influence of phage resistance development.

Lactate dehydrogenase assay

The level of LDH release was used as a marker to evaluate cellular damage74. HTB-9 bladder cells were grown to a lawn over 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Thereafter, phages (MM02 or G10400), UPEC (8923 or 7958), or the combination of both at an MOI of 10 were added in the same concentrations as in the phage invasion assays, including an untreated negative control. After an incubation period of 24 h, the medium was aspirated and centrifuged using an Eppendorf 5425 R centrifuge (Eppendorf AG, Germany) at 8000 × g for 20 min to remove cell debris and bacteria. The LDH concentration in the supernatant was quantified respecting to the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine

(IFCC) reference method using the LDHI2 assay (Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All measurements were performed in biological triplicate, and results were referenced to the untreated control.

Transmission electron microscopy

To observe phage morphology, negative staining was performed as previously described in ref. 75. In brief, phage particles in a suspension adsorbed to a thin carbon support film, which was washed twice and stained with a 2% (w/v) aqueous uranyl acetate solution (UAc), pH 5.0, before being taken up with a 300 mesh copper grid. Excessive liquid on the grid was taken up with a filter paper followed by heat drying on a light bulb. Intracellular uptake was visualized by preparing ultrathin sections as previously described, with slight modifications76. Cells were detached using 0.25% Trypsin–EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich), collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min, and the resulting cell pellets (untreated / phage-treated / phage+bacteria-treated) were fixed in EM-HEPES buffer (0.1 M HEPES, 0.09 M sucrose, 10 mM CaCl2, 10 mM MgCl2, pH 6.9) including 5% paraformaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde. After two washing steps, cells were treated with 1% OsO4 (in EM HEPES buffer) for 1 h at room temperature, washed twice with EM-HEPES buffer and dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol on ice (10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 2 × 100% EtOH for 15 min each). The 70% EtOH step included 2% UAc and an incubation over night at 4 °C. The samples were stepwise infiltrated with LR White resin and polymerized. Ultrathin sections (approx. 50–70 nm) were generated with a ultramicrotome (UC7, Leica Microsystems) and counterstained with uranylacetate and lead citrate.

Negative stained samples and ultrathin sections were examined in a Zeiss EM 910 or Zeiss Libra120Plus transmission electron microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) at an acceleration voltage of 80 kV respectively 120 kV. Image acquisition and phage measurements were done with the ITEM software (Olympus Soft Imaging Solutions, Münster, Germany).

Virucide susceptibility

Phages adhere strongly to plastic surfaces; therefore, negative controls consisting of 24-well plates filled only with cell culture medium (RPMI) and phages tested positive for phages, with titers of up to 10³ when no virucide was applied. Consequently, a set of six previously described and characterized E. coli phages68 was tested for sensitivity to the virucide ferrous ammonium sulfate. Briefly, ferrous ammonium sulfate was added to serial dilutions of all phages to a final concentration of 0.2 mM. After an incubation period of 15 min, the titration series was spotted onto agar plates according to the double agar overlay (DAO) method. Each series included a positive control consisting of phage and host strain without virucide treatment. Phage titers were determined, and the reduction in phage titer following virucide exposure was quantified. The phages MM02 and G10400 used in this study exhibited sufficient sensitivity to the virucide (3-log reduction) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Phage stability in Triton X-100 and RPMI medium

Phage stability was assessed in RPMI medium and 0.1% Triton X-100. After 24 h of incubation in the respective media at 37 °C, phage titers were determined using the DAO method and compared with those of the same phage stocks stored at 4 °C in PBS. Phage titers remained stable in RPMI medium. Notably, titers following incubation with Triton X-100 were slightly higher than those of PBS controls, and plaque visibility was enhanced.

Statistical analysis

R (Version 4.5.1)77 was used for data aggregation, statistical analysis, and visualization. LDH levels were evaluated using Kruskal–Wallis tests. Log10-transformed phage titers and CFU counts were compared using two-sided Welch’s t-tests. A significance level of 0.05 was applied throughout. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing per outcome and combination of UPEC and phage using the Holm–Bonferroni method. Dunn’s tests were used for post hoc pairwise comparisons following Kruskal–Wallis tests.
