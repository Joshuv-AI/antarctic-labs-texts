High-flow nasal cannula in adults with chronic respiratory diseases during physical exercise: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Eduardo Moya-Gallardo, Jeniffer Fajardo-Gutiérrez, Karol Acevedo, Francisca Verdugo-Paiva, Rocío Bravo-Jeria, Luis Ortiz-Muñoz, Felipe Contreras-Briceño, Maximiliano Espinosa-Ramírez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) affect at least 545 million people globally, leading to symptoms such as dyspnoea, fatigue and limited physical activity. Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) programmes aim to improve the exercise capacity and quality of life of patients with CRD through exercise training. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) therapy shows potential as an adjunct treatment during exercise, but its effects on CRD populations are unclear. The purpose of this systematic review was to evaluate the effects of HFNC during exercise in people with CRD.MethodsA systematic review was conducted and eight databases and other resources were searched from inception (28 June 2022) to 4 April 2023. Studies that used adult patients with CRD and randomised controlled trial that compared the effect of HFNC versus standard care (conventional oxygen therapy or room air) during exercise were included. Two authors independently selected trials, extracted the data, assessed risks of bias and employed the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach to judging the certainty of evidence. We pooled trials using random-effect models and inverse variance estimation.ResultsSeventeen studies (n=8406) were included in the review (570 patients). The evidence suggests that HFNC increases exercise time after multiple training sessions (weighted mean difference (WMD)=160.58 s; 95% 95% CI=67.32–253.83, 2 studies) and increase after a single session (WMD=72.10 s; 95% CI=28.95–115.24, 11 studies). HFNC may result in little improvements in secondary outcomes (quality of life, dyspnoea, comfort, complications and adherence).DiscussionThe evidence suggests that HFNC may increase functional exercise capacity and positively enhance secondary outcomes. Continued research is justified to elucidate the role of HFNC in PR during exercise training.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42022336263.

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) are a diverse group of conditions that impair the structure and function of the airways and lung tissue.1 These diseases are prevalent worldwide, with estimates suggesting that they affect 7.4% of the global population.2 Despite differences in the clinical presentations of these diseases, many individuals experience cross-cutting symptoms, such as cough, dyspnoea and peripheral fatigue, which negatively impact their functional exercise capacity and health-related quality of life (QoL).1 3 The intensity of these symptoms is directly associated with signs of systemic disorders, such as sarcopenia and physical effort intolerance.4 These impaired conditions often result in a gradual limitation of physical activity and social participation restriction.1 4 In this context, pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) programmes emerge as a comprehensive intervention aimed at improving the social, psychological and physical state of people with CRD, promoting better adherence to treatment and improving their overall health.1 4 Exercise is the cornerstone of PR, and while safe, adjunctive therapies to mitigate symptoms during physical activity are often necessary to enhance tolerance to therapy and optimise its benefits.157

A novel adjuvant therapy is the high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), which delivers air-flow oxygen at a high rate (>20 L/min), providing additional effects such as inspired air conditioning,8 promoting washout of carbon dioxide levels in nasopharyngeal dead space,9 an increase of positive expiratory pressure in the upper and lower airway1012 and end-expiratory lung volume.13 14 These physiological effects help to reduce the load on the respiratory muscles during conditions where this inspiratory effort increases, an aspect lacking in conventional oxygen therapy (COT).12 15 16

Recently, there has been increased interest in using HFNC during exercise in PR as another clinical context that may increase inspiratory effort.1520 The effect of HFNC during exercise has shown some benefits through retrospective and experimental studies.1820 To our knowledge, four systematic reviews have addressed the effect of HFNC during exercise training.2124 However, these reviews are focused on people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), so the effects of HFNC in other populations with CRD are unknown. These reviews concluded that HFNC could improve exercise capacity in certain patients with COPD, but a high heterogeneity was observed.21 22 Thus, it is currently unknown whether adding HFNC during exercise is better than the standard of care (COT or room air) in PR for people with CRD. Accordingly, this systematic review aimed to evaluate the impact of the effects of HFNC during exercise in people with CRD.

Methods

This review is under the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.25 26 The protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO (ID: CRD42022336263) and published.27

Search strategy

Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), PubMed, Embase, Epistemonikos, Lilacs, Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro), International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP), ClinicalTrials.gov and other resources according to our protocol27 were searched from inception (28 June 2022) to 4 April 2023 with no restriction on language, adapting the search strategy to the syntax of each database (see online supplemental appendix A for the full details of the search strategy). Reference lists from identified studies and previous relevant systematic reviews were also examined to identify additional articles not captured by the database search. We performed forward citation searches with Google Scholar using included studies as a reference index. We also reviewed annual conference abstracts from the American Thoracic Society and the European Respiratory Society. Two reviewers (EM-G and ME-R) independently assessed the identified publications for eligibility.27 Disagreements were settled through discussion among the reviewers (EM-G and ME-R). A third reviewer (FP-V) resolved any disagreement.

Selection criteria

The articles included met the following inclusion criteria according to our published protocol27: (1) randomised controlled trials (RCTs); (2) assessed participants who were adults (>18 years old) with CRD that affected the respiratory system (pulmonary parenchyma, alveolar-capillary barrier and airway); (3) met the criteria for admission or were already admitted to PR according to international guidelines (ambulatory or stable conditions)1; (4) employing HFNC during exercise through commercial devices (without restricting criteria to a minimum or maximum fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) or temperature parameters); (5) reporting at least one of the following outcomes: functional exercise capacity (eg, exercise time; distance in 6-minute walk test (6MWT)), QoL (eg, St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire ; COPD Assessment Test (CAT)), dyspnoea symptoms (eg, modified British Medical Research Council (mMRC); Borg score), functionality (eg, Barthel Index), comfort (eg, comfort scale), complications or adherence. Studies were excluded if: (1) evaluated the effects in animal models or ‘in vitro’ conditions, physical or computational models, and preclinical studies; (2) assessed adults and children under 18 years of age and the latter exceeded 50% of the total population; performed high-flow delivery by ‘home-made’ devices; (3) used HFNC in the control group with a total flow delivery of >20 L·min-1; (4) presented a washout period of at least 12 hours; (5) included co-interventions within a study when there are external differences between the control and experimental groups unrelated to HFNC (eg, medication, non-invasive mechanical ventilation, or respiratory muscle training in one group only) (see full description in online supplemental appendix B for detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria).

Data extraction

Two authors conducted data extraction independently (JF-G and KA) using a standardised data abstraction form developed according to the protocol to extract specific characteristics from included studies27 (see online supplemental appendix B for the full details of data extraction). A third judge decided if agreements could not be resolved (EM-G). We contacted study authors to obtain additional information whenever data appeared missing or unclear (see online supplemental appendix C for the full details of the authors’ response).

Risk of bias assessment and GRADE

Two authors (EM-G and JF-G) independently assessed the Risk of Bias (RoB) of the included studies and conference abstracts. For this purpose, we used the RoB 2 tool developed by the Cochrane Collaboration to assess the RoB in RCTs.28 Disagreements were resolved through consensus and were adjudicated by a third reviewer in case the discrepancy persists (FV-P).

We assessed reporting bias by comparing outcomes reported in the published report against the study protocol whenever this could be obtained. Whenever the protocol was not available, we compared outcomes listed in the Methods section with those whose results were reported. When the information was insufficient to judge bias, we noted that the meta-analysis had ‘some concerns’ RoB.

To assess the certainty of the evidence, the review authors independently assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. We used methods and recommendations described in Chapters 8 and 14 of the Cochrane Handbook.29 We prepared Summary of Findings tables through GRADEpro software for primary and secondary outcomes.3033

Statistical analysis

For data analysis, pooled-effect estimates were acquired by comparing two categories: (1) HFNC and (2) standard of care (SC), which includes COT, HFNC at low flow (<20 L/min) or room air. The weighted mean difference (WMD) and risk ratio (RR), along with their (95% CIs), were calculated as effect estimates for continuous and dichotomous variables, respectively. When data were measured on different scales, the standard mean difference (SMD) with 95% CI was used. Also, when parallel RCTs were encountered with two or more experimental arms employing HFNC in different settings, we selected the two most comparable and least biased groups for analysis: one group using HFNC and one control group using SC. These groups were combined to form a single pairwise comparison, which was included in the meta-analysis.

During the RoB analysis, studies with more than 5% attrition were considered high RoB for this review.28 We assessed statistical heterogeneity by visually inspecting the forest plots, considering the χ2 test (with a significance level set at p value <0.10).29 Also, we used the I2 statistic, which describes the percentage of total variation across studies due to heterogeneity rather than sampling error.29 An I2 value of 0% denotes no heterogeneity, while 30–60%, 50–90% and >75% denote moderate, substantial and considerable heterogeneity, respectively.29

Publication bias and small study effects were assessed statistically via funnel plots and Egger’s test34 if at least 10 studies were included in the meta-analysis and were performed using the ‘meta’ package35 in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Meta-analysis was performed using a generic inverse-variance approach and the Cochrane Collaboration’s Review Manager Software V.5.4, provided by Cochrane Collaboration.36 For randomised crossover trials and parallel groups, the effect estimate was obtained using a generic inverse-variance approach incorporating the mean difference and the SE of the between-condition difference to approximate a paired analysis. The random-effects model was used for all analyses, and a forest plot was used to compare the results between studies that compare HFNC versus SC.27 For each primary outcome, subgroup analyses were conducted where sufficient RCTs were available (at least two) according to the protocol registered in PROSPERO. We performed a sensitivity analysis for available primary and secondary outcomes through forest plots and meta-analysis, excluding studies with a high RoB according to our protocol.27

Patient and public involvement

No patient involvement was undertaken in this study’s design, conduct or evaluation. Patients were not consulted when developing the research questions or outcome measures. They were not involved in the planning, executing or assessing the intervention’s burden. Study results will be disseminated through appropriate academic channels and via conference presentations.

Study characteristics

The included studies assessed patients with the following CRD: 11 studies for patients with COPD,3747 3 studies for patients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease (FILD),4850 1 study for patients with cystic fibrosis,51 1 study for cancer of primary or secondary lung involvement,52 and 1 study for patients with CRDs,53 including patients with COPD, FILD and bronchiectasis. The sample size ranged from 137 to 10 participants, with a total of 570 participants (394 men, 163 women, and 13 with no information; age range: 29–83 years) included for analysis. Of the 17 studies included, 16 compared an HFNC intervention with SC in at least one patient,375153 and only 1 study compared with low-flow air (2 L/min).52 Control interventions included venturi mask (n=8),373941 47 nasal cannula (n=8),404246 52 53 and venturi mask or nasal cannula.51 The delivery of supplemental oxygen therapy was administered according to different objectives: nine studies aimed to achieve equal pulse-oxygen saturation (SpO2) targets in both groups,373942 43 45 47 48 51 three studies delivered an equal fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) in both groups,41 49 50 four studies administered standardised FiO2 from baseline with imbalances between groups,40 44 46 53 and one study did not use supplemental oxygen therapy in both groups.52 In addition, 13 studies evaluated the HFNC acute effect after a single exercise session,3738 40 41 4346 48 whereas 4 studies did so after multiple training sessions.39 42 47 53 HFNC flow rates varied across studies: two studies used flows between 30 and 40 L/min,40 51 five studies between 40 and 50 L/min,41 42 45 49 50 and six studies between 50 and 60 L/min,39 43 44 46 48 52 while three studies did not report the flow rate.37 38 53 Similarly, the temperature settings for HFNC varied: three studies reported a temperature of 31°C,42 43 46 one study reported a range of 32–38°C,52 two studies reported 37°C,49 51 one study reported a range of 30–37°C,39 while nine studies did not report the temperature.37 38 40 41 44 45 48 50 53 Other characteristics of the studies, including samples, designs, exercise protocols, intervention and control settings, and outcomes assessed, are presented in table 1 and online supplemental table E.1. RoB 2 and GRADE assessment

RoB was determined to be some concern in 7 studies37 38 40 43 47 49 52 and high risk in 10 studies.3941 42 4446 48 50 51 53 The RoB 2 assessment is presented in figure 2. The most common biases within RoB 2 at high risk were as follows: ‘bias due to deviations from intended interventions’ (patient and outcome assessors were not blinded to the interventions, and no intention-to-treat analysis was performed in most studies with dropouts of more than 5% of the sample size) and ‘bias in measurement of the outcome’ (patients and outcome assessors were aware of the intervention, the knowledge of the intervention received likely influenced the assessment of the outcome). The most common biases in some concerns were as follows: ‘bias arising from the randomization process’ (lack of information about the allocation concealment process) and ‘bias in the election of the reported result’ (lack of information about the prespecified analysis plan using protocols published in scientific journals or databases such as ClinicalTrials.gov or WHO ICTRP). Four of the 12 studies with a crossover design provided information on the effect of the period concerning the first order and cluster characteristics of the first order of randomisation.43 46 49 50 The certainty of the evidence was rated for meta-analytic primary and secondary outcomes as low or very low overall for individual study outcomes (online supplemental appendix F). The main reasons for downgrading the evidence were study limitations (RoB) and imprecision. We did not grade down due to publication bias following our prespecified criteria (minimum of 10 studies for evaluating each outcome).27 Only indirectness was downgraded in QoL and functional dyspnoea outcome, as only the COPD population was represented.

Primary outcomes: effect of HFNC on functional exercise capacity compared with SC (COT or room air)

Exercise time during Constant Work Rate Exercise Test (CWRET) after multiple training sessions

Two trials reported this outcome.39 53 HFNC may result in a large increase in exercise time during CWRET compared with SC after multiple training sessions (WMD=170.94 s; 95% CI 54.71 to 287.17; p=0.004; I2=0%; GRADE: low; figure 3). The pooled effect of HFNC on time limit after multiple training sessions in different subgroups of patients could not be calculated due to insufficient studies.

Comparison of high-flow nasal cannula versus conventional oxygen therapy during Constant Work Rate Exercise Test (CWRET) after a single exercise session and after multiple training sessions.

Exercise time during CWRET after a single exercise session

Ten studies with one conference abstract were included.3741 4346 48 HFNC may increase the exercise time during CWRET compared with SC (WMD=72.10 s; 95% CI 28.96 to 115.24; p=0.001; I2=0%; GRADE: low; figure 3). Subgroup analyses are presented in online supplemental appendix G–H.

Subgroup analysis by CRDs

In a meta-analysis of COPD population, six studies were included.3741 4346 HFNC may improve the exercise time during CWRET compared with SC in COPD population (WMD=81.46 s; 95% CI 22.46 to 140.47; p=0.007; I2=0%; see online supplemental appendix H figure H.4). In a meta-analysis of FILD population, three studies were included.4850 HFNC may make little or no difference to exercise time during CWRET compared with SC (WMD=72.29 s; 95% CI −30.36 to 174.93; p=0.17; I2=0%; online supplemental appendix H and figure H.5). The pooled effect of HFNC on time limit in patients with lung cancer and cystic fibrosis could not be calculated due to an insufficient number of studies, or CWRET was not evaluated.51 52

Subgroup analysis by the severity of diseases

In a meta-analysis of the severity of COPD population (FEV1 <50% or ≥50% predicted)3741 4346 and severity FILD population (DLCO <60% or ≥60% predicted),4850 HFNC may make little or no difference to exercise time during CWRET compared with SC (online supplemental appendix H figures H.6 and H.7). The pooled effect of HFNC on the time limit in patients with lung cancer and cystic fibrosis could not be calculated due to an insufficient number of studies, or CWRET was not evaluated.51 52

Walking distance during 6MWT after a single and multiple exercise sessions

Two studies reported this outcome.39 53 HFNC may make little or no difference in distance compared with SC after multiple training sessions (WMD=37.28 m; 95% CI −0.47 to 75.03; p=0.05; I2=0%; GRADE: low; online supplemental appendix H and figure H.20). However, after multiple sessions of HFNC during exercise training reached MCID (14.9 to 30.5 m).54 The pooled effect of HFNC on distance after multiple training sessions in different subgroups of patients could not be calculated due to insufficient studies.

Secondary outcomes: effect of HFNC on QoL, functional dyspnoea, comfort, adherence, functionality and complications compared with SC (COT or room air)

QoL

In a meta-analysis of the assessment of QoL, two studies were included only for the COPD population.39 42 HFNC may make little or no difference in QoL through the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) compared with SC after multiple training sessions (WMD=−0.06; 95% CI −0.65 to 0.53; p=0.84; I2=0%; GRADE: very low; online supplemental appendix H and figure H.22), which also did not reach MCID (one point).55

Functional dyspnoea

Two studies were included only for the COPD population.39 42 HFNC may make little or no difference to functional dyspnoea through modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale (0–4) compared with SC after multiple training sessions (WMD=0.06; 95% CI −0.00 to 0.11; p=0.06; I2=0%; GRADE: very low; online supplemental appendix H and figure H.35).

Comfort implicated with the use of the device

Four studies were included in a meta-analysis of comfort during CWRET.41 46 49 50 HFNC may make little or no difference to comfort scale (0–10) compared with SC (SMD=−0.23; 95% CI −0.79 to 0.34; p=0.43; I2=17%; GRADE: very low; online supplemental appendix H figure H.6). Two studies reported this outcome in a meta-analysis of comfort during 6MWT.38 51 HFNC may make little or no difference to the comfort scale (0–10) compared with SC (SMD=0.74; 95% CI −1.73 to 3.21; p=0.55; GRADE: very low). Heterogeneity between the studies was substantial (I2=79%). The pooled effect of HFNC versus SC on the comfort scale during the Incremental Exercise Test (IET) could not be calculated due to insufficient studies.41

Adherence, functionality and complications after a single exercise session or multiple training sessions

Four studies were included in a meta-analysis of adherence after a single exercise session.38 41 46 51 HFNC may make little or no difference to adherence compared with SC (RR=0.99; 95% CI 0.92 to 1.06; p=0.73; I2=0%; GRADE: very low; online supplemental appendix H and figure H.34). Two studies were included in a meta-analysis of adherence after multiple training sessions.39 42 HFNC may make little or no difference to adherence compared with SC (RR=1.09; 95% CI 0.95 to 1.25; p=0.73; I2=0%; GRADE: very low; online supplemental appendix H and figure H. 34). The pooled effect of HFNC versus SC on functionality after multiple training sessions could not be calculated due to insufficient studies.39 On the other hand, pooled effect of HFNC versus SC on complications after or during exercise could not be calculated due to variability in the evaluation of different studies.5052 However, the narrative description of complications of HFNC versus SC is available in online supplemental appendix H.

Publication bias

The Egger’s test did not suggest publication bias for studies comparing the effects of HFNC versus SC during exercise after a single exercise session (CWRET) (p=0.8065; online supplemental appendix H figure H.37). For the secondary outcomes, publication bias could not be statistically assessed because the number of studies was fewer than 10 studies.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis including only studies of low risk or some concerns according to RoB2.27 28 Four studies met the criteria for analysis during CWRET,37 43 49 52 and two studies met the criteria for analysis during 6MWT.38 40

The sensitivity analysis results for functional exercise capacity after a single exercise during CWRET (WMD=59.15 s; 95% CI −0.97 to 119.28; p=0.05; I2: 0%) did not agree with the overall summary effect estimates. However, the results were consistent with the overall summary for 6MWT, showing no significant effect estimates (WMD=23.02 m; 95% CI −7.68 to 53.72; p=0.14; I2: 0%) (online supplemental appendix H and figures H.38 and H.39). Sensitivity analysis for different subgroups and secondary or indirect outcomes of participants was not performed for any outcome as the number of included studies in the sensitivity analysis was limited to one or two.

Protocol deviations compared with PROSPERO registration

We performed a meta-analysis of subgroups by examining the cut-off of 80% load intensity, as estimated directly through an IET. Additionally, we conducted a meta-analysis of subgroups based on the amount of supplemental oxygen received between groups (online supplemental appendix H figures H.11 and H.16).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, the current systematic review is the first to investigate the effects of HFNC versus SC during exercise in people with CRD. A significant difference in functional exercise capacity was identified in favour of HFNC following a PR programme, although the certainty of the evidence is low. Additionally, HFNC may increase exercise time compared with SC after a single exercise session, which is consistent with a previous systematic review in people with COPD.22 However, our sensitivity analysis showed HFNC might make little or no differences compared with SC in exercise time or walking distance. Further well-designed RCTs are needed to determine the effect of HFNC on functional dyspnoea, QoL and functionality to find minimal clinically important differences.

Primary outcomes

Functional exercise capacity after multiple training sessions

The effect of HFNC after a PR in people with CRD showed a statistical improvement in total exercise time through CWRET with an increase of 170 s (95% CI 55 to 287), with low certainty of evidence. This effect exceeded the MCID of low (100–105 s) and intermediate (150 s) boundaries of the clinically significant range for the CWRET proposed for people with COPD.56 57 However, it is essential to note that while the effect size and upper limit of the CI fall within the low and intermediate range of the MCID (100–150 s56 57), the lower limit does not reach this, which indicates that the observed effect may or may not have clinically relevant improvement. Also, this significant improvement should be viewed with caution as the studies analysed only included patients with COPD and had a sample size of 169 participants, with some differences between the studies.39 53 Chihara et al53 delivered a maximum amount of FiO2 (100%) to the HFNC group compared with the control group that used a nasal cannula (6 L/min). FiO2 at 100% could be one of the main reasons why the HFNC group increased the total exercise time for each session and at the end of the training programme.58 On the contrary, Vitacca et al39 decreased this confounding factor of additional oxygen in both groups, adjusting for the same FiO2 (50%). The evidence is controversial regarding whether additional oxygen use improves exercise time after a PR during CWRET.59 60 Therefore, it may be a factor that may bias the true effect of HFNC during multiple training sessions.59 60 On the other hand, it should be considered that both studies presented training protocols that were not feasible (5 days/week) in rehabilitation centres according to the recommendations of PR guidelines (2–3 days/week).1 However, the same training conditions were used in the two studies, the only difference being the settings devices (HFNC vs SC).

Functional exercise capacity after a single exercise session

HFNC showed an increase in total exercise time (CWRET) versus SC in people with CRD included in studies (COPD, FILD and lung cancer),3741 4346 48 although at low certainty of evidence. Small studies with other types of design are consistent with our results in increasing exercise time, even with unloaded exercise tests.61 62 However, the sensitivity analysis showed a loss of significance (p=0.05) when excluding studies with a high risk of bias, showing inconsistency with the overall summary effect estimates. CWRET is a sensitive test to detect the effectiveness of a pharmacological or non-pharmacological intervention such as oxygen delivery devices (HFNC vs SC).63 However, there are no widely accepted thresholds for interpreting acute session exercise-induced increases in exercise time regarding their practical or clinical significance. Previous experimental studies have reported clinical significance at the cut-off time of 75 s (range 46–105),46 64 consistent with our overall summary effect estimates (figure 3). However, the more robust sensitivity analysis yielded inconsistent results, indicating the need for future RCTs to elucidate this cut-off point.

Analysis by subgroups between HFNC and SC during functional exercise through CWRET

Our subgroup analyses revealed that HFNC has a significant impact on exercise capacity in COPD patients, showing an improvement in exercise time during the CWRET compared with SC,3741 4346 as previously described by Prieur et al.22 However, for patients with FILD, lung cancer and cystic fibrosis, the effects of HFNC on exercise time were inconclusive due to small sample sizes and a lack of sufficient studies.4852 Severity-based analyses also indicated that HFNC does not show significant differences in exercise time based on the severity of COPD or FILD.3741 4346 48 Therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when considering their applicability to patients with conditions other than COPD or those with different severities of CRD. The variability in results underscores the need for further research to define the role of HFNC in diverse clinical contexts and to establish more robust conclusions about its effectiveness across different populations.

Conversely, an examination of subgroups based on the principles of overload and progression in exercise training characteristics (such as volume, type, intensity and duration),65 66 precisely exercise intensity, revealed interesting findings that may change the clinical approach to assessment through CWRET. When people with CRD exercise only at an intensity <80% of the estimated maximum load, there is an increase in exercise time in favour of HFNC compared with SC and no significant effects on CWRET improvement when intensity is ≥80%. This observation is consistent with exercise physiology, as the respiratory compensation point (RCP) is known to occur at exercise intensities above 80% of intensity in maximal exercise.67 At RCP, an accumulation of lactate exceeds the bicarbonate buffering capacity of the blood, resulting in hyperventilation as a response to metabolic acidosis in healthy individuals and those with chronic pathologies.6870 In patients with CRD, this heightened minute ventilation further diminishes ventilatory efficiency at high intensities due to the combined effects of obstructive and restrictive mechanical limitations, leading to a greater than 10-fold increase in minute ventilation compared with rest and an increase in dead space.71 Also, at high intensity, the risk of losing the physiological effects of HFNC therapy is higher due to increased leak flow rates through the mouth.91417 Therefore, when exercise intensity exceeds 80% of the estimated maximum load, there is no increase in total exercise time, regardless of the device used. However, a new unplanned meta-analysis comparing HFNC and SC at intensities above and below 80% found an increase in exercise time in both subgroups when estimated directly through IET (online supplemental appendix H figure H.11). This new meta-analysis may suggest that indirect estimation using equations to determine load may overestimate exercise intensity.43 46

On the other hand, when examining subgroups related to balanced oxygen delivery based on oxygen pulse saturation goals or the same standardised FiO2 between the HFNC and COT group, there was an increase in exercise time in favour of HFNC. Likewise, in the subgroup comparing HFNC versus venturi mask at the same FiO2 in both groups, the exercise time increases with HFNC. From these meta-analyses, it could be hypothesised that the physiological effects of HFNC versus COT increase the total exercise time during CWRET-independent oxygen supply between groups (online supplemental appendix H figure H.15–H.17).

Secondary outcomes

QoL, adherence, comfort and complications

Our results indicated that the use of HFNC during a training period may make little or no difference in QoL, as measured by changes in CAT values.39 42 Unlike previous studies reporting clinically significant changes in CAT scores about 1–2 points,72 our results suggest that the impact of HFNC on QoL may be limited. Furthermore, these results are based predominantly on studies involving patients with COPD, highlighting the need for further research to explore the effects of HFNC during exercise training on QoL in populations with different CRD.

The HFNC appeared to have no adherence problems and to be safe among people with CRD, although at low certainty of evidence. This confirms findings regarding the safety of HFNC in previous research investigating the impact of HFNC in other populations with respiratory health conditions.7375 Despite a few studies on pooled effects in comfort outcomes, no statistical difference exists between groups. In another population, lower tolerance to high flows and temperatures has been reported using HFNC devices.76 However, at high inspiratory flow rates during moderate-high intensity exercise, HFNC does not appear to differ from SC in device-associated patient comfort.

Functional dyspnoea

Functional dyspnoea is one of the most important symptoms in people with CRD and often limits exercise tolerance or activities of daily living.1 77 The impact of HFNC application on functional dyspnoea through mMRC suggests no statistical or clinical benefits78 with using HFNC after multiple training sessions in patients with COPD within the pooled analysis.39 42 However, the number of studies is limited and only evaluated the COPD population, and further studies are also required to assess the impact of HFNC.

Strengths and limitations of the review

The key strengths of this review are the comprehensive literature search conducted in eight bibliographic databases, rigorous checking of the use of RoB 2 and extracted data performed by two independent authors, and consideration of a prior protocol that included subgroups based on clinical exercise physiology such as ergometer type, exercise intensity and differences in respiratory mechanics of patients with CRD.27

Despite our encouraging findings, it is important to note some limitations of the systematic review. First, the quality of the intervention studies included different designs (crossover vs parallel). Thus, caution is required when interpreting the findings. Future high-quality RCTs are required. Second, the limited number of RCTs focusing on conditions other than COPD or FILD (such as cystic fibrosis, lung cancer, bronchiectasis and other populations like long-term COVID-19 and pulmonary hypertension) restricts the generalisability of the results, and future research is required in these populations. Finally, several subgroup analyses could not be conducted due to insufficient suitable and small sample studies, and heterogeneity may have been underestimated.79

Suggestions for future RCTs

Given our results and those from past studies,20 61 62 80 we suggest that future multicentre RCTs that will assess people with CRD and exercise training should (1) investigate people with CRD other than COPD or FILD according to PR guidelines; (2) consider that supplemental oxygen should be delivered according to the patient’s oxygenation requirements (eg, pulse oxygen saturation targets above 88–90%) independently of the COT device; (3) take into account the comfort experienced by each patient, which is associated with the device setting through a personalised set of high flow rates and temperatures; (4) describe assignment concealment and include methods that minimise the RoB such as an independent and masked evaluator; and (5) investigate the mid-term effects of HFNC on functional exercise capacity and functional dyspnoea, QoL, adherence, cost-effectiveness and hospital admissions after multiple training sessions over 4, 6, 8 and 12 weeks.

Conclusion

We found that HFNC leads to increased functional exercise capacity after multiple training sessions in some populations with CRD. However, the quality of the evidence and the number of studies is poor yet. Also, we found very low to low certainty of the evidence of no clinically relevant differences in secondary outcomes between HFNC and SC. Multicentre RCTs with homogeneous methodology and intervention are required to provide stronger evidence on functional exercise capacity, QoL, functional dyspnoea and functionality.
