Energy Variational Modeling and Numerical Simulation of Open Membranes in Stokes Flow
Han Zhou, Yuan-Nan Young, Yoichiro Moricor1

Abstract
Lipid bilayer membranes are fundamental biological structures that serve as cellular boundaries, mediating transport, signaling, and maintaining structural integrity. This study introduces a novel mathematical model for open membranes immersed in Stokes flows, accounting for membrane elasticity, line tension at the open edge, and fluid–membrane interactions. The model is derived from an energy functional that incorporates Helfrich bending energy and a line energy associated with the open edge. By balancing dissipation in both the bulk fluid and the membrane surface, following the maximal dissipation principle, we derive the governing equations within an energy variational framework. Assuming axisymmetry and employing a boundary integral reduction, we transform the 3D problem into an effectively 1D problem, for which we develop a finite element-based numerical method to solve the resulting moving boundary problem. Several numerical examples are provided to validate the model and compare the results with existing studies.

Lipid bilayer membranes are fundamental structural components of cellular organelles, as they self-enclose to form a vesicle in a viscous fluid to separate interior from exterior contents. As a biomimetic model for blood cells, the hydrodynamics of giant vesicles provides fundamental insights into the mechanics of biomembranes, namely the coupling between membrane and fluid mechanics

Under normal conditions, lipid bilayer membranes close on themselves in a viscous polar solvent (such as water) to form a vesicle, characterized by its enclosed volume, surface area and bending energy

The resulting fluid-structure interaction problem involves coupling the geometric PDEs for membrane shape evolution with the momentum and continuity equations for the fluid. Accurate numerical simulations of open lipid bilayer membranes immersed in Stokes flow demands a robust and reliable computational framework that addresses both the geometrical complexity of the membrane and the intricate hydrodynamic interactions between the membrane and the surrounding fluid. The parametric finite element method has been successfully employed to model the dynamical evolution of open membranes by solving the gradient flow of the membrane energy. This approach, however, considers only the pure geometrical evolution of the open surface and neglects interactions with the surrounding fluid

Despite these advances, the numerical simulation of the Stokes flow outside an open surface, coupled with the dynamic evolution of the open surface itself, has not yet been reported in the literature.

This work develops a mathematical model and numerical framework for simulating an open axisymmetric lipid bilayer membrane immersed in an incompressible Stokesian fluid. The model is constructed from an energy-based formulation that incorporates both bending elasticity and line tension at the membrane edge. We focus on an axisymmetric reduction of the governing equations and express the solution to the Stokes equation as a boundary integral, reducing the 3D problem to a 1D problem. This approach simplifies the computational effort while preserving the essential features of the full 3D system. Based on the weak form of the reduced system, we adopted a finite element-based method combined with a semi-implicit time integration scheme to efficiently solve the time-dependent moving boundary problem. The proposed model and numerical method are validated through a series of numerical examples, with results compared against existing theoretical and numerical studies.

Model

This study focuses on modeling and simulating an open inextensible elastic membrane interacting with a viscous fluid in the Stokes regime (no inertial effect). In this section, we employ an energetic variational approach to derive the governing equations for the dynamics of the membrane.

2.2
Energy variational formulations

A lipid bilayer membrane in a given configuration stores free energy, which drives the system toward configurations of lower energy by generating conservative forces. For an open lipid bilayer membrane, the total free energy consists of contributions from both the curvature elasticity of the membrane surface and the line tension of the edge. Mathematically, this is expressed as

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}+\mathcal{E}_{\partial\Gamma}.

(2.8)

The curvature elasticity of the membrane is modeled using the Helfrich energy, which is given by

\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}=\int_{\Gamma}\left(\alpha\left(H-c_{0}\right)^{2}+\alpha_{G}K\right)\,d\mu_{\Gamma},

(2.9)

where
H
is the mean curvature,
K
is the Gaussian curvature,
c_{0}
is the spontaneous curvature, and
\alpha
and
\alpha_{G}
are the bending rigidities associated with the mean and Gaussian curvatures, respectively. The spontaneous curvature
c_{0}
reflects the natural geometry of the membrane due to its molecular composition or external environment. If
c_{0}=0
and
\alpha_{G}=0
, the Helfrich energy reduces to the simpler Willmore energy, which depends only on the mean curvature. For the line tension of the edge, we consider the following energy

\mathcal{E}_{\partial\Gamma}=\gamma\int_{\partial\Gamma}1\,ds,

(2.10)

where
\gamma
is the constant line tension, representing the energetic cost per unit length of the edge. The line tension reflects the resistance of the edge to lengthening, arising from molecular interactions at the boundary.

The conservative forces produced by the membrane can be determined by calculating the first variation of the total energy. Let’s consider an arbitrary perturbation of the surface
\Gamma
in the direction of
\boldsymbol{Y}
. The first variation of the total energy can be expressed as

\displaystyle\delta_{\boldsymbol{X}}\mathcal{E}[\boldsymbol{X}](\boldsymbol{Y})

\displaystyle=\and{\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\mathcal{E}[\boldsymbol{X}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{Y}]}{\varepsilon=0}

(2.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\alpha\left(\Delta_{\Gamma}H+2(H-c_{0})(H^{2}+Hc_{0}-K)\right)(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{Y})\,d\mu_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Gamma}\left(\alpha(H-c_{0})^{2}+\alpha_{G}K+\gamma\kappa_{g}\right)(\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\boldsymbol{Y})-(\alpha\nabla_{\Gamma}H\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}-\alpha_{G}\tau_{g}^{\prime}+\gamma\kappa_{n})(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{Y})

\displaystyle+(\alpha(H-c_{0})+\alpha_{G}\kappa_{n})\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{Y}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\,ds.

We refer readers to
[
9
]
for a detailed derivation of the first variation of the Helfrich energy.
The velocities of the bulk fluid flow and the surface flow are denoted by
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{U}
, respectively. Under the assumptions of an impermeable membrane with a no-slip condition, the compatibility constraint
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{U}
is imposed on the surface
\Gamma
. The membrane’s evolution is governed by the geometric evolution equation given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X})=\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{X}),\quad\boldsymbol{X}\in\Gamma.

(2.12)

Furthermore, it is assumed that the fluid approaches rest at infinity, adhering to the condition

|\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})|\to 0\quad\text{as }|\boldsymbol{x}|\to\infty.

(2.13)

The fluid environment within cells is commonly described as a low Reynolds number flow
[
4
,
27
]
, typically modeled using the Stokes equation. Additionally, it has been proposed that dissipation resulting from membrane viscosity plays a crucial role for large vesicles and introduces a significant regularization effect for small pores
[
4
,
9
,
58
]
. We consider both the bulk viscosity and membrane viscosity in formulating the model. Let
\mu
and
\mu_{\Gamma}
represent the constant viscosity coefficients of the bulk fluid and the membrane, respectively. The viscous dissipation in the bulk fluid and on the membrane surface can be characterized by the Rayleigh dissipation functions defined as ,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}[\boldsymbol{u}]=\int_{\Omega}\mu\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})\colon\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})\,d\boldsymbol{x},\quad\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}[\boldsymbol{U}]=\int_{\Gamma}\mu_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})\colon\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})\,d\mu_{\Gamma},

(2.14)

where
\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})
are the rate of deformation tensors for the bulk and surface fluid flows, defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{\Gamma}\left(\nabla_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{U}+\nabla_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\Gamma}.

(2.15)

Here,
\boldsymbol{P}_{\Gamma}
is the projection operator onto the tangent plane of
\Gamma
defined in Equation (
2.6
). The fluid dynamics under consideration pertain to incompressible bulk Stokes flow where the bulk velocity field is divergence-free:

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

(2.16)

To account for the incompressibility constraint in the bulk, we construct a Lagrangian functional related to the viscous dissipation within the bulk based on the Rayleigh dissipation function,

L[\boldsymbol{u},p]=\int_{\Omega}\mu\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})\colon\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})-p\left(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\right)\,d\boldsymbol{x},

(2.17)

where the pressure
p
in the bulk is a Lagrange multiplier for enforcing the incompressibility condition.
Moreover, the assumption of local inextensibility of the lipid bilayer membrane implies

\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}=0\quad\text{ on }\Gamma.

(2.18)

To address this constraint, a Lagrangian functional associated with the viscous dissipation on the membrane surface is constructed as

L_{\Gamma}[\boldsymbol{U},P]=\int_{\Gamma}\mu_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})\colon\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})-P\left(\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}\right)\,d\mu_{\Gamma},

(2.19)

where the Lagrange multiplier
P
enforces area incompressibility (Equation (
2.18

\delta\boldsymbol{u}
and
\delta\boldsymbol{U}
denote arbitrary perturbations of the bulk and surface velocities, respectively. As
\boldsymbol{x}
tends to infinity, the perturbation decays such that
|\boldsymbol{v}|\to 0
as
|\boldsymbol{x}|\to\infty
. The first variations of
L
can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\delta_{\boldsymbol{u}}L[\boldsymbol{u},p](\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\and{\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}L[\boldsymbol{u}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{v},p]}{\varepsilon=0}

(2.20)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(2\mu\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})-p\boldsymbol{I}\right)\colon\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\,d\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\Gamma}\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,d\mu_{\Gamma},

where the stress tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is defined as
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\left(\boldsymbol{u},p\right)=-p\boldsymbol{I}+2\mu\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})
,
and
\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket
denotes the jump of normal stress on
\Gamma
, given by

\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket(\boldsymbol{x})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x}-\varepsilon\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x}))-\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right)\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma.

(2.21)

Similarly, the first variations of
L_{\Gamma}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\delta_{\boldsymbol{U}}L_{\Gamma}[\boldsymbol{U},P](\boldsymbol{V})

\displaystyle=\and{\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}L_{\Gamma}[\boldsymbol{U}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{V},P]}{\varepsilon=0}

(2.22)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\left(2\mu_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})-P\boldsymbol{I}\right)\colon\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{V})\,d\mu_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\left(-\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\Gamma}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{V}\,d\mu_{\Gamma}+\int_{\partial\Gamma}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{V}\,ds,

where the stress tensor for membrane fluid flow
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\Gamma}
is defined as
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\Gamma}\left(\boldsymbol{U},P\right)=-P\boldsymbol{P}_{\Gamma}+2\mu_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})

2.3
Governing equations

The governing equations are derived via the principle of maximum dissipation, formulated as a variational condition on the rate of energy dissipation (see for example
[
59
]
). For our problem, this can be written as follows

\delta_{\boldsymbol{X}}\mathcal{E}[\boldsymbol{X}](\boldsymbol{Y})=-\left(\delta L[\boldsymbol{u},p](\boldsymbol{v})+\delta L_{\Gamma}[\boldsymbol{U},P](\boldsymbol{V})\right),

(2.23)

where the three terms above were computed in (
2.11
), (
2.20
), and (
2.22
) respectively. The above is to be satisfied for any arbitrary variations
\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{V}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
that satisfy the kinematic constraint
\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{V}=\boldsymbol{v}
on
\Gamma
in Equation (
2.12

We obtain the following governing equations for an open membrane immersed in a viscous fluid.

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.24a)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.24b)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\Gamma}+\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket+\alpha\left(\Delta_{\Gamma}H+2\left(H-c_{0}\right)\left(H^{2}+Hc_{0}-K\right)\right)\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.24c)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.24d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{U},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.24e)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{X}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{U},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.24f)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\nu}+\left(\alpha\left(H-c_{0}\right)^{2}+\alpha_{G}K+\gamma\kappa_{g}\right)\boldsymbol{\nu}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{ on }\partial\Gamma,

(2.24g)

\displaystyle\alpha\nabla_{\Gamma}H\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}-\alpha_{G}\tau_{g}^{\prime}+\gamma\kappa_{n}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{ on }\partial\Gamma,

(2.24h)

\displaystyle\alpha\left(H-c_{0}\right)+\alpha_{G}\kappa_{n}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{ on }\partial\Gamma.

(2.24i)

Equation (
2.24c
), which serves as the interfacial boundary condition for the bulk Stokes equations, states that the bulk fluid stress jump is balanced by bending forces and surface viscous forces on the membrane
\Gamma
. This is further supplemented by boundary conditions at the edge
\partial\Gamma
. Equations (
2.24g
) and (
2.24h
) express the force balance conditions in the co-normal and normal directions, respectively. Equation (
2.24i
) expresses the torque-free condition at

Our derivation ensures that in the above system the rate of change in energy is balanced by energy dissipation. Indeed, it can be shown that the above equations yield the following identity

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}=-2\int_{\Omega}\mu|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{x}-2\int_{\Gamma}\mu_{\Gamma}|\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})|^{2}\,d\mu_{\Gamma}.

(2.25)

where
|\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{u})|
and
|\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{U})|
are the Frobenius norms of the deformation tensors.

2.4
Boundary integral and weak form

The governing equations for an open membrane in Stokes flow involve coupling the surface evolution equations with the bulk Stokes equation. Given the complex surface geometry, directly solving these equations using both bulk and surface meshes necessitates sophisticated handling of the boundary conditions on the surface. To address these challenges, we employ boundary integral formulations for the bulk Stokes equation, reducing the bulk-surface coupled PDE system to a set of differential-integral equations confined to the surface.

Since the velocity field is expected to be continuous on the surface while the stress exhibits discontinuity, it is natural and common to adopt a single-layer representation for the solution of the bulk Stokes equation. It is worth noting that for problems involving an open edge, a double-layer representation of the solution is unsuitable. This is because a double-layer potential becomes multi-valued at the open edge if the function’s jump is non-zero at that location.
Let
G(\boldsymbol{x})
be the Stokeslet in 3D free spaces, whose entries are given by

G_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\frac{\delta_{ij}}{|\boldsymbol{x}|}+\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{|\boldsymbol{x}|^{3}}\right).

(2.26)

For a surface density function
\boldsymbol{f}
, define the single-layer integral operator
\mathcal{S}
as

(\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{f}])_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{\Gamma}G_{ij}\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)f_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)\,d\mu(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(2.27)

where we have followed the Einstein summation convention.
Using the Stokeslet, the velocity field of the fluid flow can be expressed as a single-layer boundary integral

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{\xi}](\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(2.28)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
represents an unknown single-layer density, introduced as an auxiliary variable. Observing the relation
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket
, this provides an additional justification for choosing the single-layer representation. The auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{\xi}
has a clear physical interpretation as the exerted force, which can be directly used to enforce the force balance on
\Gamma
in equation (
2.24c
).
The single-layer integral is continuous across the surface and its restriction on
\Gamma
is well-defined, which we also denote by
\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]
. Thus, we have
\boldsymbol{U}=\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]
on
\Gamma
.

Due to the bending force, which introduces the surface Laplacian of the mean curvature, the original equations (
2.24
) are high-order in space. Numerical approximation for such high-order PDEs is challenging and typically requires high-order basis functions, adding complexity. To simplify, we treat the mean curvature
H
as an unknown function and reformulate the equations in a mixed form. Additionally, we introduce auxiliary variables, including the mean curvature vector
\boldsymbol{H}
and the normal bending force
g
.
Then we reformulate the problem as finding
\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{U},P,\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{H},H,g
such that the following equations are satisfied

\displaystyle-\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]+\boldsymbol{U}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.29a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}-\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\Gamma}+g\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.29b)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.29c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{X}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{U},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.29d)

\displaystyle 2\boldsymbol{H}-\Delta_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{X}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.29e)

\displaystyle H-\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{H}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.29f)

3
Axisymmetric formulations

To reduce computational complexity and enable more efficient numerical simulation, we simplify the governing equation (
2.24
) and its weak form equation (
2.35
), originally formulated for open surfaces, by restricting to the axisymmetric case. While the full 3D formulation remains applicable to arbitrary geometries, it is computationally intensive. The axisymmetric reduction transforms the surface-based 3D problem into a lower-dimensional formulation on an open generating curve, effectively yielding a 1D problem. This simplification assumes that the membrane surface is rotationally symmetric about the
z
-axis and that the surrounding fluid flow is axisymmetric, with no azimuthal velocity component. As a result, the fluid velocity in the

r
- and
z
-directions, along with the pressure, are taken to be independent of the azimuthal angle
\varphi
.

Define the coordinates vectors in cylindrical coordinates by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi\\
\sin\varphi\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{e}_{\varphi}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\varphi\\
\cos\varphi\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{e}_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(3.1)

Let the surface
\Gamma
be generated by the rotation of a planar curve
\mathcal{C}:(X^{r}(s),X^{z}(s))^{\top}
, where
s\in\mathbb{T}=(0,L)
denotes the arc-length parameter along the curve, around the
z
-axis.
The resulting axisymmetric surface
\Gamma
is parameterized as

\Gamma:\boldsymbol{X}(s,\varphi)=X^{r}(s)\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+X^{z}(s)\boldsymbol{e}_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}X^{r}(s)\cos\varphi\\
X^{r}(s)\sin\varphi\\
X^{z}(s)\end{pmatrix},\quad s\in\mathbb{T},\quad\varphi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}=\mathbb{R}\setminus(2\pi\mathbb{Z}).

(3.2)

Since the surface is open, the
generating curve
\mathcal{C}
may terminate away from the
z
-axis. We denote the endpoint lying on the
z
-axis by
\partial_{0}\mathcal{C}
, and the open edge by
\partial_{1}\mathcal{C}
. Under the axisymmetric parametrization, scalar and vector-valued functions defined on
\Gamma
can be expressed in terms of the arc-length parameter
s
and azimuthal angle
\varphi
. Specifically, for a scalar field
f
and a vector field
\boldsymbol{f}
defined on the surface, we write

f\left(\boldsymbol{X}\left(s,\varphi\right)\right)=\widetilde{f}\left(s,\varphi\right),\quad\boldsymbol{f}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\left(s,\varphi\right)\right)=f^{r}\left(s,\varphi\right)\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+f^{\varphi}\left(s,\varphi\right)\boldsymbol{e}_{\varphi}+f^{z}\left(s,\varphi\right)\boldsymbol{e}_{z}.

(3.3)

If the fields are axisymmetric, then
\widetilde{f},f^{r},f^{\varphi},f^{z}
are independent of the azimuthal angle
\varphi
and the functions reduce to one-dimensional representations
\widetilde{f}(s),f^{r}(s),f^{\varphi}(s),f^{z}(s)
. In particular, under the axisymmetric assumption, there is no
\varphi
component for the functions
\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{\xi}
, and
\boldsymbol{H}
are assumed to have no azimuthal components, and their radial and axial components depend solely on
s
.

For vector fields with no azimuthal component, i.e.,
f^{\varphi}=0
, we introduce the reduced function
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(s)=\big(f^{r}(s),f^{z}(s)\big)^{\top}
. For notational simplicity, and with a slight abuse of notation, we omit the tilde and continue to denote reduced functions using the original symbol. For instance, the generating curve is written as
\boldsymbol{X}(s)=\big(X^{r}(s),X^{z}(s)\big)^{\top}
, and an axisymmetric scalar function
\widetilde{f}
is denoted as
f
.

3.1
Differential operators on the axisymmetric surface

In order to reduce the fully 3D weak form (
2.35
) to the axisymmetric case, we write geometric quantities and differential operators on
\Gamma
using the coordinate vectors (
3.1
).
The normal vector to the axisymmetric surface is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=-X^{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+X^{r}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{z}.

(3.4)

The mean and Gaussian curvatures are given by, respectively,

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2}\left(X^{r}_{s}X^{z}_{ss}-X^{z}_{s}X^{r}_{ss}+\frac{X^{z}_{s}}{X^{r}}\right),\quad K=\frac{X^{z}_{s}}{X^{r}}\left(X^{r}_{s}X^{z}_{ss}-X^{z}_{s}X^{r}_{ss}\right).

(3.5)

Note that the current configuration allows the surface to have up to two holes, which correspond to cases where both endpoints of the generating curve
\mathcal{C}
do not intersect the
z
-axis. For such cases, we provide the expressions for the co-normal vector and curvatures on the boundary
\partial\Gamma
.
The co-normal vector to
\partial\Gamma

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}=\begin{cases}X^{r}_{s}(L)\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+X^{z}_{s}(L)\boldsymbol{e}_{z},&s=L,\\
-X^{r}_{s}(0)\boldsymbol{e}_{r}-X^{z}_{s}(0)\boldsymbol{e}_{z},&s=0.\end{cases}

(3.6)

The normal and geodesic curvatures of
\partial\Gamma

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}=\begin{cases}\and{\frac{X^{z}_{s}}{X^{r}}}{s=L},&s=L,\\
-\and{\frac{X^{z}_{s}}{X^{r}}}{s=0},&s=0.\end{cases},\quad\kappa_{g}=\begin{cases}\and{\frac{X^{r}_{s}}{X^{r}}}{s=L},&s=L,\\
-\and{\frac{X^{r}_{s}}{X^{r}}}{s=0},&s=0.\end{cases}

(3.7)

Since the surface is axisymmetric, the torsion vanishes, i.e.,
\tau_{g}=0

For any scalar function
f

\boldsymbol{f}

\Gamma

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Gamma}f

\displaystyle=f_{s}(X^{r}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+X^{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{z})+\frac{f_{\varphi}}{X^{r}}\boldsymbol{e}_{\varphi},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle=f^{r}_{s}X^{r}_{s}+f^{z}_{s}X^{z}_{s}+\frac{f^{r}+f^{\varphi}_{\varphi}}{X^{r}},

(3.9)

where subscripts
s
and
\varphi

Furthermore, let
\boldsymbol{f}
and
\boldsymbol{g}
be vector-valued axisymmetric functions in the following form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}\left(s,\varphi\right)=f^{r}(s)\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+f^{z}(s)\boldsymbol{e}_{z},\quad\boldsymbol{g}\left(s,\varphi\right)=g^{r}(s)\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+g^{z}(s)\boldsymbol{e}_{z},

(3.10)

Utilizing (

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle=(f^{r}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+f^{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{z})\otimes(X^{r}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+X^{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{z})+\frac{f^{r}}{X^{r}}\boldsymbol{e}_{\varphi}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{\varphi},

(3.12)

The double dot product for surface gradient of vector-valued functions and deformation tensors are computed as follows.

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{f}\colon\nabla_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{g}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}_{s}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{s}+\frac{f^{r}g^{r}}{(X^{r})^{2}},

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{s}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}_{s}\right)+\frac{f^{r}g^{r}}{(X^{r})^{2}},

(3.14)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}\left(\boldsymbol{f}\right)\colon\boldsymbol{D}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{g})

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{s}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}_{s}\right)+\frac{f^{r}g^{r}}{(X^{r})^{2}}.

(3.15)

4
Numerical method

4.1
Finite element approximation

We approximate the weak formulation (
3.34
) for the curve evolution using the finite element method.
The time interval
[0,T]
is divided into a temporal mesh. For simplicity, we adopt uniform time stepping
t^{n}=n\Delta t
for
n=0,1,2,\dots,N_{t}
with
T=N_{t}\Delta t
.

Note that even for the inextensible membrane surface, the arc-length parameter
s
of the time-dependent generating curve
\mathcal{C}(t)
changes during its evolution. Given a function
\Phi(\alpha)
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}=(0,1)
with
\Phi^{\prime}(\alpha)>0
, we reparameterize the curve by
s=\Phi(\alpha)
where
\alpha
is the new parameter defined on a fixed reference domain
\mathbb{I}
. Denote by
(\Phi^{*}f)(\alpha,t)=f(\Phi(\alpha),t)
the pullback of
f
to the reference domain
\mathbb{I}
. The curve is now represented by the function
\left(\Phi^{*}\boldsymbol{X}\right)(\alpha,t),\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
.
With the pullback, we are able to approximate functions defined on the curve in (
3.34
) by directly approximating their pullback functions on a fixed reference domain.
Note that
\alpha
is no longer the arc-length parameter and we have
\partial_{\alpha}\Phi=|\partial_{\alpha}(\Phi^{*}\boldsymbol{X})|
. The derivative of
f
with respect to the arc-length parameter
s
can be expressed as
\Phi^{*}(\partial_{s}f)=|\partial_{\alpha}(\Phi^{*}\boldsymbol{X})|^{-1}\partial_{\alpha}(\Phi^{*}f)
. The inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}
under the pullback is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}=\int_{\mathbb{I}}(\Phi^{*}f)(\alpha)(\Phi^{*}g)(\alpha)(\Phi^{*}X^{r})(\alpha)|\partial_{\alpha}(\Phi^{*}\boldsymbol{X})(\alpha)|\,d\alpha.

(4.1)

To simplify notation in further derivations, we will omit the pullback notation
\Phi^{*}
and use the same symbol for a function and its pullback.

The reference domain
\mathbb{I}
is discretized uniformly into a uniform mesh with
N
smaller intervals with size
h=1/N
, to get the nodes
\alpha_{i}=ih
, for
i=0,1,\dots,N
.
With this uniform partition, we define a one-dimensional continuous piecewise polynomial finite element space as

\displaystyle V^{N}_{k}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in C\left(\bar{\mathbb{I}}\right):\and{v}{[\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i}]}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}\left([\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i}]\right),1\leq i\leq N\right\}

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\text{span}\left\{\phi_{k}^{1}\left(x\right),\phi_{k}^{2}\left(x\right),\cdots,\phi_{k}^{M_{k}}\left(x\right)\right\},

(4.3)

where
\mathbb{P}_{k}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)
is the functional space of polynomials of degree no greater than
k
.
Let
\{\boldsymbol{X}^{n}\}_{0\leq i\leq N_{t}}
be an numerical approximation to
\{\left(\Phi^{*}\boldsymbol{X}\right)(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
with
\boldsymbol{X}^{n}\in[V^{N}_{2}(\mathbb{I})]^{2}\cap V_{\partial}(\mathbb{I})
. The curve
\mathcal{C}^{n}
is defined as
\boldsymbol{X}^{n}\left(\bar{\mathbb{I}}\right)
and geometric quantities such as the normal vector can be computed using
\boldsymbol{X}^{n}
. We use quadratic elements for the surface velocity
\boldsymbol{U}
and linear elements for the surface tension
P
. This choice of finite element spaces mimics the Taylor-Hood element, which guarantees the inf-sup stability condition for Stokes equations on planar domains. For other quantities, we adopt quadratic element spaces for their approximations. Denote by
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
,
P^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{X}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{H}^{n}
,
H^{n}
,
g^{n}
the numerical approximations to the pullbacks of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
,
\boldsymbol{U}
,
P
,
\boldsymbol{X}
,
\boldsymbol{H}
,
H
,
g
at
t=t^{n}
, respectively.
Given an initial curve
\mathcal{C}(0)
, its numerical approximation
\boldsymbol{X}^{0}
is obtained by interpolation. For
n\geq 0
, we find

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{n+1}\in[V^{N}_{2}(\mathbb{I})]^{2},\quad\boldsymbol{U}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{X}^{n+1}\in[V^{N}_{2}(\mathbb{I})]^{2}\cap V_{\partial}(\mathbb{I}),

(4.4)

\displaystyle P^{n+1}\in V^{N}_{1}(\mathbb{I}),\quad g^{n+1}\in V^{N}_{2}(\mathbb{I}),\quad H^{n+1}\in V^{N}_{2}(\mathbb{I})\cap H^{1}_{g}(\mathbb{I}).

(4.5)

such that

\displaystyle-\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathcal{S}^{h,n}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{n+1}]\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h,\ast}+\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{U}^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in[V^{N}_{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)]^{2},

(4.6a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}+2\mu_{\Gamma}\left\langle\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{s},\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}_{s}^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}+2\mu_{\Gamma}\left\langle\frac{\psi^{r}}{X^{r,n}},\frac{U^{r,n+1}}{X^{r,n}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}-\left\langle\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{s}+\frac{\psi^{r}}{X^{r,n}},P^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}

\displaystyle\,+\left\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},g^{n+1}\boldsymbol{n}^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}=-\left\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\left(\alpha\left(H^{n}-c_{0}\right)^{2}+\alpha_{G}K^{n}+\gamma\kappa_{g}^{n}\right)\boldsymbol{\nu}^{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{C}^{n}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi}\in[V^{N}_{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)]^{2}\cap V_{\partial}\left(\mathbb{I}\right),

(4.6b)

\displaystyle-\left\langle Q,\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}_{s}^{n+1}+\frac{U^{r,n+1}}{X^{r,n}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}=0,\quad\forall Q\in V^{N}_{1}\left(\mathbb{I}\right),

(4.6c)

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{\omega},\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{n+1}-\boldsymbol{X}^{n}\right)-\boldsymbol{U}^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\omega}\in[V^{N}_{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)]^{2},

(4.6d)

\displaystyle 2\left\langle\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{H}^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}+\left\langle\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{s},\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}+\left\langle\frac{\zeta^{r}}{X^{r,n}},\frac{X^{r,n+1}}{X^{r,n}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{C}^{n}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in[V^{N}_{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)]^{2}\cap V_{\partial}\left(\mathbb{I}\right),

(4.6e)

\displaystyle\left\langle\eta,H^{n+1}-\boldsymbol{n}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{H}^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}=0,\quad\forall\eta\in V^{N}_{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)\cap H^{1}_{0}\left(\mathbb{I}\right),

(4.6f)

\displaystyle\left\langle\chi,g^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}+\alpha\left\langle\chi_{s},H_{s}^{n+1}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}=2\alpha\left\langle\chi,\left(H^{n}-c_{0}\right)\left(\left(H^{n}\right)^{2}+H^{n}c_{0}-K^{n}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h}

\displaystyle\,+\left\langle\chi,-\gamma\kappa_{n}^{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{C}^{n}},\quad\forall\chi\in V^{N}_{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right).

(4.6g)

Here, the discrete inner-product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}^{h}
is an approximation to the inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}
by Gauss-Legendre quadrature, and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}^{h,\ast}
is based on a special quadrature designed for the single-layer integral. Note that
\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathcal{S}^{h,n}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{n+1}]\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}^{n}}^{h,\ast}
is designed to approximate the term
\left\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]\right\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}
, which is a double-integral. We numerically approximate the double-integral by numerical quadrature and the single-layer integral
\mathcal{S}
is approximated by a discrete single-layer integral
\mathcal{S}^{h,n}
.

Due to the presence of a high-order spatial derivative, any explicit scheme suffers from numerical stiffness, imposing a severe constraint on the time step. While fully implicit schemes are generally more stable and can sometimes be unconditionally stable, the nonlinear nature of the equation makes such schemes challenging, as they result in nonlinear algebraic systems that are difficult and time-consuming to solve. The current numerical scheme (
4.6
) uses a semi-implicit time discretization designed to balance stability and efficiency. In this scheme, only the geometry and boundary conditions are treated explicitly, while terms with high-order spatial derivatives are treated implicitly, thus removing the severe time-step constraint and yielding a linear system.

In the numerical scheme summarized in (
4.6
), due to the semi-implicit treatment, there are geometric quantities that depend on solutions at the time level
t^{n}
. Since
\boldsymbol{X}^{n}
is defined as a piecewise polynomial, we can directly compute its derivative
\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{n}
and the normal derivative
\boldsymbol{n}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{n},\kappa_{n}^{n},\text{ and }\kappa_{g}^{n}
. The mean curvature
H^{n}
can be directly obtained from the solution at the previous time step, except for the first step, for which we use the analytical value as the initial condition for
H
. Recalling the expression in (
3.5
), we compute
K^{n}
by

\displaystyle K^{n}=\frac{X^{z,n}_{s}}{X^{r,n}}\left(2H^{n}-\frac{X^{z,n}_{s}}{X^{r,n}}\right),

(4.7)

for which we only have to compute the first derivative of
\boldsymbol{X}^{n}
.

The resulting coefficient matrix is mostly sparse, except for the block corresponding to the single-layer integral, which remains dense. However, due to the axisymmetric reduction, the problem is effectively one-dimensional, keeping the size of the linear system small. This enables efficient solution using direct methods, such as
SparseLU

[
60
]
.

4.2
Singularity capturing mesh refinement

The solution to the open membrane problem typically exhibits singular behavior near the open edge, such as
\boldsymbol{\xi}
diverging as
|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\sim d^{-1/2}
, where
d
is the distance to the edge. Such singularities are challenging to handle using a uniform computational mesh, leading to highly inaccurate results. It is well-known that local mesh refinement is preferred for resolving singular behavior. Here, we present a direct method for introducing local mesh refinement by modifying the initial parametrization of the generating curve.

For the initial reparametrization of
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
, if the parameter
\alpha
is chosen to be proportional to the arc-length parameter of the initial curve, then a uniform discretization in
\alpha
results in almost uniform spacing of points along the curve. Moreover, by carefully selecting the reparametrization, a uniform mesh in
\alpha
can produce locally refined points along the curve, which is particularly useful for capturing singular behavior in the solution.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}(s),s\in(0,L)
represent the initial curve, parameterized by the arc-length
s
. We construct the locally refined reparametrization function
\Phi(\alpha)
to satisfy the following properties:

1.

The function
\Phi
maps the two endpoints
0
and
1
to
0
and
L
, respectively, i.e.,
\Phi(0)=0
,
\Phi(1)=L
;

2.

The reparametrization is non-singular, i.e.,
\Phi^{\prime}(\alpha)>0
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
;

3.

The reparametrization
\Phi
has a local refinement property near the open edge, i.e.,
\Phi^{\prime}(\alpha)
is close to
0
for all
\alpha
such that
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\left(\Phi(\alpha)\right)\in\partial_{0}\mathcal{C}
.

When both
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}(0)
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}(1)
represent open-edge ends, we choose the following function:

\displaystyle\Phi(\alpha)=\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\Psi(\alpha)+\varepsilon L\alpha,

(4.8)

where
\varepsilon>0
is a small constant, typically chosen as
\varepsilon=10^{-3}
, and
\Psi
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi(\alpha)=\frac{L}{2}\left(1-\cos\left(\pi\alpha\right)\right).

(4.9)

Similarly, if only
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}(1)
corresponds to an open edge, one may choose

\displaystyle\Psi(\alpha)=L\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(1-\alpha)\right).

(4.10)

It can be verified that
\Phi
satisfies the three properties.
We note that the regularization parameter
\varepsilon
is not essential and is introduced primarily for numerical convenience. Since the boundary condition involves geometric quantities such as
\kappa_{n}
and
\kappa_{g}
, which are treated explicitly, setting
\varepsilon=0
can lead to numerical instability due to poor parametrization at the endpoints, where
\Psi^{\prime}(\alpha)=0
. However, these geometric quantities at the endpoints are inherently independent of the parametrization and can be computed using an alternative and regular local parametrization of the generating curve. In such cases, one can simply set
\varepsilon=0
.
Under this cosine reparametrization, the mesh points become clustered near the endpoints. In particular, when
\varepsilon=0
, the mesh spacing is approximately
h=\mathcal{O}(N^{-2})
near the endpoints and
h=\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
in regions away from the endpoints, where
N
is the total number of mesh points.

4.3
Numerical quadrature for the singular integral

Assembling the coefficient matrix for the numerical scheme requires evaluating the double integral

\displaystyle I_{ij}=\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{i},\mathcal{S}[\boldsymbol{\phi}^{j}]\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}=\int_{\mathbb{I}}\int_{\mathbb{I}}X^{r}(\alpha)X^{r}(\alpha^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{\phi}^{i}(\alpha)\cdot\boldsymbol{S}(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\phi}^{j}(\alpha^{\prime})\,|\partial_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{X}(\alpha)||\partial_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{X}(\alpha^{\prime})|\,d\alpha^{\prime}\,d\alpha,

(4.11)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{j}
are polynomial basis functions. Since the integral kernel
\boldsymbol{S}(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})
is a linear combination of
K(k)
and
E(k)
, where the former has a logarithmic-type singularity,

\displaystyle K(k)\sim-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1-k\right)+\mathcal{O}(1),\quad\text{ as }k\to 1^{-},

(4.12)

which corresponds to the limit
|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\to 0
.
Standard numerical quadrature methods for smooth functions, such as tensor-product Gauss quadrature, encounter difficulties in approximating such singular integrals. When quadrature points lie along the diagonal
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
, where the kernel becomes singular, the integrand becomes infinite, rendering these quadrature rules inapplicable. Even if care is taken to avoid direct evaluation at
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
, convergence remains slow due to the unbounded derivatives of the integrand, which hinder error control.
Alpert’s quadrature
[
61
]
is well-suited for handling logarithmic-type singular integrals. This method achieves high-order accuracy for integrals of the form
\int_{0}^{1}a(x)\ln(x)+b(x)\,dx
where
a(x)
and
b(x)
are smooth, and has demonstrated excellent performance for similar problems. To accurately compute the singular double integral in this problem, we will design a specialized numerical quadrature scheme inspired by Alpert’s rule, tailored for the singular behavior of the kernel.

After performing the cell-by-cell assembly of the matrix, it suffices to consider the case where the basis functions
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{j}
have compact support on intervals
\mathbb{I}_{k}
and
\mathbb{I}_{l}
with
\mathbb{I}_{k}=(\alpha_{k-1},\alpha_{k})
. Under this condition, the double integral simplifies to

\displaystyle\widehat{I}_{ij}=\int_{\mathbb{I}_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{I}_{l}}X^{r}(\alpha)X^{r}(\alpha^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{\phi}^{i}(\alpha)\cdot\boldsymbol{S}(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\phi}^{j}(\alpha^{\prime})\,|\partial_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{X}(\alpha)||\partial_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{X}(\alpha^{\prime})|\,d\alpha^{\prime}\,d\alpha.

(4.13)

Three scenarios must be considered: (a)
k=l
, (b)
|k-l|=1
, and (c)
|k-l|>1
.
For case (c), the integrand is smooth, allowing standard Gauss quadrature to be applied without modification. For both case (a) and (b), the integral has different types of singularities and certain care must be taken for numerical integration.

For case (a), the logarithmic singularity arises along the diagonal of the integration domain, i.e., when
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
. The singular behavior of the integral
\widehat{I}_{ij}
is therefore the same as the following canonical form:

\displaystyle I[f]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\ln|x-y|\,f(x,y)\,dx\,dy,

(4.14)

where
f(x,y)
is assumed to be smooth in
[0,1]\times[0,1]
. We rewrite this integral as the sum of two integrals over triangular regions

\displaystyle I[f]=\iint_{\triangle_{1}}\ln|x-y|\,f(x,y)\,dx\,dy+\iint_{\triangle_{2}}\ln|x-y|\,f(x,y)\,dx\,dy,

(4.15)

where
\triangle_{1}
and
\triangle_{2}
are two right triangles separated by the diagonal
x=y
,

\displaystyle\triangle_{1}=\{(x,y):0<x<1,\,0<y<x\},\quad\triangle_{2}=\{(x,y):0<x<1,\,x<y<1\}.

(4.16)

The singularity now appears at one of the edges of each triangle. Due to symmetry, we only consider the integral on
\triangle_{1}
since the other one can be treated similarly. By introducing the change of variables in
\triangle_{1}
,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\xi+\eta-\xi\eta\\
\eta-\xi\eta\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi\in(0,1),\eta\in(0,1).

(4.17)

the integral can be reformulated as an integral over a unit square

\displaystyle\iint_{\triangle_{1}}\ln|x-y|\,f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f(x(\xi,\eta),y(\xi,\eta))(1-\xi)\ln\xi\,d\xi\,d\eta,

(4.18)

where the logarithmic singularity only appears in the
\xi
-direction as
\xi\to 0
, while the integrand in the
\eta
-direction remains regular for fixed
\xi
. After the transformation, the application of Alpert’s quadrature rule becomes straightforward. By applying Alpert’s rule in the
\xi-
direction and the Gauss quadrature in the
\eta-
direction, we obtain the following approximation,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f(x(\xi,\eta),y(\xi,\eta))(1-\xi)\ln\xi\,d\xi\,d\eta\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}w_{1,i}\,w_{2,j}\,f(x(\xi_{i},\eta_{j}),y(\xi_{i},\eta_{j}))(1-\xi_{i})\ln\xi_{i},

(4.19)

where
\{(\xi_{i},w_{1,i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{1}}
and
\{(\eta_{j},w_{2,j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{2}}
are the quadrature points and weights for Alpert’s rule and Gauss-Legendre quadrature on
(0,1)
, respectively.

For case (b), the singularity occurs at the corner of the rectangle
\mathbb{I}_{k}\times\mathbb{I}_{l}
. We consider the integral as follows

\displaystyle I=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-1}^{0}\ln|x-y|f\left(x,y\right)\,dx\,dy=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\ln\left(x+y\right)f\left(-x,y\right)\,dx\,dy.

(4.20)

The logarithmic-type singularity is located at the corner
\left(0,0\right)
of the rectangular integration domain. Let us split the
I
into integrals over two triangular regions

\displaystyle I=-\iint_{\triangle_{1}}\ln\left(x+y\right)f\left(-x,y\right)\,dx\,dy-\iint_{\triangle_{2}}\ln\left(x+y\right)f\left(-x,y\right)\,dx\,dy,

(4.21)

where
\triangle_{1}
and
\triangle_{2}
are two right triangles separated by the anti-diagonal
y=1-x
,

\displaystyle\triangle_{1}=\{(x,y):0<x<1,\,0<y<1-x\},\quad\triangle_{2}=\{(x,y):0<x<1,\,1-x<y<1\}.

(4.22)

Then, the integral over
\triangle_{1}
is singular, while the integral over
\triangle_{2}
is regular. By introducing the change of variable as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\xi-\xi\eta\\
\xi\eta\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi\in(0,1),\;\eta\in(0,1).

(4.23)

The singular integral over
\triangle_{1}
can be reformulated as an integral over a unit square

\displaystyle\iint_{\triangle_{1}}\ln\left(x+y\right)f\left(-x,y\right)\,dx\,dy=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f(-x(\xi,\eta),y(\xi,\eta))\xi\ln\xi\,d\xi\,d\eta,

(4.24)

where the logarithmic singularity is only in the
\xi-
direction. By applying Alpert’s rule in the
\xi-
direction and the Gauss quadrature rule in the
\eta-
direction, we obtain the following approximation,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f(-x(\xi,\eta),y(\xi,\eta))\xi\ln\xi\,d\xi\,d\eta\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}w_{1,i}\,w_{2,j}\,f(-x(\xi_{i},\eta_{j}),y(\xi_{i},\eta_{j}))\xi_{i}\ln\xi_{i}.

(4.25)

Similarly, we can introduce a change of variable to reformulate the integral over
\triangle_{2}
as a regular integral over a unit square and apply the tensor product Gauss quadrature rule for the numerical integration.

Annular-shaped planar membrane

We consider the case where the membrane is planar and has an annular shape,

First, we perform the convergence check for the velocity field on the planar membrane. The planar membrane only has nonzero radial velocity and, due to the inextensible condition, the radial velocity is given by

U^{r}(r)=\frac{F}{r},

F

In this example, we investigate the dynamic behavior of an open membrane starting from an initial spherical cap shape. However, this assumption becomes less realistic in scenarios where the membrane has an open pore, particularly near the free edge

Using our numerical method, we simulate the dynamics of an open membrane initialized as a spherical cap. The parameters are set as

(5.9)

This deviation can be understood through the geometric boundary condition

Next, we incorporate the influence of line tension by setting

6
Disscusion

For an open, inextensible membrane immersed in Stokes flow, we derive a continuum model based on the principle of maximum dissipation within an energy variational framework. The membrane is represented as a zero-thickness open surface endowed with a membrane energy comprising the Helfrich bending energy and a line tension along its open edge. Dissipative effects from both the bulk and membrane viscosities are incorporated via Rayleigh dissipation functionals, while incompressibility of the surrounding fluid and inextensibility of the membrane are enforced through Lagrange multipliers. Taking first variations and balancing surface and boundary forces yields the coupled PDEs governing membrane–fluid interactions.

To conduct numerical simulations for the PDE model, we consider the axisymmetric case for both the membrane and fluid flow. For this scenario, we adopt a boundary integral formulation, reducing the 3D problem into a 1D problem along the generating curve. Due to the complex coupling between the boundary integral and geometric quantities, we developed a finite-element-based numerical scheme by rewriting the high-order PDE into a weak mixed form, which only contains first derivatives. The time discretization is semi-implicit: low-order derivatives, nonlinear terms and boundary terms are treated explicitly, while high-order derivatives, linear terms are treated implicitly. This ensures that the final scheme is both stable and can be solved efficiently. The single-layer density typically exhibits singular behavior near the open edge. To address this, we designed a local mesh refinement strategy that increases the resolution of the computational mesh near the open edge, improving the scheme’s ability to capture singularities. Using the developed axisymmetric numerical scheme, we applied it to model open membranes in different cases. The present numerical method can be applied to investigate the behavior of open membranes interacting with the bulk flow, such as the relaxation dynamics of membranes under the influence of the bulk fluid and the dynamics of pores in membranes influenced by fluid leakage.

Lipid bilayer membranes are nearly inextensible, and a weak area compressibility may result in fluid leakage that alters both the membrane dynamics
[
63
]
and the pore dynamics
[
46
]
. How does such weak area compressibility affect the transition from a (closed) vesicle to an open membrane? To answer this question we have been working on improving our PDE model for the lipid bilayer membrane to account for the physical area compressibility within the boundary integral framework.

Furthermore the current numerical method can be improved in several directions. By carefully studying the singularity behavior of the solution near the open edge, it should be possible to develop a high-order numerical method that incorporates an analytical expression for the singularity structure, reducing the need for mesh refinement. Developing numerical schemes to perform full 3D simulations for membranes with general shapes is another challenging but interesting direction.

Derivation of a simplified spherical cap model

Suppose the membrane is always a spherical cap obtained by rotating the generating curve

\mathcal{C}=\left\{(R\sin(\theta),z_{0}+R\cos(\theta)),0\leq\theta\leq\alpha\right\},

Assuming the membrane is rim-less, its velocity is given by
