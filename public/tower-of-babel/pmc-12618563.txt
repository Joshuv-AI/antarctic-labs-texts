Effects of preceding crops on potassium dynamics, bacterial communities in tobacco-planted soils, and potassium allocation in tobacco plants
Authors: Jie Zhao, Ming Liu, Kaiyuan Gu, Xianglu Liu, Juan Li, Shuyue Guan, Chengwei Yang, Haiping He, Zexian Yang, Yanxia Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrop rotation is a key strategy to mitigate the challenges associated with tobacco monoculture, such as soil nutrient depletion and microbial community imbalance. This study investigated the effects of different preceding crops—continuous tobacco (CK), barley (T1), and rapeseed (T2)—on soil potassium (K) forms, K uptake and allocation in tobacco plants, and soil bacterial communities. A randomized complete block design was employed with three replications per treatment. Soil samples were collected at multiple time points to analyze dynamic changes in various K forms (total K, available K, exchangeable K, non-exchangeable K, water-soluble K, mineral K). The bacterial community composition was assessed via 16 S rRNA gene sequencing at tobacco maturity. Key findings include: (1) Preceding crops T1 and T2 significantly enhanced soil available K (increased by 0.55–1.65 times), exchangeable K (1.85–5.77 times), and water-soluble K (3.35–7.23 times) compared to CK at maturity, and promoted the release of non-exchangeable and mineral K. (2) At harvest, the leaf K content in the T2 treatment reached 3.17%, significantly higher than in CK, indicating improved K allocation to leaves. (3) Bacterial community richness (Chao1 index) and diversity (Shannon index) were highest in T2, followed by T1 and lowest in CK. The dominant phyla were Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Acidobacteria, whose relative abundances increased under rotation. The rapeseed rotation (T2) most effectively enhanced soil K supply, bacterial diversity, and leaf K accumulation. These results demonstrate that selecting appropriate preceding crops, particularly rapeseed, can optimize soil K availability, improve bacterial community structure, and enhance potassium utilization efficiency in tobacco, providing a theoretical basis for sustainable tobacco cultivation.Keywords: Tobacco, Preceding crop, Soil potassium, Bacterial community, Bacterial diversitySubject terms: Plant ecology, Plant physiology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Yunnan is a major tobacco-producing region in China. However, limited arable land and the high economic returns from tobacco often lead to continuous monocropping, exacerbating issues like soil nutrient depletion and microbial dysbiosis, which are significant barriers to sustainable tobacco production1,2. Establishing rational farming systems, such as crop rotation, has been proven to alleviate these continuous cropping obstacles effectively3,4. For instance, rotating tobacco with wheat, rapeseed, or potatoes improves plant growth, enhances leaf curing characteristics, and harmonizes chemical composition, ultimately elevating overall tobacco quality5. Beyond agronomic benefits, crop rotation directly and indirectly optimizes soil microbial community structure. Long-term monocropping often leads to soil nutrient imbalances, reduced abundance of beneficial bacteria, and increased pathogenic bacteria, disrupting bacterial community structure6. In contrast, crop rotation helps balance soil nutrients and significantly enhances bacterial community abundance and diversity7.

Tobacco is a potassium (K)-demanding crop, with K being one of its most absorbed nutrients8. Potassium functions as a vital activator for numerous enzymes, promotes protein synthesis, and enhances photosynthetic efficiency by facilitating light energy utilization9,10. The K content in tobacco leaves is crucial for determining the quality of flue-cured tobacco. High-K tobacco typically exhibits sufficient oiliness, enhanced aroma and fragrance, improved elasticity and toughness, and superior combustibility and durability of the processed leaves11. Moreover, adequate K can lower the combustion temperature of tobacco, reducing the release of harmful substances such as nicotine and tar in smoke, thereby improving the safety of tobacco products12 Although soil is the primary source of K for plants, only 1–2% of total soil K is readily available for plant uptake13. Soil K exists in four primary forms: water-soluble, exchangeable, non-exchangeable, and mineral K14, each playing a distinct role in K availability and dynamics.

While previous studies have confirmed that crop rotation can alleviate continuous cropping barriers, the specific mechanisms linking changes in soil K forms, plant K uptake, and shifts in the soil bacterial community remain less explored. This study aims to bridge this gap by investigating the effects of two different preceding crops, barley and rapeseed, on: (i) the dynamic changes of various soil K forms during the tobacco growing season; (ii) the accumulation and distribution of K within different parts of the tobacco plant; and (iii) the structure and diversity of the soil bacterial community at harvest. We hypothesized that (a) preceding crops would enhance the transformation of less available K forms (non-exchangeable, mineral) into more plant-available forms (exchangeable, water-soluble), (b) this improved K supply would lead to greater K accumulation, particularly in tobacco leaves, and (c) these changes would be associated with a shift in the soil bacterial community towards a more diverse and beneficial composition. The novelty of this work lies in the simultaneous examination of K dynamics across the soil-plant system and its potential interconnection with the soil microbiome under different preceding crop regimes. The findings will provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing cropping systems and improving soil health in tobacco cultivation.

Materials and methods

Experimental sites

The field experiment was conducted from December 2022 to September 2023 in Weishan County, Dali Prefecture (E 100°30′, N 25°23′, altitude 2000 m). The soil was classified as red soil. Initial soil physicochemical properties (0–20 cm depth) before experiment establishment in December 2022 were: pH 6.37, organic matter 28.00 g/kg, total nitrogen 1.68 g/kg, total phosphorus 1.46 g/kg, total potassium 34.54 g/kg, available phosphorus 18.13 mg/kg, alkaline hydrolyzable nitrogen 135.23 mg/kg, and available potassium 270.23 mg/kg. The site had a history of tobacco cultivation.

Experimental design

The experiment was arranged in a randomized complete block design with three treatments and three replications, totaling nine plots. Each plot measured 10 m × 10 m (100 m2). The treatments were: CK (Control): Continuous cropping of tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L. cv. Hongda). T1: Tobacco rotated with barley (Hordeum vulgare L. cv. Kunlun 15) as the preceding crop. T2: Tobacco rotated with rapeseed (Brassica napus L. cv. Huayou 5) as the preceding crop.

The preceding crops (barley and rapeseed) were sown in December 2022. Before sowing, a base fertilizer was applied at rates of 10 kg/acre urea and 12.5 kg/acre compound fertilizer (N: P2O₅:K2O = 15:15:15). A topdressing of 2.5 kg/acre urea was applied during the growth period. The preceding crops were harvested in April 2023. Tobacco seedlings (cv. Hongda) were raised in a float system tray (128 cavities per tray) using a commercial seedling substrate (Yunnan Substrate Engineering Co., Ltd., China) in a greenhouse for 60 days before being transplanted into the field in early May 2023. Tobacco was harvested in September 2023. All other field management practices followed local standard protocols.

Measurement items and methods

Determination of soil K content in each form

Soil samples (0–20 cm depth) were collected from the root zone of tobacco plants using a five-point sampling method at five stages: before transplanting (0 d) and 30, 60, 90, and 120 days after transplanting (DAT). Gravel and plant/animal residues were removed. Samples were air-dried, ground, and passed through a 2-mm sieve for analysis of different K forms16:

Total K (TK): Determined by flame photometry after NaOH fusion.

Available K (AK): Extracted with 1.0 mol/L neutral ammonium acetate (NH4OAc, pH 7.0) and determined by flame photometry. · Water-soluble K (WSK): Extracted with deionized water at a soil: water ratio of 1:10 and determined by flame photometry.

Exchangeable K (EK): Calculated as EK = AK-WSK.

Exchangeable K (EK): Calculated as EK = AK-WSK.

Non-exchangeable K (NEK): Extracted with 1.0 mol/L boiling HNO₃ for 10 min and determined by flame photometry; NEK = HNO3-K-AK.

Non-exchangeable K (NEK): Extracted with 1.0 mol/L boiling HNO₃ for 10 min and determined by flame photometry; NEK = HNO3-K-AK.

Mineral K (MK): Calculated as MK = TK-HNO3-K.

Mineral K (MK): Calculated as MK = TK-HNO3-K.

Determination of K content in each part of tobacco plant

Tobacco plant samples were collected at 30, 60, 90, and 120 DAT (three plants per replicate plot). Plants were separated into roots, stems, and leaves. Roots were carefully washed with tap water followed by distilled water to remove soil particles. All plant parts were placed in kraft paper bags, killed at 105 °C for 30 min, and then dried at 70 °C to constant weight. Dry matter weight was recorded for each part. Dried samples were ground for K determination. Plant K content was determined using the H2SO4-H2O2 digestion method followed by flame photometry17. Nutrient accumulation (mg/plant) in each part was calculated as: K content (%) × dry matter weight (g). Nutrient distribution ratio (%) for each part was calculated as: (K accumulation in the part/Total K accumulation in the whole plant) × 100%18.

Data analysis

Raw sequencing reads were processed using QIIME2 (v.2023.5). Sequences were quality-filtered, denoised, merged, and chimera-checked using DADA2 to obtain amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomy was assigned to ASVs using the SILVA database (v.138) with a 99% similarity threshold. Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon, Coverage) were calculated within QIIME2. Differential abundance analysis was performed using STAMP software. Soil and plant K data were processed using Microsoft Excel 2010 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and means were compared using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results and analyses

Changes in soil K content in different forms under different preceding crops

Figure 1 illustrates the dynamic changes in various soil K forms under different treatments throughout the tobacco growth period. Total K (TK, Fig. 1a): Before transplanting (0 d), TK content in T1 and T2 soils was 14.84% and 11.44% higher than CK, respectively. This significant initial difference persisted throughout the experiment. At maturity (90 and 120 DAT), TK contents in T1 and T2 remained significantly higher than in CK (by 10.80% and 9.54% at 90 d; 5.49% and 11.84% at 120 d, for T1 and T2 respectively). Available K (AK, Fig. 1b): AK content fluctuated similarly across treatments, showing an initial increase, followed by a decrease, and a final increase. At 30 DAT, AK followed T1 > T2 > CK, with T1 and T2 being 165.21% and 55.10% higher than CK, respectively. At 60 DAT, the order was T2 > T1 > CK. By 120 DAT (harvest), AK was again T1 > T2 > CK, with T1 and T2 significantly exceeding CK by 613.24% and 196.74%, respectively. Exchangeable K (EK, Fig. 1c): The EK content in T1 and T2 treatments showed considerable variation, increasing initially, then decreasing, and increasing again towards harvest. In contrast, CK showed minimal change. At harvest (120 DAT), EK content in T1 and T2 was 576.84% and 185.00% higher than in CK, respectively. Non-exchangeable K (NEK, Fig. 1d): The NEK content in T1 and T2 exhibited an opposite trend to EK, decreasing initially, then increasing, and decreasing again. The trend in CK was less pronounced. At 120 DAT, NEK content in T2 was 13.33% higher than in CK. Water-soluble K (WSK, Fig. 1e): WSK content in T1 and T2 varied significantly, mirroring the trend of initial increase, decrease, and subsequent increase, while CK remained relatively stable. The peak WSK content for T1 occurred at 120 DAT (723.50% higher than CK), and for T2 at 60 DAT (680.72% higher than CK). At 120 DAT, T2 WSK was still 335.33% higher than CK. Mineral K (MK, Fig. 1f): All treatments showed a general decreasing trend in MK content over time. Before transplanting, CK had the lowest MK level. By harvest (120 DAT), MK content in T2 was significantly higher than in T1 and CK. The decrease in MK from 0 to 120 DAT was most pronounced in T1 (18.98%), followed by T2 (6.84%), and smallest in CK (5.43%).

The K content in different parts of tobacco plants throughout the growth period is shown in Table 2. Significant differences were observed among treatments and plant parts over time. K allocation in tobacco plants under different preceding crop treatments

The distribution of K within different parts of the tobacco plant is shown in Fig. 2. As the growth period progressed, the proportion of K allocated to leaves generally decreased, while the proportions allocated to roots and stems increased. Nevertheless, leaves remained the primary site for K accumulation, significantly higher than stems, with roots having the lowest accumulation. At 30 DAT, the T1 treatment allocated significantly more K to leaves (6.10% and 6.68% higher than CK and T2, respectively). At 60 DAT, T2 allocated the highest proportion of K to leaves (7.80% and 8.25% higher than CK and T1, respectively). By 90 DAT, the proportion of K in roots was significantly lower in CK than in T1 and T2, while the proportion in stems increased. At harvest (120 DAT), the proportion of K allocated to leaves decreased in the order T2 > T1 > CK. The leaf K allocation in T2 was 7.49% higher than in CK, and T1 was 5.04% higher than in CK.

Soil bacterial diversity and sequencing depth

Alpha diversity indices of the soil bacterial communities are presented in Table 2. The Chao1 index (richness) and Shannon index (diversity) were highest in the T2 treatment, followed by T1, and lowest in CK. Significant differences were observed between CK and the rotation treatments (T1, T2). The Chao1 index of CK was 12.00% and 16.21% lower than that of T1 and T2, respectively. The Shannon index of CK was 1.88% and 5.72% lower than that of T1 and T2, respectively. The Coverage indices were all above 0.98, indicating that the sequencing depth adequately captured the bacterial diversity present in the samples. The rarefaction curves (Fig. 3) plateaued, confirming that sufficient sequencing depth was achieved to represent the majority of microbial diversity, allowing for reliable downstream analysis.

Discussion

Effect of different preceding crops on the content of different forms of K in soil

Potassium is essential for tobacco growth, and its availability directly impacts yield and quality13. Soil K is categorized into available K (water-soluble and exchangeable), non-exchangeable K, and mineral K from a plant nutrition perspective14,15. Available K reflects the immediate K supply capacity of the soil16,17. Our results demonstrate that preceding crops (T1, T2) significantly enhanced the levels of available K (AK), exchangeable K (EK), and water-soluble K (WSK) compared to continuous tobacco (CK) throughout the growth period, especially at maturity (Figs. 1b, c, e). This aligns with previous findings where management practices like straw return increased soil available K19. The observed fluctuations in these available pools (increase-decrease-increase) likely correspond to plant uptake dynamics and the replenishment from less available pools.

Conversely, the non-exchangeable K (NEK) pool in T1 and T2 showed an inverse trend compared to EK (decrease-increase-decrease, Fig. 1d), suggesting a dynamic equilibrium and conversion between these pools. As plants uptake available K, NEK is released to maintain supply20,21. The general decrease in mineral K (MK) over time (Fig. 1f), most notably in T1, indicates that mineral weathering contributed to the K supply, particularly in rotation treatments22,23. The significantly higher initial TK in T1 and T2 plots (Fig. 1a) likely stems from the preceding crops’ root systems and residue incorporation, which can influence soil structure and nutrient retention24,25. The less pronounced changes in K forms in CK suggest a depleted and less dynamic K cycling system, whereas the rotation treatments fostered a more robust soil K supplying capacity by enhancing the transformation between different K pools. This transformation is potentially mediated by rhizosphere microorganisms, such as potassium-solubilizing microorganisms (KSMs), which can solubilize insoluble K into plant-available forms20,21,26.

Effects of different preceding crop treatments on the uptake and distribution of K in tobacco plants

High-quality tobacco requires a leaf K content exceeding 2%11. Potassium is highly mobile within plants, leading to spatiotemporal heterogeneity in its distribution12,27. Our results confirm this heterogeneity, showing varying K contents in roots, stems, and leaves across growth stages and treatments (Table 2). Initially (90 DAT), T2 allocated more K to roots, but by harvest (120 DAT), it significantly increased K accumulation in leaves (3.17%), surpassing both CK (1.44%) and T1 (2.41%) (Table 2). This aligns with the common pattern where K accumulation peaks during mid-growth (40–60 DAT) and may translocate from leaves to stems or soil later in the season28, a trend also observed here in CK and T1. The superior leaf K accumulation in T2 at harvest suggests that rapeseed as a preceding crop more effectively sustained K availability during the critical later stages, promoting K remobilization and retention in leaves. This could be due to better K supply from soil (as seen in higher available K, Fig. 1b) and potentially improved root function. Agronomic practices like rotation influence K distribution by altering soil nutrient availability and plant physiological processes17,29. Our study demonstrates that preceding crops, particularly rapeseed, can significantly enhance the desired allocation of K to tobacco leaves, a key factor for quality.

Effects of different preceding crop treatments on the diversity and abundance of soil bacterial communities

Cropping systems profoundly impact soil microbial community structure by altering physicochemical properties and resource availability33,34. Consistent with other studies7,30, we found that rotation with preceding crops (T1, T2) significantly increased soil bacterial richness (Chao1) and diversity (Shannon) compared to continuous cropping (CK) (Table 3). The rapeseed rotation (T2) showed the highest values, suggesting that specific preceding crops can create a more favorable environment for a diverse bacterial community. This increase in diversity is often linked to improved soil health and ecosystem functioning31. Changes in crop type influence nutrient uptake patterns and organic matter inputs, subsequently shaping the microbial community composition25,32. The increased diversity under rotation likely contributes to enhanced nutrient cycling, including K dynamics, observed in this study.

Effect of different preceding crop treatments on bacterial species composition and variation in tobacco-planting soils

The soil bacterial community was dominated by Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Acidobacteria, Firmicutes, and Chloroflexi (Fig. 6), which is typical for agricultural soils35,36. Notably, the relative abundances of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria were higher in T1 and T2 compared to CK (Fig. 6). Many members of these phyla are known for their roles in nutrient cycling. Proteobacteria often include copiotrophic bacteria responsive to organic carbon inputs37, which might be enhanced by crop residue incorporation from preceding crops. Actinobacteria are renowned for their ability to decompose complex organic compounds and some are potent KSMs, playing a crucial role in releasing potassium from insoluble minerals26,38. Firmicutes (which includes the genus Bacillus, highly abundant in CK and T1, Fig. 5) also contain many known KSMs26,39. The shift in community structure, particularly the increase in Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria, along with the overall increase in diversity, likely facilitated the enhanced K solubilization and availability observed in the rotation treatments (Fig. 1). This created a positive feedback loop: improved K availability supported plant growth, whose roots and exudates further shaped a beneficial microbiome. The unique ASVs found in T2 (Fig. 4) might include specific bacterial taxa particularly effective in promoting K release and plant health under rapeseed rotation, warranting further investigation. These findings strengthen the link between management practice (preceding crop), soil microbial community structure, and soil nutrient (K) dynamics.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that using preceding crops, particularly rapeseed, in a tobacco rotation system significantly enhances soil potassium supply capacity by promoting the transformation of less available K forms (non-exchangeable, mineral) into plant-available forms (exchangeable, water-soluble). This improved K availability resulted in increased potassium accumulation and a preferable distribution towards tobacco leaves at harvest, a key determinant of quality. Furthermore, rotation with preceding crops, especially rapeseed, fostered a more diverse and abundant soil bacterial community. The observed shifts in community composition, notably the increase in phyla like Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria known for nutrient cycling and K solubilization, provide a mechanistic link between the cropping practice, the soil microbiome, and the enhanced K dynamics. In conclusion, selecting appropriate preceding crops, with rapeseed being the most effective option in this study, optimizes soil K availability, improves bacterial community structure, and enhances potassium utilization efficiency in tobacco. These findings offer a sustainable strategy for mitigating continuous cropping obstacles and improving tobacco quality. Future research should focus on elucidating the specific mechanisms through which different preceding crops and their associated microbiomes influence potassium transformation and plant uptake.
