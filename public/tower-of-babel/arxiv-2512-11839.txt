Large Language Models as Generalist Policies for Network Optimization
Duo Wu, Linjia Kang, Zhimin Wang, Fangxin Wang, Wei Zhang, Xuefeng Tao, Wei Yang, Le Zhang, Peng Cui, Zhi Wang

Abstract
Designing control policies to ensure robust network services is essential to modern digital infrastructure. However, the dominant paradigm for network optimization relies on designing specialist policies based on handcrafted rules or deep learning models, leading to poor generalization across diverse tasks and environments. In contrast, large language models (LLMs), pretrained on Internet-scale corpora, provide a rich and unified knowledge base that encodes fundamental networking principles. Combined with their emergent abilities in generalization to unseen scenarios, LLMs offer a transformative foundation for generalist network policies that can generalize across diverse tasks and environments with minimal adaptation. In this paper, we present Trailblazer , the first systematic framework to realize such a generalist policy for networking. Trailblazer incorporates a network alignment scheme to ground the LLM in specific networking tasks, and an adaptive policy collaboration mechanism that offloads simple control cases from the LLM to a lightweight policy for computational efficiency. Through extensive simulations and large-scale real-world online evaluation on Douyin (the Chinese version of TikTok), Trailblazer, powered by a single LLM, demonstrates stronger cross-task and cross-environment generalization than conventional specialist policies. Our results validate LLMs as the foundation for generalist network policies, and position Trailblazer as the first step toward the generalist-driven paradigm that enables strong generalization with minimal efforts in policy design.

Results

2.1
Trailblazer framework

Our
Trailblazer
framework, as depicted in Fig.
2
, consists of two complementary components: (1) NIOKA to align LLMs to networking (Fig.
2
a), and (2) APC for efficient deployment in real-world network systems (Fig.
2
b).

Network input-output-knowledge alignment.
Network states (e.g., latency, packet loss) and actions (e.g., transmission rates) are fundamentally different in modality from textual data. To address this misalignment, NIOKA introduces a network state encoder that first extracts features from raw network statistics using a feature encoder, and maps the extracted features into the same semantic feature space as language tokens with a linear projection layer. This allows the LLM to interpret non-linguistic network information as if processing natural language. Besides, NIOKA replaces the LLM’s original prediction head for language tokens with a dedicated network action decoder, which transforms the LLM-produced high-dimensional feature vectors into actionable network control decisions. The state encoder and action decoder are both trainable to learn optimal projection functions. With the above adaptation, the LLM is able to function as a generalist model for processing network states and generating network actions.

To bridge the knowledge misalignment, NIOKA further fine-tunes the LLM to acquire domain knowledge for networking with an offline reinforcement fine-tuning algorithm. Specifically, we evaluate conventional non-LLM network policies across various network environments and collect their interactions with the environments, i.e., network state-action pairs and the associated rewards or near-optimal actions, as the experience dataset. This dataset serves as the training fuel for fine-tuning the LLM. By using rewards or near-optimal actions as guiding signals, the LLM can analyze the behavior of existing policies, learn from effective actions, and uncover the reasons behind poor actions, thereby automatically discovering a better performing policy. Consequently, this algorithm effectively harnesses the powerful pattern mining capabilities of the LLM to develop a superior network policy.

Adaptive policy collaboration.

Considering the inherent conflict between the high inference latency of LLMs and the stringent real-time requirements of practical network systems, we propose APC for
Trailblazer
. The key idea is to use a scheduler to selectively offload flow requests from the LLM to a lightweight rule-based policy for fast processing, thereby accelerating the processing speed of the LLM-based network systems. Specifically, for each incoming request, the scheduler evaluates its network conditions and only routes those under poor network conditions to the LLM. The rest under stable conditions are instead handled efficiently by a conventional policy. Under this mechanism, the collaboration between the LLM and conventional policy contributes to a more robust network system that exploits the strong capabilities of the LLM to address complex scenarios while offloading a large volume of simple requests to a lightweight policy to improve the overall efficiency. To further improve the system efficiency, the LLM will process requests in batches to reduce the per-request processing latency.

For the scheduler design, we adopt a set of heuristic, deterministic rules (e.g., whether packet loss exceeds a predefined threshold) to classify incoming requests.
While more sophisticated schedulers (e.g., learning-based) are possible, a rule-based design ensures minimal overhead and fast processing speed, which is critical for routing the large volume of concurrent requests in real-world network systems.

2.2.2
Generalization comparison of generalist and specialist policies

Cross-task generalization.

A core limitation of the specialist-driven paradigm is its inability to transfer knowledge across tasks. To evaluate whether a generalist-driven approach can overcome this barrier, we compare Trailblazer against specialist policies on two heterogeneous tasks ABR and CJS. As shown in Fig.
a, Trailblazer consistently outperforms all baselines, achieving 14.5%-36.6% higher QoE on ABR and reducing JCT by 6.8%-41.3% on CJS. Notably, Fig.
a depicts that conventional rule-based or learning-based policies, which rely on task specific design of control rules or model architectures, fail to generalize across different tasks. In contrast, powered by a single LLM, Trailblazer successfully generalizes across heterogeneous networking tasks, achieving stronger cross-task generalization. These results demonstrate that LLMs can serve as a unified foundation for generalist network policies, breaking the task-isolation barrier of the specialist paradigm.

Cross-environment generalization.

Robustness to unseen environments remains a critical weakness of specialist policies.
To assess whether a generalist policy can achieve stronger cross-environment generalization, we evaluate Trailblazer across various challenging out-of-distribution (OOD) test environments that differ substantially from the training conditions. For example, the bandwidth fluctuating patterns in the testing environments are more dynamic than those in the training environments.
As shown in Fig.
b, Trailblazer consistently outperforms all baselines in terms of average values and distributions across all cases. For example, compared to rule-based and learning-based policies, it improves the mean QoE by 3.9%-24.8% and 1.5%-44.3%, reduces the mean JCT by 2.5%-6.8% and 10.5%-41.6%, respectively. This consistent advantage underscores a key strength of the generalist paradigm: by leveraging the strong generalization capabilities of LLMs, it generalizes across heterogeneous environments where specialist approaches often fail.

2.2.3
Effects of knowledge and LLM model scale in networking

To gain deeper insights into the success of LLMs as generalist policies in networking, we investigate the importance of pretrained knowledge and domain-specific knowledge. We further study the impact of LLM model scale on task performance.

Insight 1:

Pretrained knowledge enables LLMs to function as generalist policies for networking.

To assess the role of pretrained knowledge, we discard the pretrained weights of the LLM, reinitialize it, and train it from scratch on each downstream task.
As shown in Fig.
3
a, this variant suffers from significant performance degradation across both the ABR and CJS tasks.

This finding validates that the pretrained knowledge of the LLM indeed encodes transferable and abstract network knowledge
, and serves as a critical prerequisite for the LLM to generalize effectively as a generalist policy for networking.

Insight 2:

Domain-specific knowledge is essential to unlock the full generalist potential of LLMs in networking.

We preserve the pretrained weights but freeze the entire LLM backbone and only fine-tune the network state encoder and action decoder. This prevents the LLM from directly acquiring task-specific networking knowledge during training. Fig.
3
a shows that despite retaining pretrained knowledge, this variant fails to generalize across different tasks,
highlighting that pretrained knowledge alone is insufficient without domain alignment. Trailblazer addresses this by bridging the gap between abstract pretrained knowledge and fine-grained domain expertise.
By jointly integrating these two facets, LLMs can serve as effective generalist policies for networking.

In addition, it is interesting to find that different tasks exhibit varying degrees of sensitivity to domain knowledge. For instance, in ABR, the Trailblazer variant still achieves good performance even without domain knowledge.

Insight 3:

LLMs exhibit early saturation in network optimization with increasing model scale.
We investigate the impact of LLM model scale on task performance with ABR as the example and use the OPT model family
63
of varying model scales for this investigation. As shown in Fig.
3
c, OPT-0.35B performs worse than baselines, potentially due to the insufficient pretrained knowledge of abstract networking principles. In contrast, all OPT-variants larger than 1B outperform all baselines, but performance saturates rapidly beyond this threshold, with larger models yielding only marginal gains. We term this phenomenon
early saturation
, which contrasts sharply with the scaling law in natural language processing (NLP) where performance consistently improves with model scale. Early saturation reveals an important insight that the model scale of LLM required for effective network optimization is relatively small. Thus, small LLMs can be utilized to achieve competitive performance while meeting the stringent low-latency requirements of real-world network systems.

3
Discussion

Over the past decades, network policy design has dominated by the
specialist-driven
paradigm, which handcrafts control rules or deep learning models for network optimization. However, this approach struggles to generalize across diverse network tasks and environments.

In contrast, LLMs offer a highly promising opportunity to establish a new generalist-driven paradigm, with the potential to truly achieve cross-task and cross-environment generalization.

Yet, their practical adoption in networking remains hindered by the misalignment between NLP and networking, as well as the computational inefficiency arising from their large model scale.

To this end, we present
Trailblazer
, the first systematic framework to ground LLMs as generalist policies for networking.

Trailblazer integrates NIOKA to achieve unified alignment between LLMs and diverse networking tasks, and APC to reduce unnecessary LLM computation via intelligent request routing.

Our extensive experiments demonstrate that Trailblazer, powered by a single LLM, significantly outperforms specialist policies in terms of cross-task and cross-environment generalization.

Crucially, large-scale online A/B tests over three weeks on Douyin show that Trailblazer outperforms the current industrial specialist policy VICC in key performance metrics, proved both reliable in production and capable of delivering measurable improvements in service quality.

Beyond the empirical success of LLMs as generalist network policies, an important question naturally arises: what drives LLMs’ generalization across diverse networking scenarios?
We argue that this generalization arises from the combination between the general pretrained knowledge of the LLM and domain-specific knowledge injected by Trailblazer.

The former provides a shared knowledge base that encodes abstract and transferable networking principles, while the latter enables the LLM to capture optimization patterns specific to network environments.

Our experiments confirm that removing either component leads to a clear drop in performance, showing that both types of knowledge are essential for effective generalization.
Besides, we further identify two counterintuitive yet critical insights. The first is early saturation, which reveals that the scaling law of LLMs does not always hold in networking tasks. Instead, with proper domain alignment, even small-scale LLMs can exhibit sufficient generalization. The second insight refers to selective invocation. In most common scenarios, lightweight conventional policies provide satisfactory performance. Designating LLMs as intelligent experts invoked only under complex network conditions achieves a better balance between efficiency and performance. These insights provide solid support for Trailblazer’s design–
the alignment scheme enables generalization for small-scale LLMs, and the selective invocation mechanism allows LLM-based generalist policies to efficiently operate under real-time network systems.

In summary, Trailblazer showcases the great potential of LLMs to serve as the foundation for generalist policies with strong generalization, and offers a practical blueprint for deploying these policies in real-world, latency-sensitive network systems. While empirically effective, the internal decision logic of LLMs remains difficult to interpret. Future work will focus on enhancing their explainability, for example, by mapping their reasoning process to explicit representations of their decision logic. Advancing this direction is essential to comprehend the capabilities and areas for improvement of LLM-based generalist policies.

Methods

4.1
Trailblazer implementation

This section describes the implementation of
Trailblazer
across three networking tasks: ABR, CJS, and CC. Specifically, we detail the design of NIOKA scheme in each task, including network state encoder, network action decoder, and offline reinforcement fine-tuning algorithm. We further present the design of the APC in the CC task, including the request scheduler that enables efficient LLM invocation, as well as specifications of the lightweight CC policy used to collaborate with the LLM.

4.1.1
Trailblazer for ABR

ABR plays a critical role in modern online video services. It divides a video into fixed-length segments (e.g., 4s) encoded at multiple bitrates (e.g., 750kbps and 2850kbps), and dynamically selects the bitrate for each segment based on observed network conditions and playback states, with the objective of maximizing the user’s QoE
43
;
25
. In this task, the state information comprises vectorized data, such as historical throughput measurements that capture bandwidth dynamics, and scalar data, such as current playback buffer length. The action space consists of discrete candidate bitrates for the next segment. The full list of states is provided in Extended Data Fig.
4
.

NIOKA implementation.
Regarding the properties of ABR, we use simple 1D convolutional neural network (CNN)
32
and a fully connected layer for the network state encoder to extract features from vectorized data and scalar data, respectively. The network action decoder is designed as a linear layer that computes a probability distribution over candidate bitrates, with the highest-probability bitrate selected for video download.

As for the offline reinforcement fine-tuning algorithm, we implement it based on the decision transformer (DT)
9
because of its efficiency. Formally, DT reformulates RL as a sequence modeling problem, which seamlessly caters to the sequence modeling nature of the LLM. To be specific, the LLM takes the historical returns, network states, and network actions to predict the next action:

LLM(\hat{a}_{i}|R_{i-w},s_{i-w},a_{i-w},\cdots,R_{i},s_{i}),

(1)

Return
R_{i}=\sum_{t}^{i}r_{t}
denotes the cumulative rewards expected to receive from state
s_{i}
, and
w
denotes the historical context window for effective learning. In ABR, rewards are defined as the QoE scores quantified by the linear combination of video bitrates, bitrate changes and rebuffering time
56
, and
w
is empirically set to 10. The training loss corresponds to the cross-entropy loss that minimizes the discrepancy between predicted action
\hat{a}_{i}
and ground-truth action
a_{i}
:

\mathcal{L}_{i}={CE}(\hat{a}_{i},a_{i}),

(2)

The core idea of this algorithm is to train the LLM to learn the distribution of actions conditioned on specific states and returns, enabling the LLM to generate actions to achieve the desired return after training. In particular, during inference, we specify a sufficiently high target return indicating the desired performance to trigger the LLM to generate high-quality actions.

4.1.2
Trailblazer for CJS

CJS is a core component of large-scale data analytics in high-performance computing systems, responsible for assigning incoming computational jobs to distributed computing resources
42
. Each job is represented as a directed acyclic graph (DAG), where nodes denote execution stages and their associated resource demands, and edges encode precedence dependencies between stages
37
. The objective of CJS is to select the next stage to execute and allocate an appropriate number of executors (i.e., computing resources) to it, so that average JCT can be minimized and the system-wide resource utilization can be maximized. In this task, the state comprises the job DAG information, while the action consists of two components: the target execution stage and the number of executors allocated to it.

NIOKA implementation.
For state encoding, we employ a graph neural network (GNN)
55
;
37
to capture structural and feature information from the job DAGs. For action decoding, we use two parallel linear layers: one predicts the next stage to execute, and the other determines the number of executors to allocate. The offline reinforcement fine-tuning follows the DT framework, consistent with the ABR implementation. The LLM is trained to predict actions conditioned on historical returns, states, and actions, with the reward defined as the negative JCT (i.e., shorter completion time yields higher reward and thereby higher return). The context window
w
is empirically set to 20.

4.1.3
Trailblazer for CC

CC is a fundamental component of computer networks, essential for improving network resource utilization and ensuring reliable data transmission
46
;
51
. For large-scale video platforms such as Douyin, CC plays a pivotal role in providing high-quality audio and video experiences to billions of users daily. Within Douyin, CC operates on the server side, continuously monitors network conditions such as delay, packet loss, and jitter, and estimates the dynamically varying available bottleneck bandwidth at fine-grained 100 ms intervals. This estimated bandwidth serves as an important factor to determine the transmission rate for each video session. In this regard, the state in the CC task comprises a set of time-varying network metrics collected from the system, while the action corresponds to the predicted bottleneck bandwidth. Detailed descriptions of the state information are provided in Extended Data Fig.
4
.

NIOKA implementation.
For the implementation of Trailblazer with the NIOKA scheme in CC, we employ a 1D CNN to extract features from the set of network metrics, and adopt a linear regression layer as the action decoder for continuous bandwidth prediction.

To enable effective offline fine-tuning, we propose a contextual imitation learning (CIL) algorithm that leverages ground-truth bottleneck bandwidths–obtained from network simulations–as near-optimal expert demonstrations to guide the LLM toward optimal congestion control policy. Although the true bottleneck bandwidth is unobservable in real-world network systems, it can be accurately inferred in controlled simulation environments during training, serving as a high-quality approximation of the optimal sending rate, the one that maximizes bandwidth utilization. We therefore use the simulated bottleneck bandwidth as the expert action
a^{e}_{i}
to supervise the LLM.
Formally, the LLM predicts the next action
\hat{a}_{i}
conditioned on a context window of historical states and actions:

LLM(\hat{a}_{i}|s_{i-w},a_{w-i},\cdots,s_{i-1},a_{i-1},s_{i}),

(3)

where
w
denotes the context length. The model is trained by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between the predicted and expert actions:

\mathcal{L}_{i}=MSE(\hat{a}_{i},a^{e}_{i}),

(4)

which is well-suited for regression over continuous bandwidth values. Note that unlike conventional imitation learning
65
;
62
that typically relies on short-term observations, CIL explicitly maintains a long context window to exploit the LLM’s powerful capability in capturing temporal patterns and contextual dependencies in network dynamics, thereby enabling more effective policy learning.

We further distinguish CIL from the DT framework, which conditions action prediction on returns-to-go in addition to states and past actions. The choice between CIL and DT to implement our offline reinforcement fine-tuning algorithm hinges on the availability of near-optimal expert demonstrations during training. When such high-quality supervision signals (e.g., bottleneck bandwidths from simulations in CC) are accessible, CIL is preferred: it directly learns to imitate optimal behavior without requiring return estimation, simplifying the learning objective. Moreover, by removing the need to encode return-to-go information, CIL reduces input sequence complexity and context length, leading to faster inference and lower computational overhead. In contrast, DT becomes more suitable in settings where near-optimal actions are not available, but historical trajectories with associated returns (e.g., cumulative QoE scores in ABR) can be reconstructed. In such cases, the return-to-go serves as a behavioral prior that guides action selection toward high-reward outcomes, even in the absence of explicit expert demonstrations. Thus, CIL and DT represent complementary paradigms: CIL excels in simulation-based offline training with oracle supervision, while DT is better suited for reward-driven learning from suboptimal traces.

APC implementation.
Due to the high inference latency of the LLM, it is impractical to deploy the fine-tuned LLM for fine-grained per-flow control, especially in the CC service of Douyin where requests must be processed within 100 ms under massive concurrency. Hence, we activate the APC of Trailblazer to improve the efficiency of LLM-based generalist policy while maintaining high performance. Specifically, the scheduler employs a lightweight rule-based classifier to distinguish between flows under good and poor network conditions. The request of a flow is classified as experiencing
good network conditions
if: (i) its last round-trip time is below a threshold
\alpha_{1}
; (ii) the packet loss rate is below
\alpha_{2}
; and (iii) the last sending rate exceeds
\alpha_{3}\times{rate}^{{req}}
. Flow requests meeting all three criteria are routed to a lightweight policy while all others are directed to the LLM for intelligent control. Here,
{rate}^{{req}}
is request rate, which denotes the application-specific upper bound on sending rate required to maintain the quality of video transmission. The request rate is computed by a dedicated module in Douyin. In practice, the three thresholds are set to
\alpha_{1}=50
(ms),
\alpha_{2}=0.05,\alpha_{3}=0.95
.

The lightweight policy used to collaborate with the LLM is simply designed to set the sending rate directly to
{rate}_{{req}}
. While this strategy tends to exhaust available bandwidth and induce congestion under poor network conditions, it performs reliably in general cases, where network conditions are stable and
{rate}_{{req}}
is often lower than actual bottleneck link capacity. This collaboration thus enables scalable deployment: the LLM is invoked only when necessary, while the majority of flow requests are handled efficiently by the lightweight policy. To further enhance system efficiency and reduce per-request processing latency, we set the LLM batch size to 64, achieving an average inference latency of 37.1 ms. This meets Douyin’s 100 ms response latency requirement for the CC task while preserving a safety margin to support system resilience. Extended Data Fig.
3
offers a detailed analysis of the impact of LLM inference batch size on CC task performance.

4.2
Experimental setup details

This section elaborates the details of experimental setups across the three networking tasks, covering simulators, datasets, metrics, baselines, as well as the construction of experience datasets for LLM fine-tuning.

4.2.1
Experimental details of ABR

Simulator.
We employ the widely adopted ABR simulator developed by Mao et al.
36
, which accurately models the dynamics of real-world video streaming applications through stateful representations of playback buffer and network environment. Given a bandwidth trace and video manifest file, the simulator computes the download time for each video chunk based on its bitrate and the current available bandwidth. It then drains the buffer by the download time to reflect ongoing playback, and adds the duration of the downloaded chunk to the buffer to simulate buffer occupancy. Owing to the faithful simulation of video streaming process, this simulator provides a flexible and efficient approach to evaluate ABR policies in diverse environments by varying the input bandwidth traces and video manifest files.

Dataset.
To evaluate the generalization capability of
our generalist approach
Trailblazer under diverse and challenging network environments, we employ a combination of real-world and synthetic datasets. For network dynamics, we use the
FCC

11
broadband measurement dataset as the primary source of bandwidth traces, which captures real-world variability across a representative sample of USA consumers. From this collection, we randomly select 485 traces, with 235 for training, 150 for validation, and 100 for testing. This results in the use of more than 324,000 seconds of bandwidth traces for experiments. Additionally, to evaluate generalization under more challenging network dynamics, we synthesize a more dynamic bandwidth dataset
SynthTrace
that comprises 100 traces with broader bandwidth ranges and more dynamic fluctuation patterns. For video content, we adopt the widely used
Envivio-Dash3

13
as the default video, and further introduce a synthetic video
SynthVideo
with larger chunk sizes for performance evaluation. Based on these datasets, we simulate various environments for generalization evaluation, as presented in Extended Data Table
1
.

Baseline.
We compare Trailblazer with state-of-the-art ABR
specialist
policies: the learning-based method GENET
56
, and rule-based methods, BBA
17
and MPC
60
. GENET is a streaming policy that combines curriculum learning and RL to train a small neural model to optimize video streaming. BBA heuristically considers buffer occupancy as a critical signal for bitrate control and designs an algorithm to maintain the playback buffer occupancy at a desired level. MPC leverages both throughput estimates and buffer occupancy to choose bitrates by optimizing a given QoE metric over a future time window.

Experience dataset construction.
To construct the experience dataset for fine-tuning the LLM with Trailblazer, the baseline GENET is adopted for experience collection. Specifically, we simulate diverse network environments, use GENET to interact with these environments, and collect their interactions in terms of states, actions and rewards as the training dataset for LLM fine-tuning.

Metric.
Following the common practice in ABR
36
;
60
;
56
, we adopt QoE as the primary performance metric, which is defined as the weighted linear combination of three metrics:

{QoE}_{i}={bitrate}_{i}-\lambda_{1}\times{rebuf}_{i}-\lambda_{2}\times|\Delta{bitrate}_{i}|,

(5)

where
{bitrate}_{i}
is the bitrate (in Mbps) of chunk
i
,
{rebuf}_{i}
is the rebuffering time (in seconds) during its download, and
|\Delta{bitrate}_{i}|=|{bitrate}_{i}-{bitrate}_{i-1}|
is the absolute bitrate change between consecutive chunks. The coefficients
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
control the trade-offs among these factors. Based on prior work
36
, we set
\lambda_{1}=4.3
and
\lambda_{2}=1
, reflecting the higher user sensitivity to rebuffering compared to bitrate fluctuations.

4.2.2
Experimental details of CJS

Simulator.
We use the open-source Apache Spark job scheduling simulator
14
to model task execution, resource allocation, and scheduling logics in Spark clusters. Given a workload trace consisting of a sequence of jobs and a number of executors representing the cluster computing resources, the simulator schedules tasks according to the specific policy within an emulated cluster environment. This enables controlled evaluation of scheduling policies under conditions that approximate real-world workloads.

Dataset.
The TPC-H benchmark
49
is used as the primary source of workload traces, which consists of a suite of business-oriented computational jobs that have broad industry-wide relevance. These jobs are characterized by large data volumes and high processing complexity, making TPC-H a widely adopted benchmark for job scheduling. By randomly sampling jobs from the TPC-H suite and varying the number of executor resources in the simulated cluster, we generate diverse experimental environments that span different workload intensities and resource availability. The simulation configurations are summarized in Extended Data Table
2
.

Baseline.
Similar to ABR, we implement one simple learning-based policy Decima
37
and two rule-based policies FIFO
5
and Fair
5
for performance comparison. Decima is an RL model for job scheduling with a GNN model to process job DAG information. Both FIFO and Fair are common scheduling policies used by big data processing system Spark
5
. The former schedules jobs in the order of their arrival and allocates the requested amount of resources to each job, while the latter schedules jobs in a “round robin” fashion to ensure that each job receives a roughly equal share of the cluster resources.

Experience dataset construction.
The experience dataset is constructed by interacting the Decima model with diverse simulated training environments. The resulting state-action-reward tuples are used as the training fuel to fine-tune the LLM based on Trailblazer.

Metric.
We use JCT as the evaluation metric following the literature
37
. Let
t_{s}
denote the job arrival time and
t_{e}
denote the job finishing time. The JCT is calculated by:

JCT=t_{e}-t_{s}.

(6)

4.2.3
Experimental details of CC

Baseline.
The production-grade VICC policy is used as the primary baseline across all experiments. Deployed in Douyin’s real-time services, VICC is an adaptive
specialist
policy designed to balance bandwidth utilization and latency across global network conditions and diverse audiovisual applications. It incorporates multiple adaptive mechanisms such as congestion response, bandwidth probing, packet loss detection, and jitter resilience. These capabilities enable dynamic adaptation to changing network states and application priorities, achieving high bandwidth efficiency while meeting strict application-specific latency requirements. As a result, VICC enhances the quality of real-time media transmission in practice, serving as a strong, production-grade baseline in our evaluation.

Experience dataset construction.
Based on Douyin’s internal development platform, we construct the experience dataset to fine-tune the LLM for the CC task. Specifically, we establish real-world video sessions using six mobile devices and nine types of media content (e.g., singing and gaming), and impose diverse and complex network conditions (e.g., varying bandwidth, delay, and packet loss) using the enterprise-grade network emulator HoloWAN
47
. The device, media, and network configurations are highly aligned with Douyin’s online settings. During data collection, for each session, CC decisions are determined by one of the four rule-based policies randomly selected from Douyin. This process yields an experience dataset comprising over 30,000 sessions and more than 10 million data samples. The dataset is further split into a 95% training subset for LLM fine-tuning and a 5% test set for performance validation, including model scale selection and evaluation of the selective invocation mechanism.

Online deployment.

For online A/B tests, we deploy six media servers that are responsible for ensuring service quality for media sessions and implement the CC logic. Incoming flow requests are randomly routed to these servers. Among them, three run the VICC policy, while the other three are configured with Trailblazer for intelligent control. In Trailblazer, the scheduler and the lightweight CC policy are deployed locally on the media servers. The LLM, in contrast, is hosted on a dedicated GPU server.
We use Qwen2.5-0.5B as the LLM and set inference batch size to 64. With these configurations, the LLM consumes approximately 4.5 GB GPU memory and takes about 30 ms per inference.
The scheduler and the LLM communicate via the WebSocket API. When the scheduler identifies incoming flows experiencing poor network conditions, it forwards their contextual information to the LLM. Upon completion of inference, the LLM returns control decisions to the scheduler for execution.

By combining a small-scale LLM, batched inference, and collaborative control, Trailblazer can handle the concurrent requests assigned by Douyin to the three media servers. It consistently keeps end-to-end request processing latency below 100 ms, which satisfies the real-time requirements of CC in Douyin.

Metric.

For offline performance validation of Trailblazer, we adopt MAPE as the primary metric, which quantifies the relative discrepancy between the predicted bandwidth and bottleneck link capacity or request rate. Given the predicted bandwidth
b^{p}_{i}
, the true simulated bottleneck bandwidth
b^{t}_{i}
and the request rate
rate^{req}_{i}
, MAPE is computed by:

{MAPE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\min(b^{p}_{i},rate^{req}_{i})-\min(b^{t}_{i},rate^{req}_{i})}{\min(b^{t}_{i},rate^{req}_{i})}\right|,

(7)

where
n
is the number of samples in the validation dataset.

To evaluate system efficiency, we measure request processing delay, which is defined as the time interval between the arrival of a flow request at the system and the receipt of the corresponding CC decision. This delay consists of LLM queuing delay and inference latency. Under high request load, queuing delay becomes dominant because LLM inference latency remains relatively stable for a fixed processing batch size.

For online A/B tests, video stall rate is used as the key performance metric, which is a key industrial indicator that directly reflects playback smoothness and user experience. The stall rate is calculated as the ratio of the total stall duration to the total playback duration across all flows:

{stall\_rate}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}^{s}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}^{p}},

(8)

where
d_{i}^{s}
denotes the cumulative stall duration of flow
i
as continuously reported by a monitor,
d_{i}^{p}
denotes the total playback duration of flow
i
, and
N
is the total number of flows. A lower stall rate generally contributes to improved user retention and engagement, making it highly relevant to business outcomes. The relative reduction of Trailblazer over VICC is then computed by:

reduction=\frac{stall\_rate_{VICC}-state\_rate_{Trailblazer}}{stall\_rate_{VICC}}.

(9)

4.3
Related work

LLM-based network optimization.
Since the introduction of our prior work NetLLM
54
, several efforts have sought to apply LLMs to optimize various networking tasks.
Jiao et al.
22
propose a 6G-oriented multi-modal model. Their work integrates LLM’s contextual understanding with wireless domain knowledge to optimize physical-layer network tasks.
Jia et al.
19
integrate decision tree generation with LLM to incorporate interpretability into adaptive bitrate streaming algorithm design. Liu et al.
35
adapt pretrained LLMs for wireless channel modeling via multi-task fine-tuning to improve few-shot performance.
Lee et al.
30
leverage client-side LLMs for cross-layer reasoning in WiFi roaming, optimizing the trade-off between roaming stability and signal quality.
He et al.
16
use LLMs to generate congestion control algorithms to accelerate the design process of network algorithms.
While these studies advance the application of LLMs in networking, they are typically confined to task-specific designs and simulation-level evaluations.
In contrast, Trailblazer introduces a systematic framework to ground LLMs as generalist policies that generalize across diverse network tasks and environments.
Besides, we move beyond simulations by conducting large-scale online A/B tests to validate Trailblazer in industrial network environments, demonstrating its superior performance and reliability in real-world network services. Furthermore, our experiments reveal two critical phenomena: early saturation and selective invocation, which are overlooked in prior work.
These insights provide practical principles for effective and efficient deployment of LLM-based generalist policies.

Large and small model collaboration.
In the field of natural language processing, multiple studies have explored collaborative mechanisms between small and large models to balance performance and computational cost
10
;
26
;
12
;
21
. For instance,
Ding et al.
12
route queries among small models and LLMs based on quality requirements and predicted difficulty to reduce LLM inference cost and maintain quality, while
Ong et al.
41
achieve the same goal by designing a dynamic router trained on human preference data.
However, these studies primarily target applications with second-level latency tolerance such as chat and question answering. Besides, they often rely on customized neural networks or LLMs for routing queries, incurring non-negligible inference overhead that limits their applications in resource-constrained equipment (e.g., CPU-only devices). By comparison, Trailblazer is designed for millisecond-level network control. It employs a lightweight, rule-based scheduler to enable fast and reliable routing decisions, addressing the real-time limitation of prior studies.
