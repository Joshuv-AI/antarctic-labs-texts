Association between information overload, asthenopia, and depressive and anxiety symptoms in patients with lung cancer: A computer-simulated network analysis
Authors: Jiahao Wei, Honglang Jiang, Yue Xiong, Cheng Lei, Mengdan Wang, Jingjing Xu, Wenhao Zhu, Xuehua Ren, Jin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine symptom-level associations among cancer information overload (IO), asthenopia, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms in patients with lung cancer and to compare model-based node-perturbation rankings with centrality rankings from a Gaussian graphical model (GGM).MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 566 patients with lung cancer completed assessments of IO, asthenopia, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. A 25-node binary Ising model was estimated. Network Intervention Response Analysis (NIRA) was used to evaluate model-estimated changes in network sum scores after hypothetical node-specific threshold perturbations. A GGM based on the corresponding non-dichotomized variables was used as a sensitivity analysis, and network stability was evaluated using bootstrap procedures.ResultsThe Ising network showed prominent conditional associations between IO4 (perceived impossibility) and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) item 2 (sad mood), asthenopia and PHQ-9 item 3 (sleep problems), and IO1 (decision overload) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7) item 2 (uncontrollable worry). Under the hypothetical alleviating perturbation, perturbing “Sad mood” produced the largest estimated decrease in the network sum score, from an unperturbed baseline of 12.71 to 10.85. Under the hypothetical aggravating perturbation, perturbing “Trouble relaxing” produced the largest estimated increase, from an unperturbed baseline of 12.74 to 14.63. In the GGM, information skepticism and sleep problems were among the most central nodes. The Ising and GGM absolute edge-weight patterns showed broad correspondence (Mantel r = 0.8385, P < 0.001), although asthenopia was operationalized differently across models.ConclusionsThis cross-sectional study characterized model-estimated associations among IO, asthenopia, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms in patients with lung cancer. The perturbation findings are hypothesis-generating and identify candidate symptoms for future prospective testing; they do not establish clinical effectiveness, therapeutic benefit, or causal effects.

Introduction

In 2024, lung cancer was the most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide, accounting for approximately 2.6 million new cases (12.8% of all new cancer cases), and remained the leading cause of cancer death.1 Within this complex medical information environment, negative emotional states such as anxiety and depression have become increasingly prevalent among patients with lung cancer. These symptoms significantly compromise their quality of life and adherence to treatment.2 Studies indicate that the prevalence of anxiety in this population ranges from 38.0% to 43.5%, while depression prevalence varies from 44.5% to 57.1%.3,4 Therefore, identifying factors that contribute to anxiety and depression is essential for developing effective support strategies tailored to patients with lung cancer.

Currently, the management of lung cancer is shifting towards multidisciplinary and precision-based approaches, resulting in prolonged patient survival. This advancement, however, is accompanied by an overwhelming volume of complex and often contradictory medical information related to diagnosis, treatment options, symptom management, prognosis, and survivorship care. Information overload (IO) is defined as a psychological state in which individuals feel overwhelmed by excessive cancer-related information in their environment.5 Studies indicate that approximately 80.2% of patients with cancer obtain information about treatment and rehabilitation through social media platforms.6 While these channels provide abundant information and communication opportunities, input exceeding an individual’s processing capacity may increase cognitive burden, impair decision-making, and elevate psychological distress.7

Asthenopia is a multifactorial syndrome primarily characterized by ocular strain, dryness, blurred vision, and headaches.8 Among frequent electronic device users in the general population, the prevalence of asthenopia can be as high as 90%.9 However, existing research has predominantly focused on adolescent populations.10 For patients with lung cancer, who frequently read medical materials, use electronic devices for communication, and search for health-related information, asthenopia may represent a significant yet under-recognized clinical concern.

Current evidence suggests that negative emotions in newly diagnosed patients with lung cancer are significantly associated with increased cancer-related IO.2 Moreover, visual impairment has been shown to mediate the relationship between short-video addiction and depression in rural older adults.11 The Limited Capacity Model of Motivated Mediated Message Processing (LC4MP) proposes that individuals possess finite cognitive resources for information processing, allocated based on motivational systems such as self-defense and information-seeking.12 Activation of these motivational systems consumes cognitive capacity and triggers physiological arousal and emotional responses. The treatment landscape for lung cancer is complex and rapidly evolving, which can easily overwhelm patients with IO.13 Disorganized or excessive information intake can exacerbate psychological stress, thereby inducing or intensifying negative emotions and asthenopia. Although prior studies have identified associations among these variables, most have relied on aggregate score-level analyses and static mediation models, limiting insight into dynamic, symptom-level interactions. The symptom-level associations among specific dimensions of IO, asthenopia, depression, and anxiety remain insufficiently understood. Additionally, an integrated empirical model from a holistic perspective is lacking. Previous network analysis studies identified potential intervention targets by calculating centrality metrics (e.g., strength, betweenness) for all nodes within the symptom network.14 However, while centrality indices reflect the connectivity and potential importance of symptoms, they do not explicitly model dynamic interactions among symptoms over time. Moreover, in clinical practice, symptoms rarely resolve completely after intervention and may be prone to relapse.

NIRA is a computational algorithm that applies hypothetical node-specific threshold perturbations within a fitted Ising model and recalculates the model-implied network sum score.15 This simulation generates predicted responses across all other symptoms, mapping cascading effects and revealing the network’s stressor-response dynamics. Comparisons of network structures before and after simulated interventions may help identify candidate targets for future prevention and intervention studies, based on the extent of estimated network-wide change. Thus, this study adopts a computational, simulation-based network analysis approach to address the following research questions: (1) How is IO associated with specific symptoms of asthenopia, depression, and anxiety? (2) Which symptom-specific threshold perturbations produce the largest estimated decreases or increases in the network sum score within the fitted Ising model? (3) Do node rankings obtained from GGM centrality analysis differ from rankings based on NIRA perturbation estimates?

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted between January and March 2025, adhering to the STROBE guidelines.16 Participants were recruited via convenience sampling from the Department of Oncology and Department of Thoracic Surgery at the Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, Southern Medical University in Guangzhou, China. Standardized training was provided to investigators, who explained the study objectives in detail and informed participants of their right to withdraw at any time. Inclusion criteria were: (a) a clinical diagnosis of lung cancer; (b) ability to communicate in Mandarin Chinese; and (c) provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria at the participant-recruitment stage were: (a) hearing, visual, or cognitive impairment that interfered with questionnaire completion; and (b) a documented history of a major psychiatric disorder. Previous studies have proposed simulation-based approaches for sample size planning in cross-sectional network models.17 A total of 581 questionnaires were collected. Following data-quality screening using the criterion described in Section 2.4, 15 questionnaires were excluded. Consequently, 566 of the 581 collected questionnaires (97.4%) were retained for analysis.

Measurement

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Based on previous literature and clinical experience,18 the sociodemographic variables collected were age, religious affiliation, self-reported time spent seeking digital health information per session, occupation, place of residence, marital status, education level, and monthly income (CNY). Cancer-related clinical characteristics included tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, disease duration, treatment regimen, recurrence, and metastasis.

Cancer IO

Cancer IO was assessed using the Cancer IO Scale (CIOS).5 The Chinese-language version used in this study was based on the Chinese translation reported by Ding et al.,19 who obtained permission from the original developer and evaluated its reliability and validity in Chinese patients with atrial fibrillation. This Chinese-language version has also been used in Chinese patients with head and neck cancer undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy.20 The CIOS is a unidimensional, self-report measure consisting of eight items. Each item is rated on a four-point Likert scale from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 4 (“completely agree”). A total score is calculated by summing all items, ranging from 8 to 32, with higher scores indicating greater perceived cancer-related IO. In this study, the CIOS demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.88).

Asthenopia

Asthenopia severity was evaluated using an 11-item questionnaire developed and validated in a Chinese population by Deng et al.21 The questionnaire comprises two subscales: ocular symptoms, and visual function combined with systemic symptoms. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale from 0 (“none”) to 4 (“extremely severe”). Total scores ranged from 0 to 44, calculated by summing all responses, with higher scores indicating more severe asthenopia. For the network analysis, the 11 asthenopia items were not entered separately. Instead, an aggregated binary asthenopia node (AS) was derived from the total asthenopia score. Participants with a total score > 8.5 were coded as 1, indicating the presence of asthenopia, whereas those with a total score ≤ 8.5 were coded as 0, indicating the absence of asthenopia. This instrument has been validated in Chinese populations and demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94) in the current sample.

Anxiety

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Chinese version of the seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7), originally developed by Spitzer et al.22 The Chinese version was translated and validated among Chinese general-hospital outpatients by He et al.23 Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”). Total scores range from 0 to 21, calculated by summing all responses, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety severity. In clinical screening, a total score ≥ 10 is the optimal cutoff to identify probable anxiety cases, demonstrating sensitivity of 89% and specificity of 82%. In this study, the GAD-7 showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89).

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Chinese version of the nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), originally developed by Kroenke et al.24 The Chinese version has been validated in the general Chinese population by Wang et al.25 Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”). Total scores range from 0 to 27, calculated by summing all items, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. In this sample, the PHQ-9 demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.82).

Statistical analysis

Before analysis, all questionnaires underwent data-quality screening. The four study instruments, the CIOS, ASQ-11, GAD-7, and PHQ-9, comprised 35 items in total. Questionnaires with eight or more unanswered items, representing more than 20% of these items, were excluded. Missingness was assessed across all 35 items collectively rather than separately for each instrument. Of the 581 questionnaires collected, 15 were excluded based on this criterion, leaving 566 for analysis. None of the 19,810 expected item responses were missing from the retained questionnaires; therefore, missing-data imputation was neither required nor performed.

Differences in demographic characteristics, clinical features, and total scale scores between excluded (n = 15) and included (n = 566) groups were compared. Continuous variables were analyzed using independent-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were assessed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Results showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups (P > 0.05), indicating that data exclusion did not introduce systematic bias.

Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 26.0. To identify potential targets for health prevention and intervention, a network of IO, asthenopia, anxiety, and depressive symptoms was modeled using the Ising model and NIRA.26 All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1. The Ising network was estimated using the IsingFit package (version 0.4), with the extended Bayesian information criterion hyperparameter explicitly set to γ = 0.25 and the AND rule applied for edge selection. Network visualization and bootstrap analyses were performed using qgraph (version 1.9.8) and bootnet (version 1.6), respectively. Network intervention response analysis was conducted using nodeIdentifyR (version 1.0.0) and IsingSampler (version 0.2.4). The Mantel test was performed using vegan (version 2.7.1) with 9999 permutations. Complete software and package-version information is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Because the Ising model and NIRA require binary variables, all network variables were prepared as binary nodes before analysis. Individual items from the CIOS, GAD-7, and PHQ-9 were entered separately into the network. For GAD-7 and PHQ-9 items, responses were coded as 0 if the original item score was 0 and as one if the original item score was ≥ 1, indicating symptom presence.27 For CIOS items, a score of 1 was coded as 0, indicating no perceived overload, whereas scores of 2–4 were coded as 1, indicating the presence of perceived overload. The asthenopia questionnaire was treated differently: the 11 asthenopia items were first summed to obtain a total score, and this total score was then dichotomized using the established cutoff. A total score > 8.5 was coded as 1, indicating asthenopia presence, and a total score ≤ 8.5 was coded as 0, indicating asthenopia absence. Therefore, the final Ising network consisted of 25 observed binary nodes: eight CIOS items, one aggregated asthenopia node (AS), seven GAD-7 items, and nine PHQ-9 items.

Model parameters were estimated using logistic regression in an iterative process. Each variable was regressed against all others.28 The intercept from each regression corresponds to the node’s threshold parameter, representing its baseline activation probability within the network. The network comprising IO, asthenopia, anxiety, and depressive symptoms was estimated using the IsingFit R package and visualized with the qgraph R package.29 In the resulting Ising network plot, nodes represent symptoms, and edges represent regularized conditional associations estimated as logistic regression coefficients. Blue solid edges indicate positive associations, red dashed edges indicate negative associations, and thicker edges indicate larger absolute edge weights. The regularization parameter (λ) was selected by minimizing the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) with hyperparameter (γ) set to 0.25, balancing sensitivity and specificity. The “AND” rule was applied to determine final edges, requiring mutual predictability between nodes.

The choice of the Ising model was guided by the mathematical structure of the NIRA algorithm. Within the Ising-model framework, NIRA estimates network-wide changes following hypothetical node-specific threshold perturbations. The resulting rankings may be used to identify candidate symptoms for future prospective testing; however, they do not establish clinically optimal intervention targets. The Ising model defines the joint probability distribution of a set of binary variables using the energy-based formula:P(Y=y)=1zexp(-βH(y))where the energy function is given by:H(y)=-∑i=1mτiyi-∑i<jωijyiyjhere, τi represents the threshold parameter for node i, its baseline tendency to be active (symptom present) versus inactive (absent). ωij represents the interaction strength between nodes i and j, and β is the inverse temperature parameter scaling overall connection strength. Lower energy states correspond to more probable symptom configurations, with positive ωij promoting symptom co-activation.30 NIRA performs intervention simulations by systematically altering the threshold (τi) of a given node and recalculating the expected sum score across the entire network. This evaluates how a targeted change in one node’s threshold shifts the global energy landscape. This framework is currently well-established only for Ising models with binary variables.31 Although Mixed Graphical Models (MGM) can estimate conditional dependence in continuous or mixed data, extending NIRA to continuous-variable frameworks requires fundamentally different mathematical approaches and remains under methodological development.

The NIRA algorithm was then applied to conduct intervention simulations. Based on the Ising model estimates, 5000 simulated observations were generated. Two types of symptom-specific interventions were simulated for each node: (a) an alleviating intervention, decreasing the node’s threshold by two standard deviations (2 SD), and (b) an aggravating intervention, increasing the threshold by 2 SD. It should be noted that the 2 SD threshold perturbation is primarily a technical parameter used in the NIRA simulation, and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of clinically meaningful change in symptom severity. In the Ising model, the threshold (τi) determines the log-odds of activation for node i, as expressed by: logit(P(Xi=1))=τi.

After applying a 2 SD intervention, log-odds activation uniformly changes by this constant, preserving relative node order.32 These adjustments systematically affected activation probabilities of all nodes. For each node-specific threshold perturbation, responses were simulated from the fitted Ising model and the corresponding model-implied network sum score was calculated. The fitted edge-weight matrix was held constant, while the threshold of the focal node was perturbed under the alleviating or aggravating condition.33

For each node-specific perturbation, the model-implied network sum score was compared with that of the unperturbed fitted model. A larger absolute difference indicated a larger model-implied change associated with perturbing that node’s threshold under the specified simulation condition. These differences represent outputs from the fitted Ising model and should not be interpreted as changes observed after a clinical intervention.14

Because the Ising model and NIRA required dichotomized variables, a GGM was estimated as a sensitivity analysis using the original non-dichotomized scores.34 The GGM included 25 variables in the same node order as the Ising network: the eight original CIOS item scores (IO1-IO8; each scored 1–4), the continuous asthenopia total score (AS; sum of the 11 asthenopia items, range 0–44), the seven original GAD-7 item scores (GAD1-GAD7; each scored 0–3), and the nine original PHQ-9 item scores (PHQ1-PHQ9; each scored 0–3). Thus, the AS node was represented by a binary asthenopia classification in the Ising model but by the continuous asthenopia total score in the GGM. No individual asthenopia items were entered separately into either network (Supplementary Table S1). Standardized node centrality indices, including strength, closeness, and betweenness, were calculated from the GGM and expressed as z scores.

A Mantel test using Pearson correlation and 9999 permutations was performed to assess the correspondence between the absolute edge-weight matrices of the Ising and GGM networks. Node labels and ordering were matched across the two matrices. However, because the AS node was operationalized differently in the two models, the Mantel correlation was interpreted as an overall measure of correspondence between the edge-weight patterns rather than as evidence that the two networks were based on identically represented variables.

Results

Demographic and cancer characteristics

A total of 566 patients were included in the analysis (Table 1). Most participants (54.6%) were aged 46–60 years. Regarding religion, 530 patients (93.6%) reported no religious affiliation. Concerning digital health information-seeking behavior, 240 participants (42.4%) spent more than 2 hours per session. Occupation distribution showed that 168 (29.7%) were freelancers and 130 (23.0%) were farmers. Additionally, 230 participants (40.6%) resided in rural areas. Most respondents (88.2%) were married with children. Educationally, 188 participants (33.2%) had a junior college degree. For monthly income, 288 (50.9%) earned between 3000 and 8000 CNY. Disease staging indicated 237 patients (41.9%) had TNM stage II. The disease duration was 6–12 months in 234 patients (41.3%). Combination therapy was the most common treatment (34.5%). Estimated Ising model network

An Ising network was constructed using 25 observed binary nodes representing four symptom domains: IO, asthenopia, anxiety, and depression. These nodes included eight CIOS items, one AS, seven GAD-7 items, and nine PHQ-9 items (Fig. 1). The AS node represented the dichotomized presence of asthenopia based on the total asthenopia score rather than an individual asthenopia item. For clarity, only edges with absolute weights greater than 0.1 are shown in the presented network. The results indicated that the network was densely interconnected, with variables frequently co-occurring. Particularly strong connections were observed between depressive and anxiety symptoms. Relatively strong conditional associations were observed between IO4 (Perceived impossibility) and PHQ2 (Sad mood), between asthenopia and PHQ3 (Sleep problems), and between IO1 (Decision overload) and GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry). Simulation of alleviating and aggravating interventions

Simulated node-specific threshold perturbations produced different estimated changes in the total network sum score (Fig. 2). Under the alleviating perturbation condition, the largest estimated decreases were observed following perturbations of PHQ2 (Sad mood), IO4 (Perceived impossibility), and GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry). Among these nodes, perturbing PHQ2 (Sad mood) produced the largest estimated decrease in the network sum score, lowering it from an unperturbed baseline of 12.71 to 10.85 points. Under the aggravating perturbation condition, perturbing GAD4 (Trouble relaxing) produced the largest estimated increase in the network sum score, raising it from an unperturbed baseline of 12.74 to 14.63 points. Network stability analysis

Case-dropping bootstrap analysis was conducted to evaluate the stability of the estimated Ising network. The CS coefficient for edge weights was 0.749, and the CS coefficient for node strength was 0.594, both exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50, indicating acceptable stability of the estimated network structure and node strength centrality (Supplementary Fig. S1A–B). Nonparametric bootstrap analysis was further performed to assess the accuracy of edge-weight estimates. The bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals for edge weights are shown in Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3. In addition, bootstrap difference tests were conducted to compare node strength centrality across nodes, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. As a sensitivity analysis, a GGM was estimated using 25 non-dichotomized variables: eight CIOS item scores, the continuous asthenopia total score, seven GAD-7 item scores, and nine PHQ-9 item scores. The resulting GGM network and standardized centrality indices are presented in Fig. 3, Fig. 4, respectively. The Mantel test showed a strong overall correlation between the absolute edge-weight patterns of the Ising and GGM networks (Mantel r = 0.8385, P < 0.001). However, the AS node was represented as a binary asthenopia classification in the Ising model and as a continuous total score in the GGM. Discussion

This study applied network analysis and computer-simulated interventions to examine complex interrelationships among IO, asthenopia, and anxiety-depressive symptoms in patients with lung cancer. The Ising network showed strong internal connectivity and notable symptom co-occurrence, especially marked between depressive and anxiety symptoms. PHQ-9 item 2 (PHQ2; sad mood) produced the largest estimated decrease in the network sum score under the simulated alleviating perturbation. Notably, IO7 (Information skepticism) and PHQ3 (Sleep problems) appeared as the most central symptoms, results diverging from targets identified through simulated interventions.

The Ising model revealed a positive conditional association between IO4 (Perceived impossibility) and PHQ2 (Sad mood). This association directly reflects how cognitive appraisals shape core emotional experiences in patients with lung cancer. “Perceived impossibility” represents more than dissatisfaction with information quantity; it denotes a negative appraisal of one’s self-efficacy.35 Research suggests these appraisals often lead to persistent feelings of guilt, helplessness, and failure.36 Asthenopia was also conditionally associated with PHQ3 (sleep problems), consistent with previous studies.37 Prolonged digital-screen use has been associated with dry-eye symptoms and ocular-surface abnormalities, potentially through reduced blink frequency, incomplete blinking, tear-film instability, and ocular inflammation.38,39 Conversely, insufficient or disturbed sleep may exacerbate ocular-surface instability and dry-eye symptoms, potentially reinforcing asthenopia.40 Because the present study was cross-sectional, the direction of this association cannot be determined. Additionally, a positive conditional association was observed between IO1 (Decision overload) and GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry). Patients with lung cancer frequently face rapid processing of multi-path treatment information, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and palliative care.41 This situation requires substantial cognitive resources for comparison, analysis, and decision-making. Within the LC4MP framework,42 such complex and uncertain informational environments may approach or exceed an individual’s cognitive capacity, triggering anxiety.

The simulated threshold-perturbation analysis indicated that PHQ2 (Sad mood) produced the largest model-estimated decrease in the network sum score among the examined nodes, a ranking broadly consistent with findings from previous network studies of depression and anxiety in adolescents and older adults.43,44 This result highlights the transdiagnostic and cross-demographic relevance of sad mood as an affective symptom. According to network theory, psychopathological states emerge and persist through dynamic symptom interactions. In the simulated alleviating condition, perturbing the threshold of “Sad mood” produced the largest estimated decrease in the network sum score among the examined nodes. As a high-arousal negative affective state, sad mood strongly predicts both depression and anxiety.45 Experimental evidence suggests that sadness, particularly when accompanied by maladaptive affect-regulation responses, may interfere with executive functioning and information processing.46 In addition, experimental evidence indicates that sadness intensity can modulate the scope of visual attention, although the direction and magnitude of this effect may depend on emotional intensity and task context.47 These observations provide a theoretical context for future investigation but do not explain the perturbation ranking observed in the present study, because cognitive-resource and physiological mechanisms were not directly assessed. Accordingly, sad mood should be regarded only as a candidate symptom for prospective evaluation. The present cross-sectional model does not demonstrate that reducing sad mood would release cognitive resources or cause changes in other symptoms.

Regarding model interpretation, perturbation of PHQ2 (Sad mood) was associated with an estimated 1.86-point decrease in the network sum score within the fitted Ising model. This value is a model-derived quantity and should not be interpreted as an observed reduction in clinical symptom burden or compared directly with the Minimal Clinically Important Difference for the PHQ-9 or GAD-7. It does not demonstrate cascading symptom deactivation, therapeutic benefit, clinical recovery, or causal change. Rather, the result provides a quantitative basis for nominating sad mood as a candidate symptom for future prospective testing. Longitudinal and interventional studies are required to determine whether modifying this symptom produces changes elsewhere in the symptom network. Notably, this study underscores a fundamental difference between GGM and NIRA in identifying intervention targets. In GGM, node centrality reflects the sum of conditional associations with other nodes within the symptom network.48 Thus, symptoms like IO7 (Information Skepticism) and PHQ3 (Sleep Problems) closely co-vary with numerous other symptoms, maintaining network connectivity and stability. In contrast, NIRA estimates model-implied changes in network sum scores under hypothetical node-threshold perturbations. These estimates provide a different model-based node ranking that may support hypothesis generation but do not represent observed cascades of symptom change or establish clinical intervention priorities. Therefore, compared with traditional GGM-based centrality metrics, NIRA’s clinical value may be considered for further testing in prospective intervention studies.49 Specifically, the simulation suggested sad mood as a possible primary point for future testing.

Implications for research and practice

Methodological implications

The divergence between the GGM centrality rankings and the NIRA perturbation rankings indicates that the two approaches summarize different properties of the fitted network. GGM centrality describes patterns of conditional connectivity, whereas NIRA quantifies model-estimated changes following hypothetical node-specific threshold perturbations. Neither approach, when applied to cross-sectional data, identifies an effective clinical intervention target. Their complementary value lies in generating hypotheses and candidate symptoms for subsequent prospective evaluation.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as direct practice recommendations. Sad mood, IO, and asthenopia may warrant further attention in future nursing assessment and intervention research for patients with lung cancer. However, the present cross-sectional network analysis did not evaluate any screening pathway, nursing intervention, psychosocial treatment, eHealth intervention, decision-support tool, or follow-up model. Therefore, the clinical utility of the identified nodes remains unconfirmed.

Prospective studies should evaluate the psychometric properties and clinical utility of candidate measures, including the PHQ-2,50 GAD-2,51 eHealth literacy assessment,52 and visual-fatigue assessment, in patients with lung cancer before using them to identify clinically meaningful symptom risk profiles. Similarly, supportive-care components such as psychosocial support, eHealth-based support, decision-support tools, or structured follow-up should be considered only as potential intervention components requiring formal evaluation.53,54 Before any specific clinical recommendations can be made, longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to determine whether modifying sad mood, IO, or asthenopia produces broader changes across the symptom network.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the integration of network estimation with computer-based node perturbation analysis, which enables comparison of model-implied network changes and generation of testable hypotheses for future longitudinal and interventional studies. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, reliance on self-reported questionnaires introduces potential recall bias; future studies could mitigate this risk using advanced assessment tools or methods. Second, simulated intervention outcomes derived from cross-sectional data cannot represent true causal effects. Although NIRA simulations assume the estimated cross-sectional network approximates symptom-generating mechanisms, this approach does not capture actual within-person causal dynamics over time.55 Furthermore, the current NIRA algorithm only accommodates single-level data structures, limiting its application to certain clinical research contexts involving nested data.55 Therefore, NIRA results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Third, single-center recruitment limits the representativeness and generalizability of findings. Future research should adopt systematic sampling strategies and broaden recruitment sources to enhance external validity. Fourth, dichotomizing continuous and ordinal data to fit the Ising model represents a significant methodological limitation. This transformation, required by the NIRA algorithm optimized for binary Markov Random Fields, inevitably reduces statistical power and omits nuanced information about symptom severity.56 Future developments allowing dynamic interventions in continuous or mixed-data structures (e.g., Mixed Graphical Models) are needed to validate and refine these findings. Fifth, the network used a hybrid node structure: IO, anxiety, and depression were represented at the item level, whereas asthenopia was aggregated (binary in the Ising model and continuous in the GGM). This difference may affect edge estimates, centrality measures, NIRA rankings, and cross-model comparisons. Clinical characteristics collected in this study were used only to describe the sample and not included as network nodes or covariates. This decision aimed to maintain model simplicity and stability. However, advanced disease stages or aggressive treatments may be unmeasured confounders, simultaneously increasing IO and psychological distress. Future studies with larger sample sizes should employ moderated network models or include clinical factors as covariates to distinguish direct symptom interactions from the impacts of disease progression.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional network study characterized model-estimated associations among IO, asthenopia, and emotional symptoms in patients with lung cancer. Within the fitted Ising model, perturbation of sad mood produced the largest estimated decrease in the network sum score, whereas perturbation of trouble relaxing produced the largest estimated increase. These perturbation-based rankings differed from the GGM centrality rankings, in which information skepticism and sleep problems were the most central nodes. The findings are hypothesis-generating and identify candidate symptoms for future prospective testing; they do not establish that modifying these symptoms would reduce clinical symptom burden, exacerbate clinical risk, or produce causal or therapeutic effects. Because the study used cross-sectional, single-center data and primarily included patients with early-to-middle-stage lung cancer, the findings require validation in longitudinal, experimental, and more clinically diverse samples.
