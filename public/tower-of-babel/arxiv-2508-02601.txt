StructSynth: Leveraging LLMs for Structure-Aware Tabular Data Synthesis in Low-Data Regimes
Siyi Liu, Yujia Zheng, Yongqi Zhang

Abstract
The application of machine learning on tabular data in specialized domains is severely limited by data scarcity. While generative models offer a solution, traditional methods falter in low-data regimes, and recent Large Language Models (LLMs) often ignore the explicit dependency structure of tabular data, leading to low-fidelity synthetics. To address these limitations, we introduce StructSynth , a novel framework that integrates the generative power of LLMs with robust structural control. StructSynth employs a two-stage architecture. First, it performs explicit structure discovery to learn a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) from the available data. Second, this learned structure serves as a high-fidelity blueprint to steer the LLM’s generation process, forcing it to adhere to the learned feature dependencies and thereby ensuring the generated data respects the underlying structure by design. Our extensive experiments demonstrate that StructSynth produces synthetic data with significantly higher structural integrity and downstream utility than state-of-the-art methods. It proves especially effective in challenging low-data scenarios, successfully navigating the trade-off between privacy preservation and statistical fidelity.

Introduction

Tabular data is a cornerstone format in fields like healthcare
(
Choi et al. 2017
)
, finance
(
Rundo et al. 2019
)
, and education
(
Luan and Tsai 2021
)
, valued for its broad coverage and ease of use.
However, the application of Machine Learning (ML) to tabular data is critically constrained by data scarcity,
in particular, in low-data applications such as
rare medical conditions, emerging financial sectors, or underrepresented populations
(
Fonseca and Bacao 2023
)
.

This paradox, the widespread availability of tabular formats and the critical lack of sufficient data in key areas, undermines the training, validation, and deployment of robust and equitable ML solutions
(
Chai et al. 2022
;
Borisov et al. 2022
)
.

To overcome data scarcity, a variety of generative models have been developed for data augmentation. Deep learning approaches, including Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
(
Patki, Wedge, and Veeramachaneni 2016
)
, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
, and Diffusion Models
(
Kotelnikov et al. 2023
)
, learn complex feature dependencies implicitly from the data distribution. However, the fidelity of this implicit learning process is severely compromised when training data is sparse.
Another line of research demonstrates that explicitly modeling the data’s underlying relational structure is crucial for robust generation. This principle is not only central to foundational methods like Bayesian Networks
(
Ankan and Panda 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2017
)
but has also been integrated as a strong inductive bias into advanced deep learning frameworks such as DECAF
(
Van Breugel et al. 2021
)
and GOGGLE
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
.
However, the relational structure hinges on
accurately learning the dependency graph from the data—an assumption that collapses when samples are scarce,
rendering their generation process highly error-prone.

Recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a powerful paradigm for few-shot data generation, leveraging vast prior knowledge to seemingly bypass data scarcity
(
Seedat et al. 2024
)
. However, this approach has a critical flaw: LLMs infer dependencies solely from a linearized textual representation of the data, neglecting explicit relational information. This reliance on sequential input fundamentally limits their ability to capture complex graphical relationships
(
Liu, Wang, and Chen 2024
)
. Consequently, the generation process often produces synthetic data that fails to respect the true underlying structure.

These motivate two fundamental research questions regarding the generation of high-fidelity synthetic tabular data in low-data regimes: (1)
How to reliably discover the underlying dependency structure when data is limited?
and (2)
How to leverage the generative capabilities of LLMs to produce synthetic data that respects this discovered structure?

To address these challenges, we propose
StructSynth
, a decoupled, two-stage framework for
Struct
ure-aware tabular data
Synth
esis.
Specifically, we first introduce a
Dependency Structure Discovery
stage, which infers feature-level relationships from limited training samples and encodes them as a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)

Subsequently, we develop a
Structure-Guided Synthesis
stage that leverages this DAG as a structural blueprint, guiding the LLM’s generative process.
Data synthesis proceeds autoregressively according to the DAG’s topological ordering, ensuring that each generated feature is conditioned explicitly on its parent nodes. This decoupled design guarantees, by construction, that the synthetic data strictly adheres to the identified dependency structure, thus preserving structural integrity throughout data generation.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We introduce
StructSynth
, a novel two-stage framework that resolves the conflict between the generative power of LLMs and the need for explicit structural control, enabling high-fidelity tabular data synthesis from scarce samples.

•

Our method synergizes two powerful components: an LLM-based structure learning phase from scarce data, and a subsequent structure-aware generation stage that leverages the discovered graph to ensure high-fidelity output.

•

Extensive experimental validation demonstrates that
StructSynth
produces synthetic data with superior structural integrity and downstream utility, proving its effectiveness in challenging low-data regimes.

2
Related Works

2.1
Conventional Tabular Data Synthesis

Tabular data synthesis addresses data scarcity and privacy concerns by learning generative models to sample realistic data. Early approaches, such as the SMOTE
(
Chawla et al. 2002
)
and methods based on Copulas
(
Xue-Kun Song 2000
)
, were foundational but often struggled to capture complex, non-linear relationships. This prompted a shift towards more expressive deep generative models (DGMs) that learn the data distribution implicitly. Advances in deep generative modeling, including Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) such as TVAE
(
Patki, Wedge, and Veeramachaneni 2016
)
, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) exemplified by CTGAN
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
, and Normalizing Flow-based methods
(
Papamakarios et al. 2021
)
, have significantly improved fidelity through implicit distribution learning. More recently, diffusion-based methods like TabDDPM
(
Kotelnikov et al. 2023
)
and TabSyn
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
achieved state-of-the-art performance. However, since DGMs implicitly learn complex feature dependencies, they offer limited fidelity and control over the generation process.

2.2
Structure-Aware Tabular Data Synthesis

To enhance generative fidelity and controllability, structure-aware methods explicitly incorporate feature dependency structures into data synthesis. Early methods based on Bayesian Networks
(
Ankan and Panda 2015
)
and PrivBayes
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
generate samples guided by learned graphical structures, with PrivBayes introducing privacy-preserving mechanisms on top of the Bayesian framework. Recent deep-learning methods integrate structured inductive biases more effectively; DECAF
(
Van Breugel et al. 2021
)
leverages causal reasoning for fairness, while GOGGLE
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
employs a graph-based VAE. However, their effectiveness heavily depends on accurately learned graphs from data, posing challenges especially under limited data scenarios.

2.3
LLM-Based Tabular Data Synthesis

Large Language Models (LLMs), leveraging in-context learning and extensive prior knowledge, have recently advanced tabular data synthesis significantly. Fine-tuning approaches such as GReaT
(
Borisov et al. 2023
)
, TAPTAP
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, and AIGT
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
autoregressively generate data but incur high computational costs and deteriorate performance in low-data scenarios. Prompt-based methods, notably CuratedLLM (CLLM)
(
Seedat et al. 2024
)
, alleviate these drawbacks by using curated examples to guide data generation without updating model parameters. Nevertheless, their reliance on linearized textual inputs restricts their ability to explicitly represent and generate structurally coherent synthetic data
(
Liu, Wang, and Chen 2024
)
.

3.1
Problem Definition

Consider a small tabular training dataset, denoted as
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{train}}=(\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{train}},\mathbf{A})
.
\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{train}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
represents a set of
n
instances (or rows), where the number of available samples is severely limited (
n\leq 100
). The set
\mathbf{A}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}\}
defines the schema of the data through its
K
attributes (or columns). Our primary objective is to employ an LLM-based generation function,
f_{\mathtt{LLM}}
, to produce a synthetic dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{synth}}=(\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{synth}},\mathbf{A})
, that is both realistic and diverse. The ultimate goal is to demonstrate that a downstream model trained on an augmented dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{aug}}=(\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{train}}\cup\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{synth}},\mathbf{A})
, exhibits superior performance on a held-out test set,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{test}}
, when compared to a model trained exclusively on the original, smaller dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{train}}
.

3.2
Dependency Structure Discovery

The first step in
StructSynth
is to uncover the dependency structure present in the limited training data, represented as a DAG
G=(V,E)
. The node set
V\subseteq\mathbf{A}
consists of selected attributes from the dataset schema
\mathbf{A}
, while the edges
E
capture probabilistic dependencies among these attributes. The framework employs an iterative, breadth-first search (BFS) guided by an LLM to progressively construct the dependency graph. The core steps are described below.

Graph Initialization

We initiate the graph construction by identifying source nodes attributes that are not influenced by any others in the dataset. A small subset of the training data,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{few\_shot}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{train}}
, serves as in-context examples for the LLM, which is guided by a specifically designed prompt,
\pi_{\mathtt{source}}
. Formally, the initial set of source nodes,
V_{\mathtt{source}}
, is determined by the following operation:

V_{\mathtt{source}}=f_{\mathtt{LLM}}(\pi_{\mathtt{source}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{few\_shot}})).

(1)

Consequently, the initial graph is set to
G_{0}=(V_{\mathtt{source}},\emptyset)
. To manage the discovery process, a queue
Q
for the Breadth-First Search (BFS) is initialized with these source nodes, i.e.,
Q\leftarrow V_{\mathtt{source}}
, and a set for tracking explored attributes,
V_{\mathtt{visited}}
, is initialized as empty.

Expansion and Reasoned Link Generation

The framework expands the graph
G
through an iterative, breadth-first process. In each iteration
t
, a node
A_{i}
is dequeued from the processing queue
Q
and added to the set of visited nodes,
V_{\mathtt{visited}}
. To inform the model’s link generation with empirical data, we compute a vector of association scores,
\mathcal{S}(A_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
, between
A_{i}
and all other attributes in
\mathbf{A}
. These scores are calculated using dependency measures specific to the data types of each attribute pair:
Pearson’s R
for continuous-continuous pairs,
Correlation Ratio
for categorical-continuous pairs, and
Cramér’s V
for categorical-categorical pairs
(
Agresti and Kateri 2011
)
.

Subsequently, the LLM is queried with a comprehensive prompt,
\pi_{\mathtt{generate}}
, which integrates the current graph state
G_{t}
, the node
A_{i}
, and the statistical scores
\mathcal{S}(A_{i})
. The model’s task is to propose a set of direct successors for
A_{i}
and provide a textual rationale for each proposed relationship. The structured output of this query is a set of successor-rationale pairs, denoted as
P_{i}
:

P_{i}=f_{\mathtt{LLM}}(\pi_{\mathtt{generate}}(A_{i},G_{t},\mathcal{S}(A_{i}),\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{few\_shot}})).

(2)

From this set, we define the proposed new edges,
E_{\mathtt{prop},t}
, and the set of newly discovered nodes,
V_{\mathtt{new},t}
, which are not already in the graph’s vertex set
V_{t}
:

\displaystyle E_{\mathtt{prop},t}

\displaystyle=\{(A_{i}\to A_{j},r_{ij})\mid(A_{j},r_{ij})\in P_{i}\},

(3)

\displaystyle V_{\mathtt{new},t}

\displaystyle=\{A_{j}\mid(A_{j},\_)\in P_{i}\land A_{j}\notin V_{t}\}.

These new nodes are then added to the queue
Q
for subsequent exploration, ensuring the continued expansion of the graph boundary.

LLM-based Cycle Resolution

To ensure that the graph remains a DAG throughout, the framework resolves any cycles that may result from integrating proposed edges
E_{\mathtt{prop},t}
into the current graph
G_{t}=(V_{t},E_{t})
. Specifically, we construct the candidate edge set
E_{\mathtt{candidate}}=E_{t}\cup E_{\mathtt{prop},t}
and apply a cycle detection algorithm to identify all elementary cycles,
\Psi
.

If cycles are detected (
\Psi\neq\emptyset
), the framework initiates a reasoned resolution process. For each cycle
\psi\in\Psi
, where
\psi
represents the specific subset of edges from
E_{\mathtt{candidate}}
that forms the circular path (e.g.,
\{(A\to B,r_{ab}),(B\to C,r_{bc}),(C\to A,r_{ca})\})
, the LLM is queried with the prompt
\pi_{\mathtt{resolve}}
. The prompt’s input,
\psi
, provides the model with the complete logical contradiction, including the rationales for both newly proposed and previously established edges. The model’s task is to analyze this conflict and identify the single edge whose removal is most justified. This resolution logic is applied to every detected cycle. The complete set of pruned edges,
E_{\mathtt{pruned}}
, is thus defined by collecting the output for each cycle in
\Psi
. The state for the next iteration,
G_{t+1}
, is then formally defined by removing these pruned edges from the candidate set:

\displaystyle E_{\mathtt{pruned}}

\displaystyle=\{f_{\mathtt{LLM}}(\pi_{\mathtt{resolve}}(\psi))\mid\psi\in\Psi\}

(4)

\displaystyle G_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(V_{t}\cup V_{\mathtt{new},t},(E_{t}\cup E_{\mathtt{prop},t})\setminus E_{\mathtt{pruned}}).

This general mechanism ensures the graph remains acyclic and empowers the model to revise its structure based on new, more confident evidence.

Continuation of BFS

The process continues iteratively. Following the cycle resolution step, the newly discovered nodes,
V_{\mathtt{new},t}
, which are now part of the updated graph
G_{t+1}
, are added to the processing queue
Q
. The framework then returns to the
Expansion and Reasoned Link Generation
step to process the next node from the queue. This iterative expansion-resolution cycle continues until
Q
is empty, at which point the complete, acyclic dependency graph
G
is returned as the final output.

3.3
Structure-Guided Synthesis

Upon learning the dependency structure
G=(V,E)
, we utilize it to guide synthetic tabular data generation. Constructing each synthetic data point
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
is performed in two sequential phases aligned with the underlying data structure.

Generating Graph-Based Values

This phase generates values for attributes
V
defined by the learned dependency graph. Initially, the nodes
V
are partitioned into topological layers
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\dots,L_{m})
. For each layer
L_{i}
, we extract the corresponding dependency subgraph
G_{i}=G[L_{i}\cup\Gamma^{-}(L_{i})]
, where
\Gamma^{-}(L_{i})
represents the parent nodes of attributes in
L_{i}
. Additionally, we select the relevant slice of the few-shot dataset as
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{few\_shot}}^{i}=\Pi_{L_{i}\cup\Gamma^{-}(L_{i})}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{few\_shot}})
, where
\Pi
denotes the projection operator onto specified columns.

Values for attributes in layer
L_{i}
, denoted as
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,L_{i}}
, are generated conditionally based on previously generated values for preceding layers
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,<i}
:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,L_{i}}=f_{\mathtt{LLM}}\left(\pi_{\mathtt{data\_gen}}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,<i},\ G_{i},\ L_{i},\ \mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{few\_shot}}^{i}\right)\right).

(5)

Iterating through all layers yields a partial synthetic data point
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,V}
comprising values for all graph-dependent attributes.

Generating Independent Values

Subsequently, values for isolated attributes
A_{\mathtt{iso}}=\mathbf{A}\setminus V
are generated collectively in a single step, conditioned upon the complete set of previously generated graph-based values
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,V}
:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,A_{\mathtt{iso}}}=f_{\mathtt{LLM}}\left(\pi_{\mathtt{data\_gen\_iso}}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,V},\ A_{\mathtt{iso}},\ \mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{few\_shot}}^{\mathtt{iso}}\right)\right),

(6)

where
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{few\_shot}}^{\mathtt{iso}}=\Pi_{A_{\mathtt{iso}}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{few\_shot}}).

Final Assembly

A complete synthetic data point
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
is formed by concatenating the graph-dependent and independent attribute values as
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=\mathtt{concat}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,V},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,A_{\mathtt{iso}}})
. Repeating this procedure
s
times yields the synthetic dataset
\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{synth}}=\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{s}\}
, resulting in the final synthetic dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{synth}}=(\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{synth}},\mathbf{A})
.

Experimental Setups

Datasets

We conduct experiments on six real-world tabular datasets: Adult

Becker and Kohavi 1996
)

(

Notably, Anxiety and Salary were released after the knowledge cutoff of modern LLMs, allowing us to assess performance in the absence of prior exposure. Full dataset details are provided in the Appendix section
B.1
.

Baselines

We compare our method against nine baselines from three categories (implementation details in Appendix section A.2.2): 1)
Deep Generative Models (DGMs)
, including TVAE
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
, CTGAN
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
, TabDDPM
(
Kotelnikov et al. 2023
)
, and NFlow
(
Papamakarios et al. 2021
)
; 2)
Structure-Aware Methods
, including Bayesian Networks
(
Ankan and Panda 2015
)
, GOGGLE
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
, and DECAF
(
Van Breugel et al. 2021
)
; and 3)
LLM-based Methods
, including GReaT
(
Borisov et al. 2023
)
and CLLM
(
Seedat et al. 2024

Evaluation Metrics

Following previous methods
(
Borisov et al. 2023
;
Seedat et al. 2024
)
, we evaluate the quality of synthetic data using three different metrics, with detailed definitions provided in Appendix section
C
:

•

Downstream Model Performance:
This metric evaluates the data’s utility by training a downstream model on the augmented dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{aug}}=(\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{train}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{synth}})
and conducting an evaluation on the real test set
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{test}}
. We report Area Under the Curve (AUC) for classification and R-squared (
R^{2}
) for regression.

•

Statistical Fidelity:
This metric evaluates how well the statistical properties of the synthetic dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{synth}}
, replicate those of the original dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{train}}
. We calculate it as the mean absolute difference between the pairwise correlation matrices of
\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{synth}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{train}}
, where a
lower score indicates higher fidelity
.

•

Privacy Risk Assessment:
This metric assesses privacy risk by measuring if records in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{synth}}
are overly similar to the
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{train}}
. For each instance in
\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{synth}}
, we find its nearest neighbor in the set of all real records (
\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{train}}\cup\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{test}}
). The score is the fraction of synthetic records whose nearest neighbor is from
\mathbf{X}_{\mathtt{train}}
, where a
value approaching 0.5 indicates better privacy preservation
.

Implementation Details

We use
gpt-4o-mini
with a temperature of 0.9 for both the CLLM and
StructSynth
models. Our experiments evaluate the low-data regime, generating 1000 synthetic samples (
s=1000
) from 100 original samples (
n=100
). Downstream performance is assessed using an XGBoost model. All experiments are repeated 10 times, and we report results as mean
\pm
standard deviation to ensure robustness. Full hyperparameter configurations are provided in Appendix section
B.3
.

Privacy Preservation and Statistical Fidelity

We evaluate each generative model on the competing objectives of statistical fidelity and privacy preservation to analyze this critical trade-off. Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
StructSynth
, a novel two-stage framework that addresses the challenge of high-fidelity tabular data synthesis in low-data regimes by synergizing the generative power of Large Language Models (LLMs) with explicit structural control. Our method first discovers an underlying dependency graph from scarce data and then leverages this graph as a high-fidelity blueprint to steer the LLM’s generative process, guaranteeing adherence to feature dependencies by design. Extensive experiments demonstrate that StructSynth consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods in downstream utility while successfully navigating the critical fidelity-privacy trade-off.

Appendix A
Extended Related Works

A.1
Conventional Structure Learning

Conventional approaches to dependency structure discovery primarily fall into two categories: constraint-based and score-based methods. Constraint-based methods, such as the Peter–Clark (PC) algorithm
(
Spirtes et al. 2000
)
and the Fast Causal Inference (FCI) algorithm
(
Spirtes, Meek, and Richardson 1995
)
, utilize conditional independence tests to iteratively prune edges from an initially fully connected graph. While PC assumes causal sufficiency (no hidden confounders), FCI is specifically designed to handle latent variables. In contrast, score-based methods optimize a global scoring criterion to identify the most suitable graph structure. A representative example is the Greedy Equivalence Search (GES)
(
Chickering 2002
)
, which searches through equivalence classes to maximize a scoring function. However, both constraint-based and score-based approaches typically yield only Markov equivalence classes, thereby failing to distinguish between structurally distinct graphs that encode identical conditional independencies. To address this limitation, Functional Causal Models (FCMs) impose stronger assumptions regarding the data generation process. FCMs model effects explicitly as functions of direct causes combined with independent noise terms. These assumptions enable the identification of causal directions, since independence between cause and noise typically breaks down when reversed
(
Shimizu et al. 2006
;
Hoyer et al. 2008
;
Zhang and Hyvärinen 2009
)
. Recent advancements have generalized this approach, introducing conditions such as Generalized Independent Noise (GIN) to manage latent causal variables in linear, non-Gaussian settings
(
Xie et al. 2020
)
.

However, the statistical power of these traditional algorithms heavily relies on a large number of samples, making them unreliable for structure discovery in the low-data regimes addressed by our work.

A.2
LLM-based Structure Learning

Leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) for structure learning has recently emerged as a promising direction due to their inherent reasoning capabilities. Several paradigms have been proposed for integrating LLMs into structure discovery. Initially, methods focused on querying LLMs about pairwise causal relationships, showing strong accuracy in determining causal directions between variable pairs
(
Kiciman et al. 2023
)
. To overcome the quadratic complexity inherent to pairwise approaches, more scalable methods employing breadth-first search (BFS) strategies have been introduced, reframing structure learning as a graph traversal task and significantly reducing the required number of queries to the LLM
(
Jiralerspong et al. 2024
;
Zanna and Sano 2025
)
. A third paradigm enhances reliability by integrating an iterative supervision loop, in which traditional structure-learning algorithms propose initial graphs that are subsequently validated or corrected by LLMs. This method grounds LLM-generated structures in empirical evidence, thereby reducing hallucination risks
(
Ban et al. 2023
)
. LLM-based structure discovery methods have demonstrated notable effectiveness across diverse domains, including medicine
(
Naik et al. 2024
;
Antonucci, Piqué, and Zaffalon 2023
;
Arsenyan et al. 2024
)
, economics
(
Sokolov et al. 2024
)
, and social sciences
(
Manning, Zhu, and Horton 2024
)
.

Inspired by the success of LLMs in leveraging prior knowledge for domain-specific reasoning, our work integrates a scalable, LLM-driven paradigm for robust structure discovery.

Appendix C
Evaluation Metrics

C.1
Downstream Model Performance

The Downstream Model Performance metric serves as the primary measure of the synthetic data’s practical utility. It evaluates whether the synthetic data, when used to augment the original training set, can improve the performance of a machine learning model on a real, unseen test set. A high score indicates that the generated data not only captures the underlying patterns of the original data but also enhances the model’s ability to generalize.

Evaluation Procedure

For each generative method, we create an augmented training set,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{aug}}
, by combining the original limited training data,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
, with the generated synthetic data,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{synth}}
:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{aug}}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\text{synth}}

(7)

A downstream machine learning model (in our experiments, an XGBoost model) is then trained exclusively on this augmented dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{aug}}
. The performance of this trained model is subsequently evaluated on the held-out real test set,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
, which contains data that the model has not previously encountered during training.

Performance Metrics

The specific metric used for evaluation depends on the nature of the downstream task associated with each dataset:

For binary and multi-class classification tasks, we report the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). The AUC score represents the model’s ability to distinguish between different classes, with a value of 1.0 indicating a perfect classifier and 0.5 indicating performance equivalent to random chance. A higher AUC score is better.

For regression tasks, we report the R-squared (
R^{2}
) score, also known as the coefficient of determination. The
R^{2}
score measures the proportion of the variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variables. An
R^{2}
score of 1.0 indicates that the model perfectly predicts the target values, while a score of 0 indicates that the model performs no better than a simple mean-based prediction. A higher
R^{2}
score is better.

Interpretation

Higher scores in either AUC or
R^{2}
signify greater downstream utility. This indicates that the synthetic data has provided valuable and non-redundant information to the training process, enabling the machine learning model to learn a more robust and generalizable decision boundary or regression function.

C.2
Privacy Risk Assessment Metric

The Privacy Risk Assessment metric evaluates the extent to which a generative model overfits or memorizes the training data,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
. A high degree of similarity between the synthetic data,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{synth}}
, and the training data suggests a potential privacy risk. The metric quantifies this risk by calculating the probability that the nearest neighbor of a synthetic data point, within the pool of all real data, originates from the training set. A value approaching 0.5 indicates robust privacy preservation, implying that the synthetic data is as close to the unseen test data as it is to the training data.

Formal Definition

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
be the training set and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
be the holdout test set. The complete set of real data instances is defined as
\mathcal{D}_{\text{real}}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
. To ensure an unbiased comparison where the ideal probability is exactly 0.5, we first downsample the larger of the two sets (
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
or
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
) to match the size of the smaller one, ensuring that
|\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}|=|\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}|
for the purpose of this evaluation.

For any two data points,
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, from the mixed-type dataset, we define a distance function
d(x_{i},x_{j})
as the sum of the L1 distance for numerical features and the Hamming distance for categorical features:

d(x_{i},x_{j})=\sum_{k\in\text{numerical}}|x_{i,k}-x_{j,k}|+\sum_{l\in\text{categorical}}\mathbb{I}(x_{i,l}\neq x_{j,l})

(8)

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function, which is 1 if the condition is true and 0 otherwise.

For each synthetic sample
\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{synth}}
, we identify its nearest neighbor (
NN
) from the entire set of real data instances,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{real}}
:

NN(\tilde{x})=\underset{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{real}}}{\arg\min}\,d(\tilde{x},x)

(9)

The Privacy Risk score is then calculated as the fraction of synthetic samples whose nearest neighbor is an instance from the training set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
:

\text{PrivacyRisk}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{synth}}|}\sum_{\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{synth}}}\mathbb{I}(NN(\tilde{x})\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}})

(10)

Interpretation

A score approaching 0.5 suggests that the generative model has not memorized the training data, as the synthetic samples are statistically equidistant to both the training and test sets. Conversely, a score approaching 1.0 indicates a significant privacy risk, as it implies that the synthetic data points are consistently closer to the training samples they were generated from than to unseen real data.

C.3
Statistical Fidelity Metric

The Statistical Fidelity metric quantifies how well the synthetic data,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{synth}}
, preserves the pairwise statistical relationships present in the real data,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{real}}
. The metric is computed as the mean absolute difference across all pairs of columns, where the specific correlation measure depends on the data types of the columns involved. A lower score indicates higher fidelity, signifying that the statistical dependencies between features in the synthetic data closely mirror those in the real data.

Formal Definition

For each unique pair of columns
(C_{i},C_{j})
in the dataset, a difference score,
\Delta(C_{i},C_{j})
, is calculated. The calculation method varies for the three possible data type combinations:

•

Numerical-Numerical Pairs:
For two numerical columns, the difference is the absolute difference between their Pearson correlation coefficients (
\rho
) in the real and synthetic datasets:

\Delta(C_{i},C_{j})=|\rho(C_{i,\text{real}},C_{j,\text{real}})-\rho(C_{i,\text{synth}},C_{j,\text{synth}})|

(11)

•

Categorical-Categorical Pairs:
For two categorical columns, the difference is measured as the Total Variation Distance (TVD) of their joint probability distributions. This is calculated as half the sum of the absolute differences between the normalized contingency tables of the real and synthetic data:

\displaystyle\Delta(C_{i},C_{j})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v}\big|

\displaystyle P_{\text{real}}(C_{i}=u,C_{j}=v)

\displaystyle-\

\displaystyle P_{\text{synth}}(C_{i}=u,C_{j}=v)\big|

(12)

where
u
and
v
iterate over all possible categories for columns
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
, respectively.

•

Numerical-Categorical Pairs:
For a mixed pair of a numerical column
C_{i}
and a categorical column
C_{j}
, the numerical column is first discretized into a categorical representation,
C^{\prime}_{i}
, by binning its values into
q=10
quantiles. The difference is then calculated using the same TVD method as for categorical-categorical pairs:

\Delta(C_{i},C_{j})=\Delta(C^{\prime}_{i},C_{j})

(13)

Final Score

The overall Statistical Fidelity score is the mean of these difference scores computed over all unique pairs of columns in the dataset:

\text{StatisticalFidelity}=\frac{1}{\binom{K}{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\Delta(C_{i},C_{j})

(14)

where
K
is the total number of columns.

Interpretation

A score approaching 0 indicates high statistical fidelity, as it implies that the pairwise relationships between columns in the synthetic data are nearly identical to those in the real data. Conversely, a higher score signifies a greater divergence in these statistical properties.
