An oncologic cardiotoxicity masquerading as ST-elevated myocardial infarction: a case report
Authors: Amarpal Karamjit Singh, Rifly Rafiudeen, Chiew Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFluoropyrimidines, such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), when used in combination with bevacizumab are associated with diverse cardiotoxic effects, including vasospasm, myocarditis, and myopericarditis. These toxicities may closely mimic acute coronary syndrome, creating diagnostic uncertainty and risk of inappropriate management. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is central in distinguishing inflammatory myocardial injury from ischaemic pathology.Case summaryA 71-year-old man undergoing long-term maintenance FOLFIRI–bevacizumab for metastatic colorectal cancer presented with acute chest pain and inferior ST-segment elevation 12 days after chemotherapy. Coronary angiography demonstrated non-obstructive coronary artery disease without a culprit lesion. Serial electrocardiographies showed dynamic evolution from ST elevation to widespread T-wave inversion, while peak high-sensitivity troponin reached 457 ng/L. Initial echocardiography revealed normal left ventricular ejection fraction. Cardiac magnetic resonance performed 1 week later demonstrated subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement and elevated regional T2 signal in the lateral segments, fulfilling the 2018 Lake Louise criteria for myopericarditis. Chemotherapy was withheld, and the patient was treated with colchicine, beta-blockade, and intermittent nitrates. Six-month follow-up imaging showed complete recovery of systolic function, permitting safe reintroduction of FOLFIRI–bevacizumab.DiscussionThis case highlights a diagnostically challenging presentation of fluoropyrimidine-associated myopericarditis masquerading as ST-elevated myocardial infarction (STEMI). Synergistic endothelial injury from 5-FU and bevacizumab may lower the threshold for cardiotoxicity even after multiple well-tolerated cycles. Multimodality imaging, particularly CMR, was essential in confirming myocardial inflammation, excluding ischaemia, and guiding treatment decisions. Increased awareness of chemotherapy-related STEMI mimics is critical to prevent unnecessary interventions and allow safe continuation of oncologic therapy following cardiac recovery.

Introduction

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) exerts its antineoplastic effect by inhibiting thymidylate synthase (TYMS), impairing DNA repair and promoting genomic instability.1 The degradation product α-fluoro-β-alanine is metabolized to fluoro-citrate, which disrupts the citric acid cycle, leading to citrate accumulation, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) depletion, and myocardial ischaemia. These effects can manifest as ischaemic electrocardiography (ECG) changes, including T-wave inversion and ST-segment abnormalities, alongside myocardial ATP depletion and citrate accumulation.2 Chronic low-dose 5-FU administration can cause distal coronary artery cell death, while high-dose therapy induces proximal coronary vasospasm.3 Bevacizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody generated via recombinant DNA technology, neutralizes vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and inhibits angiogenesis through blockade of endothelial receptors kinase insert domain-containing receptor (KDR) and fms-like tyrosine kinase (Flt-1).4 This mechanism suppresses neovascularization but is associated with reversible cardiomyopathy and left ventricular (LV) dysfunction in ∼2%–4% of patients, as well as venous/arterial thromboembolism, haemorrhage, and hypertension.4 This case illustrates a diagnostically challenging presentation of chemotherapy-related myopericarditis mimicking STEMI in a patient receiving 5-FU and bevacizumab for metastatic colorectal cancer.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old Caucasian man woke up with 2 h of sudden retrosternal chest pain at 6 a.m., radiating to the throat and arms, associated with diaphoresis 12 days after completing a cycle of FOLFIRI–bevacizumab for metastatic sigmoid cancer. He had been on maintenance FOLFIRI–bevacizumab for over 2 years, which was administered via a 46-h portable pump and represented the 44th treatment cycle without prior cardiotoxicity. His next cycle was scheduled in 2 days. The pre-hospital ECG performed by paramedics in response to his chest pain showed ST elevation consistent with inferior ST-elevated myocardial infarction (STEMI). He was haemodynamically stable and had an unremarkable cardiopulmonary exam.

Our patient has an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group score of 0 and a history of T4N1 sigmoid cancer (mismatch repair (MMR) rat sarcoma (RAS) wild type), which was first diagnosed in July 2016 (Figure 1). He underwent laparoscopic-assisted major liver resection for colorectal metastasis followed by high anterior resection, complicated by pancreatic fistula and pulmonary embolism, for which apixaban 5 mg BD was commenced. He completed 12 cycles of adjuvant FOLFOX (folinic acid 320 mg, 5-FU 640 mg bolus, oxaliplatin 160 mg) over 6 months, ceasing oxaliplatin after Cycle 9 due to peripheral neuropathy. He remained in remission until October 2021 when computed tomography (CT) surveillance revealed multiple peritoneal nodules. Positron emission tomography (PET) confirmed anastomotic recurrence and peritoneal metastases. Following multidisciplinary consensus, the treatment intent was palliative. He commenced FOLFIRI–bevacizumab (folinic acid 320 mg, 5-FU 640 mg, irinotecan 288 mg, bevacizumab 96 mg). Cytoreductive surgery was deemed unsuitable due to extensive disease. Staging CT in December 2023 showed stable peritoneal disease, and PET revealed no fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-avid recurrence. He continued maintenance FOLFIRI–bevacizumab from October 2021 to February 2024, receiving 5-FU 3840 mg infusions over 46 h per cycle (cumulative 5-FU dose of 230 g). He had no conventional cardiac risk factors, including lifelong non-smoker, normotensive, haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) 5.1%, LDL 2.9 mmol/L, and no family history of ischaemic heart disease. He was independent in daily activities and had not received cardioprotective medications during chemotherapy.

Medical history timeline and exposure to 5-flurouracil and bevacizumab from the time of diagnosis of sigmoid cancer in July 2016 to index admission in February 2024 with suspected ST-elevated myocardial infarction.

Pre-hospital ECG (Figure 2) showed sinus rhythm with marked ST elevation in II, III, aVF, and V3–V6, with reciprocal depression in aVL and aVR. He received aspirin 300 mg and ticagrelor 180 mg but no heparin (due to morning apixaban dose). Coronary angiography (see Supplementary material online, Videos S1–S2 and S1–S2) revealed a right-dominant system with smooth 50% right coronary artery (RCA) stenosis and mild–moderate proximal left anterior descending (LAD) disease with TIMI 3 flow in all vessels. No culprit lesion or vasospasm was seen. Left ventricular gram (see Supplementary material online, Video S3) demonstrated normal systolic function without regional wall motion abnormality. No diagnostic or intra-procedural complications were observed.

Pre-hospital electrocardiography showing ST elevation on inferior leads (II, III, aVF) and anterior leads (V3–V6) with reciprocal depression in aVL and aVR (25 mm/s, 10 mm/mV).

Next day, transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showed normal left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) without regional wall motion abnormalities and trivial pericardial effusion. Peak troponin reached 457 ng/L. Inflammatory markers were normal (C-reactive protein < 6 mg/L, no leukocytosis, antinuclear antibody (ANA) negative), reducing likelihood of autoimmune or infectious myocarditis. The patient denied fever, coryzal or gastrointestinal symptoms, recent viral illness, or sick contacts. No mucosal ulcers or rash were present. Viral serologies were not performed given the absence of a viral prodrome and normal inflammatory markers. The patient was also adherent to therapeutic apixaban for underlying pulmonary embolism. Electrocardiography evolved over 7 days, showing progressive ST depression of inferior leads (see Supplementary material online, S3) and T-wave inversion in anterior leads (see Supplementary material online, S4). Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) 1 week later (Figures 3–5) revealed regional T2 signal elevation, late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) of subepicardial lateral segments, and LVEF of 37%, fulfilling the 2018 Lake Louise criteria for myopericarditis5 and excluding other differentials, such as Takotsubo cardiomyopathy. T2 signals seen in the subepicardium of the basal lateral segment were based on T2-weighted short tau inversion recovery (T2w-STIR) imaging with a T2 signal intensity more than 2 SD greater than the mean of the normal tissue (1460 vs. 584 signal intensity). T2 mapping was not performed. Normal T1 mapping excluded fibrosis and infiltrative disease. Chemotherapy was withheld to allow myocardial recovery.

He received colchicine 500 mg BD for 3 months and metoprolol 25 mg BD for treatment of myopericarditis. Sublingual glyceryl trinitrate as needed approach was prescribed over regular nitrates or calcium channel blockers to treat potential coronary vasospasm as it alleviated some chest pain symptoms while avoiding significant hypotension given patient’s baseline low blood pressure. Additionally, the patient’s low baseline blood pressure precluded him from other heart failure with reduced ejection fraction therapy. Twelve months of antiplatelet therapy with clopidogrel 75 mg daily alongside apixaban 5 mg BD was prescribed to treat pre-existing pulmonary embolism and current Type 2 myocardial infarction. Six-month follow-up TTE demonstrated recovered LVEF to 59%, permitting resumption of FOLFIRI–bevacizumab at similar dose with continuous infusion completing over 48 h and without further 5-FU bolus. Repeat CMR was considered but deferred given complete normalization of systolic function and absence of heart failure symptoms. The diagnostic yield of repeat CMR was expected to be low. By May 2025, CT pulmonary angiogram showed resolution of pulmonary embolism, and apixaban ceased. He remained on aspirin for primary prevention and isosorbide mononitrate 30 mg without further chest pain during subsequent chemotherapy. The patient continues to be functionally independent and symptom-free, consistent with recovered myocardial function and stable oncologic disease.

Discussion

A multimodality approach, including coronary angiography, serial ECGs, biomarkers, transthoracic echocardiography, and CMR, was essential in excluding obstructive coronary disease and confirming myocardial inflammation. Cardiac magnetic resonance findings of subepicardial LGE and elevated regional T2 signal fulfilled the 2018 Lake Louise criteria for myopericarditis.5 This report adds to the limited literature describing 5-FU–associated myopericarditis, with a possible contributory role of bevacizumab, presenting with dynamic ST-segment elevation but without irreversible ischaemic injury. Recovery of systolic function following cessation of therapy supports the reversible nature of fluoropyrimidine-induced myocardial dysfunction.6

This case has important limitations. Coronary vasospasm—an established mechanism of fluoropyrimidine cardiotoxicity—was not directly demonstrated, as provocative testing was not performed, and endomyocardial biopsy was not obtained. It remains quite possible for RCA vasospasm to cause the initial ECG changes in this case followed by myocarditis seen on CMR. Disentangling the individual cardiotoxic contributions of 5-FU and bevacizumab remains challenging. Although fluoropyrimidine toxicity typically manifests early, delayed presentations have been described, particularly with continuous infusion, high cumulative exposure, or endothelial injury. Proposed mechanisms include microvascular dysfunction and subclinical myocardial inflammation.7 Preventive strategies include utilizing the Heart Failure Association–International Cardio-Oncology Society-based risk stratification and periodic ECG, biomarker, and global longitudinal strain surveillance.8,9 Pharmacokinetic-guided 5-FU dosing may reduce toxicity and improve outcomes.10 In summary, this patient developed myopericarditis, most likely secondary to chronic 5-FU exposure, with a possible contributory effect from bevacizumab-induced endothelial dysfunction. The evolving ST-segment elevation with initially preserved systolic function and subsequent CMR-confirmed myocarditis illustrate the dynamic interplay between ischaemic and inflammatory mechanisms in fluoropyrimidine cardiotoxicity. In instances where the underlying cause of myocardial infarction remains indeterminate following coronary angiography, early non-invasive imaging techniques, such as CMR, can be instrumental in identifying the aetiology of myocardial injury and inform treatment strategies for patients with myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA).11,12 Follow-up CMR imaging within 6 months is recommended to assess myocardial recovery.12
