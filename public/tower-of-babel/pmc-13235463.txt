Knowledge among primary care physicians on urticaria treatment: Impact of educational interventions
Authors: Jorge Sánchez, Ana Caraballo, Jaime Ocampo, Elizabeth García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary care physicians (general practitioners, family physicians, emergency physicians, among others) are often the first point of medical contact for patients with urticaria. Appropriate initial management enables early disease control and reduces the overall burden on patients and health care systems.ObjectiveWe evaluated changes over time in primary care physicians’ (PCPs) knowledge regarding diagnosis and management of urticaria. Additionally, we explored the characterization of access barriers reported by urticaria specialists.MethodsWe used a multimodal approach to assess changes in knowledge across two periods (2022 and 2025). Three sources of information were analyzed: (1) a knowledge test using a standardized questionnaire, (2) review of medical records from 3 health care centers, and (3) analysis of the Colombian national medical prescription registration system, SISPRO. Diagnostic and treatment domains were evaluated across all information sources.ResultsWe observed that the level of knowledge of guidelines among PCPs has increased in the different clinical domains (diagnosis, therapy). However, more than 50% of PCPs still have gaps in their understanding of the correct management practices. Several barriers were identified among PCPs but also among specialists (allergists, dermatologists), generally related to the administrative process.ConclusionEducational initiatives appear to positively influence PCPs’ knowledge and management of urticaria; however, persistent gaps highlight the need for ongoing, targeted educational strategies and system-level improvements to reduce barriers in medical care.

Urticaria imposes a substantial burden on patients, their families, and health care systems. Acute urticaria (AU), defined as symptoms lasting fewer than 6 weeks, often produces significant anxiety and motivates many patients to seek emergency care, generating high direct and indirect costs. Although chronic urticaria (CU) is less frequent than AU (1% vs 20%), its impact is far greater, as symptoms can persist for years or even decades. CU is classified into spontaneous and inducible; both significantly impairing the quality of life of patients and their families; about 30% to 80% of patients report avoiding certain medications,1 food,2,3 or activity (eg, exercise).4

Different academic groups, such as the Urticaria Centers of Excellence (UCARE), have developed several guidelines and consensus documents aimed at helping clinicians and patients obtain better disease control.5, 6, 7 These recommendations are based on the best available evidence and consider aspects such as the efficacy and effectiveness of therapeutic strategies. When new therapies are close to be introduced in the market, pharmaceutical information also contributed to education programs around the diseases and the mechanisms of action of new interventions—for example, dupilumab and remibrutinib in urticaria. Globally, educational campaigns and digital support tools have also been implemented to facilitate guideline uptake and empower health care professionals in daily clinical practice, thus reducing the personal and social burden of the disease.8, 9, 10 However, few studies have examined whether such guidelines and educational interventions translate into meaningful improvements in clinical practice,11,12 and most existing evaluations have focused on specialists rather than primary care physicians (PCPs).

In Colombia, the EAACI/GA2LEN/EuroGuiDerm/APAAACI guideline7 has been widely adopted for CU management. In addition, a national consensus—Consensus for the Practical Management of Urticaria in Primary Care—was published in 2023, with contributions from multiple PCP-oriented scientific and academic associations.13 That same year, factors affecting urticaria management in Colombia were systematically characterized.14 These efforts aimed to equip PCPs with practical tools for early and effective management of urticaria, enabling them to provide timely and appropriate patient care. Importantly, these initiatives resulted from collaboration between several international scientific societies in Latin American, including UCARE Latin America centers as well as societies from allergy, dermatology, internal medicine, pediatrics, emergency medicine, general medicine, and family medicine.

We aimed to evaluate the impact of urticaria-focused educational campaigns on routine clinical practice among PCPs at both the primary-care level and among specialists (allergists and dermatologists). On the basis of these results, potential barriers can be identified and solutions proposed to achieve the goal of providing the best possible medical care for patients with CU.

Methods

Study design

The aim of this study was to evaluate the knowledge of urticaria among PCPs focusing on diagnostic and treatment domains. To achieve this, we used a multimodal assessment strategy integrating 3 complementary information sources across different evaluation periods (from 2022 to 2025): (1) knowledge assessment through a standardized questionnaire administered in both years; (2) medical record review from 4 health care centers (2 hospital-based and 2 outpatient clinics), using International Classification of Disease 10 L50.0 to L50.9 codes; and (3) review of national prescription data, obtained from the Colombian Comprehensive Social Protection Information System (SISPRO), covering all urticaria-related prescriptions issued during the evaluation period. Each data source was used to evaluate two main domains: diagnosis (laboratory tests, clinical scales, causality assessment, comorbidities) and treatment (pharmacologic management, treatment duration, referral to a urticaria specialist).

Knowledge questionnaire on urticaria

Through scientific societies, professional networks, and social media platforms, PCPs from Colombia were invited to complete a questionnaire regarding the diagnosis and treatment of urticaria. Content domains were aligned with the national consensus13 and international guidelines.7 Three years later, a new invitation was made to complete the questionnaire among the initial cohort and new participants.

Medical records review

A review of electronic medical records of 4 health care centers (2 hospital based and 2 outpatient clinics) was performed. Records were selected on the basis of the urticaria International Classification of Disease 10 diagnostic codes (L50.0, L50.1, L50.2, L50.3, L50.4, L50.5, L50.6, L50.8, L50.9, L56.3). Treatment patterns, diagnostic workup, and adherence to guideline recommendations were evaluated for each year.

National medical prescription system

SISPRO is a Colombian national system for medical prescriptions. We evaluated records associated with urticaria-related codes for urticaria and their correspondence with therapeutic prescriptions by PCPs. An indication for biological therapies (omalizumab) was only available for allergist or dermatologists. Other therapies (eg, dupilumab, remibrutinib) were not yet available in Colombia.

Identification of access barriers

To identify potential access barriers in the clinical management of urticaria, a structured questionnaire was conducted among delegates of the scientific societies of PCPs and urticaria specialists (allergists, dermatologists).

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the ethics committees of the participating scientific associations as well as “Alma Mater de Antioquia” Hospital (approval IN41-2022).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed according to the nature and distribution of the data (mean, median, confidence interval (CI), standard deviation, interquartile range, minimum, maximum). Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparisons between continuous variables. Categorical variables were analyzed by Pearson chi-square test. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used to compare pre and post measurements. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

To define the “minimal difference” in questionnaires performed that could be the result of the educational processes we implemented, we reviewed medical records from 2014 to 2020 (the period before the release of the latest update to the guidelines). The year-over-year variation in diagnostic and management issues was consistently below 13%; therefore, a difference over this percentage was considered potentially related to educational initiatives. The optimal sample size of 224 was based on a difference of at least 15% for a change in the questionnaire with a power of 80% and a significance level of 5%. In clinical centers and SISPRO systems, all the records available during the time evaluation period were included.

A total of 487 PCPs participated in the 2022 knowledge questionnaire and 621 in 2025 (Table I). Of these, 227 physicians completed the questionnaires in both periods (Fig 1, A). Knowledge among PCPs regarding urticaria diagnosis

The questionnaire showed an increase in urticaria diagnostic comprehension among PCPs from 2022 to 2025 (Fig 2, A). Questions evaluated knowledge of lesion characteristics, disease duration, and differential diagnosis. By 2025, a greater percentage of PCPs achieved a correct diagnosis compared to 2022 (Fig 2, A). This corresponded to a net overall increase of 20.5% (95% CI, 16, 24, P < .01) and a relative increase of 126.3% (95% CI, 68, 154, P < .01). Among the 227 participants who completed both assessments, diagnostic accuracy showed a net increase of 12% more than the overall group, although the 2025 group still showed better performance than the 2022 group (9%; 95% CI, 6, 16, P < .01). Knowledge about clinical scoring systems in the questionnaire increased from 12% (2023) to 21% (2025) (P = .02). However, in the medical records, only 9% of physicians used any severity scales (eg, from “0 to 10” or “0 to 100,” or the categorical scales “good, very good, fair–poor, very poor” referring to pruritus). There were no records of the use of urticaria control test, urticaria activity score, or other specific urticaria scales among PCPs records.

Laboratory tests requested for urticaria

According to the questionnaire, there was an improved recognition of appropriate laboratory tests for CU and a sustained decrease in unnecessary tests between PCPs (Fig 3, A). By 2025, 42% (95% CI, 37, 51) of physicians knew which laboratory tests were recommended in guidelines for CU (Fig 3, A). This represented a significant increase compared to the first evaluation, where 88% of PCPs indicated tests not recommended by the guidelines. In the evaluation of the medical records, prescription of guideline-recommended tests increased over time (Fig 3, B). Treatment of urticaria by PCPs

The survey showed a significant increase in the recognition of the basic principles of antihistamines as first-line treatment, and there was also a significant decrease in participants who recommend first-generation antihistamines (58% in 2022 vs 27% in 2025) (Fig 4, A). We also observed a greater number of PCPs who recognized increasing the dose of antihistamines as an option for managing urticaria (27% vs 52%) when the conventional dose was not enough. According to clinical records, several of the results observed in the survey were replicated over time, showing a greater use of second-generation antihistamines over first-generation ones and a greater number of physicians prescribing doses above the conventional daily dose when disease did not respond to the standard dose (Fig 4, B). However, unlike the survey, in 44% of the clinical histories, the doctors recommended prescribing antihistamines for only 1 or 2 months even in cases of CU.

In the knowledge test, a decreased use of other treatments such as antileukotrienes, systemic steroids, and topical steroids was observed as part of the urticaria control treatment (Fig 4, C). A similar result was observed when reviewing medical records (Fig 4, D). However, steroid use in at least one course was high; 44% of patients reported having received a course of steroids annually. Furthermore, among records of patients with urticaria during >3 years, 67% reported having received more than one course of systemic steroids at some point to manage an exacerbation. Four percent of patients received other systemic treatments such as methotrexate or azathioprine for urticaria.

Identification of triggers and restrictions in urticaria

According to the questionnaire, the predominant triggers of urticaria were adequately identified by 49% (95% CI, 42, 54) of PCPs in 2025 versus 29% (95% CI, 27, 33) at the initial evaluation (P < .001). In the survey, 44% of PCPs reported the need for dietary restrictions in patients with CU; this decreased to 29% in 2025 (P = .03). A total of 17% in 2025 versus 12% in 2022 recognized that nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs could be potential triggers for urticaria exacerbations.

However, clinical records showed by 2022, 32% of records including explicit recommendations for a food restriction—most frequently pork, strawberries, and sausages—and 8% for a medication, especially nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; this frequency showed little change over time (24% and 9%, respectively, in 2025). We identified that 33% of food restrictions were advised by physicians using a general list of foods, without a clearly documented history of patient reactions. In 83% (95% CI, 79, 89) and 78% (95% CI, 72, 93) of the clinical records, there was no clear evaluation of drugs or physical activity as potential triggers in urticaria, respectively.

Referral to specialist

The documentation of referral indications to a urticaria specialist (allergology, dermatology) increased slightly, from 83% of PCPs during 2022 to 87% in 2025 (Fig 5, A). During 2022, according to medical records, 65% of patients were referred to a urticaria specialist. From these records, reason for remission was specified in 56% of the records; in 26% of cases, more than one reason per patient was identified; 34% were referred because urticaria did not go into remission after 6 to 12 months of follow-up; 40% were referred for inadequate clinical response; 16% were referred for allergy tests or biopsy; 24% were referred because an allergy was suspected (eg, food or drug allergy); and 12% were referred for respiratory symptoms associated with angioedema. Barriers to clinical treatment

During 2025, on the basis of information given by PCPs, urticaria specialists, and the clinical records, some barriers for treatment were identified. At the PCP level, barriers for prescription of some or any second-generation antihistamines were identified (median [95% CI], 12% [8, 23]), as well as barriers for dose escalation of antihistamines (40% [18, 60]), barriers for PCPs clinical follow-up (19% [9, 16]), and barriers or delays for referral to urticaria specialist (36% [24, 53]). Discussion

Continuous learning is an essential part of medical practice.15,16 On the basis of local epidemiology and mortality rates, some diseases tend to motivate continuous academic updates among PCPs. However, it is difficult for PCPs to keep up to date on all diseases, and the management offered to patients does not always correspond to current knowledge.17 Therefore, continuing education offered by scientific societies, focused on essential patient needs, becomes indispensable to reduce the gap between medical advances and the management offered in daily practice by PCPs.

The GA2LEN initiative, through the Urticaria Centers of Excellence (UCARE), has connected various specialized urticaria centers around the world (ucare-network.com); it has also synthesized the best currently available evidence on urticaria into international guidelines endorsed by more than 100 scientific societies.7,18 Despite their comprehensive nature, these guidelines are primarily oriented toward specialists, making their full implementation challenging in primary care settings. Previous studies evaluating the impact of the urticaria clinical guidelines11,12,19,20 have identified that 60% to 90% of patients with urticaria referred to specialists have not received adequate first-line treatment from PCPs. This contributes to increased disease burden, poorer patient outcomes, and higher health care costs. Although these studies clearly identify a problem about the management of urticaria among PCPs, they do not evaluate the impact of educational measures as a possible solution; our study is the first to evaluate this aspect directly.

Since 2022, as part of an initiative from UCARE centers in Colombia and various scientific medical societies, some educational projects seeking to improve the knowledge about urticaria among PCPs are being developed.

Our study demonstrates that knowledge about urticaria has improved substantially among the PCP population in recent years, likely due to educational campaigns. Our results suggest that these efforts have had a meaningful impact: We observed substantial improvements across several key areas, including diagnostic accuracy, appropriate prescription of second-generation antihistamines, and recognition of treatment escalation strategies. Importantly, these improvements were consistent across multiple data sources (questionnaires, clinical records, national prescription data), thus strengthening the robustness of our findings. Additionally, the study identifies areas of diagnosis and treatment where there has been little progress over time, and it identifies barriers that delay adequate disease management among patients with urticaria.

Considering that in the 10 years before 2022 medical records showed a variation of less than 13%, it is valid to assume that the magnitude of change observed after 2022 is unlikely to reflect natural fluctuations alone, and that the educational measures implemented since 2022 may have influenced the significant increase in knowledge about the diagnosis and treatment of urticaria among PCPs. Efficient treatment reduces the burden of the disease, resulting in less time and resources needed to achieve adequate disease control by promptly implementing the steps recommended in the urticaria guidelines. This translates into a better quality of life for patients.

Despite these gains, several gaps persist. One area with limited progress is the continued recommendation of unnecessary dietary and medication restrictions. Although such advice sometimes comes from patient-reported suspicions (many of which represent erroneous causal association), it may also reflect persistent misconceptions among PCPs. While cautionary avoidance is not always inappropriate, generalized restrictions without documented reactions can increase the patient’s burden and negatively affect quality of life.2 Such doubts should prompt referral to a specialist. In the case of restrictions on physical activities or medications, we observed in clinical records that these were recommended, but we also rarely observed evidence that they were asked—which agrees with what was observed in the questionnaire where these aspects are points to improve.

We also observed that a significant number (although decreasing) of PCPs still prescribe first-generation H1 antihistamines despite guideline recommendations favoring second-generation agents because of their better safety and tolerability profiles, making it necessary to continue and intensify educational efforts in these areas. The persistence of these behaviors appears to be multifactorial. We consider the lack of continuing education to be the primary cause, but sometimes the health care system hinders access to the most appropriate therapies.

The barriers observed by both PCPs and specialists indicated different administrative problems within the health care system regarding the treatment of urticaria, such as denial of second-generation anti-H1 antihistamines or dose escalation. Therefore, it is crucial to engage health care decision-makers in acquiring basic knowledge about the disease; delays in management or the use of less effective therapies not only affect the patient but also generate unnecessary costs for the health care system.21, 22, 23

This study has several strengths. It is the first to explore the direct impact of educational measures on urticaria in PCPs. Additionally, we used different sources of information, which allows us to infer that the information obtained is quite robust. Familiarity effect could explain the better response in the questionnaire, but we did the evaluation using 3 different approaches—review of clinical history and also evaluation of prescriptions according to national register—and for that reason, results suggest that learning is a more probable explanation than the familiarity effect. However, the study also has limitations. The results suggest that educational measures play a key role in increasing PCPs knowledge of urticaria, but we did not evaluate each educational measure individually (webinars, talks, podcasts, communications from societies, local consensus, etc), so we cannot identify which tools are the most effective. Pharmaceutical companies are active in medical education when they are in the product launch phase; globally, dupilumab and remibrutinib are already approved for urticaria, but in Colombia, there is still no approval, and current regulations do not allow promotion until there is local approval, so we believe that the observed results were not due to an active process on the part of the pharmaceutical laboratories. Additionally, although general practitioners and family physicians represent the majority of PCPs in Colombia, the participation of other specialties such as pediatrics or internal medicine was relatively smaller, limiting subgroup analysis. If we had an adequate sample size, it would have been interesting to be able to evaluate each group of PCPs (pediatricians, internal medicine, etc) separately.

In conclusion, educational measures tailored to the specific needs of patients improve the knowledge and management of urticaria among PCPs.
