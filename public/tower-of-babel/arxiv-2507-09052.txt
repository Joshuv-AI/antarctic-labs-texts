Can Contrastive Learning Improve Class-Imbalanced Diffusion Model?
Fang Chen f chen20@ucmerced.edu, Alex Villa a villa49@ucmerced.edu, Gongbo Liang g liang@tamusa.edu, Xiaoyi Lu x iaoyi.lu@ucmerced.edu, Meng Tang m tang4@ucmerced.edu

Abstract
Training data for class-conditional image synthesis often exhibit a long-tailed distribution with limited images for tail classes. Such an imbalance causes mode collapse and reduces the diversity of synthesized images for tail classes. For class-conditional diffusion models trained on imbalanced data, we aim to improve the diversity of tail class images without compromising the fidelity and diversity of head class images. We achieve this by introducing two deceptively simple but highly effective contrastive loss functions. Firstly, we employ an unsupervised InfoNCE loss utilizing negative samples to increase the distance/dissimilarity among synthetic images, particularly for tail classes. To further enhance the diversity of tail classes, our second loss is an MSE loss that contrasts class-conditional generation with unconditional generation at large timesteps. This second loss makes the denoising process insensitive to class conditions for the initial steps, which enriches tail classes through knowledge sharing from head classes. Conditional-unconditional alignment has been shown to enhance the performance of long-tailed GAN. We are the first to adapt such alignment to diffusion models. We successfully leveraged contrastive learning for class-imbalanced diffusion models. Our contrastive learning framework is easy to implement and outperforms standard DDPM and alternative methods for class-imbalanced diffusion models across various datasets, including CIFAR10/100-LT, PlacesLT, TinyImageNetLT, and ImageNetLT.

Recent advances in diffusion models

These diffusion-based generative models rely on large-scale datasets for training, which often follow a long-tailed distribution with dominant data for head classes and limited data for tail classes.
Similar to the class-imbalanced recognition model

We aim to increase the diversity of tail class images while maintaining the fidelity and diversity of head class images for diffusion models.

We look into a highly related and crucial problem of long-tailed image recognition, for which contrastive learning is an effective method

;
li2022targeted
.
We are curious
whether contrastive learning can improve long-tailed diffusion models for image synthesis
.
Contrastive learning is a general learning paradigm of minimizing the distance of positive pairs while maximizing the distance of negative pairs, which has attracted lots of research interest for self-supervised representation learning
chen2020simple
.
On the other hand, diffusion model has also emerged as an effective model for representation learning

;
baranchuk2021label

We explicitly incorporate contrastive learning regularization to the diffusion model to further enhance representation learning towards better image generation.

While at first glance, integrating contrastive learning into diffusion models seems straightforward, identifying suitable positive and negative pairs and designing an effective loss function are far from obvious.
Also, training class-conditioned diffusion models involves both conditional generation and unconditional generation at multiple timesteps, which is more complicated than a feed-forward CNN or transformer.
We found the devil is in the details and designed effective contrastive losses tailored for long-tailed diffusion models.

Our first loss is an InfoNCE loss
infonce
with negative samples only, serving as regularization for denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM).
Given that mode collapse during inference manifests as generated samples being overly similar, particularly for tail classes,
we introduce unsupervised contrastive learning with negative samples to maximize the distances between generated images.
Unlike supervised contrastive learning
khosla2020supervised
, unsupervised contrastive learning does not differentiate between embedding/representation for different classes.
Instead, it distinguishes between representations of images of the same class, hence increasing image diversity.

Our second loss is an MSE loss designed to contrast estimated noises from conditional and unconditional generation, which effectively minimizes the KL divergence between latent distributions for conditional and unconditional generation.
While such conditional-unconditional alignment seems undesirable with less controllability, a critical aspect of our approach is that our MSE loss is weighted more for larger timesteps corresponding to the initial stage of the reverse process.
Given the known overfitting in conditional generation for tail classes for both GAN and diffusion models,
we are motivated by the success of conditional-unconditional alignment for long-tailed GAN
utlo
;
transitioncgan
, which facilitates knowledge sharing between head classes and tail classes.
Notably, unconditional GAN generation has been observed to achieve superior FID than conditional generation under limited data
transitioncgan
.
We adapt conditional-unconditional alignment from GAN to diffusion models.
Unlike the GAN-based method
utlo
aligns conditional generation and unconditional generation for low-resolution images exhibiting intra-class similarity, we leverage observed image similarity during the initial denoising steps for tail classes and head classes
si2024freeu
.
Hence, we propose to match conditional generation with unconditional generation for large timesteps.

While contrastive learning has been proposed for diffusion models to improve adversarial robustness
ouyang2023improving
as guidance during inference and to find semantically meaningful directions
dalva2024noiseclr
, we effectively leveraged contrastive learning as regularization losses for class-imbalanced diffusion models. Our work has positive societal impact for content generation of underrepresented groups.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

Class-imbalanced Image Generation
Generative models such as VAE

Many methods address the problem of class imbalance by augmenting training data for the tail classes.
A VAE is fine-tuned on tail classes under a majority-based prior

It is observed that GAN

Recent work addresses class-imbalanced diffusion models

;

For example, CBDM

DiffROP

We aim to reduce overfitting for tail classes, while DiffROP

The first part of our loss, the InfoNCE loss with negative pairs only, is defined for any pair of images regardless of their classes.
However, DiffROP only considers pairs of images of different classes as negative pairs without regularizing images of the same class.
Conceptually, we generalize DiffROP by including extra negative pairs of images within each class, hence promoting intra-class diversity.

We are inspired by UTLO
utlo
for long-tailed generation with GAN, which shares the latent representations of conditional GAN with unconditional GAN and implicitly shares knowledge between the head class and tail class.
The motivation with UTLO
utlo
is that low-resolution images from GAN are similar for head classes and tail classes.
We observe a similar phenomenon for denoised images in the initial steps of the denoising process, which motivates our contrastive loss.

We adapt the conditional-unconditional alignment method from GAN

;
utlo
to diffusion models.

Contrastive Learning for Representation Learning
Both unsupervised contrastive learning

;
moco
and supervised contrastive learning
khosla2020supervised
;

are approaches used in representation learning to produce feature embeddings by maximizing the similarity between positive pairs and minimizing the similarity between negative pairs.
Unsupervised contrastive learning does not require labels and typically augments the same data to form positive pairs

;

Supervised contrastive learning incorporates class labels and pulls together all samples from the same class while pushing apart samples from different classes.
Contrastive learning is an effective method for self-supervised learning

;
li2022targeted
.
We effectively utilized contrastive learning for class-imbalanced diffusion models.
Our work is different from the contrastive-guided diffusion process

Another difference is that we focus on contrastive learning during training, while contrastive-guided diffusion process
ouyang2023improving
is about guidance for inference.
While the diffusion model originally for generation tasks becomes an emerging technique for representation learning

;
baranchuk2021label
, we directly integrate contrastive learning regularization to diffusion models for better generation on imbalanced data.
Notably, contrastive loss with negative samples exclusively focuses on separating dissimilar instances in the embedding space, which we leverage to address mode collapse, as detailed in Sec .
3.1
.

(

3.1
Negative Pairs and InfoNCE loss

We observed the synthesized images of DDPM for a tail class concentrated around the limited training images in the latent space, leading to the issue of mode collapse, as shown in the Fig.
1
, and the Fig.
5
in the appendix
A.3.1
with visualizing the distribution of synthetic images for the tail class.
By applying the InfoNCE loss with negative samples only, we intend to increase the distance of each synthesized image from other images in the latent space, hence increasing the diversity of synthetic images in particular for tail classes.

We consider InfoNCE loss with negative samples only, where the negative samples are based on noisy images from a mini-batch.
Specifically, an encoder
e(\cdot)
encodes an image with noise
x_{t}
to a low-dimensional latent
h
, which is decoded to noise
\epsilon
by a decoder
d(\cdot)
in a denoising network.
Our InfoNCE loss is defined for the latents
h
with each image in a mini-batch treated as an anchor.
All other images are treated as negative samples.
The anchor sample
x_{t}^{i}
and negative samples
x_{t}^{j}
are fed into the encoder
e(\cdot)
to get the latents
h_{anc}^{i}=e(x_{t}^{i})
and
h_{neg}^{j}=e(x_{t}^{j})
.
Our InfoNCE contrastive loss
\mathcal{L}_{nceclr}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{nceclr}=-\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}{\log\frac{\pi^{i}_{anc}}{\pi^{i}_{anc}+\sum_{j\in{B},j\neq i}{\pi^{j}_{neg}}}},\text{with }\pi^{i}_{anc}=\exp(\frac{h_{anc}^{i}\cdot h_{pos}^{i}}{\tau}),\pi^{j}_{neg}=\exp(\frac{h_{anc}^{i}\cdot h_{neg}^{j}}{\tau}),

(1)

where
B
denotes a mini-batch, and
\tau
is a temperature for softmax which we keep
0.1
as the default setting.
Note that it is common to augment an anchor image
x_{t}^{i}
to be a positive sample in many unsupervised contrastive learning methods. Here, we don’t apply any data augmentation and directly treat the anchor latent
h_{anc}^{i}
itself as the positive vector in the InfoNCE loss, i.e.,
h_{pos}^{i}:=h_{anc}^{i}
.

3.2
Positive Pairs and MSE loss

Conditinal-unconditinal Alignment Facilitates
Knowledge Sharing for Class-imbalanced GAN
We take inspiration from UTLO
(
utlo
)
, which addresses long-tailed generation with GANs, and observes that the similarity between head class and tail class images increases at lower resolutions.
To share knowledge between the head class and tail class, UTLO
(
utlo
)
proposes unconditional GAN objectives for lower-resolution images and conditional GAN objectives for subsequent higher-resolution images.
More specifically, the lower-resolution image/feature becomes less sensitive to class labels, and the second part of the generator generates the fine details of images.
Utilizing unconditional generation for lower resolution is effective in increasing the diversity and quality of tail class images.

In a different setting of training GAN with limited data, Transitional-GAN
(
transitioncgan
)
leverages unconditional generation in a two-staged training scheme, as unconditional generation can give a better FID than conditional generation when data is limited.

Conditional-unconditinal Alignment
for Class-imbalanced Diffusion Model
We aim to adapt the
conditional-unconditional alignment
approach used in GANs
(
utlo
;
transitioncgan
)
to diffusion models.
Our key insight is that the diffusion model denoises images recursively, and the similarity between head class images and tail class images is higher during the
initial timesteps
of the denoising process.
Our approach is different from
(
utlo
)
, which proposes unconditional generation for
lower-resolution
images.
We visualize the reverse processing of unconditional generation and conditional generation with different class labels of the original DDPM, starting from the same Gaussian noise, as shown in Fig.
3
with more examples in appendix
A.3.4
.
Images from unconditional generation and conditional generation share similar low-frequency components, e.g., the coarse shape of synthesized objects and background region, especially at the initial time steps

si2024freeu
.

The leftmost column shows synthetic images with different classes but with the same initial noise or random seed. We visualize the low-frequency component and the high-frequency component from the reverse processing.
It shows that low-frequency components are similar during initial time steps for different classes with the same initial noise
si2024freeu
.

Motivated by this observation, we propose to match unconditional generation and conditional generation at the initial time steps, to enable knowledge sharing between tail classes and head classes with abundant data.

Another motivation is that class conditions are not necessary for all timesteps. In fact, using adaptive guidance weight with a small weight in the initial steps can yield a better FID compared to using constant guidance weight
cfgweightscheduler
.

From Conditional-unconditinal Distributin Maching to MSE Loss
We penalize KL divergence between the conditional distribution
p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t}^{i},c^{i})
and unconditional distribution
p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t}^{i})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{i,t}_{mseclr}=D_{KL}[p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t}^{i},c^{i})||p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t}^{i})].

(2)

Suppose
p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t},c)=\mathcal{N}(x_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c),\sigma_{t}^{2}I),p_{\theta}(x_{x-t}|x_{t})=\mathcal{N}(x_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(x_{t},t),\sigma_{t}^{2}I)
, then we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{i,t}_{mseclr}=\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}||\mu_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i},t,c^{i})-\mu_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i},t)||^{2}]+C,

(3)

where C is constant, and

\displaystyle\mu_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i},t,c^{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}x_{t}^{i}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i},t,c^{i}),\hskip 9.24994pt\mu_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}x_{t}^{i}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i},t),

(4)

where
\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t},\bar{\alpha}_{t}\!\!=\!\!\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\beta_{i})
,
\{\beta_{t}\}_{1:T}
is the variance schedule.

Then,
\mathcal{L}_{mseclr}
simplifies to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{i,t}_{mseclr}\propto||\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i},t,c^{i})-\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i},t)||^{2}.

(5)

As shown in Fig.
2
, the anchor vector
h_{anc}^{i}
is fed into decoder
d(\cdot)
to get unconditional noise estimation
\epsilon_{unc}^{i}:=\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i};t,\emptyset)
.
Meanwhile, the anchor image
x_{t}^{i}
is also incorporated with the class label condition
\mathbf{c^{i}}
fed into the network to get the conditional noise estimation
\epsilon_{cond}^{i}:=\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i};t,\mathbf{c^{i}})

The MSE contrastive loss is defined between the unconditional and conditional estimation:

Our MSE loss is similar to CBDM loss

Our motivation is different from CBDM, but we end up with a similar but different loss. Our work offers different interpretations of previous work and makes insightful connections.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{mseclr}=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}{\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{0}}[\frac{t}{T}\|\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i};t,\mathbf{c^{i}})-\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i};t,\emptyset)\|^{2}}].

(6)

The loss is weighted linearly by timesteps

In other words, we align conditional generation and unconditional generation for the initial steps.

Limitation of our method
Our MSE loss involves both conditional generation and unconditional generation, which requires two passes of the denosing network during training and increases training time. In practice, our training time for one epoch is 1.6x of that of DDPM. However, our method doesn’t increase inference latency.

5
Conclusion

Real-world data for training image generation models often exhibit long-tailed distributions.
Similar to class-imbalanced GANs
(
utlo
)
, class-imbalanced diffusion models tend to overfit on tail classes due to data scarcity.
Inspired by the success and impact of contrastive learning for long-tailed recognition, we are interested in whether contrastive learning can improve the class-imbalanced diffusion model.
With well-motivated losses and carefully crafted positive/negative pairs, we effectively leveraged contrastive learning with noticeable improvement.
Our framework involves two contrastive losses.
Firstly, we employ an unsupervised InfoNCE loss with negative samples only to contrast the latents of different synthetic images at every denoising step, promoting intra-class diversity in particular for tail classes.
Our second MSE loss contrasts class-conditional generation with unconditional generation for large timesteps.
This encourages the initial denoising steps to be class-agnostic, thereby enriching tail classes through knowledge sharing from head classes–a principle demonstrated to enhance long-tailed GAN performance
transitioncgan
;
utlo
, which we successfully adapt to diffusion models.
With the two losses, our contrastive learning method outperforms standard DDPM and other state-of-the-art methods, including CBDM
cbdm
and OCLT
oclt
for class-imbalanced diffusion models.
Extensive experiments on multiple datasets, including CIFAR10LT, CIFAR100LT, PlacesLT, TinyImageNetLT, and large-scale ImageNetLT, demonstrate the effectiveness and impact of our method.

During inference, we perform a grid search algorithm for each method to determine the optimal guidance strength
\omega
of CFG, ensuring each model achieves its best performance.

A.4
Dimensional Collapse in Latent Space

Figure 9:

Singular values of the covariance matrix for latent embeddings of synthetic images generated by DDPM and our method.
‘t’ denotes the timestep.
Compared to DDPM, our method’s gives a covariance matrix with more significant singular values, which the latent embeddings effectively span more dimensions in the available space.
On the other hand, DDPM has more severe dimensional collapse in the latent space, which leads to overfitting.

Dimensional collapseis the phenomenon in representation learning when embeddings span a lower-dimensional subspace instead of the entire available space.
It is considered undesirable and is highly correlated with overfitting.
To investigate the effectiveness of the proposed infoNCE loss, we visualize the singular values of covariance matrix of embedding vectors for synthetic images generated by DDPM and our method, as shown in Fig.
9
.
We evaluate dimensional collapse by first collecting the latent vectors
h
on 1024 synthetic images.
Each latent vector has a dimension of
d=256
.
Then, we compute the covariance matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
of the latent space:
C=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}{(h_{i}-\bar{h})(h_{i}-\bar{h})^{T}}
, where
\bar{h}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}{h_{i}}/N
and
N
is the number of samples.
Fig.
9
shows the sorted sigular values of this matrix (
C=USV^{T},S=diag(\delta^{k})
) in logarithmic scale (
\log(\delta^{k})
).
For DDPM, a number of log singular values less than zero, indicating collapsed dimensions.
Compared to DDPM, our method has more effective features with the log of sigular values greater than zero in the latent space with less dimensional collapse, implying higher diversity of the synthetic images.
