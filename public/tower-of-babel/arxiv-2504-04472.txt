Fast Maximization of Current Flow Group Closeness Centrality Zhongzhi Zhang is the corresponding author.
Haisong Xia, Zhongzhi Zhang

Abstract
Derived from effective resistances, the current flow closeness centrality (CFCC) for a group of nodes measures the importance of node groups in an undirected graph with n n nodes. Given the widespread applications of identifying crucial nodes, we investigate the problem of maximizing CFCC for a node group S S subject to the cardinality constraint | S | = k ≪ n |S|=k\ll n . Despite the proven NP-hardness of this problem, we propose two novel greedy algorithms for its solution. Our algorithms are based on spanning forest sampling and Schur complement, which exhibit nearly linear time complexities and achieve an approximation factor of 1 − k k − 1 ​ 1 e − ϵ 1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}-\epsilon for any 0 < ϵ < 1 0<\epsilon<1 . Extensive experiments on real-world graphs illustrate that our algorithms outperform the state-of-the-art method in terms of efficiency and effectiveness, scaling to graphs with millions of nodes.



I
Introduction

As a fundamental problem in the field of complex networks, the identification of crucial nodes has garnered widespread research interest
[
1
,
2
]
. Due to its importance in network analysis, this problem has been applied to various areas, including graph mining
[
3
]
, leader-follower opinion dynamics
[
4
]
, and influence maximization
[
5
,
6
]
. The most intuitive approach to solve this problem is to design a centrality that evaluates the importance of each node, thus transforming the identification problem into a computational one. Previous studies
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
have proposed various centralities to analyze the roles of nodes in a graph, with a large proportion focusing on closeness centrality. The closeness of a node is defined as the reciprocal of the sum of shortest path distances between it and all other nodes in the graph. Despite its advantage over local centralities, closeness centrality only considers the shortest paths. This sensitivity to noise potentially leads to counterintuitive results
[
11
]
.

To address the issue of sensitivity, current flow closeness centrality (CFCC)
[
12
]
is presented based on resistance distance in the corresponding electrical networks. Resistance distance is a crucial metric for solving diverse fundamental problems in the field of graph theory. Specifically, in the data management community, resistance distance is utilized to design effective graph systems and applications
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Many researchers also develop efficient approximation of resistance distance to facilitate data management tasks
[
17
,
18
]
.
Since CFCC is defined via resistance distance, it has been applied to analyze the vulnerability of power grids
[
19
,
20
]
and optimize the performance of conductive films
[
21
]
. Additionally, resistance distance considers contributions from all paths between node pairs, which mirrors the propagation of electrical spike signals in brain networks
[
22
]
. This property enables CFCC to identify active neurons in the analysis of brain networks
[
23
]
. In other types of networks, the current flow-based closeness also outperforms classical counterparts in identifying crucial nodes
[
11
]
.

While most previous studies focus on identifying individual nodes, many practical problems essentially require finding a group of nodes that is the most important among all node groups with capacity
k\ll n
, where
n
denotes the number of all nodes. For instance, how to place resources on
k
peers in P2P networks for easy access by others
[
24
]
. Meanwhile, placing sensors of wireless networks involves selecting an optimal subset of nodes for sampling physical signals such as radiation or temperature
[
25
,
26
]
. Finally, the challenge of point cloud sampling
[
27
,
28
]
lies in selecting a representative subset of points to preserve the geometric features for reconstruction.

Recently, the concept of current flow closeness centrality (CFCC) has been extended from individual nodes to node groups
[
29
]
. Additionally, the problem of selecting a node group of size
k
, aiming to maximize its CFCC, has been proposed. The state-of-the-art algorithm has been developed to approximately address it, which utilizes a fast Laplacian solver. However, its running time, while nearly linear in the number of edges, remains prohibitively long for large-scale networks with more than 10 million edges.

Contributions.

In light of this limitation, we establish the connection between Current Flow Closeness Maximization (CFCM) and rooted spanning forests, motivating our proposal of a greedy Monte Carlo algorithm
ForestCFCM
. Based on sampling forests,
ForestCFCM
is pleasingly parallelizable while maintaining an approximation factor for solving CFCM. To further enhance efficiency and effectiveness, we develop another greedy algorithm
SchurCFCM
. Based on the estimation of Schur complement,
SchurCFCM
accelerates sampling spanning forests and enhances the quality of CFCM solutions, while still preserving an approximation factor. Numerical experiments validate the superiority of
SchurCFCM
over both
ForestCFCM
and the state-of-the-art method in terms of efficiency and effectiveness.

Our contributions of this work are summarized as follows.

•

We propose two Monte Carlo algorithms,
ForestCFCM
and
SchurCFCM
, for approximately maximizing CFCC
C(S)
under the cardinality constraint
\left|S\right|=k
. Both algorithms sample rooted spanning forests, with
SchurCFCM
additionally leveraging Schur complement.

•

Both of our algorithms achieve an approximation factor of
1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}-\epsilon
for
0<\epsilon<1
. Meanwhile,
SchurCFCM
exhibits a nearly linear time complexity.

•

We conduct extensive experiments on real-world graphs. The results demonstrate that both of our proposed algorithms are up to
370\times
faster than the state-of-the-art method, while maintaining superior effectiveness.

II-B

Graphs and Laplacian Matrices

We use
G=(V,E)
to denote connected undirected graph with
n=\left|V\right|
nodes and
m=\left|E\right|
edges, where
V
and
E
denote, respectively, the node set and edge set of
G
. We use
e=\left(u,v\right)
to denote an edge
e
linking node
u
and node
v
.

The adjacency matrix of
G
is denoted as
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
: for two nodes
i\in V
and
j\in V
,
\bm{A}_{{i}{j}}=\bm{A}_{{j}{i}}=1
if
i
and
j
are adjacent, and
\bm{A}_{{i}{j}}=\bm{A}_{{j}{i}}=0
otherwise. The degree vector of
G
is defined as
\bm{d}=\bm{A}\bm{1}=\left(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n}\right)^{\top}
, where
d_{i}
represents the degree of node
i
. If we denote the degree diagonal matrix as
\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}\left(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{n}\right)
, then the Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
of
G
is defined as
\bm{L}=\bm{D}-\bm{A}
.
Since
\bm{L}
is not invertible due to its null space
\bm{1}
, we turn to use its pseudoinverse
\bm{L}^{\dagger}=\big(\bm{L}+\frac{1}{n}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\big)^{-1}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}
.
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
appears in many quantities related to random walks, such as the Kemeny constant
[
30
]
. Moreover, It is easy to verify that Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
and its submatrices
\bm{L}_{-{S}}
are Symmetric Diagonally Dominant (SDD).

II-C

Spanning Forests and Random Walks

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, a spanning subgraph of
G
retains all nodes from
V
while including only a subset of edges from
E
. A spanning forest is a spanning subgraph of
G
, whose connected components are trees or isolated nodes. A rooted spanning forest of
G
is a spanning forest of
G
where a particular node in each tree is designated as its root. The set of all root nodes forms the root set of this spanning forest.

Many studies focus on methods for uniformly sampling rooted spanning forests, including Wilson’s algorithm
[
31
]
based on loop-erased random walks. For a connected graph, a classical random walk is defined by its transition matrix
\bm{P}
. At each step, if the walker is at node
i
, it moves to an adjacent node
j
with equal probability
\bm{P}_{{i}{j}}
. It follows that
\bm{P}=\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}
. To generate a spanning forest with root set
S
, the loop-erased random walk is simulated iteratively. Initially
\mathcal{F}=S
, when the random walker visits a node in
\mathcal{F}
, the loop-erased path from the source node to this node is added into
\mathcal{F}
. Notably, it has been proved in
[
31
]
that the distribution of forests sampled by Wilson’s algorithm is independent of the order of source nodes.

II-D

Resistance Distance and Relevant Centrality

For an arbitrary undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, we define its corresponding electrical network by treating edges as unit resistors and nodes as junctions between resistors
[
32
]
. For graph
G
, if we denote
\Phi_{i,j}(u)
as the voltage of node
u
when a unit current enters at node
i
and leaves at node
j
, then the resistance distance
R{({i},{j})}\triangleq\Phi_{i,j}(i)-\Phi_{i,j}(j)
is defined as the effective resistance between
i
and
j
in the corresponding electrical network
[
33
]
.

For a connected graph
G
, the resistance distance
R{({i},{j})}
between two nodes
i
and
j
can be expressed in terms of
\bm{L}^{\dagger}

[
33
,
34
]
and the inverse for submatrices of
\bm{L}

[
35
]
as:

\displaystyle R{({i},{j})}=\bm{L}^{\dagger}_{{i}{i}}+\bm{L}^{\dagger}_{{j}{j}}-2\bm{L}^{\dagger}_{{i}{j}},

(1)

\displaystyle R{({i},{j})}=\big(\bm{L}_{-{i}}^{-1}\big)_{{j}{j}}=\big(\bm{L}_{-{j}}^{-1}\big)_{{i}{i}}.

(2)

Since the resistance distance exhibits properties of a metric, we can use it to represent the proximity between node pairs, and measure the importance of each node by defining relevant centrality, such as the current flow closeness centrality (CFCC)
[
12
]
. For an
n
-node connected graph, the CFCC of a node
u
is defined as
C(u)\triangleq n/\left(\sum_{v\in V}R{({u},{v})}\right)
. According to (
1
), we can easily represent
C(u)
in terms of the diagonal elements of
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
as
C(u)={n}/\big(\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{L}^{\dagger}\big)+n\bm{L}^{\dagger}_{{u}{u}}\big)
.

II-E

Group Centrality and Its Maximization

To measure the importance of node groups, Li
et al.

[
29
]
extended the concept of CFCC to multiple nodes. In defining CFCC for a node group
S
, all nodes in
S
are assumed to be grounded, with their voltages being consistently at
0
. Therefore, we denote the voltage of node
v
when a unit current enters at node
u
and leaves at an arbitrary node in
S
as
\Phi_{u,S}(v)
. According to
[
36
]
, the resistance distance
R{({u},{S})}
between an individual node
u
and a grounded node group
S
is defined as
\Phi_{u,S}(u)=\big(\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\big)_{{u}{u}}
. Therefore, for an
n
-node graph, the CFCC of a node group
S
can be defined as
[
29
]
:

C(S)\triangleq\frac{n}{\sum_{u\in V}R{({u},{S})}}=\frac{n}{\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\big)}.

(3)

Equation (
3
) illustrates that a smaller sum of
R{({u},{S})}
indicates greater accessibility for a node
u\in V\setminus{S}
to nodes in
S
, suggesting that
S
is more significant in the network. This connection between accessibility and importance forms the basis for terming this measure as group centrality. Therefore, the problem of finding the most important node group with cardinality constraint is naturally given in
[
29
]
:

Definition II.2
(Current Flow Closeness Maximization, CFCM)

For an
n
-node graph
G=(V,E)
and an integer
k\ll n
, the goal is to find a node group
S^{*}
of size
k
that maximizes its CFCC. In other words,
S^{*}\triangleq\argmax\limits_{S\subset V,\left|S\right|=k}C(S)
.

II-F

State-of-the-art Method

Algorithm Design.

As a combinatorial optimization problem, CFCM is proved in
[
29
]
to be NP-hard. Therefore, designing a polynomial-time algorithm to solve CFCM is nearly impossible. However, the reciprocal of CFCC is also proved in
[
29
]
to be monotone and supermodular. These findings indicate that a naive greedy strategy yields a solution with an approximation factor of
1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}

[
37
]
. Set
S
is initially empty, then
k
nodes from
V\setminus{S}
are added to
S
iteratively. For the first iteration, the task is to find the node
u\in V
with minimum sum of resistance distances, which is expressed as

\sum_{v\in V}R{({u},{v})}=\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{L}^{\dagger}\big)+n\bm{L}^{\dagger}_{{u}{u}}.

(4)

For subsequent iterations, the node
u\in V\setminus{S}
with maximum marginal gain
\Delta(u,S)
is selected, which is expressed as

\small\Delta(u,S)\triangleq\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\big)-\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{L}_{-{(S+u)}}^{-1}\big)=\frac{\big(\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-2}\big)_{{u}{u}}}{\big(\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\big)_{{u}{u}}}\ (S\neq\varnothing).

(5)

Equations (
4
) and (
5
) indicate that computing the marginal gain involves calculating diagonal elements of
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
for the first iteration and diagonal elements of
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}
and
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-2}
for subsequent iterations. Leveraging the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma
[
38
]
, the state-of-the-art method
ApproxGreedy
transforms estimating diagonal elements of matrix inverse into solving linear equations related to the Laplacian matrix. Rather than directly solving linear equations,
ApproxGreedy
introduces a nearly linear-time Laplacian solver
[
39
]
.

Performance Discussion.

The key limitation of
ApproxGreedy
stems from its reliance on Laplacian solver. Each iteration requires solving
O(\epsilon^{-1}\log n)
linear equations, yielding a time complexity of
\tilde{O}(k\epsilon^{-3}m)
dominated by the number
m
of edges. This creates two fundamental bottlenecks. First, for dense graphs where
m=O(n^{2})
, the quadratic scaling becomes prohibitive. Second, even on sparse graphs,
ApproxGreedy
depends on highly optimized implementations of Laplacian solver. This dependency limits deployment in environments where computing resource is constrained or language flexibility is critical.

Our proposed algorithms address these limitations by replacing Laplacian solvers with other techniques. Through spanning forest sampling and improvements from Schur complement, the time complexity of our algorithms is nearly linear in the node number
n
. These approaches enable better scalability to denser graphs, as demonstrated in Section
V
.

III
Algorithm Based on Forest Sampling

In this section, we propose a greedy algorithm to approximately solve CFCM through sampling rooted spanning forests, addressing the challenge of estimating the marginal gain defined in (
4
) and (
5
).
This section proceeds as follows.
We first derive unbiased estimators for entries of
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}
via connections between spanning forests and electrical network analysis (Lemma
III.3
). Next, we approximate diagonal elements of
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-2}
by reducing its dimension (Lemma
III.4
). Additionally, we reformulate diagonal elements of
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
in terms of
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}
to overcome weak diagonal dominance (Lemma
III.5
). An adaptive sampling strategy using empirical Bernstein inequality is then introduced to optimize efficiency. Finally, these components culminate in the algorithm
ForestCFCM
, which achieves nearly linear complexity and rigorous approximation guarantees (Theorem
III.11
).

III-A

Estimation of Laplacian Submatrix Inverse

For an undirected graph
G
, let
N
denote the total number of spanning trees. Given two nodes
u,v
and an edge
\left(a,b\right)
, we denote
{N}_{u,v}^{{a}\to{b}}
as the number of spanning trees where the unique path from
u
to
v
traverses edge
\left(a,b\right)
from
a
to
b
. According to
[
40
]
, we have

Lemma III.1

In the corresponding electrical network of a graph
G
, suppose a unit current flows from
u
to
v
. The current through
\left(a,b\right)
is then given by
\frac{1}{N}\big({N}_{u,v}^{{a}\to{b}}-{N}_{u,v}^{{b}\to{a}}\big)
.

Since CFCC is defined for node groups, we subsequently extend Lemma
III.1
to the case of multiple target nodes. Similarly, we denote
\mathcal{N}(S)
as the total number of spanning forests with root set
S
, and let
{\mathcal{N}}_{u,S}^{{a}\to{b}}
denote the number of spanning forests where the unique path from
u
to an arbitrary node in
S
traverses edge
\left(a,b\right)
in the direction from
a
to
b
.

Lemma III.2

Let
G
be a graph with a node group
S
. For a unit current flowing from
u
to an arbitrary node in the grounded node group
S
, the current through
\left(a,b\right)
is given by
\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}(S)}\big({\mathcal{N}}_{u,S}^{{a}\to{b}}-{\mathcal{N}}_{u,S}^{{b}\to{a}}\big)
.

Proof:

To extend Lemma
III.1
to multiple targets, we map spanning forests in
G
to spanning trees in an augmented graph
G^{*}=(V\cup\{r^{*}\},E\cup\{\left(r^{*},s\right)|s\in S\})
. Each spanning forest of
G
rooted at
S
bijectively corresponds to a spanning tree of
G^{*}
rooted at
r^{*}
. Applying Lemma
III.1
to
G^{*}
with unit current from
u
to
r^{*}
, the current through edge
\left(a,b\right)
in
E
equals
\frac{1}{N}({N}_{u,r^{*}}^{{a}\to{b}}-{N}_{u,r^{*}}^{{b}\to{a}})
. Translating via the forest-tree correspondence yields
\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}(S)}({\mathcal{N}}_{u,S}^{{a}\to{b}}-{\mathcal{N}}_{u,S}^{{b}\to{a}})
for
G
, completing the proof.
∎

Given a graph
G
and a unit current with source node
u
and grounded target node group
S
, Lemma
III.2
enables us to approximate the current through any edge of
G
. Leveraging this, we can estimate the voltage of any node in
G
, yielding an unbiased estimator of
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{e}_{v}
.

Lemma III.3

Let
G
be a graph with a node group
S
. Suppose we sample
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(S)
spanning forests with root set
S
, and let
{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{u,S}^{{a}\to{b}}
denote the number of sampled forests where the unique path from
u
to an arbitrary node in
S
traverses edge
\left(a,b\right)
from
a
to
b
. Then the quantity

\overline{\Phi}_{u,S}(v)\triangleq\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(S)}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in\mathcal{P}_{{v},{S}}}\big({\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{u,S}^{{a}\to{b}}-{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{u,S}^{{b}\to{a}}\big)

is an unbiased estimator of
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{e}_{v}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{{v},{S}}
denotes edges on paths from
v
to an arbitrary node in
S
.

Proof:

According to
[
36
]
, the voltage
\Phi_{u,S}(v)
is equal to
\big(\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\big)_{{u}{v}}
, which also equals the sum of currents along any path from
v
to
S
by Kirchhoff’s law. Recall from Lemma
III.2
that
\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(S)}\big({\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{u,S}^{{a}\to{b}}-{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{u,S}^{{b}\to{a}}\big)
unbiasedly estimates the current in edge
\left(a,b\right)
, summing these estimators over
\mathcal{P}_{{v},{S}}
and applying linearity of expectation yields
\mathbb{E}\left[\overline{\Phi}_{u,S}(v)\right]=\Phi_{u,S}(v)
.
∎

III-B

Estimation of Diagonal Elements for Squared Laplacian Submatrix Inverse

Despite the relationship between
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}
and random spanning forests, there is no obvious physical meaning of
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-2}
. Therefore, we transform estimating the diagonal element
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-2}\bm{e}_{u}
into approximating the squared
2
-norm
\left\|\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}\right\|^{2}
. However, the time of directly computing one squared
2
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\Omega(n)
. To accelerate this evaluation, we introduce the Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma (JL Lemma)
[
38
]
.

Lemma III.4
(JL Lemma
[
38
]
)

Given
n
fixed vectors
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\dots,\bm{v}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
0<\epsilon<1
, let
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
be a matrix with each entry equal to
\pm k^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
with equal probability. If
k\geq 24\epsilon^{-2}\log n
, then
\left\|\bm{v}_{i}-\bm{v}_{j}\right\|^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}\left\|\bm{Q}\bm{v}_{i}-\bm{Q}\bm{v}_{j}\right\|^{2}
holds with probability at least
1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}
for all pairs
i,j\leq n
.

Lemma
III.4
indicates that the pairwise distances of vectors
\bm{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are almost preserved if we project them into a lower-dimensional space spanned by
O(\log n)
random vectors. Therefore, we can use this lemma to reduce the dimension of
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}
with bounded error. Concretely, we denote
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{w\times n}
as random matrix where
w\geq 24\epsilon^{-2}\log n
, then we have

\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-2}\bm{e}_{u}=\left\|\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}\right\|^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}\left\|\bm{W}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}\right\|^{2}.

(6)

Incorporating (
6
) into Lemma
III.3
, we transform estimating
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-2}\bm{e}_{u}
into solving linear equations
\bm{w}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}
for
1\leq i\leq w
. From this, we can further derive the unbiased estimator of
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-2}\bm{e}_{u}
. Specifically, the unbiased estimator for
\bm{w}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}
is defined as
\overline{\varPhi}_{\bm{w}_{i},S}(u)\triangleq\sum_{v\in V\setminus{S}}w_{iv}\overline{\Phi}_{v,S}(u)
, which is represented as

Lemma III.5

\small\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{e}_{u}=\begin{cases}\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}-\frac{2}{n}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\bm{1}&u\neq s\\

III-D

Adaptive Forest Sampling

After deriving an unbiased estimation of the marginal gain, we need to determine the upper bound of sample size to achieve an approximation factor for solving CFCM. Lemma
III.10
uses Hoeffding’s inequality to derive an upper bound. However, this inequality does not consider the variance of random variables, providing a loose theoretical bound. We thus resort to the empirical Bernstein inequality
[
42
]
:

Lemma III.6

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
be
n
real-valued i.i.d. random variables that satisfy
0\leq X_{i}\leq X_{\mathrm{sup}}
. If we denote
\bar{X}
and
X_{\mathrm{var}}
as the empirical mean and the empirical variance of
X_{i}
, then we have

\Pr\left(\left|\bar{X}-\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}]\right|\geq f\left(n,X_{\mathrm{var}},X_{\mathrm{sup}},\delta\right)\right)\leq\delta,

where
f\left(n,X_{\mathrm{var}},X_{\mathrm{sup}},\delta\right)\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{2X_{\mathrm{var}}\log\left(\nicefrac{{3}}{{\delta}}\right)}{n}}+\frac{3X_{\mathrm{sup}}\log\left(\nicefrac{{3}}{{\delta}}\right)}{n}
.

Lemma
III.6
differs from Hoeffding’s inequality in that it incorporates the empirical variance of random variables. While the empirical variance remains unknown a priori, it can be efficiently maintained throughout the sampling process. We introduce the empirical Bernstein inequality while retaining the Hoeffding bound to preserve the theoretical guarantee. Meanwhile, if the empirical error of estimators provided by Lemma
III.6
falls below the threshold parameter, we terminate sampling rooted spanning forests. Notably, the approximation factor provided in Theorem
III.11
remains unaffected by applying this adaptive strategy.

IV
Algorithm Based on Schur Complement

To address the scalability and accuracy limitations of
ForestCFCM
in large networks, we present an enhanced algorithm
SchurCFCM
.
The key motivation lies in leveraging the Schur complement to introduce an auxiliary root set
T
. By analyzing the Laplacian submatrix
\bm{L}_{-{S\cup T}}^{-1}
, we observe two advantages. First, the entrywise monotonicity of
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}

[
29
]
ensures reduced complexity of
RandomForest
. Second,
\bm{L}_{-{S\cup T}}^{-1}
exhibits stronger diagonal dominance compared to
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}
, which enhances approximation accuracy.

This section proceeds as follows. We first introduce the concept of Schur complement to improve sampling efficiency by leveraging higher-degree nodes in
T
(Lemma
IV.3
). Next, we establish connections between the Schur complement and rooted probabilities in spanning forests (Lemma
IV.2
), enabling unbiased estimation of the Schur complement. We then derive spectral sparsification guarantees (Lemma
IV.4
) to ensure accurate approximation with reduced complexity. Finally, we integrate these innovations into
SchurCFCM
, proving its complexity and approximation guarantee (Theorem
IV.7
).

IV-A

Schur Complement and Its Properties

Definition IV.1
(Schur complement)

For a square matrix
\bm{M}
, we can adjust its index order and rewrite
\bm{M}
in block form as
\bm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{M}_{{U}{U}}&\bm{M}_{{U}{T}}\\
\bm{M}_{{T}{U}}&\bm{M}_{{T}{T}}\end{pmatrix}
. The Schur complement of
\bm{M}
onto index subset
T
is then defined as
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{M})\triangleq\bm{M}_{{T}{T}}-\bm{M}_{{T}{U}}\bm{M}_{{U}{U}}^{-1}\bm{M}_{{U}{T}}
.

For an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with non-empty node subsets
S\subset V
and
T\subset V\setminus{S}
, we denote
V\setminus{(S\cup T)}
as
U
. Then
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}
can be represented as
[
50
]
:

\small\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{L}_{{U}{U}}^{-1}+\bm{F}\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}\bm{F}^{\top}&\bm{F}\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}\\
\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}\bm{F}^{\top}&\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

(11)

where
\bm{F}
denotes the matrix
-\bm{L}_{{U}{U}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{{U}{T}}
.

Equation (
11
) shows that the computation of
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}
can be transformed into calculations involving
\bm{L}_{{U}{U}}^{-1}
,
\bm{F}
, and
\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}
. While
\bm{L}_{{U}{U}}^{-1}
can be estimated by sampling spanning forests with root set
S\cup T
, we will demonstrate that
\bm{F}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
can also be represented by quantities related to random spanning forests.
First, we derive the connection between
\bm{F}
and rooted probability.

Lemma IV.2

For random spanning forests of graph
G
with root set
S\cup T
, let
\Pr\left(\rho_{u}={t}\right)
denote the probability that
u\in U
belongs to the tree whose root is
t\in T
. Then we have

\bm{F}_{{u}{t}}=-\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{{U}{U}}^{-1}\bm{L}_{{U}{T}}\bm{e}_{t}=\Pr\left(\rho_{u}={t}\right).

(12)

Proof:

Following the interpretation of absorbing random walk
[
51
]
,
\bm{F}_{{u}{t}}
equals the probability that a walk starting at
u
is absorbed at
t\in T
. In the initial round of loop-erased random walk from node
u
, the walk behaves identically to an absorbing random walk with target set
T
. By Wilson’s algorithm
[
31
]
, the sampling distribution is invariant to the order of source nodes, thus
\bm{F}_{{u}{t}}=\Pr\left(\rho_{u}={t}\right)
holds for all
u\in U
.
∎

Motivated by Lemma
IV.2
, we define an unbiased estimator of
\bm{F}_{{u}{t}}
. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(S\cup T)
be the number of sampled spanning forests with root set
S\cup T
, and
{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}\left(\rho_{u}={t}\right)
denote the number of these forests where node
u
is rooted at
t\in T
. Then
{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}}\left(\rho_{u}={t}\right)/\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(S\cup T)
serves as an unbiased estimator of
\bm{F}_{{u}{t}}
.

Next, we address the approximation of
\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|T\right|\times\left|T\right|}
. Given that
\left|T\right|\ll\left|V\right|
is still relatively small, we can efficiently compute the inverse matrix if
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
is obtained. To achieve this, we first provide a relationship between
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L})
and the Laplacian matrix of another graph, then extend this relationship to the case of
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
with non-empty node subset
T\subset V
, the Schur complement
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L})
is also the Laplacian matrix of a weighted graph denoted as
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)
, whose node set is precisely
T

[
52
]
. Regarding the Laplacian submatrix
\bm{L}_{-{S}}
, we establish a connection between
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
and
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle(S\cup T)}(G)
.

Lemma IV.3

For a graph
G=(V,E)
with non-empty node subsets
S\subset V
and
T\subset V\setminus{S}
, the Schur complement of
\bm{L}_{-{S}}
onto
T
is equivalent to the Laplacian submatrix of
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle(S\cup T)}(G)
:

\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})=\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle(S\cup T)}(\bm{L})\big)_{\scriptscriptstyle-S}.

Proof:

From
[
52
]
,
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle(S\cup T)}(\bm{L})
is the Laplacian matrix of weighted graph
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle(S\cup T)}(G)
, which can be rewritten as

\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle(S\cup T)}(\bm{L})=\bm{L}_{{(S\cup T)}{(S\cup T)}}-\bm{L}_{{(S\cup T)}{U}}\bm{L}^{-1}_{{U}{U}}\bm{L}_{{U}{(S\cup T)}}.

(13)

Additionally,
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
can also be represented as

\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})=\bm{L}_{{T}{T}}-\bm{L}_{{T}{U}}\bm{L}^{-1}_{{U}{U}}\bm{L}_{{U}{T}}.

(14)

Combining (
13
) with (
14
), we observe that
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
can be obtained from
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle(S\cup T)}(\bm{L})
by removing elements with row indices or column indices in set
S
, completing the proof.
∎

According to Definition
IV.1
,
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
can also be represented as
\bm{L}_{{T}{T}}+\bm{L}_{{T}{U}}\bm{F}
. Combining this expression with Lemma
IV.2
, the entry
(i,j)
of
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
can be rewritten as

\small\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\bm{e}_{j}=\begin{cases}\bm{L}_{{i}{i}}-\sum_{\left(u,i\right)\in E}\Pr\left(\rho_{u}={i}\right)&i=j\\
\bm{L}_{{i}{j}}-\sum_{\left(u,i\right)\in E}\Pr\left(\rho_{u}={j}\right)&i\neq j\end{cases}.

(15)

By integrating Lemma
IV.2
with (
11
) and (
15
), we finally demonstrate that
\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}
can be estimated by sampling spanning forests with root set
S\cup T
, incorporating the additional approximation of rooted probabilities. Lemma
III.7
suggests that replacing root set
S
with
S\cup T
enhances the efficiency of forest sampling.

Lemma IV.4

Let
{\tilde{G}}^{(i)}
denote the

\left(a,b\right)
in
{\tilde{G}}^{(i)}

is less than

, then
\tilde{G}
is an
\epsilon
-spectral sparsifier of
G
with high probability. In other words,
\bm{x}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{L}}\bm{x}\approx_{\epsilon}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{L}\bm{x}
holds for any real vector
\bm{x}
, where
\tilde{\bm{L}}
and
\bm{L}
are Laplacian matrices of
\tilde{G}
and

, respectively.

Next, the relative error guarantee of

Lemma IV.5

For an undirected graph
G
and the error parameter

, if the sample size

satisfies

r\geq 8(\nicefrac{{\epsilon}}{{15}})^{-2}\tau^{2}d_{\max}^{2\tau+2}(S\cup T)\log(2n),

(16)

for

with probability of

.

Proof:

We establish the relative error guarantee through three key steps.

Step 1: Spectral Sparsification of Schur Complement.

From Lemma
IV.4
, an
\nicefrac{{\epsilon}}{{15}}
-spectral sparsifier
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
of
\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})
can be constructed with high probability when the sample size satisfies (
16
). This follows because:
(i) Edge weights in random subgraphs are bounded by
\frac{d_{\max}(S\cup T)}{r}
via (
15

(ii) Maximum resistance distance

ensures that edge weights meet the condition
\frac{(\nicefrac{{\epsilon}}{{15}})^{2}}{R{({a},{b})}\log n}\geq\frac{d_{\max}(S\cup T)}{r}
.

Step 2: Denominator Estimation.

Equation (
11
) decomposes the denominator
\big(\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\big)_{{u}{u}}
into three terms:.

\displaystyle\big(\bm{L}_{-{S}}^{-1}\big)_{{u}{u}}=

\displaystyle\big(\bm{L}_{{U}{U}}^{-1}\big)_{{u}{u}}+\big(\bm{F}\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}\bm{F}^{\top}\big)_{{u}{u}}

\displaystyle+\big(\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}\big)_{{u}{u}}.

For the first term, the sample size satisfying (
16
) yields
\overline{\Phi}_{u,S\cup T}(u)\approx_{\epsilon/3}\big(\bm{L}_{{U}{U}}^{-1}\big)_{{u}{u}}
by Lemma
III.10
. For the second term, let

if
u

III.8

\big(\tilde{\bm{F}}\big(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}\tilde{\bm{F}}^{\top}\big)_{{u}{u}}\approx_{\nicefrac{{\epsilon}}{{3}}}\big(\bm{F}\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}\bm{F}^{\top}\big)_{{u}{u}}.

The third term directly follows from Lemma

Step 3: Numerator Estimation.

The numerator

\bm{F}\big(\mathcal{S}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(\bm{L}_{-{S}})\big)^{-1}

Environment.

All experiments are conducted on a Linux server equipped with a 72-core 2.1GHz CPU and 256GB of RAM. We implement
ApproxGreedy
in Julia, maintaining consistency with
[
29
]
, which relies on a Julia-based Laplacian solver
[
39
]
. Our proposed algorithms and other baseline methods are implemented in C++. Given that all algorithms are pleasingly parallelizable, we execute each experiment using 72 threads.

Baselines and Parameters.

To demonstrate the superiority of our algorithms, we first implement the state-of-the-art method
ApproxGreedy

[
29
]
as a baseline. Since this method also utilizes Lemma
III.4
, we set the error parameter
\epsilon
to be
0.2
, matching
ForestCFCM
and
SchurCFCM
. We also implement the greedy algorithm
Exact
, which calculates
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
and
\Delta(u,S)
through matrix inversion. To ensure that our greedy algorithms outperform other heuristic strategies in solving CFCM effectively, we implement two additional heuristic methods:
Degree
and
Top-CFCC
.
Degree
selects
k
nodes with the largest degrees, while
Top-CFCC
selects top-
k
nodes with the largest CFCC of single nodes.

While the nodes of additional root set
T
in
SchurCFCM
are selected according to maximum degree, we also need to determine the size of additional root set
T
. When
\left|T\right|
becomes larger, the time of directly inverting the Schur complement grows cubically. When
\left|T\right|
becomes smaller, the upper bound of
SchurDelta
increases due to the growing
d_{\max}(T)
. Therefore, we attempt to reach a balance between these two factors, setting the size as
\left|T^{*}\right|=\argmin_{\left|T\right|}\left\{\big|\left|T\right|-d_{\max}(T)\big|\right\}
. Table
II
presents the value of
\left|T^{*}\right|
for each tested graph, which is very small due to the scale-free property of real-world graphs.

V-B

Results on Real-world Networks

V-B
1
Efficiency

We first evaluate the efficiency of our algorithms. For each graph, we solve CFCM with cardinality constraint
k=\left|S\right|=20
. Table
II
reports the running times of our algorithms and baselines. We present results for both of our algorithms with
\epsilon\in\left\{0.3,0.2,0.15\right\}
. Note that
Exact
is infeasible for medium-scale graphs due to time-consuming matrix inversion, while
ApproxGreedy
is infeasible for large-scale graphs, as its running time exceeds 27 hour.

Table
II
shows that for every real-world graph, both of our algorithms outperform all the baselines in efficiency. Notably, the speed-up of our algorithms over
ApproxGreedy
is more pronounced on denser graphs, such as
buzznet
and
Actor
. This observation aligns with our earlier theoretical analysis that the time complexity of
ApproxGreedy
is dominated by number of edges rather than number of nodes.

Meanwhile, although both of our algorithms share the same theoretical upper bound of time complexity, the running time of
SchurCFCM
is always lower than that of
ForestCFCM
. This advantage lies in the introduction of Schur complement, letting
SchurCFCM
sample spanning forests with more root nodes. During the sampling process, the random walker terminates once hitting the former trajectories. As
RandomForest
is the most time-consuming part of
ForestCFCM
and
SchurCFCM
, the walker of
SchurCFCM
is easier to terminate, significantly reducing running time.

, which may be attributed to the inaccuracy of Laplacian solver for small graphs.

VI
Related Work

Computation of resistance distance.

As a fundamental metric, resistance distance has found wide applications in the data management community, such as recommendation systems
[
60
]
and graph embedding systems
[
13
]
. Numerous algorithms have been proposed to compute resistance distance efficiently
[
17
,
18
,
61
,
62
]
. However, these approaches are based on the interpretations of resistance distance expressed in (
2
), rather than the marginal gain represented in (
5
). Therefore, existing methods for computing resistance distance cannot be directly applied to solving CFCM.

Other node selection problems.

There exist various importance measures of a group of nodes, based on graph structure
[
63
]
or dynamic processes
[
64
]
. As the criteria for identifying crucial node groups are application-dependent
[
65
]
, many previous studies have focused on selecting
k
nodes to optimize related quantities. Recently, influence maximization (IM) has garnered wide interest, with information spread as the optimized quantity. However, the predominant processes for information diffusion are independent cascade
[
66
,
5
,
6
,
67
]
and linear threshold
[
68
,
69
]
, both of which differ greatly from the electrical network model. Consequently, existing approaches for IM cannot be transferred to solving CFCM.

Discussion of edge selection problems.

Beyond node selection, significant efforts have been devoted to the problem of adding
k
edges connecting to a given node to maximize its centrality. This problem has been studied via diverse centrality measures, including betweenness centrality
[
70
]
, information centrality
[
71
]
and absorbing random-walk centrality
[
72
]
. In contrast to single-node scenarios, the problem of maximizing the centrality of a node group by adding
k
edges connecting nodes within the group has been studied for only a few centralities, such as group betweenness
[
73
]
. Previous works have not solved the edge selection problem for maximizing CFCC, which presents an opportunity for future research.

VII
Conclusion

For real-world graphs with
n
nodes, we developed two greedy algorithms to approximately maximize the current flow closeness centrality of node groups with cardinality constraint
k\ll n
. Our first algorithm,
ForestCFCM
, is based on spanning forest sampling and leverages an adaptive sampling technique.
ForestCFCM
exhibits a nearly-linear time complexity with respect to
n
, outperforming the state-of-the-art method. To further improve efficiency and effectiveness, we proposed our second algorithm
SchurCFCM
, which additionally utilizes the Schur complement. Both of our greedy algorithms achieve a guaranteed approximation factor of
1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}-\epsilon
for any error parameter
0<\epsilon<1
. Numerical results demonstrate that both algorithms are significantly faster than the state-of-the-art method, scaling to real-world graphs with 3 million nodes. Notably,
SchurCFCM
outperforms all other approaches in terms of both efficiency and effectiveness.
