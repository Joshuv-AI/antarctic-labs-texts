Key characteristics of medical parole applications in Massachusetts in 2022–2023
Authors: Michelle Suh, Samantha Chao, Kristin Shanabrook, Jasdeep S. Kler, Nicole Mushero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The United States has a rapidly aging prison population with complex medical comorbidities. Medical parole has been proposed as a mechanism to address the heavy medical burden posed by the aging and chronically ill prison population. Current studies demonstrate medical parole is underutilized, but little is known about the factors considered when rendering a medical parole decision. The aim of this study was to describe the characteristics of medical parole applications in Massachusetts and identify predictors of the final parole decision. We conducted a retrospective review of 31 applications submitted by a non-profit legal services organization between 2022 and 2023. Univariate analysis demonstrated that a supportive superintendent recommendation and prison-contracted clinician assessment that an applicant could not perform ADLs independently were statistically significant factors in approval of medical parole. Unexpectedly, a history of disciplinary action while incarcerated was also associated with a higher likelihood of application approval, suggesting prior disciplinary action does not decrease the chances of a successful parole application. Our study suggests the superintendent and prison medical clinicians are key stakeholders with influence on the likelihood of approval of a medical parole application. Further work is needed to assess parole board decision-making when evaluating applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24358-0.

Introduction

The United States has one of the largest systems of incarceration in the world with almost 2 million people incarcerated in jails and prisons1. Due to the phenomenon of accelerated aging, older people experiencing incarceration, defined as 50 or 55 years and above, are aging at a faster pace than their non-incarcerated peers2–5. Over the past 30 years alone, there has been a five-fold increase in the number of older incarcerated adults while the non-incarcerated US population 55 years or older increased by only 50%5,6. Europe has mirrored this demographic trend as well, with almost 20% of the incarcerated population being 50 and older7. Despite complex medical conditions, older incarcerated adults often face barriers accessing healthcare and adapting to the carceral environment8–11.

Proposed as a potential mechanism to relieve carceral facilities of the burden of advanced illness in older incarcerated adults, medical parole (often called compassionate release) describes the release of people who are incarcerated due to terminal illness or permanent incapacitation. The implementation of medical parole policies has been varied with individual states and the federal prison system all having unique eligibility criteria and application processes12–14. In Massachusetts, medical parole legislation requires that eligible applicants have a “debilitating condition that appears incurable, that will likely cause the death of the inmate in not more than eighteen (18) months or a debilitating physical or cognitive incapacitation that appears irreversible as determined by the Department’s medical provider” (G.L. c. 127 § 119 A). Additionally, their release must not “be incompatible with the welfare of society” and they must be able to “live and remain at liberty without violating the law” after release. People who are incarcerated or someone on their behalf can submit a petition for medical parole15. Upon receipt of a medical parole petition, the superintendent or sheriff has 21 days to provide the Massachusetts Department of Correction (MADOC) commissioner with (1) a recommendation regarding release, (2) a plan for medical parole, (3) a written diagnosis by a healthcare professional, and (4) a violence risk assessment of the applicant. The commissioner then has 45 days to render a decision.

Medical parole is seldom granted in the United States. On a federal level, the US Department of Justice Office of the Inspector General reported problematic underutilization of the Federal Bureau of Prisons’ (BOP) compassionate release policies16. States also have not seen large-scale releases via medical parole17–21. In Massachusetts, only 101 incarcerated adults have been granted parole as of June 2024 since the passage of legislation in 2018, despite an annual population of almost 6,000 older incarcerated adults, one of the oldest incarcerated populations in the country22–24. A review of MADOC deaths in custody from January 2021 to 2023 found 87 deaths, 92% of which were natural causes and almost half of which were from chronic conditions25.

Research has focused on barriers to initiating the medical parole process. Studies have shown lack of awareness of medical parole among people who are incarcerated, lack of resources to apply, and delays in reviewing applications13,26. However, while there are clear barriers to initiating the medical parole application process, little is known about the factors considered when rendering a decision once an application is being reviewed. Our study sought to review MADOC medical parole applications and analyze what factors may be associated with the medical parole decision. Insight into factors that contribute to application decisions may enhance the identification of eligible applicants and highlight information in an application critical to rendering a decision.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study analyzed medical parole applications submitted by a non-profit legal services organization in Massachusetts to examine factors associated with medical parole decisions. The final dataset of applications submitted between January 1, 2022 and December 31, 2023 included comprehensive applicant information, including demographics, legal history, disciplinary history, health status, administrative details, the decision rendered with its justification, and supplemental documentation cited to support both the application and decision (which included, but was not limited to, medical records, opinions from government officials and clinicians, and sometimes personal letters from individuals connected to the applicant or their victims). Similar supplemental information and decisions rendered for appeals were included if applicable. Data were de-identified, and three team members abstracted variables from each official medical parole application (MS, SC, KS); disagreements were resolved via consensus discussions. The study was found exempt by the Boston University Institutional Review Board (IRB). Methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed consent was waived by the IRB.

Study population

The study population was people who were incarcerated in MADOC facilities who submitted a medical parole application through the legal services organization. The unit of analysis was a single parole applicant, as some applications also included an appeal.

Variables and measurements

The primary outcome was the final decision on the medical parole application. This was a three-level categorical variable: approved, denied, or died before decision. In situations with appeals, applications were coded with the final decision.

To facilitate analysis, several variables were derived, including the time from application to final decision. Categorical variables with numerous levels, such as medical diagnosis and primary sentence were recoded into broader categories to protect patient confidentiality, ensure adequate statistical power, and aid interpretation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Software, Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Descriptive statistics for the cohort were generated for all variables. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables while means, standard deviations (SD), and ranges were calculated for continuous variables. A correlation matrix was generated to explore the bivariate relationships between all key continuous and categorical variables (Appendix 1). The categorical variables were converted into numeric dummy variables to allow for their inclusion in the matrix. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated for all paired variables to assess the strength and direction of these relationships.

In addition to the correlation analysis, Pearson’s chi-square tests were used for categorical variables and t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables. To examine the factors influencing a definitive parole board decision, we conducted a series of univariate logistic regression models on a subset that included only applicants with a final status of “approved” or “denied.” This approach allowed for the examination of the individual association between each predictor variable and the binary outcome (approved vs. denied) without adjusting for other factors. Odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the strength and direction of these associations. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to the exploratory nature of this analysis, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons.

Results

Cohort demographics

A total of 31 medical parole applications were analyzed. The mean age of applicants was 71.1 years (SD = 10.7), and they had served an average of 26.4 years (SD = 18.3) at the time of application. The cohort was predominantly male (86.7%). Based on the provided race/ethnicity data, 37% of applicants were White, 33.3% were Black, 14.8% were Hispanic, and 29.6% were other (including Asian, Native American or Alaska Native, Middle Eastern, Hispanic and Latino, and White and Hispanic). In contrast, the annual MADOC population is 36% White, 31% Black or African American, 30% Hispanic, 2% Asian or Pacific Islander, and <−1% American Indian or Native Alaskan. The overall MADOC population is 97% male with an average age of 44 years compared to 42 years for the female population27. In terms of application outcome, 14 (45.2%) were approved, 10 (32.3%) were denied, and 7 (22.6%) applicants died before a decision was rendered. These results are summarized in Table 1.

Cohort characteristics

The primary reason used to apply for medical parole was permanent incapacitation (70.0%); 13.3% of applications cited terminal illness, and 16.7% of applications used both as a justification. The medical diagnosis cited in the majority of applications (n = 14) was a neurological disorder, including dementia, cognitive impairment, stroke, and traumatic brain injury. The second most cited medical diagnosis was cancer (29.0%). The third most cited medical disease was a chronic disease or a combination of diseases (25.8%). Examples include cardiovascular conditions, rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis, and neurofibromatosis. The parole plan for the majority of applicants involved developing a plan with the help of the MADOC or the Veterans Affairs (VA) (56.6%). Most applicants (56.7%) were anticipated to require placement in a facility if granted medical parole. The average time between the submission date of an application and final decision was 4.2 months (SD = 5.9). Ten applicants (32.3%) appealed their final decision with some applicants going through the appeals process up to three times.

In terms of applicants’ health and functional status, 40% were being housed in their facility’s health services unit; 77.8% were hospitalized within the last year; and 93.3% were not actively hospitalized in the MADOC-affiliated hospital. For six applicants (24.0%), their application decision cited the fact that they had declined recommended therapy or treatment for their primary medical diagnosis. Most applicants used an assistive walking device or wheelchair (75.0%) or required some form of helper, aide, or supervision (84.6%). When an external clinician retained by the legal services organization assessed the applicants’ ability to perform ADLs, the external clinician determined the applicant could not perform ADLs independently in all cases. In contrast, the MADOC-contracted clinician determined the applicant could not perform ADLs independently in 20% of cases where this assessment was available. Interrater reliability between the external clinician and MADOC-contracted clinician in assessing applicant ability to perform ADLs was poor, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0. Only six applicants (24.0%) were determined to have a prognosis of 18 months or less by the MADOC-contracted clinician.

The primary crimes for applicants were all felony offenses, including first-degree and second-degree murder, rape, rape of a child, and drug-related offenses. Forty-three percent of applicants were serving time for multiple concomitant sentences. The majority of applicants were serving life sentences; 31.0% had the possibility of parole, and 41.4% did not have the possibility of parole. Nearly half (48.2%) had previously been incarcerated. Most (83.8%) had experienced disciplinary action while incarcerated. A majority of applicants held employment while incarcerated (80.0%) or participated in programming (80.0%).

The superintendent opposed the majority of applications for medical parole (70.8%). The district attorney also opposed a majority of applications (65.4%). In seven (26.9%) cases, the district attorney neither supported or opposed the application and cited not having enough information to make a clear determination. A COMPAS score was available in 16 cases, and the majority (93.8%) of these applicants had a low score indicating a low risk of recidivism. A TCU Drug Screen score was available in 20 cases, and most of these applicants (70.0%) had a low substance use risk score.

Finally, 10 applicants were described as disagreeing with the official version of the crime in the medical parole decision letter. In eight cases, the victim or family of the victim submitted a statement regarding the impact releasing the applicant would have on them. Fourteen applications cited the applicant’s religious faith. Factors associated with medical parole decision

The univariate logistic regression analysis, conducted on the subset of applications with a final decision of approved or denied, revealed three factors significantly associated with the outcome. The following predictors showed a statistically significant association with the likelihood of a medical parole application being approved: superintendent recommendation, functional status, and disciplinary action.

The superintendent’s recommendation was strongly associated with the parole outcome (LR Chi-Sq = 11.2, p = 0.0008). The assessment of an applicant’s inability to perform ADLs by the MADOC-contracted clinician was also significantly associated with application outcome (LR Chi-Sq = 6.76, p = 0.0093). By contrast, the ADL assessment performed by an external physician retained by the legal services organization submitting the application was not significantly associated with the outcome. Notably, the interrater agreement between the MADOC-contracted clinician and the external physician was very low with a Cohen’s kappa of 0. In all cases for which a final decision was rendered, the external clinician reported that the applicant was unable to perform some or all ADLS, whereas the MADOC-contracted clinician made this determination in only five cases.

A history of disciplinary action while incarcerated was also significantly associated with the application outcome (LR Chi-Sq = 5.31, p = 0.0212). The number of disciplinary actions and attempted appeal were not significantly associated with likelihood of approval.

Discussion

Our study of 31 medical parole applications submitted to MADOC during the 2022–2023 years found that the superintendent recommendation, the applicant’s inability to perform ADLs, and the applicant’s history of disciplinary action were the only factors with a statistically significant correlation to the likelihood of medical parole being granted. Almost a quarter of applicants in our study died before a decision was rendered, highlighting the need for expedited review mechanisms. Our findings suggest several potential mechanisms to increase medical parole applications and future directions to elucidate medical parole eligibility.

Our data suggests the superintendent’s recommendation is associated with the ultimate outcome of the parole decision. This may be because the superintendent of the facility is a trusted colleague of the commissioner, has more familiarity with the applicant, and is perceived to have more accurate understanding of the applicant’s medical and functional status and/or the risk they present on release. Future work could investigate reasons why the superintendent may be a key stakeholder, factors that contribute to their assessment and recommendation of medical parole, and potential implementation strategies involving their office to improve utilization of medical parole.

The MADOC-contracted clinician’s assessment of an applicant’s ability to perform ADLs also appeared to be associated with the final medical parole decision. Having deficits in multiple ADLs is likely interpreted as being more “incapacitated” and less of a public safety threat on release, as per the terms of the medical parole statute. It is common practice to retain an external expert’s medical opinion when petitioning for parole. However, our study found poor interrater reliability between the MADOC-contracted clinician and the external expert in assessing an applicant’s ability to perform ADLs. Furthermore, the external expert’s assessment itself had no significant association with the application’s final outcome. This suggests the commissioner puts greater validity into the findings of MADOC-contracted staff, underscores the subjectivity of parole decisions, and calls into question the utility of an external expert opinion in a medical parole application. External experts are often limited in their ability to evaluate the applicant in person and may rely solely on medical record review to formulate an assessment; in contrast, MADOC-contracted clinicians may be perceived as having more longitudinal relationships with applicant, although this is variable in reality. Dual loyalty or biases may potentially influence MADOC-contracted clinicians to be more conservative in their assessments28. Further work is needed to assess discrepancies between the assessment of MADOC-contracted clinicians and external clinicians and the impact on an individual’s medical parole application, as well as how to standardize assessments of ability to perform ADLs to improve interrater reliability. Ultimately, our study highlights the need to explore the perspectives of carceral staff and healthcare workers (HCWs) as successful implementation strategies likely rely on their buy-in. For example, carceral HCWs may be able to improve access to medical parole by screening for difficulty with ADLs and other eligibility criteria and referring these individuals to begin the application for medical parole.

Although the Massachusetts medical parole criteria include risk to public safety, requiring that the petitioner’s release must not “be incompatible with the welfare of society” and the older incarcerated adults must be able to “live and remain at liberty without violating the law,”15 it is unclear how risk is assessed. Our results showed those with disciplinary histories were more likely to have their application approved. This may correlate with the number of years an applicant had served as those who had been incarcerated longer have a higher likelihood of incurring disciplinary issues. This may also be due to the small number of those without disciplinary history (only three of 31 petitioners), but further investigation is needed. Furthermore, the other risk assessments included in the applications were also statistically insignificant. Our study found no correlation between petition decision and the length of sentence, history of primary incarceration, years served, or the MADOC’s own internal recidivism risk calculator. Future work is needed to determine how risk is assessed in medical parole applications, especially as this was routinely cited in denial letters.

There are several limitations to our findings. First, given the idiosyncrasies of each state’s medical parole policies, our study may not be generalizable to other states. Second, our dataset was limited to MADOC medical parole applications filed through the non-profit legal organization and some information for applicants was not available or incomplete. Additionally, overall rates of medical parole success in MADOC in 2022 and 2023 were 25% and 28%, respectively, indicating that our sample contains a higher concentration of successful petitions than on average22. Third, we were unable to report the relationship between primary crime and application; although we did find a statistically significant relationship, we lacked enough samples to clarify the relationship. Further work is needed to confirm similar significance of variables in applications filed by other sources, such as the incarcerated person themselves or their family members. Finally, correlation between petition results and statistically significant factors does not necessarily indicate that the factors determined the outcome of the applications. For instance, although the superintendent’s recommendation closely correlated with the parole outcome, the decision-making of the commissioner has not been directly explored. This is a preliminary study, and further research is needed.

There are several directions for future study based on our findings. Examining the perspective of key stakeholders, including the commissioner, superintendent, carceral staff and clinicians may elucidate their decision-making regarding medical parole. Given the low risk of recidivism for older offenders, even for people initially imprisoned for violent offenses29, we recommend investigating the use of current methods of risk stratification given the lack of meaningful relationship with medical parole decision. Additionally, the use of medical prognosis is a controversial criterion that needs further research to determine its utility. There are current calls to reconsider the reliance of prognostication given the evidence of physicians’ limited ability and tendency to overestimate. Our study found that the MADOC-contracted clinician’s assessment of a prognosis less than 18 months was not associated with the application outcome despite being an eligibility criterion per the letter of the law30,31.

The need for medical parole as a mechanism for addressing the heavy medical burden of a rapidly aging prison population is increasing, yet it remains underutilized in the United States. Our study sought to elucidate the salient factors in the review of medical parole applications in Massachusetts and found that the superintendent recommendation, the applicant’s inability to perform ADLs, and the applicant’s history of disciplinary action were statistically important. Further research is needed to clarify the decision-making of medical parole boards and potential interventions to increase adoption of medical parole.
