Response to letter regarding “Development and internal validation of a prognostic nomogram for poor outcomes in neonatal bacterial meningitis”
Authors: Min Yu, Yiping Fang, Xiaofeng Yang
Published: 2026

We thank Dr. Finsterer for his interest in our article (1) and for the thoughtful comments regarding our prognostic nomogram for neonatal bacterial meningitis (NBM) (2). Below, we provide a point-by-point response to the issues raised.

First, regarding the retrospective study design. Dr. Finsterer correctly notes the inherent limitations of retrospective studies compared with prospective trials (3). We fully acknowledge this limitation, which we explicitly stated in our manuscript. However, given the relatively low incidence of NBM and the ethical constraints of prospective observational studies that would require withholding standard care, well-designed retrospective cohorts remain a valuable source of real-world evidence. To mitigate bias, we employed Firth penalized logistic regression specifically designed to reduce small-sample bias, combined with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-based variable selection and 1,000 bootstrap internal validations. The resulting optimism-corrected concordance index (C-index) of 0.77 indicates satisfactory model stability despite the retrospective design. We agree that prospective external validation is the necessary next step and explicitly called for it in our conclusions.

Second, regarding the “missing” clinical parameters such as headache, meningismus, and photophobia. We respectfully contend that this criticism overlooks a fundamental characteristic of our study population: neonates. Unlike adults or older children, newborns cannot verbally express headache or photophobia, and classic meningeal signs (e.g., nuchal rigidity, Kernig’s and Brudzinski’s signs) are notoriously unreliable and nonspecific in the neonatal period due to immature neurological development and poor neck muscle tone. Therefore, we deliberately focused on objective indicators—seizures, lethargy, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers—which are the most clinically meaningful and feasible bedside parameters for this age group. While immunological and nutritional status are undoubtedly relevant, they are not routinely available at the time of admission, which limits their utility for early risk stratification.

Third, regarding the inclusion of “probable” (microbiologically unconfirmed) cases. We agree that diagnostic certainty is desirable, but excluding culture-negative cases would introduce substantial selection bias in real-world neonatal practice. As many as half of neonates with bacterial meningitis receive empirical antibiotics prior to lumbar puncture, resulting in negative cultures despite true infection. Excluding these cases would not only severely reduce statistical power (from 214 to 103 cases) but also limit the generalizability of our model to routine clinical settings. Importantly, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted exclusively to confirmed cases [Tab. S5, derived from our original article (1)], which yielded consistent effect estimates and supported the robustness of our primary model. We believe this two-pronged approach—primary analysis including probable cases plus sensitivity analysis in confirmed cases—best balances clinical applicability with methodological rigor.

Fourth, regarding the source of infection (e.g., pneumonia, sepsis). We agree that the primary infectious source is clinically relevant. However, our model was designed for early risk stratification at the time of admission, when the precise source of infection is often not yet confirmed. Including such a variable would render the model unusable in the critical early decision-making window. Furthermore, in our multivariable analysis, we included “assisted ventilation” and “positive blood culture”, which partially reflect systemic infection severity. We also performed pathogen-stratified univariable analyses; while pathogen type (e.g., Escherichia coli, Group B Streptococcus) was associated with outcomes, it was not retained in the LASSO-selected multivariable model, suggesting that its prognostic contribution was largely captured by the CSF biomarkers (protein and glucose) included in the final nomogram.

Fifth, regarding treatment efficacy and antibiotic appropriateness. Dr. Finsterer raises an important point about pathogen-directed therapy. In our cohort, the most frequently isolated pathogens were Escherichia coli and Group B Streptococcus, which are reliably covered by standard empirical regimens (ampicillin plus gentamicin or a third-generation cephalosporin) recommended for neonatal meningitis. Importantly, our study endpoint—“poor outcome”—was determined at discharge and largely reflects the severity of the host inflammatory response and blood-brain barrier disruption induced by the infection. Even with appropriate antibiotics, irreversible neurological injury may occur due to excessive inflammation, which is precisely what our CSF biomarkers (elevated protein and reduced glucose) capture. Therefore, we believe that treatment variables, while important for quality-of-care assessment, are conceptually distinct from baseline prognostic factors intended for early risk stratification.

In conclusion, while we appreciate Dr. Finsterer’s theoretical considerations, our responses clarify that these concerns either do not apply to the neonatal population or were already addressed through rigorous sensitivity analyses. Our nomogram serves as a bedside-accessible, baseline risk stratification tool to assist neonatologists in early clinical decision-making. We hope that future multicenter prospective studies will incorporate additional variables to further refine prognostic accuracy.
