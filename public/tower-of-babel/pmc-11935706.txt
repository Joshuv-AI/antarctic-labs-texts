A Window of Opportunity: Leveraging Lung Ultrasound to Enhance Prognostication After ST‐Segment–Elevation Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Amer Abdulla, Muhammad Raja, Joseph Allencherril
Published: 2024

Point‐of‐care (POC) lung ultrasound (LUS) is an effective, easy‐to‐perform, and rapid tool for the detection of subclinical pulmonary edema. This condition is identified by the presence of B‐lines, which are echogenic lines extending from the pleural line into the lung tissue. 1 Heart failure is a known complication of ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), with increasing severity portending a worse prognosis. Several risk scores have been developed to stratify and prognosticate cases of acute coronary syndrome (ACS). The GRACE (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events) risk score quantifies risk of death and myocardial infarction in patients with ACS, 2 incorporating the Killip classification, a physical examination–based assessment of severity of heart failure in patients with ACS. 3 LUS has the power to identify subclinical pulmonary edema, particularly in patients with Killip class I or those with no apparent clinical findings of congestion. Several studies have established the role of LUS in enhancing prognostication for major adverse cardiovascular events in the index hospitalization or in the short term for patients presenting with STEMI. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Few studies have examined the utility of LUS for assessing long‐term outcomes in patients with STEMI.

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Carreras‐Mora and colleagues demonstrated that LUS significantly enhanced prognostication of patients with STEMI at 1 year and that LUS enhanced the accuracy of the GRACE score. 10 In this multicenter, prospective cohort study, 373 patients who presented with STEMI underwent an 8‐field LUS within the first 24 hours of presentation after angiography. Two separate LUS‐based scores were derived for each patient: categorization as being “wet” or “dry” (wet defined as at least one field with three or more B‐lines) or a continuous, total sum of B‐lines in all lung fields, with a score of 0 to 3 for each field for a total possible score of 0 to 24. The primary end point was a composite of all‐cause mortality, hospitalization for heart failure, ACS, or stroke within 1 year. Multivariate analysis revealed the LUS score to be an independent predictor of the primary end point, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.06 (95% CI, 1.01–1.10; P=0.009) for each additional B line. Among patients with Killip class I, the categorical classification of wet or dry lungs was an independent predictor of the primary end point, with an HR of 3.12 (95% CI, 1.34–7.31; P=0.009). LUS enhanced the GRACE score with a net reclassification index of 31.6% and an increase in the area under the curve from 0.705 for GRACE alone versus 0.791 for GRACE plus LUS (P=0.0020).

While use of LUS can be learned even after formal medical training has ended, developing the skills and habit of routine use during clinical workflow is challenging and may still pose a limitation to widespread adoption. Only 382 patients of the 1089 patients with STEMI admitted among the three study sites ultimately underwent LUS, primarily because of a lack of availability of independent and expert LUS operators. There is a need for standardization in POC ultrasound imaging protocols as opposed to “ad hoc” approaches to image acquisition. This study confirms the utility of a simple, convenient, and practical protocol to rapidly distinguish wet and dry lungs. Eight‐zone LUS can be rapidly learned and performed in approximately 3 minutes as opposed to the 28‐zone protocol used in other studies. 1 While interoperator variability of LUS findings in the real world may affect the reliability and reproducibility of image acquisition and interpretation, interobserver variability appeared low when comparing LUS operator and offline investigator analyses. The study's exclusion of certain high‐risk patient populations, such as patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis, patients with severe lung disease, and those who survived cardiac arrest, limits the generalized applicability of the findings, especially regarding critically ill patients. However, it is likely that such populations presenting with STEMI inherently have poor prognoses; thus, LUS may be less effective in reclassification of long‐term prognostic risk, as dry lungs are infrequently observed in patients with higher Killip classes and are more likely to be seen in such critically ill patients. The work of Carreras‐Mora and colleagues corroborates that existing literature underscoring pulmonary congestion as a common finding in patients hospitalized with acute coronary syndrome, correlating with long‐term adverse outcomes. 6 While previous work 7 has demonstrated the value of LUS in augmenting the Killip classification of patients with STEMI, this appears to be the first study, to our knowledge, to use LUS to enhance the predictive value of the GRACE score in this population and to provide prognostic data 1 year following STEMI.

The ongoing development and release of newer, quicker, lighter, and less expensive POC ultrasound probes heralds a future in which the use of ultrasound will be increasingly routine in the care of patients with cardiovascular disease. LUS can be integrated into the physical examination or can be quickly performed as an add‐on study using the same probe employed for echocardiography. 11 Rapidly identifying patients with higher‐risk STEMI facilitates proper allocation of resources, such as more intensive monitoring and aggressive treatment.

More studies are needed to refine and establish the routine use of LUS in the care of patients with STEMI. Larger, multicenter trials are needed to further validate risk scores, ideally with much higher rates of available LUS operators. Given the high variability in LUS practice (eg, number of fields scanned and experience of operator), there is a need for a universal, evidence‐based protocol and standardized training of operators. Future randomized studies should investigate the use of early LUS before angiography as opposed to after angiography, as in this study. As the protocol would take less than 3 minutes in experienced hands, LUS would not delay door‐to‐balloon time and could be rapidly performed in the emergency department or even in the catheterization laboratory, in parallel with equipment set up.

Carreras‐Mora and colleagues' study highlights the utility of an LUS score as an independent predictor of major adverse cardiac events at 1 year in patients with STEMI, in contrast to prior studies focusing on shorter‐term outcomes. The present study also demonstrated the LUS score's ability to enhance the accuracy of the GRACE risk score. LUS has the potential to become a routine component of physical examinations, similar to the stethoscope, and could significantly improve the prognostication and management of patients with STEMI.
