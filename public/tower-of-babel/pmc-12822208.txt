PcWRKY33 enhances Fusarium oxysporum resistance in Polygonatum Cyrtonema Hua via salicylic acid-mediated defense activation
Authors: Yuqing Niu, Dagang Tian, Rongfeng Xiao, Luanxiang Li, Yujing Zhu, Meixia Zheng, Yanming Zhu, Hong Chen, Huiyong Zheng, Hailan Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe production of Polygonatum cyrtonema Hua is currently encountering substantial challenges due to root rot disease, primarily caused by the pathogen Fusarium oxysporum. The WRKY transcription factors play crucial roles in mediating disease resistance across a wide range of plant species; however, it is unclear whether members of this family improve resistance to F. oxysporum infection in P. cyrtonema Hua. To putatively determine the F. oxysporum responsive PcWRKY members, we analyzed full-length transcriptome sequencing data obtained after F. oxysporum infection in this study, 271 WRKY transcription factors were detected and PcWRKY33 was found to be responsive to F. oxysporum infection. Furthermore, overexpression of PcWRKY33 in both P. cyrtonema Hua and Nicotiana benthamiana increased tolerance to F. oxysporum infection, and the expression level of 20 disease-resistance genes containing the TGAC motif were regulated using transcriptome sequencing analysis post-inoculation with F. oxysporum in PcWRKY33 overexpression seedlings. We also determined that F. oxysporum infection increased both the endogenous salicylic acid (SA) content and the expression of disease-resistant genes in PcWRKY33-overexpressing plants. Our results demonstrated that PcWRKY33 plays an important role in the resistance of P. cyrtonema Hua to F. oxysporum by influencing SA signaling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07921-9.Keywords: Polygonatum Cyrtonema Hua, Fusarium oxysporum, PcWRKY33, Salicylic acid

Introduction

Polygonatum cyrtonema Hua, a perennial herb of the genus Polygonatum in the Asparagaceae family, has thick rhizomes rich in polygonatum polysaccharides, which can enhance cellular superoxide dismutase activity and immunity, and has excellent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-depressive effects [1–3]. P. cyrtonema Hua, as a traditional Chinese medicine that is both edible and medicinal, has seen its market demand gradually expand due to its outstanding edible and medicinal value. However, P. cyrtonema Hua is threatened.

by several diseases, especially root rot, which seriously affects its quality and yield [4–6]. Recent studies have found that Fusarium oxysporum has become an important pathogen causing root rot in the growth of P. cyrtonema Hua [7]. At present, the main method for preventing root rot is chemical agents. However, due to pathogen resistance and environmental issues, the therapeutic effect of chemical agents is not satisfactory [8]. Therefore, resistance to root rot has become the main breeding goal for the improvement of high-quality varieties of P. cyrtonema Hua. To achieve this goal, it is crucial to identify the genes related to P. cyrtonema Hua resistance and study their molecular mechanisms.

Upon attack by pathogens, plants initiate a series of intricate defense mechanisms. These include the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), the deposition of callose, the activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade, alterations in plant hormone levels, and the reorganization of transcription factors, among other responses [9, 10]. WRKY transcription factors (TFs) represent one of the most extensive families of transcriptional regulators in plants and play a crucial role within the signaling networks that govern numerous plant processes [11–13]. These WRKY proteins are characterized by a conserved WRKYGQK peptide sequence and possess either a C2H2 or C2HC-type zinc finger motif [14, 15]. Moreover, WRKY TFs exhibit specific binding affinity to W-box motifs, characterized by the sequence (C/T) TGAC (C/T), located within the promoters of numerous downstream genes associated with biological stress responses. This binding regulates gene transcription and augments the defensive capabilities of plants [16, 17]. For instance, CaWRKY30 positively regulates pepper immunity by targeting CaWRKY40 against Ralstonia solanacearum inoculation through modulating defense-related genes [18]. In roses, RcWRKY41 was identified as a candidate regulatory gene in the response to Botrytis cinerea infection [19].

The activation of WRKY TFs, in conjunction with various signaling cascades, including those mediated by salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA), initiates the plant’s defense mechanisms against pathogenic bacteria [20]. For instance, PtrWRKY73, a SA-inducible poplar WRKY transcription factor, is involved in disease resistance in Arabidopsis thaliana [21]. In upland cotton, GmWRKY53 modulates tolerance to Verticillium dahliae by regulating genes involved in the SA and JA signaling pathways [22]. Similarly, in apple, the MdVQ37-MdWRKY100 complex enhances resistance to Glomerella leaf spot by modulating SA content and the expression of MdRPM1 [23]. Among plant hormones, SA plays major role in systemic acquired resistance and often functions downstream of WRKY-mediated signaling. However, the involvement of the SA signaling pathway and its upstream WRKY regulators in conferring root rot resistance in P. cyrtonema Hua remains uncertain.

Although the roles of WRKY transcription factors in the resistance mechanisms of plants have been extensively studied, it is unclear whether members of the WRKY family in P. cyrtonema Hua contribute to the response to root rot disease. In this study, we identified a WRKY transcription factor gene in the genome of P. cyrtonema Hua through comprehensive transcriptomic sequencing and bioinformatics approaches. We then functionally characterized this candidate WRKY transcription factor gene in response to F. oxysporum using transgenic biotechnology techniques. Our results indicated that overexpression of PcWRKY33 in P. cyrtonema Hua and N. benthamiana enhanced their resistance to F. oxysporum. Following infection with F. oxysporum, there was an increase in SA content in P. cyrtonema Hua, which contributed to improved resistance against the pathogen. Furthermore, PcWRKY33 modulated the expression of several disease resistance-related genes.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, growth conditions and fungal strains culture

The tissue culture tubers of P. cyrtonema Hua grown under sterile conditions for 1 month, cultivated under long-day (LD) conditions (16 h: 8 h, light: dark) at 27 ± 1 °C. Nicotiana benthamiana was grown in a growth chamber under LD conditions at 25 ± 1 °C. For pathogen response assays, strain ‘1933’ (isolated and preserved by our laboratory, The GenBank accession number is OR35211) was cultured in potato dextrose agar (PDA) in a 28 °C incubator for 5–7 days, and the resulting culture was used for inoculation. The liquid inoculation method was employed using tissue culture tubers of P. cyrtonema Hua that had been cultivated for 1 month as the experimental subjects. These tubers were immersed in a F. oxysporum spore suspension at a concentration of 5 × 10⁶ conidia/mL for 30 min, whereas the mock-inoculated controls were treated identically with PDA(Potato Dextrose Agar) liquid medium. For the stem inoculation method, the tissue culture tubers of P. cyrtonema Hua for one month were taken as the research object. Mycelium from PDA blocks, each with a diameter of 5 mm, was applied to the tubers of P. cyrtonema Hua. Sterile agar blocks served as the control treatment and were similarly placed on the tubers. The inoculated mycelial blocks were positioned on filter paper moistened with water and incubated under LD conditions at a temperature of 27 ± 1 °C. The inoculation experiment was conducted three times or more.

Full-length transcript analysis

Tissues, including roots, rhizomes, stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits, were collected from the tubers of P. cyrtonema Hua for full-length transcriptome sequencing in Fuzhou City, Fujian Province, China. The total RNA of different tissues of P. cyrtonema Hua was extracted by the TRIzol method. cDNA libraries were constructed using the Iso-Seq Express 2.0 kit and the Kinnex full-length RNA kit, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Based on the PacBio Revio third-generation sequencing platform, the original offline data were processed using the official PacBio software package SMRTlink. Firstly, circular consensus sequencing (CCS) reads were obtained and classified as full-length or non-full-length transcripts based on the presence of both 5’ and 3’ primers and the polyA tail. Full-length reads were then clustered using the hierarchical n*log(n) algorithm to generate consensus sequences. Finally, the consensus sequences were polished to produce high-quality isoforms. These polished consensus sequences were subsequently corrected with second-generation sequencing data, and redundancies were removed using the CD-HIT software [24]. The genes without functional annotation were annotated using the protein and nucleic acid databases, the databases used include: NR, NT, Pfam, KOG/COG, Swiss-prot, KEGG, GO [25–29]. Coding sequence (CDS) prediction and analysis were performed using ANGEL software [30]. All relevant data have been submitted to the NCBI SRA database, with accession numbers: PRJNA1311733.

WRKY family screening and analysis

The protein sequences of the WRKY gene family from A. thaliana were utilized as query sequences, and a BLASTp alignment was performed against the protein database predicted by the full-length transcriptome of P. cyrtonema Hua. The reference sequence source of A. thaliana WRKY (TAIR10) and the E-value threshold of BLASTp (1e-5). Subsequently, the conserved domains of the candidate WRKY family proteins were validated using Batch CD-Search (https://www.Ncbi.Nlm.Nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) to remove repetitive, redundant, and incomplete sequences, resulting in the identification of the final WRKY family in P. cyrtonema Hua. The amino acid length, relative molecular mass, and isoelectric point of the proteins encoded by the WRKY family were predicted using the ExPASy (https://web.Expasy.org/protparam/) online platform [31]. The sequences of the WRKY family members from A. thaliana were obtained from the Plant Transcription Factor Database. Multiple sequence alignment of the WRKY family proteins from P. cyrtonema Hua and A. thaliana was conducted using MEGA 7.0 software. An evolutionary tree was constructed employing the maximum likelihood method, with a bootstrap repetition value set at 1000, while other parameters were maintained at their default settings. The phylogenetic tree was visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) online tool (https://itol.embl.de/). The conserved motifs within the WRKY transcription factor of P. cyrtonema Hua were identified with a specification of 10 motifs using the MEME Suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) and subsequently visualized through TBtools [32].

De Novo transcriptome sequencing and analysis

The samples for the De novo transcriptome sequencing were taken from the laboratory of the Institute of Crops, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, located in Fuzhou, Fujian Province. P. cyrtonema Hua samples were extracted by the TRIzol method, and the NEB common library was used. The quality control and sequencing of the library were carried out on the Illumine platform. Paired-end sequencing was performed with a read length of 150 bp. The raw bases all exceed 6G. Raw reads are first removed through fastp software to obtain clean reads by removing reads containing connectors, reads containing ploy-N, and low-quality reads. The Q20, Q30 scores, and GC content of the clean data were calculated. Subsequent analyses were conducted using these high-quality, curated datasets. The RNA-seq data underwent processing and splicing via the Trinity software. Transcripts generated by the Corset program were employed for hierarchical clustering. Clustered transcript sequences were used as references for downstream analyses, including quality control, database annotation, quantification, differential significance analysis, and functional enrichment. The transcriptome obtained by Trinity splicing was used as the reference sequence (Ref). The clean reads of each sample were mapped onto the Ref. reads with alignment quality values lower than 10, reads on non-paired alignments, and reads aligned to multiple regions of the genome were filtered out respectively. During the comparison process, we adopted the RSEM software [33]. The differential expression analysis of the two groups was conducted using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0). The selection criteria for DEGs are |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and p ≤ 0.05. Pathway enrichment of differentially expressed genes was performed using the KEGG database, and the P value was < 0.05. All relevant data have been submitted to the NCBI SRA database, with accession numbers: PRJNA1311955, PRJNA1311801.

Transcriptional activation and subcellular localization assays of PcWRKY33

The full-length coding sequence of PcWRKY33 was cloned into pGBKT7 (BD vector) and transformed into yeast strain AH109. The empty pGBKT7 vector and pGBKT7-OsWRKY36 (positive control) [34] were used as controls. Transformants were selected on SD/-Trp/-Leu medium supplemented with X-α-GAL.

The complete PcWRKY33 coding sequence was inserted into the pYBA1132 vector, which utilizes the CaMV 35 S promoter and contains a green fluorescent protein (GFP) tag. This construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 via the freeze-thaw method. The Agrobacterium cultures were subsequently cultivated, centrifuged, and resuspended in an infiltration buffer composed of 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, and 200 µM acetosyringone, adjusted to a pH of 5.6, to achieve an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 1.0. These suspensions were then infiltrated into the leaves of four-week-old N. benthamiana plants following a 3 h dark incubation at room temperature. The GFP signal was observed using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM880, Germany) 2 days post-infiltration,, with a scale bar of 20 μm.

The transformation of N. benthamiana plants and the overexpression of P. cyrtonema Hua tubers

PcWRKY33 transgenic N. benthamiana plants were obtained by using Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disk transformation method [35]. The coding sequence of PcWRKY33 was cloned into the vector BGV007. Agrobacterium (Agrobacterium tumefaciens, strain GV3101) was resuspended in the infiltration buffer (5% sucrose, MS 4.47 g/L, 5 mg/L AgNO3, 100 µmol/L acetyleugenone) (final OD600 = 1.2). One-month-old tissue-cultured tubers of P. cyrtonema Hua were immersed in the Agrobacterium suspension. After vacuuming for 5 min, they were left to stand for infection for 15 min. After infection, the tubers were transferred to MS solid medium (5% sucrose, MS powder 4.47 g/L, 5 mg/L AgNO3, 100 µmol/L acetylsyringone, Agar 5 g/L, pH = 6.0), dark cultured for 24 h, followed by LD (16 h: 8 h, light: dark) cultured for 3 days at 27 ± 1 °C. Transgenic positive lines were identified using a two-step verification process: integration of the transgene was first confirmed by PCR amplification using vector-specific primers, followed by RT-qPCR analysis to verify a significant upregulation of PcWRKY33 expression. Lines that fulfilled both criteria were considered positive.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (LABLEAD, Beijing, China; R1200), and 2 µg RNA was subjected to first-strand cDNA synthesis using EasyScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen, Beijing, China; AE311). qPCR was then performed using the PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen, AQ601) on a CFX connect Real-time system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). ACTIN was used as the internal control [36]. Sequences of the primers are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Measurement of SA contents

To assess the endogenous SA levels in P. cyrtonema Hua, the samples were immersed in a F. oxysporum suspension (5 × 106 spores/mL) for a duration of 30 min, while control groups were treated with PDA liquid medium. Six tissue tubers constituted one biological replicate. The SA content was quantified using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) system[37].

Calibration standards for SA were prepared by spiking the appropriate phytohormone stock solutions into a phytohormone-free plant matrix. Salicylic acid-D4 was used as the internal standard (IS). For sample preparation, approximately 0.1 g of frozen plant tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 400 µL of ice-cold 50% acetonitrile containing the internal standards. The homogenate was incubated at 4 °C for extraction and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. A 300 µL aliquot of the supernatant was loaded onto an HLB solid-phase extraction cartridge. The flow-through was collected, and the cartridge was subsequently eluted with 500 µL of 30% acetonitrile. Both the initial flow-through and the eluate were combined to form the final analyte for analysis.

UHPLC-MS/MS system (ExionLC™ AD UHPLC-QTRAP 6500+, AB SCIEX, USA). Separation was achieved on a Waters XSelect HSS T3 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.5 μm) maintained at 45 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.01% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.01% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B), with a flow rate of 0.30 mL/min. The gradient program was as follows: 10% B at 0 min, increased to 50% B by 1 min, to 65% B by 4 min, to 70% B by 6 min, to 100% B by 7 min, returned to 10% B at 9.1 min, and held until 12 min.

The mass spectrometer was operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The ion source parameters were set as follows: ion spray voltage at ± 4500 V (negative/positive mode), curtain gas at 35 psi, temperature at 550 °C, and ion source gas 1 and 2 at 60 psi. Quantification was based on a calibration curve constructed from the concentration-to-internal standard ratio versus the peak area ratio. A correlation coefficient (r) > 0.99 was required for linearity. The limit of quantification (LOQ) was determined as the concentration yielding a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) of 10:1 [38, 39].

Statistical analysis

Three biological replicates were evaluated, with three technical replicates per biological replicate. All the data were analyzed using ANOVA and t-tests to determine significant differences with SPSS 24.0 software. Tukey’s at P = 0.01 or P = 0.05 was used for multiple comparisons of the mean.

Results

Screening of WRKY family and isolation of PcWRKY33

Full-length transcriptome sequencing was conducted on various organs of P. cyrtonema Hua, including roots, rhizomes, stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits, to predict TF families, identifying 271 WRKY TFs. Analysis of the physicochemical properties revealed that the amino acid count for these 271 WRKY TFs ranged from 107 to 1074 amino acids. The isoelectric points (pIs) of the proteins varied between 4.79 and 11.08. Notably, 165 proteins exhibited an pIs below 7, classifying them as acidic proteins, which constituted 60.88% of the total protein count (Table S2). To study the evolutionary relationships among the WRKY family members of P. cyrtonema Hua, a phylogenetic tree of P. cyrtonema Hua (271 PcWRKYs) and Arabidopsis thaliana (71 AtWRKYs) was constructed (Fig. 1). 271 PcWRKYs were divided into three groups: Group Ⅰ, Group Ⅱ and Group Ⅲ. The classification of the WRKY gene family is mainly based on the widely used phylogenetic framework [40]. Group Ⅰ comprised 51 members, all of which contained the C2H2-type zinc finger motif, and was further divided into two distinct subclades (Ⅰa, Ⅰb). Group Ⅱ contains 83 members, and was further divided into two distinct subclades (Ⅱa, Ⅱb). Select 10 motifs to analyze the conserved structural domain of the PcWRKYs protein of P. cyrtonema Hua (Fig. S1−3). Different WRKY proteins contain different numbers of motifs. PcWRKYs protein of P. cyrtonema Hua all have the typical WRKY domain characteristic sequence WRKYGQK (Fig. S4).

Phylogenetic analysis of WRKY gene families in P. cyrtonema Hua and A. thaliana

The phylogenetic tree was generated in MEGA 7.0 using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates, based on multiple alignments of full-length amino acid sequences from 271 PcWRKY genes in P. cyrtonema Hua and 71 in A. thaliana.

De-novo transcriptome profiling of P. cyrtonema Hua challenged with F. oxysporum revealed the expression pattern of 271 PcWRKY genes. PcWRKYs with no detectable expression were excluded from further analysis (Fig. 2). The majority of PcWRKYs exhibited either negligible expression or a muted response to the pathogen. Strikingly, the gene highlighted in the red box displayed a pronounced transcriptional surge post-inoculation. Sequence annotation identified this transcript as an orthologue of Arabidopsis thaliana AtWRKY33, hence designated PcWRKY33.Together, these findings establish PcWRKY33 as a key regulator in the defense response of P. cyrtonema Hua to F. oxysporum.

Subcellular localization and transcriptional activity of PcWRKY33

To demonstrate the subcellular location of PcWRKY33, the coding region of PcWRKY33 fused with GFP was introduced into N. benthamiana leaves. The results indicated that PcWRKY33 was localized in the nucleus, whereas free GFP proteins were diffusely distributed in the cytoplasm as well as the cell nucleus (Fig. 3A). The transcriptional activation potential of PcWRKY33 was assessed using the yeast strain AH109. Upon fusion of PcWRKY33 with the DNA-binding domain of yeast GAL4, it facilitated the activation of the LacZ reporter gene, which is regulated by the upstream activation sequence of GAL4 (Fig. 3B). This observation demonstrates that PcWRKY33 possesses transcriptional activation activity.

PcWRKY33 enhances the resistance of transgenic P. cyrtonema Hua and N. benthamiana to F. oxysporum

To define the defensive role of PcWRKY33 during F. oxysporum attack, the gene was over-expressed in both N. benthamiana and P. cyrtonema Hua. In N. benthamiana, leaves over-expressing PcWRKY33 and wild-type (WT) controls were inoculated with identical F. oxysporum plugs. Five days after inoculation, The leaf lesion area of N. benthamiana overexpressing PcWRKY33 was significantly smaller, averaging 0.07 ± 0.02 cm², compared to 0.48 ± 0.02 cm² for the wild-type plants. (Fig. 4A–B). A parallel assay was conducted in P. cyrtonema Hua. tubers transiently over-expressing PcWRKY33 exhibited consistently higher transcript levels than WT (Fig. S5). When challenged with GFP-labelled F. oxysporum, in-vivo imaging revealed markedly larger fluorescent lesions in WT tubers, accompanied by severe reddening and rot. The tubers with overexpressed PcWRKY33 showed significantly smaller fluorescent areas, with an average of 0.12 ± 0.01 cm², while the wild-type leaves had an average of 0.32 ± 0.01 cm². It is noteworthy that the extensive tissue maceration and collapse in WT tubers ultimately limited superficial mycelial growth, whereas the restricted colonization on PcWRKY33-overexpressing tubers, which maintained tissue integrity, allowed for more visible hyphae on the surface. This distinct phenotypic pattern confirms that PcWRKY33 overexpression effectively contains the pathogen and preserves host tissue structure (Fig. 4C–E). Similar results were obtained with F. oxysporum mycelial discs, the tubers with overexpressed PcWRKY33 showed significantly smaller fluorescent areas, with an average of 0.08 ± 0.01 cm², while the wild-type leaves had an average of 0.27 ± 0.01 cm² (Fig. 4F–H). Collectively, these data establish PcWRKY33 as a positive immune regulator that confers robust resistance to F. oxysporum in both heterologous and native host systems.

PcWRKY33 regulates the expression of immune-related genes

To elucidate the molecular mechanism of PcWRKY33 in response to F. oxysporum infection, we performed a comparative transcriptome analysis between the OEPcWRKY33 and WT plants at 0, 12, 24, and 48 post-inoculation (hpi) post-inoculation. The sequence information for each sample subjected to RNA-Seq and of the mapped loci is presented in Table S3. The comparison of significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was set as fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.50. Respectively 928, 4628, 1106, and 11,880 DEGs were identified from these four time-point comparisons between OEPcWRKY33 and WT.

To further illustrate the function of PcWRKY33 in promoter region of target genes, we selected DEGs that contained a TGAC motif in their promoter regions (2000 bp upstream of the start codon) for analysing. 2010 DEGs were identified by comparing OEPcWRKY33 and WT at 12, 24, and 48 hpi (Table S4). A total of 250 upregulated DEGs were identified at 12, 24 and 48 hpi (Table S5). Further, 30 most significant GO terms, including 20 biological processes such as poly(A) + mRNA export from the nucleus, N-terminal protein amino acid methylation, and innate immune response, 4 cellular components such as transmembrane transporter complex, and 6 molecular functions such as protein dimerization activity, were significantly enriched between the comparsion of OEPcWRKY33 and WT (Fig.S6). Then we selected 26 genes specifically associated with immune response-related pathways for an additional round of GO enrichment analysis (Table S6), including responses to stress and stimulus (Fig. 5A). Name these genes according to the annotations in the compared database (Table S7). In the OEPcWRKY33 line, various defense-related genes exhibited dynamic and diverse expression patterns, revealing a temporal and complex defense response mediated by PcWRKY33. Genes such as FGFs and GAGs showed significantly higher expression levels in the OEPcWRKY33 line than in the WT during the initial stages 0–12 hpi. This suggests that PcWRKY33 may pre-activate or rapidly potentiate certain basal defense pathways. Genes including RNAP and TIR were specifically and significantly activated in the OEPcWRKY33 line at 24 hpi, with their expression levels substantially exceeding those in the WT. This indicates that PcWRKY33 initiates a distinct set of defense programs as the pathogen infection progresses. Notably, genes such as GABAT, TOIA, and CAT2 displayed a unique expression pattern. Their expression was suppressed or showed no significant difference compared to the WT during the early infection stages (12–24 hpi). However, their expression was strongly induced and became significantly higher in the OEPcWRKY33 line at 48 hpi. This delayed activation pattern may reflect the engagement of secondary defense or metabolic compensation mechanisms in the overexpressing line during the later stages of infection, potentially enhancing antioxidant capacity or facilitating damage repair, thereby contributing to sustained resistance. The predicted functions of these genes are shown in Table S7. It is worth noting that PCL is a sialic acid-binding lectin gene in the NT (NCBI nucleotide sequences) database. These results indicate that PcWRKY33 may improve the resistance to F. oxysporum of OEPcWRKY33 by regulating stress-related genes.

PcWRKY33 stimulated genes associated with Flavonoid biosynthesis and the SA Signaling pathway

To identify DEGs that were significantly enriched in GO terms and metabolic pathways, Functional enrichment analysis, including GO and KEGG analyses, was performed for all the DEGs. The top 20 GO terms in each comparison were selected. Analysis of DEGs at 0 hpi comparison showed that the most significant categories were external encapsulating structure, oxidoreductase activity, catalytic activity cell wall organization or biogenesis, antioxidant activity and cytosol (Fig. S7A). At 12 hpi comparison, the most significant functional terms were transferase activity, carbohydrate metabolic process, antioxidant activity, motor activity, cell wall, transmembrane transport, lipid metabolic process, extemal encapsulating structure, microtubule-based movement, transporter activity, sulfur compound metabolic process and cell wall organization or biogenesis (Fig. S7B). At 24 hpi comparison, the most significant functional terms were catalytic activity, oxidoreductase activity, sulfur compound metabolic process, cell wall, external encapsulating structure, cell wall organization or biogenesis and antioxidant activity (Fig. S7C) For the comparison at the 48 hpi comparison, the genes with the highest content were associated with multiple pathways, including catalytic activity, oxidoreductase activity and sulfur compound metabolic process (Fig. S7D).

Further KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the most significantly enriched (p < 0.01) pathways at 0 hpi comparison were associated with Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, Plant hormone signal transduction and Brassinosteroid biosynthesis (Fig. 6A). DEGs at the 12 hpi comparison were involved in Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, Flavonoid biosynthesis and Plant hormone signal transduction (Fig. 6B). DEGs at the 24 hpi comparison were involved in Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, Flavonoid biosynthesis and Plant hormone signal transduction (Fig. 6C). Different from the former 3 time-point, the most highly enriched pathways were Glycine, serine and threonine metabolism at the 48 hpi comparison (Fig. 6D). Notable, the Flavonoid biosynthesis was activated at 12 and 24 hpi (Fig. 6A-D). Moreover, those genes encoding NPR1, TGA and PR1 associated with SA biosynthesis pathway were regulated in the OEPcWRKY33 plants (Fig.S8). Further, we compared the levels of SA in both WT and OEPcWRKY33 before and after inoculation. The finds indicated that F. oxysporum in the experimental group significantly stimulated the accumulation of SA in OEPcWRKY33. Quantification of SA levels revealed that, even prior to inoculation (0 hpi), the basal SA content in OEPcWRKY33 tubers (3.62 ± 0.02 µg/kg) was lower than that in WT tubers (15.14 ± 0.26 µg/kg). After 24 h inoculation, the free SA content in the OEPcWRKY33 tubers increased sharply to 28.60 ± 0.05 µg/kg, which was approximately 9.3 times that of the un-inoculated wild-type tubers (3.78 ± 0.03 µg/kg) (Fig. 6E). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that PcWRKY33 transcript levels were rapidly upregulated after SA treatment (Fig.S9), this result confirms the existence of a positive feedback loop, where in PcWRKY33 activation leads to SA accumulation, which in turn further amplifies PcWRKY33 expression. To further determine whether SA is required for defense against F. oxysporum, we treated the P. cyrtonema Hua exogenously with SA, and then inoculated them with F. oxysporum. The tubers with SA-treated showed significantly smaller fluorescent areas, with an average of 0.10 ± 0.06 cm², while the mock treated tubers had an average of 0.51 ± 0.02 cm² (Fig. 6F-H). Consequently, SA mediated immunity is crucial for the resistance of P. cyrtonema Hua against F. oxysporum.

Discussion

WRKY-type transcription factors are integral to the functioning of all eukaryotic organisms. Notably, the WRKY gene family has been comprehensively characterized in species such as peanuts [41], legumes [42], and tomatoes [43]. The genomic information for P. cyrtonema Hua remains unpublished. In this study, we identified 271 WRKY transcription factors using full-length transcriptomic analysis and conducted a phylogenetic examination of these factors (Fig. 1). This research significantly advances our understanding of WRKY transcription factors in P. cyrtonema Hua.

Previous studies have emphasized the critical role of transcriptional reprogramming of various genes, including WRKY transcription factors, in mediating plant responses to F. oxysporum infection, as seen in Lilium regale Wilson [44] and cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) [45]. The functional diversity of WRKYs in this context is striking. For instance, CmWRKY6-1 in Chrysanthemum morifolium negatively regulates resistance to F. oxysporum by influencing ROS and SA signaling pathways [46], whereas in Lilium regale Wilson, LrWRKY2 acts as a positive regulator [47]. Similarly, in chickpea, CaWRKY40 enhances resistance by modulating defense genes [48]. Our work establishes that in P. cyrtonema, PcWRKY33 functions as a positive regulator (Figs. 2 and 4), expanding the repertoire of known resistance-associated WRKYs and highlighting their species-specific roles.

To overcome the protracted growth cycle of P. cyrtonema, we employed an Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression system, a well-established expedient approach for gene functional analysis [49]. This was complemented by heterologous expression in the model plant N. benthamiana, a common strategy for studying genes from non-transformative systems, as demonstrated in studies on cotton GhMAP3K65 [50] and Chinese cabbage BrIQD35 [51]. Our confirmation of PcWRKY33 resistance function in both systems underscores its conserved role in enhancing immunity.

The core of plant disease resistance often revolves around SA signaling [52]. Our study provides several layers of evidence that PcWRKY33 augments resistance through SA-mediated defense. Firstly, SA treatment itself enhanced resistance in P. cyrtonema Hua (Fig. 6F-H). Secondly, PcWRKY33 overexpression led to a significant accumulation of SA post-inoculation (Fig. 6E). The intriguing observation that OEPcWRKY33 tubers displayed reduced basal SA levels at 0 hpi warrants further discussion. We hypothesize that this may represent an energy-saving strategy under non-stress conditions, a notion that finds parallels in other systems where WRKYs fine-tune basal defense to optimize growth [53, 54]. The subsequent pathogen-induced surge in SA in OEPcWRKY33 lines indicates a potent activation of the SA biosynthesis pathway upon challenge.

The fluctuation of SA in WT higher at 0 hpi, decreasing at 24 hpi, and rising again by 48 hpi suggests a complex, multi-phasic interaction with the pathogen. The initial decrease in WT SA levels at 24 hpi could be a result of pathogen suppression or the activation of antagonistic signaling pathways. A key question arising from this dynamic is the potential role of JA and SA/JA homeostasis. While our study did not measure JA levels, it is a well-established concept that WRKY transcription factors often function as nodes in the cross-talk between SA and JA pathways [53, 54]. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that PcWRKY33 influences or is influenced by JA signaling, and elucidating this interplay should be a priority for future research. This would determine if the SA dynamics we observed are part of a broader hormonal rebalancing act during infection.

Beyond SA, PcWRKY33 also regulated genes in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway. Flavonoids, with their inherent antibacterial properties [55], and their intricate interplay with SA signaling [56], represent a crucial secondary defense layer. The induction of this pathway aligns with findings in Arabidopsis thaliana, where quercetin augments SA biosynthesis [57], and in tomato, where SA and nitric oxide jointly bolster flavonoid-mediated resistance to F. oxysporum [56]. This suggests that PcWRKY33 may orchestrate a coordinated defense by simultaneously activating SA-dependent signaling and flavonoid-based chemical barriers.

Conclusion

In this investigation, a total of 271 WRKY transcription factors from P. cyrtonema Hua were identified and subjected to analyses of their phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, and conserved domains. Transcriptomic sequencing analysis indicated that PcWRKY33 was responsive to F. oxysporum exposure. The overexpression of PcWRKY33 in both P. cyrtonema Hua and N. benthamiana resulted in enhanced resistance to F. oxysporum. RNA sequencing further demonstrated that PcWRKY33 modulated the expression of 20 disease-resistance genes containing TGAC motifs in their promoters. Following inoculation of P. cyrtonema Hua with F. oxysporum, SA levels were elevated, and SA was found to augment the plant’s disease resistance. This study offers valuable insights into the potential function of WRKY genes in conferring resistance to root rot disease in P. cyrtonema Hua and establishes a theoretical foundation for the development of P. cyrtonema Hua cultivars with enhanced resistance to F. oxysporum.
