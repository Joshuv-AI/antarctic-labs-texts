Circulating protein levels of insulin-like growth factor 1 signaling pathway and the predisposition to colorectal carcinogenesis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fatemeh Naderi Noukabadi, Elahe Daskar Abkenar, Sascha Tierling, Sajad Shojaee, Sara Ashtari, Amir Sadeghi, Nayeralsadat Fatemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe insulin-like growth factor (IGF) family plays a critical role in cancer progression, with the insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor IGF-1Rsignificantly regulating cellular growth through specific signaling pathways. These pathways may enhance cell proliferation and colorectal cancer (CRC) advancement. Some studies suggest an association between serum IGF levels and reduced CRC risk. To further elucidate this relationship, we conducted a systematic literature review and meta-analysis investigating the association between blood IGF levels and CRC onset.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science databases for studies published until February 15, 2025. A total of 71 eligible studies, comprising 7360 CRC cases and 15982 controls, were included. Gene expression data for IGF-1, IGF-1R, insulin receptor substrates (IRS1), IGF-2, and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3 (IGFBP3) were analyzed. The pooled effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess relationships between gene expression levels and CRC susceptibility. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 14.0 and R, version 4.4.1.ResultsThe meta-analysis demonstrated a significant association between elevated circulating IGF-1 levels and an increased risk of colorectal cancer (WMD = 11.24; 95% CI: 4.18–18.30; p = 0.002) as well as precancerous colorectal lesions (WMD = 21.35; 99% CI: 3.37–34.89; p = 0.002). Conversely, reduced IGFBP-3 levels were significantly associated with a higher CRC risk (WMD = − 138.20; 95% CI: −231.77 to − 44.64; p = 0.004). No significant association was observed for IGF-2 (WMD = − 5.40; 95% CI: −35.11 to 24.31.ConclusionsElevated IGF1 levels and reduced IGFBP3 levels are correlated with increased CRC risk, suggesting a potential role of these factors in CRC development. These findings provide insight into CRC pathogenesis and could inform future diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15496-9.

Introduction

The insulin-like growth factor (IGF) protein family participates in cellular growth, differentiation, and survival [1]. The IGF system includes IGF-1 and IGF-2, their receptors (IGF-1R and IGF-2R), and a group of six high-affinity IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs 1–6), which regulate IGF activity [2]. In normal cellular physiology, IGFs exert their effects through endocrine, paracrine, and autocrine mechanisms by binding to their receptors to activate signaling pathways that promote cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis. This system is tightly regulated by IGFBPs, which modulate the availability of IGFs to their receptors and maintain cellular homeostasis [3]. IGF pathways play critical roles in cancer development and progression. The main components of these pathways include IGF-1 and IGF-2, their receptors (IGF-1R, IGF-2R), and IGFBPs. When IGFs bind to IGF-1R, they activate downstream signaling pathways such as the PI3K/AKT and RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK pathways leading to directed and controlled cellular proliferation and survival [4]. Uncontrolled activation of these pathways is frequently observed in various cancers, including colorectal, breast, prostate, and lung cancers, promotes metastasis while inhibiting apoptosis and contributes to tumor growth and resistance to therapies [5].

Insulin receptor substrates (IRS1 and IRS2) are essential mediators of this signaling pathway, facilitating the transmission of signals from the IGF-1R to downstream targets that promote cell proliferation, survival, and metastasis. Elevated levels of both IRS1 and IRS2 are correlated with CRC progression, and their interaction with IGF-1R enhances oncogenic processes. These proteins contribute to treatment resistance and poor prognosis, positioning them as potential targets for therapeutic strategies. Modulating the activities of IRS1 and IRS2 could yield novel insights into treatment options for CRC [6]. In particular, in CRC expressed serum protein levels of IGFs and their receptors (IGF-1R and IGF-2R) are significantly altered and increased expression of IGF-1 and IGF-2, along with their receptor IGF-1R, is observed in CRC tissues compared with normal tissues. This overexpression is associated with enhanced cell proliferation, enhanced survival, and metastasis, contributing to progression and poor prognosis of the disease [7]. Studies have shown that inhibiting IGF-1R function can inhibit colon cancer growth and angiogenesis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. Additionally, IGF-1 can induce the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) via hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1), promoting angiogenesis, which is critical for tumor growth and metastasis [8, 9]. Moreover, IGFBPs, particularly IGFBP3, also show elevated expression levels in CRC, indicating their potential role in tumor biology and as prognostic markers [10]. In particular, the presence of reduced IGFBPs suppressor activity further promotes neoplastic transformation since it is often found in coincidence with overexpression of the IGF-R family.

These findings suggest that targeting the IGF system may offer a promising strategy for CRC treatment, either by directly inhibiting IGF-1R or by modulating the activity of IGFBPs to restore balance and inhibit tumor progression [5, 8]. Given that the IGF pathway plays an important role in the development and progression of CRC, genetic changes affecting pathway members may play a role in the risk to develop CRC. However, the integration of serum protein expression levels associated with the IGF pathway has been limited in previous studies. This review and meta-analysis aimed to address the existing gap in knowledge by systematically analyzing the available evidence regarding the association between IGF-1, IGFBP3, IGF-1R, IGF-2, IRS1 and IRS2 gene expression and the risk of developing CRC. Initially, IRS2 was included in our study framework; however, we found no studies that met our inclusion criteria for IRS2. One of the major limitations of this meta-analysis is the insufficient number of high-quality studies available in the literature concerning IRS2, which may have affected the robustness of our findings. The novelty of this study lies in its analysis of serum protein levels associated with these genes in the development and progression of CRC. This research may provide valuable insights into the complex genetic mechanisms underlying CRC and encourage future investigations into prevention and treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Literature search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement [11]. The study has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with ID CRD420251008850. Searches were carried out in the PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science databases up to February 15, 2025. The complete, final search strategies for each database, including exact syntax and date filters, are provided in the Supplementary Text. The search was limited to studies in English and those involving human subjects. Two reviewers independently conducted the literature search, screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts, and consulted a third author in cases of disagreement.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Case-control, cross-sectional, and cohort studies involving CRC populations and adenoma polyp, colorectal precancerous lesions (CRPCLs); (2) Studies that provided quantitative analysis of the relationship between serum level of proteins involved in the IGF pathway (IGF-1, IGFBP3, IGF-2, IGF1R and IRSs) and CRC risk; (3) Availability of full text in English. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), reviews, letters, comments, editorials, case reports, conference abstracts, personal communications, and studies focused solely on hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC) and familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) were excluded from the analysis.

Data collection and quality assessment

In accordance with the established inclusion criteria, two reviewers meticulously extracted the following data from the selected studies: the name of the first author, year of publication, participants’ race and ethnicity, study design, number of cases and controls, family history of cancer, body mass index, age, clinical outcomes, type of drug administered, and stages of CRC. The quality of each study was assessed utilizing the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist by three reviewers (refer to Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Any discrepancies arising during the screening process and quality evaluation were resolved through discussions or by consulting a fourth author.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using the weighted mean difference (WMD) as the effect size measure, as all included studies reported outcomes in the same units. The 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated to assess the certainty of the effect size estimate. Sample mean and standard deviation (SD) were estimated from the sample size, median, and interquartile range using Wan et al.‘s approach [12], and meta-analysis pooled estimates were computed accordingly. A significant p-value indicates that the observed association between a given exposure and a clinical outcome is unlikely to have occurred by chance alone, assuming no true underlying effect.

Pooled estimates were computed using fixed or random effect models, employing the inverse-variance method and DerSimonian and Laird’s estimator. Subgroup and overall results were visualized using forest plots. Model selection was guided by heterogeneity assessment. A low I-squared value suggests little to no heterogeneity, while a high value indicates substantial variability between studies. Heterogeneity among studies was examined using Cochran’s Q test, the I² statistic, and τ² [13]. When heterogeneity was considerable, the interpretation of pooled estimates should be approached with caution, as the observed effect may not be robust or generalizable due to uncertainty in the true underlying effect size.

Drapery plots [14] were also employed to visualize the distribution of effect sizes across studies, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of the data and potential patterns that may not be visible in standard plots. The plot incorporates p-value thresholds for different levels of statistical significance (p = 0.01, p = 0.05, and p = 0.1), which further enhances the interpretation of the results. This method serves as a sophisticated graphical tool that aids in identifying outliers and assessing the overall trend in the findings. In summary, the drapery plot complements the forest plot by illustrating the P-value curves of all primary studies and the pooled estimates, along with a shaded prediction region.

The funnel plot was used to visually assess potential publication bias, particularly when the number of included studies was fewer than 10 (k < 10). The Egger test, a regression-based method, was also conducted to quantitatively assess small study effects and publication bias [15]. In cases where asymmetry was suspected, the trim-and-fill method was applied to estimate the potential impact of publication bias by imputing missing studies and adjusting the pooled effect size accordingly [16, 17].

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results by systematically removing one study at a time to determine if the overall effect size remained consistent. This approach helped ensure the reliability and generalizability of the findings.

Influential analysis was also performed to determine the impact of individual studies on the overall effect size. By systematically excluding studies and observing changes in the pooled estimates, this analysis helped to identify any studies that disproportionately influenced the results.

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA 14.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and R 4.4.1, employing the “meta”, “metafor”, and “dmetar” packages for meta-analytic procedures.

Results

Search result, study characteristics, and quality assessment

A total of 3460 articles were retrieved through electronic and manual searches. After reviewing the titles and abstracts, we removed 1332 duplicates and excluded 2025 articles that were not in English or did not meet our inclusion criteria. A diagram illustrating the study selection process is presented in Fig. 1. The baseline characteristics of the included manuscripts are summarized in Table 1, focusing on CRC and CRPCLs. Overall, we included 17 cohort studies and 52 nested case-control studies, which were published between 1994 and 2023. These studies were conducted in a variety of locations, including Germany, the Netherlands, the United States, the UK, Sweden, Japan, Australia, Denmark, China, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Serbia, Korea, Turkey, Russia, Taiwan, Poland, Spain, Finland, North Carolina, Native Hawaiian populations, Latino communities, and Italy. A quality appraisal revealed that all included studies meet high-quality standards.

IGF-1, IGFBP3, IGF-2, IGF1R and IRS and their association with the risk of CRPCLs and CRC

IGF1 and CRC risk

The association between CRC and protein levels of IGF-1 have been most intensely investigated (Table 2). The association between circulating level of IGF1 and CRC risk was assessed in thirty-one studies. Eleven studies showed that IGF-1 serum levels in CRC patients were notably higher compared to the control group (P ≤ 0.007) [21, 22, 28, 31, 38, 45, 87]; Tripkovic et al. ‘s findings indicated an IGF-1 serum level association with a 3.15-fold increased colon cancer odds, suggesting its significant role in cancer development [67]. In a case-control study involving 9345 Japanese-American men in Hawaii from 1971 to 1977, and 177 colon cancer and 105 rectal cancer cases up to 1996, IGF-1 levels were significantly altered in colon cancer patients (CRC P: 0.36, Colon cancer P = 0.02 and rectal cancer P = 0.13) [76]. A study found that the body mass index (BMI) in the case group was 23.4 ± 3.5 kg/m2. The research aimed to evaluate the impact of plasma IGF-1 levels on the number of dysplastic aberrant crypt foci (ACF) and the associated risk of developing CRC (P = 0.0177). The study suggested that higher IGF-1 levels could promote dysplastic ACF [55]. Individuals with colorectal adenoma and carcinoma had elevated serum IGF-1 concentrations (P = 0.003 and P = 0.0001, respectively). In this study, individuals aged 40 or older exhibited a correlation between age and an increased association with colorectal adenoma (OR: 1.09, P = 0.01) and carcinoma (OR: 1.11, P = 0.03). However, the expression level of IGF-1 did not show a significant association with colorectal adenoma (P = 0.464, r = 0.161) or carcinoma (P = 0.497, r = 0.497) in relation to age [40]. A study conducted by Jiang et al. reported significantly higher serum IGF-1 levels in CRC patients both before and after surgery compared to healthy individuals (pre-operation P < 0.001, post-operation P < 0.05). The correlation between serum IGF-1 levels and BMI in colon cancer patients was also significant (p < 0.05) [39]. Additionally, research by DeLellis et al. found that a higher intake of animal protein, particularly from dairy and meat sources, was significantly associated with increased plasma IGF-I levels (p < 0.001). Calcium intake demonstrated a positive correlation with IGF-I levels (p < 0.01). In contrast, no significant associations were found between total fat or carbohydrate intake and IGF-I levels. Furthermore, physical activity was linked to slightly lower IGF-I levels (p < 0.05), while overall higher BMI was associated with reduced IGF-I levels (p < 0.03). An elevated BMI may contribute to insulin resistance, which can impact the metabolism of IGF-I. Ethnic and gender subgrouping indicated that African Americans and men exhibited higher IGF-I levels (p < 0.001), highlighting the influence of protein, dairy, and demographic factors on IGF-I concentrations. In summary, these findings underscore the significant role of protein and dairy intake in modulating IGF-I levels, which are pertinent to cancer risk [75]. Other studies reported no significant difference in serum level of IGF-1 in CRC patients compared to the control group (P > 0.05) [26, 61, 64, 77, 84]. In line with this, a case-control study from Japan’s Public Health Center-based prospective cohort found no significant change in plasma IGF-1 levels in males and females (men: P = 0.27, women P = 0.99) [68]. In a case-control study involving 1260 patients with colon or rectum cancer and an equal number of controls, which was conducted as part of European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC), IGF-1 ‘s relationship with the incidence of CRC was investigated. The findings showed no statistically significant correlation because the p-value was 0.16 or 0.09 for colon cancer and rectal cancer, respectively [88]. A study with a prolonged follow-up time of 9.0 years that t were 93 and 48 colon and rectal cancers respectively, and 2 men had both colon and rectal cancer discovered that higher IGF-1 levels were not associated with an increased risk of CRC in men aged 70 and older in the Western Australia community Health In Men Study (HIMS) cohort based in Perth, Western Australia) [32].

Even though, most study outcomes concerning CRC patients and IGF-1 serum levels show no statistical difference with the control group [30, 58, 78], some studies reported meaningful differences with lower IGF-1 serum levels for CRC patients compared to those of the control group [23, 34, 36]. For example, Huang et al. compared CRC patients with cachexia and found the IGF-1 levels were 155.08 ng/ml compared to the control group with 195.15 ng/ml; this was a meaningful difference considering the p-value of 0.05 [74]. Also, Ollberding, N. J., et al. mentioned that in serum samples, the median levels of IGF-1 were lower for cases than for controls [47]. This evidence was confirmed by others showing lower IGF-1 levels in cases [57].

IGF1 and CRPCLs

The association between circulating IGF-1 levels and the risk of CRPCLs was assessed in thirteen studies (Table 2). Among those, five studies showed that the serum IGF-1 levels in patients with adenomas were significantly higher compared to those in the control group (P ≤ 0.02). Ashktorab et al.‘s research findings validate the association between income and the expression levels of IGF-1 in colorectal adenomas. The comparison with BMI and age was found to be significant (p < 0.05) [33, 43, 72, 77]. Hang et al. studied American citizens and found that elevated IGF-1 levels were associated with an increased association with serrated polyps in the full model. However, these associations did not reach statistical significance (OR = 1.09, 95% CI = 1.00 to 1.19, P = 0.04, and OR = 1.09, 95% CI = 1.01 to 1.17, P = 0.02, respectively). On the contrary, no association was observed between IGF-1 and the association with conventional adenoma [18, 29]. Only one study by Janiak et al. observed low serum levels of IGF-1 in the tubular and tubulo-villous classifications of polyps, independent of location (proximal and distal colon), or size. The outcomes were significant for the type and size of polyps [46]. A study has noted modest race-specific differences regarding the association between IGF-1 and adenomas. Particularly, researchers observed a link between circulating levels of IGF-1 and adenomas solely in African Americans (95% CI 1.06–2.68). However, in this study, no statistically significant association was found in CRC samples (P: Caucasians 0.22, African Americans 0.450) [41]. However, three additional studies involving 53–239 cases have shown no significant difference in IGF-1 levels between the adenoma and control groups (P > 0.05) [42, 73, 80].

IGFBP3 and CRC risk

The association between CRC and IGFBP3 expression levels has been examined in twenty four studies (Table 3). Among these, nine studies identified a statistically significant decrease in IGFBP3 protein levels compared to normal groups and the remaining ten studies showed no significant difference in IGFBP3 levels between CRC and normal groups.

According to the Multiethnic Cohort study, a variety of factors can impact IGFBP3 levels. The levels of IGFBP3 vary significantly based on participants’ racial or ethnic backgrounds, regardless of gender (p < 0.01). Additionally, no significant associations were found between total fat or carbohydrate intake and IGFBP-3 levels. However, a higher BMI was associated with reduced IGFBP-3 levels (p < 0.01). While dietary and lifestyle factors influence IGF-I levels, IGFBP-3 appears to be less sensitive to variations in dietary macronutrients. Ethnic and gender differences also affect IGFBP-3 levels, as a higher BMI corresponds to lower levels of this binding protein, which modulates IGF-I activity and has implications for cancer risk [75]. The levels of IGFBP3 in the CRC patient group were notably lower than those of the control group, indicating a statistically significant distinction (P ≤ 0.008) between the two groups [22, 30, 31, 38]. Based on two additional research studies, this outcome has been reaffirmed, although with decreased statistical significance (P ≤ 0.05) [27, 36]. In the study by Suzuki et al., the odds ratio for the highest versus the lowest quantile of IGFBP-3 levels was reported, yielding a p-value of less than 0.05. This indicates a statistically significant association between elevated IGFBP-3 levels and a decreased risk of CRC [57]. In addition, in Nedić et al.‘s study, the serum concentrations of IGFBP3 in patients with CRC were half as low as those in healthy individuals [44]. In the study conducted by Jiang et al., it was found that serum levels of IGFBP-3 were significantly lower in CRC patients compared to healthy controls. The specific results indicated that the level of IGFBP-3 was significantly decreased, with a p-value of P < 0.001 when comparing pre-operative patients to normal controls. Additionally, post-operative patients exhibited lower levels of IGFBP-3, which correlated with an increased association with of CRC. The exact p-value is not provided in the search results [39]. Furthermore, Palmqvist et al. reported that elevated serum levels of IGFBP-3 were associated with a reduced risk of CRC (P = 0.24). This result for colon cancer was significant (P = 0.02) [78]. Wei et al. reported an inverse relationship, although not statistically significant, between the concentration of IGFBP3 and the likelihood of developing colon cancer during subsequent follow-up periods [71]. In another study, the average levels of IGFBP3 in both colon and rectal cancer cases were slightly lower than those in the control group; However, these differences were not statistically significant [76].

According to Ollberding et al. ‘s research, individuals with the highest prediagnostic IGFBP3 levels were less likely to develop CRC (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.34–0.83; Ptrend = 0.003). Moreover, the risk was elevated for those with a [OR (T1 vs. T3), 1.58; 95% CI, 1.09–2.28; Ptrend = 0.011] [47]. Conversely, In a fully-adjusted analysis, higher levels of IGFBP3 were linked to a greater incidence of CRC (SHR = 1.20, 95% CI 1.01–1.43; P = 0.041 for each 1 SD increase in IGFBP3). This increase in IGFBP3 was independently associated with a higher risk of CRC in community-dwelling men aged 70 and older, primarily due to an elevated risk to develop colon cancer within the first 5 years of follow-up [32]. Otani et al. found that higher levels of IGFBP-3 were significantly associated with a reduced risk of CRC. The study revealed a protective effect of 0.62 (95% confidence interval: 0.46–0.83) for CRC risk per one standard deviation increase in IGFBP-3 levels, with a p-value of less than 0.01 [68].

In a study conducted by Gunter et al., higher levels of IGFBP-3 in postmenopausal women were associated with a 15% reduction in the risk of CRC for every 1 ng/mL increase, with a p-value of 0.02. This finding suggests a protective effect against CRC [64].

El Atiq et al. found that the concentration of IGFBP3 in the serum of CRC patients (4.2 ± 0.6) was significantly higher than in healthy elderly individuals (0.8 ± 0.12; P < 0.0005) and represented 60% of the normal adult value (6.9 ± 0.6; P < 0.002) [87]. In a study examining the impact of increased physical activity through a home-based exercise program on biomarkers in stage II–III CRC patients, the baseline level of IGFBP3 was 2.67 ± 1.48 µg/ml, with a p-value of 0.013 for the effect after 12 weeks of activity [45].

Hang et al. conducted a study on American citizens and did not identify any statistically significant association when looking at plasma IGFBP3 levels [29]. In the study by Kaaks et al., higher levels of IGFBP-3 were associated with a reduced risk of CRC in the female group [84]. In other studies, it has been suggested that IGFBP-3 levels may have a protective effect against the development of CRC [50, 62, 86].

IGFBP3 and CRPCLs

The association between CRPCLs and IGFBP3 expression levels has been examined in twelve studies (Table 3). Three of them have found that IGFBP3 levels in their serum was associated with the presence of colon polyps.

In one study, Caucasians with colorectal adenomas had significantly higher IGFBP3 serum levels than African Americans (all P < 0.002). Additionally, Caucasian patients had higher IGFBP3 levels than Caucasian controls (P = 0.02) [41]. In a study by Janiak et al. patients with adenomatous polyps had markedly higher IGFBP3 levels in their blood serum post-irradiation than the control group [46].

However, in another study conducted in the UK, individuals with high-risk adenomas exhibited significantly lower serum IGFBP3 levels compared with those with low-risk adenomas and combined normal colonoscopy results (P = 0.14/high risk with two group: 0.05) [80]. In seven studies, no correlation was found between serum IGFBP3 levels and developing adenomas [18, 37, 42, 72, 73, 77, 82].

IGF-2 and CRC risk

The relationship between CRC and IGF-2 serum levels have been most intensely investigated (Table 4). IGF-2 has been assessed in eight different studies, two of which have found an association between IGF-2 levels and CRC but no significant results were obtained in four studies. A study was conducted with two subclassifications based on adenoma/carcinoma location. The study found that IGF-2 levels were significantly higher in patients with rectal adenoma and/or carcinoma (638.97 ± 173.66) compared to those with colonic tumors (493.80 ± 170.78, P < 0.05) [70]. A case–control study found a significant correlation between IGF-2 levels and CRC (P < 0.0005) [87]. Conversely, the results of three case-control studies do not suggest any association between IGF-2 levels and CRC [38, 48, 89]. Furthermore, Liou et al. identified that IGF-2 levels were similar between CRC patients and controls, indicating that IGF-2 levels are not useful in distinguishing between CRC patients and controls. IGFBP-3 levels can have a protective effect against the development of CRC, levels of IGF-2 were higher in patients with stage II and III disease compared to stage I disease patients [53] suggesting a stage-related connection.

IGF-2 and CRPCLs

The relationship between polyp formation and IGF-2 protein levels has been extensively studied (Table 4). Additionally, five studies have examined the link between circulating IGF-2 levels and the association with CRPCLs. Two of them have found that serum IGFBP3 levels were associated with developing colon polyps and three others not. It has been reported that patients with adenomatous polyps have significantly elevated levels of IGF-2 (P = 0.0001) [82]. Fuchs et al.‘s study reveals that there is a significant association between higher initial IGF-2 levels and a reduced risk of disease progression (P = 0.0005) [65]. IGF-2 levels were lower in patients with advanced (990.8 ng/ml) and stage I (944.5 ng/ml) colon polyps compared to normal controls [53]. However, the association between IGF-2 serum levels and colorectal adenoma risk was not corroborated by other studies [73]. A similar observation was made by Gao et al. when they found that the elevated risk of advanced colorectal adenoma remains consistent even after accounting for IGF2, with minimal alteration [49]. Kang et al. have demonstrated that there were no significant differences in recurrent adenomas and non-recurrent adenomas (P = 0.57) [42].

IGF-1R and CRC risk

A study conducted by Chen and Hong examined the relationship between age and serum levels of IGF-1 and IGF-1R in the development of CRC among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Their findings indicated significant associations between serum IGF-1 and IGF-1R levels and the development of CRC in T2DM patients (P < 0.05). Additionally, serum levels of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) were significantly higher in CRC patients with T2DM aged 55 years or older compared to those younger than 55 (85.70 ± 9.3 pg/ml vs. 69.67 ± 15.12 pg/ml), demonstrating a statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.027) [20].

IRS1 and CRC risk

IRS is proposed to play a central role in determining tumor cell responses to microenvironmental signals, including growth factors and hormones. Constitutive activation of IRS-1 has been observed in CRC [90]. A study by Hanyuda, A., et al. suggests that the benefits of physical activity after a CRC diagnosis may depend on the level of IRS1 expression in the tumor. The study indicates potential benefits for individuals with low or no IRS1 expression, and possibly adverse effects for those with high IRS1 expression (P = 0.005) [35].

Association of IGF-1, IGF-2 and IGFBP3 with OS

A cohort study by Fuchs et al. involving 527 metastatic CRC patients undergoing first-line chemotherapy found that higher plasma levels of IGF-1, IGF-2, and IGFBP3 were associated with improved overall survival (OS) (P < 0.0001). However, Increased baseline circulating IGFBP3 levels were significantly associated with a longer time to tumor progression (P = 0.03), while circulating IGF-1 levels showed no association with disease progression (P = 0.95) [65].

Haydon et al. worked on 526 CRC samples. They found that when all cases of IGF-1 and IGFBP3 levels were analyzed together, IGF-1 levels were not found to be associated with CRC-specific survival, as evidenced by a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.95 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.80–1.14). In contrast, IGFBP3 demonstrated a HR of 0.87 (95% CI 0.74–1.03) concerning CRC-specific survival. Notably, an increase in IGFBP3 levels was associated with a 32% reduction in mortality, yielding an HR of 0.68 (95% CI 0.51–0.92; P = 0.01). Similarly, an increase in IGF-1 correlated with a 33% reduction in mortality, reflected by an HR of 0.67 (95% CI 0.48–0.93; P = 0.02) [69].

Wolpin et al. conducted a cohort study of 373 patients with non-metastatic CRC and found no association between mortality and IGF-1 or IGFBP3, two components of the IGF axis [54].

In a case-control study, Bademler et al. identified IGFBP3 serum levels as the statistically significant predictor of survival. Patients with higher IGFBP3 levels (median OS: 48 months) had significantly longer survival than those with lower levels (P < 0.001). In contrast, there was no difference in the OS of patients in terms of IGF-1 and IGF-1/IGFBP3 serum levels (P = 0.193, P = 0.579). (IGF-1 median OS in months (95% CI): P = 0.193 and IGFBP3 median OS in months (95% CI): P < 0.001) [30].

Through univariate analysis using Cox regression, Chen et al. discovered that IGF-1 (HR = 2.30; P = 0.007) is significantly associated with OS [20].

Liou et al. found that patients with higher plasma IGF-2 levels had a significantly better survival rate (P = 0.032). At the same time, IGF-2 loss of imprinting was significantly associated with worse OS (P = 0.003) in patients with stage IV disease. Furthermore, in CRC patients, an association between higher plasma IGF-2 levels and improved OS was observed, whereas loss of IGF-2 imprinting in stage IV CRC was linked to a worse OS [53].

In a study by Haydon, A.M. et al., individuals with a 26.2 nmol/L increase in IGFBP3 had a 48% reduced risk of CRC deaths over a median follow-up of 5.6 years (adjusted HR 0.52, 95% CI 0.33–0.83; P = 0.006). IGF-1 was not significantly associated with the reduced risk (adjusted HR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.55–1.45, P = 0.65). Physical activity was not associated with differences in IGF-1 or IGFBP3 levels and survival for those with specific diseases [69].

In a study by Chen and Hong, it was investigated that IGF-1 and IGF-1R were found to be correlated with AGEs in CRC patients with T2DM. IGF-1 (HR = 2.30; P = 0.007) and IGF-1R (HR = 1.00, P = 0.004) were significantly associated with overall survival [20]. It is important to note that the articles reviewed in this study did not mention the relationship between Progression-Free Survival (PFS) and IGF levels; therefore, this relationship was not further investigated.

Meta-analysis result

IGF-1 and CRC risk

A total of 25 studies were included for the investigation of protein measures in IGF-1 CRC patients and compared with control group. Heterogeneities were assessed within and between study designs for Africa (I2 = 99.3%, p = 0.001), America (I2 = 83.4%, p = 0.001), Asia (I2 = 97.6%, p = 0.001), Europe (I2 = 94.1%, p = 0.001), and overall (I2 = 96.4%, p = 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Therefore, the use of a random-effect model for the analysis was confirmed. Pooled effect analysis indicated a significant higher protein level in CRC patients (WMD (95% CIs) = 11.24 (4.18, 18.30), p = 0.002). However, subgroup analyses by continent did not show statistically significant differences in any geographic region (Fig. 2A). Given the substantial heterogeneity among the studies, results should be interpreted with caution, and the observed association should not be considered a definitive relationship.

IGFBP3 and CRC risk

A total of 18 studies were included for the investigation of protein measures in IGFBP3 CRC patients and compared with control group. Heterogeneities were assessed within and between study designs for America (I2 = 67.5%, p = 0.005), Asia (I2 = 95.3%, p = 0.001), Europe (I2 = 95.4%, p = 0.001), and overall (I2 = 93.3%, p = 0.001) (Fig. 3A). Pooled effect analysis indicated a smaller and statistically non-significant difference in protein levels in CRC patients (WMD (95% CIs) = −138.20 (−231.77, −44.64), p = 0.004). Moreover, after applying subgroup analysis, a significant difference was observed for Africa (WMD (95% CIs) = 1157.11 (718.62, 1595.60), p = 0.001), and Asia (WMD (95% CIs) = −392.37 (−685.29, −99.45), p = 0.009) (Fig. 3A). In light of the considerable heterogeneity across studies, cautious interpretation is warranted, and the association cannot be regarded as conclusive.

Publication bias

To investigate the potential publication bias in this study, funnel plots and Egger’s test analyses were performed (Supplementary file 1). The findings indicated no significant publication bias in most meta-analyses (IGF-1 CRC: p = 0.384; IGF-2 CRC: p = 0.766; IGF-1 polyp: p = 0.083; IGFBP3 polyp: p = 0.998), except for one analysis (IGFBP3 CRC: p = 0.043).

In light of this finding, we performed a trim-and-fill analysis specifically for the IGFBP3–CRC association (Supplementary File 1). Prior to adjustment, the pooled effect size was statistically significant (WMD [95% CI] = − 138.20 [–231.77, − 44.64]; p = 0.004). The trim-and-fill procedure identified six potentially missing studies and after adjusting for these imputed studies, the pooled WMD didn’t remain statistically significant (WMD [95% CI] = 39.11 [–63.95, 142.16]; p = 0.457). However, Egger’s test after adjustment showed no evidence of publication bias (p = 0.930) (Supplementary file 1).

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the reliability of the findings from the primary data analyses. This involved systematically excluding one study at a time and examining the effect on the overall results of the meta-analysis. The pooled WMD and 95% confidence intervals for the comparison of protein measurements between patients and the control group remained consistent throughout the analysis, indicating that the results are stable and not overly impacted by any individual study (Supplementary file 1).

Besides, for the primary biomarker-outcome relationship (IGF-1 and CRC), which constituted the main focus of our synthesis and included the largest number of studies, we re-fitted the meta-analysis before and after removing high-influence studies. Although certain individual outlier studies could potentially compromising the integrity of the overall results, no such undue impact was observed in this case. The influential analysis showed no change in the statistical significance or direction of the pooled effect (Before: WMD (95% CIs) = 11.24 (4.18, 18.30), p = 0.002; After: WMD (95% CIs) = 5.96 (1.24, 10.68), p = 0.013), indicating robustness of our findings (Supplementary file 1).

Discussion

The onset of CRC arises from changes in the normal colonic lining, which can lead to the formation and growth of adenomatous polyps. These polyps can proliferate and increase in size, leading to the gradual accumulation of genetic and epigenetic mutations over time [91]. Substantial evidence shows that the IGF/insulin signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the development and progression of various cancers, making it a significant prognostic factor for patients [92, 93].

The reports show a statistically significant relationship between IGF-1, IGF-2, IGF-1/IGFBP3 ratio, and insulin to the association with adenoma [29, 49] and CRC [94, 95]. Additionally, elevated IGF-1 and IGF-1R levels can lead to increased cell growth, survival, and resistance to cell death, promoting tumor progression [96]. The IGF system promotes CRC growth by activating pathways like MEK1/2 and p70S6K1, enhancing tumor cell survival [97]. Impairment in the regulation of the IGF axis plays a significant role in both the proliferation of cells and the inhibition of apoptosis. This disruption further influences cellular transformation by affecting the synthesis of various regulatory proteins [98]. Over the years, extensive research has focused on elucidating the significance of the IGF axis, particularly regarding its potential influence on cancer risk. Despite the breadth of investigation in this area, the relationship between genes associated with the IGF axis and CRC risk remains inadequately understood, with studies presenting conflicting findings. These discrepancies may be influenced by various factors, including sample size, ethnicity, sex, age of participants, changes in residency, lifestyle choices, and dietary habits. In our analysis, we further strengthened this perspective by demonstrating that, although the overall trend supports an association between the IGF axis and colorectal carcinogenesis, including precancerous lesions, the pooled effects of the WMDs should be interpreted with great caution due to the considerable heterogeneity observed across studies [4]. In the current study, we conducted a systematic study and meta-analysis based on the previous findings to obtain a more accurate estimation of the correlation between the genes associated with the IGF pathway (IGF-1, IGF-2, IGFBP3, IGF1R and IRS1) and the risk of CRC and precancerous polyps. Sensitivity analyses were performed by systematically excluding individual studies in various CRC comparisons.

An important gene in this pathway is IGF-1, which can potentially increase the association with CRC by influencing cell growth and programmed cell death [99]. Research has shown that higher serum levels of IGF-1 are associated with a greater risk of CRC, likely due to its significant role in promoting cancer cell growth. IGF-1 is produced within different tissues, including skeletal muscle, and regulates tissue growth through local paracrine and autocrine actions [25, 100]. Moreover, IGF-1 stimulates cellular proliferation and inhibits apoptosis. Consequently, it can be inferred that abnormal expression of the IGF-1 gene contributes to the development and progression of CRC [99, 101]. Our research findings indicate a significant correlation between elevated serum levels of IGF and an increased association with developing CRC. These results are consistent with a previous study that also identified a link between IGF levels and cancer susceptibility [20]. This supports the idea that the IGF pathway plays a crucial role in the risk and development of cancer [102]. However, there were conflicting results regarding the relationship between IGF-1 serum levels and the association with developing CRC, with outcomes showing contrast, where low levels of IGF-1 were significant [23]. A recent study has identified a decrease in serum IGF-1 levels among patients with CRC compared to a control group. This decline in IGF-1 levels has been associated with the downregulation of the MEG3 gene, which is recognized as significant in the pathophysiology of CRC. Furthermore, obese CRC patients demonstrated notably lower levels of IGF-1 and significantly higher levels of IGFBP3. These observed variations may be influenced by genetic differences, laboratory methodologies, or other environmental factors [19]. The relationship between IGF1 levels, tumor stage, and lymph node metastasis in non-obese CRC patients is presented [19]. Previous studies reported a statistically non-significant association between CRC odds and IGF-I levels. In individuals with polyps, variations in IGF-I levels were observed, with higher levels reported in cases compared to controls [103], lower levels of IGF-1 in polyps [46], and no significant changes [42]. However, our meta-analysis study revealed a significant relationship between IGF serum levels and CRC, indicating a more pronounced IGF-1 measurement in CRC patients. Subgroup analysis also showed a considerable difference in Asia with significantly increased IGF-1 levels in polyp patients. Further subgroup analysis demonstrated significant differences in America and Europe.

Another crucial gene in this pathway is IGFBP3, which has been demonstrated to impact the risk of CRC. IGFBP3 controls the availability of IGF-1 by binding to it, thereby regulating IGF-1’s influence on cell proliferation and apoptosis. Alterations or polymorphisms in the IGFBP3 gene could disrupt this equilibrium, potentially increasing association with CRC. Studies have suggested that abnormal IGFBP3 levels could disrupt this control, creating cellular conditions more favorable for cancer development [104, 105]. A recent study found that IGFBP3 levels were significantly lower in patients with CRC compared to the control group. This decrease in IGFBP3 levels was particularly evident in patients with liver and lymph node metastasis, those with lymphovascular invasion, and individuals with a family history of the disease. Furthermore, the study demonstrated a significant negative correlation between IGFBP3 levels and tumor size, consistent with the anticipated anti-tumorigenic properties of IGFBP3. Previous epidemiological studies exploring the association between IGFBP3 and CRC have presented conflicting results, including inverse, positive, and null findings [22]. Reduced IGFBP3 levels, through various mechanisms across different carcinomas, have been linked to an increased association with cancer, either dependent on or independent of IGF signaling [31]. IGF-1 and IGFBP3 showed a strong correlation, while there was a moderate inverse correlation between age and total IGF-1, as well as age and IGF-1 adjusted for IGFB-3 levels [19]. In women, a single measurement of circulating IGF-1 and IGFBP3 levels was found to predict the risk of CRCs and large or villous adenomas. However, there was no consistent relationship between these biomarkers and small tubular adenomas. This suggests that the IGF axis may play a more significant role in later stages of adenoma growth and transformation, or that only certain adenomas are responsive to IGF. Large or villous adenomas are clinically significant, while smaller, tubular adenomas are of uncertain relevance [21]. Our findings demonstrated a significant correlation between low serum IGFBP3 levels and a strong association with CRC development [27], consistent with a study that identified a link with cancer susceptibility. However, the relationship between IGFBP3 levels and CRC risk has produced conflicting results, with some studies showing significant outcomes at higher IGFBP3 levels [47]. Additionally, earlier research found no statistically significant association between IGFBP3 levels and the risk of CRC. Variations in IGFBP3 levels have also been noted in polyps, with levels being higher in cases compared to controls [46], lower levels in some polyps [80], and no significant changes in others [72]. However, our meta-analysis revealed a significant relationship between IGFBP3 serum levels and CRC, indicating that IGFBP3 levels were significantly lower in CRC patients. Furthermore, a significant difference was observed for the African cohort after conducting a subgroup analysis.

The results for polyps indicated a non-significant IGFBP3 measurement in patients with polyps. In addition, after applying a subgroup analysis, no significant differences were observed across any continent.

One of the most important genes in this axis is IGF-2, which is another growth factor similar to IGF-1. It plays a crucial role in cell growth and development [24]. It has been implicated in the promotion of tumorigenesis through its action on IGF1R, although it is less studied compared to IGF-1. IGF-2 can activate IGF1R and has autocrine and paracrine effects that contribute to cancer progression. Overexpression of IGF-2 has been observed in CRC tissues. IGF-2 is involved in regulating cell proliferation and apoptosis, both of which can influence tumor development and progression. Its overexpression has been linked to an increased association with various cancers, including CRC, due to its role in promoting cell growth and inhibiting programmed cell death. This pathway can be critical for understanding cancer mechanisms and developing targeted therapies [106, 107]. Our current results are consistent with a previous study that reported a high level of IGF-2 in the serum of CRC patients [108]. However, there are conflicting findings in the literature, with some studies indicating low levels of IGF-2 linked to CRC [48]. Comprehensive information on the serum levels of IGF-2 in probands with polyps is lacking. One study showed higher levels of IGF-2 in cases compared to controls [48], while another study reported lower levels of IGF-1 in different polyp classifications [53]. Our meta-analysis using a random-effects model revealed a non-significant decrease in protein levels in CRC patients.

IGF-1R is primarily located in the digestive system and is involved in the proliferation of colon crypts [104]. The activation of IGF-1 and IGF-1R is associated with cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis, linking them to the onset and progression of cancer, as well as poor survival outcomes [109].

IRSs function as scaffold proteins that facilitate the IGF/insulin signaling pathway. IRS1 is a key adaptor protein that transmits signals from the IGF1R to downstream signaling pathways. High expression levels of IRS-1 serve as positive prognostic factors for colon cancer, particularly in stages I–III. It emphasizes the relevance of these proteins in predicting favorable outcomes and their potential roles in cancer development [110]. Overexpression or enhanced activity of IRS1 can lead to increased cell proliferation and survival, contributing to cancer development [111]. IRS2, like IRS1, is an adaptor protein that mediates signaling from IGF1R and insulin receptors. Its role in cancer is context-dependent, but overexpression of IRS2 has been associated with aggressive cancer phenotypes [112]. They play essential roles in regulating cell growth, proliferation, and metabolism. IRS proteins do not have intrinsic kinase activity; instead, they function by organizing signaling complexes that initiate downstream intracellular signaling cascades. This makes them vital for transmitting signals from insulin and IGF receptors to downstream effectors, which control cellular activities such as glucose uptake and protein synthesis [90, 113]. A significant limitation observed in this study is the limited number and variable quality of available research on IRSs, particularly IRS2, in the serum of CRC patients. Further research is recommended to yield more comprehensive and reliable results. It is crucial to highlight that certain genetic variations in IGFRs and IRSs were not encompassed in the meta-analysis due to the limitations in sample size. In our systematic review, we have diligently included all pertinent research related to our research questions and performed a comprehensive quality assessment for each study. To our knowledge, this review makes a significant contribution to the current understanding of how alterations in the IGF/insulin pathway are associated with CRC risk. Furthermore, we illustrate connections between various clinical outcomes, including overall survival. There are several limitations to this study. First, that clinical data are often insufficient or unspecific. Our research was grounded in studies published in indexed journals, which could introduce potential biases, particularly related to time lag and publication bias. Time-lag bias may occur when studies yielding negative results are published at a slower pace compared to those with positive outcomes. In terms of publication bias, it is possible that studies with small sample sizes and unfavorable findings are underrepresented, while those with small sample sizes and positive results are more quickly disseminated. Additionally, our literature search was confined to English-language publications, which introduces the possibility of English language bias. Furthermore, non-differential misclassification may be an issue, as it is conceivable that control groups in the study could develop cancer in the future. It is also important to note that our data were based on unadjusted results. A more comprehensive analysis, which accounts for confounding factors such as age, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and other environmental influences, could yield a more accurate estimation. Additionally, we conducted a meta-analysis to quantify the impact of serum protein levels on CRC risk. Although studies indicate that fasting status can influence circulating IGF-I levels, the magnitude of this effect depends on the study population and the duration of fasting. In short-term fasting studies (8–12 h), reductions in IGF-I are generally modest and sometimes not statistically significant, whereas longer fasting periods or severe energy restriction can lead to substantial and significant decreases in IGF-I [68]. These findings suggest that differences in fasting protocols and blood sample timing may contribute to variability across studies and help explain the limitations of the available data in our meta-analysis. Therefore, standardization and precise reporting of fasting status and sample collection time are essential in future studies.

The results of the meta-analysis provide a comprehensive evaluation of the association between IGF family protein levels and CRC risk, highlighting notable trends across various populations and study designs. Elevated levels of IGF-1 were consistently linked to an increased risk of CRC,

Overall, the certainty of evidence ranged from low to moderate. IGF-1 showed a consistent positive association with CRC and polyp risk, though heterogeneity and inconsistent reporting of fasting or assay conditions reduce confidence. IGFBP-3 tended to be lower in CRC but results were highly variable. IGF-2 did not demonstrate a significant relationship. Standardized reporting and adjustment for pre-analytical factors are required to strengthen future evidence (Table 5). Subgroup analyses by continent revealed some variations; however, no consistent geographic pattern emerged. Significant differences were observed across studies, likely due to variations in study design, assay methods, fasting status, and timing of sample collection. This highlights the need for careful interpretation of the observed associations.

Conclusion and future perspective

Recent studies highlight those elevated levels of IGF-1, IGF-2, IGF1R and IRS1 are associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer, as these factors promote tumor growth and survival. Conversely, IGFBP-3, which usually inhibits IGF activity, is found at lower levels in colorectal cancer, reducing its protective effect. This imbalance in the IGF signaling pathway contributes significantly to the development and progression of CRC.​.

In summary, our findings suggest that certain IGF-related factors are associated with an increased risk of CRC, making them potential diagnostic biomarkers for the disease. Identifying specific genetic variants linked to this increased risk could guide future research focused on prevention and treatment strategies, potentially leading to the development of personalized medicine approaches that target interventions for at-risk populations. However, considering the limitations of this study, such as sample size and population diversity, larger epidemiological studies involving a broader range of populations and incorporating more comprehensive assessments are necessary to validate these findings. Additionally, the limited information on early CRC detection and the need to consider gene-environment and gene-gene interactions highlight the importance of further research to improve early detection methods.
