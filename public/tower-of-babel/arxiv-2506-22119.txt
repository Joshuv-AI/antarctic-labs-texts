Harder, shorter, sharper, forward: A comparison of women’s and men’s elite football gameplay (2020-2025)
Rebecca Carstens, Raj Deshpande, Pau Esteve, Nicolò Fidelibus, Sara Linde Neven, Ramona Ottow, Lokamruth K. R., Paula Rodríguez-Sánchez, Luca Santagata, Javier M. Buldú, Brennan Klein, Maddalena Torricelli

Abstract
Elite football is believed to have evolved in recent years, but systematic evidence for the pace and form of that change is sparse. Drawing on event - level records for 13,067 matches in ten top - tier men’s and women’s leagues in England, Spain, Germany, Italy, and the United States (2020-2025), we quantify match dynamics with two views: conventional performance statistics and pitch - passing networks that track ball movement among a grid of pitch (field) regions. Between 2020 and 2025, average passing volume, pass accuracy, and the percent of passes made under pressure all rose. In general, the largest year - on - year changes occurred in women’s competitions. Network measures offer alternative but complementary perspectives on the changing gameplay in recent years, normalized outreach in the pitch passing networks decreased, while the average shortest path lengths increased, indicating a wider ball circulation. Together, these indicators point to a sustained intensification of collective play across contemporary professional football.

Football seasons can be quite diverse from year to year, due to structural shifts such as the transfers of key players or a change of the coach, and thereby the tactics. Performance patterns of a specific team can vary from year to year, especially for newly promoted clubs or following a large squad turnover. Some performance metrics are likely to improve throughout the season as players become accustomed to new coaching techniques or improve their physical health, while others are expected to decline due to the physical strain the system places on players. 2
Methods & Data

2.1
Dataset: Hudl Statsbomb Events Data

The data analyzed in this work, provided by a license from
Hudl StatsBomb
, encompasses data from more than 13,067 matches played between 2020 and 2025. These matches are from the football leagues of five countries: La Liga and Liga Femenina (Liga F) of Spain, the Premier League and FA Women’s Super League of England, the Serie A and Serie A Women of Italy, the Major League Soccer (MLS) and National’s Women’s Soccer League (NWSL) of the USA, and the Bundesliga and Frauen Bundesliga of Germany. For each match, the dataframe contains information on the starting lineup, and all
events
, which are actions occurring on the pitch, such as passes, fouls, and goal attempts, that occurred throughout the match. We chose to focus on events associated with the passes and the shots made. Passes are the main form of interactions for the players on the pitch, while shots made are the driving action in the match. These measures were divided into two different approaches: statistical- and network science-based measures. To illustrate our analytical approach and data structure,
Figure 1
presents a comprehensive schematic of match events data and network construction using a representative 2024 match between Arsenal and Liverpool, two top tier men’s teams in England. We classify team performance into three tiers (top, mid and bottom), corresponding to the upper, middle and lower third of the year and ranking. Within the following subsections, a description of which measures were taken, and their interpretation in the context of a football game, will be provided.

2.2
Statistical approach

We utilize a diverse set of basic and advanced performance metrics to capture the dynamics of matches across various leagues and seasons. The inclusion of fundamental statistics is crucial, as they not only provide interpretable insights into team strategies and playing styles but also serve as essential baseline features that complement more complex analytical measures. These fundamental metrics include the
number of passes
,
total shots
, and
shot accuracy
, all of which are core indicators of a team’s offensive intent and efficiency.

In addition to these, we incorporate several advanced, preprocessed measures such as
expected goals
(xG), which estimate the probability of a shot resulting in a goal based on historical outcomes of similar attempts. We also analyze passing sequences leading up to shots, and categorize pass height types—ground, low, and high—offering insight into how teams vary their buildup play. Derived metrics such as
passes before shot
(PBS) and
passes completed per possession
(PPP) are also included to quantify the link between passing networks and shot creation.

Furthermore, we develop novel features to enrich our analysis, for instance,
pass center of mass X
captures the average horizontal starting position of completed passes, providing a proxy for a team’s horizontal distribution and spatial tendencies in possession.
Shot distance
is calculated as the Euclidean distance from the location of shot initiation to the center of the goal, capturing the quality and risk associated with shooting decisions.

We also compute
passes under pressure percentage
, defined as the proportion of completed passes made under pressure, normalized over total pass attempts. This measure highlights a team’s ability to retain possession in high-intensity scenarios.
Average pass length
is determined by calculating the Euclidean distance between the initiation and completion coordinates of each pass, providing insight into a team’s preferred passing range. A comprehensive list of all statistical features employed, along with descriptions, is presented in
Table A.1
.

2.3
Network science approaches

These statistical measures were complemented and extended through the use of network analysis. A pitch passing network was produced for both teams in a match. This was produced by using the standardized pitch sizes and binning the pitch into an optimal number of regions. The 5-by-10 grid dimensions were chosen as the optimal number of regions for a pitch network is around 49 regions, which is supported by
[
42
]
. An example representation is shown in
Figure A.2
. In a network, the nodes represent the regions while the links represent the passes between these regions. These links are weighted and directional, indicating the flow and frequency of passes between regions throughout the entire match. To further simplify the network, the link weights were normalized by the total network strength.

We analyze the pitch networks by applying graph- and node-level measures that capture the structural characteristics of a match. For node-level measures, we further calculate the mean and standard deviation. These measures quantify how the ball flows between different pitch locations, revealing patterns of spatial connectivity and preferences in team tactics. A complete overview of all selected measures is provided in Appendix
Subsection A.3
.

3
Results

While various methods exist to study football’s development, our combined approach captures both broad performance trends and the structural dynamics of team play. The results are organized into three parts: first, we examine the evolution of passing, pace, and gameplay; next, we explore heterogeneities across league, season, and gender; and finally, we analyze pitch-passing networks to uncover shifts in spatial and organizational strategies over time.

3.1
Evolution of passing, pace, and gameplay

To further examine the tactical evolution trends identified in our analysis, we conducted a detailed investigation of passing statistics across different team performance tiers over the five-season period.
Figure 2
displays the results of the analysis of passing statistics across team tiers, showing that top teams consistently completed the highest average number of passes per year (approximately 550 on average), followed by mid-tier teams (around 450 on average), and bottom teams (around 400 on average). This ranking is mirrored in
pass accuracy
(the overall percentage of successful passes), with top teams achieving the highest accuracy, followed by mid-tier and bottom teams. Notably, there is no clear trend in the number of passes or pass accuracy across the five seasons analyzed.

The analysis of passing statistics revealed distinct patterns across team tiers and temporal trends (
Figure 3
). PPP, which quantifies the average number of completed passes during each team’s ball possession, demonstrate a clear hierarchy with top teams averaging the highest values but also exhibiting the greatest variability, followed by mid-tier teams, while bottom teams show the lowest average values with smaller variability. All three groups display increasing numbers in passes per possession over time, particularly in recent years. The percentage of passes under pressure, remained stable on average across all team tiers, suggesting similar overall exposure to pressure situations. However, all groups exhibit a pronounced temporal increase in passes under pressure, clearly evident through rising median values and expanding interquartile ranges across seasons. PBS, indicating the number of completed passes preceding an attempt to shoot a goal, followed the expected performance hierarchy with top teams recording the highest averages, followed by mid-tier teams, and bottom teams showing slightly lower values, though all three groups demonstrated similar variability. This measure shows, if anything, only a slight increase over time.

In general, the percentage of accurate ground passes is different between men’s and women’s leagues, with men’s leagues averaging between 90%-95% (
Figure 4
A) and women’s leagues averaging between 85%-90% (
Figure 4
B)—with percentages increasing gradually in recent years, across most men’s and women’s leagues. Similarly, the average distance between the shot positions and the goal has gradually decreased in recent years, across most men’s and women’s leagues (
Figure 4
C and
Figure 4

3.2
Heterogeneities across league, season, and gender

An advantage to the scope and size of the data used in this study, we are able to identify measures that uncovers heterogeneities across leagues, season, genders, and team performance.
Figure 5
shows the Percentage of completed passes under pressure for all leagues across team tiers and gender over the last five years, showing a clear increasing trend over the years for the majority of the leagues. In particular, for both English and Spanish leagues, independent of gender, this increase is especially consistent and strong. On the other hand, we find differences in the Italian and German leagues, where the change for the women’s leagues are more notable than for the male leagues.

The Italian Serie A Women league’s top teams have, on average, lower percentage of passes under pressure (approximately 10%), followed by slightly higher percentage for the mid tier team (approximately 11%), and bottom teams has the highest rate (approximately 12%). The corresponding male league has, on average, 10.5% of passes under pressure and does not show the increasing pattern as the women’s league (compare Panels
Figure 5
G and
Figure 5
H). Furthermore, the German Frauen Bundesliga’s mid-tier teams exhibit a large increase in the 2024-2025 season and the percentage of passes under pressure is for the last season even the highest across all years and genders (see mid tier pattern in
Figure 5

Neither the male or female leagues in the United States show a consistent increasing or decreasing percentage of passes under pressure year-to-year, nor consistent variability between team performance levels. The absence of consistent trends in passing under pressure within United States leagues likely reflects the distinct developmental trajectory of football in North America. As a younger and rapidly evolving football ecosystem, American leagues may be experiencing different phases of tactical maturation, where structural changes in league composition and coaching methodologies create more variable patterns rather than the linear evolutionary trends observed in established European competitions.

In sum, the scale of data used in this study offers more precise comparisons between leagues, seasons, genders, and team performance; we see significant evidence that the women’s game is changing—sometimes in ways that are commensurate with the men’s game and other times not. Along the particular measure used in this analysi, percentage of passes played under pressure, this suggests that both male and female games are becoming more physical or high-pressure in recent years.

In addition to the network outreach, there are a range of possible network measures that can characterize the pitch passing networks in this study.
Figure 7
A shows the distribution of largest eigenvalues of the pitch passing networks, separated by team tier (top, mid, bottom) and season, between the 2020-2021 and 2024-2025 seasons. Higher eigenvalues indicate a stronger and more robust passing network and structural cohesion of the overall ball passing of a match. Top teams exhibit the highest maximum eigenvalue values, while bottom teams show the lowest values, with mid-tier teams falling between these extremes. The variability in maximum eigenvalue is highest for top teams, moderate for mid-tier teams, and lowest for bottom teams. Across the five seasons, there is no consistent temporal trend for any tier team. However, bottom teams display a slight increase in maximum eigenvalue values during recent seasons from 2021-2022 to 2024-2025, suggesting a modest improvement in network robustness and structural cohesion among lower-performing teams over time.

Figure 7
B compares the
average shortest path lengths
across top, mid-tier, and bottom teams. The average shortest path length in a pitch passing network captures how easily the ball can circulate through the team’s passing structure, with shorter paths indicating more efficient ball circulation. On average, top teams exhibit the shortest passing path length, indicating more efficient passing networks and pointing to their ability of reaching any region of the pitch more easily. Mid-tier teams show slightly higher values, while bottom teams display the highest path lengths. Moreover, top teams show a less spread value distribution with less variability, mid-tier teams exhibit moderate spread, and bottom teams display the most dispersed distributions. In recent seasons, top teams demonstrate a clear increasing pattern in average shortest path length during recent years. This observation is aligned with the fact that the average pass (Euclidean) length has decreased during the last years, probably reducing the existence of shortcuts in the network. In contrast, mid-tier teams show no substantial change across the five seasons, while bottom teams exhibit a possible slight decrease in average shortest path length, indicating a potential improvement in their passing game.

While the aforementioned trends characterize the general evolution of football across our dataset, it is important to note that not all patterns hold uniformly across every league or gender division. Examining specific competitions reveals distinct evolutionary trajectories that merit individual consideration. For example, the Serie A Women’s league exhibits a markedly different pattern in pitch advancement compared to the aggregated trends (

Additionally, the variability in pitch advancement, as indicated by the interquartile range, decreases substantially over time, suggesting increased tactical consistency within the league. This contrasts with the relatively stable pitch advancement patterns observed in the aggregated data from other competitions, where median values remain consistent across seasons. Such league-specific variations highlight the heterogeneous nature of tactical evolution in football and emphasize the particular value of analyzing women’s competitions separately, as they may be undergoing unique developmental processes distinct from their male counterparts or other women’s leagues.

4
Discussion

This study analyzed an extensive dataset of football matches spanning the past five years across elite European and American leagues, including the English Premier League, Women’s Super League, Major League Soccer, National Women’s Soccer League, Bundesliga, Frauen Bundesliga, Serie A, Serie A Women, La Liga, and La Liga F. Our primary objective was to examine the evolution of football across different seasons while identifying meaningful differences between competitions and genders.

To address these research questions, we employed established statistical analysis and network-based methodologies from the sports analytics literature to illuminate patterns of change and consistency across seasons, leagues, and gender divisions. Our analytical approach operated at multiple levels, aggregating data by gender and league while stratifying teams into top-, mid-, and bottom-tier categories for each season. We conducted both cross-gender comparisons and within-gender analyses to draw comprehensive conclusions about competitive dynamics.

Our findings reveal particularly novel and significant insights regarding female competition, where we leveraged an unprecedented volume of match data including multiple seasons to uncover evolving gameplay patterns and strategic approaches to the sport. The comprehensive nature of our dataset, combined with our multi-tiered analytical framework, has enabled us to identify distinctive trends in how football has developed across different competitive contexts.

Our analysis reveals four fundamental trends that characterize the contemporary evolution of football across all competitive levels and genders. From the statistical perspective, pressure intensity is systematically increasing across seasons, indicating a sport-wide shift toward greater physicality in the game. Pass lengths are being progressively reduced, suggesting teams are prioritizing ball security over territorial advancement. Third, the number of passes per possession is steadily increasing, a trend that has been shown to be increasing from before our chosen time period, reflecting more patient, methodical build-up play
[
7
]
. Remarkably, these trends persist regardless of team quality, appearing consistently among top-, mid-, and bottom-tier teams across all leagues studied.

These findings point to a fundamental strategic paradigm shift in modern football: teams are prioritizing ball possession above all else. By reducing pass distances, teams minimize the risk of losing possession through interceptions or misplaced passes, even at the cost of immediate attacking progression. This conservative passing approach naturally extends possession sequences, resulting in longer, more sustained periods of ball control. The corollary to this possession-focused strategy is an intensification of pressing when out of possession. Teams seek to recover the ball as quickly as possible, implementing high-intensity pressing systems that mirror the tactical philosophies popularized by coaches like Pep Guardiola, whose influence on modern football’s tactical evolution toward possession-based, high-pressing play is evident in these league-wide trends
[
43
]
. This tactical convergence across different competitive tiers suggests that modern football’s strategic evolution transcends individual team resources or capabilities, representing a sport-wide adaptation to contemporary tactical thinking and player development methodologies.

The network-based analytical approach, based on well-established pitch network representations
[
42
]
, leverages network analysis to gain insights about trends and aspects of modern football. The analyses reveal that network parameters largely do not capture the temporal evolution of football over the past five years. Only network outreach shows a monotonic evolution, decreasing over the years, which aligns with our statistical finding of reduced pass lengths. However, network parameters effectively distinguish between top and bottom-tier teams. Two key results emerge: first, the largest eigenvalue is higher for top teams, indicating that elite teams possess stronger passing networks characterized by more passes and enhanced connectivity between high-centrality network regions
[
44
]
. Second, the average shortest path length is lower for top teams, revealing that elite teams move the ball more efficiently across pitch regions, with higher probability of reaching any area of the pitch.

While our general findings demonstrate consistent trends across competitive levels and genders, a closer examination of specific leagues reveals important nuances that merit attention. For instance, examining the Serie A Women’s league (
Figure 8
) shows a distinct evolutionary pattern in pitch advancement compared to the aggregated trends observed across other competitions. The Serie A Women displays a progressive increase in pitch advancement over the seasons from 2020-2025, with notably higher variability in the earlier seasons that stabilizes in recent years. In contrast, the aggregated data from other leagues shows relatively stable pitch advancement patterns with consistent variability across the same period. This divergence highlights the importance of league-specific analysis, particularly for women’s football, where tactical evolution may follow different trajectories than in established male competitions. Such findings underscore the value of our comprehensive dataset in capturing the unique developmental patterns within women’s football, suggesting that female competitions may be undergoing distinct tactical modernization processes that warrant dedicated analytical attention rather than broad generalizations across all competitive contexts.

Our results suggest that modern football is evolving toward greater intensity and tactical sophistication. The ability to maintain possession under increasing defensive pressure, combined with the rising number of passes per possession sequence, indicates that both individual player performance and team coordination are improving systematically. These developments enable teams to sustain ball control for extended periods while operating under heightened competitive pressure. Examining performance across different competitive tiers reveals that higher-ranked teams demonstrate superior ball retention capabilities and more efficient attacking sequences. Elite teams exhibit enhanced ability to maintain possession under pressure, though the relationship between pass quantity and scoring efficiency requires careful interpretation. Higher-ranked teams consistently show tactical advantages in their possession management and space utilization across the pitch. The trend of increasing passes per possession, documented from the 2008/09 season onward
[
45
]
, continues to characterize elite football, with our findings extending this pattern through recent seasons across multiple leagues and both male and female competitions.

Several methodological considerations warrant discussion. The comprehensive dataset utilized in this study provides unprecedented analytical opportunities, though data accessibility through proprietary licensing may present reproducibility challenges. The manual annotation methodology employed by data providers ensures high accuracy but may introduce minor observational limitations based on video frame visibility. Our pitch network analysis employed a 5×10 grid configuration (50 nodes total), selected based on established research demonstrating optimal analytical resolution for meaningful network insights while maintaining computational efficiency
[
42
]
.

These trends appear universally across competitive levels and gender divisions, suggesting sport-wide adaptation to modern tactical paradigms. The network analysis reveals that while temporal evolution is subtle at the network level, clear distinctions exist between teams of different competitive tiers, with elite teams exhibiting more efficient and connected passing networks. These findings provide valuable insights for coaches, analysts, and researchers seeking to understand the evolving landscape of modern football tactics and performance.

Future work.

The methodological framework developed in this study demonstrates considerable versatility and can be readily adapted to investigate tactical evolution at the team level, potentially revealing individual tactical signatures and club-specific patterns. Furthermore, the approach can be extended to incorporate additional football metrics such as defensive actions, set-piece analysis, or player positioning data, providing a flexible foundation for comprehensive football analytics across various competitive contexts.

Several limitations warrant consideration for future research directions. Extending the temporal scope of analysis would strengthen our findings, particularly for men’s football, where the professional landscape spans decades and may exhibit longer-cycle evolutionary patterns that our five-season window cannot fully capture. While our study provides valuable cross-gender insights, we recommend that future investigations place greater emphasis on identifying trends and patterns within each gender division rather than direct comparisons between them. This approach acknowledges that men’s and women’s football, while sharing fundamental tactical principles, operate within distinct professional ecosystems with different developmental trajectories and contextual factors that influence tactical evolution. The women’s professional game, being a more recently established competitive environment, may demonstrate unique evolutionary patterns that merit dedicated analytical attention. Additionally, future studies could benefit from normalizing metrics by effective playing time rather than total match duration, accounting for variations in ball-in-play time across different competitions and seasons, which would enhance the precision of temporal trend analysis
[
46
]
. This normalization is particularly important as effective time may be higher for teams with superior ball control and possession, while simultaneously accounting for the physical demands and fatigue accumulation that result from extended periods of active play, both of which can influence tactical performance and decision-making patterns
[
47
,
48
]
.

Future work can extend this study in several meaningful directions. Team-specific analysis using pitch passing networks offers a promising approach to identifying unique tactical identities and stylistic signatures. A deeper investigation into evolving trends, particularly the rapid transformation within women’s football leagues, warrants focused attention. Recent studies have highlighted increased investment, professionalization, and tactical sophistication in women’s football, all of which contribute to shifting dynamics in match play. Exploring how these changes manifest in passing networks over time could provide quantitative evidence of this evolution. The development of additional pitch network-based measures could enhance our ability to differentiate elite teams from others. Prior work
[
49
]
has shown that network topology correlates with competitive success, suggesting that more sophisticated descriptors may provide predictive power or deeper tactical insights. Furthermore, efforts should be made to validate and understand why the game is becoming increasingly physical, as suggested by rising averages in the percentage of passes made under pressure. Finally, applying machine learning techniques to pitch passing networks opens opportunities for both descriptive and predictive modeling.

A.2
Pitch division schema

The football pitch used in this analysis follows standard FIFA regulations with dimensions of 120 meters in length and 80 meters in width. For the purpose of constructing passing networks, the pitch was discretized into a 5×10 grid system, resulting in 50 distinct regions as illustrated in
Figure A.2
.
The discretization process divides the pitch into rectangular zones of equal size, with each region measuring 24 meters in length (120m ÷ 5 rows) and 12 meters in width (80m ÷ 10 columns). Each region is numbered sequentially from 1 to 50, starting from the top-left corner and proceeding row by row from left to right.
This grid-based approach enables the transformation of continuous positional data into discrete network nodes, where each region represents a potential node in the passing network. The passing connections between regions form the links of the network, with link weights determined by the frequency and characteristics of passes between corresponding zones.
The 5×10 configuration was selected to balance spatial resolution with analytical tractability, providing sufficient granularity to capture meaningful tactical patterns while maintaining a manageable number of network nodes for computational analysis. This discretization scheme allows for consistent comparison of passing networks across different matches and teams, as all positional data is standardized to the same grid system regardless of the actual pitch dimensions used in individual matches.

Pitch discretization grid system.
The football pitch is divided into a 5×10 grid of 50 equally-sized regions (24×12 meters each), numbered sequentially from 1 to 50.

A.3
Node-level measures

In the main text, we summarized node-level measures into network-level means and standard deviations. In our initial analyses, we computed the following measures:

Outreach
is defined as the sum of outgoing link weight times Euclidean distance of the link. The formula is

O_{\text{out}}(i)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}w(i,j)l(i,j)

(A.1)

where
l(i,j)
is the Euclidean distance. This measure represents a combination of passes between two regions, prioritizing distant but well-connected nodes.

In-Strength
is defined as the sum of weights of all incoming links to a node. The formula is

S_{in}(i)=\sum_{j\in V}w(j,i)

(A.2)

This metric represents the total number of passes received by a pitch position and measures how often the ball arrives at this location.

Out-Strength
is defined as the sum of weights of all outgoing links from a node. The formula is

S_{out}(i)=\sum_{j\in V}w(i,j)

(A.3)

This metric represents the total number of passes originated from a pitch position and measures how active this location is in ball distribution.

In-Closeness Centrality
is defined as the average distance from all other nodes to node
i
. The formula is

Cl_{\text{in}}(i)=\frac{1}{\sum_{j\neq v}d(j,i)}

(A.4)

where
d(j,i)
is the shortest path distance in number of steps. The distance of a link is the inverse of the link’s weight. This measure indicates how easily the ball arrives to node
i
from any other node in the pitch.

Out-Closeness Centrality
is defined as the average distance between node and all other nodes. The formula is

Cl^{\text{out}}(i)=\frac{1}{\sum_{j\neq i}d(i,j)}

(A.5)

where
d(i,j)
is the shortest path distance in number of steps. The distance of a link is the inverse of the link’s weight. This measure indicates how easily the ball arrives to any other node
j
in the pitch from node
i
.

Betweenness centrality
measures the extent to which nodes in the network are on paths between other nodes. The formula is

B(i)=\sum_{s,t\in V}\frac{\sigma(s,t|i)}{\sigma(s,t)}

(A.6)

where
\sigma(s,t)
is the number of total shortest paths between nodes
s
and
t
, and
\sigma(s,t|i)
is those passing through
i\notin\{s,t\}
. This metric identifies the most commonly passed through region of pitch.

Eigenvector centrality
of node
i
is obtained as the
i
th element of the eigenvector associated to the largest eigenvalue of the weighted adjacency matrix. The formula is

E(i)=v_{1}(i)

(A.7)

where
v_{1}
is the eigenvector associated to the largest eigenvalue of the weighted adjacency matrix. This measure represents the importance of the pitch-position within the team’s passing network. In programming implementation, a small offset is used to avoid problems with sinks by adding small weight to non-links.

A.4
Graph-level measures

In the main text, we compared the network outreach, largest eigenvalue, and the average shortest path lengths of the pitch passing networks. However, in our initial analyses, we computed the following measures:

Isolated nodes
are defined as the number of isolated nodes. The formula is

|I|=|{i\in V:deg(i)=0}|

(A.8)

This measure identifies which areas/positions are not used to pass or receive a ball.

Sink Nodes
are defined as the number of sink nodes. The formula is

S_{\text{sink}}(G)=|{i\in V:deg^{\text{out}}(i)=0,deg^{\text{in}}(i)>0}|

(A.9)

This measure identifies which areas/positions receive the ball, but never have outgoing passes.

Source Nodes
are defined as the number of source nodes. The formula is

S_{\text{source}}(G)=|{i\in V:deg^{\text{in}}(i)=0,deg^{\text{out}}(i)>0}|

(A.10)

This measure identifies which areas/positions pass the ball, but never have incoming passes.

Self-Loops
are defined as self-loops in the network. The formula is

L(G)=\{(i,i)\in E\}

(A.11)

This measure represents the number of passes that start and end in the same region. A ball is considered to start/end in a region with top-left corner at
(x_{\text{min}},y_{\text{max}})
and bottom-right corner at
(x_{\text{max}},y_{\text{min}})
if for its start/end-coordinates
(x_{B},y_{B})
holds
x_{\text{min}}<x_{B}\leq x_{\text{max}}
and
y_{\text{min}}\leq y_{B}<y_{\text{max}}
. The total number, the value of the maximum self-loop, and the average weight of self-loops are calculated.

Strength
is defined as the sum of weights of all links. The formula is

S(G)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w(i,j)

(A.12)

This measure represents the total number of passes.

Average Shortest Path Length
is defined as the average over all shortest paths of the network, where the distance of a link is the inverse of the link’s weight. The formula is

SP_{\text{avg}}(G)=\frac{n}{n-1}\sum_{i,j\in V:i\neq j}d(i,j)

(A.13)

with
d(i,j)
being the shortest path from node
i
to
j
, and
n
is the total number of nodes. This measure captures how efficiently the ball can circulate through the team’s passing structure.

Degree Assortativity
is defined as the correlation coefficient between (in-/out-) degrees of nodes and the (in-/out-) degrees of connected nodes. In directed networks, four different assortativities can be calculated according to the degree of the starting and ending node of a given link: in-in, out-out, in-out, out-in. The formula is

A(G)=\frac{\sum_{k}e_{kk}-\sum_{k}a_{k}b_{k}}{1-\sum_{k}a_{k}b_{k}}

(A.14)

where
e_{kk^{\prime}}
is the fraction of links in a network that connects a node of (in-/out-)degree
k
to one of (in-/out-)degree
k^{\prime}
, and
a_{k}
the sum of all links departing in a node with (in-/out-)degree
k
, and
b_{k^{\prime}}
the sum of the links ending in nodes with (in-/out-)degree
k^{\prime}
. This measure indicates whether pitch positions that start/receive many passes tend to connect to other positions that also start/receive many passes.

Largest Eigenvalue
is defined as the principal eigenvalue of the weighted adjacency matrix. The formula is

E_{\text{val}}(i)=\lambda_{1}(i)

(A.15)

where
\lambda_{1}
is the largest eigenvalue of the weighted adjacency matrix. The largest eigenvalue is related to the network strength, robustness, and structural cohesion.
