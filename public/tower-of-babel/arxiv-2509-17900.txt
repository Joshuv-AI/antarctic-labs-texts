The 3-strand braid group with torsion
Ethan Dlugie, Tahsin Saffat

Abstract
In the 1950s, H. S. M. Coxeter considered the quotients of braid groups given by adding the relation that all half Dehn twist generators have some fixed, finite order. He found a remarkable formula for the order of these groups in terms of some related Platonic solids. Despite the inspiring apparent connection between these “truncated” braid groups and Platonic solids, Coxeter’s proof boils down to a finite case check that reveals nothing about the structure present. We give a topological interpretation of the truncated 3-strand braid group that makes the connection with Platonic solids clear. One of our key tools is a formalism for orbifolds developed by A. Henriques that we think others would find interesting.

Introduction

The braid groups
B_{n}
are a ubiquitous family of groups in modern mathematics. These groups have “half twist” generators
\sigma_{1},\dotsc,\sigma_{n-1}
with braid relations between adjacent generators and commuting relations between non-adjacent generators. See for instance
[
Gon11
]
for an introduction to braid groups.

. The crossings considered from left-to-right match up with the word in the group read left-to-right.

In this paper, we will be considering what we call the
truncated braid groups
. Such a group, which we call
B_{n}(d)
, is the
n
-strand braid group with the extra relation that each generator
\sigma_{i}
has a fixed finite order
d\geq 2
.

B_{n}(d)=\left\langle\sigma_{1},\dotsc,\sigma_{n-1}\mid\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1},\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}\text{ if }|i-j|>1,{\color[rgb]{0.5859,0.457,0.8047}\sigma_{i}^{d}=1}\right\rangle

These braid group quotients, and similar quotients for mapping class groups, have received attention in the last few decades in low-dimensional topology, geometric group theory, and quantum topology
[
Hum92
,
MS25
,
DHS20
,
FK14
]
.
Already over half a century ago, Coxeter considered the truncated braid groups
[
Cox59
]
. He found that
B_{n}(d)
is finite in only a few exceptional cases. What is more, he discovered an incredibly remarkable formula for the order of the finite quotients.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
Cox59
]
)
.

The braid group quotient
B_{n}(d)
is finite if and only if
n=2
,
d=2
or the pair
(n,d)
is one of the five exceptional pairs

(3,3),(3,4),(3,5),(4,3),\text{ or }(5,3).

In such cases, the size of the quotient is given by the formula

|B_{n}(d)|=\left(\frac{f(n,d)}{2}\right)^{n-1}n!

(1)

where
f(n,d)
is the number of faces in the Platonic solid made of
n
-gons with
d
at each vertex.

See
Section

2
for a brief explanation of how to interpret this formula in the various cases.

Coxeter’s theorem hints at a tantalizing connection between braid groups and Platonic solids. So how does he prove that his formula counts what is claimed? Well, there are only five exceptional cases of finite
B_{n}(d)
. Each of these is a finitely presented group. Coxeter goes one-by-one and computes the order of
B_{n}(d)
either by recognizing the presentation of some known finite group or by running some coset enumeration algorithms. Then he computes the value given by the formula in (
1
) and checks that the values agree in all five cases.

If the reader is shocked at this proof, they are not alone. The present authors sought to find a more natural proof that connects the geometry and topology of braid groups with the geometry and combinatorics of Platonic solids. In the present work, we report on a new proof of
Theorem

1.1
in the
n=3
case that is purely topological. Specifically, we find the order of a central element in
B_{3}(d)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Delta^{2}=(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})^{3}=(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1})^{2}
denote the full twist braid in
B_{3}
. Then the order of
\Delta^{2}
in
B_{3}(d)
is
\frac{f(3,d)}{2}
. This value, and so the order of the full twist, is finite if and only if
2\leq d\leq 5

The quotient by the central element
B_{3}(d)/\langle\Delta^{2}\rangle
is isomorphic to the (orientation-preserving) triangle group
\Delta(2,3,d)
, giving the short exact sequence

1\to\langle\Delta^{2}\rangle\to B_{3}(d)\to\Delta(2,3,d)\to 1

(2)

Already in the 19th century mathematicians saw the connection between the abstract triangle groups and the Platonic solids
[
FK97
]
. It is classical that the order of
\Delta(2,3,d)
is given by
3\cdot f(3,d)
, and so the classical result together with
Theorem

1.2
yields Coxeter’s formula for the 3-strand braid group quotients. This is summarized later as
Corollary

5.9
.

Our proof of
Theorem

1.2
is purely topological. It makes use of the trefoil knot complement (
Section

3
), the topology of orbifolds (
Section

4
), and a long exact sequence of homotopy groups for an orbifold fibration (
Section

5
). We found that the standard treatments of orbifolds – by an atlas of charts with group actions, by Lie groupoids, or by topological stacks – were too cumbersome in various ways to allow us to use the tools we wanted to employ. In
Section

4
we explain the
Borel construction
employed in
[
Hen01
]
, which allows us in a way to simply treat orbifolds as topological spaces. This allows us to employ all of the standard tools of algebraic topology with no alterations. We employ these tools to prove
Theorem

1.2
in
Section

5
. Finally,
Section

6
contains some closing thoughts about our techniques.

2
Special cases of Coxeter’s formula

We take a moment to explain how to interpret Coxeter’s formula (
1
) in the cases when it is not immediately obvious, i.e. in the non-sporadic finite cases and in the generic infinite case. The key is to think of a Platonic solid not as a Euclidean polyhedron but as a regular tiling of the sphere.

Example 2.1

(The
n=2
case)
.

Here we have that the group

B_{2}(d)=\langle\sigma_{1}\mid\sigma_{1}^{d}\rangle

is just a finite cyclic group of order
d
. To interpret the quantity
f(2,d)
, we seek a tiling of the sphere by 2-gons with
d
of them at each vertex. This is just the decomposition of the sphere by
d
-many equally spaced meridional arcs, as in
Figure

3
.

Figure 2:
A tiling of the sphere by 2-gons cut out by meridional arcs.

Figure 3:
A tiling of the sphere by two congruent spherical polygons meeting at the equator.

There are
d
-many 2-gons in this tiling, so Coxeter’s formula gives

\left(\frac{f(n,d)}{2}\right)^{n-1}n!=\left(\frac{(d)}{2}\right)^{(2)-1}2!=d,

which is indeed the order of the finite cyclic group
B_{2}(d)
.

Example 2.2

(The
d=2
case)
.

Group theorists will quickly recognize from the presentation that
B_{n}(2)
is isomorphic to the symmetric group on
n
-elements. The quotient map
B_{n}\to B_{n}(2)
is exactly the canonical map that sends a braid to the permutation it induces on its strands.

For Coxeter’s formula, we seek a tiling of the sphere by
n
-gons with 2 at each vertex. The picture here is a simple splitting of the sphere by a great circle, with
n
equally spaced vertices along the great circle, as depicted in
Figure

3
. Now
f(n,2)=2
and Coxeter’s formula yields

\left(\frac{f(n,d)}{2}\right)^{n-1}n!=\left(\frac{(2)}{2}\right)^{n-1}n!=n!,

which is famously the order of the symmetric group.

Example 2.3

(An infinite case)
.

Consider the group
B_{5}(4)
. To compute the number
f(5,4)
, we seek a tiling composed of regular pentagons with 4 of them at each vertex. This is impossible on the sphere, but it is possible as a tiling of the hyperbolic plane as in
Figure

4
.

Figure 4:
A portion of the regular tiling of the hyperbolic plane by pentagons with 4 pentagons at each vertex.

Evidently then
f(5,4)=\infty
, which agrees with Coxeter’s assertion that
B_{5}(4)
is an infinite group.

It becomes clear now that all of the cases when
B_{n}(d)
is infinite correspond to the regular tilings of the Euclidean or hyperbolic planes. This point of view even works for the
d=\infty
case (i.e. the usual braid group) for which one would take a tiling of the hyperbolic plane by regular
ideal

n
-gons.

3
The 3-strand braid group and the trefoil knot

In this section we explain the connection between the 3-strand braid group and the trefoil knot, a classical story. We will upgrade this to a connection between the truncated braid group
B_{3}(d)
and an orbifold in the next section. We collect the necessary facts in a sequence of propositions.

Proposition 3.1
.

The 3-strand braid group is isomorphic to the fundamental group of the trefoil knot complement.

Proof.

There are several ways to see this result; we take the route of configuration spaces. Recall that the braid group is the fundamental group of the configuration space of
n
distinct, unlabeled points in the plane. We think of these points as complex numbers
z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
. By translating and dilating a collection of such points, we find that a homotopy equivalent configuration space is that for which the points have center of mass at the origin and which satisfy a chosen equation on their norms. For our
n=3
case then, we will recognize the 3-strand braid group as the fundamental group of the space

\left\{\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\}\subset\mathbb{C}\mid z_{i}\neq z_{j},z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3}=0,|z_{1}z_{2}+z_{2}z_{3}+z_{3}z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{1}z_{2}z_{3}|^{2}=1\right\}.

The last condition may seem strange, but it becomes more natural if we think of the three complex numbers as the roots of a monic cubic polynomial. Then our space is

\{z^{3}+az+b\mid\Disc_{z}(z^{3}+az+b)\neq 0,|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1\}.

where
\Disc_{z}(p(z))
is the discriminant of a polynomial
p
. In this case, this finally lets us see the space as

\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\mid|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1,-4a^{3}-27b^{2}\neq 0\}\subset S^{3}.

(3)

The space here is the subset of the unit 3-sphere
S^{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
that lies outside of some polynomial variety. The particular polynomial here cuts out a
(2,3)
-torus knot, better known as the trefoil.
∎

To go along with later notation, we give the following name to the space encountered in this proof.

Definition 3.2
.

Write
E_{0}=\{(a,b)\in C^{2}\mid|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1,-4a^{3}-27b^{2}\neq 0\}
for the subset unit 3-sphere in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
away from the discriminant locus.

Remark 3.3
.

We would also write
E\subset\mathbb{C}
for the entire 3-sphere, but we will construct
E
later as a space glued from
E_{0}
and a solid torus. We will recognize this glued space as the 3-sphere in
Lemma

4.8
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Any meridional loop around the trefoil knot represents the conjugacy class of a
\sigma_{i}
half-twist generator of
B_{3}
.

Proof.

All meridional loops around the knot are freely homotopic (up to orientation), and so they represent the same conjugacy class in the fundamental group. One such loop can be taken as the family of polynomials
p_{t}(z)=(z+1+1\exp(i\pi t))(z+1-1\exp(i\pi t))(z-1)
(with the roots rescaled so that the coefficients satisfy the norm condition in (
3
)). See
Figure

5
to see that this loop is indeed a meridian of the trefoil knot. This family of polynomials represents the braid generator
\sigma_{2}
that swaps two points with a right-handed half twist, which gives the proposition.

Figure 5:
A computer-generated image of the discriminant locus (black/white) in
S^{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
and the loop (red) traced out by a family of polynomials whose roots swap with a half Dehn twist. We see that the discriminant locus cuts out a trefoil knot in the 3-sphere and the polynomial family traces out a meridional loop.

∎

Proposition 3.5
.

The trefoil complement has an oriented Seifert fiber structure. The loop traced by the generic orbit represents the central twist
\Delta^{2}\in B_{3}
.

Proof.

An oriented Seifert fiber structure on a 3-manifold is equivalent to an action on the manifold by the circle group with finite point stabilizers. Here we consider
S^{1}\subset\mathbb{C}
as the unit complex numbers and use the action that multiplies a collection of points in
\mathbb{C}
by an element of
S^{1}
. The loop traced through configuration space by a generic orbit here clearly represents the full twist braid
\Delta^{2}\in B_{3}
. Considering how the action affects the coefficients of a degree 3 polynomial, we have

\zeta.(a,b)=(\zeta^{2}a,\zeta^{3}b).

(4)

The reader can verify that this action preserves the discriminant condition of (
3
), i.e. we have a well-defined action on the trefoil complement
E_{0}
. We think of this as a weighted version of the circle action that gives the Hopf fibration.

The only points in the 3-sphere that have non-trivial stabilizer under this action are points of the form
(a,0)
with stabilizer
\{-1,1\}\approx C_{2}
, and points of the form
(0,b)
with stabilizer
\{1,\exp(2\pi i/3),\exp(4\pi i/3)\}\approx C_{3}
.
∎

We record the definition of the circle action for future reference.

Definition 3.6
.

Define the action
S^{1}\curvearrowright S^{3}
by
\zeta\cdot(a,b)=(\zeta^{2}a,\zeta^{3}b)
. This also restricts to an action
S^{1}\curvearrowright E_{0}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

The base orbifold of the Seifert fibration is the
(2,3,\infty)
triangle orbifold.

Proof.

The “base orbifold” of a Seifert fibration is the quotient orbifold of the circle action, with orbifold points corresponding to the exceptional orbits having nontrivial stabilizer. Here we have pairs of complex numbers considered up to a weighted complex multiple. The map
(a,b)\mapsto[a^{3}:b^{2}]
gives a well-defined, surjective, continuous map from
S^{3}
to
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
whose fibers are exactly the orbits of the
S^{1}
action. In other words, the quotient space of the circle action is topologically
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. But now, we see
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
with one orbifold point of order 2 at the point
[1:0]
, one orbifold point of order 3 at the point
[0:1]
, and a puncture at the point
[27:-4]
corresponding to the trefoil knot. The situation is depicted in
Figure

6
.
∎

Note that the exact sequence arising from this Seifert fiber structure is the short exact sequence (
2

4
Orbispaces

Our goal is to fill in the trefoil knot with an orbifold locus of order
d
and thereby obtain the group
B_{3}(d)
as an orbifold fundamental group. The Seifert fibration depicted in
Figure

6
should fill in to give a fiber bundle in the category of orbifolds, and we want to use the associated long exact sequence of homotopy groups to analyze the homomorphism
\Delta^{2}\to B_{3}(d)
. This makes sense intuitively, but we run into problems making it formal (e.g. how does one define “higher homotopy groups” of orbifolds in a rigorous but not-too-complicated way?).

To deal with these problems, we utilize an overlooked construction of André Henriques that gives a definition of “orbispace” that is both intuitive and easy to work with on a formal level
[
Hen01
]
. Roughly, Henriques defines an orbispace
M
to be a continuous map of topological spaces
p:PM\to QM
which looks something like a fibration with
K(G,1)
fibers. These are locally modeled on the projection
p:(X\times EG)/G\to X/G
where
G\curvearrowright X
is a “nice” action of a discrete group and
EG
is a contractible space with free
G
-action. The base space is the intuitive picture of an almost-free quotient while the “Borel space” above, which has no singularities, captures the algebraic topology of the quotient. We encourage the reader to read Henriques’s paper, which the authors find is quite approachable and has good examples.

We start by defining all of the spaces we will need. We make extensive use of the infinite dimensional unit sphere in Hilbert space

S^{\infty}=\left\{\vec{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dotsc)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})\mid\sum|z_{i}|^{2}=1\right\}.

The
\infty
-sphere is contractible and has free, proper actions by the circle group
S^{1}
and all finite cyclic groups
C_{n}
(by viewing these as groups of complex roots of unity) via complex multiplication.

For any group action
G\curvearrowright X
, we will write the elements of the quotient
X/G
as
G\cdot x
or
Gx
for
x\in X
.

Definition 4.1
.

Write
D^{2}
for the unit disk in the complex plane and
D^{2}_{\circ}=D^{2}\setminus\{0\}
for the disk punctured at the origin. Define the

d

to be the space

\mathbb{D}^{2}(d)=(D^{2}\times S^{\infty})/C_{d}

where the action
C_{d}\curvearrowright D^{2}\times S^{\infty}
is simultaneous multiplication by complex roots of unity.

Define the

to be the subspace
\mathbb{D}^{2}_{0}(d)=(D^{2}_{0}\times S^{\infty})/C_{d}
.

The orbidisk has a canonical projection
p_{D}:\mathbb{D}^{2}(d)\to D^{2}/C_{d}
by just forgetting the
S^{\infty}
factor.

There are free actions by the circle group
S^{1}\curvearrowright\mathbb{D}^{2}(d)
and
S^{1}\curvearrowright D^{2}/C_{d}
by

\displaystyle\zeta\cdot(C_{d}(p,\vec{z}))

\displaystyle=C_{d}(\zeta^{1/d}p,\zeta^{1/d}\vec{z})

\displaystyle\text{for }p\in D^{2},\vec{z}\in S^{\infty},\zeta\in S^{1},\text{and}

\displaystyle\zeta\cdot(C_{d}(p))

\displaystyle=C_{d}(\zeta^{1/d}p)

\displaystyle\text{for }p\in D^{2},\zeta\in S^{1}.

Note that this is well-defined since the

C_{d}
. Note also that

is now a

-equivariant map.

We frequently switch back and forth between a disk

Definition 4.2
.

Define

\psi(p)=C_{d}\cdot|p|\left(\frac{p}{|p|}\right)^{1/d}

with the limiting value

Define

Note that here one needs

5
Analysis of the long exact sequence

The starting point of our argument is the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

The projection map
\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{B}
is a fiber bundle with fiber
S^{1}
.

Proof.

This is simply because the projection map is the quotient by the free action of a compact Lie group on a completely regular topological space. See
[
Gle50
, Theorem 3.6]
for a primary reference.
∎

This lemma along with our understanding of the projection maps from
Proposition

lets us draw the fiber bundle as in

.

A standard result in algebraic topology now gives us an associated long exact sequence (see e.g.
[
Hat02
, Theorem 4.41]
).

Corollary 5.2
.

There is a long exact sequence of homotopy groups

\dotsb\to\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})\xrightarrow{\partial}\pi_{1}(S^{1})\to\pi_{1}(\mathbb{E})\to\pi_{1}(\mathbb{B})\to\pi_{0}(S^{1})\to\dotsb

(5)

Our main result
Theorem

1.2
follows from a computation of the boundary map
\partial:\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})\to\pi_{1}(S^{1})
. The rest of this section is devoted to computing this map as well as the other terms shown in this exact sequence.

Proposition 5.3
.

There is an isomorphism
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{E})\approx B_{3}(d)
. The homomorphism
\pi_{1}(S^{1})\to\pi_{1}(\mathbb{E})
takes the generator
1\in\mathbb{Z}
to
\Delta^{2}\in B_{3}(d)
.

Proof.

Note that

\pi_{1}(\mathbb{D}^{2}(d)\times S^{1})=\pi_{1}(\mathbb{D}^{2}(d))\times\pi_{1}(S^{1})=C_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}.

The identification of
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{D}^{2}(d))
follows because
\mathbb{D}^{2}(d)
is the quotient of a contractible space by a covering space action of the cyclic group
C_{d}
.

Also we have

\pi_{1}(E_{0}\times S^{\infty})=\pi_{1}(E_{0})\times\pi_{1}(S^{\infty})=B_{3}\times\{1\}

as was shown in
Proposition

3.1
.

These two spaces are glued together along a subspace homeomorphic to
D^{2}_{0}\times S^{\infty}\times S^{1}
to construct
\mathbb{E}
. The Seifert-Van Kampen theorem allows us to compute the new fundamental group (as in e.g.
[
Hat02
]
). In our case, the
D^{2}_{0}
factor identifies the
C_{d}
generator with the meridian loop in
E_{0}
, i.e. a half twist generator
\sigma_{i}\in B_{3}
as per
Proposition

3.4
. The
S^{1}
factor identifies the generator of
\mathbb{Z}
with the orbit of the
S^{1}
action in
E_{0}
, i.e. the full twist
\Delta^{2}\in B_{3}
as per
Proposition

3.5
. The resulting fundamental group is
B_{3}(d)
.
∎

To continue, we recall a construction of Henriques.

Definition 5.4

(
[
Hen01
]
)
.

For a continuous map
p:X\to Y
, the
fiberwise-
\pi_{0}
is defined to be the space

\fibpizero(X\xrightarrow{p}Y)=X/\sim

where
x_{1}\sim x_{2}
if and only if
p(x_{1})=p(x_{2})=y\in Y
and
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
lie in the same path component of
p^{-1}(y)
.

The fiberwise-
\pi_{0}
gives a nice formalization of the local branched cover of an orbifold. We use this perspective in the next proposition.

Proposition 5.5
.

The order of
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{B})
is given by
3\cdot f(3,d)
.

Proof.

This is a classical result (with the right setup), but we give a proof again here for completeness. The strategy is to compute the degree of the universal cover
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
of
\mathbb{B}
. This will come down to considering the following commutative diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\fibpizero(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\to B)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{CP}^{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\approx}

As per
Proposition

4.9
, the map
p_{B}
is an
S^{\infty}
bundle over all but three points of
B
. Since each
S^{\infty}
fiber is simply connected, it lifts to
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
. Thus the full preimage of each such fiber in
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
is a disjoint union of copies of
S^{\infty}
. Taking the fiberwise-
\pi_{0}
of the map
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\to B
collapses each of these copies of

to a point, and we see that the resulting map
\fibpizero(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\to B)\to B
is a covering space away from the three special points of
B
. Considering those points as well, the map is a branched cover. The generic degree of the branched cover is the same as the degree of the cover
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\to\mathbb{B}
, and this is the quantity we seek.

So now we consider this branched covering of
B
branched along 3 points. Considering the local structure over those points, again as provided by
Proposition

4.9
, we see that
\fibpizero(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\to B)
will have the local structure of a branched cover of the disk with branching order dividing 2, 3, or
d
(according to the point in question).

We construct a branched cover

and will see that
X
is our
\fibpizero(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\to B)
. So split

along a pair of embedded arcs disjoint on their interiors, one arc from
[0:1]
to
[27:-4]
and the other from
[27:-4]
to
[1:0]
. The resulting topological quadrilateral is simply connected and has no branch points in its interior. Create a surface

by gluing edges of this quadrilateral edge-to-edge with 2 copies around preimages of
[0:1]
, 3 copies around preimages of
[1:0]
, and
2d

[27:-4]
. See
Figure

9
. Now

is a surface with a branched covering to

of the appropriate local branching orders. The quadrilateral tiles fit together into a tiling of

by “equilateral” triangles that meet

at each vertex. Each such triangle is tiled by three copies of the quadrilateral.

The surface

is a sphere when

. In any case,

. This shows that

.

Finally to understand the degree of the coverings here, note that we have described a regular tiling of

by triangles with

at each vertex. There are

-many of these triangles by definition. Each triangle is tiled by 3 copies of the lifted quadrilateral, each of which projects injectively to

on its interior. Thus the branched-cover

is

-to-

∎

Remark 5.6
.

Geometrically, one can take
X
in the above proof to be the round sphere (
d\geq 5
), the Euclidean plane (
d=6
), or the hyperbolic plane (
d\geq 7
). The group of deck transformations of the branched-cover can be made to act by isometries on

, giving an action of the group on the principal
S^{1}
-bundle that is the unit tangent bundle of
UT(X)
. Then the group acts on the the associated
S^{\infty}
bundle over
X
, which we denote
\mathbb{X}
. The structure group of these

bundles lies in the unitary group

, which is contractible by Kuiper’s theorem
[

. Thus these

bundles are trivial, as is the
S^{\infty}
bundle
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\to X
. In this way we gain the perspective
B\approx X/\Delta(2,3,d)
and
{\mathbb{B}}\approx\mathbb{X}/\Delta(2,3,d)
.

To finish our analysis, we need a quick lemma from low-dimensional topology.

Lemma 5.7
.

Any two regular orbits of the action
S^{1}\curvearrowright S^{3}
have linking number 6 (viewed as oriented knots in the 3-sphere).

Proof.

There are many ways to compute this. In

we show two regular orbits sitting on a common standard torus in

. Considering this as an oriented link diagram, we can count the crossings of the two components (with sign) and divide by 2 to get the linking number of the two knots (see e.g.
[

, §5.D]
).

We see that there are 12 crossings, all positively oriented. This gives the lemma.

Proposition 5.8
.

When
d\leq 5
, we have
\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})\approx\mathbb{Z}
. Otherwise,
\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})
is trivial. In either case, the boundary map
\partial:\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})\to\pi_{1}(S^{1})
has image
\frac{f(3,d)}{2}\mathbb{Z}
in
\pi_{1}(S^{1})\approx\mathbb{Z}
(where we interpret this as the trivial subgroup when

is infinite).

Proof.

Since higher homotopy groups are preserved by covers, we have that
\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})
is identical to
\pi_{2}
of the universal cover
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
. But as in the proof of
Proposition

5.5
, the space
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
is an
S^{\infty}
-bundle over the model geometry
X
. Since
S^{\infty}
is contractible, this gives
\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})\approx\pi_{2}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})\approx\pi_{2}(X)
. This group is
\mathbb{Z}
when

is spherical (i.e. when
d\leq 5
) and is trivial when

is contractible (i.e. when

). For the rest of the proof, suppose

so that we are in the nontrivial case with

. The reader should have the construction of

Now recall how the boundary map in a long exact sequence works. An element of
\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})
is represented by a map
\delta:(D^{2},\partial D^{2})\to(\mathbb{B},\text{pt})

\tilde{\delta}:(D^{2},\partial D^{2})\to(\mathbb{E},F)
where

is the circle fiber over the chosen basepoint of
\mathbb{B}
. The boundary of this lifted map is a loop
\partial D^{2}\to F
, and then the induced element in the fundamental group is the image of the element
\delta\in\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})
in the fiber group
\pi_{1}(F)
.

In our case,
\pi_{2}(\mathbb{B})\approx\pi_{2}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})
is infinite cyclic, generated by a map
(D^{2},\partial D^{2})\to\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
that projects to
\delta:D^{2}\to\mathbb{B}
which then lifts to
\tilde{\delta}:D^{2}\to\mathbb{E}
. We arrange things so that

is smooth, 1-to-1 on its interior, and maps the boundary of the disk to a point not fixed by the triangle group action. Then

is smooth and generically
3f(3,d)
-to-1 by the constructions of

5.5
. We’ll conflate notation now and write
\delta
for

and
\tilde{\delta}
for

.

6
Final remarks

Of course, it would be ideal to give a topological interpretation of the connection between
any
truncated braid group and its associated regular tiling from
Theorem

1.1
. Parts of our story work just the same:

•

The braid group
B_{n}
is the fundamental group of the complement of a discriminant variety in
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
. This complement deformation retracts to the complement of a knotted subspace of
S^{2n-3}
.

•

There is an
S^{1}
action on
S^{2n-3}
that preserves the discriminant locus. Generic points have trivial stabilizer and trace out a loop that represents the full twist
\Delta^{2}\in B_{n}
.

•

The quotient space for the group action
S^{2n-3}/S^{1}
is topologically
\mathbb{CP}^{n-2}
, but the projection has singularities for the exceptional orbits. Some subspace is removed for the discriminant locus.

One would want to “sew in” an order-
d
orbifold set along the discriminant locus just as we did along the trefoil knot, but the higher dimensional story is more complicated. For instance, the discriminant locus in
S^{5}
for the
n=4
case is an immersed
S^{3}
with self-intersection. The self-intersections correspond to collisions of more than two points in the configuration space of points.

Supposing that one could circumvent these difficulties and fill in the discriminant locus, we hope that the orbifold version of
\mathbb{CP}^{n-2}
would carry a geometric structure. In the finite cases, the orbifold covering space might correspond to so-called “regular complex polytopes” as described in
[
Cox91
]
.

As a final thought, the truncated braid groups are examples of what are known as
complex reflection groups

[
LT09
]
. These are like real reflection groups, i.e. Coxeter groups, except that a “complex reflection” can have any finite order, not just order 2. Perhaps the techniques in this article, and the extensions proposed in this section, could be applied to understand complex reflections groups more generally. The first author wonders if there could be some application to understanding Artin groups as well.
