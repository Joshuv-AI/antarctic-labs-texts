Challenges and uncertainty faced by Chinese Men who have Sex with Men (MSM) in the Post-COVID Era: an online, qualitative study of their stress and coping strategies
Authors: Shuochi Wei, Yuchen Li, Edmond Pui Hang Choi, William Chi Wai Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has a significant impact on global mental health. In China, men who have sex with men (MSM) faced more challenges and experienced psychological distress due to social and cultural factors. The pandemic has further exacerbated their situation. However, few studies have examined mental health and coping strategies among Chinese MSM in the post-COVID era.MethodsA semi-structured interview was conducted with 32 Chinese MSM participants to discuss stress and coping in the post-COVID era. Participants were recruited online. Thematic analysis was used to analyse the data to identify key patterns and themes regarding stressors and copings.ResultsIt was found that Chinese MSM faced multiple stressors, including economic instability, family expectations, and digital overload. Coping strategies included emotional minimalism or active engagement in daily life. Financial independence and supportive connections were identified as protective factors. However, the legal invisibility of same-sex relationships and the homophobic content on online platforms still affected their mental health.ConclusionsThis study explored the challenges faced by MSM in China post-COVID, increasing the understanding of the mental health challenges faced by this group. There is a need for culturally competent mental health services. Mental health professionals should provide culturally appropriate mental health services and support to improve the well-being of MSM in the post-COVID era.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04641-z.Keywords: Stress, Men who have sex with men, China, Coping, Mental health, COVID-19

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic resulted in significant challenges and caused a global mental health crisis [1]. Men who have sex with men (MSM) are a marginalized group facing more mental health issues due to a variety of structural discrimination, stigma, and culturally embedded expectations regarding family and heterosexual norms [2, 3]. In China, MSM reported higher levels of psychological distress than the general population during the pandemic, including depression, anxiety, and fear which were mainly related to increased social isolation, support networks breakdown, or fear of infection [2–4]. While the immediate impacts of COVID-19 have received much attention, its longer-term effects on MSM in specific socio-cultural contexts remain underexplored.

In the post-COVID era, sexual minorities continue to experience an accumulation of stressors, many of which predate the pandemic but have been exacerbated by the pandemic. Evidence suggests that psychological distress can persist beyond the acute phase of the pandemic, particularly when social support is disrupted and broader uncertainties remain unresolved [5]. Prolonged or renewed co-residence with parents during and after disruptions intensified exposure to heteronormative family expectations, which has been associated with poorer mental health and diminished well-being among young sexual minority people [6]. At the same time, digital platforms serve as important channels for connection but sustained reliance on them may amplify exposure to stigma or distressing information and heighten privacy-related concerns, thereby contributing to ongoing anxiety and emotional exhaustion [7, 8]. In China, the limited public visibility of LGBT topics has compressed the living and social space of LGBT+ communities, leading many MSM to rely on the internet and dating apps for social connection and support [9, 10]. The use of dating apps amongst MSM increased during the pandemic leading to more short-term or one-time encounters [11], and such behaviour has not disappeared after pandemic. While this behaviour may reflect a coping mechanism during periods of isolation, it has continued post-pandemic, possibly contributing to a sense of disconnection or further complicating the search for long-term, meaningful relationships, which are crucial for mental well-being.

In China, family norms are closely tied to the concept of filial duty and continuing the family line [12]. Because these traditional family roles often directly conflict with their sexual orientation, MSM face a unique form of familial minority stress in which the home transforms from a traditional site of support into a source of significant psychological pressure [13, 14]. This is most acute for only children, who bear the singular responsibility for carrying on the lineage [15]. Compounding these challenges, the lack of legal recognition for same-sex relationships further complicates these expectations, as MSM have few socially accepted pathways to form partnerships [16]. During and after the pandemic, reduced offline social support and spending more time with family could worsen the mental health of some MSM [2]. This combination of familial expectations and reduced social support highlights the intersection of cultural norms and social isolation, which significantly contributes to the mental health challenges faced by MSM in China. This study adopted a qualitative approach to explore the structural, interpersonal and individual stressors experienced by Chinese MSM and their copings in the post-COVID era. By examining the mental health challenges and factors affecting this marginalized group, the study provides understandings that could inform targeted interventions and improve public health for this group.

To enhance conceptual clarity, this study was guided by two frameworks. First, we draw on minority stress theory to understand the distal and proximal stressors faced by sexual minority [17]. Building on this, we primarily adopt a socio-ecological framework to further analyse multi-level stress, providing a clearer and more structured understanding. This framework conceptualizes stress as shaped by interacting influences across structural/policy, interpersonal, and individual levels [18]. It provides a systematic way to situate personal experiences within broader social contexts, and to consider how pressures and resources may operate across levels rather than only within the individual. Previous qualitative research with MSM highlighted that individual vulnerability is linked to broader social settings and community norms, emphasizing the need for multi-level responses [19]. In this study, we also adopted the transactional model of stress and coping [20]. This framework defines coping as the cognitive and behavioural efforts to manage demands that are experienced as taxing. It focuses on appraisal and perceived capacity to respond and helps explain how participants described their evaluations of controllability and resources, and the strategies they used to manage distress. In a study of immigrant Latino MSM, participants described multiple co-occurring stressors and used varied coping strategies such as passive coping, attempts to change stressors, seeking support, and seeking distractions [21]. Together, these frameworks offer a foundation for exploring post-COVID stress and coping among Chinese MSM. Recent qualitative work with Chinese MSM living with HIV has combined a multi-level socio-ecological perspective with the transactional model of stress and coping and identified multi-level stress alongside coping and support needs [22].

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited online. The inclusion criteria were: (1) individuals aged 18 years or older; (2) self-identified as Chinese MSM; and (3) current residents of mainland China. Recruitment advertisements were disseminated on Rednote and WeChat, which were among the most popular social media and communication platforms. Interested individuals were invited to complete an online questionnaire and submit their contact details via a link. Eligibility was assessed based on questionnaire responses, after which the research team contacted the eligible individuals. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (project number UW25-089).

Thirty-two participants took part in the interviews. The mean age was 28.5 ± 6.9 years, ranging from 19 to 47 years. All participants were cisgender. Twenty-seven participants identified themselves as gay and five identified as bisexual. Eight had a junior college degree, fifteen had a bachelor’s degree, seven had a master’s degree and two had a doctoral degree. Five participants lived with their same-sex partners. Fourteen people did not come out to their family, colleagues, or heterosexual friends. Sixteen were only children and sixteen had siblings. Three participants were living with HIV.

Procedure

The study was conducted between March and August 2025. All interviews were conducted by the first author who is a native Mandarin speaker and grew up in China. This approach was adopted to ensure consistency and reduce bias during the interviews. A semi-structured interview guide was developed and was pilot tested with a few participants. Revisions were made based on their feedback to enhance clarity and relevance. All interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese. The first author completed verbatim transcription and transcript cleaning before any coding began. Translation into English was carried out after coding and theme development. The first author translated the quotations for inclusion in the manuscript.

Given the common use of digital platforms in China and the sensitivity of sexual minority issues, online interviews were a reliable and effective method of data collection. The privacy and anonymity enabled participants to discuss sensitive topics such as sex, identity and family pressures. Interviews were conducted online via Tencent Meeting, a widely used videoconferencing platform in China with privacy-protection features. Each interview lasted 40 to 60 min. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. The interviewer transcribed the recordings into text. Each participant received compensation of 50 HKD (approximately 6.4 USD) for their time and contribution.

Data analysis

A hybrid process of inductive and deductive thematic analysis [23] was used to analyse the data on Nvivo software. The socio-ecological framework guided the organization of codes across structural, interpersonal, and individual levels, while the transactional model of stress and coping informed the analysis of coping processes. Interview data were transcribed verbatim, and transcripts were read and re-read to ensure familiarity with the content. Two researchers with master’s degrees in psychology (SCW and YCL) coded the data independently and identified key themes. The codebook included both deductive and inductive components: deductive codes were derived from the two frameworks and covered stressors at different socio-ecological levels and coping processes, while inductive codes captured unanticipated issues emerging from participants’ narratives in the post-COVID context. The codebook was reviewed and updated throughout the coding process to maintain conceptual clarity and incorporate new themes. SCW and YCL then organized the data into meaningful categories and refined the themes. Any discrepancies in coding or theme identification were resolved through discussion to ensure validity and consistency.

Rigor

Both independent coders used the codebook to analyse transcripts. Regular discussions were held between the coders to address discrepancies or uncertainties. A third coder was consulted when necessary to reach consensus. The team conducted regular meetings to compare coding, refine themes, and ensure agreement. Researchers wrote memo-writing to record their interpretations and ensure reflexivity, which supported the development of coding decisions and maintained awareness of potential biases.

Results

Three key themes were found from the analysis: (1) multilevel stress among Chinese MSM in the post-COVID era; (2) coping strategies among Chinese MSM; (3) facilitators and barriers for MSM well-being.

Multilevel stress among Chinese MSM in the Post-COVID Era

Structural stress on Chinese MSM in the Post-COVID Era

Post-COVID economic insecurity and employment instability

One theme among interviewees was a sense of economic vulnerability during the post COVID. Many participants experienced unemployment, forced career changes, or reduced incomes. “I had a stable job before, but lost it during the pandemic, and my income dropped drastically. Even though the pandemic is over, finding stable work again feels uncertain.” (Interviewee 3) Although the pandemic has ended, this stress drained emotional and mental resources.

Some also mentioned that as employment opportunities tighten, they are increasingly worried about not finding work or being laid off. Many also referred to the current “involution (内卷)” phenomenon in the job market, where compared to pre-pandemic times, job requirements and demands on workers have significantly increased, but wages have not kept pace, leading to feelings of fatigue and burnout among workers. “The job market is insanely competitive now. The demands and pressure keep growing, but wages aren’t increasing.” (Interviewee 8) Someone mentioned that, against the backdrop of the US-China trade war, international companies were reducing their businesses in China leading to a reduction in a relatively open and inclusive work environment. “I work for an international company, but recently it has become increasingly difficult to work for international companies, and the entire company is reducing its business.” (Interviewee 17) The loss of these potentially more tolerant workplaces removed a buffer against discrimination and added another layer of stress related to concealing or managing their identity in less certain or potentially less accepting domestic work environments.

Geopolitical tensions and the erosion of identity liberation

For some participants, studying or working abroad was an important pathway to self-realization and relational legitimacy. However, in the post-COVID era, a more tense geopolitical condition has greatly reduced such opportunities. “I have always wanted to study abroad because other countries are relatively more tolerant of that (same-sex relationships). However, I feel that the international situation has become somewhat tense, and applying to study abroad has become more difficult.” (Interviewee 6) With stricter U.S. VISA policies for Chinese STEM students, some participants had to cancel or change their study-abroad plans. Meanwhile, some participants already living abroad have been forced to return home because of the pandemic and international uncertainties. However, they must face the previous pressure when they return. “Now that I’m back in China, my family has started pressuring me to get married, which is quite annoying.” (Interviewee 11).

Implicit pressures from mainstream fertility policies

The current pro-birth policies in China created indirect but pervasive stress on MSM individuals. They are in a context of lack of legal recognition for same-sex relationships and the exclusion of non-traditional families from fertility-related policymaking. While some interviewees noted that the government does not actively persecute same-sex relationships, as one participant explained: “The government cannot legalize same-sex relationships, but it also does not persecute people for it …” (Interviewee 20). The implementation of the ‘three-child policy’ still prioritizes heterosexual marriage and procreation, creating additional societal pressure on MSM individuals to conform to traditional family structures.

Interpersonal pressure and emotional exhaustion

Family pressure and its lingering effects in Post-COVID Era

Many participants were forced to stay with family members during the pandemic restrictions. Some participants mentioned that repeated talks about marriage increased their mental stress. “During the epidemic, I stayed with my parents for a long time, and they always talked about my marriage when they had nothing else to do… it is really annoying.” (Interviewee 4) Living together for a long time exacerbates already poor family relationships among the participants. “My relationship with my father has not been very good … We hardly communicated at all and the atmosphere was very oppressive. I felt uncomfortable the whole time.” (Interviewee 16).

Overreliance on digital socialization and emotional burnout

Online apps became the main way for MSM to maintain social connections during the pandemic. After the pandemic, many participants continue to use these for social interaction. Some people said that long-term use has reduced their sense of fulfilment. “We can meet people face-to-face now, but online chatting is still more convenient. However, sometimes after chatting for a long time, I feel a sense of emptiness.” (Interviewee 22).

Individual mental stress and sexual health tension

Post-COVID stress residue and adaptation difficulties

The interviews revealed that individuals are experiencing ongoing stress and difficulty adapting to the rapid changes in the pace of society. Many interviewees reported that the pandemic disrupted the stability of their daily lives, leaving them with long-lasting uncertainty and insecurity. “The pandemic disrupted many things, and I still feel uneasy. Although the pandemic is over, I find it difficult to return to my previous routine, and I just feel like doing nothing and putting things off.” (Interviewee 26) Many people had developed chronic stress symptoms such as anxiety and obsessive thoughts due to the experiences of infection, isolation, or even death of family members. These symptoms did not simply disappear with the pandemic but remained for some time. “The pandemic has made my life unstable, and I feel uncertain about the future. I often feel insecure.” (Interviewee 4).

Shifting sexual behaviors and anxiety in the Post-COVID period

The interview found that there had been a shift in sexual behavior among MSM after the pandemic. During the peak of the pandemic, concerns about COVID infection, social distancing policies, and worries about digital contact tracing resulted in MSM reducing their dating and sexual activities. “Back then, I wouldn’t dare to hook up with someone, because if I accidentally became a close contact, everything I did, where I went, and when I did it would be made public.” (Interviewee 27) Following the pandemic, this repression turned into a strong compensatory release. Some interviewees described a “rebound phase” — a period of increased dating and sexual contact, partly to make up for what they had missed. However, this behavioral rebound was quickly affected by anxiety related to sexually transmitted infections. “Later, I started dating and interacting with others frequently. I also heard that many of my friends had similar feelings and wanted to find opportunities to release their sexual desires… Sometimes, when I dated too much, I would worry about whether I would get a sexually transmitted and blood-born infections.” (Interviewee 27).

Coping Strategies Among Chinese MSM in the Post-COVID Era

Emotional minimalism or active engagement

In the post-COVID era, participants in this study adopted two internal coping strategies. They reduced expectations and simplified their life. “I think I can live quite well on my own. As long as I’m comfortable, I don’t worry about anything else.” (Interviewee 9) Some participants increased their engagement in daily life. They felt a sense of control through such daily activities. “Now I’m going to play hard and travel and meet friends …” (Interviewee 13).

Staying detached or seeking closeness

Two different relationship coping strategies were identified. Some participants said that they experienced uncertainty during the pandemic and some would avoid making long-term commitments. “Many things have become very uncertain. Sometimes I deliberately lower my expectations for love, so that at least I won’t easily feel disappointed” (Interviewee 9) However, other participants were more actively seeking stable emotional relationships. “I really want to find a boyfriend because I feel like it’s too hard to be alone during the pandemic. It would be much better if I had a partner.” (Interviewee 17) They regarded intimate relationships as a resource for coping with uncertainty. They expected that intimate relationships could enhance the stability of their lives.

Post-COVID excuses for avoidance

Many participants said that they would avoid uncomfortable conversations by remaining silent or changing the talk subject. “When I do not want to answer, I deliberately change the subject or say, “Let’s talk about something else for now.” Sometimes I also emphasize that I am still in college, have to face graduation and job hunting, and my life is still unstable, to avoid the pressure to get married.” (Interviewee 2) In the post-COVID situation, they were more likely to use widely recognized difficult circumstances, such as employment pressure and economic instability, as “reasonable excuses” to avoid family pressure to marry and have children. “Now, I’ll just say that life is too unstable at the moment, work is getting harder and harder, and there have been too many changes since the pandemic. When they hear this, they just accept it.” (Interviewee 1) They believed that compared to the vague evasions of the past, such excuses are more persuasive and acceptable in the post-COVID era.

Facilitators and barriers for MSM well-being

Late marriage trends reduce family pressure

Some participants said that late marriage and childbearing were more common in China despite current policies encouraging childbirth. This had reduced some of the pressure they had felt to marry at a young age. Others had mentioned that their parents were becoming more accepting of late marriage, although it did not solve the marriage issues they faced, but it reduced some of the pressure. “Personally, I think it’s fortunate that we are now entering an era where non-marriage is becoming more prevalent. So even though parents may not be very open-minded about same-sex relationships, they will realize that young people today don’t like getting married, don’t like dating, and prefer to remain single. Therefore, they won’t dwell on other possibilities. I believe that if we were to go back a few years or even 20 years, the pressure might be even greater, because back then, not getting married would have been even more unusual. So, I feel that the pressure is actually not as intense now.” (Interviewee 2) The societal shift toward late marriage would be an implicit protection and allowed them to avoid discussions about their marriages.

Financial independence increases autonomy

Many participants emphasized the importance of economic independence. Some said that having a stable income helped reduce anxiety. “Actually, I feel that once you have a certain level of financial stability, it doesn’t really matter whether you get married or not, and it doesn’t matter whether you have children or not.” (Interviewee 16) Some participants said that economic independence indicated that they were grown-up and reduced parents’ worries about them. “I don’t have any plans to come out yet. If I do in the future, it will be when I have a stable job and am financially independent. Then I can talk to them (my parents) about it… (Financial independence) is important to me. If I am financially independent, I can prove to them that I can live well on my own.” (Interviewee 21).

Supportive relationships provide emotional strength

Most participants mentioned that family support was important to them. Parental acceptance reduced the pressure from family and society “Coming out to my family and having them accept me was actually my greatest encouragement and support” (Interviewee 3) Other participants also said that friends were a very important source of emotional support. “I have quite a few online friends, and I have about five or six friends in real life. I listen to them talk about their work and life… I think it’s quite helpful for me, as they give me some advice.” (Interviewee 17). Some also mentioned that romantic partners played a key role in their emotional support systems. This allowed them to build a sense of family and intimacy, helping them resist social pressures. “I will communicate with my partner, vent my feelings, and seek their help or emotional support, which is also very useful for me. I have been living with my partner for two years, and most of the difficulties and pressures we have faced have been overcome through mutual communication and support, or rather, by providing each other with emotional value.” (Interviewee 3).

Short video platforms amplify homophobic stereotypes

The rise of live-streaming and short video platforms in China had created new challenges for MSM, as some content creators had spread negative and harmful portrayals of homosexuality to attract attention. Some participants had said that some content creators had amplified homophobic content, reinforcing negative stereotypes that depicted MSM as deviant or immoral. “Some media outlets are disgusting…They stigmatize sexual minorities. In order to attract attention, these platforms deliberately link sexual minorities with sexually transmitted diseases.” (Interviewee 9).

Lack of professional services

Reduced access to LGBT-friendly counselling services was a barrier to support for MSM. “In terms of school, I feel that some psychological counsellors are not professional or friendly enough. I went to the school counselling center, but the counsellor advised me to have more contact with girls, in case I might develop feelings for them. I felt that the counsellor was very unprofessional, as if they were denying my self-identity.” (Interviewee 17) Many MSM did not have access to professional and friendly support which affects their mental health.

Legal gaps and social marginalization

Although China had abolished criminal penalties for homosexuality, same-sex relations are still not legally recognized or protected. As one interviewee mentioned: “I don’t think it’s realistic (same-sex marriage) in China now” (Interviewee 10). Some participants also mentioned that mainstream media had an avoiding attitude toward homosexuality. “I don’t know why, but it seems that the mainstream media are now very unwilling to report on events related to sexual minorities, whereas ten years ago or so, there were still some positive reports” (Interviewee 10). In this context, the absence of legal recognition may indirectly lead to social prejudice and reinforce their marginalization.

Discussion

This study explored the stresses and coping strategies among Chinese MSM in the post-COVID era. Previous studies have focused on HIV infection amongst them [22]. However, there is a lack of in-depth research describe the stressors and coping strategies of MSM in the Chinese context, making this study essential for understanding their unique experiences [24]. Our findings would help enhance understanding of the stress faced by Chinese MSM and their coping mechanisms.

This study enhances current understanding by showing how the post-COVID context in China reshapes the ecology of minority stress for MSM [25]. Minority stress theory emphasizes that health disparities arise not only from stigma-specific processes such as expectations of rejection and internalized stigma, but also from their interaction with general life stressors [17]. The findings of this study suggest that in the post-COVID era, structural conditions have not merely been coexisting with stress related to sexuality; rather, they have influenced this stress through the narrowing of resources, the reduction of perceived control, and the increase in the perceived costs of identity disclosure and relational planning [26].

This study identified a perceived contraction of viable life pathways which link macro-level changes to MSM wellbeing in the post-COVID era. Participants’ descriptions of “involution” and employment instability reveal more than mere economic hardship [27]. They describe a sustained uncertainty that steadily consumes time, emotional stability, and economic security [27]. In this context, concealment and conformity are viewed as safer ways to protect employment and social standing [28]. These findings complement prior qualitative work on minority stress among Chinese MSM by highlighting how structural change can intensify stigma processes by shrinking protective contexts and by increasing dependence on socially acceptable life [14, 29]. Geopolitical tension has become particularly relevant in the post-COVID era [30]. For some participants, overseas study or employment offered a path to greater identity freedom and more legitimate relationships. When these opportunities diminished, they felt pushed back into family-centered expectations, which reactivated earlier life-course pressures [31]. This illustrates how macro-level opportunity structures shape perceived possibilities for intimacy and long-term life planning [32].

Participants’ narratives also contextualize minority stress within China’s post-pandemic pronatalist environment [33]. They did not describe direct persecution but noted that heteronormative policies define adulthood through heterosexual marriage and childbirth, which renders same-sex relationships institutionally invisible [34]. Contemporary analyses of China’s population policy emphasize its continuity with heteropatriarchal norms [35]. Our findings demonstrate how such discourse can act as a diffuse structural stressor for MSM by intensifying societal and familial expectations, and by limiting socially validated prospects [34].

At the interpersonal level, family pressure should be understood not as a fixed cultural norm but as a relational context in which broader structural forces are enacted through everyday interactions. Previous qualitative research has demonstrated how stigma and filial duties influence disclosure and marriage decisions among Chinese MSM [36]. Our findings extend this literature by showing that post-COVID era uncertainty operates both as a limiting condition and as a discursive mechanism for managing social expectations [37]. Participants used post-COVID era excuses such as financial instability and disrupted plans to delay marriage pressure and manage disclosure risks [37]. However, this strategy may reduce acute conflict while leaving underlying tensions unresolved, which could convert episodic stress into chronic anticipatory stress.

Digital life emerged as both a coping resource and a stress amplifier [38]. Post-COVID reliance on online socialization was not merely ‘screen fatigue’; it reflected a continued dependence on digital spaces for connection due to constrained offline safety and geographic dispersion [39]. However, participants also highlighted short-video and live-stream platforms as environments that amplify homophobic stereotypes and moralized commentary [38]. This duality is consistent with previous research showing that technology can buffer minority stress through community and information, while also amplifying exposure to stigma and social comparison, thereby producing emotional exhaustion [40]. The post-pandemic period may intensify this dynamic by increasing digital dependence and platform-driven exposure to hostile content and stereotypes.

At an individual level, post-COVID stress residue can be understood as the residual effect of prolonged uncertainty and chronic identity management [41]. There is evidence to suggest that the impact on mental health can persist beyond the acute phase of the pandemic, particularly when economic and social disruptions remain unresolved [5]. Furthermore, participants’ descriptions of changing sexual behaviours and renewed anxiety about sexual health are consistent with broader evidence indicating that the sexual behaviour and service access of MSM changed before, during, and after the pandemic in China [42]. The key finding in our data is not simply behavioural change, but rather the emotional significance of post-COVID re-engagement: the pursuit of intimacy coexisted with a heightened sense of risk and uncertainty, demonstrating how mental and sexual health concerns can reinforce each other.

The coping strategies reported in our study (emotional minimalism versus active engagement, and detachment versus closeness-seeking) can be understood as context-contingent adaptations [20, 43]. Emotional minimalism resembles acceptance-based coping, which can reduce distress when stressors are perceived as uncontrollable. However, when used chronically, it can lead to avoidant regulation, which restricts emotional expression and reduces help-seeking [44]. In contrast, active engagement resembles behavioural activation and agency-building [45]. However, it often requires resources such as time, stable income and safe social spaces that are precisely constrained under post-COVID volatility. This helps to explain why coping strategies were experienced as both protective and costly, and why supportive intimate relationships and financial independence functioned as powerful facilitators in our data.

In summary, this study provides an analysis of post-COVID minority stress among Chinese MSM, showing how macro-level changes produce future foreclosure, how pronatalist discourse sustains relational pressure and how digital environments intensify both connection and exhaustion. While issues like family pressure, legal gaps, and social marginalization existed before the pandemic, their impact was exacerbated in the post-COVID era. The pandemic disrupted daily life, heightened uncertainty, and increased reliance on digital platforms, which further burdened MSM. This study reveals how these challenges have been reshaped in the current context.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. (1) Online recruitment and voluntary participation may have introduced self-selection bias. Those who are more digitally connected, open to discussing sensitive topics or already engaged with online MSM communities were more likely to participate. In contrast, more hidden, socially isolated or privacy-conscious MSM may have been underrepresented. (2) The sample size was skewed towards participants with a higher level of education, which may limit its representativeness. MSM from rural areas, lower socioeconomic backgrounds, or under stronger familial constraints may experience different stressors and coping patterns, thus restricting the transferability of findings. (3) Although online interviews are convenient and can enhance anonymity and safety, they are technologically mediated and involve joint data construction. This can limit the representativeness, depth of interaction, contextual information and richness of the data, and it can influence what participants disclose due to the reduced sense of presence and fewer non-verbal cues. In future, a combination of online and offline methods could be used when more in-depth context is required. (4) Reporting and social desirability biases are possible due to the sensitivity of topics such as sexual behaviour, mental health, and family pressure in the Chinese socio-cultural context. Participants may have underreported distress, risk behaviours, or identity-related struggles, and retrospective accounts may also be affected by recall bias. (5) The first author conducted all interviews, transcriptions, and translations. This concentration of data collection and early processing in a single researcher may have introduced bias, including the influence of interviewer positionality on participant disclosures and interpretive decisions during transcription and translation. The lack of independent corroboration for the translations is a potential limitation, which may affect the accuracy and faithfulness of the data presented.

Implications

The findings have several implications for practice and policy. The combined pressures of economic uncertainty, family expectations and digital stigma highlight the importance of providing Chinese MSM with accessible support and culturally tailored community interventions. In clinical practice, providers should adopt LGBT-affirming and culturally competent approaches that assess multiple stressors, the burden of concealment, and post-COVID uncertainty, rather than focusing solely on symptoms. Interventions should integrate support from families, communities and society, including promoting family acceptance, providing offline community support and promoting more legal protections and social services for marginalized groups. Mental healthcare should also provide vocational support and anti-discrimination employment advocacy. At a policy level, expanding LGBT-friendly counselling services and improving privacy protections could reduce minority stress and encourage greater use of services. MSM in the West have shown greater psychological resilience, partly due to the strength of their community organisations and the existence of legal protections. This could inform the development of improved policies in China. Future research should employ longitudinal and mixed methods designs to examine how structural instability and constrained life planning impact long-term mental and sexual health outcomes among Chinese MSM.

Conclusion

This study reveals the mental health challenges and coping faced by Chinese MSM in the post-COVID era increasing the understanding of the mental health challenge faced by such group. These findings highlight the need for tailored mental health support and social change for MSM in China calling for more inclusive policies and improved access to mental health resources.
