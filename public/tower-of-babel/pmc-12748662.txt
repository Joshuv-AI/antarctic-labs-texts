Analysis of urban residents’ willingness to pay for packaging cancer screening and its influencing factors: a cross-sectional study in Sichuan, China
Authors: Zhigang Zhong, Xiaoxia Liu, Liang Qiao, Xingyue Pu, Lina Li, Jing Ma, Jiahao Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Packaging cancer screening (PCS) offers a cost-effective approach to detecting multiple cancers simultaneously, optimizing resource use and expanding coverage in resource-limited settings. This study investigates urban Chinese residents’ willingness to pay (WTP) for PCS and its determinants to guide policy on cancer screening expansion. Data were collected from urban residents aged 40–74 in China using face-to-face questionnaires. Univariate analysis of willingness to pay for PCS was performed using the chi-square test. A logit regression model was used to conduct the multivariate analysis. Of the 3268 survey respondents included in the analysis, 75.98% were willing to undergo annual screening when the cost of PCS was completely free, and only 22.37% were willing to do so in the case of out-of-pocket payments for the screening. A total of 56.90% of the respondents were willing to pay a portion of the PCS cost. The median WTP was CNY 500, and the mean WTP ranged from CNY 493.15 to 761.96, accounting for 16.44 to 25.40% of the total screening cost. Moreover, WTP was associated with age (50 ~ < 60 years old, OR = 0.762, 95% CI 0.598–0.972; 60 ~ < 70 years old, OR = 0.588, 95% CI 0.454–0.762; and ≥ 70 years old, OR = 0.519, 95% CI 0.364–0.742), education level (junior high school, OR = 1.398, 95% CI 1.168–1.647; high school/technical secondary school/technical school, OR = 1.353, 95% CI 1.064–1.721; university and above, OR = 1.798, 95% CI 1.257–2.571), annual family income (OR = 1.652, 95% CI 1.406–1.942), self-rated health status (fair, OR = 0.759, 95% CI 0.645–0.892; poor, OR = 0.678, 95% CI 0.523–0.879), and region (Shuangliu district, OR = 0.421, 95% CI 0.338–0.525; Zilujing district, OR = 0.196, 95% CI 0.162–0.237). Screening cost significantly influences PCS participation frequency among urban Chinese residents. Free screening increases willingness for annual screening, whereas out-of-pocket payments reduce participation. Over half of residents are willing to contribute partially, covering about 25% of costs. Public awareness campaigns and multi-source financing (government, society, insurance) are recommended to reduce economic barriers and expand screening coverage.

Introduction

Cancer is the leading cause of death in most countries worldwide and is a significant barrier to increasing life expectancy1–3. The incidence and mortality rates of lung cancer, oesophageal cancer, gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, liver cancer, and breast cancer in China have always remained at a high level4. Thus, these types of cancers must be focused on for cancer prevention and control. Cancer screening is a key measure for reducing cancer mortality and improving cancer survival rates. Many studies have demonstrated that there are currently effective screening methods for the above-mentioned key cancer types, including pulmonary Low-Dose Computed Tomography(LDCT), breast ultrasonography and molybdenum target examination, colorectal endoscopy, upper gastrointestinal tract (including oesophagus and stomach) endoscopy, liver ultrasonography plus serum Alpha-Fetoprotein(AFP) test, cervical human papillomavirus DNA test and liquid-based cytology examination5–11.

Although screening technology is becoming increasingly mature, cost considerations must be fully considered when promoting the sustainable development of cancer screening. Most developed countries have established a relatively stable payment mechanism for cancer screening. In these cases, Japan’s cancer screening guidelines recommend that out-of-pocket payments cover 30% of the cost of gastric cancer screening, and the remaining 70% is subsidised by the government12. The United Kingdom provides free cervical, breast, and colorectal cancer screening to the entire population, with the cost of screening borne entirely by the government13. In South Korea, the cost of gastric cancer screening is covered by the government and social insurance for those on the Medical Top-Up Program and those with household incomes below the median14. These mechanisms exemplify the health economics principle of ‘risk pooling’, where financial resources are aggregated to reduce the burden on individuals. They also reflect different societal approaches to navigating the equity-efficiency trade-off inherent in health resource allocation, balancing broad access against fiscal sustainability. Unlike developed countries, China faces practical problems such as limited health resources, unbalanced regional development, large population sizes, a special spectrum of high-incidence cancers, and complex medical insurance systems. Currently, organized cancer screening and early detection programs in China are fully funded by the government for the participants; however, the coverage of these programs remains limited, leaving a substantial portion of the population without access to free screening services. Therefore, improving the utilisation efficiency of existing resources and maximising the social welfare of cancer screening have become urgent issues that need to be addressed.

Packaging cancer screening (PCS) has been proposed as a strategy to intervene in multiple cancer types simultaneously, with the potential to maximize screening efficiency, save health resources, and expand coverage in the face of limited funding and scarce medical resources. In this context, ‘packaging’ primarily refers to the strategic bundling of several established cancer screening tests into a unified program. This integrated approach aims to streamline both financing (via a single, bundled cost for the service package) and service delivery by offering them as a coordinated screening episode within a public health or clinical initiative. The primary goal is to improve efficiency and accessibility.The conceptual framework for packaging multiple health services during a single clinical encounter, such as a cervical cancer screening visit, to optimize resource allocation under constraints has been previously established and provides a methodological foundation for this approach15. Funding for cancer screening directly impacts the sustainability of screening programs and population participation and determining the appropriate personal payment ratio is particularly important. At present, there are many studies on willingness to pay (WTP) for single cancer screening, including colorectal, breast, upper gastrointestinal, and prostate cancer16–19. Evidence supporting the feasibility and potential effectiveness of screening for multiple cancers is emerging. For instance, the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian (PLCO) cancer screening trial, a large randomized controlled study, suggested potential mortality reductions from multi-cancer screening20. Furthermore, large-scale national programs within China, such as the Cancer Screening Program in Urban China which encompasses lung, breast, colorectal, upper gastrointestinal, and liver cancers, demonstrate the ongoing practical implementation and exploration of such integrated approaches21. In such programs, the scheduling of the individual tests (e.g., low-dose CT, endoscopies) would be arranged based on clinical protocols, facility logistics, and participant convenience, and does not necessarily imply that all tests are physically conducted within a single day or a single clinical visit.While detailed cost-effectiveness analyses for specific packaged services remain an area for further research, these initiatives provide a rationale for investigating the implementation mechanisms, including financing and willingness-to-pay, for PCS models. In contrast, research on the WTP for PCS is relatively limited. A study by Zhu et al. investigated WTP for a multiple-cancer packaging screening program in urban China22, primarily using descriptive analysis to report the proportion willing to pay, alongside a study conducted in rural China that applied the CVM23. However, evidence quantifying the precise amount of WTP for a defined PCS bundle of major cancers using rigorous valuation methods like the DBDC format remains scarce. This study aims to address this gap by specifically investigating the WTP, using the DBDC-CVM, for a PCS program encompassing lung, oesophageal, gastric, colorectal, liver, and breast cancers among urban residents, thereby providing a more precise economic valuation.

The present study aimed to investigate the WTP for PCS for six common cancers (lung cancer, oesophageal cancer, gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, liver cancer, and breast cancer) among urban residents in China, as well as its influencing factors. This study may provide a reference for developing rational cancer screening strategies in China and other developing countries.

Methods

Study population

The study population was determined using a cross-sectional multi-stage cluster sampling method considering the level of economic development and geographical location between November 2021 and April 2022.Chengdu and Zigong cities in Sichuan Province were selected as the study areas. From these cities, 1 to 2 urban districts (Longquanyi, Shuangliu, and Zilujing districts) and 2 to 5 communities within each district were chosen. This survey targeted permanent residents aged 40 to 74 with local household registration (Hukou) or who had resided locally for ≥ 6 months. The exclusion criteria were: (1) individuals who had severe complications of the heart, brain, kidney, or other vital organs and were unable to complete the survey; or (2) those who suffered from mental and neurological disorders, deafness, mental retardation, or other related diseases and were unable to cooperate in completing the survey. A total of 3270 questionnaires were retrieved. After removing 2 unqualified questionnaires, 3,268 valid questionnaires were obtained resulting in an effective rate of 99.94%. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sichuan Cancer Hospital (Ethics No: SCCHEC-02–2021-082) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant Chinese regulations. All participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed about the study objectives, procedures, potential risks/benefits, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Questionnaire design

In this study, uniformly trained investigators conducted a face-to-face survey of respondents using a self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire included basic information on the respondents, WTP, the payment amount for PCS, reasons for unwillingness to pay, and other information. All respondents provided written informed consent before the survey.

In assessing WTP for the screening, the classic Contingent Valuation Method (CVM) was adopted in this study24–26. We employed the guidance technology of WTP in a Double-bounded Dichotomous Choices format (DBDC) to investigate the WTP for PCS . The total cost of the PCS service was set at CNY 3,000. This amount was determined primarily based on the actual service prices of medical institutions in the surveyed areas and was aligned with the cost reference from a previous related study20 to ensure comparability. First, the following study scenario hypothesis was introduced to the respondents: “The government is currently planning to implement a PCS service involving 4 to 6 types of cancer screening services (specifically, screening for lung, oesophageal, gastric, colorectal, liver, and breast cancer using established methods such as low-dose CT for lung cancer, endoscopy for upper gastrointestinal cancers, etc.) are packaged together, and the total cost is approximately CNY 3,000. This fee would be a one-time payment for the bundled service episode. If only part of the cost is covered by the government funds and individuals need to pay a certain fee, conceived as an out-of-pocket payment at the time of service, are you willing to pay part of the cost?” The respondents were then asked whether they were willing to pay a portion of the PCS fee. If so, they randomly chose an initial value from a series of pre-set payment amounts (CNY 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900, 1,000, and above). Respondents who chose a higher or lower initial value were asked to make a second choice until the maximum value of the WTP was reached. Statistical analysis

EpiData 3.1 and SPSS 21.0 software were used for data entry and statistical analysis, respectively. The WTP expectation was used to determine the WTP amount. The chi-square test was used to conduct a subgroup analysis of different demographic characteristics on WTP for PCS to analyse the influencing factors of WTP. A logit regression model was used to conduct the multivariate analysis.

Discussion

In the context of limited health resources, PCS can simultaneously intervene in several cancer types, improve screening efficiency, and save health resources. Thus, this screening strategy is a good choice for many developing countries. The present study builds upon the limited existing evidence, including the preliminary work in urban China22, by providing a more precise quantification of WTP for a defined PCS bundle using the DBDC-CVM approach, and explores its influencing factors among urban residents, thereby complementing existing studies in rural areas23. This study provides a reference for promoting PCS and developing a reasonable screening payment mechanism.

Here, we found that 75.98% of the respondents were willing to undergo screening once a year in response to free PCS. In the case of out-of-pocket payments for the screening, only 22.37% were willing to do so. This sharp contrast in the acceptable screening frequency under free versus out-of-pocket scenarios quantifies the profound suppressive effect of personal costs on screening adherence and underscores that establishing a stable financing mechanism is critical for achieving high participation. Consistently, a study in Japan showed that reducing individuals’ out-of-pocket costs for screening can increase the participation rate of screening for breast cancer and cervical cancer in the population27. A study in the United States demonstrated that the reimbursement rate of Medicaid was closely related to the rate of cancer screening participation28. In China, establishing a long-term and stable government- or society-based financing mechanism for screening costs is the key to the long-term and efficient operation of PCS services.

WTP is an important factor affecting compliance, and research on this topic is necessary29. In the present study, 56.90% of survey respondents were willing to pay part of the PCS cost. This proportion was comparable to that reported for rural residents in China (57.66%)23, yet was substantially lower than the WTP for single-cancer screening programs reported among urban Chinese populations, such as for colorectal cancer screening (91.7%)29 and upper gastrointestinal cancer screening (81.7%)30. It was also lower than the WTP for single-cancer screening documented in the United Kingdom (88%)31, the United States (72%)16, and Iran (82%)32. This substantial discrepancy in WTP is likely attributable to the considerably higher upfront cost of the PCS bundle compared to any single-cancer screening, which presents a significant financial barrier and may deter participation despite the broader screening coverage it offers. Among the 1408 respondents unwilling to pay for the screening, 80.30% were unwilling to pay because of “unaffordable cost”, and 19.30% were unwilling because of “no time”. On the one hand, this survey indicates that residents have limited payment ability, and their economic level restricts their screening attitudes. On the other hand, these findings also suggest a lack of awareness of screening, a failure to recognise the benefits of screening, and a reluctance to spend economic and time on cancer screening programs among residents. In this survey, the median amount respondents were willing to pay was CNY 500. The mean WTP ranged from CNY 493.15 to CNY 761.96. When comparing the median WTP from this urban study (CNY 500) with the mean WTP reported in the rural study (CNY 622)23, it appears urban residents’ WTP might be lower. However, this comparison requires careful interpretation. The median is a more robust indicator for skewed WTP data, while the mean can be influenced by high WTP outliers. The observed difference may reflect both true urban–rural disparities in economic capacity and the methodological choice of the central tendency measure. Nonetheless, the proportion of the total cost that individuals are willing to cover appears broadly consistent, with our urban sample’s WTP amounting to 16.44%-25.40% of the total cost across sensitivity scenarios, compared to 20.73% in the rural study23.

Herein, the analysis of influencing factors revealed that age was related to WTP. It has been shown that the elderly population is less willing to pay for PCS33. These results may be associated with different health beliefs or a lower perceived benefit of preventive screening among older individuals, who are reluctant to pay for disease prevention and accept new screening forms. Consistent with previous studies34–36, we observed that residents with higher incomes and education levels were more willing to pay for cancer screening. This observation may be related to the affordability of these individuals as well as their knowledge and understanding of health information. This socioeconomic gradient in WTP risks exacerbating existing health inequities, as those with greater resources can better access preventive services. This directly relates to the equity-efficiency trade-off in health resource allocation discussed in the Introduction, where over-reliance on individual payments can compromise equitable access. Moreover, the present study revealed that self-rated health status was also related to WTP. In this case, those with better health status tended to pay more attention to their physical condition and were more willing to pay a certain fee to utilise cancer screening services, and vice versa. In accordance with research in rural areas of China19, the present study identified region as another factor influencing the WTP for cancer screening. This result can be attributed to differences in the economic level between regions. According to the "2023 Sichuan Statistical Yearbook", the per capita GDP of Chengdu is greater than that of Zigong. Thus, regions with higher economic levels have greater affordability. The above result may be related to the health literacy of residents in cancer prevention and treatment among regions. Residents in regions with higher overall levels of health literacy have greater compliance with screening and are more willing to utilise screening services. Based on the above findings, we recommend that the financing plan for PCS should favour elderly people and people with poor economic status who are often more prone to cancer but lack the ability to pay, as well as the awareness of screening. Compared to the cost of screening, the enormous cost of treatment in the event of cancer is undoubtedly even more unaffordable.

This study has several limitations. First, the proposed PCS package represents a conceptual model for exploratory purposes, and its cost-effectiveness remains to be substantiated in future studies. Importantly, the model does not incorporate risk stratification, which is essential for clinically and economically efficient screening. Second, the survey was conducted in only three cities within Sichuan Province. While this provides valuable initial data, the findings are not nationally representative due to socioeconomic variations across China’s urban areas. Additionally, our sampling frame may underrepresent internal migrant populations. Since these groups are often more economically vulnerable and have less stable health insurance, this underrepresentation limits the applicability of our WTP estimates to the entire urban population, particularly in large cities with high migrant inflows.Third, only the influencing factors of WTP were analysed, and no in-depth study of the influencing factors of the amount of payment was conducted.Future research should analyze payment levels to uncover potential nonlinear relationships and could employ specialized models (e.g., Tobit models) suited for such WTP data. Fourth, while the factors influencing WTP were investigated regarding demographic characteristics, factors such as health literacy for cancer prevention and treatment, healthy behaviour and lifestyle, and screening providers were not addressed.Future studies could incorporate these psychosocial and behavioral dimensions, for instance, by applying frameworks like the Andersen Behavioral Model to comprehensively understand screening utilization.

In conclusion, urban residents in China have low WTP for PCS, and the amount they are willing to pay is limited. There is still a certain distance to go in promoting PCS programs. To improve accessibility and equity, particularly for vulnerable populations, a multi-source financing model (combining government, social, and insurance funds) should be implemented. Our finding that individuals are willing to cover approximately 25% of the total cost suggests that public and social funds would need to subsidize the remaining majority, approximately 75%, to ensure affordability and broad participation.This model, which embodies the risk-pooling principles evident in the financing systems of countries such as Japan, the UK, and South Korea, is essential to keep out-of-pocket costs affordable and reduce financial barriers to screening in China.
