Protocol for an immunocompetent KPC mouse model of pancreatic cancer resection and recurrence for perioperative treatment studies
Authors: Jacob L Léger, Nawal Amhis, Sarah Mansouri, Maria Fernanda Meza Pacheco, Lauren Daniel, Hugo Giguère, Yves Collin, Lee-Hwa Tai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn mouse models of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, surgical removal of orthotopic tumors typically involves removing the primary tumor along with the spleen. Here, we present a protocol for surgical removal of the tumor in the tail of the pancreas while preserving the spleen. We describe steps for implantation of mouse pancreatic cancer cells followed by the identification and removal of the orthotopic tumor. We also outline perioperative treatment strategies for tumor recurrence.Subject areas: Cancer, Immunology, Model Organisms

Before you begin

This protocol outlines the establishment of an orthotopic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) model in immunocompetent mice for resection and recurrence. KPC1245 cells, derived from pancreatic tumors in LSL-KrasG12D; LSL-Trp53R172H; Pdx1-Cre mice are surgically implanted into the pancreas of C57BL/6 mice. Use newly thawed cells within a relatively low passage range and avoid prolonged in vitro culture, as changes in passage number may affect tumor growth and experimental reproducibility. Determine the number of mice required for each experimental group in advance, accounting for potential losses during tumor implantation, tumor establishment, resection, and postoperative recovery. If perioperative treatments are being tested, treatment schedules should be established before starting the experimental cohort. Ensure that all surgical rooms, instruments and supplies are available and in hand before starting. An approved standardized plan for perioperative monitoring, analgesia, recovery, and humane endpoints should also be in place and in accordance with the relevant institutional and regulatory guidelines for animal experimentation. In theory, this protocol could be adapted and optimized to other pancreatic cell lines and mice of different genetic backgrounds.

Innovation

Advanced metastatic disease is a main contributor to the poor clinical outcomes of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC).1 Surgical resection currently represents the only potentially curative treatment; however, only a small proportion of patients present with resectable disease.2,3 Even after a successful surgery, patients can experience local or distant recurrence, suggesting that metastatic or residual tumor cells persist despite a complete resection.4,5 As the use of neoadjuvant therapies increases and more patients become eligible for surgery, understanding the mechanisms driving postoperative recurrence has become increasingly important as multiple studies have demonstrated that the period following surgery is marked by a distinct state of immunosuppression, which cancer cells can exploit.6,7,8 Preclinical mouse models have been essential for studying PDAC biology and testing therapeutic strategies. Many groups have developed orthotopic and surgically resectable PDAC models in immunocompetent mice to accurately reproduce the clinical scenario of tumor removal followed by disease recurrence. These models typically include a pancreatic transection and a partial or complete splenectomy.9,10,11 However, preserving the spleen is particularly important for immunological studies as it maintains the immune competence of mice and avoids the physiological consequences associated with a splenectomy.12,13 To address this limitation, this protocol describes a distal pancreatectomy with complete splenic preservation. This challenging dissection procedure protects the fragile splenic vessels while also achieving a complete removal of the pancreatic tumor. This innovation enables the use of fully immunocompetent mice, providing a physiologically relevant model to study immune responses to treatments in the perioperative time frame.

Orthotopic implantation of KPC cells

Timing: 1 day

These steps outline how to surgically implant KPC cells into the pancreas of a C57BL/6 mouse using a small incision beneath the left rib cage. It is important to adhere to the surgical guidelines approved in your institution’s UACC protocol.

Note: Each procedure initially takes around 15–25 min to complete alone, all the way down to under 10 min after gaining experience.

Note: Before starting the procedure, ensure that the anesthesia system and the isoflurane scavenging unit are functioning properly. Throughout the surgery, carefully monitor the mouse for signs of excessive or insufficient anesthesia and adjust the isoflurane flow rate as needed.

Note: The mouse should remain on a heating pad during the surgery and throughout the recovery period.

Expected outcomes

Mice implanted orthotopically with KPC cells using this protocol are expected to develop pancreatic tumors of approximately 300 mm3 within 8 days. At this stage, distal pancreatectomy can be performed, providing a model to investigate tumor recurrence following primary tumor removal. The disease progression without including the resection surgery can result in significant morbidity as early as day 14 post-implantation.

After the resection surgery, mice develop detectable primary tumor recurrence within 5–10 days, with some exhibiting peritoneal metastasis and/or ascites at later stages of tumor progression. In this model, neoadjuvant chemotherapy administered prior to resection has shown to improve survival outcomes (Figure 3A), which recapitulates aspects of current clinical outcomes.17,18

This protocol can be used by experimenters to evaluate the tolerability and potential effects of different combination treatment regimens on tumor recurrence and survival. Perioperative treatment strategies are outlined to evaluate therapeutic approaches aimed at preventing or delaying tumor recurrence (Figure 3B).

Limitations

Cell and tumor growth may vary under both in vitro and in vivo conditions. Very rare instances of metastasis and local invasion to the spleen and abdominal wall have been documented prior to the resection. Mice exhibiting evidence of metastatic disease prior to the planned resection should be excluded from subsequent recurrence analyses. Additionally, variability in tumor establishment can influence how tumors respond to treatment, and treatment-related toxicity may confound survival outcomes.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Air bubbles form in the basement membrane matrix while preparing aliquots or the injection mix (step 5).

Potential solution

To avoid bubbles, handle the viscous solution with slow, gentle pipetting, taking care not to introduce air from the pipette tip into the gel mixture. If too many bubbles are introduced, rest on ice for 15 min to allow bubbles to rise to the surface of the solution.

Problem 2

The basement membrane matrix solution or injection mix begins to polymerize too early (step 5).

Potential solution

To avoid this issue, keep the basement membrane matrix solution on ice at all times, including during pipetting and dispensing. If the stock solution polymerizes, it must be remade. If polymerization occurs during cell preparation or surgery, discard the affected gel and prepare a new mix, ensuring the solution remains on ice throughout. A great way to avoid this issue is to prepare several aliquots of injection mix instead of one big volume solution (5 x 1 mL aliquots instead of 1 x 5 mL aliquots).

Problem 3

Cells do not become confluent after 2–3 days (Step 1).

Potential solution

This can happen for several reasons. Cells that become overconfluent may enter cell cycle arrest and fail to proliferate effectively. In such cases, thaw a new vial of cells and restart the culture protocol. Also, ensure that the incubator is properly humidified and that there is no evidence of contamination. It is not recommended to implant cells immediately after they thaw. Instead, allow them to recover and reach the exponential growth phase for at least 2–3 passages before implantation.

Problem 4

Tumor sizes observed in vivo show considerable variation (Step 12).

Potential solution

Although some variation in tumor size is normal, excessive differences can be problematic. Prior to injecting KPC cells in the pancreas, thoroughly mix the cells by pipetting to ensure even distribution. It is important to note that different clones of the KPC cell lines may exhibit differences in growth characteristics and tumor-forming capacity. Therefore, the specific KPC cell line and its source should be considered when interpreting tumor growth variability. In extreme cases, an in vivo growth curve kinetic experiment (Figure 1F) may be necessary to account for the variability of newly purchased cells. Contact L.-H.T. for more details.

Problem 5

Excessive bleeding during implantation or during resection surgery (Step 8 and Step 20).

Potential solution

Rough handling of the pancreas and surrounding tissues and prolonged surgical time may increase the risk of excessive bleeding due to poor hemostasis. Using fine surgical instruments and minimal manipulation of the organs greatly reduces the risk of bleeding. Using gentle pressure or a cautery tool remains the best option to control the bleeding; additional absorbable hemostats like SURGICEL can also be used. Practicing sham surgeries before experimental cohorts should improve speed and surgical skills.

Problem 6

High mortality rates after the resection surgery (Step 20).

Potential solution

This can happen for several reasons. Inadequate analgesia or overdosing, hypothermia during or after surgery, fluid loss or pancreatic enzyme leakage. Ensure optimized analgesic regimens, maintain a proper heat source during the surgery, administer saline before the surgery, and monitor closely the animals after the surgery. As for the pancreatic enzyme leakage, ensure that the organ is well cauterized during the resection step as it can rapidly cause digestive enzymes leakage in the peritoneum cavity (ascites buildup), which would result in an early humane endpoint for the animal. Providing softened food or a gel diet on the cage floor may become necessary if the recovery is lengthy.

Problem 7

Infection or inflammation of the surgical wound (Step 8 and Step 20).

Potential solution

Performing surgery with non-sterile surgical tools or using non-sterile surgical techniques may cause wound infections. Ensure the use of appropriate aseptic tools and techniques throughout the procedure to minimize the risk of infection. Improper wound closure may also allow soiled mice bedding to enter the wound, which could also cause infection. Inspect the wounds daily for 2–3 days after the surgery to either close it properly, administer additional antiseptic, or administer antibiotics as per your institution’s UACC guidelines.

Problem 8

No recurrence of the disease in the controls when it is expected (Step 25).

Potential solution

Although some variation is expected in disease recurrence after the resection surgery, no tumor recurrence in surgery-only controls could indicate that the surgery was performed too early before dissemination of the KPC cells to other organs. Delaying the surgery until the tumors get slightly bigger should be more biologically relevant for this KPC resection model. An in vivo growth curve kinetic experiment (Figure 1F) may be necessary to account for the variability of newly purchased cells. Contact L.H.T for more details.

Problem 9

High variability between different users of the protocol (Step 8 and Step 20).

Potential solution

Variability between users of this protocol is very probable as there is a lack of standardized training, and differences in surgical skills may explain these differences. All users of this protocol should perform multiple practice surgeries before experimental work. While this surgery procedure is very cumbersome, carefully planning for attrition in sample size calculations prior to starting experiments will prevent data variability, especially for survival.
