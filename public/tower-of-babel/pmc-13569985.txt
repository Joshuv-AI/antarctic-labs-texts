Integrating CRRT as a Virtual Organ Into Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Modeling to Optimize Antimicrobial Dosing in Septic Patients
Authors: Yuanming Xing, Yijing Zhang, Chuqi Bai, Yulan Qiu, Chuhui Wang, Lei Chen, Taotao Wang, Jiaojiao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContinuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) extracorporeal drug removal, sepsis‐related pathophysiological alterations, and reduced pathogen susceptibility to antimicrobials can lead to treatment failure in septic patients undergoing CRRT, highlighting the urgent need for precision dosing. This study aimed to develop a physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling framework to characterize antimicrobial pharmacokinetics and support dosing optimization in septic patients undergoing CRRT. Drug‐specific PBPK models were established in healthy adults and extrapolated to septic patients by scaling four pathophysiological parameters in PK‐Sim. A CRRT compartment was subsequently incorporated in MoBi as a clearance organ and parameterized by modality and intensity to construct a PBPK framework for septic patients undergoing CRRT. Vancomycin, meropenem, and ceftazidime‐avibactam were selected as representative antimicrobial agents for model development and validated by graphical comparison with reported data, fold error, and geometric mean fold error (GMFE). The final PBPK model adequately captured the observed data, with predicted area under concentration‐time curve and maximum concentration within 0.5–2.0‐fold of observed values and GMFE < 2 for all three antimicrobial agents. The validated model simulated pharmacokinetic profiles of 1000 virtual patients across glomerular filtration rate (GFR) values of 0–30 mL/min, five CRRT modalities, and CRRT intensities of 20–40 mL/kg/h, allowing quantification of their relative impact on drug exposure and guiding dosing optimization. The results showed that both GFR and CRRT intensity influenced drug exposure, with GFR showing a greater contribution. Overall, this study provides a quantitative method for predicting antimicrobial exposure and supports model‐informed precision dosing in septic patients undergoing CRRT.Keywords: antimicrobial agents, continuous renal replacement therapy, pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics, physiologically based pharmacokinetics modeling, sepsis

Introduction

Sepsis is a life‐threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, and often progresses to multiple organ failure and systemic capillary leakage [1]. Globally, it affects approximately 49 million patients each year, including 19 million with severe sepsis [2, 3], and carries a substantial mortality burden of 30%–50% [2, 4, 5]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common complication in sepsis, with an incidence of 50%–60% among septic patients [6]. Among patients with sepsis‐associated AKI, approximately 70% require continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), which helps maintain hemodynamic stability, facilitate toxin clearance, and modulate inflammatory responses [7]. CRRT modalities include continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH), continuous venovenous hemodialysis (CVVHD), and continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF). CVVH and CVVHDF can be further classified into pre‐dilution and post‐dilution modes according to the site of replacement fluid administration. Previous studies have shown that drugs with small molecular weight (< 5000 Da), low protein binding (< 80%), and low apparent volume of distribution (V d ≤ 2 L/kg) are likely to be removed during CRRT [8, 9].

The international guidelines for the management of sepsis and septic shock emphasize prompt antimicrobial administration (ideally within 1 h of recognition) and adequate dosing to improve antimicrobial efficacy and reduce mortality [10]. However, CRRT related extracorporeal drug removal may result suboptimal antimicrobial exposure. The extent of drug removal during CRRT depends on multiple factors, including CRRT modality, CRRT intensity (defined as the combined ultrafiltration and dialysate flow rates normalized to body weight) [11]. Additionally, sepsis‐related pathophysiological alterations and reduced pathogen susceptibility to antimicrobial agents further complicate dosing strategies [12]. These factors make it crucial to determine the optimal dosing regimen for septic patients undergoing CRRT.

Although several studies have reported antimicrobial dosing during CRRT [13, 14, 15], evidence remains inadequate due to: (1) the impact of CRRT on drug pharmacokinetics being drug‐specific, requiring separate trials for each drug, (2) variability in CRRT clinical practices across medical centers necessitating multiple studies encompassing different CRRT modalities and CRRT intensities. Consequently, universal dosage recommendations are difficult to apply across different patients, CRRT modalities and CRRT intensities, emphasizing the need for personalized dosage regimens tailored for septic patients undergoing CRRT.

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling offers a powerful alternative to overcome these challenges by integrating both in vitro and in vivo data, and PBPK modeling has been increasingly applied to characterize drug disposition in special populations. PBPK models represent the organism as physiologically relevant compartments, parameterized by mass‐balance equations describing drug disposition across organs and tissues, allowing extension to special populations through incorporated additional compartment [16, 17]. This study aimed to develop a PBPK modeling framework to characterize antimicrobial pharmacokinetics and support dosing optimization in septic patients undergoing CRRT.

Methods

Candidate Antimicrobial Agents for PBPK Modeling

A comprehensive systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed to identify pharmacokinetic studies of candidate antimicrobial agents published before June 30, 2025. The search strategy combined the following keywords: sepsis (“sepsis”, “septic patients”, or “critically ill patients”), CRRT (“continuous renal replacement therapy,” “continuous venovenous hemofiltration,” “continuous venovenous hemodialysis,” “continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration,” “CRRT,” “CVVH,” “CVVHD,” or “CVVHDF”), and pharmacokinetics.

A total of 408 articles were retrieved, with the screening workflow shown in Figure S1. Candidate drugs for PBPK modeling were selected based on the available pharmacokinetics (PK) evidence in septic patients undergoing CRRT, concentration‐time profiles for model verification (the number of available studies is shown in Table S1), evidence of CRRT‐related removal, and sufficient drug‐specific information for PBPK model construction. Based on these criteria, vancomycin, meropenem, and ceftazidime‐avibactam were selected as representative commonly used antimicrobial agents in this population.

Antimicrobial Agents PBPK Model Development for Septic Patients

PBPK modeling was performed using PK‐Sim software (Version 10.0; Open Systems Pharmacology Suite, https://www.open‐systems‐pharmacology.org/). The organism was represented by various compartments corresponding to relevant organs or tissues, interconnected through blood flow rates via arterial and venous blood pools. Each organ compartment was structurally refined into four sub‐compartments: plasma, blood cells, interstitial space, and intracellular space. Drug disposition across different compartments was mathematically described using mass‐balance equations, incorporating passive diffusion and tissue‐partitioning behavior predicted from physicochemical properties.

The PBPK model was first developed in healthy adults and then extrapolated to septic patients, as shown in the PBPK modeling workflow (Figure 1). The PBPK model for meropenem in septic patients was adopted from our previous study [18], whereas PBPK models for vancomycin and ceftazidime‐avibactam were developed in this study.

PBPK Model Development in Healthy Adults

Drug‐specific parameters for vancomycin and ceftazidime‐avibactam, including physicochemical properties and elimination pathways, were obtained from DrugBank (https://www.drugbank.ca/), SciFinder (https://scifinder.cas.org), and published literature (Table 1). Tissue‐to‐plasma partition coefficients were used to characterize tissue distribution, with the Schmitt method applied for vancomycin and the Rodgers and Rowland method for ceftazidime‐avibactam according to their physicochemical properties. Given that renal elimination via glomerular filtration predominates for vancomycin and ceftazidime‐avibactam [21, 22], a glomerular filtration rate (GFR) fraction of 1 was implemented to describe glomerular filtration‐mediated renal clearance. Model parameters were optimized using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, including the lipophilicity of vancomycin and the OAT3 V max of meropenem. Optimization was continued until satisfactory agreement was achieved between model predictions and observed data.

PBPK Model Evaluation in Healthy Adults

The healthy adult models were validated against clinical pharmacokinetic data identified through a systematic PubMed search with keywords “vancomycin/ceftazidime‐avibactam”, “pharmacokinetics”, and “healthy”. Extracted data included demographic characteristics (age, sex, height, weight), medication details (dose, infusion duration), plasma concentration‐time profiles, and pharmacokinetic parameters (Table S2). The concentration‐time data were digitized using WebPlot Digitizer (Version 5.2; https://automeris.io/WebPlotDigitizer).

Virtual individuals representing the mean or median characteristics of the corresponding clinical studies were generated based on reported demographic data. Default software values were applied for missing demographic parameters. The reported dosing regimens and CRRT settings were then applied to simulate plasma concentration‐time profiles. Simulated concentration‐time profiles and pharmacokinetic parameters were compared with observed data reported in the literature to confirm model predictive performance.

Extrapolation of the PBPK Model From Healthy Adults to Septic Patients

The healthy adult models were adapted to septic patients by incorporating sepsis‐associated pathophysiological changes. Specifically, scaling factors were applied to adjust GFR, renal volume, renal blood flow, cardiac output, albumin concentration, hematocrit, total body weight, and the interstitial/plasma protein ratio. These scaling factors were derived from our previously published meropenem PBPK model in critically ill patients and were calculated as the ratio of critically ill patients to that in healthy adults [18].

PBPK Model Evaluation for Septic Patients

The septic patient models were validated against clinical pharmacokinetic data from septic patients not undergoing CRRT (Table S3). Literature search and data extraction followed the same approach used for healthy adults. Model‐predicted concentration‐time profiles and pharmacokinetic parameters were compared with observed clinical data.

Integration of CRRT as a Clearance Organ Into the PBPK Model for Septic Patients

CRRT Model Development and Parameterization

Following validation of the sepsis PBPK model, a CRRT compartment was incorporated to develop the PBPK model for septic patients undergoing CRRT using MoBi (Version 10.0; Open Systems Pharmacology Suite, https://www.open‐systems‐pharmacology.org/). The CRRT compartment was implemented as an extracorporeal clearance module and consisted of three sub‐compartments: plasma, blood cells, and CRRT effluent. The total volume of the CRRT compartment was set to 0.5 L as a simplified apparent effective volume to approximate the effective mixing space of the extracorporeal circuit, including the blood‐side circuit, hemofilter, and associated tubing. To maintain consistency with the PBPK structure, the CRRT compartment was connected to both arterial blood and venous blood in the MoBi spatial structure. Although this configuration differs from the veno‐venous circuitry used in clinical CRRT, CRRT‐related clearance in the model was implemented solely as drug transfer from the CRRT venous plasma compartment to the CRRT effluent compartment.

Drug transport between plasma, blood cells, and CRRT effluent in CRRT compartment was defined by Equations ((1), (2), (3), (4)), while antimicrobial clearance was described by Equation (5). To simplify the model, CRRT clearance was assumed to occur in the plasma compartment, as three antimicrobial agents are predominantly distributed within the plasma.

Quantification of Drug Clearance During CRRT

The extracorporeal clearance of drugs during CRRT is determined by CRRT modality, CRRT intensity, and dilution method. The CVVH modality primarily removes solutes via convection, with clearance calculated using the ultrafiltrate flow rate (Q uf) and the sieving coefficient (S c), as specified in Equations (6) and (7). The CVVHD modality predominantly removes solutes via diffusion, where clearance depends on the dialysate flow rate (Q d) and saturation coefficient (S d) according to Equation (8). CVVHDF integrates both diffusion and convection for solute elimination, with clearance determined by the Q uf, Q d, and S d, as defined in Equations (9) and (10).

Given that patient net fluid balances were seldom available in the original literature, a state of fluid equilibrium was assumed, and the net ultrafiltration rate was set to zero. Accordingly, the ultrafiltration flow rate was equal to the replacement fluid flow rate (Q uf = Q rep). For studies that reported CRRT intensity, the corresponding Q uf or Q d was dynamically calculated by multiplying the reported CRRT intensity by body weight.

PBPK Model Development and Evaluation for Septic Patients Undergoing CRRT

The PBPK model development workflow for septic patients undergoing CRRT is illustrated in Figure 1. A total of 18 studies were identified for PBPK model construction and validation in septic patients undergoing CRRT (Table S4). Relevant demographic characteristics, plasma concentration‐time profiles, pharmacokinetic parameters, and CRRT operational data were extracted to generate virtual patients in PK‐Sim. Sepsis‐associated pathophysiological parameters were scaled and subsequently exported to MoBi. A CRRT organ compartment was incorporated, and CRRT‐related parameters (Q uf, Q d, and Q b) were defined according to original pharmacokinetic studies (Table S4). The S c and S d parameters for each antibacterial agent were obtained from published in vitro or clinical data and are presented in Table 1.

The PBPK model predictive performance was initially evaluated by visually comparing the predicted plasma drug concentration with the observed values. To quantitatively evaluate the model, the ratios of predicted to observed values (fold‐error) for the area under concentration‐time curve (AUC) and maximum concentration (C max) were calculated, with values within 0.8–1.25 (rigorous criterion) and 0.5–2.0 (standard criterion) considered acceptable. Model performance was further evaluated using goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) plots and calculation of geometric mean fold error (GMFE, Equation 11) for all predicted AUCs and C max values, where GMFE < 2 indicated adequate predictive performance [27]. For studies that did not report pharmacokinetic parameters, noncompartmental analysis was performed using MonolixSuite 2024R1 to derive the corresponding pharmacokinetic parameters. These parameters were used exclusively for model validation and were not used in subsequent dosage optimization.

where predicted pharmacokinetic parameteri is the predicted values of AUC or C max, observed pharmacokinetics parameteri is the observed AUC or C max values, n is the number of studies.

Sensitivity Analyses

A sensitivity analysis was conducted under the pre‐dilution CVVHDF modality, which incorporates both convective and diffusive clearance mechanisms and accounts for the influence of blood dilution on CRRT clearance, and CRRT intensity was set to 30 mL/kg/h. This analysis aimed to identify key determinants of antimicrobial exposure. The selected parameters consisted of drug‐specific properties, including pKa, lipophilicity, and fraction unbound; sepsis‐associated pathophysiological parameters, including GFR, albumin concentration, hematocrit, and the protein ratio (interstitial/plasma); and CRRT‐related parameters, including Q uf, Q d, S d, and Q b. A virtual individual was generated using septic patient demographic characteristics reported by Abraham et al. [28], corresponding to a European male aged 55 years and weighing 85 kg. The primary sensitivity analysis was performed in virtual patients with a GFR of 15 mL/min. To further assess the influence of residual renal function, an additional GFR analysis was conducted by varying GFR across 0, 5, 10, 15, and 30 mL/min. Pharmacokinetic changes across different parameters were evaluated using the following dosage regimens: vancomycin 0.5 g q8h, meropenem 0.5 g q12h, and ceftazidime‐avibactam 1.25 g q12h.

Dosage Regimen Optimization

Dosing regimen optimization was conducted using the validated PBPK model to identify optimal antimicrobial dosage regimens for septic patients undergoing CRRT (Figure 1). Systematic simulations were performed across various dosage regimens, residual renal function categories, CRRT modalities, and CRRT intensities.

A representative virtual individual was first defined based on the demographic characteristics of septic patients reported by Abraham et al. [28], corresponding to a European male aged 55 years and weighing 85 kg. This setting was selected to establish a standardized septic CRRT patient for subsequent simulation. A virtual population of 1000 individuals was generated for each simulation scenario. Inter‐individual variability in relevant anatomical and physiological parameters was automatically incorporated using the software's internal Monte Carlo sampling algorithm. Simulations were conducted under two GFR categories, including 0–15, 15–30 mL/min. Five CRRT modalities were evaluated, with CRRT intensities ranging from 20 to 40 mL/kg/h in 5 mL/kg/h increments. The blood flow rate was fixed at 200 mL/min. For the CVVHDF modality, the total CRRT intensity was evenly distributed between dialysate and replacement fluid flow rates at a 1:1 ratio. Despite the variability in clinical CRRT dilution strategies [29], only 100% pre‐dilution and 100% post‐dilution were considered to standardize the simulation settings, while mixed pre−/post‐dilution strategies were not included in the model.

Different dosage regimens were selected based on drug instructions and published literature. For vancomycin, the efficacy target was defined as AUC > 427 mg·h/L based on our previously published study. Compared with the conventional AUC > 400 mg·h/L, this threshold was specifically derived for patients undergoing CRRT based on receiver operating characteristic curve analysis [30]. In addition, an efficacy‐safety analysis was performed using an AUC range of 427–600 mg·h/L, with AUC > 600 mg·h/L considered a potential toxicity risk. For meropenem and ceftazidime‐avibactam, the PK/PD target was defined as 100% fT>MIC, indicating that the unbound plasma concentration remained above the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) throughout the entire dosing interval [31]. The probability of target attainment (PTA), defined as the proportion of virtual patients achieving the predefined PK/PD target, was evaluated for meropenem and ceftazidime‐avibactam using MIC values corresponding to their clinical susceptibility thresholds (breakpoints). The MIC values were 2 mg/L for meropenem and 8 mg/L for ceftazidime‐avibactam, according to EUCAST clinical breakpoints (https://www.eucast.org/clinical_breakpoints). The optimal dosing regimen was defined as the lowest‐dose regimen achieving a PTA ≥ 90%. For vancomycin, when a PTA ≥ 90% could not be achieved, the regimen with the highest PTA was selected.

Results

Antimicrobial Agents PBPK Model Development for Septic Patients

PBPK Model Development and Evaluation in Healthy Adults

For vancomycin, the initial LogP value was 1.11, obtained from DrugBank, and was optimized to 2.41 based on single‐dose pharmacokinetic data [32]. The optimized value improved the agreement between predicted and observed concentration‐time profiles and was therefore retained in the final model. This optimized LogP value should be interpreted as a model‐dependent apparent parameter reflecting vancomycin lipophilicity. Notably, the optimized value was comparable to those reported in previous vancomycin PBPK models, which used LogP values of 2.45 and 2.23 [33, 34]. The initial parameter values for ceftazidime‐avibactam were kept unchanged (Table 1).

Pharmacokinetic data from healthy adult studies were used to validate the PBPK model for healthy adults; the predicted values generated by the PBPK model showed good consistency with observed data (Figures S2 and S3, and Table S5). Both vancomycin and ceftazidime‐avibactam demonstrated 100% of predicted AUC and C max values within the 0.5–2‐fold range. For the more rigorous 0.8–1.25‐fold range, vancomycin predictions achieved 90.91% (10/11) for AUC and 81.82% (9/11) for C max, while ceftazidime‐avibactam showed 94.44% (17/18) and 77.78% (14/18) accuracy, respectively. GOF plot (Figure 2) further supported model accuracy and reliability. The GMFE values for both AUC and C max of vancomycin (1.08, 1.15) and ceftazidime‐avibactam (1.11, 1.13) remained below the accepted threshold of 2, demonstrating adequate predictive performance of the adult PBPK model and enabling scaling to septic patients.

PBPK Model Development and Evaluation in Septic Patients

The virtual patients were developed using demographic characteristics of septic patients from published studies (Table S3). Model accuracy was enhanced by adjusting physiological parameters including GFR, organ volumes, hematocrit, protein ratio (interstitial/plasma). The final model accurately predicted plasma concentration‐time profiles for all literature‐reported dosage regimens (Figures S4 and S5), with predicted AUC and C max values consistently within 0.5–2‐fold of observed values. Specifically, for vancomycin, 87.5% (7/8) of predicted AUC values and 75% (6/8) of predicted C max values achieved the more stringent 0.8–1.25‐fold accuracy range (Figure 2, Table S6), with GMFE of 1.10 and 1.15, respectively. For ceftazidime‐avibactam, 80% (8/10) of both AUC and C max predictions met the 0.8–1.25‐fold criterion, with corresponding GMFE of 1.09 and 1.11.

CRRT Model Development and Parameterization

The structure and application of the PBPK model, including the newly incorporated CRRT compartment, are shown in Figure S6. Antimicrobial agent concentrations in the CRRT plasma compartment were initialized to be equivalent to the venous plasma concentration, and drug concentrations declined as the compounds were removed through the effluent compartment (Figure S7), confirming that the CRRT compartment was successfully integrated and contributed to drug elimination.

PBPK Model Development and Evaluation for Septic Patients Undergoing CRRT

By incorporating the CRRT compartment, the predictive performance of the PBPK model for all three antimicrobial agents was improved. Simulated plasma concentration‐time profiles for vancomycin, meropenem, and ceftazidime‐avibactam across diverse CRRT modalities and intensities in septic patients matched observed data (Figure 3, Appendix S1), with all predicted pharmacokinetic parameters within 0.5–2.0‐fold of observed values. For vancomycin, 75% (6/8) of AUC and 87.5% (7/8) of C max predictions fell within 0.8–1.25‐fold. For meropenem and ceftazidime‐avibactam, the corresponding values were 81.8% (9/11) and 90.9% (10/11), 71.4% (5/7) and 71.4% (5/7) for AUC and C max, respectively (Figure 2, Table 2). GMFEs of AUC and C max for three antimicrobial agents, vancomycin (1.15, 1.09); meropenem (1.16, 1.18), and ceftazidime‐avibactam (1.15, 1.20), fall well within the acceptable range of < 2.0. These results demonstrate that the PBPK model reliably predicts antimicrobial exposure across CRRT modalities and intensities in septic patients.

Sensitivity Analyses

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify influential input parameters affecting AUC and C max (Figure S8). For vancomycin, lipophilicity, albumin concentration, fraction unbound, and GFR were the most influential parameters, with a greater impact on AUC than on C max. For meropenem, fraction unbound and lipophilicity were the most influential parameters, followed by OAT3‐mediated transport and CRRT‐related parameters. For ceftazidime‐avibactam, GFR, fraction unbound, Q uf, Q d, and S d had the greatest influence on exposure.

To assess the impact of residual renal function on antimicrobial exposure, changes in AUC than C max across different GFR levels were evaluated. Compared with the reference scenario (GFR = 15 mL/min), antimicrobial exposure increased when GFR decreased to 0–10 mL/min and decreased when GFR increased to 30 mL/min (Figure S9). The effect of GFR variation was more pronounced on AUC than on C max.

Dosage Regimen Optimization

Dosage Regimens Across CRRT Modalities and Intensities

Dosage regimens across different CRRT modalities and intensities were evaluated with GFR within 0–15 mL/min (Figure 4). Overall, PTA varied < 30% across different CRRT modalities, with post‐dilution CVVH showing the lowest PTA. For vancomycin, PTA increased slightly with increasing CRRT intensity, likely due to the reduced risk of excessive exposure. For meropenem and ceftazidime‐avibactam, PTA decreased with increasing CRRT intensity. The effect of CRRT intensity on PTA was more pronounced than the differences across CRRT modalities. Compared with CRRT intensities ≤ 30 mL/kg/h, greater reductions in PTA were observed at CRRT intensities > 30 mL/kg/h, particularly for ceftazidime‐avibactam. Based on these findings, post‐dilution CVVH was selected as the conservative reference modality to provide applicable dosage recommendations in subsequent analyses.

Dosage Regimens Across GFR and CRRT Intensities

Dosage regimens across different CRRT intensities under post‐dilution CVVH were evaluated in patients with different levels of residual renal function, stratified by GFR (0–15 and 15–30 mL/min), with detailed results shown in Figure 5. Higher GFR and CRRT intensity were associated with lower PTA; higher doses were needed for patients to achieve the target exposure, with GFR exerting a greater influence on PTA than CRRT intensity.

The probability of target attainment for different dosage regimens of vancomycin, meropenem, and ceftazidime‐avibactam in septic patients across GFR categories of 0–15 and 15–30 mL/min. The PK/PD targets were defined as an AUC > 427 mg·h/L for vancomycin and 100% fT > MIC for meropenem and ceftazidime‐avibactam; GFR, glomerular filtration rate.

Given the narrow therapeutic window and potential nephrotoxicity, the efficacy‐safety target attainment was further evaluated for vancomycin (Table S7). For patients with a GFR of 0–15 mL/min, 0.5 g q12 h was recommended when CRRT intensity was 20–30 mL/kg/h, whereas 0.5 g q8 h was recommended when CRRT intensity was 30–40 mL/kg/h. For patients with a GFR of 15–30 mL/min, 0.5 g q8 h and 0.75 g q12 h showed similar efficacy‐based PTA, and 0.5 g q8 h was recommended because it was associated with a lower risk of toxicity and better compatibility with the available 0.5 g vial. The higher dose of 1 g q12 h was associated with an increased risk of excessive exposure and was therefore not preferred for septic patients undergoing CRRT.

For meropenem, a regimen of 0.5 g q12 h achieved > 90% PTA in patients with a GFR of 0–15 mL/min across all evaluated CRRT intensities. In patients with a GFR of 15–30 mL/min, 0.5 g q12 h was sufficient at CRRT intensities of 20–35 mL/kg/h, whereas 0.5 g q8 h was recommended at CRRT intensities of 35–40 mL/kg/h.

For ceftazidime‐avibactam, 0.94 g q12 h was sufficient across CRRT intensities of 20–40 mL/kg/h in patients with a GFR of 0–15 mL/min. With a GFR of 15–30 mL/min, 0.94 g q12 h was recommended at CRRT intensities of 20–25 mL/kg/h, with the recommended dose increasing to 1.25 g q12 h at 25–35 mL/kg/h and 1.25 g q8 h at 35–40 mL/kg/h, respectively. The dosage recommendations are summarized in Table S8.

Discussion

The present study developed a PBPK framework for septic patients undergoing CRRT by integrating sepsis‐associated pathophysiological alterations with CRRT‐related extracorporeal drug clearance, using three representative hydrophilic antimicrobial agents for model development and evaluation. The final model demonstrated good predictive performance across different CRRT modalities and intensities and was further applied to assess antimicrobial exposure and support model‐informed precision dosing. Overall, the findings highlight the importance of considering both residual renal function and CRRT intensity when optimizing antimicrobial dosing in septic patients undergoing CRRT.

Precision dosing in septic patients undergoing CRRT remains challenging because antimicrobial exposure is influenced by both sepsis‐associated pathophysiological alterations and CRRT‐related extracorporeal drug removal. PBPK modeling provides a mechanistic approach to integrate CRRT clearance, disease‐related, and drug‐specific characteristics. Watt et al. developed a fluconazole PBPK model for children receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). An ECMO compartment was added and parameterized using ex vivo data to describe drug adsorption and degradation by the circuit [17]. Fuhr et al. incorporated a dialyzer compartment into the dabigatran PBPK model. Dialyzer‐related clearance was determined by blood and dialysate flow rates [51]. Dubinsky et al. developed an anakinra PBPK model for critically ill children receiving extracorporeal life support (ECLS); CRRT and ECMO compartments were incorporated to describe extracorporeal drug clearance and support dose recommendations [52]. Building on these pioneering studies, the present study developed a PBPK framework for vancomycin, meropenem, and ceftazidime‐avibactam in septic patients undergoing CRRT. A CRRT compartment was integrated into the model and connected to the systemic circulation, with drug removal modeled as extracorporeal clearance from the plasma to the effluent compartment. CRRT clearance was calculated using CRRT parameters, including Q uf, Q d, and Q b, and drug‐specific S c or S d. This framework enabled quantitative evaluation of antimicrobial exposure across different CRRT modalities, intensities, and residual renal function, supporting dose optimization.

For PBPK modeling in septic patients, organ volume was adjusted to reflect sepsis‐associated capillary leakage and fluid resuscitation. This approach allowed the model to capture increased third spacing in the absence of direct V d data. For vancomycin, several PBPK models have been developed using different modeling software, including Simcyp, PK‐Sim, and GastroPlus, and applied to diverse clinical scenarios such as sepsis [33], CNS penetration [53], pediatric populations [54], pregnancy [55], and nephrotoxicity assessment [56]. Despite differences in model structure and parameterization, these models consistently identify renal function as a major determinant of vancomycin clearance. Radke et al. incorporated multiple sepsis‐related physiological alterations into a vancomycin PBPK model, including changes in plasma protein concentrations, hematocrit, cardiac index, organ blood flow, creatinine clearance, and body composition [33]. In contrast, the present study chose to modify parameters with major effects on drug exposure, including protein binding, hematocrit, and GFR. This does not imply that other sepsis‐related physiological alterations are unimportant; rather, the approach aimed to balance model accuracy with the availability of routinely measured clinical parameters.

Notably, although the final model demonstrated overall acceptable predictive performance, suboptimal predictions were observed for some individuals, reflecting the inherent variability among septic patients undergoing CRRT. This highlights the need for individualized dosage optimization and therapeutic drug monitoring when necessary, particularly for drugs with a narrow therapeutic window, such as vancomycin [31, 57]. At the same time, we acknowledge that the current PBPK model requires further validation with more clinical studies data. Future studies should incorporate additional ex vivo data to better characterize CRRT‐specific clearance parameters and more detailed pathophysiological data in septic patients, such as tissue‐to‐plasma partition coefficients, to further support more mechanistic PBPK modeling.

The sensitivity analysis indicated that residual renal function and CRRT settings are determinants of antimicrobial exposure in septic patients undergoing CRRT, particularly for AUC. This supports the need to consider both patient‐specific physiological changes and CRRT settings during dose optimization. In addition, the influence of albumin concentration, fraction unbound, and lipophilicity suggests that altered protein binding and distribution in sepsis may further contribute to interindividual variability in antimicrobial exposure.

Model‐informed dose optimization indicates that CRRT modality does not significantly influence dosage regimens at CRRT intensities ≤ 30 mL/kg/h, supporting the same dosage regimen without distinguishing CRRT modality. This finding is consistent with previous reports [30]. In contrast, CRRT intensity has an impact on PTA, indicating that CRRT intensity should be considered in dosage optimization. This aligns with the findings of Roberts et al. [58], who reported increased vancomycin extracorporeal clearance (28 vs. 22 mL/min) at higher CRRT intensities (40 vs. 25 mL/kg/h). However, their study did not observe statistically significant differences for meropenem, piperacillin, and tazobactam due to intraindividual variability. Consistent with these findings, the present study also demonstrated that CRRT intensity influenced dosage regimens, and its effect was secondary to that of GFR.

This study has several limitations. First, the selection of drug was restricted to three antimicrobial agents due to limited available clinical data in patients undergoing CRRT. Further evaluation with other antimicrobial agents is needed to assess the model's generalizability and extrapolation capability. Second, dosage optimization was performed using fixed MIC values and did not consider potential variability in MIC. Despite these limitations, the present findings provide a quantitative framework to support model‐informed and individualized antimicrobial dosing in septic patients undergoing CRRT.

Conclusions

A PBPK framework for septic patients undergoing CRRT was developed by integrating CRRT‐related clearance and sepsis‐associated pathophysiological alterations. Validation using vancomycin, meropenem, and ceftazidime‐avibactam demonstrated robust predictive performance. Optimal dosage regimens should account for both GFR and CRRT intensity, with GFR exerting a greater influence, thereby supporting precision dosing in septic patients undergoing CRRT. This framework also provides a quantitative approach for guiding precision dosing of other drugs or patients receiving ECLS.
