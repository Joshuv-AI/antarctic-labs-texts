Left Atrial Septal Pouch as a Source of Recurrent Multisystem Arterial Embolism
Authors: Krishnadev Vijaykumar Pillai, Marium Ahmed, Alexander Sacher, Sabrina Bubis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft atrial septal pouch (LASP) is an under-recognized interatrial septal variant with potential thrombogenic properties. Evidence regarding its clinical significance and management remains limited.Case SummaryA 57-year-old woman with recurrent embolic cerebral infarcts presented with acute right flank pain. History of Presentation

A 57-year-old woman presented with sudden-onset right-sided flank pain. On arrival, blood pressure was 135/75 mm Hg, heart rate 86 beats/min, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, temperature 36.7 °C, and oxygen saturation 99% on room air. Physical examination was notable for right costovertebral angle tenderness.Take-Home Messages•A left atrial septal pouch can serve as a source of recurrent multisystem arterial emboli even in the absence of atrial fibrillation, and concurrent vascular pathologies such as fibromuscular dysplasia should be carefully distinguished from a central cardioembolic mechanism.•There is a critical need for consensus guidelines to guide anticoagulation and structural interventions for left atrial septal pouch–associated cardioembolic risk.

Past Medical History

Past medical history was significant for migraine with aura, hyperlipidemia, and multiple embolic cerebral infarcts. Following her initial ischemic stroke, the patient was initiated on dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel. She subsequently developed multiple silent and clinical embolic infarcts on dual antiplatelet therapy. An implantable loop recorder showed no atrial fibrillation. Transthoracic echocardiogram and cerebral vascular imaging did not reveal abnormalities, and the hypercoagulability work-up was negative. P2Y12 assay showed suboptimal platelet inhibition, and clopidogrel was switched to ticagrelor. However, the patient developed wheezing and palpitations with ticagrelor. She was maintained on aspirin and apixaban, to which she endorsed adherence.

Differential Diagnosis

Our differential diagnoses included acute ureteral obstruction, acute pyelonephritis, renal infarction, and extra-urinary inflammation/infection.

Investigations

Complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel were within the normal range. Urinalysis was negative for infection. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis revealed a large acute right renal infarction and left renal cortical scarring reflecting the sequela of prior renal infarction. Renal duplex study showed significant stenosis of the right renal artery and a patent left renal artery. CT angiography showed moderate stenosis and a beaded appearance of the right renal artery. Management

Right renal artery angiography showed multiple aneurysmal segments with areas of focal stenoses consistent with fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD), and the patient underwent balloon angioplasty with successful reperfusion. Given imaging evidence of prior left renal infarct with a normal left renal artery, recurrent arterial thrombotic events, and negative hypercoagulability work-up, these events were attributed to a cardioembolic mechanism from the left atrial septal pouch (LASP). Anticoagulation was switched from apixaban to rivaroxaban based on several considerations. First, the patient experienced a recurrent embolic event on therapeutic apixaban. Second, although both are direct factor Xa inhibitors, pharmacodynamic differences exist: a randomized crossover study demonstrated that rivaroxaban 20 mg once daily produced greater and more sustained inhibition of thrombin generation than apixaban 5 mg twice daily at steady state, with more pronounced prolongation of prothrombin time (1.66-fold vs 1.14-fold) and activated partial thromboplastin time (1.43-fold vs 1.16-fold).1 These differences may relate to rivaroxaban’s higher association rate constant for factor Xa.2 Third, once-daily dosing was considered advantageous for adherence. No head-to-head trial has compared these agents for arterial thromboembolism in this context, and the switch was made on an individualized basis.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was referred for evaluation of LASP closure. At the time of this report, she remains on rivaroxaban with no recurrent embolic events since the anticoagulant switch. Yan and Li recently reported successful transcatheter elimination of LASP using a patent foramen ovale closure device for secondary stroke prevention, with no recurrent strokes over a 3-year follow-up in the intervention group.3 This emerging evidence supports the potential role of structural intervention.

Discussion

An atrial septal pouch forms when the septum primum and septum secundum fail to completely fuse in the absence of a patent foramen ovale, creating a blind-ended pouch. The prevalence of LASP, based on autopsy findings, is estimated to be 40%.4 The thrombogenic potential of the LASP can be understood through Virchow’s triad: stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability: a framework extensively applied to left atrial appendage thrombogenesis in atrial fibrillation.5 The LASP creates a cul-de-sac where blood flow is inherently reduced. Unlike the left atrial appendage, which has pectinate muscles that may partially facilitate blood movement, the smooth-walled LASP lacks such architecture, potentially amplifying stasis.3,6 Ohanyan et al7 provided direct evidence by visualizing 2 distinct thrombotic masses emerging from the LASP.4 A meta-analysis by Tyrak et al8 including over 2,100 patients demonstrated a significantly higher risk of cryptogenic stroke in patients with LASP (OR: 1.57; 95% CI: 1.23-2.01). Notably, LASP is typically diagnosed by transesophageal echocardiography, a modality not routinely used in stroke evaluation, suggesting underdiagnosis.

FMD is a recognized cause of renal infarction through local mechanisms, including dissection, in situ thrombosis, and distal embolism from aneurysmal segments. However, several features argue against FMD as the primary embolic source in this case. First, the patient had a prior left renal infarction with a normal left renal artery, which cannot be explained by right-sided renal artery FMD. Second, cerebral vascular imaging showed no cervical FMD or dissection; in the FMD literature, cerebrovascular events are predominantly mediated by local mechanisms from cervical artery involvement rather than distant embolization from renal FMD. Third, the multisystem distribution of embolic events across bilateral renal and cerebral territories without cervical artery involvement is more consistent with a central cardioembolic source. Although FMD contributed to right renal artery stenosis and was appropriately treated, the totality of the clinical picture supports the LASP as the primary embolic source.

There is no consensus on whether LASP alone warrants anticoagulation or structural intervention. The 2022 FASEB Catalyst Conference on the Cardiac Interatrial Septum and Stroke Risk highlighted this knowledge gap and called for further research.9 Given recurrent events despite anticoagulation, the patient was referred for LASP closure evaluation.

Conclusions

This patient experienced recurrent multisystem arterial embolic events in the absence of traditional risk factors. The LASP was identified as the likely cardioembolic source after excluding atrial fibrillation, hypercoagulability, and cervical FMD. The concurrent renal artery FMD was addressed with angioplasty, but the multisystem embolic pattern supports the LASP as the primary mechanism. This case underscores the importance of recognizing LASP as a clinically significant structural variant and the need for evidence-based guidelines on its management.
