Autonomous pathfinding for underactuated AUVs using FDHNN
Authors: Mingzhi Chen, Hongfei Li, Liming Feng, Daqi Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs) are increasingly pivotal in exploring and mapping environments, due to their autonomous pathfinding capabilities. Despite the development of various algorithms for this task, many do not adequately address the challenges posed by underactuation–a common characteristic of AUVs. Moreover, the constrained field of view inherent to sonar and visual sensors introduces additional directional limitations that complicate the detection process. To overcome these obstacles, we introduce an innovative trajectory planning method known as the Finite Direction Hopfield Neural Network (FDHNN). Our method employs eight directional neurons per grid cell with customized neural connection weights to create a path that caters to underactuated AUVs, ensuring adaptability in various aquatic environments and adherence to initial and terminal directional constraints. Complementing the global planning provided by FDHNN, we have refined the Dynamic Window Approach (DWA) for local maneuvering within the velocity domain. Simulation-based validation has underscored its robustness and superior performance, particularly when benchmarked against advanced algorithms such as dual deep Q-learning and Dubins paths. Our proposed trajectory planning method markedly improves the reliability of underactuated AUVs navigating with start and end directional limitations, offering substantial advancements in underwater detection and exploration.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Ocean sciences

Introduction

In recent decades, the scope of ocean exploration has expanded significantly, but the complexity and unpredictability of underwater environments pose significant risks to manual exploration1. As a result, Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs) have become increasingly popular for their ability to carry out missions safely and efficiently. A critical component of AUV operations is the development of safe and efficient trajectories. To tackle this challenge, a wide range of algorithms has been devised, falling into two main categories: conventional and intelligent methodologies.

In AUV trajectory planning, the main goals are to ensure safe arrival at the destination and optimize efficiency, necessitating effective navigation and obstacle avoidance2. The artificial potential field (APF) method, introduced by C. W. Warren in the early 1990s, combines repulsive and attractive forces to create collision-free paths3,4. However, APF can lead to local minima issues, prompting the development of hydrodynamic and electromagnetic-inspired solutions5–7. The Dijkstra algorithm, known for its greedy search, and its more efficient successors like the A* algorithm, which uses heuristics, have been widely adopted for their ability to handle partially unknown obstacles8–10. The D* Lite algorithm further specializes in dynamic path planning11,12. These traditional algorithms are foundational in global path planning for AUVs. Intelligent algorithms are increasingly popular for trajectory planning. Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) was employed for global planning in complex settings, despite local optimization issues13. To counter premature convergence, a penalty factor was introduced to ACO14. Other algorithms like Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)15, Genetic Algorithm (GA)16, Wolf Pack Algorithm (WPA)17, and Simulated Annealing (SA)18 have also been used for AUV trajectory planning. Moreover, recent advancements in optimization algorithms, such as the fitness-based differential evolution (FDE) algorithm, have been applied to optimize AUV paths by minimizing fuel and threat costs19. However, these optimization methods still encounter local optima.

Efforts to achieve collision-free trajectory planning for autonomous systems have also spurred the development of diverse neural network algorithms. In the challenging underwater realm, AUVs must navigate intricate environments, prompting the creation of specialized solutions like the Glasius bio-inspired neural network (GBNN)20 and its continuous variant (BNN)21, both adept at handling multiple obstacles by harnessing neural activity for pathfinding. Simplification came with the Hopfield Neural Network (HNN)22, which focuses on safer trajectories through topologically organized and locally connected networks. The compact internal representation (CIR)23 concept furthered this by forming cognitive maps for secure navigation. Spiking neural networks24 have been innovatively applied to generate safe paths influenced by neural activity’s vector fields. To improve path planning for AUVs in cluttered spaces, enhanced GBNN25 and BNN26 algorithms have been introduced, alongside a fuzzy neural network (FNN)27 for mobile robots and a Potential field BNN (PBNN)28 that merges potential fields with BNNs. For expansive underwater settings, a novel Dynamic Neural Network (DNN)29 has been crafted, allowing AUVs to find shorter paths dynamically without prior learning. Yet, these advancements have not fully tackled the underactuated nature of AUVs. This research seeks to address this gap with a neural network-based strategy that considers the underactuated dynamics of AUVs, aiming to boost their navigational efficiency in complex underwater terrains.

The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence has seen reinforcement learning strategies, such as Q-learning30, being repurposed for trajectory planning endeavors. This technique relies on iterative cycles of training and learning, during which a safe route is determined through a sequence of trials. Nevertheless, the issue of infrequent rewards has necessitated the creation of globally guided reinforcement learning approaches that aim to streamline the learning phase31. Zhao et al.32 introduced a specialized associative learning algorithm for trajectory planning that progresses through three stages: initial exploration, experiential navigation, and the formation of stable connections. Moreover, an extensive evaluation has been carried out to gauge the efficacy of various reinforcement learning models, including Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C), Deep Q-Networks (DQN), Potential-based Deep Q-Networks (PDQN), Dual DQN (DDQN), and Deep Deterministic Policy Gradients (DDPG), in the context of pathfinding33. While these models demonstrate proficiency in their designated applications, their flexibility is somewhat limited, especially when confronted with changes in environmental conditions. Originally designed for holonomic robots, these algorithms could potentially be reconfigured to accommodate underactuated systems by modifying the reward structures. In this paper, we focus on the DDQN framework for a detailed examination, setting a benchmark for comparison to evaluate the performance of the innovative methods we introduce. Beyond conventional reinforcement learning frameworks, recent advances have explored task-specific reward shaping for orientation-sensitive missions. Chu et al. 34 proposed an Adaptive Reward Shape Proximal Policy Optimization (ARSPPO) algorithm for AUV docking, where a docking quality evaluation metric was incorporated to penalize both positional and angular deviations at the terminal pose. Their work confirms that explicitly handling terminal orientation constraints is essential for safe docking and inspection tasks, which strongly aligns with the motivation of the present study. However, such learning-based methods require extensive training and parallel simulation infrastructure, and their generalization to environments with dense obstacles and start-direction constraints remains limited. This further motivates our pursuit of a neural network-based deterministic planner (FDHNN) that explicitly encodes kinematic and directional constraints in its topology.

AUVs are inherently underactuated, which necessitates accounting for a minimum turning radius in their navigation. To cater to this, various algorithms have been developed specifically to handle the distinctive underactuated dynamics of AUVs. Among these, the dynamic window approach (DWA)35, a technique within the velocity space methods category, has become prominent in mobile robotics. The DWA has been proven effective in managing environments populated with both static and dynamic obstacles through numerous studies36,37. Expanding upon this, Liu et al. introduced the Underwater Dynamic Window Approach (UDWA)38 to better suit the particular requirements of underwater environments. Dubins path theory provides a robust framework for planning the trajectories of underactuated mobile robots in environments devoid of obstacles while taking into account kinematic constraints. Shi et al.39 applied the Dubins method to the trajectory planning of AUVs, taking the kinematic model of the AUV into consideration. Furthermore, recent literature over the past three years highlights the extension of such kinematically constrained algorithms to multi-vehicle systems. For example, advanced Dubins path planning has been successfully implemented for heterogeneous Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) in collaborative IoT data collection networks40. In the marine domain, pseudo-trajectory homotopy methods have been developed for Unmanned Marine Vehicles (UMVs) to optimize information collection paths while adhering to underwater communication and kinematic constraints41. However, many of these approaches assume a constant turning radius. It is crucial to recognize that an AUV’s rudder becomes less effective at lower speeds, leading to a larger turning radius than expected. This variation underscores the necessity for trajectory planning algorithms that can more precisely accommodate the changing turning radius of AUVs across different operational environments. Furthermore, recent works on networked marine surface vehicles have advanced the safety-critical control of underactuated systems by integrating Control Barrier Functions (CBF) and Control Lyapunov Functions (CLF) within quadratic programming (QP) frameworks. For example, Zhu et al.42 developed a k-connected hierarchical topology self-organization control scheme for underactuated marine vehicles, where collision avoidance and connectivity preservation are simultaneously enforced through CBF-based QP. Such optimization-based formulations demonstrate the feasibility of unifying kinematic constraints, safety requirements, and task-level objectives, which informs our design of the enhanced DWA cost function in this paper. However, these schemes mainly focus on cooperative formation maintenance without explicitly addressing path-level start/terminal orientation constraints in cluttered single-vehicle inspection scenarios.

In this study, we introduce a dual-phase algorithm tailored to address the challenges of trajectory planning for underactuated AUVs with specific initial and final directional constraints amidst multiple obstacles. To clearly articulate the advancements of our work, the primary contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

Formulation of Directionally Constrained Planning: Unlike traditional trajectory planning studies that primarily focus on point-to-point navigation3,4, we explicitly formulate and solve the path planning problem incorporating both initial and final directional constraints. This is highly practical for AUVs equipped with restricted field-of-view sensors (e.g., multi-beam sonar), extending the applicability of conventional algorithms.

Problem formulation

The deployment of AUVs for the inspection of underwater infrastructures has become increasingly prevalent. Trajectory planning for AUVs often entails devising a collision-free path to a target location without stringent orientation requirements. However, there exist various application contexts in which the orientation at the start and end points is of paramount importance. Figure 1 illustrates a real-world engineering scenario from our recent project on underwater bridge pier inspection, which directly motivates the directionally-constrained planning problem addressed in this paper.

The AUV in our project is equipped with a multi-beam imaging sonar and an optical camera, both of which have a limited horizontal FOV (typically less than 90∘ and 120∘, respectively). The pier surface to be inspected lies along a narrow vertical strip; consequently, the AUV must approach the pier along a specific heading so that the inspection surface is fully contained within the sensor FOV. Any heading deviation larger than approximately ± 20∘ will cause partial occlusion of the inspection area and lead to incomplete data acquisition. This requirement is illustrated by the green arrow at the target pose in Fig. 1.

Proposed algorithm

To address the trajectory planning challenge for the underactuated AUV with constraints on initial and final orientations, a two-phase algorithm is introduced. Initially, a finite directional Hopfield neural network is designed for global path planning, capable of generating discrete paths that adhere to the AUV’s turning limitations. Subsequently, a dynamic window approach is employed, featuring an adjusted objective function, to locally optimize the surge velocity u and the yaw rate r. This step ensures the trajectory is smoothed and aligns with the soft constraint regarding the direction of the destination.

Overview of algorithm improvements

Before detailing the mathematical models, the specific improvements of the proposed dual-phase algorithm over traditional methods are outlined as follows:

Improvement in Global Planning (FDHNN): Traditional grid-based neural network algorithms (such as BNN or GBNN) typically assign a single neuron to each grid cell. The neural activity diffuses omnidirectionally, may resulting in paths with sharp, 90-degree or 180-degree turns. These paths are kinematically infeasible for underactuated AUVs. Our improvement lies in redesigning the neural topology: we assign eight directional neurons to each grid cell. By mathematically nullifying the synaptic connection weights between neurons that represent turning angles exceeding the AUV’s maximum turning capability, the FDHNN inherently restricts the diffusion of neural activity to kinematically feasible directions only.

Finite directional hopfield neural network for global planning

As discussed in the Introduction, traditional neural network models typically assign an individual neuron to each grid cell, planning paths by following the gradient of neural activity. However, the direct paths with potentially sharp turns produced by these one-to-one neuron mappings do not comply with the kinematic constraints imposed by the underactuated nature of AUVs. To overcome this specific limitation, the proposed Finite Directional Hopfield Neural Network (FDHNN) incorporates a novel finite directional neuron architecture into the neural network path planning algorithm. As depicted in Fig. 2, the environment, including obstacles and the goal, is first encoded into a data map. In this map, free spaces are represented by 0, obstacle cells by -E, and the target cell by E. Diverging from typical neural network path planning methods, each grid cell contains eight neurons, numbered 1 through 8 in a counterclockwise fashion, each corresponding to one of the eight possible directions to neighboring cells. The neuron within the target grid cell that most closely aligns with the desired goal orientation is designated as the target neuron, indicated by a red dot in Fig. 2. The starting neuron is determined in a similar manner. Additionally, each neuron is connected only to the eight neurons in the adjacent grid cell that it points towards. For instance, in the grid cell highlighted with a yellow border in the middle, neuron 5 connects solely to the eight neurons within the obstacle grid, while neuron 8 connects only to the eight neurons within the target grid. Neurons in neighboring grid cells may also connect to those in the central yellow-bordered grid cell. These connections form a network that resembles a Hopfield network, characterized by its interconnected nature, albeit not fully interconnected.

Enhanced dynamic window approach for local planning

An advanced local path planning algorithm utilizing an enhanced version of the dynamic window approach (DWA) is introduced. This refined algorithm identifies the node on the global path that is nearest to the AUV’s current position and designates it as the local target. Once the AUV reaches this local target, the subsequent closest node on the global path is selected as the new local target. Illustrated in Fig. 5, the AUV is positioned in proximity to the initial node of the globally planned path, with the following node being assigned as its imminent local target. The propagation and stability of neural activity can be succinctly described as follows: within the target grid containing eight neurons, the neuron that is most aligned with the target direction (referred to as the target neuron) is the first to be activated, attaining an activity level of 1, while the remaining neurons in the grid remain inactive with an activity level of 0. The activation of the target neuron subsequently triggers the activation of neurons in adjacent grids that are in accordance with the underactuated constraint. Additionally, the synaptic weights between neurons are inversely proportional to their distance, resulting in a preference for shorter paths that are consistent with the underactuated constraints. Concurrently, any grid that is occupied will suppress the activation of neighboring neurons that are oriented towards it, ensuring their activity levels remain at zero. By selecting suitable values for β and γ, the synaptic weights can be adjusted to be less than 1, which guarantees that the activity levels of all neurons, with the exception of the target neuron, do not exceed 1. This creates a gradient of neural activity that points towards the target direction. Therefore, by employing the steepest ascent method, it is possible to identify a short and constraint-compliant path that is also assured to be free of collisions. The refined dynamic window approach refines the trajectory, ensuring that the AUV reaches the target position and orientation with flexible constraints. As shown in Fig. 6, the AUV ultimately arrives at the coordinates (4.81, 4.98) meters, with a heading of 80.90 degrees. This final positioning adheres to the constraints and enables the AUV to successfully carry out the inspection task on the suspicious underwater structure.

The trajectory optimization achieved by the enhanced dynamic window approach is initially contrasted with the trajectory generated by the Dubins path algorithm. The Dubins algorithm’s core concept is to generate the shortest possible path from a given starting position and orientation to a specified target position and orientation. This path is composed of a combination of straight segments and circular arcs that adhere to the AUV’s minimum turning radius constraints. Typically, Dubins paths are categorized into two primary types: CSC (Curve, Straight, Curve) and CCC (Curve, Curve, Curve). As depicted in Fig. 12, the red arrow marks the AUV’s initial heading, while the green arrow indicates the orientation necessary to execute the task. The limitation of the Dubins path algorithm becomes apparent in constrained environments; it fails to ensure a collision-free route. For instance, when the AUV attempts to navigate towards the task location at a predetermined angle, following the shortest path determined by the RSL (Right Curve, Straight Line, Left Curve) configuration, it inevitably encounters obstacles. Alternative configurations, such as other CSC or CCC combinations, are not viable as they would extend beyond the environmental boundaries.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduce a finite directional Hopfield neural network (FDHNN) tailored for the global trajectory planning of underactuated autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs), complemented by an enhanced dynamic window approach (DWA) for local maneuvering. When configured with appropriate parameters, the FDHNN generates a neural activity gradient that effectively guides the AUV to a goal with directional constraints. The refined DWA outperforms its predecessor by better avoiding local minima and aligning the AUV’s heading with the intended course. Simulations reveal that trajectories derived from the FDHNN are more efficient in terms of travel distance and time compared to those obtained using dual deep Q-learning, with both methods incorporating the enhanced DWA. Consequently, the synergy of the FDHNN and the improved DWA proves to be an effective strategy for devising concise trajectories that adhere to the directional goals and account for the underactuation characteristics of AUVs.
