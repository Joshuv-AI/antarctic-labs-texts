Structured integration of rapid antimicrobial susceptibility testing and antimicrobial resistance detection into an antimicrobial stewardship programme for bacteraemia
Authors: Ana Verónica Halperin, Sara Gómez-León, Rosa Escudero-Sánchez, María Dolores Corbacho-Loarte, Cecilia Suárez-Carantoña, Javier Saez de la Fuente, Pilar Retamar-Gentil, Rafael Cantón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBloodstream infections are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) have been shown to improve clinical outcomes when combined with antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) programmes. The lack of systematic reporting of rapid antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) can cause delays in communication to prescribers and in targeted antimicrobial treatment.ObjectivesTo evaluate the impact of a structured intervention in integrating systematic RAST and AMR detection within an existing AMS programme. The main outcomes were time to report (from Gram to AST report) (TTR), time from Gram to AMS recommendation (TAMS) and time to change antimicrobial prescription (TCAP) following this recommendation.MethodsWe performed a contemporaneous comparative study evaluating episodes of bacteraemia managed with rapid AST (RAST) and/or antimicrobial resistance mechanisms (AMR) detection integrated into AMS workflows versus episodes managed without RAST/AMR during the same implementation period.ResultsEpisodes managed with RAST/AMR had significantly shorter TTR, TAMS and TCAP compared with episodes without RAST/AMR (median absolute reduction in hours of 31, 37 and 24, respectively). There was no significant reduction in duration of antimicrobial therapy, hospital length of stay (LOS) or mortality in the RAST/AMR group. Compliance with AMS recommendations was high (90%).ConclusionsAs reported previously, RAST or AMR detection in combination with AMS was an effective strategy for targeting antimicrobial therapy. This intervention demonstrates that structured implementation of RAST/AMR detection, when embedded within the AMS workflow, can substantially shorten time to targeted therapy even in settings with pre-existing AMS infrastructure.

Introduction

Bloodstream infections (BSI) are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality1,2 and timely initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy is a key determinant of patient outcomes.3–5 While empirical therapy is essential at the onset of sepsis, early optimization towards targeted treatment is critical to reduce unnecessary broad-spectrum antimicrobial exposure and its associated harms. Delays in pathogen identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) remain a major barrier to timely treatment optimization in routine clinical practice.6–8 Moreover, delays in appropriate antimicrobial treatment beyond 12 hours from blood culture collection have been associated with increased 30-day mortality.9

Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), including rapid AST (RAST) and detection of antimicrobial resistance mechanisms (AMR), when combined with antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) interventions, have been shown to shorten the time to targeted therapy10 and, to improve clinical outcomes in patients with BSI.6,11 However, the clinical impact of these technologies depends not only on their analytical performance but also on their effective integration into clinical workflows.12 In many institutions, RAST and AMR detection are technically available but not systematically reported or consistently linked to stewardship actions, limiting their real-world effectiveness.

Antimicrobial stewardship programmes play a central role in translating microbiological information into clinical decision-making. Structured communication between the microbiology laboratory and prescribing teams, timely interpretation of preliminary results, and clear accountability for treatment modification are essential components of effective diagnostic and antimicrobial stewardship.12

Nevertheless, operational barriers such as the lack of standardized preliminary reporting, fragmented communication pathways, and variability in prescriber engagement may delay treatment optimization even in hospitals with established AMS programmes and 24/7 microbiology departments.

In our institution, RAST and AMR detection techniques were available but not routinely or systematically incorporated into AMS workflows for patients with BSI. Preliminary results were often communicated upon request and without standardization, and the laboratory information system (LIS) did not support formal preliminary reports. We therefore designed and implemented a structured intervention to integrate systematic RAST and AMR detection reporting within an existing AMS programme. The objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of this implementation on key process indicators, including time to microbiological reporting, time to AMS recommendation, and time to antimicrobial treatment modification, as well as on clinical outcomes and compliance with AMS recommendations.

Methods

Setting and antimicrobial stewardship programme

This study was conducted at Hospital Universitario Ramón y Cajal, a 900-bed tertiary-care teaching hospital in Madrid, Spain. The hospital has 24/7 Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases departments and a well-established multidisciplinary AMS programme involving Infectious Diseases, Internal Medicine, Pharmacy, Clinical Microbiology, the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), and the Emergency Department.

The AMS team performs daily reviews of patients with suspected or confirmed infections and provides written recommendations in the electronic medical record regarding antimicrobial choice, optimization and duration. Local guidelines for empirical antimicrobial therapy are available through a mobile application (TEAM iDoctus). Within the AMS programme, a dedicated bacteraemia subprogramme reviews all positive blood cultures daily.

Study design and population

We performed a contemporaneous comparative study evaluating episodes of bacteraemia managed with RAST and/or AMR detection integrated into AMS workflows versus episodes managed without RAST/AMR during the same implementation period.

All patients with at least one positive blood culture collected during two predefined sampling periods (April and July 2025) were eligible for inclusion. Only the first episode of bacteraemia per patient was included. Exclusion criteria were blood cultures considered contaminants (e.g. coagulase-negative staphylococci in a single set), patients who died before any microbiological information was available, and patients receiving comfort-only care.

Microbiological methods and RAST/AMR implementation

Blood cultures were processed according to standard laboratory procedures. After Gram stain reporting, organism identification was performed using MALDI-TOF (Bruker, Germany).

RAST and/or AMR detection was performed for all eligible episodes according to predefined criteria, as follows: RAST was carried out directly from positive blood cultures using disc diffusion in accordance with EUCAST recommendations for validated organisms,13 including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae/oxytoca, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Salmonella spp., Enterococcus faecalis/faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. For AMR, detection of carbapenemase production (OXA-48, KPC, VIM, IMP, NDM) was performed using either immunochromatographic assays (O.N.K.V.I. RESIST-5, Coris BioConcept, Gembloux, Belgium) or loop-mediated isothermal amplification (Eazyplex®SuperBug CRE, AmplexDiagnostics, Gars-Bahnhof, Germany) in patients with a previous history of colonization or infection with carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales. When Staphylococcus aureus was identified, loop-mediated isothermal amplification was used to detect mecA and mecC genes (Eazyplex®MRSA plus, AmplexDiagnostics, Gars-Bahnhof, Germany).

Conventional AST using automated broth microdilution (MicroScan-Walkaway, Beckman-Coulter, USA) or alternative methods for fastidious organisms such as a gradient strip test was performed for all non-eligible microorganisms. Additionally, automated broth microdilution was also performed in parallel for all isolates with RAST/AMR detection.

AMS workflow and communication

RAST and AMR results were communicated through structured preliminary reports entered manually into the electronic medical record and complemented by direct verbal communication with the AMS team. Based on these results, the AMS team issued written recommendations regarding antimicrobial optimization that were classified into the following categories: continuation of current therapy (i.e. no change), initiation of antimicrobial treatment, change in antimicrobial spectrum (including de-escalation and escalation), dosage adjustment, route change, change, addition or discontinuation of antimicrobial therapy. Some recommendations included more than one category.

To facilitate implementation, laboratory protocols were updated, reporting procedures were standardized, and targeted educational sessions were delivered to microbiology staff and clinical teams. Consultants rather than residents were preferentially contacted for communication of preliminary results to reduce delays related to decision-making uncertainty.

Outcomes and definitions

The primary outcomes were process indicators:

Time to report (TTR): time from Gram stain report to availability of AST results (preliminary AST for episodes eligible for RAST/AMR; final AST otherwise);

Time to report (TTR): time from Gram stain report to availability of AST results (preliminary AST for episodes eligible for RAST/AMR; final AST otherwise);

Time to AMS recommendation (TAMS): time from Gram stain report to a documented AMS recommendation;

Time to AMS recommendation (TAMS): time from Gram stain report to a documented AMS recommendation;

Time to change antimicrobial prescription (TCAP): time from Gram stain report to modification of antimicrobial therapy following an AMS recommendation. TCAP was calculated only for episodes in which the AMS recommendation required a change in antimicrobial therapy (as no treatment modification was expected when the recommendation was to maintain current therapy) and when the prescriber was compliant to the recommendation (in non-compliant cases there was no change in treatment therefore TCAP was not applicable).

Time to change antimicrobial prescription (TCAP): time from Gram stain report to modification of antimicrobial therapy following an AMS recommendation. TCAP was calculated only for episodes in which the AMS recommendation required a change in antimicrobial therapy (as no treatment modification was expected when the recommendation was to maintain current therapy) and when the prescriber was compliant to the recommendation (in non-compliant cases there was no change in treatment therefore TCAP was not applicable).

Compliance with AMS recommendation: Compliance was defined as complete concordance between the AMS recommendation and the prescribed antimicrobial regimen and analysed as a binary variable (complete versus not complete), only including patients with an AMS recommendation. Sub-analysis comparing compliance between departments was performed using a chi-square test with Monte Carlo simulation due to small expected cell counts.

Compliance with AMS recommendation: Compliance was defined as complete concordance between the AMS recommendation and the prescribed antimicrobial regimen and analysed as a binary variable (complete versus not complete), only including patients with an AMS recommendation. Sub-analysis comparing compliance between departments was performed using a chi-square test with Monte Carlo simulation due to small expected cell counts.

Secondary outcomes were duration of antimicrobial therapy, hospital length of stay and in-hospital 30-day mortality.

Behavioural and implementation considerations

Potential barriers and facilitators to implementation were explored using the COM-B model14 to inform protocol development, staff training, and communication strategies. This analysis focused on microbiology staff, AMS clinicians, and prescribing teams and was used to guide implementation rather than as a primary analytical framework.

Data sources and statistical analysis

Clinical and microbiological data were extracted from the LIS, electronic medical records, and electronic prescribing systems. Time stamps for Gram stain reporting, AST availability, AMS recommendations, and prescription changes were recorded manually. REDCap (V 13.4.11) was used as the electronic case report database.

Baseline characteristics were compared descriptively between groups with and without preliminary antimicrobial susceptibility testing to assess group comparability. Given the implementation nature of the study, no adjusted analyses were performed.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3). Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

Ethics approval was waived by the local ethics committee as this study was considered a quality improvement initiative using routinely collected clinical data, with no intervention beyond standard care.

Of these, 139 episodes were managed with RAST and/or AMR detection integrated into AMS workflows, while 77 episodes were managed without RAST/AMR (57 with non-EUCAST validated microorganisms, 13 failure to perform timely reading of disc diffusion AST, 7 polymicrobial). Overall compliance with AMS recommendations was high, with complete adherence observed in 163 of 182 episodes (89.6%). Compliance did not differ significantly across hospital departments (chi-square test with Monte Carlo simulation, P = 0.593; Table S1 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online)). Departments with the highest levels of non-compliance were the emergency department (20%) (although there was only one non-compliant case among a total of 5) and the surgical departments (17%).

Longer delays to treatment modification were observed in ICU settings, where the median TAMS-TTR and TCAP–TTR were 9.7 and 27.6 hours, respectively. These results indicate that most of the delay occurred between the AMS recommendation and the actual change in the prescription, rather than being attributable to delays in the AMS recommendation itself. Longer delays were also observed among discharged patients (TAMS-TTR = 19.6 h), reflecting challenges in communication and decision-making across transitions of care. In contrast, delays were shorter in the Emergency Department and Infectious Diseases wards.

Discussion

In this implementation study, systematic integration of rapid antimicrobial susceptibility testing and resistance mechanism detection into an established antimicrobial stewardship programme was associated with substantially shorter times to actionable microbiological reporting, stewardship recommendation, and antimicrobial treatment modification in patients with bloodstream infections. Episodes managed with RAST/AMR consistently achieved shorter time to report (TTR), time to AMS recommendation (TAMS), and time to change antimicrobial prescription (TCAP) compared with episodes managed without RAST/AMR, highlighting the value of structured diagnostic and stewardship integration beyond the mere availability of rapid diagnostic techniques.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies and meta-analyses11,12 demonstrating that rapid diagnostics, when combined with active AMS involvement, reduce time to targeted therapy in bloodstream infections. While much of the existing literature focuses on the analytical performance of rapid tests or on pre–post intervention designs, our study adds to the evidence by demonstrating the impact of a contemporaneous, workflow-based implementation strategy in a real-world setting with pre-existing 24/7 microbiology and stewardship infrastructure. This suggests that even in relatively mature AMS environments, delays in treatment optimization may persist unless rapid microbiological information is delivered in a timely, standardized, and actionable manner.

Despite significant improvements in process indicators, no differences were observed in clinical outcomes such as duration of antimicrobial therapy, length of hospital stay, or in-hospital mortality. This finding is not unexpected. The study was primarily designed to assess process measures and was not powered to detect changes in clinical outcomes, which are influenced by multiple patient- and disease-related factors. In addition, an established AMS programme for bacteraemia was already in place, and the present intervention focused mainly on facilitating earlier targeted therapy rather than altering overall treatment strategies. Consequently, its impact on duration of therapy, length of stay, or mortality would be expected to be limited. Previous meta-analyses have shown modest effects of combined RAST and AMS interventions on length of stay,11 particularly in larger cohorts, suggesting that clinical benefits may emerge when such interventions are applied at scale over longer periods.

Even with an established AMS programme, 34 patients failed to have a written recommendation. The absence of a documented AMS recommendation may also reflect operational constraints, including workload and limitations in documentation processes.

Episodes without AMS recommendations were often associated with clinical scenarios in which empirical therapy was already considered appropriate or where patient complexity, particularly in ICU settings, limited further optimization. In this context, higher clinical complexity, ongoing multidisciplinary decision-making, and therapeutic caution may limit the ability to modify antimicrobial therapy despite available microbiological data. This may have influenced the observed time-to-optimization metrics and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Another important observation was the persistence of delays between microbiological reporting, stewardship recommendation, and treatment modification in specific clinical settings, particularly in ICU and among discharged patients. These delays were driven mainly by the interval between AMS recommendation and prescription change rather than by delayed stewardship input, underscoring the importance of prescriber engagement, decision-making dynamics, and communication across transitions of care. The longer delays observed in ICU settings likely reflect the complexity of critically ill patients. In these scenarios, antimicrobial changes often require multidisciplinary decision-making, increased therapeutic caution, and alignment with overall clinical priorities, which may delay implementation despite timely AMS recommendations. ICU environments, characterized by high patient complexity and frequent competing priorities, and post-discharge settings, where clinical responsibility may be fragmented, represent critical targets for future stewardship interventions.

This study also highlights the role of diagnostic stewardship15 as a complement to antimicrobial stewardship. Rapid diagnostics alone do not ensure timely treatment optimization; rather, their clinical value depends on how results are reported, interpreted, and acted upon. Standardized preliminary reporting, direct communication with responsible clinicians, and clear stewardship accountability were key components of the intervention and likely contributed to the observed reductions in process delays. From this perspective, RAST and AMR detection functioned not only as diagnostic tools but as catalysts for coordinated microbiology–clinical collaboration.

The high overall compliance to AMS recommendations observed in this study supports the feasibility and acceptability of integrating rapid microbiological information into stewardship workflows. The absence of significant differences in adherence across hospital departments suggests that, once recommendations were delivered in a structured manner, prescriber compliance was generally high, reinforcing the importance of timely and targeted communication rather than additional enforcement mechanisms.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in our study. First, the analysis was limited to predefined sampling periods and a relatively modest number of episodes, which restricts the ability to assess rare clinical outcomes and may limit generalisability. Although impact was measured in representative samples of bacteraemia episodes, the intervention itself was implemented hospital-wide and is applicable to the approximately 1800–1900 bacteraemia episodes managed annually in our institution.

Second, the non-random allocation to RAST/AMR represents an important limitation of this study. Patients were similar in terms of sex, age, and infection acquisition, with no significant differences in ICU admission between groups. However, allocation was influenced by microbiological eligibility criteria and operational factors, which may have introduced selection bias. Many microorganisms such as yeast or Enterobacterales like Enterobacter spp. or Proteus spp. are not eligible for disc diffusion RAST, which may also have affected the differences between groups regarding site of infection. Moreover, patients with suspected multidrug-resistant organisms or those with specific microbiological profiles may have been more likely to undergo rapid testing. Therefore, confounding by indication cannot be excluded, and differences between groups may partly reflect underlying clinical or microbiological factors influencing the use of RAST/AMR. However, this reflects the real-world complexity of implementing rapid diagnostics, where both clinical prioritization and operational constraints play a key role.

Third, preliminary AST results were manually reported due to limitations of the LIS, which may affect reproducibility in settings without similar staffing or organizational support. Nevertheless, these limitations reflect real-world constraints and enhance the external validity of the findings. Importantly, these findings highlight that the clinical impact of rapid diagnostics is not uniform across patient populations and depends on both clinical complexity and healthcare delivery processes.

In this implementation study, integration of rapid antimicrobial susceptibility testing into an established antimicrobial stewardship programme was associated with earlier availability of microbiological information and facilitated timely stewardship interventions. While the overall workflow improved the time to targeted therapy, the effect on actual prescription changes varied across clinical settings, with longer delays observed particularly in critically ill patients. These findings highlight that the benefits of rapid diagnostics are closely linked to how effectively they are integrated into clinical decision-making processes. Organizational factors, multidisciplinary workflows, and clinical complexity may influence the translation of rapid results into therapeutic action. However, as this was a non-randomized implementation study, confounding by indication and selection bias cannot be excluded, and the observed differences should be interpreted with caution. Despite these limitations, the study reflects real-world clinical practice and provides valuable insights into the operational integration of rapid diagnostics within antimicrobial stewardship programmes. Overall, our results suggest that integration of rapid AST into antimicrobial stewardship programmes may improve timeliness of care, although its impact appears to depend on local workflow and clinical context. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings and to better understand how to optimize the implementation of rapid diagnostics in different healthcare settings.
