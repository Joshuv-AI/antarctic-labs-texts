Postprandial Glucose Spikes in Free‐Living Adults With Normoglycaemia Are Associated With Modifiable Dietary and Temporal Factors: A Real‐World CGM Study
Authors: Je Hyun Jung, Tae Jung Oh, YoonJu Song
Published: 2026

Introduction

Postprandial glucose excursion, often referred to as glucose spikes, has increasingly been recognised as important indicators of metabolic health and potential targets for dietary management. Despite growing interest, the definition of a glucose spike remains inconsistent, and modifiable meal characteristics associated with postprandial glucose elevation under real‐world conditions are not well elucidated. Previous studies have described glucose excursion using various metrics, including peak postprandial glucose concentrations [1] and thresholds for postprandial hyperglycaemia [2], but no universally accepted definition has been established. Moreover, postprandial glycaemic responses are influenced by multiple factors, including meal composition, meal timing and individual characteristics such as age and microbiome [3]. However, the combined effects of modifiable meal characteristics on postprandial glucose spikes in free‐living conditions remain insufficiently understood.

Meal composition is a major determinant of postprandial glycaemic responses. Numerous controlled feeding studies using standardised meals under tightly controlled experimental conditions have demonstrated that macronutrient composition critically influences postprandial glycaemia [4]. Beyond meal composition, broader meal contexts—including behavioural and environmental factors surrounding meal consumption—also contribute to postprandial glycaemic responses [5].

A large‐scale study identified meal composition and context factors, such as meal timing, exercise and sleep, as major contributors to postprandial metabolism, whereas genetic factors explained relatively little of the observed variation [6]. These findings suggest that glucose excursions reflect complex interactions among meal composition, meal context and individual metabolic variability. With widespread CGM adoption, recent precision nutrition studies have utilised machine‐learning algorithms to predict individual glycaemic responses and develop personalised dietary recommendations [3, 7]. However, despite modest improvements in certain cardiometabolic outcomes, these approaches have not consistently improved insulin and glucose levels compared with standard dietary advice [8], and have largely focused on food‐specific recommendations with limited consideration of broader real‐world meal contexts.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify dietary and temporal meal‐level factors of postprandial glucose excursions using CGM and to quantify risk‐associated levels in adults with normoglycaemia.

Methods

Participants

This prospective observational study evaluated postprandial glycaemic responses using 14‐day continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) in healthy adults aged ≥ 18 years. During the CGM period, participants concurrently recorded dietary intake and meal timing using a mobile application under free‐living conditions (Figure S1). Additional detailed methods are provided in the Supporting Information: Methods.

Postprandial Glucose Spike

Postprandial glucose metrics were derived by aligning meal times recorded in daily logs with CGM data. For each meal event, CGM glucose values during the subsequent 120‐min period were extracted to construct a meal‐level dataset. A glucose spike was defined as a peak glucose level ≥ 140 mg/dL within the 120‐min postprandial window, consistent with diabetes guidelines and previous studies [2, 9].

Statistical Analysis

Meal‐related variables were analysed using linear mixed‐effects models and generalised estimating equations (GEE) to account for repeated meal events within participants.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 97 participants were included, with a mean age of 22.0 ± 1.9 years and BMI of 23.6 ± 4.3 kg/m2 (Table S1). Mean glucose was 100.4 ± 7.3 mg/dL and was higher in men than in women. Mean daily energy intake was 1858.2 kcal, with macronutrient distribution of 51.2%, 15.8% and 33.0% for carbohydrate, protein and fat, respectively.

Meal Characteristics and Postprandial Glycaemic Response

A total of 3013 main meals and 2758 snacks were analysed in Table 1. Spike meal occurrence was higher for main meals than snacks (39.2% vs. 27.7%, p < 0.001). Main meals resulted in higher peak glucose and iAUC120, whereas snacks were associated with higher pre‐meal and nadir glucose levels. Main meals contained higher energy and macronutrient intake, whereas snacks had a higher proportion of carbohydrate and sugar intake compared with main meals. Meal timing differed between meal types, with snacks consumed later in the day and more frequently within 120 min of a previous meal compared with main meals.

Factors Associated With Spike Meals

Factors associated with spike meals are presented in Figure 1, with corresponding category levels and predicted spike probabilities provided in Table S2.

Meal‐level factors associated with glucose spike occurrence by meal type. Points and error bars represent odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), respectively, estimated using generalised estimating equation models. The reference category for each variable is indicated by OR = 1. Q, quartile; T, tertile. Corresponding category levels and predicted spike probabilities are provided in Table S2.

Among main meals, the highest quartile of carbohydrate intake (median 125 g) was associated with 2.26‐fold higher odds of spike meals (95% CI: 1.78–2.86) compared with the second quartile (59 g). Higher quartiles of pre‐meal glucose also showed graded associations with spike occurrence. Compared with breakfast, lunch and dinner were associated with higher odds of spike meals. Among snacks, higher carbohydrate intake, elevated pre‐meal glucose and shorter meal interval were associated with increased odds of spike meals.

Sensitivity analyses yielded generally consistent associations across alternative spike thresholds of 150 mg/dL and 160 mg/dL (Figures S2 and S3). Similarly, excluding meals overlapping within the 120‐min postprandial window did not substantially alter the observed associations (Figures S4 and S5).

Discussion

In this real‐world study, we identified and quantified modifiable meal‐level dietary and temporal factors associated with postprandial glucose excursions in adults with normoglycaemia.

Meal carbohydrate intake emerged as the dominant dietary factor associated with spike meal occurrence. This finding is consistent with previous evidence demonstrating that the amount of dietary carbohydrate is a primary determinant of postprandial glucose elevation [6, 10]. Importantly, by quantifying risk across intake quartiles, we identified intake levels associated with increased spike probability under real‐world conditions. However, these categories were derived from the distribution of intake levels within the study population and should not be interpreted as biologically defined thresholds or clinical recommendations.

Pre‐meal glucose also emerged as an important meal‐level factor and may function not only as an associated factor but also as a potential mediator reflecting prior dietary exposures and physiological states. Previous research has shown that pre‐meal glucose predicts subsequent postprandial glycaemic responses and peak glucose levels [11].

Beyond carbohydrate intake, short meal intervals before snack were associated with higher spike occurrence, potentially reflecting the second‐meal effect described previously [12]. Protein intake and meal timing were also associated with glucose spike occurrence, although their effect sizes were smaller than those of meal carbohydrate and pre‐meal glucose, suggesting a complementary rather than primary role in mitigating glucose spike occurrence.

These findings highlight that postprandial glucose responses are influenced by multiple interacting dietary and physiological factors, underscoring the difficulty of predicting glycaemic responses based solely on individual foods or defining fixed thresholds for glucose spikes. In this context, intermittent or short‐term use of CGM may help identify modifiable meal‐related contributors to glucose excursions and support behavioural modifications.

This study has several limitations. First, several non‐dietary factors known to influence glycaemic regulation–including physical activity, sleep patterns, circadian rhythms, hormonal influences and gut microbiota–were not fully accounted for in the analyses. Second, meal data were based on self‐reported dietary logs, which may have introduced recall bias and inaccuracies in portion size estimation. Third, the study population consisted of young Korean adults from a single ethnic and geographic background, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups, populations with metabolic disease and different dietary cultures or settings. Fourth, applying a fixed glucose spike threshold may not fully capture inter‐individual variability in glycaemic responses. Lastly, given the observational study design and these limitations, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Despite these limitations, this study provides novel real‐world evidence that glucose spikes occur frequently even among healthy young adults and identifies key modifiable dietary and temporal factors associated with spike occurrence. These findings may provide clinically relevant insights for developing meal‐based behavioural strategies aimed at mitigating postprandial glucose excursions in real‐world settings.
