Leadership and management competency frameworks in undergraduate medical education: a scoping review
Authors: Ahmed Alhadithi, Mohammed Almansour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical leadership and management (MLM) competencies are increasingly recognized as essential for preparing physicians to navigate complex healthcare systems. Undergraduate medical education (UME) provides an optimal stage to introduce these skills; however, curricular approaches vary widely, and a comprehensive global synthesis is still lacking. This scoping review systematically mapped the international literature on MLM in UME, focusing on core competency frameworks, pedagogical approaches, and strategies for assessment and program evaluation.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and ERIC databases was conducted up to February 2025, complemented by a gray literature search. Following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, two independent reviewers screened 1,547 sources and selected full-text English-language publications related to MLM in UME. Data on competency domains, teaching methods, student assessment, and program evaluation were extracted and synthesized. Competencies were coded into predefined intrapersonal, interpersonal, and organizational categories.ResultsSixteen publications met the inclusion criteria. Competencies were analyzed using a three-level analytical framework. Curricula predominantly featured interactive and experiential teaching methods such as simulation and project-based learning. Assessment commonly relied on reflective practice and multi-source feedback. Despite increased curricular activity, gaps remained in the integration of organizational competencies (e.g., systems thinking, strategic planning) and in the use of systematic, long-term evaluation strategies.ConclusionThis review highlights growing international momentum toward comprehensive MLM education in UME. While intrapersonal and interpersonal competencies are commonly addressed, organizational leadership remains underdeveloped. Strengthening the organizational-level domain, along with implementing longitudinal program evaluation, is essential for aligning MLM training with the evolving leadership demands of contemporary healthcare systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08489-6.Keywords: Medical education, Undergraduate, Medical leadership, Management, Curriculum, Competency

Introduction

Effective leadership and management are fundamental to organizational success [1, 2]. This is particularly pertinent in complex sectors such as healthcare, where robust MLM capabilities are increasingly viewed as essential [3, 4]. Current evidence highlights a significant gap in organizational leadership skills among physicians [5, 6], a shortfall made more visible during the COVID-19 pandemic, which underscored the importance of adaptive leadership during times of crisis [7, 8]. Strategic collaboration between medical schools and healthcare institutions is, therefore, imperative to bridge this gap and cultivate strong clinical leadership capabilities [9, 10]. Competent clinical leadership has a direct impact on healthcare quality, cost-efficiency, and, ultimately, patient outcomes [11–13].

Despite a growing body of literature, defining leadership remains challenging due to its varied interpretations across contexts [14]. Broadly, leadership encompasses envisioning future goals, effective communication, managing people and organizations, and achieving shared objectives [15]. In healthcare contexts, Gabel [16] defined leadership as “the appropriate and ethical influence exerted by one individual to alter, modify, or change the reactions, attitudes, or behaviors of other individuals to maintain or further core values of the health professions”. Essential leadership attributes, such as emotional intelligence, resilience, personal development, and relationship management, are increasingly recognized as essential components of effective clinical practice [14, 17]. In contrast, management typically refers to transactional and operational functions, including goal setting, planning, resource allocation, and performance oversight [18, 19]. However, contemporary theories increasingly view leadership and management as complementary and overlapping roles in practice [2, 8, 20, 21].

Investing in MLM training offers substantial personal and institutional benefits, including enhanced resilience, emotional intelligence, and improved healthcare system performance [22, 23]. Evidence strongly supports UME as the optimal phase for introducing these foundational skills, allowing competencies to be integrated early, thereby promoting sustained professional development and preparing students for diverse leadership roles [4, 24].

While prior reviews have significantly advanced the understanding of MLM training in UME, many have focused on specific national or institutional contexts [5, 25]. James et al. [26] provided a more global perspective by examining international efforts to integrate MLM training in UME, reviewing literature published up to 2020 and offering an important foundation for understanding MLM curricula worldwide. Building on this earlier global review, the present scoping review provides an updated and more structured synthesis of MLM competency frameworks, pedagogical approaches, assessment strategies, and program evaluation methods in UME, while also incorporating relevant gray literature. This work is particularly timely given the growing international emphasis on MLM as a core component of professional identity formation in UME, and its increasing recognition by accreditation bodies as an essential graduate competency.

Our review was guided by the three-level leadership framework proposed by McKimm et al. [19], which categorizes MLM competencies into intrapersonal, interpersonal, and organizational domains. This model was selected for its structured, holistic lens, aligning with the developmental progression of medical students from ‘leading self’ to ‘leading others’ to ‘leading systems’. This progression mirrors the evolving roles and responsibilities of medical students, as they advance through their training. Using this framework as an analytical lens, this scoping review systematically mapped the literature on MLM in UME across four key areas: competency frameworks, pedagogical approaches, assessment strategies, and program evaluation methods. The synthesis incorporates both peer-reviewed and gray literature, aiming to provide a comprehensive overview of current practices and identify critical gaps in the field.

Methods

This scoping review followed the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [27], which comprises five stages: (1) identifying the research questions; (2) identifying relevant studies; (3) selecting studies; (4) charting the data; and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting the results. Reporting adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [28]. A scoping review approach was selected for its methodological flexibility to explore heterogeneous literature and facilitate the categorization, synthesis, and dissemination of evidence on MLM education in UME contexts [29].

Identifying relevant studies

A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and the Education Resources Information Center (ERIC) databases was independently conducted by two investigators (AA and MA) up to February 2025 to ensure accuracy and completeness. Search outputs from both investigators were compared, and any discrepancies in the retrieved citations were reviewed and reconciled prior to the screening stage. This search was supplemented by manual screening and reference checking of key articles to identify relevant gray literature and additional publications. Study selection

All retrieved citations were exported and imported into Covidence [30], an online platform that facilitates blinded, independent screening. After automatic and manual removal of duplicates, two reviewers (AA and MA) independently screened titles and abstracts against predefined inclusion criteria.

Publications were included if they explicitly addressed MLM competencies within UME settings, regardless of study design or publication type. Publications were excluded if they were inaccessible as full texts, non-English language publications, or lacked a primary focus on MLM competencies. Full-text screening of potentially relevant articles was then conducted independently by both reviewers, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. Charting the data

Data charting was conducted independently by two investigators (AA and MA) using a standardized extraction form developed for this review (Supplementary File 1) and guided by the study objectives and analytical framework. For each included publication, the following data were extracted: bibliographic details (title, first author, year, country), publication type (e.g., review, program description, national framework), study objectives and methodology, stakeholder groups involved, referenced or developed MLM frameworks, identified MLM competencies, pedagogical strategies, student assessment methods, and program evaluation approaches.

For the purposes of this review, MLM competencies were conceptualized as knowledge, skills, behaviors, or attributes that support effective MLM within healthcare systems. Identified competencies were coded into predefined intrapersonal, interpersonal, and organizational domains derived from McKimm et al.’s three-level leadership framework. When a competency could plausibly fit more than one domain, each reviewer coded it independently and a “best-fit” categorization was agreed upon based on the dominant emphasis within the original publication. Notes on overlapping competencies and coding decisions were recorded in the extraction form, and any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Results

From an initial pool of 2,422 articles retrieved through database searches and an additional 24 identified through manual searching, 1,547 records remained after the removal of duplicates (n = 899). Title and abstract screening led to the full-text assessment of 31 publications, of which 16 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Among these, one was a non-peer-reviewed publication: the Faculty of Medical Leadership and Management (FMLM) Indicative Curriculum [31]. The selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1 using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

Characteristics of included publications

The 16 included publications varied widely in geographic origin, publication type, and methodological approach, reflecting the diverse ways in which MLM education is conceptualized and delivered within UME. Most studies (n = 10) originated from North America [5, 7, 25, 32–38]. Two studies were conducted in Europe [31, 39], one in Australasia [40], one in the Middle East [41], and one in South Asia [42]. One review adopted a global perspective [26].

In terms of publication type, three were review articles synthesizing the existing literature on leadership curricula in UME [5, 25, 26]. Two publications proposed national-level guidance frameworks, one for the United Kingdom (UK) [31] and one for India [42]. One study was a multi-institutional survey [40], while the remaining ten were single-institution primary research studies or program descriptions. These included curriculum descriptions [32, 33, 35–37] as well as formative studies exploring stakeholder perspectives to inform curriculum development [34, 38, 39, 41].

MLM competency development approaches

The included studies used several approaches to identify and define MLM competencies, including empirical data collection involving students, faculty, and healthcare leaders, as well as the adaptation of existing national or institutional frameworks. Four studies identified competencies through qualitative or mixed-methods research. These included structured interviews and critical incident analyses with leaders, clinicians, students, and interprofessional staff [39, 41], open-ended surveys exploring student perceptions during the COVID-19 pandemic [34], and a mixed-methods study combining faculty interviews, administrator focus groups, and student surveys [38]. Furthermore, one program reviewed healthcare leadership models and conducted stakeholder meetings with deans, students, and hospital leadership to build a transformational leadership framework [33].

Many curricula were informed by established national or institutional frameworks. A prominent example was FMLM’s Leadership and Management Standards for Undergraduate Medical Education, a comprehensive UK-based framework for UME, derived from the UK Medical Leadership Competency Framework [31, 43]. This framework outlined competencies across five domains: Demonstrating Personal Qualities, Working with Others, Managing Services, Improving Services, and Setting Direction [31]. Similarly, one curriculum was aligned with the CanMEDS “Leader” role to support competency-based medical education [36, 44], while others incorporated competencies consistent with Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) guidance, as described in the review by Evans et al. [5].

In addition to adopting national standards, some institutions developed and validated their own leadership frameworks tailored to program-specific needs. A notable example is the “FourCe-PITO” model, created by the Uniformed Services University of the Health Sciences for its military medical leadership program [35]. The framework integrated four core components: Character, Competence, Context, and Communication, with four levels of leadership interaction: Personal, Interpersonal, Team, and Organizational [35, 45].

This framework categorizes competencies related to leading oneself, leading others, and leading within broader healthcare systems. Teaching and learning methods

The majority of curricula included structured didactic components aimed at introducing core leadership theories and concepts. These elements established a theoretical foundation for the development of practical competencies. Common instructional methods included formal lectures, intensive workshops, and curated readings from business and medical literature [5, 31, 37, 40]. Two publications cited asynchronous online modules to provide flexible, self-directed theoretical instruction [5, 36]. In one program, the partnership with a business school shaped the instructional design, embedding business pedagogy and case-based leadership instruction within the curriculum [37].

Additionally, interactive and collaborative learning methods were widely employed to support the development of interpersonal competencies. Small-group seminars and structured discussions were frequently used to encourage communication, peer-to-peer learning, and collaborative problem-solving [5, 7, 35, 40]. Case-based learning (CBL) was another commonly reported strategy, enabling students to engage with complex, real-world scenarios and apply leadership theories in context [5, 25, 33, 37, 38]. Team-based learning (TBL) was also cited in some papers as a means to promote group decision-making and enhance understanding of team dynamics [5, 26, 41].

Experiential learning was a recurrent theme across the included publications, providing students with meaningful opportunities to apply leadership competencies in authentic settings. Simulations and role-plays were frequently employed to allow learners to practice leadership behaviors, such as navigating challenging conversations or managing clinical scenarios in a structured and low-risk environment [5, 33, 38, 39]. Many programs incorporated project-based learning, engaging students in initiatives such as quality improvement projects, community outreach activities, and health needs assessments [5, 25, 32, 38]. Additionally, integrated clinical and extracurricular experiences, such as clinical rotations and student-run clinics, were utilized as informal yet intentional platforms for leadership skill development [7, 33, 40].

Five publications emphasized personalized learning and development, highlighting mentorship and coaching as critical components for individualized guidance and professional identity formation [7, 25, 26, 31, 33]. Reflective practice was also widely implemented, often through structured reflective writing assignments and portfolios [37, 39, 42].

Implementation formats of MLM curricula

Across the included publications, MLM curricula were implemented using a range of delivery formats. Several interventions were delivered as stand-alone courses or seminar-based programs alongside the core curriculum, typically combining didactic teaching with small-group learning, case-based activities, and project work [33, 36–38, 41]. Other programs were integrated within existing educational structures, including longitudinal leadership curricula and clinical or community-based learning experiences such as rotations, community projects, or student-run clinics [7, 32, 35, 40]. In contrast, national frameworks provided broad direction for implementation but did not prescribe a single format. The FMLM framework described MLM learning as integrated throughout the undergraduate program and reinforced across academic and clinical settings [31]. Another national framework emphasized the need for local adaptation, noting that MLM content could be incorporated within existing curricular structures according to institutional resources, capacity, and priorities [42].

Assessment methods

The reviewed literature described a wide array of approaches used to assess MLM competencies, ranging from reflective self-appraisal to structured, performance-based evaluation. One common strategy was the use of reflective writing, such as narrative assignments and journaling, to encourage students to critically analyze their leadership experiences [33, 36, 37, 42]. These reflections were often compiled into portfolios that documented longitudinal development and were assessed by faculty using standardized rubrics.

Observation and feedback-based methods were also widely reported. Multi-source feedback, also known as 360-degree evaluations, was used to assess interpersonal skills through input from faculty, peers, and other collaborators [31, 33, 41]. This was frequently complemented by peer assessment following group activities, as well as direct faculty or observer evaluations during simulated or clinical tasks [25, 35, 40].

Performance-based assessments featured prominently in several studies. Simulated scenarios were commonly integrated into Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs), with stations specifically designed to evaluate leadership skills such as communication, teamwork, and ethical decision-making [31, 40, 41]. Workplace-based tools, such as the Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise (Mini-CEX), were employed to assess professionalism and decision-making during real clinical encounters [31, 40]. In addition, the evaluation of student-led projects, such as quality improvement initiatives and community outreach, served as a means of assessing applied leadership outcomes [31, 32].

Moreover, traditional assessments were also used to evaluate foundational knowledge. Written exams and quizzes measured students’ understanding of leadership theories and healthcare systems [35, 36, 40]. Additional validated instruments used to assess specific competencies included the Leadership Practices Inventory (LPI), the Relational Coordination Scale (RCS), the Baer Mindfulness Scale (BMS), and the Leadership Inventory for Medical Education (LIME) [32, 33].

Program and course evaluation methods

Curricula were evaluated using a range of strategies, with many studies moving beyond basic satisfaction surveys to adopt more rigorous, multi-level approaches to assessing program effectiveness. The most commonly reported method was the collection of immediate participant feedback, typically obtained through anonymous student surveys administered after individual sessions, at mid-course, or following program completion [5, 25, 26, 33, 35, 39, 40]. These surveys often employed a mixed-methods design, combining quantitative rating scales with open-ended qualitative questions to assess satisfaction and identify specific areas for iterative curriculum refinement.

To guide evaluation, the Kirkpatrick Model [46] was referenced in four publications as a structured framework encompassing outcomes from participants’ immediate reactions (Level 1) to learning gains (Level 2) and behavioral changes (Level 3) [5, 26, 39, 42]. To measure the direct impact on learning and behavior (Kirkpatrick Levels 2 and 3), many programs employed pre- and post-training evaluation, often using validated instruments to measure changes in students’ knowledge, skills, and attitudes toward leadership [5, 32, 33]. In addition, qualitative approaches, including focus groups, participant interviews, and analysis of reflective portfolios, were reported in three studies as methods for capturing in-depth learner perspectives [5, 38, 42].

Longitudinal evaluation strategies were described in three studies. These included follow-up surveys conducted months or years after program completion to assess the retention and real-world application of leadership competencies [5, 25, 39]. One study reported a formal, long-term follow-up on career outcomes, designed to track the leadership development of graduates over time [35].

Discussion

This scoping review examined MLM competency frameworks, curricular content, teaching strategies, assessment methods, and evaluation practices reported across UME settings globally. The findings demonstrate a growing international emphasis on formal MLM training, reflecting increasing recognition that leadership competencies are essential for physicians in today’s complex healthcare systems. To guide analysis, MLM competencies were categorized using a structured three-level framework encompassing intrapersonal, interpersonal, and organizational domains [19]. Within this framework, most curricula addressed intrapersonal (e.g., self-awareness, emotional intelligence) and interpersonal competencies (e.g., communication, teamwork), while organizational-level skills, such as strategic planning and resource management, were comparatively underrepresented. This distribution highlights persistent challenges institutions face in systematically integrating MLM training across all domains of leadership.

Medical schools offer rich opportunities for leadership development, leveraging both formal instruction and informal learning experiences during clinical training [47, 48]. However, over-reliance on informal learning can be problematic, as role modeling in clinical settings may reinforce both desirable and undesirable leadership behaviors [49]. Incorporating explicit, evidence-based MLM frameworks can enhance the consistency, quality, and intentionality of leadership education [50].

Curriculum design and integration

Our review identified wide variability in the content, delivery, and evaluation of MLM training across UME programs. This variability has been noted in prior reviews and reflects diverse institutional priorities, educational cultures, and resource capacities [5, 25]. The variability observed across programs makes the implementation of a fully standardized, one-size-fits-all MLM curriculum challenging in practice. Our findings instead point toward models that combine a shared core of commonly reported competencies (e.g., emotional intelligence, self-awareness, communication, teamwork) with context-sensitive adaptation to institutional missions, health-system demands, and learner needs.

Factors contributing to this variability include inconsistent terminology, overlapping boundaries between leadership and adjacent domains such as professionalism and communication, as well as differences in faculty expertise and institutional capacity [26, 40]. Institutional commitment, such as the availability of dual-degree programs or dedicated leadership tracks, also plays a critical role in determining the depth and extent of MLM integration [51].

Although medical students generally acknowledge the importance of leadership and management skills for their future practice, barriers persist. These include limited protected curricular time, low uptake of voluntary leadership opportunities, and a lack of structured or longitudinal programming [52, 53]. While faculty often support MLM education, they may prioritize clinical content or lack training and institutional support to deliver leadership teaching effectively [26, 54]. These challenges are further exacerbated by curriculum saturation, which consistently emerges as a barrier to implementing additional educational content [55].

Proposed strategies to address these constraints include offering MLM training as elective modules or student-selected components, allowing learners to engage with leadership content without displacing core clinical instruction [55]. Other recommendations include adopting blended or online learning modalities to reduce resource burdens and promoting interprofessional collaboration with fields such as business, public health, or administration to enrich the learning experience [53, 56, 57].

Pedagogical approaches to MLM education

This review identified broad consensus favoring interactive and experiential learning approaches over traditional didactic methods in the teaching of MLM. Common strategies included simulations, mentorship, student-led projects, and quality improvement initiatives, all of which were employed to foster active engagement and real-world leadership skills. These approaches align with findings in the broader medical education literature, which identify experiential learning as a highly effective method for MLM competency development [26, 47, 58].

Despite these pedagogical advances, a persistent gap was noted in the standardization of assessment strategies for MLM competencies. Many programs relied on reflective writing and multi-source feedback to evaluate intrapersonal and interpersonal domains; however, the assessment of organizational-level competencies, such as systems leadership, financial decision-making, or strategic planning, was infrequently described. This gap has been consistently highlighted in the literature, which emphasizes the need for validated tools capable of capturing the complex nature of leadership in undergraduate learners [59, 60].

In addition to structured teaching, many MLM competencies are reinforced through informal learning within clinical environments. Medical schools can provide opportunities for leadership development through role modeling, team-based clinical activities, and the hidden curriculum [47, 48, 61]. Without explicit alignment with formal MLM competency frameworks, these experiences may be inconsistent or may inadvertently reinforce unhelpful norms [49]. Purposefully linking experiential and simulation-based learning with clearly articulated MLM competencies may therefore help harness the strengths of the hidden curriculum while promoting greater consistency and intentionality.

Enablers and barriers to implementation

Successful integration of MLM training has been associated with strong institutional leadership, student-driven demand, and interprofessional education initiatives that promote collaborative learning environments [4, 7, 57]. The presence of internal champions, whether faculty members or institutional leaders advocating for leadership education, has also been identified as a key enabler for overcoming organizational resistance and promoting program buy-in [55, 62].

However, several implementation challenges remain. Beyond curriculum overcrowding and limited faculty capacity, the absence of systematic, long-term program evaluation continues to hinder program development [5]. Additionally, the lack of consensus on standardized MLM competency frameworks has contributed to fragmented curricular design, with institutions often developing their own models in the absence of unified guidance [25]. Closing these gaps is crucial to sustaining leadership education and achieving lasting program impact.

Addressing organizational competencies

The review identified a consistent underrepresentation of organizational leadership competencies, such as strategic planning, resource management, and systems thinking, within UME. This gap represents a significant limitation in preparing students for broader leadership roles they will encounter in real-world healthcare settings. Without exposure to organizational-level competencies, students may be less equipped to understand, navigate, and influence the complex systems in which they will practice [4]. Given the increasing complexity of healthcare environments, characterized by resource limitations, interprofessional collaboration, and continuous system reform, these organizational competencies are essential for physicians to lead change, enhance care delivery, and advocate for systemic improvements [40, 63].

Delaying the development of these skills until postgraduate training limits opportunities to integrate leadership concepts into students’ early clinical experiences. Integrating organizational leadership training at the undergraduate level through case-based learning, quality improvement initiatives, or dedicated health systems modules would help establish a stronger foundation for system-level thinking and earlier engagement in leadership roles [26, 57].

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. First, it was restricted to English-language publications identified through selected databases, which may have excluded relevant non-English studies or those indexed in alternative databases. Second, the inclusion criteria prioritized studies that explicitly described standalone MLM frameworks or articulated leadership and management competencies. As a result, studies embedding MLM training within broader curricular domains, such as professionalism, interprofessional education, or patient safety, may have been inadvertently excluded. Third, substantial variability in study designs, pedagogical strategies, and reporting standards limited the ability to draw direct comparisons across included publications. Future reviews would benefit from expanded search strategies that encompass additional languages, databases, and studies in which MLM is integrated more implicitly into medical curricula.

Conclusions

This scoping review offers a comprehensive overview of MLM competencies, teaching strategies, and assessment approaches within UME globally. While intrapersonal and interpersonal skills are well represented, organizational leadership competencies remain notably underemphasized. The marked variability in MLM content, delivery, and evaluation across programs makes the implementation of a fully standardized, one-size-fits-all curriculum challenging in practice. Our findings instead support approaches that combine a shared core of commonly addressed competencies (e.g., emotional intelligence, self-awareness, communication, teamwork) with context-sensitive adaptation to institutional missions, health system priorities, and learner needs. Embedding MLM as a longitudinal, explicitly structured component of UME, supported by experiential learning opportunities and robust program evaluation, can better prepare future physicians to lead effectively in increasingly complex and evolving healthcare systems.
