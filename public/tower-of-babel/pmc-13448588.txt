Association between inpatient AUDIT-C alcohol screening scores and postoperative outcomes: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Toby Chen, Anne C Fernandez, Sarah Evilsizer, Chiao-Li Chan, Vidhya Gunaseelan, Jennifer F Waljee, Mark C Bicket
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate whether alcohol use severity, as measured by the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test-Consumption (AUDIT-C), is associated with postoperative complications, hospital utilisation and opioid prescribing among adult surgical inpatients.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingSingle tertiary academic hospital in the USA, using data from inpatient surgical admissions between April 2021 and September 2023.ParticipantsAdult patients (≥18 years) admitted for inpatient surgery with a completed AUDIT-C screening during their admission.MeasuresAUDIT-C scores were categorised as at-risk (≥5 for men, ≥4 for women) or low risk (other non-zero scores). Patients with AUDIT-C scores of 0 were described in the cohort but excluded from the primary comparative analyses to account for potential J-shaped associations between abstinence and health outcomes. The primary outcome was a composite of surgical complications, emergency department visits, hospital readmissions or mortality within 30 days of surgery. Secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay and postoperative opioid prescribing from discharge through 6 months. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations between AUDIT-C scores and postoperative outcomes. Additional analyses included Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, receiver operating characteristic curves and feature importance modelling.Results9538 (31.1%) of 30 708 eligible surgical patients completed the AUDIT-C, and screening was more common among younger, healthier patients. Among screened patients, 5440 (57.0%) were female and 7405 (77.6%) identified as non-Hispanic White. A total of 805 (8.4%) of screened patients reported at-risk alcohol use. The primary outcome, composite adverse postoperative outcomes, occurred in 13.9% of low-risk patients and 14.5% of at-risk patients (p=0.637). In unadjusted analyses, no significant differences were observed between alcohol risk groups for individual postoperative outcomes, including complications, 30-day readmissions, emergency department visits or mortality. Multivariable logistic regression confirmed no association between AUDIT-C category and the primary composite outcome (average marginal effect 0.01, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.03). Median hospital length of stay differed slightly between groups (3.0 days (IQR 4.0) vs 3.0 days (IQR 5.0); p=0.01). In adjusted Cox proportional hazards modelling, at-risk alcohol use was associated with a slightly lower hazard of discharge compared with low-risk alcohol use, corresponding to a modestly longer time to discharge (HR=0.92, p=0.041). Time to readmission did not differ significantly. Opioid prescribing at discharge was similar across groups (58.4% vs 58.9%, p=0.763).ConclusionsIn this real-world surgical cohort, observed AUDIT-C risk category among patients who completed screening was not significantly associated with short-term postoperative outcomes or opioid prescribing. While small differences in length of stay reached statistical significance, their clinical relevance is uncertain. Limitations include limited number of at-risk patients, non-response bias and the absence of standardised follow-up for at-risk patients, which may have contributed to null findings. Future efforts should focus on improving screening through workflow integration, surgical team engagement and follow-up interventions to fully realise benefits from alcohol screening.

Introduction

Excessive alcohol consumption remains one of the most prevalent and preventable public health issues in the USA and is strongly associated with increased morbidity, mortality and healthcare expenditures.1–5 Recent data demonstrate escalating rates of alcohol use and alcohol use disorders across various demographic groups in the USA, a trajectory notably accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic.6–8 These increases have been particularly pronounced among women, with recent studies reporting steeper rises in alcohol-related mortality compared with men, highlighting potential sex-specific differences and emphasising the need for tailored approaches to alcohol risk assessment and interventions.9 10 Such developments have critical implications for surgical populations, given the established associations between hazardous alcohol use and adverse postoperative outcomes, including infections, cardiopulmonary complications, prolonged hospitalisation, intensive care utilisation, impaired wound healing and elevated mortality.11–15 Accordingly, surgical societies and public health authorities recommend preoperative alcohol screening based on evidence that short-term preoperative abstinence can significantly mitigate these risks.16 17 Nonetheless, despite widespread acknowledgement of alcohol’s clinical relevance, major gaps remain in understanding how specific levels of alcohol use, particularly as quantified by validated screening instruments, translate into differential surgical risks across diverse patient populations.

Although clinical guidelines frequently advocate for perioperative alcohol screening, few studies have empirically evaluated the association between structured alcohol screening scores and real-world postoperative outcomes in diverse surgical populations. Much of the existing literature examining alcohol use and surgical outcomes has been derived from the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) system, with reports on care for male, older veterans undergoing elective surgeries.18 19 While these studies have offered key insights, it is unclear whether and to what extent these studies generalise to broader surgical populations across different care settings, including women, non-veterans and patients undergoing non-elective procedures. Furthermore, these prior investigations often rely on mailed versions of alcohol screening questionnaires, administered weeks to months before the surgical date, creating a temporal mismatch with screening for alcohol well before the surgical episode.20–25 This temporal misalignment has the potential to reduce the clinical utility of screening by failing to capture recent shifts in alcohol use that may influence perioperative risk. By contrast, screening closer to the time of surgery may better identify patients at heightened risk for complications or withdrawal.19 25–27 Given these limitations, it is critical to determine whether alcohol use severity, as measured by validated screening tools, correlates with postoperative outcomes in real-world hospital settings. If such associations are observed, inpatient alcohol screening may serve as a valuable tool for perioperative risk stratification and targeted interventions. Conversely, if no strong relationships emerge, the utility of inpatient screening may be more focused on identifying patients at risk for withdrawal or other immediate management needs, rather than as a predictor of broader postoperative outcomes in real-world surgical care.

Because alcohol screening in many hospitals occurs at the time of admission rather than during longitudinal preoperative evaluation, understanding how this point-of-care screening information relates to postoperative outcomes is important for evaluating its real-world clinical utility. To address this gap, we investigated whether alcohol use severity, as measured by Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test-Consumption (AUDIT-C) scores obtained at the time of inpatient surgical admission, is associated with key postoperative outcomes across a large, diverse surgical population. While prior literature has established a general association between hazardous alcohol use and surgical complications, few studies have evaluated whether specific risk levels identified through structured screening tools predict adverse outcomes in real-world inpatient surgical settings. We hypothesised that patients with higher-risk alcohol use, as indicated by elevated AUDIT-C scores, would be associated with increased odds of 30-day postoperative complications, defined using a composite outcome including hospital readmissions, emergency department (ED) visits or postoperative mortality. In addition, we anticipated that higher-risk alcohol use would be associated with longer hospital length of stay and greater opioid prescribing after discharge from surgery. By clarifying these associations, this study seeks to determine whether standardised inpatient alcohol screening at hospital admission meaningfully contributes to perioperative risk stratification, targeted preventive strategies and improved clinical outcomes.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study used routinely collected clinical data from the electronic health record (EHR), extracted through a customised data request from the Data Office for Clinical and Translational Research (DOCTR) at the University of Michigan. The primary objective was to evaluate the association between perioperative alcohol consumption, as assessed by the AUDIT-C, and postoperative outcomes. Prior work examined the feasibility and implementation of AUDIT-C screening.21 The study protocol was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Michigan and was deemed exempt from the requirement for patient informed consent, as it involved retrospective analyses of clinical data collected during standard care delivery. Investigators had access to the deidentified analytic dataset generated through DOCTR extraction but did not have direct access to the full source EHR database.

Study population

The cohort consisted of adult surgical patients aged 18 years or older admitted to inpatient care at the University of Michigan Hospital between 1 April 2021 and 30 September 2023. Inclusion criteria required that the hospital admission date coincide precisely with the date of surgery, thereby specifically identifying patients who were hospitalised primarily for surgical intervention. Patients with an inpatient length of stay shorter than 24 hours were excluded, as these admissions generally do not fulfil criteria for inpatient surgical stays and instead reflect brief observation periods or Post-Anesthesia Care Unit-only stays. Additionally, patients who recorded an unknown gender were also excluded from the study cohort.

AUDIT-C score

The primary exposure was perioperative alcohol consumption risk, measured using the AUDIT-C tool, a brief, validated three-question questionnaire developed by the WHO to identify hazardous drinking. The AUDIT-C has been validated extensively as a reliable method to detect hazardous drinking behaviours in both outpatient and inpatient clinical environments.22 23 28–30 Recent work has also demonstrated high test-retest reliability of AUDIT-C responses when completed by patients in routine clinical care.31 Recognising national gaps in perioperative alcohol risk assessment, our institution recently implemented a hospital-wide AUDIT-C screening protocol embedded directly into the institutional EHR through a standardised template as part of routine inpatient intake processes for surgical admissions.21 Screening was integrated into the hospital’s electronic admission navigator and was typically completed by nursing staff during intake. Completion was not mandatory.

AUDIT-C scores range from 0 to 12 and were categorised into clinically relevant risk groups using validated sex-specific cutoffs for hazardous alcohol use. For men, scores of 1–4 were classified as no or low-risk use, while scores of 5–12 indicated at-risk use. For women, a more conservative threshold was applied, scores of 1–3 were considered no or low-risk, and scores of 4–12 were categorised as at-risk, reflecting sex-specific risk profiles for alcohol-related harm due to differences in body composition, alcohol metabolism and associated health risks.22 29 32 33

Notably, we adopted a modified risk stratification approach informed by epidemiologic literature describing a J-shaped relationship between alcohol use and health outcomes.34 Prior studies have found that individuals who report no alcohol use (ie, AUDIT-C score of 0) may paradoxically have higher morbidity or mortality compared with those reporting low levels of use.19 35 Because individuals reporting abstinence may represent a heterogeneous group, including former heavy drinkers, individuals with significant comorbidities or those abstaining due to underlying illness, patients with an AUDIT-C score of 0 were retained in descriptive analyses of the screened cohort but excluded from the primary comparative analyses to reduce potential confounding related to abstainer heterogeneity. To evaluate the robustness of this analytic decision, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which patients with an AUDIT-C score of 0 were included in the low-risk category (online supplemental table 1).

Encounters that lacked a completed AUDIT-C form or contained partial or declined responses were designated as non-respondents. For encounters with more than one AUDIT-C score recorded during the hospital stay, the highest score was retained for analysis. An exploratory analysis used a three-level stratification scheme of low-risk use (scores 1–4), moderate-risk use (scores 5–8) and high-risk use (scores 9–12). No sex-specific thresholds were used for exploratory analysis.

Postoperative outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was a composite postoperative outcome within 30 days of surgery, defined as the occurrence of any diagnosed surgical complication, mortality, ED visit or hospital readmission. Individual surgical complications evaluated included acute lung failure, pneumonia, myocardial infarction, deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, acute renal failure, surgical site infection, haemorrhage (including gastrointestinal bleeding) and postoperative delirium, as identified via International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes recorded within 30 days of the surgical procedure. For patients with the same diagnosis recorded within 30 days before surgery, the diagnosis was not counted as a new postoperative complication. ICD-10 Clinical Modification diagnosis code lists used to define postoperative complications are provided in online supplemental table 9.

Secondary outcomes encompassed postoperative measures for hospital length of stay (calculated from admission to discharge), rates and patterns of discharge opioid prescriptions (issued day of hospital discharge after surgery or 1 day before hospital discharge after surgery) and the total number of opioid prescriptions within 6 months after discharge from the date of surgery.

Covariates

A comprehensive set of demographic, clinical and surgical covariates was extracted from the institutional EHR. Demographic variables included patient age in years (categorised as 18–44, 45–64 and ≥65), sex (male and female), race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Hispanic, other and unknown), insurance type (Medicare, Medicaid, private and unknown) and relationship status (partnered, non-partnered, unknown/other). Clinical variables included the Elixhauser comorbidity index to quantify patient comorbidity burden; case status classified as scheduled, urgent or emergent; and presence or absence of documented preoperative opioid prescriptions within the 12-month period preceding surgery. Surgical variables comprised the primary surgical service (eg, acute care surgery, orthopaedic, cardiac and plastic surgery).

Statistical analysis

Initial analyses involved descriptive statistics summarising demographic, clinical and surgical characteristics of the overall patient cohort, AUDIT-C risk status (low-risk alcohol use vs at-risk alcohol use). Continuous variables were presented as means with SD and compared across groups using independent t-tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were conducted using χ² tests. Data cleaning included exclusion of encounters with invalid or partial AUDIT-C responses, retention of the highest AUDIT-C score when multiple scores were recorded and restriction to encounters meeting predefined eligibility criteria.

Univariate logistic regression analyses assessed associations between AUDIT-C-defined alcohol risk categories and postoperative composite outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression modelling adjusted for potential confounders identified a priori, including demographic factors (age, sex, race/ethnicity), clinical variables (comorbidity index, preoperative opioid prescriptions) and surgical factors (case status, surgical service), with report of estimates using average marginal effects. Because the primary outcome was defined as a composite event occurring within 30 days of surgery, logistic regression models were used to facilitate comparison with prior surgical outcomes literature that employs standardised postoperative outcome windows.

To evaluate the predictive accuracy of the AUDIT-C screening, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) was calculated for each outcome model, with comparisons drawn between models incorporating AUDIT-C scores and those without. These predictive analyses were intended to assess the incremental prognostic value of AUDIT-C risk category, rather than to estimate the causal effect of alcohol use on postoperative outcomes.

Kaplan-Meier survival analyses and Cox proportional hazards modelling were employed to examine time-to-event outcomes (eg, discharge, mortality, readmission), with patients stratified by AUDIT-C risk categories. Interaction analyses investigated potential moderation effects by case status, patient age group and sex on the relationship between AUDIT-C scores and postoperative outcomes.

Several sensitivity analyses tested the robustness of findings, including comparisons between respondents and non-respondents to examine possible selection bias introduced by non-response. Additional sensitivity analyses assessed outcomes based on AUDIT-C categories of low, moderate and high-risk to examine threshold effects and linearity assumptions.

To account for potential non-random completion of AUDIT-C screening, we conducted a two-step selection-correction sensitivity analysis based on Heckman’s sample selection framework.36 This approach was used to evaluate whether selective AUDIT-C completion may have influenced the association between AUDIT-C risk category and the primary composite postoperative outcome. In the first step, a probit model estimated the probability of completing AUDIT-C screening using workflow-related and procedure-related variables, including surgical service, case status, procedure location and principal anaesthesia technique. These variables were selected because they reflect operational and procedure-related factors plausibly associated with completion of the inpatient admission navigator and AUDIT-C documentation during routine clinical care. This first-stage model included patients who did not complete AUDIT-C screening and patients with completed AUDIT-C scores greater than 0, excluding patients with AUDIT-C score 0 to align with the primary comparative analytic cohort. The inverse Mills ratio derived from the first-stage model was then included in a second-stage multivariable logistic regression model evaluating the association between AUDIT-C risk category and the primary composite postoperative outcome. Because the primary outcome was binary and the second-stage model used logistic regression for consistency with the main analysis, this approach was interpreted as a sensitivity analysis rather than a definitive correction for selection bias.

Lastly, feature importance analyses (using permutation importance methods) evaluated the relative predictive contribution of each covariate included in the multivariable models.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Because the primary analysis focused on a single prespecified composite outcome, formal adjustments for multiple testing were not applied; secondary analyses were intended to aid interpretation of the primary findings. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (V.16.1).

Results

Patient demographics

The final analytic cohort included 30 708 adult inpatient surgical admissions at a large academic medical centre between 1 April 2021 and 30 September 2023. Of these, 9538 patients (31.1%) had a completed and valid AUDIT-C alcohol screening recorded during their surgical hospitalisation (table 1). Compared with non-respondents, those who completed screening were more likely to be younger, with 34.2% aged 18 to 44 in the screened group versus 23.0% 18 to 44 within non-screened patients. Screened patients were also more likely to be female (57.0% vs 49.7%) and enrolled in Medicaid (14.2% vs 11.2%). Conversely, patients with an Elixhauser Comorbidity Index ≥6 were screened less frequently (26.5%) than those with scores of 0 (43.8%) or 1–5 (34.3%).

Screening rates varied substantially by surgical characteristics. Elective procedures had lower screening completion (29.1%) compared with urgent (48.4%) and emergent (51.7%) cases. Screening also differed across surgical services: obstetrics and gynaecology had the highest completion rate (52.4%), followed by ophthalmology (49.3%) and acute care surgery (43.5%), whereas internal medicine (15.0%) and cardiovascular surgery (15.2%) had the lowest (table 1).

AUDIT-C scores

Among the 9538 patients with completed AUDIT-C screening, 5880 (61.6%) were classified as having no alcohol use, 2853 (29.9%) were classified as low-risk alcohol use and 805 (8.4%) were classified as at-risk based on sex-specific thresholds. When examining the distribution of AUDIT-C scores more granularly, 2853 (29.9%) reported low-risk use (scores of 1–4), 488 (5.1%) reported moderate-risk use (scores of 5–8) and 130 (1.4%) reported high-risk use (scores of 9–12) (table 2).

Postoperative complications

The composite postoperative outcome, defined as any surgical complication, ED visit, hospital readmission or death within 30 days, occurred in 13.9% of patients with low-risk alcohol use and 14.5% of those in the at-risk group. This difference was not statistically significant (table 3).

Similar findings of no significant differences between low-risk and at-risk alcohol use appeared when examining diagnosed surgical complications (7.5% low-risk vs 7.1% at-risk), postoperative death (0.9% low-risk vs 1.4% at-risk), readmission within 30 days after surgery (7.3% low-risk vs 6.2% at-risk) and hospital visit within 30 days after surgery (9.6% low-risk vs 9.4% at-risk).

Hospital length of stay

Hospital length of stay showed differences between groups. Median length of stay was 3.0 days (IQR=4.0) among low-risk patients and 3.0 days (IQR=5.0) among at-risk patients (p=0.01). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed a significant difference in time to discharge between groups (log-rank p=0.002), although no significant differences were observed in time to readmission (log-rank p=0.45) (figure 1). In adjusted Cox proportional hazards modelling, at-risk alcohol use was associated with a slightly lower hazard of discharge compared with low-risk alcohol use, corresponding to a modestly longer time to discharge (HR=0.92, p=0.041) (online supplemental table 2). Due to limited sample size, we were unable to conduct Cox models for time to death, ED visit or readmission.

Opioid prescribing patterns

Patterns of opioid prescribing were largely similar between alcohol risk groups (table 4). At the time of discharge, 58.3% of low-risk patients and 58.9% of at-risk patients received an opioid prescription (p=0.763). The average number of postoperative opioid prescriptions on discharge was 1.0 for low-risk patients and 1.0 for those in the at-risk group. At 6-month follow-up, 68.8% of low-risk and 70.1% of at-risk patients had received at least one opioid prescription (p=0.495). Patients received a comparable number of prescriptions from surgery to 6 months after discharge, with an average of 1.0 prescriptions for both low-risk and at-risk groups (p=0.076).

Multivariable analyses

Adjusted analyses showed no difference for AUDIT-C categories and the composite outcome (marginal effect: 0.01; 95% CI −0.02 to 0.03). Due to limited event counts, multivariable models estimating average marginal effects could not be reliably estimated for individual complications, mortality, readmission or ED visits (table 5).

While most covariates were not independently associated with outcomes, a subset of variables, including surgical service, comorbidity burden and case status, showed modest associations in select logistic regression models. Selection-correction sensitivity analysis

In the two-step selection-correction sensitivity analysis based on Heckman’s sample selection framework, the first-stage probit model demonstrated that AUDIT-C completion varied significantly by workflow-related and procedure-related characteristics, including surgical service, case status, procedure location and anaesthesia technique (online supplemental table 4). In the second-stage outcome model, the inverse Mills ratio was statistically significant (OR 0.25, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.52; p<0.001), indicating evidence of non-random selection into AUDIT-C screening (online supplemental table 5). After accounting for this selection term, at-risk alcohol use was not significantly associated with the primary composite postoperative outcome compared with low-risk alcohol use (OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.36; p=0.703). These findings were consistent with the primary analysis and suggest that the main finding was robust to this sensitivity analysis, while not eliminating the possibility of residual selection bias.

Feature importance

Feature importance analyses using standardised regression coefficients and permutation methods consistently ranked AUDIT-C as the least informative predictor in the model, with negligible impact on AUC across all models and outcome variables (figure 3). Variables like Elixhauser index, surgical service and case status ranked higher in predictive importance, although none yielded substantial improvement in discrimination.

Exploratory analyses

We conducted a series of exploratory analyses to assess whether the relationship between AUDIT-C alcohol risk and postoperative outcomes varied across demographic or clinical subgroups. Exploratory interaction analyses did not demonstrate statistically significant effect modification by case status, age group or sex in the association between AUDIT-C risk category and the primary composite outcome (online supplemental table 6). The interaction between AUDIT-C risk category and sex approached but did not reach statistical significance (OR 1.62, 95% CI 0.97 to 2.70, p=0.067), and these subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously given limited numbers of at-risk alcohol users.

Results were unchanged for analyses using the three-level AUDIT-C classification (low-risk, moderate-risk and high-risk groups) with no statistically significant dose-response or threshold effect (online supplemental table 7). While not statistically significant, patients who reported lower alcohol use had lower crude rates of the composite outcome compared with those with higher alcohol use: 13.7% in the low-risk group, 15.4% in the moderate-risk group and 16.2% in the high-risk group. In a sensitivity analysis that included patients with an AUDIT-C score of 0 in the low-risk category, postoperative outcome patterns were materially unchanged compared with the primary analysis (online supplemental table 1).

Finally, we examined whether other patient and procedural characteristics meaningfully improved predictive model performance. When added individually to a base logistic regression model for the composite outcome, surgical service, Elixhauser comorbidity index and case status slightly modulated model discrimination (AUC from 0.73 to 0.71). No changes in AUC were observed for AUDIT-C, age, sex, race and ethnicity, insurance, relationship status or preoperative opioid prescription (online supplemental table 8).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of over 30 000 inpatient surgical admissions at a large tertiary academic medical centre, observed AUDIT-C alcohol risk categories measured during inpatient admission were not significantly associated with 30-day postoperative outcomes among patients who completed screening, including surgical complications, ED visits, readmissions or mortality. Among the 9538 patients who completed inpatient alcohol screening during hospitalisation, only 8.4% met criteria for at-risk alcohol use. Further, we observed no clear dose-response relationship or threshold effect when alcohol use was stratified into more granular risk levels. Postoperative opioid prescribing patterns, including both discharge prescribing and prescriptions up to 6 months postsurgery, were similarly unaffected by alcohol risk category. Predictive analyses, including AUC-ROC and feature importance modelling, suggested limited incremental prognostic contribution of observed AUDIT-C categories for short-term postoperative events, whereas association models similarly did not identify a significant relationship between AUDIT-C risk category and the primary composite outcome. While hospital length of stay and time to discharge showed statistically significant differences by alcohol use category, the absolute difference in median days was small and of uncertain clinical relevance. Collectively, under conditions of selective screening, measurement limitations and restricted variance in alcohol risk, AUDIT-C scores, operationalised as observed clinical categories, did not demonstrate meaningful incremental predictive value within standard regression models in this specific clinical context. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that alcohol use is clinically unimportant for surgical risk, but rather as reflecting the challenges of using brief, admission-based screening data for perioperative risk prediction in routine clinical workflows.

Our study had several methodological strengths in examining these questions. The AUDIT-C was implemented as a routine part of the admission navigator in a large academic hospital, allowing us to assess alcohol use screening as it is performed in actual clinical workflows, across diverse surgical services, case urgencies and demographic subgroups. Unlike prior studies relying on preoperative clinic settings or mailed surveys, our analysis captured contemporaneous data from a large, real-world perioperative population.20 22–26 We included both urgent and emergent cases, enhancing the generalisability of findings. The analytic approach was comprehensive, employing regression, predictive modelling, survival analysis and feature importance to explore both predictive utility and implementation variability. We also examined postoperative opioid prescribing, a critical yet understudied outcome in alcohol screening research. Importantly, we attempted to address a known methodological limitation in alcohol-related research by accounting for the J-shaped relationship between alcohol use and health outcomes. Specifically, we excluded patients with an AUDIT-C score of 0 from our primary reference group to mitigate potential confounding due to abstainers with elevated baseline risk. While this strategy may not have fully eliminated bias related to abstention, it represents a methodological advance in acknowledging and adjusting for the complex epidemiologic patterns associated with alcohol use. Despite these strengths, several limitations merit consideration. One major limitation was the substantial proportion of non-responders, approximately 69% of the total surgical cohort, which may introduce bias if the likelihood of screening was associated with both alcohol use behaviours and postoperative outcomes. AUDIT-C completion was not random but varied by demographic and procedural characteristics, suggesting that our findings are based on a selected subset of patients who may not represent the broader surgical population. To further assess this concern, we conducted a two-step selection-correction sensitivity analysis based on Heckman’s sample selection framework. The inverse Mills ratio was statistically significant, indicating evidence of non-random selection into AUDIT-C screening. The direction of the selection term suggested that patients with unmeasured characteristics associated with AUDIT-C completion were less likely to experience the composite postoperative outcome. However, adjustment for the selection term did not materially change the association between AUDIT-C risk category and the primary composite outcome. These findings suggest that selective screening was present and that the primary finding was robust to one reasonable correction for selection, but they should not be interpreted as evidence that selection bias was eliminated. As with any selection-correction approach using observational EHR data, residual selection bias and unmeasured differences between screened and unscreened patients may remain, and the extent to which selection bias contributed to the observed null findings cannot be fully determined. Only a small fraction of screened patients were classified as moderate-risk or high-risk alcohol use (5.1% and 1.4%, respectively), despite US surveys suggesting that 15% and 5% report moderate-risk and high-risk drinking, potentially reducing our ability to detect differences.37 Additionally, reliance on self-reported alcohol use, particularly during hospital admission, introduces well-documented bias due to stigma, illness acuity or inconsistent framing by healthcare providers.38–40 Although recent evidence suggests that AUDIT-C responses demonstrate high test–retest reliability in routine clinical care, reliability and reporting accuracy may differ when screening is conducted during inpatient surgical admission.31 Because AUDIT-C is a brief three-item screening instrument, it may function as an imperfect proxy for the broader latent construct of alcohol-related surgical risk. In this study, AUDIT-C was evaluated as an observed clinical screening measure because that is how it is used in routine inpatient workflows. However, reporting bias, contextual effects during hospitalisation and differential under-reporting among higher-risk patients may have introduced measurement error or attenuation bias, limiting our ability to detect non-linear, threshold or subgroup-specific associations. Future studies with item-level AUDIT-C data could use latent-variable or item-response approaches to further evaluate item discrimination, measurement error and whether latent alcohol-risk constructs predict perioperative outcomes beyond observed total scores or categorical thresholds. Furthermore, while we evaluated opioid prescribing, our inability to calculate opioid morphine equivalents, capture pain intensity or examine actual consumption limits the interpretation of those results. Alcohol use was measured at a single time point during hospital admission, and the analysis could not account for longitudinal changes in alcohol consumption or time-varying clinical factors such as in-hospital interventions, medication use and illness severity. Future studies incorporating repeated or longitudinal assessments of alcohol use may provide a more complete understanding of how time-varying exposure influences perioperative outcomes. Prior research has shown that alcohol-related interventions, particularly when embedded within perioperative workflows, can reduce complications and improve recovery outcomes.16 41 42

Despite robust evidence linking heavy alcohol use to impaired healing, bleeding, infection and cardiovascular instability, our study did not observe a statistically or clinically significant difference in outcomes between low-risk and at-risk groups. Although prior research reported strong associations between alcohol misuse and surgical complications, our findings serve to contextualise instead of conflict with that body of work. Prior studies primarily relied on VHA cohorts, which often involve older, predominantly male populations undergoing elective surgeries with standardised preoperative protocols and follow-up.18 19 These studies also tend to employ longer screening instruments or structured interviews, which provide a richer and potentially more accurate assessment of alcohol use. In contrast, our study focused on a high-volume, heterogeneous surgical population with varying admission urgencies and limited opportunities for preoperative risk assessment.20 22–26 Additionally, AUDIT-C screening in our study was conducted during inpatient admission rather than during a longer preoperative exposure window, meaning that the measure should be interpreted as a pragmatic clinical screening tool rather than a complete assessment of longitudinal alcohol exposure relevant to surgical risk. The AUDIT-C was used as a brief, routine screen embedded in EHR workflows, where time constraints, documentation burden and workflow variability likely impacted both completion and accuracy. Moreover, we used sex-specific AUDIT-C thresholds per national guidelines, which may classify risk differently than uniform cutoffs.22 29 32 33 By adopting a real-world, pragmatic lens, our study highlights that the transition from research-grade screening tools to point-of-care implementation may influence their observed performance in predictive models. These findings add to the literature by showing that even well-validated tools like AUDIT-C may not function equally across clinical contexts, especially when used in high-throughput environments with inconsistent screening practices or limited follow-through.

The lack of observed associations across major outcomes warrants careful interpretation. The lack of significant associations persisted across subgroup and interaction analyses stratified by sex, age and case status. However, these analyses were exploratory and intentionally limited to clinically plausible comparisons to avoid extensive post hoc testing and the relatively small number of at-risk alcohol users may have limited power to detect modest subgroup-specific effects. Postoperative opioid prescribing also did not differ by alcohol risk category, with both low-risk and at-risk groups receiving a median of 1.0 prescription from discharge to 6 months. This lack of difference may reflect limitations in the data, including the inability to assess total opioid dosage, prescription duration or pain intensity. Interestingly, hospital length of stay differed slightly between alcohol risk groups, with median stays of 3.0 days for both groups but a broader IQR among at-risk patients. This reached statistical significance (p=0.01), and time-to-discharge analysis via Kaplan-Meier survival curves also revealed a significant difference (log-rank p=0.002). In adjusted Cox proportional hazards modelling, at-risk alcohol use was associated with a slightly lower hazard of discharge compared with low-risk alcohol use, corresponding to a modestly longer time to discharge (HR=0.92, p=0.041). These findings suggest a modest association between alcohol use and length of hospitalisation, although the effect size is small and of uncertain clinical relevance. It is worth noting, however, that some crude comparisons suggested slightly higher rates of adverse outcomes in low-risk versus at-risk groups; though these differences were not statistically significant and were not consistent across outcomes. This raises the possibility that subtle associations may have been obscured by the limited number of at-risk patients. Feature importance analyses ranked traditional surgical risk factors, such as surgical service, Elixhauser index and case status, above AUDIT-C in terms of predictive value. These variables are well-established predictors of adverse postoperative outcomes, and their prominence further supports the presence of non-response bias in our analysis. Specifically, non-screened patients tended to have higher comorbidity burden and more complex surgical characteristics, meaning that individuals with higher underlying surgical risk were also more likely to lack AUDIT-C data. As a result, the screened cohort may have systematically underrepresented those at greatest risk, diluting the associations between alcohol use and adverse outcomes and biasing our findings toward the null. These findings emphasise the complexity of perioperative risk and suggest that alcohol use was a contributing, but not a determinative, factor in this analysis when modelled alongside a broader clinical picture. Together with the selection-correction sensitivity analysis, these patterns suggest that AUDIT-C data generated through routine inpatient workflows should be interpreted as selectively observed clinical screening information rather than as complete population-level alcohol exposure data. This limits generalisability and may partly explain why expected relationships between alcohol risk and postoperative outcomes were not observed. It is also possible that alcohol-related intervention and follow-up, rather than screening alone, led to improved care that equalised surgical outcomes. If at-risk patients identified by the AUDIT-C did not consistently receive appropriate interventions, such as addiction medicine referrals or perioperative management adjustments, then the screening tool’s potential clinical impact may not have been fully realised. Conversely, some at-risk patients may have received targeted interventions that successfully mitigated their risk, while lower-risk patients may have received no intervention or standard care, thereby narrowing or masking differences in outcomes between groups. In this context, the lack of observed associations may reflect variation in how screening results were acted on, rather than a failure of the screening tool itself to identify clinically meaningful risk. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and may reflect limitations of implementation, measurement and sample composition rather than absence of alcohol-related surgical risk. Another key consideration is the influence of the J-shaped relationship between alcohol use and health outcomes. While we excluded patients with an AUDIT-C score of 0 from our primary low-risk reference group based on prior evidence of increased risk among abstainers in observational alcohol epidemiology, the precise inflection point of this curve remains uncertain.43 44 Some studies suggest the nadir of risk may occur at an AUDIT-C score of 1 or even higher, raising the possibility that our reclassification strategy did not fully account for this complexity.45 46 Many individuals abstain due to comorbidities, medication interactions, prior substance use disorders or other contraindications, factors not easily captured in structured EHR data. Thus, abstention may reflect a broad range of clinical realities rather than uniformly lower risk. Although we performed sensitivity analyses including abstainers within the low-risk category and observed similar results, this heterogeneity may still complicate interpretation of alcohol-related risk in observational datasets. Patients’ diverse motivations for low or no alcohol use further limit our ability to stratify them accurately. Moreover, key behavioural dimensions, such as binge drinking, intoxication patterns and withdrawal history, can significantly modify perioperative risk but are not captured by the AUDIT-C alone.47 48 Similarly, other lifestyle-related factors that may influence postoperative outcomes, including smoking, physical inactivity and nutritional status, were not consistently available in structured EHR fields and therefore could not be included in our analyses. As a result, relying solely on the AUDIT-C may obscure more nuanced drinking behaviours and hinder our ability to fully distinguish between truly low-risk and higher-risk abstaining patients. Individual parts of the composite adverse event may represent clinically distinct events that arise through different mechanisms during postoperative recovery. Although we attempted to examine individual outcomes separately, limited event counts precluded reliable multivariable modelling for several complication-specific and mortality outcomes. Similarly, although sensitivity analyses using more granular AUDIT-C categories did not reveal a clear dose-response or threshold pattern, the relatively small number of moderate-risk and high-risk alcohol users limited our ability to evaluate more complex non-linear or heterogeneous risk structures using approaches such as LOESS, spline-based regression or mixture modelling. A prior study that demonstrated significant associations between unhealthy alcohol use and hospital admissions analysed cohorts of over 3 00 000 patients with a much higher representation of at-risk alcohol users.45 By contrast, our cohort of at-risk alcohol users may not have captured enough patients to detect clinically meaningful differences.

The implications of these findings are multifaceted and bear relevance for clinicians, hospital administrators and health policy leaders. Alcohol use is known to impair immune function, delay wound healing, increase bleeding risk and contribute to cardiovascular instability; all factors that can complicate surgical recovery. This study does not argue against the importance of identifying hazardous alcohol use in surgical populations but rather underscores the need to refine how, when and why we screen. The current analysis, with many persons not undergoing screening, may be insufficiently sensitive, consistent or integrated to yield actionable insights. AUDIT-C, while brief and validated, may not capture the full spectrum of alcohol-related risk, especially in time-limited inpatient workflows. More complete screening implementation may provide more informative data to identify persons at risk of poor postoperative outcomes. The clinical value of AUDIT-C screening may therefore depend heavily on the completion rates for its implementation. For example, AUDIT-C screening may be more valuable to identify patients at risk of alcohol withdrawal, guiding anaesthesia management or initiating behavioural health referrals, all important outcomes not captured in our models. It may also be more appropriate in certain specialties (eg, trauma, obstetrics and gynaecology) or for emergent/urgent admissions, where rapid, high-level screening is necessary. Importantly, our findings reveal variability in screening implementation across surgical services, with lower completion rates among outpatient and elective cases. These patterns introduce systematic selection bias that likely diluted the predictive value of AUDIT-C scores and limited the generalisability of our findings. Other predictors, like comorbidity burden and case status, were more closely associated with outcomes, reinforcing that alcohol use cannot be interpreted in isolation and must be contextualised within a broader framework of surgical risk. Thus, while AUDIT-C may not independently predict postoperative complications, it could still add value when integrated into a multidimensional framework and linked to intervention pathways. Future work should prioritise not only expanding and standardising alcohol screening across diverse surgical populations, but also ensuring that positive screens are meaningfully connected to perioperative risk mitigation and behavioural health interventions. AUDIT-C screening could potentially serve as a valuable integration point for triggering targeted behavioural and medical interventions, which may unlock the clinical value of alcohol risk identification in surgical settings. Most importantly, future studies should focus on enhancing the granularity, consistency and clinical integration of alcohol screening in surgical care. First, larger studies with enriched cohorts of at-risk alcohol users, potentially recruited across multiple institutions, are needed to clarify whether alcohol use interacts with comorbidities like liver disease, depression or polysubstance use to influence surgical outcomes. Incorporating data from toxicology screening, behavioural health assessments or unstructured clinical notes could yield a more nuanced understanding of patient risk. Second, researchers should examine postoperative pain, recovery and opioid-related outcomes with greater precision, accounting for total opioid dosages, duration and functional recovery measures. Understanding whether alcohol use modifies pain perception, medication needs or patient-reported outcomes could inform more tailored perioperative management strategies. Finally, implementation science approaches are needed to evaluate how AUDIT-C screening can be systematically deployed, interpreted and acted on in perioperative workflows. Screening tools must be coupled with provider training, workflow integration and clear pathways for intervention. Additionally, health systems should consider embedding alcohol screening within broader risk stratification frameworks that incorporate multiple domains, physical health, mental health, substance use and social determinants. Overall, while observed AUDIT-C risk categories did not demonstrate meaningful incremental predictive value in this selectively screened cohort, the selection-correction sensitivity analysis provides evidence that AUDIT-C completion was non-random and that the primary finding was robust to one reasonable correction for selection. However, this analysis does not eliminate the possibility of residual selection bias, and these findings should be interpreted within the context of real-world screening implementation rather than as definitive evidence regarding alcohol-related surgical risk. This analysis raises broader questions about the role, timing and implementation of alcohol screening in perioperative care. Still, when appropriately implemented as part of an integrated, patient-centred system, alcohol screening remains a promising lever for improving surgical equity, safety and personalisation.

Conclusions

Observed AUDIT-C risk category measured during hospital admission was not significantly associated with short-term postoperative complications, ED visits, readmissions, mortality or postoperative opioid prescribing among patients who completed screening. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as selective screening, reliance on self-reported alcohol use and the use of a single admission-based screening measure may have attenuated observable associations. Importantly, these results do not imply that alcohol use is unimportant in surgical risk but rather highlight the challenges of translating validated screening tools into real-world perioperative practice. Future research should focus on improving the consistency and timing of alcohol screening, incorporating more granular measures of alcohol exposure and evaluating whether linking screening results to targeted perioperative interventions can better identify and mitigate alcohol-related surgical risk. When integrated into broader perioperative risk assessment and care pathways, alcohol screening may still serve an important role in improving surgical safety and patient-centred care.
