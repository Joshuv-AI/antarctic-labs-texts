The role of protein embeddings for protein–protein interaction prediction with graph neural networks
Authors: Laura Balbi, Rita T Sousa, Pedro Cotovio, Catia Pesquita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraph neural networks are a natural fit for protein–protein interaction (PPI) prediction because they exploit the graph structure of interactomes, yet it remains unclear how much they benefit from protein-level representations and which sources of protein information matter most. We address this by surveying and organizing the field into a coherent taxonomy that describes three paradigms: network-level approaches (shallow and deep graph representation learning), protein-level approaches (sequence, structure, and function), and hybrid methods that combine both paradigms—providing a cohesive synthesis that highlights their respective strengths, complementarities, and tradeoffs. We developed a unified framework to systematically compare these paradigms that incorporates multiple representative methods and evaluates them on two widely used benchmarks: OGBl-ppa and HuRI. Our results indicate that hybrid methods have improved performance, with function-driven representations emerging as the most informative protein-level representations for PPI prediction. This work provides a taxonomical map of the field, a reproducible framework for evaluating representative methods across paradigms, and practical guidance for future PPI prediction efforts.Keywords: protein–protein interaction, PPI networks, protein language models, graph neural networks

Introduction

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have become a powerful tool for predicting protein–protein interactions (PPIs)—fundamental to almost all biological processes (BP) yet challenging to identify [1]—but it remains unclear how their performance is impacted by incorporating protein-level information into their node representations.

Over the past decades, machine learning (ML) approaches have been increasingly employed to predict PPIs, offering a scalable, efficient, and cost-effective alternative to traditional experimental methods [2–6]. A variety of methods have demonstrated success for the task: shallow graph representation learning (GRL) methods [7, 8], convolutional- and recurrent neural network-based architectures [9, 10], generative adversarial networks (GANs) [11], and GNNs such as the heterogeneous motif GNN [12] and higher-order graph convolutional networks (GCNs) [13].

Most approaches fall under one of two fundamental paradigms: protein-oriented methods, which take as input features of individual proteins, and network-oriented methods, which take as input a PPI network [14].

The protein-oriented paradigm is based on the notion that protein features, such as their sequence or structure, can be used to learn a model for PPI prediction. It typically leverages deep learning techniques to extract features that are used as rich, high-dimensional representations of proteins for downstream PPI prediction tasks, often outperforming traditional handcrafted features by capturing more nuanced and biologically relevant information [15].

In contrast, the network-oriented paradigm leverages the global organization of PPI networks, where proteins are represented as nodes and interactions as edges, by following the “guilty-by-association” principle [16, 17] to learn PPI models based on the topological properties of the network. Notably GRL has been successfully applied to various tasks based on PPI networks [18, 19] and, in fact, graphs are fast becoming the major paradigm for modeling, learning [20, 21], and reasoning [22] in biomedical systems [23].

The two paradigms have the potential to complement each other when combined to give a more holistic view of proteins [24], with protein-oriented methods capturing the intrinsic properties of proteins and network-oriented methods modeling the extrinsic factors and relational context where they operate. While recent methods have explored this complementarity more explicitly, there is still a lack of a systematic survey of these methods, which is critical to fully understand their respective tradeoffs, limitations, and synergistic potential in advancing PPI prediction.

To elucidate the impact of the paradigms and of different protein representation approaches in PPI prediction, this work contributes the following:

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: We begin by introducing key concepts and surveying the state of the art in protein and PPI network representation learning. We then review representation learning approaches applied to PPI prediction. Next, we present a comparative analysis of representative protein representations coupled with a GNN-based network representation learning approach, trained on two PPI prediction tasks using the OGBl-ppa [41] and HuRI [42] benchmarks. Finally, we contextualize the experimental findings within the framework of the survey, discussing network versus protein representations, protein-level information sources, and hybrid representation setups’ trade-offs, to distill practical guidance for PPI prediction.

A primer on representation learning for protein–protein interaction prediction

This section introduces methods and techniques employed by PPI prediction methods, organized under a taxonomy with two overarching paradigms: network-level representation approaches, which explore PPI network topology, and protein-level representation learning approaches that model the intrinsic properties of proteins.

Network representation learning

One of the most effective ways to represent interactions in a PPI network is through graph-based representations, which allow the study of topological properties that provide insights into biological functions and the uncovering of functional relationships and prediction of disease-associated proteins [43, 44].

A PPI network is typically represented as an undirected graph where is the set of vertices that correspond to individual proteins and is the set of edges that connect proteins for which there is a known interaction [45, 46]. PPI networks can be homogeneous or heterogeneous graphs, with different types of edges being established between proteins according to the confidence or type of evidence used to determine the interaction.

Since PPI networks are inherently complex and high-dimensional, GRL techniques help encode these networks into lower-dimensional vector spaces, making the networks machine-readable and suitable to be used in ML while preserving their structural and functional properties [47]. GRL seeks to learn meaningful representations of graph components (nodes, edges, or subgraphs) in a low-dimensional vector space in a way that captures their structural and relational properties [48, 49]. In the context of PPI network-based problems, these methods support node classification tasks (predicting protein functions or properties), link prediction tasks (i.e. PPI prediction), and graph classification (e.g. identifying functional modules or specific interaction patterns and subgraphs).

Among these, link prediction is the task that directly addresses the discovery of new interactions by finding missing edges between vertices (proteins) in a PPI graph.

The prediction of novel interactions is done by ranking all non-existent edges by and considering the top k scoring pairs as candidate PPIs.

On the other hand, link prediction can also be handled as a classification problem that treats each candidate pair as an instance in a binary classification task. GRL methods handle the link prediction problem by embedding network elements into continuous vector spaces that capture the local and global interaction contexts to leverage for predicting novel interactions. Under this paradigm, each protein is mapped via to a vector that captures its interaction context, and these embeddings can serve as inputs for the link prediction scoring functions.

These methods can be broadly grouped into two main categories [23, 50, 51]: traditional shallow methods that generate fixed and task-agnostic representations via unsupervised objectives [8, 52]; and deep methods [53, 54] that leverage neural architectures for jointly learning representations and task-specific predictive models through message-passing mechanisms that aggregate multi-hop neighborhood information and integrate input node features directly into the representations’ space. Due to this integration, they can explore rich protein-level features, such as sequence, structural, or functional-based embeddings, for a more accurate and biologically informed PPI prediction [20, 55–61].

Graph embedding methods

Graph embedding methods compute low-dimensional vector representations for each node by factorizing graph proximity measures or optimizing neighborhood preservation objectives. They are considered shallow because they do not incorporate node attributes or perform iterative, multi-hop feature aggregation, and they learn task-agnostic representations independently from downstream task supervision.

Random-walk–based methods that generate node sequences via random walk sampling strategies and then apply word-embedding objectives to learn node vectors from the sequences. DeepWalk [48] popularized this idea by running short truncated random walks to collect node “contexts” and then training vectors with a SkipGram objective as in [62]. The result favors nodes that frequently co-occur within the same walk windows, yielding embeddings that emphasize homophily and community structure. Node2Vec [8] keeps the same learn-from-sequences recipe but biases the walks themselves. Two hyperparameters smoothly interpolate between breadth-first–like exploration (staying near the source to encode local neighborhoods) and depth-first–like trajectories (venturing further to capture structural roles). Proximity-preserving methods that explicitly model first- and second-order proximities in the graph through matrix factorization or negative-sampling objectives. LINE [52] separates what it means for two nodes to be close: first-order proximity captures the direct strength of an edge, while second-order proximity captures similarity of neighborhoods. Training optimizes two complementary objectives, often with negative sampling for scalability, and the final embedding can concatenate the two parts. LINE is fast, memory-efficient, and well-suited to very large, possibly weighted networks where immediate and neighborhood signals are both informative. GraRep [63] pushes beyond local neighborhoods by factorizing multi-step (e.g. -hop) co-occurrence statistics derived from random walks. By learning a set of embeddings over increasing path lengths and concatenating them, GraRep captures both local and global regularities.

Asymmetry-aware and dynamic methods that incorporate strategies for handling asymmetric transitivity in directed graphs or for handling dynamic graphs that account for the evolution of PPI networks over time, as new interactions are discovered or as interaction patterns shift across different cellular conditions. HOPE [64] targets directed graphs by factorizing high-order proximity measures (e.g. Katz index) that preserve asymmetric transitivity. This yields separate source and target representations so that can differ from , which is crucial in settings like citation or follower networks. Dynamic methods instead address evolving graphs. DynGEM [65] incrementally updates embeddings with an autoencoder while penalizing large deviations between consecutive time steps, maintaining stability as edges appear or vanish. tNodeEmbed [66] follows a snapshot-by-snapshot reconstruction view with an explicit temporal smoothness regularizer, balancing fidelity to each snapshot’s adjacency with consistency over time.

Overall, these shallow approaches are computationally efficient and can scale to large networks [8, 66–68], but—unlike GNNs—they do not leverage node attribute information or adaptively refine embeddings via multi-hop feature aggregation, which limits their ability to adapt embeddings to task-specific supervision [69].

Graph neural networks

GNNs are a general formalism first proposed by Scarselli et al. [70] for deep neural networks that take graph data as input. This approach was developed as a solution to the limitations of shallow embedding methods, whilst simultaneously opening the door to the challenge of handling graph-based data [71].

To address this, the original GNN architecture has since seen several variations [37, 38, 72], all of which share a defining feature: the use of a neural message-passing mechanism where vector messages are exchanged and aggregated between nodes, and updated using neural networks [73]. By aggregating and updating information across arbitrary graph topologies, GNNs effectively generalize functions to complex, non-Euclidean domains such as the relational structures inherent to graphs and PPI networks.

Since every message-passing iteration updates a hidden embedding that corresponds to a given node according to the information aggregated from the node’s neighborhood. This ensures that the final embeddings of each node incorporate both structural and feature-based information from the entire k-hop neighborhood of the node. Protein representations

Protein information and knowledge is kept in biomedical databases designed for human curation and scientific exchange. Protein sequence data are stored as variable-length strings or multiple sequence alignments; structures as irregular clouds of atoms with arbitrary orientation; and protein functionality as multi-label hierarchical annotations to ontologies (i.e. GO). None of these formats are directly learnable by standard ML. Hence, the need for representation learning methods [75] that translate these heterogeneous and complex data formats into learned numerical encodings that are ML-readable while preserving the biological meaning of protein data [76–78]. In the subsections that follow, we detail these representation learning methods, following the categorization in Section “A primer on representation learning for protein–protein interaction prediction” based on protein information sources (i.e. sequence, structure, and function).

Protein sequence representation

Proteins’ aminoacid sequences encode biochemical information such as conserved interaction motifs, domain patterns, and co-evolutionary signals that may help to understand a protein’s interaction affinity. Moreover, the vast amount of sequencing data in biological repositories provides a large training bed for PPI-based ML methods.

Building on these observations, the evolution of sequence-based approaches has moved from early convolutional [79]—i.e. the first AlphaFold [80]—and recurrent neural networks [81–84] to transformer-based architectures [85], and more recently to protein language models [25, 86] that pretrain transformers on massive amounts of unlabeled sequences using self-supervised objectives to learn contextual protein sequence representations [87].

A PLM is an ML approach that applies a probability distribution over sequences of aminoacids to predict the most likely residue to follow based on the preceding sequence [88]. These models are trained in an unsupervised manner with protein sequencing data—from databases like the UniProt [89], which contains over 60 million protein sequences—with an end-goal of enhancing functional protein design and engineering strategies [27, 80, 90].

Current PLMs’ transformer-based architectures leverage self-attention mechanisms [91] to capture long-range dependencies within protein sequences by accessing their previous states, which enables deeper insights into a protein’s biochemical and functional properties.

The first transformer-based traditional PLMs to emerge were the ESM-1 (Evolutionary Scale Modeling) series PLM [86], and the TAPE [92] and ProtBERT [25] models, adapted from the bidirectional LLM BERT [93] to learn meaningful protein representations.

Building on these, more advanced PLMs emerged, including the ProtTrans [94], based on the Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer (T5) [95], and ESM-2 [96], a much larger, more parameterized version of its predecessor ESM-1, that leveraged the RoPE [97] positional encoding embeddings to allow for an arbitrary length of protein sequences. The ProGen2 [27] followed with a similar RoPE embedding approach and an architectural variant of the original transformer that separates the attention networks from the feedforward network to allow for parallel computing.

While PLMs have traditionally been trained over protein sequence databases for folding prediction, their applicability for protein function and PPI prediction was hampered by the fact that both function and interactions are inherently tied to a protein’s 3D structure [98], and early PLMs lacked any means to encode the spatial or conformational information of protein structure.

Protein structure representation

Protein structural information can be leveraged to capture surface properties (e.g. binding and allostery) and other spatial features relevant for PPI prediction that are not evident from sequence alone.

The advent of high-accuracy structure prediction and the growth of structural repositories have made such information broadly available at the proteome scale, motivating structural representations amenable to learning for PPI tasks [80].

A common RL strategy is to encode a protein as a graph where residues are nodes and edges reflect the bonds or distances between them, to capture local and global protein geometry.

The task of Inverse protein folding (IF) aims to predict a protein sequence from backbone coordinates, and has long served as a canonical setting to test the mentioned RL strategies [99].

Current work in structure representation learning for IF can be organized by the primary mechanism used to model protein structure: with (i) GNNs’ message-passing, (ii) PLM encoder–decoder models adapted to sequence prediction, and (iii) Graph Transformer-based models. Early efforts towards the IF problem fell under the first category, by employing GNNs over protein structure graphs (e.g. SPIN2 [100] and ProteinMPNN [101]). With the advent of protein language models, IF was reformulated towards the second type of approaches, which use transformer encoder–decoders that generate sequences conditioned on structure, e.g. ESM-IF1 [28], the first PLM adapted to IF. There have been approaches, albeit scarce, towards discretizing proteins’ 3D coordinates into token sequences, instead of projecting them into graphs, for encoding with a fine-tuned structure-to-sequence translation ProtT5 PLM [29].

The final and most recent category, of graph Transformer-based models, covers approaches that replace GNNs’ message-passing with a global attention mechanism over proteins’ structure graphs. PiFold [30] couples a Graph Transformer (GTN) [102] to extract atom-aware residue features with PiGNN layers that capture multi-scale residue interactions. Similarly, the work of Zhou et al. [103] represents structures as proximity graphs using a GTN with an entropy-based refinement to weight and fuse model predictions.

Across all of these categories, backbone geometry is mapped to embeddings that encode local frames and global context; GNNs emphasize localized geometric aggregation, PLM-based models add sequence-generation objectives conditioned on structure, and GTNs provide global attention-based context over the protein residues graph (see Fig. 2). All three directions enable long-range dependency modeling for IF and downstream PPI tasks.

Protein function representation

Proteins that are functionally related, whether by participating in the same BP and pathways or performing complementary functional roles, frequently co-occur in interaction networks. Therefore, quantifying proteins’ functional similarity provides a strong signal across biomedical tasks, especially PPI prediction. Several knowledge bases support the annotation of proteins to structured biomedical concepts [36, 89, 104, 105]. In practice, most of these resources can either be expressed as a e.g. (KG) or serve as sources from which a KG can be constructed. Conceptually, a KG is a machine-interpretable representation whose purpose is to integrate real-world entities and the relationships between them to enable linking, querying, and reasoning over knowledge. Formally, a KG is a labeled, directed graph whose edges are typed triples ; with nodes denoting entities (e.g. proteins, concepts) and relations encoding semantic relationships (e.g. “is_a,” “part_of”), and with being the set of asserted facts.

The most widely used resource on biological knowledge is the GO [36], a structured, standardized representation that provides over 50 000 classes to describe proteins based on three aspects: the BP they participate in, the molecular functions they execute, and the cellular components they are located in [106]. It can be represented as a graph, with classes as nodes and different types of relationships between GO classes (e.g. ; ; ; ; and ) as edges [36]. The annotation of proteins to their functional aspects on the GO [107] results in a biomedical KG [108] from which functional-based representations can be extracted [109–111]. Knowledge graph representation learning (KGRL) offers a powerful approach to embedding this functional knowledge [112]. KGRL methods embed entities and relations into a continuous low-dimensional vector space that preserves positionality and connectivity information but also KG semantics, while bridging KG-structured data and traditional ML methods.

The state-of-the-art in KGRL can be broadly categorized into four groups: (i) translation-based; (ii) semantic matching-based; (iii) random-walk-based; and (iv) neural-based methods.

Neural network-based methods (e.g. GCNs and GATs) generate deeper representations based on the learning of graph structure to solve a specific task, in opposition to the latter methods, which are shallow and mainly use simple linear transformations. Graph-based networks have been popular in KGRL due to accounting for both structure and node features in the representation learning process by pooling representations of node local neighborhoods in the KG [50, 70], under the assumption that connectivity and proximity in the KG reflect similarity. These methods exploit both structural and semantic relations and properties of a KG, which differentiate them from GRL neural methods that focus on capturing graph structure/topology and on propagating node features over local graph structures under the assumption of similarity between neighbors.

Protein–protein interaction prediction

To provide a thorough overview of state-of-the-art representation learning approaches for PPI prediction, we surveyed over 250 peer-reviewed studies published through 2025 identified from the top results of querying the Google Scholar academic search engine (accessed in July 2025) with keyword “PPI prediction” alone and in combination with “sequence,” “structure,” and “functional annotations” or “function.” The publications were screened and >50 representative works were organized into the taxonomy established in the previous section: (i) network-level methods, subdivided into shallow and GNN-based approaches; (ii) protein-level methods that operate solely on intrinsic representations, grouped by type of representation, i.e. sequence, structure, or function, or categorized as multimodal approaches if they combine more than one representation; and (iii) hybrid methods that integrate protein- and network-level information for PPI prediction.

Graph embedding-based network representations

Random-walk-based graph embeddings [7, 8, 122–124] are widely used to score missing PPIs. While traditional methods treat the interactome as a homogeneous graph and ignore cross-type biology, recent works have embedded heterogeneous molecular networks that exploit system-level context and interaction types for PPI prediction [125].

Additionally, temporal aspects of PPIs have been considered in dynamic network embedding frameworks such as temporal embeddings [66, 68] for learning time-evolving representations that reflect how protein interactions may change under different cellular conditions or stages of development.

Graph neural network representations

Foundational studies such as [126] offered structural and evolutionary evidence that proteins interact not merely based on similarity but through complex network-based relationships and offered a strategy that extends on the triadic closure principle of how nodes with mutual neighbors are likely to be connected to learn paths of length three to guide PPI prediction.

Building upon such findings, the Open Graph Benchmark (OGB) project released a multi-species PPI prediction task as a benchmark, with a large undirected protein–protein association graph constructed from STRING, where nodes are proteins from 58 species, and edges indicate biologically meaningful associations (physical interaction, co-expression, homology, and genomic neighborhood). In their benchmark paper [41], the OGB team evaluated standard GNN baselines (e.g. GCN and GraphSAGE) over the task. They used a one-hot species identifier as a handcrafted species-related feature to be leveraged during message-passing over the network, and scored candidate PPIs with simple dot-product or bilinear decoders. Subsequent PPI-oriented have largely standardized on this task and setup [39, 127].

Beyond the OGBl-ppa task, a range of more complex methods have since been developed to tackle specific challenges of PPI network representation, pursuing three complementary directions: (i) Approaches that predict missing PPIs by reconstructing the interaction signal, such as inductive matrix completion methods that estimate a PPI probability matrix [128], and graph autoencoder variants for PPI [129, 130]. (ii) Works using generative models to synthesize or score plausible interactions, such as a topology-conditioned GAN that learns to generate candidate PPIs [11], or a transformer-based model that linearizes PPI graphs and applies transformer sequence modeling to learn sequence patterns over walks [131]. (iii) Approaches that focus on the issue of structured label dependencies in PPI networks, such as multi-granularity encoders that aggregate signals from local motifs to larger enclosing subgraphs to capture hidden patterns [132]. A different approach has been to build both a protein graph and a label graph to co-model protein relations and label co-occurrence [133].

Overall, network-based PPI prediction has evolved from simple topological heuristics and standard GNNs to more expressive graph representation methods that leverage network connectivity and model higher-order structural and label dependencies, which reflects a shift in focus towards the relational modeling of PPI networks.

Protein sequence representations

PPIs are closely linked to evolutionary signals in protein sequences, such as conserved motifs and domains indicative of potential interactions. Traditional methods have exploited this relationship using handcrafted sequence features [166] or evolutionary information from PSSMs [167–169] and MSA-derived profiles [80, 170], but these approaches often failed to capture long-range dependencies and contextual information within sequences.

Subsequent approaches replaced manual features with task-specific encoders, be it multi-scale CNNs for cross-species transfer [134]; Siamese Residual RCNNs to learn pairwise similarity directly [9]; RCNNs with ordinal regression to predict calibrated interaction confidence [135]; and multi-branch CNNs to capture diverse sequence cues [136]. Subsequent hybrids added self-attention [137] or coupled a CNN–BiLSTM with attention [138] to extend the receptive field. Yet, these models were still trained from scratch on limited labelled data.

The field then shifted PLMs as powerful tools using large-scale pretraining to supply rich sequence representations that capture not only local and global sequence patterns but also evolutionary and functional information through the learning of long-term dependencies [171] for informative protein feature extraction [172].

In this research line, works diverge along: (i) pairwise classifiers on PLM embeddings (fine-tuned MP-BERT within MPB-PPI [140]; xCAPT5 with Siamese pooling and wide convolutions [143]; LPBERT with local/global encoders [144]); and (ii) protein representation pooling from internal attention states (Pool PaRTI, PageRank-weighted pooling over ESM-2 [142]; TUnA, adding spectral-normalized neural Gaussian processes to temper overconfidence [141]; AlphaFold2 over paired MSAs with fold-and-dock strategy to evaluate PPIs [139]).

Protein structure representations

Under this paradigm, two types of approaches have emerged. The first type covers protein-geometry-as-graph encoders that represent and learn over a protein’s structure as a residue graph [21]. For example, SpatialPPIv2 [145] employs a GAT over a protein residue graph where residues’ features are of an LM’s word embedding encoding of their position; similarly, Si et al. encode structures with ESM-IF1 [28] embeddings (augmented with MSA features) and learn on geometric graphs with a residual module for PPI prediction [146]. These graph-based approaches natively handle variable size, respect spatial neighborhoods, and localize interface signals.

The second type uses structure-aware PLMs as feature extractors. Here, the structure signal enters via pretrained representations rather than explicit geometric graphs. Representative models are the inverse-folding PLM MIF-ST [147] and SaProt [148], a PLM trained on large structural corpora whose representations outperformed MIF-ST over the well-known HumanPPI benchmark [173].

SpatialPPI [149] combines a variant of the AlphaFold2 for protein structure prediction, the AlphaFold Multimer, with 3D convolutional networks to represent proteins’ 3D structures for 3D PPI prediction.

Protein function representations

Recent approaches have leveraged GO functional annotations for PPI prediction [150, 151, 154–156, 174], recognizing their value in providing detailed semantic descriptions of proteins. Early methods used semantic similarity measures between GO terms [150, 151, 156], where similarity scores were used as features for ML models to predict PPIs. The hierarchical structure of GO has enabled embedding-based approaches to integrate functional annotations with PPI data. For example, OPA2Vec [152] combines ontological structure and textual definitions into embeddings, EL [153] models annotations using EL++ semantics, TrueWalks uses embeddings based on protein positive and negative functional annotations [155], and HiG2Vec [157] uses hyperbolic embeddings of GO. GNN-based methods such as DeepViral [158] learn end-to-end over protein–GO edges, while others [159] generate protein embeddings by pooling term embeddings from a GO KG.

Protein multimodal representations

Previous sections have covered how each type of protein representation captures different facets of protein behavior that contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of their interactions, and together they have the potential to learn a richer portrait than a single representation alone. Recent works have proposed hybrid approaches that explore this for PPI prediction.

One research line fuses sequence with function representations, with works such as PPI-MetaGO [160], that ensembles multiple learners over a hybrid of sequence features and GO-based semantic similarity to exploit the intuition that functionally similar proteins tend to interact. Along the same axis, Zhao et al. [161] encode GO annotations with a PLM (NCBI-blueBERT [175]) and sequences with an RNN, and then fuse both views to predict PPIs.

A second strategy has been to couple function and structure representations. OntoProtein [162] integrates GO KG embeddings into a contrastive pipeline with a sequence representation encoder PLM, while GOProteinGNN [163] refines PLM-derived structural embeddings via a GNN knowledge-injection module that propagates signals over the protein–GO graph.

A final paradigm for fusing representations involves combining sequence and structure representations. For example, TAGPPI [164] extracts sequence features with a CNN and structural cues with a GAT run on contact maps derived from AlphaFold, learning complementary interface signals. Similarly, ProBASS [165] pairs the ESM-2 PLM for sequence representation learning with the ESM-IF1 for structure representations, demonstrating gains from jointly leveraging learned protein sequence and structure priors.

Overall, these works show a clear shift in protein-level PPI prediction from handcrafted and annotation-based features to learned representations that capture richer biological context. Sequence-based methods have advanced towards neural and PLM-based embeddings, structural methods towards richer 3D-aware representations through graphs or structure-aware pretraining, and function-based methods towards ontology-aware embedding and graph-based models that better exploit the semantics of functional annotations. This consensus around learned embedding representations as the state-of-the-art approach for modeling proteins reflects their ability to encode complex biological information in a unified and expressive representation for downstream use in PPI prediction, in particular as node features for GNNs.

Combining protein and protein–protein interaction network representations for protein–protein interaction prediction

Integrating protein representations with network-level information can allow models to make more precise and context-aware predictions. Recent studies have leveraged publicly available biomedical data [176–178] to derive such protein representations and combine them with network features for enhanced PPI prediction (see Table 3 for an overview).

GNN-based models have been particularly effective in capturing topological properties of PPI networks [39, 194], and recent works have sought to incorporate additional biological information to enrich their representations [186, 189, 193, 195]. Existing approaches broadly fall into three themes: (i) GNNs that enrich message-passing with biological features; (ii) works that separate protein and network representations via disconnected learning objectives; and (iii) reconstruction-driven and generative models that denoise or assign PPIs via graph auto-encoding or similar strategies.

Works that fall under the first theme build on traditional GNNs by introducing protein representations as meaningful node features for the message-passing mechanism to model directly into the learned model [39, 194]. For example, Qiu and Chen [188] use R-GCNs to model different types, directed interactions while injecting functional knowledge as protein features. Similarly, Kang et al. [183] couple sequence representations from the ESM-1b PLM with GATs. Other variants integrate sequence encoders (e.g. Inception networks and CNNs) with a GNN head [185] of mix sequence and structure encoders with a graph module, e.g. contact-map GATs alongside CNNs [164].

The second theme covers approaches that learn protein and network representations independently and combine them for PPI prediction. For example, Shijie et al. [180] pair SeqVec [196] sequence representations with Net2Vec random-walk embeddings [197] over the network; similarly, Hu et al. [181] combine ESM-2 and CNN sequence encoders with Node2Vec; and in [182] a meta-learning and ensembling strategy (CNN/LSTM/GCN) is employed to support predictions across the models’ representation-specific views.

The third type of approaches resorts to generative models that explicitly model noise and missing edges through a graph reconstruction objective. Notable examples include [179], where a signed variational GAE captures positive/negative relations while conditioning on sequence information. More recently, MPAE-PPI [191] uses a VQ-VAE to encode “microenvironment-aware” sequence/structure features, which a GIN then propagates over the PPI graph for link inference.

Beyond sequence and structural information, efforts have also been made to combine protein function representations with network representations. One such example is TANGO [190] introduces a novel taxonomy-aware embedding method to extract representations of the classes that proteins are annotated to in the GO and employs a GAT-based model to obtain protein representations by combining GO embeddings based on their contribution towards the protein’s semantic description. Wenjun et al. [192] introduced the Pro-CoRL multi-modal approach that combines the ESM-1b model for sequence representation, the GearNet-Edge LLM for GO function representation, and interaction graphs for network representation. The approach optimizes a joint contrastive loss to approximate functionally similar proteins.

In summary, the recent surge of these hybrid approaches suggests that jointly learning from protein- and network-level representations builds on top of single-modality methods by capturing both relevant individual protein aspects and global interaction context to enhance predictions. However, the diversity of approaches and representation learning methods makes it challenging to directly compare their performance. This serves as motivation for the comparative analysis presented in the next section, where we comprehensively evaluate representative hybrid approaches over two benchmark PPI prediction tasks and under several experimental settings.

Protein–protein interaction network benchmarks

Numerous PPI benchmarks have been derived from public repositories to evaluate ML models for interaction prediction. Single-species benchmarks such as BioGRID [198], yeast PPIs [199], BioSNAP’s MINER datasets [200–202], HIPPIE [203], and HuRI [42, 204] assess models in species-specific contexts where proteins are more likely to share BP. In contrast, multi-species benchmarks like DIP [205], MINT [206], and the OGBL-ppa dataset [41] require cross-species generalization, making them more challenging. Most benchmarks focus on predicting new interactions from known PPIs and lack detailed protein-level features—OGBL-ppa being an exception, with species-level one-hot encodings.

Comparative analysis

This empirical analysis aims to answer the following research questions (RQs) and evaluate articulated hypotheses:

RQ1: Does adding informative node features to GNNs for PPI prediction significantly improve their performance?Hypothesis: As GNNs’ message-passing relies on network topology as well as node features, the usage of relevant biological information as features (instead of heuristics or random features) for a PPI network builds upon topology-informed PPI prediction and improves model performance.

RQ1: Does adding informative node features to GNNs for PPI prediction significantly improve their performance?

Hypothesis: As GNNs’ message-passing relies on network topology as well as node features, the usage of relevant biological information as features (instead of heuristics or random features) for a PPI network builds upon topology-informed PPI prediction and improves model performance.

RQ2: Which source of node features (protein sequence, structure, or functional information) leads to better PPI prediction performance?Hypothesis: Node features derived from knowledge on protein functionality (e.g. on functional roles, pathway membership, and regulatory states) encode the conditions (shared processes and cellular locations) under which protein interactions occur, and therefore are more informative/relevant than sequence and structure information, which capture mainly binding potential, for PPI prediction.

RQ3: Does combining network and protein representations via a GNN’s message-passing improve PPI prediction compared with approaches that exploit solely protein representations as data features?Hypothesis: Combining protein and network representations will outperform protein representation-only methods because topology contributes with “guilty-by-association” signals that translate into common protein interactants and protein hubs/complexes. This macro interaction information complements the individual (sequence and structure) and functional information of protein representations.

Hypothesis: Combining protein and network representations will outperform protein representation-only methods because topology contributes with “guilty-by-association” signals that translate into common protein interactants and protein hubs/complexes. This macro interaction information complements the individual (sequence and structure) and functional information of protein representations.

The following sections describe the experimental design and evaluation used to address the RQs. Benchmark datasets, constructed from raw PPI data with standardized tasks and evaluation protocols, are selected to ensure fair and reproducible comparison.

Data

For evaluation, two complementary datasets were selected: OGBL-ppa, a widely adopted multi-species benchmark with randomly sampled negatives that assumes a closed-world setting, and HuRI, a human-specific dataset with experimentally validated true negatives that supports evaluation under more realistic conditions.

All protein identifiers were mapped to UniProt using the portal [89]. Protein sequences were retrieved from UniRef50 [207] and subsequently mapped to PDB identifiers in CaTH for structure retrieval. The biomedical KG was built by linking proteins in the PPI graph to the GO [208] classes that describe them, using STRING (https://version11.string-db.org/) and GOA data (http://release.geneontology.org/2020-06-01/ontology/index.html) matched to the STRING release employed by OGB. The KG was then converted into a directed, labeled RDF graph following W3C OWL-to-RDF mapping guidelines(https://www.w3.org/TR/owl2-mapping-to-rdf/), enabling our approach to embed complex axioms and generate vector representations using various KG embedding methods.

Experimental design

The experimental setup evaluates three types of protein-level representations—sequence, structure, and function—each with three representative protein embedding methods, both (i) as standalone protein representations in a protein-only baseline and (ii) as node features injected into standard GNN approaches for PPI prediction, to quantify the combined contribution of protein and network representations.

Sequence embeddings (1024-d) were generated using PLMs ESM-2 [96], ProGen2 [27], and ProtTrans [94]; structure embeddings (1024-d) used inverse folding methods ESM-IF1, PiFold, and ProtT5; and function embeddings (128-d) were obtained via KG embedding methods TransE, ComplEx, and RDF2Vec. GNN models included GCN [37], GraphSAGE [38], NGNN with either GCN or GraphSAGE branches, and NGNN combined with SEAL [39, 40], alongside a majority-vote ensemble. Randomly initialized GNNs served as baselines, reproducing leaderboard hyperparameters (OGBl-ppa) to ensure comparability. To address RQ3, an ablation study reformulated PPI prediction as a binary classification task using concatenated protein embeddings with an MLP, with random embeddings as baselines, isolating the contribution of protein-level features independent of network structure. Supplementary material Table S2 contains original OGBl-ppa leaderboard performances. All LLM parameters are presented in Tables S3, KGE’s parameters in S5 and S6 and GNNs’ parameters in S7.

These results illustrate that incorporating biologically meaningful node features can significantly enhance PPI predictions by complementing network-derived representations, directly addressing RQ1. Comparing feature types (RQ2), functional representations consistently outperformed sequence and structure embeddings, indicating that node features derived from protein functional annotations—captured via KG embeddings—provide the most relevant information for PPI prediction. No consistent difference was observed between sequence- and structure-based approaches, suggesting that either the information content or the representation learning method could determine performance. This pattern should, however, be interpreted in light of annotation coverage. Annotation bias can favor well-studied proteins, potentially limiting generalizability to understudied proteins and non-model organisms. To contextualize this issue, we report annotation distribution in the OGBl-ppa dataset (HuRI proteins are fully annotated), where 10.6% (61 352) of proteins are not annotated, of which 7.8% are also test proteins. Annotated proteins have a median number of 7 and a minimum of 1 annotation. Interestingly, function-based embeddings still improved predictive performance despite incomplete and often sparse annotation coverage, suggesting that partial functional information can remain informative. This may also reflect that in the strongest-performing settings in our study—the hybrid approaches that combine protein function embeddings with network encoding—network topology and neighborhood context can partially mitigate the effects of sparse annotations. Broader evaluation on more weakly annotated benchmarks is needed to assess robustness more directly.

Viewed against the taxonomy in Tables 1 and 2, these results suggest a practical division of roles across representation families. Network-level methods are attractive because they exploit interaction topology directly, but their performance likely depends on graph density, degree distribution, and the type of interactome PPI evidence. Sequence and structure-based representations are broadly available and scalable, yet in our benchmarks they yielded less consistent improvements when used as protein features. Sequence embeddings are mainly appealing when only primary structure is available and large-scale coverage is needed, whereas structure representations are useful when 3D or fold-aware information is expected to reflect interaction interfaces, but depend on the availability of experimental or predicted structures. Function-derived representations were the strongest overall, suggesting that higher-level biological context is particularly informative for PPI prediction, although this advantage comes with greater dependence on annotation availability and quality. Thus, no single representation is universally optimal; the best protein representation depends on the data regime and the type of biological prior available.

Comparisons across datasets reveal that models trained on single-species data (HuRI) often outperform multi-species models (OGBl-ppa), consistent with the expectation that single-species networks provide more homogeneous interaction patterns. Part of this difference may stem from negative sampling: HuRI includes experimentally validated true negatives, whereas OGBl-ppa uses randomly sampled negatives under a closed-world assumption, which can introduce false negatives and blur model decision boundaries. Degree heterogeneity in OGBl-ppa further influences performance: species with high-degree distributions (e.g. metazoa, fungi, alveolata; see Fig. 5) allow network-based methods to exploit rich neighborhood information, while low-degree species benefit more from intrinsic protein features. This pattern aligns with RQ1–RQ3 expectations, showing that biologically informative node features contribute most when network topology is sparse or less informative.

Each boxplot pertains to one of 55 species, sorted by median degree and colored by kingdom, out of the following 8: Pseudomonadati, Bacillati, Fungi, Metazoa, Viridiplantae, Discoba, Amoebozoa, and Alveolata.

An ablation study on HuRI with an MLP using only concatenated protein embeddings confirms this synergistic effect: KG-derived functional information alone achieves performance close to simpler GNN baselines, demonstrating that combining network structure with protein-level features maximizes predictive capability (RQ3). This complementary effect also helps explain the appeal of hybrid approaches despite their added cost. Compared with protein-only approaches, hybrid models generally introduce additional computational and modeling complexity, either through a multi-step setting, where protein representations are first computed and then injected into the network learning, or as an end-to-end framework that jointly processes network structure and protein-derived inputs. The trade-off is increased preprocessing burden and larger and/or higher-dimensional inputs with approximately linear scaling of the computational cost and more complex training, in exchange for the ability to exploit complementary protein-level and network-level signals.

Overall, these findings underscore the importance of selecting biologically relevant node features, carefully considering negative sampling strategies, and accounting for degree heterogeneity in multi-species datasets. Future work should report degree-stratified performance and leverage benchmarks with validated negatives to more accurately assess the contribution of protein representations in PPI prediction.

Conclusions

PPI prediction has advanced along two complementary tracks: modeling protein-level information from sequence, structure, and function, and learning directly from the topology of PPI networks using graph-based methods. Hybrid approaches have emerged that integrate protein-level representations as node features within GNNs, combining intrinsic biological information with network context to improve predictive performance. Despite the proliferation of GNN-based approaches that leverage these paradigms, a cohesive synthesis highlighting the respective strengths, complementarities, and trade-offs of combining protein- and network-level information was still lacking.

We surveyed >250 peer-reviewed works and distilled their relevant findings into a coherent taxonomy that describes three paradigms: network-level approaches (shallow and deep GRL), protein-level approaches (sequence, structure, function), and hybrid methods that combine both paradigms.

We defined RQs to compare protein-level, network-level, and hybrid PPI prediction paradigms. To support this, we developed a unified framework enabling three assessments: (i) protein-level models using embeddings directly, (ii) network-level models exploiting structure alone, and (iii) hybrid models integrating protein representations as GNN node features. Models were evaluated on two benchmarks OGBl-ppa (multi-species, random negatives, closed-world assumption) and HuRI (single-species, experimentally validated negatives), using multiple sequence and structure PLMs, KG embedding methods, and GNN architectures for controlled, directly comparable experiments. An important direction for future work would be to complement this analysis with an assessment of robustness and generalizability using sequence homology-based partitioning and stratifying performance by annotation coverage and network context.

Our results lead to three main takeaways: (i) adding biologically meaningful node features generally boosts GNN performance over topology-only approaches; (ii) the function-driven paradigm performed best out of all protein representations, likely because it encodes more informative contextual knowledge, (iii) while combining protein and network representations is vastly beneficial, function-based approaches can outperform some hybrid methods using sequence or structure information.

In sum, this paper contributes a consolidated map of the landscape of approaches for PPI prediction, a unified experimental framework, and actionable guidance on how to combine protein representations with network-based approaches. These insights can inform method selection and experimental design in future PPI prediction studies.
