Adopting a Comprehensive Approach to Improve Nutritional Status among Critically Ill Children in Low-Resource Settings: A Narrative Review

ABSTRACT
Critically ill children account for a significant burden of morbidity and mortality reported among the pediatric age group in low-resource settings. The objectives of the article are to ascertain the nutritional needs of critically ill children, enlist the identified challenges in maintaining nutrition among critically ill children in low-resource settings, and propose targeted public health interventions to overcome these identified challenges. The results obtained from a review article that studied the cases admitted in pediatric critical care units revealed that almost 48–84% of the pediatric patients were malnourished, which happens to be a key factor accounting for poor clinical outcomes. A wide range of challenges and gaps have been identified about the management of pediatric critical illnesses in low-resource settings. Acknowledging the high prevalence of malnourishment among critically ill children, the nutritional needs, and the presence of multiple factors at the policy level, health system level, and family level, the need of the hour is to adopt a comprehensive plan to reduce the existing burden of malnutrition. In conclusion, children from low-resource settings are quite vulnerable to malnutrition and other predisposing factors, which can affect the prognosis of the children. This calls for the need to adopt a comprehensive public health approach, involving different sectors to ensure that critically ill children receive the nutritional care they require for quick recovery and wellbeing.

Introduction

Critically ill children account for a significant burden of morbidity and mortality reported among the pediatric age group in low-resource settings.[12] The findings of a large-scale multinational prospective cohort study revealed that 13% of hospital admissions across 19 nations were due to acute critical illnesses in children.[1] Moreover, the reported prevalence of acute critical illnesses was the highest (28%) in nations with a low sociodemographic index.[1] The results from another systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that the leading cause of hospital-based mortality among children was due to acute infectious, respiratory, and gastrointestinal diseases.[2] We must acknowledge that critically ill children in low-resource settings are exposed to a combination of predisposing factors (like high disease burden, deficiencies in the healthcare system, malnutrition, etc.), underscoring the urgent need for implementation of integrated public health measures.[123] The objectives of the article are to ascertain the nutritional needs of critically ill children, enlist the identified challenges in maintaining nutrition among critically ill children in low-resource settings, and propose targeted public health interventions to overcome these identified challenges.

Nutritional Needs of Critically Ill Children

The results obtained from a review article that studied the cases admitted in pediatric critical care units revealed that almost 48–84% of the pediatric patients were malnourished, which happens to be a key factor accounting for poor clinical outcomes.[3] Generally, critically ill children tend to have higher energy (namely, increased energy expenditure due to fever, healing, inflammation) and protein (to accelerate the process of tissue repair, immune response, and to prevent muscle wasting) requirements.[4] At the same time, these children are also prone to fluid and electrolyte imbalance, and nutritional support should be accordingly modified to avoid the development of any complications, especially among children with renal or cardiac involvement.[5] The nutritional needs in these children might vary depending on the specific diseases, and it is a must that customization should be done depending on the food items which are locally available, literacy of caregivers, and alignment with the existing cultural and feeding practices.[67]

Moreover, to strengthen immunity and improve healing, micronutrients (namely, zinc, selenium, Vitamin A and D, etc.) must be supplemented based on the deficiency pattern.[8] Nutrition supplementation should be preferably administered through the enteral route to maintain gut integrity and reduce the risk of infection.[6] It is recommended that this supplementation should be started early, within 24–48 hours of stabilization of ill children, for better clinical outcomes.[6] Delayed nutrition has been linked with atrophy of gut mucosa, higher incidence of infections, longer duration of mechanical ventilation, and more time for recovery.[69] Further, as malnutrition often impacts the absorption, metabolism, and efficacy of drugs, it is a must that adequate nutrition is administered for accomplishing optimal response to treatments.[10] In addition, children who have received appropriate nutrition during hospitalization are at less risk of developing relapse or complications following their discharge from hospitals.[11] Finally, optimal nutrition ensures the growth and development of children, including cognitive development and psychosocial outcomes, thereby improving the quality of life.[456]

Identified Challenges and Gaps in Low-Resource Settings

A wide range of challenges and gaps have been identified with regard to the management of pediatric critical illnesses [Figure 1].[12361213141516171819202122] In low-resource settings, most critically ill children are managed in general wards due to inadequate access to specialized pediatric intensive care units, which tend to have the provision of advanced nutritional monitoring tools and protocols.[312] The problem is further complicated by the shortage of trained healthcare professionals (namely, pediatricians, dietitians, etc.), and this results in inconsistent or incorrect feeding practices that delay or jeopardize the recovery process.[12] In addition, poor infrastructure and lack of basic equipment (namely, feeding tubes, refrigerators for breast milk or formula storage, etc.) hinder the process of effective nutritional support.[1213] Moreover, limited availability or poor affordability of nutritional formulations (like high-protein feeds, amino acid-based formulations, etc.) forces healthcare professionals to either opt for adult formulations or precipitate inadequate management.[14]

Identified challenges

We just cannot ignore the fact that many critically ill children in low-resource settings are already undernourished at the time of admission (because of food insecurity, infections, etc.), which makes the process of nutritional rehabilitation extremely complex and urgent.[12] In addition, the absence or outdated nature of nutritional protocols in resource-constrained settings exaggerates the practice of variability in the delivery of standardized, evidence-based care.[15] In addition, we also lack context-specific feeding protocols, which makes the practical implementation of nutritional supplements quite difficult.[1516] Further, because of these flaws in protocols or lack of training of healthcare professionals, more often than not, the assessment of nutritional status is often missed at the time or in the early hours upon admission.[1517] It is noteworthy that routine nutritional assessment tools (like mid-upper arm circumference, etc.) are either not used or unavailable, because of which malnutrition is detected quite late, which worsens the prognosis and augments the risk of mortality.[1819]

It is a grave concern that nutritional care in low-resource settings often ends with the discharge of the children from the hospitals.[20] In other words, postdischarge nutritional follow-up is often inadequate because of poor referral mechanisms or weak linkages between communities and healthcare facilities.[20] At the family level, poor health literacy (like the necessity to continue feeding during illness, reluctance to hospital-based nutritional supplementation, etc.) or the prevailing cultural practices (namely, food taboos, fasting, etc.) remarkably delay or reduce feeding support.[621] Even in the presence of nutritional support, families from the lower socioeconomic class cannot afford the available services, which results in early discharge, discontinuation of feeding plans, and/or decreased food consumption just for the sake of cost-cutting.[22] The combination of all the above factors makes the process of nutritional supplementation extremely difficult for families from low socioeconomic groups.[1213141516171819202122]

Public Health Interventions

Acknowledging the high prevalence of malnourishment among critically ill children, the nutritional needs, and the presence of multiple factors at the policy level, health system level, and family level, the need of the hour is to adopt a comprehensive plan to reduce the existing burden of malnutrition [Table 1].[1213141516181920232425262728] The primary approach is to formulate and implement simplified, context-specific feeding protocols (like how to initiate feeds, feeding frequency, etc.) in pediatric wards and intensive care units.[1516] These developed protocols must be integrated into existing national programs or initiatives, like Integrated Management of Newborn and Childhood Illnesses, etc.[1523] This integration will ensure that nutrition is not acknowledged as a solitary concern but a core component of pediatric health services.[1623] However, mere formulation of protocols will not deliver results, and there arises the need to organize capacity-building initiatives, wherein healthcare professionals, including community health workers, are trained in the domain of assessment of nutritional status, managing feeds, and recognizing the need to refer patients.[24]

Identified challenges and proposed public health interventions

Realizing that nutrition is a complex phenomenon, the best results can be attained by creating interdisciplinary teams (consisting of clinicians, nutritionists, pharmacists, etc.), which can evaluate and monitor the nutrition of critically ill children.[67] Once protocols and the team are ready, the next domain will be to ensure uninterrupted supply of essential nutritional components (namely, therapeutic formulas, micronutrient powders, etc.) and equipment, either through government support or via public–private partnerships.[121314] One of the essential strategies to avert undernourishment is via the promotion of early initiation of breastfeeding, advocating the practice of exclusive breastfeeding, and supporting mothers in wards for the same.[25] Mid-upper arm circumference tapes should be regularly used as a standard tool for quick assessment of nutritional status by outreach health workers, and based on their assessment, at-risk children should be timely referred to higher centers.[1819] Community health workers must be trained in mobile-based applications to help them track child growth, send periodic reminders about feeds, and report complications.[24]

Moreover, screening programs for detecting malnutrition must be initiated in school settings as early detection can reduce the possibility of the development of nutrition-associated complications when children are critically ill.[18] At the community level, multiple measures need to be implemented, such as running awareness campaigns for caregivers and members of the community regarding healthy nutritional practices and the necessity to continue feeding during illness, with the help of local leaders and different modes of mass media, including social media.[26] There is a definite need to promote the use of affordable and locally available nutritious food (like peanut-paste, porridge, etc.) and fortify them with essential nutrients, which can be afforded by people from lower socioeconomic classes.[26] Upon discharge of the children, families should be provided with simple-to-use nutrient supplement packages with easy instructions, mid-upper arm circumference measurement tapes, and a timeline for follow-up visits through community health workers.[182027] Finally, we must strengthen community-based management of acute malnutrition to avoid a burden on hospitals, and use them preferably for management of complications.[28]

Conclusions

To conclude, children from low-resource settings are quite vulnerable to malnutrition and other predisposing factors, which can affect the prognosis of the children. This calls for the need to adopt a comprehensive public health approach, involving different sectors to ensure that critically ill children receive the nutritional care they require for quick recovery and wellbeing.
