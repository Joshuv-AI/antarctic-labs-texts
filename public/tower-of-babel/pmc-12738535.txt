Evaluation of dental ages of children with type I diabetes using different dental age estimation methods: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Deniz Sıla Özdemir Çelik, Melek Belevcikli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Type 1 diabetes may affect skeletal and dental development, but its influence on dental age estimation remains unclear. The aim of this study was to determine the estimated dental age of children with type 1 diabetes by using Cameriere, Willems and Demirjian methods. Panoramic radiographs of 58 children (29 with type 1 diabetes and 29 healthy controls) aged 4–15 years were retrospectively evaluated. Dental ages were estimated using the Cameriere, Willems, and Demirjian methods and compared with chronological age by group and gender. Statistical software SPSS version 23 (SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY) was used for all analyses. No statistically significant difference in estimated dental age was found between the diabetes and control groups using all three methods (p > 0.05). Willems method is better than the Cameriere and Demirjian methods in estimating dental age in all subgroups (control, diabetes and gender) (p < 0,001). Among all subgroups, the Willems method showed the highest accuracy, while the Cameriere method slightly underestimated and the Demirjian method overestimated chronological age. Dental age in children with well-controlled type 1 diabetes appears comparable to healthy peers. Willems method gives more reliable results than Cameriere and Demirjian methods in pediatric patients with type 1 diabetes. It is important to use different dental age determination methods together to obtain more reliable results.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes is a metabolic disease that causes inadequate insulin production and consequent hyperglycemia as a result of autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells1. According to the International Diabetes Federation, approximately 651,700 children aged 0–14 years worldwide were living with type 1 diabetes in 2019, with an estimated 108,300 new cases diagnosed annually2. The global incidence continues to rise, particularly in high- and middle-income countries. In Turkey, recent studies have reported an annual incidence ranging between 7.2 and 16.7 per 100,000 person-years among children, indicating a gradual increase over the past decade3,4. Studies examining the effect of type 1 diabetes on calcified tissues in the head and neck region of children have found a decrease in skeletal maturation and linear and angular cephalometric measurements in patients with type 1 diabetes based on height, weight, lateral cephalometry and wrist radiography5. Similarly, metabolic disorders observed in type 1 diabetes cause changes in the mineral content of teeth. It has been proven that high glucose levels in blood and saliva, diabetic neuropathy, vascular dysfunction and diabetes-related low resistance may indicate dental abnormalities and tooth loss6.

Many factors can affect the development of teeth, including genetically determined disorders, calcium-phosphate disorders, hypo or hypervitaminosis, metabolic disorders, allergies, febrile illnesses, immunologic diseases, specific and non-specific inflammations, endocrinopathies (including Diabetes Mellitus), abnormal blood pressure, injuries after radiation, maternal conditions (number of pregnancies, age of the pregnant woman, pathologic changes in the uterus) and medications. These factors can have an impact both during pregnancy and in the neonatal and postnatal period7. Numerous reports confirm that diabetes, especially type 1, as a metabolic disorder, can affect changes in the structure of the facial skeleton and mineralized dental tissues. This leads to an increased susceptibility to caries development and orthognathic disorders5,8–10. It is stated in the literature that further research should be carried out on larger patient groups and odontogenesis to fully understand this mechanism of type 1 diabetes.

There are several methods for age estimation based on dental tissues, including morphological (tooth wear rate, tooth discoloration), metric, radiomorphological, radiometric. In recent years, a large number of studies on the Demirjian method have been conducted in various populations. However, many recent studies and meta-analyses have reported that the Demirjian method overestimates chronological age in different populations11–16. Some studies have also shown that the Willems method is more accurate than the Demirjian method13,17–20. In 2006, Cameriere et al. described a linear regression formula to estimate tooth age based on measurements of the open apices and tooth length of seven left mandibular teeth in an Italian subpopulation21. Recently, many researchers have reported that the Cameriere method is accurate and useful for assessing dental age in some countries21–25.

To our knowledge, there are no studies in the literature that evaluate dental age estimation methods in patients with type 1 diabetes. The aim of this study was to determine the estimated dental age of children with type 1 diabetes by using Cameriere, Willems and Demirjian methods.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted on children aged 4–15 years who attended the Pediatric Dentistry Clinic of Bolu Abant İzzet Baysal University, Faculty of Dentistry, between 2017 and 2023 and had panoramic radiographs taken during routine examinations. The study included a total of 58 children: 29 with type 1 diabetes and 29 healthy controls. The control group comprised children without systemic diseases who presented to the clinic during the same period, with similar chronological age and gender distribution to the diabetic group. Informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians for the scientific use of examination and radiographic findings. Ethical approval was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Bolu Abant İzzet Baysal University Faculty of Medicine (Ethics No: 2023/435).”

Sample size calculation

In the research hypotheses, it was planned to compare the averages of two independent and dependent groups and the calculation that gives the highest sample size according to statistical methods was taken into consideration. In the study, sample sizes were calculated at 95% confidence level by using ‘G. Power-3.1.9.2’ programme. As a result of the analysis for the experimental-control groups, when α = 0.05, standardised effect size was calculated as 0.986326.; 8.6 ± 0.7 and 9.5 ± 0.5) from a similar study previously conducted in this field and with a theoretical power of 0.80, the minimum sample size was obtained as 36 (18 in each group). Two patients with diabetes mellitus were excluded due to poor quality of the panoramic radiographs and 9 patients were excluded due to the presence of dental anomalies. The final study group consisted of 29 pediatric patients with type 1 diabetes and the control group consisted of 29 pediatric patients without any systemic disease.

Dental age calculation

Radiographic evaluations were performed in a dimly lit, quiet environment under x150 magnification using Image J software (ImageJ 1.46r, NIH, Maryland, MD, USA).

In the Demirjian method27, which is one of the most commonly used methods of dental age determination, the radiographic mineralization stages of seven teeth in the left mandible are calculated by using tables symbolized by the letters A-H for each tooth and created separately for each age group and gender, and the total dental scores of the individuals are obtained. Age is determined by matching the values corresponding to these scores on the chart. In the Willems method17, the same A-H mineralization stages used in the Demirjian method are taken into account, but age values are calculated by scoring on the gender-specific tables created by Willems et al. In the Cameriere method21, root development and the degree of closure of the apical tip of seven teeth in the left mandible are evaluated. The apical openings of the teeth and the distances from the top of the tubercle to the root tip are measured. Dental ages were determined by 2 researchers who were intra/inter-calibrated before the study. Before starting the study, 10 randomly selected radiographs were scored twice by both researchers at different times in order to train and calibrate the methods. Inter-observer and intra-observer agreements were determined using ICC (intraclass correlation coefficient).

Statistical method

Normality was checked for continuous variables using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data from two independent groups were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Data from three or more dependent groups were compared using Repeated Measures ANOVA (Post-Hoc: Paired samples t-test with Bonferroni correction) or Friedman test (Post-Hoc: Dunn test with Bonferroni correction). The success of dental age methods in estimating chronological age was assessed using Simple Regression Analysis. Statistical software SPSS version 23 (SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY) was used for all analyses. The intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated for the purpose of inter-observer and intra-observer agreement analysis. The significance level was determined as p < 0.05.

Discussion

Determination of dental age is of great importance in forensic analysis, archaeological research, and clinical disciplines such as pedodontics and orthodontics29–32. Examination of radiologic images is one of the methods used to determine age in children33,34. Metabolic disturbances in diabetes are the main drivers of numerous complications, such as extensive changes in bone tissue mineral metabolism leading to delayed bone maturation, affecting linear growth and body structure in people with diabetes5. Furthermore, the rate of mineral loss from bone tissues is significantly higher in patients with impaired glucose metabolism despite increased insulin support5. Since teeth are mineralized organs, metabolic changes can affect the structure, development and eruption time of teeth. In our study, we aimed to compare multiple patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus with healthy children using different dental age assessment methods.

Many methods such as Cameriere, Haavikko, Demirjian, Willems, Nolla, Smith are used in radiologic age determination30,35,36. Kumaresan et al.37 showed that the Cameriere method is more precise and accurate when estimating the age of Demirjian, Nolla, Haavikko and Willems teeth in a Malaysian population. Javadinejad et al.38showed that Cameriere’s method was less accurate compared to Smith’s method, but more accurate compared to Willems and Demirjian’s method in the Iranian population aged 3 to 15 years. Hostuiv et al14. reported that the Cameriere method was useful in estimating chronologic age with an error of less than one year. Nik-Hussein et al.39 compared Demirjian and Willems methods in Malaysian children and reported that Willems method was more accurate. Hato et al.40 reported that Nolla, Willems and Cameriere methods were more accurate than Cameriere method in a dental age determination study on Turkish population. Altan et al.41 reported that dental age estimation with the Willems method was more accurate than the Demirjian method in the Southern Turkish population. Apaydın and Yaşar42, reported that the Willems method was more reliable for the Turkish population, as the Cameriere method overestimated age in younger age groups (5–6 and 6–7 age groups) and underestimated age in older age groups. In our study, Demirjian, Willems and Cameriere methods were evaluated for both children with type 1 diabetes and healthy children. Among the three methods, the closest dental age estimate to the chronological age in both groups was made by the Willems method.

Emeksiz et al.43 evaluated Nolla and Cameriere methods in children with hypothyroidism and reported that there was no significant difference between hypothyroid patients and the control group in terms of tooth age evaluated by both methods. In a study evaluating skeletal and dental age in children with type 1 diabetes, Bezerra et al.44 reported that there was no difference in skeletal and dental development between patients with type 1 diabetes and the control group. In this study, no difference was observed between the three dental age determination methods in children with type 1 diabetes. The dental age estimation results obtained with the Willems method were more successful than those of Cameriere and Demirjian. This suggests that the metabolic derangements of diabetes may not significantly delay odontogenesis in children with well-controlled glycemic status. On the other hand, mechanistic evidence from ultrastructural studies has shown that hyperglycemia and insulin deficiency can impair the mineralization of dental hard tissues; for example, diabetic children have significantly lower calcium and phosphorus content in the enamel of their primary teeth45. Experimental animal studies similarly support the occurrence of ameloblast disorders and hypomineralization in diabetic conditions46.

In other words, while the potential biological mechanisms (altered calcium-phosphate metabolism, impaired ameloblast function, vascular changes) are well supported, the clinical manifestation (i.e., detectable delay in tooth age) may be attenuated in a cohort with good metabolic control. This may explain why our study, as in previous clinical studies, found no statistically significant difference between the diabetic and control groups.

It is possible that children with strong diabetes management experience less disruption in mineralization processes, allowing tooth development to proceed at a near-normal rate. Conversely, the ultrastructural changes described above may reflect subclinical effects that do not lead to significant changes in age estimation methods. Accordingly, the lack of a significant delay in dental age in our cohort may reflect an interaction between disease control, disease duration, and the methodological sensitivity of dental age assessment.

Taken together, our results and the literature suggest a model in which type 1 diabetes may affect dental tissue mineralization, but the magnitude of the effect on dental age estimation depends on factors such as glycemic control, systemic growth status, and population-specific maturation patterns. Future research integrating metabolic biomarkers (e.g., HbA₁c, IGF-1), dental age assessments, and imaging of enamel/dentin ultrastructure will help clarify how these biological mechanisms are reflected in radiological age estimations.

It is seen that there are different results in the literature in the evaluation of the reliability of dental age determination methods according to gender. Pinchi et al.47 evaluated Demirjian, Willems, Cameriere and Haavikko methods according to gender and reported that Demirjian and Willems were more accurate in girls, while Willems was the most accurate method in boys despite overestimating age. Cherian et al.48 reported that the Willems method gave reliable results in both boys and girls in a study conducted in North India. Cortés et al.20 reported in a study conducted on the Spanish population that the Willems method gave reliable results in both genders. Apaydın and Yaşar42 reported that the Willems method is more ideal for determining dental age for the Turkish population, regardless of gender. When we evaluated the dental age determination methods according to gender in our study, it was observed that the Willems method gave results closer to the chronological age than the Cameriere and Demirjian methods in both boys and girls in all groups. It has been reported in the literature that the Willems method may show social differences in dental age calculations due to ethnic differences49.

The present study population consisted of children residing in the Western Black Sea region of Turkey who attended the Pediatric Dentistry Clinic of Bolu Abant İzzet Baysal University. Therefore, the findings primarily reflect the characteristics of this regional pediatric population. The study was conducted with a relatively small sample size and should be regarded as a pilot investigation. Consequently, the results cannot be generalized to broader populations, and further multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate and expand these findings.

This study also has several strengths. It is the first to compare the Cameriere, Willems, and Demirjian methods in children with type 1 diabetes. The inclusion of a well-matched control group enabled reliable comparisons of dental ages. All radiographs were evaluated by two calibrated observers with high interobserver agreement, ensuring methodological reliability. Moreover, the combined use of three validated dental age estimation methods provided a comprehensive assessment of dental maturity in children with type 1 diabetes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Willems method provided the most accurate dental age estimates among the three methods evaluated in children with type 1 diabetes. No significant differences were observed between the diabetic and control groups, suggesting that well-controlled type 1 diabetes may not substantially affect dental maturation. The combined use of multiple dental age estimation methods may enhance the reliability of dental age assessment in pediatric populations.
