Unsupervised Machine Learning of Intrapancreatic Fat Deposition Reveals Distinct Clinical Phenotypes: A CT Study
Authors: Wenlu Zong, Yuning Chu, Yingjie Guo, Pin Meng, Chen Hao, Xiaoyan Yin, Zibin Tian, Xiaoyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIntrapancreatic fat deposition (IPFD) is an ectopic fat phenotype associated with metabolic abnormalities. However, the clinical heterogeneity of IPFD in relation to generalized adiposity remains insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to explore clinical profiles associated with IPFD using unsupervised clustering and characterize their metabolic features.MethodsIn this retrospective, single-center cross-sectional study, 558 adults undergoing non-contrast abdominal computed tomography were included. K-means clustering was performed using age, body mass index (BMI), pancreas-to-spleen attenuation difference, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and triglyceride (TG) levels. Clinical characteristics, metabolic comorbidities, and circulating biomarkers were compared across the derived exploratory clusters.ResultsThree exploratory clinical phenotypes were identified: an obesity-dominant cluster (n=140), an FPD-like cluster characterized by greater IPFD (n=182), and a reference cluster (n=236). The FPD-like cluster showed the lowest pancreas-to-spleen attenuation difference, older age, and lower BMI than the obesity-dominant cluster. In contrast, the obesity-dominant cluster showed stronger associations with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and higher uric acid levels. Free fatty acid concentrations were highest in the FPD-like cluster. Differences in glycemic and lipid-related characteristics should be interpreted cautiously because FPG and TG were included as clustering variables.ConclusionIn this exploratory cross-sectional analysis, a cluster characterized by greater IPFD, older age, and lower BMI than the obesity-dominant cluster was associated with less favorable metabolic characteristics. These findings suggest that IPFD may provide complementary information beyond BMI-based assessment; however, the identified clusters should be considered exploratory clinical profiles rather than validated biological subtypes. External validation in independent populations and longitudinal studies are required to confirm their reproducibility and clinical relevance.

Introduction

Systemic obesity is a well-established risk factor associated with metabolic disease, yet traditional anthropometric metrics such as body mass index (BMI) provide limited information regarding regional fat distribution and ectopic fat accumulation and may fail to identify individuals with adverse metabolic characteristics despite the absence of marked obesity.1,2 The adipose tissue expandability hypothesis posits that when the storage capacity of subcutaneous adipose depots is saturated, a process frequently exacerbated by aging, excess lipids may spill over and accumulate in non-adipose tissues.3 This ectopic fat accumulation has been increasingly recognized as an important component of metabolic dysfunction, contributing to altered organ-specific metabolism and insulin resistance through complex interactions among lipid storage capacity, inflammation, and metabolic regulation.4,5

Among the various ectopic fat depots, intrapancreatic fat deposition (IPFD) has emerged as an important organ-specific fat deposition phenotype associated with metabolic abnormalities. The Melbourne Consensus has emphasized that IPFD represents a dynamic process of lipid deposition within the pancreas and should be distinguished from fatty pancreas disorder (FPD), which refers to excessive or pathological IPFD.6 Due to the close anatomical relationship between pancreatic adipose tissue and endocrine compartments, greater IPFD may be associated with alterations in the local metabolic environment, including changes in lipid exposure, inflammatory signaling, and β-cell function.7 These alterations may represent one of several mechanisms potentially linking ectopic fat accumulation with impaired glucose metabolism, although the causal contribution of IPFD remains to be established.8

Ectopic fat accumulation frequently involves multiple metabolic organs, with the liver serving as a central regulator of lipid storage, redistribution, and systemic metabolic homeostasis. Hepatic steatosis, representing the initial accumulation of fat within the liver, may progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) in individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors.9 IPFD and hepatic fat accumulation often coexist and may reflect shared upstream mechanisms, including insulin resistance, altered lipid flux, and adipose tissue dysfunction. Recent population-based evidence has further suggested that IPFD is associated with a broad range of systemic health outcomes, highlighting its potential relevance as an organ-specific component of metabolic dysfunction.10 However, the relationship between hepatic fat accumulation and IPFD remains complex and may vary according to age, adiposity status, and metabolic context.

Advances in quantitative imaging have enabled more objective assessment of IPFD. Non-enhanced CT-based approaches, including pancreas-to-spleen attenuation difference, provide accessible surrogate measures for pancreatic fat quantification and have demonstrated associations with metabolic characteristics.11–13 MRI-based proton density fat fraction (PDFF) provides a more standardized quantitative assessment of pancreatic fat content, although its availability may limit widespread clinical application.14 More recently, artificial intelligence-based imaging approaches have further facilitated automated IPFD quantification and highlighted potential clinical applications, although standardization and external validation remain necessary.15 Emerging clinical studies and systematic evidence have demonstrated associations between IPFD and metabolic abnormalities, including components of metabolic syndrome, while emphasizing the need for further validation across diverse populations.10,16

Despite increasing recognition of IPFD as an important ectopic fat phenotype, the clinical heterogeneity of individuals with IPFD remains insufficiently characterized. Individuals with similar levels of generalized adiposity may exhibit different patterns of IPFD and metabolic characteristics, suggesting that BMI-based classification alone may not adequately capture clinically relevant variation in metabolic risk. For example, some individuals with obesity may maintain relatively preserved metabolic characteristics, consistent with the concept of metabolically healthy obesity,17,18 whereas others with lower BMI, particularly older individuals, may exhibit greater IPFD accompanied by unfavorable metabolic characteristics. Therefore, data-driven approaches specifically designed to characterize IPFD-associated phenotypic heterogeneity may provide new insights into the clinical variation associated with pancreatic fat accumulation.

Previous data-driven metabolic phenotyping studies have primarily focused on diabetes subtypes or obesity-related metabolic heterogeneity, demonstrating the value of clustering approaches for identifying clinically meaningful subgroups.19,20 However, whether similar approaches can identify distinct clinical patterns among individuals characterized by IPFD remains unclear. Therefore, data-driven approaches specifically designed to characterize IPFD-associated phenotypic heterogeneity are needed to improve understanding of the relationship between pancreatic fat deposition and metabolic variation.

To address this critical knowledge gap, we applied an unsupervised machine learning clustering algorithm to a retrospective cohort of adults undergoing opportunistic non-contrast abdominal computed tomography (CT) imaging. By integrating multidimensional anthropometric, imaging-derived, and metabolic variables, this study aimed to explore latent clinical patterns associated with IPFD without imposing predefined clinical categories. Unlike previous data-driven metabolic phenotyping studies that primarily focused on diabetes subtypes or obesity-related heterogeneity, this study specifically investigated whether unsupervised clustering could identify distinct clinical profiles among individuals with varying degrees of IPFD. We hypothesized that IPFD may exhibit heterogeneous clinical patterns that are not fully explained by generalized adiposity alone, and that individuals with different combinations of IPFD, anthropometric characteristics, and metabolic features may represent distinct exploratory clinical profiles.

Methods

Study Design and Participant Selection

This retrospective, cross-sectional investigation was conducted utilizing the electronic medical records system at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (Approval No. QYFY WZLL 42,030). Given the retrospective and observational nature of the research, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. All procedures strictly adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

To construct a high-fidelity analytical cohort for metabolic phenotyping, we systematically queried the hospital database for adult individuals (aged ≥ 18 years) who underwent non-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) imaging between January 2015 and December 2025. The initial screening targeted radiological reports containing indicative terms such as “intrapancreatic fat deposition”, “pancreatic steatosis”, or “fatty pancreas”. To rigorously minimize the influence of potential confounding factors on both imaging and metabolic profiles, strict exclusion criteria were applied. Participants were omitted from the final analysis if they met any of the following conditions: (1) a documented history of significant alcohol consumption (>20 g/day for females and >40 g/day for males) to rule out alcohol-induced steatosis; (2) pre-existing primary structural or inflammatory pancreatic pathologies, including acute/chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cystic lesions, or malignancies; (3) concurrent use of pharmacological agents known to substantially alter glucose or lipid homeostasis; (4) suboptimal CT image quality resulting from motion artifacts; or (5) the absence of matching fasting biochemical laboratory data within a 7-day window surrounding the CT acquisition. Following this rigorous filtering process, a final cohort comprising 558 eligible participants was established for the subsequent data-driven clustering analysis.

Clinical Assessments and Definitions

Baseline demographic and anthropometric data, including age and body mass index (BMI), were extracted from electronic medical records. A comprehensive panel of fasting biochemical parameters, encompassing fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), free fatty acids (FFA), and serum uric acid (UA), was assessed using standard automated laboratory protocols following an overnight fast of at least 8 hours.

Target-organ metabolic comorbidities were defined according to internationally recognized guidelines. Specifically, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) was diagnosed by the radiological presence of hepatic steatosis coupled with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, strictly adhering to the latest multi-society Delphi consensus statement.9 Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension(HTN), coronary heart disease (CHD) and metabolic syndrome (MetS) were identified based on established clinical criteria and documented medical history.21–24

Quantitative Assessment of Intrapancreatic Fat Deposition (IPFD)

IPFD was quantitatively evaluated using non-enhanced abdominal CT imaging. Non-enhanced abdominal CT examinations were performed on 16-slice CT scanners using a standardized acquisition protocol, including a tube voltage of 120 kVp, automatic exposure control, a slice thickness of 5 mm, and a standard soft-tissue reconstruction kernel. To standardize attenuation measurements across various scanner vendors and mitigate baseline physiological variations, we employed the pancreas-to-spleen (P-S) attenuation difference as an internally normalized metric, a methodology widely validated for non-invasive steatosis assessment.11–13

Three uniform circular regions of interest (ROIs, approximately 1.0 cm2) were manually positioned within the distinct anatomical segments of the pancreas (head, body, and tail), carefully avoiding the main pancreatic duct, visible peripancreatic fat, and major vascular structures. Corresponding ROIs were placed within the spleen at matching axial levels. The final P-S difference (expressed in Hounsfield Units, HU) was derived by subtracting the mean splenic attenuation from the mean pancreatic attenuation. Consequently, a lower (or more negative) P-S difference value denotes greater IPFD accumulation.

To validate the consistency of our radiological evaluation, a randomly selected sample of 50 scans underwent independent review by two investigators who were completely masked to the participants’ clinical profiles. Inter-reader agreement was quantified via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). In instances where the absolute difference in attenuation values between the two initial readers exceeded 10 HU, a senior radiologist was consulted to adjudicate and reach a consensus. Following the confirmation of robust measurement reproducibility (predefined as an ICC > 0.80) within this pilot subset, the quantitative assessments for the remainder of the study cohort were executed by a single designated researcher. This methodological validation phase yielded exceptional inter-observer agreement, with ICCs reaching 0.997 and 0.988 for pancreatic and splenic attenuations, respectively.

Missing Data Handling

Missing data among clustering variables were assessed before model development. Because FPG and TG showed skewed distributions, logarithmic transformation was applied before imputation and clustering. Missing values were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with predictive mean matching (PMM), generating five complete datasets. The first imputed dataset was subsequently used for k-means clustering after Z-score standardization of all clustering variables.

Unsupervised Machine Learning and Cluster Analysis

To objectively discover underlying clinical phenotypes without predefined clinical thresholds, we applied an unsupervised k-means clustering algorithm. The multidimensional model integrated five continuous pathophysiological variables: Age (representing aging), BMI (systemic adiposity), P-S difference (a quantitative imaging marker of IPFD), FPG, and TG (metabolic function). Among these variables, FPG and TG were included as phenotype-defining features rather than independent outcomes, and therefore subsequent comparisons of these parameters across clusters were interpreted as descriptive characteristics contributing to cluster identification rather than independent metabolic associations. Prior to algorithmic training, the imputed clustering dataset underwent Z-score standardization to ensure uniform dimensional weighting.

The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined through a dual-evaluation strategy using the Elbow method based on total within-cluster sum of squares (WSS) and the Silhouette method assessing cluster separation. The WSS values for k=2, 3, 4, and 5 were 2301.94, 2019.17, 1827.85, and 1665.19, respectively, showing a marked reduction up to k=3 with diminishing improvement thereafter. The three-cluster solution was selected, with an average silhouette width of 0.156, based on the combined consideration of statistical criteria and clinical interpretability.

Internal Validation and Statistical Analysis

Given the data-driven nature of this study, the internal stability of the derived clusters was rigorously evaluated using bootstrap resampling techniques.11 We performed 500 bootstrap iterations to calculate cluster-wise Jaccard similarity indices, aiming to confirm that the identified phenotypes represented genuine biological patterns rather than stochastic data overfitting.

Subsequent phenotypic comparisons across the identified clusters were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables, and Chi-square tests for categorical variables. The normality assumption for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For variables showing significant differences across clusters, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s test after ANOVA or Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction after Kruskal–Wallis testing, as appropriate. Binary logistic regression models were constructed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between cluster membership and predefined clinical metabolic outcomes, including T2DM, CHD, MASLD, and HTN. Cluster 3 (Control phenotype) was specified as the reference group. Because the clustering algorithm itself incorporated age, BMI, P–S difference, fasting plasma glucose, and triglycerides as input variables, additional adjustment for these variables was not performed to avoid over-adjustment and distortion of cluster-outcome associations. Given the exploratory nature of this unsupervised clustering study, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The final sample size was determined by the availability of eligible participants during the predefined study period (January 2015 to December 2025). Cluster robustness was additionally evaluated using bootstrap resampling with Jaccard similarity indices. All statistical analyses were executed using R software (version 4.2.0), and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cluster 1 (Obese phenotype) represented a predominantly younger demographic (median age 50.00 years) characterized by the highest degree of systemic adiposity (median BMI 28.51 kg/m2). Despite the higher BMI, this cluster showed relatively less pronounced IPFD and comparatively favorable glycemic characteristics.

In stark contrast, Cluster 2 (FPD-like phenotype) exhibited the highest level of IPFD, reflected by the lowest P–S difference values, with a median value of −74.00 HU. Paradoxically, this profound ectopic fat accumulation occurred despite a significantly lower systemic BMI (median 26.45 kg/m2) when compared to Cluster 1. This subgroup comprised older individuals (median age 70.00 years) who showed higher levels of FPG and TG; however, these differences should be interpreted as descriptive characteristics because both variables contributed directly to cluster construction.

Cluster 3 (Control phenotype) was characterized by lower BMI and less IPFD compared with the other clusters, comprising individuals with the lowest BMI (24.24 kg/m2), minimal intrapancreatic fat infiltration, and the most favorable overall metabolic parameters. Collectively, the morphometric distributions across core clinical variables (shown in Figure 3A–D) and the integrated radial profile (shown in Figure 3E) illustrate a phenotypic dissociation between generalized adiposity and IPFD.

While both pathological clusters were independently associated with MASLD, the magnitude of risk was disproportionately higher in the Obese phenotype (OR 4.31; P < 0.001) compared to the FPD-like phenotype (OR 2.53; P < 0.001). These findings suggest that different phenotypes may exhibit distinct patterns of metabolic comorbidity profiles.

Correlation analyses (Supplementary Table S1) revealed that intrapancreatic fat accumulation was intrinsically linked to an adverse circulating lipid milieu. Consistent with this, patients in Cluster 2 (FPD-like phenotype) exhibited the highest circulating levels of FFA (P = 0.018; Figure 5A). Additionally, both pathological clusters demonstrated significantly depleted HDL-C concentrations compared to the control group (P < 0.001; Figure 5C).

Interestingly, the distribution of serum uric acid provided further evidence of phenotypic decoupling (Figure 5B). Evaluated as a comparative biomarker representing systemic metabolic load, uric acid demonstrated a significant positive correlation with systemic BMI (r = 0.17, P < 0.001, Supplementary Table S1), suggesting an association between BMI-related adiposity and UA levels within our cohort. Consistently, the Obese phenotype (Cluster 1) displayed pronounced hyperuricemia (P = 0.0065). This nuanced biomarker divergence aligns with our pathophysiological framework: whereas generalized adiposity (Cluster 1) is predominantly associated with systemic hyperuricemia and hepatic steatosis, localized intrapancreatic fat (Cluster 2) is uniquely accompanied by elevated FFA, representing a distinct circulating lipid profile observed in this phenotype.

Discussion

In the present study, we applied an unsupervised machine learning approach to explore the clinical heterogeneity associated with IPFD. Our analysis identified three exploratory clinical clusters that differed in age, generalized adiposity, pancreatic fat burden, and metabolic characteristics. Specifically, the cluster characterized by greater IPFD showed less favorable metabolic characteristics than the other clusters, including a higher prevalence of T2DM.1 These findings suggest that traditional anthropometric measures, such as BMI, may not fully capture the heterogeneity of IPFD-related clinical characteristics and associated metabolic profiles. Consistent with the Melbourne Consensus,6 these findings support the consideration of IPFD as an organ-specific ectopic fat phenotype that may provide complementary information beyond conventional anthropometric assessment.

The clinical contrast between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 illustrates the heterogeneity of clinical phenotypes associated with IPFD and highlights that IPFD may not be fully captured by generalized adiposity measures such as BMI. This observation is consistent with the Melbourne Consensus, which emphasizes that IPFD represents an organ-specific fat deposition process that may occur independently of overall body fat accumulation.6 Despite having the highest BMI, individuals in Cluster 1 had relatively preserved glycemic profiles, a pattern that may be consistent with metabolically healthy obesity.17,25 In contrast, Cluster 2 was characterized by lower BMI, older age, greater IPFD, and less favorable glycemic and lipid-related measures. According to the Melbourne Consensus framework,6 excessive IPFD has been conceptualized as an organ-specific pathological state referred to as fatty pancreas disorder (FPD). However, the observed relationship between excessive IPFD and metabolic abnormalities may reflect complex interactions among aging, adipose tissue distribution, metabolic status, and other unmeasured factors rather than a direct causal pathway. Given the cross-sectional design of our study and the use of CT-derived P–S difference as a surrogate marker of IPFD, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of a causal role of IPFD in metabolic deterioration. This pattern is broadly consistent with the adipose tissue expandability hypothesis, whereby limited subcutaneous lipid-storage capacity may be associated with increased ectopic fat deposition.26 In our cohort, Cluster 1 was associated with higher serum UA concentrations, whereas Cluster 2 had higher circulating FFA levels. These differences may reflect distinct metabolic profiles related to generalized adiposity and pancreatic fat accumulation. However, because FPG and TG were included as clustering variables, between-cluster differences in glycemic and lipid-related outcomes should be interpreted primarily as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than as fully independent confirmation of metabolic risk.

The older age of participants in Cluster 2 may also be relevant to the observed phenotype. Aging has been associated with changes in adipose tissue distribution and a greater tendency toward visceral and ectopic fat accumulation.27,28 Experimental studies have suggested that IPFD may be associated with alterations in islet function through mechanisms involving local lipid exposure, inflammatory signaling, and impaired insulin secretion.7,29 Although the elevated FFA levels observed in Cluster 2 are compatible with an altered lipid metabolic profile, our cross-sectional findings cannot determine whether IPFD contributes to these metabolic abnormalities, results from metabolic dysfunction, or reflects shared underlying factors. Therefore, the observed coexistence of greater IPFD, elevated FFA levels, and impaired glycemic characteristics should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence of a direct causal relationship. These findings may instead reflect shared metabolic susceptibility, age-related changes, or other unmeasured factors and warrant confirmation in longitudinal and mechanistic studies.30

These findings may have implications for metabolic risk assessment. Current clinical evaluation often relies on BMI and waist circumference to identify individuals at elevated metabolic risk. Our findings suggest that some older adults with normal or moderately elevated BMI may have substantial pancreatic fat accumulation and unfavorable metabolic characteristics. Accordingly, CT-derived IPFD measures may provide complementary information to conventional anthropometric indices in selected clinical settings. Opportunistic assessment of pancreatic fat on routine non-contrast abdominal CT may be feasible; however, its clinical implementation requires careful consideration of incremental clinical benefit, radiation exposure, cost-effectiveness, workflow feasibility, and measurement reproducibility before routine adoption.31

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although participants were identified over a 10-year period, the cross-sectional design captures associations at a single time point and cannot determine the temporal sequence between IPFD and metabolic abnormalities. It remains unclear whether IPFD precedes metabolic dysregulation, develops as a consequence of metabolic alterations, or shares common upstream determinants. Longitudinal studies are required to clarify the directionality of these associations. Second, the CT-derived P–S difference is an accessible and validated surrogate measure of IPFD; however, it does not provide the absolute quantification of pancreatic fat available with MRI-derived proton density fat fraction.14 Third, the cluster solution demonstrated moderate internal stability, with Jaccard similarity indices ranging from 0.451 to 0.697 across clusters. Although these findings support partial reproducibility of the identified patterns, the stability was not uniformly strong, particularly for Cluster 2. Therefore, the derived clusters should be interpreted as exploratory clinical profiles rather than definitive biological subtypes. The observed overlap between clusters may reflect the continuous nature of aging, adiposity, and ectopic fat accumulation, and external validation in independent cohorts is required to determine the reproducibility and clinical applicability of these phenotypes. Fourth, because FPG and TG were included as input variables for cluster derivation, differences in glycemic and lipid-related characteristics across clusters should be interpreted cautiously. These variables contributed directly to the construction of the phenotypes and therefore cannot be considered independent outcomes of clustering. Future studies incorporating external metabolic variables not used for clustering will be important to further validate the metabolic distinctiveness of the identified phenotypes. Fifth, this retrospective single-center study may limit the generalizability of our findings. The identified phenotypes require validation in independent cohorts involving diverse populations and healthcare settings before broader clinical application. Finally, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Although major medication-related influences were minimized through exclusion criteria, incomplete information regarding medication history, lifestyle factors such as physical activity and dietary habits, and other unmeasured metabolic determinants may have influenced both IPFD and metabolic characteristics.

In conclusion, our data identified three exploratory clinical clusters associated with IPFD, including a cluster characterized by greater IPFD, older age, and lower BMI than the obesity-dominant cluster, which was associated with less favorable metabolic characteristics. These findings are consistent with the concept that IPFD represents an organ-specific ectopic fat phenotype and may provide complementary information beyond conventional anthropometric measures such as BMI. However, given the exploratory nature and cross-sectional design of this study, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causality. External validation in independent populations and prospective longitudinal studies are required to confirm the reproducibility and clinical relevance of these phenotypes.
