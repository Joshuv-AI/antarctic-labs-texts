Sensitivity Analysis on the Sphere and a Spherical ANOVA Decomposition
Laura Weidensager

Abstract
We establish sensitivity analysis on the sphere. We present formulas that allow us to decompose a function f : 𝕊 d → ℝ f\colon\mathbb{S}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} into a sum of terms f 𝒖 , 𝝃 f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}} . The index 𝒖 \bm{u} is a subset of { 1 , 2 , … , d + 1 } \{1,2,\ldots,d+1\} , where each term f 𝒖 , 𝝃 f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}} depends only on the variables with indices in 𝒖 \bm{u} . In contrast to the classical analysis of variance (ANOVA) decomposition, we additionally use the decomposition of a function into functions with different parity, which adds the additional parameter 𝝃 \bm{\xi} . The natural geometry on the sphere naturally leads to the dependencies between the input variables. Using certain orthogonal basis functions for the function approximation, we are able to model high-dimensional functions with low-dimensional variable interactions. Keywords ANOVA decomposition, high-dimensional sphere, orthogonal polynomials Mathematics Subject Classification (2020) – 41A10, 41A63, 65D15, 65D40

Introduction

Functions on the sphere arise in diverse applications, ranging from geophysics to quantum mechanics. To efficiently analyze and approximate such functions, a deep understanding of their internal structure is essential.
The ANOVA (analysis of variance) decomposition provides a remarkably elegant framework for understanding the structure of multivariate functions. In the classical setting, where the domain is a tensor product space, the decomposition exhibits a particularly transparent form: component functions corresponding to different variable subsets are orthogonal, and the hierarchical interaction structure can be characterized cleanly. However, when the domain is the high-dimensional sphere, this clarity gets lost. The lack of a natural product structure complicates both the definition and computation of ANOVA components, making the extension of classical results to spherical domains highly nontrivial.

In this paper, we develop a spherical ANOVA decomposition that allows us to split the total variance of the function into contributions of specific groups of variables. This approach not only yields valuable insights into variable importance but also forms the theoretical foundation for adaptive approximation schemes.

The key difference to the classical decomposition is that we do not have a tensor product structure of the domain here, which makes modeling more difficult and forces the introduction of additional parameters and structures. We found that the decomposition of the function into terms of different parity is a key ingredient for the spherical ANOVA decomposition.

Our main contributions

•

We propose a spherical ANOVA decomposition for the uniform distribution on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Due to the structure of the sphere, we have to additionally distinguish between even and odd terms and therefore introduce a parity vector.

•

We introduce reduced orthogonal bases to describe the lower dimensional terms, which we use to implement high-dimensional function approximation on the sphere. Additionally, we define Sobol indices on the sphere in order to apply sensitivity analysis for the spherical ANOVA decomposition.

•

We show in numerical examples that the spherical ANOVA decomposition captures the expected non-zero spherical ANOVA terms, similar to how one would expect the terms from the classical ANOVA decomposition.

However, extending the ANOVA decomposition from a tensor product domain to the sphere leads to theoretical challenges arising from the geometry of the domain. In Section
2.4
, we recall this classical ANOVA decomposition. The intrinsic curvature of the sphere automatically induces variable interactions. Even if the function has a simple structure in cartesian coordinates, the manifold’s constraints enforce coupling between variables, effectively creating artificial interactions. Second, the natural algebraic dependence of the variables (i.e.,
\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}}\right\rVert=1
) implies that the inputs cannot vary independently. Consequently, it is generally impossible to construct a decomposition consisting of strictly orthogonal terms in the classical sense.

The analysis of functions on the sphere, the ball and the simplex are related. Here, we focus on the analysis of the sphere, but the key idea can be transferred to the unit ball and the simplex. Due to the different geometry on the unit ball and the simplex, the dependencies between the ANOVA terms must be considered carefully. So, we leave the considerations of our approach on the ball and the simplex open for future work.

Related work

Several alternative approaches for dimension reduction and approximation on the sphere exist. Geometric approaches such as those described in
[
4
]
attempt to approximate the data using optimal (rotated) submanifolds. Global polynomial approximation methods for scattered data typically require a number of samples exponential in
d
, as described in
[
9
]
.

Employing the Dirac measure for high-dimensional approximation leads to the anchored ANOVA decomposition, see for example
[
18
]
. Due to the geometric properties on the sphere, it is not possible to to generalize this decomposition to the sphere.

In the context of sensitivity analysis, quantifying the influence of groups of variables remains a challenging task when inputs are correlated. As recently discussed by
[
19
]
, defining the explicit contribution of subsets of variables requires careful consideration in the presence of dependence.
Recent advances in global sensitivity analysis for dependent variables also include kernel-embedding techniques and Shapley effects,
[
5
]
as well as random Fourier features,
[
28
]
.

To date, no dedicated ANOVA decomposition for the sphere has been established in the literature. The approach proposed in this work fills this gap and aims to identify the importance of the original physical variables and their specific interactions.

2.2
Spherical harmonics

A polynomial
p\colon\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
homogeneous of degree
\ell
if

p(r\bm{x})=r^{\ell}p(\bm{x})\quad\text{for all }r>0,\ \bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}^{d}
denote the space of real homogeneous polynomials of degree
\ell
. A polynomial is called
harmonic
if it satisfies the Laplace equation

\Delta p=0,\qquad\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}.

For
\ell=0,1,\ldots,
we denote the linear spaces of real harmonic polynomials of degree
\ell
by

\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\ell}\coloneqq\{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}^{d}\mid\Delta p=0\}.

The restriction of a homogeneous harmonic polynomial
p
of degree
\ell
to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is called a
spherical harmonic of degree
\ell
.
We denote the corresponding space by

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\coloneqq\bigl\{\,p|_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\;\colon\;p\text{ homogeneous, harmonic, and of degree }\ell\,\bigr\},

see for example
[
6
,
7
]
for more details about these spaces.
The spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
form an orthogonal decomposition

L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d}),

and any
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
admits the spherical harmonic expansion

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\dim(\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))}\hat{f}_{\ell,m}\,Y_{\ell,m}(\bm{x}),

where
\{Y_{\ell,m}\}_{m=1}^{\dim(\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
and
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))
denotes its dimension.

Remark 2.2

(Polynomial representatives and coordinate dependence)
.

Each spherical harmonic
Y_{\ell,m}\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is the restriction of a
homogeneous harmonic polynomial
P_{\ell,m}:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
\ell
, i.e.

Y_{\ell,m}(\bm{x})=P_{\ell,m}(\bm{x})\big|_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}.

The polynomial
P_{\ell,m}
can be expressed as a linear combination of monomials

x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\,x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{d+1}^{\alpha_{d+1}},\qquad|\bm{\alpha}|:=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d+1}=\ell,

whose coefficients are determined by the harmonicity condition
\Delta P_{\ell,m}=0
.
Thus, every spherical harmonic of degree
\ell
involves monomials of total degree
\ell
in the Cartesian coordinates.
In particular, the index
\ell
corresponds to the total polynomial degree, while the secondary index
m
enumerates the independent harmonic combinations of these monomials, forming a basis of the space

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d})=\operatorname{span}\bigl\{\,P_{\ell,m}(\bm{x})\big|_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}:1\leq m\leq\dim(\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))\,\bigr\}.

Consequently, each
Y_{\ell,m}
generally depends on all coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1}
, though with specific parity and symmetry relations determined by the monomial exponents
\bm{\alpha}
.

For
d=2
,
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))=2
. In polar coordinates
(x_{1},x_{2})=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, this basis is given by

Y_{\ell}^{(1)}=r^{\ell}\cos(\ell\theta),\quad Y_{\ell}^{(2)}=r^{\ell}\sin(\ell\theta).

(2.8)

2.3
Decomposition into odd and even parts

Let the domain be
\mathbb{D}\in\{\mathbb{S}^{d},\mathbb{B}^{d+1}\}
. We analyze the parity properties of the underlying functions. In the one-dimensional case, a function
f
is defined as
odd
if
f(-x)=-f(x)
and as
even
if
f(-x)=f(x)
almost everywhere. Then, an arbitrary function
f
is decomposed into its even and odd parts by

f(x)=f_{\mathrm{e}}(x)+f_{\mathrm{o}}(x),\qquad f_{\mathrm{e}}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(f(x)+f(-x)),\quad f_{\mathrm{o}}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(f(x)-f(-x)).

Similarly, we do this for a function on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
or
\mathbb{B}^{d+1}
by using the parity vector
\bm{\xi}=(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}\in\{0,1\}^{d+1}
and defining

\displaystyle f(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{d+1}}[\Xi_{\bm{\xi}}f](\bm{x}),

(2.9)

\displaystyle\Xi_{\bm{\xi}}\colon L_{2}(\mathbb{D})\rightarrow L_{2}(\mathbb{D})\quad\text{ with }\quad[\Xi_{\bm{\xi}}f](\bm{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2^{d+1}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\{-1,1\}^{d+1}}\left(\underbrace{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d+1}k_{i}^{\xi_{i}}\right)}_{\in\{-1,1\}}f(\bm{k}\odot\bm{x})\right)

where
\xi_{i}=1
belongs to an odd function in direction
i
and
\xi_{i}=0
belongs to an even function in direction
i
. In (
2.9
) the construction of the terms is exactly such that one factor
(-1)
is added for every
i\in[d+1]
, for which
k_{i}=-1
and
\xi_{i}=1
.

Definition 2.3
.

We denote the vector
\bm{\xi}
as the
parity
of the function
g
, if for all
i\in[d+1]
, for almost all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{D}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\xi_{i}=0&\Leftrightarrow g(\bm{x})=g(\bm{x}_{[d+1]\backslash i},-x_{i}),\\
\xi_{i}=1&\Leftrightarrow g(\bm{x})=-g(\bm{x}_{[d+1]\backslash i},-x_{i}).\end{split}

For
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{D})
we define the corresponding function spaces

L_{2}(\bm{\xi})=\{f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{D})\mid f\text{ has parity }\bm{\xi}\}.

The spaces
L_{2}(\bm{\xi})
are orthogonal, i.e.

L_{2}(\mathbb{D})=\bigoplus_{\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{d+1}}L_{2}(\bm{\xi}).

2.4
The classical ANOVA decomposition

The classical ANOVA (analysis of variance) decomposition was introduced by Hoeffding
[
13
]
and has been studied in many contexts, see for example
[
8
,
16
,
34
,
24
,
18
]
and references therein. Often this model is also called high-dimensional model representation, see also
[
29
]
for an overview.
The ANOVA decomposition decomposes a
d
-variate function into
2^{d}
ANOVA terms where each term belongs to a subset
\bm{u}\subseteq[d]

of coordinate indices. Each single term only depends on the variables in the corresponding subset and the number of
these variables is the order of the ANOVA term. The ANOVA decomposition is a valuable tool for understanding the structure and behavior of functions and enables to focus on the most significant factors when constructing approximation models.
It has extensively been used for the analysis of quasi-Monte Carlo methods for integration, see e.g.
[
3
,
20
,
23
,
33
,
11
]
and references therein. Applications to sparse grids can be found in
[
2
,
10
,
12
,
14
]
.

Let
\mathbb{D}^{d}\in\{\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d},[0,1]^{d}\}
be a tensor product domain, for a function
f\colon\mathbb{D}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the
ANOVA decomposition
(Analysis of variance) is given by

f(\bm{x})=f_{\varnothing}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{\{i\}}(x_{i})+\sum_{i\neq j=1}^{d}f_{\{i,j\}}(x_{i},x_{j})+\cdots+f_{[d]}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{u}\subseteq[d]}f_{\bm{u}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}).

(2.10)

It is common to refer to the terms
f_{\bm{u}}
where
|\bm{u}|=q
collectively as the
order-
q
terms
. In general, there are many possibilities for a decomposition (
2.10
). Indeed, an arbitrary choice of
f_{\bm{u}}
for all
|\bm{u}|<d
can be accommodated by taking
f_{[d]}
to be
f

minus all the other terms. But with some additional conditions the decomposition is unique.
Typically, one demands orthogonality of the involved terms with respect to some density
\mu
.
Examples for the application of the classical ANOVA decomposition can be found in:

•

For periodic functions on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, trigonometrical polynomials are used as basis in
[
27
]
and wavelet function in
[
22
]
.

•

On the cube
[0,1]
the half-period cosine basis was studied in
[
26
]
.

•

For the use of random Fourier features for the generalized ANOVA decomposition on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
see
[
28
]
.

The classical ANOVA decomposition has the following properties

•

The density
\mu(\bm{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}(x_{i})
is a tensor product density.

•

For the definition of the terms a projection operator is used, which integrates the function over all variables not in
\bm{u}
by

P_{\bm{u}}f=\left[\prod_{i\in\bm{u}}P_{i}\right]f=\int_{\mathbb{D}^{|d\backslash\bm{u}|}}f(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\left(\prod_{i\in[d]\backslash\bm{u}}\mu_{i}(x_{i})\right).

(2.11)

•

The ANOVA terms are defined by

f_{\bm{u}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})=\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{u}}(-1)^{|\bm{u}|-|\bm{v}|}P_{\bm{v}}f.

(2.12)

•

All terms are orthogonal:
0=\langle f_{\bm{u}},f_{\bm{v}}\rangle_{\mu}
for all
\bm{u}\neq\bm{v}
. This is a necessary condition for sensitivity analysis, where the total variance of the function is the sum of the variances of the ANOVA terms.

For a generalization of the classical decomposition to some non-tensor product densities see for example
[
15
,
21
,
30
,
28
]
. The needed assumption there is that the domain is grid-closed to maintain at least some tensor product structures. But this assumption is not fulfilled on the sphere. For this reason, the aim of this paper is to define a spherical ANOVA decomposition for functions
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on the sphere, whereby we want to retain as many properties of the classical decomposition as possible.
For the following reasons we can not apply the classical or generalized ANOVA decomposition directly to the sphere:

•

Using the classical decomposition on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
or
[0,1]^{d+1}
is not even with the generalized ANOVA decomposition possible, since
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is not a grid-closed domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Problems arise at the intersection points of the axes with the sphere.

•

Using spherical coordinates would lead to the loss of interpretability of the variables, which is a key advantage of the ANOVA decomposition.

•

For tensor product domains, the domain of the lower dimensional functions
f_{\bm{u}}
are the lower-dimensional tensor product domains. For functions on the sphere this is not possible. If
f_{\bm{u}}\colon\mathbb{S}^{|\bm{u}|-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, these functions have to be extended to higher dimensional spheres. We will later define the spherical terms
f_{\bm{u}}\colon\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, defined on lower dimensional balls, such that they are automatically expanded to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

•

For the classical ANOVA decomposition, one uses an orthonormal basis in
L_{2}
and then finds a connection between the basis functions and the ANOVA terms. This works well because of the tensor product structure of the basis in the
d
-dimensional function space
L_{2}
. In contrast, on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, there is no basis of tensor product form, which can be used for this purpose.

•

Defining the projection operator by
P_{j}(f)=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},c_{j},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{d})
for some anchor point
\bm{c}=(c_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
leads to the anchored ANOVA decomposition, see for example
[
18
]
. Due to the geometric properties on the sphere, it is not possible to generalize this anchored decomposition to the sphere. On the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{d+1}
, the anchored ANOVA decomposition with anchor point
\bm{0}
can be calculated equally to the tensor product domain.

While proposing a spherical ANOVA decomposition in this paper, we will also highlight the differences and challenges in comparison to the classical ANOVA decomposition.

3
A spherical ANOVA decomposition and its properties

Analyzing high-dimensional functions on the sphere poses a significant challenge, as separating local dependencies from global structures is often non-trivial. The ANOVA decomposition provides a systematic framework for this task by hierarchically decomposing the function into main effects and higher-order interactions. This enables us not only to quantify the influence of individual variables but also to understand the underlying coupling structure of the function.

Denote
\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\{\bm{u}\subseteq[d+1]\mid|\bm{u}|\neq d\}
. For a function
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we will study a decomposition of the form

f(\bm{x})=f_{\varnothing}+\sum_{q=1}^{d-1}\sum_{|\bm{u}|=q}f_{\bm{u}}\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}\right)+f_{d+1}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{D}}f_{\bm{u}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}),

where the component functions
f_{\bm{u}}\colon\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are functions on lower-dimensional balls and
f_{d+1}\colon\mathbb{S}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The domain of the functions
f_{\bm{u}}
is the lower-dimensional ball
\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|}
, but naturally with this decomposition, the terms
f_{\bm{u}}
are also defined on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
We do not need terms for the indices
|\bm{u}|=d
, since due to the normalization
\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}}\right\rVert=1
, the last variable would be specified, except for the sign, from fixing all other variables. We treat all these parts of the function
f
in the very last term
f_{[d+1]}
.

We propose to combine the spherical ANOVA decomposition (
3
) with the decomposition (
2.9
) into even and odd parts. We will always use the index
\bm{u}
for the spherical ANOVA terms and the index
\bm{\xi}
for the parity of the term. Remember that
\xi_{i}=1
belongs to an odd function in direction
x_{i}
and
\xi_{i}=0
to an even function in direction
x_{i}
. Since the function

does not depend on the variables

for

, in the term

there can be only terms with parity vector

with
\bm{\xi}_{\bm{u}^{c}}=\bm{0}
. For that reason we introduce the index-set

\Upsilon_{\bm{u}}\coloneqq\{\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{d+1}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\bm{u}^{c}}=\bm{0}\},

where
|\Upsilon_{\bm{u}}|=2^{|\bm{u}|}
.
We study in this paper the decomposition of the form

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{\bm{\xi}\in\Upsilon_{\bm{u}}}f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}).

(3.1)

In this decomposition we have in total

\sum_{q=0}^{d-1}\binom{d+1}{q}2^{q}+2^{d+1}=\sum_{q=0}^{d+1}\binom{d+1}{q}2^{q}-2^{d}\,(d+1)=3^{d+1}-2^{d}\,(d+1)

(3.2)

spherical ANOVA terms.

Every function

Definition 3.5

\displaystyle=\left[P_{\bm{u}}f\right](\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})-\sum_{\bm{v}\subset\bm{u}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f](\bm{x}_{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle[\mathcal{A}_{[d+1]}f](\bm{x})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{x})-\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{D}\backslash[d+1]}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f](\bm{x}_{\bm{v}}).

In this definition the terms

\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f\colon\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
depend only on the variables
\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}
.

Another formula for the terms, which follows by induction directly from the iterative definition of the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}
is

\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f=\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{u}}(-1)^{|\bm{u}|-|\bm{v}|}P_{\bm{v}}f.

(3.5)

This is similar to the definition of the classical ANOVA decomposition, see Section
2.4
.
Additionally, all terms sum up to the original function, and we obtain the decomposition on the sphere,

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f](\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}).

(3.6)

A closer look at Definition
3.5
and equation (
3.6
) reveals that, for the definition to be well-defined, for any function
f
consisting of a single term,
f=\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f
, it must hold that

\mathcal{A}_{\bm{a}}f=\begin{cases}f&\text{ if }\bm{u}=\bm{a},\\
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

This automatically means by (
3.5
) that for
\bm{a}\neq\varnothing
and a function
f
with
f=\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f
,

\left[P_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f\right](\bm{y}_{\bm{a}})=\begin{cases}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f](\bm{y}_{\bm{u}})&\text{ if }\bm{a}\supseteq\bm{u}\\
0&\text{ in all other cases}.\\
\end{cases}

(3.7)

The terms defined in Definition
3.5
are orthogonal with respect to the uniform measure on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, which the following theorem shows.

Theorem 3.6
.

For
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathcal{D}
and
\bm{v}\neq\bm{u}
the terms
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f
and
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f
, defined in Definition
3.5
, are orthogonal with respect to the surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
,

\langle\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f,\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}=0.

Proof.

Set
\bm{w}=\bm{u}\cap\bm{v}
. If
\bm{w}=\varnothing
, we skip the following first step and proceed with the inner integral. Otherwise let without loss of generality
\bm{u}\backslash\bm{w}\neq\varnothing
, then

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f,\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}\right)[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{v}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:split_integral}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{w}|}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{|\bm{w}^{c}|-1}_{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}\right)[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{v}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{d-|\bm{w}|}(\bm{x}_{\bm{w}^{c}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}.

For the inner integral we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{|\bm{w}^{c}|-1}_{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}\right)[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{v}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{d-|\bm{w}|}(\bm{x}_{\bm{w}^{c}}),\quad b\coloneqq\sqrt{1-\frac{\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}\backslash\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert^{2}}{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}},

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:split_integral_r}}}{{=}}\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}^{|\bm{w}^{c}|-1}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}\backslash\bm{w}|}}\frac{1}{b}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}_{b}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}\right)[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{v}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}^{c}}}{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\frac{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}\backslash\bm{w}}}{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad=\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}^{|\bm{w}^{c}|-1}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}\backslash\bm{w}|}}\frac{1}{b}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}_{b}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{v}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}^{c}}}{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\frac{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}\backslash\bm{w}}}{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}.

Again, we proceed with the inner integral by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}_{b}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f]\left(\bm{x}_{\bm{v}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}^{c}}}{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}^{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}_{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f](\bm{x}_{\bm{v}})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}^{c}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{w}}}\right\rVert^{2}}^{|\bm{u}^{c}|-1}\,m_{\bm{u}}(\bm{y}_{\bm{u}})}\,[P_{\bm{u}}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{v}}f]](\bm{x}_{\bm{w}})=0.

These terms are zero due to (
3.7
).
∎

For the classical ANOVA decomposition with tensor product structure, it is enough to require that
P_{i}\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f=0
for all
i\in\bm{u}
due to the tensor product structure (
2.11
) of the operator
P_{\bm{u}}
. On the sphere, these conditions are slightly more complicated since the projection operator
P_{\bm{u}}
is not the product of one-dimensional projections.

Lemma 3.7
.

The operators
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}
in Definition
3.5
fulfill condition (
3.7
) if the integral conditions

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}\backslash\bm{a}|}}\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}\backslash\bm{a}}}\right\rVert^{2}}^{d-|\bm{u}|-1}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f]\left(\bm{y}_{\bm{a}},\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{\bm{a}}}\right\rVert^{2}}\bm{x}_{\bm{u}\backslash\bm{a}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{\bm{u}\backslash\bm{a}}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\text{ for all }\bm{a}\subset\bm{u},\bm{y}_{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{a}|}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{\bm{a}}}\right\rVert^{2}-r^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}\backslash\bm{a}|}}\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}\backslash\bm{a}}}\right\rVert^{2}}^{m}[\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f]\left(\bm{y}_{\bm{a}},\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{\bm{a}}}\right\rVert^{2}-r^{2}}\,\bm{x}_{\bm{u}\backslash\bm{a}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{\bm{u}\backslash\bm{a}},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\text{ for all }\bm{a}\subset\bm{u},\bm{y}_{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{a}|},0\leq r\leq\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{\bm{a}}}\right\rVert^{2}},m=-1,0,1,\ldots,d-|\bm{u}|-2

are fulfilled.

Proof.

In Lemma
3.4
we applied the projection operator
P_{\bm{a}}
to a function, which depends only on the variables
\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}
. The function
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f
is such a function. Hence, this lemma follows directly from Lemma
3.4
.
∎

These conditions are similar to the integral conditions for the classical ANOVA decomposition, where we fix one
\bm{y}_{\bm{a}}
in
f_{\bm{u}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})
and integrate over all other variables. These integrals then vanish for all
\bm{y}_{\bm{a}}
. Shortly, the ANOVA terms have to fulfill (
3.7
). For the classical ANOVA decomposition due to the tensor product structure (
2.11
), it is enough to demand
P_{\{i\}}f_{\bm{u}}
for all
i\in\bm{u}
. In the spherical case, this is much more complicated and we have to demand the integral conditions in Lemma
3.7
. As we will see in the next subsection, only odd functions
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f
fulfill these conditions.

3.3
The parity of the terms
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f

In Definition
3.5
we defined operators
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}
, which split a spherical function into lower dimensional terms. In Lemma
3.7
we showed integral conditions that the terms
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}f
have to fulfill. In fact, only odd functions fulfill these conditions.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let a function
g\in L_{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
, where
n=|\bm{u}|\leq d-1
.
That the function
g
fulfills the integral conditions (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) is equivalent to the function
g
being odd in every direction.

Proof.

If the function
g
is odd, it easily follows that (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) hold true, so one direction follows directly.

For the other direction, let
g\in L_{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
be arbitrary and fulfill the integral conditions (
3.8
) and (
3.9
).
Then, decompose
g
into its even and odd parts
g_{\bm{\xi}}
like in (
2.9
).

Let us fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then, (
3.9
) for
\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i}
and
m=0
states that

0=\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i}}\right\rVert^{2}-r^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}g\left(\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i},\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i}}\right\rVert^{2}-r^{2}}\,x_{i}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}

for all
0\leq r\leq\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i}}\right\rVert^{2}}
.
Using the decomposition (
2.9
) of
g
into odd and even parts, in this integral of the terms
g_{\bm{\xi}}
with
\xi_{i}=1
are zero, such that we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i}}\right\rVert^{2}-r^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}}{{\xi_{i}=0}}}g_{\bm{\xi}}\left(\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i},\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i}}\right\rVert^{2}-r^{2}}\,x_{i}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i}}\right\rVert^{2}-r^{2}}}^{\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i}}\right\rVert^{2}-r^{2}}}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}}{{\xi_{i}=0}}}g_{\bm{\xi}}\left(\bm{y}_{[n]\backslash i},x_{i}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}.

That means, that the function
G\coloneqq\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}}{{\xi_{i}=0}}}g_{\bm{\xi}}
is orthogonal to all step functions of the form
\mathbf{1}_{[-r,r]}(x_{i})
. The symmetric indicator functions of arbitrary step functions are a linear combination of the indicator functions of the form
\mathbf{1}_{[-r,r]}(t)
. Furthermore, it follows that
G
is also orthogonal to all step functions on
\mathbb{B}^{n}
, which are supported on a tensor-product domain and are even in
x_{i}
direction. Since step functions are dense in
L_{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n})
, it follows that

\langle G,h\rangle_{L_{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}=0\quad\text{for all even }h\in L_{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n})\text{ with }h(\bm{x})=h(\bm{x}_{[n]\backslash i},x_{i})\text{\,a.e.}

Hence
G
must vanish almost everywhere. Inserting the definitions of the functions
g_{\bm{\xi}}
from (
2.9
) into
G
gives then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}}{{\xi_{i}=0}}}g_{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}}{{\xi_{i}=0}}}\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}k_{\ell}^{\xi_{\ell}}\right)g(\bm{k}\odot\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}}{{\xi_{i}=0}}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}k_{\ell}^{\xi_{\ell}}\right)g(\bm{k}\odot\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}g(\bm{k}\odot\bm{x})\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}}{{\xi_{i}=0}}}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}k_{\ell}^{\xi_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}g(\bm{k}\odot\bm{x})\sum_{\bm{\xi}_{[n]\backslash i}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}\left(\prod_{\ell\neq i}k_{\ell}^{\xi_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\left(\underbrace{2^{n}g(\bm{x})}_{k=\bm{1}}+\underbrace{2^{n}g(\bm{x}_{[n]\backslash i},-x_{i})}_{k=\bm{1}-2\bm{e}_{i}}\right),

since all the other terms cancel out. It follows directly that

g(\bm{x})=-g(\bm{x}_{[n]\backslash i},-x_{i})
.
Similarly, this is true for all other
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, such that
g
is odd in all directions.
∎

3.4
The definition of the spherical ANOVA decomposition

While the decomposition into even and odd parts is straightforward in Euclidean space, the algebraic constraint of the sphere introduces specific dependencies between the variables. Here we discuss how multivariate parity impacts the linear independence of the basis terms and identify which symmetry patterns lead to redundant representations.

The ANOVA operators
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}
from Definition
3.5
produce only odd terms. This means, in the spherical ANOVA decomposition (
3.1
) only terms of the form
f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
with
\bm{\xi}=(\bm{1}_{\bm{u}},\bm{0}_{\bm{u}^{c}})
are produced by applying
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}
to the function
f
itself and all even parts of a function
f
are contained in the last term
\mathcal{A}_{[d+1]}f
.

To include the even terms also in the lower-dimensional terms of the spherical ANOVA decomposition, we propose to define a spherical ANOVA decomposition of the form (
3.1
) by the following.

Definition 3.9
.

For a function
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
we use the decomposition into even and odd parts as defined in (
2.9
) and the projection operator defined in (
3.4
). With these ingredients we introduce the spherical ANOVA decomposition of the form (
3.1
) by

1. The constant term:

\displaystyle f_{\varnothing,\bm{0}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq P_{\varnothing}f=\frac{1}{\omega_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{d}(\bm{x}).

2. The mixed terms:

For all
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{D}\backslash\varnothing
and
\bm{u}\supseteq\bm{v}
set
\bm{\xi}=(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})\in\{0,1\}^{d+1}
. If
d+1\notin\bm{v}
, then
d+1\notin\bm{u}
and let

\displaystyle f_{\bm{u},(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq P_{\bm{u}}\left[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\right]-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}})

\displaystyle\text{ if }|\bm{v}|\leq|\bm{u}|\leq d-1,

\displaystyle f_{[d+1],(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\left[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f\right](\bm{x})-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\in\mathcal{D}\backslash[d+1]}f_{\bm{a},(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}}).

3. The pure even terms:
For all
\bm{u}\subset[d]\backslash\varnothing
let

\displaystyle f_{\bm{u},\bm{0}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq P_{\bm{u}}\left[\Xi_{\bm{0}}f\right](\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})-\sum_{\bm{a}\subsetneq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{0}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}}).

We will discuss in Section
3.5
why we do not include the terms where
d+1\in\bm{u}
with
\xi_{d+1}=0
.
Our definition of spherical ANOVA terms is of the form (
3.1
), all the terms
f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
have parity
\bm{\xi}
and

\Xi_{\bm{\xi}}f(\bm{x})=\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bm{a}\in\mathcal{D}}}{{\textrm{supp\,}\bm{\xi}\subseteq\bm{a}}}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}}).

In Example
3.1
we considered the case
d=2
. A visualization of all the possible indices
\bm{u}
and corresponding
\bm{\xi}
for the terms
f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
for

can be found in Figure
2
. There we show the totality of all terms

. Later, in Section

we will explain which of these terms we have to omit due to redundancy on the sphere.

The terms in Definition
3.9
can be calculated more easily by the following.

Lemma 3.10
.

The Definition
3.9
is equivalent to this definition of spherical ANOVA terms,

1. The constant term:

\displaystyle f_{\varnothing,\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=P_{\varnothing}\Xi_{\bm{0}}f=\frac{1}{\omega_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{d}(\bm{x}).

2. The mixed terms:
For all
\varnothing\neq\bm{v}\subseteq[d+1]
and
\bm{u}\supseteq\bm{v}
set
\bm{\xi}=(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})\in\{0,1\}^{d+1}
. If
d+1\notin\bm{v}
, then
d+1\notin\bm{u}
and let

\displaystyle f_{\bm{u},(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subseteq\bm{u}}(-1)^{|\bm{u}|-|\bm{a}|}P_{\bm{a}}[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f].

(3.11)

3. The pure even terms:

For all
\bm{u}\subset[d]\backslash\varnothing
let

\displaystyle f_{\bm{u},\bm{0}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{a}\subseteq\bm{u}}(-1)^{|\bm{u}|-|\bm{a}|}P_{\bm{u}}\left[\Xi_{\bm{0}}f\right](\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}).

Proof.

This proof is similar to the proof of (
2.12
) for the classical ANOVA decomposition using induction:
For
\bm{u}=\bm{v}
the sum in (
3.11
) is just one summand, namely
P_{\bm{v}}[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f]
. For the induction step let (

with

\displaystyle f_{\bm{u},(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{u}}\left[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\right]-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}})

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{u}}\left[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\right]-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}}\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{b}\subseteq\bm{a}}(-1)^{|\bm{a}|-|\bm{b}|}P_{\bm{b}}[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f]

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{u}}\left[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\right]-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{b}\subseteq\bm{u}}\sum_{k=0}^{|\bm{u}\backslash\bm{b}|-1}\binom{|\bm{u}\backslash\bm{b}|}{k}(-1)^{k}P_{\bm{b}}[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f]

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{u}}\left[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\right]+\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{b}\subseteq\bm{u}}(-1)^{|\bm{u}|-|\bm{b}|}P_{\bm{b}}[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{b}\subseteq\bm{u}}(-1)^{|\bm{u}|-|\bm{b}|}P_{\bm{b}}[\Xi_{(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})}f].

For the pure even case, the proof is the same with
\bm{v}=\varnothing
.
∎

In our spherical ANOVA decomposition we have separated the parities and established the hierarchy. Our proposed definition of a spherical ANOVA decomposition has similarities to the classical ANOVA decomposition, whereby we cannot transfer all properties of the classical ANOVA decomposition to the sphere. See Section
6
for example functions and their decomposition into spherical ANOVA terms.

In our decomposition we have some orthogonality for the spherical ANOVA terms, which we will show in the following section.

3.5
Orthogonality and Redundancy

The decomposition into parity parts belonging to the parity vectors
\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{d+1}
, see also (
2.9
), is an orthogonal decomposition, which also means that for the spherical ANOVA terms defined in Definition
3.9
we have the orthogonality for all
\bm{u},\tilde{\bm{u}}
with
\bm{u}\neq\tilde{\bm{u}}
,

\langle f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}},f_{\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\bm{\xi}}}\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}=0\quad\text{ if }\bm{\xi}\neq\tilde{\bm{\xi}}.

Unfortunately, we do not have orthogonality for spherical ANOVA terms with the same parity vector
\bm{\xi}
.
Even worse, the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}x_{i}^{2}=1
on the sphere naturally leads to redundancy of the even terms. Every even function
h(x_{d+1})
can also be written as a
\tilde{h}(x_{d+1}^{2})=\tilde{h}(1-x_{1}^{2}-\cdots-x_{d}^{2})
, which is a function depending on the variables
x_{1}
to
x_{d}
. Additionally,
\tilde{h}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
is even in all directions.

For that reason we propose to omit some terms in the Definition
3.9
. Namely by the substitution

x_{d+1}^{2}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}

we propose in Definition
3.9
to omit all terms, which depend on
x_{d+1}
and where the dependence is even, i.e.,

f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}=0\quad\text{ if }d+1\in\bm{u}\text{ and }\xi_{d+1}=0.

3.6
The challenges with the even terms

The aim of this paper is to construct a spherical ANOVA decomposition of the form (
3.1
), which combines a decomposition into ANOVA terms with the decomposition into parity parts. In Section
3.3
we showed that the terms
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}
should be odd in all directions
i\in\bm{u}
. This means that with the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{u}}
we could only represent functions
f
, which have non-zero spherical ANOVA terms only for
\bm{u}
with
\bm{\xi}=(\bm{1}_{\bm{u}},\bm{0}_{\bm{u}^{c}})
. We also include even parts of the function
f
in our decomposition in Definition
3.9
. We analyze these parts here in more detail.

If the function
f
is a sum of terms, which are odd in all direction, the Definition
3.9
coincides with the decomposition, which we would expect from the classical ANOVA decomposition: Consider for example

f(\bm{x})=\underbrace{x_{1}x_{2}^{3}}_{f_{\{1,2\},\bm{e}_{1}+\bm{e}_{2}}}+\underbrace{2x_{3}x_{4}^{5}}_{f_{\{3,4\},\bm{e}_{3}+\bm{e}_{4}}}+\underbrace{0.05x_{5}}_{f_{\{5\},\bm{e}_{5}}}

for more details about the calculations see
A
. We marked in Figure
2
all the terms, which are odd in all directions. We do not need to do anything else for these terms.

But as soon as some even term is involved, we run into challenges. We begin with an easy example which shows the problems of the underlying dependence of the variables on the sphere.

Example 3.11
.

Let the function be
f(\bm{x})=x_{1}^{2}
, which is a pure even function, which depends only on
x_{1}
, but due to the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}=1
on the sphere, the operators
P_{\bm{u}}
applied to this function give

\displaystyle P_{\varnothing}f

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{d+1}

\displaystyle P_{1}f

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{2},

\displaystyle P_{i}f

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}(1-x_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad i=\{2,\ldots,d+1\},

\displaystyle P_{\{1\}\cup\bm{a}}f

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\text{ for }\bm{a}\subset[d+1]\backslash\{1\},

\displaystyle P_{\{\bm{a}\}}f

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{d-|\bm{a}|}}(1-\left\lVert\smash{x_{\bm{a}}^{2}}\right\rVert)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{|\bm{a}^{c}|-1}}x_{1}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{|\bm{a}^{c}|-1}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}^{c}})=\frac{1}{d+1-|\bm{a}|}\left(1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{a}}}\right\rVert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\text{ for }\bm{a}\subset[d+1]\backslash\{1\}.

For details about the calculation of the involved integrals, see also Appendix
A
.
Despite the function depending only on
x_{1}
, the application of the operator
P_{\bm{u}}
on the function leads to non-constant terms, even if
1\notin\bm{u}
. In this easy example, we would expect that the spherical ANOVA decomposition is
f_{\varnothing,\bm{0}}=\frac{1}{d+1}
,
f_{\{1\},\bm{0}}=x_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{d+1}
and all other terms are zero. The Definition
3.9
with the operator
P_{\bm{u}}
leads to a much more complicated decomposition than the expected one, it introduces unnecessarily additional terms. This is due to the natural dependence of the terms
f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
if some entry of
\bm{\xi}_{\bm{u}}
is zero. Namely, Definition
3.9
leads to

\displaystyle f_{\varnothing,\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{d+1},

\displaystyle f_{\{1\},\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{d+1},

\displaystyle f_{\{i\},\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}(1-x_{i}^{2})-\tfrac{1}{d+1},

\displaystyle f_{\{1,i\},\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=P_{\{1,i\}}f-f_{\{1\},\bm{0}}-f_{\{i\},\bm{0}}-f_{\varnothing,\bm{0}}=-f_{\{i\},\bm{0}}.

And also similar for the higher order terms, which are not zero with this definition we have that

f_{\{i\}\cup\bm{a},\bm{0}}=-f_{\bm{a},\bm{0}}\quad\text{ for all }\bm{a}\subset\{2,3,\ldots,d+1\}.

Our desired function
f=x_{1}^{2}
is completely included in two terms, more precisely,
f=f_{\varnothing,\bm{0}}+f_{\{1\},\bm{0}}
. The only non-zero terms are
f_{\bm{a},\bm{\xi}}
with
\bm{a}\subseteq\bm{u}=\{1\}
. All the other terms cancel each other out, such that we can set them to zero.

This example shows how we will modify Definition
3.9
.
To mitigate the problem of dependencies and make our spherical ANOVA decomposition more similar to the classical decomposition, we show the following.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let the function
f(\bm{x})
have the form
f(\bm{x})=g(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})
, depend on all variables in
\bm{u}
and have parity
\bm{\xi}
, i.e.
\Xi_{\bm{\xi}}f=f
. Let furthermore the parity vector
\bm{\xi}
have the form
\bm{\xi}=(\bm{1}_{\bm{v}},\bm{0}_{\bm{v}^{c}})^{\top}
with
\bm{v}\subset\bm{u}
.
Then for all
\varnothing\neq\bm{b}\subseteq[d+1]\backslash\bm{u}
we have for the spherical ANOVA terms defined in Definition
3.9
that

0=\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subseteq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a}\cup\bm{b},\bm{\xi}}.

(3.13)

\bm{u}
\bm{v}
,
\bm{\xi}_{\bm{v}}=\bm{1}
\bm{b}

Proof.

We illustrate the indices in this proof in Figure

We do the proof by induction over the order
|\bm{b}|
. First, let
\bm{b}=i
. Then by definition,

\displaystyle f_{\bm{u}\cup i,\bm{\xi}}

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{u}\cup i}[\Xi_{\bm{\xi}}f]-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}\cup i}f_{\bm{a},\bm{\xi}}=P_{\bm{u}}[\Xi_{\bm{\xi}}f]-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subseteq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{\xi}}-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a}\cup i,\bm{\xi}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a}\cup i,\bm{\xi}}.

Thereby we used that

f=\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subseteq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{\xi}}.

(3.14)

It follows (
3.13
) for
\bm{b}=\{i\}
. For the induction step assume that (

) holds for all
\bm{b}
with
|\bm{b}|<k
and choose now
\bm{b}
with
|\bm{b}|=k
, then

\displaystyle f_{\bm{u}\cup\bm{b},\bm{\xi}}

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{u}\cup\bm{b}}[\Xi_{\bm{\xi}}f]-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}\cup\bm{b}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{\xi}}

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{u}}[\Xi_{\bm{\xi}}f]-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subseteq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{\xi}}-\sum_{\varnothing\neq\tilde{\bm{b}}\subset\bm{b}}\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subseteq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a}\cup\tilde{\bm{b}},\bm{\xi}}-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a}\cup\bm{b},\bm{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{f-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subseteq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{\xi}}}_{=0,\text{ due to \eqref{eq:f=sum_subsets}}}-\sum_{\varnothing\neq\tilde{\bm{b}}\subseteq\bm{b}}\underbrace{\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a}\cup\tilde{\bm{b}},\bm{\xi}}}_{=0\text{ due to induction assumption}}-\sum_{\bm{v}\subseteq\bm{a}\subset\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a}\cup\bm{b},\bm{\xi}}.

Hence, (

) is also fulfilled for

with

For the spherical ANOVA decomposition we want that a function of the form
f(\bm{x})=g(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})
, which depends on all the variables
\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}
and not on the variables
\bm{x}_{\bm{u}^{c}}
, has only non-zero spherical ANOVA terms
f_{\tilde{\bm{u}},\bm{\xi}}
if
\tilde{\bm{u}}\subseteq\bm{u}
. The Definition
3.9
does not fulfill this necessarily. But with the previous lemma, we are able to modify the decomposition by setting

f_{\tilde{\bm{u}}\cup\bm{a},\bm{\xi}}=0
for all
\bm{a}\subseteq[d+1]\backslash\bm{u}
and
\tilde{\bm{u}}\subseteq\bm{u}
. Then, we do not have an analytical spherical ANOVA decomposition anymore, but the decomposition is much more similar to the classical ANOVA decomposition.

We will show in the following how to compute the spherical ANOVA decomposition numerically.

4
Choice of basis

For periodic functions one can write every function in
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with the Fourier series. There is a direct connection between the index
\bm{k}
of the Fourier coefficients and the ANOVA terms
f_{\bm{u}}
, i.e. which parts of the Fourier series belong to which ANOVA term, see
[
27
]
. Similarly, for orthogonal polynomials with tensor product structure, there is an easy connection between the ANOVA index
\bm{u}
and the frequency indices. See also
[
35
]
for an overview about different basis choices for modeling the ANOVA decomposition on tensor product domains.

In our case here, with the sphere as domain, we do not have a tensor product structure of the domain available. However, for every spherical ANOVA term
f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
we construct in the following a basis
\mathcal{B}_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
.

4.1
The one-dimensional terms

Let
i\in[d+1]
. To describe the space of one-dimensional spherical ANOVA terms
f_{\{i\},\bm{\xi}}
with a basis
\mathcal{B}_{\{i\},\bm{\xi}}
, we may choose an orthogonal basis
\eta_{k}(x_{i})
, such that

\delta_{k,\ell}=\langle\eta_{k}(x_{i}),\eta_{\ell}(x_{i})\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\eta_{k}(x_{i})\,\eta_{\ell}(x_{i})\,m_{i}(x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x_{i},

which means that the basis functions have to be orthogonal on
[-1,1]
with respect to
m_{i}(x_{i})\sim\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}^{d-2}
, which are the
Gegenbauer polynomials
with parameter
\alpha=\tfrac{d-1}{2}
, usually denoted by
C_{k}^{\alpha}
, see
[
1
]
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
define

C_{k}^{\alpha}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\tfrac{k}{2}\rfloor}(-1)^{i}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+k-i)}{i!\,(k-i)!}(2x)^{k-2i}.

The first polynomials have the form:

\displaystyle C_{0}^{(\alpha)}(x)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle C_{1}^{(\alpha)}(x)

\displaystyle=2\alpha x,

\displaystyle C_{2}^{(\alpha)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\alpha+2\alpha(1+\alpha)x^{2}.

For one-dimensional terms, there are only the two possibilities: even and odd functions. The Gegenbauer polynomials have the nice property that for odd
k
they are odd functions and for even
k
they are even functions. Since the linear spaces of odd and even function on
[-1,1]
are orthogonal with respect to
m_{i}(x_{i})\sim\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}^{d-2}
, we receive an orthogonal basis of the space of odd functions on
[-1,1]
for describing the one-dimensional odd spherical ANOVA terms in Definition
3.9
by

\mathcal{B}_{i,\bm{e}_{i}}=\left\{C_{k}^{(d-1)/2}(x)\mid x\in[-1,1]\right\}_{k=1,3,5,\ldots},

which depend on the ambient dimension
d
. For the even one-dimensional term, naturally a basis can be constructed by the even Gegenbauer polynomials. Let
\bm{u}=\{i\}
, then due to our Definition
3.9
,

\displaystyle P_{\varnothing}f_{\{i\},\bm{\xi}}

\displaystyle=P_{\varnothing}\mathcal{E}_{1}P_{1}f-P_{\varnothing}P_{\varnothing}f=0,

which means that the coefficient for the constant in the even one-dimensional term is zero, such that an even basis for
f_{\{i\},\bm{0}}
reads

\mathcal{B}_{i,\bm{0}}=\left\{C_{k}^{(d-1)/2}(x)\mid x\in[-1,1]\right\}_{k=2,4,6,\ldots}.

4.2
The higher-dimensional terms

For describing the spherical ANOVA terms
f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
on the domain
\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|}
, we are searching for an orthogonal basis
\mathcal{B}_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}=\{\eta_{k}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\mid\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|}\}_{k}
with orthogonality with respect to the weight function
m_{\bm{u}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\sim\sqrt{1-\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert^{2}}^{d-1-|\bm{u}|}
, since then the basis is orthogonal on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
with respect to the uniform measure on the sphere
\mu_{d}
. Changing coordinates to polar coordinates, this weight function becomes

m_{\bm{u}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\sim\sqrt{1-r^{2}}^{d-1-|\bm{u}|},

which leads us to the Jacobi polynomials.

Remark 4.1
.

Jacobi polynomials

P^{\alpha,\beta}_{n}(x)
form an orthonormal basis in
L_{2}([-1,1])
with respect to the weight
(1-x)^{\alpha}\,(1+x)^{\beta}
.
For polynomials on
[0,1]
we use the substitution
t=r^{2}-1
, which means that

\displaystyle\delta_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P^{\alpha,\beta}_{k}(t)P^{\alpha,\beta}_{\ell}(t)(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{1}P^{\alpha,\beta}_{k}(2r^{2}-1)P^{\alpha,\beta}_{\ell}(2r^{2}-1)\,[2(1-r^{2})]^{\alpha}(2r^{2})^{\beta}r\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=4\,\cdot 2^{\alpha+\beta}\int_{0}^{1}P^{\alpha,\beta}_{k}(2r^{2}-1)P^{\alpha,\beta}_{\ell}(2r^{2}-1)\,(1-r^{2})^{\alpha}\,r^{2\beta+1}\,\mathrm{d}r.

With this, an orthogonal basis on
\mathbb{B}^{n}
, used in the literature
[
6
, Prop. 11.1.13]
,
[
7
, Prop. 5.2.1]
is the following.
For
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq j\leq\tfrac{N}{2}
and
1\leq k\leq\dim(\mathcal{H}_{N-2j}(\mathbb{S}^{|\bm{u}|}))
define

P_{j,k}^{N}(\bm{x})=P_{j}^{\tfrac{d-1-|\bm{u}|}{2},N-2j+\frac{d-2}{2}}(2\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}}\right\rVert^{2}-1)Y_{k,{N-2j}}(\bm{x}).

(4.1)

These form an orthogonal basis of the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree exact
N
with respect to the inner product

\langle f(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}),g(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}=\langle f(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}),g(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\rangle_{(\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|},m_{\bm{u}})}=c\int_{\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|}}f(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})g(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})m_{\bm{u}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{\bm{u}},

where
c
is a normalization constant.

4.2.1
The two-dimensional terms

In the two-dimensional case the spherical harmonics are precisely the cosine and sine functions. For the sake of simplicity of the notation, we will construct here a basis for the terms
f_{\{1,2\},\bm{\xi}}
, but for all other two-dimensional terms the procedure is the same.

In two-dimensional polar coordinates
(x_{1},x_{2})=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)

as basis

2.8

) for
\mathbb{B}^{2}

\displaystyle\left\{P_{j}^{\tfrac{d-3}{2},N-2j}(2r^{2}-1)r^{N-2j}\cos((N-2j)\theta)\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}_{0},0\leq j\leq\tfrac{N}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\bigcup\left\{P_{j}^{\tfrac{d-3}{2},N-2j}(2r^{2}-1)r^{N-2j}\sin((N-2j)\theta)\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}_{0},0\leq j<\tfrac{N}{2}}.

The case

A rationale for discarding the terms with
N=2
for
\bm{\xi}=(0,0)
is the specific geometry of the sphere. The radial term
P_{1}(2r^{2}-1)
and the angular term

correspond to linear combinations of
x_{1}^{2}
and
x_{2}^{2}

f_{\{i\},\bm{0}}
already span the space of

(orthogonalized against the constant), including these

In contrast, the term
r^{2}\sin(2\theta)\sim x_{1}x_{2}

\bm{\xi}_{\{1,2\}}=(1,1)
and represents a pure interaction that cannot be formed by sums of univariate functions.

The case
\bm{\xi}_{\{1,2\}}=(0,0)

x_{1}
and
x_{2}

\tfrac{N}{2}+1
, which includes the two one-dimensional terms
x_{1}^{N}
and
x_{2}^{N}
. This means from the possibilities for
j
, namely
0\leq j\leq\tfrac{N}{2}
we can keep only
\tfrac{N}{2}-1
and have to omit two, which are redundant.

The case
\bm{\xi}_{\{1,2\}}=(1,0)
:

Let
N
be odd, then the space of homogeneous polynomials in two variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
of the form
x_{1}^{2k+1}x_{2}^{2\ell}
with

has dimension

\tfrac{N+1}{2}
, which includes the two one-dimensional term
x_{1}^{N}
. This means from the possibilities for
j
, namely
0\leq j\leq\tfrac{N-1}{2}
we can keep only
\tfrac{N-1}{2}
and have to omit one, which is redundant. The case
\bm{\xi}_{\{1,2\}}=(0,1)
is similar.

A similar approach can be used for higher-dimensional terms. However, we will limit ourselves here to this explanation for two-dimensional terms. We do reduce the redundancy between the terms of different order. However these terms are not necessarily orthogonal.

5
Low dimensional approximation

In many applications, functions on the sphere depend on a large number of variables but are effectively dominated by terms of low order. The ANOVA decomposition allows us to identify this effective dimension and approximate the function by a sum of terms involving only a few variables. This is particularly valuable for mitigating the
curse of dimensionality
and for developing efficient quadrature or interpolation algorithms. Approaches for low dimensional approximation without the ANOVA decomposition can be found for example in
[
36
]
.

Similar to the approaches
[
27
,
22
]
for the classical ANOVA decomposition or
[
28
]
for the generalized ANOVA decomposition, we propose to approximate the function
f
using only low-dimensional terms, i.e.,

f\approx f_{\varnothing}+\sum_{1\leq|\bm{u}|\leq q}\sum_{\bm{\xi}\in\Upsilon_{\bm{u}}}f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}\quad\text{ for some }q\ll d.

The next step is to approximate every term by using the specific basis
\mathcal{B}_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}=\{\eta_{k}^{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}\}_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{\bm{u}}}
introduced in Section
4
,

f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\approx\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{\bm{u}}}a^{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}_{k}\,\eta_{k}^{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}).

(5.1)

Substituting the basis expansion (
5.1
) into the truncated ANOVA decomposition leads to the global approximation ansatz

f(\bm{x})\approx a_{\varnothing}+\sum_{1\leq|\bm{u}|\leq q}\sum_{\bm{\xi}\in\Upsilon_{\bm{u}}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{\bm{u}}}a^{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}_{k}\,\eta_{k}^{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}}).

To determine the unknown coefficients
a^{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}_{k}
, we rely on a set of sampling nodes

\mathcal{X}=\{\bm{x}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{x}^{(M)}\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}

and the corresponding function values
\bm{f}=(f(\bm{x}^{(1)}),\dots,f(\bm{x}^{(M)}))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
Let
N
denote the total number of basis functions in the truncated expansion and let
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the vector stacking all coefficients
a^{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}_{k}
.
Evaluating the approximation ansatz at the sampling nodes yields the linear system of equations

\bm{A}\bm{a}\approx\bm{y},

where the system matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
contains the evaluations of the basis functions. Specifically, the entry
A_{i,\ell}
corresponds to the
\ell
-th basis function evaluated at the
i
-th sampling node
\bm{x}^{(i)}
.

In practical applications, the number of samples
M
is typically chosen to be larger than the number of unknowns
N
, resulting in an overdetermined system. Consequently, we seek the coefficient vector
\bm{a}
that minimizes the residual in the Euclidean norm, solving the least squares problem

\min_{\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|\bm{A}\bm{c}-\bm{y}\|_{2}.

(5.2)

While this problem can be solved using direct methods (e.g., via QR decomposition or SVD), the dimensions of
\bm{A}
can become large for high-dimensional approximations. Moreover, as discussed in previous sections, the basis on the sphere may exhibit linear dependencies or ill-conditioning for certain parity combinations.
To address these challenges efficiently, we propose to solve (
5.2
) using the
LSQR algorithm

[
25
]
. LSQR is an iterative Krylov subspace method that is particularly well-suited for large, sparse, or ill-conditioned systems and possesses intrinsic regularization properties when the iteration is terminated early.

Due to the dependence of the terms of different order, the following procedure may be beneficial: We propose to do the approximation first using all terms of order one. Then, we do a second approximation for the residuum using all terms of order
2
, which could be done further.
In Section
6
and Figure
4
we apply both possibilities, the plain LSQR with all terms at once and the iterative increase of the order of the terms.

5.1
Variances for sensitivity analysis

Global sensitivity analysis has become a cornerstone of computer experiments, providing essential insights into the input-output dependencies of numerical simulation models. Building on the foundational works
[
32
,
31
]
, the field has evolved significantly over the last two decades, yielding a wide array of advanced statistical methodologies and refinements of the original Sobol’ indices.

While classical variance-based sensitivity analysis (Sobol’ indices) relies on the orthogonality of the ANOVA terms to decompose the total variance into a sum of partial variances, this property assumes independent input variables. On the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, the inputs are intrinsically coupled via the constraint
\left\lVert\smash{\bm{x}}\right\rVert=1
. Consequently, for even-parity terms, a strict orthogonality cannot be maintained, and the covariance between interaction terms is generally non-zero.
Despite this lack of perfect orthogonality, the variances
\sigma^{2}(f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}})
remain meaningful sensitivity measures for the following reasons:

•

Following the framework of sensitivity analysis for dependent variables, see for example
[
17
,
28
]
, the contribution of a variable is always a combination of its marginal effect and its correlation with other variables. On the sphere, these correlations are not artifacts but physical properties of the domain. Our indices capture the combined effect of a variable and its induced changes on the sphere.

•

A crucial advantage of our proposed construction is the rigorous exclusion of redundant basis terms. By ensuring that our dictionary of basis functions is linearly independent, we prevent variance inflation. In a redundant system, variance could be arbitrarily shifted between linearly dependent terms (e.g., between
f_{1}
and a redundant part of
f_{1,2}
), leading to unstable and uninterpretable indices. Our restrictive construction ensures that the computed variances are stable and reflect the intrinsic coupling of the sphere rather than numerical artifacts.

Lemma 5.1
.

The spherical ANOVA terms defined in Definition
3.9
have mean zero, i.e.,

P_{\varnothing}f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}=0\quad\text{ for all }\bm{u}\in\mathcal{D}\backslash\varnothing\text{ and all }\bm{\xi}\in\Upsilon_{\bm{u}}.

Proof.

Since the spherical ANOVA terms
f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
defined in Definition
3.9
have parity
\bm{\xi}
, the mean is automatically zero, if the function
f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
is odd in at least one direction. The only remaining case is when
\bm{\xi}=\bm{0}
. But in this case we have for
\bm{u}\neq\varnothing
,

\displaystyle P_{\varnothing}f_{\bm{u},\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=P_{\varnothing}\left(P_{\bm{u}}[\Xi_{\bm{0}}f](\bm{x}_{\bm{a}})-\sum_{\bm{a}\subsetneq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{0}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}})\right)=P_{\varnothing}f-P_{\varnothing}\left(\sum_{\bm{a}\subsetneq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{0}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}})\right)

\displaystyle=P_{\varnothing}\left(\sum_{\varnothing\neq\bm{a}\subsetneq\bm{u}}f_{\bm{a},\bm{0}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}})\right)=\sum_{\varnothing\neq\bm{a}\subsetneq\bm{u}}P_{\varnothing}f_{\bm{a},\bm{0}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{a}}).

By induction this finishes the proof by using the induction assumption.
∎

Using the previous lemma, the variances of the spherical ANOVA terms can be easily calculated by

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\,\mathrm{d}{\mu_{d}}(\bm{x})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mu_{d}}(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}|f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mu_{d}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:subs_Bsp}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|}}|f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})|^{2}\,m_{\bm{u}}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{\bm{u}},

which is a weighted variance of the term
f_{\bm{u}}
on
\mathbb{B}^{|\bm{u}|}
. In Section
4
we introduced the basis
\mathcal{B}_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}
, which is orthogonal with respect to the weight function
m_{\bm{u}}
. Using the approximation (
5.1
), the variances are approximated by

\sigma^{2}(f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}})\approx\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left|f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}(\bm{x})\right|^{2}\approx\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{\bm{u}}}a^{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}_{k}\eta_{k}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\right|^{2}.

Since terms with different parity vector are orthogonal with respect to
m_{\bm{u}}
, we define for
\bm{u}\neq\varnothing
the Sobol indices on the sphere,

S_{\bm{u}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(f)}\sum_{\bm{\xi}\in\Upsilon_{\bm{u}}}\sigma^{2}(f_{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}})\approx\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(\bm{f})}\sum_{\bm{\xi}\in\Upsilon_{\bm{u}}}\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{\bm{u}}}a^{\bm{u},\bm{\xi}}_{k}\eta_{k}(\bm{x}_{\bm{u}})\right|^{2}\eqqcolon\tilde{S}_{\bm{u}},

(5.3)

where
\sigma^{2}(\bm{f})
is the variance of the data vector
\bm{f}
and the expression
\tilde{S}_{\bm{u}}
can be calculated easily using parts of the the matrix
\bm{A}
and the coefficient vector
\bm{a}
.
We interpret the normalized ratio
S_{\bm{u}}
and its approximation
\tilde{S}_{\bm{u}}
not strictly as a percentage of disjoint variance, but as a relative importance index reflecting the stable amplitude of the corresponding spherical interaction terms.

For the classical ANOVA decomposition the Sobol indices (or sometimes called global sensitivity indices) also denote the fraction of the variance of the ANOVA term and the total variance of the function. Due to the orthogonality between all terms, they sum up to one.

6.1
Summary and outlook

The spherical ANOVA decomposition is a powerful mathematical framework designed to analyze high-dimensional functions defined on the high-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Unlike the classical ANOVA, this approach respects the intrinsic geometry of the sphere, making it essential for problems involving directional data or isotropic constraints.

Our decomposition allows for the breakdown of a complex function into a sum of orthogonal terms, where each term represents the contribution of specific subsets of variables. This reveals the effective dimension of the function.

By calculating global to Sobol indices, we can quantify how much variance is driven by single variables versus complex interactions, effectively opening the black box of high-dimensional models.
We are able to construct sparse approximation algorithms that avoid the curse of dimensionality by identifying that a function is dominated by low-order interactions. It paves the way for designing specialized cubature rules that focus computational effort only on the relevant subspaces identified by the ANOVA terms. In fields dealing with normalized feature vectors (such as text embeddings or gene expression profiles), our spherical ANOVA decomposition can serve as a robust feature selection and noise reduction tool.
