Variational Method for Interacting Surfaces with Higher-Form Global Symmetries
Kiyoharu Kawana

Abstract
We develop a variational method for interacting surface systems with higher-form global symmetries. As a natural extension of the conventional second-quantized Hamiltonian of interacting bosons, we explicitly construct a second-quantized Hamiltonian formulated in terms of a closed surface operator ϕ ^ ​ [ C p ] \hat{\phi}[C_{p}] charged under a p p -form global symmetry. Applying the variational principle, we derive a functional Schrödinger equation analogous to the Gross-Pitaevskii equation in conventional bosonic systems. In the absence of external forces, the variational equation admits a uniform solution that is uniquely determined by a microscopic interaction potential U ⁡ ( ψ ∗ ​ ψ ) U(\psi^{*}\psi) and the chemical potential. This uniform solution describes a uniform gas of bosonic surfaces. Using the obtained energy functional, we show that low-energy fluctuations contain a gapless p p -form field A p A_{p} when the p p -form global symmetry is U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1) , whereas the p p -form field becomes massive for discrete symmetries, whose low-energy limit is described by a BF \mathrm{BF} -type topological field theory. As a consequence, the system exhibits abelian topological order with anyonic surface excitations. In the presence of external forces, however, solving the functional equation in full generality remains challenging. We argue, however, that the problem reduces to solving the conventional Gross-Pitaevskii equation when external forces act separately on the center-of-mass and relative motions. In addition, we present analytic solutions for topological defects as analogs of vortex and domain-wall solutions in conventional bosonic systems. Finally, as a concrete microscopic model, we study a ℤ N \mathbb{Z}_{N} lattice gauge theory and apply our variational method to this system.

2
Variational method

We develop a variational method for nonrelativistic bosonic systems with higher-form global symmetries.
To distinguish different higher-form symmetries, we denote a
p
-form global symmetry generally as
G^{[p]}
, which is an Abelian group for
p\geq 1
.
For
p=0
,
G^{[0]}
can be non-Abelian.

2.1
Conventional variational method

Let us recap the variational method in conventional bosonic systems with an abelian
0
-form global symmetry
G^{[0]}
.
The existence of the symmetry
G^{[0]}
implies that there exists an
(D-1)
-dimensional symmetry (or topological) operator
\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-1}]
that commutes with a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
,

\displaystyle[\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-1}],\hat{H}]=0~,

(1)

where
\theta
is a transformation parameter.
When
G^{[0]}=\mathrm{U}(1)^{[0]}
, the symmetry operator is associated with a
\mathrm{U}(1)
conserved charge:

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-1}]=e^{i\theta\hat{Q}[\Sigma_{D-1}]}~,\quad\hat{Q}[\Sigma_{D-1}]=\int_{\Sigma_{D-1}}\star\hat{J}_{1}~,

(2)

where
\hat{J}_{1}
is a conserved
1
-form current,
d\star\hat{J}_{1}=0
.
Typically, such a theory contains a charged local operator
\hat{\phi}(x)
satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-1}]\hat{\phi}(x)\hat{U}_{\theta}^{-1}[\Sigma_{D-1}]=e^{iq\theta}\hat{\phi}(x)~,

(3)

where
q
is a charge (i.e. representation) under
G^{[0]}
.

Suppose that we are given a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
that (i) is constructed from
\hat{\phi}(x)
and (ii) satisfies Eq. (
1
).
The primary objective is to identify a ground state and to understand its various phases.
For this purpose, we introduce the
trivial state

|0\rangle
via

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(x)|0\rangle=0\quad\text{for }^{\forall}x\in\Sigma_{D-1}~,

(4)

and define a variational state by

\displaystyle|\psi_{G}\rangle\coloneq\sqrt{{\cal N}}\exp\left(\int_{\Sigma_{D-1}}d^{D-1}x\sqrt{g(x)}\psi_{G}(x)\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}(x)\right)|0\rangle~,

(5)

where
\psi_{G}(x)
is a variational wave function,
g(x)
is the determinant of the spatial metric
g_{ij}(x)
, and
\sqrt{{\cal N}}
is a normalization factor. (Here, the subscript
G
means ground state.)
When
\Sigma_{D-1}
is a discretized space such as a hypercubic lattice, this state can be also written as

\displaystyle|\psi_{G}\rangle=\underset{x\in\Sigma_{D-1}}{\otimes}|\psi_{G}(x)\rangle~,

(6)

that is, it is a product state of local coherent states
|\psi_{G}(x)\rangle
.

Taking the expectation value of
\hat{H}
and
\hat{Q}
, we obtain the free-energy functional

\displaystyle F[\psi_{G}]\coloneq\langle\psi_{G}|\hat{H}-\mu(\hat{Q}-Q)|\psi_{G}\rangle~,

(7)

where
\mu
is the chemical potential.
Then, the minimization of the free energy

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F[\psi_{G}]}{\delta\psi_{G}^{*}(x)}=0~

(8)

leads to the variational equation along with the charge condition

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F[\psi_{G}]}{\partial\mu}=0\quad\leftrightarrow\quad Q=\langle\psi_{G}|\hat{Q}|\psi_{G}\rangle~.

(9)

As an example, let us consider the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\int_{\Sigma_{D-1}}d^{D-1}x\sqrt{g(x)}\left[\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}(x)\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\Box+V(x)\right)\hat{\phi}(x)+U(\phi^{\dagger}\phi)\right]~,

(10)

where

\displaystyle\Box=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g(x)}}\partial_{i}(\sqrt{g(x)}g^{ij}(x)\partial_{j})

(11)

is the Laplace operator, and the field operator
\hat{\phi}(x)
satisfies the commutation relation

\displaystyle[\hat{\phi}(x),\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}(y)]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g(x)}}\delta^{(D-1)}(x-y)~.

(12)

Here,
V(x)
is an external potential which is independent of the field.
It is straightforward to check
[\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-1}],\hat{H}]=0
and the
\mathrm{U}(1)^{[0]}
transformation

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-1}]\hat{\phi}(x)\hat{U}_{\theta}^{-1}[\Sigma_{D-1}]=e^{i\theta}\hat{\phi}(x)~

(13)

with

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-1}]=e^{-i\theta\hat{Q}[\Sigma_{D-1}]}~,\quad\hat{Q}[\Sigma_{D-1}]\coloneq\int_{\Sigma_{D-1}}d^{D-1}x\sqrt{g(x)}~\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\phi}(x)~.

(14)

In this case, the free-energy functional is

\displaystyle F[\psi_{G}]

\displaystyle=E[\psi_{G}]-\mu(Q[\psi_{G}]-Q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma_{D-1}}d^{D-1}x\sqrt{g(x)}\left[\psi_{G}(x)^{*}\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\Box+V(x)-\mu\right)\psi_{G}(x)+U(\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G})\right]+\mu Q~.

(15)

Taking the variation with respect to
\psi_{G}(x)^{*}
, we obtain the variational equation

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\Box+V(x)-\mu\right)\psi_{G}(x)+\frac{\delta U(\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G})}{\delta\psi_{G}(x)^{*}}~,\quad Q=\int_{\Sigma_{D-1}}d^{D-1}x|\psi_{G}(x)|^{2}~,

(16)

which is known as the Gross-Pitaevskii equation in condensed matter physics
[
9
,
29
,
38
]
.

Several comments are in order.
First, the field operator can be generally expanded as

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(x)=\sum_{i}\hat{a}_{i}\psi_{i}(x)~,

(17)

by an orthonormal set of functions
\{\psi_{0}=\psi_{G}/\sqrt{Q},\psi_{k}\}
satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{D-1}}d^{D-1}x\sqrt{g(x)}\psi_{i}(x)^{*}\psi_{j}(x)=\delta_{ij}~,

(18)

and

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{ij}~,\quad\text{others}=0~.

(19)

Then, Eq. (
5
) is equivalently written as

\displaystyle|\psi_{G}\rangle=\sqrt{{\cal N}}e^{\sqrt{Q}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}}|0\rangle~,

(20)

which implies that the ground state is a coherent state of
\hat{a}_{0}
and
\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}
.

Second, we can also derive the action which leads to the Hamiltonian (
10
) and the commutation relation (
12
) as

\displaystyle S[\phi]=\int_{\Sigma_{D}}\star 1~\left[i(\phi(t,x)^{*}\dot{\phi}(t,x)-\phi(t,x)\dot{\phi}(t,x)^{*})-\phi(t,x)^{*}\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\Box+V(x)\right)\phi(t,x)-U(\phi^{*}\phi)\right]~,

(21)

whose equation motion
\frac{\delta S[\phi]}{\delta\phi^{*}(x)}=0
describes a dynamics of the condensate, i.e., the time-dependent Gross-Pitaevskii equation.

2.2
Generalization to surface systems

Now, let us consider many-body bosonic systems with higher-form global symmetries.
A
p
-form global symmetry
G^{[p]}
implies that there exists a
(D-p-1)
-dimensional symmetry (or topological) operator
\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]
that commutes with a Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle[\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}],\hat{H}]=0~,

(22)

where
\Sigma_{D-p-1}
is a
(D-p-1)
-dimensional closed subspace in
\Sigma_{D-1}
and
\theta
is a transformation parameter.
For
G^{[p]}=\mathrm{U}(1)^{[p]}
, the symmetry operator is associated with the conserved charge as

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]=e^{-i\theta\hat{Q}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]}~,\quad\hat{Q}_{p}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]=\int_{\Sigma_{D-p-1}}\star J_{p+1}~,

(23)

where
J_{p+1}
is a
(p+1)
-form conserved current, namely, it satisfies
d\star\hat{J}_{p+1}=0
.
Analogous to the
0
-form case, such a system typically contains a charged

p
-dimensional surface operator
\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]
that obeys an equal-time operator relation

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\theta}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]\hat{U}_{\theta}^{-1}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]=e^{iq\theta\mathrm{I}[C_{p},\Sigma_{D-p-1}]}\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]~,

(24)

where
C_{p}
is an embedded surface of
S^{p}
in
\Sigma_{D-1}
, and
\mathrm{I}[C_{p},\Sigma_{D-p-1}]
is the intersection number between them:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}[C_{p},\Sigma_{D-p-1}]\coloneq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma_{D}}\delta_{D-p}(C_{p})\wedge\delta_{p}(M_{D-p}^{\varepsilon})~,

(25)

where
M_{D-p}
is the
(D-p)
-dimensional spacetime region enclosed by
C_{D-p-1}^{\varepsilon}\coloneq\Sigma_{D-p-1}(t+\epsilon)\cup\overline{\Sigma}_{D-p-1}(t-\epsilon)
, and
\delta_{D-q}({\cal M}_{q})
is the Poincare dual form of a
q
-dimensional manifold
{\cal M}_{q}
in general.
See Fig.
1
for example.

24
).

The trivial state
|0\rangle
is similarly defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]|0\rangle=0\quad\text{for }^{\forall}C_{p}~,

(26)

and a variational state is introduced by

\displaystyle|\psi_{G}\rangle=\sqrt{{\cal N}}:\exp\left({\int}{\cal D}Xw[C_{p}]~\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]\psi_{G}[C_{p}]\right):|0\rangle~,

(27)

where
\psi_{G}[C_{p}]
is a variational wave function,
\int{\cal D}X
is the path-integral over all embeddings
C_{p}=\{X^{i}(\xi)\}_{i=1}^{D-1}:S^{p}\rightarrow\Sigma_{D-1}
,
w[C_{p}]
is a weight functional, and
:\cdots:
denotes a prescription for properly accounting for identical states when expanding the exponential in general.
See the next subsection about the weight functional
w[C_{p}]
.

The free-energy functional is now given by

\displaystyle F[\psi_{G};\Sigma_{D-p-1}]=\langle\psi_{G}|\hat{H}-\mu_{p}(\hat{Q}_{p}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]-Q_{p})|\psi_{G}\rangle~,

(28)

where
\mu_{p}
is the chemical potential for the
p
-form global charge.
Taking the variations with respect to
\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}
and
\mu_{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F[\psi_{G};\Sigma_{D-p-1}]}{\delta\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}}=0~,\quad\langle\psi_{G}|\hat{Q}_{p}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]|\psi_{G}\rangle=Q_{p}~.

(29)

Here, a couple of comments are in order.
First, a concrete microscopic system can also possess additional higher-form global symmetries
\mathrm{U}[1]^{[q]}
with
q\leq p
.
In such a case, we can also add another Lagrange term
\mu_{q}(\hat{Q}_{q}-Q_{q})
to the above free energy.

Second, when the spatial manifold
\Sigma_{D-1}
is topologically trivial, i.e.,
\Sigma_{D-1}=\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
, the
p
-form global charge
Q_{p}
cannot take a nonzero value since
\mathrm{I}[C_{p},\Sigma_{D-p-1}]=0
for all
C_{p}
and
\Sigma_{D-p-1}
.
In this case, Eq. (
28
) becomes independent of
\Sigma_{D-p-1}
, and condensation of the system is governed by the coupling constants in
\hat{H}
.
On the other hand, when the topology of
\Sigma_{D-1}
is nontrivial so that it contains topologically nontrivial subspaces along which
C_{p}
and/or
\Sigma_{D-p-1}
can wind, the ground state can carry a nonzero
p
-form global charge
Q_{p}\neq 0
.

In this model, the variational state is simply given by

\displaystyle|\psi_{G}\rangle=\sqrt{{\cal N}}\exp\left(\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]\psi_{G}[C_{p}]\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]\right)|0\rangle~

(43)

without the prescription.

(44)

\displaystyle F[\psi_{G};\Sigma_{D-p-1}]=\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{g\mathrm{Vol}[C_{p}]}\sum_{\hat{i}\in C_{p}}\delta_{\hat{i}}\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}\delta_{\hat{i}}\psi_{G}[C_{p}]+V[C_{p}]\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}\psi_{G}[C_{p}]+U(\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G})\right\}

\displaystyle-\mu_{p}\left(\langle\psi_{G}|\hat{Q}_{p}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]|\psi_{G}\rangle-Q_{p}\right)-\mu\left(\langle\psi_{G}|\hat{Q}|\psi_{G}\rangle-Q\right)~.

(45)

Finally, by taking the variations with respect to
\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}
and chemical potentials, we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\left(-\frac{1}{gw[C_{p}]}\sum_{\hat{i}\in C_{p}}\delta_{\hat{i}}\frac{w[C_{p}]}{\mathrm{Vol}[C_{p}]}\delta_{\hat{i}}+V[C_{p}]-\mu_{p}\mathrm{I}[C_{p},\Sigma_{D-p-1}]-\mu\right)\psi_{G}[C_{p}]+\frac{\delta U(\psi^{*}_{G}\psi_{G})}{\delta\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}}~,

(46)

\displaystyle\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]\mathrm{I}[C_{p},\Sigma_{D-p-1}]{\psi}_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}\psi_{G}[C_{p}]=Q_{p}~,\quad\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]{\psi}_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}\psi_{G}[C_{p}]=Q~.

(47)

Equation (

16

2.4
More on surface interactions

Since the content of this subsection is not essential for the following discussion, readers may skip this part on a first reading.

Although we have formulated the variational method in a similar manner to conventional bosonic systems, several important issues have been intentionally omitted in order to keep our formulation as simple as possible.
First, surfaces can split and merge without violating the
\mathrm{U}(1)
higher-form global symmetry.
For example, we can consider the following nonlocal interaction

\displaystyle\int[dC_{p}^{1}]\int[dC_{p}^{2}]\int[dC_{p}^{3}]\delta(C_{p}^{1}-C_{p}^{2}-C_{p}^{3})\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}[C_{p}^{1}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}^{2}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}^{3}]+{\rm h.c.}~,

(59)

where
\delta(C_{p}-C_{p}^{{}^{\prime}})
is the continuum version of the delta function (
38
).
This interaction represents a merging of two surfaces
C_{p}^{2},C_{p}^{3}
into another surface
C_{p}^{1}
and is invariant under the
p
-form global transformation (
24
) as

\displaystyle\delta(C_{p}^{1}-C_{p}^{2}-C_{p}^{3})\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}[C_{p}^{1}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}^{2}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}^{3}]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\delta(C_{p}^{1}-C_{p}^{2}-C_{p}^{3})e^{i\theta\int_{C_{p}^{2}+C_{p}^{3}-C_{p}^{1}}\Lambda_{p}}\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}[C_{p}^{1}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}^{2}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}^{3}]=\delta(C_{p}^{1}-C_{p}^{2}-C_{p}^{3})\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}[C_{p}^{1}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}^{2}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}^{3}]~.

(60)

On the other hand, this interaction explicitly breaks
\mathrm{U}(1)^{[0]}
, namely, the total number of surfaces is not conserved when this interaction exists.

Second, it is possible to promote
\mathrm{U}(1)^{[0]}
to a general (non-abelian) group
G^{[0]}
by assigning a nontrivial representation
R
of
G^{[0]}
to the surface operator,
\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]\rightarrow\hat{\phi}_{a}[C_{p}]
, where
a=1,2,\cdots,\mathrm{dim}R
denotes the component of the representation
R
.
However, invariance under such a nontrivial group
G^{[0]}
is typically incompatible with the interactions such as Eq. (
59
) because they cannot be expressed in a
G^{[0]}
invariant manner.
In other words, one typically needs to break
G^{[0]}
in order to introduce interactions describing splitting and merging of surfaces.

Third, a microscopic Hamiltonian such as Eq. (

This can be explicitly seen by noting that an infinitesimal volume element

(61)

where
\delta D_{p+1}
is the interior region of an infinitesimal deformation
\delta C_{p}
as depicted in Fig.

By introducing the center-of-mass coordinates and the relative coordinates as

\displaystyle x^{k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}[C_{p}]}\int_{S^{p}}d^{p}\xi\sqrt{h(\xi)}X^{k}(\xi)~,\quad Y^{k}(\xi)\coloneq X^{k}(\xi)-x^{k}~,

(62)

which shows that the area-volume elements are independent of the center-of-mass position for

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\int[dC_{p}]\bigg\{\frac{1}{m}\partial_{k}\hat{\phi}_{a}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]\partial_{k}\hat{\phi}_{a}[C_{p}]

where

Mean field analysis

In this section, we perform a mean-field analysis based on the generalized Gross-Pitaevskii equation (

See also Refs.
[

,

,

,

,

for similar studies.
In this section, we consider
\Sigma_{D-1}=\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
except in Section
3.4
.

3.1
Uniform bosonic surfaces

When the external potential
V[C_{p}]
is absent, the variational equation (
55
) becomes

\displaystyle 0=(-\Box_{p}-\mu)\psi_{G}[C_{p}]+\frac{\delta U(\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G})}{\delta\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}}~,

(65)

which has the uniform solution

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{1}{\psi_{G}[C_{p}]}\frac{\delta U(\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G})}{\delta\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}}~,

(66)

when the potential
U(\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G})
is bounded below.
For example, the standard quartic interaction
U(\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G})=\lambda(\psi_{G}^{*}\psi)^{2}/2~,~\lambda>0
leads to

\displaystyle n\coloneq|\psi_{G}[C_{p}]|^{2}=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}~,

(67)

which is related to the total number of surfaces as

\displaystyle Q=\int[dC_{p}]|\psi_{G}[C_{p}]|^{2}=V_{D-1}n~,

(68)

where
V_{D-1}
is a regularized volume of
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
and we have used the normalization condition (
2.3
).
The corresponding ground state is written as

\displaystyle|\psi_{G}\rangle=\sqrt{{\cal N}}\exp\left(\sqrt{n}\int[dC_{p}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]\right)|0\rangle~,

(69)

which is a superposition of all embedded surfaces and can be interpreted as a surface generalization of the string condensation in topologically ordered phases in
(1+2)
dimension
[
30
]
.
By construction, we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{G}|\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]|\psi_{G}\rangle=\sqrt{n}\neq 0\quad\text{for all $C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}$}~,

(70)

which implies that the
\mathrm{U}(1)

p
-form global symmetry is spontaneously broken.

NON-CONDENSED PHASE

It is also instructive to consider
\mu\leq 0
.
As long as the interaction potential
U(X)
is an monotonically increasing function of
X
, the uniform solution is
\psi_{G}[C_{p}]=0
, and the corresponding ground state is trivial,
|\psi_{G}\rangle=|0\rangle
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{G}|\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]|\psi_{G}\rangle=0\quad\text{for all $C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}$}~,

(71)

i.e., the
\mathrm{U}(1)

p
-form global symmetry is unbroken.

At this point, recall that the presence or absence of condensation is characterized by the long-distance behavior of the two-point correlation function

\displaystyle G(x)\coloneq\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\phi}(0))~

(72)

in conventional bosonic systems, where
\hat{\rho}
denotes a general density matrix.
By substituting the mode expansion (
17
), this correlation function is written as

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\psi_{i}^{*}(x)\psi_{j}(0){\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j})~.

(73)

When the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
represents a uniform equilibrium ensemble such as the grand-canonical ensemble, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j})=\delta_{ij}{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i})\equiv\delta_{ij}\langle\hat{N}_{i}\rangle~,

(74)

and Eq. (
72
) becomes

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\langle\hat{N}_{i}\rangle\psi_{i}^{*}(x)\psi_{i}(0)=\frac{\langle\hat{N}_{0}\rangle}{Q}n+\sum_{k\neq 0}\frac{\langle\hat{N}_{i}\rangle}{V_{D-1}}e^{-ik\cdot x}~,

(75)

where we have assumed a uniform bose gas, and
n
is the particle number density at zero temperature such as Eq. (
67
).
Bose-Einstein condensation is realized when
\langle\hat{N}_{0}\rangle\propto Q
, which then implies

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}G(x)=\frac{\langle\hat{N}_{0}\rangle}{Q}n<\infty~

(76)

by the Riemann-Lebesgue theorem.
This is the off-diagonal longe-range order and is widely accepted as the criterion for Bose-Einstein condensation
[
9
,
29
,
38
,
15
,
47
,
39
]
.
When the system is not in the condensed phase, the long-distance behavior of the correlation function is typically

\displaystyle G(x)\approx\int\frac{d^{D-1}k}{(2\pi)^{D-1}}\langle N_{k}\rangle e^{-ik\cdot x}\propto e^{-f(|x|/\xi)}~,

(77)

where
f(y)
is a smooth monotonically increasing function and
\xi
is a correlation length.

Now let us view this criterion from the perspective of higher-form global symmetries.
A key observation is that the bilocal operator
\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\phi}(y)
can be regarded as a closed surface operator by regarding the two end points,
x
and
y
, as boundaries of a straight line
L_{1}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\partial L_{1}=\{x,y\}~.

(78)

In this sense, the two-point correlation function (
72
) is an expectation value of the closed surface operator
\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\phi}(y)
, which exhibits an exponential behavior (
77
) in the non-condensed phase.
In the case of
p
-form symmetry, the counterpart of Eq. (
78
) is

\displaystyle\partial L_{p+1}=C_{p}~,

(79)

where
L_{p+1}
is the
(p+1)
-dimensional minimal surface bounded by
C_{p}
.
This highlights a crucial difference between
0
-form and higher-form cases.
In the higher-form case, the boundary consists of a single closed surface
C_{p}
, which implies that the counterpart of
\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\phi}(y)
is the surface operator
\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]
itself.
Accordingly, condensation of surfaces is characterized by the asymptotic behavior of the expectation value

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]\rangle\coloneq\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{\phi}[C_{p}])~,

(80)

for
\mathrm{Vol}[L_{p+1}]\rightarrow\infty
.
As long as we focus on the ground state,
\hat{\rho}=|\psi_{G}\rangle\langle\psi_{G}|
, this expectation value trivially vanishes as in Eq. (
71
).
At a finite temperature, however, there can exist small corrections, analogous to Eq. (
77
), for
\mathrm{Vol}[L_{p+1}]\rightarrow\infty
.
To illustrate this, let us consider the grand-canonical ensemble:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\rm eq}=\frac{e^{-\beta\hat{H}+\mu\hat{Q}}}{Z}~

(81)

as a concrete example.
By following the standard path-integral technique, the expectation value can be expressed in a path-integral form as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]\rangle_{\beta}=\frac{1}{Z}\int{\cal D}\phi^{*}{\cal D}\phi~\phi[0,C_{p}]e^{-S_{E}[\phi;\beta]}~,

(82)

where

\displaystyle S_{E}[\phi;\beta]=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int[dC_{p}]\bigg\{\phi^{*}\partial_{\tau}\phi+\int_{S^{p}}d^{p}\xi\frac{\sqrt{h(\xi)}}{\mathrm{Vol}[C_{p}]}\sum_{k=1}^{D-1}\frac{\delta\phi^{*}}{\delta\Sigma^{k}(\xi)}\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta\Sigma_{k}(\xi)}-\mu\phi^{*}\phi+U(\phi^{*}\phi)\bigg\}~

(83)

is the Euclidean action, and we have set
V[C_{p}]=0
for simplicity.
In general, the path-integral is dominated by saddle-point configurations determined by

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{\tau}\phi-(\Box_{p}+\mu)\phi+\frac{\delta U(\phi^{*}\phi)}{\delta\phi}~.

(84)

In the following, we focus on static solutions,
\phi=\phi[C_{p}]
for simplicity.
The trivial solution is, of course, the ground state
\phi=0
, which contributes to the Euclidean action as

\displaystyle S_{E}[0,\tau]=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int_{\Sigma_{D-1}}{\cal D}Xw[C_{p}]U(0)=\beta V_{D-1}U(0)~.

(85)

This is nothing but the vacuum-energy contribution.
Note that we have used the normalization condition (
2.3
) again.
Can we find other nontrivial saddles ?
The finiteness of action density requires the boundary condition

\displaystyle\phi\rightarrow 0\quad\text{for $\mathrm{Vol}[L_{p+1}]\rightarrow\infty$}

(86)

which in turn leads to the approximate saddle-point equation

\displaystyle 0\approx(\Box_{p}+\mu)\phi[C_{p}]

(87)

for
\mathrm{Vol}[L_{p+1}]\rightarrow\infty
.
This is a generalized wave equation, where the Laplace operator
\Box_{p}
is defined in terms of the functional derivatives, as given in Eq. (
56
).
Even in this simplified form, solving this equation in full generality is a highly nontrivial problem.
Below, we present one concrete solution that corresponds to the exponential behavior
G(x)\propto e^{-|x|/\xi}
in the particle case.

As an ansatz, let us consider

\displaystyle\phi[C_{p}]=f(\mathrm{Vol}[L_{p+1}])~,\quad f(z)\in\mathbb{R}~,

(88)

where
f(x)
is supposed to satisfy the boundary condition
f(\infty)=0
.
Substituting this ansatz into Eq. (
87

\displaystyle p(z)f^{\prime\prime}(z)-q(z)f^{\prime}(z)-|\mu|f(z)\approx 0~,\quad z=\mathrm{Vol}[L_{p+1}]~,

(89)

where

\displaystyle p(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{p}}d^{p}\xi\frac{\sqrt{h(\xi)}}{\mathrm{Vol}[C_{p}]}\left[\frac{1}{p!}(E_{p+1}(\xi))^{kk_{1}\cdots k_{p}}(E_{p}(\xi))_{k_{1}\cdots k_{p}}\right]^{2}~,

(90)

\displaystyle q(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w[C_{p}]}\int_{S^{p}}d^{p}\xi\frac{\sqrt{h(\xi)}}{\mathrm{Vol}[C_{p}]}\frac{1}{p!}\frac{\delta[w[C_{p}](E_{p+1}(\xi))^{kk_{1}\cdots k_{p}}(E_{p}(\xi))_{k_{1}\cdots k_{p}}]}{\delta\Sigma^{\mu}(\xi)}~.

(91)

Here,
(E_{p+1}(\xi))_{k_{1}\cdots k_{p+1}}
is the normalized volume element of the minimal surface
L_{p+1}
, and we have used
[
17
,
23
,
24
,
26
]

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathrm{Vol}[L_{p+1}]}{\delta\Sigma^{k_{1}\cdots k_{p+1}}(\xi)}=(E_{p+1}(\xi))_{k_{1}\cdots k_{p+1}}~.

(92)

Since we are interested in topological aspects of the solution, it is sufficient to consider a spherically symmetric surface
C_{p}\simeq S^{p}
characterized by a radius
r
, and embedded in the
(p+1)
-dimensional subspace with the polar coordinates
(r,\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{p})
.
In this case, the weight functional reduces to an ordinary function,
w[C_{p}]=w(r)
, and the normalized volume elements are explicitly given by

\displaystyle(E_{p}(\xi))^{k_{1}\cdots k_{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{k_{1}\cdots k_{p}}}{\sqrt{h(\xi)}}~,\quad k_{i}\in\{\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{p}\}~,

(93)

\displaystyle(E_{p+1}(\xi))^{k_{1}\cdots k_{p+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{k_{1}\cdots k_{p+1}}}{\sqrt{h(\xi)}}~,\quad k_{i}\in\{r,\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{p}\}~,

(94)

by which we obtain

\displaystyle p(z)=1~,\quad q(z)=\frac{1}{r^{p}}\frac{d\log w(r)}{\partial r}~.

(95)

This implies that the equation of motion (
89
) is approximated by

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(z)-|\mu|f(z)\approx 0~

(96)

for
z\rightarrow\infty
, and we obtain the area-law behaviour

\displaystyle f(z)\propto e^{-|\mu|z}=e^{-|\mu|\mathrm{Vol}[L_{p+1}]}~.

(97)

In particular, one can see that this reproduces the large-distance behavior of the two-point function
G(x)\sim e^{-|x|/\xi}
by formally putting
p=0
.

Summarizing all, there are at least two saddle-point solutions that contribute to the expectation value (
82
) as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]\rangle_{\beta}=0+c(\beta,\mu)e^{-|\mu|\mathrm{Vol}[C_{p}]}+\cdots~,

(98)

where
c(\beta,\mu)
denotes an undetermined relative weight between the two saddle-point solutions.
Although we cannot exclude the existence of other saddle-point solutions at this stage, the criterion for condensation shall be now stated as follows: In the condensed phase, the expectation value of the surface operator
\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]
approaches a finite constant for
\mathrm{Vol}[L_{p+1}]\rightarrow\infty
(i.e., the perimeter law), whereas it obeys the area law in the non-condensed phase.
See also Refs.
[
28
,
19
,
17
]
and references therein for the related studies.

3.2
Low energy excitations

In ordinary interacting bosonic systems, low-energy excitations can be analyzed by expanding a given Hamiltonian in terms of the fluctuation
\delta\hat{\phi}(x)=\hat{\phi}(x)-\psi_{G}(x)
and diagonalizing the second order terms.
Then, we typically find gapless sound-wave modes whose dispersion relation is
\varepsilon_{k}\simeq v|k|
, where
v\geq 0
is the sound velocity.

In the present theory of interacting surfaces, however, it is challenging to carry out a similar Bogoliubov analysis in a systematic manner due to the functional nature of the field
\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]
.
Nevertheless, the presence or absence of gapless modes can be still analyzed by focusing on specific functional degrees of freedom.
For instance, a natural candidate for low-energy gapless excitation is given by the phase modulation
4
4

4

Here, we use
\psi[C_{p}]
instead of

(99)

3.3
Nonuniform bosonic surfaces

In experimental realizations of Bose-Einstein Condensation, dilute atoms are trapped by an external potential
V(x)
induced by external magnetic fields.
In this case, the ground state is a bound state whose characteristic quantum-mechanical length is fixed by the oscillation frequency
\omega^{2}=V^{\prime\prime}(0)/m
.

Within the present framework, we can also consider analogous trapped systems.
Here, let us focus on a class of models in which the external potential
V[C_{p}]
takes a separable form as

\displaystyle V[C_{p}]=V_{\rm CM}(x)+V_{R}[\{Y\}]~,

(107)

where
x
denotes the center-of-mass position and
\{Y(\xi)\}=\{Y^{k}(\xi)\}_{k=1}^{D-1}
are the relative coordinates introduced in Eq. (
62
).
In this case, we can employ the method of separation of variables as

\displaystyle\psi_{G}[C_{p}]=\varphi_{G}(x)\Psi_{G}[\{Y\}]~

(108)

with the normalization condition

\displaystyle Q=\int[dC_{p}]|\psi_{G}|^{2}=\int d^{D-1}x|\varphi_{G}|^{2}\times\int{\cal D}Yw[C_{p}]|\Psi_{G}|^{2}~.

(109)

Without loss of generality, we can always adopt the normalization

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}Yw[C_{p}]|\Psi_{G}|^{2}=1\quad\leftrightarrow\quad Q=\int d^{D-1}x|\varphi_{G}|^{2}~,

(110)

which coincides with Eq. (
16
).
Substituting Eq. (
108
) into Eq. (
54
) and including the kinetic term of the center-of-mass position as well, we obtain the effective energy functional of
\varphi_{G}(x)
:

\displaystyle E[\varphi_{G}]=\int_{\Sigma_{D-1}}d^{D-1}x\left\{\frac{1}{2m}|\partial_{k}\varphi_{G}(x)|^{2}+V_{\rm CM}(x)|\varphi_{G}(x)|^{2}+U_{\rm eff}(|\varphi_{G}|^{2})\right\}~,

(111)

where

\displaystyle U_{\rm eff}(|\varphi_{G}|^{2})=\int{\cal D}Y\omega[C_{p}]\bigg\{|

\displaystyle\varphi_{G}|^{2}\left(\int_{S^{p}}d^{p}\xi\frac{\sqrt{h(\xi)}}{\mathrm{Vol}[C_{p}]}\frac{\delta\Psi_{G}^{*}}{\delta\Sigma^{k}(\xi)}\frac{\delta\Psi_{G}}{\delta\Sigma_{k}(\xi)}+V_{R}[\{Y\}]|\Psi_{G}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+U\left(|\varphi_{G}|^{2}|\Psi_{G}|^{2}\right)\bigg\}~

(112)

is the effective potential for
\varphi_{G}(x)
.
The first term in the right-hand side of Eq. (
112
) can be absorbed into the chemical potential, while the second term leads to an effective interparticle interaction.
As a result, Eq. (
111
) is nothing but a conventional energy functional for interacting bosons, whose variation yields the standard Gross-Pitaevskii equation (
16
).

Let us study one concrete example.

HARMONIC POTENTIAL

The simplest example is a spherically symmetric harmonic potential acting on the center-of-mass position:

\displaystyle V[C_{p}]=V_{\rm CM}(r)=\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{2}r^{2}~,\quad r=|x|~.

(113)

In this case, the relative-motion part is completely irrelevant,
\psi_{G}[C_{p}]=\varphi_{G}(r)
, and we can employ the Thomas-Fermi approximation
[
9
,
29
,
38
]
to examine the qualitative behavior of the ground state.
Assuming the quartic interaction
U_{\rm eff}(|\varphi_{G}|^{2})=\lambda(|\varphi_{G}|^{2})^{2}/2
, we have

\displaystyle(V_{\rm CM}(r)+\lambda|\varphi_{G}(r)|^{2})\varphi_{G}(r)=\mu\varphi_{G}(r)~,

\displaystyle\therefore

\displaystyle\quad n(r)\coloneq|\varphi_{G}(r)|^{2}=\frac{\mu-V_{\rm CM}(r)}{\lambda}

(114)

in the region where the right-hand side is positive and
\varphi_{G}(r)=0
otherwise.
The boundary radius
R
is determined by

\displaystyle\mu=V_{\rm CM}(r)\quad\therefore R=\frac{\sqrt{2\mu}}{\omega_{p}}~,

(115)

and the normalization condition is

\displaystyle Q=\int d^{D-1}x|\varphi_{G}|^{2}=\frac{2\mu}{\lambda(D+1)}\mathrm{Vol}[B_{D-1}(R)]\propto\mu^{\frac{D+1}{2}}~,

(116)

where
B_{D-1}(R)
is the
(D-1)
-dimensional ball with radius
R
.
This equation provides a relation between
\mu
and
Q
.
The thermodynamic relation implies

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{\partial F[\varphi]}{\partial Q}=\frac{\partial E[\varphi]}{\partial Q}\bigg|_{\varphi=\varphi_{G}}~,

(117)

which leads to the energy per surface as

\displaystyle\frac{E[\psi_{G}]}{Q}=\frac{D+1}{D+3}\mu~.

(118)

In this simple trapped model, the thermodynamic properties of the ground state are completely identical to those of trapped bosons since the relative motion plays no role.
It is particularly interesting to consider a nontrivial external potential
V_{R}[\{Y\}]
for the relative motion as well, and to analyze how such an internal force can modify the thermodynamic properties of the ground state.
We leave this problem for future investigation.

3.4
Discrete symmetries and topological order

We can also consider discrete higher-form global symmetries.
The simplest realization of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{[p]}
can be obtained by adding

\displaystyle\lambda_{N}\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{N}+{\rm h.c.}~,\quad\lambda_{N}>0~.

(119)

to a
\mathrm{U}(1)
invariant potential
U(\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G})
.
Then, the continuous degeneracy of the uniform solution is lifted and reduced to a discrete set,

\displaystyle\psi_{G}[C_{p}]=ve^{\frac{2\pi}{N}m}~,\quad m=0,1,\cdots,N-1~.

(120)

Correspondingly, the phase modulation
A_{p}
is no longer gapless but acquires a mass gap, as we discuss below.

In what follows, we derive the effective action of
A_{p}
starting from Eq. (
57
).
By putting Eq. (
99
) into Eq. (
119
), one can see that
A_{p}
obtains a potential

\displaystyle 2v^{N}\lambda_{N}\cos\left(N\int_{C_{p}}A_{p}\right)~.

(121)

Here, we can apply the Villain formula
[
42
]
:

\displaystyle\exp\left(-2iv^{N}\lambda_{N}\int dt\int[dC_{p}]\cos\left(N\int_{C_{p}}A_{p}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left(-2iv^{N}\lambda_{N}\int dt\int[dC_{p}]\left\{1-\frac{1}{2}\left(N\oint_{C_{p}}A_{p}-2\pi n\right)^{2}\right\}\right)

(122)

which can be further deformed into a path-integral form by introducing two auxiliary higher-form fields as

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}f_{p}\int{\cal D}B_{D-p-1}\exp\Bigg(-

\displaystyle 2iv^{N}\lambda_{N}\int dt\int[dC_{p}]\left\{1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\oint_{C_{p}}(NA_{p}-f_{p})\right)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma_{D}}B_{D-p-1}\wedge df_{p}\Bigg)~,

(123)

where the integration of
B_{D-p-1}
imposes the flatness condition
df_{p}=0
, which in turn corresponds to the quantization condition
\int_{C_{p}}f_{p}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
.
As shown explicitly in Ref.
[
17
]
, this can be further written as

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}f_{p}\int{\cal D}B_{D-p-1}\exp\left(-i\int_{\Sigma_{D}}\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\left(A_{p}-\frac{f_{p}}{N}\right)\wedge\star\left(A_{p}-\frac{f_{p}}{N}\right)-\frac{i}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma_{D}}B_{D-p-1}\wedge df_{p}\right)~,

(124)

where
\lambda_{2}
is a coupling constant.
Furthermore,
f_{p}
can be eliminated by using the equation of motion as

\displaystyle\frac{f_{p}}{N}=A_{p}-\frac{N(-1)^{(D-p)(p+1)}}{2\pi\lambda_{2}}\star dB_{D-p-1}~,

(125)

and we obtain the effective action:

\displaystyle S_{\rm eff}[A_{p},B_{D-p-1}]=(\text{kinetic terms})-\frac{N}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma_{D}}B_{D-p-1}\wedge dA_{p}~.

(126)

In the low-energy limit, the kinetic terms are neglected, and the system is governed by the
\mathrm{BF}
-type topological field theory, exhibiting topological order.
In addition to the original symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{[p]}
, Eq. (
126
) possesses an emergent
\mathbb{Z}_{N}

(D-p-1)
-form global symmetry:

\displaystyle B_{D-p-1}\quad\rightarrow\quad B_{D-p-1}+\frac{1}{N}\Lambda_{D-p-1}~,\quad d\Lambda_{D-p-1}=0~,\quad\int_{C_{D-p-1}}\Lambda_{D-p-1}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}~,

(127)

where
\Lambda_{D-p-1}
is a closed
(D-p-1)
-form and
C_{D-p-1}
is a
(D-p-1)
-dimensional closed subspace in
\Sigma_{D}
.
The corresponding charged object is the dual Wilson-surface operator

\displaystyle U[C_{D-p-1}]=\exp\left(i\int_{C_{D-p-1}}B_{D-p-1}\right)~,

(128)

which acts on the Wilson-surface operator

\displaystyle W[C_{p}]=\exp\left(i\int_{C_{p}}A_{p}\right)

(129)

as a
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{[p]}
symmetry operator.

TOPOLOGICAL ORDER

Let us examine topological order in more detail.
Here, we focus on the ground state specified by
\psi_{G}[C_{p}]=v
:

\displaystyle|v\rangle=\sqrt{\cal N}:\exp\left(v\int[dC_{p}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]\right):|0\rangle~,

(130)

which satisfies
\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]|v\rangle=v|v\rangle
for all
C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}
by definition.
The
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{[p]}
symmetry operator is

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{2\pi/N}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]=\exp\left(i\frac{2\pi}{N}\int[dC_{p}]\mathrm{I}[C_{p},\Sigma_{D-p-1}]\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]\right)~.

(131)

Since we focus on ground-state properties, it is convenient to express the field operator

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]=ve^{i\hat{\theta}[C_{p}]}\equiv v\hat{W}[C_{p}]~.

(132)

\Sigma_{D-1}=\mathbb{R}^{D-1}

\displaystyle\langle v|\hat{W}[C_{p}]|v\rangle=1~,\quad\langle v|\hat{U}_{2\pi/N}[\Sigma_{D-p-1}]|v\rangle=1~

(133)

for all
C_{p}
and
\Sigma_{D-p-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
.
Namely, all surface operators are acting on the ground state trivially.

On the other hand, when the topology of spatial manifold is non-trivial, these operators can wind along nontrivial subspaces.
For example, let us consider
\Sigma_{D-1}=S^{p}\times S^{D-p-1}
, namely,
C_{p}=S^{p}
and
\Sigma_{D-p-1}=S^{D-p-1}

In this case,

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{2\pi/N}[S^{D-p-1}]\hat{W}[S^{p}]\hat{U}_{2\pi/N}^{-1}[S^{D-p-1}]=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{N}}\hat{W}[S^{p}]~

(134)

as an (equal-time) operator relation.
Then, consider a new state

\displaystyle|v^{\prime}\rangle\coloneq\hat{U}_{2\pi/N}[S^{D-p-1}]^{-1}|v\rangle~,

(135)

which has the same energy as
|v\rangle
because
\hat{U}_{2\pi/N}[S^{D-p-1}]

Using Eq. (
134

\displaystyle\hat{W}[S^{p}]|v^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{U}[S^{D-p-1}]^{-1}\bigg(\hat{U}[S^{D-p-1}]\hat{W}[S^{p}]\hat{U}[S^{D-p-1}]^{-1}\bigg)|v\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{N}}|v^{\prime}\rangle~,

(136)

namely,
|v^{\prime}\rangle
is another eigenstate of
\hat{W}[S^{p}]
with the same energy as
|v\rangle
.
By repeating the same procedure, one can see that there are
N
degenerate ground states

\displaystyle\left(\hat{U}_{2\pi/N}[S^{D-p-1}]^{-1}\right)^{m}|v\rangle\quad m=0,1,\cdots,N-1~,

(137)

when
\Sigma_{D-1}=S^{p}\times S^{D-p-1}
.

D,p
, as well as on the topology of
\Sigma_{D-1}
.
For instance, when
D=3
,
p=1
, and
\Sigma_{2}=T^{2}

4.1

We assign a

p

In the following, we simply set

First, we focus on the parameter region

\displaystyle\hat{H}\sim-h\sum_{\sigma_{p}\in\Delta_{p}}\hat{X}_{N}(\sigma_{p})+{\rm h.c.}~,

(198)

and the ground state is given by the trivial product state

\displaystyle|\text{unbroken}\rangle=|0\rangle_{X}^{\otimes|\Delta_{p}|}~,

(199)

where
|\Delta_{p}|
is the total number of
p
-cells.
The expectation value of the Wilson-surface operator
\langle\text{unbroken}|\hat{W}[C_{p}]|\text{unbroken}\rangle
is trivially zero, i.e., the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}

p
-form global symmetry is unbroken.
Note that the gauge invariance (
190
) is trivially satisfied as well.

In this unbroken phase, an excited state can be obtained by acting
\hat{Z}_{N}(\sigma_{p})
on a finite number of
p
-cells.
However, the gauge invariance (
190
) constrains such a configuration to form a
p
-dimensional closed intersecting surface
C_{p}
made by
(N-1)
types of
p
-cells as

\displaystyle|C_{p};\{k\}\rangle\coloneq\prod_{\sigma_{p+1}\in\Delta_{p+1}}(\hat{B}(\sigma_{p+1}))^{m(\sigma_{p+1})}|\text{unbroken}\rangle~,\quad m(\sigma_{p+1})\in\{1,2,\cdots,N-1\}~,

(200)

which is gauge invariant due to the commutativity (

Here,
\{k\}=\{k(\sigma_{p})\}_{\sigma_{p}\in\Delta_{p}}
is the set of eigenvalues of
\frac{N}{2\pi i}\log\hat{X}_{N}(\sigma_{p})
on each

-cell
\sigma_{p}
:

\displaystyle k(\sigma_{p})=\sum_{\sigma_{p+1}\in\Delta_{p+1}}\langle\partial\sigma_{p+1},\sigma_{p}\rangle m(\sigma_{p+1})\quad\text{mod $N$}~

(201)

In particular, these eigenvalues satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{p}\in\Delta_{p}}\langle\sigma_{p},\overline{\partial}\sigma_{p-1}\rangle k(\sigma_{p})\equiv 0\quad\text{mod $N$}~

(202)

on each
\sigma_{p-1}\in\Delta_{p-1}
by the nilpotency condition (

This is interpreted as the Gauss low in the present gauge theory.
We should note that, even when the shape of
C_{p}
is fixed, a different assignment of
k(\sigma_{p})
on each
\sigma_{p}
leads to a different surface state
|C_{p};\{k^{\prime}\}\rangle
for

.

BROKEN PHASE

Next, let us focus on the parameter region
h\ll g
.
This regime corresponds to the toric code (
193
) (with
g^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty
).
The gauge invariance (
190
) implies that the ground state, denoted as
|\text{broken}\rangle
, is now a simultaneous eigenstate of both surface operators:

\displaystyle\hat{A}(\sigma_{p-1})|\text{broken}\rangle=|\text{broken}\rangle~,\quad\hat{B}(\sigma_{p+1})|\text{broken}\rangle=|\text{broken}\rangle~.

(203)

The second condition indicates that the ground state cannot be a simple closed-surface state like Eq. (
200
), but is given by the equal-weight superposition of all
p
-dimensional closed surfaces as

\displaystyle|\text{broken}\rangle=\sqrt{{\cal N}}\sum_{\text{all}~C_{p}}\sum_{\text{all}~\{k\}}|C_{p};\{k\}\rangle~,

(204)

where
\sqrt{{\cal N}}
is a normalization factor.
Or by introducing the projection operator

\displaystyle\hat{P}(\sigma_{p+1})\coloneq\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\hat{B}(\sigma_{p+1}))^{n}~,

(205)

the above state can be also expressed as

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\cal N}\prod_{\sigma_{p+1}\in\Delta_{p+1}}\hat{P}(\sigma_{p+1})|0\rangle_{X}^{\otimes|\Delta_{p}|}~.

(206)

Obviously, this expression implies

\displaystyle\langle\text{broken}|\hat{W}[C_{p}]|\text{broken}\rangle=1~

(207)

for
{}^{\forall}C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}
, indicating that
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{[p]}
is spontaneously broken.

4.3
Variational method

Let us now apply the variational method developed in the previous sections to the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}

p
-form gauge theory.

The Wilson-surface operator
\hat{W}[C_{p}]
plays a role of the fundamental field
\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]
in the general framework, and the variational state is

\displaystyle|\psi_{G}\rangle=\sqrt{{\cal N}}:\exp\left(\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}~\hat{W}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]\psi_{G}[C_{p}]\right):|0\rangle_{X}^{\otimes|\Delta_{p}|}~,

(208)

where the prescription
:\cdots:
is defined such that each surface state appears with equal weight when expanding the exponential.
For example, let us focus on a surface state which takes a form of

\displaystyle\cdots\hat{W}[C_{p}]\hat{W}[C_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}]|0\rangle_{X}^{\otimes|\Delta_{p}|}~,\quad C_{p}~,~C_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\in\Gamma_{p}~,

(209)

where
\cdots
denotes other surface contributions.
When
C_{p}+C_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}\in\Gamma_{p}
, however, this is identical to

\displaystyle\cdots\hat{W}[C_{p}+C_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}]|0\rangle_{X}^{\otimes|\Delta_{p}|}~

(210)

by the fusion property of the Wilson-surface operator
\hat{W}[C_{p}]\hat{W}[C_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}]=\hat{W}[C_{p}+C_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}]
.
In this way, a naive expansion of the exponential in Eq. (
208
) contain infinitely many identical states, and the prescription
:\cdots:
is therefore introduced to count these states equally.
By definition, we have

\displaystyle\hat{W}[C_{p}]|\psi_{G}\rangle=\psi_{G}[C_{p}]|\psi_{G}\rangle~

(211)

for
{}^{\forall}C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}
.
8
8

8

In this case, this coherence property originates from the concatenation property of the Wilson surface
W[C_{p}](W^{\dagger}[C_{p}])^{n}=(W^{\dagger}[C_{p}])^{n-1}
rather than the commutation relation.

Furthermore, this coherence property leads to additional constraint

\displaystyle\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{n}=1

(212)

for
{}^{\forall}C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}
if
\psi_{G}[C_{p}]\neq 0
.
Accordingly, the variation of free-energy functional must be taken under this condition.

In order to examine the expectation value of the Hamiltonian (
197
) in the above variational state, we first need to deform the kinetic term in an appropriate way.
For this purpose, we represent the infinitesimal surface deformation (
40
) in an operator form as
[
45
,
3
]

\displaystyle\Pi_{\pm\partial\sigma_{p+1}}\hat{W}[C_{p}]\coloneq\begin{cases}\hat{W}[C_{p}\pm\partial\sigma_{p+1}]&\text{for }C_{p}\pm\partial\sigma_{p+1}\in\Gamma_{p}\\
0&\text{for }C_{p}\pm\partial\sigma_{p+1}\notin\Gamma_{p}\end{cases}~.

(213)

Using this operator, we can express the kinetic term in Eq. (
197
) as

\displaystyle-g\sum_{\sigma_{p+1}\in\Delta_{p+1}}(\hat{B}(\sigma_{p+1})+\hat{B}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{p+1}))

\displaystyle=-g\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]\hat{W}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]{\cal H}\hat{W}[C_{p}]~,

(214)

where the operator
{\cal H}
is defined by

\displaystyle{\cal H}\hat{W}[C_{p}]\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{p+1}\in\Gamma_{p}\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=\pm}\Pi_{s\partial\sigma_{p+1}}\hat{W}[C_{p}]~.

(215)

This is proved as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]\hat{W}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]{\cal H}w[C_{p}]\hat{W}[C_{p}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]\hat{W}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]\sum_{s=\pm}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{p+1}~{\rm s.t.}\\
C_{p}+s\partial\sigma_{p+1}\in\Gamma_{p}\end{subarray}}\hat{W}[C_{p}+s\partial\sigma_{p+1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma_{p+1}\in\Delta_{p+1}}\sum_{s=\pm}\hat{B}(s\sigma_{p+1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{p}~{\rm s.t.}\\
C_{p}+s\partial\sigma_{p+1}\in\Gamma_{p}\end{subarray}}w[C_{p}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma_{p+1}\in\Delta_{p+1}}(\hat{B}(\sigma_{p+1})+\hat{B}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{p+1}))~,

(216)

where we have used Eq. (
35
) from the second line to the third line.
Then, using Eq. (
211
), the expectation value of the kinetic term can be evaluated as

\displaystyle-g\left\langle\psi_{G}\bigg|\sum_{\sigma_{p+1}\in\Delta_{p+1}}\left(\hat{B}(\sigma_{p+1})+\hat{B}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{p+1})\right)\bigg|\psi_{G}\right\rangle=-g\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}{\cal H}\psi_{G}[C_{p}]~.

(217)

Similarly, the expectation value of the second term in Eq. (
197

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi_{G}\bigg|\sum_{\sigma_{p}\in\Delta_{p}}\left(\hat{X}_{N}(\sigma_{p})-1\right)\bigg|\psi_{G}\right\rangle+{\rm h.c.}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\psi_{G}\bigg|\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}\sum_{\sigma_{p}\in C_{p}}\left(\omega^{-\langle\partial\sigma_{p+1},\sigma_{p}\rangle}-1\right)\hat{W}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]\psi_{G}[C_{p}]\bigg|\psi_{G}\right\rangle+{\rm h.c.}

\displaystyle=-4\left(\sin\frac{\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}|C_{p}|\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}\psi_{G}[C_{p}]~,

(218)

where we have used

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Delta_{p}}(\hat{X}_{N}(\sigma_{p})-1)\right)\hat{W}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]|0\rangle_{X}^{\otimes|\Delta_{p}|}=\sum_{\sigma_{p}\in C_{p}}\left(\omega^{-\langle\partial\sigma_{p+1},\sigma_{p}\rangle}-1\right)\hat{W}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]|0\rangle_{X}^{\otimes|\Delta_{p}|}

(219)

for
{}^{\forall}C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}
.
In the following, the overall coefficient
4\left(\sin\frac{\pi}{N}\right)^{2}
is absorbed into
h

Collecting all terms and taking the constraint (
212

9

9

\displaystyle F[\psi_{G}]

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{G}|\hat{H}|\psi_{G}\rangle+\lambda\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]\left(\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{N}-1\right)+{\rm h.c.}

\displaystyle=\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}w[C_{p}]\bigg\{-g\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}\left({\cal H}-n[C_{p}]\right)\psi_{G}[C_{p}]+|C_{p}|\left(h-g\frac{n[C_{p}]}{|C_{p}|}\right)\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{*}\psi_{G}[C_{p}]

\displaystyle\hskip 256.0748pt+\lambda\psi_{G}[C_{p}]^{N}+{\rm h.c.}\bigg\}~,

(220)

where

10
10

For example, when

These factors depend on

Here, readers might question why

GROUND STATES

First, let us see how the ground states obtained in the previous subsection are reproduced in the present variational approach.
As long as we focus on ground states, we can neglect the kinetic term in Eq. (
220

In the weak gauge-coupling regime

\displaystyle|C_{p}|h\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G}+\lambda\psi_{G}^{N}+{\rm h.c}~,\quad h,\lambda>0~,

\displaystyle|\psi_{G}\rangle=|0\rangle_{X}^{\otimes|\Delta_{p}|}~,

On the other hand, in the strong gauge-coupling limit

\displaystyle-ag\psi_{G}^{*}\psi_{G}+\lambda\psi_{G}^{N}+{\rm h.c}~,

(223)

This has

\displaystyle\psi_{G}=v\times\omega^{m}~,\quad v\neq 0~,\quad m=0,1,\cdots,N-1~,

(224)

\displaystyle|\psi_{G}\rangle=\sqrt{\cal N}:\exp\left(v\sum_{C_{p}\in\Gamma_{p}}\hat{W}^{\dagger}[C_{p}]\right):|0\rangle_{X}^{\otimes|\Delta_{p}|}~,

(225)

which coincides with
|\text{broken}\rangle

204

Thus, this represents a condensation of surfaces and corresponds to the spontaneously broken phase of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{[p]}

Surface excitations can be also constructed by following the general argument in Section

Finally, let us discuss the continuum limit of the variational free energy (

Since low-energy effective theory would be independent of microscopic details, we here choose a hypercubic lattice with a lattice spacing

Then, employing the projected area derivative (

\displaystyle F[\psi_{G}]=\int[dC_{p}]

5
Summary and discussion

We have investigated the variational method for interacting surface systems with higher-form global symmetries.
Analogously to conventional bosonic systems, we constructed a second-quantized Hamiltonian for the surface operator
\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]
, which possesses a higher-form global symmetry
G^{[p]}
.
Taking the expectation value of the Hamiltonian in a variational coherent state, we obtained the free-energy functional, whose variation leads to the functional Schrödinger equation, analogous to the Gross-Pitaevskii equation in conventional bosonic systems.
Based on this generalized Gross-Pitaevskii equation, we have performed the mean-field analysis and uncovered various physical consequences associated with the spontaneous breaking of higher-form global symmetries.
Finally, as a concrete microscopic model, we studied a
\mathbb{Z}_{N}

p
-form lattice gauge theory and applied our variational framework in detail.

Many important issues remain to be addressed.
First, one can also consider gauging our second-quantized system by introducing a
(p+1)
-form gauge field
A_{p+1}
.
Such a theory correspond to a higher-form generalization of the conventional Ginzburg-Landau theory and is expected to describe a superconducting phase of extended objects
[
23
]
.

Second, as a related problem, it seems possible to extend our formulation to fermionic surface systems by promoting the fundamental field
\hat{\phi}[C_{p}]
to a fermionic surface operator that obeys the canonical anti-commutation relation,
\{\phi[C_{p}],\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}[C_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}]\}=w[C_{p}]^{-1}\delta_{C_{p},C_{p}^{{}^{\prime}}}
.
It is particularly interesting to ask whether we can generalize the BCS theory for interacting fermions to such a fermionic theory of surfaces with higher-form global symmetries.
In addition, it would be of great interest to construct and analyze supersymmetric theories by incorporating both bosonic and fermionic surface operators.

Third, applying our variational method to systems with mobility constrains is also a fruitful research direction
[
8
,
16
,
41
,
18
,
10
,
11
]
.
In such systems, higher-form global charges are not commutative with certain spacial translation operators, leading to the emergence of fracton excitations in the low-energy limit.
It is quite interesting to investigate whether effective field theories of these fractons can be obtained by applying our variational approach as presented in this paper.

We hope to discuss these problems in the near future.
