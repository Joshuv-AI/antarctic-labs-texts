Flexoelectricity and ion transport synergy for high efficient energy conversion
Authors: Dongze Yan, Li-Hua Shao, Huiling Duan, Wanlin Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ion transport is a fundamental process governing biological functions and energy conversion in electrochemical systems, but breaking through the efficiency limitations of these systems remains a challenge. Here, we report an ion transport mechanism in electrolytes driven by flexoelectricity of a carbon nanotube–filled porous polydimethylsiloxane composite. Because of the synergistic effect of flexoelectricity and ion transport, the efficiency of converting the total mechanical work into electrical energy reaches 6.54%, and the net energy conversion efficiency without the reversible elastic deformation energy being reckoned reaches 28.81%. The mass-specific and deformability-specific current of polydimethylsiloxane is enhanced by 1,000,000 times compared to that of solid truncated pyramid polydimethylsiloxane in air. The mass-specific electrical energy per unit stress is over 10 times that of the reported nanogenerators, and the performance remains stable for 10,000,000 cycles. Compressing 1.02 gram of the material once can generate enough electricity to light 800 light-emitting diodes. Our work provides a practical route for compliant and low-cost energy converting devices for applications in sensing, energy harvesting, and biosignaling.

INTRODUCTION

Ion transport underpins critical processes spanning battery energy storage/conversion (1, 2), mineral separation (3, 4), optoelectronic functionality (5, 6), and fundamental biological systems (7, 8). At the biological cell scale, ion pumps establish transmembrane ion concentration gradients (9, 10), and these gradients drive passive ion transport through subsequently opened channels [e.g., Piezo channels (11)], generating electrical signals without adenosine triphosphate consumption (9, 12). Analogously, lithium-ion transport underpins both charge/discharge cycles and the energy conversion/storage process in batteries (13, 14). These ion transports are driven by biological processes or biased external electric fields. Given the ubiquity of mechanical stimuli in the ambient environments, harnessing mechanically induced ion transport has emerged as a high-efficiency energy-conversion pathway (15, 16). Ion transport induced by electrochemical potential difference in electrolytes has also been used to convert mechanical energy into electrical energy with a high net energy conversion efficiency of 22.4% by twisting stiff carbon nanotube (CNT) yarns (15). Today, powering distributed systems requires light-weight, flexible, and high-efficiency energy harvesting from ambient high-entropy sources (17, 18). However, breaking the current limits on energy-conversion efficiencies and ensuring viability have been challenging issues (19).

Flexoelectricity, which is ubiquitous in dielectric materials including soft polymers (20, 21) and biological materials (22, 23), is an effective way to convert mechanical deformation into electricity (24–26). It is demonstrated that through structural and material design of porous materials (27–29), the mass-specific and deformability-specific current of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) has been increased by 10,000 times (27), but the overall energy conversion efficiency is still very low in air (less than 10−6). The salient advantages of the inherent large surface areas and flexibility of porous structures offer a potential novel mechanism for driving ion transport in electrolytes to magnify energy converting efficiency via flexoelectricity.

In this work, we report a synergistic effect of flexoelectricity and ion transport through a light-weight, flexible CNT-filled porous PDMS composite immersed in electrolyte, enabling efficient energy conversion. This synergistic mechanism achieves a mechanical-to-electrical energy conversion efficiency 6.54% of the total input mechanical work, and a net energy conversion efficiency 28.81% of the input mechanical work minus the reversible elastic deformation energy of the system. The mass-specific and deformability-specific current exhibits 1,000,000 times greater than the flexoelectricity of solid truncated pyramid PDMS matrix material in air. Moreover, the mass-specific electrical energy of the flexible porous material per unit stress exceeds the values reported for rigid-material nanogenerators by more than an order of magnitude.

RESULTS

Fabrication of the porous PDMS/CNT/electrolyte system

The porous PDMS/CNT composite with 4.5 vol % of semiconducting multiwall conductive CNTs was synthesized via sacrificial templating, as shown in Fig. 1A. CNTs and sodium chloride (NaCl) particles were dispersed into uncured liquid PDMS, then the thoroughly mixed PDMS/CNT/NaCl composite was cast into a mold. After curing at 40°C for 3 hours and subsequently leaching in ultrapure water for 72 hours to dissolve all NaCl particles, a porous structured PDMS/CNT composite material was obtained, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. The detailed information on the materials and fabrication can be found in Materials and Methods. The interior of the porous material has a dense network of microscale ligaments (with an average thickness δ of 20 μm), among which pores with an average diameter l of 235 μm are formed (Fig. 1, B and C). The PDMS/CNT composite ligament exhibits an average resistivity of ca. 39 Ω·cm, owing to the formation of an interconnected CNT network within the PDMS matrix (Fig. 1, D and E).

A porous PDMS/CNT composite block (2 × 2 × 1 cm3) was immersed in an electrolyte within a petri dish. Unless otherwise specified, a 0.01 M NaCl solution was used as the electrolyte. A flexible silver layer (2 × 2 cm2, 100 μm thick) was applied on top of the block surface to function as the counter electrode (CE). It was electrically insulated from the underlying porous block by a micrometer-thick solid PDMS interlayer, as illustrated in Fig. 1C. We measured the electrical output using four kinds of CE materials, namely, PDMS/CNT (volume fraction 4.5%), Cu, flexible Ag, and Au, and found that the peak output voltage is independent of these CE materials, attributed to a constant peak potential difference between the working and counter electrodes under the same deformation (see experimental detail in section S9). The CNT network serves two functions: First, it acts as a conductive pathway within the porous block matrix; second, the CNTs exposed to the electrolyte function as the working electrode (WE) to form the solid-liquid interface. Two copper wires were used to connect the WE and CE to a current amplifier (fig. S2). Specifically, one copper wire was fixed between the CE and the PDMS layer on the top surface of the porous block; the other was embedded in a solid conductive PDMS/CNT composite thin layer. This thin layer was bonded directly to the lower surface of the porous block, linking the wire to the conductive CNT network embedded within the PDMS matrix and thus preventing contact with the electrolyte. The segment of the copper wire immersed in the electrolyte was coated with a thin PDMS layer for electrical insulation (Fig. 1C). The details of the electrical measurement and testing environment (25° ± 3°C and 30% RH) are given in Materials and Methods.

The porous block was subjected to a cyclic deformation by a dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA) with a peak compressive strain of 50% and a deformation frequency of 1 Hz. Consequently, the porous block/electrolyte system generated a peak current of 11.4 μA. The corresponding peak current density of the system reached 2.85 A/m3, calculated on the basis of the macroscopic volume of the porous block (orange curve in Fig. 1F). This value is approximately 100 times higher than that measured for the identical porous composite block in air (0.1 μA).

Analysis of potential mechanisms

The current output induced by deformation of the porous block immersed in an electrolyte involves three mechanisms: (i) streaming current generated by electrolyte flow through the charged ligaments, (ii) ion convection current caused by the deformation of the charged ligaments in the electrolyte, and (iii) the synergistic mechanism of flexoelectricity and ion transport.

First, we examine the mechanism for the streaming current. As the porous structure contains numerous microchannels, the deformation of the porous block immersed in the electrolyte induces fluid flow within these channels. The surfaces of the ligaments absorb ions in the electrolyte due to the zeta potential of the solid materials (30, 31), forming an electric double layer (EDL). The shear forces generated by fluid flow displace the ions within the EDL’s diffuse layer, producing a streaming current. The maximum streaming current is estimated to be 81.5 nA, accounting for merely 0.71% of the peak current output shown in Fig. 1F (see the detailed calculations in section S1).

Second, we analyze the mechanisms for the ion convection current. The EDL structure at the solid/liquid interface creates a spatially distributed charge (local net charge density) within the electrolyte adjacent to the ligament surfaces. The deformation-induced motion of the ligaments drives fluid flow in this region, generating a convection current. The maximum convection current is 37.8 nA, accounting for less than 0.34% of the peak current in Fig. 1F (see section S2 for the details).

Thus, the total contributions of the streaming and convection currents are less than 1.1% of the measured peak current of the porous block/electrolyte system. We then investigate the synergistic mechanism of flexoelectricity and ion transport.

The synergistic mechanism of flexoelectricity and ion transport

It has been demonstrated that the porous PDMS-based materials exhibit a remarkable flexoelectricity in air (27, 28). The PDMS is a typical centrosymmetric dielectric material with flexoelectric effect (21, 27, 28). Although the CNT network in the ligaments exhibits conductor-like behavior, the solid PDMS/CNT composite and its porous structure still exhibit flexoelectricity rather than being shielded like a metallic conductor as demonstrated in figs. S7 and S8. Since the density of CNTs exceeds that of PDMS, CNT sedimentation occurs during sample preparation, creating a nonuniform distribution within the ligaments (fig. S7C and table S1). This asymmetry enables the CNT conductive network to preferentially sense charges on the CNT-enriched bottom surface of a bent ligament, thereby eliminating charge mutual cancellation. Meanwhile, the interconnected CNT conductive network acts as nanoscale conductive bridges to gather the net flexoelectric polarization charges captured by CNT fillers dispersed within the PDMS matrix. This feature renders the flexoelectric response of the PDMS/CNT composite superior to that of neat PDMS, as elaborated in section S10. In addition, the piezoelectric effect of the PDMS/CNT composite is shown to be negligible, and the contribution of the intrinsic flexoelectricity of the CNTs to the overall or effective flexoelectric coefficient of the PDMS/CNT composite is negligible, as detailed in section S11.

Under cyclic macroscopic compression of the porous block, the microligaments within it undergo cyclic bending, thereby generating a time-dependent flexoelectric polarization Pl(t)=μijklεij,k(t), where μijkl, εij,k(t), and t are the flexoelectric coefficient, time-dependent strain gradient, and time variable of mechanical loading, respectively. The porous block composed of PDMS/CNTs exhibits net flexoelectric polarization primarily in the loading direction when subjected to uniaxial compression (fig. S10, E and F), as the symmetry of its structure is broken by gravitational settling during the fabrication process. The degree of this structural asymmetry can be regulated by controlling the curing temperature to manipulate the sedimentation of the uncured composite (see details in section S12). Since the electrical output performance of the system is directly proportional to the flexoelectric output of the porous block, regulating the structural asymmetry can control the flexoelectric output and thus the electrical output of the system. In addition, when the flexoelectric polarization field of the porous block reverses direction, the electrical output direction of the system reverses correspondingly (see details in section S13). As illustrated in Fig. 1E, the polarized potential change detected by the conductive CNT network connecting the CE induces electron migration in the CE, resulting in a potential difference between the WE and the CE. The magnitude of this potential difference is directly determined by the dominant flexoelectric polarization intensity of the porous block. The resulting potential difference electrostatically attracts cations to the WE, that is, the exposed CNT segments in the electrolyte, leading to the formation of an EDL at the WE/electrolyte interface. Simultaneously, the anions accumulate at the CE, establishing a complementary EDL. Notably, ion transport arising from the intrinsic chemical properties of the CNTs makes a negligible contribution to the measured current, as detailed in section S14. Thus, we now investigate the ion transport arising from the aforementioned dynamic relaxation of the EDL under the influence of the flexoelectric polarization, and the resulting current.

To model ion transport arising from the synergistic effect of the time-varying potential induced by flexoelectricity and the ionic response in the electrolyte, we first examine the influence of flexoelectricity in a single centimeter-scale solid composite beam (dimensions: 6 × 1 × 0.5 cm3) immersed in the electrolyte (fig. S18A). For the flexoelectric analysis of a porous block in air, the beam model was used as the fundamental unit representing the ligament between two nodes within the porous structure (27, 28). The beam model is justified for simulating how nonuniform deformation in the ligaments induces flexoelectric polarization and consequently drives ion transport, for the following three reasons: (i) The single-beam model circumvents structural complexity while preserving the mechanistic link between strain gradient–induced polarization and ion transport. (ii) While size effects induce quantitative discrepancies in polarization intensities between the macroscale beam and the microscale ligament, the underlying physical mechanism governing the synergy of flexoelectricity and ion transport is scale-invariant. (iii) Modeling a single beam in an electrolyte directly elucidates the role of the flexoelectricity in modulating ion transport, providing a physical foundation and quantitative basis for understanding ion transport behavior in complex porous block/electrolyte system.

As the surface potential approaches a plateau (around t = T/4), the magnitudes of IM and −ID become nearly equal, resulting in a near-zero value of ΔIt (Fig. 2Ac). Consequently, the total current (It) reaches its maximum value (Fig. 2Ad). At this stage, negative charges accumulate at the WE, while positive charges accumulate at the CE. The associated charge density decays exponentially with the distance from the electrodes (Fig. 2B).

Conversely, during the interval T/4 < t < T/2, when Δψt < 0, the density of the negative charge at the WE decreases. In this period, the magnitude of −ID exceeds that of IM, resulting in ΔIt < 0 and a corresponding reduction in It. The subsequent intervals T/2 < t < 3 T/4 and 3 T/4 < t < T display electrical responses that are antisymmetric to those of 0 < t < T/4 and T/4 < t < T/2, respectively.

Throughout the entire period (0 < t < T), the current of the system follows the deformation trajectory of the beam. Moreover, both the potential distribution and cation distribution in the vicinity of the WE also conform to the beam’s deformation trajectory, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (C and D, respectively).

Since the distance between WE and CE substantially exceeds the Debye length λd = κ−1, our analysis based on eq. S26 reveals that ΔIt∝Δψt. This relationship implies that ΔIt is governed by the change in surface potential, demonstrating its independence from the rate of potential variation. Consequently, the peak value of It is determined by the maximum value of ψ(t), and remains independent of the deformation frequency (Fig. 2E).

We define the current enhancement coefficient as n = fEDL/f (as shown in eq. S31), where fEDL denotes the relaxation frequency of the system and f is the deformation frequency. This coefficient represents the ratio of the peak current of the sample measured in electrolyte to that measured in air under identical loading conditions. This implies that the peak current output can be enhanced by increasing fEDL. As shown in Fig. 2F, fEDL exhibits an approximately linear relationship with L−1, which suggests that the peak current of the sample in electrolyte can be enhanced by reducing L. This result confirms an inverse proportionality between It and L, which is evidenced by the quantitative agreement between experiment and theory (fig. S18D).

Having analyzed the flexoelectricity driven ion transport of the beam model in electrolyte, we now turn to the porous block/electrolyte system. The porous block comprises numerous microscale beam substructures; thus, the total electrical output of the block/electrolyte system is ideally the sum of contributions from individual ligaments in the electrolyte. Using the beam model, the theoretical output peak current of the porous block/electrolyte system under 50% compressive strain is 14.6 μA, as detailed in section S4. This predicted value is in close agreement with the experimentally measured value of 11.4 μA. The smaller experimental value arises from actual ion transport occurring through a cyclically deformed and disordered pore network, which results in nonlinear ion trajectories that effectively increase the distance between WE and CE.

With an increase in the CNT content v, both the resistivity and the total resistance of the porous composite decrease, and the peak current increases as di/dv > 0. Figure 3B demonstrates that the volume-specific peak current increases with v under varying applied macroscopic compressive strains, illustrating that the experimental current is in qualitative agreement with the prediction from Eq. 3.

In general, the flexoelectric peak charge of a material is independent of the deformation frequency, whereas the flexoelectric peak current is proportional to this frequency (27, 28). However, as demonstrated by Eq. 3 and experimentally verified in Fig. 3C, the peak current of the porous block/electrolyte system remains independent of low deformation frequency (1 to 5 Hz) under identical macroscopic strain conditions. This unusual phenomenon arises from a constant change in the electrical potential of the WE before and after deformation under a fixed peak compressive strain, irrespective of the loading frequency. As established by eq. S26, the peak current is determined by the amplitude of the potential change, and is independent of the rate of potential variation. As a result, the porous block/electrolyte system displays a frequency-independent response in peak current. It is important to emphasize that the frequency-independent peak current does not negate the existence of flexoelectric polarization; rather, it demonstrates that the measured current, which results from ion transport, is governed by the frequency-independent peak value of the flexoelectric field, indicating that it is an ionic conduction current rather than a flexoelectric displacement current. Furthermore, the transferred peak charge Q is inversely proportional to the loading frequency f, as expressed by Q = i/2πf, which is verified experimentally as shown in Fig. 3C. However, when the loading frequency exceeds 5 Hz, the peak current output of the system gradually decreases with increasing frequency (fig. S19A). This decrease is due to the attenuation of the flexoelectric polarization in PDMS and the limitation of the system relaxation at high-frequency loading (see detailed analysis in section S16).

Performance of the porous block/electrolyte system

The electrical performance of the porous block/electrolyte system can be further enhanced by designing a compression/torsion configuration with a 30° twisting angle between the top and bottom surfaces (fig. S20D), as demonstrated by Yan et al. (27). We define the mass-specific and deformability-specific electrical output as Wsec = i/(mTε), where mT denotes the total mass of the system (the combined mass of both the electrolyte and the porous block). The porous block/electrolyte system exhibits a Wsec value as high as 3.41 μA/g (fig. S25A), exceeding that of the solid matrix material (truncated pyramid PDMS) (28) by 1,000,000 times. Moreover, the system demonstrates leading volume-specific and mass-specific maximum power among common electric nanogenerators (fig. S25, B and C). We further examined the operational stability and parameter sensitivity of the system. The electrical output increases with rising electrolyte temperature, exhibits an initial increase followed by a decrease with increasing electrolyte concentration, and is insensitive to the types of cations and anions as well as environmental humidity (see the experimental details in sections S18 to S20).

We now evaluate the energy conversion efficiency of the porous block/electrolyte system. To ensure comprehensive comparison, two definitions of efficiency are used. The first definition is the ratio of the output electrical energy to the total mechanical work done to deform the porous block in one loading cycle, which has been used in the literature for other types of small scale generators (35–38). This efficiency is termed the “energy conversion efficiency” (ECE, denoted by ηECE). The second definition is the ratio of the output electrical energy to the net input mechanical work of the system to generate electricity, that is, the total mechanical work done to deform the system minus the reversible elastic deformation energy of the system. This definition has also been used in the literature (15, 16, 39–41), which is termed the “net energy conversion efficiency” (NECE, denoted by ηNECE). The details of methods for calculating these two kinds of efficiencies are given in section S22. Our porous block/electrolyte system achieves an ηECE of 6.54%, and a ηNECE of 28.81%, which are 160,000 times higher than that of the porous block in air, and exceed the corresponding efficiency values of the generators reported in the literature (15, 16, 35–51) (Fig. 4A).

Another notable feature and advantage of our porous material is its compliance (softness), which makes the system a good kind of “soft” generators suitable for scenarios where the stimulating loads are low such as waves, human motion, winds, etc. We define a parameter Eσ, namely, the stress-specific electrical energy density, to characterize the electrical energy generated by a unit mass (the mass considered here is solely that of the porous block) of the porous material under a unit stress (force per area). We compare the values of Eσ of different kinds of small-scale generators that convert mechanical energy into electricity through unidirectional cyclic loading, as shown in Fig. 4B. These generators include those based on the materials with piezoelectric-like effects, nanomaterial assemblies (e.g., CNT-based nanogenerators), and strain-gradient generators relying on the flexoelectricity of porous materials in an air environment. As illustrated in Fig. 4B, among the reported generators, our system has much higher value of Eσ than those in the literatures (15, 16, 27, 29, 39, 41). The parameters for calculating Eσ are given in table S2.

Durability and scalability are key factors for applications. Figure 4C shows that the system maintains a highly stable electrical output over 10,000,000 cycles of cyclic compression, which demonstrates the excellent durability. The system conforms to both series and parallel battery models, as shown in fig. S27, enabling scalable power output via voltage or current amplification. To demonstrate this capability, three systems connected in series (with a total active material mass of 1.02 g) charged a 4.7-μF capacitor to over 2 V through a single compression. As the discharge time of the charged capacitor was less than 1 ms, the discharge instantaneously generated a current of approximately 8.5 mA and an instantaneous power of 20.6 mW, which was sufficient to illuminate 800 parallel-connected light-emitting diodes (LEDs), as shown in Fig. 4D. Further details on capacitor charging and LED illumination can be found in section S24, as well as in movies S1 and S2. Moreover, the underlying mechanism is applicable to a wide range of materials and electrolytes combinations, such as silicone (as the matrix of a composite), graphene (as the WE), and tap water (as the electrolyte), as illustrated in fig. S30, with porous PDMS/CNT exhibiting the highest value and porous pure PDMS the lowest.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we have discovered a synergistic mechanism of flexoelectricity and ion transport in an electrolyte that enables efficient energy conversion. This synergistic effect underpins an efficient energy conversion system exhibiting ultralow modulus and exceptional durability. These attributes position it as a prime candidate for converting low-frequency, low-amplitude, and omnidirectional random loads, such as waves, thereby indicating its broad applications. Crucially, strain gradient–induced polarization directs selective cation/anion migration, permitting mechanically gated ion transport and species-specific enrichment when integrated with a unidirectional permeable membrane. Beyond energy conversion, the system also provides an avenue for studying biosignaling.

Fabrication

We used a sacrificial templating method to fabricate the porous PDMS/CNT composites with specific macro and micro structures. First, the PDMS prepolymer and curing agent were mixed at a standard mass ratio of 10:1 to form a precuring PDMS mixture. Subsequently, the CNTs were dispersed in the precuring PDMS at a specific volume fraction, with particular attention to ensuring dispersion uniformity via a batch mixing approach. Then, the NaCl particles as the sacrificial template with a specific mass ratio were uniformly dispersed in the precuring PDMS/CNT mixture by stirring. The PDMS/CNT/NaCl mixture was injected into a predesigned mold and cured at 40°C for 180 min. The mold was customized using high-precision 3D printing technology (MakerBot PABH65, USA) to produce the specific macroscopic configurations of the PDMS/CNT composite. After curing, the solid PDMS/CNT/NaCl composite was immersed in an 80°C ultrapure water bath for 72 hours to dissolve the NaCl particles. Afterward, the sample was dried in a convection oven (Jinghong DHG-9030A, China) at 80°C for 2 hours to remove the residual moisture and obtain the porous composite as shown in fig. S1. It is noteworthy that the ligament thickness can be effectively controlled by adjusting the mass ratio w of the sacrificial template to the matrix (27). The pore diameter remains constant during this adjustment, since the pore structure originates from the dissolved template. As w increases, the ligament thickness decreases.

Next, a very thin layer of precuring PDMS was applied onto the top surface of the porous composite block, followed by thermal curing at 80°C for 30 min to form an insulating layer. To construct the electrical circuit, two copper wires were used to connect WE and CE to a current amplifier (fig. S2). At the top surface of the porous block, one copper wire was fixed between the CE and PDMS layer; the other was embedded in a solid conductive PDMS/CNT composite thin layer, which was bonded directly to the lower surface of the porous block, linking the wire to the embedded conductive CNT network within the PDMS matrix avoiding the contact with electrolyte. Last, the assembly was subjected to a thermal curing process at 80°C for 1 hour to complete the fabrication.

Experimental setup and electrical performance characterization

Before each electrical signal measurement, the electrostatic charge of the sample was removed by using an electrostatic eliminator (KEYENCE SJ-F036, Japan). A DMA (TA ElectroForce-DMA3200, USA) was used to apply the cyclic loading. The voltage signal was measured by a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research SR-830, USA). The current signal was measured and converted into a voltage signal by a current preamplifier (Stanford Research SR-570, USA), operating in low-noise gain mode and incorporating a low-pass filter technique. The real-time voltage signal was captured and recorded by an oscilloscope (Tektronix MDO-3034, USA). The piezoelectric coefficient d33 was measured by YE2730A d33 METER (Sinocera Piezotronics INC., P.R. China). The zeta potential was measured by Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern, UK). All measurements were conducted with a minimum of three samples, and three independent replicate experiments for each. The only exception is the durability measurement presented in Fig. 4C, which was performed using a single sample. During the durability measurement, the electrolyte was replaced every 90 min using a sterile syringe to maintain constant ionic concentration. All tests were conducted under ambient conditions of 25° ± 3°C and 30% RH, unless otherwise specified.

Figure S2 schematically illustrates the in situ cyclic compression experimental setup, where the push rod of DMA was PDMS-coated for electric insulation and avoiding triboelectricity. In beam bending tests, the cyclic loading was applied via a PDMS-coated push rod as well. The copper wires within the electrolyte were PDMS-insulated to prevent the exposure to the electrolyte, as shown in fig. S2.

Before testing, the porous composite was fully immersed in electrolyte and subjected to compression-release cycles via the push rod. During compression, this process expels trapped air from the pores, while the ensuing elastic recovery draws electrolyte into the porous structure, thereby ensuring full saturation.

Material characterization

A scanning electronic microscope (SEM; Hitachi SU8020, Japan) was used to characterize the microscopic structures. The average values of the microstructure sizes were obtained using the Nano Measurer software.
