Entrustment in Doctor–Patient Communication in Family Medicine Training - a Systematic Review of the Literature
Authors: Joana Silva-Monteiro, Laura Ribeiro, Paulo Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver recent decades, postgraduate medical education has shifted increasingly towards competency-based frameworks. Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) have emerged as a key approach to integrating the assessment of professional practice. While communication is widely recognised as a core competence in Family Medicine, its explicit operationalisation as EPAs remains unclear. This review aimed to map and conceptually analyse how EPAs have been applied to doctor–patient communication in Family Medicine training and to identify communication-intensive clinical activities that can be conceptualised as EPAs. We conducted a systematic review of the literature with qualitative evidence synthesis across five electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, EBSCO, and SciELO), complemented by citation searching, for studies published in English, Portuguese, Spanish, and French up to October 2025. Eight studies met the inclusion criteria, all using qualitative methodologies, including Delphi studies, expert consensus processes, and focus groups. Communication was most often addressed as a transversal competence embedded within broader EPAs. However, five studies identified 10 discrete communication-focused EPAs, clustered into four domains: breaking bad news, shared decision-making and patient-centred care, dealing with strong emotions, and motivational interviewing or behaviour modification. The findings indicate that, although communication is central to Family Medicine practice, its framing as a set of autonomous, communication-intensive EPAs is still in its early stages of development. Conceptualising high-stakes communication encounters as EPAs may support a more integrative and practice-oriented approach to workplace-based assessment, while highlighting the need for further research on their longitudinal educational impact in postgraduate training.

Introduction

Over the last few decades, postgraduate medical training has shifted from an outcomes-based approach to a competency-based medical education1 to better address society’s health needs and concerns regarding professionalism.2 One of the challenges in medical training is determining how to assess residents’ performance in an authentic and entrustable manner. The concept of Entrustable Professional Activity (EPA) emerged in 2005 as an effort to ensure an integrated assessment of competencies from different domains and their transference to everyday practice.3

EPAs are units of professional practice that can be entrusted to trainees once adequate competence has been demonstrated.4,5 They consist of observable, assessable, time-bounded activities that support explicit entrustment decisions under supervision.6

At a broader level, a medical speciality can be conceptualised as a set of EPAs, as these activities assess real-life professional performance by integrating multiple competency domains—such as medical knowledge, communication, and practice management—into authentic clinical tasks.7–10 While individual competency domains contribute differently across EPAs,5,7 competencies and EPAs can be represented as a two-dimensional matrix, through which residents progressively achieve the level of performance required for unsupervised practice.3,6,11 An example of EPA and levels of entrustment is presented in Table 1. By supporting workplace-based assessment (WBA), EPAs contribute to patient safety, resident autonomy, and more meaningful supervisory feedback.13

EPAs are already included in the medical curricula in several countries such as USA,8–10 Canada,13 Germany,14 the Netherlands,15,16 as well as in South Africa2 and Brazil,17 in both postgraduate and undergraduate education, covering various specific issues. The adoption of EPAs thus extends well beyond high-income Western healthcare systems, reflecting a global momentum towards competency-based frameworks in medical training. In the case of Family Medicine, the World Organisation of Family Doctors (WONCA) recommends that EPAs should be “investigated and implemented” involving the opinion of both residents and supervisors to “stay up-to-date with new knowledge, frameworks and approaches”.18

Clinical communication skills are of paramount importance to all medical specialities. In Family Medicine, they play a pivotal role in doctor–patient relationship strengthening over time and in the formation of the therapeutic alliance, thereby ensuring the achievement of health outcomes.14 The value of this personal relationship is determined by the Family Doctor’s communication skills and is, itself, therapeutic.18

Communication skills are shared by several different EPAs and are represented both in the CanMeds framework (role of “doctor as a communicator”)16 and in ACGME descriptors (“interpersonal and communication skills”)15 as “domains of competence”. Some recurrent, high-stakes clinical tasks depend almost exclusively on doctor–patient communication, making them particularly suitable for operationalisation within an EPA framework. However, to date, there is no consensus on which competencies should be acquired during residency, their timing, or the best way to evaluate their acquisition and autonomous execution.19

The purpose of our review was to map and conceptually analyse how EPAs have been applied to doctor–patient communication in Family Medicine training, and to identify specific communication-related activities that may be conceptualised and operationalised as EPAs within a workplace-based assessment framework. To our knowledge, no previous systematic review has focused specifically on the operationalisation of doctor–patient communication as EPAs in Family Medicine training. Given the exploratory nature of the research question, the review focused on mapping and conceptually synthesising existing literature, rather than concentrating on effectiveness outcomes.

All phases were performed by two independent reviewers, with a third serving as a tie-breaker when needed, considering the eligibility criteria. Search Strategy

Identification Phase

We defined the search expression from an initial set of studies and calibrated it by searching PubMed with various combinations of terms. The most relevant search queries23,24 were then used (Appendix 1). We took advantage of the authors’ language fluency in English, Spanish, Portuguese, and French, and surveyed the literature in these languages. The search was performed in five electronic databases (PubMed®, Scopus®, Web of Science®, EBSCO®, Scielo®). Additionally, we used a snowballing approach to identify pertinent articles from reference lists, Google Scholar, and Google. Studies were included from inception until October 3rd, 2025, the date when the search was conducted.

Study Selection

Eligibility Criteria

To meet the inclusion criteria, studies had to describe EPAs relevant to doctor–patient communication in Family Medicine or closely related primary care training contexts, including undergraduate education in community medicine where the EPAs described were directly applicable to Family Medicine practice. Articles were excluded if: (a) they were merely descriptive of the process of EPA implementation without listing the proposed EPAs, (b) they did not include the list of proposed EPAs, and (c) their typology was comments, editorials, or related manuscripts.

Screening Phase

Once all articles were obtained, duplicates across databases were identified and excluded using EndNote. The titles and abstracts of the remaining studies were then evaluated by two independent reviewers (JSM and PS).

Eligibility Phase

The full texts of all included articles were extracted. Two independent reviewers applied the eligibility criteria, and a third served as a tie-breaker. The reference lists of each eligible article were scrutinised to check for omitted studies.

Quality Assessment and Risk of Bias

The quality and risk of bias of the eligible studies was assessed using the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR),25 a list of 21 items essential for the complete and transparent reporting of qualitative research. Disagreement between the reviewers was resolved by consensus involving the three authors.

Data Collection Process

A standard data extraction form was created, and general data extracted from each study included the following characteristics: article titles, first authors’ names, publication type, country of origin, year of publication, aims/research questions, and summary/ general findings. Two reviewers independently extracted the data. Differences in the extracted data were again resolved through consensus.

Data Synthesis

We analysed the EPAs proposed in each study and conducted a qualitative evidence synthesis.26,27

Relevant EPAs were thoroughly analysed, including their subcomponents or competencies, when available, to identify specific EPAs designed to assess Clinical Communication Skills and outcomes of their implementation. Results

Search and Study Selection

Our initial search yielded 397 articles: PubMed (n = 206), Scopus (n = 104), EBSCO (n = 49), Scielo (n = 3), and Web of Science (n = 35). After removing duplicates, a total of 305 articles remained and were evaluated in the screening phase (title and abstract review) by two independent reviewers (JSM and PS). Of these, 290 articles were excluded because their type and article’s typology and subject matter were not related to EPAs.

Eligibility Phase

The full texts of all included articles (n = 15) were extracted and screened for the inclusion criteria. Additional sources (websites and citation searching) were also consulted, resulting in the retrieval of three additional articles. Finally, we included eight articles in the review that mentioned EPAs in Family Medicine and/or doctor–patient communication. The eligibility criteria were reapplied by two independent reviewers and a third as a tie-breaker. The reference lists of each eligible article were scrutinised to check for omitted studies.

Study Characteristics

The eight included articles were published between 2013 and 2025. Seven of these were qualitative development studies (including Delphi studies and focus groups), and one was an iterative group study. Most studies were conducted in high-income countries, predominantly in Western healthcare systems (n = 5). A summary of their characteristics, as well as suggested EPAs, is presented in Table 2.

Five of these articles were Delphi studies, one was a focus group, one was an iterative group process with consensus, and the other was a consensus of a panel of experts.

EPAs and Family Medicine

All the qualitative studies included in this review described the process of defining EPAs in different parts of the world, mainly from Western Countries, as expected. They defined EPAs for Family Medicine undergraduate (n = 2) and postgraduate students (n = 6) using existing frameworks. Six studies mentioning EPAs related explicitly to doctor–patient communication10,19,29–31 are outlined in Table 4. One study specifically aimed to define EPAs for Communication Skills.19 The remaining studies included EPAs as more complex activities that require underlying communication competencies for their correct and entrusted execution. Quality of Articles

Overall, the quality assessment of articles using the SRQR 21-item questionnaire25 showed no significant methodological issues (Table 3).

Discussion

This review suggests that using Entrustable Professional Activities to frame and assess doctor–patient communication in Family Medicine training is still in its early stages of development. While communication is widely recognised as a core component of Family Medicine practice, it has seldom been translated into clearly defined, communication-focused EPAs.

Traditionally, communication competencies were already considered as part of certain EPAs, both in Family Medicine and other medical specialities, as can be confirmed both in CanMeds16 (Canada), ACGME15,32 (USA), and EPAs3,5 (The Netherlands). Despite being a daily task in doctors’ clinical practice, we only found a small number of communication skills-related EPAs. This may reflect the lack of awareness about the importance of Communication Skills assessment in Postgraduate training, specifically in Family Medicine, as it should be included as part of the speciality postgraduate training and evaluation; however, this is not yet the case.

The ten proposed EPAs identified in this review can be organised into four distinct areas: breaking bad news, shared decision-making/patient-centred care, dealing with strong emotions, and motivational interviewing/behavioural modification. These areas correspond to recurrent, high-stakes clinical encounters in Family Medicine and reflect core professional commitments of the Family Doctor, including person-centred care, a comprehensive approach, and holistic practice.14 From an educational perspective, these activities lend themselves to operationalisation as EPAs because they can be clearly delineated in terms of observable behaviours, levels of supervision, and entrustment decisions. For example, an EPA addressing “breaking bad news” can include a structured observation of a consultation, with explicit criteria for progression from direct to indirect supervision, and provide constructive feedback linked to specific communication behaviours. Similarly, shared decision-making and motivational interviewing encounters can be assessed using workplace-based tools that align with authentic clinical practice.

This operational potential raises important educational questions regarding how such communication-focused EPAs should be defined, taught, assessed, and organised in a sequential manner throughout residency training. In particular, it highlights the need to explore the preparatory training required, the process of making entrustment decisions in real clinical settings, and how these EPAs contribute to the development of autonomy in patient-centred care - questions that directly informed the subsequent phases of our research.

Evidence suggests that EPA-based assessment facilitates more specific feedback, individualised learning goals, and progressive entrustment decisions when compared to a traditional competency-based assessment framework, allowing for a more precise and authentic evaluation of trainees.33–35 However, none of the identified studies examined the longitudinal impact of communication-focused EPAs in postgraduate training. As a result, further research is needed to determine whether sustained use of EPAs in doctor–patient communication contributes to meaningful improvements over time rather than isolated assessment moments, particularly in terms of communication effectiveness, resident development, supervisory decision-making, and patient experience.

Entrustable Professional Activities have been implemented across multiple medical specialities, including General Surgery,34,36,37 Paediatrics,35 Internal Medicine,38 Psychiatry,35 Radiology,39 and Family Medicine,28 supporting their feasibility and educational utility in WBA. However, in most of these contexts, communication is treated as a transversal competence embedded within broader clinical activities, rather than as the primary focus of entrustment decisions.

While communication itself does not constitute an EPA, specific, discrete, observable, and high-stakes clinical communication activities may be meaningfully conceptualised as autonomous EPAs. Examples include conducting a breaking bad news consultation, leading a shared decision-making encounter for a complex or preference-sensitive condition, or managing a consultation involving strong patient emotions or conflict. These activities are recurrent in Family Medicine, have clear boundaries, carry significant implications for patient care, and require supervisors to make explicit entrustment decisions regarding the level of autonomy granted to trainees.

Framing such communication-intensive clinical activities as standalone EPAs can contribute to a more structured approach to assessment and to the longitudinal development of communication skills in clinical practice, complementing their traditional role as a transversal competence within other professional activities.

Implications for Teaching and Learning

Conceptualising discrete, high-stakes communication activities as Entrustable Professional Activities has important implications for teaching and learning in Family Medicine and supports more meaningful WBA. By shifting the focus from abstract communication competencies to observable professional activities embedded in real clinical encounters, communication-focused EPAs provide educators with a practical framework to align teaching, assessment, and entrustment decisions within workplace-based learning.

From a teaching perspective, these EPAs can help clarify learning expectations for residents and support the intentional teaching of complex communication encounters, such as breaking bad news or shared decision-making in preference-sensitive situations. For supervisors, communication-focused EPAs may facilitate more specific, behaviour-oriented feedback and contribute to greater consistency in assessment and entrustment decisions across faculty members.

Importantly, framing communication-intensive activities as EPAs supports a longitudinal view of learning, allowing residents’ progressive autonomy in doctor–patient communication to be monitored and supported over time, rather than assessed through isolated or episodic evaluation moments. At a programmatic level, communication-focused EPAs may enhance the coherence between curricular goals, assessment strategies, and the core values of patient-centred care in Family Medicine training, while also providing a framework for faculty development and supervisory calibration.

Nonetheless, we identified only a limited number of studies that explicitly address communication-related EPAs, most of which relied on qualitative methodologies. As a result, the conclusions of this review are largely informed by expert consensus and conceptual work, which constitutes a limitation and introduces an unavoidable degree of subjectivity.

Although the EPA framework has been increasingly adopted across medical education since its initial development in 2005, evidence supporting its impact - particularly in the assessment of communication-intensive activities - remains limited. This underscores the need for further empirical research, including quantitative and longitudinal studies, to better understand the educational value of EPAs in postgraduate training. This review, rather than generalising the results, raises new questions and guides future work on this topic.

Only one study reported results from EPA implementation in undergraduate training,17 and none in postgraduate training. Future studies could help determine the impact of implementing EPAs for Family Medicine and/or doctor–patient communication in Postgraduate training, particularly in Family Medicine specialisation.

The main question remains: what communication skills does a doctor need to demonstrate to be competent, and, before that, what activities related to doctor–patient communication does a resident need to demonstrate to be considered skilled and able to perform autonomously? An empathetic, patient-centred relationship is based on effective communication skills and constitutes the cornerstone of medical care,40 with potential therapeutic benefits. Our review highlights areas where we need to evolve further, considering communication-intensive clinical activities as EPAs, rather than viewing communication solely as a transversal competence.

Furthermore, it is essential to determine whether the systematic integration of communication-focused EPAs in Family Medicine will lead to a significant improvement beyond current isolated assessment events, particularly in terms of efficiency and satisfaction among all parties involved. Future studies could be conducted to compare traditional assessment approaches with phased EPA implementation models, using mixed-methods designs to capture longitudinal outcomes, such as residents’ progress in autonomy and confidence in managing complex communication encounters, quality and specificity of supervisor feedback, consistency of entrustment decisions across supervisors, but also other aspects like perceived workload or cognitive burden associated with assessment. It may also be relevant to consider the patient’s perspective. This can be achieved by using outcomes such as perceived involvement in decision-making, clarity of information received, patients’ trust in their physician, empathy, patient-centeredness, and overall satisfaction with the consultation. At the program level, feasibility, acceptability, and sustainability of communication-focused EPAs within routine clinical practice should also be explored.

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Although we followed a rigorous systematic methodology, including PROSPERO registration, a comprehensive multilingual search strategy, independent screening, and structured quality appraisal, the available evidence base is inherently limited. All included studies used qualitative approaches. As a result, the evidence remains exploratory and heterogeneous, with no longitudinal or quantitative evaluations of EPA implementation in postgraduate Family Medicine training. This positions the present work as a systematic review with qualitative evidence synthesis, and it constrains the strength of empirical inferences that can be drawn.

In addition, the heterogeneity of EPA definitions, the variability in how communication competencies were conceptualised across studies, and the absence of standardised implementation outcomes limit the comparability of findings. Publication bias may also be present, as expert-driven EPA development studies are more likely to be published than unsuccessful or abandoned initiatives. Finally, the small number of eligible studies reflects the early stage of this field and underscores the need for more robust, empirical, and longitudinal research to evaluate the educational impact of communication-focused EPAs in real training environments.

Conclusions

Framing discrete, high-stakes doctor–patient communication activities as Entrustable Professional Activities may support a more integrative and practice-oriented approach to assessing patient-centred care in Family Medicine. EPAs can provide a structured approach to align teaching, assessment, and core entrustment decisions within workplace-based learning, shifting the focus from generic communication competencies to observable professional activities that are incorporated into daily clinical encounters.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to map communication-focused EPAs specifically in the context of Family Medicine training. It provides a foundation for training programme directors and supervisors to begin structuring the assessment of high-stakes communication encounters – such as breaking bad news, shared decision-making, and motivational interviewing – as observable, entrusted professional activities rather than as background competencies. This review highlights the need for more precise directives on how communication-intensive activities can be defined, taught, and assessed during residency training, and underscores communication-focused EPAs as a promising framework to support both learner development and high-quality clinical interactions.
