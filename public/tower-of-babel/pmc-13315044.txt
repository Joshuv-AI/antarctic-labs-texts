An Evidence-Based Pathway from Functional Classification to Intraocular Lens Specialization: A Case Example Applying the PECO Framework
Authors: Joaquín Fernández, Filomena Ribeiro, Burkhard H Dick, Carlos Rocha-de-Lossada, Manuel Rodríguez-Vallejo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSimultaneous vision intraocular lenses (SVIOLs) aim to extend postoperative depth of field (DOFi) and reduce spectacle dependence after cataract or refractive lens exchange surgery. To support evidence-based selection among the growing number of SVIOL models, the predict–explore–confirm–optimize (PECO) framework is proposed as a structured SVIOL evidence development framework to guide systematic evidence generation and clinical decision-making. PECO is implemented as a sequential pathway for evidence generation, in which optical bench testing is performed first (Predict), early clinical experience is subsequently accumulated (Explore), outcomes and safety are then confirmed in large peer-reviewed clinical studies (Confirm), and patient- and procedure-specific factors are finally addressed through targeted research (Optimize). This review applies the PECO framework to a case example: the Liberty trifocal SVIOL, which has been clinically validated as a Full-DOFi Smooth SVIOL. Beyond standard safety and efficacy outcomes, evidence in the Optimization stage addresses patient expectations, dysphotopsia risk, contrast sensitivity, biometric accuracy, corneal aberrations, pupil size, centration, and posterior capsular opacification. The Liberty example illustrates how evidence extending beyond functional classification can refine patient selection, surgical planning, and counseling. Overall, the PECO framework emphasizes that optimal SVIOL outcomes depend not only on confirmed functional performance, but also on individualized, evidence-informed specialization tailored to patient characteristics and clinical context.Keywords: Intraocular lens, Functional classification, Evidence, Simultaneous vision

Introduction

Simultaneous vision intraocular lenses (SVIOLs) are used in cataract and refractive lens exchange surgeries to increase postoperative depth of field (DOFi) compared with the preoperative condition without spectacles [1, 2]. Evidence in this field continues to grow, supported by a global portfolio of more than 100 intraocular lens (IOL) models from various manufacturers [3]. The most frequently published studies report standard clinical outcomes at various follow-up intervals, including safety, efficacy, predictability, etc. [4]. The substantial number of studies describing these standard outcomes enabled the development of the functional classification, which groups IOLs into six performance categories: Partial-DOFi (Narrowed, Enhanced, Extended) and Full-DOFi (Steep, Smooth, Continuous) [5–7]. Although similar outcomes are expected for IOLs assigned to the same category, variability is consistently observed as a result of confounding factors related to clinical procedures and patient characteristics. Therefore, once the patient’s expectations have been established and the likelihood that a given functional classification category will meet those expectations is understood, the next step is to select a specific IOL within that category [8]. Evidence at the level of IOL selection extends beyond the standard outcomes described above and can address specific clinical questions related to ocular characteristics and procedural factors encountered during the patient’s journey toward spectacle independence. Accordingly, the present review is intended as the next step after the practical pathway outlined by Fernández et al. [8]: whereas that earlier review focuses on functional classification and category selection, this manuscript builds on that starting point by showing how evidence then evolves to support lens-specific refinement and optimization within the chosen category. Readers who have not yet read the previous review are encouraged to consult it first, as it sets the stage for the progression developed here.

Methods

This work is presented as a narrative, practice-oriented review aimed at illustrating the application of the PECO (Predict–Explore–Confirm–Optimize) framework, with particular emphasis on the Optimization stage. The first part of the manuscript introduces the PECO framework as a conceptual structure to organize the different stages of evidence generation in SVIOLs. This section is intended to provide a general perspective on how evidence evolves from theoretical prediction to clinical optimization.

In the second part, the Liberty trifocal IOL (Medicontur Medical Engineering Ltd. Inc., Zsámbék, Hungary) is used as a practical example to illustrate the application of the framework. A formal systematic search of the literature was not followed because the primary objective was not to address a narrowly defined clinical question or to provide a quantitative synthesis of outcomes from Liberty but rather to introduce and illustrate a PECO framework example. The literature reviewed in this example was selected on the basis of the authors’ research experience gained over the years, providing a practical illustration of the approach they took during the Optimization phase, with the primary goal of expanding their knowledge to inform individualized decision-making in their routine clinical practice, with a particular focus on the specialization with a particular IOL. Importantly, this IOL is used solely as an illustrative model, and the PECO framework is intended to be platform-independent and applicable across different SVIOLs.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Discussion

Evidence-Based Intraocular Lens Selection Framework

To appropriately weigh the evidence supporting SVIOLs, it is essential to acknowledge that different study types do not contribute equally to clinical decision-making. Evidence for any new IOL typically follows an implicit, stepwise pathway in which its performance is progressively demonstrated. Although similar stepwise processes exist in the regulatory and development pathways of medical devices, including IOLs, these are primarily designed to demonstrate safety and efficacy for market approval [9]. In contrast, the generation of clinical evidence to support everyday decision-making follows a different objective: not only to confirm that a device performs adequately but to understand how it behaves across diverse patient profiles and clinical scenarios.

The SVIOL evidence development framework illustrated in Fig. 1 begins with optical bench characterization, assessing the IOL in controlled laboratory conditions to determine its optical properties and Predict theoretical functional performance [10]. This stage plays a role comparable to preclinical evaluation in drug development: essential for establishing feasibility and safety expectations, yet insufficient to draw conclusions about patient outcomes [9].

Simultaneous vision intraocular lens evidence development framework illustrates the progressive generation of evidence supporting intraocular lens performance. This framework is analogous to the logical progression in drug development, where confidence increases as evaluation advances from laboratory assessment to clinical studies

Next, initial clinical experience studies Explore early insight into safety, visual performance, and predictability in small patient cohorts with results presented at congresses. These studies resemble phase I drug trials, necessary for establishing feasibility in humans but insufficient for broad generalization [11]. Substantial clinical confidence only emerges once an IOL is evaluated in larger, peer-reviewed clinical studies using standardized endpoints. These investigations, analogous to phase II/III drug trials [12], are essential for Confirming an IOL’s true functional performance and determining its alignment within the functional classification [5]. Clinicians rely on this level of evidence to understand not just whether the lens works but how it performs relative to alternatives [8]. Finally, once an IOL’s functional category is validated, ongoing research Optimizes clinical outcomes for individual patients by addressing sources of variability such as ocular biometry, residual astigmatism, pupil diameter, and predicted residual target, among others. Mirroring post-marketing phase IV drug studies, this specialized evidence refines clinical indications, enhances predictability, and supports personalized selection of the optimal model for each patient [13].

In this context, the novelty of the PECO framework lies not in redefining the stages of evidence generation but in explicitly structuring them within a unified, clinically applicable model that extends beyond validation toward optimization. In particular, the formal inclusion of the Optimization stage emphasizes a shift from demonstrating average performance to refining individualized outcomes, which remains underrepresented in traditional evaluation paradigms.

Altogether, the PECO framework underscores that, while optical bench data provide a critical foundation, they cannot replace clinical validation. The process of IOL evidence generation should not conclude with the reporting of standard outcomes but must progress to an Optimization stage, aimed at enhancing individual results through targeted research questions and enabling surgeon specialization in IOL selection tailored to patient-specific needs.

Case Example for a SVIOL

At this point, having described the process of generating evidence based on the PECO framework, we will illustrate a practical case that highlights the path from development to specialization with an IOL.

The Liberty trifocal IOL (Medicontur Medical Engineering Ltd. Inc., Zsámbék, Hungary) reflects general optical trade-offs common to diffractive SVIOLs, particularly in balancing light distribution across distance, intermediate, and near focal points. Evidence surrounding Liberty optics, previously described in the literature [14, 15], has grown in alignment with the PECO framework, progressing from optical bench investigations [16, 17] to clinical studies confirming a Full-DOFi Smooth performance [15, 18–20]. This narrative review specifically focuses on the Optimization stage (“O”) within the previously described PECO framework for the Liberty optics. Beyond the assessment of standard clinical and visual outcomes, this stage encompasses a broader set of evidence-driven research questions aimed at refining patient selection, surgical planning, and postoperative performance. Accordingly, the following points integrate and discuss evidence addressing several complementary topics that have emerged from clinical experience and published data. Collectively, these sections illustrate how the available evidence extends beyond conventional endpoints to support more informed preoperative decision-making, including identification of optimal and suboptimal patient profiles for Liberty optics implantation.

Preventing Dissatisfied Patients

To prevent patient dissatisfaction, it is essential to align clinical outcomes with patient expectations and, whenever possible, achieve results that meet or exceed those expectations, while minimizing adverse events in populations at higher risk [21, 22]. In our experience with Liberty optics, we have sought evidence-based methods that can also be transferred to other SVIOLs and that help complement the shared decision-making process with objective measures, thereby supporting clinical decisions with greater confidence. Our first approach to achieving this goal is based on the premise that the probability of achieving a particular postoperative endpoint can be estimated or measured preoperatively. For example, on the basis solely of the functional classification of an IOL, we can estimate the probability of achieving a given mean visual acuity in a sample [8], and the probability of spectacle independence can also be predicted from that visual acuity value [23]. Using the standard reported outcomes for Liberty, the estimated probabilities of achieving binocular uncorrected visual acuities of 20/20, 20/25, and 20/32 were 53%, 91%, and 100%, respectively [15]. On the other hand, 85.4% of patients achieved complete near spectacle independence, while only 7.3% required near correction all or most of the time [15].

Whereas managing expectations solely through visual acuity and spectacle independence may be sufficient in healthy eyes with LOCS III cataract grade 2, since distance vision is fully restored and improved regardless of the implanted SVIOL compared to the preoperative period [24], patients with clearer crystalline lenses, such as those undergoing refractive lens exchange, may require the assessment of additional endpoints, including the size of photic phenomena and contrast sensitivity measurements, or even expectations can be managed with the use of advanced IOL simulators [16, 25]. Regarding photic phenomena and positive dysphotopsia [22], Liberty optics demonstrated a median postoperative monocular light distortion index (LDI) of 15.21% and an ocular scatter index (OSI) of 2.9. In contrast, preoperative patients presenting with LDI values > 15.2% and OSI values ≥ 2.8 showed a higher probability of transitioning from slight to moderate bother related to photic phenomena postoperatively [26]. These findings suggest that Liberty optics operate near the threshold separating slightly from moderate dysphotopsia in terms of average patient-reported outcomes. Consequently, if patients at the preoperative stage report moderate or greater subjective bother from dysphotopsia despite exhibiting LDI < 15.2% or OSI < 2.8, such cases should be approached with increased caution, as sclerosis of crystalline lens cannot justify the reported symptom severity. This discrepancy may reflect heightened patient sensitivity or nonoptical factors influencing subjective perception. A similar rationale applies to contrast sensitivity (CS). With Liberty optics, monocular CS at high spatial frequencies (approx. 18 cycles per degree) have demonstrated a mean postoperative value of approximately 0.8 logCS. Patients presenting preoperative CS values below this threshold, yet reporting satisfactory preoperative visual quality, may have a higher likelihood of achieving their postoperative expectations, as distance CS is expected to be restored or improved following implantation [27]. Indirectly, CS values below 0.8 logCS are more commonly observed in patients older than 62 years and in eyes with OSI values exceeding 1.25 [27]. Beyond the OSI, the authors have established corresponding threshold values for other indices and measurement systems, as detailed in the original references [26, 27].

Intraocular Lens Power Calculation

The scientific literature contains many studies comparing IOL power calculation formulas, which may increase uncertainty regarding appropriate formula selection for individual patients. In our efforts to improve outcomes with the Liberty IOL, several key questions were identified, including the magnitude of errors arising from, manufacturer tolerance, optical biometry and clinical refraction [28–30], the clinical relevance of published comparative studies [31], and even the feasibility of developing an optimized formula that accounts for the Liberty optical design, the biometry platforms available at our center, and our specific clinical setting [32]. Based on our experience with these complex studies, our primary recommendation is to avoid wasting time on blind experimentation with formulas reported to perform better in retrospective database studies, as such studies are frequently influenced by multiple confounding factors inherent to their design. On the other hand, these basic steps can be followed:

Use the Barrett Universal II (BUII) formula, which is widely regarded as the gold standard for IOL power calculations, based on data from the IOLEvidence database (Qvision Academy, https://apple.co/3NQ6Feu) [33], where more than 60% of studies employ this formula. Interestingly, the SRK/T formula remains the second most used (approx. 20%), and although it generally provides satisfactory outcomes [31], there is sufficient evidence that newer-generation formulas, such as BUII, Kane, or EVO, can increase the proportion of eyes within ± 0.50 D by approximately 5–10% in routine clinical practice with Liberty [15, 34].

Regarding toric IOL calculation, various methods have been evaluated, and findings consistently support the inclusion of posterior corneal astigmatism [38]. Considering the posterior cornea reduces the systematic overcorrection in with-the-rule astigmatism and undercorrection in against-the-rule astigmatism that occurs when relying solely on anterior keratometry. Both direct measurement and estimation techniques have shown comparable effectiveness; notably, the Abulafia correction available via Medicontur’s toric calculator (https://toriccalculator.net) performs similarly to the Barrett approach [38]. However, surgeons should avoid incorporating surgically induced astigmatism (SIA) values derived from anterior corneal measurements when used in conjunction with these correction methods [39].

Corneal Higher-Order Aberrations

Several questions have long been raised regarding the effect of corneal higher-order aberrations (HOAs) in patients implanted with IOLs. These include whether the positive corneal spherical aberration of the normal cornea should be compensated [40], which magnitude and coefficients of aberrations increase DOFi [41], and what level of corneal HOAs can be tolerated in patients implanted with SVIOLs [42]. A pilot study conducted using the Liberty platform found no significant correlations between visual performance and the root mean square at a 4-mm pupil (RMS-4), spherical aberration at a 6-mm pupil, or spherical aberration (SA) measured under mesopic conditions [40]. The last case was highlighted by the authors as the most appropriate parameter to assess instead of apertures at 6 mm, as patients implanted with IOLs rarely achieve mesopic pupil diameters of 6 mm (less than 5% of cases) [43].

Given the limitations of this preliminary study, a more comprehensive analysis was subsequently performed to further investigate potential correlations and to establish the normal range for total HOAs measured with Pentacam at a 4-mm pupil [42]. In healthy candidates for Liberty implantation, the range evaluated for RMS-4 was between 0.06 and 0.60 µm. The authors concluded that values below 0.3 µm for RMS-4, 0.2 µm for coma and trefoil, and 0.1 µm for SA at a 4-mm pupil can be considered completely normal and safe. Higher values were uncommon but still might be safe, and despite a limited number of cases exceeding 0.5 µm for RMS-4, the authors suggested a cutoff value of approximately 0.5 µm at 4 mm as a reasonable clinical threshold, consistent with previous theoretical recommendations [44].

Pupil Diameter

Pupil diameter is one of the most important variables for understanding individual visual performance after SVIOL implantation. As with other SVIOLs [45–48], Liberty shows the highest performance across the DOFi for small pupil diameters (≤ 2.5 mm) [17, 42], meaning that patients with smaller pupils are expected to achieve better visual acuity across all distances. Visual acuity at far and near distances can improve by approximately one line in eyes with photopic pupils ≤ 2.5 mm compared with eyes with photopic pupils > 4.0 mm [17]. Moreover, these differences may be greater at intermediate vision, reaching up to two lines between these pupil-size extremes [17]. Therefore, although monocular functional visual acuity (≤ 0.3 logMAR) can be maintained across the range of pupil diameters observed in our clinical practice, 1.77 to 4.99 mm under photopic conditions measured with the Keratograph (equivalent to 2.01 to 5.03 mm measured with the Pentacam) and 2.50 to 6.67 mm under mesopic conditions measured either with Keratograph or Pentacam AXL Wave [15, 43], this decrease in visual acuity with the increment of pupil size, especially at intermediate vision and secondary at near, should be considered during preoperative counseling to manage patient expectations, especially in patients with big photopic pupil sizes > 4 mm and high demands regarding intermediate and near visual acuities (≤ 0.1 logMAR). On the other hand, Liberty, with a diffractive structure limited to 3 mm, unlike other SVIOLs whose diffractive surface covers a larger area, has not shown a relationship between the size of photic phenomena and mesopic pupil diameter [49–52]; it maintains a median light distortion index below 15% [52], which corresponds to a high probability of patients reporting that they are not bothered or only slightly bothered by dysphotopsia (≤ 7.7%) [15, 26, 53].

Ocular Angles and IOL Centration

The influence of ocular angles on functional performance and the criteria for IOL selection has historically been a controversial topic and remains so despite the growing body of evidence addressing this issue [54, 55]. Currently, several studies apply arbitrary cutoff criteria based on population-derived normal ranges, without clear evidence of an associated impact on subjective visual performance [55]. In our early experience with Liberty, no correlations were found between chord mu (often misnamed the kappa angle) and visual acuity across the defocus range. These findings were later confirmed by Cervantes-Coste et al., although they reported significant correlations involving chord alpha (often misnamed the alpha angle) [18]. The observed correlations with chord alpha may be explained by multicollinearity with IOL centration, as crystalline lens centration may influence the final IOL position, which is likely the true determinant of visual performance outcomes [40, 55]. Notably, across several studies evaluating Liberty centration in relation to visual acuity and the size of photic phenomena, we observed that a slight temporal decentration of less than 0.5 mm was associated with reduced photic phenomena and improved intermediate visual performance [40, 52]. Therefore, Liberty users should not apply exclusion criteria based on ocular angles (neither chord mu nor chord alpha). Contrary to earlier assumptions, a slight temporal decentration ≤ 0.5 mm appears to enhance visual performance, whereas nasal centration relative to the vertex normal (first Purkinje image) may account for dysphotopsia and reduced visual acuity [40, 52].

Posterior Capsular Opacification

Material composition and IOL platform design have been associated with the risk of posterior capsular opacification (PCO) requiring Nd:YAG capsulotomy [56]. Liberty is manufactured from a flexible copolymer composed of both hydrophobic and hydrophilic monomers. The material contains 25% water and incorporates an ultraviolet-blocking agent and a blue-light filter, with a refractive index of 1.46 and an Abbe number of 58. When compared with other SVIOLs made of similar materials, Liberty has demonstrated lower rates of PCO requiring Nd:YAG capsulotomy. This finding may be explained by the squared-edge optic design or the IOL platform [57, 58]. The impact of PCO on visual performance can be detected at 12 months through contrast sensitivity defocus curves, although similar Nd:YAG capsulotomy rates may be observed in the short term [57, 58]. This short-term follow-up may explain why the 12-month Nd:YAG rates in our center were comparable between Liberty (2.43% for the four closed-loop platform and 6.9% for the double-loop platform) and ELON, a hydrophobic partial-DOFi IOL from the same manufacturer (4.35% for the double-loop platform) [15, 57, 59]. Therefore, a follow-up period of at least 3 years appears necessary to enhance the detection of differences and to adequately assess potential variations in Nd:YAG capsulotomy rates between different materials of the same manufacturer [56].

Conclusion

The Optimization stage of the PECO framework represents a critical transition from establishing the general functional performance of a SVIOL to refining its use in individual patients. Once an IOL has been validated within a specific functional vision classification category, further gains in clinical outcomes depend on the alignment between patient characteristics, surgical planning, and realistic postoperative expectations. This narrative review uses Liberty optics as a case example to illustrate how evidence generated beyond standard safety and efficacy endpoints can inform the Optimization stage of the PECO framework.

For Liberty optics, the accumulated evidence supports a preoperative strategy in which expected postoperative performance is first interpreted using population-based outcomes, including visual acuity and spectacle independence. These global expectations are subsequently refined through the preoperative assessment of additional visual and optical endpoints for which clinically meaningful cutoff values have been identified. In practical terms, patients older than 62 years old with OSI ≥ 1.25 might present lower risk of postoperative complaints of contrast sensitivity and even of dysphotopsia for OSI ≥ 2.8.

On the other hand, IOL power calculation with Liberty optics can be optimized by adhering three fundamental principles: (1) selecting a modern, well-validated formula such as Barrett Universal II; (2) optimizing IOL constants according to the biometry platform in use; and (3) prioritizing high-quality, reproducible corneal measurements. For toric IOLs, explicit consideration of posterior corneal astigmatism should be regarded as mandatory. Together, these steps provide a practical framework to minimize refractive error, reduce postoperative surprises, and support confident IOL calculation in routine clinical practice.

Additional anatomical and optical considerations can further refine Liberty IOL selection. Corneal higher-order aberrations within established normal ranges appear to be well tolerated; however, an RMS (4-mm zone) exceeding 0.5 µm may be considered a practical exclusion criteria cutoff for implantation with Liberty optics. Greater attention should be paid to photopic pupil size, as an improvement of approximately one line of monocular visual acuity favoring very small pupils (≤ 2.5 mm) may be observed compared with larger photopic pupils (> 4.0 mm). This advantage may increase to up to two lines at intermediate vision and should be addressed during preoperative counseling to align postoperative expectations.

Ocular angles, such as chords mu and alpha, do not appear to constitute reliable exclusion criteria. Instead, subtle variations in IOL centration may play a more relevant role in functional outcomes and in the perception of photic phenomena. Performance may be optimized with slight temporal decentration versus the normal vertex, provided decentration remains < 0.5 mm. Finally, proactively advising patients about long-term considerations, particularly posterior capsular opacification and the possible need for Nd:YAG capsulotomy, can reinforce shared decision-making and patient confidence. Scheduling long-term postoperative follow-up around the average time at which Nd:YAG appears to be necessary in the average population can facilitate the timely restoration of visual quality to the levels the patient experienced in the immediate postoperative period.

In summary, optimization of SVIOL implantation extends beyond confirmation of its functional classification and requires an integrated, evidence-informed approach to patient selection and surgical planning. By combining realistic performance expectations, precise biometric calculation, and individualized assessment of optical and anatomical factors, clinicians can improve predictability, reduce dissatisfaction, and support confident, patient-centered IOL indication within routine clinical practice. Finally, the findings of the Optimization stage discussed for this particular IOL should be interpreted with caution since these are derived from limited datasets and a single-center experience. A systematic review of the literature might have resulted in inconsistent findings, particularly regarding higher-order aberrations, pupil size, and ocular angles.
