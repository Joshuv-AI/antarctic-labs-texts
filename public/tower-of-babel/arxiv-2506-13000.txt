Recovery of initial displacement and velocity in anisotropic elastic systems by the time dimensional reduction method
Trong D. Dang, Chanh V. Le, Khoa D. Luu, Loc H. Nguyen

Abstract
We introduce a time-dimensional reduction method for the inverse source problem in linear elasticity, where the goal is to reconstruct the initial displacement and velocity fields from partial boundary measurements of elastic wave propagation. The key idea is to employ a novel spectral representation in time, using an orthonormal basis composed of Legendre polynomials weighted by exponential functions. This Legendre polynomial-exponential basis enables a stable and accurate decomposition in the time variable, effectively reducing the original space-time inverse problem to a sequence of coupled spatial elasticity systems that no longer depend on time. These resulting systems are solved using the quasi-reversibility method. On the theoretical side, we establish a convergence theorem ensuring the stability and consistency of the regularized solution obtained by the quasi-reversibility method as the noise level tends to zero. On the computational side, two-dimensional numerical experiments confirm the theory and demonstrate the method’s ability to accurately reconstruct both the geometry and amplitude of the initial data, even under substantial measurement noise. The results highlight the effectiveness of the proposed framework as a robust and computationally efficient strategy for inverse elastic source problems.

Let
\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d}

Let

denote the displacement field of the medium. It is governed by the following initial value problem for the elastic wave equation:

\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{tt}(\mathbf{x},t)=\operatorname{div}\left(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\right),&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t\in(0,\infty),\\
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\mathbf{u}_{t}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

The primary goal of this paper is to investigate the following inverse problem, which concerns the recovery of the initial conditions from boundary observations.

Problem 1.1

(Recovery of initial data in the elastic wave equation)
.

Let

be a bounded open domain with smooth boundary

1.1

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t),\quad\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)=\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t),\qquad\text{for }(\mathbf{x},t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,T],

(1.2)

where

\partial\Omega

\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{u}

Reconstruct the unknown initial data:

\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},0),\quad\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{u}_{t}(\mathbf{x},0),\qquad\text{for all }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.

To guarantee the well-posedness of the forward problem and the uniqueness of the time-reduced model developed in this work, we impose the following assumptions on the elasticity tensor
\mathbb{C}=\{C_{ijkl}\}_{i,j,k,l=1}^{d}

Symmetry: Each component

Coercivity: For every symmetric matrix

\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{d}C_{ijkl}(\mathbf{x})\,\xi_{ij}\,\xi_{kl}\geq\Lambda|\bm{\xi}|^{2},

The inverse problem formulated in Problem

The ability to recover initial displacement and velocity fields from such data, particularly under minimal a priori assumptions, is thus of considerable practical interest.

Solving the inverse source problem, and in particular the inverse initial data problem, for the elasticity equation is challenging due to the coexistence of compressional and shear waves.
Several methods have been proposed in the literature to address inverse initial data problems for wave-type equations, including the elastic wave equation. A fundamental theoretical tool is the use of Carleman estimates, which have been successfully applied to establish uniqueness and stability results when partial initial data is known. These estimates provide strong conditional uniqueness guarantees and are thoroughly treated in the literature
[
23

42
]

[

Another class of approaches relies on optimization-based formulations, where the initial data are obtained by minimizing a misfit between observed and simulated boundary data, often under regularization. These methods are flexible and effective in practical scenarios, especially when adjoint-state techniques are used to compute gradients efficiently
[
16

In cases where the forward problem is linear and underdetermined, the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse framework provides a principled way to select a unique solution by enforcing a minimal-norm criterion. This idea underlies many regularization schemes and is particularly well-suited for inverse problems with non-unique solutions
[
21

Together, these approaches form a diverse toolkit, each with specific strengths: Carleman estimates provide theoretical uniqueness, time-reversal offers intuitive and direct numerical recovery, optimization and regularization frameworks accommodate noisy data, and pseudoinverse-based methods yield canonical solutions in ill-posed scenarios.

In this work, we introduce a novel orthonormal basis for the time variable, constructed by combining Legendre polynomials with an exponential weight. This Legendre polynomial–exponential basis enjoys key approximation properties such as orthogonality, smoothness, and non-degeneracy. These structural features enable the stable representation of time-dependent functions and spectrally facilitate differentiation of time components. Compared to previous bases (e.g., the polynomial–exponential basis in
[
25
]
), the Legendre–exponential basis provides enhanced numerical stability and approximation quality, especially near the initial time.

Building on this basis, we propose a time-dimensional reduction method to solve the inverse problem of reconstructing initial displacement and velocity fields in elastic wave equations. The idea is to express the space-time solution as a finite series in the time basis, reducing the inverse problem to a sequence of elliptic PDEs in the spatial domain. This approach significantly reduces computational complexity and transforms the ill-posed inverse problem into a well-structured optimization problem over Fourier coefficients.

To justify the method, we prove a convergence theorem which ensures that, under suitable choices of regularization parameter and truncation level, the reduced solution converges strongly to the true solution in appropriate Sobolev spaces. This theoretical result guarantees that the reconstruction is reliable, even in the presence of noise, and provides a rigorous foundation for the proposed numerical scheme.

2
The Legendre polynomial-exponential basis

In this section, we introduce the Legendre polynomial-exponential basis and highlight several of its key properties that are instrumental in establishing the convergence of the time-dimensional reduction method. This basis is formed by multiplying the classical Legendre polynomials by an exponential function. Compared to the polynomial-exponential basis introduced in
[
25
]
, the Legendre polynomial-exponential basis possesses structural advantages that make it more suitable for the convergence of the time reduction model.

Let
n\geq 0
be fixed. The Legendre polynomial of degree
n
on the interval
(-1,1)
is defined by Rodrigues’ formula

P_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\frac{{\rm d}^{n}}{{\rm d}x^{n}}\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{n}.

(2.1)

To construct a corresponding orthonormal family on the finite interval
(0,T)
, the change of variables
x=\frac{2t}{T}-1
is applied, mapping
(0,T)
onto
(-1,1)
. This yields the transformed functions

Q_{n}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{T}}P_{n}\left(\frac{2t}{T}-1\right).

(2.2)

The set
\{Q_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
forms an orthonormal basis for the Hilbert space
L^{2}(0,T)
.
Since the Legendre polynomials satisfy the self-adjoint equation

\left[(1-x^{2})P_{n}^{\prime}(x)\right]^{\prime}+n(n+1)P_{n}(x)=0,\quad x\in(-1,1),

(2.3)

by using the product rule with the change of variable
t\mapsto x=2t/T-1
, we can derive from (
2.3
) the analogous self-adjoint differential equation for
Q_{n}
, read as

\left[t(T-t)Q_{n}^{\prime}(t)\right]^{\prime}+n(n+1)Q_{n}(t)=0,\quad t\in(0,T).

(2.4)

Defining

\Psi_{n}(t)=e^{t}Q_{n}(t),\quad t\in(0,T),n\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.5)

The set of functions
\{\Psi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
form an orthonormal basis of the weighted Hilbert space

L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)=\left\{u:\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}|u(t)|^{2}\,{\rm d}t<\infty\right\},

equipped with the inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{L_{e^{-2t}}^{2}(0,T)}=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}u(t)v(t)\,{\rm d}t.

Definition 2.1

(Legendre-polynomial exponential basis)
.

The set
\{\Psi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, defined in (
2.5
), is referred to as the orthonormal Legendre-exponential basis of the space
L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)
.

The introduction of this basis represents one of the central conceptual contributions of the paper. It provides the foundational framework for constructing the time-dimensional reduction method and offers structural advantages essential for establishing convergence estimates and understanding the behavior of the time-dimensional reduction model, which approximates the original inverse problem. See Section
3
for details. We introduce several concepts associated with the Legendre-polynomial exponential basis, which play a central role in our time dimension reduction framework.

Definition 2.2
.

1.

For any
u\in L^{2}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega))
, the
n
-th Fourier mode of
u
with respect to the Legendre-polynomial exponential basis is defined as

u_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\left\langle u(\mathbf{x},\cdot),\Psi_{n}\right\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}u(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{n}(t)\,{\rm d}t

(2.6)

for
{\bf x}\in\Omega
.

2.

Given an integer
N\geq 0
and a vector
U=\begin{bmatrix}u_{0}&u_{1}&\dots&u_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{N+1}
, we define the space-time reconstruction (or expansion) of
U
as

\mathbb{S}^{N}[U](\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}u_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}(t),

(2.7)

for all
(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
.

3.

For
N\geq 0
, we define the finite-dimensional subspace

\mathbb{V}_{N}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\Psi_{0},\Psi_{1},\dots,\Psi_{N}\right\}\subset L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T).

(2.8)

Given a function
u\in L^{2}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega))
, its orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{V}_{N}
in the time variable is given by

P^{N}u(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left\langle u(\mathbf{x},\cdot),\Psi_{n}\right\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\Psi_{n}(t),

(2.9)

for all
(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega_{T}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For every
n\geq 0
, the function
\Psi_{n}(t)=e^{t}Q_{n}(t)
is infinitely differentiable on the interval
(0,T)
, and none of its derivatives of any order is identically zero on this interval.

Proof.

Since
\Psi_{n}(t)=e^{t}Q_{n}(t)
is the product of the analytic function
e^{t}
and the polynomial
Q_{n}(t)
of degree
n
, it follows that
\Psi_{n}
is infinitely differentiable on
(0,T)
.
To show that
\Psi_{n}^{(k)}
is not identically zero for any
k\geq 0
, we apply the Leibniz rule:

\Psi_{n}^{(k)}(t)=\frac{{\rm d}^{k}}{{\rm d}t^{k}}(e^{t}Q_{n}(t))=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}e^{t}Q_{n}^{(j)}(t)=e^{t}\left(Q_{n}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k}{j}Q_{n}^{(j)}(t)\right).

(2.10)

Here,
Q_{n}(t)
is a degree-
n
polynomial, while each derivative
Q_{n}^{(j)}(t)
for
j\geq 1
is a polynomial of degree strictly less than
n
. Thus, the expression inside the parentheses in (
2.10
) remains a degree-
n
polynomial.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
\Psi_{n}^{(k)}(t)\equiv 0
on
(0,T)
. Then the polynomial

Q_{n}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k}{j}Q_{n}^{(j)}(t)

must vanish identically. However, since it is a polynomial of degree
n
, this would imply it has more than
n
roots, which is only possible if it is the zero polynomial. This is a contradiction, and hence,
\Psi_{n}^{(k)}(t)
cannot vanish identically.
∎

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
, depending only on
k
and
T
, such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{2k}\left|\langle u,\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\right|^{2}\leq C\|u\|_{H^{k}(0,T)}^{2},

(2.11)

for all
u\in H^{k}(0,T)
where
C
is a constant depending only on
T.

Proof.

We define the differential operator
K:H^{2}(0,T)\to L^{2}(0,T)
by

Ku(t):=-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(t(T-t)\frac{{\rm d}u}{{\rm d}t}\right),\quad\text{for }u\in H^{2}(0,T),\ t\in(0,T).

From the self-adjoint differential equation (
2.4
), it follows that

KQ_{n}=n(n+1)Q_{n}.

Using the eigenvalue relation we have

K^{m}Q_{n}=n^{m}(n+1)^{m}Q_{n}.

Hence, we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle K^{m}(e^{-t}u),Q_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T)}

\displaystyle=\langle e^{-t}u,K^{m}Q_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T)}

\displaystyle=n^{m}(n+1)^{m}\langle e^{-t}u,Q_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T)}

\displaystyle=n^{m}(n+1)^{m}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}u(t)\Psi_{n}(t)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=n^{m}(n+1)^{m}\langle u,\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}=n^{m}(n+1)^{m}u_{n},

where
u_{n}:=\langle u,\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}
.

We consider two cases:

Case 1:
k=2m
for some integer
m\geq 1
.

Applying Parseval’s identity in
L^{2}(0,T)
for the orthonormal basis
\{Q_{n}\}
, we obtain:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{2m}(n+1)^{2m}|u_{n}|^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left|\langle K^{m}(e^{-t}u),Q_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T)}\right|^{2}=\|K^{m}(e^{-t}u)\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}.

Since multiplication by
e^{-t}
is a bounded operator on
H^{2m}(0,T)
, we deduce that

\|K^{m}(e^{-t}u)\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq C\|u\|_{H^{2m}(0,T)},

which implies (
2.11
) in this case.

Case 2:
k=2m+1
.
In this case, we use the spectral energy identity for
\{Q_{n}\}
, which states:

\int_{0}^{T}t(T-t)\left|\left(K^{m}(e^{-t}u)\right)^{\prime}\right|^{2}{\rm d}t=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n(n+1)\left|\langle K^{m}(e^{-t}u),Q_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T)}\right|^{2}.

Substituting the expression for the Fourier coefficients, we have:

\langle K^{m}(e^{-t}u),Q_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T)}=n^{m}(n+1)^{m}u_{n},

and therefore,

\int_{0}^{T}t(T-t)\left|\left(K^{m}(e^{-t}u)\right)^{\prime}\right|^{2}{\rm d}t=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2m+1}(n+1)^{2m+1}|u_{n}|^{2},

which establishes the desired estimate (
2.11
) for odd
k
as well.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
n\geq 1
,

\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\leq Cn^{3/2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\leq Cn^{7/2}

where
C
is a constant depending only on
T
.

Proof.

For convenience, throughout this proof, we use
C
to denote a generic positive constant that may vary from line to line but depends only on
T
.

By differentiating Rodrigues’ formula (
2.1
) and using direct algebra, we obtain

P_{n}^{\prime}(x)=(2n-1)P_{n-1}(x)+P_{n-2}^{\prime}(x),\quad\text{for all }n\geq 2.

(2.12)

By recursively applying the identity (
2.12
), we derive the expansion

\displaystyle P_{n}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=(2n-1)P_{n-1}(x)+P_{n-2}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=(2n-1)P_{n-1}(x)+(2n-5)P_{n-3}(x)+P_{n-4}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\left(2n-(4k+1)\right)P_{n-(2k+1)}(x)+P_{r}^{\prime}(x),

(2.13)

where

r=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }n\text{ is even},\\
1,&\text{if }n\text{ is odd},\end{cases}\quad\mbox{and }\quad P^{\prime}_{r}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }n\text{ is even},\\
1,&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

It follows from (
2.13
) that

\left\|P_{n}^{\prime}-P_{r}^{\prime}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(-1,1)}=\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\left(2n-(4k+1)\right)P_{n-(2k+1)}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(-1,1)}.

(2.14)

Since the Legendre polynomials
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
form an orthogonal basis in

, and satisfy the norm identity

\|P_{n}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}=\frac{2}{2n+1},

we can compute the squared norm of the truncated expansion:

\displaystyle\|P_{n}^{\prime}-P_{r}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\left(2n-(4k+1)\right)^{2}\cdot\|P_{n-(2k+1)}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\left(2n-(4k+1)\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{2}{2n-(4k+1)}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\left(2n-(4k+1)\right)\leq Cn^{2},

(2.15)

for some constant
C>0
independent of
n
.
Since
P^{\prime}_{r}
is either

or
1
, it follows from (
2.15
) that

\|P_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}\leq Cn^{2}.

Using the chain rule and the definition of

2.2
), we obtain the analog estimate

\|Q_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq Cn^{3/2}.

(2.16)

We next estimate
\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}
. From (
2.5
) and (
2.16
), we have

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}|\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}|e^{t}Q_{n}^{\prime}(t)+e^{t}Q_{n}(t)|^{2}{\rm d}t

\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\leq Cn^{3/2}.

To estimate
\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}

From the identity (

Using the triangle inequality and the known estimate
\|P_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\leq Ck
, we deduce

\|P_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor-1}(2n-(4k+1))\cdot\|P_{n-(2k+1)}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}\leq C\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor}n(n-2k)\leq Cn^{3}.

We now estimate
\|Q_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}
based on the definition (
2.2
).
Differentiating twice and applying the chain rule gives

Q_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{4\sqrt{2n+1}}{T^{5/2}}\cdot P_{n}^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{2t}{T}-1\right).

By the change of variable
x=\frac{2t}{T}-1
, it follows that

\|Q_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}=\frac{4(2n+1)}{T^{3}}\cdot\|P_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}\leq Cn^{7}.

Finally, to estimate
\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}
, we use the identity

\Psi_{n}(t)=e^{t}Q_{n}(t)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)=e^{t}(Q_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)+2Q_{n}^{\prime}(t)+Q_{n}(t)).

Thus,

\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)|^{2}{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}|Q_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)+2Q_{n}^{\prime}(t)+Q_{n}(t)|^{2}{\rm d}t.

Applying the inequality
(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3(a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2})
and using the estimates
\|Q_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}=1
and
\|Q_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq Cn^{3/2}
, we obtain

\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}^{2}\leq 3\left(\|Q_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}+4\|Q_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}+\|Q_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\right)\leq C(n^{7}+n^{3}+1)\leq Cn^{7}.

Hence,
\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\leq Cn^{\frac{7}{2}}
.

∎

The following property plays the key role in the study of the behavior of the time dimensional reduction model in Section
3

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
p\geq 0
and
u\in L^{2}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega))
.
If the series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle u(\cdot,\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\,\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)

(2.17)

converges in
L^{2}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega))
, then the generalized derivatives
u_{tt}
exists in

L^{2}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega))
and the series coincides with
u_{tt}
.

(b) If
u\in H^{k}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega))
with
k\geq 5
then the series (
2.17
) converges in
L^{2}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

(a) Define the limit function

w(\mathbf{x},t):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\langle u(\cdot,\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\,\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t).

We show that
w=u_{tt}
. Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
be an arbitrary test function. We compute:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}w(\mathbf{x},t)\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}\langle u({\bf x},\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)\right)\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\langle u({\bf x},\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\,\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)\,\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}{\rm d}t.

This interchange of the limit and the integral above is justified by the convergence in
L^{2}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega))
of the partial sums to
w
, while
\varphi
is compactly supported and bounded.
Next, we integrate by parts in time:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\langle u({\bf x},\cdot),\Psi_{n}

\displaystyle\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_{\Omega}\langle u(\mathbf{x},\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)\varphi(\mathbf{x},t){\rm d}t\right){\rm d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\langle u(\mathbf{x},\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\Psi_{n}(t)\varphi_{tt}(\mathbf{x},t){\rm d}t\right){\rm d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{T}}u(\mathbf{x},t)\varphi_{tt}(\mathbf{x},t)\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}{\rm d}t,

where we used completeness of the
\{\Psi_{n}\}
basis in
L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)
, convergence of the series, and the dominated convergence theorem again.
Hence,
w=u_{tt}
in the distributional sense, and since both sides lie in
L^{2}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega))
, the equality holds almost everywhere.

(b)
We establish the convergence of the series in
L^{2}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega))
. From Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
, we have the estimates

\left\|\langle u(\cdot,\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\right\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}\leq Cn^{-k},\qquad\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\leq Cn^{7/2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\|\langle u(\cdot,\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\|\langle u(\cdot,\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\right\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}\cdot\|\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{-k}\cdot n^{7/2}=C\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{7/2-k}.

The series on the right converges whenever
k\geq 5
, so the sum is convergent in
L^{2}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega))
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

The convergence result established in Theorem
2.1
crucially relies on the special structure and analytical properties of the Legendre polynomials
\{P_{n}\}
. In particular, each polynomial
P_{n}
is an eigenfunction of the Sturm–Liouville operator

u\mapsto-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\left[(1-x^{2})\frac{{\rm d}u}{{\rm d}x}\right],

which endows them with strong regularity, decay properties, and orthogonality in weighted Sobolev spaces. These features directly lead to the decay estimates for the Fourier coefficients and controlled growth of their second derivatives, which are key in proving the convergence of the second-order expansion in time.

Without leveraging the spectral theory of Legendre polynomials and the corresponding eigenvalue structure, establishing convergence of the time-dimensional reduction method becomes significantly more difficult. Indeed, in our earlier works, see e.g.,
[
22
,
30
,
34
,
40
]
, the lack of such a framework posed major analytical challenges in justifying the convergence of analogous series representations.

3
The uniqueness of minimum solution and the time-dimensional reduction method

To numerically solve Problem
1.1
, we aim to approximate the exact displacement field
\mathbf{u}
, which satisfies the following boundary value problem:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t^{2}}={\rm div}\left(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\right),&\text{in }\Omega_{T},\\
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{f}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t),&\text{on }\Gamma_{T}:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{g}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t),&\text{on }\Gamma_{T},\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
\mathbf{f}^{*}
and
\mathbf{g}^{*}
denote the exact boundary displacement and traction data. In practice, only noisy measurements
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{g}
are available for computation. We will discuss the noise analysis later in Section
4
. In this section, we study the minimum-norm solution and the time-dimensional reduction method to compute it.

It is well known that problem (
3.1
) admits at least one solution, as it corresponds to the restriction of the solution to the initial value problem (
1.1
) on the domain
\Omega
. However, since no initial conditions are specified, the solution is generally nonunique.
Among all possible solutions to (
3.1
), we choose to compute the one with the smallest norm in a suitable Hilbert space. In fact, we use the Hilbert space

L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d})
which is
L^{2}((0,T);H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d})
with the inner product

\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d})}=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\langle\mathbf{v}(t),\mathbf{w}(t)\rangle_{H^{2+p}(\Omega^{d})}{\rm d}t.

This minimum norm solution, motivated by the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse framework, is unique and provides a stable and well-defined approximation. The use of minimal norm solutions is widely accepted in the inverse problems community, particularly when dealing with ill-posed or underdetermined systems.

Theorem 3.1

(Existence and Uniqueness of a Minimal-Norm Solution)
.

Let
p\geq 0
and define the set of admissible solutions

S=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in L^{2}\left(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d}\right)\cap H^{2}\left(0,T;(H^{p}(\Omega))^{d}\right)\,\middle|\,\mathbf{u}\text{ satisfies }\eqref{2.1111}\right\}.

Assume that the system (
3.1
) admits at least one solution in
S
. Then, there exists a unique function
\mathbf{u}^{*}\in S
such that

\mathbf{u}^{*}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{\mathbf{u}\in S}\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}\left(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d}\right)}.

Proof.

Define

\bm{\alpha}=\inf\left\{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}:\mathbf{u}\in S\right\}.

Let
\{\mathbf{u}^{n}\}\subset S
be a minimizing sequence such that

\bm{\alpha}\leq\|\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}\leq\bm{\alpha}+\frac{1}{n}.

(3.2)

Since
\mathbf{u}^{n}
satisfies the wave equation
\mathbf{u}^{n}_{tt}=\operatorname{div}(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{u}^{n})
, and
\mathbb{C}
is smooth, the second time derivatives
\mathbf{u}^{n}_{tt}
are uniformly bounded in
L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{p}(\Omega))^{d})
. Therefore, there exists a subsequence (still denoted
\mathbf{u}^{n}
) and a function
\mathbf{u}\in S
such that

\mathbf{u}^{n}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{u}\text{ weakly in }L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d}),\quad\mathbf{u}^{n}_{tt}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{u}_{tt}\text{ weakly in }L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{p}(\Omega))^{d}).

By weak lower semicontinuity and (
3.2
), it follows that

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}=\bm{\alpha}.

To show uniqueness, suppose there exists another
\mathbf{v}\in S
such that

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}=\bm{\alpha}.

Then the average
\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})
also belongs to
S
, and satisfies

\left\|\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})\right\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}\geq\bm{\alpha}.

Applying the parallelogram identity gives

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}

\displaystyle=2\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}+2\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle-4\left\|\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}

\displaystyle=4\bm{\alpha}^{2}-4\left\|\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T;(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d})}\leq 0.

Hence,
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}
, and the minimal-norm solution is unique.
∎

We now develop a numerical strategy to approximate the minimal-norm solution of the boundary value problem (
3.1
). To this end, we employ a spectral representation in time using the new Legendre polynomial-exponential basis
\{\Psi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
of the weighted space
L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)
. For each spatial point
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
, the displacement field
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)
is approximated by its projection on to
\mathbb{V}_{N}
in the time variable:

\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}(t)\approx P^{N}{\bf u}({\bf x},t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}(t),\quad(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega_{T},

(3.3)

where the coefficients
\mathbf{u}_{n}
are given by the
n-th
Fourier mode of
{\bf u}
, see (
2.6
)

\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},\cdot),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{n}(t)\,{\rm d}t.

(3.4)

The truncation parameter
N
will be chosen based on the desired accuracy and computational constraints.
Due to Theorem
2.1
,

\partial_{tt}\mathbf{u}({\bf x},t)\approx\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t),\quad(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega_{T}.

(3.5)

Substituting the approximations in (
3.3
) and (
3.5
) into the elastic wave equation in (
3.1
), we obtain:

\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\rm div}\left(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}(t)\right),\quad(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega_{T}.

(3.6)

Multiplying both sides of (
3.6
) by
e^{-2t}\Psi_{m}(t)
and integrating over
(0,T)
yields the following elliptic system for each
m\in\{0,\dots,N\}
:

{\rm div}(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x}))-\sum_{n=0}^{N}s_{mn}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})=0,

(3.7)

where the matrix coefficients
s_{mn}
are defined as

s_{mn}=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)\Psi_{m}(t)\,{\rm d}t\quad 0\leq m,n\leq N.

The boundary conditions for the spatial coefficients
\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})
are derived from the boundary data in (
1.2
) and the Fourier coefficient formula (
3.4
), leading to:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{m}(t)e^{-2t}\,\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{m}(t)e^{-2t}\,\,{\rm d}t=:\mathbf{f}_{m}(\mathbf{x}),

(3.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{m}(t)e^{-2t}\,\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi_{m}(t)e^{-2t}\,\,{\rm d}t=:\mathbf{g}_{m}(\mathbf{x}),

(3.9)

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega
and
m=0,\dots,N
.

Combining equations (
3.7
), (
3.8
), and (
3.9
), we arrive at the time dimension reduction model for the problem we want to solve (
3.1
), namely

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\rm div}\left(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})\right)-\sum_{n=0}^{N}s_{mn}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})=0,&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}_{m}(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{g}_{m}(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(3.10)

for
m=0,1,\dots,N
.

Remark 3.1
.

The system (
3.10
) forms the analytical backbone of our time-reduction approach and serves as the foundation of the proposed computational framework. A central theoretical difficulty in deriving this system lies in justifying the approximation

\partial_{t}^{2}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\approx\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t).

Establishing the validity of this representation is one of the principal analytical contributions of this work. The key insight involves the use of the Legendre polynomial–exponential basis, whose spectral properties and regularity enable effective control over the behavior of the series.

This is formalized in Theorem
2.1
, which rigorously proves the convergence of the series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)

in a suitable Sobolev space, and identifies the limit as the second time derivative
\partial_{t}^{2}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)
. Without this spectral framework, such a convergence result would be difficult to obtain, as experienced in our earlier investigations. Theorem
2.1
therefore provides a crucial theoretical foundation for the entire time-dimensional reduction methodology.

Remark 3.2
.

Recall that Proposition
2.1
guarantees that for every
n\geq 0
, the basis function
\Psi_{n}(t)=e^{t}Q_{n}(t)
is infinitely differentiable on
(0,T)
, and none of its derivatives, including the second derivative
\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)
, are identically zero. This nonvanishing property is essential: if
\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)
were identically zero for some
n
, the corresponding term
\mathbf{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)
would vanish, and thus the influence of
\mathbf{u}_{n}
would be completely omitted in the approximation of
\partial_{tt}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)
in (
3.5
).

Such an omission would lead to an artificial loss of information and introduce unnecessary error into the numerical approximation. Therefore, the structural properties ensured by Proposition
2.1
are indispensable for the consistency and accuracy of the time reduction method.

This observation also highlights why more conventional bases, such as the classical Legendre polynomials or the trigonometric functions used in standard Fourier expansions, are not suitable in this context. These traditional bases may include functions whose second derivatives vanish identically. As a result, they cannot guarantee that each Fourier mode contributes meaningfully to approximations. The exponential-modulated Legendre basis
\{\Psi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
avoids this issue, making it particularly well-suited for time-differential problems such as the one considered here.

The core idea of the time dimension reduction method is to compute a finite collection of spatial modes

U^{N}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf u}_{0}&\mathbf{u}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{u}_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\left[H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d}\right]^{N+1},

which solves the reduced elliptic system (
3.10
). These spatial coefficients are then used to reconstruct a time-dependent approximation of the displacement field via the truncated expansion (
3.3
). The resulting projection provides an estimate of the full space-time solution, from which the initial displacement and velocity can be inferred.

In the next section, we present the quasi-reversibility method to compute the solution to (
3.10
).

4
The convergence of the quasi-reversibility method

Due to the approximation inherent in the representation (
3.3
) and the presence of noise in the boundary data
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{g}
, the reduced system (
2.16
) may not admit an exact solution. To explicitly indicate the presence of noise, we denote the measured (perturbed) data by
\mathbf{f}^{\delta}
and
\mathbf{g}^{\delta}
, corresponding to the ideal (noiseless) data
\mathbf{f}^{*}
and
\mathbf{g}^{*}
, respectively. The term
noise
refers to the deviation between these measured and true boundary values, and is quantified by the inequality

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(|\mathbf{f}^{\delta}-\mathbf{f}^{*}|^{2}+|\mathbf{g}^{\delta}-\mathbf{g}^{*}|^{2}\right)\,{\rm d}\sigma(\mathbf{x})\,{\rm d}t\leq\delta^{2},

where
\delta>0
denotes the prescribed noise level.
To address the potential nonexistence of a solution in the presence of such noise, we adopt a regularization strategy by formulating a Tikhonov-type variational problem. This regularized approach is commonly referred to as the
quasi-reversibility method
, originally introduced in
[
28
]
, and is particularly well-suited for stabilizing ill-posed inverse problems.
For
m\in\{1,\dots,N\},
define the noisy boundary data of (
3.10
) as

{\bf f}_{m}^{\delta}({\bf x})=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}{\bf f}^{\delta}({\bf x},t)\Psi_{m}(t){\rm d}t,\quad{\bf g}_{m}^{\delta}({\bf x})=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}{\bf g}^{\delta}({\bf x},t)\Psi_{m}(t){\rm d}t,

More precisely, let
p\geq 0
be the number in Theorem
3.1
, and define the functional

J_{N,\eta,\delta}:[H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d}]^{N+1}\to\mathbb{R}

by

J_{N,\eta,\delta}(W)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\Big\|{\rm div}\left(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{w}_{m}(\mathbf{x})\right)-\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{mn}\mathbf{w}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\Big\|^{2}_{[H^{p}(\Omega)^{d}]^{N+1}}\\
+\sum_{m=0}^{N}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(|\mathbf{w}_{m}-\mathbf{f}_{m}^{\delta}|^{2}+|\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{w}_{m}-\mathbf{g}_{m}^{\delta}|^{2}\right)\,{\rm d}\sigma(\mathbf{x})+\eta\sum_{m=0}^{N}\|\mathbf{w}_{m}\|^{2}_{H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d}},

(4.1)

where
W=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf w}_{0}&\mathbf{w}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{w}_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in[H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d}]^{N+1}
and
\eta
is the regularization parameter.

The functional
J_{N,\eta,\delta}
defined in (
4.1
) consists of three key components: the first enforces the elliptic system derived from the time-reduction model (
3.10
); the second ensures consistency with the (potentially noisy) boundary measurements; and the third serves as a regularization term to stabilize the minimization process and promote selection of a minimal-norm solution. The regularization is measured in the Sobolev norm corresponding to the solution space specified in Theorem
3.1
.

The following lemma reformulates the functional
J_{N,\eta,\delta}
in space-time form. This representation facilitates the analysis and plays a key role in establishing the convergence theorem.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
W=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{0}&\mathbf{w}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{w}_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\left[(H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d})\right]^{N+1}
, and let
\mathbf{w}=\mathbb{S}^{N}[W]
denote its space-time expansion of
W
, see Definition
2.2
and (
2.7
). Then the functional
J_{N,\eta,\delta}(W)
admits the following integral form:

J_{N,\eta,\delta}(W)=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\left\|{\rm div}(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},t))-\partial_{tt}\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},t)\right\|^{2}_{H^{p}(\Omega)^{d}}{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{\Gamma_{T}}e^{-2t}\left|\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},t)-P^{N}\mathbf{f}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\sigma(\mathbf{x}){\rm d}t+\int_{\Gamma_{T}}e^{-2t}\left|\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},t)-P^{N}\mathbf{g}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\sigma(\mathbf{x}){\rm d}t\\
+\eta\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d})},

(4.2)

where
P^{N}
, as defined in (
2.9
), denotes the orthogonal projection onto the finite-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{V}_{N}\subset L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)
, defined in (
2.8
).

Proof.

Since
\{\Psi_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
is an orthonormal basis of the finite dimensional space
\mathbb{V}^{N}
of
L^{2}(0,T)
, we have

\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\left\|{\rm div}(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},t))-\partial_{tt}\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},t)\right\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}{\rm d}t\\
=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{m=0}^{N}\big|\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq p}\langle D^{\alpha}[{\rm div}(\mathbb{C}:\nabla\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\cdot))-\partial_{tt}\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},\cdot)],\Psi_{m}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\big|^{2}{\rm d}{\bf x}.

(4.3)

The right-hand side of (
4.3
) is exactly the first term in the definition of
J_{N,\eta,\delta}(W)
in (
4.1
).
Similarly, we can use the same argument to match the other terms of (
4.2
) and their corresponding terms on the right-hand side of (

4.2
).
∎

Let
U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf u}_{0}^{N,\eta,\delta}&{\bf u}_{1}^{N,\eta,\delta}&\dots&{\bf u}_{N}^{N,\eta,\delta}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
denote the minimizer of the functional

. Once this minimizer is computed, its space-time reconstruction
\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}]
provides an approximate solution
{\bf u}^{*}
to the boundary value problem (
3.1
), and thus
\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}]({\bf x},0)
and
\partial_{t}\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}]({\bf x},0)
yields a numerical approximation to the solution of the inverse problem posed in Problem
1.1
.
This solving procedure naturally raises the following fundamental questions:

1.

Does the minimizer
U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}
exist and is it unique?

2.

Does the reconstructed approximation

\mathbf{u}^{N,\delta,\eta}_{\min}({\bf x},t):=\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\delta,\eta}_{\min}]({\bf x},t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathbf{u}_{n}^{N,\delta,\eta}({\bf x})\Psi_{n}(t),\quad({\bf x},t)\in\Omega_{T}

of (

) as

and
\eta,\delta\to 0

We address these questions in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
p\geq 0
. Assume that the system (
3.1
) has
the unique minimum solution
u^{*}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2+p}(\Omega)^{d})\cap H^{2}(0,T;H^{p}(\Omega)^{d})

as in Theorem

and that the series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x},.),\Psi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)

converges in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{p}(\Omega)^{d})
.
Then the following statements hold:

1.

(Existence and uniqueness of the minimizer)
Assume that the boundary data sequences
(\mathbf{f}_{m}^{\delta})_{m=0}^{N}
and
(\mathbf{g}_{m}^{\delta})_{m=0}^{N}
belong to
([L^{2}(\partial\Omega)]^{d})^{N+1}
. Then the functional
J_{N,\eta,\delta}
admits a unique minimizer
U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}\in[(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d}]^{N+1}
, satisfying

U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{W\in[(H^{2+p}(\Omega))^{d}]^{N+1}}J_{N,\eta,\delta}(W).

2.

(Approximation of the second derivative)
For any fixed noise level
\delta>0
, there exists a threshold
N(\delta)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
N\geq N(\delta)
, the projection
P^{N}\mathbf{u}^{*}
of the minimal norm solution
\mathbf{u}^{*}

\left\|P^{N}\mathbf{u}^{*}_{tt}-(P^{N}\mathbf{u}^{*})_{tt}\right\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega)^{d})}^{2}\leq\delta^{2}.

(4.4)

3.

(Convergence of the regularized solution)
Assume that the regularization parameter
\eta=\eta(\delta)
satisfies
\eta(\delta)\to 0
and
\delta^{2}=o(\eta(\delta))
as
\delta\to 0
. Define the admissible parameter set

\lim_{\Theta\ni(N,\eta,\delta)\to(\infty,0^{+},0^{+})}\Big[\big\|\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}]-{\bf u}^{*}\big\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega)^{d})}\\
+\big\|\partial_{t}\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}]-{\bf u}^{*}_{t}\big\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}((0,T);H^{p}(\Omega)^{d})}\Big]=0.

(4.5)

Before proving Theorem
4.1
, we prove the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
T>0
. There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|v^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\leq C\left(\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}+\|v^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\right)

for all
v\in H^{2}(0,T)
.

Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that the inequality does not hold. Then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a function
v_{n}\in H^{2}(0,T)
such that

\|v_{n}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\geq n\left(\|v_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}+\|v_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\right).

Define the normalized sequence
w_{n}:=v_{n}/\|v_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}
. Then
\|w_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}=1
and

n\left(\|w_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}+\|w_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\right)\leq 1.

This implies that
\|w_{n}\|_{H^{2}(0,T)}^{2}=\|w_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T)}+\|w_{n}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T)}+\|w_{n}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq 2e^{2T}
.

Hence,
\{w_{n}\}
is bounded in
H^{2}(0,T)
. By the compact embedding
H^{2}(0,T)\hookrightarrow H^{1}(0,T)
, there exists a subsequence
\{w_{n_{k}}\}
and a function
w\in H^{2}(0,T)
such that

\displaystyle w_{n_{k}}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup w\quad\text{weakly in }H^{2}(0,T),

\displaystyle w_{n_{k}}

\displaystyle\to w\quad\text{strongly in }H^{1}(0,T).

As a result,

\|w_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\to\|w\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)},\quad\|w_{n_{k}}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\to\|w^{\prime}\_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}.

However, from the normalization and the inequality above, we have
\|w_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}=1
and
\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}\leq 1/\sqrt{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Therefore,
\|w\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}=0
and
\|w^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e^{-2t}}(0,T)}=1
. This implies
w=0
and
w^{\prime}\neq 0
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

The first statement of the theorem is a direct consequence of the classical Lax-Milgram theorem, and we omit the standard details.

To prove the second part, we invoke Theorem

. It is straightforward to verify that the projection

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2t}\left\|P^{N}\mathbf{u}_{tt}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)-\partial_{tt}P^{N}\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)\right\|^{2}_{H^{p}(\Omega)^{d}}\,{\rm d}t=0.

(4.6)

which implies the estimate (

Remark 4.1
.

1.

Although our theoretical framework does not require uniqueness, it is highly plausible that problem (
3.1
) admits a unique solution due to the presence of Cauchy boundary data. In such cases, the time-dimensional reduction method not only produces a stable approximation but also guarantees convergence to the true physical solution. This reinforces the practical reliability of our approach in realistic settings where uniqueness is expected.

2.

Importantly, the convergence result in Theorem
4.1
remains valid even when the boundary data for the inverse problem are available only on a proper subset of the boundary. That is, if the measurements are restricted to

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t),\quad\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)=\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\quad\text{for }(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Gamma\times[0,T],

where
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
, the proposed method and the convergence analysis remain applicable. This flexibility significantly enhances the scope of the method in practical inverse problems, where full boundary data are often unavailable.

Remark 4.2
.

A direct consequence of (
4.5
) is the following:

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}](\cdot,0)

\displaystyle\to{\bf u}^{*}(\cdot,0)\quad\text{strongly in }H^{p}(\Omega)^{d},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}](\cdot,0)

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup{\bf u}^{*}_{t}(\cdot,0)\quad\text{weakly in }H^{p}(\Omega)^{d},

as
\Theta\ni(N,\eta,\delta)\to(\infty,0^{+},0^{+})
.
These convergence results guarantee that the initial displacement field
\mathbf{p}
and the initial velocity field
\mathbf{q}
can be approximated by
\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}](\cdot,0)
and
\partial_{t}\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{N,\eta,\delta}_{\min}](\cdot,0)
, respectively.

5
Numerical study

We numerically solve Problem
1.1
in two-dimensional case (
d=2
) and define the computational domain as
\Omega=(-1,1)^{2}
. To generate boundary data on
\partial\Omega
, we need to solve the elastic wave equation in (
1.1
). Since computations over the unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are not feasible, we approximate it by a sufficiently large bounded domain
G=(-3,3)^{2}
. The forward problem is numerically solved on this larger domain using an explicit finite difference scheme, and the resulting solution is then restricted to the original domain
\Omega
.
To carry out the discretization, we construct a uniform spatial grid
\mathcal{G}
consisting of
121\times 121
points on
G
. For the temporal discretization, we set the final time
T=1
and define a uniform partition
\mathcal{T}
consisting of 6400 time steps. The details of the numerical steps using an explicit finite difference scheme for solving the forward problem on the discrete domain
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{T}
, as well as the domain truncation and restriction procedures, are standard in the literature and are therefore omitted here for brevity.

Assuming the forward simulation has been carried out, let
\mathbf{u}^{*}
denote the exact (noiseless) solution. The corresponding boundary data are extracted as

\mathbf{f}^{*}=\mathbf{u}^{*}|_{\partial\Omega\times[0,T]},\quad\mathbf{g}^{*}=\partial_{\nu}\mathbf{u}^{*}|_{\partial\Omega\times[0,T]}.

To simulate measurement noise, the noisy data are then defined by

\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}^{*}(1+\delta\,\mathrm{rand}),\quad\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}^{*}(1+\delta\,\mathrm{rand}),

(5.1)

where
\delta=10\%
, and
\mathrm{rand}
is a function generating uniformly distributed random values in the interval
[-1,1]
. For simplicity in implementation, we set
p=0
in our computation. In this case,
H^{p}(\Omega)
is understood as
L^{2}(\Omega).

With the boundary data available, we proceed to solve the inverse problem. The key computational steps of our proposed method, based on the time dimension reduction framework, are outlined in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Solution procedure for Problem
1.1
via the time dimension reduction method

1:

Select a truncation parameter
N
and a regularization parameter
\eta
.

2:

Solve the minimization problem for the strictly convex functional
J_{N,\eta,\delta}
defined in (

). Let the minimizer be denoted by

3:

Reconstruct the displacement field
{\bf u}^{\rm comp}
using the truncated series expansion:

{\bf u}^{\rm comp}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbb{S}^{N}[U^{\rm comp}]=\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\bf u}_{n}^{\rm comp}(\mathbf{x})\Psi_{n}(t),

\mathbf{p}^{\rm comp}(\mathbf{x})={\bf u}^{\rm comp}(\mathbf{x},0),

for all

.

\mathbf{q}^{\rm comp}(\mathbf{x})=\partial_{t}{\bf u}^{\rm comp}(\mathbf{x},0),

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
.

5.1
Numerical implementation of Algorithm
1

In this subsection, we detail the numerical implementation of the proposed Algorithm

, including the selection of parameters, solution of the optimization problem, and practical considerations relevant to reproducing the results.

Step 1
. The parameters
N
and
\eta
are selected through a trial-and-error process, using Test 1 in Subsection

The implementation of the remaining steps is straightforward.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we have proposed a novel method for solving the inverse source problem for the elastic wave equation, focusing on the recovery of initial displacement and velocity fields from boundary measurements. A key contribution is the introduction of a new orthonormal basis in time, constructed from Legendre polynomials and exponential weights, which enables stable and accurate spectral representation of time-dependent functions. This basis facilitates a time-dimensional reduction strategy that transforms the original inverse problem into a sequence of spatial elliptic problems for the Fourier coefficients.

We have established a rigorous convergence theorem that ensures the reliability of the method under appropriate regularization and truncation conditions. The theoretical results confirm that the approximated space-time solution converges to the true minimal-norm solution as the noise level tends to zero. The convergence analysis is supported by a variational framework and compactness arguments that accommodate the ill-posedness of the inverse problem.

Numerical experiments in two spatial dimensions demonstrate the method’s effectiveness in recovering both the qualitative structure and quantitative amplitude of the initial data. The reconstructions remain stable under noisy measurements, and the results show good agreement with the true profiles, even in challenging scenarios involving multiple inclusions or complex geometries.
