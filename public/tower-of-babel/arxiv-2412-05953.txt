On the role of semismoothness in the implicit programming approach to selected nonsmooth optimization problems
Helmut Gfrerer, Michal Kočvara, Jiří V. Outrata

Abstract
The paper deals with the implicit programming approach to a class of Mathematical Programs with Equilibrium Constraints (MPECs) and bilevel programs in the case when the corresponding reduced problems are solved using a bundle method of nonsmooth optimization. The obtained results allow us to supply the bundle algorithm with suitable, easily computable “pseudosubgradients”, ensuring convergence to points satisfying a stationary condition. Both the theory and computational implementation heavily rely on the notion of SCD (subspace containing derivatives) mappings and the associated calculus. The approach is validated via a complex MPEC with equilibrium governed by a variational inequality of the 2nd kind, by a shape optimization problem with a nonsmooth objective and by an academic bilevel program with a nonsmooth upper-level objective.



keywords
Nonsmooth problems, Semismoothness, Implicit programming approach

Introduction

It is well-known that most rules of generalized differentiation attain the form of inclusions, unless rather restrictive qualification conditions have been applied.
This might cause difficulties in connection with computational techniques such as subgradient and bundle methods of nonsmooth optimization. Beginning with
[
26
,
27
]
, mathematicians attempted to ensure convergence of these methods even in cases where exact subgradients could not be provided at every iteration. This lead to the concept of pseudosubgradients, as introduced in
[
23
]
.
Subsequent research has recognized that this challenge can be addressed using certain semismoothness property, which does not necessarily rely on generalized gradients or generalized Jacobians; see, e.g.,
[
21
,
3
,
19
]
. A recent study,
[
14
]
, provides an in-depth analysis of this issue in the context of the “model” problem

\displaystyle\min\

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y)

subject to

\displaystyle 0\in F(x,y),

(1)

\displaystyle x\in U_{ad},

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz function,
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a set-valued mapping, and
U_{ad}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed set.

The main goal of this paper is to extend the results from
[
14
]
to the case when (
1
) corresponds either to a
mathematical program with equilibrium constraints
(MPEC) or to a problem of
bilevel programming
.

It is known that, under suitable assumptions on the problem data, one can convert problem (
1
) to the so-called reduced problem in variable
x
only and solve it using an appropriate method of nonsmooth optimization. This methodology is referred to as the
implicit programming approach
(ImP approach) or
reduced approach
, and it is also widely applied in connection with other minimization techniques.
It has been extensively studied in numerous works, including the monographs
[
22
,
29
,
5
]
. However, in many problems it is not always possible to provide the bundle method with correct Clarke subgradients of the reduced objective, as required. This limitation arises due to the above mentioned inclusions inherent in the applied calculus rules.

It was presumably
[
6
]
where the authors for the first time utilized the pseudosubgradient theory in a specific bilevel program of the form (
1
). In
[
5
]
one then finds a procedure for the computation of pseudosubgradients, provided the inclusion constraint in (
1
) amounts to KKT-conditions of a mathematical program in variable
y
, parameterized by
x
. In
[
14
]
the authors address this issue in a general framework, by analysis of the model program (
1
). In particular, it is shown there
that the Clarke subgradients of the reduced objective in the employed bundle method can be effectively replaced with computable pseudosubgradients, provided

1.

the mapping
S:x\mapsto y
, defined implicitly through the inclusion

0\in F(x,y)
, satisfies some natural assumptions, and

2.

the mappings
\varphi
and
S
are semismooth in the prescribed sense.

Under these conditions the described variant of the ImP approach works and generates solutions which are stationary in a reasonable sense.

This paper deals with a particular instance of
(
1
), when it models either an MPEC or a bilevel program. In the former case one has

F(x,y)=f(x,y)+Q(x,y),

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
Q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
whereas, in case of bilevel programming,

S(x)=\mbox{argmin}_{y}\{\psi(x,y)+q(x,y)\}

with
\psi
and
q
mapping
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
into
\mathbb{R}
and
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, respectively. In this way we address here a substantially broader class of lower-level equilibria than those investigated in
[
5
]
(and also in
[
22
,
29
]
).

In accordance with
[
14
]
, we rquire that multifunction
Q
possesses the so-called SCD (subspace containing derivative) property; cf.
[
12
]
. This assumption is not restrictive and allows us to weaken the assumptions needed for applying this approach, as well as to simplify the computation of the pseudosubgradients. That is why we (in Sections 3.1, 3.2) explain in detail how to compute appropriate SCD subspaces for several commonly encountered multifunctions
Q
.

As mentioned already in
[
14
]
, the solutions of MPECs, computed by the presented approach, satisfy a stationarity condition that is always weaker (less restrictive) than the Clarke stationarity. On the other hand, under some additional assumptions, they satisfy the Clarke stationarity condition of an equivalent MPEC, where an additional control arises as a canonical perturbation. This serves as a kind of “seal of quality” for this approach.

The usage of the semismoothness and the SCD properties allows us to substantially extend the class of lower-level equilibria and upper-level objectives, to which this type of ImP approach can be used. Further, we need not to evaluate elements from the limiting coderivative like in
[
2
]
. On the other hand, since we use a standard bundle method for solving the reduced problem, our approach is limited to problems where the number
n
of control variables is not too large.

As a byproduct, we may apply the concept of the so-called
\Psi
-semismoothness also to the numerical solution of a decomposable optimization problem in two variables. This problem has the structure of the model problem (
1
) and can be efficiently solved by the semismooth Newton method discussed in
[
21
,
33
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. The preliminary Section 2 includes basic notions of variational analysis and all elements from the theory of semismooth and SCD mappings, needed throughout the text. Section 3 focuses on MPECs. In addition to theoretical results, it contains two challenging test examples: a complex Stackelberg game of producers/firms in an oligopolistic market adapted from
[
15
]
and a shape optimization problem from continuum mechanics adapted from
[
2
]
. The approach to bilevel programs, including an illustrative test example, is discussed in Section 4. It can be viewed as an alternative to
[
5
, Section 6.2]
, where pseudostationary points are computed under different assumptions. In both test examples we use algorithms and software from the family BT (bundle trust), described in
[
32
,
34
,
31
]
.
Finally, Section 5 discusses the proposed approach for handling the considered decomposable programs.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Nonsmooth analysis

Consider a mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open set. In the sequel we denote by
{\cal O}_{F}
the set of points where
F
is (Fréchet) differentiable and we denote the Jacobian by
\nabla F(x)
,
x\in{\cal O}_{F}
. We use this notation also for real-valued functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
when
\nabla f(x)
is an
1\times n
matrix, i.e., a row vector.

The
B-Jacobian (B-differential)
is defined as

\overline{\nabla}F(\bar{x})=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{F}}\bar{x}}\{\nabla F(x)\},\ \bar{x}\in\Omega,

where "Limsup" stands for the outer/upper set limit in the sense of Painleve-Kuratowski. see, e.g.,
[
30
, Chapter 4]
.
Then the Clarke generalized Jacobian is given by
{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F(\bar{x})
.

If
F
is locally Lipschitz, by Rademacher’s Theorem the set
\Omega\setminus{\cal O}_{F}
is negligible, i.e., has Lebesgue measure
0
. Since the Jacobians
\nabla F(x)
,
x\in{\cal O}_{F}
, are also locally bounded by the Lipschitz constant, we obtain
\emptyset\not=\overline{\nabla}F(x)\subset{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F(x)
,
x\in\Omega
.

For a locally Lipschitz function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, we can define the
Clarke subdifferential
as

\partial_{c}f(x):={\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}f(x)^{T}:=\{g^{T}\,\big|\,g\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}f(x)\},\ x\in\Omega.

The elements of
\partial_{c}f(x)
are called
subgradients
and are column vectors.

Given any extended real-valued function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, we always assume that
f
is proper, i.e.,
f(x)>-\infty
for all
x
and
\mathrm{dom}\,f:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,f(x)<\infty\}\not=\emptyset
. Given a point
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
, the
regular (Fréchet) subdifferential
of
f
at
\bar{x}
is given by

\widehat{\partial}f(\bar{x}):=\Big\{x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,\liminf_{x\to\bar{x}}\frac{f(x)-f(\bar{x})-\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle}{\|x-\bar{x}\|}\geq 0\Big\},

while the
limiting (Mordukhovich) subdifferential
is defined by

\partial f(\bar{x}):=\{x^{*}\,\big|\,\exists(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,\widehat{\partial}f}(\bar{x},x^{*})\mbox{ with }f(x_{k})\to f(\bar{x})\}.

If
\Omega:=\mathrm{dom}\,f
is open, then clearly
{\cal O}_{f}\subset\mathrm{dom}\,\widehat{\partial}f
and
\overline{\nabla}f(\bar{x})^{T}\subset\partial f(\bar{x})
, provided that
f
is continuous at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
. If
f
is locally Lipschitz on
\Omega
then

\partial_{c}f(x)={\rm conv\,}\partial f(x),\ x\in\Omega;

cf.
[
30
, Theorem 9.61]
. Further, if
f
is a lower semicontinuous (lsc) convex function then
\partial f
coincides with the Moreau-Rockafellar subdifferential from convex analysis.

2.2
Variational geometry and differentiation of multifunctions

Consider a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
and a point
\bar{x}\in A
.The
tangent (contingent, Bouligand) cone
to
A
at
\bar{x}
is given by

T_{A}(\bar{x}):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{A-\bar{x}}{t},

whereas the set

\widehat{N}_{A}(\bar{x}):=(T_{A}(\bar{x}))^{\circ},

i.e., the (negative) polar cone to the tangent cone
T_{A}(\bar{x})
, is the
regular (Fréchet) normal cone
to
A
at
\bar{x}
, and

N_{A}(\bar{x}):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}}}\widehat{N}_{A}(x)

is the
limiting (Mordukhovich) normal cone
to
A
at
\bar{x}
.

Given a multifunction
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a point
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, the multifunction
D^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined by

\mathrm{gph}\,D^{\ast}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{(y^{*},x^{*})\,\big|\,(x^{*},-y^{*})\in N_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\},

is called the
limiting (Mordukhovich) coderivative
of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.
We will now recall some basic properties of so-called
SC (subspace containing) derivatives
, which were very recently introduced in
[
12
,
13
]
.
Let us denote by
{\cal Z}_{nm}
the metric space of all
n
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
equipped with the metric

d_{{\cal Z}_{nm}}(L_{1},L_{2}):=\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|,

where
P_{i}
is the symmetric
(n+m)\times(n+m)
matrix representing the orthogonal projection onto
L_{i},i=1,2
.

For notational reasons, we usually identify
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e., for a subspace
L\in{\cal Z}_{nm}
we denote its elements by
(u,v)
instead of
\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}
. Analogously, given two matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
so that
L={\rm rge\;}\!\!\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix}
, we write
L={\rm rge\;}(A,B)
and we call
(A,B)
a basis for
L
. Hence, given a matrix
C=(C_{x}\,\vdots\,C_{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times(n+m)}
, partitioned into submatrices
C_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times n}
and
C_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times m}
, the set

CL=C\,{\rm rge\;}(A,B)=C\left\{\begin{pmatrix}Ap\\
Bp\end{pmatrix}\,\Big|\,p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}=\{(C_{x}A+C_{y}B)p\,\big|\,p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{l}
. If
(A,B)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is an arbitrary basis for a subspace
L\in Z_{nm}
, the set of all bases for this subspace is given by

\{(AC,BC)\,\big|\,C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\mbox{ nonsingular}\}.

With each
L\in{\cal Z}_{nm}
one can associate its
adjoint
subspace
L^{*}
defined by

L^{*}:=\{(-v^{*},u^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,(u^{*},v^{*})\in L^{\perp}\}.

Since
\dim L^{\perp}=m
, it follows that
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{mn}
(i.e., its dimension is
m
).
Further we have
(L^{*})^{*}=L
and
d_{{\cal Z}_{mn}}(L_{1}^{*},L_{2}^{*})=d_{{\cal Z}_{nm}}(L_{1},L_{2})
for all subspaces
L_{1},L_{2}\in{\cal Z}_{nm}
and thus the mapping
L\to L^{*}
is an isometry between
{\cal Z}_{nm}
and
{\cal Z}_{mn}
; cf.
[
13
]
.
In what follows, the symbol
L^{*}
signifies both the adjoint subspace to some
L\in{\cal Z}_{nm}
as well as an arbitrary subspace from
{\cal Z}_{mn}
. This double role, however, cannot lead to a confusion.

Definition 2.1
.

Consider a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

1.

We say that
F
is
graphically smooth of dimension

n
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in{\cal Z}_{nm}
. By
\mathcal{O}_{F}

we denote the subset of
\mathrm{gph}\,F
, where
F
is graphically smooth of dimension
n
.

2.

The
SC (subspace containing) derivative
of
F
is the mapping
{\mathcal{S}}F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows{\cal Z}_{nm}
given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y):

\displaystyle=\{L\in{\cal Z}_{nm}\,\big|\,\exists(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\cal O}_{F}}(x,y)\mbox{ such that }\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{{\cal Z}_{nm}}\big(L,T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x_{k},y_{k})\big)=0\},

and the
adjoint SC derivative
of
F
is the mapping
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows{\cal Z}_{mn}
defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y):

\displaystyle=\{L^{*}\,\big|\,L\in{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y)\}.

Both
{\mathcal{S}}F
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
are in fact special generalized derivatives of
F
whose elements, by virtue of the above definitions, are subspaces. Further we have the relation

\displaystyle L^{*}\subset\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)\ \forall L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y);

(2)

cf.
[
13
, Eq (15)]
. Let us mention that in
[
13
]
the SC adjoint derivative
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
was defined in a different but equivalent manner.

Since
{\cal Z}_{nm}
is compact, cf.
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
,
\mathrm{dom}\,{\mathcal{S}}F=\mathrm{dom}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F={\rm cl\,}{\cal O}_{F}
. Further,
\mathrm{gph}\,{\mathcal{S}}F
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times{\cal Z}_{nm}
and, due to the isometry
L\leftrightarrow L^{*}
,
\mathrm{gph}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times{\cal Z}_{mn}
as well.
On the basis of SC derivatives we may now introduce the following notion.

Definition 2.2
.

A mapping

F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is said to have the
SCD property
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, provided
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\neq\emptyset
. Further
F
has the SCD property
around
a point
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if
F
has the SCD property at all points
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
close to
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. Finally,
F
is termed an
SCD mapping
if it has the SCD property at all points of
\mathrm{gph}\,F
.

Using the above definition, the SCD property at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is obviously equivalent with the condition
{\mathcal{S}}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\not=\emptyset
.

By virtue of (
2
), the adjoint SC derivative
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
forms some kind of skeleton for the limiting coderivative
D^{*}F
for SCD mappings. It contains the information which is important in applications, in particular for numerical computations, but it is much easier to compute than the limiting coderivative. Note that in these applications we only have to compute
one
subspace belonging to
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y)
and not the whole adjoint SC derivative. On the other hand, the limiting coderivative is a more general notion important for arbitrary set-valued mappings.

We now provide some important examples of SCD mappings; cf.
[
12
,
13
]
.

Example 2.3
.

1.

Every locally Lipschitz mapping
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, is an SCD mapping and for every
x\in\Omega
there holds

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}f(x):={\mathcal{S}}\big(x,f(x)\big)=\{{\rm rge\;}(I_{n},A)\,\big|\,A\in\overline{\nabla}f(x)\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{*}f(x):={\mathcal{S}}^{*}\big(x,f(x)\big)=\{{\rm rge\;}(I_{m},A^{T})\,\big|\,A\in\overline{\nabla}f(x)\}.

2.

Every maximally monotone mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an SCD mapping.

3.

In particular, the subgradient mapping
\partial q
of an lsc convex function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is an SCD mapping. Further,
{\mathcal{S}}(\partial q)(x,x^{*})={\mathcal{S}}^{*}(\partial q)(x,x^{*})
,
(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q
and every subspace
L\in{\mathcal{S}}(\partial q)(x,x^{*})
is self-adjoint, i.e.,
L=L^{*}
and admits the basis representation
L={\rm rge\;}(B,I_{n}-B)
for some symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\|B\|\leq 1
; cf.
[
12
]
. For instance, when
q
is twice continuously differentiable then
B=(I_{n}+\nabla^{2}q(x))^{-1}
.

4.

Also the subgradient mapping of prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous functions is an SCD mapping. Recall that an lsc function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is prox-regular at
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,q
for a subgradient
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
, if there exist
\epsilon>0
and
r\geq 0
such that

q(x^{\prime})\geq q(x)+\langle x^{*},x^{\prime}-x\rangle-\frac{r}{2}\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}

whenever
x^{\prime}\in{\cal B}_{\epsilon}(\bar{x})
and
(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q\cap({\cal B}_{\epsilon}(\bar{x})\times{\cal B}_{\epsilon}({\bar{x}^{\ast}}))
with
q(x)<q(\bar{x})+\epsilon
. Further,
q
is called subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}
if for every sequence
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q}(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
one has
q(x_{k})\to q(\bar{x})
.

If
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is both prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,q
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
, then
q
has the SCD property around
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
and for every
(x,x^{*})
sufficiently close to
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
there holds
{\mathcal{S}}(\partial q)(x,x^{*})={\mathcal{S}}^{*}(\partial q)(x,x^{*})
. Every subspace
L\in{\mathcal{S}}(\partial q)(x,x^{*})
is again self-adjoint and admits now a basis representation
L={\rm rge\;}(P,W)
, where
P
and
W
are symmetric
n\times n
matrices satisfying

P^{2}=P,\ \ W(I_{n}-P)=I_{n}-P\,;

cf.
[
9
]
. For instance, when
q
is twice continuously differentiable at
\bar{x}
then
P=I_{n}
,
W=\nabla^{2}q(\bar{x})
. In case when
q
is convex, the matrix
W
is positive semidefinite.

All the examples above are special cases of
graphically Lipschitz mappings
(
[
30
, Definition 9.66]
), which possess also the SCD property; cf.
[
13
]
.

The following definition tailors the so-called semismooth
∗
property introduced in

]

Definition 2.4

(cf.
[

)
.

We say that the mapping

is SCD-semismooth

and for every

such that

\displaystyle|\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle-\langle y^{*},y-\bar{y}\rangle|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\epsilon\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|\|(y^{*},x^{*})\|\ \forall(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\mbox{ and }\forall(y^{*},x^{*})\in L^{*},\ L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y).

. Dort wurde die semismooth

.

2.3
Closure of mappings

Recall that a mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called
locally bounded
at
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if there is a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
and a nonnegative real
M
such that

\|y\|\leq M\ \forall y\in\Psi(x)\ \forall x\in U.

(3)

Further,
\Psi
is called
upper semicontinuous (usc)
at
\bar{x}
if
\Psi(\bar{x})
is closed and for every open set
V\supset\Psi(\bar{x})
the set
\{x\,\big|\,\Psi(x)\subset V\}
is a neighborhood of
\bar{x}
.

We now discuss a possibility how to extend an arbitrary locally bounded mapping to a usc mapping.
Given
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we denote by
{\rm cl\,}\Psi
the mapping whose graph equals to the closure of
\mathrm{gph}\,\Psi
, i.e.,

\mathrm{gph}\,({\rm cl\,}\Psi)={\rm cl\,}(\mathrm{gph}\,\Psi)=\{(x,y)\,\big|\,(x,y)=\lim_{k\to\infty}(x_{k},y_{k})\mbox{ for some sequence }(x_{k},y_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\Psi\}.

Further, we denote by
\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi
the mapping defined by

(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi)(x):={\rm conv\,}\big(({\rm cl\,}\Psi)(x)\big),\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Obviously,
{\rm cl\,}({\rm cl\,}\Psi)={\rm cl\,}\Psi
and for every
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,({\rm cl\,}\Psi)
the set
({\rm cl\,}\Psi)(x)
is closed. Further,
\mathrm{dom}\,({\rm cl\,}\Psi)\subset{\rm cl\,}(\mathrm{dom}\,\Psi)
and it is easy to see that
\mathrm{dom}\,(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi)=\mathrm{dom}\,({\rm cl\,}\Psi)
.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
14
, Lemma 2.7]
)
.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping. If
\Psi
is locally bounded at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
{\rm cl\,}\Psi
and
\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi
are also locally bounded at
x
and
(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
is a convex and compact set. Further, both
{\rm cl\,}\Psi
and
\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi
are usc at
x
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, be locally Lipschitz and consider the mapping
\Psi_{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
given by

\Psi_{F}(x):=\begin{cases}\{\nabla F(x)\}&\mbox{if $x\in{\cal O}_{F}$}\\
\emptyset&\mbox{else.}\end{cases}

Then for every
x\in\Omega
there holds
\overline{\nabla}F(x)=({\rm cl\,}\Psi_{F})(x)
and
{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F(x)=(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi_{F})(x)
. Further
{\rm cl\,}\overline{\nabla}F={\rm cl\,}({\rm cl\,}\Psi_{F})={\rm cl\,}\Psi_{F}=\overline{\nabla}F
.

2.4
On
\Psi
-semismooth single-valued mappings

The following definition is closely related to the definitions of Newton maps
[
21
]
, G-semismoothness
[
16
]
, Newton (slant) differentiability
[
3
,
19
]
and semismooth operators in Banach spaces; cf.
[
33
, Definition 3.1]
.

Definition 2.8

(
[
14
, Definition 3.1]
)
.

Consider the mapping
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, and the multifunction
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.

1.

We say that
F
is
semismooth with respect to
\Psi
(shortly
\Psi
-ss) at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
if
\mathrm{dom}\,\Psi
is a neighborhood of
\bar{x}
,
\Psi
is locally bounded at
\bar{x}
and

\lim_{{\textstyle{x\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{\Omega}\bar{x}\atop x\not=\bar{x}}}}\frac{\sup\{\|F(x)-F(\bar{x})-A(x-\bar{x})\|\,\big|\,A\in\Psi(x)\}}{\|x-\bar{x}\|}=0.

2.

We say that
F
is
\Psi
-ss
on a subset

U\subset\Omega
if
F
is
\Psi
-ss at every
x\in U
.
The mapping
\Psi
is called a semismooth derivative of
F
on
U
.

Whereas in the context of solving nonsmooth equations
F(x)=0
by semismooth Newton methods the semismooth property of
F
at a solution
\bar{x}
is important, in this paper we are interested in the
\Psi
-semismoothness of
F
on an open set. We refer the interested reader to
[
14
]
for a discussion on this subject.

Clearly, continuously differentiable mappings
F
are
\nabla F
-ss. Moreover,
\Psi
-ss mappings obey the following chain rule.

Lemma 2.9

(
[
14
, Lemma 3.4]
)
.

Consider the mappings
F_{1}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
F_{2}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
are open sets with
F_{1}(U)\subset V
. Assume that
F_{1}
is
\Psi_{1}
-ss at
\bar{x}\in U
and that
F_{2}
is
\Psi_{2}
-ss at
F_{1}(\bar{x})
, where
\Psi_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
,
\Psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
. Then the composite mapping
F:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by
F(x):=F_{2}(F_{1}(x))
is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}
with
\Psi
given by

\Psi(x):=\{BA\,\big|\,A\in\Psi_{1}(x),\ B\in\Psi_{2}(F_{1}(x))\}.

One can also show that for given mappings
F_{i}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
being
\Psi_{i}
-ss,
i=1,2
, their sum
F_{1}+F_{2}
is
(\Psi_{1}+\Psi_{2})
-ss, cf.
[
14
, Lemma 3.5]
.

The next auxiliary statement is important in the application we have in mind.

Lemma 2.10

(
[
14
, Lemma 3.6]
)
.

Let
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, be continuous and assume that
F
is
\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
. Then
F
is also
\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi
-ss at
\bar{x}
.

Consider now the following definition.

Definition 2.11

(
[
26
,
27
]
)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
generalized differentiable
if there exists an usc mapping
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
with nonempty, convex and compact values
G(x)
such that
for every
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
there holds

\sup_{g\in G(x)}|f(x)-f(\bar{x})-\langle g,x-\bar{x}\rangle|=o(\|x-\bar{x}\|).

The elements of
G(x)
are called
pseudogradients
in
[
23
]
and we will call them
pseudosubgradients
.

By Lemma
2.10
, a
\Psi
-ss function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is also generalized differentiable with

G(x)=\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\big|\,g^{T}\in(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi)(x)\}.

Note that pseudosubgradients are column vectors whereas the elements of
\Psi
are
1\times n
matrices, i.e., row vectors.

Generalized differentiable functions have many interesting properties, cf.
[
26
,
27
,
23
]
, which can be carried over to
\Psi
-semismooth functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open. Some of these results can be generalized to vector-valued mappings.

Proposition 2.12

(
[
14
, Proposition 3.9]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and assume that
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
\Psi
-ss on
\Omega
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

F
is locally Lipschitz on
\Omega
.

(ii)

For every
x\in\Omega
there holds
\emptyset\not={\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F(x)\subset(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
.

(iii)

Let
\Omega_{\Psi}
denote the set of all
x\in\Omega
such that
(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
is a singleton. Then
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\Psi}
has Lebesgue measure zero. Moreover, the mapping
F
is strictly differentiable at every
x\in\Omega_{\Psi}
with
\{\nabla F(x)\}=(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi)(x)
and the mapping
x\mapsto\nabla F(x)
is continuous on
\Omega_{\Psi}
.

In particular, on
\Omega_{\Psi}
there holds
(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi)={\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F(=\{\nabla F\})
and therefore the set, where
\mathrm{cocl}\,\Psi
differs from
{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F
, has Lebesgue measure zero. Moreover, from Proposition
2.12
(ii) together with Lemma
2.10
it follows that there exists
\Psi
such that
F
is
\Psi
-ss on
\Omega
if and only if
F
is
\overline{\nabla}F
-ss (or
{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F
-ss).

Consider now the situation that the mapping
F=F_{l}\circ F_{l-1}\circ\ldots F_{1}
is the composition of various mappings
F_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
,
i=1,\ldots,l
, each being semismooth with respect to
\overline{\nabla}F_{i}
. By the chain rule (Lemma
2.9
) we know that
F
is semismooth
with respect to

\Psi(x)=\{A_{l}A_{l-1}\ldots A_{1}\mid A_{i}\in\overline{\nabla}F_{i}((F_{i-1}\circ\ldots\circ F_{1})(x)),\ i=1,\ldots,l\}.

For numerical purposes, in many applications it suffices to compute for given
x
the value
F(x)
and
one
element
A\in\Psi(x)
.
To this end it is only necessary that for every mapping
F_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,l
, we have at our disposal an oracle which returns for
x_{i-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}}
the value
F_{i}(x_{i-1})
and one element
A_{i}\in\overline{\nabla}F_{i}(x_{i-1})
. This feature is appropriate for the usage of automatic differentiation tools.
This procedure actually defines a single-valued mapping
\tilde{\Psi}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times n_{0}}
with
\tilde{\Psi}(x)\in\Psi(x)
. Since
F
is also
\tilde{\Psi}
-ss, by Proposition
2.12
(ii) we always have that
\emptyset\not={\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}F(x)\subset(\mathrm{cocl}\,\tilde{\Psi})(x)
, regardless which element
A_{i}\in\overline{\nabla}F_{i}(x_{i-1})
our oracle selects.

Similar considerations apply to the case when
F
is the sum of semismooth mappings.

2.5
On semismoothness of solution mappings to generalized equations

In this section we consider generalized equations (GEs) of the form

0\in F(x,y):=f(x,y)+Q(x,y),

(4)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is continuously differentiable and
Q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
has a closed graph.

For the analysis of mappings
x\mapsto y(x)
implicitly defined by (
4
), the so-called
regular subspaces
play an important role. Let us denote by
{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}^{\rm reg}
the collection of all subspaces
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}
satisfying

(z^{*},x^{*},0)\in L^{*}\Rightarrow z^{*}=0,\ x^{*}=0.

(5)

By
[
14
, Proposition 5.6]
, for every subspace
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}^{\rm reg}
there are are unique matrices
Z_{L^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
X_{L^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
such that

L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(Z_{L^{*}},X_{L^{*}},I_{m})
.
It was shown in
[
14
, Lemma 5.9]
that for given
((x,y),z)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
the condition
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F((x,y),z)\subset{\cal Z}_{m,n+m}^{\rm reg}
is equivalent with the boundedness of the modulus

{\rm scd\,reg\,}F((x,y),z):=\sup\{\|(z^{*},x^{*})\|\,\big|\,(z^{*},x^{*},y^{*})\in L^{*},L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F((x,y),z),\|y^{*}\|\leq 1\}<\infty.

Using this notation, one can show the following result:

Theorem 2.13

(cf.
[
14
, Corollary 5.12]
)
.

Consider a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a continuous mapping
\sigma:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open, satisfying
0\in F(x,\sigma(x))
,
x\in\Omega
. Further assume that for every
x\in\Omega
the mapping
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
((x,\sigma(x)),0)
and
{\rm scd\,reg\;}F((x,\sigma(x)),0)<\infty
. Then
\sigma
is semismooth on
\Omega
with respect to the mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
given by

\Psi(x):=\begin{cases}\{-X_{L^{*}}^{T}\,\big|\,L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F((x,\sigma(x)),0)\}&\mbox{if $x\in\Omega$},\\
\emptyset&\mbox{else.}\end{cases}

In particular, for every function
\varphi:D\to\mathbb{R}
defined on an open set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
containing
\mathrm{gph}\,\sigma
, which is semismooth on
\mathrm{gph}\,\sigma
with respect to some mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{1\times(n+m)}
, there holds that the function
\theta:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\theta(x):=\varphi(x,\sigma(x))
is semismooth on
\Omega
with respect to the mapping
\Theta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
given by

\Theta(x):=\begin{cases}\{g_{x}^{T}-g_{y}^{T}X_{L^{*}}^{T}\,\big|\,(g_{x}^{T},g_{y}^{T})\in\Phi(x,\sigma(x)),L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F((x,\sigma(x)),0)\}&\mbox{if $x\in\Omega$},\\
\emptyset&\mbox{else.}\end{cases}

In order to apply this theorem to (
4
), observe that
\mathrm{gph}\,F=\{\big((x,y),z\big)\,\big|\,\big((x,y),z-f(x,y)\big)\in\mathrm{gph}\,Q\}
and therefore

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F((x,y),f(x,y)+z)=\Big\{\left(\begin{matrix}I_{m}&0\\
\nabla f(x,y)^{T}&I_{n+m}\end{matrix}\right)L^{*}\,\big|\,L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}Q((x,y),z)\Big\},

by
[

By considering basis representations

for elements of

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F((x,y),f(x,y)+z)=\Big\{{\rm rge\;}(Z^{*},\nabla_{x}f(x,y)^{T}Z^{*}+X^{*},\nabla_{y}f(x,y)^{T}Z^{*}+Y^{*})\,\big|\,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{\rm rge\;}(Z^{*},X^{*},Y^{*})\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}Q((x,y),z),Z^{*},Y^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},\ X^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\Big\}.

We conclude from this formula that a subspace

3
On the ImP approach to mathematical programs with equilibrium constraints

This section addresses the specific case of model (
1
) in which the multifunction
F
attains the form (
4
). This results in a parameter-dependent GE that is very well suited for the description of the behavior of a large class of parameter-dependent equilibrium problems. Problem
(
1
) is now formulated as the following MPEC

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{ minimize }&\varphi(x,y)\\
\mbox{ subject to }&0\in f(x,y)+Q(x,y),\\
&x\in U_{ad},\end{array}

(8)

where
x,y
can be interpreted as the
control
and
state
variables, respectively, and
U_{ad}
is the set of
admissible
controls.

Throughout the whole section, we will impose the following standing assumptions:

(A1)

The
solution mapping

S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by

S(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}|\mbox{ GE \eqref{eq-2} is fulfilled }\}

(9)

is single-valued and continuous on an open set
\Omega\supset U_{ad}
.

(A2)

U_{ad}
is a convex polyhedron.

(A3)

Q
is SCD-semismooth
∗
(in the sense of Definition
2.4
) at every point
((x,S(x)),-f(x,S(x)))
,
x\in\Omega
.

Assumption (A1) is indispensable and is imposed essentially in all works using the ImP approach to the numerical solution of MPECs. Its fulfillment can be ensured in different ways. One possibility is to combine the Mordukhovich criterion ensuring the Aubin property of
S
with a condition guaranteeing the uniqueness of the solution to (
4
) for admissible controls
x\in U_{ad}
. Alternatively, if
Q
depends only on
y
, one could employ the notion of strong regularity; cf.
[
7
, Chapter 3B]
,
[
29
, Chapter 5]
.

Under (A1), MPEC (
8
) amounts to the single-level nonsmooth optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{ minimize }&\vartheta(x)\\
\mbox{ subject to }&x\in U_{ad},\end{array}

(10)

where the objective
\vartheta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined via the composition
\vartheta(x):=\varphi(x,S(x))
. Various available ImP techniques differ in the way how a solution to (
10
) is computed. Here we will use, similarly to
[
29
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
20
]
, the bundle trust-region (BT) algorithm of Schramm and Zowe
(
[
32
,
34
]
) which is able to deal only with constraints in form of linear equalities and inequalities. This is the reason for imposing (A2).

Assumption (A3), at the first glance, might look rather restrictive, but it turns out that it holds in case of most major classes of equilibria considered in the literature so far. In particular, it is fulfilled in all classes of MPECs considered below.

In its original form, the BT algorithm requires from the user (similarly to most existing bundle methods) to compose a so-called oracle in which, given an
x\in U_{ad}
, one computes the corresponding state variable
y=S(x)
, the function value
\vartheta(x)
and a subgradient
\xi\in\partial_{c}\vartheta(x)
. Then it can be shown that under the additional assumption that
\vartheta
is weakly semismooth and under boundedness of the sequence of iterates
{x}^{(k)}
there is an accumulation point
\hat{x}
of this sequence which is C-stationary, i.e.,
0\in\partial_{c}\vartheta(\hat{x})+N_{U_{ad}}(\hat{x})
.

As observed in
[
6
]
, it is not really necessary that the oracle returns a Clarke subgradient to ensure that the BT-algorithm works. Only a pseudosubgradient
\xi
in the sense of Definition
2.11
is required, i.e., a certain semismoothness condition has to be fulfilled. The price we have to pay is that no accumulation point needs to be C-stationary. However, it holds that at least one of them fulfills the weaker necessary optimality condition saying that
0
is the sum of a pseudosubgradient and a normal to
U_{ad}
.

We clarify this issue in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
14
, Theorem 3.10]
)
.

Consider problem (
10
) and assume that the objective
\vartheta
is semismooth with respect to a mapping
\Theta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
on an open set
\Omega\supset U_{ad}
.
Assume that the BT-algorithm is applied to this problem along with an oracle which returns for each
x\in U_{ad}
besides the function value
\vartheta(x)
an element
\xi
with
\xi^{T}\in\Theta(x)
. If, moreover, the BT-algorithm generates a bounded sequence of iterates
{x}^{(k)}
, then this sequence possesses an accumulation point
\hat{x}
satisfying the optimality condition

0\in(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Theta)(\hat{x})^{T}+N_{U_{ad}}(\hat{x}).

(11)

Recall that in the ImP approach the function
\vartheta
is given by
\vartheta(x)=\varphi(x,S(x))
. By Corollary
2.14
,
\vartheta
is semismooth with respect to the mapping
\Theta
given by (
7
) (with
\sigma
replaced by
S
) under the following additional assumptions:

(A4)

For every
x\in\Omega
and for every subspace
L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(Z^{*},X^{*},Y^{*})\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}Q((x,S(x)),-f(x,S(x)))
, where
Z^{*},Y^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
X^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, the
m\times m
matrix
\nabla_{y}f(x,S(x))^{T}Z^{*}+Y^{*}
is nonsingular.

(A5)

The function
\varphi
is semismooth on
\{(x,S(x))\,\big|\,x\in\Omega\}
with respect to some mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{1\times(n+m)}
.

Since for every subspace
L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}Q((x,S(x)),-f(x,S(x)))
we have
L^{*}\subset D^{*}Q((x,S(x)),-f(x,S(x)))
, and by taking into account (
6
), one may conclude from the Mordukhovich criterion
[
30
, Theorem 9.43]
that (A4) is fulfilled, whenever
F
is metrically regular around
(x,S(x),0)
,
x\in\Omega
, i.e., the implication

(0,0)\in D^{*}F(x,S(x),0)(z^{*})\ \Rightarrow\ z^{*}=0

holds. This, in turn, implies that the validity of (A4) can be ensured by assuming that the
enhanced
solution mapping
\widetilde{S}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
, given by

\widetilde{S}(u,x):=\{y\,\big|\,u\in f(x,y)+Q(x,y)\},

(12)

has the Aubin property around all pairs
((0,x),S(x))
,
x\in\Omega
. Note that metric regularity of the constraints is a standard assumption (qualification condition) in optimization theory.

Assumption (A5) is tailored to the situation that the mapping
\varphi
is the composition of various semismooth mappings as discussed at the end of Subsection
2.4
.
Note that in the formulas (
13
), (
15
) below for computing one element
\xi^{T}\in\Theta(x)
we only need one element
g^{T}\in\Phi(x,S(x))
.

We are now in the position to explicitly formulate the oracle needed for applying the BT-algorithm to problem (
10
).

Oracle:

1)

Given
\bar{x}\in U_{ad}
, compute
\bar{y}=S(\bar{x})
and select some arbitrary vectors

.

2)

Determine a subspace

(Z^{*},X^{*},Y^{*})
with matrices

Compute the unique solution
\bar{p}
of the linear system

(13)

and set

(14)

4)

Return the function value
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and the column vector

\xi=g_{x}+x^{*}.

(15)

Remark 2
.

Since
L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(Z^{*},X^{*},Y^{*})\subset\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}Q((\bar{x},\bar{y}),-f(\bar{x},\bar{y}))
, we have
(X^{*}\bar{p},Y^{*}\bar{p})\in D^{*}Q((\bar{x},\bar{y}),-f(\bar{x},\bar{y}))(Z^{*}\bar{p})
and thus the inclusion

\begin{pmatrix}x^{*}\\
-g_{y}\end{pmatrix}\in\nabla f(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{T}z^{*}+D^{*}Q((\bar{x},\bar{y}),-f(\bar{x},\bar{y}))(z^{*})

(16)

holds with

.

By Corollary
2.14
, under the posed assumptions we obtain that

\xi
, returned by the oracle, is a pseudosubgradient of
\vartheta
at
\bar{x}
. Thus, the convergence result of Theorem
3.1
holds and we want to illustrate the sharpness of condition (
11
). To this aim assume that a strengthened variant of (A1) is fulfilled, namely

(A1’)

The mapping
\widetilde{S}
given by (
12
)
is single-valued and locally Lipschitz on
{\cal B}_{\delta}(0)\times\Omega
for some
\delta>0
.

In particular, (A1’) guarantees that (A4) is fulfilled.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under Assumption
(A1’)
there holds for every
(u,x)\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(0)\times\Omega
that

{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(f+Q)((x,\tilde{S}(u,x)),u)=\{{\rm rge\;}(B_{u}^{T},-B_{x}^{T},I_{m})\,\big|\,B=(B_{u}\,\vdots\,B_{x})\in\overline{\nabla}\tilde{S}(u,x),B_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},B_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\}.

(17)

Proof.

Consider the linear mapping
\Xi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by
\Xi(x,y,u)=(u,x,y)
. Then

\mathrm{gph}\,(f+Q)\cap(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times{\cal B}_{\delta}(0))=\{(x,y,u)\in(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times{\cal B}_{\delta}(0))\,\big|\,\Xi(x,y,u)\in\mathrm{gph}\,\tilde{S}\}.

Since
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}\tilde{S}((u,x),\tilde{S}(u,x))=\{{\rm rge\;}(I_{m},B^{T})\,\big|\,B\in\overline{\nabla}\tilde{S}(u,x)\}
,
(u,x)\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(0)\times\Omega
, equation (
17
) follows from
[
13
, Theorem 4.1]
.
∎

Further, define the function
\tilde{\vartheta}:{\cal B}_{\delta}(0)\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\tilde{\vartheta}(u,x)=\varphi(x,\widetilde{S}(u,x))

and consider the
enhanced
problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{ minimize }&\tilde{\vartheta}(u,x)\\
\mbox{ subject to }&(u,x)\in\{0\}\times U_{ad}.\end{array}

(18)

We observe that under (A1’) problem (
18
) is well-defined and
\hat{x}
is a solution of (
10
) if and only if
(0,\hat{x})
is a solution of (
18
).

Proposition 3.3
.

Apart from
(A1’)
assume that
\varphi
is continuously differentiable on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then (
11
) amounts to the Clarke stationarity (C-stationarity) condition for problem (
18
) at
(0,\hat{x})
.

Proof.

By comparing (
6
) and (
17
) we can deduce that for every
x\in\Omega
and every subspace
{\rm rge\;}(Z^{*},X^{*},Y^{*})\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}Q((x,S(x)),-f(x,S(x)))
,
Z^{*},Y^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
X^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, there is a matrix
B=(B_{u}\,\vdots\,B_{x})\in\overline{\nabla}\tilde{S}(0,x)
such that

B_{u}^{T}=Z^{*}(\nabla_{y}f(x,y)^{T}Z^{*}+Y^{*})^{-1},\quad-B_{x}^{T}=(\nabla_{x}f(x,y)^{T}Z^{*}+X^{*})(\nabla_{y}f(x,y)^{T}Z^{*}+Y^{*})^{-1},

and vice versa. Hence the mapping
\Theta
from Corollary
2.14
has the representation

\Theta(x)=\{\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,S(x))+\nabla_{y}\varphi(x,S(x))B_{x}\,\big|\,B=(B_{u}\,\vdots\,B_{x})\in\overline{\nabla}\tilde{S}(0,x)\},

implying
\Theta(x)=({\rm cl\,}\Theta)(x)
and

(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Theta)(x)=\{\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,S(x))+\nabla_{y}\varphi(x,S(x))B_{x}\,\big|\,B=(B_{u}\,\vdots\,B_{x})\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}\tilde{S}(0,x)\}.

By the chain rule
[
4
, Theorem 2.6.6]
we have

\partial_{c}\tilde{\vartheta}(0,x)^{T}=\{(\nabla_{y}\varphi(x,S(x))B_{u},\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,S(x))+\nabla_{y}\varphi(x,S(x))B_{x})\,\big|\,B=(B_{u}\,\vdots\,B_{x})\in{\rm conv\,}\overline{\nabla}\tilde{S}(0,x)\}.

Thus, when the optimality condition (
11
) holds, we have

\begin{pmatrix}u^{*}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\in\partial_{c}\tilde{\vartheta}(0,\hat{x})+\{0\}\times N_{U_{ad}}(\hat{x})

for some
u^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Since
N_{\{0\}\times U_{ad}}(0,\hat{x})=\mathbb{R}^{m}\times N_{U_{ad}}(\hat{x})
, we can conclude that the C-stationarity condition

0\in\partial_{c}\tilde{\vartheta}(0,\hat{x})+N_{\{0\}\times U_{ad}}(0,\hat{x})

for the enhanced problem is fulfilled.
∎

Although the multifunction
(\mathrm{cocl}\,\Theta)^{T}
differs from
\partial_{c}\vartheta
on
\Omega
only on a set of measure
0
, we are aware that the optimality condition (
11
) can be definitely less sharp than standard C-stationarity conditions for (
10
). This can be explained by the theory of explicit and implicit variables (
[
1
]
) and it is documented by the following example.

Example 3.4
.

Consider an MPEC (
8
) with
x\in\mathbb{R},y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},U_{ad}=[-1,1]
,

\varphi(x,y)=-0.5y_{1}+y_{2},

and an equilibrium governed by the linear complementarity problem

0\in f(x,y)+Q(y):=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}y_{1}+y_{2}-x\\
y_{2}+x\end{array}\right)+N_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(y).

It is easy to see that

S(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(x,0)^{T}&\mbox{ for }x\geq 0,\\
(0,-x)^{T}&\mbox{ otherwise, }\end{array}\right.\qquad\vartheta(x)=\begin{cases}-0.5x&\mbox{if $x\geq 0$,}\\
-x&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(19)

and so assumption (A1) is fulfilled. Clearly, (A2) is fulfilled as well and (A3) holds true by
[
13
, Proposition 5.3]
.

We observe that

N_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(S(x))\ni-f(x,S(x))=\begin{cases}(0,-x)^{T}&\mbox{if $x\geq 0$,}\\
(2x,0)^{T}&\mbox{else}\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{*}N_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(S(x),-f(x,S(x)))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\{(\mathbb{R}\times\{0\})\times(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})\}&\mbox{if $x>0$,}\\
\{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{(0,0)\},(\mathbb{R}\times\{0\})\times(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}),(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})\times(\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}),\{(0,0)\}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\}&\mbox{if $x=0$,}\\
\{(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R})\times(\mathbb{R}\times\{0\})\}&\mbox{if $x<0$\,;}\end{cases}

cf. Section
3.1
below. Some calculations yield that the mappings
\Psi
and
\Theta
from Corollary
2.14
are given by

\displaystyle\Psi(x)=\begin{cases}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right\}&\mbox{if $x>0$,}\\[10.76385pt]
\left\{\begin{pmatrix}2\\
-1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right\}&\mbox{if $x=0$,}\\[10.76385pt]
\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-1\end{pmatrix}\right\}&\mbox{if $x<0$,}\end{cases}\quad\Theta(x)=\begin{cases}\{-0.5\}&\mbox{if $x>0$,}\\
\{-2,\ -0.5,\ -1,\ 0\}&\mbox{if $x=0$,}\\
\{-1\}&\mbox{if $x<0$.}\end{cases}

We see that for all
x\not=0
there holds
\Psi(x)=\overline{\nabla}S(x)
,
\Theta(x)=\partial_{c}\vartheta(x)
, but
\Psi(0)\not=\overline{\nabla}S(0)
and
\Theta(0)\not\subset\partial_{c}\vartheta(0)=[-1,-0.5]
.

Consequently, if the BT-algorithm encounters one iterate
{x}^{(k)}=0
, our oracle could return the pseudosubgradient
\xi=0
and the BT-algorithm would stop. Nevertheless, note that if we start with a randomly chosen starting point
{x}^{(0)}
, then all iterates

will be different from

with probability 1 and actually, the BT-algorithm will terminate after a few iterations with the exact solution
\hat{x}=1
.

The only complicated step in the above approach is the specification of a suitable linear subspace
L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}Q(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and its basis representation as range of suitable matrices
Z^{*},X^{*},Y^{*}
(or
Z^{*},Y^{*}
, if
Q
does not depend on
x
).

In the next sections we will explain how these subspaces and their basis representations can be computed in some frequently arising cases.

3.1
Normal cone mapping to convex polyhedral sets

Consider first the (easy) case that
Q(y)=\widehat{N}_{C}(y)
, where

C:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\,\big|\,\langle a_{i},y\rangle\leq b_{i},i=1,\ldots,l\}

is a convex polyhedral set having the representation above with vectors
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and reals
b_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,l
. Given
(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,N_{C}
it is well-known (
[
13
, Proposition 5.3]
) that

{\mathcal{S}}N_{C}(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})={\mathcal{S}}^{*}N_{C}(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})=\{(F-F)\times(F-F)^{\perp}\,\big|\,F\mbox{ is face of }{\cal K}_{C}(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})\},

where
{\cal K}_{C}(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})=\{v\in T_{C}(\bar{y})\,\big|\,\langle\bar{y}^{*},v\rangle=0\}
denotes the critical cone to
C
at
\bar{y}
with respect to
\bar{y}^{*}
.
The computation of
all
the subspaces in
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}N_{C}(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})
might be puzzling, but for our oracle we only need
one
of them. This subspace can be chosen as the one which is induced by the face
E:={\rm lin\,}T_{C}(\bar{y})
, the
lineality space
of the tangent cone
T_{C}(\bar{y})
given by
{\rm lin\,}T_{C}(\bar{y})=\{v\,\big|\,\langle a_{i},v\rangle=0,\ i\in I(\bar{y})\}
, where

I(\bar{y}):=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}\,\big|\,\langle a_{i},\bar{y}\rangle=b_{i}\}.

Note that this lineality space
E
is for every normal
y^{*}\in N_{C}(\bar{y})
a face of the critical cone
{\cal K}_{C}(\bar{y},y^{*})
.
Since
E
is a subspace, we have
E=E-E
and the subspace
L^{*}
, required for our oracle, can be chosen as
L^{*}=E\times E^{\perp}
. There remains to compute a basis representation of
L^{*}
.
To this aim we may employ the (full) QR factorization of the matrix
D
, where
D
is composed from the normalized vectors
a_{i}/\|a_{i}\|
,
i\in I(\bar{y})
, as columns. Let

DP=Q\begin{pmatrix}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

where
P
is an
|I(\bar{y})|\times|I(\bar{y})|
permutation matrix,
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is orthogonal,
R_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
is a nonsingular upper triangular matrix,
R_{12}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times(|I(\bar{y})|-s)}
and
s
is the rank of
D
. If we partition
Q=(Q_{1}\,\vdots\,Q_{2})
with
Q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s}
,
Q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(m-s)}
, there holds
E={\rm rge\;}Q_{2}
,
E^{\perp}={\rm rge\;}Q_{1}
and consequently
L^{*}=E\times E^{\perp}={\rm rge\;}(Z^{*},Y^{*})
with
Z^{*}=(Q_{2}\,\vdots\,0)
,
Y^{*}=(0\,\vdots\,Q_{1}))
. Here we use the convention that the range of an
m\times 0
matrix is
\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

3.2
Normal cone mapping to inequality constraints

Consider now the case when
Q(y)=\widehat{N}_{\Gamma}(y)
with

\Gamma=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}|g_{i}(y)\leq 0,~i=1,2,\ldots,l\},

(20)

where the functions
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are convex and twice continuously differentiable. This situation has been thoroughly investigated in
[
29
]
(even for
x
-dependent inequalities and equalities) under the
linear independence constraint qualification
(LICQ). Here we present a modification of this approach which is simpler and enables us to replace LICQ by the following (weaker) assumption.

(CQ):

For all
x\in U_{ad}
and
y=S(x)
the inequality system in (
20
) fulfills the
Mangasarian-Fromovitz
CQ (denoted by MFCQ) and the
constant rank
CQ (denoted by CRCQ).

(CQ) will allow us to invoke a result from
[
18
]
or
[
11
]
, essential in the proof of Proposition
3.5
below.

So, throughout the rest of this section we assume that (CQ) is fulfilled and consider a triple
(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*},\bar{\lambda})
, where
\bar{y}=S(\bar{x})
for some admissible control
\bar{x},\bar{y}^{*}\in Q(\bar{y})
and
\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
is an arbitrary
Lagrange multiplier
satisfying the conditions

\bar{y}^{*}=\sum\limits^{l}_{i=1}\bar{\lambda}_{i}\nabla g_{i}(\bar{y})^{T},~~\bar{\lambda}\geq 0,~~\langle\bar{\lambda},g(\bar{y})\rangle=0

(21)

with
g(\bar{y})=(g_{1}(\bar{y}),\ldots,g_{l}(\bar{y}))^{T}
.

Further, for the sake of notational simplicity, we introduce the notation

A:=\sum\limits^{l}_{i=1}\bar{\lambda}_{i}\nabla^{2}g_{i}(\bar{y})=A^{T}~\mbox{ and }~{\cal K}_{\Gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*}):=\{h\in T_{\Gamma}(\bar{y})\,\big|\,\langle\bar{y}^{*},h\rangle=0\},

i.e., it is the critical cone to
\Gamma
at
\bar{y}
with respect to
\bar{y}^{*}
. Let

I(y):=\{i\in\{1,2,...,l\}|g_{i}(y)=0\}

denote the index set of active inequalities (at
y
).
Recall that the critical cone
{\cal K}_{\Gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})
has the representation

{\cal K}_{\Gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})=\left\{z\,\bigg|\,\begin{matrix}\nabla g_{i}(\bar{y})z=0,\ i\in I(\bar{y}):\ \bar{\lambda}_{i}>0\\
\nabla g_{i}(\bar{y})z\leq 0,\ i\in I(\bar{y}):\ \bar{\lambda}_{i}=0\end{matrix}\right\}.

Proposition 3.5
.

In the setting described above let

E:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}|\nabla g_{i}(\bar{y})z=0~\mbox{ for }~i\in I(\bar{y})\},

and consider the subspace
M\in{\cal Z}_{mm}
given by

M=E\times E^{\perp}.

Then for

L:=\left\{(h,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\left|\left(\begin{array}[]{l}h\\
k-Ah\end{array}\right)\in M\right.\right\}

one has

L=L^{*}\in\mathcal{S}Q(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})=\mathcal{S}^{*}Q(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*}).

(22)

Proof.

Consider a multiplier
\bar{\lambda}
satisfying (
21
) and let
J:=\{i\in I(\bar{y})\,\big|\,\bar{\lambda}_{i}=0\}
denote the index set of active constraints with vanishing multipliers.
Next let us construct a perturbed multiplier
\tilde{\lambda}
such that, with some
\varepsilon>0
,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\tilde{\lambda}_{i}=\bar{\lambda}_{i}&\mbox{ for }&i\in I(\bar{y})\setminus J\\
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}=\varepsilon&\mbox{ for }&i\in J.\end{array}

It follows that with

y^{*}_{\varepsilon}:=\sum\limits_{i\in I(\bar{y})}\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\nabla g_{i}(\bar{y})

one has

{\cal K}_{\Gamma}(\bar{y},y^{*}_{\varepsilon})=E.

We may now invoke
[
18
, Theorem 3.1]
or
[
11
, Theorem 1]
, according to which

T_{\mathrm{gph}\,Q}(\bar{y},y^{*}_{\varepsilon})=\{(h,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}|k\in A_{\varepsilon}h+N_{E}(h)\}=\{(h,k)|h\in E,k-A_{\varepsilon}h\in E^{\perp}\},

where
A_{\varepsilon}=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in I(\bar{y})}\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\nabla^{2}q_{i}(\bar{y})
. Since
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,Q}(\bar{y},y^{*}_{\varepsilon})
admits the representation

T_{\mathrm{gph}\,Q}(\bar{y},y^{*}_{\varepsilon})=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}I&0\\
-A_{\varepsilon}&I\end{array}\right)^{-1}M=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}I&0\\
A_{\varepsilon}&I\end{array}\right)M

(23)

and
M\in{\cal Z}_{mm}
, it follows that
T_{\mathrm{gph}\,Q}(\bar{y},y^{*}_{\varepsilon})\in{\cal Z}_{mm}
as well.

To prove (
22
) it suffices now to invoke
[
12
, Lemma 3.1(iii)]
. Since
A_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow A
for
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, it follows from
[
12
, Lemma 3.1]
that

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}I&0\\
A_{\varepsilon}&I\end{array}\right)M=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
A&I\end{pmatrix}M=L\in\mathcal{S}Q(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*}).

(24)

Finally, since
Q
is the subdifferential mapping of the convex lsc function
\delta_{\Gamma}
, the relations
L=L^{*}
and
{\mathcal{S}}Q(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})={\mathcal{S}}^{*}Q(\bar{y},\bar{y}^{*})
follow and we are done.
∎

It remains to find a suitable basis representation of
L^{*}
. Denoting by
D
the
m\times|I(\bar{y})|
matrix having the normalized vectors
\nabla g_{i}(\bar{y})^{T}/\|\nabla g_{i}(\bar{y})\|
,
i\in I(\bar{y})
, as columns, we can proceed as in Section
3.1
to find an orthogonal
m\times m
matrix
Q=(Q_{1}\,\vdots\,Q_{2})
with
Q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s}
,
Q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(m-s)}
, where
s
denotes the rank of
D
, so that
M={\rm rge\;}((Q_{2}\,\vdots\,0),(0\,\vdots\,Q_{1}))
and by using (
24
) we obtain that

L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(Z^{*},Y^{*})\mbox{ with }Z^{*}=(Q_{2}\,\vdots\,0),\ Y^{*}=(AQ_{2}\,\vdots\,Q_{1}).

Thus the linear system (
13
) and formula (
14
) amount to

(\nabla_{y}f(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{T}+A)Q_{2}p_{1}+Q_{1}p_{2}=-g_{y}\quad\mbox{with}\quad p_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m-s},\ p_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}

(25)

and

\begin{array}[]{ll}&x^{*}=\nabla_{x}f(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{T}Q_{2}\bar{p}_{1}.\end{array}

(26)

By premultiplying the linear system (
25
) by
Q_{2}^{T}
, the computation of
\bar{p}_{1}
simplifies to the solution of the reduced system

Q_{2}^{T}(\nabla_{y}f(\bar{x},\bar{y})^{T}+A)Q_{2}p_{1}=-Q_{2}^{T}g_{y}

in
m-s
variables. This system is smaller than the one used in the approach from
[
29
]
where a system of
m+s
equations has to be solved. On the other hand, the approach of
[
29
]
enables us to handle the situation, when
\varphi
depends also on the multiplier
\lambda
.

3.3
Stackelberg games

Consider the case when the equilibrium is governed by a variational inequality of the second kind that can be written down in the form
(
4
) with

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ y=(y^{1},y^{2},\ldots,y^{l})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{l}\quad\mbox{and}\quad Q(x,y)=Q(y)=\partial q(y)=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\partial q^{i}(y^{i}).

(27)

In (
27
) functions
q^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
are proper convex and lower semicontinuous.

In this way one can model, e.g., a parameter-dependent Nash game, where
x
is the parameter,
y^{i}
is the strategy of the
i
-th player,
i=1,2,\ldots,l
, and
q^{i}
is the nonsmooth part of his objective which does not depend on the strategies of the remaining players. Put
m:=nl
. As already pointed out in Example
2.3
,
Q=\partial q
is an SCD mapping. Further, due to the imposed separability of
q
and
[

, the matrices

have the block diagonal structure

Z^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{1}^{*}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&Z_{2}^{*}&&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&Z_{l}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\quad Y^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}Y_{1}^{*}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&Y_{2}^{*}&&0\\

0&0&\cdots&Y_{l}^{*}\end{pmatrix},

(28)

where
{\rm rge\;}(Z_{i}^{*},Y_{i}^{*})\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}\partial q^{i}(\bar{y}^{i},{\bar{y}^{*}}{}^{i})
,
i=1,2,\ldots,l
, with
\bar{y}^{*}=-f(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

As a numerical test example of an MPEC with such an equilibrium we will consider an oligopolistic market, where each player (firm) produces at most
n
commodities and tries to maximize his profit. When each player knows all data of his rivals, the equilibrium state of such a market is the Cournot–Nash non-cooperative equilibrium (
[
29
, Chapter 12]
). Consider now the situation, when one of the players decides to replace his original non-cooperative strategy by the Stackelberg one and becomes thus the Leader. The remaining players share the rest of the market by computing a new Cournot–Nash equilibrium among themselves. In this case the Leader has to solve MPEC (
8
), where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is his own production,
U_{ad}(=:\Omega^{0})
is his set of admissible productions and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is his loss function. In the respective GE (
4
) the
i
-th component of the single-valued part
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
amounts to

f^{i}(x,y)=\nabla_{y^{i}}\psi^{i}(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ i=1,2,\ldots,l,

where
\psi^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is the loss function of the
i
-th firm (depending naturally on all strategies of his rivals including the Leader). Finally, the multi-valued part is given by (
27
) and

q^{i}(y^{i})=\tilde{q}^{i}(y^{i})+\delta_{\Omega^{i}}(y^{i}),\ i=1,\ldots,l,

(29)

where
\Omega^{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the closed convex set of admissible strategies of the
i
-th player.
The convex nonsmooth functions
\tilde{q}^{i};\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
model the so-called costs of change, representing the expenses associated with any change of the production profile of the
i
-th firm. Naturally, these expenses may be nil. In particular, we choose
\tilde{q}^{i}(y^{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}^{i}|y^{i}_{j}-a^{i}_{j}|
, where
a^{i}
denotes the ”previous” production portfolio of the
i
-th player and
\beta^{i}_{j}
are positive weights. The sets
\Omega^{i}
are convex polyhedral sets of the form
\{y^{i}\,\big|\,\xi^{i}_{j}y^{i}\leq\zeta^{i}_{j},j=1,\ldots,p^{i}\}
and the loss functions are

\varphi(x,y)=c^{0}(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\pi_{j}(t_{j}(x,y))+\tilde{q}^{0}(x),\ \psi^{i}(x,y):=c^{i}(y^{i})-\sum_{j=1}^{n}y^{i}_{j}\pi_{j}(t_{j}(x,y)),

where
t(x,y):=x+\sum_{i=1}^{l}y^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the sum of all available commodities on the market and
\pi_{j}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes the so-called

for the
j
-th commodity.
The concrete form of the continuously differentiable functions
c^{i}
and
\pi_{j}
has been taken over from
[
15
]
. It is important to note that the assumptions, imposed in
[

]
on the above listed problem data, ensure in particular the fulfillment of principal assumptions (A1)-(A5) and thus the possibility to solve this MPEC via the suggested approach. Concretely,
by (

) we compute subspaces

,
i=1,\ldots,l

we have

L^{i}=E^{i}\times{E^{i}}^{\perp}

and
J^{i}_{0}(\bar{y}^{i})=\{j\,\big|\,\bar{y}^{i}_{j}=a^{i}_{j}\},\ I^{i}_{0}(\bar{y}^{i})=\{j\,\big|\,\langle\xi^{i}_{j},\bar{y}^{i}\rangle=\zeta^{i}_{j}\}

3.1

∗

3.4
Shape optimization in three dimensional contact problems with Coulomb friction

The aim of this example is to demonstrate the ability of the proposed method to solve MPECs with a nonsmooth objective and a large number of state variables. We have chosen a shape optimization problem from
[
2
]
, where one considers a 3D elastic body being in possible contact with a rigid obstacle and subject to surface tractions on a part of its boundary. Our aim is to optimize the shape of another part of the boundary in such a way that, after loading, the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of the stress along the respective contact area will be minimized. Whereas the infinite-dimensional model can be found, e.g., in
[
2
]
, we work with the discrete (algebraic) model taken over from
[
10
]
. It attains the form of the GE

0\in A(x)y-l(x)+Q(x,y),

(30)

where
x
specifies the shape of the optimized boundary and
y
comprises the displacements of all nodes of the considered body.
A
is the stiffness matrix (dependent on
x
),
l
models the given surface tractions and the multifunction
Q
ensures

(i)

the satisfaction of the unilateral (Signorini) contact condition and

(ii)

the Coulomb friction law at nodes lying in the contact region.

More precisely, we consider elastic bodies of the form

\Omega(x)=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,\big|\,(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in[0,2]\times[0,1],\alpha(x;\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\leq\xi_{3}\leq 1\},

where
\alpha(x;\cdot,\cdot)
describes the bottom surface of the body in dependence of our design variable
x
. In our test example, we take a uniform grid of control points
(\frac{k-1}{4},\frac{j-1}{4}),k=1,\dots,9,\ j=1,\ldots 5\}
on
[0,2]\times[0,1]
and define

\alpha(x;\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):=\sum_{k=1}^{9}\sum_{j=1}^{5}x_{kj}s_{k}(\xi_{1})t_{j}(\xi_{2}),\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{9\times 5},\ (\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
s_{k}
,
t_{j}
are cubic splines specified by the conditions

\displaystyle s_{k}(\frac{i-1}{4})=\delta_{ki}:=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if $i\neq k$},\\
1&\mbox{else,}\end{cases}\quad s_{k}^{\prime\prime}(0)=s_{k}^{\prime\prime}(2)=0,\ k,i=1,\ldots,9,

\displaystyle t_{j}(\frac{i-1}{4})=\delta_{ji},\ t_{j}^{\prime\prime}(0)=t_{j}^{\prime\prime}(1)=0,\ j,i=1,\ldots 5.

The rigid obstacle is given by the half-space
\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,\big|\,\xi_{3}\leq 0\}
and the contact region of the body
\Omega(x)
is its bottom surface
\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\alpha(x;\xi_{1},\xi_{2}))\,\big|\,(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in[0,2]\times[0,1]\}
.
The reference body
\Omega(0)
, a prism of size
2\times 1\times 1
, is uniformly carved into
n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}
bricks yielding
(n_{1}+1)(n_{2}+1)(n_{3}+1)=:N
nodes. The finite element discretization is performed by using trilinear elements and the partition of each
\Omega(x)
was constructed from the
partition of the reference body
\Omega(0)
by a suitable coordinate transformation in the
\xi_{3}

direction ensuring that the mapping
(x,y)\mapsto A(x)y-l(x)
is continuously differentiable. At the left boundary, i.e. for
\xi_{1}=0
, we assume Dirichlet boundary conditions (no displacements) and therefeore the number of state variables, i.e., the number of unknown displacements, is
m=3M
with
M=n_{1}(n_{2}+1)(n_{3}+1)
. Further we have
p=n_{1}(n_{2}+1)
nodes in the contact region without the Dirichlet part. We assume that the nodes of our discretization are numbered in such a way that the first
p
nodes
\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{p}
are exactly the ones lying on the contact boundary and we use the following ordering for an arbitrary vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

y=(y^{1},\ldots,y^{p},y^{R})\mbox{ with }y^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},i=1,\ldots,p,\ y^{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{3(M-p)}.

For every vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we denote by
z_{12}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}z_{1}\\
z_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
the vector containing the first two elements. Then

\displaystyle Q(x,y)=\prod_{i=1}^{p}Q^{i}(x,y^{i})\times\{0_{3(M-p)}\}\mbox{ with }Q^{i}(x,y^{i}):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}-{\cal F}\mu\partial\|y^{i}_{12}\|\\
\mu\end{pmatrix}\,\Big|\,\mu\in N_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\big(y^{i}_{3}+\alpha(x;\xi^{i}_{1},\xi^{i}_{2})\big)\right\}.

Here
{\cal F}>0
denotes the coefficient of Coulomb friction. For each

let
\alpha^{i}
denote the
9\times 5

\alpha^{i}_{k,j}=s_{k}(\xi^{i}_{1})t_{j}(\xi^{i}_{2})

and

for the moment as vectors from
\mathbb{R}^{45}

Further we consider the mapping

4
Bilevel programming

In this section we are dealing with an optimistic bilevel program of the form

\displaystyle\min\;

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y)

(34)

subject to

\displaystyle y\in\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{y}\{\psi(x,y)+q(x,y)\},

\displaystyle x\in U_{ad},

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz,
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable and
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is an lsc function. Consider now the following assumption.

(B1)

There is an open set
\Omega\supset U_{ad}
such that for every
x\in\Omega
the set of optimal solutions

\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{y}\{\psi(x,y)+q(x,y)\}=:\{\sigma(x)\}

is a singleton. Moreover, for every
\bar{x}\in\Omega
the function
q(\cdot,\sigma(\bar{x}))
is continuous at
\bar{x}
and there is a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
such that the set of global solutions
\{\sigma(x)\,\big|\,x\in U\}
is bounded.

Lemma 4.1
.

Under Assumption
(B1)
the mapping
\sigma
is continuous on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Consider
\bar{x}\in\Omega
. By the posed assumption, we can find a neighborhood
U
and a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\{\sigma(x)\,\big|\,x\in U\}\subset{\rm int\,}K
. Then we may add to
q(x,y)
the indicator function
\delta_{K}(y)
without changing the optimal solution sets for
x\in U
and
for the level set of the lower-level problem one has

\{y\,\big|\,\psi(x,y)+q(x,y)+\delta_{K}(y)\leq\alpha\}\subset K,\ x\in U,\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

Now continuity of
\sigma
at
\bar{x}
follows from
[
30
, Theorem 1.17]
.
∎

Obviously,
\sigma(x)
satisfies the first-order optimality conditions

0\in\nabla_{y}\psi(x,\sigma(x))^{T}+\partial_{y}\,q(x,\sigma(x))

and is therefore a solution of the GE (
4
) with
f:=\nabla_{y}\psi^{T}
and
Q:=\partial_{y}\,q
. However, the solution set
S(x)
consists of all stationary solutions of the lower-level program and might not be a singleton. Nevertheless, if all the assumptions (A2)–(A5) are fulfilled with
S(x)
replaced by
\sigma(x)
, Theorem
3.1
remains valid by virtue of Corollary
2.14
.

Example 4.2
.

Consider the bilevel program (
34
) with
n=1
,
m=2
and

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y)=-x+y_{1},\ U_{ad}=[-1,1],

\displaystyle\psi(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}y_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}y_{2}^{2}-xy_{1},\quad q(x,y)=\delta_{C}(y)\mbox{ with }C:=\{(y_{1},y_{2})\,\big|\,-\frac{1}{4}y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}y_{1}\}.

Then

\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{y}\{\psi(x,y)+q(x,y)\}=\{\sigma(x)\}=\begin{cases}\{(\frac{4}{3}x,\frac{2}{3}x)^{T}\}&\mbox{if $x\geq 0$,}\\
\{(0,0)^{T}\}&\mbox{if $x<0$}\end{cases}

and it follows that Assumption (B1) is fulfilled. For the reduced objective function of the leader we obtain

\vartheta(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{3}x&\mbox{if $x\geq 0$}\\
-x&\mbox{if $x<0$}\end{cases}

and hence
(\bar{x},\bar{y})=(0,(0,0))
is a solution of the problem.

However, the mapping
S(x)=\{y\,\big|\,0\in\nabla_{y}\psi(x,y)^{T}+\partial_{y}\,q(x,y)\}
of stationary solutions of the lower-level problem can be computed as

S(x)=\begin{cases}\{\sigma(x),(\frac{16}{15}x,-\frac{4}{15}x)^{T},(x,0)^{T}\}&\mbox{if $x\geq 0$,}\\
\{\sigma(x)\}&\mbox{if $x<0$,}\end{cases}

showing that
S
is not single-valued. Further, there does not even exist a single-valued localization of
S
around
(0,(0,0))
. Nevertheless, Assumptions (A2)-(A5) are fulfilled with
S
replaced by
\sigma
,
f(x,y)=\nabla_{y}\psi(x,y)^{T}
and
Q(x,y)=\partial_{y}q(x,y)=N_{C}(y)
. Clearly, (A2) and (A5) are fulfilled. Since
\delta_{C}
is convex lsc and
\mathrm{gph}\,Q=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{gph}\,N_{C}
is the union of finitely many convex sets, (A3) holds by virtue of Example
2.3
and Proposition
2.5
. Since
C
is a convex polyhedral set, the adjoint SC derivative of
Q=N_{C}
can be computed via

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{*}N_{C}(\sigma(x),-\nabla_{y}\psi(x,\sigma(x))^{T})=\begin{cases}\{L_{1}^{*}\}&\mbox{if $x>0$,}\\
\{L_{2}^{*}\}&\mbox{if $x<0$,}\\
\{L_{1}^{*},L_{2}^{*},L_{3}^{*},L_{4}^{*}\}&\mbox{if $x=0$}\end{cases}

with
L_{2}^{*}={\rm rge\;}(0,I_{2})
,
L_{4}^{*}={\rm rge\;}(I_{2},0)
and

L_{1}^{*}={\rm rge\;}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1/2&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\right),\ L_{3}^{*}={\rm rge\;}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-1/4&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&1/4\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\right);

cf. Section
3.1
.
The matrices
\nabla_{y}f(x,\sigma(x))^{T}Z^{*}+Y^{*}
which have to be checked in (A4) are therefore given by

\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2\\
-1/2&-1\end{pmatrix},\ I_{2},\ \begin{pmatrix}1&1/4\\
1/4&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

and are obviously nonsingular. Thus (A4) also holds and it follows that we can solve this bilevel optimization problem with the BT-algorithm using the ImP approach, provided we can actually compute a global solution of the lower-level problem, which is of course a very stringent assumption.

5
Conclusion

The main contributions of the paper can be summarized as follows.

(i)

We have substantially extended the class of MPECs, in which pseudosubgradients of the reduced objective can be computed. This concerns both the lower-level problem and the upper-level objectives. The respective requirements are expressed in terms of problem data. Further, we have clarified the nature of stationarity conditions that are fulfilled by the accumulation points of sequences generated by the applied bundle algorithms.

(ii)

Apart from MPECs, satisfying the standing assumptions, we have employed the achieved theoretical results also in a class of bilevel programming problems. This leads to reduced problems solvable by a bundle method as well.

(iii)

Finally, the consequent usage of the recently developed theory of SCD mappings has enabled us to weaken the imposed assumptions and to simplify the solution of adjoint equations for the computation of pseudosubgradients.
