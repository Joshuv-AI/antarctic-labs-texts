Tangential approach in the Dirichlet problem for elliptic equations Thanks: The first author was supported by EPSRC Grant EP/W032880/1. Thanks: No data were created or analysed in this study.
Jonathan Bennett, Arnaud Dumont, Andrew J. Morris

Abstract
. It is well-known that solvability of the L p \mathrm{L}^{p} -Dirichlet problem for elliptic equations L u := − div ( A ∇ u ) = 0 Lu:=-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)=0 with real-valued, bounded and measurable coefficients A A on Lipschitz domains Ω ⊂ ℝ 1 + n \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n} is characterised by a quantitative absolute continuity of the associated L L -harmonic measure. We prove that this local A ∞ A_{\infty} property is sufficient to guarantee that the nontangential convergence afforded to L p \mathrm{L}^{p} boundary data actually improves to a certain tangential convergence when the data has additional (Sobolev) regularity. Moreover, we obtain sharp estimates on the Hausdorff dimension of the set on which such convergence can fail. This extends results obtained by Dorronsoro, Nagel, Rudin, Shapiro and Stein for classical harmonic functions in the upper half-space.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background: Laplace’s equation in the upper half-space

A classical theorem of Fatou states that for any
p\in[1,\infty]
and
f
belonging to the Lebesgue space
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the harmonic extension

u_{f}(t,x):=(P_{t}\ast f)(x)

of
f
to the upper half-space
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:t>0\right\}
admits nontangential limits almost everywhere on its boundary
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This means that

\lim_{\Gamma_{a}(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}u_{f}(t,x)=f(x_{0})

for almost every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
where

\Gamma_{a}(x_{0}):=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}:\left|x-x_{0}\right|<at\right\}.

In this context the set
\Gamma_{a}(x_{0})
is referred to as a
nontangential approach region
, being a cone with vertex
x_{0}
and aperture parameter
a>0
. We recall that
P_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the classical Poisson kernel, and that
u_{f}(t,x)
, being a harmonic function on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
, solves Laplace’s equation

\Delta u:=\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}=0

on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
. We note that the original version of this convergence result is stated in the context of harmonic extensions to the unit disc rather than the half space; see
[
19
]
.

It is well known that taking a
tangential
approach to the boundary can lead to the failure of such convergence. A curve
\gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
for which
(0,0)\in\overline{\gamma}
(the closure of
\gamma
) is said to be
tangential
if for all
a>0
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

(1.1)

\left\{(t,x)\in\gamma:0<t\leq\varepsilon\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}\setminus\Gamma_{a}(0).

For any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
let
\gamma+x_{0}=\left\{(t,x+x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}:(t,x)\in\gamma\right\}
be the corresponding translate of
\gamma
.
The following result shows that Fatou’s theorem is in some sense best possible.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
be a tangential curve such that
(0,0)\in\overline{\gamma}
. There exists
f\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with Poisson extension
u_{f}(t,x)=(P_{t}\ast f)(x)
such that the limit

\lim_{\gamma+x_{0}\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}u_{f}(t,x)

does not exist for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

This theorem follows from results due to Aikawa
[
2
,
3
]
. Much earlier, Littlewood
[
32
]
obtained the weaker conclusion of almost everywhere non-existence of the limit (in the context of bounded holomorphic functions on the unit disk). See
[
33
]
and
[
11
, Theorem 2.22]
for similar results that predate the aforementioned work of Aikawa. Nagel and Stein
[
39
]
, however, constructed approach regions which are not contained in any nontangential region
\Gamma_{a}(0)
(which is weaker than the tangentiality condition (
1.1
)) for which the conclusion of Fatou’s theorem still holds.

Theorem
1.1
essentially states that no degree of integrability of
f
can help with the convergence properties of
u
at the boundary along tangential approach paths. It is therefore natural to ask if any additional
regularity
assumptions on the function
f
can allow for the convergence of the Poisson extension
u_{f}(t,x)=(P_{t}\ast f)(x)
through tangential approach regions. For any
\beta\in(0,1]
,
a>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we follow
[
39
, Section 4]
and consider the
tangential
approach region

(1.2)

\Gamma^{\beta}_{a}(x_{0})=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}:\left|x-x_{0}\right|<at^{\beta}\textup{ if }0<t\leq 1,\textup{ and }\left|x-x_{0}\right|<at\textup{ if }t\geq 1\right\},

and for any measurable
u:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
the corresponding maximal function is given by

(1.3)

N_{*,\beta}^{a}(u)(x_{0})=\sup_{(t,x)\in\Gamma^{\beta}_{a}(x_{0})}\left|u(t,x)\right|,\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Naturally, only the behaviour of
u_{f}
close to the boundary
\partial\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
is of relevance to boundary convergence questions, and we point out that
\Gamma^{\beta}_{a}(x_{0})
coincides with the standard (conical) approach region
\Gamma_{a}(x_{0})
in the range where
t\geq 1
.
As may be expected, the aperture parameter
a>0
is of little significance, and all of the results stated below hold regardless of its value; when
a=1
we shall denote
\Gamma^{\beta}_{a}(x_{0})=\Gamma^{\beta}(x_{0})
,
\Gamma_{a}(x_{0})=\Gamma(x_{0})
and
N_{*,\beta}^{a}=N_{*,\beta}
.
In this context it turns out to be natural to impose regularity on the initial datum
f
in the scale of the classical Bessel potential spaces
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which informally, consist of functions
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that possess
\alpha
derivatives in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
; see Section
2.2
below for clarification. The following result was obtained by Nagel–Rudin–Shapiro
[
38
, Theorem 5.5]
; see also Nagel–Stein
[
39
, Theorem 5]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\alpha\geq 0
be such that
\alpha p<n
. If
\beta\geq 1-\frac{\alpha p}{n}
,
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
u_{f}(t,x)=(P_{t}\ast f)(x)
, then
\left\lVert N_{*,\beta}(u_{f})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. As a consequence,

\lim_{\Gamma^{\beta}(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}u_{f}(t,x)=f(x_{0})\quad\textup{for a.e. }x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We remark that Theorem
1.2
can be extended to treat the case
\alpha p=n
, with the conclusions holding for all
\beta>0
(see the proof of Lemma
2.1
below). If
\alpha>\frac{n}{p}
, then any element of
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
has a unique (Hölder) continuous representative vanishing at infinity (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 1.2.4]
), and therefore
u_{f}\in C(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}})
(see, e.g.,
[
41
, Chapter III, Section 2]
).

Fatou’s theorem asserts that the divergence set

\left\{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lim_{\Gamma(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}u_{f}(t,x)\neq f(x_{0})\right\}

has zero Lebesgue measure. A natural question is whether additional regularity assumptions on
f
can make this set smaller in some appropriate sense, such as that measured by the Hausdorff dimension
\dim_{H}
. The following result can be obtained by combining
[
38
, Theorem 3.13, Proposition 5.3]
with a classical result relating Hausdorff measure and Bessel capacity (see, e.g.,
[
45
, Theorem 2.6.16]
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
p\in(1,\infty)
,
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n}{p}]
and
\beta=1-\frac{\alpha p}{n}
. If
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
u_{f}(t,x)=(P_{t}\ast f)(x)
, then for any
\beta^{\prime}\in(\beta,1]
, it holds that

(1.4)

\dim_{H}\left(\left\{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lim_{\Gamma^{\beta^{\prime}}(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}u_{f}(t,x)\neq f(x_{0})\right\}\right)\leq n-n(\beta^{\prime}-\beta).

Note that the limiting case
\beta^{\prime}=\beta
is covered by Theorem
1.2
above. The right-hand side of (
1.4
) is smallest when
\beta^{\prime}=1
and equals
n-\alpha p
.
It is necessary to choose a preferred representative of the equivalence class
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for the statement (
1.4
) to make sense, as different representatives can differ on sets of any Hausdorff dimension. We let this representative be the function
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
defined in (
2.3
) in Section
2.2
below.

1.2.
The Dirichlet problem for elliptic equations

The aim of this paper is to generalise Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
to solutions to the Dirichlet problem for second order divergence form elliptic equations with variable coefficients, under minimal regularity assumptions, in the upper half-space
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
, and in bounded Lipschitz domains in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
.
In general, for an open subset
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
we let
A:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{(1+n)\times(1+n)}
be a matrix of measurable, real-valued functions on
\Omega
. We assume that there are constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda<\infty
such that the bound and uniform ellipticity

(1.5)

\left|\langle A(X)\xi,\eta\rangle\right|\leq\Lambda\left|\xi\right|\left|\eta\right|\quad\textup{and}\quad\langle A(X)\xi,\xi\rangle\geq\lambda\left|\xi\right|^{2}

hold for all
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
and all
X\in\Omega
.
Consider the elliptic equation

(1.6)

Lu=-\textup{div}(A\nabla u)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\partial_{i}\left(A_{ij}\partial_{j}u\right)=0

where, as usual, a measurable function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a (weak)
solution
to this equation in
\Omega
if
u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{\textup{loc}}(\Omega)
and
\int_{\Omega}\langle A\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X=0
for all smooth compactly supported functions
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
.

It follows from the classical De Giorgi–Nash–Moser estimates that solutions of the equation
Lu=0
are locally bounded and Hölder continuous in the sense that

(1.7)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B)}\lesssim\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{2B}\left|u(X)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X\right)^{1/2},

and there exists
\alpha_{L}>0
such that

(1.8)

\left|u(X)-u(Y)\right|\lesssim\left(\frac{\left|X-Y\right|}{r}\right)^{\alpha_{L}}\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{2B}\left|u(X)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X\right)^{1/2}\quad\textup{ for all }X,Y\in B,

for all balls
B
of radius
r>0
such that the dilate
2B\subseteq\Omega
, where
\alpha_{L}
and the implicit constants only depend on
n
,
\lambda
and
\Lambda
.

Let us now consider the special case when
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}=\left\{(t,x):t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}
, whose boundary is identified with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The nontangential maximal function
N_{*}u
of a measurable function
u:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

(1.9)

(N_{*}u)(x_{0})=\sup_{(t,x)\in\Gamma(x_{0})}\left|u(t,x)\right|,\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Given
p\in(1,\infty)
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the Dirichlet problem
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}
is to find a function
u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+})
such that

\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}\hskip 14.22636pt\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}Lu=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+};\\
N_{*}(u)\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\\
\lim_{\Gamma(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}u(t,x)=f(x_{0})\text{ for a.e. }x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{array}\right.

The Dirichlet problem
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}
is said to be
well-posed
if for every boundary datum
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
there exists a unique solution
u
with the three properties listed above.

There exists a family of Borel probability measures
\left\{\omega^{(t,x)}_{L}\right\}_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, known as the
L
-harmonic measures
, such that for any compactly supported continuous function
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the function

(1.10)

u_{f}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{(t,x)}_{L}(y),\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}

is a solution (the unique bounded solution) to the classical Dirichlet problem with boundary datum
f
, in the sense that it is a weak solution to
Lu_{f}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
and that
u_{f}\in C(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}})
with
u_{f}|_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}}=f
(see, e.g.,
[
26
, Section 5B]
in the more general context of degenerate elliptic equations).

We say that
(\mathcal{D})_{p}^{L}

holds
if for all
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the classical solution
u_{f}
in (
1.10
) satisfies

(1.11)

\left\lVert N_{*}u_{f}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

It is well-known that this implies the well-posedness of
(\mathcal{D})^{L}_{p}
(see Lemma
3.2
below).

Our first main result is the following generalisation of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
p\in(1,\infty)
be such that
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}
holds. Let
\alpha>0
be such that
\alpha p\leq n
, and
\beta=1-\frac{\alpha p}{n}
. Let
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
u_{f}
be the unique solution to
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}
with boundary datum
f
. For any
\beta^{\prime}\in(\beta,1]
it holds that

(1.12)

\dim_{H}\left(\left\{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lim_{\Gamma^{\beta^{\prime}}(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}u_{f}(t,x)\neq f(x_{0})\right\}\right)\leq n-n(\beta^{\prime}-\beta).

In addition, if
\alpha p<n
, then

(1.13)

\left\lVert N_{*,\beta}(u_{f})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

and

(1.14)

\lim_{\Gamma^{\beta}(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}u_{f}(t,x)=f(x_{0})\quad\textup{ for a.e. }x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

If
\alpha p=n
, then (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) hold for all
\beta>0
.

Just like Theorem
1.2
, this result holds more generally for the tangential approach regions
\Gamma^{\beta}_{a}(x_{0})
for arbitrary
a>0
.
Again, it is necessary to choose a preferred representative of the equivalence class
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for the statement (
1.12
) to make sense. We choose this representative to be the function
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
defined in (
2.3
) in Section
2.2
below.

In order to extend Theorem
1.4
to general bounded Lipschitz domains (see Theorem
1.7
below), we shall require an analogue of this result for boundary data in the
Sobolev–Slobodeckij spaces
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see Section
2.2
for the definition of these spaces). This is achieved by building on the results of Dorronsoro
[
16
]
, who extended the results of Nagel–Rudin–Shapiro
[
38
]
and Nagel–Stein
[
39
]
(specifically Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
above) beyond the class
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. More specifically, Dorronsoro considered certain fractional mean oscillation spaces
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(defined in Section
2.2
; see also
[
15
]
), and proved the following analogue of Theorem
1.2
(see
[
16
, Theorem 3]
).

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\alpha>0
be such that
\alpha p<n
. If
f\in\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
u_{f}(t,x)=(P_{t}\ast f)(x)
, then
\left\lVert N_{*,\beta}u_{f}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
for all
\beta\geq 1-\frac{\alpha p}{n}
. If
\alpha p=n
, then this holds for all
\beta>0
.

The spaces
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
were originally introduced by Calderón and Scott in
[
10
]
and were thoroughly studied by DeVore and Sharpley in
[
14
]
. If
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\alpha>0
, the Bessel potential space
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is continuously embedded in
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see
[
14
, Theorem 7.5]
). More generally
[
16
, Proposition 3]
shows that the Triebel–Lizorkin spaces
\mathrm{F}^{\alpha}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are continuously embedded in
\mathrm{C}_{\alpha}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
1\leq p,q<\infty
and
\alpha>0
.
The spaces
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
provide a natural setting to extend the conclusions of Theorem
1.4
. In fact, we obtain the analogous results for boundary data
f\in\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
instead of
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in Theorem
3.12
in Section
3.4
. Theorem
1.4
can therefore be obtained as a corollary of this more general result. A more self-contained proof of Theorem
1.4
that does not rely on the embeddings described above, however, is presented earlier in Section
3.3
.

We now consider the case of a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
with surface measure
\sigma
on the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
For any
X_{0}\in\partial\Omega
and
a>0
, we consider the standard nontangential approach region

\Gamma_{\Omega,a}(X_{0})=\left\{X\in\Omega:\left|X-X_{0}\right|<(1+a)\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)\right\}.

For
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, the corresponding nontangential maximal function is given by

(N_{*}^{a}u)(X_{0})=\sup_{X\in\Gamma_{\Omega,a}(X_{0})}\left|u(X)\right|,\quad X_{0}\in\partial\Omega.

Let us denote
\Gamma_{\Omega}(X_{0})=\Gamma_{\Omega,1}(X_{0})
and
N_{*}u=N_{*}^{1}u
.

As above, given
p\in(1,\infty)
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
, the Dirichlet problem
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p,\Omega}
is to find a function
{u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{\textup{loc}}(\Omega)}
such that

\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p,\Omega}\hskip 14.22636pt\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}Lu=0\text{ in }\Omega;\\
N_{*}u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega);\\
\lim_{\Gamma_{\Omega}(P)\ni X\to P}u(X)=f(P)\text{ for }\sigma\text{-a.e. }P\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

The Dirichlet problem
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p,\Omega}
is said to be
well-posed
if for every boundary datum
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
there exists a unique solution
u
with the three properties listed above.

There exists a family of Borel probability measures
\left\{\omega^{X}_{L}\right\}_{X\in\Omega}
on
\partial\Omega
, known as the
L
-
harmonic measures
, such that for any continuous function
f\in C(\partial\Omega)
, the function

(1.15)

u_{f}(X)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}_{L},\quad X\in\Omega,

is a solution to the classical Dirichlet problem with boundary datum
f
, in the sense that it is a weak solution to
Lu_{f}=0
in
\Omega
and that
u_{f}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
with
u_{f}|_{\partial\Omega}=f
(see, e.g.
[
31
, Chapter 1, Section 2]
).

In this context, we say that
(\mathcal{D})_{p,\Omega}^{L}

holds
if for all
f\in C(\partial\Omega)
, the classical solution
u_{f}
in (
1.15
) satisfies

\left\lVert N_{*}u_{f}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}.

Again, it is well-known that this implies the well-posedness of
(\mathcal{D})^{L}_{p,\Omega}
(see, e.g.,
[
31
, Theorem 1.7.7]
).

The analogue of the tangential approach regions
\Gamma^{\beta}(x_{0})
in
\Omega
are now defined as follows. For
X_{0}\in\partial\Omega
,
\beta\in(0,1]
and
c>0
, we let

\Gamma^{\beta,c}_{\Omega}(X_{0})=\left\{X\in\Omega:\left|X-X_{0}\right|<\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1+c)\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)^{\beta},&\textup{if }\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)\leq 1;\\
(1+c)\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega),&\textup{if }\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)\geq 1\end{array}\right.\;\;\;\right\},

and for a measurable
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, the corresponding maximal function is defined by

(N_{*,\beta,c}u)(X_{0})=\sup_{X\in\Gamma^{\beta,c}_{\Omega}(X_{0})}\left|u(X)\right|,\quad X_{0}\in\partial\Omega.

Some of these approach regions are sketched in Figure
1
below, where only the local part (close to the boundary) is included for clarity.

\partial\Omega
of a Lipschitz domain
\Omega

,

,

Remark 1.6
.

The definition of the approach regions
\Gamma^{\beta,c}_{\Omega}(X_{0})
is, of course, not limited to the case of bounded Lipschitz domains. If
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
, then a comparison with the regions defined in (
1.2
) shows that
\Gamma^{\beta}_{c}(x_{0})\subseteq\Gamma_{\Omega}^{\beta,c}(X_{0})\subseteq\Gamma^{\beta}_{1+c}(x_{0})
when
X_{0}=(0,x_{0})\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Besides, observe that a certain order of tangential approach is permitted at a cusp singularity, whereas nontangential approach is prohibited.

In the context of the bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega
that we consider here, we shall quantify the regularity of the boundary data in terms of the Sobolev spaces
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
. These may be defined for smoothness exponents
0\leq s\leq k+1
when the domain
\Omega
is of class
C^{k,1}
for an integer
k\geq 0
(the case
k=0
corresponding to Lipschitz domains). This is discussed in Section
4.3
.

Our second main result is the following extension of Theorem
1.4
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
be a bounded domain of class
C^{k,1}
for some integer
k\geq 0
. Let
p\in(1,\infty)
be such that
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p,\Omega}
holds. Let
0\leq s\leq k+1
be such that
sp\leq n
, let
\beta=1-\frac{sp}{n}
and
c>0
. If
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
and
u_{f}
is the unique solution to
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p,\Omega}
with boundary datum
f
, then for any
\beta^{\prime}\in(\beta,1]
it holds that

(1.16)

\dim_{H}\left(\left\{Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega:\lim_{\Gamma^{\beta^{\prime},c}_{\Omega}(Q_{0})\ni X\to Q_{0}}u_{f}(X)\neq f(Q_{0})\right\}\right)\leq n-n(\beta^{\prime}-\beta).

In addition, if
sp<n
, then

(1.17)

\left\lVert N_{*,\beta,c}(u_{f})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}

and

(1.18)

\lim_{\Gamma^{\beta,c}_{\Omega}(Q_{0})\ni X\to Q_{0}}u_{f}(X)=f(Q_{0})\quad\textup{ for }\sigma\textup{-a.e. }Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega.

If
sp=n
, then (
1.17
) and (
1.18
) hold for all
\beta>0
.

The estimate (
1.16
) will be shown to hold for a specific representative
\tilde{f}:\partial\Omega\to(-\infty,\infty]
of the function
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
(see (
4.17
) at the start of Section
4.8
).

It is well-known that the bound and ellipticity of the coefficients
A
in (
1.5
) are not sufficient for solvability of
(\mathcal{D})_{p,\Omega}^{L}
, even on smooth domains (see
[
9
]
).
We conclude this introduction by mentioning some conditions which ensure the well-posedness of the Dirichlet problem. We start with the upper half-space
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
.
If the coefficients
A:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{(1+n)\times(1+n)}
are
t
-independent and satisfy (
1.5
), it has been shown by Hofmann–Kenig–Mayboroda–Pipher that there exists
p_{0}\in(1,\infty)
such that
(\mathcal{D})^{L}_{p}
holds for all
p\geq p_{0}
(see
[
25
, Theorem 1.23]
). If
A_{0},A_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{(1+n)\times(1+n)}
are coefficients satisfying (
1.5
), let their difference function be given by

(1.19)

a(X)=\sup_{\left|X-Y\right|<\delta(X)/2}\left|A_{0}(Y)-A_{1}(Y)\right|

for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
, where
\delta(X)=\textup{dist}(X,\partial\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+})
. Let
L_{0}=-\textup{div}(A_{0}\nabla)
and
L_{1}=-\textup{div}(A_{1}\nabla)
denote the corresponding elliptic operators.
If there is
C>0
such that

(1.20)

\frac{1}{\left\lvert Q\right\rvert}\iint_{T(Q)}\frac{a(X)^{2}}{\delta(X)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X\leq C

for all surface cubes
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(where
T(Q)=(0,\ell(Q))\times Q
is the corresponding Carleson box), then R. Fefferman, Kenig and Pipher have shown that there is
p_{0}\in(1,\infty)
such that
(\mathcal{D})^{L_{0}}_{p}
holds for all
p\geq p_{0}
if and only if there is
p_{1}\in(1,\infty)
such that
(\mathcal{D})^{L_{1}}_{p}
holds for all
p\geq p_{1}
(see
[
20
, Theorem 2.3]
and note, as is well-known, that the symmetry assumption on the coefficients made therein is not required).

For the Laplacian
\Delta
in a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
, we mention the classical theorem of Dahlberg
[
12
,
13
]
stating that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the Dirichlet problem
(\mathcal{D})^{\Delta}_{p}
holds for all
2-\varepsilon<p\leq\infty
. As above, the well-posedness of the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
Dirichlet problem is preserved under small perturbations of the coefficients, in the way quantified by (
1.20
), see
[
20
, Theorem 2.3]
and references therein.

2.1.
Notation

Throughout
n\geq 1
is an integer, and we use the convention that
\mathbb{N}=\left\{0,1,\ldots\right\}
.
We let
\left|x\right|
denote the Euclidean norm of an element
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\Delta(x,r)
denote the Euclidean ball with centre
x
and radius
r
,
and
\langle x,y\rangle
denote the standard scalar product of vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We use the notation
x\vee y=\max\left\{x,y\right\}
and
x\wedge y=\min\left\{x,y\right\}
.
As usual, the distance between two subsets
E,F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\textup{dist}(E,F)=\inf\left\{\left|x-y\right|:x\in E,y\in F\right\}
, and for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
\textup{dist}(x,F)=\textup{dist}(\{x\},F)
. The closure of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\overline{A}
, and the indicator (or characteristic) function of such a set is denoted by
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
.
The upper half-space
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
is defined as

\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:t>0\right\}
.
The
\ell^{\infty}
norm of a vector
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\left|x\right|_{\infty}=\sup_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|x_{i}\right|
, and the cube
Q
of centre
x_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and sidelength
\ell(Q)=\ell>0
is the set
Q=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left|x-x_{Q}\right|_{\infty}<\ell/2\right\}
. The point
X_{Q}=(\ell(Q),x_{Q})\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
denotes the corresponding corkscrew point in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
.

If the Lebesgue measure of
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted
\left\lvert A\right\rvert
, is finite, we let

\left(f\right)_{A}=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{A}f=\frac{1}{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}\int_{A}f(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

denote the average of an integrable function
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
over the set
A
. For a locally integrable function
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, its centred Hardy–Littlewood maximal function is defined for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

Mf(x)=\sup_{r>0}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta(x,r)}\left|f(y)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, we let
\mathrm{L}^{p}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the space of functions
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
f(x)\geq 0
for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
If
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open,
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(O)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
is a multi-index, then the
\alpha
th partial (distributional) derivative of
f
is denoted
\partial^{\alpha}f
. In the case when
\alpha=(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0)
(where
1
is in the
k
th component), we let
\partial_{k}f=\partial^{\alpha}f
.

For a normed space
X
, we let
\mathcal{L}(X)
denote the space of all bounded linear operators
{T:X\to X}
equipped with the usual operator norm
\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(X)}
. The class of Schwartz functions is denoted by
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and the space of tempered distributions is denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

If
x,y\geq 0
, we say that
x\lesssim y
if there exists
C>0
, independent of both
x
and
y
, such that
x\leq Cy
. We define
x\gtrsim y
in a similar way, and use the notation
x\eqsim y
to express that both
x\lesssim y
and
x\gtrsim y
hold. We shall occasionally use the symbol
\lesssim_{a}
to indicate that the implicit constant depends on some parameter
a
, or directly denote the constant as
C_{a}
.

2.2.
Sobolev spaces

In this section we introduce the various Sobolev (or smoothness) spaces referred to in the introduction in the order that they appear – the Bessel potential spaces
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the fractional mean oscillation spaces
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and the Sobolev–Slobodeckij spaces
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}
. For each we collect some basic facts that we shall appeal to in subsequent sections.

The Bessel potential spaces

A natural criterion that captures the smoothness of a function in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is expressed in terms of the smoothing operators
\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}=(I-\Delta)^{-\alpha/2}
for
\alpha\geq 0
: a function
f
belongs to the Bessel potential space
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if there exists
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
f=\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}g
. The operator
\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}
is given by convolution with the
Bessel kernel

G_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]
, defined via the Fourier transform by
\widehat{G}_{\alpha}(\xi)=(1+4\pi^{2}\left|\xi\right|^{2})^{-\alpha/2}
, or spatially by

(2.1)

G_{\alpha}(x)=c_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left({-\pi\frac{\left|x\right|^{2}}{t}}{-\frac{t}{4\pi}}\right)t^{-\frac{(n-\alpha)}{2}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{t};

here
c_{\alpha}>0
ensures that
\left\lVert G_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}}=1
. It follows that
G_{\alpha}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\})\cap\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and is nonnegative for each
\alpha>0
, and

\left\lVert\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}(g)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
by Minkowski’s inequality. It also follows from the representation (
2.1
) that the Bessel kernel exhibits exponential decay at infinity, and in particular

(2.2)

\left|G_{\alpha}(x)\right|\lesssim\left|x\right|^{-(n-\alpha)}e^{-\left|x\right|/2},\quad\left|\nabla G_{\alpha}(x)\right|\lesssim\left|x\right|^{-(n-\alpha+1)}e^{-\left|x\right|/2},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}.

Of course
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, being a subspace of
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, consists of equivalence classes of functions
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and the
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}
-norm of
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is naturally defined by

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
if
f=\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}(g)
.

As outlined in the introduction, it is necessary to choose a preferred representative of the equivalence class
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for the statements of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
to make sense (specifically for (
1.4
) and (
1.12
)). If
\alpha>0
and
p\in(1,\infty)
, a reasonable representative of
f=G_{\alpha}\ast g\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the function
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
defined by

(2.3)

\tilde{f}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta(x,r)}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y,&\textup{if the limit exists in }\mathbb{R};\\
\infty,&\textup{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Indeed,
[
1
, Proposition 6.1.3]
shows that
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta(x,r)}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=(G_{\alpha}\ast g)(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
(G_{\alpha}\ast\left|g\right|)(x)<\infty
, that is, away from a set of Hausdorff dimension no greater than
n-\alpha p
; see (
2.7
) in Section
2.3
below. We note that
|\tilde{f}(x)|\leq(G_{\alpha}\ast\left|g\right|)(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In this setting we also define the
Riesz kernel
, or fractional integral kernel,
I_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]
by
\widehat{I}_{\alpha}(\xi)=(2\pi\left|\xi\right|)^{-\alpha}
, or equivalently by

(2.4)

I_{\alpha}(x)=c_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{{-\pi\frac{\left|x\right|^{2}}{t}}}t^{-\frac{(n-\alpha)}{2}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{t}=\gamma_{\alpha,n}\left|x\right|^{-(n-\alpha)}

for a suitable constant
\gamma_{\alpha,n}>0
. It follows from (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) that
0<G_{\alpha}(x)\leq I_{\alpha}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and that
\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{G_{\alpha}(x)}{I_{\alpha}(x)}=1
whenever
0<\alpha<n
. The reader may refer to
[
41
, Chapter V, Section 3]
for more on Riesz and Bessel potentials.

The fractional mean oscillation spaces
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

Another, somewhat less common, criterion to measure the smoothness of functions in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is by quantifying their local approximability by polynomials.
For integers
k\geq 0
we let
\mathbb{P}_{k}
denote the space of real polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with degree at most
k
. For
\alpha>0
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
consider the maximal function

S_{\alpha}f(x)=\sup_{\Delta\ni x}\inf_{P\in\mathbb{P}_{k}}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\Delta\right\rvert^{\alpha/n}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}\left|f(y)-P(y)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y,

where the supremum is taken over all balls
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and where here and in what follows
k=\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
is the greatest integer less than or equal to
\alpha
.
The maximal functions
S_{\alpha}f
measure the smoothness of
f
.
For example, when
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have the elementary inequality

\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|\lesssim\left|x-y\right|^{\alpha}(S_{\alpha}f(x)+S_{\alpha}f(y))

for almost every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see, e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 2.5]
).

The maximal function
S_{\alpha}f
can be defined using a minimising polynomial.
The space
\mathbb{P}_{k}
is a Hilbert space with the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
inner product
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\Delta(0,1)}f(x)g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
. Starting from the set
\left\{x^{\gamma}\right\}_{\left|\gamma\right|\leq k}
of all monomials in
\mathbb{P}_{k}
, arranged in lexicographic order, the Gram–Schmidt process produces an orthonormal basis
\left\{\varphi_{\gamma}\right\}_{\left|\gamma\right|\leq k}
of
\mathbb{P}_{k}
. The operator
P^{k}_{\Delta(0,1)}:\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{P}_{k}
defined by

P_{\Delta(0,1)}^{k}f(x)=\sum_{\left|\gamma\right|\leq k}\langle f,\varphi_{\gamma}\rangle\varphi_{\gamma}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is a projection (by construction) and it clearly satisfies
\lVert P_{\Delta(0,1)}^{k}f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Delta(0,1))}\lesssim\int_{\Delta(0,1)}\left|f\right|
. For a general ball
\Delta=\Delta(x_{0},r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the corresponding projection
P_{\Delta}^{k}:\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{P}_{k}
by

P_{\Delta}^{k}f=P_{\Delta(0,1)}(f\circ A)\circ A^{-1},

where
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\to x_{0}+rx
. It follows by a change of variables that

(2.5)

\lVert P_{\Delta}^{k}f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Delta)}\lesssim\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}\left|f\right|.

The definition shows that
P_{\Delta}^{k}f
is the unique polynomial in
\mathbb{P}_{k}
such that

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}(f(y)-P_{\Delta}^{k}f(y))y^{\gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=0

for all multi-indices
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
\left|\gamma\right|\leq k
. In particular,
P^{0}_{\Delta}f=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}f
.

It can be seen using (
2.5
) that the maximal function

f_{\alpha}^{\sharp}(x)=\sup_{\Delta\ni x}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\Delta\right\rvert^{\alpha/n}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}|f(y)-P_{\Delta}^{k}f(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

is such that
S_{\alpha}f(x)\eqsim f_{\alpha}^{\sharp}(x)
, see
[
14
, Lemma 2.1]
.
For
p\in[1,\infty]
and
\alpha>0
, the space
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined as the subspace of
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
consisting of the (equivalence classes of) functions
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
f_{\alpha}^{\sharp}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. It is equipped with the norm

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\lVert f^{\sharp}_{\alpha}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

which makes it into a Banach space (see
[
14
, Lemma 6.1]
); we clarify that
f_{0}^{\sharp}
is the classical sharp maximal function of C. Fefferman and Stein, and
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\eqsim\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. Just as with the Bessel potential spaces
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, if
\alpha>\frac{n}{p}
and
f\in\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
f
has a continuous representative (see
[
14
, Theorem 9.1]
).

We also consider the following closed subspace of
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
:

\mathrm{F}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{f\in\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\lVert f_{\alpha}^{\sharp}(t,\cdot)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=0\right\},

where

f_{\alpha}^{\sharp}(t,x)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta\ni x\\
\textup{diam}(\Delta)\leq t\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\Delta\right\rvert^{\alpha/n}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}|f(y)-P_{\Delta}^{k}f(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\quad\textup{ for all }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}.

The space
\mathrm{F}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
coincides with the closure of the smooth compactly supported functions
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see
[
16
, Proposition 1]
).
The reader can refer to the monograph of DeVore and Sharpley
[
14
]
for an extensive study of the spaces
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We note that in
[
14
,
16
]
the maximal functions
S_{\alpha}f
and
f^{\sharp}_{\alpha}
are defined as suprema over cubes
Q\ni x
rather than Euclidean balls
\Delta\ni x
. This obviously leads to pointwise equivalent maximal functions, and so the resulting spaces
\mathrm{F}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are identical as sets with equivalent norms.

The Sobolev–Slobodeckij spaces
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}

As we shall see, the spaces
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are very natural from the point of view of our pointwise convergence problems in the setting of the upper half space. However, their definitions do not appear to extend in a convenient way to the setting of general open subsets
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A more suitable approach (for our purposes at least) is to define fractional Sobolev spaces in domains as follows. If
p\in[1,\infty)
and
m\geq 0
is an integer, the Sobolev space
\mathrm{W}^{m,p}(U)
is defined as usual by

\mathrm{W}^{m,p}(U)=\left\{f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(U):\partial^{\alpha}f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)\textup{ for all multi-indices}\left|\alpha\right|\leq m\right\},

equipped with the norm
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{m,p}(U)}=\left(\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|\leq m}\left\lVert\partial_{\alpha}f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(U)}^{p}\right)^{1/p}
. The Sobolev spaces of fractional order are defined using the Slobodeckij seminorms given by

\left[f\right]_{\sigma,p,U}=\left(\int_{U}\int_{U}\frac{\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|^{p}}{\left|x-y\right|^{n+\sigma p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right)^{1/p}\quad\textup{ for }\sigma\in(0,1).

If
s=m+\sigma
, where
m\geq 0
is an integer and
\sigma\in(0,1)
, then we define

\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U)=\left\{f\in\mathrm{W}^{m,p}(U):\left[\partial^{\alpha}f\right]_{\sigma,p,U}<\infty\textup{ whenever }\left|\alpha\right|=m\right\},

and equip this space with the norm
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U)}=\left(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{m,p}(U)}^{p}+\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=m}\left[\partial_{\alpha}f\right]_{\sigma,p,U}^{p}\right)^{1/p}
.

If
k\geq 1
is a positive integer and
p\in(1,\infty)
, then
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathrm{W}^{k,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with equivalent norms (see, e.g.,
[
41
, Chapter V, Theorem 3]
).
Furthermore, the continuous inclusion
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\mathscr{L}^{p}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
holds for all
s>0
if
p\in(1,2]
, and the converse inclusion holds in the range
p\in[2,\infty)
; see, for example,
[
41
, Chapter V, Theorem 5]
.

2.3.
Fractional dimensional measures and Frostman’s lemma

For
s\in[0,\infty)
, a positive Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
s
-dimensional
if

c_{s}(\mu)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},r>0}\frac{\mu(\Delta(x,r))}{r^{s}}<\infty.

We let
\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the collection of all
s
-dimensional probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The following lemma shows the relevance of
\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the study of the Bessel potential spaces
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This result follows from
[
1
, Theorem 7.2.2]
and a classical estimate on the Bessel capacity of balls. For completeness we shall prove this in the context of general fractional dimensional measures. See also
[
45
, Theorem 4.7.2]
for some related estimates.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
,
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n}{p}]
and
s>n-\alpha p
. If
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\left\lVert G_{\alpha}\ast g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim(c_{s}(\mu)^{1/p}\vee 1)\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}
.

Proof.

We first treat the case when
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n}{p})
. Let
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We shall prove the stronger estimate
\left\lVert I_{\alpha}\ast g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim(c_{s}(\mu)^{1/p}\vee 1)\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}
. Note that Fubini’s theorem followed by Hölder’s inequality show that
\left\lVert I_{\alpha}\ast g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\leq\left\lVert I_{\alpha}\ast\mu\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
, hence it suffices to prove that
\left\lVert I_{\alpha}\ast\mu\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim(c_{s}(\mu)^{1/p}\vee 1)
. Using Fubini’s theorem we can write for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(I_{\alpha}\ast\mu)(x)&=\gamma_{\alpha,n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|x-y\right|^{-(n-\alpha)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\eqsim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\left|x-y\right|}^{\infty}r^{-(n-\alpha)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{-(n-\alpha)}\mathbbm{1}_{\Delta(x,r)}(y)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(\Delta(x,r))r^{-(n-\alpha)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}.\end{split}

For
r>0
, we consider the function
F_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
defined by
F_{r}(x)=\mu(\Delta(x,r))
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
\mu
is a probability measure, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\lVert F_{r}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mu(\Delta(x,r))^{p^{\prime}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mu(\Delta(x,r))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\Delta(x,r)}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lvert\Delta(y,r)\right\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\lesssim r^{n},

where Fubini’s theorem was used to obtain the last equality. In addition, since
\mu
is
s
-dimensional, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\lVert F_{r}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mu(\Delta(x,r))^{p^{\prime}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mu(\Delta(x,r))^{p^{\prime}-1}\mu(\Delta(x,r))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq(c_{s}(\mu)r^{s})^{p^{\prime}-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mu(\Delta(x,r))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\lesssim(c_{s}(\mu)r^{s})^{p^{\prime}-1}r^{n}.

Let us fix
R>0
to be determined later. Minkowski’s inequality and (
2.6
) imply that

\displaystyle\left\lVert I_{\alpha}\ast\mu\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert F_{r}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}r^{-(n-\alpha)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}

\displaystyle\lesssim c_{s}(\mu)^{1/p}\int_{0}^{R}r^{\frac{s}{p}+\frac{n}{p^{\prime}}}r^{-(n-\alpha)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}+\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{\frac{n}{p^{\prime}}}r^{-(n-\alpha)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}

\displaystyle\eqsim c_{s}(\mu)^{1/p}R^{\alpha-\frac{n}{p}+\frac{s}{p}}+R^{\alpha-\frac{n}{p}},

where we have used that
s>n-\alpha p
and that
\alpha<\frac{n}{p}
to show that both integrals are finite. Since
\frac{n-\alpha p}{s}\in(0,1)
, choosing
R=c_{s}(\mu)^{-1/s}
yields

\left\lVert I_{\alpha}\ast\mu\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim c_{s}(\mu)^{\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{n-\alpha p}{s}\right)}\leq c_{s}(\mu)^{\frac{1}{p}}\vee 1.

Finally, if
\alpha=\frac{n}{p}
, then by assumption
s>0
and we may choose
\gamma\in(0,\frac{n}{p})
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\alpha=\gamma+\varepsilon
and
s>n-\gamma p
. Using the estimate proved in the first part of the proof we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\lVert G_{\alpha}\ast g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert G_{\gamma}\ast G_{\varepsilon}\ast g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\leq\left\lVert I_{\gamma}\ast G_{\varepsilon}\ast g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\lesssim(c_{s}(\mu)^{\frac{1}{p}}\vee 1)\left\lVert G_{\varepsilon}\ast g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq(c_{s}(\mu)^{\frac{1}{p}}\vee 1)\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.\qed

For any Borel subset
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)\in[0,\infty]
denote the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
E
. The Hausdorff dimension of
E
is denoted by
\dim_{H}(E)
. The relevant definitions can be found in
[
34
, Chapter 4]
. To obtain the estimates on the Hausdorff dimension of the divergence set in Theorem
1.4
, we will rely on both Lemma
2.1
and the following result, a proof of which can be found in
[
34
, Theorem 8.8]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Frostman’s lemma)
.

Let
E
be a Borel subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
s>0
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)>0
;

(ii)

There exists a finite
s
-dimensional Radon measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\textup{supp}\left(\mu\right)\subseteq E
and
\mu(E)>0
.

In order to briefly describe the general principle that we shall use to obtain the Hausdorff dimension estimates in Theorem
1.4
, let us see how Lemma
2.1
and Frostman’s lemma can be combined to study the Bessel potential spaces
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. If
\alpha>\frac{n}{p}
, then it is well-known that all elements of
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be represented by (Hölder) continuous functions vanishing at infinity (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 1.2.4]
).
If
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n}{p}]
,
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f=G_{\alpha}\ast g
, then this convolution is defined everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(though it may take the value
\infty
on a set of Lebesgue measure zero). For any
s>n-\alpha p
and any measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it follows from Lemma
2.1
that

\mu\left(\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)=\infty\right\}\right)=0.

It follows from Frostman’s lemma that
\mathcal{H}^{s}\left(\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)=\infty\right\}\right)=0
. Since
s>n-\alpha p
is arbitrary, this implies that

(2.7)

\dim_{H}(\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)=\infty\right\})\leq n-\alpha p.

This is optimal, as Žubrinić
[
44
, Theorem 2]
has shown that for any
0<\lambda<n-\alpha p
, there exists a compact set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a function
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\dim_{H}(A)=\lambda
and
A=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(G_{\alpha}\ast g)(x)=\infty\right\}
.

3.1.
Nontangential approach in the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-Dirichlet problem in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}

We let

satisfying the boundedness and ellipticity assumptions (

L=-\textup{div}{(A\nabla)}
defined in (

As described in the introduction, there exists a family of Borel probability measures
\left\{\omega^{(t,x)}_{L}\right\}_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}}
on

, known as the

-harmonic measures, such that for any compactly supported continuous function

(3.1)

u_{f}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{(t,x)}_{L}(y),\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+},

is a solution (the unique bounded solution) to the classical Dirichlet problem with boundary datum
f
, in the sense that it is a weak solution to
Lu_{f}=-\textup{div}(A\nabla u_{f})=0
in

and that
u_{f}\in C(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}})

[

As stated in the introduction, for
p\in(1,\infty)
, we say that
(\mathcal{D})^{L}_{p}
holds if for all
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the classical solution
u_{f}
in (
3.1
) satisfies

\left\lVert N_{*}u_{f}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

The
L
-harmonic measures
\left\{\omega^{X}_{L}\right\}_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}}
are all mutually absolutely continuous by the Harnack inequality (see, e.g.,
[
26
, Lemma 5.6]

\omega_{L}^{X}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(i.e. the surface measure on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then we let
{k(X,\cdot)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}_{L}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]}
denote the corresponding Radon–Nikodym derivative. This function is referred to as the Poisson kernel for
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
.

The following well-known result describes how the well-posedness of
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}
relates to the quantitative absolute continuity of the
L
-harmonic measure with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. See, for example,
[
28
, Theorem 3.1]
, and note that
p
should be replaced by

in items (b) and (c) of this reference. Recall that
X_{Q}
is the corkscrew point corresponding to the cube

.

Theorem 3.1
.

The following properties are equivalent:

(i)

\omega_{L}^{X_{Q_{0}}}\in A_{\infty}(Q_{0})
, uniformly for all cubes
Q_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In other words, for every cube
Q_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
depending only on

,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
and
\varepsilon
, such that if

is a cube and

, then
\left\lvert E\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon\left\lvert Q\right\rvert
.

(ii)

There exists
p\in(1,\infty)
such that
(\mathcal{D})_{p}^{L}
holds.

(iii)

The measures
\left\{\omega^{X}_{L}\right\}_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}}

and
C_{0}>0
such that for all cubes

, the Poisson kernel

satisfies

(3.2)

\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{Q}k(X_{Q_{0}},y)^{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right)^{1/q}\leq C_{0}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{Q}k(X_{Q_{0}},y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C_{0}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{-1}

and

Remark 3.3
.

We take this opportunity to rectify a slight imprecision that appears in the proof of the nontangential maximal function estimate in the proof of
[

, Lemma 5.32]
(which concerns weights
\mu
that here we only consider when

M_{s}
and convolutions.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
q\in[1,\infty)
. For all non-negative
G\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and non-negative
g\in\mathrm{L}^{q}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
it holds that

M_{q}(G\ast g)(x)\leq(G\ast M_{q}(g))(x)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
be fixed. By Minkowski’s inequality and a change of variables we obtain that

\displaystyle\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta(x,t)}(G\ast g)^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\Delta(x,t)\right\rvert^{-1/q}\left\lVert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(\cdot-z)G(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Delta(x,t))}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\Delta(x,t)\right\rvert^{-1/q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lVert g(\cdot-z)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Delta(x,t))}G(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\Delta(x-z,t)\right\rvert^{-1/q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Delta(x-z,t))}G(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(M_{q}(g))(x-z)G(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=(G\ast M_{q}(g))(x).\qed

We can now use this lemma to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of the divergence set below. Again, it is implicit in Proposition
3.5
below that the function
f
in (
3.7
) is the preferred representative
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
from (
2.3
).

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
be such that
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}
holds. If
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n}{p}]
,
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
u_{f}
is the unique solution to
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}
with boundary datum
f
, then

(3.7)

\dim_{H}\left(\left\{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lim_{\Gamma(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}u_{f}(t,x)\neq f(x_{0})\right\}\right)\leq n-\alpha p.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
f=G_{\alpha}\ast g\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The unique solution to
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}
with boundary datum
f
is given by (
3.3
).

By relying on the self-improvement property of the reverse Hölder estimate in item (ii) of Theorem
3.1
, it is shown in the proof of
[
26
, Lemma 5.32]
(see also Remark
3.3
above) that there exists
1<r^{\prime}<p
such that

(3.8)

(N_{*}u_{f})(x_{0})\lesssim(M_{r^{\prime}}f)(x_{0})

for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note that the maximal function
M_{r^{\prime}}
is bounded on
\mathrm{L}^{p}
since
p>r^{\prime}
, and let us remark that this recovers the usual
\mathrm{L}^{p}
nontangential maximal function bounds.
It follows from (
3.8
) and Lemma
3.4
that

\displaystyle(N_{*}u_{f})(x_{0})\lesssim(M_{r^{\prime}}(G_{\alpha}\ast\left|g\right|))(x_{0})\leq(G_{\alpha}\ast(M_{r^{\prime}}g))(x_{0})

for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consequently, if
s>n-\alpha p
and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then it follows from Lemma
2.1
that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left\lVert N_{*}u_{f}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim\left\lVert G_{\alpha}\ast(M_{r^{\prime}}g)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim\left\lVert M_{r^{\prime}}g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

where the implicit constants also depend on
c_{s}(\mu)
, as described by Lemma
2.1
, but this is unimportant here.
The bound (
3.7
) now follows by a standard approximation argument and application of Frostman’s lemma, which we detail here for completeness.

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\tilde{f}
be the representative of
f
given by (
2.3
). As explained in Section
2.2
, it holds that
|\tilde{f}(x)|\lesssim(G_{\alpha}\ast\left|g\right|)(x)
for
all

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consequently, Lemma
2.1
shows that
\lVert\tilde{f}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. Since there exists a Schwartz function
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\varepsilon
, it follows that

\lVert\tilde{f}-h\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\varepsilon.

Even if
h\not\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by lack of a compact support, a simple density argument shows that
u_{h}\in C^{0}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}})
with
u_{h}|_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}}=h
. Observe that for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
it holds that

\left|u_{f}(t,x)-\tilde{f}(x_{0})\right|\leq\left|u_{f-h}(t,x)\right|+\left|u_{h}(t,x)-h(x_{0})\right|+\left|h(x_{0})-\tilde{f}(x_{0})\right|,

and since
u_{h}\in C^{0}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}})
it follows that

\limsup_{\Gamma(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}\left|u_{f}(t,x)-\tilde{f}(x_{0})\right|\leq(N_{*}u_{f-h})(x_{0})+\left|\tilde{f}(x_{0})-h(x_{0})\right|.

As a consequence, we can use Markov’s inequality, (
3.9
) and Lemma
2.1
to obtain for any
\lambda>0
that

\displaystyle\mu\left(\!\left\{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\limsup_{\Gamma(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}\left|u_{f}(t,x)-\tilde{f}(x_{0})\right|>\lambda\right\}\!\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert N_{*}u_{f-h}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\!+\!\lambda^{-1}\lVert\tilde{f}-h\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\varepsilon\lambda^{-1}.

Since
\varepsilon
and
\lambda
are arbitrary, this implies that

\mu\left(\left\{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\limsup_{\Gamma(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}\left|u_{f}(t,x)-\tilde{f}(x_{0})\right|\neq 0\right\}\right)=0.

Since
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
was arbitrary, Frostman’s lemma (Lemma
2.2
) implies that

(3.10)

\mathcal{H}^{s}\left(\left\{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\limsup_{\Gamma(x_{0})\ni(t,x)\to(0,x_{0})}\left|u_{f}(t,x)-\tilde{f}(x_{0})\right|\neq 0\right\}\right)=0.

Since
s>n-\alpha p
was arbitrary, this concludes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Further properties of Bessel potentials

For
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the classical Poisson kernel
P_{t}(x)
is defined by

P_{t}(x)=t^{-n}P_{1}(x/t)=\frac{c_{n}t}{(t^{2}+\left|x\right|^{2})^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}

where
c_{n}>0
guarantees that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}P_{t}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=1
for all
t>0
.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
\alpha\in[0,n)
and
T>0
, then
t^{\alpha}P_{t}(x)\lesssim\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}(P_{t})(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
0<t\leq T
. In particular, for all
p\in[1,\infty]
and
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
it holds that
t^{\alpha}(P_{t}\ast g)(x)\lesssim(P_{t}\ast\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}(g))(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
0<t\leq T
.

Proof.

This is trivial when
\alpha=0
. Let us assume that
\alpha\in(0,n)
. There is some
\delta>0
such that
G_{\alpha}(y)\gtrsim\left|y\right|^{-(n-\alpha)}
for all
\left|y\right|<\delta
(see, e.g., (29) in
[
41
, Chapter V, Section 3]
). We may assume that
\delta\leq T
. For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
0<t\leq T
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}(P_{t})(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}P_{t}(x-y)G_{\alpha}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\gtrsim t^{-n}\int_{\left|y\right|<\delta}P_{1}\left(\frac{x-y}{t}\right)\left|y\right|^{-(n-\alpha)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=t^{\alpha-n}\int_{\left|z\right|<\delta/t}P_{1}\left(\frac{x}{t}-z\right)\left|z\right|^{-(n-\alpha)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\geq t^{\alpha-n}\int_{\left|z\right|<\delta T^{-1}}P_{1}\left(\frac{x}{t}-z\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z,

where we have made the change of variables
z=y/t
and used that
\left|z\right|^{-(n-\alpha)}\geq 1
for all
\left|z\right|<\delta T^{-1}\leq 1
. We conclude by observing that
P_{1}(y-z)\gtrsim P_{1}\left(y\right)
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\left|z\right|<1
.
∎

The following elementary lemma will serve as a substitute for the classical Poincaré inequality in the context of Bessel potential spaces of fractional order.

Lemma 3.7

(
\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}
-Poincaré)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
p\in[1,\infty]
and
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. If
f=G_{\alpha}\ast g
, then for almost all
y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
it holds that

(3.11)

\left|f(y)-f(z)\right|\lesssim\left|y-z\right|^{\alpha}\left(Mg(y)+Mg(z)\right).

Consequently, for all
q\in[1,\infty)
and all balls
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of radius
r>0
it holds that

\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}\left|f-(f)_{\Delta}\right|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\lesssim r^{\alpha}\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}\left(Mg\right)^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

Proof.

The proof is based on the fact that

(3.12)

\left|G_{\alpha}(x-y)-G_{\alpha}(x)\right|\lesssim\left|y\right|\left|x\right|^{-n+\alpha-1}

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\left|x\right|\geq 2\left|y\right|
, which follows from (
2.2
) and the mean value theorem. Since
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, there is a null set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\left|f(x)\right|<\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E
. If
y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E
and
y\neq z
, then

(3.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|f(y)-f(z)\right|&\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|G_{\alpha}(y-x)-G_{\alpha}(z-x)\right|\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\left|z-x\right|\geq 2\left|z-y\right|}\left|G_{\alpha}((z-x)-(z-y))-G_{\alpha}(z-x)\right|\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{\left|z-x\right|<2\left|z-y\right|}\left|G_{\alpha}(y-x)-G_{\alpha}(z-x)\right|\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\lesssim\left|z-y\right|\int_{\left|z-x\right|\geq 2\left|z-y\right|}\left|z-x\right|^{-n+\alpha-1}\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{\left|y-x\right|<3\left|z-y\right|}\left|y-x\right|^{-n+\alpha}\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\left|z-x\right|<2\left|z-y\right|}\left|z-x\right|^{-n+\alpha}\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

where we have used (
3.12
), the estimate
G_{\alpha}(x)\leq I_{\alpha}(x)
, and the fact that
\left|z-x\right|<2\left|y-z\right|
implies
\left|y-x\right|<3\left|z-y\right|
. For any
r>0
, observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\left|z-x\right|\geq r}\left|z-x\right|^{-n+\alpha-1}\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{2^{k}r\leq\left|z-x\right|<2^{k+1}r}\left|z-x\right|^{-n+\alpha-1}\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2^{k}r)^{\alpha-1}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{B(z,2^{k+1}r)}\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\lesssim r^{\alpha-1}Mg(z),

using that the series converges because
\alpha<1
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{\left|y-x\right|<r}\left|y-x\right|^{-n+\alpha}\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{2^{-(k+1)}r\leq\left|y-x\right|<2^{-k}r}\left|y-x\right|^{-n+\alpha}\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2^{-k}r)^{\alpha}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{B(y,2^{-k}r)}\left|g(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\lesssim r^{\alpha}Mg(y),

using that the series converges because
\alpha>0
. Combining these two estimates with (
3.13
) yields (
3.11
).
∎

We remark that the previous lemma is reminiscent of one implication of the well-known Hajłasz–Sobolev characterisation of Sobolev spaces, which asserts that if
f\in\textup{W}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
1<p<\infty
then

\left|f(y)-f(z)\right|\lesssim\left|y-z\right|\left(M(\nabla f)(y)+M(\nabla f)(z)\right)

for almost every
y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see, e.g.,
[
24
, Theorems 2.1 and 2.2]
).

We shall use Lemma
3.7
in conjunction with the following well-known result due to Calderón.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\alpha\geq 1
. Then
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if
f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\partial_{k}f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for each
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
. Moreover,
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}}\eqsim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha-1}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\lVert\partial_{k}f\right\rVert_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha-1}}
.

Theorem
3.8
relies on a special case of the following lemma, whose proof follows from the arguments in the proof of
[
41
, Chapter V, Lemma 3]
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
p\in(1,\infty)
,
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f=\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}g\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For all multi-indices
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
0<\left|\gamma\right|=k\leq\alpha
, it holds that
\partial^{\gamma}f\in\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\alpha-k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the representation

\partial^{\gamma}f=\mathscr{J}_{\alpha-k}\left(\mathcal{R}^{\gamma}(\mu_{k}\ast g)\right),

where
\mathcal{R}^{\gamma}=\mathcal{R}^{\gamma_{1}}_{1}\ldots\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\gamma_{n}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
is a composition of Riesz transforms, and
\mu_{k}
is a finite (signed) Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, for any multi-index
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
0\leq\left|\gamma\right|=k\leq\alpha
, there exists
g^{(\gamma)}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\partial^{\gamma}f=\mathscr{J}_{\alpha-k}(g^{(\gamma)})
and
\lVert g^{(\gamma)}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\lesssim\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}
.

We now introduce some notation. Given a function
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with weak partial derivatives of order
k\geq 1
in
\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we let

(3.14)

\nabla^{k}f=\left(\partial^{\gamma}f\right)_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\left|\gamma\right|=k},

where the vector is arranged in lexicographic order (for example).
In general, we denote the norm of a vector
x=(x^{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\left|\gamma\right|=k}
, where
x^{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
\left|\gamma\right|=k
, by
\left|x\right|=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\left|\gamma\right|=k}\left|x^{\gamma}\right|
. With that in mind, in the context of Lemma
3.9
and in the pointwise almost everywhere sense, it holds that

(3.15)

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}f|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\left|\gamma\right|=k}\left|\partial^{\gamma}f\right|\leq\sum_{\left|\gamma\right|=k}\mathscr{J}_{\alpha-k}(|g^{(\gamma)}|)=\mathscr{J}_{\alpha-k}(|g^{k}|),

where
g^{k}=(g^{(\gamma)})_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\left|\gamma\right|=k}
, and therefore
\lVert|g^{k}|\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}
. We extend this notation to the case
k=0
by setting
\nabla^{0}f=f
and
g^{0}=g
, so that (
3.15
) trivially holds in this case.

3.3.
Tangential maximal functions

For any exponent
\beta\in(0,1]
, and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we recall the tangential approach region

\Gamma^{\beta}(x_{0})=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}:\left|x-x_{0}\right|<t^{\beta}\textup{ if }0<t\leq 1,\textup{ and }\left|x-x_{0}\right|<t\textup{ if }t\geq 1\right\}.

For
\alpha\geq 0
,
\beta\in(0,1]
,
p\in[1,\infty]
and
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, consider the maximal function

(3.16)

\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\beta}(g)(x_{0})=\sup_{(t,x)\in\Gamma^{\beta}(x_{0})}\left|\left(P_{t}\ast\mathscr{J}_{\alpha}g\right)(x)\right|,\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Theorem
1.2
states that
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\beta}
is bounded on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
provided that
p\in(1,\infty)
,
\alpha p<n
and
\beta\geq 1-\frac{\alpha p}{n}
. As mentioned in the introduction, if
\alpha=\frac{n}{p}
then
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\beta}
is bounded on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
\beta>0
.

For any
p\in(0,\infty)
and
u:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
, following
[
39
, Section 4]
we also consider the local maximal function

\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta}(u)(x_{0})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t,x)\in\Gamma^{\beta}(x_{0})\\
0<t\leq 1\end{subarray}}t^{\frac{n(1-\beta)}{p}}\left|u(t,x)\right|,\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This corresponds to the maximal function
M_{p}(u)(x_{0})
for the domain
\Omega=\Gamma^{\beta}(0)
in the notation of
[
39
, Section 3]
.
The factor
t^{\frac{n(1-\beta)}{p}}
mitigates the growth of
\left|u(t,x)\right|
as
(t,x)
approaches
(0,x_{0})
within
\Gamma^{\beta}(x_{0})
, allowing the maximal function
\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta}(u)
to essentially be dominated by the standard nontangential maximal function
N_{*}(u)
. In fact, as a special case of
[
39
, Theorem 4
′
]
it holds that

(3.17)

\left\lVert\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta}(u)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert N_{*}(u)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Note that the continuity assumption made on
u
in the aforementioned reference is unnecessary.

The final lemma that we shall need for the proof of Theorem
1.4
is the following analogue of
[
39
, Lemma 11]
. Here we have to consider some
\mathrm{L}^{q}
averages of the function
f
instead of working directly with the Poisson extension
u(t,x)=(P_{t}\ast f)(x)
as in the aforementioned reference.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
1\leq q<p<\infty
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let
v_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}\to[0,\infty]
be defined as

v_{f}(t,x)=\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{\Delta(x,2t)}\left|f\right|^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

For integers
j\geq 0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, consider the maximal function

(3.18)

\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta,j}(v_{f})(x_{0})=2^{\frac{nj}{p}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left|x-x_{0}\right|<t^{\beta}\\
0<t<2^{-j/(1-\beta)}\end{subarray}}t^{\frac{n(1-\beta)}{p}}\left|v_{f}(2^{j}t,x)\right|,\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then,
\left\lVert\mathcal{M}_{p,\beta,j}(v_{f})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}
, where the implicit constant does not depend on
j
.

Proof.

Observe that
N_{*}(v_{f})(x_{0})\lesssim(M_{q}f)(x_{0})
for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and therefore

\left\lVert N_{*}(v_{f})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\lesssim\left\lVert M_{q}f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}

for all
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, since
p>q
. Moreover, the dilations
f_{\delta}(x)=f(\delta x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, have the property that

Remark 3.11
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
be any subset (typically an approach region with
(0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
as an accumulation point). For any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let us denote

\Gamma+x_{0}=\left\{(t,x)+(0,x_{0}):(t,x)\in\Gamma\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}.

For

, consider (with a slight abuse of notation) the corresponding maximal function

N_{*,\Gamma}u(x_{0})=\sup_{(t,x)\in\Gamma+x_{0}}\left|u(t,x)\right|,\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

If there are

and

such that
\left|u(t,x)\right|\leq(G\ast\left|v(t,\cdot)\right|)(x)

Indeed, if
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
(t,x)\in\Gamma+x_{0}
, then
(t,x-z)\in\Gamma+(x_{0}-z)

\displaystyle\left|u(t,x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}G(z)\left|v(t,x-z)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}G(z)(N_{*,\Gamma}v)(x_{0}-z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=(G\ast N_{*,\Gamma}v)(x_{0}).

We now have all the ingredients needed for the proof of Theorem

Proof.

It follows from

that
\left|f(y)\right|\lesssim(I_{\alpha}\ast S_{\alpha}f)(y)
for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Combining this with
[
16
, Lemma 3]
shows that

(3.31)

Mf(x)\lesssim(I_{\alpha}\ast S_{\alpha}f)(x)

for
all

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n}{p})
, then (the first part of the proof of) Lemma
2.1
shows that

\left\lVert Mf\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim\left\lVert I_{\alpha}\ast S_{\alpha}f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim\left\lVert S_{\alpha}f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

If
\alpha=\frac{n}{p}
, then
s>0
and we may choose
\gamma\in(0,\frac{n}{p})
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\alpha=\gamma+\varepsilon
and

. Lemma
3.13
shows that there exists

such that

and

. It follows from Lemma

that

Mf(x)\leq(G_{\varepsilon}\ast Mg)(x)\lesssim(I_{\gamma}\ast G_{\varepsilon}\ast S_{\gamma}g)(x)

for all

(3.32)

\left\lVert Mf\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim\left\lVert G_{\varepsilon}\ast S_{\gamma}g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\left\lVert S_{\gamma}g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\eqsim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.\qed

The previous lemma can be combined with a well-known approximation argument to obtain the following result. We spell out the details in the interests of clarity.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
,
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n}{p}]
and
f\in\mathrm{F}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. There exists a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{\Delta(x,r)}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
exists for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E
, and
\mu(E)=0
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
s>n-\alpha p
.

Proof.

For
R>0
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, consider the maximal function

D_{R}f(x)=\sup_{0<r<R}\left|\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta(x,R)}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta(x,r)}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right|

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The operator
D_{R}
has the following elementary properties:

(i)

D_{R}(f+g)(x)\leq D_{R}f(x)+D_{R}g(x)
for all
f,g\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
R>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(ii)

D_{R}f(x)\leq 2Mf(x)
for all
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
R>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(iii)

If
g
is uniformly continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that
D_{R}g(x)<\varepsilon
for all
R\in(0,\delta)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda>0
be fixed. Since
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
\mathrm{F}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see
[
16
, Proposition 1]
), there exists
g\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\lambda
. Let
h=f-g\in\mathrm{F}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and note that
D_{R}f(x)\leq 2Mh(x)+D_{R}g(x)
for all
R>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by properties (i) and (ii). Since
g
is uniformly continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, property (iii) provides some
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,g)>0
such that
\sup_{0<R<\delta}D_{R}g(x)<\varepsilon/2
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It follows that

(3.33)

\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sup_{0<R<\delta}D_{R}f(x)>\varepsilon\right\}\subseteq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Mh(x)>\varepsilon/4\right\}.

Observe that the map
\eta\mapsto\sup_{0<R<\eta}D_{R}f(x)
is non-decreasing for all fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Consequently, if
s>n-\alpha p
and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then (
3.33
) and Lemma
3.14
show that

\mu\left(\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\sup_{0<R<\eta}D_{R}f(x)>\varepsilon\right\}\right)\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\left\lVert Mh\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\lambda\varepsilon^{-1}.

Since
\lambda>0
was arbitrary, the set
E_{\varepsilon}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\sup_{0<R<\eta}D_{R}f(x)>\varepsilon\right\}
satisfies
\mu(E_{\varepsilon})=0
. Let
E=\cup_{k\geq 1}E_{1/k}
. Then
\mu(E)=0
and
\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\sup_{0<R<\delta}D_{R}f(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E
, i.e.
\left\{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta(x,r)}f\right\}_{0<r<1}
is a Cauchy net as
r\to 0^{+}
.
∎

Let

\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}|f(y)-P_{\Delta}^{k}f(y)|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\lesssim r^{\alpha}\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}f_{\alpha}^{\sharp}(y)^{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right)^{1/q}.

Finally, we make the following important remark about the decomposition of a function in the space
\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as the difference of two non-negative functions. As we shall see momentarily, this allows matters to be reduced to nonnegative

Remark 3.18
.

Let
\alpha>0
,

and

. There are

and

shows that there is

, where

with

\left\lVert f_{\pm}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{C}^{p}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.

r
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
. If
Q\in\partial\Omega
(not to be confused with our use of
Q
for a cube in earlier sections) then we let
\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r)=B(Q,r)\cap\partial\Omega
be the corresponding surface ball.

We say that
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain if every point on the boundary
\partial\Omega
has a neighbourhood on which, in new coordinates obtained from the canonical ones by a rigid motion,
\Omega
coincides with the epigraph of a Lipschitz function. More precisely, we assume that there is
M>0
(referred to as the Lipschitz constant of
\Omega
) such that for all
P\in\partial\Omega
there exist

(i)

a real number
r_{P}>0
;

(ii)

a Lipschitz function
\varphi_{P}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with Lipschitz constant (at most)
M
, such that
\varphi_{P}(0)=0
;

(iii)

an isometry
T_{P}:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
with
T_{P}(0)=P
,

such that the special Lipschitz domain
\Omega_{P}=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:t>\varphi_{P}(x)\right\}
and the cylinder

R_{P}=R(r_{P})=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:\left|x\right|<r_{P}\textup{ and }\left|t\right|<(1+M)r_{P}\right\}

are such that

\Omega\cap T_{P}(R_{P})=T_{P}(\Omega_{P})\cap T_{P}(R_{P}).

It follows that

(4.1)

\partial{\Omega}\cap T_{P}(R_{P})=T_{P}(\partial\Omega_{P})\cap T_{P}(R_{P})

for all
P\in\partial\Omega
, and a local parametrisation of
\partial\Omega
around
P
is given by

(4.2)

\Phi_{P}:\Delta(0,r_{P})\to\partial{\Omega}\cap T_{P}(R_{P}):x\mapsto T_{P}\left((\varphi_{P}(x),x)\right).

Its inverse is given by

\Phi_{P}^{-1}:\partial\Omega\cap T_{P}(R_{P})\to\Delta(0,r_{P}):X\to\Pi(T_{P}^{-1}(X)),

where
\Pi:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:(t,x)\mapsto x
.

More generally, we say that such a domain
\Omega
is of class
C^{k,1}
for an integer
k\geq 0
if the functions
\varphi_{P}
can additionally be chosen to be of class
C^{k,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, i.e. with Lipschitz continuous partial derivatives of order
k
, for all
P\in\partial\Omega
.

Remark 4.1
.

Note that if
Q\in\partial\Omega\cap T_{P}(R_{P})=T_{P}(\partial\Omega_{P})\cap T_{P}(R_{P})
, then there exists
x\in\Delta(0,r_{P})
such that
\tilde{Q}=(\varphi_{P}(x),x)\in\partial\Omega_{P}\cap R_{P}
and
Q=T_{P}(\tilde{Q})
. Since
\varphi_{P}(0)=0
and
T_{P}
is an isometry, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|Q-P\right|

\displaystyle=|T_{P}(\tilde{Q})-T_{P}(0)|=|\tilde{Q}|=\left(\left|\varphi_{P}(x)\right|^{2}+\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq(M^{2}+1)^{1/2}\left|x\right|<(1+M)r_{P}.

The boundary
\partial\Omega
is equipped with the subspace (metric) topology and the surface measure
\sigma=\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{\partial\Omega}
, where
\mathcal{H}^{n}
denotes the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
.

Let us henceforth assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
is a bounded (at least) Lipschitz domain. For any Borel subset
A\subseteq\partial\Omega\cap T_{P}(R_{P})
, it follows from the area formula (see, e.g.,
[
18
, Sections 3.3.2 and 3.3.4 (c)]
) and the bijectivity of
\Phi_{P}
that

(4.3)

\sigma(A)=\int_{\Delta(0,r_{P})}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\left(\Phi_{P}(x)\right)\left(1+\left|\nabla\varphi_{P}(x)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Consequently, for any non-negative Borel measurable function
f:\partial\Omega\to[0,\infty]
with support in
\partial\Omega\cap T_{P}(R_{P})
, it follows from classical measure-theoretic arguments that

(4.4)

\int_{\partial\Omega}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{\Delta(0,r_{P})}f\left(\Phi_{P}(x)\right)\left(1+\left|\nabla\varphi_{P}(x)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

In particular, this shows that
\sigma
is
n
-Ahlfors–David regular, i.e. that

(4.5)

\sigma(\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r))\eqsim r^{n}\quad\textup{ for all }r\in(0,\textup{diam}(\Omega)].

4.2.
The
L
-harmonic measure in Lipschitz domains

Recall the definition of the elliptic operator
L\!=\!-\textup{div}{(A\nabla)}
on
\Omega
introduced in Section
1.2
. For all
{f\in C^{0}(\partial\Omega)}
there is a unique
u_{f}\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{\textup{loc}}(\Omega)\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
Lu_{f}=0
and
u_{f}|_{\partial\Omega}=f
(see
[
31
, Chapter 1, Section 2]
). By the maximum principle and the Riesz representation theorem there exists a family
\left\{\omega^{X}_{L}\right\}_{X\in\Omega}
of regular probability measures on
\partial\Omega
such that

(4.6)

u_{f}(X)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}_{L}

for all
X\in\Omega
.
In accordance with
[
31
]
, we say that
(\mathcal{D})_{p,\Omega}^{L}

holds
if for all
f\in C^{0}(\partial\Omega)
, the classical solution
u_{f}
in (
4.6
) satisfies the estimate

\left\lVert N_{*}u_{f}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}.

As stated in the introduction, this implies the well-posedness of
(\mathcal{D})^{L}_{p,\Omega}
(see
[
31
, Theorem 1.7.7]
).

We shall now fix a point
X_{0}\in\Omega
, and refer to
\omega_{L}=\omega_{L}^{X_{0}}
as the
L
-harmonic measure
.
The following theorem is well-known (see
[
31
, Theorem 1.7.3]
). It is also well-known that the symmetry assumption on the coefficients made therein is not required (see, e.g.,
[
30
]
).

Theorem 4.2
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

\omega_{L}\in A_{\infty}(\sigma)
;

(ii)

There exists
p_{0}\in(1,\infty)
such that
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p_{0}}
holds;

(iii)

There exists
q_{0}\in(1,\infty)
such that
\omega_{L}\in B^{q_{0}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma)
, i.e.
\omega_{L}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\sigma
, and the Radon–Nikodym derivative
k=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega_{L}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma}\in\mathrm{L}^{q_{0}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma)
satisfies the reverse Hölder inequality

\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}k^{q_{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/q_{0}}\lesssim\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.58105pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.95024pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.3545pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.1863pt}}\!\int_{\Delta}k\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma,

for all surface balls
\Delta\subseteq\partial\Omega
. Moreover, it holds that
\frac{1}{q_{0}}+\frac{1}{p_{0}}=1
in
(ii)
and
(iii)
.

4.3.
Sobolev spaces on the boundary of a Lipschitz domain

We recall the following two elementary lemmas from the theory of Sobolev spaces (see
[
23
, Chapter 1]
or
[
36
, Chapter 3]
).
If
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
k
is a non-negative integer, we denote by
C^{k,1}(E)
the space of all functions on
E
which are restrictions to
E
of functions of class
C^{k,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
be two bounded open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
\psi:\overline{U_{1}}\to\overline{U_{2}}
be a bi-Lipschitz bijection. Let
k\geq 0
be an integer, and let
0\leq s\leq k+1
. If
\psi
and
\psi^{-1}
are both of class
C^{k,1}
, then
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U_{2})
if and only if
f\circ\psi\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U_{1})
, and in this case
\left\lVert f\circ\psi\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U_{1})}\eqsim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U_{2})}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
U
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
k\geq 0
be an integer, and let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{k,1}(\overline{U};\mathbb{R})
. If
0\leq s\leq k+1
, then
\varphi f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U)
for all
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U)
, and
{\left\lVert\varphi f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U)}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U)}}
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer,
p\in[1,\infty)
and let
0\leq s\leq k+1
. Let
\Omega
be a domain of class
C^{k,1}
.
Following
[
23
, Definition 1.3.3.2]
, the Sobolev space
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
is defined as the space of functions
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma)
such that
f\circ\Phi_{P}\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{P}))
for all
P\in\partial\Omega
and corresponding local parametrisations
\Phi_{P}
of
\partial\Omega
defined in (
4.2
). Lemma
4.3
shows that this space is well-defined.
To equip
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
with a norm we use the compactness of
\partial\Omega
to find an integer
N\geq 1
and
\left\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{N}\right\}\subset\partial\Omega
such that
\partial\Omega\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}T_{P_{i}}(R_{P_{i}})
. We then let

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert f\circ\Phi_{P_{i}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{P_{i}}))}^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

It follows again from Lemma
4.3
that any other such finite covering of
\partial\Omega
will yield an equivalent norm on
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
. Moreover, when
s=0
, the formula (
4.3
) shows that this norm is equivalent to the usual norm on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma)
.

In the same way, if
\Omega
is a domain of class
C^{k,1}
for some integer
k\geq 0
, and
0\leq l\leq k
is an integer, we define the function space
C^{l,1}(\partial\Omega)
as the space of continuous functions
f:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f\circ\Phi_{P}\in C^{l,1}(\Delta(0,r_{P}))
for all
P\in\partial\Omega
. The following elementary lemma, whose proof is left to the reader, justifies that this is a well-defined function space, and shows that it embeds into the Sobolev spaces
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
for suitable
s\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
be two bounded open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
\psi:\overline{U_{1}}\to\overline{U_{2}}
be a bi-Lipschitz bijection. Let
0\leq l\leq k
be integers. If
\psi
and
\psi^{-1}
are both of class
C^{k,1}
, then

(i)

If
f,g\in C^{l,1}(U_{2})
, then
fg\in C^{l,1}(U_{2})
;

(ii)

f\in C^{l,1}(U_{2})
if and only if
f\circ\psi\in C^{l,1}(U_{1})
;

(iii)

If
f\in C^{l,1}(U_{2})
,
1\leq p<\infty
and
0\leq s\leq l+1
, then
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(U_{2})
.

We note that our definition of
C^{0,1}(\partial\Omega)
coincides with the classical space of Lipschitz functions on
\partial\Omega
. The reader can refer to
[
21
, Section 6.2]
for more details and some properties of these spaces.
We shall need the following density result.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
be a bounded domain of class
C^{k,1}
. If
1\leq p<\infty
and
0\leq s\leq k+1
, then
C^{k,1}(\partial\Omega)
is a dense subspace of
\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
.

Proof.

By compactness of
\partial\Omega
, there are

(i)

An integer
N\geq 1
, numbers
r_{1},\ldots,r_{N}>0
and
M>0
;

(ii)

Points
\left\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{N}\right\}\subset\partial\Omega
;

(iii)

Functions
\left\{\varphi_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
of class
C^{k,1}
, such that
\varphi_{j}(0)=0
,
\left|\varphi_{j}(x)-\varphi_{j}(y)\right|\leq M\left|x-y\right|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
;

(iv)

Isometries
\left\{T_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
such that
T_{j}(0)=P_{j}
for all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
;

(v)

Special Lipschitz domains
\Omega_{j}=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:t>\varphi_{j}(x)\right\}
;

(vi)

Cylinders
R_{j}=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:\left|x\right|<r_{j}\textup{ and }\left|t\right|<(1+M)r_{j}\right\}
,

such that

\partial\Omega\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}T_{j}(R_{j}),

and

\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j})=T_{j}(\Omega_{j})\cap T_{j}(R_{j})

for all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
.
Let

\Phi_{j}:\Delta(0,r_{j})\to\partial\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j}):x\mapsto T_{j}((\varphi_{j}(x),x))

denote the corresponding local parametrisations of
\partial\Omega
for
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
.

Let
(\eta_{j})_{1\leq j\leq N}
be a smooth partition of unity for this covering, i.e.
\eta_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n},[0,1])
with
\textup{supp}\left(\eta_{j}\right)\subseteq T_{j}(R_{j})
for all
1\leq j\leq N
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}=1
on a neighbourhood of
\partial\Omega
.
Let
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
and
\varepsilon>0
. By definition, it holds that
f\circ\Phi_{j}\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{j}))
for all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
, hence there exist
\tilde{g}_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Delta(0,r_{j})})
(i.e. the restriction to
\overline{\Delta(0,r_{j})}
of a function in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
) such that
\left\lVert f\circ\Phi_{j}-\tilde{g}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{j}))}<\varepsilon
for all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
(this is because
\Delta(0,r_{j})
is a domain with a continuous boundary; see, e.g.,
[
23
, Theorem 1.4.2.1]
).

For each
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
, let
\chi_{j}=\eta_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
denote the restriction of
\eta_{j}
to
\partial\Omega
, and note that
\textup{supp}\left(\chi_{j}\right)\subseteq\partial\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j})
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\chi_{j}=1
. We also let
g_{j}=\tilde{g}_{j}\circ\Phi_{j}^{-1}:\partial\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j})\to\mathbb{R}
for all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
and define
g=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\chi_{j}g_{j}:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, where it is understood that

(\chi_{j}g_{j})(P)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\chi_{j}(P)g_{j}(P),&\textup{if }P\in\partial\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j}),\\
0,&\textup{if }P\in\partial\Omega\setminus(\partial\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j})).\end{array}\right.

We claim that
\chi_{j}g_{j}\in C^{k,1}(\partial\Omega)
for all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
, and hence that
g\in C^{k,1}(\partial\Omega)
. Let
i,j\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
be fixed. The function
(\chi_{j}g_{j})\circ\Phi_{i}
is only non-zero on the open set
\Phi_{i}^{-1}(\partial\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j}))\subseteq\Delta(0,r_{i})
, and on this set it holds that

\displaystyle(\chi_{j}g_{j})\circ\Phi_{i}=(\eta_{j}\circ\Phi_{i})(\tilde{g}_{j}\circ(\Phi_{j}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{i})).

By assumption, the map

(4.7)

\Phi_{j}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{i}|_{\Delta(0,r_{i})\cap\Phi_{i}^{-1}(\partial\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j}))}:\Delta(0,r_{i})\cap\Phi_{i}^{-1}(\partial\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j}))\to\Delta(0,r_{j})\cap\Phi_{j}^{-1}(\partial\Omega\cap T_{i}(R_{i}))

and its inverse are of class
C^{k,1}
. Since
\tilde{g}_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Delta(0,r_{j})})
and
\eta_{j}\circ\Phi_{i}\in C^{k,1}(\Delta(0,r_{i}))
, it follows from Lemma
4.5
that
(\chi_{j}g_{j})\circ\Phi_{i}\in C^{k,1}(\Delta(0,r_{i}))
, and this proves that
g\in C^{k,1}(\partial\Omega)\subseteq\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
.

Since
f-g=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\chi_{j}f-\chi_{j}g_{j})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\lVert f\chi_{j}-\chi_{j}g_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\eqsim\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert(f\chi_{j})\circ\Phi_{i}-(\chi_{j}g_{j})\circ\Phi_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{i}))}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert(f\chi_{j})\!\circ\!\Phi_{j}\!\circ\!\Phi_{j}^{-1}\!\circ\!\Phi_{i}\!-(\chi_{j}g_{j})\!\circ\!\Phi_{j}\!\circ\!\Phi_{j}^{-1}\!\circ\!\Phi_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{i})\cap\Phi_{i}^{-1}(\partial\Omega\cap T_{j}(R_{j})))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert(f\chi_{j})\circ\Phi_{j}-(\chi_{j}g_{j})\circ\Phi_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{j})\cap\Phi_{j}^{-1}(\partial\Omega\cap T_{i}(R_{i})))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\lVert(f\chi_{j})\circ\Phi_{j}-(\chi_{j}g_{j})\circ\Phi_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{j}))}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\lVert(\chi_{j}\circ\Phi_{j})\cdot(f-g_{j})\circ\Phi_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{j}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\lVert f\circ\Phi_{j}-\tilde{g}_{j}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\Delta(0,r_{j}))}\lesssim\varepsilon,

where in the fourth line we have used Lemma
4.3
and the fact that the map in (
4.7
) and its inverse are of class
C^{k,1}
, and in the last line we have used Lemma
4.4
and the fact that
\chi_{j}\circ\Phi_{j}\in C^{k,1}(\overline{\Delta(0,r_{j})})
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, this concludes the proof.
∎

4.4.
Corkscrew points and geometry in Lipschitz domains

Since
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, it satisfies a corkscrew condition. This means that there is a (small) constant
c_{\Omega}>0
such that for all
t\in(0,c_{\Omega})
and
Q\in\partial\Omega
, there exists (at least) one corresponding
corkscrew point

A_{t}(Q)\in\Omega
with the property that

\textup{dist}(A_{t}(Q),\partial\Omega)\eqsim\left|A_{t}(Q)-Q\right|\eqsim t.

We first give an explicit description of corkscrew points in special Lipschitz domains. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz with constant
M>0
, and let
\Omega_{\varphi}=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:t>\varphi(x)\right\}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
. If
Q_{0}=(\varphi(x_{0}),x_{0})\in\partial\Omega_{\varphi}
, then
A_{t}(Q_{0})=(\varphi(x_{0})+t,x_{0})\in\Omega_{\varphi}
is a corkscrew point for
Q_{0}
.

Proof.

It clearly suffices to prove that
\textup{dist}(A_{t}(Q_{0}),\partial\Omega_{\varphi})\gtrsim t
. We first treat the case when the Lipschitz constant
M
of
\varphi
satisfies
M\leq 1
. Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
it holds that

\displaystyle\left|(\varphi(x_{0})+t,x_{0})-(\varphi(x),x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left(\left|\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(x)+t\right|^{2}+\left|x-x_{0}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(x)+t\right|+\left|x-x_{0}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(t-\left|\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(x)\right|+\left|x-x_{0}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(t+\left(M^{-1}-1\right)\left|\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(x)\right|\right)\geq\frac{t}{2},

hence
\textup{dist}(A_{t}(Q_{0}),\partial\Omega_{\varphi})\geq\frac{t}{2}
. We now assume that
M>1
. First, if
\left|x-x_{0}\right|<\frac{t}{2(M-1)}
, then

\displaystyle\left|(\varphi(x_{0})+t,x_{0})-(\varphi(x),x)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(t-\left|\varphi(x_{0})-\varphi(x)\right|+\left|x-x_{0}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(t+(1-M)\left|x-x_{0}\right|\right)\geq\frac{t}{4}.

On the other hand, if
\left|x-x_{0}\right|\geq\frac{t}{2(M-1)}
, then we simply have

\left|(\varphi(x_{0})+t,x_{0})-(\varphi(x),x)\right|\geq\left|x-x_{0}\right|\geq\frac{t}{2(M-1)},

hence
\textup{dist}(A_{t}(Q_{0}),\partial\Omega_{\varphi})\geq t\min\left\{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2(M-1)}\right\}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

In the rest of this section, we assume that
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain with Lipschitz constant
M>0
.
The following lemma provides explicit corkscrew points in coordinate cylinders.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
P\in\partial\Omega
and
r>0
be such that

\Omega\cap T_{P}(R(C_{M}r))=T_{P}(\Omega_{P})\cap T_{P}(R(C_{M}r))

for
C_{M}=10(1+M)
.
Let
Q\in T_{P}(R(r))\cap\partial\Omega=T_{P}(R(r))\cap T_{P}(\partial\Omega_{P})
, and let
x\in\Delta(0,r)
such that
Q=\Phi_{P}(x)=T_{P}((\varphi_{P}(x),x))
in (
4.2
).
If
0<t\leq 3(1+M)r
, then
A_{t}(Q)=T_{P}((\varphi_{P}(x)+t,x))
is a corkscrew point for
Q
.

Proof.

Observe that
\left|Q-P\right|<(1+M)r
by Remark
4.1
.
Similarly, if
Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega\setminus T_{P}(R(C_{M}r))
, then
\left|P-Q_{0}\right|\geq C_{M}r
, and therefore
\left|Q-Q_{0}\right|\geq 9(1+M)r
. Let
0<t\leq 3(1+M)r
. Note that
A_{t}(Q)\in T_{P}(R(C_{M}r))
and therefore
A_{t}(Q)\in\Omega
. Observe that

\displaystyle\left|Q_{0}-A_{t}(Q)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|Q_{0}-Q\right|-\left|Q-A_{t}(Q)\right|\geq 9(1+M)r-t\geq 2t.

Since
Q\in T_{P}(R(r))\cap\partial\Omega
and
\left|Q-A_{t}(Q)\right|=t<2t
, we have that

\displaystyle\textup{dist}(A_{t}(Q),\partial\Omega)

\displaystyle=\inf_{Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega}\left|A_{t}(Q)-Q_{0}\right|=\inf_{Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega\cap T_{P}(R(C_{M}r))}\left|A_{t}(Q)-Q_{0}\right|

\displaystyle=\inf_{Q_{0}\in T_{P}(\partial\Omega_{P})\cap T_{P}(R(C_{M}r))}\left|A_{t}(Q)-Q_{0}\right|=\inf_{P_{0}\in\partial\Omega_{P}\cap R(C_{M}r)}\left|T_{P}^{-1}(A_{t}(Q))-P_{0}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\textup{dist}(T_{P}^{-1}(A_{t}(Q)),\partial\Omega_{P})\gtrsim t,

where we have used Lemma
4.7
to obtain the last equivalence.
∎

The following lemma will allow us to localise the study of the approach regions
\Gamma^{\beta,c}_{\Omega}(Q_{0})
. Note that equality actually holds in (
4.8
) below, but we shall only need the stated inclusion.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
P\in\partial\Omega
and
r>0
be such that

\Omega\cap T_{P}(R(C_{M}r))=T_{P}(\Omega_{P})\cap T_{P}(R(C_{M}r))

for
C_{M}=10(1+M)
. For all
Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega
,
\beta\in(0,1]
and
c>0
it holds that

(4.8)

T_{P}^{-1}(\Gamma_{\Omega}^{\beta,c}(Q_{0})\cap T_{P}(R(r)))\subseteq\Gamma_{\Omega_{P}}^{\beta,c}(T_{P}^{-1}(Q_{0}))\cap R(r).

Proof.

Let
X\in\Gamma_{\Omega}^{\beta,c}(Q_{0})\cap T_{P}(R(r))
. Since
T_{p}
is an isometry, it holds that

\displaystyle\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)

\displaystyle=\textup{dist}(T_{P}^{-1}(X),T_{P}^{-1}(\partial\Omega))=\inf_{Y\in T_{P}^{-1}(\partial\Omega)}\left|T_{P}^{-1}(X)-Y\right|

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{Y\in T_{P}^{-1}(\partial\Omega\cap T_{P}(R(C_{M}r)))}\left|T_{P}^{-1}(X)-Y\right|=\inf_{Y\in\partial\Omega_{P}\cap R(C_{M}r)}\left|T_{P}^{-1}(X)-Y\right|,

where in the last equality we have used the assumption to deduce the property corresponding to (
4.1
).
By assumption it holds that
T_{P}^{-1}(X)\in R(r)
, and therefore
\left|T_{P}^{-1}(X)\right|<(2+M)r
.
If
Y\in\partial\Omega_{P}\setminus R(C_{M}r)
, then
\left|Y\right|\geq C_{M}r
, and therefore
\left|T_{P}^{-1}(X)-Y\right|\geq\left|Y\right|-\left|T_{P}^{-1}(X)\right|>8(1+M)r
. Since
0\in\partial\Omega_{P}\cap R(C_{M}r)
and
\left|T_{P}^{-1}(X)-0\right|<(2+M)r<8(1+M)r
, it follows that

\inf_{Y\in\partial\Omega_{P}\cap R(C_{M}r)}\left|T_{P}^{-1}(X)-Y\right|=\inf_{Y\in\partial\Omega_{P}}\left|T_{P}^{-1}(X)-Y\right|.

This shows that
\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)\leq\textup{dist}(T_{P}^{-1}(X),\partial\Omega_{P})
. From this and the fact that
\beta\in(0,1]
it easily follows that
T_{P}^{-1}(X)\in\Gamma_{\Omega_{P}}^{\beta,c}(T_{p}^{-1}(Q_{0}))
.
∎

4.5.
Pointwise estimates on solutions

In this section we fix
p_{0}\in(1,\infty)
such that
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p_{0}}
holds. It follows from Theorem
4.2
that
\omega_{L}\in B^{q_{0}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma)
, and we let
k=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega_{L}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma}\in\mathrm{L}^{q_{0}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma)
denote the corresponding Radon–Nikodym derivative.

We need to introduce some notation for the proofs of the following lemmas. For any
X\in\Omega
, the measures
\omega^{X}_{L}
and
\omega_{L}
are mutually absolutely continuous (see, e.g.,
[
31
, Lemma 1.2.7]
). In accordance with
[
31
, Definition 1.3.10]
, we denote by
K(X,Q)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}_{L}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega_{L}}(Q)
the corresponding Radon–Nikodym derivative.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p_{0}}(\partial\Omega)
and
u(X)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}_{L}
for
X\in\Omega
. There is
\alpha_{L}>0
depending only on
n
and
\lambda
such that for all
Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega
,
t\in(0,c_{\Omega})
and corresponding corkscrew point
A_{t}(Q_{0})\in\Omega
it holds that

\left|u(A_{t}(Q_{0}))\right|\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-\alpha_{L}j}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma(\Delta_{j})}\int_{D_{j}}\left|f\right|^{p_{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/p_{0}},

where
\Delta_{j}=\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q_{0},2^{j+1}t)
for all
j\geq 0
,
D_{j}=\Delta_{j}\setminus\Delta_{j-1}
for
j\geq 1
, and
D_{0}=\Delta_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
j\geq 1
. It follows from
[
31
, Lemma 1.3.12]
that

\textup{ess sup}_{P\in D_{j}}K(A_{t}(Q_{0}),P)\lesssim 2^{-\alpha_{L}j}\omega(\Delta_{j})^{-1}

where
\alpha_{L}>0
depends only on
n
and
\lambda
. By a similar and simpler reasoning, this also holds for
j=0
.
The essential supremum in this estimate is understood with respect to the
L
-harmonic measure
\omega_{L}
. Since
\omega_{L}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the surface measure
\sigma
, the essential supremum can be taken equivalently with respect to
\sigma
. As a consequence, if we let
X=A_{t}(Q_{0})
, then we can estimate

\displaystyle\left|u(X)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|f\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}_{L}=\int_{\partial\Omega}K(X,P)k(P)\left|f(P)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma(P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{D_{j}}K(X,P)k(P)\left|f(P)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma(P)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-\alpha_{L}j}\omega_{L}(\Delta_{j})^{-1}\left(\int_{D_{j}}\left|f\right|^{p_{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/p_{0}}\left(\int_{\Delta_{j}}k^{q_{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/q_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-\alpha_{L}j}\sigma(\Delta_{j})\omega_{L}(\Delta_{j})^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma(\Delta_{j})}\int_{D_{j}}\left|f\right|^{p_{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/p_{0}}\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta_{j}}k^{q_{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/q_{0}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-\alpha_{L}j}\omega_{L}(\Delta_{j})^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma(\Delta_{j})}\int_{D_{j}}\left|f\right|^{p_{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/p_{0}}\left(\int_{\Delta_{j}}k\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-\alpha_{L}j}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma(\Delta_{j})}\int_{D_{j}}\left|f\right|^{p_{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/p_{0}}.\qed

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega
and
r_{0}>0
. If
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p_{0}}(\partial\Omega)
is such that
\textup{supp}\left(f\right)\subseteq\partial\Omega\setminus\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q_{0},r_{0})
and
u(X)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}
, then
\left|u(X)\right|\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p_{0}}(\partial\Omega)}
for all
X\in\Omega\cap B(Q_{0},r_{0}/2)
.

Proof.

The argument of the proof of
[
31
, Lemma 1.3.13]
shows that there are constants
C>0
(depending on
r_{0}
) and
\alpha>0
(depending only on
n
and
\lambda
) such that

K(X,P)\leq C\left(\frac{\left|X-Q_{0}\right|}{r_{0}}\right)^{\alpha}\leq C

for all
P\in\partial\Omega\setminus\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q_{0},r_{0})
and
X\in\Omega\cap B(Q_{0},r_{0}/2)
. As a consequence, for these values of
X
, we can estimate

\displaystyle\left|u(X)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q_{0},r_{0})}K(X,P)k(P)\left|f(P)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma(P)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\partial\Omega}k(P)\left|f(P)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma(P)\leq\left\lVert k\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{q_{0}}(\partial\Omega)}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p_{0}}(\partial\Omega)}.\qed

4.6.
Tangential approach regions in Lipschitz domains

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
be a Lipschitz domain. For
Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega
,
\beta\in(0,1]
and
c>0
, we recall that the tangential approach region
\Gamma^{\beta,c}_{\Omega}(Q_{0})
is given by

\Gamma^{\beta,c}_{\Omega}(Q_{0})=\left\{X\in\Omega:\left|X-X_{0}\right|<\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1+c)\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)^{\beta}&\textup{if }\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)\leq 1;\\
(1+c)\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)&\textup{if }\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)\geq 1.\end{array}\right.\quad\right\},

and the corresponding approach regions in the upper half-space are given for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\Gamma^{\beta}_{c}(x_{0})=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}:\left|x-x_{0}\right|<ct^{\beta}\textup{ if }t\in(0,1],\textup{ and }\left|x-x_{0}\right|<ct\textup{ if }t\geq 1\right\}.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function, and consider the associated special Lipschitz domain
\Omega_{\varphi}=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:t>\varphi(x)\right\}
.
The domain
\Omega_{\varphi}
can be transformed to the upper half-space
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
by the bi-Lipschitz mapping

F_{\varphi}:\Omega_{\varphi}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}:(t,x)\mapsto(t-\varphi(x),x),

with inverse

F_{\varphi}^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}\to\Omega_{\varphi}:(t,x)\mapsto(t+\varphi(x),x).

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
Q_{0}=(\varphi(x_{0}),x_{0})\in\partial\Omega_{\varphi}
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For all
\beta\in(0,1]
and
c>0
it holds that
\Gamma_{\Omega_{\varphi}}^{\beta,c}(Q_{0})\subseteq F_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\Gamma^{\beta}_{1+c}(x_{0})\right)
.

Proof.

Note that

F_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\Gamma^{\beta}_{1+c}(x_{0})\right)=\left\{(t+\varphi(x),x):\left|x-x_{0}\right|<\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1+c)t^{\beta}&\textup{ if }t\in(0,1];\\
(1+c)t&\textup{ if }t\geq 1\end{array}\right.\right\}.

Let
X=(t,x)\in\Gamma_{\Omega_{\varphi}}^{\beta,c}(Q_{0})
. Since
(t,x)=(t-\varphi(x)+\varphi(x),x)
, it suffices to show that
\left|x-x_{0}\right|<(1+c)(t-\varphi(x))^{\beta}
if
0<t-\varphi(x)\leq 1
, and that
\left|x-x_{0}\right|<(1+c)(t-\varphi(x))
if
t-\varphi(x)\geq 1
. If
\textup{dist}((t,x),\partial\Omega_{\varphi})\leq 1
, then by definition of
\Gamma_{\Omega_{\varphi}}^{\beta,c}(Q_{0})
, we have

\displaystyle\left|x-x_{0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|(t,x)-(\varphi(x_{0}),x_{0})\right|<(1+c)\textup{dist}((t,x),\partial\Omega_{\varphi})^{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq(1+c)\left|(t,x)-(\varphi(x),x)\right|^{\beta}=(1+c)(t-\varphi(x))^{\beta}.

Similarly, if
\textup{dist}((t,x),\partial\Omega_{\varphi})\geq 1
, then
\left|x-x_{0}\right|<(1+c)(t-\varphi(x))
.
Observe that since
\beta\in(0,1]
, if
t-\varphi(x)\geq 1
then
(t-\varphi(x))^{\beta}\leq t-\varphi(x)
, and the converse inequality holds if
t-\varphi(x)\leq 1
. This concludes the proof.
∎

4.7.
First part of the proof of Theorem
1.7

We now have all the ingredients needed for the proof of the tangential maximal function estimate (
1.17
) and the corresponding
\sigma
-a.e. convergence (
1.18
) in Theorem
1.7
.

Before starting with the proof, let us say a few words about the essential idea of the argument. The first step is to use linearity to reduce matters to the case when
f
is supported in a small cylinder
R_{P}
on which the boundary
\partial\Omega
can be represented by a Lipschitz graph as in (
4.1
). Given a point
Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, we estimate the maximal function
N_{*,\beta}u_{f}(Q_{0})
by splitting the tangential approach region
\Gamma^{\beta}_{\Omega}(Q_{0})
into two parts - one that is bounded away from the boundary, and one that is close to the boundary and is denoted by
\Gamma^{\beta}_{\Omega,\textup{loc}}(Q_{0})
. The part that is bounded away from the boundary is dominated in a simple way by the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
norm of
f
using the estimate (
4.11
) below. To estimate the part of the maximal function
N_{*,\beta}u_{f}(Q_{0})
corresponding to the localised region
\Gamma^{\beta}_{\Omega,\textup{loc}}(Q_{0})
we treat two cases. If
Q_{0}
is far from the support of
f
, then the maximal function is estimated in a simple way using Lemma
4.11
. If
Q_{0}
is close to the support of
f
, then the situation is essentially reduced to that of the region above a Lipschitz graph. The boundary can be “flattened” using the mappings
F_{\varphi}
introduced in Section
4.6
, and Lemma
4.12
reduces matters to the situation of the upper half-space considered in Section
3
, and effectively to an application of Remark
3.19
.

Proof of (
1.17
) and (
1.18
) in Theorem
1.7
.

Let
k\geq 0
and let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of class
C^{k,1}
. Let
0\leq s\leq k+1
and let
p\in(1,\infty)
be such that
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p}
holds. The self-improvement property of reverse Hölder inequalities (see, e.g.,
[
29
, Theorem 2]
) and Theorem
4.2
show that there is
p_{0}\in(1,p)
such that
\left(\mathcal{D}\right)^{L}_{p_{0}}
holds. Suppose that
0<s\leq\frac{n}{p}
and let
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
. We let
\beta=1-\frac{sp}{n}\in(0,1)
if
s<\frac{n}{p}
, and we let
\beta\in(0,1]
be arbitrary if
s=\frac{n}{p}
.

Let
M>0
be the Lipschitz constant of
\Omega
. For every
P\in\partial\Omega
, following the definition from Section
4.1
, there is
r_{P}>0
such that

\Omega\cap T_{P}(R(r_{P}))=T_{P}(\Omega_{P})\cap T_{P}(R(r_{P})).

For any fixed constant
C_{M}\geq 1
, and letting
\tilde{r}_{P}=r_{P}/C_{M}
, this clearly implies that

\Omega\cap T_{P}(R(\tilde{r}_{P}))=T_{P}(\Omega_{P})\cap T_{P}(R(\tilde{r}_{P})).

By extracting a finite covering from the open cover
\partial\Omega\subseteq\bigcup_{P\in\partial\Omega}T_{P}(R(\tilde{r}_{P}/2))
, we obtain

(i)

An integer
N\geq 1
;

(ii)

Points
\left\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{N}\right\}\subset\partial\Omega
and real numbers
r_{1},\ldots,r_{N}>0
;

(iii)

Functions
\left\{\varphi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
of class
C^{k,1}
, such that
\varphi_{i}(0)=0

and
\left|\varphi_{i}(x)-\varphi_{i}(y)\right|\leq M\left|x-y\right|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
;

(iv)

Isometries
\left\{T_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
such that
T_{i}(0)=P_{i}
for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
;

(v)

Special Lipschitz domains
\Omega_{i}=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:t>\varphi_{i}(x)\right\}
,

such that

(4.9)

\partial\Omega\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}(R(r_{i}/2)),

and

(4.10)

\Omega\cap T_{i}(R(C_{M}r_{i}))=T_{i}(\Omega_{i})\cap T_{i}(R(C_{M}r_{i}))

for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
, where
C_{M}=20(1+M)^{3}
and we recall the notation for the cylinders

R(s)=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:\left|x\right|<s\textup{ and }\left|t\right|<(1+M)s\right\},\quad\textup{ for all }s>0.

Let
\Phi_{i}:\Delta(0,C_{M}r_{i})\to\partial\Omega\cap T_{i}(R(C_{M}r_{i}))
denote the corresponding local parametrisations of
\partial\Omega
for
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
(see (
4.2
)). Note that the functions
\Phi_{i}
can be naturally extended to functions of class
C^{k,1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by (iii) above.

To simplify the notation we shall only prove Theorem
1.7
for the regions
\Gamma^{\beta,c}_{\Omega}(Q_{0})
corresponding to
c=1/2
, but it will be clear that our argument works for any
c>0
. In doing so we will also require (the proof of) Theorem
3.12
for the regions
\Gamma_{a}^{\beta}(x_{0})
with general aperture
a>0
. As mentioned in the introduction the argument of the proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
3.12
applies equally well to these regions.

Let
r=\min_{i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}}r_{i}>0
. For
Q_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, we introduce the local approach regions

\Gamma_{\Omega,\textup{loc}}^{\beta}(Q_{0})=\left\{X\in\Omega:\left|X-Q_{0}\right|<(1+\tfrac{1}{2})\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)^{\beta}\textup{ and }\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)<\left(\tfrac{r}{12}(1+M)\right)^{1/\beta}\right\}.

Let
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a smooth partition of unity with respect to the covering (
4.9
), i.e.
\eta_{i}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n};[0,1])
are such that
\textup{supp}\left(\eta_{i}\right)\subseteq T_{i}(R(r_{i}/2))
for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}=1
in a neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
.
It follows from arguments similar to those used in the proof of Lemma
4.6
that
f_{i}:=f\eta_{i}|_{\partial\Omega}\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
with
\left\lVert f_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}
. Moreover,
\textup{supp}\left(f_{i}\right)\subseteq T_{i}(R(r_{i}/2))\cap\partial\Omega
for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
, and
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}
.
It suffices to prove that
\left\lVert N_{*,\beta}u_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim\left\lVert f_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}
, where
u_{i}(X)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}_{L}
for all
X\in\Omega
and all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
. Indeed, if this holds then we get that
u(X)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}_{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}(X)
and

\displaystyle\left\lVert N_{*,\beta}u\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert N_{*,\beta}u_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert f_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)},

as desired.

Let us now fix
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
and suppose that
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
is such that
\textup{supp}\left(f\right)\subseteq T_{i}(R(r_{i}/2))\cap\partial\Omega
. Let
u(X)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{X}_{L}
for
X\in\Omega
.
If
\varepsilon>0
is such that

\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\left\{X\in\Omega:\textup{dist}(X,\partial\Omega)\geq\varepsilon\right\}\neq\emptyset,

then we claim that

(4.11)

\sup_{X\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left|u(X)\right|\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}.

Indeed, without loss of generality we may assume that
X_{0}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
(where
X_{0}
is the point fixed in Section
4.2
), and therefore (see, e.g.,
[
31
, Definition 1.3.10]
) for all
X\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and
Q\in\partial\Omega
it holds that

K(X,Q)=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\omega^{X}_{L}(\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r))}{\omega^{X_{0}}_{L}(\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r))}\leq C_{\varepsilon,\Omega},

since a Harnack chain argument, connecting
X
to
X_{0}
, shows that

\omega^{X}_{L}(\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r))\leq C_{\varepsilon,\Omega}\omega^{X_{0}}_{L}(\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r)).

See, for example,
[
31
, Chapter 1, Section 3]
. As a consequence, for all
X\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
it follows from Hölder’s inequality that

\displaystyle\left|u(X)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|f(Q)\right|K(X,Q)k(Q)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma(Q)\leq C_{\varepsilon,\Omega}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\left\lVert k\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\partial\Omega)},

\displaystyle\lVert N_{*,\beta,\frac{1}{2}}u\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega\cap T_{i}(R((1+M)r_{i})))}

4.8.
Second part of the proof of Theorem
1.7

We shall now turn to the proof of the estimate (
1.16
) on the Hausdorff dimension of the divergence set. In the context of the space
\partial\Omega
, we work directly with the Hausdorff content
\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{\infty}
instead of working with a general fractional dimensional measure
\mu
and appealing to Frostman’s lemma. It may be possible to use the version of Frostman’s lemma on compact metric spaces stated in
[
34
, Theorem 8.17]
, but we do not pursue this here.

The first part of this section is devoted to showing that for any
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
, the function
\tilde{f}:\partial\Omega\to(-\infty,\infty]
defined by

(4.17)

\tilde{f}(Q)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r)}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma,&\textup{if the limit exists in }\mathbb{R};\\
\infty,&\textup{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

is a representative of
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
for which the estimate (
1.16
) makes sense (see Lemma
4.16
below). Recall that the surface measure
\sigma
on
\partial\Omega
is
n
-Ahlfors–David regular by (
4.5
), and is therefore doubling. In particular,
\partial\Omega
is a (compact) space of homogeneous type, thus the limit in (
4.17
) exists for
\sigma
-almost every
Q\in\partial\Omega
(see, e.g.,
[
42
, Chapter I, Theorem 1]
) and
\tilde{f}
is indeed a representative of
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
.

We need some preliminaries about classical maximal functions on
\partial\Omega
and their relation with Hausdorff content. The (centred) Hardy–Littlewood maximal function of
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\partial\Omega)
is defined by

M_{\partial\Omega}f(Q)=\sup_{0<r\leq\textup{diam}(\Omega)}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r)}\left|f\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\quad\textup{for all }Q\in\partial\Omega.

More generally, for
0\leq\alpha<n
we shall need the fractional maximal function defined by

M_{\partial\Omega}^{\alpha}f(Q)=\sup_{0<r\leq\textup{diam}(\Omega)}\sigma(\Delta(Q,r))^{\tfrac{\alpha}{n}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r)}\left|f\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\quad\textup{for all }Q\in\partial\Omega.

For
\gamma\geq 0
and
d\geq 1
, the
\gamma
-dimensional (unlimited) Hausdorff content of
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by

\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{\infty}(A)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\textup{diam}(E_{i})^{\gamma}:A\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i}\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all countable covers of
A
by arbitrary subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that
\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}(A)=0
if and only if
\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{\infty}(A)=0
(see, e.g.,
[
34
, Lemma 4.6]
).

A Vitali covering argument analogous to that used in the proof of
[
16
, Lemma 1]
yields the following result.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n}{p}]
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
. For all
\lambda>0
and
\gamma\geq n-\alpha p
it holds that

\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{\infty}\left(\left\{Q\in\partial\Omega:M^{\alpha}_{\partial\Omega}f(Q)>\lambda\right\}\right)\lesssim\lambda^{-p}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)}^{p}.

The same estimate also holds with
M^{\alpha}_{\partial\Omega}f
replaced by
(M^{\alpha s}_{\partial\Omega}\left|f\right|^{s})^{1/s}
for any
1\leq s\leq p
.

A proof of the following lemma can be found, for example, in
[
16
, Lemma 2]
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\alpha\in(0,\frac{n}{p})
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For all
\lambda>0
and
\gamma>n-\alpha p
it holds that

\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{\infty}\left(\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:I_{\alpha}\ast f(x)>\lambda\right\}\right)\lesssim(\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})^{\frac{p\gamma}{n-\alpha p}}.

The following lemma is the main ingredient needed for the proof of Lemma
4.16
. Coincidentally, its proof contains the essence of the argument that we shall use in the remaining part of the proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
,
s\in(0,\frac{n}{p})
and
f\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)
. For all
\lambda>0
and
\gamma>n-sp
it holds that

\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{\infty}\left(\left\{Q\in\partial\Omega:M_{\partial\Omega}f(Q)>\lambda\right\}\right)\lesssim(\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\partial\Omega)})^{p}+(\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)})^{\frac{\gamma p}{n-sp}}.

Proof.

As in the first part of the proof of Theorem
1.7
, we can use the covering (
4.9
) and a subordinate partition of unity to decompose
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}
, with
\textup{supp}\left(f_{i}\right)\subseteq\partial\Omega\cap T_{i}(R(r_{i}/2))
and
\left\lVert f_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\partial\Omega)}
for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
. The sub-additivity of the maximal operator
M_{\partial\Omega}
shows that for all
\lambda>0
it holds that

\left\{Q\in\partial\Omega:M_{\partial\Omega}f(Q)>\lambda\right\}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\left\{Q\in\partial\Omega:M_{\partial\Omega}f_{i}>\lambda/N\right\}.

We can therefore assume from now on that
\textup{supp}\left(f\right)\subseteq\partial\Omega\cap T_{i}(R(r_{i}/2))
for some
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
.

If
Q\in\partial\Omega\setminus T_{i}(R((1+M)r_{i}))
(i.e. if
Q
is far from the support of
f
), then
\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,\delta_{i})\cap\textup{supp}\left(f\right)=\emptyset
, where
\delta_{i}=\frac{r_{i}}{2}(1+M)
(see (
4.12
) in the first part of the proof of Theorem
1.7
). Consequently, it follows from the
n
-Ahlfors–David regularity of
\sigma
that

(4.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}M_{\partial\Omega}f(Q)&=\sup_{\delta_{i}<r\leq\textup{diam}(\Omega)}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r)}\left|f\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\\
&\lesssim\sup_{\delta_{i}<r\leq\textup{diam}(\Omega)}\sigma(\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r))^{s/n}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r)}\left|f\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\leq M^{s}_{\partial\Omega}f(Q).\end{split}

If
Q\in\partial\Omega\cap T_{i}(R((1+M)r_{i}))
(i.e. if
Q
is close to the support of
f
), then
Q=\Phi_{i}(x_{0})=T_{i}((\varphi_{i}(x_{0}),x_{0}))
for some
x_{0}\in\Delta(0,(1+M)r_{i})
(using the notation introduced at the start of the proof of Theorem
1.7
) and we may use (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) to obtain that

(4.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}M_{\partial\Omega}f(Q)&=\sup_{0<r\leq\textup{diam}(\Omega)}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.44173pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.81091pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.2152pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.04697pt}}\!\int_{\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r)}\left|f\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\\
&\eqsim\sup_{0<r\leq\textup{diam}(\Omega)}\frac{1}{\sigma(\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(Q,r))}\int_{\Delta(0,r_{i}/2)}\left|f\circ\Phi_{i}(x)\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\Delta_{\partial\Omega}(\Phi_{i}(x_{0}),r)}(\Phi_{i}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\lesssim\sup_{0<r\leq\textup{diam}(\Omega)}r^{-n}\int_{\Delta(0,r_{i}/2)\cap\Delta(x_{0},r)}\left|f\circ\Phi_{i}(x)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\lesssim M(f\circ\Phi_{i})(x_{0}),\end{split}

where we have also used the fact that
\left|x-x_{0}\right|<r
if
\left|\Phi_{i}(x)-\Phi_{i}(x_{0})\right|<r
in the third line.
Since
f\circ\Phi_{i}\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{C}^{p}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see Remark
3.19
), the estimate (
3.31
) and Lemma
4.14
show that for all
\lambda>0
and
\gamma>n-sp
it holds that

5.
Further contextual remarks

The tangential convergence problems considered in this paper are of course not confined to boundary value problems that are elliptic. In this section we connect with some similar statements concerning parabolic, dispersive and wave equations.

It was shown in the celebrated work of Aronson
[
4
]
that the fundamental solution
g(t,x)
of the equation
\partial_{t}u=\textup{div}_{x}(A(t,x)\nabla_{x}u)
enjoys Gaussian bounds on any strip
(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
provided the real matrix-valued coefficients
A
are bounded and uniformly elliptic in the sense of (
1.5
), uniformly in
t\in(0,T]
. Specifically, the solution
u_{f}
to the associated initial value problem satisfies
u_{f}(t,x)\lesssim(H_{t}*f)(x)
where
H_{t}
is a Euclidean heat kernel; moreover one has a similar lower bound, and all implicit constants depend only on
n
,
T
and the bounds in (
1.5
). Consequently the convergence properties of
u_{f}
to its initial data
f
inherit those of the constant coefficient case, and these are easily seen to follow from the classical theory discussed in the introduction (e.g. pointwise domination of the heat kernel by the Poisson kernel and application of Theorem
1.2
shows that
u_{f}(t,x)
tends to
f(x_{0})
in the parabolic tangential approach region
\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}:\left|x-x_{0}\right|<t^{\beta/2}\textup{ if }0<t\leq 1,\textup{ and }\left|x-x_{0}\right|<t^{1/2}\textup{ if }t\geq 1\}
). In certain circumstances at least, similar Gaussian bounds may also be obtained for equations with complex coefficients; see
[
5
]
.

The parabolic setting is somewhat akin to that for elliptic equations with
t
-independent coefficients in the (block) form
-\partial_{t}^{2}u-\textup{div}_{x}(A(x)\nabla_{x}u)=0
on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
. In that case, the unique solution
u_{f}
to the Dirichlet problem satisfies
u_{f}(t,x)\lesssim(P_{t}\ast f)(x)
, where
P_{t}
is the classical Poisson kernel. This follows from the Gaussian upper bound for the kernel of the heat semigroup
e^{-tL_{A}}
of the elliptic operator
L_{A}=-\textup{div}_{x}(A\nabla_{x})
(see, e.g.,
[
40
, Theorem 6.10]
), since functional calculus then yields the required bounds for the kernel of the Poisson semigroup
e^{-tL_{A}^{1/2}}
.

Problems of the type that we address in this paper have also attracted interest in the setting of dispersive equations, most notably the free Schrödinger equation
i\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{x}u
on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
. Here matters are very different in that
some smoothness is
required
of the initial datum
u_{0}
in order for
u(t,x)\rightarrow u_{0}(x)
to be true for almost every
x
as
t\rightarrow 0
. Identifying the regularity threshold for
u_{0}
in the scale of
\mathrm{L}^{2}
Sobolev spaces
\mathrm{H}^{s}
was a longstanding problem of Carleson, and one that has only recently been resolved for general dimensions
[
17
]
– the most basic point being that the Schrödinger kernel, unlike the Poisson and heat kernels, is not a classical approximate identity. As a result there are significant phenomenological differences from the elliptic and parabolic settings. For example, it is shown in
[
6
]
that the dimension of the divergence set as
t\rightarrow 0
has discontinuities as a function of
s
.
For tangential convergence results of the type
u(t,x+\gamma(t))\rightarrow u_{0}(x)
we refer to
[
37
]
and the references there, and for bounds on the Hausdorff dimensions of sets of divergence we refer to
[
17
,
35
]
and the references there. Some of the methods employed in this setting have also proved effective in addressing similar questions for the wave equation
\partial_{t}^{2}u=\Delta_{x}u
on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}_{+}
– see for example
[
6
]
.

Finally we briefly point out that tangential approach maximal functions similar to
N_{*,\beta}
arise naturally in the study of the Hörmander symbol classes, along with other families of oscillatory Fourier multipliers. In that setting the relevant maximal functions are essentially the auxiliary maximal functions of Section
3.3
, taking the form

\mathfrak{M}_{\alpha,\beta}u(x_{0}):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t,x)\in\Gamma^{\beta}(x_{0})\\
0<t\leq 1\end{subarray}}t^{\alpha}|u(t,x)|,

where
u(t,x)=P_{t}*f(x)
, or some other suitable average of a given function
f
.
For
\alpha>0
the operator
f\mapsto\mathfrak{M}_{\alpha,\beta}u
may be interpreted as a fractional maximal averaging operator, effectively generalising that given by (
1.3
).
This connection bears some analogy with the fundamental relationship between Calderón–Zygmund singular integrals and the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function; see
[
7
,
8
]
.
