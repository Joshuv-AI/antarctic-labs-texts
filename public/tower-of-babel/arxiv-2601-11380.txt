Central flashes during stellar occultations
B. Sicardy, L. Dettwiller

Abstract
Context. Central flashes may occur during stellar occultations by solar system objects. Aims. Catalog diffraction effects on the flash with point-like stars, monochromatic waves and different cases of spherical transparent atmosphere; Describe the corrections due to stellar diameters. Methods. Diffraction involves the Huygens principle, the Sommerfeld lemma and the stationary phase method, while finite stellar diameter cases involve Clausius’ theorem. Results. For point-like stars, the central flash shape is that of the classical Poisson spot, but with larger height. For tenuous atmospheres that cannot focus the stellar rays at shadow center, the flash is amplified by the factor ( R 0 / r 0 ) 2 (R_{0}/r_{0})^{2} compared to the Poisson spot, where R 0 R_{0} and r 0 r_{0} are the object and the shadow radii, respectively. For denser atmospheres that can focus the rays at shadow center, the flash peaks at 2 ​ π 2 ​ ( R CF / λ F ) 2 ​ ϕ ⟂ ​ ( 0 ) 2\pi^{2}(R_{\rm CF}/\lambda_{\rm F})^{2}\phi_{\perp}(0) , where R CF R_{\rm CF} is the central flash layer radius, λ F \lambda_{\rm F} is the Fresnel scale and ϕ ⟂ ​ ( 0 ) \phi_{\perp}(0) is the flux that would be observed at shadow center without focusing. For isothermal atmospheres with scale height H H , the height is 2 ​ π 2 ​ ( R CF ​ H ) / λ F 2 2\pi^{2}(R_{\rm CF}H)/\lambda_{\rm F}^{2} . Fringes surrounding the central flash are separated by λ P = λ F 2 / R CF \lambda_{\rm P}=\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/R_{\rm CF} , related to the separation between the primary and secondary stellar images. For a projected stellar diameter D ∗ ≳ λ P D_{*}\gtrsim\lambda_{\rm P} , the flash is described by complete elliptic integrals, and has full width at half maximum of 1.14 ​ D ∗ 1.14D_{*} and peak value 8 ​ H / D ∗ 8H/D_{*} . Conclusions. For Earth-based occultations by Pluto and Triton observed in the visible with point-like stars, diffraction causes flashes with very large heights ∼ \sim 10 4 -10 5 , spread over a very small meter-sized region in the shadow plane. In practice, the flash is usually smoothed by the stellar diameter, but still reaches high values of ∼ \sim 50 and ∼ \sim 200 during Pluto and Triton occultations, respectively. Diffraction dominates when using millimeter wavelengths or longer. Effects of departure from sphericity, atmospheric waves and stellar limb darkening are discussed.

Introduction

During a stellar occultation by a body with an atmosphere, the latter acts like a lens that refracts the light rays coming from the star. For an observer at distance
\Delta
from the occulter, and if the atmosphere is dense enough, there exists a layer (called the central flash layer) with radius
R_{\rm CF}
that focuses the rays towards the shadow center.
The value of
R_{\rm CF}
depends on the molecular refractivity and on the number density profile of the atmosphere.
When approaching the shadow center, an observer records a surge of brightness known as the central flash.

One of the first occurrences of a central flash came from the occultation of
\epsilon
Geminorum by the Martian upper atmosphere on 8 April 1976. A modeling of the flash was made in the framework of pure geometrical optics, i.e. without accounting for diffraction effects
(
11
)
.
It included the calculation of the caustic created by an oblate atmosphere near the shadow center, and it also mentioned the effect of the finite apparent stellar diameter.

However, diffraction was considered more than a century ago as the technique of stellar occultation by bodies without atmospheres came into use.
10
was one of the first to describe the effect of Fresnel diffraction during occultations of stars by the lunar limb.
Diffraction fringes were observed three decades later during a lunar occultation of Regulus by
1
, who gave an upper limit of 2.0 milli-arcsec for the apparent angular diameter of this star.
With the advent of fast photometers, and starting with
36

27

28
and the review by
37
, who mention 124 stellar diameter measurements.
Besides stellar diameters, lunar occultation also provided another important parameter, the local limb slope
(
13
)
.

After the discovery of rings around Uranus and Neptune, diffraction caused by narrow semi-transparent rings (or small opaque objects) was formalized by
29

Concerning occultations by bodies with an atmosphere, considerations of some effects due to both diffraction and stellar diameter dates back to at least
14

16

5

40

4.2
Near the shadow edge

As one approaches the shadow edge, the phase
\varphi_{1}(R)
admits a stationary value at
R=r\approx R_{0}
, so that the Sommerfeld lemma cannot be used anymore. The stationary condition (Appendix
B
) then provides an evaluation of the integral in Eq.
22
through Eq.
77
, where
a=R_{0}
,
b=+\infty
,
f(v)=\sqrt{v}
and
g(v)=\pi(v^{2}/2-vr)/\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}
.
Elementary calculations then provide

\phi_{\rm Fr}(r)=|a_{1}(r)|^{2}=\left|\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{1+{\rm i}}F_{\rm r}\left(\frac{r-R_{0}}{\lambda_{\rm F}}\right)\right|^{2}.

(30)

This describes the classical Fresnel fringes near the edge of a shadow cast by an opaque body, see Fig.
3
. The flux decays like
[\lambda_{\rm F}/(r-R_{0})]^{2}/(2\pi^{2})
for
r\lesssim R_{0}
and the oscillations of the Fresnel fringes damps like
(\sqrt{2}/\pi)[\lambda_{\rm F}/(r-R_{0})]
for
r\gtrsim R_{0}
, see Appendix
B
. These Fresnel fringes are the unique diffraction effect considered in the early work of
14
.

In the presence of an atmosphere, we will find that both the Poisson spot and the Fresnel fringes survive, but with a different peak value and a different spacing, respectively. In spite of these differences, we will still use the nomenclature Poisson spot, Poisson fringes or Fresnel diffraction to describe these features.

5
The tenuous atmosphere case

5.1
Inside the dark shadow, far from the edge

The presence of an atmosphere is accounted for by the term
\varphi_{\rm a}(R)
in Eq.
16
, where we define the phase

\varphi(R):=\varphi_{\rm a}(R)+\frac{\pi R^{2}}{2\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}{\rm\penalty\ for\penalty\ }R\geq R_{0}.

(31)

If the observer is deep inside the dark shadow of the object (see Fig.
1
), then
no stellar image is seen by the observer. This implies that
\varphi(R)
is nowhere stationary, i.e. that
\varphi^{\prime}(R)\neq 0
for any
R>R_{0}
. The Sommerfeld lemma (Eq.
64
) can then be applied. As detailed in Appendix
A
, the flux observed near the shadow center is then

\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)=\left(\frac{R_{0}}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}J_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{\pi R_{0}r}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right).

(32)

This is Eq.
27
, except for the amplification factor
(R_{0}/r_{0})^{2}
.
For an airless body, we retrieve the classical Poisson spot with
R_{0}=r_{0}
.
As the atmosphere increases in density while remaining tenuous, the Poisson spot is amplified by the ratio of the projected surface area of the body to the surface area of its shadow, a factor that can reach several times unity. Remarkably, this amplification occurs even though no rays are focused towards the shadow center in the geometrical optics regime. This is actually due to the fact that the atmosphere decreases the rate of change of
\varphi(R)
near
R_{0}
, when compared to the airless case. This stems from the fact that
\varphi^{\prime}_{\rm a}(R_{0})<0
(Eq.
33
), which decreases the value of
g^{\prime}(v)
in Eq.
64
, thus increasing the value of
\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)
.

5.2
Outside the dark shadow, far from the edge

We assume here that
r_{0}\gg\lambda^{2}_{\rm F}/\pi R_{0}
, i.e. that the size of the dark shadow is much larger that the diameter of the Poisson spot.
We consider an observer well outside the dark shadow (in the sense that Fresnel diffraction is negligible).
Such observer sees the primary stellar image, but not the secondary image. This means that the integral in Eq.
22
is negligible compared to the integral in Eq.
21
.
The contribution to the integral in Eq.
21
mainly comes from the neighborhood of
R_{1}
where
\varphi_{1}(R)
is stationary, i.e. where
\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(R)=0
. Using Eq.
23
, this yields
\varphi_{\rm a}^{\prime}(R_{1})=-\pi(R_{1}-r)/\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}
. As the deviation angle at
R_{1}
is
\omega(R_{1})=(R_{1}-r)/\Delta
(Fig.
1
), we have
\varphi_{\rm a}^{\prime}(R_{1})=-(\pi\Delta/\lambda_{\rm F}^{2})\omega(R_{1})
. We note that the stationary condition must be satisfied for any value of
r
, hence of
R_{1}(r)
, thus

\varphi^{\prime}_{\rm a}(R_{1})=-\frac{\pi\Delta}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\omega(R_{1})=-k\omega(R_{1}).

(33)

This is an expression of the Malus-Dupin theorem, which states that the pencil of light emerging from the atmosphere is perpendicular to the local wave front.
From Eq.
33
, we have
\varphi_{1}^{\prime\prime}={\rm d}\varphi^{\prime}_{1}/{\rm d}R=(\pi/\lambda_{\rm F}^{2})(1-\Delta{\rm d}\omega/{\rm d}R)
. From Eq.
4
, we obtain

\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{1}(R_{1})=\frac{1}{\phi_{\perp 1}(r)}\left(\frac{\pi}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right),

(34)

where we recall that
\phi_{\perp 1}(r)
is the flux received at
r
from the primary stellar image in the geometrical optics limit, without the focusing effect of the limb curvature.

Because
\varphi^{\prime}_{1}=0
at
R_{1}>R_{0}
, the Sommerfeld lemma (Eq.
64
) cannot be used anymore. In order to evaluate the integral in Eq.
21
, we have to use instead Eq.
71
, where
f(R)=\sqrt{R}
and
g(R)=\varphi_{1}(R)
.
Let us consider the width
A
of the first Fresnel zone in
R-R_{1}
,
A=\sqrt{2\pi/\varphi^{\prime\prime}(R_{1})}=\lambda_{\rm F}\sqrt{2\phi_{\perp 1}(r_{0})}
. Since
\phi_{\perp 1}<1
, the effect of the refraction is to diminish
A
. This is one of the aspects of the Fresnel zone flattening considered by
39
.
This said, for
R_{1}\gtrsim R_{0}+
several times
A
, then the bounds of the integral (
R_{0}
and
+\infty
) can be replaced by
-\infty
and
+\infty
, providing

a_{1}(r)\approx\sqrt{\phi_{1}(r)}\exp[{\rm i}\varphi_{1}(R_{1})].

(35)

Here we have used the fact that
\phi_{1}(r)=(R_{1}/r)\phi_{\perp 1}(r)
is the flux received at P from the primary stellar images accounting for the focusing effect of the limb curvature (Eq.
5
).
The Eq.
35
merely states that the observed flux,

\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)=|a_{1}(r)|^{2}=\phi_{1}(r),

(36)

is the flux calculated in the geometrical optics approximation (Eq.
5
), with no noticeable diffraction effects.

5.3
Near the dark shadow edge

As
R_{1}\approx R_{0}
, edge effects come into play and we have to introduce the Fresnel function
F_{\rm r}
defined by Eq.
75
.
Using Eq.
77
, we get

a_{1}(r)\approx\sqrt{\phi_{1}}\exp({\rm i}\varphi_{1})\left[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{1+{\rm i}}F_{\rm r}\left(\frac{R_{1}-R_{0}}{\sqrt{\phi_{\perp 1}(r)}\lambda_{\rm F}}\right)\right]

(37)

and the corresponding flux

\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)=\phi_{1}(r)\left|\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{1+{\rm i}}F_{\rm r}\left(\frac{R_{1}-R_{0}}{\sqrt{\phi_{\perp 1}(r)}\lambda_{\rm F}}\right)\right|^{2}.

(38)

Formally
R_{1}
does not exist anymore for
r<r_{0}
. However,
\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)
can still be evaluated by noting that from Eq.
4
, we can replace
R_{1}-R_{0}
by
\approx\phi_{\perp 1}(r_{0})(r-r_{0})
.
This is equivalent to extrapolating the atmospheric profile a little bit below the radius of the body, which provides

\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)\approx\phi_{1}(r_{0})\left|\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{1+{\rm i}}F_{\rm r}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\phi_{\perp 1}(r_{0})}(r-r_{0})}{\lambda_{\rm F}}\right)\right|^{2}.

(39)

Hence for (
r>r_{0}
), and compared again to the airless case, the Fresnel fringes are locally stretched by the factor
1/\sqrt{\phi_{\perp 1}(r_{0})}
as seen in the observer plane, a result already obtained by
16
. For
r<r_{0}
, the flux approaches zero, as predicted by Eq.
80
. This yields, for
r
smaller than
r_{0}
minus a few times
\lambda_{\rm F}/\sqrt{\phi_{\perp 1}(r_{0})}
,

\phi_{\rm Edge}(r)\approx\frac{R_{0}}{2\pi^{2}r_{0}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\rm F}}{r-r_{0}}\right)^{2}.

(40)

This equation describes how the flux asymptotically decays to zero as the observer gets deeper into the dark shadow. The flux reaches half the value
\phi_{1}(r_{0})
for

r-r_{0}\approx-\frac{\lambda_{\rm F}}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{R_{0}}{r_{0}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\phi_{1}(r_{0})}}.

(41)

This shows that if
\phi_{1}(r_{0})
is small, the damping of the flux in the observer plane may be significantly greater than
\lambda_{\rm F}
.

In principle, the measurement of the stretching factor
1/\sqrt{\phi_{\perp 1}(r_{0})}
can be used to detect tenuous atmospheres. In practice, however, this requires a high signal-to-noise ratio and a high cadence of acquisition, as the first Fresnel fringes cover a fraction of a second only for typical Earth-based occultations. Also, it requires a good knowledge of the topography of the object, as the fringe spacing depends of the angle between the apparent path of the star relative to the local limb.
Another way to detect atmospheres is to measure the shrinking
r_{0}-R_{0}
of the shadow radius with respect to the actual radius
R_{0}
of the object. However, this requires that the size of the object is known using independent results, for instance from in situ space missions. This method was used for a Titania occultation observed in 2001, by comparing the radius derived from the occultation (
r_{0}=788.4\pm
0.6 km) and the Voyager 1 result (
R_{0}=788.9\pm 1.8
km), providing an upper limit of 70 nbar for the surface pressure of a CO
2
atmosphere
(
38
)
.

6
The dense atmosphere case

6.1
Immediate vicinity of the center

As the atmosphere becomes denser, the radius
r_{0}
of the dark shadow decreases. At some point,
r_{0}
approaches zero, which marks the transition between the tenuous and dense atmosphere cases (Fig.
2
). The Eq.
32
predicts in principle that
\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)
diverges. However, the Sommerfeld lemma cannot be used anymore since the phase
\varphi(R)
in Eq.
31
becomes stationary (
\varphi^{\prime}(R)=0
) at the radius
R_{\rm CF}>R_{0}
. Using again Eq.
71
and for
R_{\rm CF}\gtrsim R_{0}+
several times
\lambda_{\rm F}\sqrt{\phi_{\perp}(0)}
, we have

a(r)=(1-{\rm i})\pi\left(\frac{R_{\rm CF}}{\lambda_{\rm F}}\right)\sqrt{\phi_{\perp}}(r)\exp\left[{\rm i}\varphi(R_{\rm CF})\right]J_{0}\left(\frac{\pi R_{\rm CF}r}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right),

(42)

so that

\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)=|a(r)|^{2}=2\pi^{2}\left(\frac{R_{\rm CF}}{\lambda_{\rm F}}\right)^{2}\phi_{\perp}(0)J_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{\pi R_{\rm CF}r}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right).

(43)

This result is true for any spherically symmetric dense atmosphere, regardless of the details of the
\tau(R)
expression in Eq.
10
. In the particular case of an isothermal atmosphere,
\phi_{\perp}(0)\approx H/R_{\rm CF}
(Eq.
8
), so that Eq.
43
becomes

\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)\approx 2\pi^{2}\left(\frac{R_{\rm CF}H}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right)J_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{\pi R_{\rm CF}r}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right),

(44)

which is
21
’s result (Eq.
1
).

In the transition regime where
R_{\rm CF}-R_{0}\approx\lambda_{\rm F}\sqrt{\phi_{\perp}(0)}
, i.e. when the bright annulus causing the central flash is seen by the observer as lying just about the limb of the occulter, Fresnel diffraction occurs. This requires the corrective factor introduced in eq.
77
, that is

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)=2\pi^{2}\left(\frac{R_{\rm CF}}{\lambda_{\rm F}}\right)^{2}J_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{\pi R_{\rm CF}r}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right)\phi_{\perp}(0)\times\\
\\
\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{1+{\rm i}}F_{\rm r}\left(\frac{R_{\rm CF}-R_{0}}{\sqrt{\phi_{\perp}(0)}\lambda_{\rm F}}\right)\right|^{2}.\end{array}

(45)

The comparison of Eq.
43
with Eq.
27
shows that the central flash caused by a dense atmosphere has the same functional dependence on
r
as the Poisson spot. However, instead of peaking at one, it peaks at

\phi_{\rm Diff}(0)=2\pi^{2}\left(\frac{R_{\rm CF}}{\lambda_{\rm F}}\right)^{2}\phi_{\perp}(0)\approx 2\pi^{2}\left(\frac{R_{\rm CF}H}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right),

(46)

in the general case and in the isothermal case, respectively.

We note that the flash has a typical width of
\sim\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/R_{\rm CF}
(Eq.
29
) and a height
2\pi^{2}(R_{\rm CF}^{2}/\lambda_{\rm F}^{2})\phi_{\perp}(0)
. So contrarily to the classical Poisson spot (Eq.
27
), it does not disappear when
\lambda
approaches zero, as expected from geometrical optics. We will see in Section
8
that for objects like Pluto or Triton,
\phi_{\rm Diff}(0)
can be very large (some
10^{4}
-
10^{5}
) while
\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/R_{\rm CF}
is very small (a few meters).

The thickness
\Delta R_{\rm Diff}
of the central flash layer can be defined as the interval of
R
which most contributes to the integral in Eq.
71
, i.e. a few times
1/\sqrt{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(R_{\rm CF})}
.
Based on Eq.
34
, this provides a thickness of the order of

\Delta R_{\rm Diff}\sim\lambda_{\rm F}\sqrt{\phi_{\perp}(0)}\approx\lambda_{\rm F}\sqrt{\frac{H}{R_{\rm CF}}},

(47)

in the general and isothermal cases, respectively.

The flash layer thus acts as an annular lens with radius
R_{\rm CF}
, width
\Delta R_{\rm Diff}
and focal length of
\Delta
. The image of the star produced by this lens in the shadow plane (with radial profile
\propto J_{0}^{2}(u)
) resembles, but is not identical to the Airy disk (radial profile
\propto[J_{1}(u)/u]^{2}
) produced by a lens of radius
R_{\rm CF}
, where
u
is defined in Eq.
17
. A comparison between the Poisson spot and the Airy disk in given in Appendix
C
.

6.2
Extended vicinity of the center

For
r\gtrsim\lambda^{2}_{\rm F}/\pi R
, and in the gray shaded region shown in the lower panel of Fig.
1
, the two stellar images are seen by the observer. The amplitude caused by the primary image (Eq.
35
) is unchanged, while the stationary phase method can be used to calculate
a_{2}(r)
, yielding

\displaystyle\displaystyle a_{1}(r)\approx\sqrt{\phi_{1}(r)}\exp[{\rm i}\varphi_{1}(R_{1})],

(48)

\displaystyle\displaystyle a_{2}(r)\approx-{\rm i}\sqrt{\phi_{2}(r)}\exp[{\rm i}\varphi_{2}(R_{2}))].

(49)

This provides the total normalized flux,

\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)=|a_{1}+a_{2}|^{2}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}+2\sqrt{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}}\sin\left(\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1}\right),

(50)

where
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are short-hand notations for
\varphi_{1}(R_{1})
and
\varphi_{2}(R_{2})
, respectively.

We retrieve the
-{\rm i}
prefactor in the expression of
a_{2}
(hence its quadrature advance) already discussed in Section
3
. This explains the presence of the term
\sin(\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1})
in Eq.
50
, instead of the usual factor
\cos(\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1})
encountered in the two-slit Young’s experiment.

Apart from this difference, we obtain the simple result that for
r\gtrsim\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/R
, the Poisson fringes merely results from the interferences of two sources with respective fluxes
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
.
If one of the two images is blocked (e.g. if it is absorbed by an atmospheric haze layer), or if the secondary image does not exist
(as in Section
4
), then the received flux reduces to
\phi_{1}
or
\phi_{2}
, with no more interference pattern.

For
r\ll R_{1},R_{2}
, we have
R_{1}\approx R_{2}\approx R_{\rm CF}
and
\varphi_{2}(R_{2})-\varphi_{1}(R_{1})\approx 2\pi R_{\rm CF}r/\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}
. Moreover, for an isothermal atmosphere, we have
\phi_{1}\approx\phi_{2}\approx H/r
near centrality, so that

\phi_{\rm Diff}(r)=\frac{2H}{r}+\frac{2H}{r}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi R_{\rm CF}r}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right).

(51)

The flux oscillates around the average value
2H/r
(consistent with Eq.
9
) with a fringe spacing

\lambda_{\rm P}=\frac{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}{R_{\rm CF}}=\frac{\lambda\Delta}{2R_{\rm CF}},

(52)

identical to Eq.
28
and to the fringe separation obtained in the Young’s experiment with two slits separated by
2R_{\rm CF}
.

As
r
increases, the secondary image gets deeper in the atmosphere while the primary image gets higher, so that
R_{2}<R_{1}
and
\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{2}(R_{2})>\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{1}(R_{1})
, hence
\phi_{1}>\phi_{2}
. Consequently the visibility of the fringes,

V=\frac{2\sqrt{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}}}{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}},

(53)

decreases from unity in the vicinity of the shadow center, where
\phi_{1}\approx\phi_{2}
, and becomes weak as
\phi_{1}\gg\phi_{2}
.

6.3
Outer domain

As
r
becomes larger than
r^{\prime}_{0}
(see the lower panel of Fig.
1
), the secondary image disappears behind the limb, producing Fresnel diffraction fringes just before its disappearance. As
r
continues to increase, the observer enters the outer domain of the shadow where the secondary wave cannot be approximated by geometrical optics (modified by the
-i
prefactor as in Eq.
49

\varphi_{2}(R)
does not admit anymore a stationary value for

must be evaluated using Sommerfeld’s lemma. From Eq.

a_{2}(r)\approx\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{R_{0}}{2r}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\rm F}}{r-r^{\prime}_{0}}\right)\exp\left\{{\rm i}\left[\varphi_{2}(R_{0})-\frac{\pi}{4}\right]\right\}.

(54)

Using the value of
a_{1}(r)
given by Eq.
48
, we obtain the flux observed in the outer domain,

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\phi_{\rm out}(r)=|a_{1}(r)+a_{2}(r)|^{2}=\phi_{1}+\frac{R_{0}}{2\pi^{2}r}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\rm F}}{r-r^{\prime}_{0}}\right)^{2}+\\
\\
\displaystyle\sqrt{\phi_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{2R_{0}}{\pi^{2}r}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\rm F}}{r-r^{\prime}_{0}}\right)\cos\left[\varphi_{2}(R_{0})-\varphi_{1}(R_{1})-\frac{\pi}{4}\right].\\
\end{array}

(55)

The term
1/(r-r^{\prime}_{0})
describes the evanescent side of the Fresnel fringes associated with the disappearance of the secondary image, over a scale in
r
of
\sim\lambda_{\rm F}\sqrt{R_{0}/r^{\prime}_{0}}/(\pi\phi_{2}(r^{\prime}_{0}))
, by similarity with Eq.
41
.

7
Effect of stellar diameter in geometrical optics

Eq.
9
assumes a point-like star and predicts an infinite flux at exact alignment of the star, the occulter and the observer (
r=0
). In practice however, the star appears as a disk with finite radius
r_{*}
when projected at the body distance.

As discussed later (Section
9
), during ground-based stellar occultations by remote objects observed in the visible, diffraction effects are usually averaged out by the stellar diameter. The flash profile can then be calculated using geometrical optics, noting that the atmosphere forms two distorted images of this disk. Clausius’ theorem states that for a transparent atmosphere, the radiance (also called specific intensity or brightness) of these images is conserved
(
14
)
. Consequently, if the radiance of the stellar photosphere is uniform, the received flux is proportional to the sum of the surface areas of these two images.

This means in particular that near centrality, the two images are flattened perpendicular to the limb by a factor
\phi_{\perp}(0)
(Eq.
4
). At the same time, they are stretched by a factor
\approx R/r
along the limb as long as
r>r_{*}
, see Eq.
9
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}G(w)=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{w}\left[E(w)-(1-w^{2})F(w)\right],\\
&\\
{\rm where}&\displaystyle E(w):=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1-w^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\rm d}\theta\\
&\\

\end{array}

(59)

are the complete elliptic integrals of the second and first kinds, respectively.

\phi_{\rm Star}(r\gg r_{*})\approx 2(R/r)\phi_{\perp}(0)

(60)

in the general and isothermal cases, respectively.

(61)

From

r=0
,

\phi_{\rm Star}(0)=\left(\frac{4R}{r_{*}}\right)\phi_{\perp}(0)=\frac{4H}{r_{*}}=\frac{8H}{D_{*}},

(62)

in the general and isothermal cases, respectively, where
D_{*}:=2r_{*}
is apparent stellar diameter (a result already mentioned by

for an isothermal atmosphere). We note that we retrieve the divergence of the flux at

Discussion

The examples of Pluto and Triton occultations considered in the previous section concern Earth-based events observed in the visible, so that
\lambda_{\rm F}\sim 1
km and
R_{0}\sim 1000
km. Consequently,
\lambda_{\rm F}/\sqrt{\phi_{\perp}}
extends over several kilometers, while
\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/R_{0}
represents about a meter.
This means that Fresnel fringes can be resolved during occultations by outer solar system objects (e.g.
26
), while resolving Poisson’s fringes is much more challenging.
A first reason for this is that the shadows of remote bodies typically move on Earth’s surface at some 20 km s
-1
, so that resolving the Poisson fringes requires an acquisition rate of tens of thousands images per second. With stars fainter than magnitude 13, this means an extremely small signal per exposure.
The best resolutions currently attained in the shadow for ground-based occultation is about 1 km. At that scale, the Poisson fringes are averaged out and cannot be seen in occultation light curves, see Fig.
9
.

Besides the practical difficulties to resolve the Poisson spot in the visible, physical effects in the occulter atmosphere can destroy the spot.
One is the departure of the atmosphere from sphericity. A flattened layer with a difference
\delta R
between its equatorial and polar radii creates a diamond-shaped caustic curve in the shadow plane with sides of about
4\delta R

(
11
)
.

The Poisson spot disappears when the diamond-shaped caustic is larger than the the Poisson spot diameter,
4\delta R\gtrsim\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/R
.
For Pluto and Triton events observed in the visible, this corresponds to very small values
\delta R\lesssim 1
m.
Pluto and Triton are slow rotators with periods of 6.4 and 5.9 days, respectively, implying equatorial velocities
v_{\rm eq}\sim
15 m s
-1
for both objects. Ignoring possible zonal winds, these velocities cause a minimum atmospheric flattening
\delta R=R^{2}v_{\rm eq}^{2}/(2GM)
, where
G
is the gravitational constant and
M
is the mass of the body
(
23
)
. This yields
\delta R\sim 150
m, which is much larger than the condition
\delta R\lesssim 1
m derived above in the visible.
However, observing at 1 mm would result in a value of
\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/R
one thousand times larger, thus preserving the Poisson spot even with a flattening of
\delta R\sim 150
m.

This said, we note that the Poisson fringes are more robust against departure from sphericity that the Poisson spot. This is due to the fact that at any time, the two stellar images can interfere even if the atmosphere is distorted.

Another cause of destruction for the Poisson spot is that Pluto’s and Triton’s atmospheres are not perfect lenses. In particular, internal gravity waves cause local density fluctuations that induce stellar scintillation which eventually blurs the central flash, see for instance
20
;
30
;
33
for the cases of Neptune’s and Titan’s central flashes.
This is illustrated in Fig.
13
for Titan. The non centrosymmetric distortion of the atmosphere causes a more complex caustic than the diamond-shaped figure mentioned above, while gravity waves add streaks to the flash map.
These disruptive effects constitute a subject in their own right, and remain out of the scope of the present paper.

Ignoring these difficulties, we consider a thought experiment, in which a telescope with aperture
D_{\rm T}
observes the occulter during an occultation. A natural question is then: what would an observer see through this telescope as the two stellar images move around the limb of the occulter, and in particular during the rapid flux variations caused by the interferences between the two stellar images?

Such telescope produces two pseudo-images, i.e. two Airy disks with angular sizes
\sim\lambda/D_{\rm T}
, i.e. with linear sizes
\sim\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/D_{\rm T}
when projected at the body. These images are separated by
2R
, so that if
D_{\rm T}\lesssim I
, then
\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/D_{\rm T}\gtrsim R
. this means that the observer sees two Airy disks that merge into a single Airy disk. As the two stellar images interfere constructively or destructively, the observer sees this single Airy disk switching on and off as
r
varies.

Conversely, using a larger telescope with
D_{\rm T}\gtrsim I
means that
\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/D_{\rm T}\lesssim R
, so that is the two stellar images are separated. But in this case, the Poisson fringes are averaged out over the telescope aperture, and no more flux variations are observed. What the observer sees are now two distinct Airy disks, each with the flux predicted by geometrical optics.
A quantitative theory of a comparable situation is presented by
7
, for the case where the fringes are due to the diffraction by a single slit, with a discussion of the separation of the images of the two edges of the slit.

Conclusion

We have studied in this paper the structure of the shadow cast by an opaque spherical body surrounded by a transparent and spherical atmosphere. We have examined in particular the effects of diffraction and finite stellar diameter in the structure of the central flash. The analytical expressions given in the main text are summarized in Table
3
, while numerical applications to ground-based occultations by Pluto and Triton are provided in Table
2
.

Our starting point has been an airless and opaque circular body of radius
R_{0}
illuminated by a monochromatic wave of wavelength
\lambda
coming a star at infinity and of negligible angular diameter.
At distance
\Delta
, the body casts a shadow with Fresnel fringes at its edge and a Poisson spot at its center, see Eq.
27
and Fig.
3
. The Poisson spot has a full width of
1.53\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/R_{0}
, where
\lambda_{\rm F}=\sqrt{\lambda\Delta/2}
is the Fresnel scale, and a peak value of unity, when normalized to the unocculted stellar flux.

As a tenuous atmosphere is introduced, the ensuing deviation of the stellar rays creates a dark shadow of radius
r_{0}<R_{0}
(Fig.
1
). The Poisson spot remains unchanged in terms of radial profile, but it is amplified by a factor
(R_{0}/r_{0})^{2}

(Eq.
32
).
For a dense enough atmosphere, defined as an atmosphere that can focus the stellar rays to the shadow center through the existence of a central flash layer, the central flash has still the radial structure of the Poisson spot, but with a peak value of
2\pi^{2}(R_{\rm CF}H)/\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}
for an isothermal atmosphere with scale height
H
, where
R_{\rm CF}
is the radius of the central flash layer.

The central flash is surrounded by circular Poisson fringes whose oscillations decrease like
1/r
and with separation
\lambda_{\rm P}=\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/R_{\rm CF}
. This separation is the same as the one obtained during a Young’s experiment with two sources separated by
2R_{0}
interfering in the shadow plane.

For ground-based occultations by Pluto or Triton observed in the visible, the expected peak value of the flash is very large, some
10^{4}
-
10^{5}
and the Poisson fringe spacing
\lambda_{\rm P}

is very small, about one meter (Table
2
). In practice, the Poisson fringes are unobservable using current technology. However, observations conducted in radio wavelengths (Fig.
10
) may resolve both the central flash and its associated fringe pattern during Pluto or Triton occultations.

In the visible and near IR domains where the Poisson fringes are usually blurred by the projected stellar diameter
D_{*}
, the flash profile is a combination of complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kinds (Eqs.
57
and
61
). The full width at half-maximum of the flash is
1.14D_{*}
and its peak value is
8H/D_{*}
. For Pluto or Triton occultations with typical
D_{*}\sim 0.5
km,
8H/D_{*}
is of the order of 50 and 240, respectively. This implies that in this case, the flash structure is largely dominated by
D_{*}
and by geometrical optics. However, for observations conducted in millimetric wavelengths with
D_{*}\sim 0.5
km, diffraction starts to dominate the smoothing effect of stellar diameter.

Our calculations remain limited in terms of applications because, as mentioned in the Introduction, we assume a perfectly spherical atmosphere. In practice, even slight deviations from sphericity usually dominate the shape of the flash. However, our analysis serves as a firm basis for future calculations that we plan to do, in particular for describing the role of diffraction near the caustic created by a distorted atmosphere, such as the one displayed in Fig.
13
.

Appendix A
The Sommerfeld lemma

The Sommerfeld lemma (also known as the Sommerfeld expansion,
34
) states that
if
f(v)
and
g^{\prime}(v)
vary much more slowly than
\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]
and
if
g^{\prime}(v)\neq 0
everywhere in the interval
[a,b]
, then

\int_{a}^{b}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v\approx\left|\displaystyle f(v)\frac{\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]}{{\rm i}g^{\prime}(v)}\right|_{a}^{b}

(64)

We use this lemma to evaluate Eq.
14
, which should strictly speaking include the Rayleigh-Sommerfeld inclination factor
\cos\chi(R,\theta)
(Fig.
2
),

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle a(r)=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\times\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{R_{0}}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\chi(R,\theta)\exp\{{\rm i}[\varphi_{\rm a}(R)+\varphi_{\rm g}(R)]\}R{\rm d}R{\rm d}\theta.\end{array}

(65)

For values of
r\ll R_{0}
, the dependence of
\chi(R,\theta)
on
\theta
is weak, so that

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle a(r)\approx\frac{\pi}{{\rm i}\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\times\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{R_{0}}^{+\infty}\cos\chi(R)\exp\left\{{\rm i}\left[\varphi_{\rm a}(R)+\frac{\pi R^{2}}{2\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right]\right\}J_{0}\left(\frac{\pi Rr}{\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}}\right)R{\rm d}R.\end{array}

(66)

The change of variable
v=\pi R^{2}/2\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}
yields

\begin{array}[]{l}a(r)=\\
\\
\displaystyle-{\rm i}\int_{v_{0}}^{+\infty}\cos\chi(v)J_{0}\left(\frac{r}{\lambda_{\rm F}}\sqrt{2\pi v}\right)\exp\{{\rm i}[\varphi_{\rm a}(v)+v]\}{\rm d}v,\end{array}

(67)

where
v_{0}=\pi R_{0}^{2}/2\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}
.
This is Eq.
64
, where

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle f(v)=-{\rm i}\cos\chi(v)J_{0}\left(\frac{r}{\lambda_{\rm F}}\sqrt{2\pi v}\right)\\
\\
g(v)=\varphi_{\rm a}(v)+v,\end{array}

(68)

with
a=v_{0}
and
b=+\infty
.

We first consider
f(b)=-{\rm i}\cos\chi(b)J_{0}(r\sqrt{2\pi b}/\lambda_{\rm F})
as

approaches infinity.
Then
\cos\chi(b)=\Delta/l\approx\Delta/R
approaches zero, as does

J_{0}(r\sqrt{2\pi b}/\lambda_{\rm F})
for

(while

Consequently, the term
f(b)\exp[{\rm i}g(b)]/{\rm i}g^{\prime}(b)
vanishes in Eq.
64
for
b=+\infty
.

This leaves only the term
f(a)\exp[{\rm i}g(a)]/{\rm i}g^{\prime}(a)
(where
a=v_{0}
).
We have
g^{\prime}(v_{0})=\varphi^{\prime}_{\rm a}(v_{0})+1
, where here the derivation is made with respect to
v
. We have
\varphi^{\prime}_{\rm a}(v_{0})=({\rm d}\varphi_{\rm a}/{\rm d}R)({\rm d}R/{\rm d}v)(R_{0})

{\rm d}R/{\rm d}v=\lambda_{\rm F}^{2}/\pi R_{0}
and Eq.

\omega_{0}

Appendix B
The stationary phase method

If the function
g(v)
becomes stationary in the interval
[a,b]
, i.e. if there exists a value
v_{\rm s}\in[a,b]
where
g^{\prime}(v_{\rm s})=0
, then Eq.
64
cannot be used. In this case, assuming again that
f(v)
varies much more slowly than
\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]
, most of the contribution to the integral
\int_{a}^{b}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v
comes from a neighborhood of
v_{\rm s}
, a situation known as the stationary phase condition. This neighborhood covers an interval of
v
whose width is a few times
\sqrt{2\pi/|g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})|}
(which is the width of the first Fresnel zone associated with
g
near
v_{\rm s}
). If
v_{\rm s}
is well inside
[a,b]
, in the sense that
v_{\rm s}
is several times
\sqrt{2\pi/|g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})|}
away from both bounds
a
and
b
, then formally (
9
)

\int_{a}^{b}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v\approx(1+{\rm i})f(v_{\rm s})\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})}}\exp[{\rm i}g(v_{\rm s})].

(71)

We note that the modulus of the right-hand term of Eq.
71
is the width of the first Fresnel zone
associated with
g
near
v_{\rm s}
, times
|f(v_{\rm s}|
.
If
g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})>0
(local minimum for
g(v)
),

\int_{a}^{b}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v=(1+{\rm i})f(v_{\rm s})\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})}}\exp[{\rm i}g(v_{\rm s})].

(72)

If
g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})<0
(local maximum for
g(v)
), we take by convention
\sqrt{g^{\prime\prime}}:={\rm i}\sqrt{-g^{\prime\prime}}
, so that

\int_{a}^{b}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v=-{\rm i}(1+{\rm i})f(v_{\rm s})\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{-g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})}}\exp[{\rm i}g(v_{\rm s})].

(73)

Thus, considering a local maximum of
g(v)
instead of a local minimum introduces a
\pi/2
phase shift in the evaluation of the integral, due to the prefactor
-{\rm i}
in the second equation.

As
v_{\rm s}
approaches one of the bounds, say
a
, Eq.
71
is no more valid as an edge effect occurs. Assuming
g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})>0
and
b-a\gg 1/\sqrt{g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{a}^{b}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v\approx&\displaystyle\int_{a}^{v_{\rm s}}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v+\\
\\
&\displaystyle\int_{v_{\rm s}}^{+\infty}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v\end{array}

(74)

The second integral is just the half of the integral in Eq.
71
. The first integral can be evaluated by expanding
g
to the second order near
v_{\rm s}
,
g(v)\approx g(v_{\rm s})+g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})(v-v_{\rm s})^{2}/2
. We define the Fresnel function as

F_{\rm r}(\alpha):=\int_{0}^{\alpha}\exp\left[{\rm i}\left(\frac{\pi t^{2}}{2}\right)\right]{\rm d}t,

(75)

which yields

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{a}^{v_{\rm s}}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v\approx\\
\\
\displaystyle f(v_{\rm s})\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})}}\exp[{\rm i}g(v_{\rm s})]F_{\rm r}\left[\sqrt{\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})}{\pi}}(v_{\rm s}-a)\right],\end{array}

(76)

so that

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(v)\exp[{\rm i}g(v)]{\rm d}v=(1+{\rm i})f(v_{\rm s})\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})}}\exp[{\rm i}g(v_{\rm s})]\times\\
\\
\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{1+{\rm i}}F_{\rm r}\left[\sqrt{\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(v_{\rm s})}{\pi}}(v_{\rm s}-a)\right]\right\}.\end{array}

(77)

The function
1/2+F_{\rm r}(\alpha)/(1+{\rm i})
is the classical normalized complex amplitude of a plane wave diffracted by a sharp opaque rectilinear edge, where
\alpha
is the position of the observer (normalized to the Fresnel scale) relative to the limit of the geometrical shadow. The Sommerfeld lemma applied to
\int_{-\infty}^{\alpha}\exp({\rm i}\pi t^{2}/2){\rm d}t
and
\int_{\alpha}^{+\infty}\exp({\rm i}\pi t^{2}/2){\rm d}t
provides the following asymptotic expressions,

\displaystyle\alpha\rightarrow-\infty,\penalty\ \penalty\ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{F_{\sf r}(\alpha)}{1+{\rm i}}\approx-\left(\frac{1+{\rm i}}{2\pi}\right)\frac{e^{{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha^{2}}}{\alpha}

(78)

\displaystyle\alpha\rightarrow+\infty,\penalty\ \penalty\ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{F_{\sf r}(\alpha)}{1+{\rm i}}\approx 1-\left(\frac{1+{\rm i}}{2\pi}\right)\frac{e^{{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha^{2}}}{\alpha},

(79)

so that

\alpha\rightarrow-\infty,\penalty\ \penalty\ \left|\frac{1}{2}+\frac{F_{\sf r}(\alpha)}{1+{\rm i}}\right|^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}}

(80)

\alpha\rightarrow+\infty,\penalty\ \penalty\ \left|\frac{1}{2}+\frac{F_{\sf r}(\alpha)}{1+{\rm i}}\right|^{2}\approx 1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi\alpha}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha^{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)+\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}}.

(81)

Eq.
80
shows that the flux decays like
1/\alpha^{2}
inside the shadow, while Eq.
81
shows that the Fresnel fringe visibility damps like
1/\alpha
outside the shadow. Numerical integrations show that the approximations in Eqs.
80
and
81
agree with their respective exact values
to within 1% for
\alpha\lesssim-2.6
and
\alpha\gtrsim+2.2
, respectively.

Appendix E
Effects of limb darkening

We consider a subdisk of radius
\rho
nested inside the stellar disk of radius
r_{*}
. From Eqs.
57
and
61
, the contribution of the subdisk to the surface areas of the two stellar images is

S(\rho)=C\rho f\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right),

(85)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}C=8R\Phi_{\perp}(0)\\
\\
f(s)=E(1/s){\rm\penalty\ for\penalty\ }s\geq 1\\
\\
f(s)=G(s){\rm\penalty\ for\penalty\ }s\leq 1.\\
\end{array}

(86)

Consequently, an annulus of width
{\rm d}\rho
of the star provides a surface area of the images that amounts to

{\rm d}S=C\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d\rho}}\left[\rho f\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)\right]{\rm d}\rho.

(87)

The limb darkening of the stellar disk is described by a radiance profile
I(\rho/r_{*})
. Weighing the surface area
{\rm d}S
of each annulus by
I(\rho/r_{*})
and normalizing it to the total flux received from the unocculted star, we obtain the normalized central flash profile

\phi_{\rm LD}(r)=\phi_{\rm Star}(r)\frac{r_{*}}{2f(r_{*}/r)}\frac{\int_{0}^{r_{*}}I(\rho/r_{*})\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}\rho}\left[\rho f(\rho/r)\right]{\rm d}\rho}{\int_{0}^{r_{*}}I(\rho/r_{*})\rho{\rm d}\rho},

(88)

where
\phi_{\rm Star}(r)=Cf(r_{*}/r)/(\pi r*)
is the normalized flux that would be observed without limb darkening (Eqs.
57
and
61
). Using the variable
u=\rho/r_{*}
and integrating by parts the numerator of Eq.
88
, we obtain

\phi_{\rm LD}(r)=\frac{\phi_{\rm Star}(r)}{2\int_{0}^{1}I(u)u{\rm d}u}\left[I(1)-\frac{\int_{0}^{1}f(r_{*}u/r)I^{\prime}(u)u{\rm d}u}{f(r_{*}/r)}\right],

(89)

where
I^{\prime}={\rm d}I/{\rm d}u
. For a uniform stellar disk (
I^{\prime}(u)\equiv 0
), we retrieve the expected result
\Phi_{\rm LD}(r)=\phi_{\rm Star}(r)
.

In the limiting case
r\gg r_{*}
, we have
f(\rho/r)=G(\rho/r)\approx\pi\rho/(4r)
. Using this approximation in Eq.
88
, we obtain
\Phi_{\rm LD}(r)\approx\phi_{\rm Star}(r)
. Thus, the limb darkening has no effect on the flash profile for
r\gg r_{*}
. This is expected since in this case the surface elements of the stellar disk are uniformly distorted by the atmosphere.

The peak value of the flash is obtained by making
r=0
in Eq.
88
. Then
f(\rho/r)=E(r/\rho)=E(0)=\pi/2
. Injecting this value in Eq.
88
, we get

\phi_{\rm LD}(0)=\phi_{\rm Star}(0)\frac{\int_{0}^{1}I(u){\rm d}u}{2\int_{0}^{1}I(u)u{\rm d}u}.

(90)

Elementary calculations show that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{0}^{1}I(u){\rm d}u=&2\int_{0}^{1}I(u)u{\rm d}u+\\
\\
&\int_{0}^{1/2}[I(u)-I(1-u)](1-2u){\rm d}u.\\
\end{array}

(91)

In the last integral, we have
1-2u\geq 0
. Moreover, the limb darkening causes a monotonic decrease of

I(u)
between 0 and 1, so that
I(u)\geq I(1-u)
for
u\in[0,1/2]
. Consequently,
\int_{0}^{1}I(u){\rm d}u\geq 2\int_{0}^{1}I(u)u{\rm d}u
and
\phi_{\rm LD}(0)\geq\phi_{\rm Star}(0)
. In other words, for a given stellar radius
r_{*}
, the limb darkening increases the height of the flash compared to the case without limb darkening. This is expected since the limb darkening causes a concentration of light near the star center, reducing its effective radius.

The various integrals appearing in Eqs.
89
-
90
can be evaluated by using an expression of the form
(
4
;
3
)

I(u)=1-\sum_{k=1}^{4}c_{k}(1-\mu^{k/2}),{\rm\penalty\ where\penalty\ }\mu=\sqrt{1-u^{2}}.

(92)

This form introduces infinite slopes of
I(u)
at
u=1
, and a singularity for
I^{\prime}(u)
at that point. In order to avoid the resulting numerical difficulties when evaluating the integrals in Eqs.
89
and
90
, we can use the change of variable
v=(1-u)^{1/4}
, so that

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f\left(\frac{r_{*}}{r}u\right)I^{\prime}(u)u{\rm d}u=\\
\\
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{4}2kc_{k}\int_{0}^{1}f\left[\frac{r_{*}}{r}(1-v^{4})\right](1-v^{4})^{2}(2-v^{4})^{\frac{k}{4}-1}v^{k-1}{\rm d}v.\end{array}

(93)

Likewise, we can use the change of variable
v=(1-u)^{5/8}
, to obtain

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}I(u){\rm d}u=&\displaystyle\left(1-\sum_{k=1}^{4}c_{k}\right)+\\
\\
&\displaystyle\frac{8}{5}\sum_{k=1}^{4}c_{k}\int_{0}^{1}(2-v^{\frac{8}{5}})^{\frac{k}{4}}v^{\frac{2k+3}{5}}{\rm d}v,\end{array}

(94)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}I(u)u{\rm d}u=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\sum_{k=1}^{4}c_{k}\right)+\\
\\
&\displaystyle\frac{8}{5}\sum_{k=1}^{4}c_{k}\int_{0}^{1}(2-v^{\frac{8}{5}})^{\frac{k}{4}}(1-v^{\frac{8}{5}})v^{\frac{2k+3}{5}}{\rm d}v.\end{array}

(95)
