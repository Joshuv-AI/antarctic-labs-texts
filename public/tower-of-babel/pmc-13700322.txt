Full-length transcriptome analysis reveals candidate genes involved in phenylpropanoid pathway in Euphorbia maculata
Authors: Sanbao Guo, Meiling Song, Mingming Gui, Wuhua Yu, Qingyang Wu, Zechang Rao, Zirun Zhang, Chunxiang Chen, Shenghe Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEuphorbia maculata is a herbaceous plant in China, known for its medicinal properties. The main active components of E. maculata are flavonoids. However, the biosynthesis mechanism of flavonoids in E. maculata remains poorly understood due to the limited availability of genetic information. In order to explore the flavonoid biosynthesis mechanism of this species, we carried our study.ResultsA total of 20,238 non-redundant transcript isoforms were acquired with an N50 length of 2,994 bp and an average length of 2,766.82 bp. Of these isoforms, 20,069 (99.16%) were successfully annotated in NR, Swissprot, KOG, GO, and KEGG databases. Additionally, 20,085 coding sequences (CDSs), 117 long noncoding RNAs (LncRNAs), and 971 transcription factors (TFs) were identified from the full-length transcriptome data. The RNA-Seq data was then analyzed using the full-length transcriptome as a reference sequence, revealing 27 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) involved in phenylpropanoid pathway in E. maculata. These included 15 PAL, 5 C4H, 4 4CL, and 3 CHS genes. The expression levels of these DEGs were found to vary in the leaf and stem across five different periods, with the majority of phenylpropanoid pathway genes being upregulated in the stem compared to the leaf at the same period. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis revealed that the bHLH, HD-ZIP, bZIP, MYB, FAR1, and AP2 may regulate the expression of 5 C4H and 3 CHS. Furthermore, through analyzing the correlation between the expression levels of DEGs and the accumulation of flavonoids in various tissues of E. maculata at different stages, we found that 2 PAL, 2 C4H, 1 4CL, and 2 CHS may play a crucial role in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis in this species.ConclusionsOur study enriched the genomic resources and revealed candidate genes involved in phenylpropanoid pathway in E. maculata. These findings provide candidate gene resources for further investigation of the flavonoid biosynthesis mechanism in E. maculata.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13213-9.Keywords: Euphorbia maculata, Full-length transcriptome, Flavonoid biosynthesis, Gene annotation, Differentially expressed genes

Introduction

E. maculata, an annual herbaceous plant belonging to the Euphorbiaceae family and Euphorbia genus, is widely distributed in China [1]. As a traditional Chinese medicine, E. maculata has been widely used to treat various diseases such as dysentery, diarrhea, hemoptysis, hematuria, hematochezia, and metrorrhagia [2, 3]. This species is rich in flavonoids, terpenoids, phenolic acids, and alkaloids. Among these chemical components, flavonols (belonging to flavonoids, including quercetin, kaempferol, myricetin, and their glycosides) are the main medicinal components in E. maculata, with a wide range of pharmacological effects including anti-bacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidation, anti-tumor, and coagulation activities [4–6]. In fact, quercetin is the only indicator component used to determine the quality of E. maculata in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, as it is present in relatively high levels [2]. However, the quality of commercially available E. maculata varies greatly, making it difficult to ensure its effectiveness in clinical applications. This is due to various factors such as different varieties, genetic characteristics, origin, and harvest season. Unfortunately, the lack of genetic information has hindered the development of basic research on this plant, including cloning, identification, and regulation of key genes involved in the biosynthesis of active ingredients.

Flavonoids are synthesized in plants through the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, and the key enzymes involved in this process have been identified [7, 8]. This process involves the catalysis of phenylalanine by PAL, C4H, 4CL and CHS to produce naringenin chalcone, which serves as the starting material for flavonoid biosynthesis. Subsequently, naringenin chalcone is catalyzed by late synthesis enzymes such as CHI, F3H, F3’H, F3’5’H, FLS, FNS, DFR, ANS, ANR, and LAR to generate various flavonoids (Fig. 1). Therefore, CHS is considered to be the first key enzyme in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. The active ingredients are an important indicator for evaluating the quality of medicinal herbs. Flavonoids are the main active ingredients in E. maculata. However, the biosynthesis of flavonoids in E. maculata remains poorly understood. Therefore, it is crucial to identify candidate genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis not only to clarify the accumulation of flavonoids in E. maculata but also to improve its varieties by biotechnology.

In the previous studies, we carried out RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis and revealed candidate genes involved in quercetin biosynthesis in E. maculata [9]. However, we found that the RNA-seq only generated short reads with amplification bias, which limits the acquisition of high-quality full-length transcript sequences and may lead to the loss of some important information. With the development of sequencing technology, the single molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing technology carried out in the PacBio Sequel Ⅱ system provided a new platform for third-generation sequencing. SMRT sequencing generates long read lengths and high consensus accuracy compared to RNA-Seq [10]. Currently, SMRT sequencing is widely used in the studies of gene expression, metabolic regulation, and genetic diversity in plants [10, 11]. Therefore, in this study, we used SMRT sequencing to obtain a more comprehensive and accurate full-length transcriptome data for E. maculata.

Liu et al. reported that the content of total flavonoids varied among three different periods, with the highest content during the early flowering period and the lowest content during the vegetative period [12]. However, due to limited genetic information available for E. maculata, the potential molecular mechanism of variations in total flavonoids content remains poorly understood. To address this gap, the present study aimed to sequence the full-length transcriptome of E. maculata from two different tissues at different stages of growth. Functional annotation of the full-length transcriptomes was then performed, and the CDSs, LncRNAs, and TFs were identified. Furthermore, to gain insight into the molecular mechanism of total flavonoids biosynthesis in E. maculata, we determined the total flavonoid content in various tissues of E. maculata at different growth stages, and analyzed the correlation between the total flavonoid content and the expression levels of DEGs involved in the phenylpropanoid pathway. This study provides valuable genetic resources for investigating the biosynthesis of active ingredients in E. maculata and lays the groundwork for developing varieties with improved quality through genetic engineering.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

E. maculata was cultivated in the experimental field of Fuzhou Medical College (E116°39′, N27°95′, Fuzhou, China), and the plant seeds were sourced from Jiangxi Tianshikang Ecological Chinese Herbal Medicine Planting Co., Ltd. (Yingtan, China). The plant materials were identified by Associate Professor Canhui Tang. Leaves and stems were collected at different stages of growth (1st, 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th days after anthesis) and designated as LT1, LT20, LT40, LT60, LT80, ST1, ST20, ST40, ST60, and ST80. Each sample consisted of a mixture of at least 3 plants. The samples used for transcriptome sequencing were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 ℃. For the extraction experiments of total flavonoids, the samples were first dried to constant weight at 60 ℃, then crushed in a grinder, and finally packed in polyethylene bags before being stored at -80 ℃.

RNA extraction

Ten RNA samples were extracted from the leaves and stems using the OmniPlant RNA Kit (Cowin Biotech, Taizhou, China). The purity and concentration of the RNA were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and the contamination and integrity of the RNA were assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis and Agilent 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA).

PacBio SMRT library construction, sequencing, and analysis

Equal quantities of qualified RNA from the ten individual samples were pooled. The mixed RNA was used to construct the full-length transcriptome library with SMRTbell® prep Kit 3.0 (Pacifc Biosciences, California, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and the qualified library was sequenced on PacBio Sequel Ⅱ platform (Pacifc Biosciences, California, USA). SMRT Link V8.0.0 [13] was utilized to process raw reads. First, the high-quality CCS (circular consensus sequence) reads were extracted from the offline data. Next, the FLNC (full-length non-chimeric) reads were identified using Isoseq3.1 software by searching for the 5’ primer, 3’ primer and poly A tail signal in CCSs. The similar FLNC reads were hierarchically clustered using Minimap2 software to generate unpolished consensus isoforms, followed by the application of the Quiver algorithm for further unpolished consensus isoforms correction. The high-quality isoforms (HQ isoforms, prediction accuracy ≥ 0.99) were obtained according to the accuracy of the output sequence. Finally, the final transcriptome isoform sequences were obtained by removing the redundant sequences with CD-HIT-v4.6.7 software.

The full-length transcripts were annotated using five databases, including NR [14], SwissProt [15], KEGG [16], KOG [17], and GO [18] databases. The transcription factors (TFs) were annotated using hmmscan alignment of the sequences against the plant transcription factor database (PlantTFDB) [19]. CPC [20], CNCI [21], PLEK [22], and Pfam database [23] were used to predict the long noncoding RNAs (LncRNAs) from isoforms that were not annotated in the above five databases.

Illumina RNA-Seq library construction, sequencing, and DEGs analysis

The qualified RNA from the ten individual samples was used to construct Illumina transcriptome libraries using the Hieff NGS® Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The ten qualified libraries were then sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). The full-length transcriptome of E. maculata was used as a reference sequence to analyze the data from the Illumina RNA-Seq. The expression levels of each transcript were quantified using the fragment per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) method. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software [24]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute fold change (FC) > 2. The identified DEGs were then annotated using the GO and KEGG databases.

CDS analysis

The isoforms of E. maculata were aligned using Blastx against the NR, SwissProt, KEGG, and KOG databases (evalue < 1 × 10− 5) in order of priority, and the protein with the highest degree of similarity was selected as the coding sequences (CDSs). Subsequently, the CDSs were translated into the corresponding amino acid sequence. The unaligned isoforms were analyzed using ANGEL [25] software to identify the CDSs and translate them into the amino acid sequences.

PPI analysis

TFs and DEGs were mapped into the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) to predict their relationship. A combined-score of 400 was used as the cut-off criterion for constructing the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. The higher the score, the stronger the role of TFs in regulating DEGs expression.

RT-qPCR analysis

The Spin Column Plant Total RNA Purification Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) was used to extract RNA from ten samples following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA contamination, purity, and concentration were detected using agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). The high-quality RNA was then used to synthesize cDNA through reverse transcription using the PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). EmGDI1 was used as the internal reference gene, and RT-qPCR amplification was performed with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ Ⅱ (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) on a CFX Connect™ Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, California, USA). The specific primers (Table 1) were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software. The conditions of PCR amplification reaction were as follows: 95℃ for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 30 s, 57℃ for 30 s, and 72℃ for 30 s. Each selected gene was subjected to three biological and technical replicates, respectively. The relative expression levels of the selected genes were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Determination of total flavonoid content

Total flavonoid was extracted using a modified method [2]: the powder of stems and leaves from E. maculata at different stages were each added to 50 mL of 80% methanol and sonicated at room temperature for 10 min, and then extracted at 65 ℃ for 1.5 h. After extraction, extracts were filtered and stored at -20 ℃.

A modified method [26] was used to determine the total flavonoid content in each sample: samples solution (1 mL), 5% NaNO2 (w/v, 1 mL) and water (6 mL) were mixed for 6 min, and then 10% AlCl3 (w/v, 1 mL) was added and mixed altogether. After 6 min, 4% NaOH (w/v, 10 mL) was added. The solutions were then diluted to 25 mL with methanol. Ten minutes later, the absorbance of the solutions was measured using a spectrophotometer (A590, Aoe Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 510 nm. A standard curve was plotted using rutin (National Institutes for Food and Drug Control, Beijing, China) as a standard. Different concentrations of rutin were prepared, and their absorbance was measured at 510 nm using spectrophotometer. The results were expressed as mg rutin/g dry weight by comparing them to the standard curve of rutin.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS26.0 software, with one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan post-hoc test. The results were presented as the mean ± SE, and P < 0.05 was used to determine significant differences.

Results

Full-length transcripts analysis of E. maculata

Full-length transcriptome sequencing was performed on various tissues of E. maculata at different developmental stages. The raw data were analyzed using SMRT Link V8.0.0 software, yielding a total of 15,311,374 subreads with an N50 length of 3,018 bp and a mean length of 2,789 bp. From the offline data, 287,073 high-quality CCS reads were extracted, exhibiting a mean length of 2,921 bp and an average number of passes amounting to 38. The distribution in terms of quantity and length for the CCS reads is illustrated in Fig. 2A. Isoseq3.1 software was employed to identify FLNCs within the CCS reads; this process included clustering and correction that resulted in the identification of 24,960 high-quality isoforms. By utilizing CD-HIT-v4.6.7 software to eliminate redundant sequences, we obtained a total of 20,238 non-redundant transcript isoforms which served as our reference transcriptome. The lengths of these isoforms ranged from 280 to 9,805 bp (Fig. 2B), with an N50 length measuring at 2,994 bp and an average length calculated at approximately 2,766.82 bp; additionally, the GC content was determined to be 43.90% (Table 2).

Functional annotation of full-length transcripts

The non-redundant transcript isoforms were aligned against the NR, Swissprot, KOG, GO, and KEGG databases using BlastX. As a result, 20,069 isoforms (99.16%) were annotated in at least one of the five public databases, while 169 isoforms (0.84%) remained unannotated. Notably, 13,424 isoforms (66.33%) received annotations across all five databases. The majority of isoforms were annotated in the NR database, amounting to 19,975 (98.70%), followed by KEGG with 19,779 (97.73%), SwissProt with 18,111(89.49%), GO with 17,584 (86.89%), and KOG with 14,835 (73.30%) (Fig. 3A).

E KEGG pathway enrichment of full-length transcripts

The NR database was employed to evaluate the similarity between the transcriptome of E. maculata and other species, resulting in matches with a total of 250 species. Notably, E. maculata exhibited the highest sequence similarity with Hevea brasiliensis, by sharing 5,905 sequences (29.56%), followed with Jatropha curcas (4,116, 20.61%), Ricinus communis (3,843, 19.24%), Manihot esculenta (3,336, 16.70%), and Quercus suber (459, 2.30%) (Fig. 3B).

The full-length transcripts from E. maculata were classified into three major categories and 50 subcategories within the GO database (Fig. 3C). In the biological process term, a significant proportion of isoforms fell under “Cellular process” (14,334) and “Metabolic process” (12,351). “Cellular anatomical entity” (11,476) and “Protein-containing complex” (4,919) dominated in the cellular component term. A substantial number of isoforms also categorized under molecular functions included “Binding” (13,002) and “Catalytic activity” (10,928).

A total of 14,835 isoforms were categorized into 25 groups within the KOG database (Fig. 3D). The category with the highest representation was “General function prediction only” (3,036, 20.47%). Notable proportions of isoforms were also classified under “Signal transduction mechanisms” (2,983, 20.11%), “Posttranslational modification, protein turnover, chaperones” (1,772, 11.94%), “Transcription” (1,057, 7.13%), and “Intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport” (1,052, 7.09%). Furthermore, 783 isoforms (5.28%) were annotated to “Secondary metabolites biosynthesis, transport and catabolism”, which may be associated with the synthesis, transport and metabolism of flavonoid in E. maculata.

In the KEGG pathway analysis, 19,779 isoforms from E. maculata were annotated in the KEGG database and mapped to 135 KEGG pathways. The top three pathways identified included “Metabolic pathways” (ko01100, 3,009), “Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites” (ko01110, 1,766), and “Carbon metabolism” (ko01200, 568) ( Fig. 3E). A focused investigation on flavonoid biosynthesis revealed that 65 isoforms mapped to “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” (ko00940), 20 isoforms mapped to “Flavonoid biosynthesis” (ko00941), and 6 isoforms mapped to “Isoflavonoid biosynthesis” (ko00943).

Identification of CDSs, lncRNA and TFs

Blastx and ANGEL software were used to predict the CDSs of the E. maculata transcriptome, resulting in the identification of a total of 20,085 CDSs. The length distribution predominantly ranged from 1000 to 3000 bp (14,853, 73.95%) (Fig. 4A). The identification of LncRNAs was performed on unannotated full-length transcripts, leading to the discovery of a total of 117 LncRNAs through the integration of four computational methods: CPC, CNCI, PLEK, and Pfam (Fig. 4B). Given the critical role that TFs play in plant growth and development as well as their regulation in secondary metabolite biosynthesis, isoforms were aligned using hmmscan against PlantTFDB. Consequently, a total of 971 TFs were identified and classified into 41 distinct TF families. Among these families, the ARF (91, 9.37%), C3H (83, 8.55%), and GRAS (81, 8.34%) ranked in the top three. Additionally, bHLH, C2H2, and HD-ZIP families each accounted for more than 5% of the overall TF population (Fig. 4C).

Identification and functional annotation of DEGs in the same tissue at different periods

Differential expression analysis across five developmental stages in both stem and leaf tissues of E. maculata revealed twenty comparison groups: LT1-vs-LT20, LT1-vs-LT40, LT1-vs-LT60, LT1-vs-LT80, LT20-vs-LT40, LT20-vs-LT60, LT20-vs-LT80, LT40-vs-LT60, LT40-vs-LT80, LT60-vs-LT80, ST1-vs-ST20, ST1-vs-ST40, ST1-vs-ST60, ST1-vs-ST80, ST20-vs-ST40, ST20-vs-ST60, ST20-vs-ST80, ST40-vs-ST60, ST40-vs-ST80, and ST60-vs-ST80. Among these twenty comparison groups, the highest number of DEGs were observed in the LT20-vs-LT40 (2,633 upregulated and 3,578 downregulated), followed by ST1-vs-ST80 (2,391 upregulated and 3,257 downregulated), LT1-vs-LT20 (3,186 upregulated and 2,298 downregulated), and LT20-vs-LT80 (2,660 upregulated and 2,577 downregulated). These results indicate that DEGs are more abundant when comparing closer developmental periods in stems while fewer DEGs are noted among closely related developmental periods in leaves (Fig. 5).

The statistics of DEGs in E. maculata

To explore the functions of DEGs in the same tissues at different periods, GO enrichment analysis was performed. In the comparison groups LT1-vs-LT20, LT20-vs-LT60, LT20-vs-LT80, LT60-vs-LT80, and ST1-vs-ST60, a significant proportion of DEGs were enriched in the GO categories “Intrinsic component of membrane” (GO: 0031224, Fig. S1A), “Sulfur compound metabolic process” (GO: 0006790, Fig. S1F), “Small molecule metabolic process” (GO: 0044281, Fig. S1G), “Plastid” (GO: 0009536, Fig. S1J), and “Carbohydrate transmembrane transporter activity” (GO: 0015144, Fig. S1M), respectively. In the comparison groups LT1-vs-LT40, ST1-vs-ST40, and ST40-vs-ST80, most DEGs were associated with the GO term “Transmembrane transporter activity” (GO: 0022857, Fig. S1B, L, and S). For the comparison groups LT1-vs-LT60, LT1-vs-LT80, LT20-vs-LT40, LT40-vs-LT60, LT40-vs-LT80, and ST20-vs-ST80, the major GO term was “Catalytic activity” (GO: 0003824, Fig. S1C, D, E, H, I, and Q). Meanwhile, in the comparison groups ST1-vs-ST20, ST1-vs-ST80, and ST40-vs-ST60, the main GO term was “Anion transport” (GO: 0006820, Fig. S1K, N, and R). In the comparison groups ST20-vs-ST40, ST20-vs-ST60, and ST60-vs-ST80, numerous DEGs were associated with the GO term “Carbohydrate metabolic process” (GO: 0005975, Fig. S1O, P, and T).

The DEGs from each comparison group were aligned against the KEGG database for further analysis. Among these comparisons, the DEGs of LT20-vs-LT40 comparison group were mapped to the most KEGG pathways (131), followed by LT20-vs-LT60 (130), LT1-vs-LT20 (129), and ST1-vs-ST80 (128) comparison groups, and the DEGs of ST20-vs-ST40 comparison group were mapped to the fewest KEGG pathways (98). The top 20 enriched pathways in each comparison group showed that many DEGs were enriched in pathways associated with “Metabolic pathways”, “Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites”, “Starch and sucrose metabolism” (ko00500), “ABC transporters” (ko02010), and “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” (Fig. S2). These results suggest a potential relationship between the functions of DEGs and the developmental periods of E. maculata in the same tissue.

DEGs in the phenylpropanoid pathway analysis

Flavonoids constitute the primary pharmacologically active compounds in E. maculata, with their biosynthetic pathway and key enzymatic components having been elucidated [7, 8]. Using the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway from KEGG and relevant literature [27, 28], we predicted the genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis in E. maculata (Fig. 1). According to the criteria of | log2 FC | > 1 and FDR < 0.05, 27 DEGs involved in phenylpropanoid pathway were identified in the full-length transcriptome of E. maculata, including 15 PAL (KO ID: K10775), 5 C4H (KO ID: K00487), 4 4CL (KO ID: K01904), and 3 CHS (KO ID: K00660). Flavonoids are synthesized via the phenylpropanoid pathway, with the initial three steps involving PAL, C4H, and 4CL. In our analysis of a single tissue type, expression levels of most PAL and C4H were significantly higher on day 60 post-anthesis compared to other time points examined. The expression patterns of 4CL exhibited variability between leaf and stem tissues across five distinct developmental stages. Comparing the FPKM values of the three CHS transcripts in the same tissue at different periods, the results showed that Isoform0017283 displayed an initial upregulation followed by downregulation. Conversely, Isoform0018609 and Isoform0019438 demonstrated a trend of gradual decline throughout the development of E. maculata. Notably, the majority of phenylpropanoid pathway genes were upregulated in the stem compared to the leaf at the same period (Fig. 6).

Analysis of PPI between TFs and DEGs

To identify the TFs that regulate the expression of phenylpropanoid pathway DEGs in E. maculata, we used the STRING online platform to predict PPI between TFs and DEGs. Analysis revealed that the TF families involved in the regulation of CHS expression were the most, including bHLH, HD-ZIP, bZIP, MYB, FAR1, and AP2. Among them, 3 bHLH (Isoform0012893, Isoform0012910, and Isoform0017396) had the highest combined-score of 848 with CHS, indicating that they may play a crucial role in regulating CHS expression. Furthermore, we found that these 3 bHLH isoforms were the only ones related to the regulation of C4H with a combined-score of 400 as the cut-off criterion, and no TFs regulating PAL and 4CL were identified (Table S1).

RT-qPCR validation of DEGs in the phenylpropanoid pathway

CHS is the primary key enzyme in flavonoid biosynthesis, facilitating the condensation of trimolecular malonyl-CoA and 4-coumaroyl-CoA to yield chalcone. In the previous studies, we cloned 4CL1b (GenBank accession number OR826275), CHS1 (GenBank accession number ON652865), and CHS3 (GenBank accession number PV974498) based on transcriptome data [29]. RT-qPCR was employed to assess the expression levels of these 3 genes in the stem at five different stages (Fig. 7). The relative expressions of CHS1, CHS3 and 4CL1b were compared with the FPKM values corresponding to their respective transcripts. Analysis indicated that the trends in relative expression and FPKM values were largely consistent across both pairwise comparisons (4CL1b-vs-Isoform0016693, CHS1-vs-Isoform0018609, and CHS3-vs-Isoform0017283). Previous research also measured the relative expression levels of C4H (with its corresponding transcript being Isoform0017862, GenBank accession number MT951411), CHS1, and CHS2 (GenBank accession number OP751804) in both the stem and leaf of E. maculata over various periods, and the results showed that expression levels were consistently higher in the stem than in the leaf at the same period [29–31]. Comparative analysis among C4H, 4CL1b, CHS1, CHS2, and CHS3 revealed a basically consistent expression pattern between RT-qPCR findings and RNA-seq data, underscoring the reliability of transcriptomic sequencing outcomes.

Analysis of total flavonoid content

To investigate the distribution of total flavonoid in E. maculata, the content of total flavonoid in various tissues at different developmental stages was measured using ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometry. The results showed that the leaf had a higher total flavonoid content compared to the stem at the same stage (Fig. 8). In the leaf, the total flavonoid content was highest at T80 and lowest at T20, with a increasing trend as E. maculata matured. In the stem, the content of total flavonoid was highest at T1 and lowest at T20.

Discussion

The phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway is a crucial secondary metabolic pathway in plants, playing a significant role in plant development and response to environmental stress [11]. This pathway utilizes phenylalanine as a substrate to synthesize various secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, lignin, and aromatic compounds, through the catalysis of a series of enzymes [32]. SMRT sequencing offers researchers an economical approach for annotating the transcriptomes of non-model crops, which will greatly enhance future research on less-studied plant species. This sequencing technology has been successfully applied across multiple plant species for functional annotation and analysis of the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, such as Atraphaxis bracteata [33], Lonicera japonica [34], and Toxicodendron vernicifluum [35].

In this study, we combined SMRT sequencing with RNA-seq to generate a comprehensive transcriptome from various tissues of E. maculata at different developmental stages. This effort resulted in the identification of 20,238 non-redundant transcript isoforms with an N50 length of 2,994 bp and an average length of 2,766.82 bp. These transcripts demonstrate significantly higher integrity and quality compared to those previously reported for this species using RNA-seq (N50 length: 1,721 bp; mean length: 1,004 bp) [9]. E. maculata exhibited the highest sequence similarity with Hevea brasiliensis (29.56%) when analyzed against NR databases; furthermore, 20,069 isoforms (99.16%) were successfully annotated in at least one of five public databases—substantially surpassing the annotation rates observed for Chimonobambusa sichuanensis (85.03%) [36], Glechoma longituba (85.56%) [37], and Blumea balsamifera (92.18%) [38]. These findings significantly enrich the genomic resources available for E. maculata and will contribute to further investigations into gene functions within this species.

A total of 117 LncRNAs were identified by CPC, CNCI, PLEK, and Pfam. LncRNAs involved in the induction of flavonoid biosynthesis have been identified in a variety of plants [39]. Through LncRNA-Seq sequencing, 11,742 LncRNAs were identified from sucrose-treated tea plant, and analysis found that some LncRNAs not only target structural genes (CsCHIc, CsF3’5’Hb) involved in flavonoid biosynthesis but also regulate TFs (CsMYB, CsbHLH) involved in flavonoid biosynthesis [40]. In Ginkgo biloba, 1,270 LncRNAs were identified through strand-specific RNA sequencing, and it was releaved that LncRNAs regulate target genes (PAL, CHI, F3H, ANR) to participate in flavonoid biosynthesis [41]. The expression level of 4CL in Populus tomentosa was regulated by LncRNA [42]. It is possible that LncRNAs also play a role in the flavonoid biosynthesis of E. maculata by regulating related genes. Further investigation into their regulatory effects is planned for the future.

It has been reported that TFs play a crucial role in the biosynthesis of flavonoid by regulating the expression of structural genes [8, 43]. Anthocyanins (belonging to flavonoids, including pelargonidin, cyanidin and delphinidin etc.) are synthesized through a specific branch of the flavonoid metabolic pathway. In studies investigating anthocyanins biosynthesis in Rosaceae species, it was observed that the expression levels of MYB10 genes are closely linked to the concentrations of anthocyanins in both fruits and flowers, and this correlation has been validated through research conducted on tobacco, strawberry, and apple [44]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, overexpression of PabHLH1 from Plagiochasma appendiculatum enhanced the expression of structural genes associated with both early and late stages of flavonoid biosynthesis leading to increased accumulation of flavonoid [45]. Additionally, studies have shown that transcription factors such as HD-ZIP and bZIP can also regulate flavonoid biosynthesis [46, 47]. Our study identified a total of 971 transcription factors within E. maculata. PPI analysis revealed that the bHLH, HD-ZIP, bZIP, MYB, FAR1, and AP2 may regulate the expression of 5 C4H and 3 CHS. Further study is needed to fully understand the mechanism of regulating flavonoid biosynthesis in E. maculata through the co-expression of these TFs with C4H and CHS. These findings establish a foundational basis for future investigations into the transcriptional regulation governing flavonoid metabolism in E. maculata.

It is well established that the expression levels of genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis significantly influence flavonoid accumulation. For example, CHS expression levels exhibit a positive correlation with flavonoid content across various tissues and developmental stages in citrus species [48]. Similarly, the upregulation of phenylalanine PAL, C4H, and 4CL genes has been shown to enhance total flavonoid accumulation in pakchoi [49]. Moreover, alterations in the expression of key structural genes, encompassing both early and late biosynthetic genes within the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, have been implicated in peel discoloration phenomena [50]. To investigate the correlation between the expression of genes involved in the phenylpropanoid pathway and the accumulation of total flavonoid in E. maculata, we identified 27 DEGs (including 15 PAL, 5 C4H, 4 4CL, and 3 CHS genes) associated with the phenylpropanoid pathway and determined the content of total flavonoid in various tissues at different stages. Our findings indicate that the majority of these genes were upregulated in stem relative to leaf at the same growth stage. Notably, the expression profiles of these phenylpropanoid pathway genes in E. maculata parallel those observed in Fagopyrum dibotrys [51] and Acorus gramineus [52]. Comparative analyses of C4H, 4CL1b, CHS1, CHS2, and CHS3 revealed concordant expression patterns between RT-qPCR validation and RNA sequencing data, thereby affirming the reliability of the transcriptomic dataset. Furthermore, we revealed that the expression trends of the 2 PAL (Isoform0011671 and Isoform0014970) and 2 C4H (Isoform0017224 and Isoform0017706) were basically consistent with the accumulation trends of flavonoid as the leaves of E. maculata matured, suggesting that these 4 genes involved in the phenylpropanoid pathway may play a crucial role in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis in the leaves of E. maculata. Similarly, the expression trends of 1 4CL (Isoform0015705) and 2 CHS (Isoform0018609 and Isoform0019438) were basically consistent with the accumulation trends of flavonoid as the stems of E. maculata matured, indicating that these 3 genes may also be key regulatory genes of flavonoid biosynthesis in the stems. Combined with the results of PPI analysis, we speculated that 3 bHLH (Isoform0012893, Isoform0012910, and Isoform0017396) may increase the accumulation of flavonoid by regulating the expression levels of 2 C4H (Isoform0017224 and Isoform0017706) and 2 CHS (Isoform0018609 and Isoform0019438). These results establish a foundational framework for subsequent investigations into the mechanisms of flavonoid biosynthesis in E. maculata.

Conclusion

In summary, the integration of SMRT sequencing and RNA-seq was employed to investigate multiple tissues of E. maculata across distinct developmental stages. A total of 20,238 non-redundant transcript isoforms, 20,085 CDSs, 117 LncRNAs, 971 TFs, and 27 DEGs associated with phenylpropanoid pathway were identified. Notably, 20,069 isoforms (99.16%) were successfully annotated using the NR, SwissProt, KOG, GO, and KEGG databases. Furthermore, we analyzed the relationship between the expression levels of DEGs and the accumulation of flavonoid in various tissues of E. maculata at different periods, and found that 2 PAL and 2 C4H may play key roles in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis in leaf, while 1 4CL and 2 CHS may be key regulatory genes of flavonoid biosynthesis in stem. These findings offer valuable genetic resources for advancing studies on genetic diversity, molecular identification, and the biosynthetic mechanisms of bioactive compounds in E. maculata. Moreover, this work establishes a foundation for future biotechnological efforts aimed at enhancing the total flavonoid content in this species.
