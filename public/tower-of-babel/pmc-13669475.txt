Comparability of food frequency questionnaires between Korean Children’s Environmental Health Study (Ko-CHENS) and Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) in pregnant women
Authors: Minsu Cho, Bom Kim, Hyesook Kim, Sunghun Na, Se Jin Lee, Tae Gyu Ahn, Kyung Ho Son, Woo Jin Kim, Jung Eun Lee, Ko-CHENS Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESThe Korean Children’s Environmental Health Study (Ko-CHENS) developed a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) to assess maternal dietary intake during pregnancy. This FFQ shares high structural similarity with the validated FFQ of the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), a representative dietary assessment tool for the Korean adult population, which includes 97 of the 112 items in the Ko-CHENS FFQ. The comparability of the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs among pregnant women was evaluated.SUBJECTS/METHODSNinety-four women recruited from Kangwon National University Hospital between December 2024 and February 2025 were included. The participants consecutively completed the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs. The nutrient intakes were adjusted for the total energy intake using the residual method. Log-transformed intakes of energy, nutrients, and food groups were compared using the correlation coefficients, cross-classification percent agreement, weighted kappa statistics, and Bland–Altman plots.RESULTSThe Ko-CHENS FFQ yielded higher absolute intake estimates for energy and the majority of nutrients and food groups than the KoGES FFQ. The Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients ranged from 0.34 (vitamin E) to 0.68 (β-carotene) for energy and nutrients and from 0.15 (sugars and jams) to 0.77 (salted vegetables) for food groups, with median values of 0.55 and 0.61, respectively. The median proportions of the participants classified into the same or adjacent quartiles were 96.39% for energy and nutrients and 96.52% for food groups. The weighted kappa statistics ranged from 0.23 to 0.52 for energy and nutrients and from 0.06 to 0.59 for food groups, with median values of 0.38 and 0.45, respectively. The Bland–Altman plots for energy and macronutrients showed that the data points were primarily within the limits of agreement.CONCLUSIONThe Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs showed fair-to-moderate comparability, suggesting that the Ko-CHENS FFQ can be used for ranking Korean pregnant women based on their dietary intake levels.

INTRODUCTION

In large-scale epidemiological studies, the food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) is used widely because of its ability to capture long-term dietary habits with a relatively low burden on participants and cost [1]. Although many FFQs have been assessed for reproducibility and validity, few studies have examined whether different FFQs provide comparable estimates of the nutrient intake. Considering that dietary intake estimates may vary depending on the dietary assessment methods used, evaluating the comparability and consistency of different FFQs is essential for the reliable interpretation of results.

The Korean Children’s Environmental Health Study (Ko-CHENS), a nationwide birth cohort in Korea, was established to investigate the effects of prenatal and postnatal environmental exposures on children’s health [2]. Maternal dietary intake during pregnancy was assessed by administering a semi-quantitative 112-item FFQ to pregnant women enrolled in the Ko-CHENS [2]. This FFQ has a highly similar structure to the questionnaire used in the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), which includes 97 of the 112 food items in the Ko-CHENS FFQ. The KoGES developed a standardized FFQ for the general adult population, and its reproducibility and validity have been previously confirmed [34]. Although the Ko-CHENS FFQ shares structural similarities and major food items with the KoGES FFQ, it is unclear whether it performs comparably in estimating the nutritional intake among pregnant women.

Therefore, this study compared the intakes of energy, nutrients, and food groups among Korean pregnant women estimated by the Ko-CHENS FFQ with those from the validated KoGES FFQ.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study population

This study was conducted on pregnant women who visited the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at Kangwon National University Hospital. Pregnant women receiving prenatal care at the study site were enrolled between December 2024 and February 2025. The participants were not eligible if they met any of the following criteria: those diagnosed with gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, or cancer; those with type 2 diabetes or hypertension prior to pregnancy; those with multiple gestations; or those adhering to specific dietary restrictions for religious, ethical, or health-related reasons (e.g., vegetarianism or weight control). Among the 100 participants recruited, 6 were excluded because of implausible total energy intake, defined as values exceeding ± 2 SDs from the mean of log-transformed total energy intake. The final study population comprised 94 participants. All participants provided informed consent prior to enrollment, and the Institutional Review Board of Kangwon National University Hospital approved the study protocol (KNUH-2024-10-015).

Dietary assessment

The dietary intake during pregnancy was assessed using the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs, which were completed concurrently on the same day by the same participants through an online platform (K-Stat Research Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea). The Ko-CHENS FFQ, the primary instrument of interest, was administered first to minimize the potential learning effects that might occur if the KoGES FFQ had been completed beforehand. The participants were provided with a web link to access the online survey platform. The platform displayed one food item at a time, requiring the participants to select the intake frequency and, except for those selecting “almost never,” the portion size before proceeding to the next item, thereby preventing missing responses for individual FFQ items.

The Ko-CHENS FFQ comprised 112 food items categorized into major groups, including staple foods, meats (beef, pork, and poultry), fish and seafood, eggs, vegetables, salted vegetables (kimchi), seaweed, legumes, fruits and fruit juices, milk and dairy products, beverages, and fats and sugars. For each item, the participants reported their average intake frequency and usual portion sizes during pregnancy. The intake frequency was classified into 9 categories ranging from almost never to 3 or more times per day, with an additional tenth category for beverages (4 or more times per day). For fruit, the specific season of intake (spring, summer, autumn, or winter) was also recorded. The reference portion sizes for 82 items were established based on the gram weights specified in the questionnaire. For the remaining items, the portion sizes were determined using the medium portion sizes from the KoGES FFQ (25 items) or the sum of ingredient weights from the computer-aided nutritional analysis program (CAN-Pro) database of the Korean Nutrition Society (5 items). The portion sizes were recorded as less than, equal to, or greater than the reference size, which were quantified as half, equal to, or one-and-a-half the reference weight, respectively.

The KoGES FFQ consisted of 106 food items [4]. The food items included in the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs are listed in the Supplementary Table 1. The participants reported their intake frequency over the past year by selecting from 9 categories ranging from never or seldom to 3 or more times per day. For beverages, the daily intake frequency was assessed using the categories of once to twice, 3 to 4 times, or 5 to 6 times per day. For fruits, the intake duration was also recorded in 4 categories (3, 6, 9, or 12 mon). The portion sizes were reported as small, medium, or large relative to the reference portion size, which were converted to half, equal to, or one-and-a-half or twice the reference portion size, respectively. Supplementary Table 2 summarizes the portion sizes of the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs. For the KoGES FFQ, representative photographs of the serving sizes for each food item were provided to enhance the accuracy of the survey.

The recipe database for the Ko-CHENS FFQ was developed using 3 recipe databases: 73 food items from the CAN-Pro, 34 from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) FFQ [5], and 5 from the KoGES FFQ. The nutrient composition database was constructed using the nutrient databases from KNHANES and CAN-Pro. The daily energy and nutrient intakes were estimated by converting the reported intake frequencies into daily equivalents (e.g., once per month to 0.033 times per day) and multiplying the results by the corresponding portion sizes and nutrient contents.

Based on the 2 FFQs, the daily intakes of energy and nutrients, including macronutrients (carbohydrate, protein, and fat), vitamins (vitamin A, retinol, β-carotene, vitamin E, vitamin C, thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, and folate), minerals (calcium, phosphorus, sodium, potassium, iron, and zinc), sugar, dietary fiber, cholesterol, and water, were estimated for each participant. In addition, the food items in the FFQs were disaggregated into individual ingredients based on standard recipes and then categorized into 25 food groups. Supplementary Table 3 provides details on the food groups.

Assessment of other factors

Additional information on the participants was collected via the online platform, including the birth date, gestational age at dietary assessment (weeks and days), education level, current height, and body weight before pregnancy. The maternal age at the dietary survey was calculated by subtracting the birth date from the survey completion date. The pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was calculated using self-reported values as the weight in kilograms before pregnancy divided by the height in meters squared.

Statistical analysis

The daily intakes of energy, nutrients, and food groups estimated by the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs were expressed as the mean and SD. The nutrient intakes were adjusted for the total energy intake using the residual method [6]. A complex survey design was not applied in the analyses because this study did not use nationally representative survey data. The Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated for normally and non-normally distributed variables, respectively, after a log-transformation to improve normality. The intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the absolute agreement of continuous intake estimates between the 2 FFQs. This coefficient measured the proportion of total variance explained by between-person differences, with higher values indicating lower within-person variation. The cross-classification method was used to evaluate the agreement by categorizing the participants into quartiles based on their intakes of each nutrient and food group. The proportions of exact agreement (same quartile), adjacent agreement (adjacent quartiles), extreme misclassification (opposite quartiles), and the sum of exact and adjacent agreement were calculated. The reliability of these categorical classifications was assessed using the weighted kappa coefficients with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Bland–Altman plots were constructed to examine the agreement for intakes of energy and macronutrients (carbohydrate, protein, and fat). The plots revealed the mean of the intakes obtained from both questionnaires on the x-axis and the difference between them on the y-axis [7]. The differences were calculated by subtracting the KoGES FFQ estimates from the Ko-CHENS FFQ estimates. The mean difference and the corresponding 95% limits of agreement (mean difference ± 1.96 SD) were plotted to evaluate the extent of agreement.

Subgroup analyses were performed to examine whether the findings differed according to age (< 33 or ≥ 33 yrs), pregnancy trimester at the dietary survey (first to second or third trimester), pre-pregnancy BMI (< 23 or ≥ 23 kg/m2), or education level (college diploma and below or bachelor’s degree and above). All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The statistical tests were 2-sided, and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

In subgroup analyses, participants aged < 33 yrs showed lower comparability between the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs than those aged ≥ 33 yrs (Supplementary Tables 4, 5, 6). The participants in their first or second trimester showed a relatively lower correlation and agreement for the nutrient intakes than those in their third trimester (Supplementary Tables 7, 8, 9). The participants with pre-pregnancy BMI < 23 kg/m2 showed a higher correlation between the 2 FFQs for energy intake than those with pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2, but the overall comparability did not differ by pre-pregnancy BMI (Supplementary Tables 10, 11, 12). A better correlation and agreement were observed for energy and nutrient intakes among participants with a bachelor’s degree or above compared to those with lower education levels (Supplementary Tables 13, 14, 15).

DISCUSSION

The present study compared the intakes of energy, nutrients, and food groups estimated by the Ko-CHENS FFQ with those from the KoGES FFQ among 94 Korean pregnant women. Although the Ko-CHENS FFQ generally yielded higher intake estimates than the KoGES FFQ, the 2 instruments showed acceptable comparability in ranking the participants according to their intake levels. The Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients ranged from 0.34 to 0.68 for energy and nutrients and from 0.15 to 0.77 for food groups. For all nutrients and food groups except for sugars and jams, the proportion of participants classified into the same or adjacent quartiles exceeded 90%. Furthermore, the Bland–Altman plots indicated fair-to-moderate agreement between the 2 questionnaires.

Consistent with these findings, previous studies have reported acceptable comparability between different FFQs in estimating dietary intakes. One study examined whether 3 different instruments, the Health Habits and History Questionnaires (HHHQ), the Harvard semi-quantitative FFQ (HFFQ), and the New York State Cohort FFQ (CFFQ), produced comparable estimates of dietary intake [8]. The Spearman correlation coefficients between the HHHQ and HFFQ ranged from 0.62 to 0.80 for energy and macronutrients and from 0.56 to 0.80 for micronutrients, suggesting their reasonable comparability. When the CFFQ, a shorter questionnaire, was compared with the HHHQ or HFFQ, the correlation coefficients remained within the range of 0.29 to 0.62. Similarly, a Norwegian study compared a semi-quantitative FFQ (SFFQ-2007) with its earlier version (SFFQ-1999) among infants, where the SFFQ-2007 was developed by modifying the frequency categories, number of food items, and food composition database of the SFFQ-1999 [9]. The 2 FFQs showed reasonable comparability for most nutrients and foods, with Spearman correlation coefficients ranging from 0.26 to 0.52.

Relatively low correlation and agreement were observed for vitamin E among nutrients and for sugars and jams among food groups. This may be attributed to differences between the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs in the number of food items, portion sizes, and recipe databases. Unlike the open-ended dietary assessment tools that capture detailed cooking methods and ingredients, the FFQs estimate the intake by applying standardized recipes to all participants. Considering that major dietary sources of vitamin E in Korea include oils and seasonings such as soybean oil and red pepper powder [10], the actual intake levels may be highly sensitive to specific cooking practices. In line with the present results, prior validation studies have reported the lowest correlation (r = 0.27 in the Netherlands [11] and r = 0.30 in Spain [12]) and agreement (69% classified into the same or adjacent quartiles in China [13]) between the FFQ and multiple 24-h recalls for vitamin E intake during pregnancy. Moreover, the Ko-CHENS FFQ included sugars, syrups, and honey, as well as cooking oils, within standardized recipes of various dishes, reflecting their common use as flavorings in Korea, whereas the KoGES FFQ only accounted for sugar added to coffee. This discrepancy in the recipe database likely contributed to the weak correlation. Similarly, Jordanian pregnant women showed the weakest correlation between the FFQ and 24-h recalls for sweets and sugars (r = 0.08) [14]. These results highlight the importance of developing FFQ recipes that fully represent the cultural dietary habits of the study population.

Although the reason the energy and nutrient intake estimates were higher on the Ko-CHENS FFQ than the KoGES FFQ remains unclear, the order of administration may have played a key role. Previous studies assessing the reproducibility of FFQs have often found that the first administration yields higher mean values than the subsequent one, even with a time interval between assessments [12141516]. As the present study participants completed the Ko-CHENS FFQ first, they may have exhibited greater motivation and attention to detail during the initial assessment, potentially contributing to overreporting. Differences in the reference portion sizes between the 2 FFQs may also have been a factor. Among the 97 corresponding food items across the 2 questionnaires, 41 items had larger reference portion sizes in the Ko-CHENS FFQ than in the KoGES FFQ, while 43 and 13 items had identical and smaller sizes, respectively. In addition, the 2 FFQs differed in their reference periods. The Ko-CHENS FFQ assessed dietary intake during pregnancy, whereas the KoGES FFQ assessed habitual dietary intake over the preceding year. Further research is required to determine how the administration order, portion sizes, and reference periods influence comparability between questionnaires.

Despite generally higher estimates of nutrient and food group intakes in the Ko-CHENS FFQ, the estimated intakes of some nutrients (protein, fat, and cholesterol) and food groups (grains and grain products, noodles and dumplings, fast food, salted vegetables, and mushrooms) were higher in the KoGES FFQ. This may be attributable to differences in reference portion sizes and the composition of food items between the 2 FFQs. For example, the reference portion sizes for rice and salted vegetables (kimchi) were slightly larger in the KoGES FFQ than in the Ko-CHENS FFQ, which may have contributed to the higher estimated intakes of grains and salted vegetables. In addition, although the Ko-CHENS FFQ combined dumplings and rice cakes into a single item, the KoGES FFQ assessed these foods as separate items, potentially resulting in higher estimated intakes of noodles and dumplings. Furthermore, the inclusion of multiple questionnaire items for different types of fish in the KoGES FFQ may partly explain the higher estimated intakes of protein, fat, and cholesterol.

In subgroup analyses, the comparability between the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs was lower among participants younger than 33 yrs and those in the first or second trimester of pregnancy, but higher among those with higher educational attainment. Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted with caution.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is one of the few studies that examined the comparability between different FFQs, particularly among pregnant women. Nevertheless, this study had several limitations. First, this study assessed the comparability between the 2 FFQs rather than the validity against 24-h recalls, dietary records, or biomarkers. Although the KoGES FFQ was previously validated through a comparison with 12-day dietary records, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.23 to 0.64 [4], this validation was conducted in the general adult population rather than pregnant women, and the KoGES FFQ itself does not constitute a gold standard for dietary assessment during pregnancy. Further studies comparing the Ko-CHENS FFQ with independent reference methods will be needed to establish its validity. Second, differences in the reference period between the Ko-CHENS and KoGES FFQs may have influenced the observed results. In addition, potential order effects, including learning, memory, or fatigue effects, may have also contributed to the observed differences between the 2 instruments because the 2 FFQs were administered in a non-randomized order. Despite this, these factors are unlikely to have introduced substantial systematic bias, and the 2 FFQs remained comparable in their ability to rank participants according to dietary intake. Third, the relatively small sample size and single-center study design may have limited the generalizability of these findings. In particular, subgroup analyses should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Finally, the analysis was restricted to 23 nutrients available in the KHNANES FFQ nutrient database. Additional nutrients may need to be evaluated as the database expands.

In conclusion, these findings showed that the FFQ used in the Ko-CHENS had an acceptable correlation and agreement with the KoGES FFQ for ranking pregnant women according to their intakes of energy, nutrients, and food groups during pregnancy. Nevertheless, further validation studies involving larger populations and comparisons with open-ended dietary assessment tools will be needed to confirm its reliability and effectiveness.
