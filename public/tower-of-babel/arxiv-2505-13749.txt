A Complexity Dichotomy for Semilinear Target Sets in Automata with One Counter
Yousef Shakiba, Henry Sinclair-Banks, Georg Zetzsche

Abstract
In many kinds of infinite-state systems, the coverability problem has significantly lower complexity than the reachability problem. In order to delineate the border of computational hardness between coverability and reachability, we propose to place these problems in a more general context, which makes it possible to prove complexity dichotomies. The more general setting arises as follows. We note that for coverability, we are given a vector 𝒕 \bm{t} and are asked if there is a reachable vector 𝒙 \bm{x} satisfying the relation 𝒙 ≥ 𝒕 \bm{x}\geq\bm{t} . For reachability, we want to satisfy the relation 𝒙 = 𝒕 \bm{x}=\bm{t} . In the more general setting, there is a Presburger formula φ ⁡ ( 𝒕 , 𝒙 ) \varphi(\bm{t},\bm{x}) , and we are given 𝒕 \bm{t} and are asked if there is a reachable 𝒙 \bm{x} with φ ⁡ ( 𝒕 , 𝒙 ) \varphi(\bm{t},\bm{x}) . We study this setting for systems with one counter and binary updates: (i) integer VASS, (ii) Parikh automata, and (i) standard (non-negative) VASS. In each of these cases, reachability is 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -complete, but coverability is known to be in polynomial time. Our main results are three dichotomy theorems, one for each of the cases (i)–(iii). In each case, we show that for every φ \varphi , the problem is either 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -complete or belongs to 𝖠𝖢 1 {\mathsf{AC}}^{1} , a circuit complexity class within polynomial time. We also show that it is decidable on which side of the dichotomy a given formula falls. For (i) and (ii), we introduce novel density measures for sets of integer vectors, and show an 𝖠𝖢 1 {\mathsf{AC}}^{1} upper bound if the respective density of the set defined by φ \varphi is positive; and 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -completeness otherwise. For (iii), the complexity border is characterized by a new notion of uniform quasi-upward closedness . In particular, we improve the best known upper bound for coverability in (binary encoded) 1 1 -VASS from 𝖭𝖢 2 {\mathsf{NC}}^{2} (as shown by Almagor, Cohen, Pérez, Shirmohammadi, and Worrell in 2020) to 𝖠𝖢 1 {\mathsf{AC}}^{1} .



I
Introduction

Reachability and coverability problems.

Deciding reachability is one of the most fundamental tasks in the verification
of infinite-state systems. Here, we are given a configuration
\bm{t}
of some
infinite-state system and want to decide whether there is a run from an
initial configuration that arrives in
\bm{t}
. While reachability algorithms
are powerful tools, the complexity of reachability is often exceedingly high.
For example, in
vector addition systems with states
(VASS), which
feature a set of non-negative counters that can be incremented and decremented, the reachability problem is
Ackermann-complete
[
19
,
10
,
20
]
.
Moreover, even in VASS with one counter (and binary-encoded updates),
reachability is already
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete
[
17
]
.

In contrast to reachability, deciding
coverability
is usually much more
efficient. In this setting, there is an ordering
\leq
on the set of
configurations, and we want to know whether for the given configuration

\bm{t}
, there is a reachable configuration
\bm{x}
with

\bm{x}\geq\bm{t}
. For example, in VASS, coverability is

{\mathsf{EXPSPACE}}
-complete (thus considerably lower than the Ackermann complexity of
reachability)
[
22
,
24
]
. In well-structured transition systems, reachability is often
undecidable (e.g. in VASS with resets
[
11
]
),
whereas coverability is decidable under mild
assumptions
[
1
,
14
]
. In
fixed-dimensional VASS—where the number
d
of counters is fixed—the complexity of reachability is still far from understood, but known to
be non-elementary in dimension
8

[
9
]
.
Coverability, however, is
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-complete in every dimension
d\geq 2

[
25
,
4
,
13
]
. In
1
-VASS (i.e. VASS with one counter), coverability is known to
be in
{\mathsf{NC}}^{2}

[
3
]
, a circuit complexity class within polynomial
time. In systems with one integer counter (i.e. that is
allowed to go below zero), a classic result is that coverability is even in

{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
(see
Footnote

2
), a subclass of
{\mathsf{NC}}^{2}

[
27
]
; this is in stark contrast to the
{\mathsf{NP}}
-completeness
of reachability for both integer
1
-VASS
and standard
1
-VASS
.
Moreover, deciding
coverablity is usually sufficient for practical verification tasks. Thus,
coverability is both pratically relevant and admits significantly more
efficient algorithms.

What makes coverability easier?

Reachability and coverability are similar decision problems: In each
setting, we are given a vector
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and are asked whether
we can reach a configuration
\bm{x}
that relates to
\bm{t}
in a
particular way.
For reachability, we have the relation “
\bm{x}=\bm{t}
”, whereas for
coverability, we have “
\bm{x}\geq\bm{t}
”. This viewpoint raises the question: What
makes the relation “
\bm{x}\geq\bm{t}
” so much more amenable to analysis
than “
\bm{x}=\bm{t}
”? Therefore, we propose to study reachability
problems as above, where the relation between
\bm{t}
and
\bm{x}
is
specified by an arbitrary—but fixed!—formula in Presburger arithmetic, the
first-order theory of the structure
\langle\mathbb{Z};+,\leq,0,1\rangle
. Presburger arithmetic
can define the relations “
\bm{x}=\bm{t}
” and “
\bm{x}\geq\bm{t}
”, and
its expressiveness is well-understood. This makes it a promising
candidate to explore the space around reachability and coverability.

Understanding the complexity of Presburger target sets.
More
precisely (but still informally), we want to understand the following
type of reachability problems. For each fixed formula
\varphi
of Presburger
arithmetic and a class
\mathcal{C}
of counter systems, we define the

\varphi
-reachability problem
as follows. We are given (i) a system
from
\mathcal{C}
with
d
counters and (ii) a vector
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
. And
we are asked whether there is a reachable configuration with
counter values
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\varphi(\bm{t},\bm{x})
holds.
Note that we allow
p
to differ from
d
, which provides even more flexibility
in specifying the set of allowed
\bm{x}
.

In this setting, our goal is to understand how the complexity of

\varphi
-reachability depends on the formula
\varphi
.

Our contributions.

We initiate this line of research by studying
\varphi
-reachability in systems
with one counter (with binary encodings). As mentioned above, in this context,
reachability is usually
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete, whereas for coverability,
polynomial-time algorithms are available. Our main results are
three
complexity dichotomies
for Presburger target sets, each regarding one model of
one-dimensional counter systems. Specifically, we consider the following
models of computation
1
1

1

Note that we do not consider
one-counter
automata
, where zero tests are available, since in this setting, there is a
trivial dichotomy (see
Section

A.I
)
:

•

1-dim.
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS: Updates and configurations are integers.

•

1-dim. Parikh automata: All updates (and hence also configurations) are natural numbers.

•

1-dim. VASS: Updates are integers, but only natural numbers are valid in configurations.

Each of our three dichotomies shows that for every
\varphi
,

\varphi
-reachability for the respective model is either in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
or is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete
,
and provides a characterization for the respective formulas. Since we
always have

, the formula
\varphi
always defines a subset of

S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
; the first

components refer to parameter vectors
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
, and the right-most component carries the counter values

.

One-dimensional
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS and Parikh automata.

For
1
-dim.
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS
and for
1
-dim. Parikh automata
, the
resulting decision problems are denoted
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
and
\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
,
respectively.
We obtain dichotomies based on

. We
introduce two density measures for subsets
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
: the

local density of
S
, denoted

, and the

density
, denoted

. Here,
D(S)
and

, then

is in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
, and otherwise,
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.
Similarly, if
D^{+}(S)>0
, then
\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
is in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
, and

\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.

Our local density measures are similar in spirit to (but different from) the

Shnirel’man density
from Additive Number Theory
[
23
]
(see

Remark

III.1
). Intuitively,
D(S)
measures the probability
of hitting
S
when randomly picking a point in an interval (or

S
.

One-dimensional VASS.

For
1
-VASS
(we also refer to this setting as “VASS semantics”), we
denote
\varphi
-reachability as
\Reach_{\mathrm{VASS}}(S)
. We show that if
S
is
“uniformly quasi-upward closed” (defined precisely in
Section

III
),
then
\Reach_{\mathrm{VASS}}(S)
is in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
. Moreover, in all other cases,
\Reach_{\mathrm{VASS}}(S)

is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete. In particular, we show that coverability in
1
-VASS is in

{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
(with a surprisingly simple algorithm!), improving upon the
previously best-known upper bound of
{\mathsf{NC}}^{2}
.

Here, the border
between tractability and
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness is more subtle than expected. For
example, for
S_{1}=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mid x=0\vee x\geq t\}
(i.e. we have
p=1

and
\bm{t}
consists of a single number
t
), and for
S_{2}=\{(t,x)\mid\text{$x=0$ or $x$ is odd}\}
, we have
{\mathsf{NP}}
-completeness, whereas for
S_{3}=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mid\text{$x$ even or ($x$ odd and $x\geq t$)}\}
and
S_{4}=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mid x=t-5\vee x\geq t\}
, the problem is in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
.

Finally, we show
decidability
of the dichotomies.
For each dichotomy, we show that given a Presburger formula for

S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
, it is decidable on which side of the dichotomy
S

falls.

Key ingredients.

Our results rely on several novel concepts and new algorithmic techniques.
Our contributions for
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS
and Parikh automata are substantially more challenging than for
1
-VASS.
The case of Parikh automata can be
deduced from the one for
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS, making the
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS setting the most
involved result of this work. The first difficulty lies within concrete sets

S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
for which it is not clear whether it is solvable in
polynomial time. For example, consider

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{Z}^{1+1}\mid t\geq 0,~x\in[t,2t]\}.

Then the complexity of
\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
is not clear: The interval
[t,2t]
is
finite, and thus coverability techniques are not applicable. The interval
is also “too wide” (compared to its distance from
0
) to reduce, for example, to subset
sum. One of our new algorithmic approaches is to construct a semiring (defined by a set of equations) for

S
such that reachability in
\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
can again be decided in polynomial time
(and even in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
) via repeated matrix squaring. The semiring construction also applies to
more complicated settings where
S
contains multiple intervals, such as

S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2+1}
, the set of all
\langle s,t,x\rangle
where

s,t\geq 0,~x\in[s+t,s+2t]\cup[s+3t,2s+4t].

More generally, it applies whenever
x
is constrained to belong to a
“
\rho
-chain of intervals”, where each interval’s distance to the next (or
to zero) is at most
\rho
times its own length. In
S^{\prime}
, the interval

[s+t,s+2t]
has length
t
, and distance
t
to the next interval

[s+3t,2s+4t]
. The latter, in turn, has length
s+t
, and has distance

s+3t\leq 3(s+t)
from zero.

Second, using ideal decompositions from WQO theory, we show that whenever

D(S)>0
, then
S
decomposes into
\rho
-chains
of intervals.
Conversely, we show that if
D(S)=0
, then subset sum reduces to

\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
.

Third, we show that for every semilinear
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+d}
, both

\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
and
\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
reduce in logspace to the case of acyclic
automata. This even applies to higher dimensions
d
, and is crucial for
applying matrix squaring logarithmically many times. The reduction relies on
Eisenbrand & Shmonin’s Carathéodory theorem for integer
cones
[
12
]
.

For the VASS semantics, the
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
upper bounds rely on a new
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
procedure for coverablity in
1
-VASS, improving upon the previously best-known upper bound of
{\mathsf{NC}}^{2}
.
The procedure is surprisingly simple and although we claim that our contributions for
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS are the most technically challenging, the correctness of our dichotomy for 1-VASS is not obvious.
It relies on viewing coverability as computing weights for a weighted automaton over a newly introduced semiring.
Deciding coverability in one-dim.
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS via weighted automata over the tropical semiring
(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\},\max,+,0,1)
is a classical technique
2
2

2

For example, for the closely related shortest path problem for graphs with positive (binary-encoded) weights, this was mentioned in
[
7
, remarks after Corollary 4.9]
, relying on
[
2
, Lemma 5.11]
. Coverability in integer
1
-VASS can be done in essentially the same way.
:
In this approach, the automaton is turned into a matrix, for which the
n
-th power is computed, where
n
is the number of states.
Via repeated squaring, this leads to circuits of logarithmic depth, i.e.
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
.
In our setting, we introduce a semiring
\mathcal{F}
that permits the same for
1
-VASS. However, matrix squaring in
\mathcal{F}
seems to require
{\mathsf{TC}}^{0}
, meaning repeated squaring would only yield a

upper bound (see
Remark

VII.4
; recall that

{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}\subseteq{\mathsf{TC}}^{1}\subseteq{\mathsf{NC}}^{2}

Instead, we include an additional approximation step to achieve the

II
Preliminaries

We use boldface for vectors, and square brackets to index vectors.
For example, if
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, then
\bm{v}=\langle\bm{v}[1],\ldots,\bm{v}[k]\rangle
.
Given
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k_{1}}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}
, we use the shorthand
\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle
to denote the vector
\langle\bm{u}[1],\ldots,\bm{u}[k_{1}],\bm{v}[1],\ldots,\bm{v}[k_{2}]\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}
.

A
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted automaton

\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,T,q_{0},q_{1}\rangle
is an automaton where
Q
is a finite set of
states
,
T\subseteq Q\times\mathbb{Z}\times Q
is a finite set of
transitions
, and
q_{0},q_{1}\in Q
are the
initial
and
final state
, respectively.
Here, for a transition
\langle q,w,r\rangle\in T
, the number
w
is the
weight
of the transition.
Transition weights are always encoded in binary.

We define three semantics. A
configuration
is a pair
\langle q,x\rangle
for some
q\in Q
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For configurations
\langle q,x\rangle,\langle r,y\rangle
, we write
\langle q,x\rangle\xrightarrow{}_{\mathbb{Z}}\langle r,y\rangle
if there a transition
\langle q,w,r\rangle\in T
with
y=x+w
.
Moreover,
\langle q,x\rangle\xrightarrow{}_{\mathrm{VASS}}\langle r,y\rangle
means
\langle q,x\rangle\xrightarrow{}_{\mathbb{Z}}\langle r,y\rangle
and also
x,y\geq 0
.
Finally,
\langle q,x\rangle\xrightarrow{}_{\mathbb{N}}\langle r,y\rangle
means
\langle q,x\rangle\xrightarrow{}_{\mathrm{VASS}}\langle r,y\rangle
and also
x\leq y
.
These step relations are called
integer semantics
(
\xrightarrow{}_{\mathbb{Z}}
),
natural semantics
(
\xrightarrow{}_{\mathbb{N}}
), and
VASS semantics
(
\xrightarrow{}_{\mathrm{VASS}}
). By
\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, we denote the reflexive, transitive closure of
\xrightarrow{}_{\mathbb{Z}}
(analogously for
\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathrm{VASS}}
and
\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{N}}
).

The decision problems we study are parameterized by a set
S\in\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
of
vectors. The input will consist of a
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted automaton and a vector

\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
, and we want to know whether we can reach a number in
the set

S[\bm{t}]\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\langle\bm{t},x\rangle\in S\}.

With this, we can define the decision problems. Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
.
Then
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
is the following problem.

Given.

A
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted automaton
\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,T,q_{0},q_{1}\rangle
and a vector
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
in binary.

Question.

Is there an
x\in S[\bm{t}]
with
\langle q_{0},0\rangle\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}\langle q_{1},x\rangle
?

The problems
\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
and
\Reach_{\mathrm{VASS}}(S)
are defined in the same way, except
that
\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is replaced with
\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{N}}
and
\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathrm{VASS}}
, respectively.
Note that by definition of
\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
, we can only use transitions with non-negative weights.
In addition, for
\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathrm{VASS}}
, this means for
\Reach_{\mathrm{VASS}}(S)
, we only consider runs where the counter value remains non-negative.

Presburger arithmetic
is the first-order theory of the structure

\langle\mathbb{Z};+,<,0,1\rangle
. Sets definable in Presburger arithmetic are
precisely the semilinear sets
[
15
]
, but we will not
need the definition of semilinearity (except when we will define it
explicitly), so we will use the terms “Presburger-definable” and
“semilinear” interchangeably. Note that almost all algorithms in this work
will assume a
fixed
Presburger-definable set
S
, the algorithmic
complexity of changing between representations of semilinear sets will not be
relevant.

We assume basic familiarity with circuit complexity (however, no circuit
complexity is needed to see that our
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
procedures work in polynomial
time). We will work with
{\mathsf{AC}}^{i}
, the class of decision problems solvable by
polynomial-sized
unbounded
fan-in Boolean circuits of depth
(\log n)^{i}

(for input size
n
), and sometimes
{\mathsf{NC}}^{i}
, the class of problems decidable by
polynomial-sized
bounded
fan-in circuits of depth
(\log n)^{i}
(for
input size
n
). Finally,
{\mathsf{TC}}^{i}
is the class of problems solvable by polynomial-sized unbounded fan-in circuits with Boolean gates
and majority gates
, of depth
(\log n)^{i}
(for input size
n
). Recall the inclusions
[
27
, Fig 4.6]
:

{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}\subseteq{\mathsf{TC}}^{0}\subseteq{\mathsf{NC}}^{1}\subseteq{\mathsf{L}}\subseteq{\mathsf{NL}}\subseteq{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}\subseteq{\mathsf{TC}}^{1}\subseteq{\mathsf{NC}}^{2}.

We will not mention details explicitly, but our
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
upper bounds refer to (deterministic) logspace uniform
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
.
In our algorithms, the only knowledge we use is that additions and comparisons of pairs of binary-encoded integers can be computed in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}

[
27
, Thm 1.15]
.

III
Main results

III-A

Integer semantics

For a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
k
-density of
A
at point
x\in\mathbb{Z}
as

\displaystyle D_{k}(A,x)\coloneqq\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{|A\cap(x+k\cdot[-n,n])|}{2n+1}

and the fraction inside the infimum is also denoted
D_{k,n}(A,x)
.
Thus,
D_{1}(A,x)
is the infimum of the probabilities of hitting
A
when choosing points in intervals centered at
x
. And
D_{k}(A,x)
is the variant where instead of intervals, we consider arithmetic progressions with period
k
, radiating outward from
x
.

Remark III.1
.

For
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
,
d(A)\coloneqq\inf_{n\geq 1}|A\cap[1,n]|/n
is
the
Shnirel’man density
of
A
, a tool in Additive
Number Theory for showing that every natural can be written as a sum of
boundedly many naturals of a particular set
[
23
, Section 7.4]
.

For
A\neq\emptyset
as a whole, we define its
local density

D(A)
as

D(A)\coloneqq\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\inf_{x\in A}D_{k}(A,x).

Moreover, we set
D(\emptyset)\coloneqq 1
.
More generally, for a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
, we define its
local density

D(S)
as

D(S)\coloneqq\inf_{\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}}D(S[\bm{t}]).

We say that
S

has positive local density
if
D(S)>0
.

Theorem III.2
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
be semilinear. Then:

1.

If
D(S)>0
, then
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
is in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
.

2.

Otherwise,
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.

Let us see some examples.

Example III.3
.

Let
S_{1}\coloneqq-2\cdot\mathbb{N}\cup(2\cdot\mathbb{N}+1)
.
In other words,
S_{1}
contains the non-positive even integers and the positive odd integers.
It is not hard to see that
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S_{1})
belongs to
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
, because we can check whether a given automaton can reach a negative even counter value by slightly modifying the
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
procedure for coverability.
Likewise, we can check if it can reach a positive odd counter value.
In
Section

A.II
, we show how this follows from
Theorem

III.2
(see
Claim

A.2
).

Example III.4
.

Let
S_{2}\coloneqq 2\cdot\mathbb{Z}\cup\{1\}
.
In other words
S_{2}
contains the even integers and
1
.
Then
D_{2}(S_{2},1)=0
, and thus
D(S_{2})=0
.
Here,
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness is not difficult to see.

Example III.5
.

Let
S_{3}[t]=(-\infty,0]\cup[t,2t]
.
The density of
S_{3}
is at least
\frac{1}{4}
(see
Fig.

1
); accordingly,
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S_{3})
is in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
.

Example III.6
.

Consider
S_{4}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2+1}
, where for
t,s\geq 0
,

S_{4}[t,s]\coloneqq(-\infty,0]~\cup~[t+s,2t+2s]~\cup~[3t+2s,4t+2s]

and
S_{4}[t,s]=\emptyset
otherwise.
Thus, for
t,s\geq 0
,
S_{4}[t,s]
contains all negative numbers, then there is a gap of size
t+s
, before an interval of size
t+s
, then a gap of size
t
, and then an interval of size
t
.
The density of
S_{4}
is at least

III-C

VASS Semantics

To state our dichotomy for when VASS semantics are used, we need some terminology.
For
\delta\geq 1
, we say that
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
\delta
-upward closed
if

A+\delta\subseteq A
, where
A+\delta\coloneqq\{x+\delta\mid x\in A\}
.
For
M\geq 0
and
\delta\geq 1
, we say that
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is

\langle\delta,M\rangle
-upward closed
if there is a set
F\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
|F|\leq M

such that
A\cup F
is
\delta
-upward closed. Further,
A
is

quasi-upward closed
if there are
\delta\geq 1
and
M\geq 0
such that

A
is
\langle\delta,M\rangle
-upward closed. A set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times\mathbb{N}
is

uniformly quasi-upward closed
if there are
\delta\geq 1
and
M\geq 0

such that for every
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
, the set
S[\bm{t}]
is

\langle\delta,M\rangle
-upward closed.

Theorem III.13
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times\mathbb{N}
be semilinear.

1.

If
S
is uniformly quasi-upward closed, then
\Reach_{\mathrm{VASS}}(S)
is in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
.

2.

Otherwise,
\Reach_{\mathrm{VASS}}(S)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

It may not seem surprising that a variant of upward closedness describes the polynomial-time cases.
Therefore, the following examples illustrate some
subtleties in delineating the exact complexity border.

Example III.14
.

Consider the set
S_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{p+1}
with

and

.
Then

is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete (see
Example

E.2
on

Note that

is

-upward closed for each

.
In particular, to achieve polynomial time, it does not suffice to require that for every

, the set

is
\delta
-upward closed for some

. This is why we require one

.

IV
General techniques

In this short section, we collect some facts that are used across the later sections.
First, the
{\mathsf{NP}}
upper bounds are a simple consequence of small Presburger formulas for reachability relations of
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS
[
16
]
and
1
-VASS
[
21
]
(see
Section

B.I
).

Theorem IV.1
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
be semilinear.

\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
,
\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
, and
\Reach_{\mathrm{VASS}}(S)
are in
\mathsf{NP}
.

Residue classes.

We will decompose semilinear sets
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
into residue classes modulo
some common multiple
B
of all moduli in a formula for
\varphi
. With such a

B
, each residue class modulo
B
is a
modulo-free set
, meaning it can
be defined by a Presburger formula that is
modulo-free
.
This means, the formula contains no quantifiers and no modulo constraints.
Let us make this precise.
For any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
and any modulus
B\geq 0
, we define the
residue classes of
S
as

[S]_{B,\bm{b}}\coloneqq\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}:B\bm{u}+\bm{b}\in S\},

where
\bm{b}
ranges over
[0,B-1]^{p+1}
, the set of
(p+1)
-dimensional
vectors with entries in
[0,B-1]
.
The following lemma follows from the argument above (see
Section

B.II
for details).

Lemma IV.2
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
be a semilinear set.
One can compute
B\geq 1
so that, for each
\bm{b}\in[0,B-1]^{p+1}
,
[S]_{B,\bm{b}}
is effectively definable by a modulo-free Presburger formula.

Computing interval endpoints.

If
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
is modulo-free (definable by a quantifier-free
modulo-free formula), then each set
S[\bm{t}]
is a finite union of
intervals. It is straightforward to show that the functions computing (i) the
number of intervals in
S[\bm{t}]
(which is bounded by a constant only
depending on
S
), and (ii) the endpoints of those intervals are also
Presburger computable. The exact statement is somewhat technical and can be
found in
Section

B.III
.

Computing Presburger-definable functions.
We will often need
to compute points in
S[\bm{t}]
that have certain properties (e.g. have a
large gap above them). Here, the following (shown by Chan and
Ibarra
[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
) will be useful:
It allows us to compute such points if we can define them
uniquely.

Lemma IV.3
.

Every Presburger-definable function
\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{N}
can be computed in deterministic logspace.

Integer Semantics

V-A

Removing modulo constraints

Our first step is to show that it suffices to show
Theorem

III.2
in the case where
S
is given by a modulo-free formula. Roughly speaking, this is because neither positivity of local density, nor the complexity depend on whether we take the entire set, or only residue classes. First, the former:

Lemma V.1
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1},~B\geq 1
. Then
D(S)>0
if and
only if for every
\bm{b}\in[0,B-1]^{p+1}
, we have

.

Next we show that the complexity (i.e. whether
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(\cdot)
belongs to

{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
or is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard) does not depend on whether we take the entire set or
residue classes, either.

Lemma V.2
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
be semilinear and let
B\geq 1
.
If
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}([S]_{B,\bm{b}})
is in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
for
each
\bm{b}\in[0,B-1]^{p+1}
, then
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)

belongs to
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
. Moreover, if
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}([S]_{B,\bm{b}})
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard
for some

\bm{b}\in[0,B-1]^{p+1}
, then
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
is

{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard
. Analogous statements hold for
\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(\cdot)
.

V-B

Making automata acyclic

We now show that for
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
, we may assume that the input automaton is
acyclic. Let
\acintreach(S)
be the problem
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
where the input
must be an acyclic automaton.

Theorem V.3
.

For each semilinear
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+k}
, the problem

\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
reduces in logspace to
\acintreach(S)
.

Proof sketch.

First, one can construct a polynomial-sized existential Presburger
formula for the set of vectors over the transitions that correspond to
a run reaching
S
(because the Parikh image of an NFA admits a
polynomial-sized existential Presburger
formula
[
26
, Thm. 1]
). By solution size
bounds for integer programming, this yields an exponential bound
\ell

on the length of a shortest run. This means, it suffices to construct
an acyclic automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
that simulates at least those runs of the
input automaton
\mathcal{A}
that are of length
\leq\ell
. By standard
arguments about rearranging cycles, it suffices to do this for the set
of runs that are concatenations of short
q
-cycles for some state
q
.
Here, a
short
q
-cycle
is a cycle of length at most
n
(the
number of states of
\mathcal{A}
) that start and end in
q
.

A Carathéodory-type result about integer cones by Eisenbrand and
Shmonin
[
12
, Thm. 1]
implies that the counter effect
of such a concatenation can be produced by a concatenation that
uses
\leq r

distinct
short
q
-cycles, for some polynomial

r
. Again by integer programming solution sizes (and our bound

\ell
), we may assume this modified concatenation to contain each of
the
q
-cycles at most
m
times, for some exponential
m
.

To simulate such a concatenation, we build an acyclic gadget
that, for each
j\in[0,\lceil\log m\rceil]
in sequence, guesses
a short
q
-cycle on-the-fly, and multiplies its effect by
2^{j}
.
Picking different
q
-cycles for different
j
is possible, but also
yields runs consistent with
\mathcal{A}
. Using the gadget
r
times in a row
simulates all concatenations as above. See
Section

C.IV
.
∎

V-C

Unbounded isolation

On the way to prove
Theorem

III.2
, we will characterize the
modulo-free sets of local density zero using the notion of “unbounded
isolation”. This is also used in

Theorem

III.8
.

We say that
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}

has unbounded isolation
if there exists an
\ell\geq 1
such that for every
\delta\geq 1
, there exists a
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
and an

such that:

1.

S[\bm{t}]\cap[x,x+\ell-1]\neq\emptyset
,

2.

, and

S[\bm{t}]\cap[x+\ell,x+\ell+\delta]=\emptyset
.

In other words, we can find an interval
I
of size
\ell
(namely,

[x,x+\ell-1]
) such that
I
contains a point in
S[\bm{t}]
and
on either side of
I

\delta
that does not intersect
S[\bm{t}]
. Intuitively,

is
isolated in the sense that it is neighbored by
\delta

Lemma V.4
.

Suppose

is
{\mathsf{NP}}

The conditions of unbounded isolation are directly expressible in Presburger
arithmetic. Thus, we obtain:

Lemma V.5
.

Given a modulo-free Presburger formula for

, it is
decidable whether
S
has unbounded isolation.

is a

\bm{t}
is said to be
\rho
-admissible

Otherwise, we set
S^{(\rho,m)}[s_{1},t_{1},\ldots,s_{m},t_{m},s_{m+1}]\coloneqq\emptyset.

Relating density, isolation, and building blocks

Definition V.8

(Transformed building blocks)
.

We say that a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
is a
transformed building block
if there is a
\rho>0
, an
m\geq 1
, and Presburger-definable functions
\tau\colon\mathbb{Z}^{p}\to\mathbb{Z}^{2m+1}
and
\sigma\colon\mathbb{Z}^{p}\to\{0,1\}
such that for every
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
:

S[\bm{t}]=(-1)^{\sigma(\bm{t})}\cdot S^{(\rho,m)}[\tau(\bm{t})].

In other words,
S
is essentially a set of the form
S^{(\rho,m)}
, but the parameters for
S
are not directly the interval ends—they have to be computed by a function
\tau
. Moreover, the function
\sigma
allows us to flip the sign.

Proposition V.9
.

Suppose
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
is a modulo-free set. Then the following
are equivalent:

1.

S
has positive local density.

2.

S
does
not
have unbounded isolation.

3.

S
is a finite union of transformed building blocks.

The implications
“
1
\Rightarrow
2
”
and “
3
\Rightarrow
1
” are
mostly straightforward. For the latter, one can observe that transformed
building blocks have positive local density. Intuitively, this is because when
starting from a point in some interval
I
in a
\rho
-chain of intervals, the
distance to the next interval
J
in the chain is always at most
\rho
times
the length of
I
. Therefore, we “collect enough points” during
I
to get us
over the gap to
J
, while maintaining a probability of roughly
\geq 1/(\rho+1)
of hitting
I
. Once we reach
J
, we then collect more points from

J
that will then get us over the next gap, etc. Furthermore, for

S,S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
, we have
D(S\cup S^{\prime})\geq\min(D(S),D(S^{\prime}))
, so that a positive density of the constituents
yields a positive density of the entire union. See
Claims
C.6
and
C.7

in
Section

C.VI
.

Accordingly, the remainder of this section concentrates on the implication “
2
\Rightarrow
3
”.
We first introduce some basic definitions and then some concepts from well-quasi ordering (WQO) theory.

Interval-uniform sets

For subsets
S,S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
, we write

S=S_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_{n}
if
S=S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{n}
and for each
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
, there is at most one
i\in[1,n]
such that
S_{i}[\bm{t}]\neq\emptyset
. Observe that in this case, if some
S_{i}
has unbounded isolation, then so does
S
. Moreover, if each
S_{i}
is a finite union of transformed building blocks, then so is
S
. This means, if we decompose
S
in this way, then proving the implication “
2
\Rightarrow
3
” for each
S_{i}
will also establish it for
S
. This will allow us to assume
S
to be “interval-uniform”, which we define now.

If a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is a finite union of intervals (equivalently,
A
is definable by a modulo-free Presburger formula), then
A
has a
canonical decomposition
, meaning
A
can be written uniquely as
A=I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{m}
such that

1.

I_{1},\ldots,I_{m}
are pairwise disjoint,

2.

I_{1}<\cdots<I_{m}
, and

3.

for all
i\in[2,m]
,
d(I_{i-1},I_{i})\geq 2
.

In this case, the
type
of
A
is the tuple
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})\in\{1,-\infty,\infty,2\infty\}^{m}
, where

c_{i}=\begin{cases}\infty&\text{if }I_{i}=[s,\infty)\text{ for some }s\in\mathbb{Z},\\
-\infty&\text{if }I_{i}=(-\infty,s]\text{ for some }s\in\mathbb{Z},\\
2\infty&\text{if }I_{i}=\mathbb{Z},\text{ and }\\
1&\text{if }I_{i}\text{ is finite.}\end{cases}

Thus, the type encodes (i) how many intervals
there are, and (ii) which of them are finite, one-sided infinite (and on which
side), or two-sided infinite. We say that
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
is

interval-uniform
if it is definable by a modulo-free Presburger formula,
and also all sets
S[\bm{t}]
are of the same type, except for those with

S[\bm{t}]=\emptyset
. The following is a consequence of the fact that the set
of
\bm{t}
for which
S[\bm{t}]
has a particular type is
Presburger-definable (see

Section

C.VII
).

Proposition V.10

(Inverval-uniform decomposition)
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
be a set given by a modulo-free
Presburger formula. Then
S
can be written as

S=S_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
are interval-uniform.

Ratio matrices and ideals

By our observation above—that it suffices to prove the implication
“
2
\Rightarrow
3
” for
constituents of a
\oplus
-decomposition, we now know that it suffices to prove
“
2
\Rightarrow
3
” for
interval-uniform sets
S
.

This allows us to define a key ingredient in our proof (“ratio ideals”), a combinatorial object that encodes which gaps can grow unboundedly compared to interval sizes.
This requires some definitions.
For a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
that is a finite union of intervals, let
A=I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{m}
be its canonical decomposition.
Its complement
\mathbb{Z}\setminus A
is also a finite union of intervals, and
thus has a canonical decomposition
\mathbb{Z}\setminus A=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{r}
.
Here,
r
can be
m-1
,
m
, or
m+1
; the exact value of
r
depends on the number of unbounded invervals in
A
.
In fact, the type of
\mathbb{Z}\setminus A
, only depends on the type of
A
.
The
ratio matrix of
A
is an
r\times m
matrix
M_{A}\in\mathrm{Mat}(r\times m,\mathbb{N}_{\omega})
with entries in
\mathbb{N}_{\omega}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\cup\{\omega\}
, where

(M_{A})_{i,j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\left\lceil\frac{|K_{i}|}{|I_{j}|}\right\rceil&\text{if $|K_{i}|\in\mathbb{N}$,}\\
\omega&\text{if $|K_{i}|=\infty$.}\end{cases}

Note that since all
I_{j}
are non-empty, the denominator is always non-zero.
Put in other words, the entry
(M_{A})_{i,j}
is the quotient of the size of the
i
-th gap and the size of the
j
-th interval.

If
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
is interval-uniform, then for every
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
, the set
\mathbb{Z}\setminus S[\bm{t}]
has the same number
r\in\mathbb{N}
of intervals.
Hence, we obtain a set
\mathbb{M}\subseteq\mathrm{Mat}(r\times m,\mathbb{N}_{\omega})
by defining

\mathbb{M}\coloneqq\{M_{S[\bm{t}]}\mid\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}\text{ and }S[\bm{t}]\neq\emptyset\}.

Consider a subset
U\subseteq\mathrm{Mat}(r\times m,\mathbb{N}_{\omega})
.
The
downwards-closure
of
U
, denoted
\mathord{\downarrow}U
is the set of all matrices in
M\in\mathrm{Mat}(r\times m,\mathbb{N})
such that there is a
M^{\prime}\in U
with
M_{i,j}\leq M^{\prime}_{i,j}
for all
i,j
. If
U=\{D\}
is a singleton, we also write
\mathord{\downarrow}D
for
\mathord{\downarrow}U
.
We will now take the ideal decomposition of
\mathbb{M}
.
For this, we interpret
\mathbb{M}
as a set of vectors in
(\mathbb{N}_{\omega})^{r\cdot m}
by interpreting each matrix
M_{S[\bm{t}]}\in\mathbb{M}
as a vector in
(\mathbb{N}_{\omega})^{r\cdot m}
.
It does not matter exactly how this is achieved, for example, one can append the rows of each matrix together.
The result is that there exists a finite collection of matrices
D_{1},\ldots,D_{z}\in\mathrm{Mat}(r\times m,\mathbb{N}_{\omega})
that are incomparable w.r.t.
\leq
and such that

\mathord{\downarrow}\mathbb{M}=\mathord{\downarrow}D_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathord{\downarrow}D_{z}.

The sets
\mathord{\downarrow}D_{i}
are ideals in the set
(\mathrm{Mat}(r\times m,\mathbb{N}_{\omega}),\leq)
, viewed as a WQO. Therefore, we call the sets
\mathord{\downarrow}D_{i}
the
ratio ideals
of
S
. In particular,
the existence and uniqueness (up to ordering) of
D_{1},\ldots,D_{z}
are well-known facts in WQO theory
[
18
]
.

\omega
-constellations

The notion of ratio ideals now allows
us to connect the lack of unbounded isolation with transformed building blocks:
We will show that if an interval-uniform
S
has no unbounded isolation, then
this rules out a particular pattern called “
\omega
-constellation” in the
ratio ideals.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
be interval-uniform. We say that
S
has
an
\omega
-constellation
if for some of the ratio ideals

for

, there are

such that
i_{1}<i_{2}
and, for all

such

, the entries

and

. In other words, there exist two gaps
K_{i_{1}}
and
K_{i_{2}}

whose length has unbounded ratio compared to the lengths of all intervals
I_{j}

in between these two gaps. With this notion in hand, we can phrase the following
two propositions, which together imply

2
\Rightarrow
3
” of

Proposition

V.9
(due to
Proposition

V.10
).

Proposition V.11
.

If an interval-uniform

has an

-constellation

then
S
has unbounded isolation.

Proposition V.12
.

If an interval-uniform

has no

\omega
-constellation
, then
S
is a finite union of transformed building
blocks.

For

V.11
, one observes that the two gaps

K_{i_{1}}
and

whose sizes have unbounded ratio compared to all

so that

\delta

so that all intervals and all

\left\langle\min\{|K_{i_{1}}[\bm{t}]|,|K_{i_{2}}[\bm{t}]|,~\sum_{j\in J}|I_{j}[\bm{t}]|+\sum_{i=i_{1}+1}^{i_{2}-1}|K_{i}[\bm{t}]|\right\rangle

Moreover, by picking an

Proposition

. Thus, we now fix

By interval-uniformity, there is a particular type

\tau

. By interval-uniformity, it does not depend on

V.12
requires us to show that for each
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
, we can decompose

into finitely many
\rho

. Let us begin by defining
\rho
. Let

be the largest natural number occurring in the ideal decomposition of
\mathbb{M}
.
With this, let
\rho\coloneqq 2R(m+1)
.

Our first lemma encapsulates the main idea of

V.12
.
It tells us that for every

, we can find

that is the next step within the

Such a next step will be called a “harbor”, the idea being that from

More precisely, we say that

|I_{j}[\bm{t}]|>|I_{i}[\bm{t}]|

2.

Lemma V.13
.

For every
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
and every

, if the interval

I_{i}[\bm{t}]
is finite, then it has a harbor.

Proof sketch.

Assume that there are

I_{j_{1}}[\bm{t}]
and
I_{j_{2}}[\bm{t}]
such that

I_{j_{1}}[\bm{t}]<I_{i}<I_{j_{2}}[\bm{t}]
and also both

and

I_{j_{2}}[\bm{t}]
are larger than
I_{i}[\bm{t}]
(the other cases are
similar). Here, we pick

and
j_{2}
as close to

as possible. Then
all the intervals

between
I_{i}[\bm{t}]
and
I_{j_{1}}[\bm{t}]
, or

-constellation in some ratio ideal.

.
∎

More generally, a

is a sequence

Since

We will now complete the first statement of

that

implies that

is a finite union of transformed building blocks, and for each

\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(T)
reduces to

\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S^{(\rho,m)})
for some constants
\rho,m\geq 1
in logspace (by

Lemma

), it remains to show:

Proposition V.14
.

For each rational
\rho>1
and

, the problem
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S^{(\rho,m)})

.

In the following, it is important to remember that

are

\bm{t}
are part of the input.

Growing instances

As a simple preparation, we show that we may assume that, in some sense, the
interval lengths in the input set are growing quickly. We say that
\bm{t}

is
growing
if for every
i\in[1,m+1]
and every
j\in[1,i]
, we have

|I_{i}|\geq 2\cdot d(I_{j},I_{i})
.
It is not difficult to see that
for
Proposition

).

Reachability problems via semirings

By
Theorem

V.3
, it suffices to show that
\acintreach(S^{(\rho,m)})

belongs to
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
. The known
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
algorithm for coverability in

1
-
\mathbb{Z}
-VASS (see
Footnote

2
) uses the classical idea that the
weight of a word in a weighted automaton can be computed by multiplying
matrices. In the case of coverability, one actually only needs to compute the
power
M^{n}
of a matrix
M
, which can be done by repeated squaring: If

n=2^{k}
, then one can compute
M^{n}
by computing
M^{1}=M
, and then

M^{2^{i+1}}=(M^{2^{i}})^{2}
for
i=2,\ldots,k
. In order to decide coverability
this way, one views
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted automata as weighted automata over the
tropical semiring
\langle\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\},\max,+,-\infty,0\rangle
. For this semiring,
since each squaring can be performed in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}
, doing this logarithmically
many times leads to an
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
procedure.

Our algorithm for
\AcReach(S^{(\rho,m)})
is structured similarly. However, in
order to capture the more general sets
S^{(\rho,m)}
, we need to construct
semirings so that (i) matrix products can still be computed in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}
and
(ii) the semiring elements reflect whether we can reach an element in

S^{(\rho,m)}
. We construct such semirings as quotients of
\mathbb{B}[X,X^{-1}]
, the semiring of
Laurent polynomials with Boolean coefficients. Intuitively, while the
tropical semiring captures only “coverability information” (i.e. the
maximal number we can reach on a set of paths), the semiring
\mathbb{B}[X,X^{-1}]

captures the entire reachability set.

Laurent polynomials with Boolean coefficients

Let
\mathbb{B}=\{0,1\}
be the Boolean semiring, where we multiply by conjunction and add by disjunction. For a semiring
\mathcal{S}
, let
\mathcal{S}[X,X^{-1}]
be the semiring of
Laurent polynomials with coefficients in
\mathcal{S}
, which consists of formal sums
\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}s_{i}X^{i}
,
where
s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, and all but finitely many
s_{i}
are zero. Addition and multiplication are defined as in polynomial rings:

\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}s_{i}X^{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}s^{\prime}_{i}X^{i}\right)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{j,k\in\mathbb{Z},\,j+k=i}s_{j}s^{\prime}_{k}\right)X^{i},

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}s_{i}X^{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}s^{\prime}_{i}X^{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}(s_{i}+s^{\prime}_{i})X^{i}.

In particular,
\mathbb{B}[X,X^{-1}]
is the
semiring of Laurent polynomials with
Boolean coefficients
. Then
\mathbb{B}[X,X^{-1}]
is isomorphic to the semiring
of finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
, where we multiply by Minkowski sum, and add by
union. We adopt the perspective of Laurent polynomials (rather than finite sets), since here, addition and multiplication are naturally defined the way we require them.
Nevertheless, we will occasionally borrow terminology from finite sets: We will identify a term
X^{i}
with the number
i
and say, e.g. that
f\in\mathbb{B}[X,X^{-1}]
“contains a point” or “intersects an interval”.

From automata to matrices

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted automaton with
n
states
Q=\{1,\ldots,n\}

and let
1
be the initial state, and
n
the final state.
We may assume that between any
p
and
q
, there is at most one transition.
Let
A\in\mathrm{Mat}(n\times n,\mathbb{B}[X,X^{-1}])
be the matrix defined as follows. For
q,r\in[1,n]
, if there is an transition from
q
to
r
with weight
w
, then
A_{q,r}=X^{w}
.
If there is no transition from
q

{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
: The algorithm

We now show that coverability in
1
-VASS is in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
. This improves on the

upper bound shown by Almagor, Cohen, Pérez, Shirmohammadi, and

[

times (for

overall.
Our algorithm can be viewed as decomposing the paths in

{\mathsf{AC}}^{0}

{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}

We first describe our algorithm as a simple combinatorial procedure. Its correctness is not obvious, but will be proven afterwards via an algebraic description.

.
We will show that in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
, given an

1
-VASS

in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
.

-VASS

-VASS

Let

-VASS with

.
Consider the acyclic

-VASS

VIII
Conclusion

We have provided dichotomies for the reachability problem in systems with one counter and integer, natural, or VASS semantics.
This raises several further questions.

First, which complexities arise in higher dimension? For fixed-dimensional
VASS, even ordinary reachability is not
understood
[
8
]
, meaning a full dichotomy
for semilinear target sets appears to be a longterm goal. For natural
semantics, there are concrete target sets
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{0+2}
(i.e.,
with
p=0
) where it is not clear whether

\Reach_{\mathbb{N}}(S)
is in polynomial time, such as
S=\{\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\mid x_{2}\in[x_{1},2x_{1}]\}
. Thus,
{\mathsf{NP}}
vs.
{\mathsf{P}}

dichotomies in higher dimension for integer and natural semantics seem
challenging as well.

This work reveals a variety of decision problems about systems with one counter
that can be solved in
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
.
Here,
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
seems to be a difficult barrier to break.
Since at least 1990, it is open whether the
{\mathsf{AC}}^{1}
upper bound for
the shortest-path problem in automata weighted with naturals can be improved
(see
[
7
, p. 13]
and
[
6
, p. 32]
);
and this is a special case of coverability in both integer and VASS semantics.

Fact A.1
.

For every set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
and
k,\ell\geq 1
and

Proof.

Take any

and write

\displaystyle|A\cap(

\displaystyle x+k\cdot[-n,n])|

\displaystyle=|A\cap[x-kn,x+kn]\cap(x+k\mathbb{Z})|

\displaystyle\geq|A\cap[x-kn,x+kn]\cap(x+k\ell\mathbb{Z})|

\displaystyle=|A\cap[x-k(m\ell+r),x+k(m\ell+r)]\cap(x+k\ell\mathbb{Z})|

\displaystyle\frac{|A\cap(x+k\cdot[-n,n])|}{2n+1}

Lemma B.2
.

Let
T\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
be defined by a conjunctive modulo-free Presburger formula.
Then there exist Presburger-definable functions
\alpha,\beta\colon\mathbb{Z}^{p}\to\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
such that, for every
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}

\alpha(\bm{t})\leq\beta(\bm{t})
and

Proof.

Suppose
T
is defined be the conjunctive modulo-free Presburger
formula
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p},y)
. Then
\varphi
is a conjunction of
inequalities, which we can write

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}

\displaystyle<y,

\displaystyle y<\bm{v}_{j}^{\top}\bm{x}

for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
j=1,\ldots,m
, where

\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}^{p}
and

\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}^{p}

\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}

\lfloor\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{t}\rfloor
is the left end-point; the largest value among all
\lceil\bm{v}_{j}^{\top}\bm{t}\rceil
is the right end-point. However, the largest lower bound could be larger-or-equal to the smallest upper bound, causing the set of

\psi(\bm{x}):=\bigvee_{i\in[1,n]}\bigvee_{j\in[1,m]}\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}\geq\bm{v}_{j}^{\top}\bm{x}

\alpha(\bm{t})=\begin{cases}\lfloor\max(\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{t}\mid i\in[1,n])\rfloor&\text{if $\neg\psi(\bm{t})$ and $n\geq 1$},\\
0&\text{if $\psi(\bm{t})$ and $n\geq 1$},\\
-\infty&\text{if $n=0$.}\end{cases}

Furthermore, we set

\beta(\bm{t})=\begin{cases}\lceil\min(\bm{v}_{j}^{\top}\bm{t}\mid j\in[1,m])\rceil&\text{if $\neg\psi(\bm{t})$ and $m\geq 1$},\\

Note that if

(\alpha(\bm{t}),\beta(\bm{t}))=(0,0)=\emptyset

.

C.I
Local density of modulo-free sets

We begin this section with an auxiliary lemma that tells us that a modulo-free
set has positive local density if and only if it has positive local

1
-density. Here,
1
-density of a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{p+1}
means that we only
measure the
1
-density at each point. Let us make this precise. The local

k
-density of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is defined as

D_{k}(A)=\inf_{x\in A}D_{k}(A,x).

Lemma C.1
.

If
A
is a disjoint union of
m
intervals, then
D_{k}(A)\geq\tfrac{1}{5m}D_{1}(A)
.

Proof.

First, observe that if
I
is an interval, then

|I\cap[x-kn,x+kn]|\leq k\cdot|I\cap(x+k\cdot[-n,n])|+k.

This implies

\displaystyle\frac{|I\cap(x+k\cdot[-n,n])|}{2n+1}\geq\frac{\tfrac{1}{k}\cdot|I\cap[x-kn,x+kn]|-1}{2n+1}

Therefore, if
A=I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{m}
is a disjoint union, then

\displaystyle D_{k,n}(A,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{|A\cap(x+k\cdot[-n,n])|}{2n+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{|I_{i}\cap(x+k\cdot[-n,n])|}{2n+1}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\tfrac{1}{k}\cdot|I_{i}\cap[x-kn,x+kn]|-1}{2n+1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{m}{2n+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{|I_{i}\cap[x-kn,x+kn]|}{2kn+k}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{m}{2n+1}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{|I_{i}\cap[x-kn,x+kn]|}{2kn+1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}D_{1,kn}(A,x)-\frac{m}{2n+1}

We now distinguish two cases to show that
D_{k,n}(A)\geq\tfrac{1}{5m}D_{1}(A)
for every
n\geq 1
.
Let
D_{1}(A)=\varepsilon
.

•

If
n>\tfrac{2m}{\varepsilon}
, then
2n+1>\tfrac{4m}{\varepsilon}
and thus
\tfrac{m}{2n+1}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}
.
This implies

D_{k,n}(A,x)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}D_{1,kn}(A,x)-\frac{m}{2n+1}\\
>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\geq\tfrac{1}{5m}D_{1}(A).

•

If
n\leq\tfrac{2m}{\varepsilon}
, then
2n+1\leq\tfrac{4m}{\varepsilon}+1\leq\tfrac{5m}{\varepsilon}
and thus

D_{k,n}(A,x)\geq\frac{1}{2n+1}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{5m}=\tfrac{1}{5m}D_{1}(A).

Thus, for every
n\geq 1
, we have
D_{k,n}(A)\geq\tfrac{1}{5m}D_{1}(A)
. This implies
D_{k}(A)\geq\tfrac{1}{5m}D_{1}(A)
.
∎

in binary,
one can construct in logspace an acyclic automaton

such that

\Eff(\Cycles_{q,n}(\mathcal{A})^{\leq\ell})\subseteq\Eff(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\subseteq\Eff(\Cycles_{q,n}(\mathcal{A})^{*}).

Moreover, if
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathbb{N}^{k}

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is as well.

Proof.

We begin with some notation. For any set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, let

A^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
be the submonoid generated by
A
. Moreover, for

F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, define
F\cdot A:=\{f\cdot\bm{a}\mid f\in F,~\bm{a}\in A\}
. Moreover,
A^{r}
is the
r
-fold Minkowski sum of
A
, meaning the set of vectors that
can be written as a sum of
r
vectors from
A
. In other
words, we have

A^{r}=\{\bm{a}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{a}_{r}\mid\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{r}\in A\}.

Furthermore, we define
A^{\leq r}=\bigcup_{i\in[0,r]}A^{r}
.
For example,
(\mathbb{N}\cdot A)^{\leq r}
is the set of all vectors that can be written as a positive integer linear combination of at most
r
distinct vectors from
A
.

Now let
C=\Eff(\Cycles_{q,n}(\mathcal{A}))\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
be the set of
effects of cycles in
\mathcal{A}
of length
\leq n
. With this notation,
our goal is to construct in logspace an acyclic automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
such
that

C^{\leq\ell}\subseteq\Eff(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\subseteq C^{*}.

A result of Eisenbrand & Shmonin
[
12
, Theorem 1]
shows that if

A
is a finite set and
M
is the maximal absolute value appearing in
vectors in
A
, then
A^{*}=(\mathbb{N}\cdot A)^{\leq r}
, where
r=2k\log(4kM)
.
Since all the absolute values appearing in
C
are at most exponential,
this implies that Eisenbrand and Shmonin’s result yields a polynomial
bound
r
with
C^{*}=(\mathbb{N}\cdot C)^{\leq r}
.

Note that each vector in
C^{\leq\ell}
has polynomial bit length. As
observed above, we have in particular
C^{\leq\ell}\subseteq(\mathbb{N}\cdot C)^{\leq r}
. This means, for each
\bm{c}\in C^{\leq\ell}
, there are vectors

\bm{c}_{1},\ldots,\bm{c}_{t}\in C
with
t\leq r
and coefficients

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
with

\bm{c}=\lambda_{1}\bm{c}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{t}\bm{c}_{t}
. By the standard
solution size bound for integer linear
programming (e.g.
[
28
, Theorem]
), since the
vectors
\bm{c},\bm{c}_{1},\ldots,\bm{c}_{t}
have polynomial bit
length, and
t\leq r
is polynomial, the equation

\bm{c}=\lambda_{1}\bm{c}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{t}\bm{c}_{t}
even has a
solution where all the
\lambda_{i}
have polynomial bit-length. This
implies that there is an exponential bound
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that every
vector from
C^{\leq\ell}
belongs to
([0,m]\cdot C)^{\leq r}
. In
summary, we observe

C^{\leq\ell}\subseteq([0,m]\cdot C)^{\leq r}\subseteq C^{*}.

Now the difficulty is that it is not clear how to construct a small
automaton whose effects are exactly those in
[0,m]\cdot C
: Such an
automaton would have to pick a factor
f\in[0,m]
(for which there are exponentially many choices), and pick a cycle in

C
, and then multiply
f
with each transition weight on this cycle.
Here, our key observation is that we can generate a
larger set

consisting of multiples of cycles just by powers of two, where we allow
to use different cycles for each power of two. This is still correct,
since this larger effect set is still included in
C^{*}
. To make this precise,
observe that

\displaystyle C^{\leq\ell}\subseteq([0,m]\cdot C)^{\leq r}\subseteq\underbrace{\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil\log m\rceil}2^{j}\cdot(C\cup\{0\})\right)^{r}}_{=:B}\subseteq C^{*}.

Therefore, it suffices to construct an acyclic automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with
\Eff(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})=B
. This is easy to do in deterministic logspace. Indeed, by definition of
C
, it is easy to construct an acyclic automaton for
C\cup\{0\}
, and thus also for
2^{j}\cdot(C\cup\{0\})
, by multiplying
2^{j}
to each transition effect. For the sum term, we then chain together
\lceil\log m\rceil
-many (which is a polynomial quantity) of such automata together. Finally, for the
r
-fold Minkowski sum, we just chain
r
copies of this automaton together.
∎

C.XI
Making instances growing

The following lemma shows that we
can always assume that our input instance is growing. This is because if it is
not growing, then for some
I_{i}
, its length is in particular bounded by a
constant factor in
I_{i-1}
. And in this case, we can consider (i) the
instance without
I_{i}
(which, because of the constant factor, will still be

\rho^{\prime}
-admissible for a larger
\rho^{\prime}
) and (ii) the instance consisting just
of
I_{i},I_{i+1},\ldots,I_{m+1}
. Since in both instances, the number of
intervals is strictly smaller, this reduction will (after at most
m

repetitions) yield a growing instance.

Lemma C.10
.

Let
\rho,m\geq 1
be constants. There are
\rho^{\prime},k\geq 1
such that
given an instance of
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S^{(\rho,m)})
, we can compute in
logspace at most
k
instances of
\Reach_{\mathbb{Z}}(S^{(\rho^{\prime},r)})
for some

r\in[1,m]
, such that the original instance is positive if and only if
one of the new instances is positive.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
m
. For
m=1
, the statement is trivial:
Since
|I_{2}|=|I_{m+1}|=\infty
, every instance for
m=1
is growing.

Now suppose
m>1
and that the intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{m+1}
for the
parameter vector
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
are not growing, say we have
|I_{i}|<2d(I_{j},I_{i})
for some
i\in[2,m]
and
j\in[1,i]
. We build two new
instances: One where we remove
I_{i}
, and one where we remove

I_{1},\ldots,I_{i-1}
.

First, let us consider
I_{1},\ldots,I_{i-1},I_{i+1},\ldots,I_{m}
. Then
for any
k\notin\{i-1,i\}
, we clearly have
d(I_{k},I_{k+1})\leq\rho\cdot|I_{k}|
. Moreover, we have
|I_{i}|<2d(I_{i-1},I_{i})\leq 2\rho\cdot|I_{i-1}|
and thus

\displaystyle d(I_{i-1},I_{i+1})

\displaystyle\leq d(I_{i-1},I_{i})+|I_{i}|+d(I_{i},I_{i+1})

\displaystyle\leq\rho\cdot|I_{i-1}|+2\rho|I_{i-1}|+\rho|I_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\rho\cdot|I_{i-1}|+2\rho|I_{i-1}|+2\rho^{2}|I_{i-1}|

\displaystyle\leq 5\rho^{2}|I_{i-1}|.

Thus, the instance
I_{1},\ldots,I_{i-1},I_{i+1},\ldots,I_{m+1}
is

5\rho^{2}
-admissible.

Second, the instance
I_{i},I_{i+1},\ldots,I_{m+1}
is clearly
\rho
-admissible.
Moreover, the union

(I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{i-1}\cup I_{i+1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{m+1})\cup(I_{i}\cup\cdots\cup I_{m+1})

is the same as
I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{m+1}
, so that the original
instance is positive if and only if one of the two new instances is
positive.

Since both new instances have strictly fewer intervals, by induction,
they can in turn be reduced to growing instances.
∎

Lemma E.5

Given a semilinear

and only if for some

Here,

Proof of
Lemma

E.5
.

Let
B\geq 1
be the constant from
Lemma

IV.2
. Then for every
\bm{s}\in[0,B-1]^{p+1}
,
[S]_{B,\bm{b}}
is defined by a modulo-free Presburger formula.

We first prove:

Claim E.6
.

S
is uniformly quasi-upward closed if and only if for every
\bm{b}\in[0,B-1]^{p}
, the set
[S]_{B,\bm{b}}
is uniformly quasi-upward closed.

First, suppose
S
is uniformly quasi-upward closed, say it is
\langle\delta,M\rangle
-upward closed. Then clearly for every
\bm{b}\in[0,B-1]^{p}
, the set
[S]_{B,\bm{b}}
is also
\langle\delta,M\rangle
-upward closed.

Conversely, suppose all residue classes are uniformly quasi-upward closed, then
S
is uniformly quasi-upward closed.
Indeed, if
[S]_{B,\bm{b}}
is
\langle\delta_{\bm{b}},M_{\bm{b}}\rangle
-upward closed for some
\delta_{\bm{b}}\geq 1
and

S
is
\langle\delta,M\rangle
-upward closed, where
\delta
is the product of all
\delta_{\bm{b}}
, and
M
is the sum of all
M_{\bm{b}}
.

This proves our claim.

It now remains to prove:

Claim E.7
.

Suppose
T
is modulo-free. Then
T
is not uniformly
quasi-upward closed if and only if
T
has unbounded gaps.

First, suppose
T
is not uniformly quasi-upward closed. Since
T
is defined by a modulo-free Presburger formula, let
\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}:\mathbb{Z}^{p}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
be the Presburger-definable functions as per
Lemma

.
Recall that these functions have the following two properties.
Firstly, for all
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}

\alpha_{1}(\bm{t})\leq\beta_{1}(\bm{t})\leq\alpha_{2}(\bm{t})\leq\ldots\leq\beta_{m-1}(\bm{t})\leq\alpha_{m}(\bm{t})\leq\beta_{m}(\bm{t}).

Secondly,

T[\bm{t}]=(\alpha_{1}(\bm{t}),\beta_{1}(\bm{t}))\cup\cdots\cup(\alpha_{m}(\bm{t}),\beta_{m}(\bm{t})).

1.

Suppose there is a
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
such that
\beta_{m}(\bm{t})\in\mathbb{N}
.
In that case,
T=[S]_{B,\bm{b}}
clearly has unbounded gaps.
Indeed, there is even a single infinite gap; that is
(\beta_{m}(\bm{t}),\infty)
.

2.

Suppose
\beta_{m}(\bm{t})=\infty
for every
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
.
Now consider, for any given
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
, the “largest gap size”:

M_{\bm{t}}=\max_{i\in[1,m-1]}\{\alpha_{i+1}(\bm{t})-\beta_{i}(\bm{t})\}.

Now if the function
\bm{t}\mapsto M_{\bm{t}}

would be
\langle 1,mM\rangle

\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}

S

Therefore, for every
M\geq 0

\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
and

\alpha_{i+1}(\bm{t})-\beta_{i}(\bm{t})\geq M

This means that

Conversely, if

\langle\delta,M\rangle
-upward closed for any
\delta\geq 1

.
This is true because

Together the two claims establish the
Lemma
