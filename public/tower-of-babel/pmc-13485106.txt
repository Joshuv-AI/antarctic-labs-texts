Systematic review of Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy – Clinical diagnosis, genetics overview and current concepts of treatment
Authors: Selvakumar Ambika, Sundaramurthy Srilekha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) is the most common mitochondrial disorder, typically causing substantial, often permanent, central vision loss in young adults. It manifests as a subacute optic neuropathy, frequently progressing sequentially in both eyes, due to selective degeneration of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs). The condition is primarily associated with three mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) point mutations—m.11778G>A, m.14484T>C, and m.3460G>A—located in complex I of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. These mutations impair oxidative phosphorylation, elevate reactive oxygen species (ROS), and trigger apoptosis of RGCs. Although historically considered untreatable, emerging therapies provide new prospects. Idebenone, a synthetic CoQ10 analog, is the first pharmacologic agent approved in Europe, demonstrating partial visual recovery in patients treated early by improving mitochondrial electron transport and reducing oxidative stress. Gene therapy using allotopic expression of ND4 via adeno-associated viral vectors (rAAV2/2-ND4) has shown improvement in both eyes even after unilateral injection. Advanced gene-editing techniques, such as zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs) and transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), offer potential approaches for correcting heteroplasmic mutations. High-throughput genetic testing, including whole-genome sequencing and clinical exome analysis, enables precise identification of nuclear modifiers that influence LHON phenotypes, facilitating early diagnosis and intervention. Current clinical trials, including RESTORE and REFLECT, emphasize the importance of prompt treatment to optimize visual outcomes.

Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) represents the most prevalent primary mtDNA disorder, initially described by Theodor Leber in 1871, with maternal inheritance later confirmed by Wallace et al. in 1988.[1] Clinically, LHON manifests as a bilateral, sequential, subacute loss of central vision, primarily due to selective degeneration of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) in the inner retina, ultimately resulting in pronounced optic nerve atrophy. This leads to significant visual impairment that profoundly affects patients’ quality of life.[2] Approximately 90% of cases are attributed to three major mtDNA point mutations: m.3460G>A in MT-ND1, m.11778G>A in MT-ND4, and m.14484T>C in MT-ND6.[3] These mutations disrupt the function of complex I, the first enzyme in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, impairing oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and causing RGC dysfunction. Inadequate electron transport through complex I elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS), promoting oxidative damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids, which is central to LHON pathogenesis.[4] Although LHON was long considered untreatable, recent pharmacologic and gene-based interventions have shown potential in preserving or partially restoring vision.[5] Treatment strategies may be mutation-specific or mutation-independent, offering possibilities for visual improvement. Further research is needed to validate these approaches and consolidate the evidence. This review highlights the clinical features, genetic testing methods for mtDNA mutations, and recent insights into the molecular mechanisms driving RGC degeneration, along with emerging therapeutic paradigms for LHON.

Methods of Literature Review

A literature review was performed to identify relevant studies on LHON, focusing on its clinical characteristics, molecular mechanisms, genetic mutations, diagnostic approaches, and emerging therapies. The search spanned publications from January 1988, when maternal inheritance of LHON was first documented, through September 2025.

Databases

Electronic databases searched included PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar, which also provided access to gray literature and recent preprints.

Search strategy

We planned to search for information using a mix of keywords and special medical terms called MeSH terms. We used words like “AND” and “OR” to find the right articles. Search included words about the disease like “Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy” and “LHON”. We also looked for words about genetics, such as “ DNA mutation,” “mtDNA,” and “complex I gene mutations”. We searched for words about how the disease works, like “retinal ganglion cell degeneration” and “optic neuropathy.”

We used words to find studies on how to diagnose the disease like “genetic testing” “Sanger sequencing,” “next-generation sequencing,” “whole exome sequencing,” “haplogroups,” “heteroplasmy,” and “homoplasmy.” When we looked for treatments, we used words like “gene therapy,” “ expression,” “AAV2-ND4,” “lenadogene nolparvovec,” “idebenone,” “antioxidant therapy,” and “mutation-independent therapy.” We also searched for words like “ trials,” “visual outcomes,” and “treatment efficacy” to see about the treatment modalities and new experiments. We even checked the reference lists of the articles we found to make sure we did not miss any information about LHON.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The studies that were part of this review came out between January 1988 and September 2025. The studies we looked at were the kind that had research like randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, case–control studies, and also some well-documented case series.

These studies looked at the disease in ways such as what it looks like, the molecular genetics, the mitochondrial haplogroups, how to diagnose it, and how to treat it with gene therapy or other methods. We liked studies that were about people. We also looked at animal studies if they could really help us understand how to make new treatments.

We did not look at some studies, like editorials or opinion pieces, because they did not have the kind of information we needed. We also did not look at abstracts from conferences if they did not have all the data or if they were published more than once. If a study did not say how it was done, or if it was not really about the disease we were interested in or if it did not have information about what happened to the patients, then we did not include it in our analysis.

Clinical presentation

LHON is a mitochondrial disorder characterized by subacute, painless, and progressive sequential loss of central vision. It affects mostly men more than women, and women develop the disease at an older age than men. Age of onset ranged from 8 to 60 years, with a mean of 27.6 years, and peak incidence between the 2nd and 4th decades. Patients with a younger age onset of disease have a better prognosis. There is a strong maternal inheritance pattern of the disease, with mothers being the carrier of the defective gene. Patients present with vision loss, defective color perception (dyschromatopsia), and dense centrocecal visual field defects.

While vision loss usually occurs sequentially between eyes, simultaneous bilateral involvement is reported in approximately 25–50% of patients, with the second eye typically affected within 2–3 months.[56] Spontaneous visual improvement occurs in 4–32% of cases, particularly in those harboring the MT-ND6 mutation. In the early stage, when optic discs appear hyperemic with peripapillary telangiectasia, optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the optic nerve can show Retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickening. Subsequently, OCT macular reveals thinning of the ganglion cell complex (GCC) [Fig. 1]. In chronic stages, the optic disc becomes pale and cupped, with pallor of the neuroretinal rim, and OCT demonstrates RNFL and GCC thinning.[7]

Beyond optic nerve involvement, a subset of patients develops “LHON-Plus,” which includes neurological complications such as seizures, movement disorders, paraplegia, encephalopathy, peripheral neuropathy, and myopathy.[8] Cardiac conduction disturbances, including asymptomatic arrhythmias, may also occur, even in patients without overt optic neuropathy.[9] Rare skeletal anomalies, including congenital hip dislocation, arachnodactyly, pes cavus, talipes equinovarus, kyphoscoliosis, arched palate, and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, have been reported. Additionally, some patients present with tremors or multiple sclerosis-like features; this overlap is referred to as Harding’s disease.

Common differential diagnoses for LHON are optic neuritis, other hereditary optic neuropathies like autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive optic neuropathy, toxic, and nutritional optic neuropathy.

Optic neuritis presents with classical subacute painful vision loss that may be unilateral or bilateral, predominantly affecting females more than males, unlike LHON, which predominantly affects males. Peak vision loss occurs within 2 weeks, and vision recovery starts by 3 weeks with or without treatment.[7] It can be associated with Central nervous system (CNS) demyelinating disease like multiple sclerosis or other demyelinating diseases like MOGAD – myelin oligodendrocyte-associated disease/NMOSD – neuromyelitis spectrum disorder associated with seromarkers. Toxic optic neuropathy is secondary to smoking, alcohol, and drugs like antituberculous drugs. Ethambutol can cause subacute painless bilateral vision loss with dense central centrocecal field defects. On stopping the offensive agent, most patients recover vision. Nutritional optic neuropathy is also similar in clinical presentation, but it also recovers with nutritional supplementation.[89] Sometimes, these toxic agents can act as stressors to a pre-existing mitochondrial mutation and trigger the RGC apoptosis cascade, resulting in the manifestation of a subclinical LHON. Presumed toxic optic neuropathy patients who do not recover vision after stopping the causative drugs should be screened for underlying mitochondrial abnormalities like LHON.

Autosomal dominant optic atrophy (caused by mutations in OPA1) usually presents with insidious vision loss that starts early in childhood, unlike rapid progressive vision loss onset as observed in LHON.[56] Leigh syndrome is an uncommon mitochondrial neurodegenerative disease that affects young children and adolescent patients and is reported in association with MT-ND6 mutations (m.14559G>A / m.14484T>C point mutations). These patients manifest cerebellar ataxia, spasticity, peripheral neuropathy, hypotonia with nystagmus, optic atrophy, and ophthalmoparesis.[8]

Etiopathogenesis

The schematic representation [Fig. 2] illustrates the impact of mtDNA mutations on complex I function, leading to impaired electron transfer within the respiratory chain. This defect results in reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis along with increased electron leakage, promoting excess ROS production. The diagram further highlights the downstream cascade of oxidative stress–induced mitochondrial dysfunction, culminating in selective vulnerability and apoptosis of RGCs. Under physiological conditions, intracellular ROS is neutralized by antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase.[10] In LHON, impaired electron flow causes electron leakage and ROS accumulation, which damages intracellular membranes and promotes calcium release from intracellular stores. Elevated cytosolic calcium increases mitochondrial membrane permeability, opens the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, and facilitates the release of pro-apoptotic factors like cytochrome c.[11]

Experimental models of optic neuropathy have demonstrated reduced SOD activity, highlighting the central role of oxidative stress in LHON pathogenesis. Dysfunctional oxidative phosphorylation also disrupts axonal calcium homeostasis, particularly via altered Na⁺–Ca²⁺ exchanger activity, further contributing to cellular injury. The papillomacular bundle is selectively vulnerable due to its small axonal diameter, high metabolic demand, and delayed myelination. In the pre-laminar optic nerve, RGC axons remain unmyelinated, while myelination initiates post-lamina cribrosa, rendering these fibers highly susceptible to energy deficits and degeneration in LHON.[1213]

Genetic mutations

Among the various mtDNA variants associated with LHON, three are considered “primary” due to their high prevalence and strong pathogenic effects [Table 1]. These mutations disrupt critical subunits of complex I (NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase), leading to impaired mitochondrial energy generation and excessive ROS production, culminating in RGC death and optic nerve degeneration.[1415] RGCs are particularly susceptible because of their long, unmyelinated axons and elevated metabolic demands.[1116] Collectively, these three mutations account for over 90–95% of global LHON cases and form the principal targets for genetic screening.[1718] Among them, the m.11778G>A mutation is the most frequent and is generally associated with poorer visual prognosis,[19] whereas m.14484T>C carries a higher likelihood of spontaneous visual recovery.[20] Additional secondary variants in complex I genes are often observed, potentially modifying mitochondrial function, ROS production, and RGC vulnerability to apoptosis.[2122]

Primary mutations and their functional impact

Heteroplasmy

Unlike many mitochondrial disorders, LHON predominantly exhibits homoplasmy or near-homoplasmy for primary mutations.[23] This feature significantly affects disease penetrance and clinical expression.[24] Despite this, penetrance remains incomplete: roughly 50% of male carriers and only about 10% of female carriers develop symptomatic vision loss, suggesting the influence of nuclear genetic modifiers, environmental factors, and mitochondrial haplogroups on disease manifestation.[2526]

Mitochondrial haplogroups

Mitochondrial haplogroups are maternally inherited clusters of mtDNA variants.[27] Certain haplogroups can modify susceptibility to LHON even among individuals carrying the same primary mutation.[26] In European and East Asian populations, haplogroups J and M7b are associated with increased disease penetrance, whereas studies in Indian cohorts reveal greater haplogroup diversity among LHON patients.[2829] The M haplogroup is prevalent in India but does not appear to significantly affect disease severity or penetrance.[30] Table 2 lists the mitochondrial haplogroups identified in various studies and their respective effects on LHON expression. Diagnostic Approaches

Techniques for gene assays in LHON

Sanger Sequencing for Primary and Secondary mtDNA Mutations

Sanger sequencing remains the gold standard for detecting point mutations in mtDNA, particularly single-nucleotide variants, due to its high accuracy and reliability.[34] It is commonly employed to identify pathogenic mutations in MT-ND1 (m.3460G>A), MT-ND4 (m.11778G>A), and MT-ND6 (m.14484T>C), as well as secondary variants implicated in mitochondrial disorders, including LHON.[1635] Specific primers targeting these mutations are designed using software such as Primer3 to ensure high specificity.[36] Following Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, products are sequenced using capillary electrophoresis platforms like the Applied Biosystems Genetic Analyzer, providing precise base-calling.[37] Sequence data are compared against the Revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS; NC_012920)[38] and interpreted using curated databases such as MITOMAP for annotation of pathogenic and polymorphic variants.[39]

In cases where primary LHON mutations are absent, Sanger sequencing can be extended to cover the entire mitochondrial coding region or selected candidate genes based on clinical suspicion.[40] While next-generation sequencing (NGS) is increasingly used for comprehensive mtDNA analysis, Sanger sequencing remains valuable for confirmatory testing and for estimating heteroplasmy levels when mutation loads are high.[41]

RFLP analysis for heteroplasmy assessment

Restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis is a rapid and cost-effective approach to determine whether a mutation is heteroplasmic or homoplasmic.[42] PCR-amplified fragments encompassing the mutation site are digested with mutation-specific restriction enzymes that either create or abolish recognition sites. Resulting fragments are resolved on a 4% agarose gel with ethidium bromide or SYBR Safe, allowing visualization of heteroplasmic (both digested and undigested bands) versus homoplasmic (single band) states. This technique has been widely used in LHON studies to confirm plasmy status following mutation detection.[43]

Next-generation sequencing

NGS has transformed mitochondrial diagnostics by providing high-throughput, comprehensive sequencing and sensitive detection of low-level heteroplasmy.[4445] Whole mitochondrial genome sequencing with NGS enables identification of both common primary mutations (m.3460G>A, m.11778G>A, m.14484T>C) and rare or novel secondary variants that may contribute to LHON pathogenesis.[4647] NGS also allows accurate quantification of heteroplasmy down to 1–2%, a level difficult to achieve with conventional Sanger sequencing.[48]

Clinical exome and targeted panel sequencing

Beyond mtDNA, clinical exome sequencing (CES) or targeted gene panels can analyze nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes, identifying potential nuclear modifiers or dual genomic causes in complex LHON cases.[49] Variants in nuclear genes involved in mtDNA maintenance or complex I assembly (e.g., TWNK, POLG, NDUFS2) may affect disease phenotype and contribute to variability among patients.[50]

Whole-exome sequencing for mtDNA-negative or atypical cases

For patients lacking primary mtDNA mutations, whole-exome sequencing (WES) offers a broader approach, interrogating all protein-coding regions to uncover novel variants in cases of unexplained or syndromic optic neuropathies.[51525354] For instance, patients previously suspected of LHON but without canonical mtDNA mutations were found to have biallelic DNAJC30-associated optic atrophy, demonstrating the utility of exome-based strategies.[55] Moreover, WES and CES can reveal nuclear modifiers that influence disease penetrance and severity in individuals harboring known mtDNA mutations, explaining phenotypic differences even within maternally related families.[26] For better clinical applicability, the advantages and limitations of commonly used genetic testing approaches are summarized in Table 3.

Treatment

Currently, no definitive cure exists for LHON. Management has primarily been supportive, including vision rehabilitation, occupational therapy, and avoidance of environmental risk factors, such as smoking.[5] Adjunctive therapies like vitamins, antioxidants, and co-enzyme Q10 have been used, though robust evidence for their efficacy remains limited. Numerous ongoing clinical trials aim to develop more effective interventions for this mitochondrial optic neuropathy. In 2015, (raxone) idebenone, an antioxidant, was approved in Europe for treating LHON, and since then, it has been widely used in clinical practice in several European countries. Alternative therapeutic strategies like gene therapy and gene editing, antioxidant and neurotrophic agents, mitochondrial biogenesis, mitochondrial replacement, and stem cell therapies are being studied in managing this potentially disabling disease. Amidst all these techniques, allotopic gene expression, gene editing, and mtDNA heteroplasmic shift are under clinical investigations. Allotopic gene therapies are in the most advanced stage of development (phase III clinical trials), while most other agents are in phase I or II trials or at preclinical stages. Mutation-Specific Therapy

Mutation-specific approaches aim to modify or correct the defective genes themselves, which is the primary cause of LHON, by either correcting the genetic defect or supplying a functional mitochondrial protein. Most of the gene therapy approaches have become challenging due to the impermeable mitochondrial membranes, which prevent the transfer of genes by viral vectors into the mitochondrial genome. Gene delivery using recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 2 (rAAV2) vectors, particularly rAAV2-ND4, is currently the primary approach, largely focusing on the MT-ND4 mutation, the most prevalent in LHON patients.

Gene Therapy in LHON

In 2011, the first human trial of gene therapy for the m.11778G>A mutation was done in Wuhan, China. Nine patients were treated with a single unilateral intravitreal (IVT) injection of their gene therapy product delivered using a recombinant AAV2 vector (“rAAV2-ND4”). Gene transfer using AAV2 vectors has advanced treatment options for genetic diseases.[56] However, the mitochondrial double-membrane barrier restricts direct delivery of therapeutic DNA, necessitating alternative strategies such as allotopic expression.

Allotopic Expression

Allotopic expression involves synthesizing a wild-type mitochondrial gene (e.g., MT-ND4) in the nucleus, followed by cytoplasmic translation and import of the encoded protein into mitochondria via a mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS), restoring complex I activity.[5758596061] This approach has been applied to MT-ND4 and MT-ND1 mutations, resulting in therapies such as NFS-02. Human trials, particularly targeting the m.11778G > A mutation, have aimed to stabilize or restore vision in affected patients. The first clinical trial in Wuhan, China (2011), administered unilateral IVT rAAV2-ND4 injections to nine patients. Remarkably, bilateral visual improvement occurred in five participants over 7.5 years, with the injected eye showing maximum vision improvement.[6263] Factors associated with better outcomes are young age onset of disease, shorter interval between disease onset and treatment.[64] A Phase I dose-escalation trial at Bascom Palmer Eye Institute using scAAV2 (Y444,500,730F)-P1ND4v2 in 28 patients showed visual improvements predominantly in eyes treated within 1 year of onset, while those with longstanding bilateral disease showed minimal change at 36 months.[6566676869] Subsequent Phase III double-masked, sham-placebo-controlled, multicentric trials, including REVERSE, RESCUE, and REFLECT, confirmed the safety and efficacy of rAAV2/2-ND4 (lenadogene nolparvovec) in patients treated within 12 months of onset.[7071727374757677] In the REVERSE trial, 37 patients with bilateral vision loss, duration of 6–12 months, and in the RESCUE trial, 38 patients with bilateral vision loss, duration of ≤6 months, were treated with unilateral IVT injection. The REVERSE study group showed bilateral improvements in vision, contrast sensitivity, and visual field. By 96-week post-injection, the mean change in logMAR acuity from baseline was −0.31 (equivalent to + 15 Early treatment diabetic retinopathy study (ETDRS) letters) in treated eyes and −0.60 (+13 ETDRS letters) in sham-treated eyes. In spite of earlier treatment, patients in the RESCUE group did not show better visual recovery than the REVERSE group. It was speculated that the presence of disc edema and axoplasmic stasis could hamper the diffusion of the viral vector and hence the gene delivery in the early phase of disease.

The RESTORE study demonstrated sustained bilateral visual improvement over 5 years. Due to the differences in vision loss duration at the time of gene therapy administration, a locally estimated scatterplot smoothing nonparametric local regression model was used. It showed a progressive and sustained improvement in Best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) from 12 to 51.5 months from the onset of vision loss.[76] Compared to LHON natural history studies, there was a statistically and clinically relevant improvement of BCVA of −0.33 (+16.5 ETDRS) in treated eyes at 48 months after the onset of vision loss (P < 0.01). Also importantly, the treatment effect was durable, with BCVA at the last follow-up remaining statistically and clinically significant when adjusted for age and duration of follow-up.[7778]

Mitochondria-Targeted AAV Vectors (MTS-AAV)

MTS-AAV vectors deliver genetic material directly to mitochondria, bypassing the need for nuclear recoding, and have shown restoration of mitochondrial respiration in preclinical LHON models.[79]

Gene-Editing Technologies

Emerging gene-editing tools, including zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs) and TALENs, selectively target and reduce mutant mtDNA.[8081828384] While promising, their application is limited in homoplasmic LHON cases, where all mtDNA copies carry the mutation.

Mutation-Independent Therapy

These therapies enhance mitochondrial function and reduce oxidative stress, improving RGC survival without targeting specific mutations. Strategies often involve antioxidants, vitamins, cofactors, and other supportive agents such as CoQ10, idebenone, L-carnitine, alpha-lipoic acid, minocycline, and glutathione.[85]

Unresolved Challenges in Genetic Therapy for LHON

Despite encouraging progress in gene-based therapies for LHON, several important challenges remain unresolved. One notable observation in clinical trials using rAAV2-ND4 vectors, including lenadogene nolparvovec, is the bilateral visual improvement following unilateral IVT injection, the mechanism of which remains unclear and may involve interocular vector transfer or systemic dissemination.[6886] Another critical issue is the optimal therapeutic window, as current evidence suggests that treatment within the 1st year of disease onset results in better visual outcomes, while the benefit in chronic cases remains uncertain.[87] Furthermore, most gene therapy trials have predominantly targeted the m.11778G>A mutation, limiting applicability to patients carrying other primary LHON mutations.[88] Long-term durability and safety of these therapies also require further evaluation, particularly with regard to sustained transgene expression and immune responses to viral vectors.[89] Addressing these unresolved questions will be essential for improving patient selection and expanding the therapeutic applicability of gene-based interventions in LHON.

Idebenone: Clinical evidence

Idebenone, a synthetic derivative of water-soluble CoQ10 analog, plays a vital role in the mitochondrial respiratory chain. Idebenone bypasses defective complex I to restore electron flow to complex III and improves ATP production.[9091] Unlike ubiquinone, idebenone crosses the blood–brain barrier. In the RHODOS trial, 85 patients with vision loss onset <5 years received 900 mg/day idebenone or placebo for 24 weeks. By 24 weeks, patients who received idebenone showed better BCVA than the placebo group. While the primary endpoint was not statistically significant,[92] follow-up studies demonstrated long-term benefit, particularly in patients with m.11778G> or m.3460G>A mutation, with mean BCVA improvement of −0.228 logMAR (+11 letters; P = 0.0011).[93] Early initiation of idebenone treatment, in particular when the RGC loss is less, can be associated with better outcomes. Excluding patients with [ND6] m.14484T>C mutation, as they are possible to have spontaneous vision recovery, the study group showed greater treatment response. In the RHODOS–OFU, the RHODOS follow-up study assessed patients with a median duration of 30 months after they stopped using idebenone and a placebo. The treatment response and BCVA improvement between both groups revealed that the idebenone group had better vision recovery than the placebo group. Carelli et al.[93] and the observational study done by the drug manufacturers as part of [EAP], an expanded access program, started. Idebenone treatment for patients with disease onset for a mean duration of 12 months was 25.6 months (2.4–70.4 months). A clinically relevant recovery (CRR) of BCVA, defined as an improvement from off-chart acuity (>1.68 logMAR) to on-chart by at least one full line (+5 ETDRS letters) or an improvement in an on-chart BCVA by at least two lines (+10 ETDRS letters; 0.2 logMAR), was observed in 40/87 patients (46.0%). Also, prolonged idebenone therapy (18–24 months) yields clinically meaningful recovery.[9495] In 2015, the European Medicines Agency approved idebenone for LHON under exceptional circumstances.[96] The LEROS study, a 2-year Phase IV externally controlled open-label trial, included 199 patients with disease onset less than 5 years and continued treatment for more than 24 months. The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients with clinically relevant benefit (CRB), defined as either a CRR or a clinically relevant stabilization (CRS) of BCVA (maintenance of BCVA <1.0 logMAR) or both. In patients treated within 12 months of symptom onset, 42.3% (60/142) of treated eyes experienced a CRB at 12 months, compared to 20.7% (40/193) in a matched external natural history cohort (P = 0.002)[88] As per the 2017 “International Consensus Statement on the Clinical and Therapeutic Management of Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy,” idebenone should be started as soon as possible in the dose of 900 mg/day in patients with disease onset less than 1 year, and for at least 1 year to assess response. If there is a CRR, treatment should be continued for another 1 year.

Clinical Diagnostic and Management Algorithm

The diagnostic evaluation of suspected LHON begins with clinical recognition of characteristic features, including subacute, painless bilateral or sequential central vision loss, dyschromatopsia, and central or centrocecal scotomas. Fundus examination may reveal optic disc hyperemia with or without peripapillary telangiectasia during the acute phase. In patients with a high index of clinical suspicion, first-line genetic testing should include targeted analysis for the three primary mtDNA mutations, namely m.11778G>A (MT-ND4), m.3460G>A (MT-ND1), and m.14484T>C (MT-ND6), which account for the majority of LHON cases. If these mutations are not identified, extended mitochondrial genome sequencing should be considered, followed by targeted nuclear gene panel testing or WES to evaluate for alternative genetic causes, including DNAJC30-associated optic neuropathy and other LHON-like disorders. Following molecular confirmation, patients should undergo risk stratification based on disease duration (less than or greater than 12 months from onset), underlying mutation type, age at presentation, and baseline visual acuity, as these factors may influence prognosis and therapeutic decision-making.

Controversies identified

Despite significant advances in the management of LHON, several important controversies and unresolved issues remain. One of the most intriguing observations from gene therapy trials is the occurrence of bilateral visual improvement following unilateral IVT administration of gene therapy, the underlying mechanism of which remains incompletely understood. The extent of visual benefit attributable to idebenone therapy also continues to be debated, as clinical studies have reported variable outcomes depending on mutation type, disease duration, and timing of treatment initiation. Another area of uncertainty concerns the optimal therapeutic window, with evidence suggesting greater efficacy when treatment is administered early, although the precise timeframe for maximal benefit has not been clearly established. Furthermore, most gene therapy studies have focused on the m.11778G>A (MT-ND4) mutation, and it remains unclear whether similar therapeutic approaches can be effectively applied to patients harboring other primary LHON mutations. Long-term safety, durability of response, and potential off-target effects of mitochondrial gene transfer also require further evaluation through extended follow-up studies and larger clinical trials.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Current Literature

While there have been many advancements achieved in LHON, some disadvantages still prevail. Some gene therapy and treatment trials had a small sample size, were unblinded, and thus had little power and external validity. Spontaneous improvement that may occur in some mutations (for instance, m.14484T>C) could also complicate the interpretation of results. Variability in metrics (such as using logMAR or ETDRS letters), as well as variability in study follow-ups and when treatments were applied, further impede comparative analysis. Most gene therapy trials have concentrated on targeting the m.11778G>A mutation, reducing the ability to generalize for other primary mutations. Long-term safety studies exceeding 5 years are also insufficient, along with studies on mitochondrial haplogroups and nuclear modifiers, which are specific to some populations. Nevertheless, an increasing amount of multicenter randomized controlled trials and follow-ups helps establish evidence and shape future approaches to treatment.

Future Directions

Future research in LHON should focus on developing therapeutic strategies that extend beyond the currently targeted MT-ND4 mutation and are applicable to a broader spectrum of disease-causing variants. Advances in mitochondrial genome editing technologies, including precision gene-editing approaches, hold promise for directly correcting pathogenic mtDNA mutations and may provide more durable therapeutic solutions. The identification and validation of reliable biomarkers capable of predicting disease progression and treatment response will be critical for optimizing patient selection and individualized management. In addition, combination treatment strategies integrating gene therapy with neuroprotective or antioxidant agents warrant investigation to determine whether synergistic effects can further improve visual outcomes. Finally, long-term post-marketing surveillance and extended follow-up studies exceeding 10 years are essential to evaluate the durability, efficacy, and safety of emerging therapies, particularly mitochondrial gene transfer approaches.

Conclusion

LHON is a mitochondrial optic neuropathy primarily affecting young men, leading to profound central vision loss. While historically untreatable, advances in gene-targeted and mutation-independent therapies offer new prospects. Gene therapy with lenadogene nolparvovec has demonstrated sustained visual improvement, especially when administered within 1 year of onset. Spontaneous recovery is more common in patients with the m.14484T>C mutation but rare in those with m.11778G>A or m.3460G>A mutation, underscoring the importance of early intervention. Idebenone remains the most evidence-based mutation-independent therapy, particularly when initiated early and continued long-term. Ongoing and future randomized controlled trials are needed to optimize treatment strategies and improve visual outcomes in this challenging disorder.
